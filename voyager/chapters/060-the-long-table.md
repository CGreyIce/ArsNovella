Floor 6 wanted water, and it wanted it from him.

He'd forgotten that. Of all the things he remembered about the Glass Waste, the heat and the glare and the mirages, he'd somehow forgotten the thirst. Or he'd remembered it the way you remember a fact, *it's hot on six, bring water*. He hadn't remembered it the way it was. A thing that started in the back of your throat an hour after you'd drunk, and climbed. A dry tightness behind the eyes. Lips that split if you smiled. The way, by the middle of the first afternoon, it was the only thing you could think about.

He walked west from the stair door with the Galeward Helm on and the barrel lid on his arm, and the sun hit the back of his neck like a hand.

There was no sky. Not the way there'd been on five. On five the sky had been blue, deep, with clouds going across it and real weather in it. Here it was white. White from edge to edge, so bright you couldn't look at any part of it. And in the middle of the white, so high he couldn't find it without turning his whole head, a sun that *hurt*. Not warm. Not like the sunrise on the Spindle. It sat on his shoulders and on his back and on the top of the helm, and pressed.

And the ground threw it back.

The dunes were glass. Crushed glass, ground fine as sand in some places and coarse as gravel in others. Blown by the hot wind into long slow hills that went out in every direction to the white horizon. Every grain of it caught the sun. Every grain threw it back up at him. The whole Waste glittered, all the time, in sheets and flashes, so that walking across it was like walking across the surface of a lake at noon. Without the helm he wouldn't have been able to open his eyes. With it, through the narrow slit, it was a dazzle of white and silver that made his head ache.

And everywhere, on the horizon, things shimmered. A lake, blue, with trees round it. A city of white towers. A line of camels, walking, upside down, above the dunes. He knew what they were. He walked past them.

"Water," said Alea, on his shoulder, for the fourth time that hour.

"I know."

"You've got a third of a skin left."

"I know, Alea."

"The town's still a day."

"I *know*." He stopped, and drank, one mouthful, warm and tasting of leather. Then he put the stopper back in before his hands could decide to drink the rest. "I remember."

He did. He remembered it now, all at once, the way you remember a smell. The first time, they'd come up the Spindle stair in the morning, all thirty-four of them. They'd walked out onto the Glass in the middle of the day, the worst possible time, because nobody had known. By the afternoon Gigi had stopped sweating, which was the bad sign. Hyvred had carried her the last six hours to the town on her back, both of them sunburned so badly they'd peeled for a week. After that they'd learned to walk at night. Everyone did, on six. You walked at night and you slept in the day, in whatever shade you could find, and you never, ever went anywhere without more water than you thought you'd need.

He'd come up at dawn, this time, which was better. But he'd still come up in the day.

He walked until the sun went down. Then he walked through the night, under real stars, with the glass crunching under his boots and the heat coming up off the ground for hours after dark. In the night it was almost bearable. The mirages went away. The glass stopped glittering. It was just dark hills, and cold stars, and his own breath in the helm.

He reached Deepwell an hour after dawn on the ninety-seventh day, with half a mouthful left.

***

Deepwell was a hole in the world with a town in it.

You didn't see it until you were nearly on top of it. The dunes just ended. In front of you, in the glass, there was a pit. Round, two hundred metres across, going down. The walls were glass too, but old glass, fused and dark and smooth, as if something very hot had happened here a very long time ago. And all the way down the walls of the pit, in rings, cut into the dark glass, were houses. Doors and windows and ledges and stairs. Ropes and pulleys and baskets going up and down between them. People moving on the ledges, small, in pale loose clothes and wide hats.

And at the very bottom, three hundred metres down, in the deep shade, there was water. A circle of black water, still as glass, with a great stone wheel turning at its edge. A chain of buckets went up from it, up and up the side of the pit, all the way to the top, slopping.

He stood on the lip of the pit and looked down at it with half a mouthful of water in his skin. He had to sit down for a minute, because his legs weren't working.

"Deepwell," said Alea quietly.

"Deepwell."

"It's exactly the same."

He looked down at the ledges, and the people, and the turning wheel at the bottom. It was. Exactly. Every door, every rope. He'd lived on the third ring down for five weeks, the first time. In a room with a round window that looked across the pit at a family who'd sung every evening, in harmony, at dusk.

He wondered if they still did. He wondered if it was the same family. It had to be. It was a month earlier than the last time he'd stood here, and a different life. Of course it was the same.

But then on floor 5 Hessa had looked at him like a stranger. On floor 5 the bird had landed. *It's all the same. Until it isn't.*

He went down.

***

The warden of the well was a big, slow, quiet man called Ostrel, with arms burned brown and a face like a piece of old leather. He sat at the top of the bucket chain in a little stone booth with a ledger and a ladle, and sold water by the cup.

"Two coppers a cup," said Ostrel. "Four a skin. You're new."

"I'm new."

"Up the stair from the birds?" Ostrel looked at him. At the white helm, the barrel lid, the dust of the Glass on him from head to foot. "Alone?"

"Alone."

Ostrel ladled out a cup of water, black and cold from the bottom of the pit, and put it on the ledge of the booth in front of him.

"First one's free," he said. "For anybody who walks in alone in the day." He looked at Ice with his slow brown eyes. "Most of 'em don't walk in. Most of 'em get carried."

Ice took the cup. He took the helm off to drink it. He saw Ostrel's eyes go over his face, the black hair and the teal, the dust and the cracked lips. Then he stopped seeing anything, because the water was cold, and real, and the best thing he'd tasted in either of his lives.

He bought four skins. He paid double. Ostrel didn't ask why.

***

He spent four days in Deepwell, learning six again, and on the hundred-and-first he went to look at the Still Water.

He'd known where it was. Of course he had. It was in the middle of the Glass, two nights east of Deepwell, where the dunes went down into a great shallow bowl. In the middle of the bowl, in a ring of black glass rocks, there was a pool. Perfectly round. Perfectly still. The only open water on the whole floor that wasn't three hundred metres down a hole. It was a deep, impossible blue, like a piece of the sky on floor 5 that had fallen into the desert and been left behind.

And in the pool lived the Prism.

He remembered the Prism very clearly. He'd stood in front of it the first time with his shield up for fifteen minutes and it had nearly killed four people. It was a thing of living glass, clear and many-sided, the size of a house. It sat in the middle of the pool with the sun shining into it. And it *bent* the sun. Took all that white light pouring down on the Glass and bent it, through its faces, into beams. Thin white lines of light that came out of it in every direction and burned whatever they touched. Glass dunes ran like water where the beams went across them. Shields went red.

They'd beaten it, the first time, at night. Of course they had. No sun, no beams. They'd waited for dark and walked in under the stars and fought it in the cool, and it had been almost easy.

"*Don't*," said Alea.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You were thinking it. *At night.*" She was sitting on his shoulder as they came over the last dune into the bowl. "That's the old way. You fight it at night and it learns night. And then on seven the thing's going to come out in the dark—"

"I know, Alea." He'd known since the Spindle. *Every floor I clear the old way, I'm teaching it.* "I'm just going to look."

He came down into the bowl in the late afternoon. The sun was low and red in the west and the Glass was throwing it back gold. The ring of black rocks round the Still Water stood up out of the bowl a kilometre ahead of him, and in the middle of it, the blue.

And between him and it, on the glass, there was a table.

***

He stopped walking.

It was a long table. Wood. Plain, old, heavy, the kind you'd find in a farmhouse kitchen, or the back room of an inn. Scrubbed pale. It stood on the glass of the bowl about two hundred metres ahead of him, in the gold light, with long benches down both sides of it. Under a canopy of white cloth on poles that moved a little in the hot wind.

He knew that table.

It was the table at the Copper Spoon. Mother Crane's long table, in the room at the bottom of the crooked street in Hearthstead, on floor 1. The one she'd fed thirty-six of them at, on credit, the first time. The one she'd fed thirty-five at, this time, on the first night, while he peeled her onions at dawn and went. He'd know it anywhere. He'd sat at it a hundred times.

And there were people at it.

All of them.

He saw Gabe first, because Gabe was always the one you saw first. At the near end of the table, big and loud, with his elbows on the wood, laughing at something with his head back. The way he laughed when something had really got him. Beside him, Deston, calm, with his hands round a cup, half smiling at Gabe the way he always half smiled at Gabe. Then Xav. Then Azac, in the middle of telling a story with both hands. Then Yan, with Suzu round his neck. Then Alice, with her crown on, gold beads catching the light. Then Calvin, with both arms. Rachael beside him. Dennis, arguing. Drew at the head of the table with his knuckles off, for once. Chris with his notebook. Hyvred, Bee, Kei, Hako, Gigi. Salty, Jared, Malcolm, John, Rui Xiang. Winter. Niko. Maigo. Ina with his mask pushed up. Tiger, Wendy, Cursiffe, Newish, Aleaka, Milli, Higu, Xola, Chi.

And at the far end of the table, in the gold light under the white canopy, Thea.

Thirty-five of them. All at one table. All alive. Talking, laughing, passing bread. The way they'd been in the white room, the first minute, before anybody had died. The way they'd never been again, the first time, not once, not all of them together, after floor 4.

And one empty place on the bench. Between Gabe and Thea. Waiting.

He didn't know he was walking until he was halfway there.

***

"*Ice.*"

Alea's voice. A long way off. He heard it the way you hear someone calling you from another room when you're asleep.

"*Ice, stop. Stop. There's nothing there.*"

He could hear them now. That was the thing. He was close enough to hear them. Gabe's laugh, big and stupid. Azac's voice going *and THEN* the way it always went before the best part. Calvin, too loud. Newish being rude to someone. Dennis arguing with somebody about who was paying. Hyvred in capitals. All of it, all at once, the sound of thirty-five people at a table, and he hadn't heard that sound in ten years and a hundred days.

"*Ice, I can't see them. There's nobody there. Ice, look at me, there's nothing, it's a hole.*"

Gabe looked up. Saw him coming. And his whole face lit up, the way it had lit up in the white room the first minute, *Dec—*

"*Dec!*" said Gabe. "There you are. Where've you *been*, you idiot? Sit down, we saved you a seat."

He kept walking.

He knew. He knew the whole time. That was the worst part, afterwards, lying in the dark trying to understand it. He *knew*. He knew it was the Glass. He knew it was floor 6, and a mirage, and the Still Water was right behind it, and the Prism. He knew Gabe was on floor 5, leaning on the wind on the Kitecliff, with a twist of Pell's herb in his pocket from Nan Gully. He knew Thea was on floor 5 with the lantern, looking at the Spindle.

He knew it, and he kept walking. Because they were *there*. All of them. At one table. Alive. And they'd saved him a seat.

Deston looked up and smiled at him. The real smile, the rare one, the one Deston saved. And held out his hand across the table, palm up, the way he held his hands out when he was linking all of them. As if to say: *come on. Sit down. We're all here.*

Ice reached out and took it.

***

It cut him.

Deston's hand wasn't a hand. It was glass. It was the edge of something sharper than any blade he'd ever held, and it went into his palm and across it, from the base of his thumb to the edge, and opened it like paper.

He felt it go in. He felt it go *deep*.

And the table changed.

Not much. Not all at once. But Deston's smile stayed exactly the same while his hand was cutting, and Ice saw that. It didn't change at all. It was a picture of a smile. And beyond Deston, at the far end of the table, Thea looked up at him from under the white canopy. She opened her mouth to say something.

She opened her mouth and nothing came out.

He stood there with his hand open and bleeding and looked at her. Her mouth moving. No sound. Three words, maybe four. The words he'd never heard, on the hundredth floor, the last thing she ever said. Being said again. And nothing coming out of her, because—

Because *he* didn't know them.

Because whatever this was, it didn't have anything that he didn't have.

"*ICE.*"

The light hit him in the face.

Teal and white and blinding. Alea, right in front of the slit of the helm. Not small. Not a fox on his shoulder. She'd flared up the way she'd never flared in ten years, the whole of herself thrown into light, straight into his eyes. He couldn't see the table. He couldn't see anything. Only her, burning, so bright the slit of the helm turned white.

"*BACK*," said Alea. "*Ice, step BACK, step back NOW, there's NOTHING there, it's a HOLE, it's a hole shaped like a table and it's EATING you—*"

He stepped back.

He didn't decide to. Her voice went into him the way it had in the House of Ash, under everything, and his feet moved. One step. Two. Five. Blind, with the light in his eyes and his hand pouring blood. On the tenth step back he heard the laughing stop. Not fade. *Stop*, all at once, like a door shutting. On the twentieth step back, Alea went dark.

He was standing on the glass in the bowl in the red evening, on his own, with his hand bleeding onto the dune.

There was no table.

There was no canopy. No benches. No bread. Nothing. Just the glass of the bowl going away gold toward the ring of black rocks and the blue water. And in the air above the place where the table had been, a shimmer. A ripple in the heat. Like a mirage. Except it wasn't moving with the heat. It was standing still in the middle of the moving air. Watching him.

A window came up. Slowly. As if it wasn't sure it was supposed to.

He didn't remember getting back to the edge of the bowl. He remembered Alea talking the whole way, low and steady, *left foot, right foot, keep going, don't look back, left foot*. He remembered sitting down hard on the glass at the top of the dune with the sun gone and the stars coming out. And he remembered his hand.

It was open across the palm. Deep, and clean, the way only glass cuts. Bleeding steadily into the dune.

He put his other hand over it, and called the ember.

It came, the way it always came. Warm, from behind his breastbone, down his arm, into the cut. *Mend Self.* And the cut began to close.

It hurt.

It always hurt. He'd stopped noticing years ago, the first time. It was just the price of the thing. You took the wound, and then you took it again, slower, coming back together. But tonight, sitting on the glass in the dark, he noticed. He felt every bit of it close, from the inside out. The deep part first, then the shallow, then the skin. Pulling. Knitting. Like a hand slowly being sewn shut with a hot needle. And then, after, the faint echo, as if the cut had been made a second time somewhere further away. He sat with his teeth together and let it.

When it was closed, he looked at it.

There was a line across his palm. From the base of his thumb to the edge. Pink, raised, ridged. The ember had closed it. The ember had closed every wound he'd had since the chapel, clean, without a mark. But it hadn't taken this one away. It was still there. A mark.

A scar.

He sat on the top of the dune and looked at his left palm. The palm where the ember had sat for twelve days on floor 3, before he'd put it into his chest. The hand he'd reached out across the table to take Deston's.

"*From six up,*" he said. His voice was very strange. "*A cut could leave a scar.*"

"Yes," said Alea.

"I said that. On five. On the first night. I was telling you about the floors."

"I know."

"I didn't think—" He stopped.

"You didn't think it'd be the first thing six did to you," said Alea quietly. "I know."

He closed his hand on it. It ached.

***

"It wasn't there," said Alea, much later. In the dark, on the dune, with the stars out and the heat going out of the glass.

"I know."

"The first time. Nothing was there. Not in the bowl. Not anywhere on six. The Prism and the Glass and the shardbacks, and that's all. I've got every step logged." Her voice was very small. "Ten years, Ice. I've never seen anything like it. Not on any floor. It's not in anything I've got."

"It's new."

"It's *new*." She was shaking. He could feel it, on his shoulder, a fine shiver of light. "The Squall was changed. This wasn't changed. This was *made*. For here. For now." She stopped. "It doesn't have a level. Everything has a level. It doesn't have anything except a name."

"*The Wanting*," said Ice.

He looked down at his scarred palm in the starlight.

"It showed me all of them," he said. "At Mother Crane's table. All thirty-five. Alive. And a seat. Between Gabe and Thea." He swallowed. "And Thea opened her mouth to say something and nothing came out. Because I don't know what she said. It didn't have anything I don't have."

Alea didn't say anything.

"It knew all their names," said Ice. "It had everyone in the right place. Gabe laughing like that. Deston's hands. It knew exactly—" He stopped. "It knew exactly what I'd want. More than anything. Out of everything in the world."

"Ice," said Alea.

He knew what she was going to say. He'd known it since the table. Since the moment he'd seen thirty-five faces in the gold light and thought, *how does it know all of them?*

"You told it," said Alea, very gently. "In the House of Ash. On the fifty-eighth day. You knelt in front of the grille and you set down every name. Every one. Where they sat. How they laughed. What they were doing when you lost them." Her voice cracked. "*To the last name.* You told the tower exactly what you carry."

He sat on the dune in the dark with his hand closed on the scar.

"And it listened," said Alea. "And then it went up two floors and made something out of it. And put it in front of the water."

The stars were very bright over the Glass. Out in the bowl below him, in the dark, he couldn't see the shimmer any more. But he knew it was there. Standing still in the moving air between him and the Still Water. Waiting, with a long table and a seat.

"I'd do it again," he said. "The trial. The hard one. I said that."

"I know you did."

"I still would." He opened his hand and looked at the scar. "I'd just like to have known what it would cost."

"You never know what it'll cost," said Alea. "That's what makes it the hard one."
