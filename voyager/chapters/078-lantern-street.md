Deston still had the note.

He hadn't told anybody. It wasn't a pocket. He'd been very clear with himself about that, on floor 3, the morning they left Reedmoor. He'd stood in front of the smoking-hall door where it had been pinned for six weeks on its nail, *be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.*, with everybody's eyes going over it every time they came in or out. And he'd taken it down.

He'd told himself it was because the eel-woman would throw it away. It was a scrap of card, the back of a starter card from a white room, with a fingertip's worth of writing on it, soft with handling. She'd have used it to light the stove.

That wasn't why.

He'd folded it once, the way it had been folded when he woke up with it under his hand. And put it in his shirt pocket. The same pocket. And it had been there ever since. Floor 4, floor 5, floor 6. Against his chest. Not a pocket, he told himself. A place to keep it.

He took it out now, at the window of the inn in Evermorrow, at three in the morning by the clock that had stopped meaning anything. He unfolded it and looked at it in the light from the square.

*be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.*

The *b*, with the open loop at the top, the way Declan had always written it. Gabe had confirmed it, on floor 3, in the smoking hall, in front of everyone. *That's his b. He's done his b like that since we were thirteen.*

Thirteen.

***

They'd met in a choir.

People didn't believe that, when he told them. Deston, the tall calm one, the one who held things together. Gabe, the big loud one who'd hit you for a joke and then hug you. And Declan, who couldn't sit still, who talked to everyone, who'd been the first to make the whole room laugh on the first day and the last to stop. Two thirteen-year-olds in the second row of a secondary-school choir, because somebody had signed them up, or because it got them out of something worse. Deston couldn't remember now which. And then, two years later, Gabe, thirteen and loud and new, shoved into the second row beside them because there was nowhere else to put him.

He remembered the second row, though. Declan on his left, and later Gabe on his right. Declan singing the wrong words on purpose until the teacher looked, and then the right ones, perfectly, with a face like an angel. Gabe trying not to laugh so hard he went purple. Deston standing between them, the tallest, being the one who kept a straight face, because somebody had to.

Thirteen years, for him and Declan. Half their lives. Eleven for Gabe, and he'd never let either of them forget he was the baby.

*He's basically family*, Deston had said once, to somebody at a party who'd asked how they all knew each other. *Brothers from a different mother.* And Gabe had overheard and said *I'm the different mother*, and Declan had laughed so hard he'd had to sit down on the floor.

And then the white room. And a fingertip of writing on the back of a card under his hand. And a hundred and fifty-two days.

Deston folded the note again and put it back in his pocket.

"You've still got it," said Gabe.

He hadn't heard him come up. Gabe could be quiet when he wanted to, for a big man. He was standing behind Deston in the dark of the inn, in his shirtsleeves, without the coat for once. His hair was sticking up on one side, as if he'd tried to sleep and given up.

"Yeah," said Deston.

"I thought you left it on three."

"I took it down." Deston didn't look round. "The morning we left. Before anybody was up."

Gabe was quiet for a moment.

"That's a pocket," he said.

"It's not a pocket. It's a place to keep it."

"It's a *pocket*, Dest."

"It's not *hiding* anything. Everybody's read it. It's just—" Deston stopped. He looked out at the square, at the clocktower, at the dark bells. "It's the first thing he said to anybody. After. In this whole place. It's the first thing he said, and he said it to me." He swallowed. "I wasn't going to leave it on a nail for an eel-woman to light the stove with."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he pulled up the chair next to Deston's and sat down in it, heavily, the way he sat down in everything. And put his elbows on the windowsill. And looked out at the square too.

They sat there.

"Do you remember the choir?" said Gabe, after a while.

Deston almost laughed. "I was just thinking about it."

"He sang the wrong words."

"Every week."

"*Every* week." Gabe shook his head. "And then the teacher'd look, and he'd just—*switch*. Like nothing happened. Face like a little angel." He was quiet. "I used to nearly wet myself."

"You went purple."

"I went *purple*." Gabe laughed, very softly, in the dark. Then he stopped. "Dest. What if he's different?"

Deston looked at him.

"Hyv says his light's gone small," said Gabe. He was looking at the square, not at Deston. "For most of six. Smaller than she's ever seen it. Dennis says he laughed, behind the door. But Dennis also said Rachael cried, and Rachael doesn't cry." He swallowed. "The man in the helm, on six. He didn't say anything to any of them. Three words, and then he ran. Hako says he moves like somebody who's been doing it for ten years. Thea won't say anything at all, she just looks at that lantern." He stopped. "What if we go in tomorrow and it's not *him*? What if it's him, but it's not—I don't know. What if he's not the guy in the second row any more?"

Deston didn't answer for a long time.

He thought about it properly. He always did. That was the thing he did, in the group. Think about it properly, and then say the true thing calmly, so everybody could hold onto it.

He thought about a white room. A fingertip on a card. *be careful.* He thought about a lantern left lit on a bench. A coat folded on another bench, that fitted Gabe exactly. Onion skins on corks. A thing on a roof that nobody had to meet. Water, paid for, seventy cups. A shape on the floor of a furnace hall, where a man had stood in a fire twice, so that by the time thirty-five people got there it had met somebody who knew how to stand in front of it.

*Train longer on the 3rd floor.*

"He's not going to be the guy in the second row," said Deston.

Gabe looked at him.

"Nobody is," said Deston. "You're not. I'm not. We've been in a tower for a hundred and fifty-two days. I've watched people die on—" He stopped. "I've watched people *nearly* die. You've stood in a fire. Rae cries now." He was quiet. "We're all different. He's probably more different than any of us. He's been on his own." He looked out at the square. "But he wrote me a note, Gabe. On the first day. Before anything. He could have written anything. He could have written *don't look for me*. He could have written *sorry*. He wrote *be careful*." His voice did something, and he let it. "That's him. That's the guy in the second row. He was always looking out for whoever was standing next to him."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he put his big hand on the back of Deston's neck, the way he'd done since they were thirteen, heavy and warm, and left it there.

"Okay," said Gabe.

"Okay."

"Second row," said Gabe.

"Second row," said Deston.

They sat at the window until the clock struck twelve and the world fell over and it was morning.

***

The inn woke up round them in the dark-gone-dawn without anybody having to say anything.

Deston went round them all. He didn't decide to. It was a thing he'd done every morning on floor 1, in Hearthstead, at the long table at the Copper Spoon, before anyone had worked out who was in charge. He'd go round. Count. Put a hand on a shoulder. *You all right? You eaten?* Drew was the general now and had been for a hundred and thirteen days. This was older than that.

Rachael, at the end of a bench, pinning her hair up, with her frown on and her eyes still red from the bakery. She caught his hand as he went past and squeezed it, once, hard, without looking up. Calvin beside her, lacing his boots very slowly and very carefully, the way you do something when it's the only thing you can control. Dennis, not eating, which had never happened before in recorded history. Azac, telling Hako a story in a low voice, and Deston could hear from the shape of it that it was about the end of something. *And so our heroes went down the street.*

Hyvred, by the window, not able to sit still, looking out at the square with her eyes moving. Reading the light. *It's so bright on Lantern Street, Dest. I can see it from here.*

Hana, with her crown on, with Milli's hand in hers. She looked up at him as he went past and said, very quietly, "What do we *say*?"

Deston thought about it.

"Nothing," he said. "We don't say anything. We let him."

Hana looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded.

And Thea, at the end of the room, sitting on a bench with the lantern in her lap. Xav was sitting beside her. Not talking. Just sitting, with his hands on his knees. Chris across from them, his notebook closed. Winter beside Chris with her arms folded. Hako, when Azac's story ended, getting up and going and sitting with them too. And Kei. And Hyvred, from the window, turning round and looking at that bench for a second before she looked back out.

Deston stopped.

There was something there. Between them. He could feel it the way he felt the gold, sometimes, before it came out of his hands. Thirteen people who weren't looking at each other in quite the same way the rest of the room weren't looking at each other. A held breath, shared.

He didn't know what it was. He'd known these people for years, most of them. He'd held all thirty-five of them together with gold threads in a furnace hall. And there was something in that room he didn't know.

He thought about asking. He thought about it for a long moment, standing in the middle of the inn in the grey morning.

Then he looked at Thea's face, bent over the lantern. And at Xav's, beside her, very still.

Not today, he thought. Whatever it is. Today's for something else.

He went and found his boots.

***

Thirty-five people went down Lantern Street at dawn.

Calvin first. He walked. Deston watched him do it, from a few places back. He watched him not run. Rachael was beside him, not holding his collar for once, just beside him. Calvin had his hand out at his side the way he always did, fingers spread, for the people behind. But he didn't say *look at my back*. He didn't say anything at all.

Then Deston and Gabe.

Nobody had decided that. It had just happened, at the inn door, when they all came out into the grey light. Everybody had sort of stopped and looked at the two of them. And then made a gap, without anybody saying anything, so that they went out together. Side by side. The way they'd stood in a second row a long time ago, except that there was a gap between them where somebody should have been.

Then everybody else.

They went across the square in the dawn. The market was setting up round them the way it set up every morning. The striped awnings going up. The cheesemonger setting out her wheels. The dog coming out from under the butcher's table with a sausage, right on time, and running, and the butcher shouting the word. Nobody looked at any of it. Thirty-five people walked through the middle of the market in silence, and the town didn't notice, because the town never noticed anything. Every morning was the first morning.

Past the well. Into Lantern Street.

It was narrow. Narrower than Deston had expected, from Dennis's description. Tall houses leaning in on both sides, their upper floors nearly touching overhead, so that the street underneath was all cool grey shadow and the sky was just a strip. Thirty-five people filled it. They walked in a long line between the walls, shoulder to shoulder in places. The sound of their boots on the cobbles came back off the walls, loud, in the morning quiet.

And the smell.

Deston smelled it before he saw anything. Rosemary. Bread. An oven, roaring, somewhere close. It came down the street at them, warm, in the cool shadow.

Beside him, Gabe made a sound.

"*Rosemary*," said Gabe.

Deston didn't say anything. He couldn't. He didn't know why rosemary. He didn't know why it went through him like that. He just knew it did, and that Gabe's hand had come up and grabbed his sleeve and was holding on.

And then they could see it. Halfway down. On the left. A little shop with a bow window full of bread, golden and brown, and a door.

A blue door.

***

Calvin stopped in front of it. Everybody stopped behind him, in a long line down Lantern Street. Thirty-five people in a narrow street in the dawn, in front of a blue door, not saying anything.

Inside, through the bow window, past the loaves, Deston could see the shop. A counter. Shelves. The glow of an oven at the back. A woman behind the counter, with flour on her arms and her hair tied up, standing very still, looking out of the window at them. At all of them. With her floury hands flat on the counter.

And behind her, at the back of the shop, a door. Shut. Into a kitchen.

Deston looked at it.

A line of light under it, from the oven on the other side. And in the line of light, at the bottom, a shadow. Somebody standing on the other side of the door. Very close to it. Very still.

He felt it start.

He didn't decide to. That was the thing about Link, it had always been the thing. On the first night on floor 1, he hadn't decided to link anybody. He'd just put his hands out, the way he put his hands out when people needed holding together, and the gold had come. *What one of you has, all of you may share.* He'd been doing it ever since. In fights, in the Kiln, in the bowl. Gold threads from his palms to everybody who needed them.

He felt it start now, and he let it.

The gold came out of his hands. He felt it go. Not to one person. Out, along the street, behind him, to everybody. One thread to Calvin, in front. One to Rachael. One to Gabe, beside him, so close it was barely a thread at all. One to Xav, standing very still a few places back. One to Thea, at the back of the line, with her lantern. Out and out, down the whole length of Lantern Street, until there were thirty-four gold threads running from his palms through the cool morning shadow. Linking all of them. Every one. So that what one of them had, all of them could share.

He felt all of it come back down the threads. All thirty-four of them, at once. Fear. Hope. Calvin's wanting-to-run. Gabe's held breath. Thea's stillness, steady as a lamp. Rachael's wet eyes. Hyvred's light-seeing. Xav, waiting. All of it, coming back to him along the gold, and going out again to everybody, shared, so that nobody had to hold any of it on their own.

And one more thread.

He hadn't sent it. He didn't know where it had come from. It came out of his palms with all the others, gold and fine, and didn't go back along the street to any of them. It went *forward*. Across the cobbles. Under the blue door. Across the floor of the shop, past the woman at the counter. Toward the kitchen door at the back, and the line of light under it, and the shadow standing in the line of light.

And stopped there. At the door. Reaching.

Thirty-fifth thread. Gold. Reaching for someone who wasn't linked to anything.

Deston stood in Lantern Street with his hands out and thirty-five threads running out of them, and couldn't see properly any more.

***

Gabe stepped forward.

He let go of Deston's sleeve. He went up the one stone step to the blue door and stood in front of it. Big and broad in the morning shadow, in the wolf coat. He'd put it on again at the inn without saying anything, in the heat of a spring morning, too warm for it. With his own shield on his arm and the other one on his back. Dark grey, with a lamp in the middle and a rim the colour of old dried blood.

He put his hand up. Flat. Toward the door.

He stopped, with it a few centimetres from the wood.

Deston watched him. The whole street watched him. Thirty-four people and a gold thread under the door.

Gabe turned his head and looked back at Deston. His face was wet. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Deston knew that face. He'd known it since they were thirteen. Since the second row, with the teacher looking, and Declan singing the wrong words, and Gabe going purple. The face that said *I'm about to laugh or cry and I don't know which, and I need you to tell me it's all right.*

Deston took half a step back.

Just half. Just enough. So that Gabe was standing in front of the blue door on his own.

"This one's yours," said Deston quietly.

Gabe looked at him a moment longer.

Then he turned back to the door. And put his hand flat on the blue wood. And in the narrow street in the morning shadow, with thirty-four people behind him and rosemary in the air and a gold thread running under the door, Deston heard it.

A small sound. Very quiet. From Gabe's window. The sound a window makes when something arrives.

Gabe went completely still.
