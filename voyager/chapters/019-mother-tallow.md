The trick with Rimehounds was that you couldn't see them.

Not because they were invisible. Because they were white, on white, in a world that was mostly white. And they never, ever came at you from where you were looking. One of them would show itself at the front, low and snarling, ice-blue eyes fixed on you, making a great deal of noise. And while you were looking at that one, the other three would be coming round behind you through the snow without a sound, the way water comes round a stone.

He'd relearned that on the first afternoon, on an open slope, with teeth in the back of his leg. The first time round it had taken all thirty-six of them a week, and Wendy's terrible pencil drawing of a wolf going round a stick: *they always go round. Clockwise. Put your back to something.*

So now he put his back to something.

He found a tree, a big one, on the third morning, on the eastern slopes, and stood with his back flat against its trunk and his shield up and his sword low. When the first Rimehound showed itself in front of him, snarling, he didn't look at it. He looked at his belt.

The Bellwether's Bell hung there on its short leather loop, small and bronze, with its hairline crack. *Taunt reaches further. Rings once for each enemy that turns.*

He used Taunt.

The bell rang. Once, small and cracked. Twice. Three times. Four.

"Four," said Alea.

"Four," said Ice, and stopped looking for them. He knew where they'd be. Clockwise. He waited for the bell's last note to fade, counted two, and swung the shield out to his left, flat and hard, without looking. The Rimehound that had been coming round the trunk ran face first into it with a sound like a dropped bag of flour.

***

He killed a great many of them over the next three days.

He wasn't proud of it. It was just the work. You stood with your back to a tree and rang the little bell to count them, and you swung where they would be, not where they were. When one got through and opened your arm or your leg, you put your hand on your heart and stood in the light from the ember in your palm, and let it reach you. Then you did it again. It was the same as peeling onions. A small, exact job with a clear beginning and a clear end. You didn't think about it. You couldn't think about anything else.

On the evening of the fifth day, a window opened that he'd been waiting for.

The first time, he'd taken the normal one at every tier until the sixth, because everyone had, and because the hard ones had looked like suicide. ***

On the sixth day, she told him about Tiger.

He was halfway up the long climb toward the high pass, on a narrow path between rock and snow, in the dead hunter's coat. His breath smoked and his legs burned. He'd been climbing since dawn, and he'd been thinking about absolutely nothing for an hour, which was the best kind of thinking.

"Ice," she said. "Yan's party came up the stair this morning."

He stopped.

"All five?"

"All five. They cleared the Fold yesterday. The second party after Deston's." A pause. "And Ice. Tiger found the camp."

It took him a second.

"The camp."

"Under the pine. Four hundred metres east. He's a Ranger. He scouted. He found the fire, and the place you slept." Another pause. "And the onion skins."

He stood on the narrow path with snow blowing past him and closed his eyes.

"I left them," he said.

"You left them."

"I didn't even— I didn't think. I just peeled them and left them in the snow like a—" He laughed, short and helpless. "Like an *idiot*. Like a man who's done it ten thousand times and forgot anyone else would ever walk past."

"He put it in World," said Alea. "Do you want to see?"

He didn't want to see. He said yes anyway.

Ice read Tiger's second line three times.

It wasn't *DECLAN*. It wasn't *I know it's you*. It was just a man he'd known for seven or eight months on Earth, a man who'd built a house in a game once and lost it when the whole world blew up, and laughed about it for an hour. A cheerful, kind, fast man standing at a cold fire in the snow, sending a message to a name he didn't know. To be nice. Because whoever had sat there had peeled the onions properly, and that seemed worth saying.

*You peel onions really well.*

He wanted to answer that one so badly it hurt more than the whisper had.

He didn't. He couldn't. Even *thanks* was a thread. He read it one more time and closed it, and stood on the path for a while with his eyes stinging, and told himself it was the wind.

"Gabe's going to go and sit by the fire," he said eventually.

"It's cold. The fire's out."

"He'll sit there anyway. He'll sit there and look at the onion skins." He started climbing again. "And then Xav'll make him get up, because it's going to snow."

"It's going to snow tomorrow night," said Alea quietly. "A lot."

"I know," said Ice. "That's why I'm going up."

***

The pass was the highest point on floor 2: a saddle of bare rock and blown snow between two peaks, so high that the painted ceiling seemed close enough to touch. The wind never stopped. And at the top of it, at the very top, there was a tower.

It was round, of grey stone, maybe fifteen metres high, roofless at the top, where an iron cradle stood black against the sky. A beacon. A great iron basket, meant for a fire big enough to be seen from both sides of the mountain. It was empty and rusted and full of snow, and it hadn't been lit in a hundred years.

Beside the tower, half buried in a drift, there was a hut. A small stone one, with a crooked chimney. And in the hut's one window, very small, very yellow, a candle was burning.

Ice stopped at the bottom of the last slope and looked at it.

"Mother Tallow," said Alea.

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"I know."

The first time, they'd come over this pass on the twentieth day. Thirty-six of them, in a long, frozen, miserable line through the snow, on their way to the stair to floor 3. There'd been no candle in the window. The door of the hut had been shut, with snow drifted halfway up it, and when Hako finally forced it open, they'd found her inside. A very old woman, in a chair by a cold hearth, with a blanket over her knees and a candle-mould in her lap, frozen. Her stores of tallow and her stacks of candles were all round her, enough to light the tower for a year. She'd died in the storm on the seventh night, they'd worked out later. Alone. With the little light in the window gone out, and nobody coming up the pass to see it.

Thea had cried. He remembered that. Thea had cried for a stranger in a hut, and none of them had known what to say, because none of them knew then what any of it meant. Whether the old woman had been real. Whether it mattered.

He'd thought about her for ten years.

He went up the last slope and knocked on the door.

***

She was tiny. That was the first thing. Smaller than Mother Crane, bent like a hook, with a face so lined it looked carved, and two very bright black eyes. She opened the door a crack, and looked up at him: the tall young man in a dead hunter's wolf-fur coat, with a shield on his back and snow in his black hair.

"Well," she said. "A Climber. Haven't seen one of you in a long while."

"Hi," said Ice.

He'd paused. He hadn't meant to. He'd stood there in the doorway with his hand raised to knock again, putting the ten years away. *She's never been frozen. She doesn't know. She's never sat in that chair.*

"You're letting the warm out," she said.

"Sorry."

"Come in, then, if you're coming."

He came in.

It was very small and very warm and smelled of tallow. Candles everywhere: on every shelf, in every corner, hanging in bundles from the rafters by their wicks, fat ones and thin ones and tiny twisted ones like fingers. A great iron pot on the hearth, with a lump of rendered fat in it, cooling. A candle-mould on the table. And in the window, the one candle, burning.

"For the road," she said, seeing him look. "Somebody keeps a light on the pass. Always has. For anyone coming over in the dark." She sniffed. "Nobody comes over in the dark. Nobody's come over in years. But somebody keeps a light."

"The tower used to," said Ice.

She looked at him sharply.

"The tower hasn't been lit since my grandmother's grandmother," she said. "How would a Climber know that?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. *Stupid.*

"There's a cradle on top," he said. "For a fire. It's rusted. So it hasn't been used in a long time."

She looked at him a moment longer with her bright black eyes. Then she seemed to decide something, and turned away to the hearth.

"Mother Tallow," she said, over her shoulder. "That's what they call me down in Larchwick, the few that remember I'm here. You can sit."

He sat.

"Storm coming," she said, poking the fire. "Tomorrow night. A big one. I can feel it in my knees. The Long Storm, they call it down in Larchwick, not that it ever goes down to them. It sits up here on the pass like a cat on a chair." She glanced at him. "You'll want to be gone down the other side before it comes, young man. The pass is no place to be in the Long Storm."

"I know," said Ice. "I'm going to stay."

***

The storm came in the late afternoon of the seventh day, and it came like the end of the world.

He'd spent the day getting ready. He'd brought in wood from the frozen stacks behind the hut, all of it, and split it with his sword, which Mother Tallow watched with deep disapproval. He'd dug the drift away from the tower door. He'd climbed the inside stair of the tower, a spiral of worn stone going up into the wind, to the iron beacon cradle at the top. He'd scraped out a hundred years of snow with his hands, and packed it with wood and with Mother Tallow's oldest, ugliest candles, the ones she said weren't fit for selling.

She'd asked him what he was doing.

"Lighting the tower," he'd said.

"With what? That wood's wet through. You'll never get it to take, not in this wind. Nobody's got it to take in a hundred years." She'd squinted at him. "Not without a proper light."

He'd looked down at his left palm, where the ember lived. It was warm, the way it was always warm.

"I've got a light," he'd said.

Then the sky went dark at three in the afternoon, and the wind came.

It came over the pass like a wall. It took the snow off the ground and threw it horizontally, so there was no up and no down, only white, roaring, everywhere. It screamed round the tower. It came down the hut's chimney and blew the fire out of the hearth in a burst of sparks. It found every crack in the old stone and came through it. And in the window, the one small candle that Mother Tallow had kept burning on the pass every night of her life leaned over sideways, flattened, flickered—

And went out.

Mother Tallow made a sound. Not a word. Just a small, sharp sound, like a woman who's been hit.

She was already cold. He'd been watching her all afternoon, and he'd seen it: the shiver she was trying to hide, and her hands, white and stiff, fumbling with the tinder box. She was ninety if she was a day. The cold had been in her bones all winter, and the fire was out, and the wood was wet, and she was going to sit down in her chair by the cold hearth with a blanket over her knees, the way she had the first time, and not get up.

"No," said Ice. "No, no, no. Come on."

He picked her up. She weighed nothing. She protested, furiously, in his arms, and he ignored her completely. He put his shield on his back and his body between her and the door and went out into the storm, ten metres of screaming white to the tower door. He got her inside, up the spiral stair, round and round and up into the wind, to the top, to the iron cradle full of wet wood and ugly candles under the howling dark.

He put her down in the lee of the cradle, against the stone, and wrapped the dead hunter's coat round her. She was shaking so hard he could hear her teeth.

Then he put his left hand flat on the wet wood in the cradle.

***

He didn't know if it would work. The Testament had said almost nothing about the second light. *Where the road is highest, the second light is kept.* That was all. Two lines, and a drawing of a tower that might have been any tower.

He'd read those two lines two thousand times. He still didn't know.

He put his hand on the wood and thought about the chapel. The statue holding the lantern out over the aisle, toward the door. The light, falling down the aisle to the man in the doorway, reaching him. *The Knight does not carry the light. He stands in it.*

But that was the first light. That had been for him. A light to stand in when you were alone.

This one wasn't for him.

*Somebody keeps a light on the pass. For anyone coming over in the dark.*

He'd been cupping the ember. He realised that. For six days, ever since the chapel, he'd kept it close, curled in his palm, warm against his own heart, the way you'd keep a candle cupped against the wind. The way Yan would. *Keep the wind off it.* That was right for a candle. It was right for a soul.

But a lantern wasn't a candle. A lantern was for other people to see.

He opened his hand. He held it up.

Not against his chest. Up, over the cradle, high, into the screaming wind. Palm open and out, the way the stone knight in the chapel held his lantern out over the aisle, toward the door. Toward whoever was coming.

The ember went out of his palm like a spark going up a chimney.

It went into the wood. And the wet wood, and the ugly old candles, and the hundred years of cold, and the screaming dark, all caught at once with a sound like a great breath being drawn in.

The beacon on the pass went up.

***

It was enormous. A column of gold light and fire standing up out of the iron cradle into the storm, so bright he had to turn his face away, so hot the snow round the top of the tower turned to rain and then to steam. The wind threw itself at it and it didn't even lean. It just burned, roaring, straight up. A light on the highest point of floor 2, so bright it would have been seen from both sides of the mountain, if the storm had let anybody see anything at all.

Mother Tallow was staring up at it with her mouth open. The light was on her lined face. Her eyes were wet.

"My grandmother's grandmother," she said, very small, "said it looked like that."

Then the Rimehounds came.

He'd known they would. Light in a storm drew things. Up the pass from both sides, out of the white, came grey-white shapes, low and fast, ice-blue eyes catching the beacon's gold. Not four. Not five. Many.

He went down the spiral stair to the tower door, because there was only one door, and he stood in it.

It was the chapel again. And it wasn't. In the chapel, he'd been holding a door for a stone statue and an empty lantern and himself. Here, at the top of the stair behind him, there was a very old woman wrapped in a dead man's coat, watching a light her family had waited a hundred years to see. There was no stepping back from this door. If he stepped back, they'd go up the stair.

He looked down at his left hand. The ember was gone; it had gone into the beacon. But his palm was still warm. Warmer than it had been. And when he raised it, the light from the tower behind him seemed to come down the stair and gather in it, and his hand glowed gold in the dark of the doorway.

He held it up. Out. Toward them.

Every Rimehound on the pass turned and looked at him.

He felt them do it, all at once, as if he'd pulled on every one of them with a single rope. The little cracked bell on his belt rang and rang and kept ringing. Eleven times. Fourteen. He stopped counting. They weren't looking at the tower any more, or at the light, or at the stair. They were looking at the man in the doorway with the light in his hand.

"Okay," said Ice, to all of them. "Okay. Come on, then."

They came.

***

He held the door until dawn.

He didn't remember most of it afterwards. That was how the long ones always went. He remembered the Alpha: a great grey-white brute the size of a pony, with a torn ear. Three times it went for his throat, and three times the shield took it, and on the fourth, he put the sword under its jaw. He remembered the cold, and his hand on his chest over and over, *stand in it, let it reach you*, though the ember wasn't there any more. He'd thought the Mend wouldn't work without it, and it did, because the beacon's light was coming down the stair behind him and he was standing in it. He remembered the little bell ringing, and ringing, and slowly ringing less.

And he remembered, at some point near the end, very faintly, from the top of the stair, through the roar of the wind and the fire, an old woman's voice. Singing. Some old song about the road, and a light in a window, over and over, to keep herself awake. So she'd be there to see the dawn.

The storm blew itself out an hour before the sun came up its rail. The last Rimehound looked at him in the grey light, at the man in the doorway with the shield and the glowing hand. It turned round and went away down the pass.

He sat down on the threshold. Of course he did.

He looked down at his left palm. In the middle of it, small as a seed, warm as honey, there was a new ember. The beacon had given it back. Brighter than before.

***

Mother Tallow came down the spiral stair at full light, very slowly, holding the wall, still wrapped in the dead hunter's coat. She stopped at the bottom and looked at him sitting in the doorway, at the drifts outside, trampled and stained, at the long trails going off down the pass.

She looked at him for a long time with her bright black eyes.

"You knew," she said. "Didn't you. You knew the storm would come. You came up here a day early, and you brought in the wood, and you stayed." She paused. "You came up here for me."

He didn't answer. He didn't know how to.

"I'm ninety-one," said Mother Tallow. "I've kept that candle in that window every night since I was nine years old, for a road nobody walks. And last night the whole tower went up. And a boy who knew that tower had a fire on it before I told him sat in the door all night with the wolves, so I'd be up there to see it." She shook her head slowly. "I'm not going to ask you how. I'm too old and too tired. But I'd like your name."

He opened his mouth.

*Ice*, he almost said. *Declan*. *The onion boy.* He'd been all of those, to people who'd fed him and taught him and been kind. It had never mattered what they called him, because there'd always been more time.

"Voyager," he said.

It was the first time he'd said it out loud to anyone. It sat strangely in his mouth, like a new tooth.

Mother Tallow considered it.

"That's not a name," she said. "That's a job."

"Yeah," said Ice. "It is."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she reached into the pocket of her apron, under the coat, and took out a candle. A small one. Pale and smooth and perfect, the best one she'd ever made, he thought. And she pressed it into his cold hand and closed his fingers round it.

"For your way up," she said. "A light for the road. In case nobody's keeping one for you."

He held it. He found he couldn't say anything at all.

"Go on, then," said Mother Tallow. "You'll want to be down the far side before noon. And take that coat, it stinks of wolf." She turned back to the stair, slowly, one hand on the wall. "I'm going to go up and sit with my tower a while."
