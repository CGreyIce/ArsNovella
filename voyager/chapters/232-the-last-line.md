Ina had been sitting on the step for a year and a half, and he'd got used to it.

It was a good step. Wide. Warm, mostly. You could see a long way from it. He'd sat down on it on nine, at a fire, when Winter told him there was a thing thirteen people were keeping for a friend and she'd tell him the day it was a fact. He'd said *thank you*. And then he'd said the other thing, the thing he'd say again if anybody asked: *if it's something people are keeping for a friend, you don't pick the lock. You wait on the step until somebody opens the door.*

So he'd waited.

And a lot of people had come and sat down on the step with him, over the year and a half. Not all at once. One at a time, mostly, without saying so. Hana, on eleven, laughing, *it's a nice step, there's room*. Calvin, on fourteen, somewhere between one stook and the next. Rui Xiang. Malcolm. Others he didn't know about, he was fairly sure. You could tell, sometimes, by the way somebody stopped asking a question halfway through and looked at their feet instead.

It had got crowded.

Chris hadn't sat down.

Chris was the one standing at the door with the notebook. Had been since floor 1. Since the fourteenth day, when he'd written *HOW?* at the top of a page in a grey notebook from Quill's and started a list under it. He'd stood at the door for eight hundred days with that list getting longer, and he'd worked out, Ina was fairly sure, everything there was to work out. He'd worked out the shape of the lock and the shape of the key and what was on the other side.

And he'd never once tried the handle.

But he'd never sat down either.

***

The third bridge was at the *39* stone, and it was black.

Not dark. Black. The stone it was made of, every block, black and smooth like the inside of a cooking pot, and it went over the river in one long low arch with no parapet at all. The river under it was deep and slow and very wide, and so dark you couldn't see into it more than a hand's depth. It didn't roar. It didn't do anything. It went under the black bridge like a held breath.

And it was quiet.

That was the first thing anybody noticed. The other bridges had roared. You stood on the crown and you could shout and nobody would hear. This river didn't make a sound. Ina stood on the bank in the hot afternoon with the white road going up the black arch in front of him and could hear the larks over the barley behind, and the goat chewing, and his own breath.

"How does anybody not hear?" said Calvin. Very quietly, for Calvin.

Sorrel looked at the bridge.

"The keeper's deaf," she said. "Same as all of them." She was quiet. "And nobody else goes near."

"Why?"

Sorrel didn't answer that.

The keeper was a man. Young, for a keeper. Thirty, maybe. Thin and dark with his hair tied back, sitting on the bench outside a stone house as black as the bridge. He watched them come down the road. He had his bundle of cord and his knife. He didn't get up.

"What's the toll?" said Chris.

Sorrel looked at him for a long moment. At the notebook. Then at the rest of them, going along the line, one face at a time.

"Something you've worked out," said Sorrel. "And never said."

Nobody moved.

Ina felt it go along the line. Not a sound. A stillness. The way the grey had gone along the ring on seventeen when Rachael was forgotten, except it wasn't forgetting. It was the opposite. Thirty-four people all at once remembering something they'd worked out and never said, and every one of them, he'd have bet his mask on it, the same thing.

Nobody looked at Ice.

Ina didn't look at Ice. He looked at the black river.

"Worked out," said Chris. Very quietly. "Not felt. Not guessed. *Worked out.*"

"Worked out," said Sorrel. "Something you put together. In your head. From what you saw. And knew. And kept to yourself." She touched her cord. Her six knots. "The bridge knows the difference. A guess won't hold you. A feeling won't. It has to be a thing you *know*. Because you worked it out."

Chris didn't say anything.

Ina looked at him sideways. Chris was standing on the white road with his notebook in his hand, the fifteenth or the sixteenth, whichever it was now, and his face had gone completely blank.

Because he'd already said it.

On the Willow Bridge. On the crown. Six words, Winter had said that night, by the fire, very quietly, to nobody, to Ina. *I couldn't hear. I watched. Six. Maybe seven.* The thing he'd been working out since the fourteenth day of floor 1 and carried for a very long time. He'd said it to a river, out loud, for the first time, because the first bridge wanted something never said.

And now the third bridge wanted something worked out and never said.

And he'd said it.

Ina watched Chris understand that. Watched it go across his face, the way a cloud goes across a field. He'd spent the only one he had.

***

They went up one at a time again.

The keeper got up, at last, when Tiger started up the black arch. He didn't watch. That was the strange thing. The other keepers had watched every one, steadily, from their benches. This one turned his back. Stood at the door of his black stone house with his face to the wall and his bundle of cord in his hands, and didn't turn round until whoever it was had come down the far side. Then he'd walk across, and tie the knot, and walk back, and turn his back again.

Ina watched the first ten go.

He didn't watch mouths. He watched the river. It didn't move, under the bridge. Not when Tiger stood on the crown and said something short. Not when Thea did, with the lantern in her hand, the amber light lying across the black water like gold on ink. Not when Higu did, or Xav, or Dennis, or Rachael, who stood up there for a long time with her hands in her pockets and then said something and came straight down without looking at the far side at all.

The river just held its breath.

Ice went near the middle.

Ina didn't look at him. He looked at the black water under the crown. He heard the footsteps go up the arch, steady, weight forward. He heard them stop.

And it was so quiet that for a moment, a very bad moment, he thought he'd hear it. Whatever Ice said. Up there on the crown of a black bridge over a river that didn't make a sound. He thought it would come down to him across the still water, clear as a bell, and he'd hear the thing that had been on the other side of the door for a year and a half, and he hadn't opened it, it had just come down the river at him and he hadn't been able to stop his ears—

He put his hands over his ears.

He didn't decide to. They were just there. Flat. Hard. Over his ears. He looked at the black water and pressed his hands over his ears so hard it hurt and hummed, inside his head, the note the town had hummed on sixteen when they sang Edda out, so that he wouldn't hear anything at all.

He felt Winter look at him. Sideways.

Then she put her hands over her ears too.

He saw it out of the corner of his eye. Winter, on the bank, beside him, with her bow on her back and her hands flat over her ears and her eyes on the river.

And then Chris did. On her other side.

And Niko. And Maigo. All along the bank. He saw it go along the line, the way the not-looking had always gone. Hands coming up. Thirty-three people standing on the bank of a silent river on a hot afternoon with their hands over their ears and their eyes on the water, while one man stood on the crown of a black bridge and said something to it.

Ina hummed.

He didn't know how long. Long enough that his arms ached. Then he felt it, through the soles of his feet, through the white road: footsteps. Coming down. The far side. Steady.

He took his hands down.

Ice was on the far bank. The keeper was tying his knot. And Ice was looking back across the black river. At the near bank. At thirty-three people taking their hands down from their ears.

His face did something Ina had never seen it do.

He didn't say anything. He sat down on the far bank, beside Thea, who'd gone before him. He put his hand on the little lantern on his belt. And he didn't look at the bridge again.

***

Chris went last.

He'd stood on the bank with his hands over his ears for Ice, and then with them down for everybody else, and he'd watched every one of them go, with his notebook in his hand and his face blank. Thirty-four. Then it was just him.

He went up the black arch.

Ina watched him go. Not his mouth. His back. Chris walked up a bridge the way he walked up everything, looking at it, working it out, every step. The black stone. The no-parapet. The held-breath river.

He got to the crown.

He stood there for a very long time.

Ina didn't know what he was going to say. He'd spent it. The one thing. On the Willow Bridge. He'd worked out the answer to *HOW?* a long time ago and carried it and said it to a loud green river under willows because the first bridge asked for something never said, and now the third bridge wanted something worked out and never said, and he didn't have anything left. Ina was sure of that. Chris didn't have anything left that he'd worked out and not said. He'd worked out everything. He'd said the biggest one.

Chris stood on the crown of the black bridge for so long that the keeper, with his back turned at the door of his house, turned round. Just once. To look. And then turned back.

Ina wanted to put his hands over his ears. He didn't. There wasn't anything to not hear. Chris wasn't saying anything.

And then he did.

Ina saw him. Not his mouth. His shoulders. They came down. All the way. The way Gabe's had come down on the flour sacks in Evermorrow. The way a man's shoulders come down when he's been holding something up for a very long time and finds out he doesn't have to.

And he said something. Short. Very short. Three words, maybe. Four.

Then he looked at the far half of the bridge.

He didn't put his foot on it straight away. He looked at it for a long moment. As if he wasn't sure. As if this was the one bridge on the whole road he didn't know the answer to.

He put his foot on it.

Black stone.

He walked down. The keeper tied his knot. And Chris came and sat down on the grass on the far bank, not next to anybody, a little apart, and put his face in his hands.

Ina went across.

He didn't decide to. He was the last one on the near bank. He went up the black arch on his own, and said his thing on the crown, quietly, to the river that didn't make a sound. It was seven words. He'd worked it out on four, across a grave on a ledge in the Red City, when Ice had said *these ones*, and Ina had heard the shape of it. *These ones. As opposed to the others.* He'd worked it out then and kept it on the step for nearly three hundred days and never once said it.

He said it.

The far side was stone.

He went down it, and the keeper tied his knot, and Ina walked across the grass to where Chris was sitting a little apart from everybody, and sat down beside him. About the width of a mask.

He didn't say anything.

After a while, Chris took his hands away from his face.

"I didn't have anything," said Chris. Very quietly. Looking at the black river. "On the crown. I'd spent it. On the first one. The thing I'd worked out. I said it to the river under the willows." He was quiet. "And I stood up there and thought, I don't have anything else. I've worked everything out. Six hundred days. There's nothing left I've worked out and not said."

Ina waited.

"And then I worked something out," said Chris. "On the crown. Standing there." He almost laughed. "I've never worked anything out that fast in my life."

"What?"

Chris looked at him.

He didn't say. Ina didn't expect him to. It was the toll. It was paid.

But he took his pack off. There, on the far bank of the black bridge, in the hot afternoon, with everybody else sitting on the grass not looking. And he went down through it, past the food and the spare shirt and the oilcloth, to the very bottom. And took out the grey notebook. From Quill's. From floor 1.

And unwrapped it. And opened it. And didn't write anything.

He just held it open, on his knee, so Ina could see the page if he looked.

Ina didn't look. Not yet.

"Tonight," said Chris. "Not here. At the inn." He shut it again. Wrapped it. Put it back. "I want to do it properly. With a pen. Sitting down."

"Sitting down," said Ina.

Chris looked at him.

Then something happened at the corner of his mouth. Ina had seen it on Chris maybe twice in two years. The thing Chris's face did when somebody had worked something out faster than he had.

"Yeah," said Chris. "Sitting down."

***

Nobody went near the black bridge at night. Sorrel had said. They found out why.

The inn below it was called the Fold, and it was set back from the river by half a mile, up a lane, behind a stone wall and a hedge of holly so thick a dog couldn't have got through it, and every window on the river side was shuttered. It was full of pilgrims with three knots, and none of them would sit on the river side of the long room, and when the sun went down the innkeeper went round and checked every shutter twice and barred the door to the lane.

"The Unpaid," said Sorrel. At the long table. Very quietly. "From the river."

"The what?"

"People who tried." Sorrel turned her cup in her hands. "To cross the third with something they hadn't worked out. A guess. A thing they wanted to be true. They got to the crown and said it and stepped onto the far side and it was water." She was quiet. "That river's deep. And slow. And it keeps what it takes." She looked at the shuttered window. "At night they come up. They walk the bank. They go up on the bridge. They look for anybody with a thing they haven't paid."

"And?"

"And take them down with them." Sorrel drank. "So nobody goes near the black bridge at night. Not with anything unpaid."

Ina looked at her cord.

Six knots. Three of them tied at the black bridge, over thirty years. She'd paid this one eleven times.

But there was a seventh she'd never paid. And she'd been carrying it up and down the Long Road for thirty years. And Maigo had sat beside her on a bench at the Barley Mow two nights ago and said *it's light now*.

She didn't look at the shutters.

He watched her not look at them, all evening.

***

He didn't sleep.

He didn't sleep much anyway. He hadn't since fifteen. His hands remembered the jam's cold, sometimes, at night, and he'd wake with them aching and lie there in the dark putting a little fire in them, just a little, the way Kei told him not to. He was lying on the floor of the Fold's long room with Winter asleep on one side of him and Niko on the other, at about the middle of the night, doing that.

And he heard the bar on the lane door lift.

Very quietly. Wood on iron. He knew it because he'd heard the innkeeper drop it at sunset.

He sat up.

There was somebody at the door. Small. With a staff. Lifting the bar, slowly, carefully, an old woman's hands, so it wouldn't scrape. And opening the door a crack. And going out into the lane, in the dark, toward the river.

Sorrel.

He was up before he'd thought. Over Niko. Across the long room. He didn't wake anybody. He didn't think about waking anybody. He thought about a cord with six knots, and an old woman who'd been carrying something for thirty years that the road had told her was light, and a river that came up at night for anybody with a thing they hadn't paid.

He went out after her.

He didn't take the mask. It was on his head, pushed up, the way he wore it. He didn't pull it down. He didn't think of it.

*Not unless someone would die.*

He hadn't thought anyone would.

***

She was on the bridge.

On the crown. In the dark. He saw her from the lane: small, black against a sky full of stars, standing on the crown of the black bridge with her staff in both hands and her face turned down to the river. Practising. He could see her lips going, even from here. Over and over. The thing she'd kept. Saying it to the black water, the way she'd said it to the road eleven times.

Not to pay it. You couldn't pay the seventh at the third. She was just saying it. To a river. In the dark. To see if it was light enough.

And the river came up.

He saw it before she did. Along the bank. Out of the black water, onto the grass, onto the black stone of the bridge's foot. Shapes. Slow. Grey-green. Shoulders, first, coming up out of the river, then heads, then arms. People. Or what was left of people that had been in a slow deep river for a very long time. Hair like weed. Faces gone soft and pale and blurred, the way a drawing goes when water's got at it. Eyes like river stones, smooth and dark and dead.

And mouths. Moving. All of them. Saying something.

He heard it as he ran. Low, under the hot summer night, from a dozen soft wet mouths. Not words. The shape of words. The same things over and over. *It was the other way round. It wasn't my fault. She'll forgive me. I was right. I was right. I was right.*

The things they'd said on the crown. The guesses. The things they'd wanted to be true. Still saying them. Still trying to pay.

They went up the bridge toward Sorrel.

***

He got to the foot of the bridge as the first of them got to the crown.

It reached for her. A long grey-green arm with weed hanging off it, reaching for the old woman with the staff who was standing there with her mouth still moving, not seeing it, not yet. And then she saw it. He saw her see it. Her face in the starlight. And she didn't scream. She lifted her staff, an old woman of seventy-something, and brought it down on the grey arm as hard as she could.

It went through. Like a stick through wet bread. The arm didn't stop.

Ina put his hand out.

Fire. Orange. Ordinary. Out of his palm, across the black stone, the way he'd put it in Sabe's hearth on fifteen. It hit the grey thing in the side and it hissed, the way wet wood hisses, and steamed, and turned its blurred face toward him.

And kept going. Toward Sorrel. Toward the thing she hadn't paid.

More of them on the bridge now. Five. Eight. Coming up both sides of the black arch out of the river. Soft and slow and wet and saying their guesses over and over in their soft wet mouths, and his ordinary fire going into them and hissing and steaming and not stopping them at all. They were too wet. They'd been in the river too long.

*The fire they're afraid of. Wind can't take it. Rain can't take it.*

He knew what would stop them.

He put his hand up to the mask.

And stopped.

He'd always put it on. That was the rule. *Not unless someone would die.* And then you pulled it down over your face, the red oni face with the horns, and the fire went white-blue and nothing could stand in front of it, and nobody could see your face. Nobody could see you were frightened. You were the bad guy, Winter had said, on two. *Put it on more. I'd rather look like the bad guy and be alive.*

He'd worn it every time. Every time someone would die. So nobody would see what his face was doing.

A window opened in the corner of his eye.

He'd had one, unopened, since the Hollow. He hadn't known what it was for. It opened now, on its own, as he stood at the foot of a black bridge with his hand on his mask and an old woman on the crown and a dozen drowned things between them.

He read it in one breath.

On the crown, the first grey thing had Sorrel's staff. It had pulled it out of her hands. She was standing with nothing, her back to the empty dark where the parapet should have been, and its arm going round her, soft and wet, and its mouth by her ear saying *I was right I was right I was right—*

He took his hand off the mask.

He left it on his head. Pushed up. His face bare. In the starlight. On a black bridge. Frightened. Completely, horribly frightened, the way he'd been frightened every single time, the way he'd hidden every single time under a red face with horns.

He put both his hands out.

And lit it.

***

It was white-blue.

He hadn't known if it would be. It had never once been white-blue without the mask. He'd thought it was the mask. He'd thought the mask was the fire. It wasn't. It came out of his bare hands, white at the heart and blue at the edges, the colour of the hottest part of a flame, the fire they were afraid of, and it went across the black bridge like a river going the wrong way.

The first grey thing let go of Sorrel.

It turned. It looked at him with its river-stone eyes. And for the first time, he saw it see him. Not the fire. Him. His face. Bare. Frightened. Lit white-blue from underneath by his own hands.

It stopped talking.

They all did. All the drowned things on the bridge. The soft wet mouths stopping, one after another, *I was right* trailing off into nothing. Turning. Looking at a young man with no mask standing at the foot of the bridge with white fire in his hands and his face doing everything it had ever done under the mask and never shown anybody.

He walked up the bridge.

He didn't hurry. The fire went in front of him. Where it touched them, they didn't hiss. They went. Not burned. Dried. The weed going pale and crisp and falling off, the soft wet grey going dry and light and pale, like river-mud in the sun, cracking, and then not there. Just dust on black stone. One. Two. They didn't fight. They didn't reach for him. They stood on the bridge and looked at his face while the white fire came, and went.

He didn't know why. He'd think about it for a long time. He'd think maybe they'd been in the river so long saying a thing that wasn't true, and here was somebody coming up the bridge with his face saying a thing that was. *I'm frightened.* As plain as a thing could be said. And it was true. And they'd never seen anything true come up the bridge at them before.

He got to the crown.

Sorrel was on her knees. On the black stone. Her staff gone over the edge into the river. Her hands over her face. Shaking.

The last of them was beside her. The first one. The one that had had her. It was standing very still, on the edge of the crown where there was no parapet, looking at Ina. Its mouth wasn't moving.

He held the fire up between them.

It looked at his face for a long time.

Then it turned round, and stepped off the edge of the black bridge, and went into the river without a sound. And didn't come up.

***

He put the fire out.

He didn't know how. *Only you can put it out.* He just closed his hands and it went. And it was dark, and the stars were out, and he was standing on the crown of the black bridge over a silent river with an old woman on her knees beside him, and his hands were shaking so hard he had to hold them together.

And his face.

He didn't know what it was doing. He'd never had to know. The mask had always known for him.

"Ina."

Winter. At the foot of the bridge. With her bow up and an arrow on the string, frost coming off the point, and Niko beside her with his sword drawn and the edge of it glowing, faintly, and behind them Chris, and Maigo, barefoot. They'd come out of the Fold behind him. He hadn't heard them. He'd been too frightened to hear anything.

She came up the bridge.

She didn't look at the river. She didn't look at Sorrel. She came up the black arch to the crown and stopped in front of him and looked at his face.

Straight. In the starlight. The bare one.

He let her.

It was the hardest thing he'd done all night. Harder than the bridge. Harder than the white fire. Standing there with no mask, with his hands shaking, letting Winter look at whatever his face was doing.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she reached out and took his wrist. Above the hand. The way she had on fifteen, on the right cliff, all night, when his hands were black.

"You didn't put it on," said Winter.

"No."

"Somebody would've died."

"Yes."

"And you didn't put it on." Her voice did something. "You went up a bridge in the dark with your face out. Frightened. I can see you're frightened. You're still frightened. You're shaking."

"I know."

"You let them see." She was looking at his face. "You let *me* see."

He didn't say anything.

Winter held his wrist and looked at him and he watched her face do something he'd never seen it do. Not the angry thing. Not the fact thing. Something underneath both.

"Good," said Winter. Very quietly. "*Good.* Don't you ever put it on again."

"I'll have to. Sometimes."

"I know you will." She didn't let go of his wrist. "Not tonight."

He read it with Winter's hand round his wrist.

Behind her, on the crown, Maigo had knelt beside Sorrel and put his bare hand flat on the black stone beside her knee, the way you'd put your hand on the ground beside a frightened animal. Not touching her. Just there.

Sorrel took her hands away from her face.

She looked at Ina. At his bare face. Then at the river, where her staff had gone. Then at Maigo's hand on the stone.

"I was practising," said Sorrel. Very small. "For the seventh. The boy with the leaves said it was light. I wanted to see." She looked at the black water. "It is."

"Light enough?" said Maigo.

Sorrel looked at him for a long time.

"Light enough," said Sorrel.

***

Chris wrote it at the Fold, at the long table, by one candle, with everybody asleep except the five of them.

Ina watched him do it.

Chris unwrapped the grey notebook from Quill's. He opened it to the page. *HOW?* In his own capitals from the fourteenth day. The list going down. *Why noon?* near the top. Twenty-two lines. *Day 525. "I came down once. To here." Gull Island.* *Day 640. The window said she'd died. She hadn't. Somebody got something wrong. Somebody who wasn't there.*

And one line. Empty. For eight hundred days, the last of them.

He uncapped the pen.

He didn't hurry. He held it over the line for a long time. Ina thought, for a while, that he wasn't going to. That he'd sit there with the pen over the line until the candle went out, the way he'd sat at the window of the Barley Mow with the notebook open.

Then he wrote.

Small. Neat. In the square hand he wrote everything in. Four words.

He put the pen down.

He turned the notebook round on the table, and pushed it, very slightly, across the wood. Toward Ina. Not all the way. Just far enough that Ina could see it if he looked.

Ina looked.

*Doesn't matter how.*

He read it four times.

Then he looked up at Chris. Chris was looking at the candle.

"That's what I worked out," said Chris. "On the crown. Standing there with nothing left to say. I'd worked out the how. A long time ago. I've known the how for longer than I've let myself. I said it to a river under the willows. I've got the whole thing. Every line on that page points at it." He looked at the page. "And I stood on the black bridge and worked out the last thing. Which is that it doesn't matter. The how. It never did. It doesn't change him. It doesn't change what he did on the Grey or the jam or the square. It doesn't change one single thing about any of it." He was quiet. "It just tells me how. And I don't need it."

Ina didn't say anything.

"I'm never going to ask," said Chris. "I'm never going to write it down. I've got it, and I'm not going to use it. Not ever." He picked up the notebook. "That's not a column. That's not a fact. That's just—" He stopped. "That's just where I've decided to sit."

He shut the grey notebook. He wrapped it in its oilcloth. He held it for a moment in both hands.

Then he looked at Ina.

"Is there room?" said Chris. "On the step."

Ina looked at him across the candle. At Chris, who'd stood at the door with a notebook for eight hundred days and worked out the lock and the key and what was on the other side, and never once tried the handle, and never once sat down.

"There's room," said Ina.

Across the table, Winter, who wasn't asleep, who'd been sitting with her back against the wall with her eyes shut, didn't open her eyes.

"About bloody time," said Winter.

Chris laughed. Out loud. At midnight in the Fold. And put the grey notebook at the very bottom of his pack, under everything, the way he always had.

He didn't take it out again.

***

They came to the last inn on the eighth day after the black bridge.

Past the fourth bridge and the fifth and the sixth. Ina didn't remember those well, afterwards. *Something you're afraid of. Something you want. Something you'd give up.* He paid them. Everybody paid them. On the sixth, Sorrel tied her sixth knot for the twelfth time and the keeper, an old, old woman, held her hands for a long moment afterwards and patted them.

The milestones went down. *20.* *11.* *4.*

*1.*

The inn at the *1* stone was called the End. That was all. A long low house of white stone at the top of a slope, with a yard and a well and swallows, the same as all the others, except nobody in it was going back the way they'd come. Every pilgrim at the End had six knots. Ina counted. Forty or fifty of them, in the yard in the long gold evening, with six knots each, sitting on the warm stones, looking down the slope.

At the bridge.

He stood at the gate of the yard with the others and looked at it.

The road went down the slope from the End, white, between the last of the hedges, and stopped. At the edge of the world, it looked like. A gorge. So wide he couldn't see the other side, only mist, gold in the evening sun, filling it from edge to edge like milk in a bowl. And going out over the gorge, into the mist, from the end of the road, a bridge.

Long. Very long. Stone, pale, old, going out over the mist on arch after arch after arch, more than he could count, getting fainter and fainter until it went into the gold and was gone. No far end. Nobody had ever seen the far end, Sorrel said. You went out on it, and you went into the mist, and nobody came back.

And at the near end, where the road met it, a house.

A tollhouse. Small. Stone. Very old. With a door. Shut.

He didn't see anybody. No keeper on a bench. Just the little house and the shut door.

And a window opened in the corner of his eye. Everybody's. He could feel it go along the line at the gate. Grey. Pale.

[ The Toll · Floor Guardian ]

Nobody said anything.

"The seventh," said Sorrel. Beside him. At the gate. With her six knots. Very quietly. "It's not deaf. The last one." She looked at the little house with its shut door. "Every keeper on the road is deaf. That one isn't." She was quiet. "That one hears everything."

Ina didn't look at Ice.

Nobody did. Thirty-four people at the gate of the last inn in the gold evening, and not one of them looking along the line to where he stood. At the back. With Thea's hand in his and Tuli's pole and the little lantern on his belt, humming its four notes, the middle wrong.

*What you've kept.*

Ina felt the step under him. Wide. Warm. Crowded now. Chris sitting on it. Winter on it, whether she'd admit it or not. Everybody.

He felt, for the first time in a year and a half, the door move.

Not open. Move. The way a door moves in its frame when somebody on the other side puts their hand on the handle.

"Tomorrow," said Ice.

From the back. Quietly. Not to anybody. To the bridge.

Nobody answered.

Ina put his bare hands in his pockets, and his mask on his head, pushed up, where it lived now, and looked at the little stone house at the end of the Long Road, and waited.

He was good at waiting.

He'd had a year and a half of practice.
