"Again," he said.

Alea didn't say anything for a moment. She was sitting on the parapet beside him in the dark, the size of a cat, pale teal and white, with her tail round her paws and her pinprick eyes on the Wellgate below. It was the middle of the night on the four hundred and first day. Gabe was asleep on the copy-room step with Hearthwall on his knees. Everybody else was asleep inside.

"That's twelve," said Alea.

"I know."

"Since the Tellhouse. Twelve times. I'm not complaining. I'm logging it."

"I know you are."

She looked at him sideways. Then she looked back at the Wellgate.

"It's different every time," she said. "Did you know that? Not the words. The words are the same. You. The way you hold still for it. The first time, on the step, you held still like somebody waiting to be hit. The fifth time, on the Fell, you held still like somebody waiting for a bus." She was quiet. "Last night you didn't hold still at all. You were peeling an onion."

"Alea."

"I'm just saying. So you've heard it." She shifted her paws on the stone. "All right."

And she played it.

***

It wasn't her voice. That was the thing he still couldn't get used to. Alea had been a voice in his ear for ten years and four hundred days, and she'd only ever been herself: dry, quiet, a little bit mean when she needed to be. She'd never once been anybody else.

But the log wasn't her. The log was what she'd kept. Every sound, every number, every second of ten years, the way a book keeps every word. And when she played it, it wasn't Alea talking. It was the log. It was *then*.

He heard the room first. The last room. The top of the tower. He didn't want to and he always did. A great high noise like a storm in a bell, and under it, close, very close, somebody breathing fast and hard, which was him. And Deston's voice, a long way off, saying something, and stopping. And Drew's, saying *now*, and something huge going still.

And then, for ten years, there'd been a hole.

Three seconds. That was all. At the very end. Three seconds of the log where the sound just went. Not quiet. *Gone*. A gap in the record, like a page torn out at the stitching. On one side of it, the storm in the bell. On the other side, nothing at all, and him on his knees, and the white room. And in the middle, three seconds, and Thea's face turned toward him, a hand's width away, with her mouth moving.

He'd asked Alea about the hole every night for a year, the first time. And then every week. And then, for nine years, he'd stopped asking, because it hurt Alea to say *there's nothing there* as much as it hurt him to hear it.

There was something there now.

The storm in the bell. His breathing. Drew's *now*. And then, out of the place where the hole had been, close, so close it was against his ear, quick, a little breathless, the voice he'd have known in his sleep in either life:

"*I love you.*"

A breath.

"*Idiot.*"

And then the gap where it ended, and the white room.

***

He sat on the parapet with his hands holding each other on his knees.

He didn't cry this time. He'd cried the first five. On the Tellhouse step, with his face in his hands and Thea's forehead on his hair. On the Fell, sitting on a rock, with Xola pretending to look at a goat. Twice in the night. Once peeling onions, which didn't count, because of the onions.

He didn't cry this time. He just sat, and let it be there. Thea's voice. Ten years ago, and four hundred days. Not a guess on a rock under the aurora any more. Not *what I'd say*. What she'd said.

"Tell me again what happened," he said. "In the log. When it filled."

"I've told you."

"Tell me again."

Alea sighed. A small dry sound. She'd learned to sigh on floor 6, he thought. From Hyvred.

"I was on your shoulder," she said. "On the step. In the Tellhouse. You'd sat down, the way you said you would, and I was keeping you company and keeping an eye on Gabe, who was keeping an eye on the clerk. And under the hill, a long way down, something went off." She was quiet a moment. "Like a bell. Not a sound. A *change*. Like the whole of Tell being read at once. Every page, every line, every scrap of paper in every house. Everything that had a gap in it."

"Cursiffe's Echo."

"Cursiffe's Echo. *What happened, and is kept anywhere, you may tell again.*" Alea flicked her tail. "Anywhere. He didn't know what he was saying when he unlocked it. He doesn't know now. He just let it go, the first time, all at once, the way you do. And it went up through the hill like water up a wick, and it found everything in reach that had lost something. And I was in reach." She looked at him. "I'm a record, Ice. That's all I am. I'm the record of the first time. The only one that came back. And I had a hole in me. Three seconds long. Right at the end."

He didn't say anything.

"And for one breath," said Alea, "it filled. Every gap in Tell filled for one breath, and then they all went again, because paper can't hold what it's lost. It just shows you, and lets go." She was quiet. "I'm not paper. When it filled in me, I held on."

"You held on."

"I held on," said Alea. "With everything I had. For you." She looked away, down the Wellgate. "And it stayed."

He looked at her for a long time. The little fox of light on the parapet, pale in the dark, with her tail round her paws. The only thing that came back.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thank Cursiffe." She was quiet. "And thank her. She said it right. On the rock. Every word, and in the right order, and the second one meaner than the first." Something happened to Alea's voice. "Two lives, and she said it the same both times. Did you notice? Even the breath in the middle."

He'd noticed.

"Again?" said Alea.

"No," said Ice. "That's enough."

She looked at him, surprised.

"That's enough," he said again. "For tonight. It's—" He stopped. "It's there. I don't have to keep checking. It's there."

Alea looked at him for a long moment.

"That's thirteen," she said. Very quietly. "You stopped at twelve."

"I'm logging it," said Ice.

And Alea laughed. The first time, he thought, in ten years and four hundred days, that he'd ever made her laugh.

***

He went out past the Fell Gate in the morning with Thea and Xola, the way he did every morning now. Twice a day. Sixty-six, sixty-seven. The padlock at seventy, and the Reckoning six days off, and he wanted to be there. He didn't know why. He just knew that whatever was going to happen in the great hall of the Assize, he wanted to walk into it with nothing held back.

The Fells were high open country north of Tell, beyond the farms. Long grey-green slopes of short grass and bracken and chalk, rising in folds toward a line of bare hills, with the wind always coming over them and the sky very big. There were fell-cats on them. Grey, long-bodied, as big as a pony, with flat heads and short tufted ears and a way of lying in the bracken so still you walked past them. They hunted in threes. In the evenings, in fives. They were Lv 62 to 66 and they didn't like him.

He didn't like them either. But they were good for the padlock.

Thea and Xola sat on a chalk outcrop a hundred metres back and watched. The way they did every day. Thea with the lantern, shuttered, in her lap. Xola with her knees up and Squiddles on her head and her face doing the thing it did now, every time, from the first moment a fell-cat rose out of the bracken to the last.

He fought the way he fought. Dodge. Turn. Never where it lands. The shield on the slope, never flat. And when something got through, a claw, a pair of jaws, and Alea said the number, he tipped his head.

At sixty, he healed.

Every time. He'd been doing it for a hundred and fifteen days now, since the Shoals, and it still felt like stepping off a ledge in the dark. His hand going gold on his side in the middle of a fight, with a healer on a rock a hundred metres back who could have had it after. A second with his hand on himself instead of on the shield. A second for the fell-cats to come in at his open side.

He took the extra step. They missed.

And on the rock, every time, Xola made a sound he could hear from a hundred metres. Not a word. A sort of strangled furious squeak.

"She's still not used to it," said Alea, on the second day, after the third fell-cat.

"She said she nearly fainted. The first time."

"She did nearly faint. I logged it. She had to sit down." Alea paused. "She's annoyed because she wants to be annoyed with you and you keep not giving her a reason."

"I'll try harder."

"Please don't," said Alea.

He finished the third one. Sixty-eight. He walked back up the slope to the chalk outcrop, with his shield on his back and a long scratch on his left forearm that he'd healed at sixty-one and that was already pink. Xola watched him come the whole way with her arms folded.

"Sixty," she said, when he got there.

"Sixty-one."

"*Sixty-one*, then. With me *right there*." She glared at him. "On a *rock*. With *nothing to do*."

"You want me to stop?"

"*No*," said Xola, furious. "Obviously *not*. I want you to keep doing it *forever*. I just want you to know that it's *very annoying*." She looked at his arm. At the pink line. And her face did the thing it did every time, under the glare, where it went soft and couldn't help it. "Come here, Icey. Let me do it properly."

He held out his arm. She did it properly. Cool and clear and moving, like water.

Thea, on the rock behind her, didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She'd unshuttered the lantern and was looking at him over it, and the light inside it was leaning toward him so hard it lay flat against the glass.

***

He went up to Aldo on the afternoon of the four hundred and third day.

Drew came with him. To the bottom of the steps. They walked up the Wellgate together, past the Tellhouse and the Countinghouse, past the second wall and the third, past the clerks on their corners who lowered their heads very slightly as Ice went past. They'd started doing it to him too, now. Not just to Thea. He didn't know what it meant. Neither did Drew.

At the top of the Wellgate, where the road turned the last corner under the great pale wall of the Assize, there was a flight of steep worn steps up to the row of old narrow houses with their backs to the wall. Drew stopped at the bottom of it.

"I'll be here," he said.

"You don't have to—"

"I'm getting good at steps," said Drew. "Everybody is. Go on."

Ice went up.

At the top, outside the last house, there was a green door with the paint coming off it in curls. And a rush chair. And in the chair, with a rug over his knees and the afternoon sun on his face and his bright bird's eyes fixed on the top of the steps, an old man.

He looked exactly the same.

That was the thing that went through Ice, standing at the top of the steps. Not *older*. He was older. The first time, Aldo had been eighty-seven too. Same age, same year, same autumn. But he'd been running. Down the hill, with two books under his arm and his coat flying and his face wet, shouting. Ice had only ever seen him moving. Running, or writing, or crying, or all three at once, at the door of the Climbers' House, every morning for seven weeks, bringing them things.

He'd never seen him sitting still.

"Forty-six days," said Aldo Pellow.

"Yes," said Ice.

"Your friend took fifteen."

"Calvin's faster than me."

"Calvin's louder than you. It's not the same." Aldo looked at him. Up and down. At the shield on his back. At the cord on his wrist. At his face. For a long time. The way a teller looks at a page before writing on it. "Sit down. Davy's put out a stool. He doesn't do that for everybody."

Ice sat down.

***

They didn't say anything for a while. Aldo looked at him. Ice looked at the town, spread out below them, ring below ring, down to the Lantern Market and the well-house in its corner and the plain beyond.

He'd sat somewhere near here, the first time. Not on this step. Further down, by the Climbers' House, on its front step, every evening. And every evening Aldo had come down the hill with something. Bread. A pot of honey. A book of the Rolls to show them their names in. Talking the whole way. Asking nothing.

*Everybody was kind. Nobody asked us anything.*

"You asked for me," said Aldo. "In the market. Hawise says you said it before she'd opened her book. *Where's Aldo.*"

"Yes."

"Calvin says you do that. Know things."

"Calvin says a lot of things."

"He does." Aldo's mouth moved at the corner. "He's the best thing that's happened on this hill in sixty years. Don't tell him I said so. He'll come up every day."

"He'd come up every day anyway."

"I know. I'm counting on it." Aldo was quiet. His knotted hands moved on the rug. "I've been sitting here," he said, "for forty-six days, working it out. Sixty years at the door-roll teaches you one thing above everything else, young man. A man doesn't know a name he's never heard." He looked at Ice. "You'd heard mine. Somewhere."

Ice didn't say anything.

"And Hawise won't tell me what the Count's hand said. At the well." Aldo's bright eyes didn't leave his face. "Disputes aren't said in the street. She's right. She's a good teller. She's the best I ever trained." He was quiet. "But I don't need her to. I know what *already told* means. It means somebody wrote you before. In Tell. In two hands." He paused. "And it's my hand that writes climbers. Always has been. Sixty years at the door, and every time I dreamed of them coming up the well, it was my hand that wrote them."

The wind came over the Assize wall behind them. Warm. Smelling of the plain.

"Was it me?" said Aldo.

***

Ice sat on the stool and looked at the old man.

He couldn't tell him. He'd known that before he came up the steps. He'd known it on the roof with Alea and on the Fell with the fell-cats and every night since Calvin came down the hill and said *he wants you to come yourself*. He couldn't tell Aldo. He couldn't tell anybody. There was one person in either world he'd ever told, and she was down there at the bottom of the hill on a lead roof with a lantern.

But Aldo had asked.

And Aldo had sat in this chair for forty-six days, working it out, and had got there on his own. The way Rachael had. The way Gabe had, and Drew, and Cursiffe with his book. Sixty years at the door-roll. A man doesn't know a name he's never heard.

He couldn't tell him.

He could tell him what he saw.

"Somebody ran," said Ice.

Aldo went very still.

"Somebody ran all the way down the hill," said Ice. His voice didn't sound like his. It sounded like Alea's log. Like *then*. "Once. A long way. With two books under his arm and his coat flying. Down every flight of steps on the Wellgate, past every wall, into the Lantern Market. Crying. Shouting. *Climbers. In my life.*" He looked at his hands. "Because he'd waited his whole life for it. Sixty years at a door. And when it came, he couldn't sit still. He couldn't even walk. He ran."

Aldo didn't move.

"I never forgot it," said Ice. "I never once forgot it."

He stopped there. He couldn't say anything else. It was already more than he should have said. He knew it. He sat on the stool with his hands holding each other on his knees, and didn't look up, and waited for the old man to ask the next thing. *When? Where? How do you know?*

Aldo didn't ask.

He sat in his rush chair with his knotted hands on his rug and his bright eyes on Ice's face for a very long time. And Ice watched his face do something. Slowly. Like a teller reading a line, and reading it again, and understanding it.

"I always said I would," said Aldo. Very quietly. "Run. If they came in my life. I told Mab. Every year. *I'll run all the way down that hill, Mab, I don't care how old I am.*"

"I know."

"And this time I didn't."

"No," said Ice. "This time you made us come to you."

Aldo laughed.

It came out of him on its own. Short and cracked and surprised, like Ice's own laugh on the jetty on floor 8. And then it turned into something else. He put one of his knotted hands over his eyes and sat there in his chair with his shoulders going, and Ice sat on the stool beside him and didn't say anything at all, and down the steps, at the bottom, Drew stood with his back to them, looking very hard at the Assize wall.

"*Climbers*," said Aldo, behind his hand. Wet. "*In my life*."

***

When he'd finished, he wiped his face on the rug, and blew his nose on a large handkerchief, and looked at Ice.

"I'm going to write you," he said. "At the Reckoning. When it asks. First."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to. I'm eighty-seven. I don't have to do anything." Aldo reached down beside his chair and picked up a little board, a writing-board, the kind tellers balanced on their knees, with a sheet of cream paper clipped to it and a pen in a horn of ink. "Hawise has my name on her list already. Top of it. I made her. But I'm going to practise." He dipped the pen. "I haven't written a climber in sixty years of wanting to. I'm not doing it badly in front of the Magistrate."

He wrote.

Ice watched him do it.

And something went through him, sitting there on the stool, that he hadn't expected. Something that came up from a long way down and stayed.

Aldo's hand went across the paper in a big, loose, eager, slanting line. It went up and down a little as it went, like a fence on a hill. As if the person writing it was in a great hurry, and couldn't keep his hand still.

*He wrote like he was running*, Thea had said. On the Countinghouse step. In the dark. *Up and down. Like a fence on a hill.*

It was the same hand. Older. Shakier. Sixty years of wanting, instead of one autumn of having. But the same.

Ice had never seen it before. Not the first time. He'd seen Aldo run, and cry, and bring honey, and talk. He'd never once looked at what Aldo wrote. It had been in a book in a building he'd never wondered about, across the road from where they lived, for seven weeks.

Thea had seen it. In the Count's book. Thirty-four names, in this hand.

He watched Aldo write one name now. One line.

Aldo held the board out at arm's length and looked at it, and frowned, and showed it to him.

*Declan. Ice. Came up the hill. Took him forty-six days. Asked for me first.*

"Too long?" said Aldo.

Ice couldn't answer him.

Aldo looked at his face for a moment. Then, very gently, he took the board back, and put it on his knee, and didn't say anything else about it.

***

"The Magistrate," said Aldo, when Ice could look at him again. "Calvin will have told you."

"Behind a screen. A voice like a page turning."

"In an empty room. Yes." Aldo looked up at the wall behind them. At the great pale side of the Assize, and the square bell tower on the end of it, with its flag. "Sixty-one Reckonings I've heard it. It sits behind the screen and it reads both hands and it asks *who writes him*, and it weighs the slates, and it says which hand stands. And it's never once come out from behind the screen. Not in sixty-one years. Not in my grandmother's time. Not in hers."

Ice waited.

"My grandmother said," said Aldo slowly, "that it's sat behind that screen since before the First Tell. That the city was built round it. And she said that if it ever came out, it would be because something on the hill didn't add up." He was quiet. "Something it couldn't reckon."

The wind came over the wall.

"You don't add up," said Aldo. "Do you."

"No," said Ice.

Aldo nodded slowly.

"Then mind yourself in there," he said. "On the day. I'm too old to run down any more hills." He looked at Ice with his bright eyes. "But I'll write you. And I'll sit in the front row and watch. And if that thing comes out from behind its screen, young man, it'll have to get past eighty-seven years of Aldo Pellow before it gets to you."

"I'll stand on your right," said Ice, before he'd thought about it.

Aldo looked at him.

"That's a thing they say," said Ice. "Where I— a thing my friends say. On the walls. The one on your right covers you."

"I know what it means," said Aldo. "I heard your Azac at the Bench." He was quiet. "I'll sit on your left, then."

***

Drew was at the bottom of the steps when he came down. Still looking at the Assize wall. Very hard.

"Okay?" said Drew.

"Yeah."

"He cried."

"Yeah."

"You cried."

"Yeah," said Ice.

Drew looked at him for a moment. Then he looked away, down the Wellgate, at the city going gold in the late afternoon.

"Is it bad?" he said.

Ice thought about it. About a fence on a hill. About *asked for me first*. About an old man who'd always said he'd run, and hadn't, and had worked it out in a chair instead.

"No," said Ice. "It's not bad."

Drew nodded.

"Good," he said. "That's the first time anybody's said *no*." He started down the Wellgate. "Somebody thank me."

"Thanks, Drew."

"You're welcome. I'll be sending a bill."
