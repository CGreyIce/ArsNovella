The last field was four acres and a bit.

Milli had paced it at dawn, in the frost, on his own, before anybody else was up. He couldn't help it. He'd paced the slip at Hythe and the deck of the *Patience* and the bridge on floor 5 he'd been cut off on, and every room he'd ever slept in on every floor, because if you knew how big a thing was you knew how long it would take, and if you knew how long it would take you could stop thinking about it. He'd walked the edges of the Barn Field in the white grass with his breath going up in front of him and counted his steps and done the sum in his head, twice.

Four acres and a bit. About six hundred and twenty paces round. Barley on the lower half, wheat on the upper, standing, gold, heavy, with the frost on the ears so they glittered. The Long Barn at the top of it, against the hill, black against the dawn. And the whole of the Acre camped round its walls, still asleep, the fires gone to ash.

Seven hundred people. Maybe eight. Five lines of mowers, if everybody who could swing a blade swung one. Say forty mowers. A field this size, with forty mowers, on the one—

Six hours. Seven. Done by mid-afternoon.

He stood at the bottom hedge with the sum done and found that it hadn't helped at all.

He was still thinking about it.

***

"Three," said Hyvred.

She was on the roof of the Long Barn. Of course she was. Nobody had seen her get up there. She was standing on the ridge of it, high, impossibly high, against the pale cold sky, with one hand over her eyes, looking back down the valley, and her voice came down to the field as if she were standing next to you.

"It took three," she said. "In the night. It's in Hollin. The long field by the river. And the one after. And the hedge." She paused. "It's three fields down. From this one. From us."

Everybody in the field stopped.

Seven hundred people, standing in the frost, with blades on their shoulders, looking up at a passer girl on a roof. And then, all at once, turning round. Looking down the valley. At the shoulder of the hill where the lane came round, where the Acre opened out again below the barn fields. You couldn't see Hollin from here. You couldn't see the Grey.

You didn't need to.

"Three fields," said Annet Sallow. At the gate. With her stick. She looked at it, the way she looked at it every morning, and ran her thumb down it to the bottom, to the three rings for the frost, each one higher than the last, the top one almost at today's notch. Then she looked at the Barn Field, standing gold in the frost in front of them.

"Then it's today," said Annet. "The whole of it. Before the bell." She didn't raise her voice. She never did. It carried anyway. "There's no tomorrow for this one. Everybody."

She turned to the end of the first line of mowers. To where Newish was standing, with her staff on her back and her nose red with cold and her breath going up.

"Count," said Annet.

Newish breathed in.

"*One*," said Newish.

Forty blades went into the barley.

***

He bound.

He was the fastest binder of the thirty-six. He'd been the fastest since the first day, because he'd watched Wren for an hour and worked out the twist, and then he'd timed himself against Wren and lost, and then he'd timed himself again and lost by less. He'd never once beaten her. He'd stopped trying on the twelfth day. He was the second-fastest binder in Haverley and he'd decided that was a reasonable thing to be.

He bound all morning in the barley behind the line, on the one, with the frost going off the stubble round his boots in a low white mist as the sun came up over the hills, and the barley's long beards scratching his wrists, and his tail stiff with cold until about nine and then not. And all morning, without meaning to, he did sums.

He couldn't stop. It was what his head did when there was something it didn't want to look at directly. It went sideways, into numbers.

How many sheaves in a stook. Eight. How many stooks in a row. How many rows in the field. How long it took a cart to go up to the barn doors and back. How many hours until the bell. How many fields the Grey took last night, and the night before, and whether it was a curve or a line, and if it was a curve where the curve went. How many days the thirty-six had been on floor 14. Thirty-eight. How many days since a white room. Four hundred and forty-seven.

How many days since the spring.

He didn't know. That was the trouble. The spring. Their date. It had been on a list, in his phone, on Earth, at the top of the list, with a little calendar beside it and a hundred things underneath it ticked off one by one. The place. The food. The rings. The people. He'd ticked every one of them. He'd measured the rings himself, four times each, with thread and a ruler, because he didn't trust the shop.

And then a white room. And the spring had been fourteen floors ago and four hundred and forty-seven days ago and no days ago at all, because on Earth it was still the same night, the same minute, nine minutes past something, with the phone face down on the bedside table and the list in it with everything ticked.

He didn't know how long ago their date was. He couldn't make it come out.

"You're doing it," said somebody.

He looked up. It was Thea. Binding, in the next row, with straw in her hair. She'd got good at it. Good enough to watch other people while she did it.

"Doing what?"

"Sums," said Thea. "You've been moving your mouth. For about an hour." She tied a band, twisted, tucked, stood the sheaf up. "Ice does it on stairs. Counts. I know the face."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She picked up another armful. She didn't look at him. "He told me once. On a stair. He said if you count it you don't have to feel it."

Milli didn't say anything.

"It doesn't work, by the way," said Thea. "He said that too." She tied the band. "You still feel it. You just feel it with numbers on."

***

By mid-afternoon there was one tuft left.

He'd known there would be. Annet had told them, at supper the night before, under the Long Barn's walls, with the whole of the Acre sitting round the fires. You didn't cut the whole field. Not the last of it. The mowers went round and round it, closing in, in a ring, the last lines going round and round a shrinking square of gold in the middle, until there was nothing left standing in the whole Barn Field but one small tuft in the very centre. A few dozen stalks. The best ones. And then they stopped.

And the oldest binder came out.

It was Ezra. The white-bearded one, the one who'd fallen behind on Bramble Piece in the heat and started all of it. He came out of the binders, slowly, across the stubble, seventy-one years old, and knelt down in front of the last tuft, and took it in his hands.

The whole Acre went quiet.

Seven hundred people. Standing in a ring round the edge of the field, round the last tuft and the old man kneeling in front of it, in the low gold light, with the shadows long. Nobody said anything. Milli could hear the stream at the bottom of the field, going over its stones. A rook, somewhere. The wind in the last tuft, which was the only thing in the whole field still standing to make that sound.

Ezra plaited it.

Slowly. Three strands, the stalks just under the ears, over and under, the way the old women had plaited Hana's crown. He plaited it until it stood up in the middle of the stubble stiff and straight and tied at the top, the ears bunched together, like a little gold figure standing on its own. A neck, Cobb had called it. *The neck.*

Then Ezra got up, and took one step back from it, and another, and went back to the binders. And the mowers came forward.

Not with their scythes. They'd laid their scythes down at the edge of the field. Each of them had a hook now. A little curved sickle, short, with a wooden handle worn black. They came forward into a ring round the neck, at twenty paces, all forty of them, and stopped. And Annet came out of the crowd with her stick, and stood beside the ring.

"You know what to do," said Annet. To the mowers. And then, without turning her head, louder, to all of them: "And passers throw too. It's the whole Acre's. Anybody that's cut in it." She looked round the field. At the thirty-six, standing in a knot by the barn-end of the ring. "That's you."

The first mower threw.

He threw underarm, the hook spinning low across the stubble, flashing, and it went past the neck and stuck in the ground beyond it. A groan went round the field. The next one threw. Closer. It went through the stalks just above the plait and took a few ears off the top. A shout. Not a cut. You had to cut it at the bottom, Cobb had said. Clean. In one. Or it didn't count.

They went round the ring. Forty hooks. Some close. None at the bottom.

Then the passers.

Annet held out a basket of hooks. The thirty-six looked at each other.

Yan went first, because of course he did. He took a hook out of the basket and weighed it in his hand and looked at it with his head on one side and then threw it, overhand, hard, much too hard, and it went straight through the neck at about knee height and out the other side and twenty metres on into the far hedge. The neck stood there with a gap through the middle of it, still standing, still plaited. The field howled. Yan looked very pleased with himself.

"That's not how you do it," said Wendy.

"It went through."

"It went through the *middle*."

Calvin threw, and missed by about four metres, and fell over. Tiger threw too fast and hit Salty's hook in mid-air. Hyvred threw from the roof, which wasn't allowed, and hit it, and Annet said it didn't count, because you had to stand in the ring.

And then Hana looked at Milli.

He'd known she would. He'd felt it coming all the way round the ring, the way you feel weather. She was standing beside him at the edge of the stubble with her ears up and her tail going slowly side to side, and she looked at him, and then at the neck, and then at him.

"Honey," she said.

"No."

"Honey."

"I'm not a hook person. I'm a knife person."

"So use a knife."

He looked at her. She looked back at him, perfectly calm, perfectly smooth, the way she looked at Annet or a guard or a frightened sea captain or anybody she wanted something from. Except that her charm didn't work on him. It never had. He'd always said it was because he was rational. She'd always said it was because he was already charmed.

He took out a knife.

***

He didn't step into the ring straight away. He stood at the edge of it and looked.

Twenty paces. His paces, not a mower's. Fourteen metres, near enough. The wind from the left, up the valley, steady, about as strong as somebody breathing out on your face. The neck: a plait as thick as two fingers at the base, where the stalks went into the ground, thinner above, the top a good forty centimetres higher than the bottom. You'd have to hit the bottom. The bottom was the thick bit. You'd have to hit it hard enough to go through.

A knife wasn't a hook. A hook spun. You threw it and it went round and round and hoped. A knife, he could put somewhere.

He stepped into the ring.

The field went quiet again. He felt it go. Seven hundred people, and the thirty-six among them, watching a passer with a cat's tail stand in a ring of mowers at the end of a harvest with a knife in his hand. He didn't look at them. He looked at the neck.

He breathed in.

And then something happened that had never happened to him before. In either the white room or anywhere since. He breathed in to throw, the way he'd breathed in to throw a thousand times on fourteen floors, and the sum in his head, the one that had been going all day, all week, all the way up the valley, stopped.

Not finished. Stopped.

He didn't know how far it was. He didn't know how hard to throw. He'd known a second ago and now he didn't. He just stood there with the knife, and the neck in front of him, standing up gold in the low sun in the middle of an empty field, and Hana behind him, and the whole of the Acre, and for the first time in his life he didn't know the number.

He threw anyway.

He didn't remember throwing. He remembered his arm going, and the knife leaving his fingers, and a line of light across the stubble very low, a flash in the sun.

And the neck fell over.

Not fell. *Leaned.* Slowly. All in one, the whole plaited figure, leaning over to the right, toward the barn, and then lying down on the stubble the way a person lies down when they're tired. And behind it, stuck in the earth, at an angle, his knife, with the last few cut stalks of the last tuft of the last field in the Long Acre lying across the blade.

Clean. At the bottom. In one.

Nobody made a sound.

"Shout," said Cobb. Behind him. Urgently. "Shout it, lad. *I have it.* You've got to shout it."

Milli turned round.

"*I have it*," he said.

It came out wrong. Too quiet. A question.

"*Louder!*"

"*I HAVE IT!*" shouted Milli.

And the whole of the Acre shouted back at him, seven hundred voices, from every side of the field at once, so loud the rooks went up out of the trees at the top of the valley in a black cloud:

"*WHAT HAVE YOU?*"

He didn't know. Nobody had told him this part.

He stood there in the ring with his mouth open. Hana, at the edge of the stubble, had both her hands over her mouth. Jared beside her was laughing. Annet Sallow was looking at him from under her hat with something on her face he'd never once seen on it, and he understood suddenly that she wasn't going to tell him. That you didn't get told. That every year somebody cut the neck, and every year the whole of the Acre shouted *what have you*, and every year that person had to find the answer on their own.

"*The last of it!*" shouted Milli.

And the field went up.

***

They put the neck on the top of the last load.

The last cart. The biggest of them, the old blue one from Haverley, with the paint gone grey, and the two great brown horses in front of it dressed for harvest-home: plaits of wheat in their manes, and ribbons, red and blue and gold, and the old women had threaded poppies along their harness. The last sheaves from the Barn Field went up onto it, every one, pitched up on the one, until the load was the height of a house and swaying. And on the very top of the load, where everybody could see it, Ezra laid the neck. The plaited gold figure, lying on its back on the top of the harvest, with Milli's cut at the bottom of it.

And then the girls went up.

Wren first, because she'd earned it. Then four or five more. Up the side of the load by the ladder, in their best aprons, with poppies in their hair, to sit on the top of the harvest round the neck in the evening sun, swinging their legs, holding on to each other, shrieking. And Wren, who'd carried water for the whole Acre every day for thirty-eight days, put both her hands round the neck, carefully, and held it up over her head.

The horses leaned into their collars, and the cart moved.

They sang it in.

He'd never heard the song. None of the passers had. It was very old, Cobb said, and nobody knew who'd made it, and it had about a hundred verses and nobody knew all of them, and it didn't matter, because there was a line that came round at the end of every verse and everybody knew that. The whole Acre walked behind the cart, up the last of the stubble, up the hill toward the Long Barn, in the low gold light, with the shadows going out long in front of them over the frost-burnt grass, and sang. Seven hundred people. The verses ragged, different villages singing different words, the old women in front, the mowers in a gang behind them with their blades on their shoulders again, the children running in and out. And then the line at the end, all together, so loud it came back off the hills:

*And we have brought the harvest home.*

He didn't sing. He couldn't. He didn't know it.

He walked behind the cart with Hana's hand in his and found that he was crying, and didn't stop, and didn't know why.

At the top of the hill, at the doors of the Long Barn, he looked back.

Just once. Down the valley. At the shoulder of the hill, and below it, very far down, beyond the last of the barn fields, the place where the lane came round from Hollin. In the last of the sun, you could see it now, from up here. A long flat grey line across the bottom of the lowest barn field, where the hedge was. Very still. Nothing moving on it. Nothing on the other side of it but grey.

The field above it was stubble. Every stalk was cut. Every sheaf was on this cart.

There was nothing left out there for it.

Behind him, somewhere down in the valley, very faint, a bell rang. Hollin's bell, from its tower, a long way down. Grey now. Ringing anyway. One stroke. Then another.

Nobody stopped. It was the one night of the year you didn't. It was the one night the bell meant *come in*, and everybody was already in.

They drove the last cart in through the great doors of the Long Barn, and the doors stood open behind it, and the whole of the Acre went in after it, singing.

***

Inside, it was gold.

He hadn't been ready for that. He'd seen the outside. A hill of stone. He'd thought the inside would be dark, the way the tithe barn at Haverley was dark, a cathedral of old straw and shadow. It wasn't. Somebody had been in all day, while the field was being cut. The old women who couldn't bind any more, and the children too small to carry, and the people who'd come up from the top villages with their tables on their carts. And they'd lit it.

Lamps. Hundreds of them. On every beam, every post, every ledge, every bracket in the long grey walls. Lanterns hung from the great crossbeams overhead on chains, swinging slightly, high up, so many of them that the roof, which he'd thought would be black, was gold, lit from underneath, the old timbers glowing like the inside of a hive. And tables. Long ones. The length of the barn. Trestles set end to end and end to end until they went away into the distance, a road of tables, three of them side by side, with benches, and cloths on them, and bread already on the cloths, and jugs, and apples from Ashwell piled in the middle in heaps.

And at the far end of the barn, against the end wall, the frame.

The iron frame. As tall as the roof. With its crosspiece like shoulders and its hoop like a head.

It wasn't empty any more.

The old women had been building him all afternoon. With the sheaves the carts had been bringing up from the Barn Field since noon, while the field was still being cut. They'd bound them onto the frame, sheaf on sheaf, layer on layer, heads out, so that the whole frame was covered in gold. A body. Arms, out to the sides from the crosspiece, bundles of straw bound round with plaited bands. A head, in the hoop, round, the size of a cartwheel, with a face plaited into it. Two eyes, of twisted straw. A mouth, a line. Something like a nose. And on top of the head, a crown of the same poppies the horses had.

The straw man.

He stood at the end of the barn in the lamplight, as tall as the roof, with his straw arms out, and his plaited face looking down the whole length of the tables at the doors.

The last cart stopped in front of him. Ezra climbed up the ladder, slowly, with Wren handing the neck down to him from the top of the load. And he took the neck, the plaited figure Milli had cut, and climbed higher, onto the frame itself, up the straw man's body, which was bound tight enough to climb, until he was at the straw man's chest.

And he put the neck there. In the middle. Where a heart would go. Tied it in with a band.

Then he climbed down.

The whole of the Acre, standing in the barn in the lamplight, lifted its hands.

"To keep the winter," said Annet Sallow.

"*To keep the winter*," said seven hundred people.

Milli looked up at the straw man's face. At the two twisted eyes. He looked at it for a long time.

He'd heard Ice say it on the first evening. At the edge of the first field. In the shade of the old barn, with Higu writing it on the rock. *A straw man as tall as the roof. In the winter, it walked.*

It wasn't winter.

He looked away.

***

"Before we eat," said Annet. "So there's bread for it."

She stood at the end of the barn, under the straw man, with her stick. The whole of the Acre was sitting down at the tables, or trying to, seven hundred people finding seven hundred places on the benches, and the noise was enormous. She didn't shout. She lifted her stick, an inch, the way Gaffer Tobin did.

The barn went quiet.

Not all at once. From the front backwards. The way it went quiet at the bell. Table by table, bench by bench, all the way to the doors, until there were seven hundred people sitting in a barn full of gold light not saying anything, looking at a woman in a straw hat with a stick.

"Anybody that means to wed," said Annet.

Hana stood up.

Milli was already standing. He hadn't sat down. He'd been standing at the end of the second table with his hands at his sides and his tail straight down and his heart doing something it hadn't done since the bridge on floor 5, when the gust took it and he was alone on an island watching the dusk come.

He looked at her.

She'd put the crown on.

He'd seen it. They'd all seen it, at supper the night before, held up on a stick. It wasn't the same, on her. On a stick it had been a plait of wheat with flowers in it. On her it was a crown of sun. The fat gold ears all facing out and up, round her head, in her lavender hair, between her ears, with the poppies red and the cornflowers blue all the way round. And at the front, in the middle of it, small and black and ugly and twisted on with one thread of wire, Jared's rose.

She'd left her own crown on the table. The little purple-and-gold one. It sat by her plate, on its side, with its bead chains in the bread.

She came round the end of the table. Jared stood up, from the bench, and came with her, on her left. Milli came round the other way. And Aleaka stood up from beside him, with Sleet at her heel and Tuppence on her shoulder, and came with him, on his right, her ears very flat and her face very fierce, the way it got when she was trying not to do something with it.

They met in front of the straw man.

Annet was standing there with a sheaf. One sheaf. From the Barn Field. The last field. Heads up, butts down, not bound, just held together in her two arms, gold.

She gave it to Jared and Aleaka.

They held it between them. Jared on one side, Aleaka on the other, a hand each round the middle, upright, so it stood between Hana and Milli like a small gold tree. Jared's hands, scarred, black at the knuckles from the forge. Aleaka's, shaking slightly, with her fox ears flat against her head.

"Bind it," said Annet.

She held out a band. Already made. Twisted from long stalks of wheat, the longest she'd been able to find, a band twice as long as a binding band, with a loose end at each end.

Hana took one end. Milli took the other.

That was the whole of it. That was what Annet had said, the night at Lammas, when Hana asked. You took one band, both of you, and you put it round the sheaf, and you twisted it, each from your own end, toward the middle. And when your hands met in the middle you tied it. Together. One knot. Both of you. And if it held, it was bound.

*And if it doesn't hold?* Milli had asked. Because of course he had.

Annet had looked at him the way the gleaners looked at anybody who asked a question like that.

*It holds*, she'd said. *Nobody's ever tied one at harvest-home that didn't.* And then, after a moment: *You'll want to tie it slow.*

He put his end round the sheaf. Hana put hers round the other side. Their hands went round, past each other, Aleaka's fingers and Jared's fingers on the sheaf between them, and he felt the band go tight.

He started to twist.

His hands were shaking. He'd thought they would be. He'd thought about it, all the way up the valley, every night, lying awake in six different barns with Hana asleep against him: his hands shaking at the band in front of seven hundred people and him getting it wrong. Getting it too loose. Too fast. The way he'd done his first hundred bands in Haverley. The way he'd done most things in his life, if he was honest, until floor 14: too fast. Before they were ready.

*You'll want to tie it slow.*

He thought about Tiger. On the deck of the *Patience*, with a hank of tarred cord across his knees. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. *Let each turn lie down where it's going before you move it again.*

He slowed down.

He twisted the band a turn. And waited. And felt it settle. And twisted another. And waited. And across the sheaf, Hana was doing the same. He could see her hands. Not shaking. Hana's hands never shook. But slow. Very slow. As slow as him. Turn. Wait. Turn. Not looking at the band. Looking at him.

Their hands met in the middle, at the front of the sheaf, over Aleaka's fingers.

They tied it.

One knot. Both of them. Her fingers and his on the same two ends of straw, pulling them through, round, through, together. He didn't think about it. There was nothing to think about. His hands knew how to tie a band now, and so did hers, and it turned out that when two people who both knew how to tie a band did it at the same time, it tied itself.

They pulled it tight.

It held.

Jared let go of his side. Aleaka let go of hers. The sheaf stood on its own, on the floor of the Long Barn, bound, in front of the straw man.

"It's bound," said Annet Sallow.

"*Aye*," said the whole of the Acre.

***

"Anything to say?" said Annet.

She said it to both of them. Quite matter-of-factly. As if she asked it every year, which she probably did. As if it were the bit where you said whatever you'd come to say, and then everybody ate.

Hana looked at him.

He opened his mouth. He'd had something. He'd had it on the list. On Earth. In his phone. *Vows*, it had said, near the bottom, under *rings* and over *music*, and he'd ticked it, because he'd written them, three drafts, late at night, very carefully, and they'd been good. Rational. Clear. He'd been proud of them.

He couldn't remember a single word.

"I measured everything," said Milli.

His voice came out very small in the enormous barn. He didn't try to make it bigger.

"I always measure everything. Ask anybody. Ask her." He looked at Hana. "The slip at Hythe. The bridge on five. This field. Our rings." He swallowed. "If I know how big a thing is, I know how long it'll take. And then I don't have to—" He stopped. "Thea said. In the field. *If you count it you don't have to feel it.* That's what I do. That's what I've always done."

Nobody said anything. Seven hundred people, not saying anything.

"I've been trying to measure this for thirty-eight days," said Milli. "How far. How long. How long since the spring. How long until—" He stopped. "And it won't come out. None of it. I don't know how far away home is. I don't know how long ago our date was, or how long until we'd have had it, or whether we're ever going to get back to it, or whether it'll still be the same minute when we do." He looked at her. "And then I threw a knife at the end of a field just now and I didn't know how far it was. For the first time in my life. I just threw it."

He breathed.

"And it went exactly where it was supposed to," he said. "So I've stopped."

"Stopped what?" said Hana. Very quietly.

"Measuring you." He was very red. He didn't care. "I'm not going to. Not ever. I don't need to know how big it is. I know it's the biggest thing. That's enough." He looked down at the sheaf between them, bound. "That's the whole thing. That's all I've got."

Hana didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then she put her hand up and took off the crown. The wheat one. Carefully, with both hands. And held it against her chest, the way she'd held her own crown on the ledge over the sea on floor 11, when she took it off for the first time in front of anybody.

She looked at him over the top of it.

"Honey," said Hana.

That was all. Not smooth. Not calm. Her voice didn't do the thing it always did, the holding-very-still thing. It broke, in the middle of the word, like a band snapping. *Hon—ey.* And she laughed, at herself, in the middle of it, with her eyes wet, and said it again, properly.

"*Honey.*"

"That's not vows," said Newish, from the front table. Croaked. "That's just his *name*."

"That's all my vows," said Hana. Not looking away from him. "He knows what it means. That's the point."

He did.

***

Jared had the rings.

He took them out of his shirt pocket, in their rag, and unwrapped them, there, in front of the straw man, in front of everybody, and held them out on his palm. Two small black iron rings, not quite even. In all that gold light they looked like nothing. Like two links off a chain.

"Salty's nail," said Jared. To the whole barn. Calm, cool, collected. The voice he used for everything, even this. "Number a hundred. From floor 1. The first thing he ever made here." He looked at Milli. "It's in the grain. The crooked. He says you can't get it out." He almost smiled. "He's right."

Somewhere at the second table, Salty made a sound and put his face in his hands. Gabe, beside him, put an arm round his shoulders without looking.

Milli took the small one. Hana's.

He held it for a moment. He didn't need to measure it. He knew it was seventeen point three. He'd told Jared, and Jared had made it, and it would be.

He ran his thumb round the inside of it, without meaning to. The way you do with a ring.

There was something there.

He stopped. He turned it to the light. And on the inside of the band, so small he'd never have seen it if his thumb hadn't found it first, a rose. Five petals and a dot. The same as the one on the front of her crown. The same as the one on the inside of the rim of the shield on Ice's back.

He looked at Jared.

Jared didn't say anything. He just looked back, steady, the way he'd always looked at Hana. The way that said *I'm here*.

"So we know which bit's yours," said Milli. Very quietly.

"So you know which bit's mine," said Jared.

Milli put the ring on Hana's finger.

It fitted. Seventeen point three. Exactly.

Hana put the other on his. Nineteen point one. Exactly.

He looked down at his hand. Black iron, on his finger, in the gold light of a barn at the end of a valley at the end of a harvest on the fourteenth floor of a tower, a long, long way from a list in a phone with everything on it ticked. It was the ugliest ring anybody had ever been married with.

He'd never once in his life wanted anything more.

"It's bound," said Annet Sallow again. Louder. To the whole of the barn. And lifted her stick.

The Long Barn went up.

***

They ate.

He didn't remember what. Everything. The whole of the Acre's harvest, the first of it, out of every village's ovens and pits and pots: bread, and bread, and more bread, every kind, round and long and plaited, with seeds and without, still warm. Cheese. Mutton. Apples from Ashwell in heaps, and apples baked, and apples in pies. Pots of beans. Plums. Honey. Jugs of the brown cloudy drink that made Calvin cough, and jugs of something clearer that the old women drank in very small cups and that made Calvin cry. He didn't eat much of any of it. He sat at the end of the second table with Hana beside him and her hand in his under the table, both rings touching, and people kept coming up to them. Gleaners. Old women who'd plaited the crown. Mowers. Cobb. Ezra. Bettris the smith, who didn't say anything at all, but stood in front of them for a while and looked at the rings on their hands, and nodded once, the way the lean mower nodded at Yan. And then went away.

And the thirty-six. All of them. One at a time.

Calvin, who hugged them both at once, very hard, and told them it was the best wedding he'd ever been to, and then admitted it was the only one. Rachael, who didn't hug anybody, and put her hand on Hana's shoulder and then on his, once each, and said "Good," and went away. Gabe, who shook his hand, very formally, and then didn't let go. Thea, who'd been crying since the band and was still crying, and didn't try to stop, and said to Hana, "You held it so slow," and to him, "You didn't count," and he said *no*, and she said *good* and laughed and cried at the same time. Yan, who looked at the two of them for a long moment with his head on one side, at the place under their breastbones, and said "They're leaning," and wouldn't explain, and Suzu round his neck did something that might have been a smile. Xola, who'd had three of the small cups, and was making Squiddles do a dance on the table.

Ice came last.

He didn't say anything for a while. He stood at the end of the table and looked at Hana in her wheat crown, and her own crown on the table by her plate with its bead chains in the bread, and then at Milli, and then at their hands.

"Alice," he said.

Hana looked at him.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her on the *Patience*, at the edge of the Undertow, when she'd held the sea for four seconds and smiled. The way you look at somebody who's come back.

Then he looked at Milli.

"The neck," said Ice. "You cut the neck."

"I didn't know how far it was."

"I know," said Ice. "I saw." His mouth did something at the corner. "That's how you do it."

And then he went to stand with Thea. And didn't look back.

***

Then they danced.

Somebody had a fiddle. Somebody always has a fiddle. Three of them, it turned out, and a drum, and a thing like a pipe with a bag on it that made a sound like a goose being stepped on, and the tables went back against the walls and the floor of the Long Barn was cleared, and the whole of the Acre danced.

Not the way people danced at home. Long lines. Two lines, facing each other, the length of the barn, men and women, or whoever wanted to be in which line, and you went down the middle, and turned, and swapped, and came back up, and the next pair went down. It went on forever. It was very complicated and everybody knew it except the passers. And the passers went in anyway, because Newish stood at the end of the lines with her staff and shouted *ONE* every time it was a passer's turn to go down the middle, and they went down on the one, and turned on it, and nobody was ever where they were supposed to be and nobody cared.

He danced with Hana. Down the middle and back. And again. And again.

And then, on about the fifth time down, he saw Ice and Xola.

They weren't in the line. They were at the edge of it, by the wall, under a lantern, the two of them, doing something that was very nearly the dance and very nearly not. Xola had pulled him in. Milli had seen it out of the corner of his eye. She'd come across the barn from the table with her three small cups in her and her gills spread out like fans and grabbed Ice by both hands and pulled, and he'd said something, *no*, probably, and she'd said something back, and he'd laughed. Properly. Out loud. And let her.

And now they were dancing. Badly. On purpose. Xola stepping on his feet, both of them, every other beat, on purpose, and him stepping back on hers, and both of them laughing so hard they kept having to stop. Xola's lavender hair going everywhere. His black hair in his eyes. Turning under each other's arms and getting tangled and turning back. Like two people who'd done this before, a long time ago. In a different room. On a call, maybe. Before.

Hana had told him once. On Earth. Ice and Xola had been best friends, a long time ago, the two of them and Thea, before he went quiet, and went off to Yan's lot, and nobody ever really knew why.

He didn't know any of it. He didn't need to. He could see it. Two people who'd been the best of friends once, and lost it, and found it again, a long way up a tower, dancing at the edge of a wedding that wasn't theirs, stepping on each other's feet.

And at the end of the table by the wall, Thea.

She wasn't dancing. She was sitting on the end of the bench with her knees up and the lantern beside her, watching them. Laughing. So hard she was crying again. With both hands over her mouth, the way Hana had stood at the edge of the field when he threw the knife. Watching Xola step on Ice's feet for the twentieth time and Ice step on hers back and both of them nearly fall over.

That was all. She didn't get up. She just watched them, and laughed, and wiped her face.

Then Xola let go, and pushed him, quite hard, back toward the table, toward Thea, and spun round on her own and went off down the middle of the line on the wrong beat with her arms out, shouting.

Ice sat down beside Thea, out of breath, still laughing. She leaned against him. He put his arm round her.

Milli turned at the bottom of the line with Hana and went back up the middle, on the one.

***

It was Hana who saw Gaffer Tobin first.

He'd been at the head of the first table all evening. In his chair. The lads had carried him in off the cart at the end, when the last load came through the doors, and set him down at the top of the barn, by the straw man, where he could see all of it. The tables. The dancing. The whole of the Acre, in one place, in the gold light, for the one night of the year.

Milli had seen him lift his stick when the last load came in. An inch. And hold it. For a long time. Thirty, he'd counted, without meaning to. Then put it down.

And he'd seen him lift it again. At the band. When it held. At the two of them. Standing in front of the straw man with the sheaf bound between them, and the whole of the Acre saying *aye*. The old man in his chair had lifted his stick, an inch, off his knees, at them. And held it.

He hadn't seen him put it down.

He'd been dancing. He'd been eating. He'd been looking at Hana. He hadn't looked at the head of the table for an hour.

Hana stopped. In the middle of the line. On the one. And Milli, who was holding her hands, stopped too, and the pair behind walked into them, and somebody laughed.

"Honey," said Hana. Very quietly.

He looked where she was looking.

Gaffer Tobin was sitting in his chair at the head of the first table, very upright, with his blanket over his knees, facing down the barn. His eyes were open. He was looking at the dancing. At the long lines, going down the middle and back. His stick was across his knees, and both his old thin hands were on it.

He wasn't holding it up any more.

He wasn't doing anything at all.

Annet had seen too. She was already going. Up the side of the barn, along the wall, past the dancing, not fast, not slow, with her own stick in her hand. She got to the head of the table. She stood beside the old man's chair for a moment, looking down at him. Then she bent, and put two fingers on the back of his hand. On the stick.

She stayed like that for a while.

The music was still going. The dancing was still going. Nobody else had seen. Seven hundred people in a barn full of gold light, going down the middle and back.

Then Annet straightened up.

She didn't say anything. She took Gaffer Tobin's stick from under his hands, very gently. And lifted it. Not her own. His. An inch, the way he had, at the barn. At the dancing. At the straw man, at the long tables, at the whole of the Acre in one place. And held it.

And put it down, across his knees, under his hands again.

The fiddles stopped.

Not all at once. One. Then another. Then the drum. Somebody at the head of the barn had seen, and then somebody else, and it went down the lines the way the quiet went down the field at the bell. Table by table. Pair by pair. The dancers stopping, in the middle of the barn, with their hands still joined, turning round.

Until the whole of the Long Barn was quiet, and seven hundred people were standing in the lamplight looking at an old man in a chair at the head of a table, with his eyes open, watching them.

"He saw it in," said Annet Sallow.

Nobody said anything.

Milli felt Hana's hand go tight in his.

He didn't know what was going to happen. He'd never been anywhere where something like this happened in the middle of a wedding. He thought, distantly, the way he thought about everything, that on Earth somebody would scream. Somebody would call somebody. The music would stop and not start again and everybody would go home.

Annet looked down the barn.

"Play," she said.

And the fiddler at the head of the barn, a young woman from Lammas with red hair, looked at her for a long moment, with her bow in the air. And then put it on the strings, and played.

The same tune. From where it had stopped.

And the Acre danced.

Not all at once. One pair, then another, starting again, going down the middle, turning. Slower than before. Then not slower. The drum came back. The goose-pipe came back. The lines went down the barn and back, the way they'd been going all night, and at the head of the first table, in his chair, watching them, was Gaffer Tobin, who'd seen seventy-four harvests in and had said he would see this one, the whole of it, and then he would stop.

He'd seen it. The whole of it. Every field. Every village. The last load. The straw man. Two passers with cat's tails binding a sheaf with one band at the end of the barn.

He'd stopped.

"That's how it's done," said somebody beside Milli. Very quietly. It was Cobb. He was standing at the edge of the line with his hands at his sides and his face wet and his chin up. "You see it in. And then you stop. On a good night." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "There's no better night than this one. Not in the whole year. Good for him."

Milli looked at him.

Then he looked at Hana. In her wheat crown, which she'd put back on, with the poppies and the cornflowers and the iron rose. With her eyes very bright.

"It's a good night to stop on," said Hana. Very quietly. "Isn't it."

He didn't measure it.

He didn't try. He stood in the middle of the Long Barn, holding her hands, with the music going round them and the lines going down the middle and back and an old man in a chair at the head of the table who'd seen everything he'd wanted to see, and he didn't count anything at all.

"Yes," said Milli.

And Newish, at the end of the lines, with her face wet and her voice nearly gone, lifted her staff and croaked, "*One*," and they went down the middle together.

***

Later, much later, when the fiddles had stopped for good and the lamps were going out one by one along the beams and seven hundred people were asleep on the benches and under the tables and in the straw along the walls, Milli woke up.

He didn't know why. He was lying on the floor of the Long Barn near the far end, on a heap of straw, with Hana asleep against him in her wheat crown with her own crown in her hand, and the ring cold on his finger. Most of the lamps were out. The barn was dark, the way it should have been all along, a great dark cathedral of old timber going up into nothing. Only a few lanterns still burning, high up, on the chains.

And at the end of the barn, in the light of the last of them, the straw man.

Tall as the roof. With his straw arms out, and his plaited face, and his crown of poppies, and the neck tied into his chest where a heart would go.

Milli looked at him.

He looked for a long time. Lying there, very still. Not because he was frightened. Because something was wrong, and he couldn't see what, and when he couldn't see what was wrong with a thing he looked at it until he could. That was how he'd found the eleven centimetres at Hythe.

It took him a while.

The head.

The old women had set it facing down the barn. Straight down. At the doors. He'd watched them do it. He'd watched them tie the hoop to the frame with a dozen bands so it couldn't move. He'd looked up at the plaited face from in front of it with a sheaf in his arms and a band in his hands, and the two twisted straw eyes had been looking straight over his head at the doors at the far end of the barn.

They weren't now.

Not much. A finger's width. Less. If he hadn't measured it, he'd never have seen. But he'd measured it. He'd measured it without meaning to, while Annet said *bind it*, the way he measured everything. And the head had turned. A finger's width, to the left.

Toward the side of the barn where the thirty-six were sleeping.

He lay there and looked at it.

It didn't move. Not while he watched. It stood there, tall as the roof, in the last of the lamplight, with its straw arms out and its eyes a finger's width to the left of where they'd been tied, perfectly still.

He thought: *I measured wrong.*

He'd never once measured wrong.

"It's listening," said somebody, very quietly.

He turned his head. Aleaka was lying in the straw an arm's length away, on her side, with Sleet curled round her and her glasses off and her ears up. Wide awake. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the straw man.

"Since the neck went in," said Aleaka. Barely a breath. "With Heed. I can hear it." She swallowed. "It's not asking. It's not calling. It's not *more*. It's just—" She stopped.

"Just what?"

"Listening," said Aleaka. "For when." Her ears went flat. "Like something waiting for a bell."

Milli looked back at the straw man.

He didn't say anything. He didn't wake Hana. He lay in the straw at the end of the Long Barn with the ring on his finger and his wife asleep against him, which was a word he'd been going to get to use in the spring, and looked at a straw man as tall as a roof, and didn't sleep again.

It didn't move.

Not while he watched.
