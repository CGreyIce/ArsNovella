Xola was in the ring, twenty paces out, facing in, and she hated every one of the twenty.

She'd never been twenty paces from anybody she was meant to be healing. That was the whole of her. You got close. You got your hands on them. Water, cool, out of nothing, out of her palms, running over the thing that was wrong and closing it, and the skin underneath pink and new and not a mark left, because axolotls didn't scar and neither did anybody she'd ever put her hands on. You couldn't do that from twenty paces. You couldn't do it from two.

And in the middle of the ring, forty paces out from the last root of Lanthorn, in the amber light, were the two people she'd loved longest in the world.

Thea, with the lantern open in her hands. Her best friend. Years, and seven hundred and eighty days, if you counted the tower, and Xola always counted the tower.

And on Thea's right, Ice.

Her other best friend. Her first one, really, if you were being honest, which Xola mostly was. Before Thea. Before the calls. Before he'd liked her and said so and she'd said *I know, Icey, I'm sorry* and he'd said *okay* and stayed, which almost nobody did. Before she'd put the two of them in a call together and it had turned out to be the best thing she ever did.

*Second best was you*, she'd told him once. On thirteen. On a step.

Squiddles was on her collarbone. Cold and damp and very still. She could feel his little heart going against her neck, fast. He didn't like the dark. He didn't like the Moth. He didn't like any of it, and neither did she.

The arrows went.

She saw them go over her head from the roots behind. Higu's first: pale, thin, the faintest moonlight she'd ever seen Higu draw, like the moon through three layers of cloud. Then Winter's, silver-blue, frost trailing off it like breath on a cold morning. Then Aleaka's. Down the slope, past the ring, past Thea, toward the great head at the bottom of the open ground.

Higu's went into the left eye.

Xola saw it go in. A pale thread of light disappearing into the dull red-black, the size of a door. And then Winter's, a breath behind it, into the same place. Into Higu's arrow, nearly.

*Hoarfrost. What you hit stays where it is.*

The eye went white.

Frost. All at once. Out from where the arrows had gone in, across the whole huge round surface of it, the way frost goes across a window on a cold morning, in feathers and ferns, until the left eye of the Great Moth was a round white blind thing, like a plate of ice.

The Moth didn't flinch.

That was the thing. It didn't make a sound. It didn't jerk its head. It didn't do anything at all. It just kept coming. The tongue, uncoiled now, thick as a man and black as leather, lifting off the moss at the bottom of the slope and swaying, and rising, and coming up the open ground toward the ring.

Toward the one light that wasn't scattered.

"Babe," said Xola. Under her breath. To Thea, forty paces away, who couldn't hear her. "Babe, babe, babe—"

The tongue came over the ring.

***

It went over Hako.

She saw it. He had his spear up and the dog behind his legs and he didn't move, and the tongue went over his head like a rope going over a fence, two metres up, and didn't touch him. It wasn't interested in him. It wasn't interested in any of them. It went over the ring and across the twenty paces of moss inside it, and the end of it lifted, and opened.

She hadn't known it opened. Nobody had said. The end of the tongue split, like a flower, into a sort of mouth, a soft dark round thing with a ring of fine pale hairs round the edge, and came down toward the lantern in Thea's hands.

And met the shield.

Gabe hadn't moved. That was what she'd remember. He hadn't stepped. He hadn't put himself in front of Thea. He'd turned, his whole body, toward her, and brought Hearthwall up and across, sideways, with his arm out straight across her front, so the shield was between the lantern and the slope and he was still beside her. Not in front. *Beside.*

The tongue hit Hearthwall flat.

It sounded like a door slammed shut in a stone house. Gabe went back half a step. Not his feet; his feet didn't move. His whole body leaned, from the ankles, the way a tree leans in wind, and his boots tore two long furrows in the moss as they slid back a hand's width, and stopped.

And the tongue stuck.

Xola saw it happen. The soft round mouth on the end of it closing on the face of the shield, the ring of pale hairs flattening against the iron. And then the pull. She'd seen the small ones do it, on the night of the moths, to a stray: a pulse, going up the tongue, the light being drunk. She saw it now. A pulse. Red. Going up the tongue from Hearthwall, away from Gabe, back down the slope, toward the great head.

Hearthwall's warmth. Being drunk.

The red glow round the shield's rim went dim.

"*Gabe*—" She didn't know who said it. Several people. Her.

Gabe didn't say anything. His teeth were together. His arm was out straight across Thea, holding the shield, and the shield was going dark, and she saw frost come up his arm. From the shield. Into his hand, his wrist, his forearm. White, in feathers. The way it had gone across the Moth's eye.

*It drinks the light*, she thought. *It drinks the warm. It's going to drink it all and then it's going to drink him.*

She was moving. She didn't decide. She was out of the ring and across the twenty paces before she'd thought *ring*, and she heard Drew shout something from the roots, and she didn't care. She got to Gabe's other side, the far side from Thea, and put both hands on his arm. On the frost.

Water. Cool. Out of her palms. Over the white, over his wrist, his forearm. The frost going back under it. The skin under it pink.

And then gold, beside hers, on his shoulder. Malcolm. He'd been sitting on the moss behind them with his slate and now he wasn't; he was on his knees beside her with his hands on Gabe's back, blessing it, one, and one behind it, in case. He didn't look at her. He'd been there already. She didn't know how. He always was.

"*It does not go cold*," said Gabe.

Through his teeth. To nobody. To the shield.

And Hearthwall flared.

It went from dark to red in a breath. From red to orange. From orange to something she'd never seen iron go, a deep bright gold-white, the colour of the inside of a forge when Jared had it roaring, the colour of the Breath on ten. The tongue on the face of it was still pulling, still drinking, still sending pulses down the slope, and every pulse it pulled out, the shield made more. It didn't go cold. It *couldn't*. It had been made in the crack in the floor of the world out of kiln-heart iron folded a hundred times by a man who'd said *a shield's never for you*, and it did not go cold.

The tongue started to smoke.

She smelled it. Burning hair. Burning leather. The ring of pale hairs on the soft mouth curling and blackening against the gold-white iron. And the pulses going down the tongue weren't red any more, they were orange, they were gold, they were too much—

The tongue tore itself off the shield.

It came away with a sound like a wet sheet ripped in half and whipped back down the slope, smoking, the end of it black and curled, and the great head at the bottom of the slope jerked. For the first time. Just once. Back.

Gabe stood there with the shield up and his arm out across Thea's front and his face white and his breath coming in shudders, and Hearthwall blazing gold-white in his hand like a star come down. Thea, behind it, with the lantern. Her face lit gold on one side and amber on the other.

Not a step. He hadn't taken a single step in front of her.

Xola saw it in the corner of her eye. Everybody's windows were showing it. She didn't care. She had her hands on Gabe's arm and it was warm again, warm all the way through, and he was shaking.

"Mate," said Gabe. Faintly. Looking at the shield. "*Mate*."

"Shut up," said Xola. "Hold still."

On the other side of Thea, Ice hadn't moved either.

He was standing exactly where he'd been. On her right. With Tuli's pole in one hand and his sword in the other. He was looking at Gabe. At the shield. At Gabe's arm, which Xola was holding.

His face was doing the thing it had done on fourteen, at the line, when he'd looked at the Grey. And then on the jam, before *help*. The thing where everything in him was standing up at once and getting ready to go.

He didn't go.

He looked at Gabe for a long moment, and then he looked at Xola, and his face did something else. Smaller.

"Thank you," said Ice. To her. Quietly.

She'd never once heard him say it during. After, sometimes. Never during.

"Shut up, ice coob," said Xola. Her voice cracked. "Stay where you are."

"I am."

"*Stay* there."

"I'm staying."

***

The Moth came down.

It had to. Higu had said. It was too big to reach that far from where it was, and the tongue had been burned off the light it wanted, and the only way to get closer was to come down. Onto the slope. Onto the open ground.

It lowered itself.

She'd never forget it. The whole vast soft grey-brown shape of it, settling. Like a tent coming down when you pull the poles. The great body sinking toward the moss at the bottom of the slope, and the head coming forward and down, and the wings—

The wings came down either side.

They were so big. They went out past the edges of the open ground, past the white trees on either side, and came down on the moss and on the trees and on everything, slowly, the way snow comes down off a roof. The rings on them went past overhead, huge, dark, like the eyes of something looking down. And through them, close now, so close she could see each one, the lights. Thousands. Pressing. Under the soft grey cloth of the wings, moving, pushing, the way faces push up against the glass of a bus window when the bus pulls away.

*Whose. Whose. Whose.*

The tip of the left wing touched the moss.

"*Now*," said Higu. From the root. Not loud.

Gigi was already there.

Xola saw her. At the left edge of the open ground, crouched, with both hands flat on the moss. And out of the moss, out from under her hands, the bramble. Black. Thick as wrists, thick as arms. Thorned, every inch, with thorns like the curved claws of a cat. It came up out of the ground the way water comes up out of a burst pipe, all at once, and went over the tip of the wing that had touched down. Over it and round it and through it, the thorns going into the soft grey cloth and hooking. And then down again, into the ground. And round. And over. And down. A dozen times. Twenty. Stitching the wing to the moss.

*Grow it where you need it. It doesn't let go.*

On the right, Maigo. Barefoot. Standing, with his feet in the moss and his eyes shut. And under the right wing, where it came down, the ground moved. Roots. Pale ones, white like the trees, as thick as Maigo's arm, coming up out of the moss and closing over the edge of the wing like fingers closing over the edge of a blanket. And pulling. Down.

The Moth pulled back.

The wings lifted. Both of them. Tried to. The whole vast shape heaving, the rings on the wings going up into the dark. And the bramble went tight, the thorns tearing long grey rents in the wing, and Gigi cried out and didn't let go. And Maigo's roots went tight, and the moss round his feet split open, and he went down on one knee with his eyes still shut, and didn't let go.

The wings came back down.

Pinned.

And the Moth, for the first time since it came up out of the dark, made a sound.

Not a roar. Not a scream. She didn't know what to call it. A sort of sigh. Huge and soft and dusty, going out over the open ground and the ring and the roots of Lanthorn, the sound a house makes when the wind gets into the roof. And under it, very faint, thousands of tiny sounds. All the same. All at once.

The lights.

She didn't have Heed. She heard them anyway. Everybody did.

*Whose.*

***

Then its legs came.

She hadn't thought about legs. Nobody had. Rui Xiang had seen it asleep, folded; you couldn't see legs on a thing folded. But it had them. Moths did. And with its wings pinned and its tongue burned and its head down on the slope, it put them out.

Three on each side. From under the great furred body. Long, jointed, grey, as thick as the trunks of the white trees, with hooked feet at the end like the hooks on a coat stand, the size of a man. Coming out from under it and up the slope. Toward the ring.

"*Ring*," said somebody. Drew, from the root. "*Ring, hold*—"

The first leg came down into the ring between Calvin and Deston.

It didn't hit either of them. It came down on the moss between them like a tree falling, with a thud she felt in her feet, and the hooked foot on the end of it dug in and *pulled*, dragging the great body up the slope a pace. Toward the lantern.

Calvin went up it.

She saw him do it and couldn't believe it. With both daggers. Up the leg, the way you'd go up a tree, his feet on the joints, Rachael's fist coming out of his collar empty and Rachael shouting *CALVIN* in a voice Xola had never heard Rachael use. Up to the first joint, the knee, if moths had knees, and both daggers into it, into the soft place where the hard grey shell of the leg bent, and twisting.

The leg buckled.

It didn't come off. It buckled, at the joint, and the hooked foot slipped on the moss, and the great body lurched sideways, and Calvin came off the leg and down onto the moss, rolling, and Rachael had him by the collar again before he'd stopped.

"*LOOK AT MY BACK*," said Calvin. To everybody. Lying on the moss. Laughing. Wild.

"*Shut up*," said Rachael. "*Shut UP, you absolute*—"

The second leg came down on the other side, on Salty's shield.

Salty held it. His greatsword up flat across both hands, and the hooked foot came down on the blade, and he went down to one knee under it and the moss round him split. And held it. The other smith, with his teeth bared, holding a leg the size of a tree off the ground with a sword he'd made himself.

Hako put his spear through the joint above Salty's head.

The leg came off the blade. Salty got up.

The third came at Thea.

Over the ring. Over the twenty paces of moss. The front leg, the longest, coming up the slope out of the dark like the arm of a crane, with the hook on the end of it open, coming for the lantern from the side. The side Gabe wasn't on.

Ice's side.

Xola saw him see it. She was on her knees by Gabe with her hands on his arm, and she saw Ice turn his head, and see the hook coming, and she saw everything in him stand up.

And then Drew was there.

She hadn't seen him come down off the root. Nobody had. He must have jumped. He was on the moss in front of Ice, between Ice and the hook, which was in front, which nobody was supposed to be, except Drew had never once in his life cared about *supposed to*. Bare hands. No iron on the knuckles since ten. And he didn't hit the hook. He didn't block it.

*Hinge. Don't stop it. Turn it.*

He put both hands on the side of the hooked foot as it came, the way you'd put your hands on a swinging door, and *turned*. His whole body. Pivoting, from the hips. And the hook, which had been coming at the lantern, went past. A metre to the left of Thea. Into the moss. Deep. And stuck.

Drew stood there with his hands still on it, breathing.

"Front," said Ice. Very quietly. "You're in front."

"Somebody has to be," said Drew. Without turning round. "It's not going to be you. And it's not going to be her." He took his hands off the hook. "Higu said."

Up on the root, Higu had another arrow on the string. Pale. Faint. Her face was wet. Xola could see it from here.

***

Yan saw it first.

He'd been in the ring, on the far side, the uphill side, near the roots, as far from the Moth as the ring went. With Working On It upright beside him and Suzu round his neck and Wendy at his elbow. He'd been there since Higu said *ring*. Not fighting. Watching. With his head on one side.

The way he watched lights.

"There's one in the middle," said Yan.

He didn't shout it this time. He said it in his ordinary voice, cool and level, and it carried. Everything carried, on the open ground, with the great soft sighing shape breathing at the bottom of the slope.

"Under the head," said Yan. "Where the body starts. In the middle of all of them. A small one." He was quiet. "Old. Very old. Older than anything I've ever seen. It's not like the others. The others are pressing. That one's still. Right in the middle." He tipped his head further. "Pulled in tight. Like hers was. At the bottom."

"What is it?" said somebody. Chris, she thought.

Yan didn't answer.

Thea did.

She was still standing at forty paces, behind Gabe's arm and Gabe's blazing shield, with the lantern open in her hands. She'd been reading the whole time. Xola knew that look. She'd known it for years. Thea reading something nobody else could see, with her lips moving.

"Under *whose*," said Thea. "There's another line. Under it. I didn't see it before. It's—" She stopped. "It's very faint. It's older than the first one. It's the first thing that was ever written there."

"What does it say?" said Ice.

Thea read it. Xola watched her read it. Watched her face.

"*Nobody came*," said Thea.

Nobody said anything.

"It was a stray," said Thea. Very quietly. Her voice was shaking now, the way it hadn't shaken once all night. "The first one. Before Lanthorn. Before people. It was a light in the dark asking *whose*, and nobody came. For so long. And it got—" She stopped. "It started taking them. The others. Into it. So it wasn't— So there'd be—" She couldn't finish.

"So it wasn't on its own," said Ice.

Thea looked at him.

He was looking at the Moth. At the place under the great lowered head where Yan had said. His face was doing something Xola had never seen it do. Not standing up. Not getting ready to go. Something else.

He knew it. Xola could see he knew it. The way you know a thing about somebody because you've been it.

"Yeah," said Thea. Barely. "So it wasn't on its own."

***

The tongue came again.

Not for Thea.

Xola saw it come up off the moss at the bottom of the slope, scorched black at the end, curled, smoking still. It had learned. It didn't go for the lantern. It didn't go anywhere near the gold-white blaze of Hearthwall. It came up the slope low, under the ring, along the moss, the way a snake comes through grass.

To Thea's right.

To the next light.

She saw what it was going for half a breath before anybody else. Because she'd been looking at him. She'd been looking at him all night. At his belt. At Gabe's terrible lantern, the worst one Nesta had ever seen, lumpy and lopsided, hanging off his hip.

With the light in it.

It had gone bright when the Moth rose. Malcolm had told her. It was bright now. Brighter than she'd ever seen a stray. Shining out through Gabe's bad knot in forty points instead of a hundred, so you could nearly see the middle. Not scattered enough. Not nearly. And in the amber of the lantern and the gold of the shield and all that dark, it was the second brightest thing on the open ground.

And it was a point.

And he still hadn't looked at it.

"*Ice*," said Xola.

He looked at her.

"*Your belt*—"

He looked down.

***

She saw him see it.

She'd remember that longer than the Moth. Longer than the shield. Longer than anything that happened on sixteen. Ice, standing on Thea's right with a pole and a sword, looking down at his own hip for the first time in thirteen falls. At the lantern Gabe had made him. At the light in it.

His face went completely still.

"Oh," said Ice.

That was all. Just *oh*. The way you'd say it if you opened a door you'd walked past every day for a year and found somebody sitting in the room behind it. Waiting. For a long time.

The light in the lantern turned toward him.

She saw it. Everybody near him saw it. The forty points of it moving, all together, toward his face. Toward his eyes. The way the lights under the Moth's wings had pressed toward John's mote at the bottom of the bowl. The way the strays had turned toward the goat.

*Whose*, it was asking. She didn't need Heed. Nobody did. It was the only word on the whole floor. *Whose are you.*

And the tongue came up off the moss beside him, the soft scorched mouth on the end of it opening, a metre from his hip.

She knew what he'd do. She'd known him for years. Longer than Thea had. He'd step. Early, not fast. He'd be somewhere else when it landed. He was never where it landed, he'd never once been where anything landed, that was the whole of how he fought, and it'd go past him and he'd turn and cut it and—

And the light would be on his hip. And it'd go past him and take the light.

He didn't step.

He put his hand over the lantern.

His left hand. He let go of Tuli's pole; it fell on the moss. And he put his bare hand flat over the top of Gabe's terrible lantern, over the light, the way you'd put your hand over a candle flame to keep the wind off. The way Hana had over her own. The way Malcolm had.

"*Mine*," said Ice.

Out loud. Not loud. Very clearly.

The tongue hit his hand.

***

She heard it hit.

She was already moving. She'd let go of Gabe, she was up, she was across the moss, past Thea, past the lantern, and she heard it hit his hand like a fist hitting a door. And stick. And pull.

Ice didn't move his hand.

She got there. She got her hands on his arm, on his wrist, below the place where the soft scorched mouth of the tongue was closed over the back of his hand. She could feel it pulling. Drinking. Through his hand. Not the light in the lantern; his hand was over it, the tongue couldn't get to it. Him.

He'd always had a bit of amber in him. Faint. You only saw it in the dark, if you were looking. Hyvred had followed it up three floors once, the first year, before any of them had found him: an amber trail, she'd said, like a lamp somebody carried and never put down. Xola had never known what it was.

She knew now. The tongue was drinking him. The way it would drink a stray.

She felt it go. Under her hands, in his arm. A pulse. Amber. Going up the tongue, away from him, down the slope.

"*Seventy*," said Ice. Through his teeth. To her. "It hurts."

"*Let go of it*—"

"No."

"Ice, *let go*, you can *step*, you can—"

"It's mine." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking down at his hand over the lantern. "It asked. It asked *whose*. Nobody ever—" A pulse. Another. "Sixty-two. It hurts. It asked and I said."

Water. Out of her palms. She didn't decide. She poured it into him, into his arm, his wrist, his hand, as fast as she'd ever poured anything into anybody, cool, everything she had. And it went in. And it went up the tongue with everything else, down the slope, into the Moth. She couldn't keep up. It was drinking faster than she could pour.

"*Sixty*," said Ice. "Sixty. It hurts. Xola. *Sixty*."

He'd promised. He healed at sixty. Every time. Out loud. Because Thea had asked.

He healed.

She felt Mend Self go into him under her hands, plain, the first thing he'd ever learned. And it went up the tongue too. And he didn't move his hand.

"*Babe*," said Xola, and it wasn't to him, it was to Thea, behind him, she didn't know why, she was crying. "Babe, he won't— he won't *move* his—"

"I know," said Thea.

Thea was right behind him. On his left now. She'd turned. She hadn't stepped round him. She'd turned where she stood, with the lantern held up, and the amber light was pouring over Ice's back and shoulders and arm and his hand on the little lantern, and leaning into him so hard it was like being leaned on by somebody you love.

"He's not moving his hand," said Thea. Her voice was perfectly steady. Her face was wet. "Because it's his. He said."

"*Fifty-four*," said Ice.

Malcolm's gold, on his back. Then Rachael's hands, on his other arm; she'd come out of the ring too. Burden. Taking it. Xola felt it go out of him into Rachael, and Rachael's arms go white to the wrist, and Rachael not let go. Then Newish, behind, counting. *One. Two. Three. One*—and the beat under all of them, so that Xola's water and Malcolm's gold and Rachael's carrying all went in on the one, together, and it was nearly enough. It was nearly enough.

"*Fifty-one. It hurts.*"

The great head at the bottom of the slope had come up the slope.

She saw it over his shoulder. The tongue pulling, and the Moth following the tongue, the way you follow your own arm when you reach for something on a high shelf. The body dragging up the moss against the bramble and the roots. The head coming up. Low. Down at the level of the ring now. Right there. Twenty paces off. The blind white frosted eye and the other one, dull, red-black, enormous, so close she could see the light moving far down in it.

And under the head, where the body started, the place Yan had said.

Low. Open. Right there.

Rui Xiang was already on it.

She didn't know how. Nobody ever knew how, with Rui Xiang. He'd been at a lone white trunk at the edge of the open ground, he'd said *I'll be there first*, and now he was on the Moth. On the side of its neck, under the head, his knees in the fur, both knives in, holding. Holding the fur apart at the place where the body joined the head. Holding the place.

*Get there first. Hold the place. Whoever you're waiting for will always find you.*

And he was looking past her. Past Ice. Past Thea. At something behind them, up the slope.

Xola turned her head.

Xav.

He'd been standing at the back of the ring the whole time. Uphill. Near the roots. Not moving. Not once. Not when the tongue hit the shield. Not when the legs came. Not when Drew went in front. Not when the tongue hit Ice's hand. She'd seen him out of the corner of her eye the whole time, standing there with his sword down and his eyes half shut, and she'd hated him for it, a bit. Waiting. Always waiting.

*Wait longer than you can.*

"*Forty-eight*," said Ice. "*It hurts*—"

Xav moved.

***

Once.

She didn't see him cross the ring. Nobody did. He was at the back of it, uphill, and then he was in front of Ice, past Ice, at the head of the Moth, beside Rui Xiang, with his sword going in. One step. One cut. From where he'd been to where it was, the way he'd done it a hundred times, as if the Moth had put its neck there on purpose for him.

Into the place Rui Xiang was holding open. Under the head. Where the body started. Where Yan had said the small old light was, still, in the middle of all of them.

Not through it. Xola saw that. He didn't cut through the middle. He cut *beside* it. Down the seam where the head met the body, the way you'd open a parcel along the fold so you didn't tear what was inside.

*It must be enough.*

It was enough.

The Moth opened.

***

She didn't have words for it afterwards. Nobody did. Chris tried, in his notebook, for three pages, and crossed all three out.

It opened along the cut like a seam. Down from the head along the body. Not blood. There wasn't any blood. Light.

It came out of the cut the way water comes out of a dam when the wall goes. All at once. Thousands of lights. Small, pale, every one of them the size of a stray, pouring out of the long seam in the furred grey body, up, into the dark, like sparks going up off a bonfire when you throw a log on it. Like a shoal of fish turning all at once in deep water. Up. And up. And out. Over the ring, over the open ground, over Lanthorn, over the white trees on every side. Thousands. *Thousands*. More than she'd seen in her whole life put together.

And the wings went.

The great soft grey-brown wings, pinned to the moss by Gigi's thorns and Maigo's roots, went pale as the lights came out from under them. Then paler. Then grey. Then they weren't wings any more; they were dust. Fine and pale and dry, the dust off a moth's wing when you touch it, except a whole sky of it, coming down out of the air over all of them like snow. Soft. Silent. On her hair, on her hands on Ice's arm, on Squiddles, who sneezed.

The tongue on Ice's hand went to dust.

She felt it go. One moment the pull, and the next, nothing. Just dust, falling off the back of his hand, and his hand still there. Over the lantern. Grey to the wrist where it had drunk him, grey and cold, but there.

And under his hand, in the lantern, the light.

Still there.

"*Forty-six*," said Ice. Very quietly. "It hurts."

She put her water into his hand. And it went in. And stayed. Nothing taking it any more. It went in and stayed and the grey went back from his knuckles, slowly, to pink.

And the lights kept going up.

***

The Hollow lit.

She saw it happen. Everybody did. They stood in the falling dust and looked up and watched the dark go away.

The lights went up and up, thousands, and spread out as they went, the way sparks spread, and the higher they went the more of the dark they lit. And the dark had edges.

There was a roof.

Far up. So far up she couldn't think how far. Rock. Grey and pale and wet, hanging with long stone teeth like the inside of a cave, the way the inside of every cave she'd ever seen had them, except each one as big as a white tree. And in the middle of the roof, a long way off, a crack. And from the crack, a thread of water, fine and silver, falling. Falling for a very long time. All the way down to a black pool in a ring of trees.

*The drip*, she thought. *That's the drip.*

And walls. Far off on every side. Rock, going up into the roof. And between the walls, all across the floor of the Hollow, as far as she could see, the white trees. Forests of them. Slope after slope. Valleys. A river, she thought, a long way off, catching the light. Terraces on the slopes, row on row, the old fields, grey with moss. Stone villages. Dozens. Empty. Waiting.

And the lights, everywhere. Settling. Drifting down slowly out of the high dark over all of it, the way snow settles. Over the forests and the villages and the terraces and the river. Thousands of strays. A whole Hollow's worth.

Lit, like a sky.

Like Edda's gran had said.

Somewhere behind her on the roots, she heard Milli say, in a strange high voice, "*Nine kilometres*. It's nine kilometres. Across. To the far wall. Nine. I can *see* it, it's *nine*—" and Hana laughing, crying, *honey, honey*—

And from the doors of Lanthorn, two hundred people coming out onto the walkways and the roots and the open moss, looking up. With their glims coming out of their lanterns, all of them, on their own, two hundred lights lifting out of the iron cages and going up a little way, just a little, toward the thousands coming down. Humming. Every one. Louder than she'd ever heard them.

And Isel's voice, small and flat and very clear, somewhere at the pool, saying a number, because the drip had come.

***

There was one left.

In the middle of the grey shape on the moss. The shape that had been the Moth, that wasn't anything now, just a long low heap of pale dust with the bramble and the roots going through it, already falling in on itself. In the middle of it, under where the head had been, where Yan had said.

A light.

Very small. Smaller than a stray. The size of a pea. Dim. Old. Not pressing, not going up. Just sitting there, in the dust, in the middle of where it had been. Very still.

*Pulled in tight*, Yan had said. *Like hers was.*

Everybody saw it. Nobody moved.

Then Yan did.

He walked down the slope with Working On It over his shoulder and Suzu round his neck and Wendy behind him with her hand on his coat. Past the ring. Past Thea and Ice and Xola. Down to the edge of the grey dust. And stopped. And looked down at the small dim light in the middle of it for a long time, with his head on one side.

Xola had seen him look at lights before. On floor 1, at Mother Crane. *Her light's lifting.* She'd never seen him look at one like this.

"It's tired," said Yan.

Quietly. Not to anybody.

"It's been asking for longer than anything there's ever been," said Yan. "And it's still asking. Listen." He was quiet. "It doesn't want to go up. Like the others. It doesn't want to be anybody's now. It's too old. It's too—" He stopped. "It wants to stop."

He stood at the edge of the dust.

*Wherever you stand, there is an Edge*, Xola thought. She knew the window. Everybody did. *A light you have waited with may go over it.*

Yan sat down.

On the moss. At the edge of the dust. With the scythe across his knees. He didn't go any closer. He didn't do anything. He sat there beside the small old light, a pole's width away, the way he'd sat on the jetty on eight beside Lune, saying nothing kind.

Waiting with it.

And behind them, from Lanthorn, from the pool, very quietly, Newish started to count.

She hadn't known Newish knew where the drip was. Newish always knew. *One*, said Newish. Out loud. Not on the drip; between drips. The long count. Under her breath, then not under. And Isel's voice joining her, small, on the same number. And then, one at a time, from the walkways and the roots and the open moss, two hundred people of Lanthorn, with their glims over their heads, starting to hum.

One low note.

The note they'd sung Edda out on.

They were singing it out.

The first light. The stray nobody had come for. Two hundred people it had taken things from, a street, a house, Corwen's mother's street, Edda's gran's fields, and they stood on the roots in the new light and hummed it out on the count, the way they'd hum out one of their own.

*Plink.*

The drip. From the crack in the roof, a thread of silver, falling all that way, into the pool.

"*Fourteen*," said Newish and Isel.

And the small light lifted.

Out of the dust. Slowly. On the hum. Up past Yan's head, where he sat with the scythe across his knees, past his face, which was turned up to watch it. Getting no bigger and no smaller. A pea of old dim light going up into a sky full of strays.

It didn't go among them.

It went up past them. Higher. Toward the roof. Toward the crack the drip came out of. And then, somewhere up there, very high, very quietly, on the count, it went out.

Not out loud. Quiet. On the beat.

Over the Edge.

Yan sat on the moss and watched the place where it had been for a long time.

"It stopped asking," said Yan. "Just at the end." He was quiet. "Somebody came."

Wendy sat down beside him and put her head on his shoulder, and he let her.
