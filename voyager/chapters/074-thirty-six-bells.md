Merle had rules. She'd had a hundred and twenty-six days to work them out, and she told him all of them on the floor of the bakery, with her back against the warm oven and her eyes red.

"You wake up in your bed," she said. "Always. Doesn't matter where you were. I walked to the end of the valley once, the far end, past the mill, to see if it stopped at the edge. Sat down on the last stone of the road and waited. Twelfth stroke, and I was in my bed. Rolls in the oven. The bell going over the door."

"You can't leave."

"You can't leave. I tried the hill road. I tried the river, on a raft I made out of Dawley's fence. I tried staying awake." She wiped her face with the back of a floury wrist. "You can't stay awake. You hear the twelfth stroke, and you blink, and it's morning. You don't get the night. You never get the night."

"I got it," said Ice. "The first one. I was in a hayloft. I heard twelve and it went light."

"Well, you're lucky." She looked at him. "I tried breaking things. Week three. I put a brick through my own window to see if it'd be broken in the morning." She jerked her chin at the bow window, whole and clean and full of bread. "It wasn't."

"And people?"

"People don't remember. Nothing. Not one thing. I can tell Mrs Penn the worst secret in the world at nine o'clock, and at ten past nine tomorrow she'll come in and tell me about her knee." Her mouth twisted. "I told the reeve. I told the priest. I told my neighbours, every one, every morning for a month. They were kind about it. The first time. Every time was the first time." She looked down at her hands. "You get tired of being somebody's first time."

Ice didn't say anything.

"And then you walked in twice," said Merle.

She looked at him. Properly. The way she'd looked at him over the tray of rolls.

"You're from up the stair," she said. "Aren't you. The hill road. The old door in the hillside that nobody uses." She nodded at the white helm on the floor beside him. "I've heard stories. My gran used to tell them. People with lights, who came down the hill and went up the tower and never came back."

"Something like that."

"And you remember." She was watching him very carefully. "Like me. Things nobody else does."

He was quiet for a moment.

He thought about all the times he hadn't answered that. Mother Crane's kitchen, on the first morning, standing there with an onion in his hand and her face not knowing him. Mother Tallow in the tower door. Hessa, on her rock, with the gulls screaming. Nan Gully, asking *can't or won't*. A hundred and twenty-seven days of standing in front of people who didn't know what he knew, and not saying.

"Yes," he said.

Merle looked at him for a long time.

"Then you know," she said quietly.

"Then I know."

She nodded slowly. She didn't ask what he remembered. She didn't ask how, or why, or what it was like. He'd have bet she wanted to. But she'd spent a hundred and twenty-six days being the only one, and she seemed to know the one thing nobody had ever known to give her.

She didn't ask.

She got up off the floor instead, and dusted her hands on her apron, and looked at the tray of rolls going cold on the counter.

"Well," she said. "They're not going to bake themselves. They never do. Every morning I check." She looked at him. "You any good with dough?"

***

He was terrible with dough.

He was a Cook. He'd been a Cook since a kitchen on floor 1, since Mother Crane's floury hands over his on an onion. He could make Kitecliff eggs and dust-hound stew and a fish so salty Nan Gully ate two. But he'd never made bread. The first time, on seven, he'd bought it. Every morning. He'd never once thought about how it got made.

Merle thought it was the funniest thing she'd seen in four months.

She made him knead. She stood beside him at the big floury table at the back of the bakery, at dawn, with the oven roaring, and watched him punch the dough like it had insulted him. And she laughed until she had to hold on to the table.

"You're *fighting* it," she said. "Stop fighting it. It's not trying to kill you. Lean on it."

He stopped.

"*Lean*," said Merle, and put her floury hands over his on the dough. "Like that. Push it away, fold it back, turn it. Lean on it, don't punch it. It wants to go. You just have to let it." She pushed his hands down into it, slow and steady. "There."

He stood at the table with his hands in the dough and her hands over his, and didn't say anything for a while.

"What?" said Merle.

"Somebody told me that," he said. "Last week. About wind."

"Well, it's true about dough too." She took her hands away. "It's true about most things, if you ask me."

He leaned on it. It went.

They baked the rosemary loaf together every morning after that. At dawn, in the roaring heat of the bakery, with the bell going over the door at ten past nine for Mrs Penn and her knee. Every morning, the flour and the dough and the rosemary were back on the shelves where they'd been yesterday, and the loaves they'd baked were gone. Every morning, Merle remembered everything, and so did he. And every morning there was a little more of the loaf that was his and not hers.

On the fifth morning, she looked at the loaf he'd made on his own, golden and cracked across the top, and didn't laugh.

"That's good," she said. Surprised. "That's actually good."

"I had a good teacher."

"You had a hundred and twenty-nine days of the same teacher," said Merle. "Same bread. Same morning. Same oven." She looked at the loaf. "You know how many times I've baked that, on my own, with nobody to show?"

"A hundred and twenty-nine."

"A hundred and twenty-nine." She broke a piece off the end and ate it. "It's better with two."

***

He went up the clocktower on the hundred-and-twenty-eighth night.

It wasn't hard. The door at the bottom was never locked. The old caretaker went home at dusk and came back at dawn, every day, the same, and never remembered that a man in a white cloak had asked him the night before which stair went to the bells. Ice climbed the narrow stone steps in the dark, round and round, past the great clock room with its slow wooden ticking and its huge iron wheels turning. Up to the belfry at the top.

It was open on all four sides. Arches, with the night outside them, and the stars, and the dark valley with the river shining in it. And under the roof, hanging from great black beams in rows, the bells.

Thirty-six.

He walked among them. They were all different. Big and small. Bronze and iron and one that looked like silver. Old ones, green with age, and new ones that shone. In the middle, the great bell, the one that rang the hours, huge and dark and very old. And round it, in rings, the other thirty-five, hanging still. From every one, a rope. Thirty-six ropes, hanging down through holes in the floor of the belfry to the ringing chamber below, where a person could stand and pull.

He went down to the ringing chamber and looked at them. Thirty-six ropes, in a circle, each with a soft woollen grip at the bottom, worn smooth by hands.

On the great beam above the ropes, carved into the black wood, there were words. Old. Worn. He held up his hand and let the amber light come up in it, faint, and read them.

*When every bell is rung at once, the Hour may be struck.*

"*The Hour*," said Alea.

He didn't answer. He sat down on the floor of the ringing chamber in the middle of the circle of ropes and waited for midnight.

***

It came out of the clock.

He heard it before he saw it. The ticking changed. In the great clock room below him, the slow wooden *tock, tock, tock* of the mechanism went faster. Then louder. Then something enormous began to move in there, grinding, the sound of iron wheels turning that hadn't turned all day.

The great bell rang. *One.*

He was on his feet, on the stairs, going down, sword out, new shield up. *Two. Three.* Into the clock room. *Four.*

The clock face was on the wall in front of him, from the inside. A huge round window of thick old glass, with the moon shining through it and the shadows of the great iron hands thrown across the floor. And the face was opening.

*Five. Six.*

It came out of the middle of the clock, where the hands met. Something tall. Taller than him by half again. It stepped down out of the clock face the way a person steps down out of a doorway, and stood in the clock room. It was made of brass, and shadow, and something in between. Long and thin and jointed like a mechanism. No face. Where a face should be, there was a smooth round dial with no numbers on it. And its right arm was long. Much longer than its left. Straight and narrow and pointed at the end, like the hand of a clock.

*Seven. Eight.*

It walked past him.

It didn't look at him. It didn't do anything to him. It walked across the clock room, past him, as if he wasn't there, to the great iron wheels of the mechanism in the middle of the floor. And put its long pointed arm against the biggest of them.

*Nine. Ten.*

[ The Hour · Floor Guardian ]

Ice went for it.

He didn't think. He moved. Low and fast, the way he'd moved through the gaps in the beams at noon, sword up, under the long arm, at its side.

The sword went through it.

Like the Wanting. Like stabbing a doorway. No weight. No resistance. He went right through the place where it was and out the other side and nearly into the iron wheels. It didn't even turn its dial toward him.

[ It cannot be struck. ]

*Eleven.*

The Hour pushed.

The great wheel turned. *Backward.* With a groan that shook the whole tower, the wheel turned the wrong way. And the other wheels turned with it, all of them, backward, grinding. Through the round glass of the clock face Ice saw the great iron hands outside swing round. Backward, past eleven, past ten, past nine, round and round and round—

*Twelve.*

The world moved.

He was standing in the clock room, and the moonlight through the clock face went white. Then grey. Then gold. The stars he could see through the glass went out like someone had blown them out. And the sky was pale, and birds were singing outside, and it was morning.

The Hour was gone. The clock face was closed. The great iron wheels were turning forward again, slowly, *tock, tock, tock*, as if nothing had happened.

Ice stood in the clock room in the dawn with his sword in his hand.

"It doesn't fight," said Alea, very quietly. "It doesn't even *look* at you."

"It doesn't need to," he said. "It just turns the day back."

***

He tried for a week.

He rang one bell. He stood in the ringing chamber at midnight with his hands on one rope, and pulled. The bell rang high and clear in the belfry above him, a single note, while the great bell rang twelve. The Hour stepped out of the clock and walked past him and turned the wheel. Nothing.

He tied the ropes together. All thirty-six, in a great knot in the middle of the chamber, so that one pull would pull them all. He pulled. Nothing rang. Not one bell. He went up to the belfry and looked, and the bells hung there, still, every one, as if the ropes weren't attached at all. As if they knew it wasn't a hand.

He ran between them. He tried to ring as many as he could, one after another, in the time between the first stroke and the twelfth. Five. Six. Seven, once, gasping. They rang one after another, not at once, a ragged tumble of notes. The Hour walked past him and turned the wheel.

He asked Merle.

She came up the tower with him on the hundred-and-thirty-first night, in her apron, with flour still on her arms. She stood at a rope across the circle from him, and at midnight they pulled together. Two bells, at once, high and low, ringing out over the dark valley in a chord.

The Hour paused.

Just for a breath. On the eleventh stroke. It stepped out of the clock face and stopped, halfway down, and its blank round dial turned. Not toward them. Up. Toward the belfry. Toward two bells ringing at once.

Then it went on, and turned the wheel, and it was morning.

"*Two*," said Merle, in the ringing chamber in the dawn, with her hands still on the rope. Her eyes were very bright. "Two, and it *stopped*. Did you see? It *stopped*."

"I saw."

"Then more," said Merle. "More of us. As many as we can get."

So they tried. On the hundred-and-thirty-third day, Merle spent the whole morning, between batches, talking to people. The smith. The Dawley boy, who was twelve and would do anything for a penny. Mrs Penn, who said she'd come for the view. The old caretaker. Two of the men who drank at the inn on the corner and would do anything for a free pint, which Ice paid for. And at midnight there were seven of them in the ringing chamber, seven hands on seven ropes, and the Dawley boy giggling, and Mrs Penn talking about her knee.

Seven bells. At once. A great ragged glorious chord over the dark valley.

The Hour stepped out of the clock and stopped. Its dial turned up toward the belfry. It stood there, halfway out of the clock face, for two breaths. Three.

Then it went on.

And in the morning, the smith sold Ice his own shield, and Mrs Penn told Merle about her knee at ten past nine, and the Dawley boy wanted to know who he was.

***

"Thirty-six," said Alea.

It was the hundred-and-thirty-fourth night. He was sitting on the floor of the ringing chamber, alone, in the middle of the circle of ropes, with his back against the wall. The moon came in through a slit window high up. Merle had gone home. She always went home at eleven, now. She said she wanted to wake up in her own bed, at least, if she had to wake up in the same day.

"Thirty-six bells," said Alea. "Thirty-six ropes. *When every bell is rung at once.*" The fox was sitting on his knee, very small, looking at the ropes hanging round them in the moonlight. "Ice. It's not a town number. It's not a valley number. The first time, it was *one bell*."

"I know."

"How many players are there in the tower?"

He didn't answer.

"The lights said thirty-five," said Alea. "On the first morning. Every one of them. *There are thirty-five players.* They said it to Gabe's face." Her voice had gone very quiet. "They lied. For you. They've been lying for you since the white room. Every light in the tower." She looked up at the bells, hanging in the dark above them. "But the tower doesn't lie. The tower *counts*."

Thirty-six.

He sat in the middle of the ring of ropes.

"It started on the morning of the white room," said Alea. "This floor. This loop. The day you came back." She was very still on his knee. "Ice. It's learned what going back is. You went back. And it watched you do it, somehow, and it didn't understand. So it tried it. On a town. Every day since day one. To see what it does."

"And Merle remembers."

"And Merle remembers. Because it's floor 7. Because things are real enough here to notice their own lives." She was quiet. "Because it doesn't know how to do it properly yet. It's still learning."

He looked at the ropes.

"And it built this," he said. "The bells. The Hour. A boss you can't hit. On a floor that's turning back on itself every night." He swallowed. "A floor you can't clear unless thirty-six people are standing in one room with their hands on thirty-six ropes."

"A floor you can't clear alone," said Alea.

"A floor *I* can't clear alone."

The fox didn't say anything.

"It can't see me," said Ice slowly. "You said. On five. It can only see what changes round me. It patched the bird because I stood in a fire. It made the Wanting out of what I told it in the House of Ash." He looked at the ropes. "And now it's built a floor where the only way through is all thirty-six of us. Together. In one place. At one time." He was very quiet. "So it can see who's standing at the thirty-sixth rope."

Alea was silent for a long time.

"It might be a trap," she said.

"It might be a trap."

"If you bring them up here. If all thirty-six of you put your hands on those ropes at midnight—"

"Then it sees me," said Ice. "Properly. For the first time. Standing with them." He looked down at the scar on his palm in the moonlight. "And it sees them standing with me. And whatever it does after that, it does to all of us."

Neither of them said anything for a long time. The great clock ticked below them. The bells hung still.

"And if you don't," said Alea at last, very softly, "Merle bakes the rosemary loaf on her own for ever."

***

The whisper came on the hundred-and-thirty-third morning. He read it on the hundred-and-thirty-fourth night, sitting there, because he'd been carrying it round for a day without being able to open it.

It wasn't from Gabe.

There were three from Gabe, pinned at the top of his window, every morning. The first, from the second day: *dec. i know its you.* The second, from floor 5: *still here. me too.* And a third, from the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, the morning Hyvred had come back over the lip of the pit at Deepwell with Thea on a litter:

*hyv didnt get to tell you. so. still here.*

He'd read that one on the stair to floor 7, on the night of the hundred-and-twenty-fifth. He'd had to sit down on the steps for a while.

The new one wasn't from Gabe.

It had come on the hundred-and-thirty-third morning, while he was in the bakery with his hands in the dough. Alea had gone very still on his shoulder and said, *Ice. There's a whisper.* And then, *It's not Gabe.* And he'd known. He'd known before she said the name. And he hadn't been able to open it all day, all through the seven bells and the Dawley boy giggling. He'd carried it round, unopened, like a letter in a coat pocket.

He opened it now, in the ringing chamber, at midnight, alone.

[ Whisper · Althea: it scarred. xola's furious about it. i'm okay. ]

He read it. He read it again.

And then he saw there was another line under it. Sent a minute after the first, as if she'd sat there for a minute deciding whether to.

[ Whisper · Althea: i heard you. ]

He sat very still in the middle of the circle of ropes.

*I heard you.*

*Not this time.* He'd said it standing over her, with his back to her, in the lantern light, to the thing coming down. Quietly. Not to her. To it. He'd thought, in the moment, nobody would hear. He'd thought the shattering would drown it. He'd thought—

*I heard you.*

She'd heard it. And she'd sat in a pit in the desert with a scar on her shoulder and thought about it. And she'd written to him. For the first time in either life. Not *thank you*. Not *who are you*. Not *why*. Just: *it scarred. i'm okay. i heard you.*

As if she was telling him something. As if she was telling him she knew what it meant.

"Ice," said Alea.

"I know."

"You're shaking."

"I *know*."

The great bell rang. *One.*

He didn't get up. He sat in the middle of the ring of ropes with the whisper open in front of him. Two. Three. He heard the clock room below start to grind. Four. Five. The Hour stepping down out of the face. Six. Seven. Walking past the place where he wasn't. Eight. Nine. Its pointed arm against the wheel.

He looked up at the thirty-six ropes hanging round him in the moonlight.

Thirty-five of them empty.

*Ten. Eleven.*

*Twelve.*

The world moved. The moonlight went white, then grey, then gold. Birds outside. Morning.

The same morning. Again.

He sat in the ringing chamber in the dawn with the whisper still open in front of him, and the thirty-six ropes hanging still round him, and the bell going over a blue door on Lantern Street, far below, at ten past nine.
