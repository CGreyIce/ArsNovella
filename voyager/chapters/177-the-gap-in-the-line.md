Deston could feel all of them, all the time.

He'd stopped noticing it, mostly. The way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Thirty-five threads, going out from somewhere under his breastbone to everybody, thin and gold, slack when they were asleep and taut when they were frightened and humming, a little, when they were happy. He'd had them since floor 3. Link. He'd thought, at first, that it was a thing you did, like a spell. It wasn't. It was a thing you were. You were the place the threads went out from, and you felt everything that came back along them, and you didn't get to choose.

On floor 14 he'd stood in the middle of a line at the doors of the Long Barn with his eyes shut and given everything he had to one of them. Not chosen to. Just done it. The way he'd done it on the Long Night, in the first wall's tunnel, at three in the morning, without knowing he could. Everything. Out along one thread, two hundred paces into the dark, to a man on the snow at fifteen.

He'd felt fifteen.

Not the number. Deston didn't hear numbers through the threads. He felt what was at the other end. And at the other end of that one, at the bottom of the Barn Field, with the winter coming up out of a knot of straw into his hands, there'd been something so small and so bright and so nearly out that Deston had stopped breathing, in the line, at the door, and not started again until Alea, or whoever it was Dec talked to, said *sixteen*.

He hadn't told anyone that. He didn't need to. He'd just given everything he had, and it had gone along the thread, and the thing at the other end hadn't gone out.

He'd been tired for a week. He didn't mind.

He minded something else. He'd been minding it since the bell, and he didn't know what to do with it, so he kept it where he kept things. Left shirt pocket. Buttoned. With a card from a white room with his own name on the front and on the back, in the grey of a fingertip, eight words and a letter.

*be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.*

He'd known since floor 3. Not *what*. That it was something. He'd seen it in Dec's face on floor 3, the day the note was read out; a thing that had happened a long time ago, for the first time, not happening. He'd decided not to ask. He'd kept deciding, every floor. Since floor 3.

And now there was a thread in him he didn't understand.

It had been there since the Barn Field. Faint. Not to one of the thirty-five. From Dec, but not *to* Deston. Through him. As if, when Deston had given everything along that thread on the Barn Field, something had come back the other way and not gone home. Something old.

He couldn't feel what it was. He could only feel that it was heavy, and that Dec had been carrying it, and that a little of it, now, a very little, was in Deston too.

*What you cannot carry*, Dec's window had said, on the hill on floor 13. *They will.*

Deston kept it in the pocket with the card. And didn't ask.

***

The stair from 11 came out on the far side of the Gate.

Four hundred steps down, in the dark, with Thea singing and Newish counting. And then the door, in a grey cliff, at the end of a high valley, and Tiger opening it, and the cold.

Real cold. Winter.

Deston came out into it behind Calvin and stood still.

He remembered it green. The far side of the Gate. They'd come through the doors on the night of the two hundred and eighty-fourth day, hand in hand, counting, into a high valley in spring, above a sea of cloud, with flowers, white and yellow and deep blue, and a tarn with reeds and a small brown singing bird. Most of them had slept in the grass all the next day. Higu had said *today, nobody does anything*.

It was white now.

All of it. The valley, the slopes, the boulders. The tarn was frozen, flat and grey-white, with the reeds standing up out of the ice, dead and brown. No birds. No flowers. The sea of cloud was still there, below the valley's lip, the peaks standing up out of it like islands, but the cloud was grey now, not white, and the sky over it was the colour of pewter. And at the far end of the valley, the other way from the door, the wall.

Black ice. Higher than any wall. Clear, and deep, and old. On this side, no doors. No line. No rings. Just ice, from the valley floor to the sky.

Higu took out the rock.

"Going down," said Higu. "Ten. What do you remember?"

Dec didn't say anything for a moment. Deston felt him look for something, along the thread. Look, and not find it.

"Nothing," said Dec. "On this side. I came through it once. In spring. Going up." He looked at the wall. "I never came back through."

Higu drew the line in the left column. She didn't say anything. She'd done it on 12 and on 11 and on 13 and she'd stopped saying anything about it. She just drew the line.

Then she looked at the wall.

"How do we get through?" said Higu.

Nobody knew.

***

They went and looked first, the way they always did.

It was a long walk up the valley in the snow. Past the tarn. Past the rock at the water's edge.

Deston felt Dec slow down at the rock.

He didn't need the thread for that. Everybody saw it. The flat grey boulder at the edge of the ice where, on the night of the two hundred and eighty-fifth day, under an aurora none of them had ever seen in either life, Dec had asked Thea to walk with him. And she'd said *yeah, okay*, and Xola had said *babe*, and Newish had started the hold-folk song so loudly and badly that nothing on the far shore could be heard, and they'd all sung it all night.

Nobody knew what had been said on the rock. Nobody had asked. They knew that the next morning Gabe had looked up from the fire and seen Thea's hand in Dec's, and Thea had said *I'm going to kill you* to Gabe, about tents.

Thea stopped at the rock.

She was holding his hand. She'd been holding it since the Salt. She stopped and looked at the boulder, at the frozen tarn round it, at the dead reeds. And then she let go of his hand, and went and sat down on it.

Not on the edge, where her feet had been in the water. In the middle. On the snow. With the lantern in her lap.

Dec stood on the ice beside it.

The rest of them walked on. Without anybody saying to. Higu first, then the others, up the valley toward the wall, leaving the two of them at the rock. Deston walked on too. He didn't look back.

He didn't need to look back. He could feel her.

Thea's thread had been the hardest to be near, since the Barn Field. It was always a strange one. Hyvred said Thea looked like a page, lines of light all over her, moving. To Deston she'd always felt like a voice, a very clear one, saying something he couldn't quite hear. Since the doorpost, since the lantern going down, it had felt like a wire. Pulled tight. So tight it sang if you touched it. He'd felt it every day for forty-six days. He'd felt it on the stair singing and in Tell when she nearly smiled and on the Salt when she walked the road with Gigi and at Hythe on the quay, when Marrit said *Ice*, and it had gone, for one second, slack, and then tight again.

He felt it now. From the rock, behind him, a long way back down the valley.

He didn't know what they were saying. He didn't want to know. That was theirs. He just felt the wire.

And it went a turn.

Not slack. Not all the way. One turn. The way a wire on a fence goes when somebody takes the strainer and backs it off one notch. Still taut. Still singing. But less. A turn less.

Deston let his breath out.

"What," said Calvin, beside him.

"Nothing," said Deston.

"You did a face."

"I didn't."

"You did your *thread* face," said Calvin. "The one where you go cross-eyed. Is it Dec? Is he all right? Is it *Thea*? Did she—"

"Calvin," said Rachael.

Calvin shut up.

Deston looked at Rachael. She looked back at him. She didn't have threads. She didn't need them. She'd seen his face.

"A turn," said Deston. Quietly. Just to her.

Rachael nodded. As if she'd known. "Good," she said.

***

The wall was black ice from the valley floor to the sky, and there was no door in it.

They stood in front of it. All of them. Thea and Dec had come up by then, walking up the valley in the snow, holding hands again. Thea's face was the same as it had been. Deston didn't look at it. He could feel the turn.

They stood at the foot of the wall and looked up.

You could see into it. That was the strange thing. It was clear, the way very old ice is clear, and deep, and you could see down into it a long way. Air bubbles, frozen, in long chains going down into the dark. Old cracks, healed. And on the other side, through the whole thickness of it, very faint, a shape. Grey. Huge. Standing. With its head bowed.

"That's it," said Dec. Very quietly. "That's the Gate. The other side of it."

"How did we come through?" said Hana.

"It opened," said Dec. "It counted us. And then it opened."

Higu looked at the wall for a long time.

Then she put the rock down in the snow.

"Count," said Higu.

Newish looked at her. Then at the wall.

"Count," said Newish.

And they counted.

Not numbers. Names. The way they did at the top of every stair. The way they'd done it on the fifth wall of the Bastion, on the evening before the Gate, going up, with Ansa watching with her fist on her chest. Newish first. Then round. One at a time. *Higu.* *Drew.* *Xola.* *Chi.* Each one a little louder than the one before, because the snow ate sound and the wall was very big. *Gabe.*

"*Deston*," said Deston.

His voice went into the ice. He felt it go. Not through the threads. Through the ice. Down into it, into the chains of frozen bubbles, into the old healed cracks, a long way down.

*Rachael.* *Dennis.* *Calvin*, very loud. *Azac.* All of them. All the way round. Thirty-five. And then the last one.

"*Ice*," said Dec. And then, without Gabe having to nudge him: "*Declan*."

"*Thirty-six*," said Newish.

The wall cracked.

***

It wasn't loud. Not like the floor of the Long Barn. A long, clean sound, high, like a glass rung with a wet finger, from somewhere deep in the ice in front of them. And a line came up.

Up through the ice. From the valley floor. Straight, and thin, and white, going up and up through the black like a thread being pulled through cloth, until it was higher than any of them could see.

Then the line opened.

Not like doors. Like ice on a river in spring, when it goes. A hand's width. Then more. The two sides of the line drawing back from each other, slowly, the black ice groaning, and light coming through the gap. Grey. Winter light. The light of the high side.

And cold. Colder than the valley. The cold that came down the pass.

The gap opened to the width of a cart. And stopped.

On the other side, in the snow, was the Gatekeeper.

Deston had stood in a ring under it on the night of the two hundred and eighty-fourth day and felt its gauntlets come down at the middle. A whole suit of plain grey armour the size of the doors, with its hands on the two great iron rings and its helm bowed and the snow of a whole winter on its shoulders. And under the helm, nothing.

It wasn't holding the rings.

It was kneeling. In the snow, just through the gap, a little to one side, out of the way. Its great hands in its lap, empty, palm up. Its helm bowed so low it nearly touched its knees. Snow on its shoulders, and on its head, and in its hands, a whole season of it, untouched. As if it had knelt down there on the night they went through, when the doors opened all the way and the gold came out, and had never got up.

Like somebody who'd been told they could sit down. And had.

Nobody said anything.

Deston felt Dec, at the front, beside Thea. Felt him look at it. Felt something go through him that wasn't fear and wasn't anything Deston had a word for.

Then Dec walked through the gap.

Not first. Higu went first. Then Tiger. Then the parties, one at a time, in order. Dec in the middle, with Thea, the way they'd gone through the first time, holding hands. And Gabe last.

As he went past the Gatekeeper, Dec stopped.

He looked down at it. At the empty hands in its lap, full of snow. And he did something Deston didn't expect. He let go of Thea's hand, and stepped off the path, two steps, to the kneeling thing, and put his hand on it. On the back of its great grey gauntlet, where it lay in its lap. Flat. The way he'd put his hand on Rafe Collier's palm in Tell. The way he'd put it on a straw man's heart.

He held it there a moment.

Then he came back and took Thea's hand, and they went on.

Deston didn't ask. He felt the thread in his pocket, the old heavy one that had come back to him on the Barn Field, move a little. As if something in it had recognised something.

He kept it where it was.

***

The place where the Steward had stood was bare.

Deston saw it as they went down from the Gate into the throat of the high side. He'd been the last but one through the doors going up, on the night of the Gate, and he'd looked back, and seen it. Standing in the snow outside the doors, with its book, not following. Its face, that wasn't a face, turned toward them. And through the closing doors, at Dec's shoulder only, so that only Dec heard: *Where did you come from?*

It wasn't there now.

But where it had been, at the top of the throat, on the path, there was a place in the snow that wasn't snow. Two marks. The size and shape of two feet, standing together. Bare rock. Dry. With the snow lying a hand deep all round them, all the way to the edges, and stopping, as if the snow didn't want to be there.

Hyvred looked at it with her glasses off for a long time.

"There's nothing there," said Hyvred. "Not grey. Not anything. It's not the tower's kind." She frowned. "It's just a place that doesn't want to be snowed on."

Nobody stepped on it.

***

The high side was full of armour.

Deston had expected that. He'd been on the pass the night they went up, at the dark of the moon, with three hundred and twelve Rimed standing in ranks along both sides of the throat, motionless, hollow helms turned to the path, watching. And when the doors opened all the way and they went through, all three hundred and twelve had collapsed. Down the whole pass. Into snow, and empty armour.

It was still there. Under a winter's snow. Heaps of it, all the way down both sides of the throat. Grey breastplates. Helms. Gauntlets. Lying where they'd fallen, half buried, with the snow drifted over them like over stones in a field.

But not all of it.

Halfway down the throat, Deston saw the sledges.

Long low hold-folk sledges, the kind they used on the walls for hauling stone. Three of them, drawn up on the path. And beside them, hold-folk. Tall, grey-eyed, in grey wool and leather, with their Breath-made shields on their backs. Six. Digging. Pulling armour out of the snow, a breastplate, a gauntlet, a helm, and stacking it on the sledges, carefully, the way you'd stack firewood.

They stopped when they saw the climbers. And stared.

One of them came up the path. A woman. Tall, with a grey braid, and a white scar through her top lip.

Brenna.

She'd kept the first wall. She'd stood at the first wall's tunnel on the morning they came up the pass from floor 9, two hundred and seventy-one days ago, and said *we have it written that climbers come*. She stood in the snow on the high side now, on the path, two hundred paces below the Gate, in a place where no hold-folk had stood in nine hundred years, and looked at them coming down.

"You came back," said Brenna.

"Everybody says that," said Calvin.

"Everybody's never had to before." Brenna looked past them, up the throat, at the open gap in the ice. Her face didn't change. Hold-folk faces didn't, much. "It opened for you."

"We counted," said Higu.

Brenna nodded slowly. As if that made sense. As if it were written somewhere. "It's good iron," she said, and jerked her head at the sledges. "The Winter's. Geir says it's the best iron he's ever had in the Breath. Nine hundred years it came down the pass and took one of us every first night. Now we come up the pass and take it." She looked at the breastplate in her hands. "He's making shields out of it. Every shield in the Bastion this year is the Winter's."

"That's—" said Dennis.

"Fair," said Brenna. "That's the word you want. It's fair."

***

The Bastion was quiet.

Deston had never heard it quiet. The first time, it had been a city at war. Five walls stepping down a narrowing pass, a city crammed between them, and every night the Rimed coming down from the high side in the dark, cracking like ice on a pond, and every night the walls held, or didn't. Horns. Shields. The deep bell on the fifth wall. The house for the wounded full, and Ingrith with her gentle hands going down the line, and Rachael beside her, a finger at a time.

It was winter again. The first snow of the second winter had come down the pass a month ago, Brenna said. And nothing in it.

They didn't know what to do.

That was what Deston felt, coming down through the walls. Not with the threads; he didn't have threads to the hold-folk. Just with his eyes. They stood on the walls. All of them, at their places, with their shields, in the snow. The way they'd stood on every snowy night for nine hundred years. Because you did. Because their mothers had, and their mothers' mothers. And nothing came. Night after night. And they stood there anyway, and looked up the pass at the dark, and nothing came down it.

"The first night," said Brenna, walking beside Deston down through the fifth wall's gate. "The first snow. We stood on the fifth wall all night. Every one of us. Shields up. Waiting for it." She looked up the pass. "And nothing came. Not one. Not all night. At dawn we were still standing there." She was quiet for a moment. "We didn't know what to do. So we sang."

"What did you sing?"

"The one we sing on the longest night. When the light comes up." She almost smiled. It looked strange on her. "We'd never sung it on the first night. We didn't know if you were allowed."

And on the fourth wall, as they came down through it, there were children.

Deston stopped.

Children. On the wall. In winter. Six or seven of them, small, in grey wool, running along the top of the fourth wall in the snow, throwing it at each other, shrieking. On the wall where every snowy night for nine hundred years their mothers had stood with shields while the dead came over the parapet with ice in their fingers.

A hold-folk mother stood at the top of the stair, watching them, with her arms folded. She looked at Deston, and at the climbers coming down, and then back at the children.

"Never been up here in winter," she said. "Not one of them. Not ever." She watched a small boy fall over in the snow and get up laughing. "They don't know what it's for."

She said it as if it were the best thing in the world.

***

Ansa was at the fifth wall's small tunnel.

Deston knew she would be. Rui Xiang had known it on the stair. He'd gone straight there, through the fifth wall's gate, without asking anybody, and when Deston came down past the tunnel's mouth, Ansa was standing inside it, by the wall, with her hand on a carved name, and Rui Xiang was standing beside her.

*TAMM, SON OF ANSA. FIFTH WALL. FIRST NIGHT.*

And a line under it. To show it was finished.

Rui Xiang had his knife in his hand. Not to use. Just holding it. Very still. The way he'd held it in the tunnel on the morning after, two hundred and sixty-four days ago, standing by Ansa while Brenna read this winter's names. Holding it still because it was the only way he knew to hold anything still.

Ansa didn't look round.

"No new ones," said Ansa. To the wall. "This winter. Not one." She moved her hand on the stone, over her son's name. "He's the last."

Rui Xiang didn't say anything.

"The last name on the fifth wall," said Ansa. "In nine hundred years. My boy." She was quiet. "I didn't know if that would be better or worse."

"Which is it?" said Rui Xiang. Very quietly.

Ansa thought about it. For a long time. With her hand on the stone.

"I don't know yet," she said. "Ask me in the spring."

***

The Long Wall was at the bottom.

The tunnel through the first wall, the oldest, from the first stone. Carved floor to ceiling, both sides, all the way through, with the names of everyone who had fallen holding the pass. Every one with a short line under it, to show it was finished. Read aloud, every year, on the longest night, from a stool in the tunnel, by lantern-light, in an order written in a book. It took all night.

Deston had read a page of it. On the Long Night. At midnight, from the stool, with his voice going at *Fifth wall. Longest night*, and Ansa down from the wall for an hour with her hand on his shoulder until he finished.

He walked into the tunnel now and the names went past him on both sides in the grey light from the far end. He didn't read them. You didn't, walking. You read them on the longest night.

Dec read them.

Deston felt it. He'd felt it the first time, going up, on the first morning. Dec walking into this tunnel and starting to read the names, fast, his eyes going over them faster than anybody could read, as if he were looking for one. And Deston had heard him say it, to Rui Xiang, outside, after: *Making sure. That none of them are ours.*

He was doing it again.

Deston felt the thread go tight. Felt Dec's eyes going over the stone, both sides, fast, the way you'd scan a crowd for a face. Not reading. Looking. Making sure. He'd made sure on the way up. Nothing had changed. There'd been no winter since. Nobody had fallen. There was no reason—

The thread stopped.

Not slack. *Stopped*. The way a heart stops for a beat when you miss a step in the dark. Deston felt it so hard he put his hand on the wall.

Dec had stopped walking.

He was standing in the middle of the tunnel, near the far end, where the newest names were. Where Brenna had carved the six from the night of the cliffs, and the ones from this winter going back. On the left-hand side, at the height of his eyes. Looking at the stone.

Deston came up behind him. And saw it.

It wasn't with the others. It was by itself. Low on the wall, at the very end of the tunnel, by the light, in a space where there'd been no carving the first time. Fresh. The cuts in the stone still pale. Five letters, deep and square, in the hold-folk hand.

*RACHAEL.*

And under it, smaller:

*CLIMBER. SHE TOOK THE COLD.*

And under that, the line.

***

Deston felt what went through Dec.

He'd felt a lot of things through that thread. On floor 3, the day the note was read. In the bowl on floor 6. On the Long Night. On the Barn Field at fifteen. He had never felt this.

It went through Dec the way the crack had gone through the floor of the Long Barn. All at once, from one end to the other. Old. So old. Older than the white room. A thing Dec had been carrying since before any of them woke up, and it was the heaviest thing Deston had ever felt at the end of a thread, and it was *this*. This name. On a wall. Of the dead. With a line under it.

And it wasn't a grave.

Deston felt that too. Right behind the first thing. Like a second wave coming in behind the first. Dec looking at a name on a wall of the dead and knowing, with the whole of himself, that the person it belonged to was standing in this tunnel. Alive. Three paces behind him. In a pair of tanner's gloves.

Deston didn't know what that meant. He didn't let himself know. He'd decided on floor 3. He kept deciding.

He kept his hand on the wall and didn't say anything.

Rachael came up.

She'd been behind Dennis. She came up the tunnel and stopped beside Dec and looked at the wall where he was looking.

She didn't say anything for a long moment.

"I said don't you dare," said Rachael.

Her voice was perfectly flat.

"You did," said Brenna. She'd come in behind them. She stood at the end of the tunnel by the light with her arms folded. "We dared."

Rachael looked at the name.

"I'm not finished," said Rachael.

"No," said Brenna. "Look at the line."

Rachael looked. They all looked.

The line under the name. The line that was under every name on the Long Wall, all the way back to the first stone, to show it was finished. Deston had seen ten thousand of them. Short, straight, one cut, from one side to the other.

This one had a gap in it.

In the middle. A finger's width. The line cut from the left, straight, and stopped. And started again a finger's width on, and went to the right, straight. Two halves of a line, with nothing in the middle.

"We've never carved anybody who didn't fall," said Brenna. "In nine hundred years. We didn't know what line." She was looking at it too. "We argued about it for a month. After you went. Every night, in the long halls. Hesk said no line, because she isn't finished. Geir said a whole line, because she did the thing, and the thing's finished. Old Orm said we shouldn't carve her at all, it'd bring bad luck." She shrugged. "And then Ingrith said leave a gap in it."

"Why?" said Rachael.

"So it isn't finished," said Brenna. "So it's a line, so everybody knows she's one of the ones that held. And so it isn't, so everybody knows she's still going." She looked at Rachael. "Ingrith said, *she does it a finger at a time. Leave a finger*."

Rachael didn't say anything.

She took her glove off. Rafe's glove. The right one. And put her bare fingers on the name. On the cuts in the stone. Cold. Deston felt the cold of it come back along the thread to him, faintly, through Dennis, who had his hand on her back.

She moved her fingers down. To the line. To the gap.

She put her fingertip in it.

It fitted. Exactly. A finger's width.

Rachael stood there with her fingertip in the gap in the line under her own name, in the tunnel of the dead at the bottom of the Bastion, with the light coming in from the far end. Her face didn't do anything. It never did. Deston had known her for longer than he'd known most people and he had never once seen her face do anything she didn't let it.

She let it now. A little. At the mouth.

"It's spelled right," said Rachael.

Brenna's scar moved. It might have been a smile. "We asked your big one. Before you went up. In case."

"Gabe?"

"The one who stands on the right."

Behind them, at the back of the tunnel, Gabe made a sound that might have been a laugh and might not.

***

Dec hadn't moved.

He was still standing in front of the name. Looking at it. Deston could feel the two waves still going through him, the old one and the new one, one on top of the other, the way the Barn Field's frost and gold had gone through him together. He was standing very still, the way you stand when you're holding something very full across a room.

Rachael looked at him.

She put her glove back on. Slowly. Finger by finger. Then she looked at him again, standing beside her in front of her own name on a wall of the dead, very still, and something went across her face that Deston didn't understand and thought he probably wasn't meant to.

"Dec," said Rachael.

He didn't look at her.

"Don't," said Rachael.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"I know. Your face was."

He shut his eyes.

Deston felt it. The thread. Felt Dec, standing there with his eyes shut, holding whatever it was, the old thing and the name and the gap, holding it so hard that it hummed. And then felt him put his hand out.

Not to her. To the wall. Flat. On the stone. Beside her name. Not on it. Beside it. The way you'd put your hand on a door you'd walked past a long time ago, in another place, that had been shut.

He held it there.

And Rachael, who was not a hugger, who had told him so on a roof on floor 13 and on the trough in Tell and probably a hundred times on Earth, leaned.

Just her shoulder. Against his arm. The way she'd done on the roof. Briefly. A second. Less.

"Don't get used to it," said Rachael.

"I won't," said Dec.

She took her shoulder away. She looked at the name once more, and at the gap, and then turned round and walked back down the tunnel to Dennis, and put her gloved hand on his arm, and didn't say anything else.

Dec took his hand off the wall.

The thread went slack. Not all the way. But slack enough that Deston could breathe.

***

They went out through the bottom of the first wall at dusk.

Brenna saw them to it. The door in the rock at the foot of the pass, below the first wall, where they'd come up out of floor 9 two hundred and seventy-one days ago into real cold and a real sky, and Dec had looked up at the sky doing a sum and not liking the answer. Three hundred and eighty-four steps going down.

The snow was coming down the pass behind them. Big, soft flakes, out of a grey sky, onto the walls and the roofs and the children on the fourth wall who'd been called in and hadn't gone. Coming down from the high side, through the open Gate, the way it had come every winter for nine hundred years.

And nothing in it.

Brenna stood at the door with her fist on her chest.

"We'll read it," said Brenna. "On the longest night. The whole wall. Same as always." She looked at Rachael. "We'll read yours. In the right place. At the end." She paused. "We'll leave a gap after it."

"A gap?" said Calvin.

"When we read it out," said Brenna. "After her name. A breath. A finger's width." She looked at Rachael. "So it isn't finished."

Rachael didn't say anything. She nodded once.

Newish counted them in. Names. Thirty-six. And they went down.

Deston was second from last. Gabe was last; he always was, going down. And Deston stood at the top of the stair for a moment, in the doorway, with the snow coming down behind him and the stair going down in front, and felt all thirty-five threads go out from him into the dark below. Thin, gold. Slack, mostly. Tired. Humming, a little.

And the other one. The old heavy one, in his pocket, with the card.

He put his hand on his shirt pocket. On the button.

*be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.*

He'd thought, on floor 3, that it was a warning. He'd thought it on every floor since. A warning from somebody who knew what was coming.

Standing in the doorway at the bottom of the Bastion, with Rachael's name on the wall behind him and a gap in the line under it, he thought for the first time that it might have been something else.

That somebody had read a wall once. A long time ago. Somewhere. And found names on it that were theirs.

And had written eight words and a letter on the back of a card, in the first minute, with a fingertip, so that this time—

He stopped the thought.

He'd decided on floor 3. He kept deciding.

He buttoned the pocket, and went down.
