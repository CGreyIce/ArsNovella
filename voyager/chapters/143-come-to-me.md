"Fifteen days," said Calvin.

Nobody answered him. It was the morning of the three hundred and seventy-second day, and they were in the copy-room yard, and everybody was busy giving things to people, and Calvin had been saying it, on and off, for about three days, and they'd stopped answering him round about the second.

He said it again anyway.

"*Fifteen days*. He said come to me. He *said*. He sat in his chair at the top of the hill and he sent his grandson all the way down to the Tellhouse to say, if climbers want Aldo Pellow, they know where he lives. And nobody went. Fifteen days." He spread his hands. "He's *eighty-something*. What if he dies? What if he dies in his chair waiting for us, and it's because we were busy fixing *hinges*?"

"Calvin," said Rachael. She was on her stool by the pump with a boy's hand in hers. The boy had a fishhook in his thumb.

"I'm just *saying*—"

"Then go," said Deston.

Calvin stopped.

Deston was on the step of the stable door, with a bowl of the anonymous ham, watching the yard the way he always watched the yard, with his hand flat on his chest. He looked at Calvin over the bowl.

"You keep saying somebody should," said Deston. "You're somebody. Go."

Calvin looked at him. Then at Rachael.

"Can I?"

Rachael didn't look up from the fishhook. "Don't talk too much."

"I'll talk exactly enough."

"You've never talked exactly enough in your life, Calvin." She did something quick with her fingers, and the boy yelped, and the hook was out, and her fingertip went white. "Go on. Before I change my mind."

***

He went up the Wellgate by himself, which he didn't like.

He'd never liked being by himself. He'd told Ina that on the Salt, the sixth night, when the silence had swallowed every voice at arm's length and he'd stood in the middle of thirty-five people and not been able to hear a single one. *I've been shouting since I was born. It's how I know where everybody is.* He'd been shaking. And Ina had taken his hand and written every name in it with one fingertip, the rakers' way, looking up at each person as he wrote them so Calvin would look too, and then folded his fingers shut over them, and said, *They're there. I've just told you who.*

Calvin had kept his fist shut ever since. Not all the time. He opened it to hold his daggers, and to eat, and when Rachael made him. But mostly, when he wasn't using it, it was shut. Nearly seven weeks. It had got to be a habit. He liked knowing they were in there.

He held it shut now, against his chest, going up the Wellgate.

Past the gate in the first wall, where the watch captain Wat with the big moustache nodded at him and said "Calvin," because everybody in the Low Town said *Calvin* now. Past the Tellhouse, with its wide steps and its door that didn't squeak. Past the Countinghouse opposite, with no windows, and a clerk on the steps, which he didn't look at. Past the second wall, and the third, where the houses got taller and narrower and cleaner, and the people in the street looked at the grey thread on his wrist and then away, and nobody up here said *Calvin*.

The Wellgate got steeper at the top. Steps, mostly, worn into dips. And at the very top, under the great pale wall of the Assize, where the road turned the last corner before the Assize gate, there was a row of very old, very narrow houses with their backs to the wall. And outside the last one, which had a green door with the paint coming off in curls, there was a rush chair. And in the rush chair, with a rug over his knees and the sun on his face, there was an old man.

He was very small. Very old. He had hands like knotted string, lying on the rug, and there was ink in the knots, dark grey, the way there was ink in Hawise's, except his had been there so long it had gone into the skin for good. He had a thin white face with a great beak of a nose and very bright eyes, and the eyes watched Calvin come up the last flight of steps all the way, without blinking, like a bird watching a worm come up out of a lawn.

Calvin stopped in front of him, a bit out of breath.

"Hi," said Calvin. "Are you Aldo?"

The old man looked at him for a long time.

"Fifteen days," said Aldo Pellow.

***

His grandson brought tea. The grandson was about twenty and called Davy, and he looked at Calvin the way you'd look at a dog that had walked into your kitchen and sat down, and went back in and came out with a second stool, and went back in again and didn't come out.

"Sit," said Aldo.

Calvin sat.

"You're the loud one," said Aldo. "From the Low Town."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I can hear you from here." Aldo picked up his tea with both knotted hands. "Not today. Most days. Down there." He nodded down the hill, over the roofs, ring below ring, all the way down to the bottom wall and the Lantern Market, small and far away in the morning. "Sound comes up a hill. You learn things, in a chair, if you sit in it long enough. I've sat in this one twenty years." He drank. "I know about your buckets. And your hinges. And the woman who heals by the pump for nothing. And the ham. I know who left the ham."

"Who?"

"I'm not telling you that." Aldo put his tea down. "Why did you come?"

Calvin opened his mouth. And shut it. He'd had a lot of things he was going to say, coming up the Wellgate. About fifteen days. About being eighty-something. They'd all gone.

"Because you said," he said. "You said come to me. And nobody came." He looked at the old man. "That's the saddest thing I ever heard."

Aldo looked at him for a long time with those bright bird's eyes. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"Sixty years," he said, "I sat at the door of the Tellhouse and wrote the door-roll. Every day. Who came in, what they asked, what they were told. And every day I wrote it, I thought: one day, in my life, the door will open, and it'll be climbers. Up the well. Like the Rolls say. Like the song." His knotted hands moved a little on the rug. "I used to tell my wife. If climbers come up that well in my life, Mab, I'm running all the way down that hill to meet them. I don't care how old I am. I'll run."

"But you didn't," said Calvin.

"No." Aldo looked down the hill. "The bell went. In the Lantern Market. The climbers' bell, three hundred years since anybody rang it. And I got up out of this chair. I got as far as that step." He nodded at the doorstep. "And I stood on it. And I found I didn't want to run down to you. I wanted to see if you'd come up to me."

"Why?"

"Because everything that ever came up that well went straight up this hill." Aldo's voice was quite dry. "It's in the Rolls. I've read every line there is about the thirty, and I've read it a hundred times. Thirty climbers came up the well, and went to the Tellhouse to be told, and went to the Assize to ask the way, and went up the stair. And not one of them knocked on a door that wasn't the Tellhouse's or the Assize's. Not one of them, in a whole summer, sat down on anybody's step." He looked at Calvin. "I wanted to know if you were the same."

Calvin didn't say anything for a moment.

"We're not," he said.

"No," said Aldo. "I've been watching." He picked up his tea again. "It took you fifteen days. But you're not."

***

They talked for most of the morning. Calvin talked. Aldo listened, mostly, with his bright eyes, and asked short questions in his dry voice, and every so often said "hm" in a way that made Calvin feel he'd said something either very clever or very stupid and he couldn't tell which.

He told Aldo about the floors. Not all of them. The good bits. The sheep, and the snow, and the bells, and the river, and the ship. He told him about the Salt, and Ina writing names in his hand. He showed Aldo his fist.

"What's in it?" said Aldo.

"Everybody."

Aldo looked at the fist for a while. He didn't ask to see. Calvin liked him for that.

"Your corded one," said Aldo eventually.

"Dec."

"Hawise says he asked for me. In the market. Before she'd so much as opened her book. *Where's Aldo*." Aldo turned his head and looked at Calvin. "First words out of a climber's mouth in three hundred years. My name."

"Yeah," said Calvin. "He does that."

"Does what?"

"Knows things." Calvin shrugged. "People's names. Where stuff is. Which way to go. He always has." He thought about it. "He knew about my arm."

"What about your arm?"

"That I've got two."

Aldo looked at him for a long moment.

"Most people have two," said Aldo.

"Yeah! That's what *I* said!" Calvin waved the arm. "On floor 8. He looked at me like he'd never seen arms before and said *you've got two good arms, Calvin*, and I said *thank you?* and he laughed so hard he nearly fell in the lake." He grinned at the memory. "He's like that. He knows stuff and he doesn't say how. You get used to it."

"Do you," said Aldo. Not quite a question.

"Yeah," said Calvin simply. "He's Dec."

Aldo was quiet for a while. His knotted fingers moved on the rug.

"Tell him to come," he said. "Himself. Before the Reckoning." He looked down the hill. "I'd like to have a look at him."

***

"What's the Reckoning?" said Calvin. "Like, properly. Everybody keeps saying it."

"The Reckoning is the day Tell adds itself up." Aldo pointed with his chin. Up, over his own roof, at the great pale wall behind the row of houses, and the square bell tower at the end of it with the flag. "High summer. Thirty-odd days from now. Every account in Tell gets closed. Every debt gets called. And the Assize sits, in the great hall, and the Magistrate hears every dispute that two hands couldn't settle all year." He put his tea down. "And your Dec's going to be one of them."

"Who's the Magistrate?"

Aldo didn't answer straight away.

"Nobody knows," he said. "Not *who*. It sits once a year. At the Reckoning. Behind a screen at the end of the great hall, carved wood, very old, so you can't see it. You hear it. Its voice." He was quiet. "I've heard it sixty-one times. Like a page turning in an empty room. My grandmother said it was the oldest thing on the hill. Older than the First Tell. She said Tell was built round it, the way a pearl's built round a bit of grit."

Calvin felt something go cold down the back of his neck. He'd been on twelve floors. He knew what *the oldest thing on the hill* meant, when you were a climber.

"What does it do?" he said. "With disputes?"

"Reads both hands. Out loud. To the whole hall." Aldo held up two knotted fingers. "The first hand, and the second. And then it asks: *who writes him?*" He put the fingers down. "And anybody in the hall who's told can stand up and write him. The way they know him. On a slate, in front of everybody. And when everybody who's going to stand has stood, the Magistrate reads them. And if enough hands agree with one of the first two, that one's true. And he's told."

"And if nobody stands?"

"Then the second hand stands," said Aldo. "And he's whatever that says."

Calvin thought about it.

"The second hand," he said slowly. "For climbers. That's the clerk's."

Aldo nodded.

"And the clerk's says *already told*."

"So Hawise tells me," said Aldo.

"Then that's easy." Calvin sat up. "We'll stand. All of us. Thirty-five of us. We'll all stand up and write him the way we know him, and it'll be thirty-five hands against one, and—"

"Can you?" said Aldo.

Calvin stopped.

"You're held to the Rolls," said Aldo. He said it gently. "Hamo Tull's order. Till the Reckoning. A held hand isn't a told hand, lad. A held hand can't write anybody. Not at the Assize. Not anywhere." He looked at Calvin with his bright eyes. "The order wasn't about your pies."

Calvin sat very still on the stool.

He thought about the crier on the well-house steps, ringing his bell. *They may not buy, nor sell, nor trade.* He thought about Dennis with his three coppers. About the pie man shaking his head. He'd thought it was about pies. Everybody had. They'd spent a week being clever about pies.

"He's not stupid," said Calvin. "Hamo Tull."

"Hamo Tull stole his brother's widow's shop with a penknife at the stitching," said Aldo. "He's a thief. He's not a fool." He picked his tea up and found it was cold, and put it down. "He saw what your scribe did to the Rolls. And then he saw a man in a cord who knows names he's got no business knowing. And he thought: I don't know what that is, but I'm not having thirty-five hands stand up for it in front of the Magistrate."

"So nobody can stand for Dec."

"Nobody *held*." Aldo looked at him. "Anybody told can."

"Who's told?"

Aldo spread his knotted hands, very slightly. Taking in the row of narrow houses, and the Wellgate going down, and the rings of roofs below, and the Lantern Market far away at the bottom, small, full of tiny people.

"Tell is," he said.

***

He told Calvin about the host's hand.

It was very old, he said. So old he had to think to remember the words of it, sitting in his chair with his eyes shut and his lips moving, and Calvin sat on the stool and didn't say anything, which he would later tell Rachael was the hardest thing he'd done on floor 13.

"In my grandfather's grandfather's day," said Aldo, with his eyes shut. "Before the Wet Year. Strangers came to Tell sometimes. From the plain. Carters. Pedlars. A tinker with a cart of pans. A man who wasn't born in Tell wasn't in the Rolls, and a man who wasn't in the Rolls wasn't told, and a man who wasn't told couldn't do anything in Tell. Couldn't buy a pie." He opened his eyes. "So a citizen could write him. Any citizen who chose to. Go to the Tellhouse with him, and stand at the door-roll, and say, *I write this man as I know him*. And write him, in their own hand. And a teller would write the same in the second. And then he was told. By Tell. Not by the Count, and not held by anybody. Told by the hand of his host."

"The host's hand," said Calvin.

"The host's hand."

"Is that still the law?"

"Nobody's ever unmade it," said Aldo. "Nobody's used it in a hundred years, either. There aren't strangers in Tell any more. The plain's all Tell's now. Everybody's born in the Rolls."

"So if somebody from Tell writes me," said Calvin slowly, "with the host's hand. Then I'm told. By Tell. And I'm not held."

"That's what the law says."

"And then I can stand up at the Reckoning. And write Dec."

"That's what the law says," said Aldo again. "What the Bench says is another matter. Hamo Tull will say his order holds you whoever writes you. And somebody'll say it doesn't, because the host's hand is older than the Bench. And they'll argue it." He looked at Calvin. "It needs the Bench to say the order doesn't touch a host-told man. Or it needs it done so many times, by so many people, that the Bench looks a fool if it says otherwise."

"Thirty-five times," said Calvin.

"Thirty-five people in Tell," said Aldo, "who'll walk into the Tellhouse in front of the whole Wellgate, with the Bench's crier in the market and a clerk on every corner, and put their own name in the Rolls next to a climber's." He leaned back in his chair. "Thirty-five people choosing to. On their own feet."

Calvin looked down the hill. At the Lantern Market, far away. At the copy-room yard, which you couldn't see from here, but he knew where it was. At the Low Town, where everybody said *Calvin* now.

He thought about the ham on the step. And the flour.

"Okay," said Calvin.

Aldo looked at him.

"I'll write you," said Aldo.

Calvin turned round on the stool.

"Me?"

"You came up the hill." The old man's mouth did the thing at the corner again. "Took you fifteen days. But you came."

***

He went down the Wellgate a different way, because he wanted to think, and because he never went the same way twice if he could help it. Down through the third ring by a crooked stair, and along a lane of coopers' yards on the second, where the smell of new barrels hung in the street. And halfway along the lane, on a doorstep, with her satchel beside her and her hands in her lap, sat Hawise.

She saw him coming. Her face went tight.

"Calvin," she said. "Go away."

"Why? What's—"

"Because it's her day." Her voice was very quiet. "She comes down this lane every week at noon. With her book. Past my door. And I sit on my step and watch her go past, and she doesn't know me. And I don't want anybody to see me do it. Go *away*, Calvin."

He knew who she meant. Wendy had told them. In the copy-room, days ago. *My cousin did. Alys. She walks past my door every week, with her book. She doesn't know me.*

He didn't go away.

He should have. He'd think about that afterwards. He should have gone away, the way Hawise asked him, because it was her step and her cousin and her week, and it was nothing to do with Calvin.

But he didn't. He stood in the lane. And down at the end of it, at the corner, by a cooper's yard, a figure in long plain grey came round the corner, with a book on its arm, and began to walk up the lane towards them.

Hawise's hands closed in her lap.

It came up the lane. Not hurrying. A tall woman, he could see now, with a plain face and the hood down and brown hair pulled back, and a light step, and her eyes on nothing. The way they all walked. It came level with the cooper's yard. With the house next door. With Hawise's step.

It didn't look at her. It didn't slow down.

Calvin didn't decide to. He'd tell Rachael that, after, and she'd say *you never decide to, Calvin, that's the problem*, and she'd be right. He just opened his mouth. The way he'd opened it on every floor since the first one. Shouting, because it was how he knew where everybody was.

"*ALYS!*"

It went down the lane like a dropped pan. Off the walls. Off the barrels. A cooper looked out of his yard. A cat went over a wall.

The clerk stopped.

Calvin saw it stop. Right there, in the lane, two paces past Hawise's step. Stopped in the middle of a stride, with one foot just set down, and stood.

It didn't turn round.

But its right hand, the one that held the grey pen, came up off the open book on its arm. A finger's width. No more. And hung there. Shaking very slightly.

Nobody in the lane moved. Hawise had both hands over her mouth.

Calvin couldn't see its face. He could see the back of its head, brown hair pulled back, and the shoulders under the plain grey, and the hand with the pen, hanging. For a long time. A very long time. Longer than he could hold his breath, and he tried.

He thought, afterwards, that he'd seen something. In the set of the shoulders. Like a fish going by under ice. Something moving, a long way down, under something hard and grey, that you only see because it blots the light for a second.

Then the hand went down. The pen touched the page.

And the clerk walked on up the lane, not hurrying, the way it always walked, and turned the corner at the top, and was gone.

***

Hawise didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said, quite quietly, "Don't you *ever*—"

And stopped. And sat down on her step, which she'd half stood up from without noticing. And put her hands flat on her knees and looked at them.

"I'm sorry," said Calvin. "I'm sorry. I'm really sorry. I just— Hawise, names *work*. On things that count. On the tenth floor there was a thing called the Steward with a book, and it counted us, it called us numbers, and Drew said *my name's Drew, not fourteen*, and its hand stopped. On the page. It just stopped. And Yan's got this thing where he says somebody's name and they turn round, and—" He stopped, because she wasn't listening. "I'm sorry."

"She stopped," said Hawise.

Calvin looked at her.

"Twelve years," said Hawise. She was still looking at her hands. "Every week. Twelve years, Calvin. I've sat here and said it. Every week. Under my breath. *Alys*. I've said it so many times it doesn't sound like a word any more." Her voice was very steady. Too steady. "She's never stopped. Not once. Not a hitch. She walks past like I'm a bit of wall."

"I'm sorry—"

"She stopped for *you*." Hawise looked up at him. Her eyes were wet. "Because you shouted it. Like you meant her to hear. Like you thought she *could*."

Calvin didn't know what to say. So for once, for very nearly the first time in his life, he didn't say anything at all.

Hawise wiped her eyes with the back of her inky wrist. Then she looked up the lane, at the corner where the grey had gone.

"Next week," she said.

"What?"

"Next week. Noon. Here." She looked at him. "Do it again."

***

He told them that night. All of it. In the copy-room, with everybody on the floor and Higu at the rock. About Aldo, and the Magistrate behind the screen, and *who writes him*, and the held hands. About the host's hand. About Alys.

He talked for a long time. Nobody told him to stop. Even Rachael.

When he'd finished, Higu wrote on the rock. Not in the columns. Across the bottom, under the chalk line, where she put the things that were neither *remembered* nor *true* yet but *plan*.

*HELD HANDS CAN'T WRITE.*

*HOST'S HAND: A CITIZEN WRITES A CLIMBER → TOLD BY TELL.*

*THEN WE CAN STAND FOR ICE.*

She put the chalk down.

"That's the order," said Drew slowly. "That's what the order was for."

"Aldo says Hamo Tull's a thief, not a fool," said Calvin.

"He's right." Drew looked at the rock. "We spent a week thinking it was about *pies*."

Across the room, by the end wall, Declan hadn't said anything. He'd listened to all of it the way he listened to everything, with his face doing nothing and the cord on his wrist. But Calvin had seen him, when he got to *Aldo didn't run*, look at the floor and smile. Just for a second. And when he got to *tell him to come*, Declan had looked up.

"You don't have to," said Declan now. Quietly. "Any of you. Get yourselves written. Get told. Get the order off you. That's the thing. Whatever happens at the Reckoning with me, that's—"

"Shut up, Dec," said Gabe.

Declan shut up.

"We're doing it," said Calvin.

"Calvin—"

"*We're doing it*, Dec." Calvin stood up. He hadn't meant to. He was just standing. "Thirty-five people. In Tell. Who'll write us. And then we're all going to stand up in front of the oldest thing on the hill and write *you*. That's what's happening."

Declan looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked down at the cord. And didn't say anything else.

"The Bench sits in four days," said Deston, from the step of the stable door. He hadn't moved all evening. He had his hand flat on his chest. "The lower hall of the Assize. Anybody in Tell can bring a matter to it. Hawise says." He looked round at them. "We'll bring one. We'll ask to be heard."

"Who speaks?" said Drew.

Deston didn't hesitate.

"Azac," he said.

Azac, on his blanket with his chin on his shield rim, lifted his head.

"Me?"

"It's a city where everybody writes everything down," said Deston. "And nobody's ever *told* them anything. Out loud. Not like you do." He looked at Azac. "Tell it to them."

***

Rachael found him on the stable stairs, late, after.

He was sitting on the third step from the bottom, in the dark, with his fist on his knee. She sat down on the step above, a little to one side, the way she did. Not touching. Rachael didn't touch people unless she was healing them.

"You talked a lot," she said.

"I talked exactly enough."

"You talked about four times enough." She was quiet. "It was good. What you did. With Aldo."

"I *shouted* at Hawise's cousin."

"That too."

They sat. Up in the copy-room, somebody was laughing. Hyvred. Somebody else was telling her to shush.

Calvin looked at his fist.

Then, slowly, he opened it.

He hadn't really looked at it in seven weeks. It was just a palm. Callused at the base of the fingers from the dagger grips. A bit dirty. There was nothing written on it. There never had been. Ina had written them with one fingertip, no ink, the rakers' way, and they'd been gone the moment he'd lifted his finger.

But Calvin knew where each one was. He'd been told.

"I've got all of them in here," he said. "All thirty-five. Ina did Dec second from last. He was standing at the edge, by the fire, with Thea. Ina looked up at him and wrote him in. Right there." He touched a place near the base of his thumb. "And then Ina, last. With the sign for *here*."

Rachael looked at his palm in the dark.

"If they won't let us write him," said Calvin. "At the Reckoning. If the Bench says no. I'm going to stand up anyway. And hold this up. In front of the Magistrate. It's not ink. But it's his name. Ina put it there."

Rachael didn't say anything for a while.

"Calvin," she said.

"What?"

She was quiet. He waited. He could feel her sitting there on the step above him, not saying something. It was a very Rachael kind of not saying something. He'd known her since before the tower. He knew what it sounded like.

"Do you ever wonder," she said, eventually, "why he looked at your arms like that?"

Calvin thought about it. He really did. He sat on the stair with his palm open and thought about the jetty on floor 8, and Declan's face, and *you've got two good arms, Calvin*.

"No," he said.

"Never?"

"Not really." He closed his fist again, gently. "He just likes my arms. They're good arms." He grinned up at her in the dark. "Rach, I don't need to know stuff. I've never needed to know stuff. I've got the names." He held up the fist. "That's the bit that's mine."

Rachael looked at him for a long time.

Then she did a thing she almost never did. She reached down, and put her hand on top of his head, very briefly, the way you'd put a hand on a dog that had done something right. And took it away again.

"Good arms," she said. And went back up the stairs.
