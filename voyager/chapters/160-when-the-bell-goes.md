In the Long Acre, the day started with the chair.

He saw it the second morning, coming out of the tithe barn into a grey-gold dawn with dew on everything. Two lads of about sixteen, brothers by the look of them, one at each side of Gaffer Tobin's chair, lifting it. With Gaffer Tobin in it. The old man sat very upright with his stick across his knees and his blanket tucked round him and his white hair combed flat, and they carried him across the green like that, carefully, without being told, the way you'd carry something that would spill. Over the bridge. Up the lane between the hedges. And out into the fields, to whichever field was being cut that day, where they set the chair down at the top of the headland, in the shade if there was any, facing the corn.

And then they went and picked up their rakes.

"Every day?" said Ice.

"Every day of harvest," said Annet Sallow, going past with her stick. "Twenty years." She didn't stop. "He likes to see it come in."

The old man sat on the headland all day. He didn't sleep, much. He didn't talk, much. People brought him water and he drank it. People brought him bread at noon and he ate a little of it and gave the rest to the sparrows. He watched. Every stroke of every mower. Every sheaf. Every stook going up in its rows. When a field was finished, when the last sheaf went into the last stook and the mowers lifted their blades onto their shoulders and turned to the next field, the old man lifted his stick an inch off his knees, and put it down again.

That was all. An inch.

The gleaners saw it, every time. You could see them see it. Nobody said anything. They just went on to the next field a little straighter.

***

Annet's stick had a notch for every day of harvest.

She showed him on the third morning. Not because he asked. He'd been looking at it, and she'd noticed him looking, the way she noticed everything, and she'd held it out to him, at breakfast, across the trestle, without saying anything, the way you'd hand somebody a thing you thought they should see.

It was hazel. Peeled, so it was pale. As long as her arm. All the way down one side, little notches. Most were cut fresh: clean, white, one a day. But some of them, at the top, had a second little cut across them. And further down, there were marks of a different kind: a line all the way round the stick. Then more notches. Then another line round.

"Days," said Annet. "From the first cut to harvest-home. That's the top." She put her finger on the first notch. "These with the cross are the fields. Every time a field's in, I cross that day." Her finger went down. "That ring is Long Meadow. That's where we should be by the full moon. That ring's the Lammas fields. That's the half. That ring there's the barn fields, at the top of the valley." Her finger stopped. "And that one's the frost."

The last ring. Near the bottom of the stick. Below it there was nothing. Just pale wood.

"Whatever's not in by the frost," said Annet, "the frost has."

"How do you know when the frost is?"

"You don't." She took the stick back. "You know about when. My mother's stick said about when. Her mother's. They cut the ring where it usually comes. Some years it comes late and you laugh at the stick. Some years it comes early and you don't." She looked at the notches. "We're on the stick. Just."

"Just?"

"Two days behind." She stood the stick up beside her on the bench. "My mowers are old. Half the young ones went up the valley to Long Meadow and won't be back till it's in. And Cobb's girl's done her wrist." She looked at him, sideways, from under the hat. "And then thirty-six passers come out of the old barn, and they can't bind, and one of them can't put a sheaf down when the bell goes."

"Calvin."

"I know his name," said Annet. "Everybody knows his name. He *told* everybody his name." She almost smiled. "He's learning. You all are. You bound eleven stooks yesterday, the lot of you, more than a whole family." She picked up her bread. "So we might be one day behind. Instead of two." A pause. "Ask me again at the full moon."

Higu put the barrows on the rock on the third night.

It was the gleaners who told them. At supper, under the oak, with the lamps lit and the last of the light going: Cobb, the old man who'd pointed at Gabe and Salty in the field and had opinions about how people carried. He'd been watching the passers for two days. He'd seen the weapons. He'd seen Yan's scythe and the lean mower's face when he saw it. He'd seen Gabe and Salty, at the smithy, in the evenings, fixing the gleaners' blades with Bettris the smith, who'd let them because they hadn't asked to be let, they'd just picked up a file.

And he'd decided something. You could see him decide it. He put his bread down and wiped his hands and said, to nobody in particular, to the whole long trestle:

"You'll be wanting to know about the bulls, then."

Everybody stopped eating.

"The barrows," said Cobb. He pointed with his chin, east, at the line of low hills going grey in the dusk across the river. "Up along the top there. The old mounds. Green humps, in a line. You'll have seen them." They had. Long low grassy mounds along the ridge of the eastern hills, a dozen of them, maybe more, like the backs of sleeping animals. "They're old. Older than the Acre. Older than the stick. There's doors in some of them. Stone. You don't go in."

"What's in them?" said Calvin.

"Bulls," said Cobb.

Calvin looked at Rachael. Rachael looked at the barrows.

"Black ones," said Cobb. "Big as a hay wain. Horns on them like—" He looked round the table, found what he wanted, and pointed at Yan, at the scythe propped against the oak behind him. "Like that. Two of that. Out of the front of their heads." He picked up his bread again. "In the winter they sleep. In the spring they're up on the tops, eating the gorse. But at harvest—" He shook his head. "At harvest they come down. At dusk. Every day. You'll see them, if you look, on the tops, about an hour before the bell. Standing in a line along the ridge. Looking at the stooks."

"And then?" said Drew.

"And then the bell goes," said Cobb, "and we come in. And they come down, after, in the dark, and they go through the stooks on the east side like they're not there, and eat half a field in a night." He chewed. "The bell's for them as much as us. That's what the bell's *for*. You hear it, you come in, and whatever's out there after is the bulls'."

"Every night?"

"Every night of harvest. Been doing it since the first harvest." He shrugged. "We lose some. Every year. You build that into the stick." He looked at Annet. "Two fields a year, on the east side, about. Eh, Reeve?"

"About," said Annet.

There was a short silence round the trestle.

"Has anybody ever fought them?" said Drew.

Cobb looked at him the way you'd look at somebody who asked if anybody had ever fought the weather.

"No," he said. "You get in when the bell goes."

He looked down the table. Past Drew. Past the rest. At Ice, at the end of the bench with his back to the oak, who hadn't said anything.

Ice had never seen a barrow-bull in his life. Either of them.

He knew why. They'd come in the winter, the first time. The bulls had been asleep in the mounds under the snow, the way Cobb said. Six weeks, and nobody had ever gone up to the ridge, because there was nothing up there but snow and old grass and stone doors you didn't go in. Nobody had known there was anything to know.

He'd walked past the chair for six weeks with snow on it. He'd walked under the barrows for six weeks and never looked up.

"I never saw one," he said. To Higu. Quietly.

Higu looked at him. Then she reached into her pack for the rock, and set it on the trestle among the plates, and wrote, on the right-hand side, under *NOBODY INDOORS*:

*BARROW-BULLS. EAST RIDGE. COME DOWN AT DUSK, AFTER THE BELL.*

And under that, small:

*(NEW. HE NEVER SAW ONE.)*

And then, without looking up, in the other column, on the left, in the space that had been empty since the field edge:

*BULLS: ASLEEP.*

Cobb leaned over and looked at it. He couldn't read it. Nobody on this floor could read their letters, any more than anybody in Tell could. He looked at the two columns and the line between them and then at Higu.

"What's that?"

"What he remembers," said Higu, pointing at the left. "And what's true," pointing at the right.

Cobb looked at Ice. Then back at the rock.

"Hm," he said. And went back to his bread.

"It's their rule," she said, when he looked at it. "Not mine. When the bell goes, the field's not yours." She looked at him, steady, half-lidded. "So when the bell goes, it's not yours either."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Higu. "I'm writing it down."

He went up onto the east ridge in the last hour before the bell, on the fourth day, with Thea and Xola.

It was a long walk. Down through Haverley, over the bridge, across the river meadows where the cattle were, the ordinary cattle, brown and slow, who watched them go past with their jaws going. Then up. Through the orchards on the lower slope, where the apples were going red and the wasps were drunk on the windfalls in the grass. Through a field of stubble that had been cut the week before they came, with the stooks still standing in it, waiting to be carted. And then out onto the open hillside above the fields, where the hedges stopped and there was only short turf and gorse and grey stones, and the wind, and the sky going orange in the west behind them over the whole of the valley.

They stopped at the top of the stubble field, by a stook at the end of a row.

"Here?" said Thea.

"Here," said Ice.

He looked up the slope.

The barrows were there. Close now. A line of them along the ridge, three or four hundred metres away up the turf, long and green and low, the grass on them a different colour from the grass round them, richer, darker. Old. He could see the stone at the end of the nearest one. Two upright slabs and a third across the top, half-buried, with a dark gap under it. A door.

Nothing in front of it.

"Cobb said an hour before the bell," said Xola. "Standing on the tops. In a line." She'd climbed up onto the stook, which you weren't supposed to do, and was sitting on the top of it with her legs dangling and Squiddles on her head, looking up at the ridge. "There's nothing. There's no bulls. There's grass."

"Wait," said Ice.

He was putting his helm on. The Galeward Helm, the closed white one, that he'd worn on the Salt for the glare. He checked the strap on the lamp shield. The shield Gabe had made him, with the lamp and the lines like the rays of a sun, and Jared's rose on the inside of the rim where only his hand would ever find it. He checked his sword.

He felt Thea watching him do it. He didn't look round. He knew what her face was doing. He'd learned it, the way she'd learned his head.

*Bell's in fifty minutes*, said Alea. *About. I can't hear it from here. It's a long way down. You'll hear it, though. Everybody hears it.*

"I know."

*And you'll come in.*

"I know, Alea."

*I'm not nagging. I'm saying it out loud. Like Higu.* A pause. *It's on my list.*

He walked up the slope.

***

The ground thrummed.

He felt it before he saw anything. Under his boots, through the short turf, a long low trembling, like a cart going over a bridge a long way off. Then again. Then a sound, from the barrow in front of him, from the dark gap under the stone. Not a bellow. Lower than that. A breath, huge and slow and wet, going in, and going out.

And then the bull came out of the hill.

It came out the way something comes out of water. The dark under the stone moved, and was a head. A vast black head, wider than his shoulders, coming out from under the lintel slowly, lower than the stone, with the horns laid back along its neck to get them through the door. And then out. The head came up. The horns swung forward.

Cobb had been right. They were like Yan's scythe. Two of them, coming out of the black curls on its forehead and going out and forward and up, long, curved, thick at the root and thin at the tip, pale grey, polished, with the evening sun running down the inside edge of each one in a thin line of light.

Then the shoulders. Then the rest of it.

It was the size of a hay wain. More. Black all over, the black of a crow's wing, with a sheen on it that went blue-green where the light hit. Shaggy at the shoulders, curls thick enough to lose your hand in, and smooth and hard over the flanks and the long back, every muscle in it moving under the skin. A white blaze on the forehead between the horns, like a splash of paint. Its eyes were small and dark and wet and very calm.

It walked out of the barrow onto the turf and stopped, and lifted its head, and smelled the evening.

*Two above*, said Alea. *And.*

The ground thrummed again. From the next barrow along. Thirty metres to his right. Another dark gap under another stone, and another head coming out of it.

*Three above*, said Alea. *Two of them. Outnumbered.* A pause. *Times six.*

The first bull looked at him.

It hadn't, until then. It had looked at the evening. At the fields far below, at the rows of stooks gold in the low sun. Now it turned its head, slowly, and the long pale horns came round with it like a gate swinging, and it looked at him, standing on the turf thirty metres away between it and the stooks, with a sword and a shield and a closed white helm.

It didn't paw the ground. It didn't snort. It didn't do any of the things the animals did on the floors below, the painted ones, the ones that showed you what they were about to do so you could get out of the way. It looked at him, calmly, for about the time it took to breathe in.

And then it came.

***

He'd known it would be fast. Everything that size was faster than it looked. He hadn't known it would be *that* fast.

It didn't build up. There was no run-up. One second it was standing still in front of the barrow and the next it was halfway to him, head down, the horns coming at him low and level across the turf, a wall of black with two blades sticking out of it, and the ground going under his feet like a drum. He went left. Late. The right horn went past his hip close enough that he felt the wind of it pull at his vest, and the bull went past him like a house going by, and he turned with it, the way you turned with something that big, inside its turn, keeping close, close enough to touch, because the horns couldn't reach what was right against its side.

It swung its head.

Not at him. Past him. It knew where he was. It swung its whole head sideways, the left horn sweeping round in a long flat arc a foot off the ground, like a mower's stroke, and he got the shield down in front of it just in time and the horn hit the rim.

He didn't block it. You didn't block a thing like that, not with your arm, not if you wanted your arm. He tilted the shield. Turned it. A door, not a wall. The horn hit the iron rim at an angle and slid, screeching, along it and off, and the weight of it went past him, and he felt it go through his whole left side anyway, from the wrist to the shoulder to the hip, a jolt like being run into by a cart.

*Eighty-eight*, said Alea.

He tipped his head. Didn't look at it. Didn't stop.

The bull had overrun him. It was turning, ten metres away, the great black body swinging round on its back legs, heavy, the turf tearing up under its hooves in long brown strips. It would come back. It would come back faster.

And the second one was already coming.

He heard it before he saw it. The ground. From the right, from behind, the drumming coming up through his boots in a different rhythm from the first. He didn't look round. He went forward, toward the first one, as it finished its turn, which was the last thing it expected, and the last thing anybody sensible would do, and he heard the second one go through the place where he'd been standing with a sound like a wave breaking.

Now they were both in front of him. Side by side. Ten metres apart. Turning.

*I'm going to say it*, said Alea. *Because you'd want me to. You're at eighty-eight. And there's two. And you've got about forty minutes.*

"I know."

*And Thea's on a stook.*

"I *know*."

The first one came again.

He'd seen how it did it now. The head went down, a fraction, before it moved. Not much. A finger's width. The muscles in the great black shoulders bunched. Then it went. He watched for the head, and when it dipped, he went, not sideways this time, *in*. Toward it. Low. Under the line of the horns, which were coming at chest height, and down, onto one knee, on the turf, with the shield up over his head at an angle like a roof, and the bull went over him.

Not over him. Through where he'd been. Its chest went over his shield, the horns went either side of him, a foot either side, and he was under it. Under the whole black weight of it, for one long second, in the dark under its belly, with its legs going past him like trees in a storm and the smell of it, grass and dung and hot animal, and he put his sword up.

Not at its belly. He'd have got the sword in and lost it. At the back leg. At the place behind the hock, above the hoof, where there was a long cord of tendon under the skin as thick as a rope, that every animal that size had, and every one of them stood on.

He cut it.

The sword went through the skin and into the cord and the cord went, with a sound like a bowstring breaking, and the bull's back end dropped. Just dropped. The great body went down at the back like a cart that's lost a wheel, still going forward with its front legs, and its hindquarters slewed sideways across the turf, and it bellowed.

That was the first sound it had made. He'd almost thought they couldn't.

It was enormous. It went out across the whole hillside and down into the valley, a long, deep, furious roar like the inside of a storm, and down on the stook a hundred metres below him Xola screamed, very short, and stopped herself.

He was up. He was out from under it. It was trying to get up, its front legs scrabbling on the turf, its back legs dragging, swinging its head from side to side, the horns sweeping, trying to find him. He kept behind the line of the horns. He kept to its broken side.

And then the second one hit him.

***

He'd forgotten it. For about a second. Not forgotten. *Lost.* He'd had it in his head, the second one, the drumming, and then the first one had bellowed and he'd been under it and out again and somewhere in there the drumming had stopped being something he was counting and started being part of the noise, and he hadn't heard it come.

It didn't get him with the horns. That was luck. It came in from his left, low, the way the first one had, and he turned, too late to go anywhere, and got the shield round, and the shield took the horn. And the rest of the bull came on behind the horn. And the horn slid off the shield the way it was supposed to, the way he'd made the first one slide, and the bull's shoulder came on after it and hit him in the side.

He went. Off his feet, sideways, three metres through the air, and down on the turf on his back, and the ground came up into him all at once like a door slammed, and everything went white.

*Sixty-three*, said Alea.

He lay there.

Not long. A heartbeat. Less. He lay on his back on the hillside with the sky going orange over him and the turf under his back and his ribs on the left side doing something he didn't like, a hot bright grinding feeling, every breath, and Alea's voice in his ear, very level, saying *sixty-three*, and he thought:

*I don't like that number.*

He didn't think about whether Xola was watching. He didn't think about whether there was a healer a hundred metres away who'd be there in a minute. He didn't think about the front of a line and what happened when the front flinched. He thought about a rock in the sea, and Thea's hands round his, wet and cold and hard, saying *when Alea says a number you don't like, you heal. And you don't wait for a worse one.*

He put his hand on his side.

The gold came. Mend Self. Up through him from somewhere under his breastbone, warm, the way it always had, and into the ribs on the left side, where the grinding was. Not much. Not fast. It wasn't a healer's heal, it was a Paladin's, and it had never once in two lives been enough to make anything stop hurting. But the grinding went from bright to dull. And the white went out of the edges of everything. And he could breathe.

*Seventy-one*, said Alea. And then, very quietly, in a voice he'd never heard her use in a fight: *Thank you.*

He rolled. The second bull's hooves came down where his head had been, both front ones, a great double thud that he felt in his teeth.

He came up on one knee with the shield over him.

It stood over him. Huge. Black against the orange sky. The horns either side of the shield, the left one so close he could have put his hand on it, pale, polished, the inside edge sharp as Working On It. It lowered its head to look at him, under the shield. Its eyes were very calm.

He put his sword into the place under its jaw where the throat was soft, all the way, up, the way Calvin had told the whole of World to do it on floor 1, to the sheep, with everybody laughing.

It stood there for a moment longer.

Then it knelt. Front legs first. Slowly. The way the Bellwether had knelt in the Fold, the first time, folding its legs under it like somebody sitting down in a chair. And then it lay on its side on the turf in the evening, very slowly, and didn't get up.

He stood on the hillside with his sword in his hand, breathing.

His side hurt. Not as much as it had. The heal had done that. But it hurt, a dull deep ache over the ribs, and his left arm hurt from the wrist to the shoulder where the horn had gone along the shield, and his back hurt from the ground. He'd have a bruise the size of a dinner plate tomorrow. He'd had worse. He'd had much worse, and walked on it, and said *fine*, every time, for ten years, to everyone who asked.

Down the slope, on the stook, Xola had stood up. She was standing on the top of a stook of wheat, which you definitely weren't supposed to do, with both hands over her mouth. Thea was on the ground beside it. She hadn't moved. She was holding the lantern, unlit, in both hands, against her chest. Her face, at a hundred metres, in the orange light, was just a pale shape. "Yes," he said. "My ribs. On the left. And my arm, where it went down the shield. And my back."

Thea closed her eyes.

Just for a second. Just closed them, and stood there, holding the lantern, with her eyes shut, in the stubble, in the last of the light, like somebody who has been holding their breath for a very long time and has just found out they're allowed to let it go.

Then she opened them.

"Xola," she said.

"I'm *doing* it, babe," said Xola, to Thea. Thickly. And came across the stubble, and put both her hands flat on his left side, over his vest, over the ribs, and the water came.

It went into him cool, the way it always did. Like putting your hand in a stream on a hot day. It went along the ribs, into the dull deep ache where the bull's shoulder had hit, and found something there, a crack, a hairline, the kind of thing he'd have walked on for a week and never told anybody, and drew it together like a rope being spliced. Then the arm. Then the back.

She didn't say anything while she did it. She didn't look at his face. She looked at her hands.

When she'd finished, she stood there with her hands still on his side, for a moment longer than she needed to. Then she took them away and wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"Icey," she said.

"Yeah."

"Don't you *ever*," said Xola, "*ever* make me watch you do the right thing again. I don't know what to *do* with it. I've got nowhere to *put* it." She sniffed hard. "I had a whole speech. About the bell. I had it *ready*."

"Sorry."

"You're *not* sorry."

"No," said Ice. "Not really."

Xola laughed. It came out of her like a hiccup, wet, surprised. She put her forehead against his shoulder, hard, for a second, the way she did with Thea, and then stepped back and climbed up onto the stook again, which you definitely weren't supposed to do, and sat down on the top of it, and put her face in her hands.

"I'm not crying," she said, through them. "I'm a *freshwater animal*. It's *dew*."

***

Thea walked him down.

Down through the stubble, in the dusk, in the warm still evening, with the swifts going to bed over their heads and the valley below them going blue and grey, field by field, lamp by lamp, as the village lit up. Xola had gone on ahead, fast, down the hill, to tell Higu he was in. To tell everybody. She'd tell the harvest. She'd tell Gaffer Tobin in his chair.

Thea didn't say anything for a long time. She walked beside him with the lantern, lit now, the amber in it leaning so hard toward him it lay flat against the glass, and she didn't take his hand, and she didn't say anything at all.

At the bottom of the stubble, at the gate into the orchard, she stopped.

He stopped too.

She turned round and looked up the hill. At the ridge, dark now against the last of the green light in the sky. At the line of barrows. At the place, a long way along, where a black shape the size of a hay wain was walking slowly down out of the dark toward the fields on the east side.

"It's going to go through the stooks," she said.

"Yes."

"The ones on the east side. Cobb's."

"Yes."

"And you could have—" She stopped. "You'd have taken it. Before. You'd have taken it on your own after the bell and come down at midnight with your ribs broken and said fine, and they'd have had all their stooks."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

"And you came in," said Thea.

"I said I would."

"I know what you said." Her voice went, and came back. "I was there. I made you say it. I didn't know you'd—" She stopped again. "I didn't know it would look like that. You. Turning your back on something. And walking away from it. Because it was the right time to walk away." She swallowed. "I've never seen you do that. Not once. Not in either—" She looked down the valley, at the lamps, and didn't finish.

*Not in either life*, said Alea, very quietly. *She nearly said it. Out loud.*

He didn't say anything.

Thea put the lantern down. On the top of the gatepost, carefully, where it would stand. And then she put both her hands on his face, the way she had on the rock in the sea, and looked at it, in the dusk, for a long time.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"You know what for." She didn't let go. "For doing it for me. When it was hard. When it was *there*. Five above, and you wanted it, I *saw* you want it, I saw your hand on your sword, and you put it away anyway." She almost laughed. "Because a bell went. And because of me."

"Because of you," said Ice.

She kissed him. At an orchard gate, in the dusk, at the bottom of a field of stubble, with the stooks standing behind them in their rows and a barrow-bull walking down the hill toward the ones on the east side and the lamps of Haverley coming on below them one by one. Not long. Just once. Her hands on his face.

Then she picked the lantern back up off the gatepost and took his hand, and they went down through the orchard to the village, with the windfalls in the grass round their boots and the drunk wasps gone to bed.

***

In the morning, the stooks on the east side were gone.

Not all of them. Cobb's field, the one under the ridge. A corner of it, about a third. Trampled flat and eaten, with great round hoof-prints in the stubble the size of buckets, and dung, and the broken bands of the sheaves scattered everywhere like straw ribbon after a fair.

Cobb stood at the gate and looked at it.

Ice went and stood beside him. He'd known he would have to. He'd known since the orchard gate.

"That was the third one," he said. "I left it. At the bell."

Cobb didn't look at him.

"I know," he said. "Xola told me." He looked at the trampled corner for a while longer. "She told everybody. She told Gaffer." He sniffed. "She told my *pig*."

"I'm sorry about the stooks."

Cobb turned his head and looked at him then. A long look, from under his eyebrows. The look the gleaners gave things, measuring, the way Annet measured the stick.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I could have stopped it."

"After the bell," said Cobb.

"Yes."

"Then it wasn't yours to stop." He said it the way you'd say the sky was up. He looked back at the field. "You killed two. Before. That's two that won't be down here tonight, or any night. That's more than anybody's done in Haverley since the first harvest." He shrugged. "And you came in when the bell went. Like a person." He paused. "I'd have thought less of you if you hadn't."

He turned and walked away along the headland to where the binders were starting, and then he stopped, and looked back.

"It's built into the stick," he said. "The bulls. Two fields a year, on the east side. About." He looked at the trampled corner. "That's a third of one. That's nothing. That's a good year." He almost smiled. "Annet'll cross it off this morning and say *about*."

She did.

***

On the sixth morning, there was dew on the stooks.

There'd been dew every morning. That wasn't it. This was different. He came out of the tithe barn at first light and walked across the green to the bridge to wash his face in the river, the way he'd done every morning, and the grass on the green was white. Not frost. Not quite. Just dew, so heavy and so cold that it lay on every blade like a breath on a window, and when he walked through it his boots left dark green prints behind him and his feet were cold through the leather.

He stood on the bridge and looked at it.

The whole valley was white. Every field. The stubble, the stooks in their rows, the hedges, the orchards on the slopes, all of it lying under a pale grey shine in the first light, very still, with mist on the river under the bridge and a smell in the air he hadn't smelled since floor 10. Sharp. Thin. Clean.

He breathed it in.

On the roof of the tithe barn, along the whole long ridge of it, there were swallows. Hundreds of them. More than he'd ever seen. Sitting in a line along the old stone slates at the top of the roof, shoulder to shoulder, feathers fluffed out against the cold, a long dark row of small bodies against the pale sky, all facing the same way. South. Down the valley. Toward the old barn, and the door they'd come out of.

They weren't flying. They weren't hunting. They were just sitting there, all of them, together, waiting.

"They're early," said somebody behind him.

It was Gaffer Tobin. In his chair. The two lads had brought him out onto the green, early, before they'd had their breakfast, and set him down by the bridge, facing the barn. He was looking at the swallows.

"They go when the year turns," said the old man. His voice was very thin, like paper. "They sit on the barn first. A few days. Getting ready. All of them. And then one morning they're gone." He looked at the long line of birds against the sky. "They're early."

"How early?"

"Eight days," said Gaffer Tobin. "Maybe ten." He looked down at his hands on his stick. "I've seen seventy-four of them go. I know when they go."

Ice looked at the swallows. At the white grass. At the mist on the river.

"Is that bad?"

The old man thought about it.

"It's early," he said at last. As if that was the answer. As if it were all of it.

Then he lifted his stick, an inch, off his knees, at the line of birds on the barn. The way he did for a field when it was in.

And put it down again.

***

Annet looked at her stick at breakfast for a long time.

She didn't say anything. She held it in both hands, under the oak, with her bread untouched in front of her, and ran her thumb down the notches. Down past the crossed ones. Past the ring for Long Meadow, which they hadn't got to yet. Past the ring for the Lammas fields. Down to the bottom ring, the one that said *frost*, with nothing under it but pale wood.

She looked at it the way Higu looked at the rock.

Then she took out her knife and, very carefully, cut a second ring round the stick. Higher up. A long way higher up than the first.

She didn't explain it. She didn't have to. Everybody at the trestle saw her do it. The gleaners stopped eating, the whole length of the table, and watched her cut it, and nobody said a word.

When she'd done, she put the knife away and the stick down beside her and picked up her bread.

"Eat up," said Annet Sallow, to all of them. "It's harvest."
