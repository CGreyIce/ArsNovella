The fish came up to look at her every morning, and Aleaka had stopped pretending it was a coincidence.

It had started on the third day. She'd gone down to the end of the last jetty early, before anybody was up, to sit with her legs over the water and think. And the lake had been flat and green and quiet, and then it hadn't. Little rings had started on the surface, here and there, the way rain starts. And then more. And she'd looked down, and there'd been fish. Dozens of them. Silver and brown and speckled green, hanging in the clear water under her boots, all with their noses pointing at her. Just looking. Like a crowd at the front of a stage.

She'd looked back at them for a long time.

"Morning," she'd said, eventually, because it seemed rude not to.

Now it was every morning. And not just the fish. The eels came, long and dark, winding up from the stilts. A heron came, on the fourth day, and stood on the next jetty along on one leg and stared at her with its yellow eye and didn't fish at all. A knapper had come up out of the shallows by the posts on the sixth morning and stopped, halfway out of the water, with its eyestalks swivelled round toward her, and stayed there like that until Sleet saw it and lost her mind.

And the little birds. The falls-wrens, the river-folk called them. Small, round, dark-brown birds with a white bib, that lived on the wet rocks by the falls and bobbed up and down on their legs all the time, as if they were constantly agreeing with something. She'd watched one walk straight into the lake off a rock on the second day, under the water, along the bottom, like it was going for a stroll, and come out the other side with a beetle. They were her favourites. There were always four or five of them on the jetty rail now when she came down. Bobbing at her.

"*They know*," Hana had said, the first morning she came down with her. Standing at the end of the jetty, with her crown on crooked and her tail round her ankles, looking at the ring of fish. "*Leaka*. They *know* you."

"They know I won't hurt them," said Aleaka. "That's all it is. That's what the skill *says*." *Kindred.* She'd had it since floor 2. *They know you won't hurt them. Most of them believe it.* "It's just a skill, Han."

"Fish don't come and *stare* at a skill."

She hadn't had an answer for that.

***

Sleet hated all of it.

Sleet was half grown now, and nobody's idea of a pup any more. A Rimehound, frost-grey, long-legged, up to Aleaka's hip, with a ruff of thick pale fur round her neck that went stiff and white with frost when she was cross. And she was cross a lot. She'd hated the gulls on floor 5, all forty of them. She'd hated the shardbacks on six. She hated the fish on eight, with a deep, personal, shivering hatred, and stood at the end of the jetty every morning with her ruff gone white and her lip up, growling at the water.

The fish didn't care. Fish, it turned out, had no respect.

"She's jealous," said Milli, on the fifth morning. He was lying on the jetty in the sun with his eyes shut, the way he lay everywhere, like a cat on a windowsill. "She thinks you're going to swap her for a trout."

"I'm not going to swap her for a *trout*."

"*She* doesn't know that."

Aleaka put her hand on Sleet's frosty ruff. Sleet stopped growling and leaned on her, hard, the whole weight of her, the way she did. As if she were saying *mine*.

"I'm not," said Aleaka. "Silly. I'm not."

She looked out at the water. At the ring of noses. And beyond them, over them, up the long green length of the lake, past the city, to where the falls came down in their great white staircase out of the grey sky.

The fish were looking at her.

But every so often, all together, they turned their heads and looked upstream.

***

The thirty-six planned the Cataract at the long table on the hundred and seventy-second evening, and Aleaka listened, and didn't say anything, which was usual.

She'd always been slow to talk in a crowd. She didn't mind it. She liked listening. Things came out of her slowly, and stumbled sometimes on the way, and by the time she'd got them in order the conversation had usually gone on somewhere else. So she sat on the bench between Hana and Jared, with Sleet's chin on her knee under the table, and listened.

Ice told them where it was.

That was Higu's rule. He told them where, and they decided how. He stood at the end of the long table with his hands flat on it and told them in that careful voice he'd had since the bakery. As if every word was something he'd put down on purpose and might have to pick up again.

"The big pool," he said. "Under the Great Fall. About a day up. You walked past it on the way down. The one I said not to swim near." He was quiet for a moment. "It's long. Very long. It's the colour of the water when the water's white. In the fall, under the fall, you won't see it. Not till it's out." He looked down the table. "It hunts what moves in the water. It comes up for it."

"And how does it die?" said Higu.

Ice looked at her. Then he took his hands off the table.

"That's yours," he said.

Higu nodded slowly, as if Ice had passed something.

So they decided. It took two hours. Drew stood at the rock with his chalk. Chris wrote everything down. Gabe and Salty said they could build a raft, a big one, logs and rope, to put out on the pool. Something moving in the water. Bait. And when it came up for the raft, everybody on the rock shelves round the pool would hit it at once.

"At once," said Drew. "Like the bells. Not thirty-six people going one after another. One hit. Everybody."

"On a count," said Higu.

And thirty-five heads turned down the table to Newish.

Newish had been leaning back on the bench with her arms folded and her feet on Calvin's knees. She looked at all of them looking at her. Then she took her feet off Calvin's knees, very slowly, and sat up.

"*Finally*," said Newish. "Somebody's realised I'm *useful*."

"You were always useful," said Ice, from the end of the table.

"I *know*. I was waiting for one of you to *say* it."

Calvin said something. Newish said "Shut *up*, Calvin," without looking at him. Half the table laughed. Drew wrote it on the rock. *THE BIG POOL. DAY 178. RAFT. NEWISH COUNTS. EVERYBODY ON ONE.*

Aleaka watched it go up in chalk.

And on the windowsill behind Drew, where three little falls-wrens had been sitting all evening in a row, bobbing, she watched all three of them stop bobbing at once. And turn their heads upstream. Toward the Great Fall.

And stay like that, very still, until the meeting was over.

***

"The Calling Stone," said Oarin.

She'd found him on the walkway outside the house, the next morning, watching the mist. She'd had to stand next to him for quite a long time before he noticed she was there. The river-folk were like that. They noticed you the way the tide comes in.

"Calling," said Aleaka. "Calling what?"

"Fish," said Oarin. He thought about it for a while. "And whatever else would come."

He told her slowly. Everything he told anybody, he told slowly. At the top of the Great Fall, where the river came to the lip, there was a stone. Old. Older than Wendmere. And in the old days, in the spring, somebody sat on it. A caller. And called the fish up the falls. Up out of the lake, up the whole great staircase of water, all the way to the top, to spawn in the high pools where the water was quiet.

"They do not come any more," said Oarin. "Not up the Great Fall. Not since the big one came to the pool. They stay below." He was quiet. "There has not been a caller in six hundred years."

"What did they do?" said Aleaka. "The callers. Did they— make them come?"

Oarin turned his long head and looked at her for a long time. With his large wet eyes. At her face, and then at the two fox ears on top of her head, and then, slowly, down at the three falls-wrens that had followed her along the walkway and were sitting on the rope rail beside her, bobbing.

"No," said Oarin. "A caller does not make anything come." He looked back at the mist. "A caller asks."

***

She found Ice on the kitchen step that evening, peeling.

He was always peeling something. He cooked for all thirty-six of them every night now, in the old kitchen at the back of the house for lights, and he'd been banned from letting anybody help since Hako tried to fry a fish whole. So he sat on the step outside the back door at dusk with a sack of something and a bucket and a small knife, and peeled. It was the only time anybody ever saw him sit still on purpose.

She sat down on the step next to him. Sleet lay down across both their feet.

"You're doing it wrong," said Aleaka.

He looked at the potato in his hand. "I'm not."

"You are. You're doing it all in one go. Nobody does it all in one go. It's showing off."

"It's not showing off. It's just how I—"

"It's showing off, Ice." She took the potato off him and looked at it. One long curl of peel, unbroken, all the way round. "*Look* at that. That's ridiculous. That's a ridiculous thing to do to a potato. Who are you trying to impress? The *potato*?"

He looked at her, and his mouth twitched, and he said, very meekly, "Sorry, Leaka."

"*Sorry, Leaka*," she said. "You should be."

It was the oldest thing they had. Older than this place. Older than the white room. Every call they'd ever had, every message, every stream she'd ever caught him in the chat of, typing something stupid at two in the morning. She'd tell him off. He'd go meek. It was the bit. It had always been the bit.

And then, always, he turned it round.

"You're cute when you're bossy," said Ice.

"I am *not*—"

"You are, though."

"*Stop it*." She shoved him with her shoulder. He let himself be shoved, nearly off the step, and laughed, and she laughed, and Sleet groaned at the disturbance and resettled across their feet.

They sat for a bit.

It was strange. It was so strange. She'd had less than a year of him, on Earth. Less than a year, before he went. A year of calls and messages and two-in-the-morning chat. And then nothing. And then a white room, and a little ball of light at her shoulder going *Welcome, Player*, and a man going through an arch without looking back. And then a hundred and fifty days of *Voyager* in World, of onion skins and a lantern on a bench and a fox. A *fox*. He'd made it a fox. Up here, where she'd never see it.

And now he was sitting on a kitchen step peeling a potato in one go and calling her cute. Like nothing had happened. Like everything had.

Sleet lifted her head.

She was looking at Ice. At his right shoulder. At the empty air just beside his ear. Her ears had come up, and her nose was working, and her ruff, which had been white with frost all evening at the fish, had gone soft and grey again. And the tip of her tail, on the step, went *thump*.

*Thump*.

Aleaka looked at Sleet. Then at Ice's shoulder.

"She can see her," said Aleaka. Slowly. "Can't she."

Ice was quiet for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. "Animals can. Some of them. Sefa can."

*Thump*, went Sleet's tail.

Aleaka looked at the empty air beside his ear. She'd been looking at it, on and off, since the bakery on seven. Since *It's called Alea, isn't it*, and *Yeah, Leaka*, and putting her hands over her face in the flour. She'd never seen anything there. She'd never asked.

"Can I?" she said.

It came out smaller than she meant it to.

Ice looked at her. Then he turned his head, a little, toward his right shoulder. The way you turn to somebody sitting next to you.

"She wants to—" he said, to the air. And stopped. And listened. "Yeah. I know. I *know* you are." He listened again. Something went across his face that was almost a laugh. "Okay. Okay. I'll tell her."

He turned back.

"She says yes," said Ice. "She says she's been waiting about a hundred and seventy days for you to ask."

And then she was there.

It wasn't like a light coming on. It was more like Aleaka had been looking at something all along without seeing it, and then she saw it. On his right shoulder. Sitting there, very upright, with her tail wrapped round her feet.

A fox.

A small one. Much smaller than Aleaka had pictured. Not much bigger than Ice's hand. Pale, and faintly glowing, like moonlight through a curtain, so that you could just see the shape of his collar through her. Neat pointed ears. A long brush of a tail. And two eyes, two tiny bright white points, like pinpricks in paper held up to a lamp, looking straight at her.

Aleaka put both her hands over her mouth.

"*Oh*," she said, through them. "Oh, she's so *small*."

Ice's face did a thing.

"She says," said Ice carefully, "she's *compact*."

Aleaka laughed so hard she nearly fell off the step. And then she was crying, and laughing, both at once, the way she had in the flour, with her hands over her face and her glasses steaming up. And the little fox on his shoulder watched her do it with her pinprick eyes, and then, very slowly, leaned forward. Out, off his shoulder, into the air. Toward her.

Aleaka held out one finger. She didn't know why. It's what you do with something small.

The fox touched her nose to the end of it.

Aleaka couldn't feel it. There wasn't anything to feel. But she felt it anyway, all the way up her arm.

"*Hello*," she said. Her voice wobbled all over the place. "Hello, Alea. I'm— you're named after me. Sort of." She sniffed. "*Obviously*."

"She knows," said Ice. Very softly. "She says she's always known."

***

She told him about the stone after. When she'd stopped crying. When Alea had gone back to his shoulder and gone back to being nothing that anybody could see, and Sleet had put her head back down on their feet and gone to sleep with her tail still thumping, slower and slower.

"The Calling Stone," said Ice. "At the top of the Great Fall."

"Have you been?"

"No." He picked up another potato. "I've never been up there."

"But you've been everywhere. You were on every floor before us. You knew where the *pool* was."

"Not this one." He started to peel. One long curl. "I came down the falls with you, Leaka. I didn't come down first. I didn't look at anything on the way." He was quiet for a moment. "There's a lot on this floor I never looked at."

She thought about that.

"I want to go up," she said. "Before the raid. To the stone." She didn't know how to explain it. It came out in pieces. "The fish keep looking up there. All of them. And the birds. When you said *the Great Fall*, at the table. All three on the windowsill. They looked up there. Like they were— like they were waiting for something to happen." She stopped. "I don't know what. I want to go and see."

Ice looked at her for a long moment, over the potato.

"Then go and see," he said.

"You're not going to tell me where it is? What it does?"

"I don't know what it does." He went back to peeling. "That's the best bit, Leaka. Nobody does."

***

Her party went up with her on the hundred and seventy-fifth morning. Of course they did.

Hana first, in her crown, with the iron rose in the front of it catching the light. Milli beside her, grumbling about stairs. Jared at the back, steady, with his shield on his arm and his eyes on everything. And Newish, who said she was only coming for the view, and then talked the whole way up about how she was going to count the raid, and how it was going to be the best count anybody had ever counted, and how they'd all be sorry they'd ever called her rude.

"Nobody's called you rude," said Jared.

"*Ice* has. On floor 2. In *World*."

"That was you calling *him* rude."

"Same thing," said Newish.

They climbed the stair beside the falls. The same stair they'd come down, three weeks ago, in the rain, behind Ice. Up past Wendmere, and the second fall, and the third. A long day. And in the afternoon they came round the last bend, and there it was.

The big pool.

They stopped well back from it. On a ledge high up the cliff, where Ice had said they'd be safe. And looked down.

It was round. Almost perfectly round, like a bowl cut into the black rock, and so dark it looked like a hole. Green-black. Still at the edges and boiling in the middle. And all round it, like seats round a stage, there were rock shelves, flat and wet and black, going down to the water in steps. And at the far side, coming down into it out of the grey sky, was the Great Fall.

It was so tall she couldn't see the top of it. A single white column, falling and falling, so far that it went from a roar at the bottom to a hiss at the middle to a silence at the top, lost in the cloud. Where it hit the pool, the water went white. All white. A great churning bloom of it, foaming and heaving, thirty metres across.

Nothing moved in the pool. Nothing came up.

"I can't see it," said Hana. Very quietly.

"You won't," said Aleaka. "He said."

She looked at the rocks round the pool. The black shelves. There were no birds on them. None. Every other rock on the whole floor had falls-wrens on it, bobbing. Not these. The shelves round the big pool were bare.

The three wrens that had come up the stair with her, hopping from rock to rock behind Sleet, had stopped. Back on the bend. They wouldn't come any closer.

***

The stair went on up beside the Great Fall. Steep, and wet, and endless. They climbed it until the light started to go yellow. And then, quite suddenly, there wasn't any more stair, and they were at the top.

The river came to the lip there. Wide and smooth and fast, sliding toward the edge over a bed of pale stones, and then going over, all at once, in a long curve of green glass, into nothing. Into the cloud. The roar came up from so far below it was hardly a sound.

And right at the edge, a metre from the lip, with the river sliding past it, there was a stone.

It was round. Waist-high. Grey, and worn smooth as an egg by six hundred years of spray. There were carvings on it, going round and round it in a spiral from the bottom to the top. Fish. Hundreds of them. All swimming up. All going round and up and up toward the top of the stone.

And on top of it, worn into the rock, there was a hollow. Shallow and smooth. The shape of two hands, cupped, side by side. As if somebody had sat here and held out their hands, the same way, in the same place, for so long that the stone had given way.

She touched it.

***

She sat on the stone until it was nearly dark.

She tried everything she could think of. She held her hands out, in the hollow, the way they must have done. She reached out with Kindred, the way she did with Sleet, with the gulls on five, the warm open feeling of *I won't hurt you*. And things came. Of course they did. They always came. Fish came up in the quiet water above the lip and hung there, noses toward her. A heron came and stood on a rock. A pair of ducks. Something small and brown and furry that she thought might be a water rat.

They came. They looked at her. They stayed.

The window didn't change.

"It's not that," she said, at last. Mostly to herself. "It's not that. They're just— they know I won't hurt them. That's all. That's not *calling*. That's just—" She didn't know the word.

"Being nice," said Milli. From a rock. With his eyes shut.

"Being *safe*," said Hana.

Aleaka looked at her hands, in the hollow. Held out. Waiting.

"What do you ask them for?" said Hana.

She was sitting on the next rock, with her knees up and her crown off, holding it in her lap. Turning it round. The iron rose. The onion skin inside it, gone thin and gold as paper. She was looking at Aleaka in that calm, careful way she had when she was thinking something through.

"When you call them," said Hana. "What are you asking them for?"

"To— come," said Aleaka.

"Come where?"

"To me."

"Why?"

Aleaka opened her mouth. And shut it again.

She didn't know. She'd been asking them to come to her. Just to come. To be near her, like Sleet, like the gulls, like the fish every morning at the end of the jetty. Because she liked it. Because it was nice. Because they always had.

*A caller asks.*

She thought about the big pool. The black round hole in the rock, boiling white in the middle. The bare shelves where no birds would land. Nothing moving. Nothing coming up. *You won't see it. Not till it's out.* Thirty-six people on those shelves in three days, with a raft, and Newish counting, and none of them able to see where it was. None of them. Not Hyvred, not Higu, not Ice.

She looked at her hands.

"I'm frightened," she said.

It came out of her very slowly. Stumbling. She wasn't saying it to Hana. She wasn't saying it to anybody. She was saying it to the river, and the stone, and the cloud, and whatever was listening.

"We have to fight the big one," said Aleaka. "In the pool. In three days. And it's the colour of the water, and nobody can see it. And somebody's going to get hurt. Somebody always gets hurt. And I can't— I can't *see*. I can't see where it is." Her voice went. She waited for it to come back. "I need somebody who can. Please. Not to come to *me*. To help. I need help." She stopped. "That's all. That's what I'm asking for."

The river went over the lip beside her.

Nothing happened.

And then, from behind the Great Fall, out of the cloud, out of the white, something small came.

***

It came out of the water.

That was the first thing she saw. It came out of the falling water itself, from behind the curtain, where the fall left the lip. Out of the white, as if the white had made it. Small and dark and round, flying low and fast and straight, the way they did, skimming the surface of the river above the lip. Up to the stone.

And landed in the hollow. Right beside her hand.

A falls-wren.

It was old. She could tell, somehow, though she'd never seen an old one. Its feathers were rough at the edges, and its white bib had gone a bit grey, and one of its feet had a toe missing. And in its left wing, among all the brown, there was one feather that was pure white. White as the fall.

It looked at her. With one bright black eye, and then the other, turning its head.

It bobbed.

She didn't read them. Not then. She didn't even look at them. She was looking at the bird.

"Hello," she whispered.

It bobbed again.

"It came out of the *fall*," said Newish, behind her. In a voice Aleaka had never heard her use. Very small. "Leaka. It came out of the actual *waterfall*."

"They live behind it," said Jared. Quietly. "Behind the water. Oarin said."

"They see under it," said Aleaka.

She didn't know how she knew. She just did. She knew it the way she knew Sleet was cross. They lived behind the fall, in the dark, in the roar, and they walked under the white water along the bottom where nothing else could see. They'd been doing it for ever. They knew every rock under every fall on the whole floor.

They knew where the big one was. They'd always known. That's why they wouldn't land on the shelves.

The old wren hopped, once, out of the hollow, onto the back of her hand.

She couldn't feel it. It was too light. It was like being stood on by a leaf.

"What are you going to call it?" said Hana. Softly.

Aleaka thought about it. She thought about it for a long time, the way she thought about everything. Not Alea. Never Alea. That one was taken. That one was on a shoulder in a kitchen doorway, being *compact*.

"Tuppence," she said.

"*Tuppence*?" said Newish.

"It's small," said Aleaka. "And it's worth a lot."

***

They came down the stair in the dark, with Jared's lamp, and the old wren riding on Aleaka's shoulder the whole way, and Sleet walking so close against her legs that she nearly tripped her on every step.

"She's jealous," said Milli.

"She's not jealous. She's—" Aleaka looked down at Sleet's frosty ruff. "Fine. She's jealous."

And at the last bend, before the stair went down past the big pool, Aleaka stopped. And turned round. And looked back up at the Great Fall, going up into the dark.

It was full of birds.

She hadn't noticed them come. Nobody had. But all along the cliffs on either side of the fall, on every wet ledge and crack and knob of rock, all the way up into the dark, there were falls-wrens. Hundreds. More. Small dark shapes in rows, with their white bibs pale in the starlight. Sitting very still. Not bobbing.

All facing her.

She hadn't called them. She'd called one.

The rest had heard.
