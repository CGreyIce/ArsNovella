Chi had been in the cistern once before, and she'd been frightened the whole time.

Nobody had known. That was the thing about being the calm one. On the hundred and twenty-third day, when they'd carried Thea in out of the bowl with her shoulder laid open and Xola's hands running water into it, Chi had walked behind them with Waddles under one arm and her shield on her back and her face doing exactly what it always did, which was nothing at all. And inside, the whole way, the whole night, she'd been somebody screaming.

She'd said one thing, that day. In the bowl, with nine of them running at a hole shaped like what they wanted. She'd put everything she had into her voice and slowed it right down, the way she'd learned to slow it years ago, in another life, when chat got ugly and somebody had to be the one who didn't.

*Look. At. The. Lantern.*

And they had. She still didn't know if it had been the skill or the voice or just luck. She'd never asked.

Now, before dawn on the five hundred and twentieth day, she came over the last dune with the moon going down behind her and saw the four broken walls of black glass standing up out of the sand like the corners of a burnt house, and her heart did the thing it did on walls. Fast. In her teeth.

She breathed. Slowly. *It's only a cistern.* Waddles, wrapped in a wet towel under her arm, made a small cross noise, because the towel was going warm at the edges and he had opinions about that.

"Nearly there," said Chi. "Breathe."

He didn't breathe any differently. He was a penguin. But she did.

***

It was cool inside. That was the whole point of it. Four walls of old fused glass round a dry stone cistern five metres deep, with a ramp of drifted sand going down one side, and at the bottom, out of the sun, the air sat still and grey and almost cold, like the inside of a cellar. Thirty-six people came down the sand ramp in the last of the dark and found places along the walls, and lay down, and were asleep almost before they'd stopped moving. They'd walked all night. They'd crossed the bowl.

Chi didn't lie down. She stood at the bottom of the ramp with Waddles and looked at the hollow.

It was still there. A long shallow dip in the sand at the far end of the cistern, against the wall, the shape of somebody who'd slept there for weeks. The fire ring of black stones beside it. The onion skins were gone, blown away or taken. But the stains were still there, in the sand at the edge of the hollow. Two of them. One small and old and brown, from a hand. And one bigger, darker, from a shoulder.

*On six, it stays.*

Ice walked past her with the crock in his arms. He walked down the length of the cistern without looking at anybody, and stopped at the edge of the hollow, and looked down at it for a long moment. At the two stains. At the shape in the sand that was his.

Then he knelt down and put the crock in the coolest corner, against the wall, and lay down in the hollow.

He fitted it. Of course he did. It was his. And Thea came down the cistern behind him with the lantern, and put the lantern down by the fire ring, and lay down in the hollow beside him, against his back, with her arm over him. The first time she'd lain in that hollow she'd been smaller than the shape. Chi remembered thinking it. Thea, lying on her side in the sand with her eyes open, looking at the lantern, in a space made for somebody bigger, who wasn't there.

It fitted two now. Just about.

Chi looked away. It seemed rude to watch.

***

Hyvred was standing at the top of the ramp, in the first grey light, looking back east.

Chi went up to her, because she'd be on watch anyway, and because Hyvred had the look she got when she was seeing something and didn't know yet whether to say it.

"What is it?"

Hyvred didn't answer for a moment. Her glasses were pushed up on her head. Her eyes were doing the thing they did now, since floor 1, looking at something that wasn't quite in front of them.

"Gigi's got a new thread," she said.

Chi looked down into the cistern. Gigi was asleep against the north wall, with Bee's head on her shoulder and her crooked green crown slipped down over one ear, and her right hand lying open in her lap. Xola was asleep on her other side. They were still holding hands.

"Every plant on the road on twelve," said Hyvred. "She's got a thread to every one of them. Brown and gold. Like a string of lamps." She lifted one hand and pointed east, back the way they'd come, across the dunes toward the bowl. "And now there's one more. Going back there. It's tiny." She lowered her hand. "It took."

Chi looked east. She couldn't see anything. Just the Glass going from grey to pink, and the rim of the bowl, and the sky.

"Should we tell her?"

"She knows," said Hyvred. "Look at her face."

Chi looked. Gigi was asleep. And her face, which had been tight and straight and chin-up for five hundred and twenty days, was just a face. Somebody asleep, in the cool, with her hand held.

***

Chi took the noon watch.

She sat at the top of the ramp in the strip of shade along the east wall with her shield across her knees and Waddles in a bucket of Xola's water beside her, sulking, and looked out at the Glass in the full white glare, and was frightened of it. Not of anything in it. There wasn't anything in it that could touch them now. They were nearly thirty levels past anything on six. A shardback had come up out of the sand at them in the night, a big one, all edges and glitter, and had taken one look at Hako and gone straight back down again.

It was just the light. It came off everything. You couldn't look at it and you couldn't look away from it, and it pressed on you like a hand, and Chi had never liked being pressed.

She breathed. She was calm at the Glass. After a while, mostly, it worked.

Ice came up the ramp a little after noon with the crock.

He looked at Xola first, asleep at the bottom with her hand still in Gigi's, and clearly decided not to wake her. He knelt in the shade, unscrewed his own water skin, and poured a little over the cloth round the crock, carefully, the way you'd water a plant. Then he lifted the corner and looked inside. Then he sat down next to Chi with the crock in his lap.

"Is it all right?" said Chi.

"It's grey on top." He frowned at it. "That's hungry. I think." He took a little cloth bag out of his pack, flour from Merle's, and fed it a pinch with his fingers, and stirred it with the end of his spoon, and put the cloth back. "It's had a year of tomorrows. It's had four days of me."

"It's doing fine."

"You don't know that."

"No," said Chi. "But you looked like you needed somebody to say it."

He looked at her sideways. Then he laughed, very quietly, so as not to wake anybody.

They sat for a while. The light pressed on everything. Down in the cistern, somebody was snoring; Calvin, by the rhythm. A long way off, out on the Glass, a mirage of a lake with trees round it came and went on the horizon like somebody breathing on a window.

"It was you," said Ice.

"What was?"

"In the bowl. *Look at the lantern.*" He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the white. "I was coming down the north slope. Running. With my eyes on the ground. And I heard it. All the way up there." He said it slowly, the way he said everything now. "It was like you'd stopped time."

"It's a skill," said Chi. "Hush. It slows things."

"It wasn't the skill."

She didn't say anything. Her heart had gone fast again, a little, in her teeth.

"I was terrified," said Chi. "That day. The whole time. In here, after. All night."

"I know."

"You can't have known. Nobody knew."

"It didn't sound like it," said Ice. "That's how I know. It never sounds like it when it works." He turned the crock a little in his lap. "How do you do it?"

Chi thought about it. Nobody had ever asked her. Not in this life, and not really in the other one either.

"You're calm *at* somebody," she said. "You pick somebody who needs it, and you're calm at them. As hard as you can. And they breathe. And then, after a while—" she shrugged "—you are too. It sort of comes back off them. Like a wall throwing the sun back."

Ice was quiet.

"You already know how," said Chi. "You do it at Thea all the time."

"Do I?"

"Badly."

He laughed again, properly this time, and had to put his hand over his mouth, and down in the cistern Thea stirred in the hollow and said "*Ice*," without waking up, and he went quiet at once like a schoolboy.

When he spoke again it was very low.

"I'm frightened," he said. "Of the Spoon."

Chi looked at him.

He'd never said that. Not to her. Not like that, plainly, with his face just being a face. She'd watched him for five hundred and twenty days being the one who didn't, the way she was the one who didn't, and she'd thought, more than once, that he was better at it than she was. That he'd had longer to practise.

"Of the fever?"

"Of being late." He swallowed. "Of being on time, and it not mattering. Of getting there with all of it, the remedy and the starter and everybody, and sitting on the stool, and it still—" He stopped. "I'm telling you. I said I'd say. On fourteen. When it turns."

"Has it turned?"

"No. It's just far off. Ninety days. Less." He breathed out. "Eighty-nine."

Chi looked out at the Glass for a long moment.

"Then I'll do it at you," she said. "At the Spoon. Every night, if you want. I'll sit on the other side of the room and be calm at you."

He turned and looked at her properly.

"Please," said Ice.

Chi nodded. And thought, a little hysterically, that everybody on this floor was getting *please* from him. Xola every hour. Now her. Five hundred days of a man who'd never asked for anything, and on six he'd started asking for everything, and saying please.

She thought it was probably the best thing she'd seen on the Glass.

***

They walked to Deepwell that night.

You didn't see it until you were nearly on top of it. Chi had forgotten that. The dunes just ended, and in front of you in the moonlight there was a hole in the world, round, two hundred metres across, going down. Old dark glass walls, fused smooth, with houses cut into them in rings all the way down, and ropes and pulleys and baskets strung across between the rings like the web of something enormous and careful, and lamps in the windows, and far, far down at the bottom, three hundred metres down, the slow creak of the great stone wheel and the bucket chain going up and down out of the black water.

Ostrel was at his booth at the top of the stair down.

He was exactly the same. Big and slow and quiet, with a face like a saddle and a ledger open on the ledge in front of him and a ladle on his belt. He looked up when the lantern came over the last dune. And then he watched them come. All of them. One after another, out of the dark, in a long line, at night, the way you were supposed to.

He didn't say anything until the last of them was standing in front of the booth. Gabe. He was always last, going down.

"Thirty-six," said Ostrel.

"Thirty-six," said Drew.

Ostrel looked at them a while longer. Then he turned the ledger round on the ledge, so it faced them, and turned the pages back. A long way back. Past a lot of small careful entries in a big slow hand. And put one thick finger on a line.

Chi read it upside down. She'd got good at that, on thirteen.

*Paid. Man in a white helm. Seventy cups. Said thirty-five were coming. Said they were his.*

Ice came forward through the line with the crock in his arms.

He didn't have the helm on. He didn't wear it any more. It lived in his pack, and came out as a sunshade or a bucket or, once, on twelve, to keep the glare off a very small plant. He stood in front of the booth with his face bare in the lamplight and looked down at the line in the ledger.

"That was me," he said.

Ostrel looked at him. A long, slow look, up and down, the way he'd look at a skin to see if it leaked.

"You walked in alone," said Ostrel. "In the day."

"Yes."

"I said, most of 'em get carried."

"I got carried," said Ice.

Ostrel looked at him for a while longer. Then he looked past him, at the thirty-five standing in the dark behind, and at Thea with the lantern beside him, and at the hand she had in his.

"Good," said Ostrel.

He turned the ledger back round and picked up his pen.

Dennis was already at the ledge with the purse out. He'd been waiting for this, Chi realised. Since Evermorrow. Since the cheesemonger.

"Thirty-six cups," said Dennis. "Two coppers each. Paid now. No tab." He counted them out onto the ledge, one at a time, in rows. "And seventy more."

Ostrel's pen stopped.

"For whoever comes up next," said Dennis. "In the day. Alone. Not knowing." He put down the last copper. "The first one's free. That's what you say. So that's somebody's second."

Ostrel looked at the coins for a long moment. Then he wrote, in his big slow hand, under a lot of other things:

*Paid. Seventy cups. For whoever comes up next.*

"By who?" said Ostrel. It was what they'd asked him, the first time. He held the pen and waited.

Dennis looked round at all of them. At the long line in the moonlight at the top of the pit.

"Thirty-six," said Dennis.

Ostrel wrote it down.

***

Waddles's bucket was still there.

Chi didn't believe it until she saw it. Right at the bottom of the pit, beside the great stone wheel, in the cool and the dark, where the black water came up the chain. The same wooden bucket they'd left him in on the hundred and twenty-second day, when ten of them had gone east after Ice and somebody had to keep a penguin from cooking. It had been painted since. Blue, with white spots, badly. And somebody had cut a little sign into the rim with a knife. Chi couldn't read it; Deepwell had its own letters. But she didn't need to.

There were children round it. Eight or nine of them, who'd come down the rings in the dark at the sound of thirty-six people on the stairs, and stood round the bucket in their nightclothes staring at the thing in Chi's arms.

Waddles saw the bucket.

He made a noise Chi had never heard him make in five hundred and twenty days. Not cross. Not shrieking. A sort of long, low, rising sound, like a kettle that had just heard its name. And he went out of her arms and across the stone and up and over the rim of the bucket in one undignified scramble and down into the black cold water, and sat there, up to his neck, with his eyes shut.

The children screamed with joy.

So, nearly, did Chi. She didn't. She was the calm one. She stood by the wheel with her empty arms and her face doing nothing at all, and inside she was somebody laughing until she cried.

"He's been gone a whole year," said the smallest girl, accusingly, to Chi.

"I know," said Chi. "I'm sorry. He was busy."

"Busy *where*?"

Chi thought about it. The Bastion, and the children on the fourth wall. The Sea, and the salt. Tell, the Countinghouse step, the long white field on fourteen. All the places Waddles had sulked his way up through.

"Up," said Chi. "Then down."

The girl considered this, and looked at Waddles in his bucket with his eyes shut, and seemed to decide it was a fair answer.

***

They took the round room on the third ring again.

Ostrel's wife rented it to them. Big and round, cut into the glass with a low curved ceiling, and a row of little windows looking out across the pit. Chi had slept in it for thirty days, last time. All thirty-five of them had. And she realised, standing in the doorway with her pack, that in thirty days not one of them had ever asked Ostrel's wife her name.

So she did.

"Imra," said Ostrel's wife. A small brown woman, quick where Ostrel was slow, with sand under her nails and a laugh that went up at the end. She looked at Chi's face and laughed. "Don't look like that. You were all very busy. Running off into the Glass after people."

"We should have asked."

"You're asking now," said Imra. "That's what counts. Most don't come back to."

The room was the same. The cushions were the same. Thea walked straight across it to the place by the third window where she'd lain for five days with a scar coming up across her shoulder, and sat down there with the lantern, and put her hand on the cushion, and didn't say anything. Xola sat down beside her. Gigi, after a moment, sat down on her other side.

Ice didn't come in.

Chi found him outside, a little way along the third ring. He was standing in front of another door. Smaller. Older. With a round window beside it, looking out across the pit. He had his hand on the frame. Just resting there. The way Xav said he'd stood outside the bakery in Evermorrow, the first time, with his hand on the blue door.

He didn't open it. There was somebody living in it now; there was a pot on the step and a shirt drying on a line across the window. He just stood there.

Chi didn't ask. She stood a little way off, the way you do.

The sun was going down. The light came in across the top of the pit, sideways, gold, and lit up the rings on the far side, and the ropes, and the baskets, and the little round windows all the way down. And Ice said, without turning round, "They'll sing in a minute."

"Who will?"

He nodded across the pit. At a window on the far side of the third ring, directly opposite, a round one like this one, with a lamp just lit inside it and shapes moving.

"Them," said Ice.

Chi looked at the window. Then at him. Then at the window again.

And across the pit, as the last of the light went off the top ring, somebody inside the round window started singing. One voice, a woman's, low. Then a second on top of it, a man's. Then a third, a child's, very high and very sure. In harmony. A slow old tune that went up and came down again like somebody walking up a hill and back, and it filled the whole pit, ring by ring, all the way down to the black water at the bottom, where the wheel had stopped creaking to listen.

Chi stood on the third ring and heard it and didn't say anything at all.

She'd been in Deepwell thirty days, last time. She'd never once heard them sing. She'd have remembered. But at dusk, on six, you were always on the stair with your pack on, going out.

*They'll sing in a minute.*

She put it with the other things. She didn't ask. Nobody ever asked.

Behind her, the door of the round room had opened. Thea was standing in it with the lantern, listening, with her face gone very soft. Then she opened her mouth and sang back.

Not the same tune. She didn't know it. Just a line of something, quiet, under theirs, the way you'd hum along with a song you'd half heard in a shop. And across the pit, at the round window, the three voices faltered for a second, surprised. And then went on, and made room.

Chi had a voice too. She'd used it for years for being calm at people. She hadn't sung with it since the white room.

She found the note under Thea's. It wasn't hard. It was just there.

And then there were five voices in the pit, two on one side and three on the other, and nobody in Deepwell went indoors until it was dark.

***

Imra brought the onions on the second evening.

A basket of them, little bitter desert onions no bigger than a thumb, still with the wet sand on them from the trays in the shade at the bottom of the pit. She put the basket down on the ledge outside the round room, where Ice was sitting with the crock, and sat down beside him, and looked at him sideways.

"There was a man in a white helm," said Imra, "sat right there on that ledge last year, and peeled a dozen of mine."

Ice didn't say anything.

"Properly," said Imra. "Skin off in one curl. The paper layer, then the tough one under, all the way round without breaking. I'd never seen anybody bother. You don't, with these. They're too small. You just take your knife to them." She picked one up and turned it in her fingers. "I've done mine properly since. Every one. So has my sister. So has half the third ring, now. It's catching."

"That was me," said Ice.

"I know," said Imra. "I've got eyes." She put the onion in his hand. "Well? Go on."

So he did. And because Chi was sitting on the ledge a little way off with Waddles's empty towel, mending it, Imra gave her one too.

Chi was terrible at it.

She knew she would be. She'd always been terrible at anything with small fiddly bits. She got her thumbnail under the papery skin and pulled and it came off in four pieces, and then the tough layer under it tore halfway round and stuck, and she ended up with a ragged half-naked onion and a little heap of brown flakes in her lap.

Ice looked at it.

"Again," he said.

He gave her another one. He didn't take hers away. He didn't take the knife out of her hand or show her by doing it for her. He just put his own onion down, and turned so he was facing her on the ledge, and held his hands up where she could see them.

"Here. Your thumb goes here. Under the root. Not the top. Everybody starts at the top." He did it, slowly, on nothing, on the air. "Then you don't pull. You turn it. You turn the onion and let the skin come. It wants to come off in one. You just have to let it."

She tried. It tore.

"Again," said Ice.

It tore.

"Again."

On the fifth one it didn't. The paper layer came off in one long brown curl, and under it the tough layer, and under that the onion, small and white and shining and whole. Chi held it up in the last of the light and looked at it.

"There," said Ice.

And Chi sat on the ledge with an onion in one hand and a curl of skin in the other and realised what he'd been doing. The whole time. *Again. Again.* Slowly. Not a flicker. As if it didn't matter at all how many it took. As if there were all the time in the world.

He'd been calm at her.

He'd been doing it at her for five onions, and she'd breathed, and her hands had stopped being in a hurry, and it had come back off her like the sun off a wall.

"Who taught you?" said Chi.

"Mother Crane." He picked up his own onion again. "In the back of the Spoon. At dawn. With her hands over mine." He turned it, and the skin came off in one. "*You do it properly, love, or you don't do it.*"

Imra laughed, the laugh that went up at the end. "Well, she's right."

"She's always right," said Ice. "Don't tell her."

When the basket was done, Imra went back down the rings and came up again with a sack. A proper sack, heavy, tied at the neck. Unpeeled ones, dry, the kind that would keep.

"For whoever the bread's for," said Imra, and nodded at the crock.

Ice looked at the sack. He didn't take it straight away.

"She's on floor 1," he said. "She taught me. She's going to be ill this winter, and we're going down to sit with her."

Imra looked at him for a long moment. Then she put the sack in his arms, on top of the crock, and patted it.

"Then take her some onions," she said. "And peel them properly. And tell her a woman at the bottom of a well on six says thank you for the trick."

He kept the skins. All of them, from the basket. Chi watched him gather them up off the ledge, every brown curl, and fold them into a square of cloth, and put the cloth in his pack next to the broth pot. He didn't say what for.

Chi thought she knew.

***

Drew gave them four days in Deepwell, and on the evening of the fourth they went west.

The line to the door ran straight across the Glass, the way it always had, because there was nowhere else for it to run. From Deepwell out across the dunes, west, toward the speck of rock on the far edge where a stone arch stood over a stair. And along it, every few hours, the rocks. Black glass standing up out of the sand like broken teeth, throwing long moon-shadows, and the line went from one to the next like a string between beads.

They came to the first rock a little after midnight.

Chi saw it first. She was near the front, behind the lantern, with Waddles under her arm in a fresh wet towel and Higu beside her, and she saw it because she was looking for it. On the shady side of the rock, at head height, scratched into the black glass with the point of a sword, an arrow. Pointing down.

The whole line stopped.

Ice came forward from the middle, with the crock and the sack, and Thea with him. He stood in front of the arrow. He looked at it the way he'd looked at the hollow in the cistern. Then he put the crock down in the sand, and the sack on top of it, and knelt, and started to dig with his hands at the foot of the rock, on the shady side, where the sand was cool.

Chi watched him. Everybody watched him. Thirty-five people standing in the moonlight on the Glass watching a man dig.

He stopped. His hands had hit something.

He lifted it out. A water skin. Full. Heavy, sweating in the cool, the leather dark with it. And then another, and another. Three skins, under the arrow, where he'd buried three skins before the thirty-five ever came up the stair.

They were new skins. Chi could see that even from where she was. Not his. Different leather, different stitching, a Deepwell knot at the neck.

And under the cork of each one, tied round the neck, a thin brown curl of onion skin. Like a ribbon on a present.

Ice knelt in the sand with a full water skin in his hands and didn't move.

"They keep them," said Higu, quietly, beside Chi. She'd worked it out before anyone. She always did. "Dov. Or Imra's sister. Somebody walks out along the line every month and fills them." She looked at the onion skin. "And ties those on. Because that's how it's done."

"Because that's how it's done," said Chi.

Ice looked up at Thea. His face was doing something Chi had only seen on a few faces in her life. On chat, once, years ago, on a bad night, when somebody had said they were going to stop and then a hundred people had typed *don't*. Like somebody who'd been shouting down a well for a year, and had just heard it answer.

"I didn't think anybody would keep them," said Ice.

Thea put the lantern down on the sand beside him and crouched, and put her hand on the back of his neck.

"Somebody always does," said Thea.

Higu sat down on the sand with the rock on her knees, and under *NOW*, by moonlight, she wrote:

*THE CACHES ARE KEPT.*
*ONION SKINS ON THE CORKS.*

She didn't underline it. She didn't need to.

Ice put the three skins back in the hole, exactly as he'd found them, and covered them, and smoothed the sand. They didn't drink from them. Nobody suggested it. They had Xola. Those were for somebody else.

***

There were four caches. They were all kept. At every rock the line stopped, and Ice knelt and dug, and lifted out three full skins with onion skins tied under the corks, and looked at them, and put them back. By the third he'd stopped looking surprised. By the fourth he was smiling the whole time, a small, steady, private smile, as if somebody had told him a joke a year ago and he'd only just got it.

The last cache was an hour from the door. They reached it as the sky started going grey in the east behind them.

Drew stopped the line. "Skins," he said. "Spares. Full ones. Everybody."

Nobody asked why. Thirty-six people unslung their spare water skins, the ones Xola had been filling every hour all the way across the Glass, and knelt round the rock, and dug the hole wider and deeper in the cool sand on the shady side, and laid them in. Thirty-six skins. On top of the three that were already there.

Then Ice took the square of cloth out of his pack, and unfolded it, and held it out.

Onion skins. From Imra's basket on the third ring. Thin brown curls, every one in a single piece, peeled properly.

Everybody took one. Calvin, Rachael with her gloves on, Dennis, Hako, Bee, Yan, Wendy with her quick fingers, Hana and Milli, Newish, all of them. They tied them under the corks of their own skins, round the neck, in the grey light, kneeling in the sand. Tiger tied his with the knot Burr had taught him. Gigi tied hers with Xola holding the skin. Chi tied hers, carefully, with Waddles watching from under her arm, and it held.

Ice tied his last. Then he looked at the hole full of skins, thirty-nine of them, every one with a little brown ribbon on it.

"Somebody will come up," he said. "In the day. Not knowing."

"And find thirty-nine," said Dennis. "With ribbons on."

"And know it's safe," said Thea.

They covered it over. Ice smoothed the sand. And then, after a moment, he stood up and took his sword out, and above his old arrow, on the black glass, at head height, he scratched another one. Pointing down. Next to the first.

Two arrows.

***

The door was where it had always been. A stone arch on a speck of rock at the western edge of the Glass, with the Glass going white behind it in the first sun, and a stair going down into the dark. Two hundred and eighty steps. Floor 5 at the bottom. The Spindle. Wind, and sky, and bridges.

Tiger put his hand flat on the door, and waited.

Higu sat down on the rock and took out the rock, and looked at the column under *NOW*, all of it, the whole floor. Then she put it away without writing anything else.

"Six is done," she said.

Chi stood near the front with Waddles under her arm. He'd cried when they took him out of the bucket. Not a cross noise. A real one. The children had cried too, and the smallest girl had told Chi, very seriously, that he could come back any time and the bucket would be there, and Chi had believed her.

Ice came up beside her. Thea was on his other side, with the lantern. The crock in his arms, the sack of onions on his back, Hild's plait in his vest. The broth pot in his pack, and a square of cloth, empty now.

"Ninety—" he started.

"Eighty-four," said Chi.

He stopped. He looked at her.

"Eighty-four days," said Ice.

He didn't say it like a man counting down to something. He just said it. Like the number of steps on a stair. Chi looked at his face and saw that he was frightened, and that he was letting her see, and that he wasn't going to stop being frightened for eighty-four days, and that that was allowed.

"Breathe," said Chi. Slowly. At him. As hard as she could.

He did.

And then, a moment later, so did she. It came back off him like the sun off a wall.

Tiger opened the door. Newish counted. Thea sang. And they went down out of the white into the cool dark, all thirty-six of them, two hundred and eighty steps toward the wind.
