On sixteen, for the first time in his life, John was the middle of something.

He didn't like it.

He'd been off to one side since he was small. At the end of tables. In the corners of rooms. On a beanbag at the edge of a screen in a little flat in Singapore, a shoulder and half a face and a voice saying *he's lying, by the way* while Dec talked in the middle with the camera on him. Best seat in the house. You could see everything from the side. Nobody looked at you. You could look at all of them.

And then a white room, and a small white light coming out of his palm on the first day, and *Mote*. Send a little light ahead. See what it sees. The corner of a room, in the air. The edge of a screen, anywhere he liked. He'd thought it was the best thing that had ever happened to him, and for two years it had been.

On sixteen everybody looked at his motes.

Because a mote was a light. Small, pale, on its own in the dark. Exactly the size and colour of a stray. And on a floor where everything that hunted, hunted lights, a thing that could make a light out of nothing and send it anywhere was suddenly the most important thing anybody had.

He'd felt it on the terrace. When the moths came up the slope following his seven motes and Xav had looked at him, afterwards, for a long time, and said *I know*, twice. He'd felt it at the bottom of the bowl, when his last mote went out over the edge and every light under the great wings turned toward it, all at once, and pressed. He'd felt it all the way back up, three falls, with everybody walking a little closer to him than they used to.

And he'd felt it most of all in the forge, on the first fall after they got back. With everybody crammed in and out of it and up the ladders. When Xav stood up off his crate and said:

"It comes up for light. It'll come up for the lantern. Sooner or later, the lantern's going to have to be open, and it's going to come. Here. To the town." He looked at Corwen. "It ate a street, last time."

Corwen didn't say anything.

"So it doesn't come here," said Xav. "It comes somewhere else. Somewhere we choose. Away from the roots. And when it comes, we're there, and we open it." He looked at Rui Xiang. "They want out. Every light it ever took. They're in there asking." He looked back at Corwen. "We let them out."

"How do you make it go somewhere else?" said Corwen.

And Xav, very slowly, turned his head, and looked at John.

So did everybody.

John sat very still in the corner of the forge, off to one side, where he'd always sat, with forty pairs of eyes on him and nowhere at all to look.

"It's a lot bigger than a moth," he said finally. Quietly.

"I know."

"I don't know if it'd follow one. A mote. I don't know if it'd even see one. It's—" He stopped. "It wanted the lantern. Not just light. *That* light. Rui Xiang saw it. It looked straight up the Hollow at Thea from the bottom of the bowl."

"I know," said Xav. "I don't know either. I'm asking if you'll find out."

John looked at the floor.

Across the forge, against the wall, Dec was sitting with Tuli's pole across his knees and Gabe's terrible lantern on his belt. Empty. John had been watching him for two years, from the side, and he'd got good at it. And he saw Dec look at him, and then look away, carefully, the way you look away from somebody being asked something hard so they don't have to answer it in front of you.

Nobody had ever looked away for John before. He'd always been the one looking away.

"Okay," said John. "I'll find out."

***

He practised at the edge of the town.

Every fall, for six falls, on a root at the bottom of the far ladders, where the walkways ended and the dark began. Gabe sat with him, mostly. Off to one side of John, which John found very funny and never said. With Hearthwall turned inward against his chest so the warmth went into him and not out into the dark.

John sent motes.

Further than he ever had. Out past the last of the lanterns, past the last of the white trees he could see, out into the dark, one at a time. Five hundred paces. A thousand. He'd never sent one a thousand paces in his life. On floor 2 they'd thinned out two-thirds of the way up the pass. Here they went and went, as if the dark were a kind of water they could swim in, and he went with them, behind his half-shut eyes, out over the pale moss and the strays and the dark.

And the moths came.

Every time. That was the part he hadn't expected. Out in the dark, every mote he sent past about six hundred paces, sooner or later, a moth found it. Grey and silent, out of nowhere, tongue uncoiling. And then the pinch. Like wet fingers on a candle. And the mote was gone, and he was sitting on a root at the bottom of a ladder with his hand on his chest, breathing.

"How many's that?" said Gabe.

"Nineteen."

"Mate."

"I'm fine."

"You said that at eleven."

"I was fine at eleven." He opened his eyes. Gabe was looking at him. Not off to one side. Straight at him. "They follow them," said John. "Every one. They don't care about anything else out there. The strays. The trees. They come straight to the mote." He looked out at the dark. "If I could make one go somewhere, and keep going, and not come back—"

"They'd follow it away."

"They'd follow it away."

"So do that."

"I can't." John looked at his hand. "They're mine. They come back. That's what they do. I send them, and I see what they see, and when I'm done they come home, or they go out. There's no—" He stopped. He didn't know how to say it. "There's no third thing. There's no *just going*. They're a bit of me. You can't send a bit of you away and not have it be you."

Gabe was quiet for a while.

"Does it hurt?" said Gabe. "When they go out."

"Thea asked me that. On thirteen." John looked at the dark. "Nobody'd ever asked me that before. Two years."

"I'm asking."

"Yeah. A bit. Like pinching a candle."

Gabe looked at him for a long time. Then he put his big hand on the back of John's neck, briefly, the way he did to Dec, and took it away again.

"Stop at twenty," said Gabe. "That's an order. I'm the back. I give orders."

"Xav gives orders."

"Xav waits. I give orders. Twenty."

John sent the twentieth. It went out a thousand paces and a moth found it and it went out and he felt the pinch and sat there with his hand on his chest, and Gabe sat beside him with his shield, and they didn't say anything else for a while.

***

The baby came on the third fall.

He didn't know there was going to be a baby. Nobody had told them; Lanthorn didn't tell you things, you found out. He was on his root at the bottom of the far ladders with Gabe, sending motes, and there was a sound from up in the roots above them that wasn't anything he'd heard in Lanthorn before. Thin. High. Angry.

A baby crying.

The whole town stopped. He felt it. The way it had stopped on the night of the moths, except the other way round. Two hundred people on walkways and in doorways and at the pool, all stopping, all turning toward the sound. And then, all over the roots, faintly, the glims in their lanterns humming louder. Just for a moment. The way people murmur when a door opens on something they've been waiting for.

Then everybody went back to what they were doing.

But they kept looking. Up at the root the sound came from. A small hollow on the far side of the pool, near Corwen's forge, with a round window and a door with a lantern hanging by it. Empty.

"Tegan's," said Isel.

She was on the walkway above him. She'd come out of nowhere, the way she did, with Edda's old lantern in her hands. She was looking up at the root. Her face was very flat.

"She's had it," said Isel. "The baby."

"That's good," said John. "Isn't it?"

Isel didn't answer. She looked out at the dark. At the edge of the town, past the lanterns, where the strays were.

Or where the strays had been.

He looked too. And understood, all at once, what she was looking at.

There weren't any.

Not near. Not round the edge of Lanthorn, where they'd drifted in a loose ring when the thirty-six first came, where the goat's ring had been on the night of the moths. Since the eyes opened at the bottom of the Hollow the moths had come every night. A few, a dozen, more. And every night the strays round the town had got fewer. Eaten, or gone. Down the slope, out into the dark, far off, away from wherever the moths were coming to. There was one, still. The stair one. The goat's. It hung by Aleaka's root, always, with the goat. But past that, out where they used to be: nothing. Dark.

"It has to come," said Isel. Very quietly. "In a day. Or three. Or a week. One comes. It always comes."

"And if it doesn't?"

Isel turned Edda's lantern in her hands.

"Gran didn't say," said Isel. "She started to say. On the first day. *A child born with no glim.* And then she stopped."

***

It didn't come.

Not on the third fall, or the fourth, or the fifth.

Tegan sat in the door of her root-hollow with the baby in her arms and an empty lantern hanging by the door. Everybody went past. Everybody looked. Nobody said anything. John watched her from his root at the bottom of the far ladders, between motes, off to one side, the way he watched everything.

She was young. Younger than Isel's mother would have been, he thought. Thin and pale and big-eyed like all of them, with her hair cut short, and the baby a small white shape in a grey blanket. It cried less on the fourth fall. On the fifth it hardly cried at all.

"It's gone quiet," said Isel. On the walkway above him. She came every fall now and sat above him while he worked, with her legs hanging over the edge and Edda's lantern in her lap. "That's what happens. Gran said. I remember now. A child with no glim goes quiet."

"And then?"

Isel didn't answer.

He sent the next mote out a thousand paces, into the dark, and a moth found it, and he sat with his hand on his chest.

He didn't count any more. Gabe counted. Gabe had made it twenty a fall, and John had stopped arguing, because Gabe was right and because he was so tired by twenty that he couldn't have sent another anyway.

And they still came back, or went out. Every one.

No third thing.

***

The moths came on the fifth night. Not many. A dozen.

He was on his root when the bells went. *Tinktinktinktink*, all over the town, quick and quiet, and the glims going into their lanterns, and the town going from lit to lace. He was up, and Gabe was up, with Hearthwall coming round off his chest onto his arm.

They came from the far side. Over the pool. Out of the dark past Corwen's forge, low and silent, wings open. And they went the way they always went: to the brightest thing.

Tegan's door.

He saw it before anybody. He had a mote out, a short one, over the pool, and he saw it through the mote's eye and through his own at once: twelve grey-brown shapes coming over the black water toward a little root-hollow with a round window, and in the door, a girl with a baby, and by the door, an empty lantern.

The baby didn't have a glim. The baby didn't have anything for them.

But Tegan did. At her shoulder. Her own glim, in the open, because she'd been sitting in the door so long with the baby she'd forgotten to put it in. Humming. The brightest thing on that side of the pool.

And the baby was in her arms.

He didn't think.

He didn't send one mote. He sent all of them. Everything he had. He'd never done it before, never in two years; he'd always sent them one at a time, carefully, a little light ahead. He sent them all at once, out of both hands, a spray of them, eight, nine, ten, small pale lights, flung out over the black pool toward the moths like somebody throwing a handful of seed.

And they turned.

All twelve. Away from Tegan's door. Toward the lights. Toward ten pale points in the dark over the water, brighter than a glim in the open, closer.

"*Away*," said John. Through his teeth. To the motes. "Go *away*. Go away from her, go—"

They went. Out over the pool, away from Tegan's root, away from the roots, toward the far side, toward the dark. And the moths went after them. Tongues out. And he felt the first one go. The pinch. And the second. And he kept them going, all the rest, further, out past the edge of the town, into the dark, the moths behind them.

Four. Five. Six.

He was on his knees on the walkway. He didn't remember kneeling. Gabe was over him, with the shield up over both of them, though nothing was coming at them, nothing at all, the moths were all going the other way.

Seven. Eight.

Out in the dark. Far off. A thousand paces. More.

Nine.

Ten.

And then nothing. All gone. Out. He knelt on the walkway with both hands flat on his chest and couldn't breathe for a moment, as if somebody had pinched out every candle in a room at once.

Out in the dark, beyond the edge of Lanthorn, twelve moths hung where his last mote had gone out. Turning. Looking for the point. A long way from Tegan's door.

They didn't come back.

He heard Isel, above him, very quietly, say something he didn't catch. He heard Tegan, across the pool, in her door, start to cry. Not loudly. The way you cry when the thing you were frightened of goes past.

"I don't know."

He sat on the root and looked at the window and didn't know. He'd never not known with motes. They'd been the easiest thing in his life. A little light, out of his palm, anywhere he liked. And now the window wanted him to make one that wasn't his, and send it somewhere he couldn't see, and never find out where it went.

*Do not watch it.*

He'd watched everything. His whole life. From the side. That was the whole point of him.

"Easy," said Gabe. "Take the easy one. You've done the easy one. You did it tonight, you sent ten of them a thousand—"

"No."

Gabe looked at him.

"Hard," said John. He didn't know he was going to say it until he had. "It's always hard. Isn't it. For all of you. You always take the hard one." He looked at his hands. "Dec takes the hard one. Every time. Rui Xiang did. On the lip. He said."

"You don't have to do it because he—"

"I'm not." He looked up. "I'm doing it because of her."

He nodded across the pool. At Tegan's door. At the girl sitting in it, with the baby quiet in her arms, and her own glim at her shoulder, put back out in the open now that the moths were gone, so the baby could see a light. And by the door, the lantern. Empty.

Gabe looked at it for a long time.

"Yeah," said Gabe. "Okay."

And then, after a moment, as if it had just occurred to him: "Mine came up tonight too."

John turned his head.

Gabe was looking at the corner of his own eye, where the windows opened. His face was doing a careful thing.

"Seventy," said Gabe. "On the walkway. When I was over you with the shield. Something must have come close. I didn't even see it." He was quiet. "There's a window."

"What's the hard one?"

Gabe didn't answer for a long time. Then he read it out. Slowly. In his flat big voice. The way he'd read the names on the wall on ten.

"*Hearthkeeper.*" He stopped. "*Cover the one beside you. When they open the light. Do not step in front.*"

Nobody said anything.

John looked across the pool. At the far root where Thea slept. Where the bundle was.

*When they open the light.*

"You're going to take it," said John.

"Yeah." Gabe didn't look away from the window. "Not tonight. It's not tonight. It says *when*." He shut it. John saw him do it: his eyes moving, the window going. "I know when."

He looked across the pool at the same root John was looking at.

Then he got up, and put his hand on the back of John's neck again, and left it there.

"Go on, then," said Gabe. "Far Light. I'll be off to one side."

***

He went to Tegan's door.

He didn't know why. He didn't know what he was going to do. He knew he couldn't do it on the far root, off to one side, with the whole dark in front of him and nobody to do it for. *Make a light that is not yours.* He didn't know what that meant. He thought maybe he'd find out if he was near the thing it was for.

Tegan looked up when he came along the walkway.

She didn't know him. She knew who he was, everybody did, the ones from below, the one who made lights. She'd seen him on his root every fall with the big one beside him. But she'd never spoken to him. She looked at him now with her big dark eyes, wet, and the baby in her arms, and didn't say anything.

"Can I sit?" said John.

Tegan looked at him a long time. Then she moved over, a little, on the step of her door.

He sat. Off to one side. On the end of the step.

The baby was very small. Smaller than he'd thought. A white face in a grey blanket, eyes shut, not crying. Breathing. Quietly. Too quietly, he thought. He didn't know anything about babies. He knew that was too quiet.

The empty lantern hung by the door, just above his head. Small. Very fine. The kind Nesta made.

"You made the lights," said Tegan. Very softly. "Over the pool. That took them away."

"Yeah."

"Thank you."

He didn't say anything. He looked at the baby.

"She hasn't got a name," said Tegan. "You don't. Not till the glim comes. Because—" She stopped. "You just don't."

"I know," said John. "Isel told me."

Tegan looked down at the baby. "Five falls," she said. "Six, nearly. It always comes. Always. In my mother's time there were so many you had to shut the window or three would come and fight over the cradle." She almost laughed. "My mother said that. Three. Fighting."

"They're out there," said John. "I've seen them. Further down. Past the split tree. Past the first village. There's some. Not many. Hiding." He looked at the dark. "They won't come up. The moths are between."

Tegan didn't say anything. She held the baby.

He held out his hand. Palm up. Over his own knee, off to one side, away from her.

He made a mote.

It came up out of his palm the way they always did. Small, white, pale. A little bit of himself. He felt it, the way he always felt them: a thread, from his chest to the light, thin and warm, so that wherever it went he went too, and saw what it saw, and it came back to him or went out.

*Make a light that is not yours.*

He looked at the thread.

He'd never looked at it before. You didn't. It was just there, the way your own arm was just there. He looked at it now, with his eyes half shut, sitting on a step in the dark beside a girl with a baby who was too quiet. A thread, from his chest to the light. Warm. His.

*Let it go.*

He'd never once let go of anything. That was what off to one side meant. You never had to. You were never holding anything; you were just watching. And the motes always came back, or went out, and either way he'd seen everything, and either way they'd been his.

*Do not watch it.*

He thought about Dec.

He didn't know why. He thought about Dec against the forge wall with the pole across his knees, saying *off you go. Come back and tell me what's there.* He thought about Dec at the top of the path, four falls, waiting, not watching. Not able to. He thought about Dec on the jam, with everything he'd ever held, and letting it go thirty-five ways, and Gabe shouting.

He'd watched Dec for two years from the side. More. On a beanbag in Singapore, a long time ago, he'd watched Dec talk for three hours in the middle of a screen and never once look at himself. *He's always looking at one of us. Making sure. Counting.* He'd written that on a slate in Tell.

And on the jam Dec had stopped counting. For a bit. And asked. And let them have it.

John looked at the thread.

*It's not mine*, he thought. *It's hers.* He looked at the baby. *It was always going to be hers. I'm just carrying it as far as the door.*

He let go.

***

It didn't hurt.

That was the first thing. He'd braced for it. The pinch, the wet fingers. It wasn't that. It was the opposite. It was like putting something heavy down that you'd forgotten you were holding. The thread went out of his chest, slowly, all the way, the way a rope runs out through your hands when you've decided to let it go and don't grip.

And then it wasn't there.

The light was. Above his palm. Small, white, pale. Exactly the same.

But he couldn't see what it saw.

He sat on the step and looked at it with his own eyes. Just his eyes. Like anybody. A little light, in the air, above his hand. He didn't know what it was looking at. He didn't know if it was looking at anything. It wasn't his.

Tegan was staring at it.

"Is that—" she said.

"I don't know," said John. "I don't know what it is." His voice wasn't steady. "It's not mine any more."

The light hung over his palm for a moment.

Then it moved.

Not toward the baby. He'd thought, if it went anywhere, it'd go there. It didn't. It went up, slowly, off his hand, and turned in the air the way strays turned. And drifted. Away from the step. Away from the door. Along the walkway, past the empty lantern, out past the edge of the roots, toward the dark.

Down the slope. Out of the town.

*Do not watch it.*

He turned his head. On purpose. Away from it. He looked at the baby instead.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever done. He'd watched everything. He'd watched every single thing for two years, from the side, the best seat in the house. And now a light that had been his a minute ago was going out into the dark where the moths were, and he didn't know where, and he wasn't going to know.

He looked at the baby's face in the grey blanket. And kept looking.

Behind him, out in the dark, a light he couldn't see went down the slope toward a split tree a long way off, and he let it.

***

He didn't know how long.

Drips. He counted, the way everybody counted now. One. Two. Isel's voice, small and flat, from the pool, saying the number. Tegan beside him, rocking the baby, not saying anything. Gabe, somewhere behind them on the walkway, off to one side, not saying anything either.

He didn't look at the dark. He looked at the baby. At Tegan's hands on the blanket. At the empty lantern above the door. At his own hands in his lap.

*Tonight*, the window had said.

Three drips. Four.

He thought, *it's gone. A moth's had it. It went down the slope and a moth found it and it went out, and I didn't even feel it, because it wasn't mine. That's what not yours means. You don't even get the pinch.*

He thought, *I'll never know.*

He kept looking at the baby.

*Plink.* Five.

"*John*," said Isel.

She was on the walkway behind him. He hadn't heard her come. Her voice was very strange. Not flat. Not small.

"Don't," said John. "I can't. I'm not allowed to—"

"*Look*," said Isel.

He didn't. He looked at the baby. "I can't, Isel. The window. *Do not watch it.*"

He turned round.

Out in the dark, beyond the edge of the roots, at the bottom of the slope where the path came up out of the white trees, there were lights.

Coming up.

Not one. A line of them. Small and pale and far apart, the way strays always were, drifting up the path out of the dark between the trunks, one behind another, slowly. Five. Eight. Twelve. More behind those, further down, he could see them coming, a long thin loose line of pale lights stretching away down the slope into the dark as far as he could see, toward the split tree. Toward the first village. Toward wherever they'd been hiding.

Following something.

He couldn't see what. He looked for it. He couldn't help it. A light in front of them, leading. A white one, small, a little brighter. He couldn't find it. There were too many, and they were all the same, and he didn't know which one had been his. He wasn't supposed to.

They came up the path, into the light of the lanterns. Into the town. Drifting along the walkways. Between the roots. Twenty. Thirty. The glims in their lanterns all over Lanthorn humming, louder and louder, the way they'd hummed when the baby cried.

*Whose*, he heard. He didn't have Heed. He heard it anyway. The whole air was full of it. *Whose. Whose.*

And one of them came along the walkway to Tegan's door.

Pale. The size of a plum. It came along the walkway slowly, past John on the end of the step, so close he felt it go by like warmth on his face. And it stopped. Over the grey blanket. Over the small white face.

The baby opened its eyes.

Enormous. Dark. Looking up.

The light came down. Slowly. The way the spark had come up out of Hana's hands into Jared's lantern. Down, and down, until it was hanging just over the baby's face, a hand's width away.

And it hummed.

And the baby, who hadn't cried for two falls, opened her mouth and *screamed*.

Tegan made a sound John would hear for the rest of his life. Then she was laughing and crying at once, holding the baby up, the baby screaming, red in the face now, furious, alive, the glim hanging over her face humming and humming as if it were laughing too.

All over Lanthorn, the lanterns were singing.

***

He sat on the end of the step and didn't move.

He didn't know which one it had been. His. The one he'd let go. He didn't know if it had gone down to the split tree and found them and led them back. "Yeah," said Gabe. "He said that too. On the jam. After." He looked across the pool. "Rachael asked him what it was like. Letting it go thirty-five ways. He said *like putting something down*."

John looked across the pool too.

Dec was at the edge of the water. He'd been there, John realised, the whole time. Since the strays started coming up the path. Standing at the edge of the black pool with Thea beside him and the bundle in her arms and Tuli's pole in his hand, watching the lights come into the town.

Not watching John. He'd never once looked over at Tegan's step, John thought. Not once. He'd stood at the pool and watched the strays and let John have it.

Off to one side.

And on his belt, Gabe's terrible lantern. Lopsided, lumpy, the worst lantern Nesta had ever seen.

It wasn't empty.

John stared at it.

There was a light in it. Small. Pale. The size of a walnut. Scattered through Gabe's terrible knot to about forty points instead of a hundred, because the weave was so bad, so you could nearly see the middle. Sitting in it. Very still.

Dec wasn't looking at it. John didn't think Dec knew it was there.

"Gabe," said John. Very quietly.

"Yeah."

"Look at his belt."

Gabe looked.

He didn't say anything for a long time. John watched his face. It did the thing it did when Dec said *it hurts*. The thing it had done on the flour sacks in Evermorrow. A man being handed something.

"It's for if," said Gabe. Very quietly. Almost to himself. "I said. I said it's for if."

"One of the strays," said John.

"Yeah. Must be."

"It went in."

"Yeah."

They sat on the step and looked at it. A light in a terrible lantern on the belt of a man who'd never had one. Who'd had nothing on his shoulder that anybody could see, for two years, while thirty-five people walked round with balls of light at their ears and nobody noticed that he didn't.

John had noticed. He'd never said.

"Don't tell him," said Gabe.

"I wasn't going to."

"He'll find it. When he finds it." Gabe's voice was rough. "Let him find it."

John looked at the light in the lantern. Still. Pale. Not humming. Not doing anything at all. Just sitting there, the way a stray sits in an empty lantern in a town where people walk round it carefully.

*Whose*, it was asking. He was sure of it. He didn't need Heed.

*Whose are you.*

Across the pool, Dec turned his head and said something to Thea, and she laughed, and he leaned on his pole, and the light in the lantern on his belt didn't move.

John didn't watch it.

He looked at Gabe instead, off to one side of him, on the other end of the step, with his big hands hanging between his knees and his face doing the thing. And thought, not for the first time, that he really did have the best seat in the house.
