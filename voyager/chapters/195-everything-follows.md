Things followed Aleaka. They always had.

Since the white room. Before, maybe; she didn't know about before, not properly. On Earth there'd been a cat that sat on her doorstep, and a dog two doors down that barked at everybody but her, and once, at a bus stop, a pigeon that had walked up to her shoe and stood on it and looked up at her as if it had been waiting all morning for her to arrive. She'd thought it was luck. Then the white room, and the first day on floor 1, and by the evening there'd been two hens and the inn's grey dog and a lamb behind her in the street in a line, and Calvin had laughed so hard he fell over, and she'd stopped thinking it was luck.

She didn't call them. That was the thing nobody understood. She'd tried to explain it to Hana once, on floor 3, and given up. She didn't call them. She just *noticed* them. That was all. Most people walked past a hen. She looked at it, and it knew it had been looked at, properly, the way you'd look at a person. And then it followed her, to see if she'd do it again.

On floor 1, in the deep part of the winter, everything followed her.

The hens from Mother Crane's yard, all nine, every time she crossed it. The inn's grey dog, who was very old now and had a white muzzle and walked like his back legs belonged to somebody else, but who heaved himself up off the inn step every morning when she went past and came after her down Crooked Street. Three lambs from the fields by the river that weren't supposed to be out of their pen and somehow always were. A cat. Two cats. A crow that sat on the spoon sign over the Copper Spoon's door and waited for her. And children. Floor-1 children, who on the first day had stood on corners and in doorways like things that had been put there and not moved. Now they came out of the doorways when she passed, and walked behind her, in a little line, behind the lambs and the dog, not saying anything, just following, to see where she was going.

And Sleet, of course. At her heel. Knee-high now, grey and white and patient, with the long-suffering face of a dog who had been on fourteen floors with her and seen everything and been followed by most of it.

"You're a parade," said Hana, on the second morning of the fifth week, coming out of the inn and finding her in the street with the dog and the lambs and the cats and four children.

"I'm going to see the goat."

"You're always going to see the goat."

"He's always there," said Aleaka.

***

He was always there.

Under the little stone bridge on the stream path, below the Fold, where the path came down out of the hills toward the valley. The bridge was humped and old and mossy, a single arch over a stream that ran fast and black under a skin of painted ice. And under the arch, in the dark, on a ledge of dry stone just above the water, the Bridge Goat lived.

He'd lived there on the first day. He'd come out from under it at dawn on the first morning, when he was young and grey-brown and had all of both his horns, and put his head down at the first thing that came down the path, which had been a man with no weapon in a hurry. And the man had punched him eleven times. Aleaka hadn't seen it. Nobody had. But they'd all heard about it by the evening, because Gabe had posted *you punched a goat you absolute idiot* in World, and the goat had been standing on the path when they came down it on the second day, dazed and cross, with a chipped horn, and had looked at Aleaka. And followed her. At a distance. All the way down to Hearthstead, and all the next day, and the day after that, never close, never more than a shape at the edge of things. On the morning Hana's party went up the path to the Fold to fight the sheep, he'd come as far as his bridge. And stopped. And stood on top of it in the middle and watched her go up, and up, and in through the door in the hill.

She'd thought about him on every floor. She hadn't told anybody that.

He was old now. That was what she'd seen on the first day back, coming down from the Fold, and it had gone into her like a splinter. A year and a half was a long time for a goat. His muzzle had gone white. His back had dipped. He walked carefully, the way the inn's dog walked, like somebody who'd learned exactly how much each step was going to cost and was budgeting. But he'd come out from under his bridge when she came down the path with thirty-five other people. He'd stood there on four thin legs in the painted snow and looked at her. Only her.

*You waited*, she'd said.

And every day since, she'd gone back.

She went down the stream path in the mornings, after the hens and before the clinic, with her parade, and sat on the parapet of the bridge in the cold with her boots dangling over the black water. And the goat came out. Every day. He came out from under the arch onto the path at the far end of the bridge, and stood there, and looked at her, and then lay down. A little closer each day. The first day, at the far end of the path, a long way off. By the end of the second week, at the foot of the bridge. By the end of the fourth, he was lying on the bridge itself, on the humped stones at the far end of the parapet, with his old legs folded under him and his white chin on his knees, a body's length from her boots.

She didn't touch him. You didn't. Not till they asked.

She talked to him. She told him about the floors. About the gulls on five that remembered her, and the wrens on eight, and Tuppence with the white feather in her wing who'd come out of the falling water and sat on her hand and then gone back into the water, because that was where she lived. About the singer on eleven, longer than a ship, grey-white like old bone, that had called up through the hull of the *Patience* in the dark and that she'd answered without a word. About the gold on fourteen. About Sleet.

He listened. Goats did. People didn't think so, but they did. He lay at the end of the bridge with his ears turned toward her and his strange flat yellow eyes half shut, and listened to all of it, and sometimes, at the bits about the sea, he made a small low noise in his chest that she didn't have a word for.

She had a word for it, actually. She had a skill for it. She'd got it on eleven, with the singer, and it had been sitting in her window ever since.

*Heed. When something calls you, you hear what it means. When you answer, it knows you came.*

He wasn't calling. Not exactly. But she heard what he meant anyway.

He meant *I'd like to see that*.

***

There was a window in her log she didn't look at.

She looked at it every day. She just didn't *look* at it, the way you don't look at a letter you know is going to be hard.

*The Call (3 of 3). Call one that cannot come.*

It had come up on eleven, the night of the singer, under the window that said *complete* and the one that gave her Heed. She'd read it then, with salt water still running off her hair, standing on the deck with Jared holding the rope round her waist and Tuppence's white feather in her pocket. *Call one that cannot come.* And she hadn't understood it, and she'd decided not to try, not yet. *She'd find out*, she'd said to Hana, who'd asked. That was what she always said. She'd find out.

She'd been finding out for nearly three hundred days.

She knew some of it. She'd known since eight, really, since the Great Fall, when Tuppence had come out of the water onto her hand and sat there, small and old and rough-feathered, and then gone back. *She said this is where she lives*, Aleaka had told Ice, at the top of the falls. And he'd looked at her, and at the water, and said *You can ask again. Later.*

Things lived on their floor. That was what she'd found out. That was the rule, and nobody had told her it, she'd just watched it be true. The gulls on five didn't come down the stair to four. The wrens didn't go up from eight. The singer couldn't leave the sea, and the sea couldn't leave eleven. Tuppence couldn't come. Sleet could, and Waddles, and Squiddles, and Dose, and Suzu, and Bee's puffs; but they'd come out of the white room with their people. They were the people's. Everything else, every creature on every floor, lived on its floor and died on its floor and never once in the whole of the Spire's long life went up a stair or down one.

People did. Hessa had, once. Gone up a long way, as a girl, and come back down to her rock. And the thirty-six went up and down. But nothing else. Not a bird. Not a fish. Not a goat.

*Call one that cannot come.*

She sat on the bridge on the twenty-ninth morning with her boots over the black water and looked at the old goat lying at the far end of the parapet with his chin on his knees.

"Would you come?" she said.

He opened his yellow eyes. He looked at her.

She heard what he meant.

*Yes.*

And under it, under the yes, the way the singer's hum had had something under it, so low you felt it in your feet before you heard it: *I can't.*

She sat with that for a long time.

"I know," she said. "Not yet."

He shut his eyes again. Sleet, beside her, put his head on her knee. The lambs had found something to eat under the snow on the bank. The children had gone home. The crow sat on the stone at the end of the bridge, between Aleaka and the goat, and looked from one to the other, and said nothing at all.

***

The Copper Spoon in the deep of the winter was the warmest place Aleaka had ever been.

Not because of the stove. The stove was good, and Mother Crane fed it like it owed her money, and the long room was always warm at the kitchen end. But it was the people. Thirty-six of them, in and out all day, in a cookhouse built for a village. You couldn't be cold in it. You couldn't be alone in it, either; there wasn't room. There was always somebody at the long table, and somebody in the kitchen, and somebody by the fire, and somebody asleep on the bench by the door with their boots on, who Mother Crane would shout at. She shouted at all of them. She'd learned their names in the first week, every single one, and she used them only to shout.

"*Calvin*. Feet."

"*Hako*. Door."

"*Salty*, if you fix that hinge one more time I'll hang you off it."

Salty had fixed every hinge in the Spoon in the first three days. Then he'd started on Crooked Street. Then the mill. By the fifth week there wasn't a door in Hearthstead that squeaked, and people had started leaving things on the Spoon's step for him, bread and cheese and once a whole cooked hen, the way the Low Town in Tell had left things on the step for all of them. Gabe and Jared had the smith's. The old smith had stopped sitting on his anvil in astonishment and started standing beside it, giving orders, which Jared said was worse. Hyvred found things. A ring, a lost lamb, a boy who'd gone into the woods after his dog and got turned round. Kei and Rachael and Malcolm and Xola had the yard every morning, and the people of Hearthstead came and stood in it among the hens, and asked.

And every morning, before any of that, before anybody else was up, Ice peeled her onions.

Aleaka saw it because she got up early for the goat. She'd come down from the inn in the grey painted dark before the sun was on its rail, and let herself in at the back door to the yard, and there they'd be. At the kitchen end of the long table, by one candle. Mother Crane in her apron with the big knife. Ice beside her with the little one. The onion sack between them. Not talking much. Peeling.

Sometimes she talked. About her husband, mostly. *He used to say*, and *he never once*, and *God rest the pig*. He'd been a carter. He'd driven a cart between Hearthstead and the farms up the valley for thirty years and put his boots on her table every night of it. He'd been dead twenty-three years and she still set his place at the end of the long table on the first night of every winter and then took it away again in the morning and never told anybody why. Ice listened to all of it. He didn't say much back. He said *yes* and *I know* and once, when she said *he was the most irritating man on the floor*, he said *I believe you*, and she laughed so hard she had to put the knife down.

Aleaka watched them from the back door with the hens round her feet, and thought about Tuppence.

On eight, at the top of the Great Fall. The old wren sitting on her hand, rough-feathered, with the white feather in her wing. Looking back at the falling water. Not wanting to go. Not wanting to stay. Looking at the water the way you'd look at a house you'd lived in your whole life, and loved, and were going to have to leave.

That was how he looked at her. Every morning. By the candle, peeling onions. Like she was home and she was going to go.

***

Chi sat across the room from him every night.

Aleaka noticed it in the first week and didn't understand it until the third. Every evening, when the long table had been cleared and the lamps were lit and people were lying down wherever they could find, Chi would go and sit on the bench by the fire at the far end of the Spoon. With Waddles in her lap, usually, damp from the river. Facing the kitchen end. Where Ice was.

She didn't talk to him. She didn't go near him. She just sat there. With her hands round a cup, looking at him across the room, very steadily, the way you'd look at a candle to keep it lit.

And he'd look back. After a while. Every night. He'd be sitting at the long table, or lying on the floor by the stove with the pot by his head and Thea asleep on his shoulder, and his face would be doing the thing it did now, the held thing, the match in the wind, and then he'd look up and find Chi across the room. And Aleaka would watch his shoulders come down. Not all the way. A bit. The way a bird's feathers settle when it's decided you're not going to hurt it.

"What's she doing?" Aleaka asked Higu, in the third week.

Higu was at the next table with the rock, adding something to the right-hand column, sideways, in very small letters.

"Being calm at him," said Higu.

"Oh," said Aleaka.

"She promised. On six. In the cistern." Higu didn't look up. "He told her he was frightened of this. Of being on time and it not mattering. And she said she'd sit across the room from him every night and be calm at him till it was over." She wrote something. "She hasn't missed one."

Aleaka looked across the Spoon at Chi on her bench, with the penguin in her lap and her cup in her hands, looking at Ice. And at Ice, looking back. And at his shoulders coming down.

"It works," she said.

"It works on everybody," said Higu. "That's why nobody notices it."

Aleaka thought about that for a long time. She thought she might be a bit like that. With the hens. She just noticed them.

***

He counted.

Aleaka heard it because she was always near the door, and the door was near the stove, and the stove was where he slept. Every night, when the lamps were out, before he went to sleep, if he went to sleep, he said a number. Very quietly. Not to anybody. To Thea, maybe, who was always there. Or to the little fox-shaped light that sat on his shoulder sometimes, that most of them couldn't see and Aleaka sometimes could, out of the corner of her eye, when it moved.

"Thirty," he said, on the thirty-third night.

"Twenty," he said, on the forty-third.

Thea said it back, every time. "Twenty." Like putting something away somewhere safe.

"Twelve."

"Twelve."

Mother Crane was fine.

That was the thing that made it worse. Aleaka could see that, the way she could see what a dog meant when it lay down across a door. Every day, Mother Crane was fine. She got up before anybody. She fed her stove. She shouted at Calvin. She carried wood, she kneaded bread, she talked to Merle's mother in the crock every morning in a voice like she was talking to a friend who'd had a hard week, and the bread that came out of the oven tasted, Newish said, like tomorrow. She went up and down the narrow stair behind the stove in eleven breaths; Kei counted them, and Aleaka saw him count them, and saw his face when it was eleven. She laughed at Newish, who'd made it her business to make her laugh once a day and had never missed. She wasn't tired. She wasn't pale. She didn't cough.

She was fine on the day that was ten. And on the day that was five.

And every night Ice said the number, and Thea said it back, and Chi sat across the room being calm at him, and Aleaka lay by the door with the hens in a heap on her feet and Sleet against her back and listened to him not sleep.

"Three," he said.

"Three," said Thea.

"Two."

"Two."

***

On the evening of the day that was one, she was fine.

She made a stew. Mutton and barley and onion and turnip and too much salt and a bit of willow bark at the back for the old men's knees, and she fed thirty-six people and a penguin and nine hens, and she shouted at Calvin three times and at Hako twice and once at Gabe, which nobody had ever seen anybody do. She ate standing up, in bites. She laughed at Newish. She went up the narrow stair at the usual time with her candle, in eleven breaths. Kei counted them.

The Spoon went quiet. The lamps went out.

Ice didn't say a number.

Aleaka lay by the door and waited for it, and it didn't come. She heard Thea not say it back. She heard the fire settle. She heard Chi, across the room, shift on her bench and not lie down.

And then she heard the hens.

They were outside, in their house in the yard, where they always slept. Nine hens on a perch. She knew their sounds the way you know the sounds of your own house at night. And they were making a sound she'd never heard them make. Low. Unsettled. A sort of fretful murmur, all of them together, the way a flock of birds sounds just before it goes up all at once for no reason you can see.

She got up.

She went out the back door into the yard. It was very cold. The painted snow was coming down in its rows. The hen-house door was shut, the way she'd shut it, and behind it the hens were murmuring, and when she put her hand on the door they went quiet, and then started again.

She went round the side of the Spoon into Crooked Street.

The inn's grey dog was lying across the Copper Spoon's front door.

He'd never done that. He slept on the inn's step at the top of the street. He'd come all the way down Crooked Street in the snow on his bad back legs, and he was lying across the doorstep of the Spoon with his old white chin on his paws and his eyes open, facing out. Like something on guard. He looked at her when she came round the corner. He didn't get up. He didn't wag. He just looked at her and then back out at the street.

And in the street, in the middle of the cobbles, in the painted snow, stood the Bridge Goat.

Aleaka stopped.

He'd never come up from his bridge. Not once. Not on the first day, when he'd followed her at a distance, and not in the five weeks since. He'd always stayed on the path, or at the bridge, or at the edge of the fields, never further. He was too old for the walk now; she could see it in the way he was standing, braced, his back legs trembling a little, snow on his back. He'd come the whole way. Up the stream path, past the fields, through the empty square, past the egg girl's corner and the well and the inn, down the whole of Crooked Street in the dark. On his own.

He was standing in the middle of the street, with the snow coming down on him in rows, looking up.

Not at her. At the window. The small one, under the eaves, above the kitchen. Dark.

The crow was on the spoon sign. It was looking at the window too.

Aleaka stood very still at the corner of the Spoon. She didn't go to him. She let herself be what she was, which was somebody who noticed things, and she noticed.

She noticed the hens, behind her, murmuring.

The dog, across the door, facing out.

The goat in the street, looking up.

The crow. The lambs, she knew without looking, would be out of their pen again, standing in the dark by the river with their faces turned the same way. And the cats. And everything on floor 1 that had ever followed her down a street. All of them, tonight, all over Hearthstead, in the painted snow, turned toward one small dark window under the eaves of a cookhouse at the bottom of a crooked street.

*Heed*, said the window in her head, very faintly. *When something calls you, you hear what it means.*

Nothing was calling. Nobody had made a sound.

She heard what it meant anyway.

***

She went back in by the yard door. The Spoon was dark except for the last of the fire. She went past the long table, past the sleeping heaps, past Calvin with his boots on the bench and Rachael's hand on his ankle. To the kitchen end. To the stove.

He was sitting up.

He hadn't been asleep. She'd known he wouldn't be. He was sitting on the floor by the stove with his back against the wall and the pot beside him and his hand on the pot, and Thea was sitting beside him, awake too, with the lantern in her lap turned down to almost nothing. Across the room, on her bench, Chi was sitting up with Waddles asleep in her lap, looking at him. Being calm. Aleaka could feel it from here, like warmth off a wall.

He looked up at her.

"They know," said Aleaka.

She said it slowly. She said everything slowly. But she wanted to get it right, and it was hard to get right.

"The animals," she said. "All of them. The hens won't settle. The dog's across your door. The goat's come up. He's in the street. He's never come up. He's looking at her window." She swallowed. "They're all looking at her window. Everything. All over the town." She looked at him. "They know it's tomorrow."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "I know."

Not *how do you know*. Not *it's nothing*. Just *I know*. The way you'd say it to somebody who'd just told you something you'd been carrying on your own for a long way and were glad, a little, not to be carrying on your own any more.

"It's tomorrow," said Ice. "It was always tomorrow."

Thea's hand found his on the pot.

"One," she said.

Ice looked at the window. Not the one under the eaves; you couldn't see that from in here. The one in the kitchen wall, small and dark, with the snow going past it in rows. He looked at it for a long time.

"One," he said.

Aleaka stood there a moment longer. Then she went back down the long room, past the sleeping heaps, and out of the front door, carefully, over the grey dog, who lifted his head and let her past and put it down again. And she went and stood in the street beside the goat.

She didn't touch him. He didn't ask her to. She just stood beside him in the middle of Crooked Street in the painted snow, with the crow on the sign above them and the dog across the door behind them, and looked up at the small dark window under the eaves with him.

*I'll stay*, she told him. Not out loud. You didn't need out loud, with Heed. You just meant it. *I'll stay up with you.*

He didn't look away from the window. But after a while, the old goat leaned. A little. Just enough that his shoulder touched her leg through her coat. Warm, and thin, and trembling with the cold and the long walk.

She stood there with him till morning.
