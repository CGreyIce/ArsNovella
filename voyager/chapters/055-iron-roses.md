Jared had been awake for twenty-six hours, and he'd made eleven links. He'd put a rose on every one.

He was thinking about the roses as he went out along the rope. It was easier than thinking about the cloud.

The rope was the thickest of the three, a hawser as thick as his wrist. It ran out from the stump where the old post had been, across a hundred metres of nothing, to a black rock on the far side where Milli had tied it round a spur of stone in the dark. Jared went out under it the way the Tether hunters had shown him an hour ago: hanging from it by his hands, his ankles hooked over it, hand over hand, foot over foot, with his back to the sky. If he turned his head he could see the cloud. Two hundred metres down. Pale and soft and endless in the dawn, like a floor you could lie down on.

He didn't turn his head.

*Hand. Hand. Foot.* He thought about the roses.

***

He'd been holding the post when it went.

That was the thing he kept coming back to. Not the span going up, not the noise. The post. His hand had been on it, flat, the way you put your hand on a doorframe while you wait for somebody. Old iron, warm from the sun, rough with rust. And he'd felt it move. A tiny shift, under his palm, before anything else happened. He'd known. He'd known before his head knew, the way a smith knows a weld's bad by the sound before it cracks. He'd thrown himself backward. He'd got an arm round Alice on the way. And the post had come up out of the rock where his hand had been.

If he'd been half a second slower she'd have gone with it. He knew that. He'd known it all night.

And Milli had been on the other side.

He'd stood at the edge with Alice in the dusk, with the empty air where the span had been, and watched the gale-kites come up over the edge of Thornback. He'd watched one small fast shape with a tail put its back to a rock. Alice hadn't made a sound. She'd just stood there with her hand round his arm so tight her nails went through his sleeve, watching, and he'd thought: *no. Not standing here. Not just standing here watching.*

So he'd gone to Drew.

"I'm a smith," he'd said. "There's a forge in Tether that does bridges. We need chain. We need a post. I'm going."

Drew had looked at him for a second. Then he'd said, "Gabe. Salty. Go with him," and turned back to the edge. That was why Drew was general.

***

Otta hadn't wanted to.

"Never forge a span at night," she'd said. She'd been banking the fire for the evening when they came running into the Linkworks, three smiths covered in dust and out of breath, and she'd looked at them with her great burn-scarred arms folded. "Bad luck. Everybody knows. You forge a span by daylight or the wind hears you."

"Ma'am," Jared had said, "there's a guy on Thornback right now who's all out of luck already. Wind's had its turn."

Otta had looked at him a long time.

Then she'd unbanked the fire.

They couldn't build a span in a night. Otta said so, flat, and she was right. A span needed a new post, sunk into the rock and poured round with lead, and that took two days by itself. It needed chain, a hundred metres of it, twice over, and planks, and rail. What they could do in a night, she said, was a line. One chain across. Enough to hang a rope on, and a man on the rope. Enough to bring somebody home.

But the Linkworks didn't have a hundred metres of chain. It had lengths. Twenty metres here, fifteen there, hanging from the rafters, all different gauges, all waiting for spans that weren't broken yet. To make one long chain out of them you had to join them. And to join them you had to make joining links: open a link at each end, forge a new one between, close it, and weld it so it'd hold a man's weight over two hundred metres of air.

Eleven joins. Eleven links.

Otta showed him the first one. Jared did the second. Gabe and Salty worked the bellows and the striking hammer, and the Linkworks rang. Out in the dark, across the town, the whispers kept coming. Alice, every few minutes, short, in lowercase. *still fighting. still fighting. hes ok. theres so many.* Jared read them with sweat in his eyes and hammered harder.

On the third link, he put a rose on it.

He didn't decide to. His hands just did it. On floor 1, at Ulla Dray's forge in Hearthstead, he'd made his first real thing. Not a nail. A rose, out of nail iron, ugly and lumpy and real, and given it to Alice, and she'd pinned it to the front of her crown and never taken it off. Since then, every time he made something that mattered, his hands wanted to put one on it. A tiny rose, worked into the iron with the tip of a punch while it was still soft. Five petals, a dot in the middle. Nobody would ever see it unless they looked.

Otta saw it.

"What's that?" she said.

"Mine," said Jared. He didn't stop hammering. "So I know which ones are mine. If one of these goes, I want to know it was mine that went."

Otta looked at him over the forge. Then she looked at the link. She didn't say anything. But when she made the next joining link herself, she handed it to him at the end, still red, without a word, and waited while he put a rose on that one too.

***

Gabe didn't talk much that night. That wasn't like him.

He worked the bellows, and the striking hammer, and he carried chain. He didn't say anything except *yeah* and *here* and *okay*. Around two in the morning, when Alice's whispers had gone quiet for a long time and then come back with *they stopped. hes ok. hes sitting down*, all four of them stopped at once. Otta too. They stood round the forge in the red light, breathing, not saying anything.

Then Gabe said, quietly, to the fire:

"He'd have known what to do."

Jared looked at him.

"Dec," said Gabe. "If he was here. He'd have known. He always knows. He'd have looked at the gap and the post and the chain and just known." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist and left a black smear. "He'd have *fixed* it."

Jared thought about that.

He'd known Ice since he was twenty-one. Online, the whole time. Never in person. Hours and hours of games and calls, banter, Ice making him laugh until he couldn't breathe. A picture of Jared eating fried chicken in a parking lot that Ice had turned into a postcard to tease Alice with, which had become a bit, which had become a whole running thing between the three of them for years. He'd never met him. He'd never shaken his hand. And he'd still have bet his life on him.

"Nah," said Jared. "He'd have done exactly this."

Gabe looked at him.

"Stood at a forge all night," said Jared, "and not said anything, and made the thing that'd hold." He picked up the next link with the tongs. "That's what he does, man. That's what he's been doing for a hundred days. He's not up there fixing things. He's up there making things that'll hold, and leaving them for us."

Gabe looked at him across the forge for a long moment. Then he picked up the striking hammer.

"Okay," he said.

***

So now it was dawn, and the chain wasn't ready. Otta's apprentices were still bringing it along the bridges from Tether in a cart, eleven joins and a hundred and ten metres of it, and it'd be noon before it was across. And Jared was hanging under a rope over the end of the world, going hand over hand toward a black rock, because he hadn't been able to wait till noon.

Alice had wanted to go. Of course she had. She'd been at the stump since dusk, all night, with her crown on and her eyes on Thornback. When the first light came and the Tether hunters said a man could go across on the hawser if he knew how, she'd stepped forward before anybody else.

"Alice," Jared had said. "No."

"He's my *husband*—"

"And he'd kill me. He'd kill me if I let you." He'd put his hands on her shoulders. "And then he'd kill himself for letting me let you. Both of them would. Him and the other one." He'd looked at her. "Let me. Please. I'm the steady one. Let me be steady."

She'd looked at him for a long time with her lavender fringe in her eyes. Then she'd stepped back.

*Hand. Hand. Foot.*

He was more than halfway now. He could feel the rope start to slope up toward Thornback. His arms were burning. His palms were raw. The wind came, and the whole rope swung, a long, slow, sickening swing out over the cloud and back. He hung there with his eyes shut and his ankles locked until it stopped.

*Lean*, Nan Gully had said, word for word, to Gabe. *Don't stand still. Lean.* Gabe had been up to the Kitecliff three times now. He came back every evening battered and full of grass and wouldn't explain. But on the last evening he'd stood at the edge of the island outside the inn in a gale, not holding anything. Just standing. Swaying, a little, all the time. And he hadn't fallen.

Jared let the rope swing. He didn't fight it. He swung with it, out and back. When it stopped, he went on.

***

Milli was sitting against a rock at the top of the slope, among the dead.

That was what Jared saw first, when he got his hands onto the black rock at the edge of Thornback and pulled himself up and lay on his face in the gorse for a second, breathing. Gale-kites. Everywhere. Lying in the gorse where they'd fallen, flat and grey and wide as doors, dozens of them, more than he could count, all round the big rock in a ring. They hadn't gone anywhere. The smell was like a fish market in a heatwave.

And in the middle of them, with his back to the rock and the ropes tied round it, sitting with his knees up in the first sun, was Milli.

His leathers were in ribbons. His arms were cut. There was blood dried black all down one side of his face and in his short brown hair, in the bright yellow streak. His tail was lying flat on the gorse beside him like it was too tired to move. And he was watching the sunrise, very calmly, with his daggers across his knees, the way you'd watch the sun come up from a porch.

He turned his head and looked at Jared lying in the gorse.

"You look terrible," said Milli.

"*I* look terrible?"

"You look slow, then." Milli's mouth did something, at the corner. "You took ages."

Jared lay in the gorse and laughed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him like something breaking, and he lay there on his back on Thornback with the sun on his face, raw-handed and twenty-six hours awake, and laughed. After a second Milli started too, quietly, with his eyes shut and his head back against the rock.

"Alice is going to kill you," said Jared, when he could talk.

"I know."

"For going first."

"I know." Milli opened his eyes. "Jared. I need to tell Chris something."

"It can wait till you're on the other side, man."

"No," said Milli. "It can't. I've been doing the sum all night. I need to say it before I fall asleep and forget how it went." He looked at Jared with his calm, rational eyes, under all the blood. "I think I know how he does it."

***

They went back across together, Milli in front, Jared behind, both of them hanging from the hawser with their backs to the sky. It took a long time. Milli's arms were shaking by the end. Jared talked to him the whole way, about nothing, about fried chicken and parking lots and Ice's stupid postcard. He didn't let him stop.

When they reached the stump, thirty pairs of hands pulled them up onto the grass.

Jared didn't watch Alice and Milli. He turned round and looked at the cloud instead, so they could have it. Out of the corner of his eye he saw her reach Milli before anybody else did. He saw her not say anything. He saw her put her face in Milli's ruined leathers, with her crown knocked sideways, and the iron rose on the front of it pressing into his chest. And he saw Milli's arms come up round her, slowly, as if they were the heaviest thing he'd ever lifted.

Jared looked at the cloud for a long time.

***

Chris was at the edge with his notebook already open.

Milli sat on the grass with Alice beside him and Rachael and Malcolm working on his arms, and told it. In his calm voice, carefully, the way he said everything, as if he was reading it off a page. Thirty-five people stood round him in a ring in the morning sun and listened.

"*Fighting alone*," said Milli. "Times two. It came up after the first three. I'd never seen it before. None of us have. We've never fought anything alone." He paused while Rachael closed a cut. "Then *Outnumbered*. Times three. When they came up over the edge, ten, twelve. I think it's more than twelve to one, or more than ten. I didn't stop to check."

Chris was writing. Fast.

"Do they stack?"

"I think so. The bar was going up—" Milli held his hands apart, very wide. "I'd do one and it'd be like ten in a party. More. I hit twenty-five about two hours in, and it's still been filling behind the padlock since then." He opened his window and turned it round so Chris could see. "Look."

Chris looked. Jared saw his face change.

The faint second bar, behind the padlock. Banked. Full nearly to the end. More experience than any of them had made in a week. In one night. Alone. On a rock.

"Above your level, too," said Chris slowly. "Were they?"

"Some of them. A couple." Milli shrugged, carefully, so as not to undo Rachael's work. "Twenty-five, twenty-six."

"So alone, outnumbered, and above your level." Chris had stopped writing. He was looking at the page. "Times two, times three, times whatever above-level is. It's—" He flipped back. Pages and pages, back to floor 1. To a graph, drawn by hand in pencil, a hundred days of dots. Thirty-five lines going up slowly together in a bunch at the bottom. And one line, on its own, going up like a ladder.

*VOYAGER.*

"Oh," said Chris.

Nobody said anything.

"Every fight," said Chris. Very quietly. "Every single fight he's ever had. Alone. Every single time. And outnumbered, mostly. And above his level, always. He cleared floor 1 on the first day. Floor 2 at level ten, two under. The Kiln, at level twenty-two or whatever he was, alone, *twice*." He was looking at the ladder line. "That's the shape. That's why it looks like that. It's not that he's—" He stopped. "It's the multiplier. He's been fighting alone the whole time, and the tower's been paying him for it."

"*Solo bonus*," said Hako, from the back. He said it the way you'd say the name of an old friend. "Oh, man. Classic. Every single one of those stories has it."

"It's always in there," said Kei. Dose was round his neck, looking at Milli with her small green head tilted. "The guy who goes off on his own gets the bonus. And then he's way stronger than the party, and the party's like, *how*." He looked at Hako. "We should have known."

"WAIT," said Calvin. "Wait. So if we all just *split up*—"

"*No*," said Drew, Deston and Rachael, all at once.

Calvin shut his mouth. Then he opened it again.

"I'm not saying we *should*," he said. "I'm saying—look at that line. Look at Milli's bar. One night. If that's real, then we should at least *know*. Properly. Not just *no*." He looked round at all of them. "Isn't that the whole thing? No more pocket. We say it out loud and we work it out."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Jared watched Drew think about it. Drew always thought about things with his whole face, frowning at the ground like it had owed him money.

"Fine," said Drew. "Properly. Chris. Two columns."

Chris turned to a clean page and drew a line down the middle. At the top of one side he wrote *FOR*. At the top of the other, *AGAINST*. He held the pencil over the page.

"For," said Drew.

"Speed," said Chris at once, and wrote it. "Times two alone, times three outnumbered. If they stack, that's times six before the above-level bonus. Milli made a week in a night. Call it six weeks of training in one." He looked up. "We've been on floor 5 for a week. We could be ready for the Spindle tomorrow."

"Skills," said Rui Xiang, from where he was sitting on the grass. He was turning a knife over his knuckles, the way he did when he was thinking hard. "Alone, you use everything you've got. Every skill, every fight. Nobody else to cover the gap, so you learn to cover it yourself." He shrugged. "You'd learn fast."

"No split," said Dennis. "XP five ways is—I mean, it's a tax. It's a *terrible* tax."

Chris wrote it down. *No five-way split.*

"We'd catch up," said Gabe.

Everybody looked at him. He was standing at the back with the sacking-wrapped shield across his shoulders, and he didn't look away.

"To him," said Gabe. "That's a pro. If we were faster we'd catch up."

Chris looked at him for a second. Then he wrote it down, without a word. *Catch up.*

"Against," said Drew.

"Death," said Rachael.

She didn't look up. She was still working on the last cut on Milli's arm, her hands on it, her frown very deep.

"It's permanent," she said. "There's no respawn for us. The bosses come back. We don't. Revival items exist and nobody here's got one, and nobody's even seen one." She closed the cut and sat back. "Write that first. Write it big."

Chris wrote it first, and big.

"No healer," said Malcolm. "If you're alone and you get cut, you keep the cut. Until it kills you, or until you get somewhere safe enough to stop and fix it yourself. Which, if you're outnumbered, is never."

"I couldn't drink a potion," said Milli quietly. Everybody looked at him. "I had two in my belt the whole night. I never got a hand free. Not once in four hours. Every time I thought about reaching for one, something came over the edge."

Chris wrote that down too.

"One mistake," said Deston. His voice was very calm, the way it always was when he was most frightened. "A party forgives you a mistake. You trip, somebody covers you. You misjudge a gust, somebody grabs your collar." He didn't look at Rachael and Calvin, but everybody else did. "Alone, the first mistake's the last one."

"Classes," said Xola, from the back. "A party's five because nobody's whole on their own. Tank, healer, damage. Alone, you're only what you are. A healer can't kill anything. A tank can't kill fast enough. A mage gets caught." She looked at Milli. "He's a rogue. Fast, and he hits hard. He was nearly the best possible person to be on that rock. And he still nearly didn't come back."

"And the gust," said Aleaka, slowly. She had Sleet in her arms. "The gust was nobody's fault. It just came. If it comes when you're alone, there's nobody at the other end of anything."

Chris's pencil had been moving the whole time. The *AGAINST* column was full. He'd started writing up the margin.

"There's one more," said Rachael.

She stood up. She brushed the grass off her knees and looked round at all of them, and her frown had gone somewhere past frowning.

"The tower doesn't pay you for nothing," she said. "Have you noticed that? Nothing here's free. Fighting above your level, you get more because you're more likely to lose. So times two for alone—" She stopped. "That's the tower telling you what your chances are. That's a *price*. Times six means it thinks you're six times more likely to die than if you had four people with you. That's not a bonus. It's a warning, written as a reward."

Nobody said anything.

Jared looked down at his raw hands, wrapped in somebody's shirt. He thought about the post moving under his palm. Half a second.

"Milli," said Drew quietly. "You did it. Last night. You're the only one who knows. Would you do it again?"

Milli was quiet for a long time. He sat on the grass with Alice's hand round his, looking at the bandages on his arms.

"No," he said.

Then: "And I'm stronger than I was yesterday. A lot stronger. Both of those are true."

He looked up at them.

"I was lucky," he said. "I want everybody to understand that. I had a rock at my back. I had Leaka's gull. I had thirty-five of you standing at the edge all night with a light, where I could see you. And I'd been hunting those things for four days. I knew how they moved. I knew where they'd come from." He swallowed. "Take any one of those away and I'm not here. If they'd been something I'd never seen before, I'm not here."

Chris looked at the two columns for a long time.

Then he turned the notebook round so everybody could see it. *FOR*, four lines. *AGAINST*, a whole column and half the margin, and at the top of it, big, *DEATH*.

"It's not close," said Chris. "It doesn't add up. Not even nearly." He closed the notebook. "The pros don't outweigh the cons. They're not even in the same weight class."

"So that's a no," said Drew. "Properly, this time. Not just because I said so." He looked round the ring. "Nobody fights alone. Not on purpose, not ever. If it happens, like last night, we come and get you, whatever it takes. Everybody?"

Thirty-five hands went up. Calvin's last. He held it up and looked at it.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Yeah. Okay."

***

And then it was very quiet. Because they'd all done the sum. Every one of them. And they'd all got to the end of it at about the same time.

It didn't add up. It was a terrible way to get strong. And there was one person in the tower who'd done it for a hundred and two days, every day, every fight.

"He knows," said Thea.

She hadn't said anything the whole time. She'd been standing at the edge with the lantern, listening. Now everybody turned and looked at her.

"He's not stupid," said Thea. "He knows the sum. He knows it better than any of us. He's done it every day." She looked down at the lantern in her hand. "He knows it doesn't add up. He's doing it anyway."

Nobody said anything.

Jared tried to imagine it. He couldn't help it. He was twenty-six hours awake, standing in the morning sun with his palms raw from a hawser and his arms aching from a night at the forge. He'd thought, at four in the morning with the hammer in his hand, that it was the longest night of his life.

He tried to imagine it every night.

Every fight, for a hundred and two days, alone. Every cut kept until he could get somewhere safe to mend it himself. Every bruise. The Kiln's fire, twice, with nobody behind him. That bird, with nobody to cut its legs while it was on him. Lying in a crack in the rock somewhere with his arm open, with nobody to put two blessings on him, with nobody to say *agree with it*. Sleeping. *Sleeping.* Who watched the door while he slept? Nobody. Every night for a hundred and two nights, nobody had watched the door. He'd lain down on four floors with nobody awake beside him. He'd woken up every morning not knowing whether he'd wake up the next.

And then got up. And gone and found the next thing alone.

"Who watches the door," said Gabe. Very quietly. He'd got there too. "When he sleeps. Who watches it."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to.

"Behind the wall on four," said Hyvred. Her voice cracked. "The light. I said it went small. When people are frightened, it goes in on itself. It was small, Gabe. All over that wall. It was small for a *week*."

"He cooked fish behind Nan's hut every night for a fortnight," said Dennis. He sounded strange. "On his own. For himself. And for her." He stopped. "Too much salt. She said too much salt."

"He stood in fire," said Malcolm. He said it to the ground. "Twice. And then he healed it himself, at night, alone. Nobody to do two in case the first one failed." His voice went. "Nobody to do *one*."

"Every day," said Rachael.

She was still standing. She'd gone very white.

"Every single day," she said, "he gets up and does the thing we just voted not to do once. Knowing what it costs. Six times. Every fight." She looked at Milli, at his bandaged arms. Then at the cloud. "What does that *feel* like? After a hundred days? What does it do to you, to hurt like that every day and nobody even sees it?"

Nobody could answer that.

Jared looked at the needle in the middle of the sky, where a man had stood on a crown of rock and killed a bird alone at dawn. So that people he wasn't with would see somebody flying.

*He's not up there fixing things*, he'd told Gabe at the forge. *He's up there making things that'll hold, and leaving them for us.*

He hadn't thought about what it cost to make them.

"Then why," said Calvin. His voice was very small for Calvin. "If it doesn't add up. If he knows. Why does he—"

"Because if he's alone on a floor," said Thea quietly, "then nobody else is on it with him."

Calvin looked at her.

"Nobody to get hurt," said Thea. "Nobody to lose. Just him." She held the lantern a little tighter. "Every floor he clears alone is a floor where the only person who can die is him. He worked out the sum, Calvin. He just got a different answer. Because he put himself on the other side of it."

The whole ring was silent.

Gabe turned round and walked to the edge of the island, and stood there with his back to all of them, looking at the needle. His shoulders were shaking. Nobody went after him. Xav took one step, and then stopped, and waited. Sometimes the right move was not to move.

***

Jared fell asleep at the long table in the inn that evening. He was sitting up, with his bandaged hands round a bowl of fish soup he hadn't finished. He woke up to Calvin asking a question.

"Okay, but practically," Calvin was saying. "*Practically.* How is he not dead?"

The long room of the inn was full. All thirty-five of them at the tables under the lamps, with the kites humming on the roof outside and the gulls settling. Milli was asleep against Hana with his bandaged arms in his lap. Gabe was at the end of the far table with the wrapped shield against his knee. He hadn't said a word since the morning.

"Milli had two potions and couldn't drink them," said Calvin. "One night. He's been doing that every day for a hundred days. So does he just—have a heal? A proper one? Can he *heal*?"

"Is that even possible?" said Salty, from the tanks' end of the table. He held up one enormous hand and counted on it. "I'm a Guardian. Nothing I've got heals. Not one thing. Taunt, guard, the big swing. That's it." He looked down the table. "Jared?"

"Nothing," said Jared. His voice was rough with sleep. "We hold. Somebody else fixes us. That's the deal."

"Same," said Azac.

"Same," said Hako.

Gabe didn't say anything.

"Tanks don't heal," said Salty. "That's the whole point of having five."

"He's not just a tank," said Hyvred. "He's a Paladin. Thea read it. On three."

"Hidden class," said Chris. He didn't need his notebook for that one.

"Tank and heal," said Dennis. He sounded relieved, the way you sound when a sum finally comes out. "Right. He heals himself. Okay. So it's fine, then. Sort of."

"It's not fine," said Rachael.

She was at the healers' end of the long table, with both hands flat on the wood. She'd hardly said anything since the morning. Her frown had come back, deeper than before.

"Everybody keeps saying that," she said. "*He can heal himself.* Like it's the end of it. Like that makes it okay." She looked down the table at all of them. "Healing doesn't take it back. It doesn't mean it didn't happen. It just closes it."

"Rae—" said Dennis.

"Ask Calvin."

Everybody looked at Calvin. Calvin's right hand had gone to his left forearm, the way it sometimes did, without him seeming to know. The arm the Woolback Ram had broken on floor 1, in two places, on the grass by the wall. The arm Rachael had held together with cold water running along the bone.

"It hurt more going back than it did breaking," said Calvin quietly. "The breaking was fast. You don't even feel it properly, at first. But when she fixed it, I felt every bit go back. Both places. Slowly." He rubbed the arm. "I didn't say. It seemed rude. She'd just saved it."

"I closed Rui Xiang's leg last night," said Malcolm. "It took me a minute. He felt the whole minute."

"Worse coming shut than going in," said Rui Xiang. He said it to his cup.

Milli, half asleep against Hana with his bandaged arms in his lap, opened his eyes. "The cutting was fast," he said. "Last night. All of it. The closing this morning was slow."

"So when he gets hurt," said Rachael, "he feels it. All of it. Same as us. And then he heals it himself, and he feels that too. Coming back together. Slowly." She stopped. She looked at her hands on the table. "And there's something else. Something I haven't told anybody."

The table went quiet.

"When you heal somebody," said Rachael, "you feel it. An echo. Just for a second. Wherever you're holding them together, you feel it in the same place on yourself." She didn't look up. "Azac's ribs, floor 1, I felt in my ribs. Calvin's arm, I felt in my left arm. Every heal. Every single one since the first day. That's what it costs. Not just the mana. You get a bit of it back."

"*Rae*," said Calvin.

"It's fine. It's a second. I'm used to it." She still didn't look up. "I never said, because it's mine to carry, and you don't put it on the person you're fixing."

"I thought it was just me," said Malcolm, very quietly. "Last night. Rui Xiang's leg. I felt it in my calf. I thought I was doing it wrong."

"You're not doing it wrong," said Rachael. "That's how it works."

Then she looked up. Not at Malcolm. Down the long table, past all of them, out of the dark window, at the needle in the middle of the sky.

"So think about it," she said. "When he heals himself, he's the one hurt *and* he's the one healing. He gets the wound. Then he holds it closed, and he feels it going back, slowly, every bit. And then he gets the echo of it, in the same place, on top." Her voice went very flat. "Twice. Every wound, he feels twice. At least. Alone. In the middle of a fight, probably, fast and ugly, while something's still hitting him."

"A heal in a fight's the worst kind," said Xola softly. Water moved round her fingers on the table, restless. "You can't do it gently. You just shove it shut."

"Pain doesn't care whether you can fix it later," said Kei. Round his neck, Dose had gone very still.

Nobody said anything.

Jared looked down at his own hands, wrapped in strips of somebody's shirt, raw from the hawser. They throbbed. They'd throbbed all day. Rachael had offered to close them that afternoon and he'd said *later, you're tired*. He thought about closing them himself, if he could. Feeling the skin come back, slowly. And then feeling it again.

At the end of the far table, Gabe had put his hand flat on the wrapped shield against his knee, and left it there.

"Every day," said Rachael. "For a hundred and two days. Twice."

"Right," said Calvin, after a long time. Very quietly, for Calvin. "Yeah." He rubbed his left arm again. Then he looked up, and his face had changed, gone sharp. "But that's the other thing I don't get. *How?* We all stood in the class hall on day one. Quill had three doors. Sword, staff, bow. There wasn't a Paladin door. Nobody got offered one. So how do you even *get* a class that isn't on the list?"

Nobody knew.

Jared watched it go round the room, from face to face, and land nowhere. A hundred and two days, and none of them had ever asked. It had just been one more thing Voyager had done, like clearing floor 1 on the first day. Impossible, and somehow normal, because it was him.

It was Azac who started.

He'd been sitting back with his arms folded, the way he did before he told a story. Now he leaned forward.

"Hidden means hidden," he said. "It's not on the list because you don't pick it. Something gives it to you." He tapped the table. "In every story I've ever read, and I've read a lot of them, there's a hidden version of something. A class that's two classes at once. And you never get it at a desk. You get it out *there*." He nodded at the dark window, the bridges, the lamps of Tether laced together with rope. "From something nobody else bothered with."

"A quest," said Malcolm.

Everybody looked at him. Malcolm had his face on, the doom one. But his eyes were busy.

"It'd be a quest," he said. "It'd have to be. A long one, probably. In steps. Somewhere nobody goes, because if it was somewhere people went, people would've found it." He turned his cup round on the table. "And it'd have conditions. Things that make it hard to start on purpose. Things you'd only do by accident." He paused. "Or if you already knew."

"Knight and Cleric," said Azac. "Tank and heal. That's what a Paladin is in every story. So the quest takes a Knight and teaches it to heal. Somewhere. Early."

"Early," said Hyvred.

She'd gone very still.

"Floor 1," she said slowly. "In the Fold. On the trail. There were warm marks on the rocks, hand-shaped, where he'd stopped. I didn't know what they were. I thought he'd put his hands on the rocks to rest." Her voice dropped. "He was healing himself. On floor 1. On the first day or the second."

"So it started on floor 1," said Malcolm. "Before day two."

The whole room had gone quiet. Even the gulls on the roof.

And then Xav, who hadn't said a word all evening, said: "The chapel."

Nobody moved.

"A stone knight," said Malcolm, very slowly, the way you'd put down something you didn't want to break. "Holding up a lantern. In a chapel nobody goes to. Lit." He looked at Xav. "*This vigil has already been kept.*"

"A knight who keeps a light," said Azac.

Jared watched him put it together. The way he put together the end of a story, all the pieces at once, his whole face changing.

"That's it," said Azac. "That's the first step. That's how a Paladin starts. And he did it before we'd even left Hearthstead. Before most of us had picked a class."

"Then there's more," said Malcolm.

He said it flatly. Pessimistically. The way he said everything, as if it was bad news. Jared thought maybe it was.

"If there's one hidden class," said Malcolm, "there's more. There's always more. One for every kind of thing a person can be, probably. And we've walked past every single one of them for a hundred days, because nobody told us to look." He turned his cup again. "There could be a chapel on every floor. We'd never know."

"There's always more," Azac agreed. "Hidden classes. Hidden quests. Things that only open if you go somewhere stupid at the wrong time of day and do the one thing nobody would think of."

"Classic," said Hako, quietly.

At the end of Chris's table, Niko had looked up. He had one of his spellbooks open next to his plate, the way he had every evening since floor 1. A Knight who read magic books after dinner, and nobody had ever asked why.

"A knight," said Niko carefully, "with spellbooks. Would that be—"

"That's a Spellsword," said Azac at once. "In about nine stories I've read."

Niko looked down at his book. He didn't say anything else. But he didn't go back to reading it, either.

"Then we look," said Drew.

He'd been listening the whole time from the head of the table. Now he got up and went to the big slate by the inn door, the one the innkeeper used for ferry times and the price of fish. He wiped a corner of it clean with his sleeve and wrote, in big square letters:

*HIDDEN QUESTS. IF IT'S STRANGE, WRITE IT HERE.*

"Every floor," said Drew. "From now on. Anything that doesn't fit. A statue. A shrine. An old book in a corner. An NPC who says something odd and then won't say it again. A light that's on when it shouldn't be. You see it, you write it, and we go and look." He turned round. "*Together.* Not alone."

"Not alone," said several people, quietly.

Chris had already turned to a clean page. At the top he wrote *HIDDEN*, and underneath it, first: *chapel, floor 1, east fields. Stone knight, lantern. Kept.*

Jared watched him write it. He was the one who watched things, steady, while everybody else talked. So he saw what Chris did next, which was stop, with the pencil still on the page, and look across the room at Thea.

She was at the end of her party's table with the little lantern, the way she always was. She met his eye.

"He found it on day one," said Chris. Very low. Nearly to himself. "Before any of us knew there were classes. Before anybody knew anything. He walked straight to it." He looked down at the page. "And Milli said this morning, if it'd been something he'd never seen before, he wouldn't be here. And Voyager fights things nobody's ever seen. First. Every floor." He was very quiet. "The bonus explains how fast. It doesn't explain how *well*." He looked back up at her. "Or how he knew where to look."

Thea held his look for a long moment.

Then she nodded, once, very slightly. As if he'd said something she'd been waiting a long time for somebody else to say.

***

They rebuilt the Thornback span over the next four days.

Otta came out from Tether with her apprentices and half the hunters. They drilled a new hole for the post, deeper than the old one, into sound rock a metre back from the edge. They poured it round with lead, smoking, and let it set a whole day. They brought the chain across on the hawser, length by length, eleven joins, and then a second line, and then the rails. Then planks, new ones, pale yellow wood that smelled of resin, and every one of them carried across by hand.

Every joining link on that span had a rose on it. Jared made sure. Five petals and a dot, on the inside of the weld, where nobody would see unless they looked.

On the last afternoon, when it was done, Otta walked out onto it to the middle and stood there, a big grey-haired woman in a burn-scarred apron, a hundred metres over the cloud. She jumped up and down on the new planks three times. The span swung. She held the rail chain and looked down at the nearest joining link by her hand and the little iron rose on it.

"Huh," she said.

She walked back. At the near end, by the new post, she stopped beside Jared and looked at him.

"Had a knight in," she said. "A month back."

Jared went still.

"Lost his shield to the bird. First time up the Spindle." Otta looked out at the needle in the middle of the Reaches. "Came in with the straps still buckled on his arm and nothing in them. Bought a barrel lid off me. Twelve silver." She sniffed. "Lost that too. Bought another. Pumped my bellows for two weeks to pay for it. Never said a word the whole fortnight, hardly. Just pumped." She paused. "Good at it. Steady."

Gabe was standing behind Jared. Jared heard him stop breathing.

"He hasn't got a shield," said Gabe.

Otta turned round and looked at him. Up, because Gabe was taller.

"He's got a barrel lid," she said. "He'll have lost that one too, by now. Everybody does."

Gabe stood there in the wolf coat in the afternoon wind on the edge of Thornback span. Strapped across his back, where it had been since floor 3, was the big round shape wrapped in sacking. Dark grey Fen iron with a lamp in the middle and lines going out from it like the lines round a sun. And a rim of raw, bare, unfinished edge, waiting.

"Otta," said Gabe. "Can I use your forge tonight?"

***

They did it in one night, Gabe and Jared, at the big forge at the back of the Linkworks. Otta didn't say anything about bad luck.

Gabe had the Kiln iron. Bars of it, dark red and heavy, from the thing in the furnace hall on floor 4. When they heated it, Jared saw something he'd never seen iron do. It didn't just go red. It went red from the *inside*, slow, as if there was a coal buried in the middle of it that woke up when the heat reached it. It glowed through the surface like light through skin. Gabe held it in the tongs and looked at it for a long time.

"He stood in front of the thing this came from," he said. "Twice. In the fire."

"I know, man."

"I want it on the edge. Where things hit."

So they drew the Kiln iron out into a long bar and bent it into a ring, a little bigger than the shield, and laid it red-hot round the raw edge of the Fen iron. And Gabe hammered it on. Slowly. All the way round. Jared held the shield steady on the anvil with the tongs and turned it a little at a time. They didn't talk. The Linkworks rang. The Kiln iron went from red to dark as it cooled, and closed round the edge of the Fen iron like a hand closing. And when it was cold, it was the colour of old dried blood, with something faint in it still, if you looked, like the last ember in a grate.

They held it up between them at four in the morning.

It was the most beautiful thing Jared had ever seen anyone make. He'd seen Otta make a hundred metres of chain in four days and he'd made eleven links with roses on them, and none of it came close. Round. Dark grey, with a dull sheen like a puddle at dusk. A lamp in the middle. Sun-lines going out from it so fine you could only see them when the light caught. And round the edge, a rim of dark red Kiln iron, with an ember in it.

"Who's it for?" said Jared.

He knew. Everybody knew. He asked anyway, because somebody should, out loud, at four in the morning, once.

Gabe looked at the shield for a long time.

"Somebody using a barrel lid," he said.

Jared laughed. He couldn't help it. It was the second time in four days he'd laughed until he couldn't stand. He sat down on the floor of the Linkworks with his back against the anvil and laughed, and after a second Gabe sat down beside him with the shield across his knees and laughed too, and Otta came in from the back to see what was happening, and looked at them, and went out again.

"He owes me a postcard," said Jared, when he could talk. "You know that? Floor 1. I said it in World. *Ice if that's you, you owe me a postcard.*"

"He owes everybody something," said Gabe.

"Yeah." Jared wiped his eyes. "Yeah, man. He does."

They sat on the floor of the Linkworks with the finished shield between them. Behind them, the big forge burned down to coals. It was the colour of the Kiln iron in the rim.

Before he left, Jared took a punch from Otta's bench and turned the shield over, and found the place on the inside of the rim, right where a hand would go. While Gabe watched, without asking, he worked a tiny rose into the Kiln iron there. Five petals and a dot. So small nobody would ever see it unless they looked.

"So he knows," said Jared, "which bit's mine."
