Higu didn't count the steps. Newish did that. Higu measured them.

It wasn't the same thing, and she'd given up trying to explain the difference to anybody but Drew, who understood it without being told, the way he understood most things about her. Newish counted so everybody would know how far. Higu measured so she'd know where. How deep each step was. How much it turned. Whether the stair went straight down under the floor above or wandered off sideways through the rock, the way the stair from the Glass had, so that the door at the bottom came out a long way from where the door at the top went in. She did it with her feet, in the dark, without thinking, and at the bottom she drew it.

The stair from five went straight down. Two hundred and forty-four steps, all the same depth, no turns. It had been built by somebody who didn't want to be on it any longer than they had to.

She'd known that. She'd drawn it once already, going up. But she drew it again in her head on the way down anyway, step by step, because she'd been thinking about something else the whole way down and she wanted something to do with her feet that wasn't that.

*What do you remember?*

She'd asked it at the bottom of nine stairs. She'd drawn nine short lines in the left-hand column of the rock, and on the ninth one, at the top of the Spindle in the wind, he'd stopped in the middle of *never* and said *once*, and she'd written two words beside a line for the first time. *GULL ISLAND.* She'd asked him if she should change the others, and he'd said no, they were still true.

This one was going to be different. She'd known that since the Long Acre, when she drew the floors going down on the back of the rock one under the other like a ladder, and got to the fourth, and found that her chalk didn't want to draw anything beside it at all.

She knew where he'd been on four. Everybody did. He'd been in the Red City for six weeks before any of them came up, and he'd killed the Quiet in the first square, and stood in the fire in the furnace hall twice, and sat every evening on the high terrace by the door until he'd worn the stone bright. She'd seen the worn place. She'd seen the dust in the House of Ash. That was this time.

The first time, he'd been here too. The first time, he'd come up the stair at the top with the rest of them, and something had happened. She didn't know what. She'd never asked. But she'd watched his face on the night of the aurora, when Thea came back from the rock with her hand still on his forearm, and she'd watched it every time anybody said *floor 4* since, and it did something. Every time. Like a door in a draught.

So she'd thought about it, all the way down the dark. How you asked a man what he remembered about a place without asking him what had happened there. She thought she had it, by the bottom.

Tiger put his hand flat on the door. He held it there. Then he pushed.

***

The furnace hall was cold.

That was the first thing. It had been hot every time Higu had been in it, going up. Hot and dark and roaring, with the furnace mouth glowing at the far end and the heat coming off it like a hand on your face. Now it was cold, and dark, and absolutely silent, and smelled of old iron and old ash and the faint sweet dead smell that the whole of floor 4 smelled of, underneath the dust.

Thea lifted the lantern. The amber light went out across the floor.

Four of them. Lying face down on the stone floor of the hall, in a rough line from the door to the furnace mouth, the way they'd fallen. Huge. Iron and fired clay, three times a man's height, with their great arms out and their furnace hearts dark. Each one with a hole through the heart. The first one grey with dust, so thick you could hardly see the iron. The last one with less. The one from day 86 that the thirty-five had killed together, with Xav moving once into the open heart. And the fourth one, the one they'd killed on the way out, twenty minutes, Gabe in the shape.

No fifth.

"On four, nothing goes anywhere," said Higu.

She said it out loud because somebody had to, and because it was the kind of thing she said. Calvin had put it on World once, a long time ago, in capitals. *THE DEAD STAY ON FOUR.* She'd put it in a column on a map. It was true on four the way the sun was hot on six.

Bee, at the back, had her face in Hako's shoulder already. Hako had his hand on the back of her head. He didn't say *classic*. He didn't say anything.

Higu sat down on the bottom step of the stair with her back against the doorframe, and took the rock out of her pack, and wet the chalk.

Nine short lines in the left-hand column. *GULL ISLAND* beside the last one. *WIND* in the middle. On the right, in Chris's words that she'd been told to put, *THE SONG HAS A NEW VERSE*, and in hers, *HESSA. ALIVE.*

She looked up. He was standing a little way out into the hall, with Thea, in the lantern light, looking at the four dead Kilns. He'd stood in the fire of two of them. Alone. On the forty-fourth day and the sixty-sixth. She'd seen the scorch marks on the floor where he'd stood, going up. They were still there. Of course they were.

"Ice," said Higu. "What do you remember?"

He didn't answer.

She hadn't thought he would. Not to that. She let it sit for exactly as long as she'd decided on the stair she'd let it sit, which was the time it took her to draw the tenth line in the left-hand column, short and straight, under the other nine.

"Where," she said. "Not how."

He turned his head and looked at her.

It was the thing she'd said on the Long Acre, in the barn, with the straw man frozen at the end of it and nobody knowing what to do. *That's all I've got. Where. Not how.* She'd said it because it was true and because it was the only thing she had. She was saying it now because she'd thought about it for two hundred and forty-four steps and it was still the only thing she had. She didn't want the how. She'd never wanted the how. She just wanted to know where on the map not to put her feet, and where he wasn't going to be able to put his.

He looked at her a long time. Then he looked up. Not at anything in the hall. Up, through the roof of it, through the rock, up the whole height of the city above them, terrace on terrace, to the top.

"The square," he said.

Higu wrote it. *THE FIRST SQUARE.* Beside the tenth line.

"And six weeks," said Ice. Quietly. "Nobody talked. Above a whisper. The whole six weeks. Not once."

She wrote that too. *SIX WEEKS. NOBODY TALKED.* Her hand was very steady. She was proud of that, afterwards.

"That's all," said Ice.

"That's plenty," said Higu, and drew a line under it.

Then she looked round, for the middle column. For what was true. She didn't have to wait. She never did. Somebody always said it.

"I'm talking," said Calvin.

He said it loudly. Not shouting. Just at the volume Calvin said everything, which was the volume of a man who had never once in his life worried about being overheard. It went out across the cold dark furnace hall and hit the far wall and came back.

"I'm talking," he said again, because the echo had liked it. "On four. Out loud. Look at me go."

Higu looked at Ice. She didn't mean to. She'd meant to look at the rock.

Ice's face had done the door-in-a-draught thing. And then it had done something else, that she'd never seen it do, and couldn't have drawn. It had come open. All of it, at once, the way a shutter comes open when the wind catches it, and stayed open, and he'd turned away so fast toward the dark that the lantern light only caught the side of it. Thea didn't turn with him. She didn't need to. She just moved her hand from his to the small of his back, and left it there.

"That's true," said Higu.

She wrote it in the middle column, in capitals, and underlined it twice.

*CALVIN: TALKING.*

***

They went up through the Red City the way they'd come down through it, going up. Which was to say, slowly, and all of them together, and with Higu at the front beside Tiger, because the city was a map and she was the one who'd drawn it.

Floor 4 hadn't changed. That was the strange thing. That was the thing she'd known in her head and not in her feet. Every floor above it had changed. Tell had changed and the Salt had changed and Hythe had changed; Highcrook had a new tree and Wendmere had a keeper; Evermorrow had bells. Gull Island had a fire lit. Everywhere they'd been, people had gone on living after they left, and when the thirty-six came back down the stair the floors were different, because people were different, because time had happened to them.

Nothing had happened to floor 4.

It was a permanent red dusk, exactly as it had been, with no sun anywhere you could see and the light coming from everywhere and nowhere, the colour of the inside of a hand held up to a lamp. The dry river at the bottom of the canyon was dry. The terraces went up both walls in steps, cut into the red rock, with the houses cut into the backs of them and the stairs between, four storeys and five, empty, every window dark. The dry wind came up the canyon from somewhere and went somewhere else and carried dust. And there were things in the dust that had been there since before they came. A broken pot on a step. A doorway with a cloth half across it. A dead dust-hound in a corner, flat and grey and folded like paper, that Rui Xiang had killed on their twentieth day here and nobody had dealt with.

And her arrows.

She'd forgotten them. She'd made them the first week, going up, with chalk, on the corners of the stairs, so nobody would get lost: small neat arrows at the height of her own hand, pointing *up*. Every stair, every turn, from the furnace hall to the first square and on to the high terrace. Over four hundred days of dry wind and dust, on a floor where nothing went anywhere, and every single arrow was still there. Exactly as bright. As if she'd drawn them yesterday.

She stopped at the first one and looked at it for a long time.

"You did those," said Drew, behind her.

"Day sixty-seven."

"They're still pointing up."

"Things do, here." She put her finger on it. It didn't smudge. Floor 4 wasn't a place where chalk smudged. "I didn't think anybody'd ever see them again."

"You did, though."

She looked at him. He was looking at the arrow, not at her, with his bare hands in his pockets, the way he stood when he was thinking about something and didn't want anybody to know he was. Drew had stopped wearing his bladed knuckles on the stairs since the Gate. He said he'd rather have his hands.

"Leave them," said Drew. "Somebody might come up."

She left them.

***

The dust-hounds found them at the second ford, where the dry river turned under the north wall and the canyon narrowed.

It was Calvin's fault. Everybody said so, afterwards, including Calvin. He'd been talking since the furnace hall. Not to anybody. Just talking. About the dust, and the red, and the fact that he'd once been so scared on this floor that he'd gone a whole week without saying a word above a whisper, which nobody believed, and which, Higu happened to know, was true. And dust-hounds hunted by sound. And Calvin had been the loudest thing on floor 4 for two hours.

They came out of the dust along the riverbed in a pack. Seven of them. Rock-coloured, long and low, with flat heads and no ears that you could see and a way of running that made no noise on the stones at all, so that the first thing you knew was the dust moving wrong.

Higu saw it move wrong. She said "Left," and put her hand on Tiger's arm.

And nothing happened.

That was the other thing about floor 4. They weren't twenty any more. They were sixty, and more. The dust-hounds came out of the dust in a grey rush along the riverbed straight at the loudest thing on the floor, and Gabe stepped out of the line without hurrying and put Hearthwall down on the stones in front of Calvin like a man putting a door in a doorway, and the first two hit it and stopped. Just stopped. Like waves on a wall. The warm shield didn't move. Behind it, Calvin hadn't even stopped talking. Azac took the third with his shield edge and it went over sideways into the dust and didn't get up. Rui Xiang had the fourth before Higu saw him move. Winter put two arrows into the fifth from forty metres back with frost along them that turned the dust white where it fell. The sixth and seventh looked at the wall, and at what was left of the other five, and stopped in the riverbed with their flat heads down and their flanks going.

And then they did a thing Higu had never seen a dust-hound do.

They backed away. Slowly. One step, then another, then a third, with their eyes on Gabe, the way a dog backs away from a man with a stick. And then they turned and went off down the dry river into the dust without a sound, and didn't come back.

"Huh," said Gabe.

"They learned," said Rui Xiang.

"In four hundred days?" said Hako.

"In one fight," said Rui Xiang. He was looking down the riverbed after them. "They just did it. On four." He frowned, as if something was wrong with the sky. "Things don't learn on four."

Higu wrote it down on her slate, not the rock. *DUST-HOUNDS BACKED OFF.* With a question mark.

She noticed, writing it, that Ice hadn't moved.

He'd been in the middle of the line with Thea. When the dust moved wrong he'd turned toward it, the way he turned toward everything, his hand going to his shield. And then he'd stopped. He'd stood where he was, in the middle, with his hand on the shield's edge and his whole body pointed at the hounds like a needle at north, and let Gabe step out, and let Azac, and let Rui Xiang and Winter. He hadn't gone in front.

He wasn't *not in front* because anybody had told him. Nobody had said it since the Long Barn. She'd written it once on the rock, *ICE: NOT IN FRONT*, small, the night of the keeping, and she'd lifted it herself, on the night he told her *where*. It wasn't a rule any more. She'd watched him since. Mostly he went in front anyway. Mostly that was right.

He hadn't, here. He'd stood on the riverbed in the red dusk with his hand on his shield and let them.

She didn't write that. She drew a small figure in the middle of a line, on the slate, standing still, and didn't label it.

***

They passed the House of Ash in the afternoon, on the third terrace.

You could see it from the stair. A long low doorway cut into the red rock, with carving over it that Cursiffe had read the first time: *Where they came to say things.* And inside, down the long hall, past the cells with their grilles carved with leaves, there was light.

Not much. You had to look for it. But it was there, the way it had been on the ninth day when Hyvred found it and came out and sat on the step with her face very still. The dust on the floor of every cell, all the way down the hall, was gold. It lay there in the red dusk glowing, very faintly, as if somebody had once lit something at the far end that had been too much for the room, and the room had soaked it up, and still had it, four hundred and some days later. On four, nothing went anywhere. Not even light.

Hyvred stopped at the door and looked in. Then she looked at Ice. Then she looked away.

Thea didn't look in at all.

Higu noticed that. The first time, on floor 4, going up, Thea had gone in there alone and stayed an hour. Malcolm had seen her come out and not speak to anybody. Higu had seen her that evening on the high terrace writing in her little book. This time Thea walked past the doorway of the House of Ash with her hand in Ice's and her eyes on the stair ahead and didn't turn her head, the way you walk past a window in a house where somebody might be listening.

Ice didn't look either. But his hand, in hers, went white at the knuckles, and stayed white, until they were up the stair and round the corner and the gold was out of sight.

Higu drew the doorway on her slate. A small rectangle on the third terrace. She coloured it in with the side of the chalk.

***

The first square was at the top of the city, just under the high terrace.

There were three ways into it from below. Higu had mapped all three the first time. The main stair, up from the third terrace through a cut in the rock, that came out on the south side of the square opposite the fountain. A lane on the east that ran along under the windows and came in at the corner. And the long way: up the far stair on the north wall, across the little stone bridge over the gap, and along the high terraces on the north side, keeping to the roofs, which brought you out above the square and round it to the high terrace without ever setting foot on the stones. That was his way. She knew it was. She'd found his trail on it the first time, going up, chalk-pale prints on the roof-edges where he'd wound cloth round his boots. Six weeks of it. He'd gone round the first square every day, the long way, so that his trail never crossed it.

She stopped at the foot of the main stair. The whole line stopped behind her.

"There's a long way," she said. She didn't turn round. She was looking up the stair, at the cut in the rock and the red light at the top of it. "Up the far stair. The bridge. Along the roofs on the north side. It comes out on the high terrace. You don't go through." She paused. "You used it."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't mind which," said Higu. "It's just where."

She heard him come up the line behind her. She knew it was him without turning; she'd have known his step in the dark on any floor of the tower. He stopped beside her at the foot of the main stair, with Thea, and looked up it.

"Through," he said.

"Okay," said Higu. "Through."

***

She'd drawn the first square twice. Once going up, from the top of this stair, on the sixty-sixth day, a few minutes after Calvin stopped talking. Once on the back of the rock on the Long Acre, from memory, when she drew the floors going down like a ladder and got to the fourth and her chalk wouldn't do anything.

She drew it a third time now, in her head, coming up out of the cut into the red light, because that was what she did when she came into a place. Twenty metres across, near enough square. Red stone paving, worn in a path from the corners to the middle. Four storeys of empty windows on every side, dark, with the long-necked carrion birds sitting in a row along the top of the north side, not moving. The high stair coming down into the square on the west, from the terrace and the door. The lane coming in at the north-east corner. The main stair, behind her, on the south. And in the middle, the fountain. Dry. A round stone basin, knee-high, with a column in the middle that had been something once, a figure, worn to nothing. An iron bell on a post beside it, the size of a bucket, that had rung exactly once in four hundred years, as far as anybody knew, on the twenty-fifth day, at dusk.

And the Quiet.

It was still there. Of course it was. It lay where it had fallen, at the foot of the fountain on the east side, where Calvin had stopped talking. Long and grey, too many joints, its limbs folded down onto the stones the way a crane folds, its smooth faceless head turned a little on one side. It had dried, the first time they saw it, after six weeks in the wind. It had dried more. It was the colour of the stones now, nearly. It lay there in the red dusk in the middle of the city with a hole through its chest where its heart had been, the only sound it had ever made, and nothing had come for it in four hundred and some days, and nothing ever would.

Calvin stopped talking.

He hadn't meant to. Higu saw it happen. He came up out of the cut behind her, still going, about something, about the fact that the first time he'd come into this square he'd said *there's a fountain* like a child at the seaside and Rachael had had her fist in his collar the whole way across. And then he saw it again, lying there, and his mouth just stopped. The same as the first time. As if it still had something of the thing it did. As if, even dead, even dried to the colour of the stones, it could still reach out and take the sound out of the nearest thing.

He stood there for a moment with his mouth open and no sound in it.

Then he shut it. And opened it again. And said, loudly, at it, across the square:

"*No*."

Everybody looked at him.

"No," said Calvin again. He was a bit red. "Not doing that. Did that last time. I'm talking. It doesn't get to—" He waved a hand at it. "It's *dead*. Dec killed it. It doesn't get a vote." He turned round to all of them, coming up out of the cut behind him. "Nobody whispers on four. New rule. I just made it. You can all talk as loud as you like and if anybody whispers I'm going to stand next to them and shout until they stop."

"That's not how rules work," said Rachael.

"It's how *mine* work."

And he walked out into the first square talking, louder than before, about the fountain, and the carrion birds, and how he'd always thought the column in the middle had been a fish, and Rachael went after him with her fist out to grab his collar and then didn't grab it, and just walked beside him instead, and the rest of them came up out of the cut and spread out across the square behind them, the way they had the first time, in a ring.

And Ice walked through the middle of them to the east wall.

***

Higu watched him go.

She didn't decide to. Her eyes did it. She had the square in her head, drawn, every line of it, and she watched him cross it the way you'd watch a pencil cross a map. From the top of the south stair, straight across the paving, past the fountain on its south side, past the Quiet, not looking at it. Not to the high stair, where everybody else was heading. Not to the corner, or the lane. To the east wall. To a place on the east wall under the windows, a little left of the middle, where there was nothing at all. Red stone. A blank stretch of it between two dark doorways, with the dust drifted against its foot.

He stopped there. A pace from it. And stood.

Thea had gone with him. She'd had to; she had his hand. She stood beside him for a moment. Then she let go, and stepped back. One step. So that he was standing on his own in front of the wall, and she was just behind his shoulder, the way you'd stand behind somebody at a grave.

Higu looked from the wall to the foot of the high stair. Measuring. She couldn't help it. It was the only thing her eyes knew how to do when they didn't know what else to do.

Twenty metres. Near enough. Twenty paces, if she walked it.

She'd walk it later. She already knew.

Nobody said anything. The ring of them round the fountain had gone quiet, all of them, the way they'd gone quiet the first time, standing over the Quiet. Except this time they weren't looking at the Quiet. They were looking at a man standing in front of a blank red wall, looking at it as if he could see something written on it in a hand nobody else could read.

Even Calvin had stopped. He was halfway to the high stair, talking, and he'd turned round mid-word and seen, and stopped. He stood there by the fountain with his mouth a little open.

And then Rachael walked over.

Higu didn't think Rachael knew she was doing it. That was the thing. She'd seen Rachael's face do this once before, in this square, on the sixty-sixth day: the frown, the puzzled one, the one that meant her body had done something and hadn't told her why. She'd taken Calvin's left hand that day, standing by the Quiet, and held it, and not let go, and not known why, and never mentioned it after. Now she was walking across the first square toward the east wall with the same frown, not fast, with her hands in her gloves at her sides, and Higu could see her trying to work out where she was going, and not being able to.

She stopped at the wall. A pace to Ice's left. She looked at it too.

"Dec," she said. "What are we looking at?"

"A wall," said Ice.

Rachael looked at it a bit longer.

"It's a wall," she agreed.

"Yes."

She didn't go. She stood there with her frown on and her gloves on, a pace away, not touching him. Rachael didn't touch people unless she was mending them. Everybody knew that. Higu knew it. Ice knew it better than anybody. He didn't look at her. But Higu saw his shoulders go, a little, the way a rope goes when somebody takes some of the weight on the other end of it.

"I'm still not asking," said Rachael.

"I know."

"I'm just going to stand here a minute."

"Okay," said Ice.

***

And then Calvin came over.

Of course he did. He'd been standing by the fountain with his mouth open for nearly a whole minute, which for Calvin was a lifetime, and he couldn't bear it any longer. He came across the square toward the wall talking again, because he'd made a rule and he wasn't going to be the first to break it. "Okay, so is this a thing? Are we doing walls now? Because I can do a wall. I'm *great* at walls. Watch." And as he came up beside Rachael, without looking, without even stopping talking, he held his left hand out to her.

He did it like a joke. Higu could see that. It was a joke between them, a small one, from the first time; a thing he did in squares, since then, and only in squares, and only to her. *You held my hand once. In a square. For no reason. I'm just saying it's available.* He held it out to her sideways with his palm up and his eyes on the wall and his mouth still going.

Rachael took it.

Without looking. Without stopping frowning. She just reached out and took Calvin's left hand in her gloved right one, the way she had on the sixty-sixth day, and held it. And the three of them stood there in a row in front of the east wall of the first square of the Red City, Ice and Rachael and Calvin, with Rachael in the middle holding Calvin's left hand at the end of his whole left arm.

Higu looked at Ice.

She'd never seen anybody's face do what his face did then. She'd seen faces stop. His had stopped often enough. This was the opposite of stopping. It was as if everything that had been held still in it for a very long time had been let go of all at once, and didn't know where to go, and went everywhere. He didn't move. He didn't make a sound. He just looked sideways, very slowly, at Rachael's hand holding Calvin's hand, beside him, at the wall. And looked. And looked.

Thea, behind his shoulder, had put her own hand over her mouth.

And then, from across the square, from beside the fountain, loud enough to bounce off four storeys of empty windows and set every carrion bird on the north roof shuffling, Dennis shouted:

"*RACHAEL!*"

Ice flinched.

His whole body. Higu saw it. Every part of him went at once, the way a man goes when something hits him from behind, and he turned his head toward the voice so fast that Thea's hand came off her mouth and went to his arm. And Dennis came across the square at a run. Running. Toward Rachael, by the wall, shouting her name.

"*Rachael*, are you holding Calvin's *hand*? At a *wall*? Is this a thing now? Should I be worried? Because I'm not going to lie, I'm a bit worried—"

He got there. He stopped. Out of breath, a bit, from twenty metres, because Dennis didn't run unless something was on fire or free. He stood on Rachael's other side and looked at the three of them, and at Thea, and at the wall.

"What are we looking at?" said Dennis.

"A wall," said Calvin.

"Great," said Dennis. "Love that. Love a wall." He looked at it. "It's not a very good wall."

"Dennis," said Rachael. "Shut up."

"I'm just saying, if we're going to stand in a line and look at one, there are better ones. There was a really good one on ten—"

And Ice laughed.

Higu didn't know what it was at first. Neither did anybody. It came out of him like something breaking. A sound in his chest that wasn't a word and wasn't a sob and wasn't anything she'd heard a person make before. He put his hand over his face. His shoulders went. And then it was a laugh, a real one, helpless, the kind you can't stop once it's started, standing in front of the east wall of the first square with Rachael on his left holding Calvin's hand and Dennis on the other side of her complaining about the wall. He laughed until he had to put his other hand on the wall to stay up.

"What's funny?" said Dennis. "What did I say?"

"Nothing," said Ice. He couldn't stop. "Nothing. It's a terrible wall. You're right. It's a terrible wall."

"*Thank* you."

And Thea laughed too. Behind his shoulder, with her hand on his arm. Out loud, short and surprised, the way she did now sometimes. Dennis turned round and opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Thea.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You were going to say a number."

"I stopped the graph," said Dennis, with dignity. "I said noted. I'm a man of my word."

Thea looked at him, still laughing, with her eyes wet. Then she stepped up beside Ice, to the wall, and put her hand flat on the red stone. The way she'd put it on his head on the step of the Countinghouse. The way you'd put it on something you were coming back for.

And her lips moved.

Higu was close enough to see it, and not close enough to hear. Nobody was close enough to hear but Ice. Two words. Higu was sure of that much. Two small shapes, under her breath, against the wall, the way she'd said thirty-four names under her breath beside the warm shield going up the hall in Tell, so softly nobody could tell they were names. Two words. Then Thea took her hand off the wall.

Ice had stopped laughing. He was looking at her.

He nodded. Once.

Higu didn't ask. She'd never asked. She'd told him on the Long Acre that she only wanted *where*, and she'd meant it, and she meant it now. She stood in the middle of the first square and looked at the five of them standing in front of the east wall. Ice. Thea. Rachael, in the middle, holding Calvin's left hand. Calvin. Dennis, still talking about the wall on ten. All five of them, alive, at a wall, twenty paces from the foot of the high stair.

And she knew, the way you know where north is without a needle, that she was looking at the shape of something. That somewhere, in some other square, on some other day, there had been a wall and some people at it, and it hadn't looked like this.

She didn't know the how. She didn't want it.

She knew the where now.

***

She walked it later, when they were making camp. From the foot of the high stair to the place on the east wall. Twenty paces. She'd known it would be.

Then she sat down on the edge of the dry fountain, with the rock on her knees, back side up, and drew the square.

Properly. The way she'd drawn the Countinghouse, and the Glass by moonlight, and the Down from what everybody told her. The square, near enough square. The fountain in the middle. The bell on its post. The Quiet, a long folded shape at the fountain's foot. The high stair on the west. The lane at the corner. The main stair on the south. The east wall.

And at the east wall, five small figures. She drew them without deciding to. Five little upright lines, in a row, a pace from the wall. And between the second and the fourth, where Rachael stood holding Calvin's hand, a tiny joining stroke.

She looked at it for a long time.

On the day she read the book in Tell, on the step of the Countinghouse, she'd said to Thea: *The left one's not wrong, is it. It's just somewhere else.* And Thea had said *no*. And Higu had gone home and wiped the line down the middle of the rock, so there were two columns with nothing between them, because she'd decided that was what was true.

She'd just drawn a map of the left one. Of the shape of it. On the right-hand side of the rock, in the now. With everybody in it standing up.

She didn't rub it out.

Drew came and sat beside her on the edge of the fountain, with his bare hands between his knees. He looked at the drawing for a while.

"Is it bad?" he said.

Higu thought about it.

"It was," she said. "Somewhere else." She put the chalk down. "It's not here."

Drew nodded slowly. He didn't ask anything else. He was getting good at that too.

***

Calvin had decided they were sleeping in the square.

Nobody argued. There was a good room off the high terrace, the one with the scorch on the ceiling, and a dozen more along the terrace, and every one of them would have been warmer and quieter and further from a dead thing. Calvin said that was the point. Calvin said the first time he'd been on four, he'd slept six weeks in a room with the door shut and not said a word above a whisper the whole time, and nobody believed him, and he didn't care. He said this time he was sleeping in the middle of the first square in the open, and talking until he fell asleep, and talking in his sleep if he could manage it, and anybody who wanted to whisper could go and do it on the high terrace on their own.

So they slept in the square.

"Can we put it somewhere first?" said Bee.

She was sitting with her back to the fountain, as far round from the Quiet as you could get and still be at the fountain. She'd got better. On four, the first time, she couldn't look at anything dead at all. Now she could look at it. She'd been drawing it, in fact, for the last half hour, on Hako's knee, small, from the other side of the basin. But she'd stopped, and put the pencil down.

"It's been lying there a year and a half," said Bee. "On its own. In the middle of everything. Nobody's done anything." She looked at the drawing. "I don't want to leave it lying there when we go. It's not—" She stopped. "I don't know. It's not *right*."

Everybody looked at the Quiet.

"You don't leave it on its own in the dark," said Hako. Quietly. It was the old woman's line from the Long Barn. Everybody knew it.

"It killed things," said Wendy.

"So do we," said Yan.

Nobody had anything to say to that.

"Tomorrow," said Rachael. She was by the east wall, sitting down now, with her back against it and Calvin's head on her knee, because Calvin had lain down there talking and fallen asleep mid-word with his head on her knee and she'd let him. "Before we go up. We'll put it somewhere." She frowned. "Where, though? It's all stone. You can't dig stone."

They all looked at Higu.

Higu didn't have to think about it. She had the map in her head. She'd had it there since the sixty-seventh day.

"Below the square," she said. "On the south side. There's a ledge under the main stair, where the rock's soft. Red. It crumbles. You can dig it with a spade." She'd found it the first week, going up, mapping. She'd noted it because soft rock on four was unusual. "It's the only place on this whole terrace you could."

She looked at Ice, to see if he agreed. She always looked at him, for where.

He was sitting on the edge of the fountain, on the south side, a little way along from her, with his hands on his knees. He'd been there since the wall. Thea beside him. He'd been all right. He'd been better than all right; he'd cooked, on the fountain's edge, barley and an onion, and looked at every face for a second before he filled the bowl, and Higu had seen him do it and written it down, *he counts us*, the way Chris had in the Long Barn.

Now he was looking at her.

His face had stopped.

Not like a clock. Like a man who has been walking in the dark through a house he knows, and has put his foot down where a stair always was, and found that it is still there.

Thea's hand had gone to his. Higu saw it go.

And Higu understood that she had just said a *where*. In the middle of the first square of the Red City, out loud, to everybody. A where that wasn't hers. That had been somebody else's, somewhere else, the way the wall had been somewhere else. She didn't know whose. She didn't know what. She knew it in her stomach the way she knew the depth of a stair in the dark.

She didn't take it back. You couldn't take back a where. It was just where the soft rock was.

"Yes," said Ice. Quietly. "There."

"Okay," said Rachael, from the wall, not looking up, stroking Calvin's hair absently with one gloved hand. "There, then. In the morning."

And she went back to listening to Dennis, beside her, who was explaining to Malcolm in some detail why the wall on ten had been a much better wall.

Higu wrote it in the right-hand column of the rock that night, under *HESSA. ALIVE.*, by the light of Thea's lantern, with thirty-six people talking round her in the first square of the Red City at the tops of their voices, and Calvin talking in his sleep, and the carrion birds on the north roof shuffling and complaining and in the end, after a long time, giving up and putting their heads under their wings.
