On floor 2, Cursiffe's ink had frozen. On floor 6, it dried before he'd finished the word.

He found that out on the hundred-and-thirty-third day. He was sitting on the lip of the pit at Deepwell in the morning with his pen and his little pot of ink. He wrote *TEST* on the back of his hand to see how long it would hold, and the *T* was dry before he'd got to the *S*. Dry, and cracked, and flaking off his skin in the hot wind like old paint.

He looked at it for a long time.

*What you write, holds. For as long as the ink is wet.*

That was Writ. That had been Writ since the first day. On floor 1 he'd written STOP in the air in front of a Woolback and it had held the thing for a second and a half, long enough for Salty to hit it. On floor 2 the ink had frozen in the pot and Yan had given him a hand-warmer to keep it liquid. And every time it had worked, he'd thought: *it's the ink. The ink is the thing. As long as it's wet.*

On floor 6, nothing stayed wet.

"That's a problem," said Yan, from beside him. He'd come up to sit on the lip too. Suzu was round his neck, small and white, with her head up.

"That's a problem," Cursiffe agreed.

He didn't panic. He didn't, as a rule. People thought he was slow, sometimes, because he talked slowly and moved slowly and thought about things for a long time before he did them. He wasn't slow. He was careful. There was a difference, and the difference was usually that careful people were still there afterwards.

He sat on the lip of the pit and thought about it.

Then he went down to the third ring and bought a jar of honey from Ostrel's wife.

***

The statue had been on floor 5, under the third bridge west of Tether. He thought about it a lot.

He'd found it on the hundred-and-sixteenth day, by accident. He'd dropped a knife off the bridge. A cheap one, but his, and it had landed on a ledge below. He'd climbed down to get it, the way you shouldn't on floor 5, with the wind trying to peel you off the rock. And there, on the ledge, tucked in under the span where nobody walking on the bridge would ever see it, there'd been a figure.

Stone. Old. A man, or a woman, or something in between, in a long robe, sitting cross-legged on the ledge with its back to the rock. Weathered smooth by a thousand years of wind. In its lap, held in both hands, a book.

A stone book. Carved shut.

And in the front cover, right in the middle, a keyhole.

He'd written it in World that night, because Drew had said to. *If it's strange, write it here.* Hyvred had written back in capitals. And the next day he'd gone back with Yan's party, all five of them, because nobody went alone. They'd stood on the ledge in the wind, round the stone scribe, and looked at the keyhole.

They'd tried everything. Every key anybody had. Salty had tried a nail. Tiger had tried a lockpick he'd bought off somebody in Tether who swore it opened anything. Wendy had stood back with her arms folded and said *it's a statue, it's not going to open, things on statues don't open*. Yan had looked at it for a long time with his pale cyan eyes and said nothing at all, the way he did when he was seeing something nobody else could.

Cursiffe had sat down on the ledge in front of it and thought.

A stone scribe. A stone book. A keyhole.

*What you write, holds.*

He'd taken out his pen. He'd uncapped his ink. And he'd written, very small, very carefully, across the keyhole itself, in wet black ink:

*OPEN*

The ink held. It sat there on the stone, wet and shining in the grey light, and he held his breath. Writ held for as long as the ink was wet. A second and a half, on floor 1. Longer now. He'd practised.

And the stone book opened.

Not with a crack, or a grinding. Quietly. The cover lifted, the way a real book's cover lifts when you put your thumb under it. Underneath, there were pages. Stone pages, thin as slate. Blank. Except the first one. Carved into it, in letters so old and worn they were barely there, were seven words.

*Write what you saw. Keep it true.*

And a window.

Wendy had said a word that Cursiffe wasn't going to repeat. Tiger had made a high delighted noise. Salty had put an enormous hand on Cursiffe's shoulder and squeezed so hard it hurt.

And Cursiffe had sat on the ledge, with the wind pulling at his hair, and thought about what to write.

*A true thing you witnessed.*

He'd witnessed a lot of things in a hundred and seventeen days. Most of them were true. But he'd known, almost at once, sitting there. There was one thing, from the week before, that he'd been carrying round in his head ever since and didn't know where to put down.

A street in Tether. An old woman on a basket by a winch, with a gull on her hat, looking up at Gabe and saying, *I asked him if you were his. He said brother. Not by blood. Since he was fifteen.* And Gabe sitting down. Right there, on the stones, in the wolf coat, with the rimless shield in his lap. And the rest of them sitting down round him, one by one, without anybody deciding to.

Cursiffe had written it on the first blank stone page. Not all of it. Just the true part. *An old woman said brother, and he sat down in the street, and we sat down with him.* In wet black ink.

And the ink hadn't dried.

He'd watched it. He'd waited for it to go dull and crack, the way ink did. It didn't. It stayed there on the stone page, wet and shining, as if he'd only just written it. He'd touched it with one finger. It didn't smudge. It didn't come off on his skin. It was wet and it was *permanent*, both at once, and that wasn't possible, and it was.

The stone book had closed again, gently. And when he'd looked down, there'd been something in his lap that hadn't been there before. A single thin leaf of stone, like one of the pages, the size of his hand. Blank on both sides. Warm.

He'd carried it in his shirt pocket ever since. It had been warm the whole time. On floor 6, in the heat, it was warmer.

And yesterday, on the lip of the pit, when he'd looked east toward the Glass, it had gone *hot*.

***

He mixed the honey into the ink at the bottom of the pit, in the cool, with the slow care he did everything. A spoonful of honey to a pot of ink. He wrote *TEST* on the back of his hand and counted. Five seconds. Ten. Twenty. He took it up to the lip of the pit and held it in the sun. It went tacky. It didn't crack. He added more honey. It got sticky and slow and stayed wet for three minutes in the full noon glare before it began to go.

Three minutes. On floor 6.

"Your ink smells like breakfast," said Tiger, sniffing.

"It's an innovation."

"It's *pancakes*."

Cursiffe put the cap on the pot and took the stone leaf out of his pocket. He held it up, flat on his palm, and turned slowly on the lip of the pit, the way you turn with a compass. West, it was warm. North, warm. South, warm.

East, toward the Glass, toward the bowl and the Still Water, it was hot. Hot enough that he had to shift it on his palm.

"It wants something out there," said Yan quietly. He was watching.

"It wants what's left," said Cursiffe. He didn't know how he knew that. He just did, the way you know where a pen wants to go on a page. "Something that's happened. And stopped. And left something behind."

He looked at the stone leaf.

There were words on it now. He hadn't seen them appear. They were just there, faint, carved into the stone as if they always had been. Seven words, like the first time.

*Read what remains. Write how it was.*

They went east on the hundred-and-thirty-fourth day. Yan's party, all five, and Hyvred, because Cursiffe asked her to and she said yes before he'd finished asking.

He'd thought about who to ask for a long time. He'd thought about the bowl, and the Still Water, and the dead thing lying in the shallows that Higu had drawn on her map. He'd thought about the burned trail Hyvred said went out through the middle of the beams at noon. And he'd thought: *if I'm going to read what remains, I need someone who can see what I can't.*

Hyvred walked in front with him, across the Glass at night, under the moon. She talked, because she always talked, cheerful and upbeat even in the dark. But every so often she went quiet and looked at something only she could see, and Cursiffe learned to stop when she did.

They reached the bowl on the second night, and came down into it at dawn.

The Prism was in the water. Cursiffe had seen it on Higu's map, and heard about it, and it still stopped him dead on the slope. A great clear thing, many-sided, the size of a house, sitting in the middle of a perfectly round pool of impossible blue. In the grey light before sunrise it was just glass. Beautiful, quiet, holding the dawn in its faces like a jewel. Harmless.

"Wait," said Hyvred.

The sun came up.

And the Prism woke. The first light hit its faces and went *in*. And came back *out*, bent, focused, a line of white so bright it made Cursiffe's eyes water from three hundred metres away. It went across the bowl in a long slow sweep, and where it touched the glass of the dunes, the glass ran. Like wax under a candle. Then another beam. And another. As the sun climbed, more and more of them. Thin white spokes coming out of the thing in the water, turning slowly, like the hands of a clock gone mad.

"He went out into that," said Hyvred quietly. "At noon. When it's worst."

Cursiffe didn't say anything. He was looking at the shore.

The other Prism lay there. The dead one. Half in the water, on its side in the shallows, like a ship run aground. It was cracked through from top to bottom in a long jagged line. Its faces had gone cloudy and dull, the way glass goes when it's been through fire. It didn't catch the sun any more. It just lay there, the size of a house, with the blue water lapping at it.

And all round it, in the glass of the bowl, there were lines.

He'd noticed them coming down the slope and not understood. Long shallow grooves in the glass floor, running out from the water in every direction. Smooth, glossy, like the beds of little streams. Hundreds of them. Crossing each other. Overlapping.

Now, watching the living Prism's beams sweep the bowl, he understood.

They were scars. Where the beams had gone, the glass had melted and flowed and set again. Every beam the dead Prism had ever fired had left a groove in the floor of the bowl. A record. Burned into the glass of the world, of every single place it had ever pointed.

*Read what remains.*

Cursiffe sat down on the slope of the bowl, in the dawn, and took out his pen and his honey-ink and a stack of paper from Ostrel's wife. And he began to read.

***

It took him two days.

He read the grooves. All of them. He walked the bowl at night, when the Prism slept and the beams were gone, with Hyvred's light and Tiger's and a lantern borrowed from Ostrel. He traced every groove in the glass with his fingers, back to the water, and drew it. Which way it ran. How deep. How wide. Which ones crossed which. Which ones were old and dusty, and which ones were sharp and new.

In the day, in the heat, he sat on the shaded side of a black rock at the edge of the ring and watched the living one. Watched its beams come out as the sun rose and sweep the bowl. And he wrote down where they went, and when, and how fast. Matching them to the grooves the dead one had left. Comparing.

Salty held a cloak over him for shade. Tiger brought him water. Wendy sat on the rock above him with her bow and watched for shardbacks and said, every few hours, *you're going to get sunstroke and die and I'm going to say I told you so at your funeral*. Yan sat beside him and didn't say anything. He kept looking at the dead Prism in the shallows with a strange expression. As if he could see something in it that wasn't there any more.

On the second day, Cursiffe understood it.

"It's the sun," he said.

Nobody answered. It was noon. Everybody was trying not to die.

"The beams," said Cursiffe. He held up a page. It was covered in lines, his small careful handwriting, and the ink was honey-sticky and smelled of breakfast. "They're not random. They look random, but they're not. Every face on it catches the sun at a different angle. As the sun moves, the faces line up, one after another, and fire. It's a *clock*." He put his finger on the page. "If you know where the sun is, you know which faces are lined up. If you know which faces are lined up, you know where the beams are going. Every one. Every second." He looked up at them. "There are gaps. Moving gaps. Between the beams. All the time. You can walk through them, if you know where they'll be."

There was a long silence.

"That's what he did," said Hyvred, slowly.

Everybody looked at her.

She was sitting at the edge of the ring of rocks, looking out at the water, where the beams were sweeping. Her eyes were moving.

"His trail," she said. "At noon, on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth. It goes out into the water. I told Higu. Straight through the middle of the beams." She swallowed. "I thought he just walked through them. Took it. Healed it. Like the Kiln." She pointed. "But it's not straight. Look. Look at the—you can't see it. It *bends*. Every few steps. Left. Then right. Then it stops, for a second, and then goes on." She put her hand over her mouth. "He was walking through the gaps."

Cursiffe looked out at the water.

He couldn't see the trail. He'd never be able to see the trail. But he looked at the sweeping beams, and at the page in his hand, and he could see it anyway. A man in white, walking out into the blue at noon. Waist-deep. Leaning, stopping, moving on, left and right. Threading the gaps between the beams, one after another, like somebody walking through a crowd that keeps moving.

"He learned it fast," said Cursiffe slowly. "If he walked the gaps. He'd have had to learn the whole clock. In—what? A day? He came here after the bowl, on the hundred-and-twenty-third, and went in at noon on the twenty-fifth." He looked at his two days of pages. "It took me two days with all of you. And I've got Hyvred."

Nobody said anything.

He didn't think about it any further than that. Cursiffe didn't belong to anything that thought about things further than that. He'd heard there was a club. Hako had said the word *club* once in his hearing, and then gone red and changed the subject. Whatever it was, he wasn't in it. He just wrote things down.

So he wrote it down.

He took a fresh page. And at the top, in honey-ink, slowly, with Hyvred describing it beside him, he wrote what she'd seen. *At noon on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, a man went out into the Still Water through the gaps in the beams. Left. Then right. Then a stop. Then on.* Every step. Every bend. Every stop.

And when he got to the end, and wrote the last word, the ink on the page moved.

He stopped breathing.

It was only for a second. Less. He'd think afterwards that he'd imagined it, and know he hadn't. The lines of honey-black ink on the page, the words he'd just written, shivered. All together. Like the surface of water when something passes under it. And for that one second, looking down at the page in the white noon light, he saw—

Not words. A shape. Very small, very faint, made of ink. A figure in a long cloak, walking across the page. Left. Right. Stop. On. Through lines that might have been beams. For one second. And then gone, and the page was just words again.

Cursiffe sat very still on the shaded side of the black rock.

"Curse?" said Wendy, above him. "You've gone a funny colour."

"I'm fine," he said. His voice was quite steady. He'd always been good at that. "It's the sun."

He folded the page very carefully and put it in his shirt, against the stone leaf, which had stopped being hot. It was just warm again. As if it had got what it wanted.

***

He gave the pages to Drew on the hundred-and-thirty-eighth day, at the bottom of the pit, in the round room on the third ring.

Not the last one. He kept that one. He didn't know why. It sat in his shirt against the stone leaf and he didn't show it to anybody.

But the rest, the clock, the grooves, the gaps, all of it. He gave it to Drew. Drew looked at it for a long time, frowning, and then passed it to Higu. And Higu looked at it for even longer. Then she looked up at Cursiffe with her pale lilac eyes and said, very calmly:

"You can read this?"

"I wrote it."

"No," said Higu. "I mean—" She stopped. She looked down at the page again. "I can read it. I've never seen this before, and I can read it. I can *see* it. When I look at it, I know where the beams are. I know where the gaps are. I can see them moving." She looked up at him again, and for the first time since Cursiffe had known her, Higu looked surprised. "That's not just writing."

Cursiffe thought about the window. *Living Manuscript. What you have kept, others may read.*

"No," he said slowly. "I don't think it is."

Drew took the pages back. He looked at them again. Then at Cursiffe, with the general's face, the one that was already planning.

"Can you do more?" said Drew. "Copies. One for everybody who'll be in the bowl."

Cursiffe thought about it, carefully, the way he thought about everything.

"Yes," he said. "Give me a day. And more honey."

***

That night he sat on the lip of the pit alone, with the moon going over, and took out the last page.

He unfolded it. *At noon on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, a man went out into the Still Water through the gaps in the beams.* All of it. Every step. He'd been careful. He'd made sure it was true. Hyvred had seen it, and he'd written it as she'd seen it, and he hadn't added anything or taken anything away.

*Write how it was.*

He'd written how it was. And for one second, the ink had moved, and he'd seen it. Not a picture of it. Not the words. *It*. A man walking through beams, at noon. Something that had already happened, somewhere, once. Coming back up off the page for a breath, the way a smell comes back up off old clothes.

He looked at the page for a long time.

He didn't tell anyone. Not Yan, not Wendy, not Drew. He didn't know how to say it. *I wrote down something that happened, and for a second it happened again.* It sounded like something out of the kind of story Hako read.

He folded the page up and put it back against the stone leaf. The leaf was warm. Just warm. Quietly, patiently, the way a stone is warm after a whole day in the sun.

*Write what you saw. Keep it true.*

He'd write more. He knew that, sitting there. Not just beams and gaps. Everything. Every true thing. He'd been the one who wrote STOP on the first day and held a sheep for a second and a half. He'd been the one whose ink froze on floor 2. He'd always thought writing was a small thing. A pen, and a pot, and a second and a half.

It wasn't small. He was starting to understand that. Writing something down was the only way it didn't disappear.

Somewhere above him, a floor up, a man in white was walking alone on floor 7, and nobody was writing any of it down.

Cursiffe took out his pen. He uncapped the honey-ink. And on a fresh page, by moonlight, slowly, he started to write what he knew. Not what he guessed. Just what he'd seen, and what Hyvred had seen, and what the slate said. A white helm. A barrel lid. Onion skins on the corks. A thrust into a speck of glass. *Not this time.*

He didn't know what it would do. He wrote it anyway.

Somebody should.
