The Linkworks was the loudest place on floor 5, and the woman who ran it didn't look up when he came in.

It sat on the edge of Tether where three of the seven bridges came in. It was a long low shed of black stone with its doors open to the wind and a forge at the back the size of a cart. Chain hung everywhere. From the rafters, from hooks on the walls, in great iron heaps on the floor: bridge chain, links as thick as his wrist, some new and grey, some old and rusted red, some broken. The air rang. Two apprentices were hammering at the anvils, and the woman at the big forge had her back to him, and she was the biggest person he'd seen since Salty. She was working a link the size of a dinner plate with a hammer the size of his head.

He waited till she'd finished the link. He knew better than to talk to a smith mid-strike.

"Shield," he said.

She turned round. Broad, grey-haired, with forearms like bridge posts and burn scars up both of them to the elbow. Old scars, white and shiny. She looked at him. At his empty left arm, and the straps still buckled round his forearm with nothing in them.

"Bird," she said.

"Bird."

"Thought so." She put the hammer down. "Every few years some fool goes up the Spindle with a shield and comes down without it. You're lucky you came down at all." She went to the back wall, where things that weren't chain hung on pegs, and took down a round shield. Plain. Oak planks, black with tar, bound with three bands of chain-iron and a rim of the same. No boss. No paint. It looked like something you'd use to cover a barrel. "That's what I've got. Common. It'll take a hit."

He took it. It was heavier than the Vigil Shield, and didn't fit his arm the way that had. Nothing would. The Vigil Shield had come off the floor of a roofless chapel on floor 1, on the first day. It had been waiting for him under the stone knight's lantern. It had fitted his arm as if it had been made for it, because in a way it had. He'd carried it up four floors. It had gone red in the Kiln's fire and held. And now it was somewhere under the cloud, spinning, down and down into whatever nobody had ever found.

He buckled the tarred oak on. It sat badly. He shifted the straps.

"How much?"

"Twelve silver."

He paid it. He didn't haggle. The woman watched him count it out.

"Otta," she said, when he'd done. "If you're going back up there, Otta'll sell you another one when you lose that." She went back to her forge. "Everybody loses the first one."

***

"It's ugly," said Alea, outside, on the bridge back to Nan Gully's.

"It's a shield."

"It's a barrel lid. It's a barrel lid with ideas." She was sitting on his shoulder, looking down at it. "You had a *chapel* shield. A *Paladin's* shield. From a stone knight. It had a vigil in it."

"And now I've got a barrel lid." He shrugged the shield higher on his arm. "It'll take a hit. That's all a shield does."

The fox didn't say anything for a while.

"You're upset about it," she said.

"I'm not."

"You touched your empty arm four times last night in your sleep."

He walked a bit further along the bridge, one hand on the rail chain, the wind coming and going.

"It was the first thing," he said. "On the first day. Before Mother Crane. Before anything. I walked into that chapel at dawn, and it was lying there under the lantern waiting for me, and it fitted." He looked at the tarred oak on his arm. "It was the first thing in this whole run that felt like *mine*."

"I know," said Alea quietly.

"It's fine. It's a shield. It's gone." He let go of the rail chain for a second to wipe his face, and the wind came, and he grabbed it again. "I'll get another one. Everybody loses the first one."

***

He went back to the Spindle on the seventy-third day and tried the crack.

It was simple. It had been simple the first time too, on the seventy-first, when he'd been lying in it bleeding and the Squall had stood outside striking at the rock. It couldn't get in. So: wait in the crack. Let it come to the mouth, the way it had. And when its head came down to look in at him, that huge yellow eye right there at the opening, put a sword in the eye.

He climbed the spiral path to the crown, with the barrel lid on his arm and the wind going round. He waited till the Squall was on the far side of the needle, and ran across the crown, and slid into the crack, and waited.

It didn't come.

It came round the needle. He watched it come, through the narrow mouth of the crack, grey and white and huge against the blue. It saw him. He knew it saw him; he saw its head turn. And then it went on past. It circled the Spindle once, high, and again, lower, and on the third time round it hung in the air off the edge of the crown with its great wings spread, holding itself still on the wind. It looked at the crack. For a long time.

Then it went up, and up, and resumed its circle, and didn't come near the crack again.

He waited four hours.

"It's not coming," said Alea, eventually.

"I can see that."

"It came on the seventy-first. When you were in here bleeding. It stood right there and hit the rock."

"And it couldn't get in." He looked out at the circling shape. "So now it doesn't try."

The fox was silent.

"It learned that too," she said. "Two days. It learned that in two days."

He came out of the crack at last, carefully, with his shield up and his eyes on the sky. The moment he stepped out onto the crown, he started moving. He didn't decide to. Some part of him that had been watching the Squall for four hours had already worked something out before the rest of him caught up. He walked. Fast, in a circle, across the bare rock, turning, never stopping, with the wind pushing at him and the shield up.

The Squall's shadow went over him. And over him again. It didn't fold its wings.

He kept walking. Round and round the crown, five minutes, ten, while the wind came and went and tried to knock him over. Twice it nearly did. Once he went down on one knee in a gust, and for half a second he was still, and he felt the shadow stop. He felt the air change above him. He was up and moving before he'd thought about it, and the shadow went on.

"*It's not stooping*," said Alea. "Ice. While you move. It's not—"

"I know."

"It only comes down when you stop."

"I know." He was breathing hard. The wind on the crown was relentless. Every gust came from a different side, and every gust wanted to push him over, push him back, push him off the edge. He'd been walking for twelve minutes and his legs were shaking with it. "It only comes down on stillness. That's the rule. That's what it's been given." He stumbled in another gust. "Move, and it can't choose you."

"Then *keep moving*—"

"I can't keep moving forever." He was at the crack again. He slid into it and sat down hard against the rock, gasping, and outside the shadow came over the crack mouth and went on. "I can't kill it by walking round in circles. I've got to hit it. To hit it I've got to be where it is. And the only time it comes to where I am—"

"Is when you stop," said Alea.

"Is when I stop."

***

He tried the decoy on the seventy-fourth.

He'd thought about it all night in Nan Gully's hut. If it went for stillness, give it stillness. Something that stood on the crown, braced, shield up, and didn't move. And while it came down on that, be somewhere else. Be under it, at its legs, with a sword, when it landed.

He made the decoy out of driftwood from Tether's woodpile, which Nan Gully sold him at four times its value: a cross of two poles lashed together, taller than him, with the barrel lid strapped to one arm. He put his own spare shirt on it, stuffed with grass, and the Hartshorn Cloak over the top. It looked like a scarecrow. From the air, he hoped, it would look like a knight with a shield, standing still on the crown, braced, the way he'd stood on the seventy-first.

He carried it up the spiral path on his back. It took two hours. He wedged its foot into a crack in the middle of the crown and piled rocks round it, so it stood upright in the wind and didn't move. Then he went to the edge of the crown, near the crack, and kept moving. Back and forth along the edge, sword drawn, with no shield, watching the sky.

The Squall came round.

It looked at the scarecrow. He saw its head turn.

It went on past.

It came round again, lower, and looked again, and went on past. On the third time round it came very low, so low that its wingtip went over the crown a few metres above the scarecrow's head. The wind of its passing bent the scarecrow nearly double and tore the Hartshorn Cloak half off it. Then the Squall went up and away and didn't come back to look.

It had known. One look, and it had known. The scarecrow wasn't alive.

He stopped walking. Just for a moment. Just to look at it. He stood on the edge of the crown with his sword in his hand, looking at the scarecrow, with his mouth open, *it knows*—

"*Ice—*"

The shadow had stopped.

He didn't look up. He went sideways, into the crack, the way you go into a doorway in an earthquake, and the Squall hit the crown where he'd been standing a second before. Talons first. It struck the rock so hard that stone came up in chips and pattered down round the crack mouth like hail. Then it stood there on the edge of the crown, huge, its wings half spread, and screamed into the crack at him.

He lay in the crack with his sword and his heart hammering and no shield, and looked at it, a few metres away.

It was looking at him. One yellow eye. Not the way a monster looked at you, on floor 1, flat, like a picture. It was *looking*. At him. The way a hawk looks at a mouse it has decided to wait for.

Then it went up, and resumed its circle.

He lay there a long time.

"It knew the scarecrow wasn't alive," said Alea, at last.

"Yeah."

"On floor 1, the Woolbacks went for anything that moved. On floor 2 the Rimehounds circled whatever was warm. They didn't—they didn't *know* things. Not like that." The fox was very quiet. "That bird knows the difference between you and a stick with your shirt on."

"It's floor five," said Ice. "Everything's realer."

"I hate that."

"I know."

He went down with the Hartshorn Cloak, which he got back off the scarecrow at a run while the Squall was on the far side of the needle. He left the scarecrow. He didn't have the strength to carry it.

The next morning, when he looked up at the crown from Tether, it was gone. Torn off in the night and thrown into the cloud.

***

"Three," he said to Nan Gully, that evening, behind her hut, out of the wind, while he gutted fish for both of them.

"Three what?"

"Three times I've been up there. Three times it's beaten me."

"You came down," said Nan Gully. She was sitting on an upturned basket with her pipe, watching him work. The gull on her hat was watching the fish. "That's not beaten. Beaten's when you don't."

"It learns. Everything I try, it learns." He cut a fish down the back, cleanly. "If I stand still, it lands on me. If I hide, it won't come. If I give it something still that isn't me, it knows. And if I keep moving, I can't hit it."

Nan Gully smoked for a while.

"You're standing wrong," she said.

He looked up.

"Saw you on the bridges. First day. Hanging off that rail chain like a man hanging off a cliff." She pointed the stem of her pipe at him. "You walk like a wall. All braced. Feet planted. Waiting for the wind to hit you, so you can not fall over." She sniffed. "That's how your lot always walk. Song says so. Third verse. *Stood like a door in a gale*." She put the pipe back. "Doors come off."

He sat back on his heels with the knife in his hand.

"How should I walk?"

"Don't ask me. I'm a ferrywoman. I sit." She looked out across the gap, at the high islands going up into the evening, and the needle in the middle with the shape going round it. "Ask Pell."

"Who's Pell?"

"Old Pell. On the Kitecliff." She jerked her head northwest, toward the highest of the islands after the Spindle: a great green-topped rock with white cliffs falling away into the cloud on every side. "His lot have lived up there since before the bird. Seven of 'em left. They don't talk to your lot. Never have." She knocked out her pipe on the basket. "Take him fish. Tell him Nan sent you, and he owes me for a basket. He'll talk to you or he'll throw you off the cliff. Either way you'll learn something."

***

The Kitecliff took him a day to reach. Four bridges, each longer and higher than the last. The last one went up at such a steep angle that the planks had been replaced by a ladder of chain.

He remembered it. Not well. The first time, they'd looked at the Kitecliff from below, and seen the shapes on top of it, and somebody had said *are those people?* Somebody else had tried to go up the chain ladder. Something had come off the cliff top and stood at the head of the ladder, tall and grey and very still, and looked down at them until they went away. Nobody had gone up again. They'd had a floor to clear.

He went up the ladder with a basket of fish on his back and his barrel-lid shield on his arm.

There was someone at the top.

He'd known there would be. He stopped three rungs from the top and looked up at them.

They were tall. Taller than him by a head, and thin, thinner than anyone should be and stand up. Their arms were long and their shoulders were narrow and high. All down the backs of their arms, from the shoulder to the wrist, and across their shoulder blades, and up the back of their neck, there were feathers. Small grey feathers, close and soft, like a gull's breast. The face was human, mostly, and old, very old, deeply lined. The eyes were large and dark and set wide, the way a bird's are. Hair like white down. They wore a long, loose coat of grey cloth with slits down the back of the arms where the feathers came through. He couldn't have said whether they were a man or a woman. It didn't seem to be the kind of thing that mattered on a cliff this high.

They looked down at him. They didn't move. The wind blew straight up the cliff past him and lifted the feathers on their arms, and they didn't seem to feel it.

"Nan sent me," said Ice. "She says you owe her for a basket. I brought fish."

The old one looked at him for a long time.

"You came alone," they said. Their voice was very light and very dry, like wind in grass.

"Yes."

"Your lot come in crowds." The large dark eyes went over him. The barrel lid on his arm. The empty straps where the Vigil Shield had been, still buckled round his forearm, because he hadn't been able to make himself take them off. "Crowds don't listen."

"I'm listening," said Ice.

The old one looked at him a while longer. Then at the basket of fish. Then they stepped back from the top of the ladder, just one step, and that was all.

"Pell," they said.

***

There were seven of them, like Nan Gully had said. Pell, and three others nearly as old, and a middle-aged pair who never spoke, and a boy of about twelve with his arm-feathers only just coming in, patchy and pale. They lived in a village of round stone huts on the top of the Kitecliff, roofed with turf and roped down even harder than Tether. Between the huts, on long poles, hung kites. Not toy kites. Great things, twice the size of a man, of grey cloth on frames of pale wood, with straps where arms would go.

"Wings," said Pell, when they saw him looking. "We had our own, once. Before the bird. My grandmother flew from here to the Spindle and back before breakfast." They looked at the kites. "Now we glide. Off the cliff, into the wind, a little way. Never far. Never high. If you go high, it sees you."

"The Squall."

"It came from somewhere. Before my grandmother's time. Nobody knows where." Pell looked toward the needle in the middle of the Reaches, and the grey shape going round it. "It's had the sky since. Your lot killed it once. In my grandmother's time. With lights in their hands." They paused. "It came back the next morning."

"They always come back," said Ice quietly. "If you stay."

Pell looked at him for a long moment with their bird's eyes.

"Yes," they said. "So we've noticed."

He told them. Not everything. He told them about the three times. Standing still and being lifted. Hiding and being ignored. The scarecrow it had known wasn't alive. He told them how it only came down on stillness, and how he couldn't keep moving and hit it at the same time.

Pell listened to all of it without moving. Then they got up, slowly, like a heron getting up off a bank, and walked to the edge of the cliff.

"Come here," they said.

He went. He stood beside them at the very edge of the Kitecliff. The cloud was a long way below, and the wind came straight up the white rock and hit him in the face like a hand. It came hard, and steady, and then not steady, gusting, dropping, coming again from the left. He planted his feet. He braced. He put his weight low and his shoulders forward, the way he'd stood in front of everything for ten years, and the wind hit him and he didn't move.

Pell watched him.

"There," they said. "That."

"What?"

"You're standing like a wall." The old voice was dry. "Feet in the rock. Waiting to be hit, so you can not fall."

"That's what you do. When something hits you, you—"

"Walls fall," said Pell. "Every wall. Every wall that was ever built up here, the wind took. That's why we tie everything down." They lifted one long feathered arm into the wind, and the feathers lifted along it. "The wind doesn't hit you. You're standing in its way. *That's* what hits you. You."

The wind gusted. He braced against it, and swayed, and caught himself.

"Lean," said Pell.

"On what?"

"On *it*." They looked at him with something that, in a person with a less strange face, might have been amusement. "Lean on the wind. Like a kite. A kite's never still, and it's never fighting. It's always leaning on something that's moving. That's what holds it up."

He looked at the drop. At the cloud, a long way down.

"If I lean over the edge of a cliff into the wind," he said carefully, "and the wind stops—"

"Then you'll learn very fast what it feels like when it stops," said Pell. "And the next time, you'll feel it coming. That's the whole of it. That's all the wind is. Leaning, and feeling it change." They turned their large dark eyes back to the needle in the middle of the sky. "The bird is the wind. Its wings are the wind. If you're leaning on the wind, you're never still. You're always moving, like a kite on a line. But you never have to *go* anywhere." They looked back at him. "You can stay exactly where you want to be. And be moving the whole time."

He stood at the edge of the cliff and looked at them.

*Never still. But never going anywhere.*

On the crown of the Spindle, under the circling shadow, moving and staying in the same place. Right where the bird had to come.

"Show me," he said.

***

It took him five days.

He fell over more times than he could count. Not off the cliff. Pell had the boy tie a rope round his waist to a post the first afternoon, without saying why. On the first day he couldn't stop bracing. Every time a gust came, his whole body locked, the way it had locked against every blow for ten years, and the gust took him off his feet and dumped him on the turf. On the second day he could lean, a bit, but only when the wind was steady. Every time it changed, he was too slow, and fell. On the third day he fell less.

Pell sat on a rock and watched him and said almost nothing. Sometimes *no*. Sometimes *better*. Once, when he'd fallen for the twentieth time that morning, they said: "You're thinking about what it's going to do. Stop. Feel what it's doing."

The boy laughed at him every time he fell, quietly, behind his hand. On the fourth day the boy brought him water without being asked, and sat beside him while he drank it, and asked in a whisper, very fast, if it was true that Ice's lot came from a place with no wind.

"There's wind," said Ice. "It's just not like this."

"What's it like?"

He thought about it. About the air in a classroom in Singapore, and a fan that clicked on every third turn in a rented room in Malacca. About standing on a street in the evening while a hot wind came down the road and you didn't even notice it, because it was just wind.

"Quieter," he said.

The boy thought about that for a long time.

On the fifth day, in the evening, he stood at the edge of the Kitecliff with the rope round his waist and the wind coming up the white rock in gusts. He didn't fall.

He wasn't standing. That was the thing. He couldn't have said what he was doing, except that it wasn't standing. His weight was out over the edge, a little, on the wind. When it came harder, he leaned into it, and it held him. When it dropped, he felt it drop half a heartbeat before it did, the change going through him like a shiver, and came back with it. When it came from the left, he went with it a little to the right. Not because it pushed him. Because he'd gone there first. His feet were on the rock. His body was moving all the time, small and constant, like a kite on a line, like a boat on water. And he wasn't going anywhere at all.

He wasn't bracing against anything. For the first time in ten years he wasn't bracing.

Pell got up off their rock and came and stood beside him at the edge. For a while neither of them said anything. The sun was going down into the cloud in the west, real and red and slow, and the whole of the Windward Reaches had gone gold round them, every island, every chain. Far off in the middle of it all, a grey shape went round and round a needle of rock.

"Better," said Pell.

"Yeah."

"You stood like a wall when you came up the ladder." The old voice was dry as grass. "Your shoulders are different now." They looked at him sideways with their large dark eyes. "Who taught you to stand like that? Before?"

He thought about it. About a stone chapel and a vigil, and a gate on floor 61, and a boss room with a broken shield. About ten years of standing in front of things, braced, so that nothing could get past him to anyone behind.

"Nobody," he said. "I just thought that's what you did. If you were the one in front."

"Hm," said Pell.

They stood at the edge together, leaning on the wind.

"Now," said Pell, after a while, "do it with a bird trying to kill you."
