The padlock came up on the Fell on the morning of the four hundred and fourth day, with a fell-cat still dying in the bracken.

He didn't see the number change. He never did. He was busy. Three of them had come up out of the bracken at once, the way they did in the mornings, flat and grey and silent, and he'd taken the first on the shield, on the slope, and turned it, and the second had come in low on his left while he was turning, and he'd stepped, and it had missed, and the third had been in the air already. He'd put the sword up into it as it came down. And then it was in the bracken, kicking, and the other two were dead, and he was standing in the wind on the side of a hill with blood on his arm and his breath going, and the window was just there.

A little grey lock, beside the seventy. Drawn in the same thin lines as the rest.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Seventy," said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. "That's the padlock."

"I know."

"You'll want to read the rest."

"I know."

He didn't, straight away. He stood in the wind and looked at the lock. He'd seen it at ten, on floor 2, the first time and this time. At twenty-five. At forty-five, on the jetty on floor 8, with thirty-five people standing behind him, and he'd read the windows out loud because they were listening and he didn't know how not to. *Kneel. Let them hold.* He'd hated that one. He'd done it anyway.

He touched the lock.

He read those and felt nothing. He knew them. Not the words, but the shape. *Carry a light.* He'd been carrying lights since floor 1. He'd left one on a bench on floor 3. He'd held one up for eleven hours in the Down on floor 9. The first time, ten years ago, he'd taken the normal one at this tier, and walked a lamp through a black place on a floor he still didn't let himself think about, and come out the other side with the lamp still lit. It had been easy, in the way that the things you're best at are easy. It had been the one thing he'd always known how to do.

*Carry a light through the dark.*

He scrolled down.

He stood on the side of the Fell in the wind and read it.

And then he read it again.

And then he sat down in the bracken next to the dead fell-cat, because his legs had decided to, the way they had on a root on floor 9, and on the path in the Down, and in the snow on floor 10 when he came round a corner and saw Dennis's arm.

***

"Ice?"

Thea's voice. A long way back up the slope. Not frightened. Careful.

He'd forgotten. They were on the outcrop. A hundred metres back. They'd seen him sit down.

He didn't move. He heard them coming. Xola first, of course, fast, slithering on the chalk, saying something under her breath that was mostly swearing. And then Thea, slower. Steady. With the lantern.

Xola got to him and stopped, a pace away, with her hands half up, and looked at him the way she'd looked at him on the root on floor 9. At the side of his head. Waiting for it to tip.

"I'm not hurt," he said. "It's not—I'm not hurt."

"You *sat down*."

"I know."

"You never sit down. You've never once sat down in a fight in your *life*, Icey, what—"

"It's the padlock," said Thea.

She'd come down the slope behind Xola and stopped. She was looking at him. Not at his face. At the air in front of his face, a little to the left, where the window was. She couldn't read his windows. Not properly. But she'd been reading under things for four hundred and four days, and she could see where they were.

"Seventy," she said.

"Seventy," said Ice.

Thea knelt down in the bracken in front of him. With the lantern. Close.

"Read it to us," she said.

He looked at her.

"You read them out on the jetty," said Thea. "On eight. Out loud, to everybody. I was there. *Kneel. Let them hold.* Your voice went funny on *kneel*." She was very steady. "Read it to us."

He read it to them.

Easy. Normal. He read those flat, the way you'd read a shopping list. And then *Hard: Lightbearer*, and he heard his voice start to go, and made it not.

"*Be carried*," he read. "*Through what you cannot walk. You will not know if they can. When it comes.*"

Nobody said anything.

The wind came over the Fell. The bracken moved. The fell-cat beside him had stopped kicking.

"*When it comes*," said Xola, at last. "What does that mean? When *what* comes? When's *when*?"

"I don't know."

"It doesn't say? It just—it doesn't say a *day*? A place? Every other one said a place. Azac's said *tomorrow night on the fifth wall*. Rachael's said *tonight*—"

"It doesn't say," said Ice.

"So it could be *anywhere*. It could be *any time*. It could be—" Xola stopped. She'd seen his face. "Oh," she said. Quite differently. "Oh, Icey."

Thea hadn't said anything.

She was kneeling in front of him in the bracken with the lantern, looking at the place where the window was. And he watched her read it. Not the words. She couldn't see the words. She was reading him reading it. The way she'd been doing since floor 1.

"You've never been carried," she said.

It wasn't a question.

He thought about it. About Calvin and Azac on the heap in the Down on floor 9, carrying him to Burr's basket because his legs had stopped. About Xola in the sea off the Shoals, with her fist in the back of his vest, towing him. About ten years, the first time, and four hundred days on top.

"Not through anything," he said. "Not when it mattered. Not when I didn't know if—" He stopped. "I carry things. That's what I do. That's all I've ever done. I carry them, and I don't put them down, and I don't let anybody—" He stopped again.

"Kneel," said Thea. "Let them hold."

He looked at her.

"You hated that one," she said. "You read it out on the jetty and your voice went and everybody heard it. And you did it. On the jetty. And in the Down. And on the Salt, under the beak, for a whole fight, and you didn't get up once." She held his eyes. "This is the same."

"It's not the same."

"It's the next one," said Thea. "Kneel was letting us stand in front of you. This is letting us pick you up." She was quiet. "It's the same, Ice. It's just further."

He didn't say anything.

"You'll take the hard one," said Thea. "You always do."

"Yes."

"Then take it." She put her hand on his knee, in the bracken. "And when it comes, whatever it is, whenever it is, you let us."

He looked down at her hand.

Then he looked at the window. At the grey lock, and under it, *Hard: Lightbearer*, and a small grey line he hadn't noticed at the bottom, by itself.

*Accept?*

"Yes," he said. Out loud. To the window. To Thea's hand. To the wind going over the Fell.

The lock stayed where it was.

***

He didn't tell anybody else.

He didn't need to. Xola told Higu, because Xola couldn't not, and Higu wrote it on the rock, small, at the bottom, under the two columns with no line between them. *ICE: TIER 5 HARD. "BE CARRIED." WHEN IT COMES.* And by supper the whole copy-room had read it, and nobody said anything to him about it at all. Which was how he knew they'd all read it.

But they looked at him differently. Not much. The way you look at somebody who's told you they're going to have to jump off something soon and they don't know when.

Calvin looked at him the most. All evening. With his fist shut against his chest.

***

Tell was getting ready for the Reckoning.

He'd never seen it the first time. They'd come up the well in the autumn, after the Reckoning had been and gone, and left in the winter before the next. He'd heard about it from Aldo, every evening at the Climbers' House, in a rush of words. The day Tell added itself up. The Count's cry. The Assize. The Magistrate behind its screen. He'd thought it sounded like a festival.

It wasn't a festival. It was the end of the year's accounts, for a whole city, and the whole city knew it.

The markets went quiet on the day before. Not shut. Quiet. People came and paid what they owed and went home. Shopkeepers stood in their doorways with their books open on their arms, like clerks, going down the columns with a finger. In the Cloth Hall, the tellers in their booths sat with stacks of paper and wrote and wrote and didn't look up. Wace the moneylender had a queue down the Lantern Market from dawn to dusk, and nobody in it was smiling.

On the hill, the great hall of the Assize stood open. He went up with Drew, on the afternoon of the four hundred and fifth day, and looked in through the doors from the top of the steps. It was enormous. Pale stone, high windows, a floor of worn flags that had been swept so clean they shone. Benches down both sides, in tiers, enough for a thousand people. And at the far end, a long table, with a stack of slates on it. And behind the table, from the floor to the high ceiling, a screen.

Wood. Very old. Dark, almost black, carved all over in a pattern he couldn't make out from the door. Leaves, maybe. Or letters. Or both. It went from one side of the hall to the other, with no door in it, no gap, no way through.

Nothing moved behind it.

He stood at the top of the steps and looked at it for a long time.

"That's it?" said Drew.

"That's it."

"It looks like a wardrobe."

"It's older than the city," said Ice.

Drew looked at him sideways. Then at the screen again.

"It still looks like a wardrobe," he said. But he said it quietly.

***

Hawise's list had seventy-nine names on it. Orlo was on the fifth sheet.

Malcolm had a pack full of chalk. Spare chalk, and spare spare chalk, and a slate of his own from Askew in case the Tellhouse slates ran out. He'd been round the copy-room twice making everybody practise writing on a slate held against a wall, because there might not be a table for everyone, and Calvin had broken four pieces of chalk and been told off.

Joan Tull came to the copy-room yard on the last evening, with Hawise, and stood by the pump in her brown dress and didn't say anything for a long time. Then she said, "Will it be there tomorrow? The page?"

"It's in the Roll," said Cursiffe. "It stayed. Askew's got it in a locked case."

"And if he—"

"It's the Magistrate's," said Hawise. "Not his. Not the Bench's. Not yours. The Magistrate reads both, and asks who writes it, and weighs it." She put her hand on Joan's arm. "And I'll stand up. And Ivo Rook will. He said. And whoever else there is who ever bought an apple off Piers Tull and knew he'd never in his life have left his shop to his brother."

Joan looked at her.

"That's half the Low Town," said Joan.

"Then half the Low Town," said Hawise.

***

Calvin found him on the roof, late.

Ice had gone up to be on his own and hadn't been, for long. Calvin came up the ladder and sat down on the lead with his back to the parapet and his fist on his knee and said nothing for nearly a whole minute, which was a record.

"Tomorrow's Cooper's Lane," he said, eventually.

Ice looked at him.

"Noon. The sixth time. It's the same day as the Assize." Calvin looked at his fist. "The Assize starts at noon too. Hawise says. The Count's cry from the steps at noon, and then everybody goes in." He was quiet. "I can't be in two places."

"No."

"Hawise says she's going to the Assize. She says—" Calvin stopped. "She says if Alys wants to find her, she'll know where. Everybody in Tell will be at the Assize tomorrow. Everybody. Even the clerks stand at the back." He looked at Ice. "Do you think she'll come?"

Ice thought about the empty corner by Cooper's Lane. About a pen on a grey floor, beside a chair, not picked up. About a woman in the fourth row watching a lantern all the way out.

"I don't know," he said honestly.

Calvin nodded. As if that was the answer he'd expected. He didn't seem to mind it.

"Dec," he said.

"Mm."

"The window. *Be carried.*" Calvin didn't look at him. "If it comes tomorrow. Whatever it is."

"It might not."

"If it does." Calvin turned his fist over on his knee. "I've got you in here. Ina put you in. Second from last. At the edge, by the fire, with Thea." He opened his fist, just a little. Just enough. "I've been carrying you around for eighty days. In my hand. You didn't even know."

Ice looked at the palm. At nothing. At a place near the base of Calvin's thumb.

"I know now," he said.

"Good." Calvin shut his fist again. "So that's one of us that's already done it. Carried you. You just have to let the rest of us catch up."

Ice didn't say anything.

Calvin got up, and went to the ladder, and stopped with one foot on the top rung.

"You've got two good arms, Dec," he said. "Did you know? You said it to me on floor 8 like it was the best thing that ever happened. I never knew why." He looked at Ice in the dark. "But you have. Two good arms. Thirty-six of us, two good arms each. That's seventy-two arms." He grinned. "That's a lot of arms. That's enough to carry anybody."

He went down the ladder.

Ice sat on the roof and didn't move for a long time.

***

Gabe was on the copy-room step when he came down. The way Gabe was every night now, since the Countinghouse. With Hearthwall across his knees, warm, its three flames glowing very faintly from inside, and his back against the door frame.

Ice sat down next to him. On his left. So Gabe was on his right.

They didn't say anything for a while.

Across the road, the Countinghouse. Shut. A hundred and eleven inside, Ice thought. Not a hundred and twelve. He'd counted them going in at dusk, the way John did, the way Drew did. A hundred and eleven.

"Higu wrote it on the rock," said Gabe.

"I know."

"*Be carried.*" Gabe looked at Hearthwall on his knees. "*When it comes.*"

"I know."

Gabe ran his thumb along the rim. The thick side. The left side. The side that covered the man on your left.

"On ten," he said. "When Tamm died. The first night. They carried him down from the fifth wall on a board, with his shield on." He was quiet. "I helped. On the stair. You remember."

"I remember."

"I thought, at the time, that's what it's for. A shield. In the end. That's what it's big enough for." Gabe didn't look up. "I made this one thicker on the left. For you. Geir said a shield's shaped by the one who'll carry it." He was quiet. "I made it big enough to carry a man on."

Ice looked at the shield on Gabe's knees.

"He was dead," he said. "Tamm."

"You won't be."

"You don't know that."

"No," said Gabe. "I don't." He turned his head, finally, and looked at Ice. "That's the point, isn't it. The window. *You will not know if they can.*" He was quiet. "You don't get to know, Dec. That's the whole thing. You've known everything, every floor, since floor 1. Where the doors are. What's behind them. Which rim. *Always.*" He said it very gently. "This one you don't get to know. You just get to let us."

Ice didn't say anything.

"I've been waiting twenty-three years," said Gabe, "for you to let anybody carry anything. I'll wait one more day."

He put his left hand out on the step between them. Palm up. The way Deston did. The way Ice had once, on a parapet on floor 10, at dawn, put his own hand in Deston's.

Ice looked at it.

Then he put his hand in it.

They sat on the step. The lamp and the fire. Across the road, the Countinghouse. Up the hill, the Assize, dark, with its square tower against the stars and its flag hanging still. Behind it, in a narrow house with a green door, an old man with a writing-board on his knees who'd always said he'd run.

Tomorrow, at noon, the whole of Tell would go up that hill.

"Gabe," said Ice.

"Mm."

"If it comes. If I can't—" He stopped.

"Then I'll carry you," said Gabe.

"You don't know where."

"Doesn't matter."

Ice shut his eyes.

Somewhere down the Wellgate, at the turn by the Lantern Market, a clerk stood under a dead lamp with its book, and its head down.
