The stair was eleven hundred and four steps.

Milli counted them. He couldn't help it. Eleven hundred and four steps cut into the black rock of the cliff, turning and turning, each one a little worn in the middle the way old steps were, as if a lot of feet had gone up them a very long time ago and then none at all. Eleven hundred and four. About two hundred and twenty metres, if the steps were twenty centimetres, which they were, he'd checked the first forty with the edge of his hand. Up through the inside of the cliff above the Narrows, in the dark, with Hana's hand in his and Hana's light going in front of them both, pale, at her shoulder, and the pale wild one that had come down to look at them somewhere above, out of sight, round the next turn. Always round the next turn.

He'd stopped thinking about the wild one at about step four hundred. He'd started thinking about it again at about step nine hundred.

It was the size of an apple. He'd seen that much. And it hadn't belonged to anybody.

At step eleven hundred and four there was a door.

Not a trapdoor, like the one in the Long Barn. Not a hole. A door: an arch cut in the rock, taller than Hana's crown, with no door in it. Just the shape. And beyond it, nothing.

Hana stopped in the arch. Milli stopped beside her. Behind them, on the stair, he heard the rest of the line stop too, step by step, all the way down, thirty-four people and a goat and a dog and Sleet coming to a halt one after another like carriages on a train. Somebody said *what*. It sounded like Calvin.

He looked out through the arch.

It was dark.

That was the first thing, and it took him a long time to get past it. It wasn't the dark of a night. He'd seen a lot of nights in two years; every floor had its own. Floor 2's was white with snow. Eleven's had been full of stars. Fourteen's had been so black over the Grey that you could hear it. This wasn't any of those. This was dark the way the inside of a closed box is dark. Dark with nothing behind it. No sky. No moon. No stars. No horizon, no line where anything stopped and something else started.

And in the dark, lights.

Far off. Everywhere. Small, pale, scattered, the way a city looks from very high up at night, except that a city stays where it is and these didn't. They drifted. Slowly. Some of them still, some of them moving in long lazy curves, some of them in twos and threes, some alone. Below him and above him and out ahead of him, as far as he could see in every direction, which was no distance at all and also forever. Hundreds of them. More. They didn't light anything. Not really. They were too small and too far. They just hung there, being lights, in the dark, the way stars would if stars were close enough to touch and had nowhere to be.

Under the nearest of them, below the arch, a long way down, he could see trees.

Pale ones. White-barked, very tall, very straight, standing close together, with leaves that caught the drifting lights and gave them back faintly, the way paper does. A forest. In the dark. Going down and away, slope after slope of pale trunks, until the lights ran out and so did the trees, and there was just dark.

Milli did what he always did.

He looked for an edge.

There wasn't one.

He looked up, for a ceiling. He was sure there must be a ceiling. They'd come up a stair inside a cliff; they were inside something; there had to be a top to it. He looked up into the dark above the arch and above the drifting lights for a line, a texture, the faint grey of rock catching light. Nothing. He looked down past the trees for a floor. Nothing. He looked out ahead, past the farthest light he could see, for a far wall. Nothing. The dark just went on, and the lights went on in it, and his eyes, which were good in the dark, better than anybody's but Hyvred's, went out and out looking for something to stop on and found nothing at all.

He couldn't measure it.

He stood in the arch with Hana's hand in his and understood, slowly, the way you understand a step isn't there when your foot's already going down, that for the first time in his life he was looking at a thing he couldn't measure. It wasn't big. Big was fine. Big was just a big number. It didn't have a size. It had no edges to put a number between.

His tail had gone stiff. He hadn't told it to.

"Honey," said Hana. Quietly.

"I know."

"You're doing the face."

"There's nothing to measure," said Milli.

Hana looked out at the dark. Her ears went flat against her crown, then came up slowly. "Oh," she said. "Yeah." She looked at him sideways. "Is that bad?"

He didn't know. That was the other thing.

***

The lights went out.

Not the far ones. Theirs.

He felt it before he saw it. The pale ball at his own shoulder, the one that had hung there since the white room, that had said *Welcome, Player* on the first day in a voice like a train announcement and nothing much else for two years except numbers and the occasional *I am not able to answer that*, went dim. Just like that. Like a lamp somebody's turned down because there's a draught.

And Hana's. At her shoulder. And behind him on the stair, he heard people make small noises, one after another, all the way down. *Oh. Hey. What—*

Thirty-five lights. All dim at once.

Not out. Dim. Pulled in. Small.

"Window," said Hana. To hers. Sharply. The way she'd say it in a fight. "Where are we?"

Her light flickered.

"I am not able—" it said. In the voice. The flat calm voice, the same for all thirty-five of them, the voice that had said *There are thirty-five players* to Gabe on the first day as if it were the only true thing in the world.

And stopped.

Mid-sentence. *I am not able—* and nothing. It didn't finish. It didn't say *to answer that*. It didn't say *Hanarisu*. It just stopped, the way a person stops talking when they walk into a room and see somebody they used to know, and it hung there at her shoulder, dim, silent, very small.

Hana stared at it.

"It's never done that," she said. "In seven hundred and forty-four days it's never not finished a sentence. It says the whole thing. It *always* says the whole—"

"Window," said Jared, behind them, to his own light.

"I am not able—"

Stop.

Down the stair, he heard it go. Thirty-four voices, all the same voice, all starting the same words and stopping in the same place. *I am not able—* and silence. *I am not able—* and silence. All the way down. Like a row of clocks all stopping at the same minute.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then, from somewhere in the middle of the stair, Chris's voice: "*Okay*. Okay. That's a column." The sound of a notebook opening. "Somebody tell me the time."

"Seven hundred and forty-four days, eleven hundred and four steps," said Milli. Without thinking. "About forty minutes since the river."

"Thank you, Milli."

He turned round in the arch and looked back down the stair, over Hana's shoulder, down the line. He could see a long way; the dimmed lights were still enough to see faces by. Jared, behind them, with his shield on his back and his own light hanging small by his ear. Newish, with her mouth open, for once not saying anything. Aleaka with the goat. And further down, past the turn, the rest. Gabe. Thea with her lantern. And beside Thea, Ice, with Tuli's pole in his hand, standing on the stair with his head tipped a little toward his own shoulder.

The way he always did. Listening to something nobody else could hear.

Ice didn't have a light. Not one anybody could see. He'd never had one, in two years. There was nothing on his shoulder but his shoulder. Milli had noticed that a long time ago, the first night they all sat round a fire with him, and noticed everybody else not noticing it, and kept it, the way you keep a number you can't use yet.

Ice's face, in the dim light, was doing the same thing Milli's was.

He looked up the stair and met Milli's eyes, past Hana, past Jared, and Milli saw him see that Milli was looking. Then Ice did something with his mouth. Not quite a smile.

"How far down does it go?" said Ice.

Up the whole length of the stair. Quietly. Not to the line. To him.

Milli opened his mouth to answer. There wasn't an answer.

"I don't know," said Milli.

"Me neither," said Ice.

And something in Milli's chest that had gone tight at step eleven hundred and four let go a bit. He didn't know why.

***

There was a path down from the arch.

A ledge, then a slope, then a path, worn into the rock and then into the pale soil under the trees, going down into the forest in long easy turns. Somebody had made it. A long time ago. They went down it in the line, slowly, nobody in front, or rather Hana in front, because Ice had asked her on the stair at the Narrows, and she'd said yes, and now she was in front and didn't seem to know what to do about it except walk.

"I'm walking," she said, over her shoulder, to Milli. "Is that right? Is that what you do?"

"I think that's most of it."

"He always looks like he knows where he's going."

"He used to," said Milli. "He doesn't now."

Hana walked a few steps. "Doesn't look any different, though, does he."

"No."

"Huh," said Hana. And walked on, a bit straighter.

The trees were bigger close to. Much bigger. Their trunks were as wide as the trunks of the pines on floor 2, and paper-white, smooth, cool when he put his hand on one, with a faint grain in them like the grain in a sheet of good paper held to the light. They went straight up out of the pale soil, without branches, a long way, further than he could see, into the dark. Somewhere up there they had leaves. He could see them, now and then, when a wild light drifted near: a pale flutter, high up, catching the light and letting it go.

The ground was soft. Moss, but not green. Pale grey, almost white, springy. It took their footprints and kept them.

It was warm. That surprised everybody. After fifteen, after the river and the jam and the cold that had gone into everybody's chest, it was warm on sixteen, warm and still and damp, like a greenhouse at night. The air smelled of wet stone and something else he couldn't place for a long time and then could. Candle smoke. Very faint. The smell of a candle that's just been blown out, in another room.

"Somebody lives here," said Newish. Behind him. In a whisper, which for Newish was enormous restraint.

"Somebody made the path," said Jared.

"Somebody lives here *and* somebody made the path. Look. The moss is cut back at the edges. Somebody keeps it." Newish looked round. "Somebody who doesn't have a lot of light."

They camped where the path flattened out, in a hollow between four of the great white trunks, where somebody had made a ring of stones a long time ago for a fire. There was old ash in it. Grey and cold. Gabe looked at it for a long time before he put any wood on it.

"Small fire," said Ice.

Everybody looked at him.

He was sitting on a root with the pole across his knees. He hadn't said anything all the way down the path. He looked surprised at himself.

"I don't know," said Ice. "I don't know why. It's dark. There's nobody's lights but those." He nodded at the wild ones, drifting far off between the trunks. "And we came down a stair with thirty-five lights on and a lantern." He looked at the fire ring. "Whoever made that ring made it small."

Gabe looked at the ring. Then at Ice. Then he put half the wood back.

The fire was small. It lit their faces and the nearest trunks and not much else. And the wild lights, out in the dark between the trees, kept their distance, and drifted, and Milli lay on his back with his head on Hana's knee and watched them, and tried to count them, and lost count at four hundred and twelve, and started again, and lost count again.

***

There was no morning.

He'd known there wouldn't be. You could tell. There was no sky to have a morning in. But some part of him had expected it anyway, the way you expect a stair to have one more step in the dark, and he woke up after he didn't know how long and lay there waiting for the grey, and it didn't come.

That was the thing that got into everybody, the first day. Not the dark. The dark was fine. The *no time*.

Nobody knew how long they'd slept. Nobody knew when to get up. People woke and lay there and went back to sleep and woke again. Calvin got up twice and announced it was morning, and both times Rachael said *it's not, lie down*, and both times he said *how do you know*, and the second time she didn't have an answer. Chris tried to keep the time by the fire, how fast it burned down, and Gabe kept putting wood on it, and they had a quiet argument about it that went on for some time.

Milli counted.

He counted his own heartbeats, lying on his back with his fingers on his wrist. Sixty a minute, near enough, at rest. He could do sums with that. He'd done it on the *Patience* at night, on watch, when the clouds were over the stars.

And at about three thousand nine hundred, he heard the drip.

He'd been hearing it the whole time, he realised. He just hadn't noticed it, the way you don't notice a clock until you're listening for something. Far off. Somewhere out in the dark, down the slope, beyond the trees. A single drop of water falling a very long way, from very high up, into still water. *Plink*. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. Everything else was so quiet.

He kept counting.

At about seven thousand nine hundred, it came again. *Plink.*

He sat up.

"An hour," he said. "And six minutes. Near enough. It's an hour and six minutes."

Hana opened one eye. "What is?"

"The drip." He pointed out into the dark. "Something drips. Out there. Every hour and six minutes." He found he was smiling. "It's a clock. There's a clock."

Newish sat up on the other side of the fire with her hair everywhere.

"You counted it," said Newish.

"I counted it."

"*How long* did you count it for?"

"Two drips." He thought. "About two and a bit hours."

Newish looked at him for a long moment. Then she lay down again.

"Marry me," said Newish, to the dark.

"He's married," said Hana.

"I know. I'm saying it anyway. Somebody on this floor knows what time it is." Newish shut her eyes. "Wake me up in sixty-six minutes."

***

The goat was gone on the second night.

Or what they'd decided to call the second night, because Chris had decided they'd call it that: twenty-two drips to a day, because Milli had done the sum and twenty-two drips was a day near enough, and you slept for seven of them, and that was that, and anybody who argued could write their own column.

Milli woke because Aleaka was standing over him.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Her face did it. In the low red of the fire she was white, and she had Sleet's scruff in one hand, and Sleet was whining, a thin high noise right at the top of her throat, looking out into the dark between the trees.

He was up before he'd thought.

"How long?"

"I don't know. I don't know." Aleaka's voice was very flat, the way it went when she was frightened and didn't want anybody to know. "He was lying against my back. He always lies against my back. I woke up and it was cold." She looked out at the dark. "He doesn't go off. He's never once gone off. Not on any floor. Not since the bridge. He goes where I go. That's the whole—" She stopped. "*With me*. That's the whole thing."

"Which way?"

She pointed. Out between two of the trunks, downslope, away from the path. Into the dark. He looked. Moss. Footprints in it. Cloven, small, going away, and the pale moss keeping every one of them.

"I'll go," said Milli.

"I'm coming."

"I know. I meant: I'll go first. I can see." He looked round the fire. Everybody asleep. Thirty-four people and a dog in heaps in the low red light. And Ice, who wasn't asleep, sitting up on his root with the pole across his knees and Thea's head in his lap, looking at them both. He didn't say anything. He didn't get up. He looked at Milli, and then out at the dark, and then back at Milli.

Then he nodded. Once.

"Take Hana," said Ice. Very quietly. "She'll want to know."

Hana was already sitting up.

"No," said Milli. "Sorry. No. You glow."

Hana looked at her light. At her shoulder. Dim. It had been dim since the arch.

"It's dim," said Hana.

"It's dim *for it*. It's the brightest thing for a mile." He looked out at the wild lights in the dark, drifting, far apart. "Everything out there's small and far apart. Whoever made the fire ring made it small. Ice said." He didn't know why he was so sure. He was. "I don't want to walk into the dark with the brightest thing for a mile."

Hana looked at him for a long moment. Her ears went down, slowly. Then she lay back down.

"Bring him back," she said. "And you. Both."

He looked at his own light.

It hung at his shoulder, dim, small, the way it had since the arch. He'd never asked it for anything but numbers. It had never done anything it wasn't asked. Two years of *Window* and *Status* and *Map* and *I am not able to answer that*, a voice like a station announcement, a thing that sat on his shoulder the way a phone sits in a pocket.

He felt stupid saying it.

"Could you be less?" said Milli. To it. Quietly. "Please. Just for a bit."

It went out.

Not out. Nearly. Down to an ember. A spark, at his shoulder, so faint he could barely see it even with his eyes.

He stared at it. So did Aleaka. So did Hana, from the moss. Nobody had ever asked one of them for anything with *please*. He'd never thought of it. You didn't say *please* to a window.

"Thank you," said Milli.

The spark didn't say anything.

Aleaka looked at hers. "Could you—"

Hers went down too. To a spark.

They went out into the dark.

***

He could see better than he'd thought.

Not much. Shapes. The great pale trunks going past on either side like the legs of something enormous. The moss, faintly grey. The footprints in it, going on ahead, small and cloven and clear. And the wild lights, out in the dark, drifting. Further off than they looked. Always further off.

He walked in front and Aleaka walked behind with her hand on the back of his coat, the way Wendy held Yan's, and Sleet walked at her heel, not whining any more, but low, with her ears flat and her nose down.

The footprints went downslope. A long way. Further than he'd have thought the goat could go. Two drips, nearly, he reckoned, by his heartbeat, before they found him. And the whole way, the further they went from the fire, the closer the wild lights came.

He noticed it first at about the halfway. One of them, drifting between two trunks off to the left, that didn't drift away when they came near. It hung there. Then another, ahead. Then three, off to the right, in a little cluster, turning slowly round each other the way gulls turn on the wind.

They were watching. He was sure of it. He couldn't have said how.

"Leaka," he said. Not loud.

"I know." Her hand was tight on his coat. "I can hear them."

He stopped. "You can—"

"Heed. *When something calls you, you hear what it means.*" Her voice was very quiet. "They're calling. All of them. They're not calling *me*. They're just—calling." She was quiet for a while. "It's the same thing. Over and over. All of them. The same word."

"What word?"

Aleaka didn't answer for a moment.

"*Whose*," she said. "They're saying *whose*. Like, *whose are you*. To everything. To the trees. To each other. To us." Her voice went, a little. "It's like a lot of children in a crowd. Calling for somebody. Not *a* somebody. *Their* somebody. *Whose. Whose. Whose.*"

Milli didn't say anything. He looked at the little cluster of three, turning off to the right.

*Whose.*

He started walking again.

***

They found the goat in a clearing.

The trees stood back from a round place in the moss, about thirty paces across, he made it, and in the middle of it there was a stone, low and flat, like a table, pale with moss. And the goat was standing beside the stone, eating the moss off it. Calmly. The way he ate everything. Mother Tallow's roof. The hems of people's coats. Calvin's notes.

And round him, the lights.

Fifty. More. Wild ones, every size from a walnut to a lamp, hanging in the air round the goat in a ring, close, so close the nearest of them were nearly touching his horns, turning slowly, all of them, round him. The light of them lay on the moss and the stone and the goat's grey back, pale and soft, and showed the iron cap Gabe had made on his chipped horn, and his beard, and his strange flat eyes, which weren't frightened at all.

He looked up when they came into the clearing. At Aleaka. And went on eating.

Aleaka made a sound Milli had never heard her make. Then she walked straight into the ring of lights, without stopping, without looking at them, the way you'd walk through a crowd to get to your kid, and put both arms round the goat's neck, and her face in his shoulder.

The lights didn't scatter. They moved round her. Let her in. Closed again behind her.

"*You*," said Aleaka. Into the goat's neck. "You absolute— You *don't*. You don't go off. That's the rule. You don't. *With me*. We said."

The goat chewed.

"I said I'd keep you. *Keep* means you stay where I can—" Her voice broke. "Don't do that. Ever. You hear me?"

The goat finished his mouthful. Then he turned his head, slowly, and put his muzzle against the side of her face. Just for a moment. The way he'd put it in her hand on the bridge on floor 1.

Then he went back to eating the moss.

Milli stood at the edge of the clearing and looked at the lights.

They were looking at the goat. He was sure of that now. Not at Aleaka. At him. Turning round him, slowly, close, the way you'd walk round something you didn't understand. A thing that had come from somewhere else. A thing that wasn't from this floor.

*Nothing leaves its floor*, Aleaka had said once. On floor 1, on the bridge, with her hand out. *Nothing. Except people. And the companions from the white room.* And then the goat had walked off his bridge and up a hundred and twelve steps and up fifteen floors, and Higu had said *that's lower than Ice's*, and everybody had laughed, and nobody had thought very much about what it meant that he'd come.

The lights had. Here, in the dark, the lights were thinking about it very hard.

"What are they saying?" said Milli. "Now. To him."

Aleaka lifted her head off the goat's neck. She listened. Her face changed.

"It's different," she said slowly. "To him. It's not *whose*." She listened a long time. "It's *how*. They're saying *how*. *How did you. How did you come. How.*" She looked round at the ring of lights, turning. "They want to know how he got here."

"Don't let them follow you," said somebody.

Milli turned round so fast his tail hit a tree.

There was a girl at the edge of the clearing. Opposite him. Between two of the trunks. Small. Ten, maybe, or eleven; he couldn't tell, she was so thin. Pale. Paler than the moss. With enormous dark eyes, much bigger than they should have been, the way eyes are on things that live in the dark. Bare feet. A grey shift, and over it a coat of something that looked like felt. In one hand she was carrying a lantern. An open one: a cage of thin iron, woven in a pattern, with no glass in it and no door on it and nothing inside.

And at her shoulder, a light.

Pale. The size of a plum. Hanging just beside her ear, the way Hana's hung beside hers.

It was humming.

He heard it. Faint, under everything, the way the drip was under everything. A low steady hum, one note, like somebody humming to themselves while they work. Not words. Not a voice. Just a sound. Warm.

"They're strays," said the girl. She had a soft flat voice, as if she didn't use it much. "They're looking for somebody. If you let them follow you home, the moths come after them." She looked at the goat. "They like him. They've never seen anything like him." She looked at Aleaka. "Is he yours?"

"Yes," said Aleaka. Fiercely.

"Is he from here?"

"No."

"From where?"

"Floor 1," said Aleaka. "A bridge. On floor 1."

The girl looked at her for a long time with her enormous eyes, as if *floor 1* were a word in another language. Then at the goat. Then, slowly, at Milli.

At his shoulder. At the spark there, nearly out.

"Yours talks," said the girl.

Milli opened his mouth. Shut it.

"How do you know?"

"It's got that look." The girl tilted her head. Her light tilted with her, humming. "Like it's holding its breath. Ours don't do that. Ours just hum." She looked at Aleaka's spark, too. "Hers as well. Both of yours." She was quiet for a moment. "Lights don't talk."

"Ours do," said Milli.

"I know. I can see." The girl looked at his spark a long time. "Who taught it?"

He stood in the clearing in the dark, with fifty wild lights turning round a goat from floor 1, and a girl with a humming light looking at him, and tried to answer, and couldn't.

He'd always known the number. All his life. How big a thing was. How long it would take. How many paces round. Seventeen point three millimetres. Eleven hundred and four steps. An hour and six minutes. If he didn't know it he went and measured it until he did.

He'd never once in two years asked where the light on his shoulder came from. Nobody had. It had been there in the white room when they woke up, and it had said *Welcome, Player*, and they'd all just— taken it. The way you take the furniture in a hotel room.

"I don't know," said Milli.

The girl nodded slowly, as if that were the answer she'd expected.

"My gran will want to see," she said. "The talking ones." She looked up into the dark above the trees, where there was nothing, and listened to something. Then: "Come after the next drip. Not this one. The one after. Follow the path down. Lanthorn's at the bottom. Bring your lights small." She looked at the goat. "And bring him. My gran'll want to see him most."

"What's your name?" said Aleaka.

"Isel." The girl lifted her empty lantern, a little, the way you'd lift a hand. "Don't let them follow you."

She went back between the trees. Her light went with her, humming, and was a small pale spot going away in the dark, and then wasn't.

***

They didn't manage it.

They tried. Aleaka said *with me* to the goat and he came, and they walked back up the slope through the dark with the goat between them and Sleet at the back, and the lights in the clearing stayed where they were, turning round the stone where the goat had been, as if he were still there.

All except one.

It followed them. All the way. Not close. Ten paces back, fifteen, drifting between the trunks, stopping when they stopped. The size of an apple. Pale. Aleaka turned round three times and said *go home* to it, and the third time she said *please*, and it hung there in the dark and didn't go, and she said, very tired, "It's the one from the stair. I think. I think it's the one from the stair," and Milli thought so too, though he couldn't have said how.

When they got back to the fire it stopped at the edge of the hollow. Between two trunks. And stayed there.

Everybody was asleep. Nearly everybody. Ice was still sitting up on his root, and Thea's head was still in his lap, and he looked at them coming in out of the dark with the goat, and Milli saw him let out a breath he'd obviously been holding for two drips.

Ice looked past them at the light between the trunks. He didn't say anything about it. He just looked at it the way he'd looked at the dark from the stair. Then at Milli.

"Big?" said Ice.

"An apple," said Milli. "About."

Ice nodded slowly. "Okay."

The goat lay down against Aleaka's back, the way he always did, as if he'd never been anywhere. Aleaka lay down with her arm over him and her face in his neck and was asleep almost at once, the way you are after you've been frightened for a long time and stop.

Milli lay down beside Hana.

She was awake. He'd known she would be. She didn't say anything. She just found his hand.

He lay on his back and looked up into the dark above the trees, where there was no ceiling, and listened for the drip. *Sixty-six minutes.* He'd know the time, at least. He could know that.

And at the edge of the hollow, the stray hung in the dark, and didn't go.

He turned his head on the moss.

Hana's light was at her shoulder, the way it always was. Dim since the arch. Small. But it wasn't still. It was leaning. Very slightly. Toward the edge of the hollow, toward the pale stray between the trunks, the way a plant leans toward a window.

And it was making a sound.

He'd never have heard it with anybody else's ears. He barely heard it with his. Under the crackle of the small fire and the breathing of thirty-five people asleep and the long silence where the drip would be, there was a sound. From Hana's light. Very faint. One low note.

Not the voice.

A hum.

He lay very still. He didn't say anything. He didn't want to wake her, and he didn't want it to stop.

He tried to measure it. How long it went on. How high the note was. How far it carried.

He couldn't. He didn't know what it was. He lay in the dark with his wife's hand in his and listened to the light on her shoulder hum, softly, to a stray it had never met, in a voice nobody had ever taught it, and for the second time that day, he didn't know the number.

He found he didn't mind as much as he'd thought.

*Plink*, went the drip. Far off.

He started counting again.

No morning. A drip, far off. An hour and six minutes. Milli counted it. Newish: "Marry me." Hana: "He's married."
The goat went off in the dark. First time ever.
Milli to his light: "Could you be less? Please." It went to a spark. Nobody had ever said please to one.
Fifty wild lights round the goat in a clearing. Aleaka, Heed: they're saying "whose." To him: "how."
Isel, with an empty lantern and a light that hums: "They're strays. If you let them follow you home, the moths come."
"Yours talks. Who taught it?" Milli: "I don't know."
