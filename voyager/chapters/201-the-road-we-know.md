Gabe carried the goat up the last forty steps.

He hadn't meant to. Nobody had meant anything, on that stair. Aleaka had gone up beside the old goat one step at a time, saying *with me* on every one, softly, with her hand on his back, and the goat had come. One hoof. Then the other. Then the back legs, which were the trouble, because they didn't bend the way they used to and the steps were steep and worn hollow in the middle. By the fortieth step the goat was stopping on every one. By the sixtieth he was stopping twice. By the seventieth he stood on the step with his old legs trembling and his flat yellow eyes on the dark above him, and didn't go on, and Aleaka stood beside him and didn't push.

Behind them, thirty-four people stood on a hundred and twelve steps in the dark and waited. Nobody said anything. Nobody would have hurried him for the world.

Gabe was last-last. He came up from the bottom of the line, past everybody, sideways, with his back to the wall, saying *sorry, sorry, mind out, sorry* the way you do on a crowded stair. Past Calvin, who said *are you serious*. Past Dec, who looked at him and didn't say anything at all. He got to the seventieth step, where Aleaka was, and the goat, and he crouched down in front of the goat so they were eye to eye.

"All right, mate," said Gabe. "I'm going to pick you up. Don't butt me."

The goat looked at him.

"He won't," said Aleaka, slowly. "He likes you. You smell of fire."

"Everybody says that."

He picked the goat up. Carefully. One arm under the chest and one under the back legs, the way you'd lift a sheep, the way he'd watched the gleaners lift the lambs on fourteen. The goat weighed about as much as a sack of Mother Crane's onions. Bony. Warm. He smelled of under-a-bridge and old hay. He didn't struggle. He put his chin on Gabe's shoulder, the way Aleaka said he'd put it on his knees on the bridge every morning, and breathed in Gabe's ear, and Gabe carried him up the last forty-two steps of the stair from floor 1 like that, with Aleaka beside him saying *with me, with me*, and Newish counting at the front, and the whole line going up again behind them.

At the top the door was open. The cold came down.

He stepped out of the stair into the snow of floor 2, and set the goat down.

The goat stood there.

In the snow. Under the pines. On the second floor of the Spire, where no goat had ever stood, because nothing ever left its floor. Ever. Not a bird, not a fish, not a goat. Aleaka had said. He stood on four thin legs in the snow with his breath going up white, and looked round at the pines and the slope and the low painted sky, and blinked.

Gabe found he was holding his breath. He didn't know what he was waiting for. Something to happen. A window. A sound. The tower to notice, and reach down, and take the goat back, the way it had tried to take everything back on floor 1.

Nothing happened.

The goat put his head down and ate some grass from under the snow.

"Huh," said Gabe.

Aleaka knelt in the snow beside the goat and put her arms round his neck. She didn't say anything. She stayed like that a long time. Sleet sat down next to her with the face of a dog who had seen everything now.

Behind Gabe, Dec came out of the stair with Thea's hand in his. He looked at Aleaka with her arms round a goat on the wrong floor. He didn't say anything either. But Gabe had known him since he was thirteen, and he saw his face.

It was the face from floor 9, the third night of the fire Gabe had made. When somebody had finally made a light so he could find his way back.

***

The onion skins were still there.

Under the great pine, four hundred metres east of the stair, where Dec had camped on the first night on two and the thirty-five had slept on the second night. The bare ring round the trunk. The cold fire. And beside it, in a little heap, half under the snow, brown and papery and whole, the skins of a dozen wild onions, peeled the way only one person on any floor of the Spire peeled them.

Dec had left them there on the way down. Fifty days ago. Gabe had been sitting by the fire in his coat when he did it. He'd watched Dec see himself do it, and stop, and look at the heap, and leave it. *For whoever comes up*, Tiger had said. And Dec had said *For Gabe. Last time it was for Gabe.*

Gabe crouched down by the heap.

He'd found the first lot on the second morning of the whole thing. Floor 2, day 2, under this same tree, after a night of not sleeping and sending a whisper at four in the morning to somebody who wasn't going to answer. *just tell me youre ok. one word. please.* He'd come down to the cold fire at first light and found a little heap of onion skins, and picked one up, and known. Not guessed. Known. The way you know your own handwriting. He'd stood under this tree with an onion skin in his hand and laughed, out loud, alone, at nothing, and then sat down in the snow and not been able to get up for a while.

He picked one up now. Brown and whole and papery and dry.

He put it in the inside pocket of the wolf coat. Next to the other one. The one from the second morning. He'd had that one six hundred and forty days. It had gone soft at the edges, like cloth.

"What are you doing?" said Dec, behind him.

"Nothing."

"You're putting an onion skin in your pocket."

"It's my onion skin. You said. *Last time it was for Gabe.*" Gabe stood up. "So I'm taking it. Last time I took one too. Now I've got a pair."

Dec looked at him for a long moment.

"You kept the first one," he said.

"Course I kept the first one." Gabe did up the coat. "It was the first thing you ever said to me. Up here. Before you said anything." He looked at the heap. "Leave the rest. Somebody might come up."

They left the rest.

***

The beacon was burning on the pass.

They saw it from the bottom of the long slope at dusk, a small orange light high up against the painted grey, steady, the way it had burned every night for a year and a half. And when they came up over the shoulder of the pass in the dark, with the Long Storm sitting quiet for once on the far peaks like a cat that had decided not to bother, there was a candle in the window of the stone hut beside the tower, and the door was open, and a tiny bent shape in four shawls was sitting in the doorway in a chair, looking down the road.

"*Well?*" shouted Mother Tallow, before they were halfway up. "*Did she live?*"

Dec stopped in the road.

Gabe, behind him, saw his shoulders go. Not the bad way. The other way. The way they'd gone at the wall in the Red City when Dennis said *it's not a very good wall*.

"She lived," Dec shouted back.

Mother Tallow sat in her chair in the doorway for a moment. Then she nodded, once, sharp, like somebody ticking something off a list.

"Good," she said. "Then I can let it go out."

"*Don't*," said Dec.

It came out of him so fast and so loud that Thea laughed. Mother Tallow looked at him down the road with her bright black bird's eyes.

"I wasn't going to," she said. "I wanted to see if you'd say *don't*." She sniffed. "You did. Good. Come here. And somebody bring that girl with the mouth on her. I've been saving things up to say."

Newish went past Gabe at a run.

They stayed one night. The two women sat in the hut by the fire until two in the morning and the noise that came out of it, Hako said, was like two kettles having an argument. Dec split wood outside the door with an axe, by lantern-light, without anybody telling him to, a whole stack of it, properly, every log split clean down the grain in one stroke. At about midnight Mother Tallow came out in her shawls and stood in the doorway and watched him for a while without saying anything. Then she went back in.

"He's doing it properly," Gabe heard her tell Newish. "Somebody's taught him."

"You did," said Newish.

"I did *not*. I told him off. It's not the same." A pause. "It's nearly the same."

In the morning she gave Aleaka a candle for the goat.

"He's a long way from home," she said, putting it in Aleaka's hand, looking at the goat, who was eating the thatch on her woodshed. "Things a long way from home want a light in the window. Put it somewhere he can see it." She looked at the goat a while longer. "Ugly thing."

"He's very handsome," said Aleaka.

"He's eating my roof."

"He's very handsome *and* he's eating your roof," said Aleaka, slowly, and Mother Tallow laughed her dry stick laugh, and went back in, and shut the door, and the candle in the window stayed lit.

***

On three, at Reedmoor, the slate had a new line on it.

Gabe saw it first, because he was tallest and it was above everybody's heads. They'd come in across the open water in the eel-fishers' punts at dusk, the drowned tower burning amber far out over the reeds behind them with its ring of small honest wisps round it, and the eel-woman had been on the walkway with her broom, and said *Told you* again, exactly the way she'd said it the first time, as if she'd been saving it. And they'd crowded into the smoking hall, and there was the nail by the door, empty, and there was the slate on its two pegs.

*THE THIRTY-SIX.* In Drew's square capitals.

And under it, in chalk, in a hand none of them knew, careful and round and a bit wobbly, the hand of somebody who didn't write much and had taken a long time over it:

*+ 1 GOAT*

Gabe laughed so hard he had to sit down on a bench.

"Who wrote that?" said Drew. He was standing in front of the slate with his mouth open. "*Who wrote that?* That's *my* slate. That's—who even *told* anybody about—"

"Climbers always come back down," said the eel-woman, from the fire, with her arms folded. "And they always want to see their slate." She looked at the goat, who had come in behind Aleaka and was standing in the middle of the smoking hall looking up at the eels hanging in the dark of the roof with an expression of deep interest. "Word gets about. On three. Boys on tussocks. Somebody said there was a goat with you." She sniffed. "You'd want it right. On a slate."

"You *wrote* that?"

"My brother," said the eel-woman. "He can write. I can't. I told him what to put."

Drew looked at the slate for a long time. Then he picked up the chalk from the ledge, the same stub, and Gabe thought he was going to rub it out. He didn't. He drew a little line under *+ 1 GOAT*, neatly, the way he underlined facts.

"That's right," said Drew. "That's correct. Thank you."

Waddles went straight to the jetty and the eel basket. The eel-woman watched him go and said, without looking round, "He can have the morning. One morning. Then he's yours again."

That night Gabe went to the forge.

The little forge at the end of the village, that the eel-woman's brother kept, where Gabe had stood every night for a month on three, a year and a half ago, making a shield for somebody who wasn't there yet. The brother was a long thin silent man with burn scars on his wrists, and when Gabe came in out of the dark he looked up from his anvil and didn't say anything for a while, and then moved over, and gave him half the fire.

Gabe made a horn-cap.

A small one. Iron. A little cup of it, shaped to fit over the end of a curled horn where the tip had been chipped off, a long time ago, by a man with no weapon in a hurry on the first morning of floor 1. He measured it by eye from memory. He'd been looking at that chip for three days. He made it twice, because the first one was ugly, and the brother watched him do it and nodded at the second one, once, which from him was a speech.

He took it out to the jetty in the morning, where Aleaka was sitting with the goat, watching Waddles fish.

"For him," said Gabe. "For his horn. The chipped one." He held it out. "So it's not sharp. So he doesn't catch it on things." He paused. "And so he can butt Dec properly next time. Not just the soft side."

Aleaka took it. She turned it over in her hands. Then she held it out to the goat, on her palm, the way she held things out to everything.

The goat looked at it. He looked at Gabe. Then he put his head down, very slowly, and let Aleaka fit the little iron cup over the end of his chipped horn, and stood there with it, blinking, while she tied it on with a thong. And then he shook his head, once, and the iron cap caught the light off the water.

He walked across the jetty to Gabe.

And butted him. In the knee. Not hard.

"*Ow*," said Gabe. "What was *that* for?"

"That's thank you," said Aleaka.

"That's not how thank you *works*—"

"It is for him," said Aleaka.

Across the jetty, sitting on the end of it with his feet over the black water and Thea beside him, Dec was laughing so hard he'd had to lie down on the planks.

On four, Higu drew arrows.

Gabe watched her do it at the top of the city, at the high terrace door, where they came out of the stair from three. Her old arrows were there, of course. Floor 4. Nothing went anywhere. The small neat chalk arrows she'd drawn on day sixty-seven at the height of her own hand on every stair corner from the furnace hall to the top, pointing up. They'd been pointing up for nearly six hundred days.

She went down the city with a stub of chalk. And at every one, she stopped, and drew another arrow beside it. The same size. Pointing down.

"Both ways," said Newish, watching. "Like the stair."

"Like the stair," said Higu.

She did every one. It took the whole day, going down through the Red City from the high terrace to the furnace hall at the bottom. Nobody hurried her.

They went through the first square. Gabe had been waiting for it. He'd heard about the wall from Higu, and from Drew, and from Calvin, who'd told it at the long table on floor 1 nine times until Mother Crane threw a turnip at him. He watched Dec come out of the high stair into the square with Thea, and cross it. Calvin first, talking. Rachael's fist in his collar. Dennis complaining about something.

Dec didn't stop at the wall.

He walked past it. On the way to the main stair down, across the square. Past the fountain, past the bell on its post, past the place at the fountain's foot where the Quiet had lain and didn't any more. And as he passed the east wall, the blank stretch of red stone between two dark doorways, twenty paces from the foot of the high stair, he put his hand out. Without stopping. Without looking. Flat on the stone. The way you'd touch the frame of a door on your way out of a house you'd lived in a long time.

And took it away again. And went on.

Thea's hand was still in his other one. She didn't say anything. Behind them, Rachael's feet went a little slower past the wall than the rest of her, and her frown came on, and then went off again, and she didn't stop.

At the ledge under the main stair, on the soft red rock, there was one grave. A carrion bird was sitting on it. It looked at them, and didn't move, and they didn't make it.

And at the bottom of the city, in the furnace hall, among the four dead Kilns face down in a line on the cold floor, Gabe stopped at the scorch mark.

He knew which one. There were two. One near the middle of the hall, black and old, where on the forty-fourth day and the sixty-sixth a man nobody had seen had stood in four seconds of fire and let it burn him, alone, twice. And one a little way from it, where on the eighty-sixth day Gabe had stood in the same fire, in the wolf coat, because somebody had to have, and he'd known it was true before he knew anything else.

They were a pace apart.

Dec had stopped beside him. He was looking at them too.

"We both stood in it," said Gabe.

"Yeah."

"Not at the same time."

"No," said Dec.

Gabe looked at the two black marks on the floor of the furnace hall, a pace apart, on a floor where nothing went anywhere and nothing ever would.

"Next time," said Gabe, "at the same time. Yeah?"

Dec looked at him.

"There won't be a next time," he said. "Not on four."

"Somewhere, then." Gabe put Hearthwall down off his back and leaned it against his leg, warm. "Somewhere there'll be a fire. And you'll stand in it. And I'll be next to you this time. Not a pace off. Not eighty-six days off." He looked at Dec. "Deal?"

Dec looked at the shield. At the two black marks. At Gabe.

"Deal," he said.

On five, Nan Gully had finished her verse.

She sang it to them at the ferry, at the edge of Tether, in the wind, sitting on her stool by the great iron wheel in her four shawls with the gull on her hat. She didn't say she was going to. She just saw them come along the edge toward her, all thirty-six and a goat, and took the pipe out of her mouth, and sang. The wind took most of it, and she let it, and they leaned in to hear.

*They came up the stair with their lights in their hands,*
*and they asked for the way to the top.*
*They went on to the sky, and they never came by,*
*and we never knew where they'd stop.*

*Then a man came alone with a lid for a shield,*
*and he stood like a door in a gale.*
*And he paid twice the fare for the ones who weren't there,*
*and he told me the rest of the tale.*

*Then they came down the Spindle, the whole of them, all,*
*and they stopped at the ferry to pay;*
*and they paid for the ones who went up and stayed up,*
*and they went on down, and away.*

She stopped. She looked at them. At the goat.

*And they came back up the stair with a goat in their care*—

She stopped again.

"That's as far as it goes," said Nan Gully. "I'm still working on the goat."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Dennis put two coppers in her hand for the goat's fare, there and back, and she looked at them, and put them in her shawls, and said, "He's not going to the blue island, is he," and Dennis said, "No," and she said, "Good. Nobody wants to go to the blue island," and put her pipe back in her mouth.

The kites came down the Spindle with them on the way up, all seven, the way they'd come down it with them on the way down. Round and round. Brin landed on the crown at the top in a clatter of rain-coloured feathers and looked at the goat for a long time with his large dark eyes.

"Can he fly?" said Brin.

"No," said Aleaka.

"Can I take him? Just round once?"

"*No*," said Aleaka, Pell and Gabe, all at once.

Gabe had carried the goat up the Spindle. All five hundred metres of the spiral path, on his shoulders, round and round, with his hands in the iron rings and the goat's chin on his head and the wind coming from a different side every corner. Aleaka had walked behind him the whole way with her hand on the goat's back. The goat had not, at any point, enjoyed it.

From the crown you could see Gull Island, two bridges south of Tether. A crooked white tooth of rock. A squat tower on the top. Smoke going up from the chimney. And on the swept ring in front of the door, small and straight as a post, a figure with a broom. Not sweeping. Standing. Looking up at the crown of the Spindle.

She'd have heard. On five, everybody heard everything.

Niko stood at the edge of the crown and lifted his hand.

The figure on the swept ring lifted the broom. Once. Up, and held there, and down.

Niko didn't say anything. He put his thumb on his sword at his hip, on the dull place and then the new letters, and the blade lit through the scabbard, warm, orange, the whole length. Gabe saw it glow. Then Niko turned round and went through the arch onto the stair to six.

***

On six, they went by night, the way you did on six.

Out of the arch on the speck of rock at the western edge, into the cold silver of the Glass under a moon you could have wept at, and east along the line of the caches. Every arrow still there. Two, at the last one, side by side. Every cache still full; nobody had come up in the day not knowing. Thirty-nine skins under the stone at the last one, with Deepwell knots and onion-skin ribbons on the corks, and three under every other arrow, kept full by somebody who walked the line every month because that was how it was done.

They didn't drink any of it. They put more in.

At Deepwell, Ostrel was at the top of the pit at the end of his ledger, the way he'd been at the end of it every time, and he looked up at thirty-six people coming in out of the night and said, "Thirty-six," and wrote it down, and then looked at the goat and said, "Thirty-seven," and wrote that down too.

Imra came up from the third ring.

She came up the long stair cut into the side of the pit in the dark with her skirts in her hands, fast, the way somebody comes up a stair when they've been waiting at the bottom of it for something. She came straight to Dec. She didn't say hello.

"Did they get there?" she said. "The onions."

"They got there," said Dec.

"Did she—" Imra stopped. "Did you tell her? What I said?"

"*A woman at the bottom of a well on six says thank you for the trick*," said Dec. Word for word. "I told her."

"What did she say?"

Dec looked at her for a moment. Gabe saw his face, side-on, in the light of Ostrel's lamp.

"She said *what trick*," said Dec. "And I said *doing it properly*. And she did one. Peeled it. In one piece. The way she does. And she asked me who taught me." He was quiet. "And then she said that whoever taught me, taught me right."

Imra looked at him. She put her hand over her mouth.

"She was ill," said Dec. "In the winter. Very. She nearly went. She's all right now. She's shouting at people." He paused. "Your onions were in the broth. When she woke up. That was the first thing she ate."

Imra didn't say anything. She stood at the top of the pit with her hand over her mouth and her eyes very bright, and then she turned round and went back down the stair to the third ring, fast, without a word, and Ostrel watched her go and wrote something in his ledger and didn't show anybody what.

And at the bowl, at the end of the canyon, where the Wanting had stood in the gold evening light and shown a man a table, there were flowers.

Gabe didn't see them at first. It was night, and the moon was on the Glass, and the bowl was silver and white and cold, and the ring of glass shards stood up in the middle of it like frost. He saw Gigi stop. And Hyvred. And then he saw, in the moonlight, at the edge of the ring of shards, in the glass sand where nothing grew, ever, on six: blue.

A patch of it. Small. Low to the ground. Tiny five-petalled flowers the colour of the sky right after the sun goes, growing up through the crushed glass in a little ragged circle about the size of a hand.

Forget-me-nots.

Gigi knelt down in the sand. She didn't touch them. She just knelt there and looked. Gabe knew the story; everybody did. A handful of dead forget-me-nots that Thea had picked by the river in Hearthstead on the first week and given to her without a word, that she'd worn dead in her buttonhole for a year and a half, that she'd planted here with Sow on the way down, in the bowl, at the ring, with her hand cut on the glass and Xola healing it before she could ask.

Thea knelt down beside her.

Then Xola, on her other side.

The three of them knelt in the glass sand in the moonlight at the edge of the ring, looking at a patch of blue flowers the size of a hand that had no business growing on six at all, and none of them said anything. Gabe looked away. You did, sometimes. You gave people the thing they were looking at.

He found he was looking at Dec. Dec was standing back, at the edge of the bowl, where his feet had once stopped by themselves for twenty days. He wasn't looking at the flowers either. He was looking at the three of them, kneeling together. And his face was doing the thing it had done on floor 2 by the stair, watching Aleaka with her arms round a goat on the wrong floor.

The face of somebody seeing a light where he'd only ever seen dark.

***

They went up the stair to seven at the end of the night, out of the Still Water arch at the eastern edge of the bowl. Three hundred and twelve steps. Tiger opened the door with his hand flat on it. Newish counted. Thea sang. Calvin went first, talking, and Rachael's fist went into his collar on the first step.

Gabe went last-last. He always would now.

He stopped at the bottom of the stair and looked back at the bowl. At the ring of shards in the moonlight. At the little dark patch at the edge of it that would be blue in the morning, when the sun came up and pressed on everything and there'd be nobody there to see.

*Somebody might come up*, Drew had said, on four.

He put his hand in his coat pocket and found the two onion skins. One soft at the edges like cloth. One new. He held them.

Ahead of him on the dark stair, a long way up, he could hear Dec say something to Thea, quietly, and Thea laugh. Gabe didn't hear what. He didn't need to.

He'd known Dec since he was thirteen. He'd watched him go up a tower alone, for a long time, ahead of everybody, without a word. He'd sent him a whisper at four in the morning on the second day that said *one word, please*, and waited a hundred and sixty-four days for the word. He'd punched him in a bakery. He'd made him a shield. He'd carried him on it, grey, through a crypt in Tell. He'd sat on his legs on floor 1 so he'd sleep.

He'd never once heard him laugh this much. Not on Earth. Not up here. Not ever.

*We're going up*, Gabe thought. *All of us. And he knows the way.*

He went up the stair.
