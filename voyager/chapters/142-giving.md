Tell was the best market Dennis had ever seen, and he'd had six days in it.

He'd found the Lantern Market in the first four minutes, obviously, by coming out of a well into the middle of it. By the end of the first day he'd found the other three. The Hay Market on the second ring, where the farmers came in from the plain with carts. The Cloth Hall, which was a market pretending to be a building. And the Shambles, a crooked lane off the Wellgate where every shop sold meat and every shopkeeper shouted, and which he'd stood at the top of with his eyes shut for a full minute, just listening, until Calvin asked if he was all right.

He was more than all right. Tell *traded*. Everything in it. Tell weighed and measured and haggled and wrote it down, in two hands, every time. Every sale on the Wellgate over a shilling went in a book. There were tellers who did nothing all day but sit in a booth in the Cloth Hall and write down who'd bought how many ells of what from whom. Dennis had stood and watched one for an hour. It had been, he told Rachael that night, the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen.

"That's the saddest thing you've ever said," said Rachael.

"You didn't *see* it," said Dennis.

So he'd had six good days. And then, on the seventh, the climbers' scribe went down a stair with a key and a leaf, and every lost line in Tell came back for one breath, and Tell went mad.

***

He didn't see it happen. Nobody in Deston's party did. They'd been in the Hay Market, all five of them, because Deston had wanted to see where the food came from, and Dennis had been halfway through a long and beautiful argument with a woman selling cheeses about the difference between a hard cheese and an old one.

And in the middle of it, the woman's tally-board, a slate hanging from a nail on her cart with the day's sales chalked on it, went *white*.

Not the chalk. The slate. For one breath, every line that had ever been chalked on that slate and wiped off came back at once, one over another, so many and so close that the whole slate went pale with them, like frost on a window. Years of sales. Old prices. A child's name, small, in the corner.

Then it was gone. Just today's sales, in chalk.

The cheese woman stood and stared at it with her mouth open.

All over the Hay Market, people were standing and staring at things. A man with a ledger open on a barrel-head had both hands flat on it. A girl was holding a letter up to the light. Somebody behind the grain stalls had started to cry.

"What was that?" said Calvin.

"Cursiffe," said Deston.

They all looked at him.

He was standing very still beside the cheese cart with his hand flat on his chest, the way he stood when he was feeling the threads. His face was strange.

"Something went through everybody," he said slowly. "All of us. All thirty-five threads, at once. Like when you hear your name across a room." He looked up the hill. At the Tellhouse, halfway up the Wellgate. "It came from there. It was Cursiffe's colour."

***

By the evening, Tell knew what had happened, and by the next morning, Tell was furious.

Not all of it. Dennis would think about that afterwards. He'd think that if he'd been asked, on that first morning, he'd have said the whole city was angry, because that was what it sounded like. But it wasn't the whole city. It was the people who were loud.

It had reached everything. That was the trouble. Not just the Rolls in the Tellhouse. Every written thing on the hill. Every ledger, every letter, every deed in every strongbox, every slate and tally-stick and shop-board and scrap of paper pushed under a door. For one breath, everything that had ever been written on it and lost came back. Wiped chalk. Faded ink. Torn-off corners. Pages that had burned.

And in a city where everything was written down, a great many people had seen, for one breath, something they hadn't been meant to see.

The Lantern Market on the morning of the three hundred and sixty-fourth day was a market where nobody was buying anything. It was full. But everybody in it was standing in knots, with papers. Comparing them. Shouting about them. A moneylender called Wace had his great book open on a trestle and a crowd round it, because for one breath every debt in his book that had ever been paid off and scratched through had come back unpaid, and every man who'd ever paid him wanted to know why the lines that came back said *unpaid* when he'd *paid*, and Wace was saying that it was a trick, it was a climbers' trick, and the debts that came back weren't *told*, they weren't in two hands, they were *nothing*.

"He's right, though," said Dennis, watching from the steps of the well-house.

"*Dennis*," said Rachael.

"No, he is. That's the problem. They're not told. They're one breath of something nobody wrote down twice." He watched a man in a leather cap shove Wace in the chest. "Half of these are going to be lies they've remembered wrong. And half of them aren't. And nobody can tell which, because there's only one hand."

"Two hands that don't agree," said Azac. He had his chin on his shield rim. "*And so the city of Tell found out that it had been lying to itself for three hundred years*."

"Not all of it," said Deston.

"No," said Azac. "Some of it. The bits that were written down."

***

The apple woman came to the copy-room yard at noon.

Dennis didn't know her. Yan did. Yan saw her come in through the yard gate with her basket on her arm and said, "Oh," quite softly, and then went to fetch Tiger.

She was middle-aged and round and tired-looking, in a brown dress with flour on it, and she stood just inside the gate as if she wasn't sure she was allowed further. Tiger came out of the stable door, saw her, and stopped dead.

"You gave me an apple," said Tiger.

"I did," said the woman. "Did you eat it?"

"No."

She looked at him for a moment.

"Why not?"

Tiger thought about it. You could see him think. His tail went slowly round his ankle.

"It was the first thing anybody gave me here," he said. "I've still got it."

The woman's face did something. She put her basket down on the edge of the trough.

"I'm Joan Tull," she said. "I want to talk to whoever's in charge of you."

That was Drew, technically, but Drew was out past the Fell Gate with Thea's party, and Deston was nearest, and Deston was the one who said, "Sit down, Mistress Tull," and fetched her a stool. And Dennis sat on the trough beside her, because there was something in her face he recognised from every market on every floor: somebody who'd been cheated, and knew exactly by how much.

"My husband was Piers Tull," said Joan. "Fruiterer. On the Wellgate, below the Tellhouse, the shop with the green shutters. Four winters ago he died. Before he died he made his will, and it was told, in two hands, in the Tellhouse. I watched them do it. Two tellers. He left the shop to me."

"But," said Dennis.

She looked at him.

"But," she said. "When I went to read it, after, the page was gone. Torn out of the Roll. Clean, at the stitching. And a will that isn't in the Rolls isn't told. So it went to his next of kin. His brother." Her mouth went thin. "Hamo Tull. Alderman Hamo Tull. Of the Bench."

"And he's got the shop," said Dennis.

"He's got the shop. I've got a basket." She lifted it slightly off the trough edge, and put it down again. "I sell apples in the Lantern Market, off his trees, that he lets me pick, if I'm grateful about it."

"And yesterday," said Deston.

"Yesterday, Hawise Brand was standing in the Reading Hall when the thing came up the stair. And she had the Roll of four winters ago in her hands, because she's had it in her hands every month for four years, because she *wrote* that will, she was one of the two hands, and she's never forgiven anybody for losing it." Joan's voice was quite steady. "And for one breath, she says, the torn edge stood up. The stub, at the stitching. It stood up into a whole page, like frost. And she read it. Every word. It was his will, the way she wrote it. The shop to me."

"And then it was gone," said Dennis.

"And then it was gone."

There was a pause in the yard. Somewhere in the stable, Hako was singing to himself.

"Hawise's hand is one," said Joan. "She saw it. She'll swear to it. But it needs two. And nobody else was looking at that page." She turned to Deston. "Your scribe. The quiet one, with the ink. Hawise says he made it happen. I want to know if he can make it happen again."

***

Cursiffe said he didn't know.

He said it in the copy-room that evening, sitting on his blanket with the iron key round his neck and the cool leaf in his pocket, looking at his own ink-stained hands as if they belonged to somebody else. He said that what he'd done on Water Street had taken everything he'd had and most of a dead city's worth of help. He said that the window said *what happened, and is kept anywhere*, and that a will was a thing that had happened, and was kept, if Hawise remembered it. He said he thought he could. He said he didn't know what it would cost.

"And Askew says it won't count," he said. "Not on its own. Not even if I do it. The Echo isn't two hands. It's one. Mine. It's for the Bench to say if it's told."

"The Bench," said Dennis. "Where Hamo Tull sits."

"Where Hamo Tull sits," said Cursiffe.

Dennis looked round the copy-room. At thirty-five people on blankets with grey threads on their wrists, and one man by the end wall with a grey cord on his.

Declan wasn't saying anything. He'd come in from the Fell Gate at dusk with Thea and Xola and a long scrape down the front of his shield, and eaten, and sat down against the wall, and listened to all of it with his face doing nothing. Dennis had watched him listen. Dennis had got quite good at watching Declan listen, since floor 10. Since the night he'd looked up from a wall in the snow, with his arm gone white to the elbow, and seen Declan come round a corner faster than he'd ever seen anything move, and stop, and stare at the arm, and sit down in the snow with his face in his hands.

*You ran to her. Shouting.*

He still didn't know what that had been. He'd decided he didn't need to. But he watched.

Declan was looking at the floor. Thinking. And Dennis would have bet every copper in Tell that whatever Declan was thinking about, it wasn't the will.

"They'll say we did it on purpose," said Declan.

Everybody looked at him.

"The Bench." He didn't look up. "If he does it again. In front of them. They'll say we came up the well and broke their Rolls, and now we're choosing which bits to fix." He turned the cord on his wrist with his thumb. "It doesn't matter if it's true. It's what they'll say."

"Is it bad?" said Calvin.

Declan was quiet for a while.

"It's people," he said. "It's just people. Deciding."

***

The Bench decided the next day.

A crier came down the Wellgate at noon on the three hundred and sixty-fifth day, in a blue coat with a staff, and stood on the steps of the well-house in the Lantern Market, where everybody could see, and rang a hand-bell until the market went quiet. Behind him, two paces back, stood a clerk of the Count, in grey, with its book on its arm, looking at nothing.

The crier unrolled a paper.

"*By order of the Bench of Tell*," he cried, "*given this day under the hand of Hamo Tull, Warden of the Bench, and a second hand: the climbers who came up the well are held to the Rolls until the Reckoning. They may not buy, nor sell, nor trade, nor be traded with, in Tell. Whoever trades with them answers to the Bench.*"

He rolled the paper up. Rang the bell once more. And went back up the Wellgate, with the clerk two paces behind him.

Dennis stood in the middle of the Lantern Market with three coppers in his hand. He'd been about to buy a pie. The pie man, three feet away, was looking at the coppers as if they'd turned into spiders.

Slowly, the pie man shook his head.

Dennis put the coppers back in his pocket.

He'd known a lot of markets. He'd stood in a ghost market on floor 4 and haggled with a woman who wrote on a slate. He'd sold glass on floor 6 and fish on floor 8 and stories for honey-pears on floor 9. He'd stood in the stores of the Bastion and found out there was such a thing as a place where nobody traded at all, and it had nearly broken his heart, until he'd learned.

He had never in his life stood in the middle of the best market he'd ever seen and been told he wasn't allowed in it.

"Well," said Calvin, beside him, after a while. "That's *rude*."

***

It was worse than rude, and by the evening they knew how much.

The baker Bertha had already had her shutter up for a week. Now so did the cheese woman, and the pie man, and the knife man, and the tavern with NO CORDS on its board, and the tavern with CORDS WELCOME, whose landlord came out and took the board down himself with a face like a man burying a dog, and said to Calvin, "I'm sorry, lad. I've three children. The Bench can take my licence." The smith by the Fell Gate, who'd let Gabe and Jared and Salty use his second forge for a penny a day, shut his gate and wouldn't look at them. The man in the Cloth Hall who'd sold Hana a length of green wool came after her down the aisle and asked for it back, and she gave it him, and he stood holding it and looked as if he might cry.

They had food for four days, Hako said. Maybe five. He'd counted.

"We can hunt," said Winter. "Outside the walls. It's not *trading* if we shoot it."

"Thirty-six people," said Hako. "Every day."

"And then what?" said Chris. "We cook it on what? Whose wood? Their wood's for sale too."

"Ina doesn't need wood."

"Ina can't cook for thirty-six on his *hands*, Winter."

"I could," said Ina, very quietly, from the corner, and everybody stopped and looked at him, and he went slightly pink and didn't say anything else.

Dennis sat on the end of the bench and listened to all of it and didn't say anything for a long time.

He was thinking about the Bastion. The stores under the long halls. Hesk, with his grey eyes, looking down at a pile of sawyer shell and eel oil and shardback glass that Dennis had carried in on his back and put on the floor.

*That's not trading. That's giving.*

*I'm learning.*

"Then we don't," he said.

The copy-room went quiet. It wasn't used to Dennis speaking up in councils. That was Drew's job, and Deston's, and Higu's with the rock. Dennis's job was knowing what things cost.

"Don't what?" said Drew.

"Trade." Dennis stood up. "The order says we can't buy, or sell, or trade. Fine. We don't. We *give*."

"Give what?" said Calvin. "We haven't got anything. That's the *point*."

"Not things." Dennis looked round at them. At thirty-five people who'd spent three hundred and sixty-five days learning how to do things. "Salty fixed a door in the Tellhouse that had squeaked for forty years. He didn't charge for it. Rachael's healed half the Lantern Market's cuts this week in the yard, and nobody's paid her a penny. Bee's drawn about forty children." He spread his hands. "We've been giving since the first day. We just didn't call it anything. Hesk told me. On floor 10. *That's not trading. That's giving.*" He looked at Hawise, who'd come up the stable stairs at the start of the council and was standing in the door with her arms folded. "Is there a law in Tell against giving?"

Hawise opened her mouth.

Then she shut it again. And frowned. And Dennis watched her go through every law in Tell in her head, the way he'd watched the teller in the Cloth Hall go through a column of figures.

"No," she said slowly. "There isn't." She looked faintly astonished. "There's a law for every kind of sale there is. There's a law for loans, and for pledges, and for hiring, and for bride-gifts, and for what a man owes his neighbour if his pig gets out. There's no law for just—*giving* somebody something. If they're not family. If you don't want anything for it." She unfolded her arms. "Nobody ever needed one."

"Then nobody can forbid it," said Dennis.

There was a silence.

Then, from the end wall, very quietly, Declan laughed.

***

They gave.

It started the next morning, and Dennis ran it, because somebody had to, and he found, somewhat to his own surprise, that running a market where nothing was sold was not very different from running one where everything was.

You found out what people needed. You found out what you had. You put the two together.

Salty went up the Wellgate with his file and his pot of fat, door by door, and fixed hinges. Every squeak on the hill, he said. He'd heard them all, coming up and down, and they'd been driving him mad. Gabe and Jared couldn't use the smith's forge, so they went along the walls instead and mended things that didn't need fire: a gate-latch, a cart-wheel, a child's iron hoop. Rachael set up a stool in the copy-room yard by the pump, with Kei and Malcolm and Xola, and anybody who was hurt could come, and a great many people did, because a great many people in Tell had something wrong with them that they'd been putting off because the barber-surgeon on the second ring charged a shilling.

Gigi and Maigo went round the window boxes. Half of them in the Low Town were dead, or dying, and the two of them went along the streets with their hands in the earth, and behind them the boxes came up green. Hako carried water. Up the Wellgate from the Lantern Market pump, two buckets at a time, for anybody too old to carry their own, which turned out to be a great many people. Hyvred found things. She stood in the Lantern Market with her glasses off and said "*Who's lost something?*" and within an hour there was a queue: a ring, a dog, a key, a child's shoe, a husband (in a tavern on the second ring, which caused a scene). Bee drew. Azac sat on the well-house steps and told stories to anybody who'd sit, which was mostly children, who sat. Winter took her bow out past the Fell Gate with Aleaka every morning, and came back every evening with birds, and gave them to the old woman with the half-door, who cooked them and gave them out again in buns.

Calvin talked. Dennis didn't know what to call that, exactly, but it was a lot of it, all day, up and down the Wellgate, to anybody who'd listen. And it seemed to Dennis that by the end of the second day, there was nobody in the Low Town who didn't know Calvin's name, and that a lot of them had started saying hello to him first.

And every morning, when they came down the stable stairs into the yard, there was food on the step.

Nobody saw who left it. Bread, one morning. Three loaves, still warm, wrapped in a cloth. The next morning a cheese, and a bag of onions, and a crock of something that turned out to be pickled eggs. The morning after that, a whole ham, which took two people to carry, and caused Tiger to make a noise Dennis had last heard him make on floor 1.

"It's not trading," said Hawise, looking at the ham with her arms folded. "If you don't know who left it."

"No," said Dennis. "It's not."

He looked out of the yard gate, down the Wellgate, towards the Lantern Market.

Bertha the baker's shutter was still up. But there was flour on the step of the copy-room. Just a little. A white smear, by the bread cloth, as if whoever had carried the loaves had come straight from somewhere floury, in a hurry, before dawn, before anybody could see.

***

On the third day of giving, Dennis noticed the clerks.

He'd stopped noticing them, mostly. Everybody had. That was the thing about the clerks: you could only notice them for so long before your eyes started to slide off them, the way your eyes slid off a piece of furniture. There was one at the corner of the Lantern Market by the eel stall, as there always was, standing quite still with its book on its arm.

And as Dennis watched, Hako came past it with two full buckets and set them down on the step of a little crooked house by the market's edge, where an old man with a stick was waiting in the doorway. And the old man said something, and Hako laughed, and picked them up again and carried them inside for him.

And the clerk wrote something in its book.

Dennis went still.

He watched. For an hour. Sitting on the well-house steps beside Azac and a ring of children, while Azac told them about a ram with a curl of iron horn, he watched the clerk at the corner of the market.

Salty fixed a hinge on a door across the square. The clerk wrote.

A girl brought Rachael a sprig of something blue from a window box, shyly, and ran away. The clerk wrote.

A woman bought a cabbage from a stall, and paid, and the stallholder chalked it on his board. The clerk didn't move.

A boy gave Calvin half of his apple. The clerk wrote.

"Azac," said Dennis, very quietly.

"*And so our hero*—" Azac stopped. "What?"

"It's counting."

Azac followed his eyes. Looked at the clerk for a long time.

"Counting what?"

"Not the trading. It doesn't care about the trading. I watched. Somebody sold a cabbage right in front of it and it didn't even blink." Dennis's mouth was dry. "It writes when somebody *gives* something. Us, or them. Every time. Hako carried that man's buckets in and it wrote it down. The boy gave Calvin half an apple and it wrote it down."

Azac was quiet.

"Why would the tower count that?" he said.

"I don't know," said Dennis.

He thought about Hesk. *That's not trading. That's giving.* He thought about the first market in Hearthstead, on floor 1, where every stall had sold exactly the same things every day, to exactly the same people, at exactly the same price, and nobody had ever given anybody anything, because nobody there had been able to.

He thought about the clerks. About Hawise, saying, *they give up their names. When they go in. Nobody makes them.*

"I think," he said slowly, "it's counting the things it didn't put there."

Azac looked at him.

"*And so*," said Azac, after a while, very softly, to nobody, "*the tower stood on every corner of the city, and wrote down every time somebody did something it hadn't told them to*."

On the steps below them, one of the children tugged his sleeve. "And then what happened to the ram?"

Azac looked down at her.

"Then the line held," he said. "It always does, in the end. Sit down. I'll tell you."

***

He took Rachael her supper at dusk.

He always did. He'd done it since floor 10, since the house for the wounded, since before the night on the fourth level when he'd run at her through a street full of Rimed with forty mouths shouting her name. He'd brought her food because she didn't stop to eat. And then he'd kept on bringing it, after, when she did stop, because it had turned into a thing he did, and he'd found he didn't want to stop.

She was still on the stool by the pump in the copy-room yard, with the light going. The queue had gone. There was a basin of pink water at her feet and her sleeves were rolled up to the elbow, and she was sitting with her hands on her knees, palms up, looking at them.

He sat down on the edge of the trough and gave her the bowl. Pickled eggs and ham and somebody's bread. She took it and didn't eat it.

He looked at her fingertips. White, to the first knuckle. Going pink.

"How many today?"

"Forty-something." She flexed her fingers. "A burn. Two bad cuts. A man with a cough that went down into his chest, I took that, that's the white. A woman who'd had a toothache for a year." She almost smiled. "That one was the worst. My whole jaw. For about ten minutes."

The echo. She'd told him about it, finally, on floor 10, after the fifth wall, when her arms had gone pink again. Every wound she closed, she felt again, after, in the same place. Every one, since floor 1. And she'd never told anybody.

"You don't have to do forty," he said.

"I know."

"You could do twenty."

"I know, Dennis." She picked up a piece of bread. Put it down again. "They come in through that gate and they've had it a year. The toothache. A *year*. Because the man on the second ring wants a shilling and she hasn't got a shilling." She looked at the gate. "And now they *can't* pay. Because it's the law. And it's the first time anybody's ever looked at her tooth."

Dennis didn't say anything. He watched her fingertips go pink.

"Dennis," she said, after a while. "Do you ever think about it? Floor 10."

"Every day."

"When you ran."

"Every day," said Dennis.

She was quiet.

"Why did he sit down in the snow?" said Rachael.

He knew who she meant. There was only ever one *he*, when anybody in Deston's party said it like that.

"I don't know," said Dennis.

"I do," said Rachael. "Nearly."

He turned and looked at her. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking across the yard. At the stable door. At the light from the copy-room window over it, where somebody had lit the one lamp they were allowed, and a shadow went past, with the edge of a grey cord at its wrist.

"I've nearly got it," said Rachael. "I've had it nearly for a long time. Since Calvin's arm. Since the jetty, when he looked at Calvin's arms like—" She stopped. "And then you, in the snow. And the clerk at the well. *Already told*." She picked up the bread again. "I keep putting it next to the echo. Mine. Feeling it again after. In the same place."

"Rach," said Dennis.

"I'm not going to say it." She ate a piece of bread, finally, as if to prove she could. "I'm just telling you I've nearly got it. So somebody knows."

He sat with that.

He didn't say *I've nearly got it too*. He didn't know if he had. He'd decided, on floor 10, that he didn't need to know, the way Deston had decided. But he sat on the edge of the trough in the dusk, in the best market he'd ever been banned from, beside the person he'd once run at through forty mouths shouting her name, and found that he was thinking about the snow. About Declan, with his face in his hands. About how somebody's face looks when they've seen a thing happen that they've seen happen before, and it hasn't happened.

"Eat your eggs," he said.

"I'm eating them."

"You're *holding* them."

Rachael looked at him sideways. And then, for the first time all day, she laughed. Short, through her nose. And ate the eggs.

Up in the window, the shadow with the cord had stopped. Just for a moment. As if it had heard her.

Then it went on.
