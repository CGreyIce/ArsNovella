Burr brought it to them on the sixth morning, with his arms folded.

"The north tree," he said.

He didn't say anything else for a moment. He stood on the edge of the big platform by the rope drum with the early light coming down green through the leaves, and looked at the trunk of the nearest of the seven, which went down past the platform into the dark of the lower branches and on, out of sight, to the Down.

"Every year," said Burr, "they get a hand's width further in."

Ice had his bowl of nut porridge halfway to his mouth. He put it down.

"The sawyers," said Tansy, from the drum, where she was sitting on a handle with her legs swinging. "They come every night it's dry. To the north tree. They saw at the foot of it. You can see where they've been." She held her hands apart, very wide. "My grandmother's grandmother said when she was little it was a scratch."

"It's not a scratch," said Burr.

"It's not a scratch," Tansy agreed.

Burr unfolded his arms. He looked at the thirty-six of them, sitting about the platform in the green morning with their bowls, and his big eyes went from face to face, quick, and then still.

"Tansy found where they sleep," he said. "Yesterday. A fallen one. West. They've bored it through like a cheese. A brood. In the day they sleep in the holes." He was quiet. "We can't. We're too small, and they don't sleep that deep. But you're walkers. You're big. And there's thirty-six of you."

The window came up in front of Ice. And in front of everyone else, he knew, at the same moment, small and blue in the green light.

Drew made it simple.

All of them. One group. Down together, in the full of the morning, west to the log, and back together by the time the light at the top of the trees started going gold. Nobody split up. Nobody went off to look at anything. Ice in the middle.

He wrote it on the rock while they finished their porridge, and everybody read it, and then everybody looked at Ice.

He knew why. It was Higu's rule. *You say where.*

He looked at his bowl.

He wanted to say no. That was the thing. He wanted to say it so much that he could feel the shape of the word in his mouth. *No. Not the Down. Not all of us. Not today.* And he didn't have a reason. They'd been down every day for five days. In the day, in parties, with the rule, and come back up every night, all of them, nobody left down. It was morning. It was all thirty-six together, in one group, with Drew. Burr's rules held. There was nothing wrong with any of it.

He'd been on this floor for six days, and he hadn't said a single thing about it that wasn't *where*.

"Sawyers," he said. "Big. About the size of a pony. Like a beetle. Black and brown, shiny. Feelers longer than they are."

"They've got two jaws," said Ice. "Here." He put his hands together in front of his face, wrists touching, fingers curved like hooks. "Like that. But they don't bite. They go side to side. They *saw*." He moved his hands. "Anything. Wood. Bone. If they get the edge of your shield between them, they'll go through it." He stopped. "They go for the rim. It's the thin bit. Keep it turned. Give them the face."

"How long to get through a rim?" said Hako.

"A night," said Ice. "Three nights. Depends on the rim."

He heard himself say it. *Three nights.* Nobody else noticed anything. They all just nodded and wrote it down and looked at their shields. Salty ran his thumb along his greatsword's chipped edge and looked mournful.

But Gabe was looking across at Ice's shield. The lamp shield. He'd started it on floor 3 out of Fen iron, for somebody who wasn't there yet, and he hadn't been able to finish it until floor 5, because the rim wanted Kiln iron. The black iron out of the furnace, that didn't take an edge and didn't give one either. Ice had it on his knees. Gabe looked at the rim for a long moment.

"Not this rim," said Gabe.

Ice looked at it.

"No," he said. "Not that one."

***

He went with Xav's party that day, because the rock said so. Gabe on his left. Rui Xiang ahead, John's motes over their heads, Malcolm behind with his backup heal already half-cast the way it always was. Xav beside him, saying nothing in the way Xav said nothing, which was a way of saying a great deal.

They went down the ropes in the green morning and walked west.

The log was the length of a street. It had been a tree once, one of the great ones, and it had come down a long time ago, so long that its top had sunk into the Down and the Grow had gone over it, and only the great trunk still showed, lying across the forest like a fallen wall, three times as high as a house. And it was full of holes. Round ones. Big enough to crawl into. Hundreds of them. All along its side, at every height, so that it looked like a honeycomb or a cheese, the way Burr had said. And under every hole, on the moss, there was a heap of sawdust. Pale and soft and fresh. Like snow. Drifts of it.

Everybody stopped at the edge of the drifts and looked.

From inside the log, very softly, there was a sound. Not sawing. Breathing. Slow and dry and enormous, and all through it, like wind through a house with every window open.

"Oh, I *hate* that," whispered Newish.

Drew looked at the log for a long time. Then he did his hand signs, the ones they'd made on floor 3 for the Fen, where things listened, and everyone moved without a word.

***

It was a good fight. Ice thought that, afterwards, for the little while that he was able to think anything about it. It was a good fight, and they did it well.

They pulled them out one at a time.

That was the plan, and it worked. Ina put a little fire into a hole, just a tongue of it, just enough. Something in the hole woke up. You heard it. The breathing stopped, and there was a dry scraping, like a cart being dragged over gravel, and then out of the round dark of the hole came the feelers first, long and jointed and whipping, and then the head.

It was black and brown, and shone. Glossy as a wet chestnut. A head as big as a cauldron, flat and wide, and out of the front of it, the jaws. Two of them. Long and curved and toothed along the inside edge, like two saws bent into hooks. They moved. Side to side. Slowly. *Rrrrrrr.* Like breathing.

Then the rest of it came out of the hole and fell onto the sawdust, and it was the size of a pony, and it had six legs, and it turned round.

Gabe was in front of it with his shield up before it had finished turning.

It went for the rim. The way Ice had said. The jaws came round, both at once, low, for the bottom edge of the shield, and Gabe turned it, a quarter turn, the way he'd been told, and gave it the face. The jaws closed on flat iron and skidded. A shriek of metal. Sparks, real sparks, orange in the green light. And Rui Xiang came in from the side, low, and put his blade into the joint behind the head, where the shiny black went soft and grey, and it was dead.

Its legs kept going. For a long time. They scraped and kicked on the moss after the rest of it had stopped, and everybody stood back and watched them, and nobody said anything.

"*Next*," said Drew.

They did eleven like that. One hole at a time. Ina's fire, the scraping, the feelers, the head, the jaws going for the rim. Hako forgot, once, and gave one the edge of his shield instead of the face, and the jaws closed on it, and in the second before Hyvred's arrow went through its eye, there was a sound like somebody sawing a plank in half. When Hako held the shield up afterwards, there was a notch in the rim as deep as his thumb.

"It was *sawing* my *shield*," said Hako, outraged. "It was *sawing* it. In *one second*."

"Rim," said Ice. "Give it the face."

"I *know*, I just—it's so *fast*—"

And then the twelfth came out of a high hole on its own, without fire, because the log had woken up. You could hear it. The breathing everywhere had stopped. All through the great dead trunk there was scraping now, from every hole at once, and the feelers came out of a dozen of them together.

"*Up*," said Drew. "Shields *up*. Newish—"

"*Got* it," said Newish.

And it went the way things went now. Names, thrown, at nobody's beat. *Milli.* And Milli was in and out with both daggers. *Tiger.* *Wendy.* Hyvred and Winter on the trunk, high, shooting down into the holes as the heads came out. *Salty.* Ina's white fire in a long sheet along the bottom of the log, where the sawdust went up like straw. *Azac.* *Chris.* Thirty-five people moving in one shape through drifts of sawdust and smoke and noise and the screaming of jaws on iron, and not one of them moving out of time, because there wasn't any time. There was only Newish, and Drew, and the next name.

One came for Ice.

Out of a low hole, right by him, from the side. He saw it come and had his shield round before he'd decided to. It hit him full in the shield, the whole weight of it, a pony's weight running, and he went back a step and set his feet, and the jaws came round for the rim.

They closed on Kiln iron.

He felt them close. Through the shield, through his arm, into his teeth. Two saws closing on the edge of the black iron from the furnace on floor 4, and grinding, side to side, side to side, *rrrrrrrrrrrrrr*, with sparks pouring off it orange and the whole shield shuddering on his arm.

And nothing.

They didn't bite. They couldn't find anything to bite. The Kiln iron didn't take an edge, and didn't give one, and the saws went side to side on it, screaming, and skated, and skated, and skated.

He looked at the jaws grinding on his rim, a hand's width from his face, in the sparks. And for a second he wasn't on floor 9 at all. He was on floor 9 a long time ago, and the jaws were on a different rim, a good one, the best shield he'd had, and they were going side to side, and side to side, and he could hear them getting through.

"Dec," said Gabe.

He put his sword through the soft grey joint behind its head. It died. Its legs kept going.

"Not that rim," said Gabe. Breathing hard, beside him, with his own shield up. He wasn't looking at Ice. He was looking at the dead sawyer on the moss with its jaws still clamped on the edge of the lamp shield, still very slowly trying to move.

"No," said Ice.

He pulled the shield free. There wasn't a mark on it.

It took them till the middle of the afternoon. When it was done, there were forty-one dead sawyers on the moss round the log, and some of them were still kicking, and the sawdust was trodden into red-brown mud, and everybody was sitting down.

The light was still green-gold. High up, at the tops of the trees, it hadn't started to turn. There was time. An hour to sit, and two to walk home, and they'd be at the rope tree before the gold. Ice sat on a root with his shield across his knees and checked it anyway. They'd been told. Up before dark.

The window came up.

Maigo felt it first.

Ice saw him. He was sitting a little way off, under the end of the log, with his eyes half shut, the way he sat now on this floor whenever he stopped, as if he were listening to something nobody else could hear. And he opened his eyes. All the way. And sat up, very fast, with his hand flat on the moss.

"It's—" said Maigo.

Then everybody heard it.

The ticking. In daylight. Not faint, the way it was in the day. Not the slow growing creak of the Down that you only heard if you stopped. Loud. Louder than at dusk. Louder than it had ever been. Ten thousand dry clicks and creaks and snaps, from everywhere, from all round, from right underneath them, so fast it was almost one sound. Like a fire taking in dry grass.

"*Up*," said Drew. "Everybody *up*—"

The ground between Chris and Hyvred came apart.

Ice saw it. Between the two of them, where they'd been sitting three metres apart on the trodden moss, something came up out of the ground. Not one thing. Lots of things. Stems. Dark red, and thick as fingers, and thorned, and they came up out of the earth like water out of a spring, fast, faster than anything that grew had any right to, and climbed over each other and thickened and branched, and in the time it took Chris to stand up, there was a hedge between him and Hyvred as high as his chest.

And then higher.

And then it wasn't only there.

It came up everywhere. Briar and root, and fern unrolling so fast you could hear it, and saplings, pale and green and whippy, going up out of the moss like thrown spears. Between Hana and Yan. Between Deston and Thea. In long lines. Curving walls. All round the clearing, all at once, splitting it, and splitting it again, into rooms.

And it didn't split them anyhow.

Ice saw that too. He saw it in the first two seconds, while everyone else was still shouting. The walls came up between parties. Not through them. Between them. Hyvred on one side and Chris on the other, and on Hyvred's side, Gigi, and Bee, and Hako, and Kei. Five. And on Chris's, Niko, and Maigo, and Winter, and Ina. Five. It went round them. It *sorted* them.

Seven rooms of five.

And then the moss under his own boots split open, right between him and Gabe, where Gabe had been sitting with his shoulder nearly against his.

Gabe got hold of his arm.

He didn't think. Ice saw him not think. The briar came up between them out of the ground, red, thorned, already thick as a wrist, and Gabe dropped his shield on the moss and reached through it with his bare hand and caught Ice by the forearm, right over the wrist, and held on.

The briar kept coming.

It came up round Gabe's arm. Through the gap between them. Branching, thickening, climbing, the way water climbs, filling the space where they were, pushing. And Gabe held on. And the thorns went into his arm.

Ice felt the thread go red.

Not a little. All at once. Ice let go.

And Gabe's hand closed on nothing.

He saw Gabe's face. Through a gap in the red stems, for one second, before the briar closed over it. He saw it do something. Then it was gone.

"*DEC*—"

The wall went up over their heads. Thick and dark and red and thorned, branching into itself, solid. Through it he heard Gabe shouting, and hacking, the sound of a sword in green wood, and Xav shouting *don't, don't, it grows back*, and Gabe still hacking, and then a sound of somebody being pulled back, and Gabe's voice saying something that wasn't words.

"*Don't cut it*," shouted Ice. "Gabe. *Don't cut it.* It grows back. *Stop*—"

The hacking stopped.

Then the voices got further away. He didn't understand that at first. Nobody was moving. He couldn't feel anybody moving. But the voices went further and further, quieter and quieter, as if somebody were winding them away on a reel. And he understood. It wasn't that they were going. It was that the wall was getting thicker. Metres thick. And thicker. Growing outward on both sides, into the space between, so fast you could hear it, until there wasn't any space between at all.

Then it was quiet.

That was what it was. A small round room, in the Down, with walls of briar and root and fern going up all round him, twice his height and still growing, and a floor of trodden moss and sawdust, and nothing else. No door. Above him, a long way up, the green light coming down through the leaves, the way it always did. Gold in the middle. Quiet.

It was very quiet.

He was on his own.

The threads were all there. He could feel them. Thirty-five, behind his breastbone, all of them. Close. Tens of metres. It was a party of one.

***

He stood in the middle of the path in the green light and didn't move.

And he thought about it. Finally. All of it. The place on this floor he didn't think about. He'd not thought about it for eighteen days on floor 8 and six days on this one and ten years before that. He stood there on the clear soft path, a long way from anyone, and let it come.

It was the seventh day on this floor. The first time.

He'd gone down the rope alone. In the morning. Because he was a head, and somebody had to find the way to the stair, and it was his job, and that was what he did. He'd told his three he'd be back by dark. Deston had said *take someone*. He'd said *I'll be faster on my own.* Gabe had said nothing at all, and that was how he'd known Gabe was angry, and he'd gone anyway.

He'd gone too far. That was all. The Down was beautiful and he'd found a path, and the path had gone where he wanted, and he'd followed it, and when he looked up the light at the tops of the trees was gold. And he'd turned round, and the path wasn't there.

So he'd cut. And it had grown back twice.

So he'd walked. Everything that walks in the Down at night walks further.

By morning he'd been on the far side of the floor. Past the last of the high ways. Out where the bough-folk didn't build and didn't look, where the trees were so old and so big that their roots made valleys, and the Grow was higher than his head and grew higher every night. He'd walked for three days. He hadn't stood still once. He hadn't made a light. It hadn't even crossed his mind. *Stand still and make a light.* He'd nodded. Walkers never listened.

And every night the sawyers came. They came for anything that moved, and he never stopped moving. The first night they'd got his shield at the rim. The second night they'd got through it. The third night there hadn't been much left to get through, and he'd held the last of it on his arm in two pieces with the strap, and kept going.

And on the third night, very late, a long way off, he'd seen a fire.

On the ground. In the Down. At night. A small orange light, very far away, between the trunks.

He'd walked toward it. It was the only thing in the whole dark forest that hadn't moved in three days, so he'd walked toward it, and walked, and it had stayed where it was. All night. He'd walked into it a little after dawn. At the foot of the seven. On the moss. With his shield in two pieces and the rest of him in not much better shape.

Gabe had been feeding the fire.

He'd been sitting on the moss beside it, in the grey light, with a heap of wood beside him, putting a branch on. And Deston and Xav asleep on either side of him with their swords on their knees. And round them, in a ring, others. Not all. Some. Sleeping against each other on the ground, at the foot of the seven, in the Down, where nobody slept, ever. And Burr's ropes hanging down from the dark above them, with Burr's people at the top of them, who hadn't slept either.

Three nights. Gabe had gone down the rope at dusk on the first night with an armful of wood and made a fire on the moss, where Ice would see it, and Burr had told him he was mad. And he'd sat there all night, with his back to the Grow, feeding it. And gone up at dawn. And come down again at dusk. Three nights.

And Gabe had looked up from the fire.

Ice had never asked about it.

Not once. Not ever. He'd walked into the light and Gabe had looked at him, and his face had done the thing, and then Ice had fallen down and slept for two days. And when he woke up, they'd gone on, the way they always went on, up, to floor 10, and nobody had ever said a word about it. Not Gabe. Not Deston, or Xav. Not him. Ten years. He'd never once asked Gabe what those three nights were like, sitting on the ground in the Down with the ticking all round him in the dark, putting wood on a fire for somebody who didn't come, and didn't come, and didn't come.

And then Gabe had died at a gate on floor 100, and he couldn't ask any more.

He stood on the path.

*Stand still and make a light.*

Gabe had done it. He understood that now, standing there. Gabe had stood still and made a light. For three nights. And Ice had found his way back by it. The only thing in the whole forest that didn't move. He'd steered by it.

He'd thought, for ten years, that he'd got himself back.

He hadn't. Gabe had.
