Tiger ran Wendmere every morning before anybody else was up, and by the third morning he'd worked out that the whole city was built for people who never ran anywhere.

It was the stairs. They were everywhere. Wet stone, worn into dips in the middle by three hundred years of webbed feet. And every one of them was a little too tall, and a little too deep, so that you couldn't take them one at a time without feeling like a child and you couldn't take them two at a time without going over on your face. The river-folk took them one at a time, slowly. Tiger took them three at a time, and only went over on his face twice.

Then the walkways. Planks, slick with spray, with rope rails strung between posts. And the bridges between the arches, which swung. And the ledges behind the falls, where you had to put your hand on the rock and lean in and get soaked and keep going. He loved it. He loved all of it. He ran it at dawn with the mist still lying on the lake and the river-folk coming out onto their balconies with their hot cups, and watching him go past, very slowly turning their heads. One of them, on the fourth morning, actually lifted a hand and waved. It took so long that he'd gone past and was two bridges away before she'd finished.

He waved back. He always waved back.

And at the bottom of it all, every morning, he hit the low jetty. And Neth.

***

The low jetty was the oldest in the city. You could tell. It went out from the bottom of the last stilts, almost level with the water, the planks green and soft and sagging, toward the far end of the lake. Toward the Edge. And it ended at a row of great posts, black and thick as trees, set in the lake bed. And strung between the posts, under the water, all the way across the lake from one shore to the other, was the net.

It was the biggest thing he'd ever seen that anybody had made by hand. He'd asked about it on the second day. The lake net. It caught the fish that came down the falls, before they went over the Edge. It caught everything that came down the falls. Branches, and dead leaves, and once, Oarin had told them, a boat with nobody in it. It had been there longer than anyone could remember, and the river-folk could remember a long way. It had been mended so many times that there was nothing left of the first net at all. Every knot in it had been tied by somebody. Every single one.

And sitting on the end of the low jetty every morning, with her legs in the water and a great heap of it across her lap, mending it, was Neth.

She was the oldest river-person he'd met, except Sefa. Her skin had gone silver-green and soft, like the inside of a shell, and lined everywhere, and her long hair was white and thin and hung down into the water. Her hands were knotted. The knuckles big and swollen, the webs between her fingers gone thin as paper. They moved very, very slowly through the net. A knot. A long pause. Another knot.

She was in the way.

That was the thing. The low jetty was the last stretch of his run, and she sat at the very end of it, right where he turned. So every morning he came pounding down the green planks at full speed and had to jump her. Over her head, and her heap of net, and land on the last post, and turn, and come back. Every morning. She never moved.

On the fifth morning, as he came down the jetty, she held up the net.

Just lifted it, slowly, a corner of it, in both hands, without looking up. Like somebody holding up a sheet to dry.

He ran straight into it.

***

"*Quick one*," said Neth.

He was lying on his back on the wet planks, tangled in the net from his ears to his knees. His tail was through one of the holes. He didn't know how. She was looking down at him. Her eyes were large and dark and still very bright, in the middle of all those lines. Her voice was thin and old and very slow, and had the river in it.

"Sit," said Neth.

"I'm *in* your net."

"Yes," said Neth. "So sit in it."

So he sat.

It took him a while to get out. She didn't help. She went on mending the part of the net that wasn't wrapped round him, slowly, while he worked his ears out, and then his arms, and then his tail, which was the hardest. When he was finally free he sat on the planks beside her, a bit breathless, with his legs crossed, and she pushed a corner of the net into his lap without looking at him.

"There," said Neth. She pointed with one knotted finger. A hole. A torn place, with loose cords hanging. "Tie that."

"I don't know how."

"Then watch."

He watched.

She tied a knot. It took her a very long time. He'd never seen anybody do anything so slowly. She took one cord in her thin webbed fingers, and laid it over the other, and stopped. And sat there, for what felt like half a minute, with the two cords crossed. Then she turned one under. And stopped again. Then she drew it through, a finger's width at a time, slowly, slowly, until it sat down snug against the knot below it, and she let it go.

It looked exactly like every other knot in the net.

"Now you," said Neth.

He did it in about a second and a half. Over, under, through, pull. It was easy. It was the same knot. He'd watched her.

He held it up. It looked fine.

Neth reached out one finger and touched it. And the knot slid apart like it had never been tied at all.

He stared at it.

"Again," said Neth.

***

He tried it thirty-one times that morning. He counted. It slipped thirty-one times.

He tried it fast. He tried it faster. He tried it with his tongue between his teeth and his ears flat back, which was how he did anything difficult, and it slipped. He tried it slowly, or what he thought was slowly, and it slipped. Every time, Neth would reach out one finger, without looking, and touch it, and it would just come apart.

"*How*," said Tiger, on the thirty-first. "*How* is it doing that. It's the same knot. I'm doing the same thing you're doing."

"No," said Neth.

"I *am*. Over, under, through—"

"You are doing it quick," said Neth. "It is the slow knot. Tied quick, it slips."

"That's not how knots *work*."

"It is how this one works." She tied another of her own. Over. A long pause. Under. A long pause. "You must let the cord sit. Each time. Let it lie down where it is going before you move it again. If you move it before it has lain down, it has not got there. It is only passing through." She drew the knot tight. Slowly. "This net catches everything that comes down the river. Everything. If the knots only pass through, the net is only a net-shape. Things go through it. Over the Edge."

He looked at the long heap of net in her lap. At the Edge, beyond the posts, where the lake went over into white.

"How long does this take you?" he said. "The whole thing. Mending it."

"All autumn," said Neth. "Every autumn. I have mended it for two hundred and sixty autumns." She tied another knot. "This is the last."

"The last what?"

"The last autumn," said Neth.

She said it very simply. The way you'd say *the last day of the week*. And went on tying.

Tiger sat with the torn corner of the net in his lap and didn't know what to do with his face.

"You're—" he said. "You mean—"

"I will go over before the ice," said Neth. "I can feel it. We always can. It is all right, quick one." She tied another knot. "I would like to finish the net first."

***

He came back the next morning. And the one after. He didn't run the jetty any more. He walked it.

He still ran the rest of the city. All of it, the stairs and the bridges and the ledges behind the falls. But when he got to the bottom of the last stilts, he stopped. And walked down the green planks to the end, and sat down next to Neth, and she pushed a corner of the net into his lap, and he tried to tie the slow knot.

It slipped. Every time. For four days.

He talked while he did it. He couldn't help it. He'd always talked, and Neth didn't seem to mind. She didn't answer most of it. She just tied her knots, slowly, and listened, and once in a while said *hm*. He told her about the white room. About being picked last, and not minding, and having more to prove. About floor 1 and the ram, and the strap, and cutting it in one stun. About the onion skins on floor 2.

He told her about the trial.

He hadn't told anybody about the trial. Not properly. Everybody else had something to say about it. Calvin had killed the thing, with both arms. Dennis had shouted from forty mouths. Gabe had told Ice to get down without making a sound. Azac had taken the hit. Seven tanks in a ring, and the healers behind, and Thea's ward, and Deston's hand. Everybody had *done* something.

Tiger had been out wide. With the quick ones. Rui Xiang, and Milli, and him. He'd gone in at the Quiet's knee eleven times and come out again eleven times and it hadn't cared. It hadn't even looked at him. And then it was over, and Calvin was on the floor going *ow*, and Ice was crying, and Tiger was standing at the edge of the ring with his blades in his hands, a bit out of breath, having done nothing at all that anybody would remember.

"I was just *there*," said Tiger. He tied the knot. It slipped. "Everybody did something, and I was just there."

Neth tied a knot. Slowly.

"Hm," said Neth.

"I know it's stupid."

"Quick ones," said Neth, "always think they are late." She drew her knot tight. "It is not a thing you can be, in a net. Late. Every knot is there. That is all a knot does. It is there, and the next one is there, and between them, nothing goes through." She touched his knot. It slipped. "Again."

***

Ice found them on the fourth day.

Tiger saw him coming down the low jetty with a sack over his shoulder. It was a big sack. It was, it turned out, a sack of onions. He was cooking for thirty-six people now, every evening, in the old kitchen at the back of the house for lights, because he was a Cook, and because nobody else had been allowed to since Hako tried to fry a fish whole. And he'd come down to the low jetty because it was the only quiet place in Wendmere, and he wanted to sit somewhere quiet and peel.

He stopped when he saw them. Tiger with a corner of net in his lap. Neth with the great heap of it over her legs, in the water.

"Sorry," said Ice. "I didn't know anybody was—"

"Sit," said Neth.

Ice looked at her for a moment. Then at Tiger.

Then he sat.

He sat down on the planks on Neth's other side with the sack between his knees, and took out an onion, and a small knife, and started to peel. Neth glanced at him once, slowly, and then went back to her knots. Nobody said anything for a while. The lake lapped at the posts. The Edge roared, softly, a long way off.

Tiger watched Ice's hands.

He'd seen it before. Not this. The *result* of this. On floor 2, in a cold camp under the pines, in the snow. A neat heap of onion skins by a dead fire, all in one piece, each one peeled off whole like a little paper lantern. He'd stood there in the snow looking at them and thought, *somebody did that properly*. Somebody had sat here, on their own, in the cold, and taken the time. He'd kept one. He'd carried it in his coat for days before he gave it to Hana, and she'd put it in her crown.

Ice's hands were very quick. And very careful. The knife went round under the skin and the skin came away in one piece, whole, every time.

"You peel onions really well," said Tiger.

Ice's knife stopped.

He looked up. And something went across his face, a long way down, that Tiger didn't understand. And then he laughed. Not loud. Just a breath of a laugh, through his nose.

"I read that," said Ice. "On floor 2. In World."

"I know. I meant it."

"I know you did." Ice looked at the onion in his hands. "I read it about forty times."

Tiger didn't know what to say to that. So he tied the knot. It slipped.

"There were onion skins at the water caches too," said Tiger, after a while. "On six. In the glass." He didn't look up. "Under the cork. Every water skin. A strip of onion skin."

"Yeah."

"I cried," said Tiger. "At the first one. I sat down in the glass holding the cork and cried in front of everybody." He tied the knot again. "I knew what it was. Straight away. I knew it was the same person. From the camp."

"I heard," said Ice. Quietly. "Gabe told me."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I put them there," he said, "in case it was you who found them."

Tiger looked up.

"Not *you*," said Ice. "Whoever. But I thought—" He stopped. "You were the one who found the camp. On floor 2. So I thought, if anybody knows what an onion skin means, it'll be him."

Tiger looked at him.

Then he looked very hard at the net in his lap, because his eyes had gone hot, and he didn't want to cry on a river-woman's net in front of Ice. Again.

"Quick one," said Neth, beside him. Not looking up. "You have stopped."

"Yeah," said Tiger.

"Good," said Neth. "Now tie it."

He tied it. It slipped.

***

Ice tried it too, in the end. Neth made him.

He put down his onion and his knife and took a corner of net and did it in about a second, over, under, through, the same as Tiger. And Neth reached out one finger and touched it. And it slid apart.

Ice stared at it.

"Another quick one," said Neth. She sounded faintly pleased. Like somebody who'd found a second fish in the trap.

"I'm not quick," said Ice. "I'm—"

Tiger laughed out loud. He couldn't help it.

"You're the quickest person I've ever *seen*," said Tiger. "You did six floors on your own. You're *ridiculous*."

"That's not quick. That's just—" Ice looked at the knot that had come apart in his hands. Then he started again. Slowly. Over. And stopped. And sat there with the cords crossed, for what felt like a long time. Under. And stopped again.

It slipped.

"I'm learning," said Ice. Ruefully. "To sit still. I've got a skill for it now. It says *kneel* on it." He looked at the net. "Turns out I'm not very good at it."

"No," said Neth. "You are not."

Ice laughed again. Properly, this time. And Neth, very slowly, smiled.

***

The gulper came down the falls on the eighth night.

Tiger was on watch. It was the rota. Drew's rock. *NOBODY WATCHES ALONE*. Two to four in the morning, the low jetty, Tiger and Hako. Hako had his spear across his knees and his shield beside him and had been telling Tiger, in a low flat voice, a long and extremely detailed story about why he was not allowed in the kitchen any more, and Tiger had been trying not to laugh too loudly, because Neth was asleep.

She slept in the water. They all did. He hadn't known that until the fourth night. The river-folk went down at night into the shallows under their houses, and lay in the dark water among the stilts, breathing through their gills, and slept. Neth slept under the low jetty, by the posts. He could see her if he looked down through the gaps in the planks. A long pale shape on the lake bed, very still, her white hair moving a little in the current.

The current that went toward the Edge.

He was looking down at her when the net jumped.

It went taut. All at once. From one end of the lake to the other. The posts groaned. The whole low jetty shuddered under them, and the water by the net went white, and something hit it from the other side, from the falls side, very hard.

"*What*—" said Hako.

Something was in the net.

He saw it in the light of Hako's lamp as it came up. Long. Very long. Longer than the jetty was wide. Grey and slick and eyeless, thick as a man round the middle, and at the front of it, where a head should have been, there was just a mouth. A round one. Open. A wide pale ring of it, the size of a cartwheel, lined all the way round and all the way in with small hooked teeth in circles, like the inside of a shell.

It was tangled in the net. Thrashing. It had come down the falls in the dark and hit the net at full speed and gone half through it. And every time it thrashed, the net tore a bit more. He heard the knots go. *Pop. Pop. Pop.* Like a string of beads breaking. Two hundred and sixty autumns of slow knots, going.

And the current was pulling. Through the hole it was making. Toward the Edge.

Neth was coming up.

He saw her rise off the lake bed. Slow, the way she did everything. Her long pale shape coming up toward the surface. Toward the net. Toward the torn place, where the gulper was, because that was her net, and somebody had torn it, and she was two hundred and sixty autumns old and she was going to *mend* it.

"*NETH*," Tiger yelled. "*NETH, NO*—"

He went in.

***

He hated water. He'd always hated water. He was half tiger, and it turned out that the half that was tiger had very strong opinions about lakes. It hit him like a slap. Cold. Black. In his ears, in his nose, in his fur. Every bit of him screamed *out, out, OUT*.

He swam.

He was quick. Even in water. Not as quick as on land, but quicker than anything had a right to be, and he got to Neth before she got to the net. Got his arm round her. She was so light. Like holding a bundle of reeds. And the current had them both, pulling, toward the torn hole in the net and the white roar beyond it.

He got his feet on a post. Held. The water dragged at them. Neth was looking at him. Her big dark eyes, very close, in the black water. Not frightened. Puzzled.

*Quick one*, her mouth said. No sound.

The gulper's mouth came round.

He saw it come out of the dark beside them. The pale ring of teeth. He got one blade up. Just one. The other arm was round Neth. And it hit him in the shoulder. The whole round mouth, closing on him, over his coat and his shoulder and the top of his arm, and the teeth went in. All of them. In rings. And *held*.

It didn't bite. That was the horrible thing. It just *held on*. And pulled.

He felt it drag him off the post. Him and Neth. Toward the hole. Toward the Edge.

He put his blade into it. Behind the mouth. Where there were little holes in a row down its side, seven of them, like gills. He put the short blade into the first one up to the hilt, and the gulper shuddered all down its length, and let go of nothing.

They went through the net.

Half through. The hole was too small for all of the gulper. Its back half was still caught. It hung there in the torn net, thrashing, with Tiger fastened on the front of it by the shoulder and Neth in his arm, and the current tearing at all of them, and beyond them, close, so close, the lake going smooth and green and fast toward the lip.

He saw the Edge.

Right there. Five metres away. The water bending over it like glass.

He could get loose. He knew he could. He could cut his way out of the mouth, it'd take his shoulder with it but he could, and swim. He was quick. He could make it back to the post. He couldn't make it back to the post with Neth. Not against that current.

He needed a line.

There was a line. Hanging off the end of the torn net. A loose cord, thick as his thumb, trailing in the water, still tied at its other end to the great black post.

He let go of his blade. Left it in the gulper's side. Got the cord in his free hand. And got it round Neth. Round her middle, under her arms. And tied it.

Over, under, through. Fast. One second.

It slipped.

He felt it go. Felt the cord slide open round her as the current pulled.

*Tied quick, it slips.*

The Edge was roaring. The gulper was pulling. His shoulder was on fire. And Tiger, who had always been fast, who had been fast his whole life, who had never in his life done anything slowly that he could do quickly, *stopped*.

He laid the cord over.

And waited.

He didn't know how long. A second. Two. It felt like a year. He held the cord crossed, in the black water, with the Edge five metres away and the gulper's teeth in his shoulder, and let it lie down where it was going.

Under.

And waited.

Through. A finger's width at a time.

He drew it tight. Slowly. Slowly. Until it sat down snug.

He let it go.

It held.

***

Hako's spear came down through the gulper's head from above like a falling tree.

He'd come out along the torn net. Along the top of it. On his knees, with the net sagging under him, with his shield on his back and his lamp in his teeth. And he'd got to the place where the gulper was caught, and stood up, on the wobbling cords, and driven his spear straight down through the top of it, behind the mouth, with his whole weight.

The gulper went rigid.

The teeth came out of Tiger's shoulder. All at once. All of them, the way they'd gone in.

He swam. Back along the cord, hand over hand, with Neth on the end of it, against the current, toward the post. It was the hardest thing he'd ever done. His shoulder wouldn't work. He did it with one arm. Hako was shouting. There were lights on the shore, coming, lots of them, and somebody was yelling his name, and he thought it was Wendy. And he got to the post, and got his arm round it, and got Neth's hand on it, and held on.

Then Salty was there. In the water. Salty, who sank like a stone, had come in off the jetty anyway and grabbed the post with one huge arm and Tiger's collar with the other, and was holding him up, the way Salty held people up.

"*Got you*," said Salty. "*Got you. Got you. You idiot. Got you.*"

***

He sat on the low jetty in the grey dawn, wrapped in three blankets, with Yan's white hands round his shoulder and Kei's poultice on top of that, shaking. Not from the cold. He'd stopped being cold an hour ago. He just couldn't stop shaking.

The gulper lay half in the water by the posts. Dead. It was even longer out of the water than in it.

Neth was sitting beside him.

She'd come out of the water on her own, after. Slowly. Wrapped in her own wet white hair. She'd sat down beside him on the planks, with the cord still knotted round her middle, and not said anything for a long time.

Now she reached down, slowly, and touched the knot.

The slow knot. Round her middle. Wet, and pulled tight by the current, and tied in the dark with one hand, five metres from the Edge.

She touched it with one knotted finger. The way she touched all his knots.

It didn't slip.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Hm," said Neth.

And then she looked at him. With her big dark old eyes. And Tiger, who had cried at a water cache in the glass on floor 6, and had nearly cried on her net in front of Ice, put his face in his blankets and cried properly.

She didn't say anything. She just put her thin old webbed hand on top of his head, between his ears, and left it there.

"*You are not late*," said Neth.

***

They mended the net together.

It took six days. The hole was big. Tiger's shoulder was stiff, but Kei said moving it was good for it, and Yan said Kei was right, and Neth said nothing, and pushed the torn edge of the net into his lap every morning.

He tied the slow knot. Every time. It didn't slip again. Not once. He'd stopped counting by the second day.

Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through.

He didn't talk as much. He noticed that on the third day. He'd sit beside her with the cords in his hands, and the mist on the lake, and the Edge roaring softly at the end of everything, and not say anything for an hour at a time. It didn't feel like being quiet. It felt like being *there*. One knot, and the next one. And between them, nothing going through.

Neth was getting slower.

He noticed that too. He didn't say anything about it. Her hands, in the net. The pauses between the knots getting longer. Sometimes she'd stop altogether with the cords crossed in her fingers and just sit, looking at the water, for a long time, before she remembered. And at night she didn't go down into the water any more. She slept on the jetty, wrapped in a blanket Wendy brought her, and in the mornings she was slow to wake.

On the sixth day, they came to the last knot.

It was the last one in the hole. In the middle. The two edges of the tear, drawn together across the whole gap, one knot left to close it. Neth took the cords in her fingers. And then she stopped. And held them out to him.

"You," said Neth.

He took them.

He tied it. Slowly. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. A finger's width at a time. And drew it tight, until it sat down snug against all the others. Two hundred and sixty autumns of knots, and one of his.

He let it go.

Neth reached out and touched it.

It held.

She looked at it for a long time. At the net. The whole great net, stretching away under the water from post to post, from one shore of the lake to the other, every knot in it tied by somebody.

"There," said Neth. "Now it is mended."

And she put her hands in her lap, and didn't pick up another cord.
