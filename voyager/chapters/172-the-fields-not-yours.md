The first arm came round low.

He saw it start. That was something. The long bundle of sheaves at the end of the iron crosspiece, as long as a cart, bound tight with frozen bands, swinging back behind the straw man's shoulder and then coming round. Not fast, at first. Then fast. The way a flail goes when a man who knows what he's doing has been threshing all day. And at the end of it, where a hand would be, the straw splayed out like fingers, white with frost, sweeping at the height of his knees across the snow.

He got the shield down.

Not square. Never square. He'd learned that before he'd learned anything, the first time, on floor 1, from a goat. You didn't stand behind a shield like a wall. You turned it. You gave the thing that was hitting you somewhere to go. *Not a wall. A door.* He put the lamp shield down at a slant, rim to the snow, and the arm hit it, and he turned it, and the arm went along it, over it, past him.

Most of it.

The rest of it went into him.

It went into his arm first, through the straps, through the shield, into the bone, and then it went into all of him, and he went. Not up. Back. Across the snow. On his feet, sliding, the way you slide on ice, three metres, four, five, with his boots skating on the white and the nothing a hand's width under it. He should have gone through. On the first step, on that, with that much behind it, he should have gone through the snow like a stone through a pond.

He didn't.

He felt it go back along the threads. All of it. The push. Not into the snow. Into them. Up the slope behind him, two hundred paces, through the doors, into thirty-five people standing in a line on the stone floor of the Long Barn with their hands on each other's shoulders. He felt them take it. He felt the whole line rock, a little, the way a line of people on a stair rocks when somebody at the bottom stumbles. And hold.

*Eighty-eight*, said Alea.

"*Eighty-eight*," said Ice.

He said it out loud. Not to Alea. To the door. He turned his head and said it up the slope, loud, into the cold. It went up the hill in the dark and he didn't know if it reached the door. He knew it reached Deston. He could feel Deston feel it.

*They heard*, said Alea. *I think they heard.*

"Good."

The second arm came round.

***

It didn't stop.

That was the first thing he learned about it, in the first ten minutes. The bulls had stopped. Between charges, they'd stood and looked at him with their calm eyes and lowered their heads and thought about it. The Rimed had stopped. Everything stopped, sometime, even the things in the tower, to breathe or to think or to see where you'd gone. The straw man didn't. It came on. Arm. Arm. A stamp, the iron foot coming up and down in the snow beside him so the white sprayed up and came down grey. Arm. As if it had been made to do one thing and was going to do it, all night, until the thing was done.

He'd fought it before. He knew that. He didn't remember it the way he remembered most things. He remembered fire. Thirty-three people in a lane with torches and oil and the straw going up in a roar, and it walking through the fire, burning, and coming on, and falling, and getting up. Two days. He didn't remember how it moved. He'd never been close enough. The first time, he'd stood in front of it with a sword and kept it busy while everybody else set it alight, and he'd never once looked at it properly.

He looked at it properly now.

It was beautiful.

He hadn't expected that. It was a straw man made by old women on a barn floor and it was trying to kill him and it was ten above him and it was beautiful. Every sheaf of it bound on so tight you could see the line of the ears all going the same way, like hair combed. The bands at the joints, plaited, three strands, over and under, each one a little different, each one by somebody's hands. The poppies on its head, gone white. The face. Twisted straw eyes. A mouth that was a line. A hundred golds of field under the frost, and the frost on all of it, glittering in the starlight like a hedge in a hard morning.

*Don't*, said Alea. *Don't look at it like that. Look at its feet.*

He looked at its feet.

The second arm came round high. He went under it. Down, onto one knee, on the snow, with the shield up over his head at a slant like a roof, and the arm went over the shield with a long scrape like a broom on a floor, and frost came down off it over him in a white shower, into his collar, down his back.

The foot came down.

He'd seen it lift. He went sideways, rolling, off the knee, and the iron came down where he'd been, and the snow round it went up in a spray and came down. And under it, where the foot was, the snow was gone. Not pushed aside. Gone. A hole, the size of a cartwheel, with the Grey at the bottom of it. Flat. Painted. The colour of nothing.

He came up on his feet on the edge of it. The snow at the edge was going. Crumbling in. Like sand into a hole on a beach.

He stepped back.

*It's eating the snow*, said Alea. *Where it stands. Every step.*

"I see it."

*You can stand on snow. Borne can hold you on snow.* Her voice was very level. *I don't know if it can hold you on nothing.*

"Then I stay on the snow," said Ice.

***

The breath came at about the twentieth minute.

He didn't see it coming. He saw the face. The plaited mouth. The line of straw that was a mouth, opening. Not like a mouth. Like a seam coming apart. And out of it, white.

Wind.

Cold. Not cold. He'd been cold. He'd been cold on floor 2 in the snow with the pines, and on floor 10 on the fifth wall with the Rimed coming over the parapet, and in the sea off the Stair with Xola towing him. This wasn't that. This was the cold that was under cold. The cold the frost came out of. It came out of the straw man's mouth in a long white roar and hit the lamp shield, and the lamp shield went white. Not frosted. White. In a second. A fur of it a finger thick, over the whole face, the lamp in the middle, the rim, the straps.

And through the shield. Through the straps. Into his arm.

He felt it go in. Into his left hand, through the glove, into the fingers. Into the wrist. Up the forearm. Not a pain. The opposite of a pain. A going-away. As if his hand had decided to stop being his, and was leaving, a finger at a time.

He got the shield turned. The wind went past him. Over his shoulder. Out across the snow behind him, a long white plume, and where it went the snow went hard and grey on top, like a skin on milk.

It stopped.

He looked at his hand. The glove was white. He couldn't feel the fingers.

*Seventy-four*, said Alea.

"*Seventy-four*," said Ice. Up the slope. Loud. And then, because it was true, and because he'd promised, and because there was nobody out here to keep his face still for: "*My hand. My left hand. I can't feel it.*"

His voice went up the hill.

Nothing came back. He hadn't thought anything would. Two hundred paces of snow in the dark. But he looked up the slope anyway, for a second, between the arms.

The doors. A rectangle of yellow at the top of the hill, small, with the shapes of people in it. And at the left of it, against the doorpost, a light. Amber. Steady. Not moving.

*She heard*, said Alea. *She heard that.*

He looked away, and the arm came round.

***

He didn't cut it.

That was the hard thing. The hardest thing. Harder than the arms, or the breath, or the foot. His body wanted to. Every fight he'd ever had, in both lives, had been about finding the place where the thing was weakest and putting the sword in it. *Look where it is. Put it there.* He'd said it to Hako forty-one times. And the straw man was made of straw and bands and iron, and the bands were the joints, and if you cut the bands at the knee the leg would go, and if you cut the bands at the shoulder the arm would drop, and he could see every one of them. He could have had it on its knees in twenty minutes.

And every sheaf he cut would be a field.

Hyvred had told them. On the end of the second table, white, holding on. *There's next year in him. In every ear.* Every sheaf of it was somebody's field. Haverley's Long Meadow and Ashwell's orchard grass and the Barn Field's barley. In the spring every village would take a piece of him home and plough it into its first furrow, so the year went back in. And if he cut it, there'd be no piece to take.

So he didn't cut it.

He turned the arms. He went under them. He went sideways from the foot. He kept the shield at a slant and his sword out to the side, not to hit with, to balance with, and he gave it nothing. A door, and a door, and a door. And every time it hit the door, most of it went past and some of it went into him, and the some went back up the threads into thirty-five people, and they rocked, and held.

He was at sixty-six when Hyvred shouted.

He didn't know where she was. Not at the door. Higher. Her voice came down the slope from above the rectangle of yellow, from the roof, from the ridge of the Long Barn in the starlight, where she'd sat on the first morning of the snow with her glasses in her lap.

"*LEFT ARM!*"

He looked. He couldn't see anything. The left arm was coming round, a long white bundle of sheaves on its iron, the same as it had come round forty times.

"*THE ELBOW! THE BANDS AT THE ELBOW!*" Hyvred's voice cracked, and went on. "*THEY'RE GONE! THERE'S NOTHING IN THEM! THEY'RE GREY ALL THE WAY THROUGH! ICE! THEY'RE ALREADY GONE!*"

He understood.

He went under the arm. Not sideways. In. Toward the elbow. The place where the long bundle was bound to the iron with three thick plaited bands, white with frost like the rest of it. He couldn't see what Hyvred saw. He couldn't see the grey in them. He trusted her.

He cut the bands.

They went like ash. That was the thing. Not like straw. Not like rope. His sword went into them and through them and they didn't resist at all. They fell apart. They came away from the iron in a soft grey shower, dry, with no sound, like the stooks on Gorse Piece when Calvin touched one and it fell in like a sandcastle.

The arm sagged.

Not all of it. The iron was still there, and the long bundle of sheaves on the end of it, gold under the frost. But it hung. The weight of it had been held up by those bands and now it wasn't, and it swung down from the elbow, loose, and the next time the straw man brought it round it came round slow. Heavy. Low. Like a flail with a broken swingle.

"*GOOD!*" Hyvred, from the roof. "*THE RIGHT HIP! TWO BANDS! THE TOP ONE AND THE ONE UNDER! NOT THE THIRD! NOT THE THIRD, ICE, THE THIRD'S STILL GOLD!*"

He went for the right hip.

***

That was how it went, after that. For an hour. More.

Hyvred, on the roof, in the starlight, with her glasses off, shouting. *The knee. The left one. One band, the outside one.* *The shoulder. No. No, not the shoulder, it's coming back, leave it.* *The wrist, the right wrist, all of it, it's all grey, cut it.* And him, on the snow, going in under the arms to the places she said and cutting only what she said and nothing else. Only what was already gone. Only the dead. The grey bands came away from the iron in soft showers and lay on the snow and the snow under them went grey, and he didn't step there.

And the straw man got slower.

Not much. A little. Every band he cut, a little. An arm that swung lower. A knee that didn't lift so high. It was still ten above him. It was still the tallest thing on the floor that was trying to kill him. But it was slower.

And so was he.

That was the other thing that happened, in that hour. He didn't notice it at first. He noticed it the way you notice you're tired on a long stair, by the count going slower in your head. The arms came round and he went under them, and it was a fraction later every time. The foot came down and he went sideways, and it was closer every time. The breath came and he turned the shield and there was more white on it every time, and more of his arm went away. Both arms, now. The left to the elbow. The right to the wrist. He held the sword with a hand he couldn't feel, and his fingers were white, and the frost was in the creases of his knuckles like salt.

*Sixty-one*, said Alea.

He'd been waiting for it.

"*Sixty*," said Ice. Up the slope. "*It hurts. Healing.*"

He put his hand flat on his chest.

The gold came. Mend Self. Up from under his breastbone, warm, the way it always came, into the places that hurt. His ribs, from a stamp he hadn't quite got out from under. His shoulder, from the arms. His legs, from the snow. Not much. Not fast. A Paladin's heal, not a healer's; it had never once, in two lives, been enough to make anything stop hurting.

It made his hands hurt.

That was new. The gold went down his arms and into the white, and his hands came back. A little. Enough to feel. And what they felt, coming back, was the cold. All of it. All at once. Like putting your hands in a fire.

He made a sound. He didn't mean to.

*Sixty-eight*, said Alea.

"*Sixty-eight*," said Ice. His voice came out wrong. He said it again, properly. "*Sixty-eight. My hands hurt.*"

He looked up the slope.

The doors. The light. And something else, now. He could see it, faintly, at the edges of the yellow. The line had changed. It had been thirty-five people, from the sill back into the barn. There were more of them now. He could see shapes behind the shapes. Lots of them. More and more, filling the doorway, going back into the barn as far as he could see.

He felt it before he understood it.

Borne got lighter.

Not weaker. Lighter. The way a load gets lighter when somebody else picks up the other end. He'd been feeling thirty-five people holding a thirty-fifth of him each, all night, and now there were more. He could feel them. Not the way he could feel the thirty-five, each one, by name. Like warmth. Like a crowd. Hundreds of them. Coming down off the tables and the stacks in the Long Barn, the gleaners, the whole of the Acre, and going to the doors, and putting their hands on the shoulders of the ones in front, the way they'd seen the passers do, and holding on.

*They're holding you*, said Alea. *The Acre. All of them. Ice. They came down off the tables.*

He couldn't say anything for a second.

"*Nobody sits alone*," said Ice. Under his breath. Not up the slope. Just to the snow.

*Nobody sits alone*, said Alea.

***

At some point it turned.

He didn't know when. He'd lost the count. He knew it was after the hour Hyvred started shouting, and before the sky began to change. The coldest part of the night. The part just before morning, when the stars go hard and small and the world holds its breath. He'd sat up through that part on a hundred floors. He knew what it felt like.

It felt like this. Only more.

The cold came down. Out of the sky. Not wind. Just cold, settling, the way snow settles, on everything. On the slope. On the snow. On him. And on the straw man.

The straw man drank it.

He saw it happen. The frost on it thickened. Glittered. Grew. Every sheaf, every band, every ear, gone from white to whiter, the fur of it standing up off the straw like the hair on a cat's back. The arm that had been sagging from the elbow came up. Slowly. Stiff with frost, all of it frozen into one piece, the bundle and the iron and the grey dust where the bands had been. The knee that had stopped lifting so high lifted.

It took a step toward him. Not a slow one.

And under his feet, the snow went.

Not all at once. Not in a hole. Thinner. He felt it thin. The white skin of it on top of the nothing, a finger deep, two fingers, going to one, to half, the Grey under it coming up through it from below the way damp comes up through a wall. All round him. All across the slope. Wherever he looked, the snow was greying from underneath.

He was standing on less.

The threads held. He felt them hold. Hundreds of people at the door, holding a part of him each. But there was less and less to hold him *on*. And when he moved, when he turned an arm or went under one, the snow under his boots crumbled at the edges, and it took longer each time to find somewhere that would bear him.

*Ice*, said Alea.

He knew what she was going to say.

*It's turned.*

He stood on the thinning snow with the straw man coming at him, ten above, strong again, faster than it had been all night, and the frost thick on his shield and in his hands, and the nothing coming up under his feet. And he looked up the slope at the door.

It was two hundred paces. A rectangle of yellow. Full of people. The light at the doorpost, amber, steady. Thea.

*I'll get out when it turns.*

He could go. He could turn round and walk up the hill on the snow while there was still snow, and Borne would hold him, and he'd be at the sill in four minutes, and over it, and in. And the straw man would come after him. Up the slope. To the doors. Bend under the lintel the way it had bent coming out. Into the barn. Onto the stone floor of the Long Barn, with seven hundred people on it, who'd come down off the tables to hold him, and were standing on the floor. With the grey coming up out of every footprint. Up the barn. To the recess. To the seed.

He couldn't come in.

He stood there for about a second and knew it, the whole of it, all the way through, and the second was very long.

He'd made her a promise. On a rock. *For you.* And he'd known, on the third night at the line, that there was a hole in it, and he'd decided what he'd do if he found himself standing in the hole. He'd decided he would say so.

He said so.

He turned his head toward the door and filled his chest with the coldest air he'd ever breathed and shouted it. Up the slope. Into the dark. As loud as he had ever said anything, in either life.

"*IT'S TURNED.*"

His voice went up the hill.

"*I CAN'T COME IN.*" He stopped. He had to stop. His chest hurt. He made himself say the rest. "*I'M TELLING YOU.*"

Nothing, for a moment. The straw man's arm, coming round. He went under it. The snow went under his knee and he came up out of it and the Grey was a finger from his boot.

And then, from the top of the hill, from the doorpost, from the light, a voice.

Thea never shouted. She'd never needed to. On every floor, in every fight, she'd said things at the volume you'd say them across a table, and people had heard. He'd never once in two lives heard her shout.

She shouted now.

"*I KNOW.*"

It came down the slope like a bell.

"*HOLD ON.*" Her voice cracked, high, and came back. "*WE'VE GOT YOU.*"

He held on.

***

It came at him harder after that. As if it knew.

The arm. He turned it. The other arm, the frozen one, swinging stiff and heavy as a beam, came round low behind the first, and he didn't see it until it was there, and he got the shield to it, and it was square. For the first time all night. Square. A wall, not a door.

It hit the wall.

The lamp shield took it. Gabe's shield. Fen iron, dark grey, with the lamp in the middle and the rim the colour of old blood. It took it, and it held. He heard it ring. A long low note, like Hearthwall had rung under the heron's beak on the Salt. It didn't break.

He did.

He went sideways. Off his feet. Through the air. And came down on the snow on his side, and the snow wasn't there.

He went through.

Not to the knee. To the waist. Down, through the white, through the half-finger of it, into the Grey. Into nothing. And the nothing wasn't anything. It wasn't cold. It wasn't wet. It wasn't dark. It was where the world had been rubbed out, and he was in it to the waist, and his legs, under him, in it, weren't anywhere. Not numb. Not gone. *Nowhere.* As if somebody had drawn a line across him at the belt and everything under the line had been a page somebody wiped.

At the door, two hundred paces up the hill, the whole line went forward.

He felt it. Hundreds of people, jerked forward a step, on the stone floor of the Long Barn. Somebody went down. More than one. He felt Calvin go down on his knees at the front, right at the sill, and not let go. He felt Gabe brace. He felt Maigo, in the sill, deep in the stone, hold, with everything he had left, which wasn't much, and the sill didn't go.

They held.

He hung there. In the Grey to the waist. With the snow round his chest and his arms out on it and the lamp shield flat on the white beside him, his arm still through the straps. Held. By the threads, by hundreds of hands. Not going down. Not coming up.

*Thirty*, said Alea.

"*Thirty*," said Ice. To the snow. He couldn't lift his head. He said it again, louder. "*Thirty.*"

The foot came up.

He saw it. The iron upright, the straw bound round it, the frost, lifting over him. Over his head. Blotting out the stars.

He couldn't move. He was in the Grey to the waist and there was nothing under him to push on and he couldn't move.

He put his hand on his chest.

*Twenty-two*, said Alea, as the gold came. It wasn't much. It hadn't been much all night. It came up from under his breastbone and went into his chest and his ribs and stopped there, and didn't go any further, because the cold was in the way.

*Twenty-seven*, said Alea.

The foot came down.

And he went sideways.

Not by himself. He couldn't have. He went sideways out of the hole the way a fish goes sideways out of the water when somebody on the bank hauls on the line. Through the snow. Out of the Grey. Across the white, on his back, fast, skidding, with the shield dragging on his arm. He felt it. Hundreds of people, at the door, all at once, *pulling*. Not up. Sideways. Away. Out from under the foot.

The foot came down where he'd been.

The snow went up. All of it. A great white spray, and the sound, a door slammed in an empty house, and the ground, the nothing, the whole slope, jumped. And something came out of it and hit him. Ice. Frozen snow. A wall of it, coming out from under the foot like a wave off a rock, and it hit him while he was still sliding, across the chest, across the head, the helm, and he went over.

Over and over. On the snow. And stopped. On his back. Looking up.

The stars were very bright.

*Fifteen*, said Alea.

He lay there.

He'd been lower. Once. A long time ago, in a different life, on a floor he didn't let himself think about, he'd been lower than this, and got up, and walked on it, and told nobody. Not once in this one. Not on any floor. Not since the white room. This was the lowest number Alea had ever said to him in four hundred and fifty-eight days.

He was awake. That was something. He could see the stars. He could feel the snow under his back, the half-finger of it, and the nothing under that. He could feel Borne, holding. Hundreds of hands. He could feel every part of himself that hurt, and that was all of it.

"*Fifteen*," said Ice.

He said it to the sky. He didn't have enough in him to shout it. It came out of him in a cloud and went straight up into the stars. He didn't know if it reached the door.

It reached the door.

He knew because he heard Xola. From the top of the hill. Not a squeak. He'd heard her squeak a hundred times, a hundred metres off, on every floor, every time he healed at sixty, *very annoying*, and this wasn't that. This was a sound like something tearing. Short. Stopped.

And then nothing.

And then Calvin's voice. Very high. Very loud. Not words. His name. The short one, the one Calvin had always used. "*DEC!*" Over and over. "*DEC! DEC! DEC!*"

And under it, Thea. Not shouting. He couldn't hear her. He didn't need to. He could feel the lantern. The light in it. His light. Two hundred paces up a hill, in a pair of hands that weren't shaking, held up against a doorpost.

*You are the light they steer by*, the window had said, on floor 3, a long time ago. *Anyone who can see you can find the way.*

He could see it from here.

*Get up*, said Alea.

Her voice was shaking. He'd never heard her voice shake.

*Please. Get up. Or don't, and let them pull you in, and let it come in after you and we'll think of something. Either. I don't care. But do something.* She was very close to his ear. *Ice. You're at fifteen. I've never said fifteen. Not in this one. I've never had to say it. Please.*

"I know," said Ice.

*Then do something.*

He lay on the snow and looked at the stars and thought.

He'd been fighting it for three hours. He'd turned every arm and gone under every arm and cut every grey band Hyvred told him to and not one gold one, and it was stronger now than when he started. Because it had turned. Because the cold had come and the straw man had drunk it and it would go on drinking it until morning and he couldn't turn arms until morning. Not at fifteen. Not on half a finger of snow.

He couldn't win it out here. Not this way. Not from outside.

The thread.

He'd forgotten it. For three hours, in the arms and the breath and the foot, he'd forgotten. It was still there. Thin. Gold. Going out of the middle of the straw man's chest, across the snow, into him. Still holding. Still asking.

*Keep him*, Ezra had sung, in an old cracked voice at the council. *And he'll keep you.*

Not from outside.

From inside.

***

He got up.

It took a long time. It was the hardest thing he'd ever done, in either life, and he'd done hard things. He rolled onto his side on the snow, and the snow crumbled under his shoulder, and Borne held him. He got his knees under him. The lamp shield came with him on his arm, white, dented. He got the sword under him, point down into the snow, and leaned on it, the way Gaffer Tobin had leaned on his stick, and got one foot flat.

"*Getting up*," said Ice. To the door. Not loud. As loud as he had. "*Getting up.*"

He got up.

The first time, he'd got up off worse than this and walked on it and told nobody, ever, and he'd thought that was what being strong was. He'd thought it for ten years. Standing there on the snow at fifteen with his arms white to the elbow and his legs not his and hundreds of people holding him up from a doorway two hundred paces away, he found out it wasn't.

It was this. Getting up, and saying so.

The straw man was coming.

Not with the arms, this time. It had seen him get up. It came up the slope toward him, through the snow, a pace, two, and it bent. The whole frame bending at the middle, the way it had bent to get under the lintel, its head coming down out of the stars. And its arms came in. Not round. In. From both sides. The long straw hands, splayed, white, coming in at him from the left and the right like somebody reaching for a child who's fallen over.

It was going to pick him up.

He'd seen this. On the pass, at the Gate. A gauntlet closing round Hako, shield and spear and all, and lifting him out of the ring like a cat out of a basket. And Calvin going up its arm with both daggers. And Winter's frost. And Hako falling.

Nobody was going to go up this arm. He'd told them not to. *Nobody hits it. It's not ours to hurt.*

He let it.

He stood on the snow at fifteen and let the hands close round him. The left one round his shield arm and his chest. The right one round his legs. Frozen straw, a thousand stalks of it, every one hard as wire with frost, closing. And they lifted him.

Up. Off the snow. Off the nothing. Up through the cold, past the straw man's knees, its hips, its belly, white, glittering, toward the middle of it. Toward the chest.

The hands tightened.

He felt his ribs go. Not break. Go. Bend. The way a basket bends when you sit on it. He couldn't breathe in. The straw was in his face, in the slit of his helm, frozen, scraping. And the cold. The cold of it, the whole winter in it, coming through the straw into him everywhere it touched.

*Fifteen*, said Alea. *Still fifteen. Ice. It's squeezing. Fifteen. Fifteen—*

It brought him in against its chest.

To crush him. He understood that. It wanted to press him into itself, into the frost and the straw, and hold him there until there wasn't anything left to hold. The way the salt had kept the birds on floor 12. The way the Grey kept the fields.

It pressed him against its chest.

Against the middle of it. Where the heart would go.

Against the knot.

***

He let go of the sword.

He didn't decide to. His right hand opened, white, and the sword went, down, out of the straw, a long way down to the snow, and he didn't hear it land. He got his right hand free. Between the straw and his chest. And put it out.

And it was there. Right in front of him. Under his hand.

The neck. Milli's neck, the last sheaf of the Barn Field, plaited in three strands, over and under, the way the old women plaited the crowns, the little gold figure standing on its own in the middle of the stubble. And tied against it, so close they touched, so close they were one thing, a sheaf from the same field bound round the middle with one band. One long band twisted from the longest stalks Annet had been able to find. Twisted from each end, toward the middle, slowly, so slowly, by two people who'd been going to get married in the spring on Earth, until their hands met, and tied it. One knot.

It held.

He put his hand on it.

White hand. Frost in the knuckles. On a knot of straw at the heart of a straw man as tall as a roof. And the thread, the gold one, the one that had been going out of here into him for three nights, the thin one, the asking one, wasn't thin any more.

It was everything.

It came into him through his hand like the sun coming through a window. Gold. Warm. All of it. Every field. Long Meadow and Gorse Piece and Old Barn Piece and Ashwell's orchard grass and the Lammas top fields and the Barn Field's barley, every one of them its own gold, a hundred golds, coming into his hand. A line of mowers going swish-step down a field in the heat. A girl with a sprained wrist carrying a water bucket. An old man in a chair lifting his stick an inch. A band twisted and tucked. A stook stood up against the light. The whole year. Everything the Acre had brought in, every ear of it, bound up and set at the top of a barn to keep the winter.

And under the gold, the winter.

He felt it. Grey-blue. The tower's kind. Not in the straw. Through it. In it. All the way through, from the feet up, the way Hyvred had said, like ink up blotting paper. Making it match. Making it walk. Making it into the thing that had come down the lane at him in the first life, so he'd burn it again, so there'd be no spring again, so the second time would be the same as the first.

*Keep him*, Ezra had sung. *And he'll keep you.*

He put his other hand on it.

He had to get it free. The left one. The shield arm. The straw had it pinned against his chest. He pulled. Something in his shoulder went. He pulled anyway, and the straps came off his arm, and the lamp shield fell, down past him, a long way, into the snow. *Bring it back*, Gabe had said. He'd go back for it.

Both hands on the knot.

He lit it.

Not Beacon. Not anything with a name. He didn't know what it was. He'd never done it. He just did what Ina did, twice a day, up the frame with his gloves off and his bare hands on the neck. He put his own light into it. Amber. His. The colour of the lantern at the doorpost. Out of his hands, into the knot, into the gold. And the gold took it.

And the winter came out.

***

It came out into his hands.

All of it.

He hadn't known how much there was. Nobody had. Ina had melted a hand's width off the neck twice a day and Rachael had pulled an hour's worth out of the foot twice a day, and it had come back, every time, *less out than in*, because there was always more. There was a whole winter in it. The tower's winter. Every frost that should have come on the Long Acre for a year, early, all at once, put into one straw man so it would walk.

It came out of the knot into his hands like water out of a broken dam.

Cold. The cold under cold. Up his wrists. His arms. Past the elbows, where they were already white, into the shoulders. Into his chest. Into the place under his breastbone where the gold came from when he healed, and it filled it, and kept coming. More than he had room for. More than anybody had room for.

*Ice*, said Alea. *Ice, it's too much. It's too much, you can't hold it, you're at fifteen, you can't hold all of—*

"I know," said Ice.

He couldn't hold it.

*What you cannot carry*, the window had said, in a hall on floor 13, with Rachael's white hands on Hearthwall and thirty-five people's names in his ears. *They will.*

He let it go.

Not out. Back. Along the threads. Up the slope. Two hundred paces of snow in the dark, to the door, to the line. To thirty-five people with their hands on each other's shoulders, and the Acre behind them, hundreds, all the way back into the barn. He let the winter go up the threads to them, the way he'd let something go up through Hearthwall into all their hands at the end, on the hill. Lightly.

A little each.

He felt it reach them. He felt the whole line go cold. All at once. Hundreds of people, at the doors of the Long Barn, with a winter going through them. Not much. A breath of it each. A shiver. The cold of a door opened on a January night. And he felt them take it.

And hold on.

He felt Azac. In the line, somewhere near the middle. And something went out of Azac along the threads, the way it had gone out round the ring at the Gate, under the Gatekeeper's hand. Overlap. *The whole line takes it. Lightly. The whole line.* The cold that came up the threads spread out. Thinner. Further. Into more of them. Into all of them.

He felt Rachael. At the sill. On her knees. With both hands flat on Hearthwall's boss. And the cold that reached her, she took, the whole of her share and more, the way she'd taken the salt off Jared's arm and the frost off the hold-folk's fingers, and put it into the shield. Into the one thing on any floor that didn't go cold. And the steam came up off the sill of the Long Barn in a great white column into the starlight, so much of it, so fast, that he saw it from here. From the chest of the straw man, two hundred paces down the hill. A pillar of cloud going up from the door.

He felt Deston. Gold. Giving. Everything.

He felt Gabe. Banked. Holding.

He felt Calvin, on his knees at the front, with his hand up.

He felt Thea.

And he didn't let go of the knot.

***

He didn't know how long.

The straw man stopped squeezing at some point. He didn't notice when. It stood there in the snow with him held against its chest and his hands on its heart, and it was very still. The arms didn't move. The head didn't move. The frost on it, the fur of it standing up off every sheaf, was going. He could see it, out of the corner of the helm slit. Lying down. Thinning. Running. Water, dripping off the straw in the dark, off the bands, the ears, the poppies.

*Fifteen*, said Alea. *Still fifteen.* And then, a long time later, in a different voice: *Sixteen.*

He didn't say it. He didn't have it in him to say it. He hoped they'd forgive him.

*Seventeen.*

The gold was coming the other way. He realised that slowly. While the winter went out of the knot into his hands and up the threads, the gold was coming into him. Not much. A little. Through his hands. The whole of the Acre's summer, a little of it, coming into him, the way the light had come back to him through Sanctuary at the Gate. Keeping him.

*He'll keep you.*

And then, from the roof, Hyvred.

"*IT'S COMING BACK!*"

He couldn't turn his head. He heard her. High, cracked, carrying, with sound.

"*THE LEGS! NEXT YEAR! IT'S COMING BACK INTO THE LEGS! ICE! I CAN SEE IT! IT'S COMING BACK!*"

He shut his eyes.

***

When he opened them, the sky had changed.

Not much. Grey, in the east, over the hills. The colour of a page before anyone's written on it. The stars going, one by one, from the edge in. Morning. Nearly.

And at the door of the Long Barn, two hundred paces up the hill, a voice he knew.

Annet Sallow's. It carried. It always carried. She never raised it and it carried anyway, across a field, across a valley. It carried now, down the slope, through the cold.

"*Bettris*," said Annet. "*The oven.*"

He didn't understand. Not at first.

Then he heard it.

A sound. One sound. Deep. Iron. Long. Not a bell, not quite. He knew what it was. The Lammas women's oven, the squat iron thing like a beehive, that had baked the bread for seven hundred people for ten days, and somebody, Bettris the smith from Haverley, who'd fixed Gabe's blades, with her hammer, swinging. And the iron rang. All of it. One great round note, going out of the doors of the Long Barn and down the slope and across the snow and on, down the whole of the Long Acre, the way the bells used to go up the valley at sunset, from village to village.

It wasn't sunset.

*It's not a bell*, somebody had said, somewhere, up there. He'd hear about it later. Wren. *That's not a bell, that's an oven.*

And Annet: *It's iron, and I'm the reeve. It's a bell if I say.*

The iron rang again.

And Annet Sallow, at the door of the Long Barn, with her stick, and seven hundred people behind her holding a passer up off the snow, said it. Out across the field. To the field. To the Grey. To the whole of the Acre, every hedge and ditch and furrow of it, under the snow, under the nothing, from the barrows to the river to the old barn at the bottom of the world. Not loud. Carrying.

"*When the bell goes*," said Annet, "*the field's not yours.*"

And the Acre said it back.

All of them. Every voice in the doorway. Seven hundred people. Gleaners and passers. Hild, on her stool. Cobb. Osric. Wren. Calvin on his knees. Thea. All of them, at once, across the snow, the way they'd said *aye* down the lane at Haverley on the day of the Grey.

"*NOT YOURS.*"

***

The ground came back.

He felt it before he saw it. Under him. Not under his feet; his feet weren't on anything, they were in the straw man's hand. Under the straw man's feet. The whole slope. He felt it come up, the way a boat touching bottom feels the sand come up under it, all at once, solid. Real. The snow on top of it settled, a finger, two fingers, sinking down onto something. Onto ground. Onto stubble, frozen, and old roots, and earth, and worms asleep.

The Barn Field.

And all down the valley. He couldn't see it. Hyvred could. He heard her, from the roof, screaming. With sound. Not words, at first. Just sound, high and long and wild, the way she'd screamed with joy in the air on the Salt when the sound came back. And then words.

"*IT'S GONE! IT'S ALL GONE! THE GREY! THE WHOLE VALLEY! THE FIELDS ARE BACK! I CAN SEE THEM! THEY'RE ALL BACK!*"

The straw man moved.

Not to crush. Not to walk. It bent. Slowly. The whole frame, groaning, bending at the middle, bringing him down. Down past its belly, its hips, its knees. Down to the snow. To the real snow, on the real ground of the Barn Field, in the first grey light.

It put him down.

Gently. The way you'd put down a child who'd fallen asleep on you. On his feet. On the snow. And the hands opened, round his chest and his legs, and let go.

He let go of the knot.

He stood there. He didn't know how. His legs were his again; he could feel them, and they were shaking. His arms were white to the shoulder. He couldn't feel his hands. He couldn't breathe in all the way. He stood there on the snow in front of the straw man and looked up at it.

It straightened. Slowly. All the way up, the way an old man straightens up from a chair. Its arms came down to its sides. Its head, in the hoop, with the poppy crown, bowed. A little. Not looking at the doors. Not looking at him. Looking at nothing. At the field. The frost running off it in the grey light, dripping off every ear, every band, onto the snow, onto the ground. And under the frost, gold. Every gold. A hundred fields of it.

It stood there, and didn't move again.

He got up.

He walked the rest of it.

***

The thirty-five, in their line, from the sill back. She didn't come down the slope to meet him.

She'd said she'd be at the door.

He got to the sill.

Gabe stood up.

He'd been crouched beside Rachael with his hand on her back. He stood up, and stepped over Maigo, and stood on the sill in front of Ice, and looked at him. At the helm. At the white arms. He stood on the stone.

Thea put the lantern down.

She looked at him.

Her face was wet. All of it. She'd been crying for a long time, he thought, and not wiped it, because her hands had been full. And under the wet, in her eyes, in her mouth, in all of her, something he'd never seen on her face. Not in this life. Not in the first one.

She was furious.

He sat down.
