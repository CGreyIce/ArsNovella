In Turnstead, Tiger waited at every door.

He'd been doing it since nine. Since Neth. *Put your hand on it first. Feel what's on the other side. Then open it.* He'd been first out of every door since the white room, and on nine a net-mender had taught him to stop for one breath with his hand flat on the wood, and he'd found out that one breath was enough. You could feel things through a door. Warm, cold, wind, still. Whether there was a room on the other side or a drop.

In Turnstead you had to.

Because in Turnstead a door wasn't a door. Not the way it was anywhere else. You opened it, and on the other side was whatever the street had turned into while nobody was watching it. The back of a house you'd never seen. Somebody's kitchen. A different street, wet, with strangers in it who looked at you sideways. Once, on the second day, a roof: he'd opened the back door of the fourth house in the Crook and there'd been nothing on the other side but slates and rain and a long drop to cobbles, because the street behind had turned and taken the yard with it.

He'd had his hand on the door. He'd felt the wind.

He hadn't gone through.

So he waited. At every door. One breath, hand flat. Feel it. Then open.

He didn't mind. That was the strange thing. He'd been fast his whole life. Faster than anything on every floor; he'd raced a girl down a tree on nine eleven times and lost every one and still been faster than anyone else alive. Being fast was who he was. And on nine he'd stopped on a bridge, halfway across, for no reason, and walked the rest. And Tansy had said *you stopped, don't forget*. And he hadn't.

*I do now*, he'd said. *Sometimes.*

In Turnstead it was all the time. And he didn't mind. He stood at doors with his hand flat on the wood and his tail still behind him, and felt for what was on the other side, and he thought about Burr's rope in his pack, the old one, the left coil, dark with years, and Burr's voice on the root-wall. *You're the quick one. You go first. Somebody down there might need a rope.*

Nobody had, yet.

He'd carried it down fourteen floors and up three. He'd tied the Float to a reed-bay in a flood with it, and taken it across the Narrows on Winter's last arrow, and Tiger had begun to think that was what it was for. Floods and rivers. Things you could see.

He hadn't thought about streets.

***

The girl went into the Loose on the fifth evening.

He heard it before he knew what it was. He was on watch, on the step of the seventh house in the Crook, facing south, with Hako on the step of the eighth facing east and the dog asleep across Hako's feet. And from two streets away, from Ottilie's, a sound. A woman's voice. Not shouting. In Turnstead nobody shouted; you didn't take your eyes off your street long enough. A woman's voice saying a name, over and over, going up at the end each time, the way you'd say it into a dark room you thought somebody was hiding in.

*Lirra. Lirra. Lirra?*

He didn't move. He was on watch. That was the rule; you didn't leave your step.

Hako did. Not his step; his head. He turned it, very slightly, and looked at Tiger out of the corner of his eye, and Tiger looked at him, and they both looked back at their streets.

"That's Ottilie's," said Hako.

"Yeah."

"That's not Ottilie."

"No."

The voice kept going. *Lirra? Lirra.*

Then the bell on the corner of the Crook rang, twice, which was Higu's bell for *come*, and six people who weren't on watch came out of the Crook's doors, and Tiger watched his street go grey in the dusk and didn't move, and heard them go past behind him toward Ottilie's.

He did his whole watch. Two hours. He didn't move.

When Wendy came to take the step, he went.

***

Ottilie was on her step.

She was always on her step. She didn't move. She had her cup in her hands and her eyes on her street, sideways, the way they always were, and her face was grey, and her hands round the cup were shaking so hard the tea was going over the edge onto her knees and she didn't seem to know.

The woman saying the name was her daughter. Jessamy. Thirty-ish, thin, with Ottilie's square face gone soft at the edges. She was standing at the far end of Ottilie's street, where it went round a corner into a narrow alley, looking down the alley. Not into it. At it. Sideways. And saying the name.

The alley went into the Loose.

You could tell. Everybody in Turnstead could tell, and after five days the thirty-six could too. Streets that were kept looked like streets. Streets near the Loose looked like streets that had forgotten. The cobbles not quite lining up. A window on the wrong floor. A door with no step under it. And the further in you looked, the more wrong it got, until you couldn't make sense of it at all and your eyes slid off it like water off a slate.

The alley at the end of Ottilie's street went in about ten paces, kept, and then forgot.

"She was watching the light," said Jessamy. To nobody. Not looking round. "The pale one. The one that came up with you. It's been going round the streets. The children love it. She was at the window all evening watching it." Her voice didn't break. "It went down the alley. At dusk. And I went to put the kettle on. And when I came back she wasn't at the window. And the door was open."

Nobody said anything.

"I was only a minute," said Jessamy. "I was watching the street. Out of the kitchen window. The whole time. I was watching the *street*." She was quiet. "I wasn't watching *her*."

Tiger looked down the alley. Ten paces of cobbles. Then the forgetting.

Thea was there, with the lantern. Ice beside her. Higu, Xav, Cursiffe with his ink book. Yan with Working On It, looking down the alley with his head on one side.

"There's a light in there," said Yan. Quietly. "A long way. Small. That's the stray." He was quiet. "And a smaller one. Under it. Close to it." He tilted his head further. "Very small. Frightened. Not moving."

Jessamy made a sound.

"She's alive," said Yan. Cool and level. He didn't say it kindly. He never did. He just said it, the way you'd say the time. "She's sitting down. Somewhere. The stray's over her."

"*Where*—"

"I don't know where. It's not a where. It's a—" Yan stopped. He looked at the alley for a long time. "Everything in there's moving. All the time. Except where the stray is."

Ottilie, on her step, without moving her eyes from her street, said in a voice like a door shutting:

"Nobody goes in the Loose."

***

Ice looked at the alley.

Tiger watched him do it. Everybody did. They'd all watched Ice look at things for two years. At doors, at stairs, at the edges of things nobody else could see over. And they'd all seen what came after, every time, for a year and a half: Ice already moving. First. Alone. A floor ahead. Into whatever it was.

Ice looked down the alley into the Loose for a long time.

His hand went to his belt. To Gabe's terrible lantern. To the light in it, humming its four notes. He didn't seem to know he'd done it.

And then he turned his head and looked at Tiger.

Not at Xav. Not at Gabe. At Tiger.

"Tiger," said Ice. Quietly.

Tiger's chest went tight.

He knew why. He didn't know how he knew, but he did. On nine, at the root-wall, with Burr's old rope in his hands for the first time. *You're the quick one. You go first. Take it down.* And Burr's two fingers and thumb round Ice's wrist the day before, saying something Tiger hadn't understood: *You were the light. On the end of it. I remember.*

He'd thought about that for three hundred days.

"Yeah," said Tiger.

He took off his pack. He opened it. He took out the rope.

Burr's. The left coil. Old and dark and smooth with three hundred years of hands, Burr had said, though Tiger had never counted. Long. Longer than it looked; it had gone across the Narrows and there'd been rope to spare. He shook it out on Ottilie's cobbles in the dusk.

"I'll go," said Tiger.

Nobody said *no*. That was the thing he'd remember. On every other floor, somebody would have said no. *Nobody goes alone.* They'd made that rule on floor 1. And he waited for it, the way he waited at a door, and nobody said it.

Because he wasn't going alone.

He tied one end round his own waist. Burr's knot. He'd tied it a hundred times since nine; his hands did it without him. Checked it twice, the way Burr did.

Then he held out the other end.

To Ice.

Ice looked at it. At the dark old rope in Tiger's hand. His face did something Tiger had never seen it do. Not the face where everything in him stood up to go. The other one. The one from the jam, after, lying on his back on the ice with Gabe's hands in his collar saying *twenty-six*.

"Burr said," said Tiger. "On nine. He said you were the light on the end of it. Once." He swallowed. "I don't know when. I don't know what he meant. I didn't ask." He held the rope out further. "*You put a light on the end. And you hold it.* That's what he told me. When he gave it to me. So." His voice went. "You're the light. Hold it."

Ice didn't move for a long moment.

Then he reached out and took the end of Burr's rope. In both hands. And wrapped it once round his wrist, the way you'd wrap a lead round your wrist with a dog that pulls.

"Okay," said Ice. Very quietly. "I'm holding it."

***

The Loose was the Down, the night after rain, if the Down had been made of houses.

That was the nearest he could get to it. Later, telling Cursiffe, he'd say that and Cursiffe would write it down and Tiger would think it wasn't nearly right. On nine, in the Down, the Grow moved at night and the paths you'd made in the day were somewhere else by morning, and you learned to stand still in the dark and not walk, because everything that walked in the Down at night got lost. Burr's rule. *If you're lost in the Down, stand still.*

The Loose was that. Except it didn't wait for night. And it wasn't plants. It was stone.

Ten paces past the edge, the alley stopped being an alley. It went on being cobbles under his feet, but the walls either side weren't the same walls from one step to the next. A door on the left, then a window, then a door again but a different colour. The roofs overhead not quite meeting, then meeting, then gone, sky, then meeting again. A corner ahead. He reached it, and there was another corner, and he reached that, and it was the first corner again, he was sure of it, there was the same crack in the same step.

He stopped.

He put his hand flat on the nearest wall. The way he did on doors. And felt.

Cold stone. Wet. And under it, faint, a movement. A grinding. The whole wall shifting, very slowly, like a ship at anchor. Wanting to turn.

*THUD.*

The wall stopped.

He felt it stop. Under his hand. On the beat. The grinding in the stone going still for a moment, as if it had heard something and was listening. Then, slowly, in the gap between beats, starting again.

*THUD.*

Still.

He took his hand off the wall.

"Okay," said Tiger. To himself. To the Loose. "Okay."

He didn't run.

He'd have run, on any other floor. That was what his legs wanted. The girl was in here somewhere with a stray over her, frightened, not moving, and he was the fastest thing on any floor, and every bit of him wanted to go, *go*, round every corner as fast as his feet would go until he found her.

On nine, in the Down at night, a man who ran got lost.

He walked. On the beat. One step every four seconds, when the hammer went and the stone went still. He put his foot down on the *THUD* and waited through the gap, and on the next *THUD* he took the next step. Like a man crossing a river on stepping stones, waiting for each one to stop moving under him.

And behind him, going back up the way he'd come, the rope.

He could feel it. Round his waist. Not tight. Just there. A line, going back through the turning streets, round the corners that weren't the same corners, through the doors that weren't the same doors, all the way back up to the edge, to the alley, to Ottilie's street. To a man standing at the top of it with the end of it wrapped round his wrist.

The streets turned round him. The rope didn't.

He didn't understand it. He'd ask Cursiffe later, and Cursiffe would think about it for two days and say *I think it's because he's watching it*. Ice, at the top. Standing at the edge of the Loose with the rope round his wrist, not looking at the Loose, not looking at the streets. Looking at the rope. Where it went into the alley and away. The whole time.

*You are the light they steer by. Anyone who can see you can find the way.*

Tiger couldn't see him. He could feel the rope.

That was enough.

***

He found the first thing on the twentieth beat.

A dog.

Not a dog. He'd fought one on the wall the first afternoon. A thing the shape of a dog, low and long, but grey, and flat, and hard, with edges, like a dog that had been made of slate by somebody who'd only ever had a dog described to them. It came out of a door that hadn't been there on the beat before, very fast, low, straight at his legs, without a sound.

He didn't have time to think. On the wall he'd killed one by being faster than it. Two blades, in, out, gone. He was faster than it. He knew that.

His feet wanted to go.

The rope round his waist said *no*.

He felt it. Not a pull. Just there. Just the line, going back, and him on the end of it. If he went sideways, fast, the way his feet wanted, round it, behind it, the way he'd done on the wall, he'd be off the beat and off his line and round a corner that might not be there on the next *THUD*.

He stood still.

He let it come.

It hit his shins. Hard. Like being hit with a paving stone. He went down on one knee on the cobbles and the slate-dog's jaws closed on his left forearm, through his sleeve, and it was like being bitten by a door.

*THUD.*

Everything went still. The walls. The cobbles. The slate-dog, too. For half a breath, on the beat, it stopped. Its jaws on his arm. Still. Listening, like the walls.

He put his right-hand blade into the place where its head joined its neck. Where the slate was thinnest. All the way in, on the beat, with his whole weight, and twisted.

It cracked. Like a slate dropped on a step. In two.

He knelt there on the cobbles with his arm bleeding and half a slate-dog's head still clamped on it, breathing. He prised the jaws off with his other blade. They came away in his hand, grey and flat and heavy, and he dropped them, and they lay on the cobbles and were just stone.

He looked at his arm. Two rows of holes, through the sleeve. Bleeding. Not badly.

*THUD.*

He got up.

There were more. He knew there would be. He could hear them now, in the gaps between beats, somewhere in the turning streets: a dry scraping, like slates sliding off a roof. More than one.

He didn't run.

He took the next step, on the beat.

***

He found her on the seventy-first.

He'd stopped counting the dogs at six. Six, and his arm and both shins and a long scrape down his back where one had come over a wall that hadn't been a wall on the beat before. He'd killed every one on the *THUD*, standing still, letting them come to him, the way he'd never once in his life fought anything. *Let them come to you.* He'd always gone to them.

He'd found out it worked. If you were standing still, on your line, on the beat, they came to you. And on the beat, they stopped. And you only had to be there.

On the seventy-first beat he put his hand on a door, and felt still air. Warm. And a light, through the cracks round the edge. Pale.

He opened it.

A courtyard.

Small, square, walled, with the sky grey and low over it. Old. Older than anything he'd seen in Turnstead. The stones of the walls were worn round at the edges, like stones in a riverbed. The cobbles underfoot were green with moss. And it was still. Not the stillness of the beat. Properly still. He felt it the moment he stepped through the door: no lean. No grinding. Nothing wanting to turn.

Because over the middle of the courtyard, at about the height of the roofs, there was a stray.

Small and pale, the size of an apple. Hanging. Turned down. Watching.

And under it, sitting on the moss with her knees pulled up to her chin and her arms round them, a little girl. Eight, maybe. Square-faced, like Ottilie. Very white. Staring at him.

And beside her, a ring of stones.

Blackened. Round. About as wide as Tiger was tall. The stones set in the moss in a careful circle, the way you'd set them if you were making a fire you meant to keep. Old. So old the black on them had gone grey, and the moss had come up between them, and inside the ring there was nothing but moss.

A campfire. A very long time ago.

He looked at it for exactly one breath. Then he looked at the girl.

"Lirra?" said Tiger.

She didn't move.

"Your mum's at the top of the alley," said Tiger. "And your gran. On her step." He crouched down. Not close. A pace off. The way Ina sat with Tuli. "She's spilling her tea. All down herself. She doesn't know she's doing it."

Lirra stared at him.

"It went in here," she said. A tiny voice. "The light. I wanted to see where it went. It went in the alley. I only went to the end of the kept bit. And then I looked back and the kept bit wasn't there."

"Yeah," said Tiger. "That happens."

"I walked and walked. And there were dogs. Stone ones." Her voice shook. "And then I found the light. And it stopped here. And I sat down. Because it was the only place that wasn't moving."

"That's exactly right," said Tiger. "That's exactly what you do. You stand still. Or you sit down. Somebody I know says that. If you're lost, stand still." He held out his hand. The good one. "That's how I found you. You stood still."

Lirra looked at his hand. Then at the rope round his waist. Going back out of the door he'd come in by, into the Loose.

"What's that?"

"A rope."

"Where does it go?"

"Back," said Tiger. "To your gran's street. There's a man at the other end holding it." He swallowed. "He's the light on the end of it."

*THUD.* Faint, from very far off. He felt it in the stones. The courtyard didn't move. It didn't need to.

Lirra put her hand in his.

***

He saw the wall as he stood up.

He hadn't looked. He'd come through the door looking at the girl, and the stray, and the stones. He hadn't looked at the walls of the courtyard at all.

He looked now. Because he had to stand up, and the wall was in front of him, and he couldn't not.

It was cut.

Not carved. Cut. With a knife, or a chisel, or the point of something hard, into the grey worn stone of the courtyard wall, at about the height of his chest. Rows of marks. Short straight lines, in groups, the way you'd count anything. Rows and rows of them. Going down the wall in a column, like a list.

And beside every row, a number.

He knew the numbers. He knew the shapes. They weren't the shapes Turnstead used, or Lanthorn, or Tell, or anywhere he'd been in two years. They were numbers from home.

*30.*

*30.*

*30.*

Down the wall. Row after row. Every one the same. He didn't count how many. He couldn't. His eyes had gone strange.

And at the bottom, under the last row, in letters. Cut deeper than the rest. Careful. The way you'd write something you wanted to stay.

Letters from home.

*WATCH EACH OTHER.*

And under that, small, one letter, and a dash before it.

*— R.*

Tiger stood in the courtyard with a little girl's hand in his and Burr's rope round his waist and read it four times.

He'd seen that letter before. Not that letter. That hand. On Drew's slate, on floor 13, copied out of the Count's book in Tell, the line everybody had looked at for a long time and then looked at Ice. *THIRTY. NOBODY LEFT DOWN. — RUTH.*

The ones before.

They'd been here. In this courtyard. Three hundred and twelve years ago, a year before somebody cried them from the Assize steps on 22. Thirty of them. Round a fire, in a ring of stones, in a courtyard that didn't move because they'd kept it. Watching each other.

And every night, somebody had counted. And cut it in the wall. *Thirty. Thirty. Thirty.*

His eyes stung.

He didn't know why. He'd never met any of them. He didn't know a single one of their names but one. He stood in front of the wall and looked at the row of *30*s going down it, all the same, every night, nobody left down, and felt something in his chest go so tight he couldn't breathe properly.

"What is it?" said Lirra. Looking up at him.

"Writing," said Tiger. His voice came out strange. "From where I'm from."

"What does it say?"

He looked at it.

"It says *watch each other*," said Tiger.

Lirra looked at the wall for a long time with her square serious face. Then up at the stray, hanging over the courtyard. Then at Tiger.

"That's what my gran says," said Lirra. "Every night. *Keep your street. Watch each other.*"

He didn't say anything.

He put his hand flat on the wall. On the cut marks. The stone was cold and the edges of the cuts were worn soft with three hundred years, but he could feel them. Every row.

*Somebody might need a rope*, Burr had said.

*You put a light on the end. And you hold it.*

He took his hand off the wall.

"Okay," said Tiger. "Home."
