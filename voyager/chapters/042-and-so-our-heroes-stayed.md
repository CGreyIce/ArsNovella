"*And so our hero,*" said Azac, to the black water, "*stood in front of the thing for the forty-first time that week, and was hit by it for the forty-first time, and got up again. Because that was the job.*"

"Who are you talking to?" said Salty, beside him, wiping mud off his greatsword.

"The audience."

"There's no audience."

"There's always an audience, Salty. It's a *device*."

Salty looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a very practical man confronted by something impractical. Then he went back to wiping his sword.

They were standing in a channel up to their waists, the two of them, side by side, in the hot afternoon. In front of them, a mireling had just come up out of the black water and gone back down again with a good deal less enthusiasm than it had started with. Behind them, on a tussock, Hako was laughing. Behind Hako, Gabe was leaning on his shield. Behind Gabe, Jared was sitting on a log, looking at a small iron rose and not paying attention to anything.

Five tanks. In a row. Up to their waists in a swamp.

Azac thought it was the funniest thing he'd ever seen.

***

It had been Drew's idea. Of course it had. Drew had looked at the slate on the forty-first morning, at the long list of names under *THE THIRTY-FIVE (FOR NOW)*. And he'd said: *We've got seven parties. We've got seven tanks. Gabe, Salty, Jared, Azac, Hako, Chi, Chris. And me. Eight, if you count me.* He'd looked round the smoking hall. *When we go up to four, we're not going to be seven parties. We're going to be one group. So the tanks need to learn to stand together.*

So they stood together. Every morning, in the channels, up to their waists. Learning when to swap, when to step in front of each other, when to let something go past one of them into the shield of the next. Drew stood on a tussock with the little slate and called it.

It was the most fun Azac had had since the white room.

He'd been a tank for two years. Ever since Declan moved to Malacca and logged on less, and the Singapore lot had been short a front, and Azac had said *I'll do it* because he was the biggest. Two years of standing in front of everything on his own, and always feeling like he was keeping somebody else's seat warm.

He wasn't on his own now. He had Salty on his left, built like a wall, saying almost nothing. He had Gabe, who'd done the hard trial and come back grim and quiet, and now hammered at the little forge every night until midnight. He had Hako, grinning, telling everyone it would be fine. He had Jared, cool and unbothered, who stood in front of things the way other people stood at a bus stop. Chi, very slow and very calm, who could talk you through anything. Chris, who saw everything coming. Drew on the tussock, turning things.

Eight of them. Taking turns.

He thought, sometimes, standing in the black water with the others on either side: *you never had this, did you. Two years ago. Out there. Wherever you are now.*

He'd heard the stories at the smoking hall. Everybody had. The chapel door, held all night, alone. The tower door on the pass, held all night, alone. The Lanternjaw, which had taken all thirty-five of them in the dark with Hyvred calling and Chi talking and John's lights in the water. Somebody had done that one alone, too. On the sixteenth day.

Every story was the same story. Somebody standing in front of something, on his own, so that nobody behind him had to.

*And so our hero,* Azac thought, *stood in front of everything. On his own. Because he thought that was the job.*

He didn't say that one out loud.

***

He did his trial on the forty-second day. Hard. He hadn't decided until he opened the window. Then he saw the third one and knew straight away.

*Hard: Stand in front of the gate until everyone behind you is through.*

He didn't tell the others what it was. He just went out at dusk, alone, the way you were supposed to, to a place in the Fen the window showed him. An old stone gateway standing in the middle of a reed bed, with nothing on either side of it. And he stood in it. Things came out of the water at him, and out of the reeds. Behind him, ghostly, faint, a line of people he couldn't quite see started walking through the gate, one by one, very slowly.

He didn't count them. He just stood there and took what came, and didn't move, until the last faint shape had gone through behind him and the gate went dark.

It took most of the night.

When he came back to Reedmoor at dawn, he could barely stand. Gabe was sitting on the end of the walkway in the wolf coat, waiting for him, the way Azac had been waiting for Gabe the morning after his. He didn't say anything. He just got up and put Azac's arm over his shoulders and helped him along the planks to the smoking hall.

"How many?" said Gabe, quietly, on the way.

"How many what?"

"Behind you. Going through the gate." Gabe didn't look at him. "Mine was a door. People going through it. I counted."

Azac thought about it.

"I didn't count," he said. "I didn't want to."

Gabe was quiet for a few steps.

"Mine was thirty-five," he said. "I counted them all. Thirty-five, and then I stood there for a long time, waiting for one more." He let out a breath. "He didn't come through."

Azac didn't say anything.

He read it in the smoking hall, sitting on the end of a bench with a bowl of eel he was too tired to eat. Then another window came, gold at the edges, and he read that too.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he started laughing, very quietly, so as not to wake the people asleep at the next table, and couldn't stop.

"*And so our hero,*" he said to his bowl, wiping his eyes, "*learned that his stupid habit was a skill all along.*"

He tried it two days later, in the channels, with the other seven, against a mireling nest. Drew had found it on the east side of the Fen and wanted them to practise on: five of the long grey things in one deep pool, all at once.

It went badly at first. It always did, the first time. The mirelings came up out of the black water from three sides, and Hako went under, and Jared hauled him out. Chi was talking, slow and calm, but there were five of them and only one of her. And Azac felt it go round the line of tanks the way fear goes round a room: everybody a fraction slower, a fraction more careful, a fraction less sure. Up to their waists in a swamp, with things underneath them.

So he said it. Out loud. In the voice.

"*And so our heroes held the line,*" said Azac, to the black water, "*and nobody went under, and nobody let go. And the things in the pool came up one at a time, and one at a time they went down again, because there were eight of them standing together and that was enough.*"

He felt it go out of him. Not like Taunt, the hook on a line. Like a warm hand on the back of everybody's neck, all at once.

And they held.

He couldn't have explained it. Nobody got stronger. Nobody got faster. But the line of eight tanks in the black water stopped being eight frightened people and started being *the line*, the way it was in the story he'd just told. Salty set his feet. Gabe's shield came up exactly when it needed to. Hako stopped going under. And the mirelings came up out of the water one at a time and went back down one at a time, exactly the way Azac had said, until the pool was still.

Drew, on the tussock with the slate, looked at him for a long moment.

"Do that again," said Drew, "on four."

"*Our hero,*" said Azac, a bit shakily, "*will do his best.*"

That night he found Gabe at the little forge at the end of the village, the one the eel-woman's brother kept, hammering. Gabe had been at it every night since his trial. He didn't look up when Azac came in. He was working on something big and round and dark on the anvil: a frame of iron, curved, with rivet holes along its rim. Half a shield.

"Fen iron," said Gabe, without stopping. "It's in the black mud, under the reeds. It's better than nail iron. Not good enough yet. But better."

"For you?"

Gabe stopped hammering.

"No," he said.

Azac looked at the half a shield on the anvil for a long time. He thought about everything he'd heard at the smoking hall, all the nights at all the doors. Somebody who always broke his shields, because he never backed off when he should have.

"*And so our hero,*" said Azac quietly, "*made a shield for somebody who wasn't there yet.*"

"Shut up," said Gabe, and went back to hammering. But he didn't sound like he meant it.

***

The window came on the forty-fourth day, at supper.

Azac would remember exactly where he was. At the long table in the smoking hall, between Calvin and Dennis, with Drew's slate leaning on the wall behind him and the note pinned by the door. *Be careful. Train longer on the 3rd floor.* Waddles was asleep in a basket by the fire, full of eels. Thirty-five people were halfway through supper. Then thirty-five windows opened at once, in the corner of thirty-five eyes, and the whole hall went still.

Azac opened the rankings. They all did.

Level twenty-four. *Knight.* Floor four. The same word it had said for six weeks.

And then Thea, at the end of her party's table, said, very quietly: "There's something under it."

Everybody looked at her. She was leaning toward her window with the taking-it-apart face, reading the words under the words, the way only she could.

"Under *Knight*," said Thea. "There's a grey line. It's been there for weeks, I think. I never thought to look under his class. I only ever looked at the level." She read it out, slowly. "*Hidden class: Paladin.*"

The hall went very still.

"*Paladin,*" said Chris, from the end of the table, very quietly, writing it in his notebook. "That's not one of the classes. Quill didn't have a door for that." He looked up. "That's a hidden class."

"He got a hidden class," said Calvin. "On his own. *Of course* he got a hidden class."

"Paladin," said Gabe. He was sitting at the end of the table in the wolf coat, looking at the window. "Tank and heal." His voice was strange. "He can heal himself now. On his own. Standing in front of things."

Nobody said anything. Azac thought about chapel doors, and tower doors, and the drowned tower, and a man standing in front of everything with nobody behind him. And it turned out, somewhere along the way, he'd found a way to keep himself standing, because nobody else was there to do it.

*And so our hero,* he thought, and couldn't finish it.

***

But the strangest thing came over the next few days. Chris was the one who noticed it, because Chris noticed everything.

Voyager didn't go up.

*Floor 4 has been cleared*, the window had said, on the forty-fourth day. And they'd all waited for the next one: the rankings to change to *Floor 5*, the way they had every time before. On floor 1, he'd gone up the same night. Floor 2, the same night. Floor 3, two days later. He never waited. Maigo had said it, at a table on floor 2, and everybody had nodded: *He doesn't wait. He never waits.*

He waited.

Day forty-five. *Floor 4.* Day forty-six. *Floor 4.* Day forty-eight, fifty. *Floor 4.* His level crept up, slowly, to twenty-five, and then a tiny padlock appeared beside it, and Thea said *he's at the next wall*. But the floor didn't change. He'd cleared it and stayed.

"He's not going up," said Chris, on the fiftieth morning, at breakfast, turning his little graph round on the table. A long line of dots, all marked with a small *4*. "Six days. He's cleared it, the stair's open, he could go to five any time he wanted. And he's just—*staying*. On four."

"Why?" said Calvin.

Nobody answered.

And then Thea did.

She'd been sitting at the end of her party's table with the little glass lantern on the bench beside her. She took it everywhere now. Its soft warm light made everything round it look slightly more like itself. She'd been very quiet for most of six weeks. Azac had been watching her, a bit, the way he'd started watching everybody, now he had a skill that cared how things ended. She hadn't said much at the meeting about the note. Since then she'd done her hard trial and come back with a nosebleed and lines of light all over the walkway, and carried the lantern, and hardly spoken.

"He's waiting," said Thea.

Everybody looked at her.

"For us." She put her hand on the lantern beside her. "He's cleared four. He's made it safe, whatever was up there, whatever made him write *be careful*." She looked at the note pinned by the door. "And now he's not going any further. Because if he goes to five, and we come up to four, there's a whole floor between us. And he doesn't want there to be a floor between us."

The smoking hall was very quiet.

"He's waiting one floor ahead," said Thea. "So that when we come up, he's right there." She looked down at the lantern. "In case."

Azac watched Gabe's face while she said it.

It did a lot of things, Gabe's face. Then it settled on one, and Azac had never seen it on Gabe before. Not since the white room. It wasn't hurt, or angry, or the grim thing from the trial. It was something much simpler.

It was relief.

"Then we'd better not keep him waiting too long," said Gabe.

***

They decided on the fifty-second day, round the slate.

Drew had the numbers. He always had the numbers. Everyone through Tier 2. Average level seventeen, the lowest fifteen, the highest nineteen: that was Rui Xiang, who learned everything faster than everybody and was smug about it. Floor 4's recommended level was eighteen. They were nearly there.

"Not nearly," said Drew. "We said twenty. Everyone at twenty." He looked round the long table at thirty-four faces. "The card says *train longer*. Not *train until you're nearly there*. Longer."

"How much longer?" said Calvin.

Drew looked at him. Then he looked at the note on the wall by the door.

"Until the person who wrote that would be happy," he said.

Nobody argued. Azac watched them not argue, thirty-five people who six weeks ago had been seven parties on two floors with nothing in common but a missing friend. They sat round one table in a swamp, under one name on a slate. *THE THIRTY-FIVE (FOR NOW).* And they agreed, without a single raised voice, to stay.

He felt the skill stir in him. *Saga.* Tell them how it ends.

He stood up. He hadn't meant to. He stood up at the long table in the smoking hall with a bowl of eel in front of him, and everybody looked at him. And he said it the way he'd been saying things since the white room, in the storyteller's voice, the one that made people laugh. Only this time it was quieter, and he meant every word.

"*And so our heroes stayed,*" said Azac.

The hall went very still.

"*They stayed on the third floor, in the swamp, in the heat. And they trained, and they got strong, and they learned to stand in front of things together, instead of alone. And they didn't go up until they were ready. Because someone had asked them to be careful. And they'd decided to trust him.*" He looked round the room. "*And one floor above them, waiting, someone who'd been standing in front of everything on his own since the first day finally got to stop. Because they were coming. All of them. Together.*"

He sat down.

Nobody laughed.

Then Calvin, beside him, said very quietly: "That one was good."

"Thanks, mate."

And across the table, at the end of her party, Thea picked up the little glass lantern and held it in both hands. The light came up soft and warm into her face, and she looked out of the smoking-hall window at the amber light on the drowned tower in the middle of the Fen. At the end of the table, Gabe pulled the wolf coat round his shoulders in the heat and did the same.

*And so our heroes stayed.*

Azac felt something settle, very quietly, behind his breastbone. Like a story that had decided how it was going to end.
