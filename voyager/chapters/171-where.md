He could feel the thread.

He didn't know when it had started. He'd been sitting at the line for three nights and somewhere in the third one, without noticing, he'd started to feel it. Not a pull. Nothing that strong. It was more like the feeling of somebody holding the other end of a piece of string. You couldn't tell from your end what they wanted. You could only tell that they were there, and holding on, and hadn't let go.

It came from the middle of the straw man's chest. From the knot. He could feel exactly where.

Thea's hand was in his. She'd sat down on his left an hour ago, on the line, not past it, shoulder against his, and he'd put his hand out without looking and she'd taken it, and neither of them had said anything since. The lantern was in her lap. The amber in it lay flat against the glass on his side. He didn't look at it. He didn't need to. He could feel that too.

*There's something on you*, said Alea.

She was on his right shoulder. Hand-sized. Pale teal at the edges, white in the middle, the faint shimmer round her like heat over a road. She'd been quiet for hours.

"I know," said Ice. Under his breath.

*It's gold.*

"I know."

*It's not the tower's.* She was looking at it, the way she looked at windows, at the place in the air between him and the straw man where the thread went. *It's the other thing. The farm thing. The keeping.* She was quiet for a moment. *It's asking you something.*

"I know what it's asking."

*So do I*, said Alea. *That's the problem.*

***

He'd decided on the second night.

He hadn't meant to. He'd sat down at the line because Higu drew it and said *there*, and he'd said *there*, and meant it. NOT IN FRONT. He'd read it on the rock and said *okay* and he'd meant that too. He'd sat on the cold stone and watched the shields and the frost and the old woman on her stool, and he'd thought, for the first night, *this is right. This is what it looks like when you let them.*

And then on the second night he'd started thinking about the first time.

He didn't want to. He did it anyway. It was what his head did on floors like this. It went back. It went down the lane at Haverley in the snow, the first time, thirty-four of them camped outside the Long Barn because the gleaners didn't want them in. The fires along the lane. The doors opening. The straw man coming out through them into the snow, tall as the roof, and coming down the lane. Not at the fires. Not at the tents. Not at Gabe, or Deston, or anyone. At him.

It had always come for him. Even then. Before it had any reason. Before he'd burned it.

He'd been in front. He'd always been in front, the first time. Not because anybody asked him to. Because he couldn't stand anybody else being there. Because if he was in front then whatever came would hit him first and maybe that would be enough, and if it wasn't enough at least he wouldn't have to watch. And it had come down the lane at him and he'd stood in the snow in front of thirty-three people and they'd burned it. Two days. It kept getting up.

He'd thought he was keeping them safe.

He'd never once, in ten years, thought about the people inside the barn.

And on the second night at the line, with Hako on the shields and Hild on her stool and seven hundred people asleep behind him, he'd done the sum. It was the same sum Kei did about everything. How much. What it cost.

If it walked, it would walk at him. Down the barn. Through whatever was between. It wouldn't go round the shields. It would go through them, or over them, and the Grey would come with it. Out of the socket. Into the floor. Under seven hundred people who had nowhere else to stand.

If he knelt, it would come to him. Sanctuary was a place, Alea said. Where they stand, so are you. But it was a place in the middle of them. And everything that came at the middle came through the edges first.

If the shields went out to meet it, they'd be standing on the Grey.

If anybody burned it, there'd be no next year.

There was only one place in the whole of floor 14 that it wanted to go. And there was only one person it would follow there. And he'd sat on the line on the second night and looked at it, and it had looked back at him, and he'd known, the way you know the shape of a room in the dark, where that place was.

Out. Away. Somewhere nobody else could stand.

That was the decision. It had taken him about a minute. The rest of the second night and all of the third he'd spent on the other thing.

*I'll heal when it hurts. I'll get out when it turns. For you.*

He'd said it on a rock in the sea, with Thea's hands round his. He'd meant it. He'd kept it. On the east ridge, with a bull five above him and the bell going, he'd put his sword away and walked down the hill with his back to it. He'd healed at sixty-three. He'd said *my ribs, my arm, my back*. He'd kept it every day since.

And there was a hole in it.

He'd found it on the third night. Sitting very still at the line with the thread on him. *I'll get out when it turns.* But what if you couldn't? What if getting out was the thing that brought it back in? What if the only way to keep the promise was to bring it through the doors after you, into the barn, onto seven hundred people and the seed?

He didn't know. He'd turned it over and over all night, the way he used to turn the four words on the hundredth floor, and he didn't know.

So in the end he'd decided the only thing he could.

If it turned, and he couldn't get out, he'd say so.

That was all. He'd tell her. Out loud. Whatever the number was. Every time. He wouldn't stand out there in front of it the way he used to and keep his face still and his mouth shut and let them find out after. He'd tell her the truth the whole way. Every word of it. That was the bit of the promise he could keep whatever happened. *For you.*

He'd never done that, in either life. Not once.

*You're going to ask*, said Alea.

"Yes."

*You've never asked.*

"I know."

*Not for anything. Not in ten years and four hundred and fifty-eight days.* Her voice was very small. *Not once.*

"I'm going to ask Higu."

*And her.*

He looked down at Thea's hand in his.

"She already knows."

*She does*, said Alea. *She's decided too.* She was quiet. *I heard her. Last night. To Hyvred.*

He didn't look round. "What did she say?"

*I'm not telling you*, said Alea. *It's hers.* And then, so quietly he nearly didn't hear it: *But you'll like it. You'll hate it and you'll like it.*

***

The last bolt went at about four.

He'd been waiting for it. He heard it before anyone. A sound like a rope stretched past what a rope could take, very low, from the right foot, getting higher. Thea's hand tightened in his. On the shields, Gabe's head came up. Azac's did.

Then it went.

Not a crack. A bang. The bolt came out of the stone like a cork out of a bottle and hit the iron upright and rang off it, a long high note, and went spinning away across the frost and skittered under the end of the first table. And the right foot of the frame jumped. Up. Out of its socket, all at once, the way the left had gone, a hand's width, and came down on the stone beside it.

The straw man stood up.

That was what it looked like. It had been standing for a week. It had been tied to the floor of the Long Barn by four bolts at each foot and the iron and a thousand years of old women's hands, and it had stood there, and leaned, and looked. Now it wasn't tied to anything. And it straightened. The whole frame, top to bottom, with a long groan of iron and a creak of a thousand frozen bands taking the strain, straightened up, and up, the way an old man straightens when he gets out of a chair. Its arms came out from its sides. Its head came up in the hoop. And the frost on all of it, every sheaf, every ear, every plaited band, glittered in the light of the one low lamp by the shields like a field in a hard morning.

The window over its head brightened.

[ The Last Sheaf · Floor Guardian ]

*Eighty-three*, said Alea.

He didn't say anything.

*Ten above. Ice. Ten.*

"I know."

Behind him, the barn woke.

He heard it go. From the top down, the way the quiet used to go down a field at the bell. Somebody near the front saw and made a sound, and the sound woke somebody else, and then it was going down the whole length of the Long Barn, seven hundred people sitting up in the straw in the dark and seeing. The horses started at the far end. Screaming. Somebody shouted. A child cried. And over all of it, high and hard and carrying, the way it always carried, Annet Sallow's voice from the Haverley end:

"*Lamps!* Get the lamps *up!*"

Somebody got the lamps up. The chains rattled. Light came up the barn in a yellow wash, lamp after lamp, all down the beams.

And in the light, the straw man took a step.

***

It wasn't a hand's width this time.

The left foot came up off the stone. The whole iron upright, the frost on it, the straw bound round it to the knee, came up and forward, slowly, a whole pace, and came down. On the stone. With a sound like a door slammed in an empty house.

And where it came down, the stone went grey.

Not frost. Not white. Grey. The Grey. Ice saw it come up out of the stone round the iron foot the way a stain comes up through a cloth. A ring of it. A foot round. Then two. Spreading out across the flagstone from where the foot had landed, soft-edged, the colour going out of the stone and not coming back. The colour of nothing.

Behind it, in the empty socket where the foot had been, the Grey welled up. Out of the hole. Over the rim. The way water wells up out of a spring.

The right foot came up. Forward. Down.

Another ring of grey.

At its feet, on her stool, Hild didn't move.

It stepped over her. Ice watched it do it. The great iron foot came up past her shoulder, past her head, so close that the frost on it must have been in her face, and went over her, the way a man steps over a sleeping dog on a doorstep, and came down on the far side of her stool, and the Grey came up round it. A pace from her. Spreading. Toward the legs of the stool.

Hako was already moving.

Ice saw him go. From the end of the shields, where he'd been standing with Salty, Hako dropped his spear and ran, across the frost, toward the old woman, toward the grey coming up round the foot of a thing ten above him and as tall as the roof, and got there, and didn't stop. He bent down and put his arms round Hild and the stool together, the whole of her, stool and blanket and all, and lifted, and turned, and ran back.

"Put me *down*," said Hild. Ice heard her. "I'm being *stubborn*—"

"Classic," said Hako, running.

He came back past the shields and over the line and set the stool down behind it on the stone with Hild still sitting on it, upright, glaring, with her blanket round her. Then he went back for his spear. He didn't look at the straw man. He picked the spear up off the floor and went back to his place at the end of the wall and grounded his shield. His hands were shaking. Ice could see them shaking from the line.

The straw man took another step.

***

It walked into the shields.

Not fast. It didn't need to be fast. It came up the aisle between the tables, a pace at a time, door-slam, door-slam, with the grey coming up out of the floor behind it in a widening fan, and it walked into the wall of shields as if the wall weren't there.

The front of it hit them. Its legs. The straw-bound iron of its shins, coming forward, into eight shields with their rims touching. Ice felt it through the floor.

The wall went back.

All of it. All eight. Their boots sliding on the frost. Gabe's voice, *HOLD*, and Azac's, under it, and they held, they did, for about a second, and then they went back again, a foot, two feet, toward the line, with the straw man leaning into them, pushing, the way you'd push a gate that was stuck.

The frost came off it onto the shields. Ice saw it go. White, racing across the faces of them, over the rims, up the arms of the people holding them. Jared's thorn-shield went white in a second. Chi's. Gabe's didn't. Hearthwall didn't go white. It steamed. A great cloud of steam off it into the lamplight, and Gabe in the middle of it, with his teeth bared, holding.

"*Door!*" That was Drew. Barefoot, at the end of the wall, empty-handed, shouting. "*DOOR, NOT A WALL!*"

They sloped. The way they'd sloped for the heron on the Salt. Eight shields turning on the slant, all together, and the straw man's weight went along them instead of into them, sideways, and slid off the end, and it staggered. A step. To the side. Toward the first table.

And the grey came with its foot.

It came up out of the floor under the end of the shield wall. Under Jared's boots. Ice saw Jared look down. Saw his left boot go in, to the ankle, into the floor of the Long Barn, which was stone, which had been stone a second ago, as if it were water.

"*Man*—" said Jared.

He pulled. His foot came out. Grey to the ankle. Dust. It came off when he stamped. He stamped again. He looked at it.

"*Back!*" Higu. From the end of the first table, standing on it, with the rock. "Off the grey! Everybody back! Behind the line! *Now!*"

They came back. The whole wall. Walking backwards, shields up, over the chalk, and the grey came after them across the stone as far as the line, and stopped. Not because of the chalk. Ice didn't think it was the chalk. It just stopped there, for now, the edge of it lying across the stone a pace from his boots like the edge of a tide.

Behind him, Annet was shouting.

"*Up!* On the tables! On the stacks! Get the children up! *Off the floor!*"

He heard it happen. Seven hundred people getting up off the floor of the Long Barn in the lamplight, all at once. Benches going over. People climbing onto the long tables, three deep, lifting children over their heads. People going up the stacks of harvest against the walls, the sheaves, like climbing a hill. The horses at the far end, screaming, and the people with them holding their heads. Somebody praying. Somebody, somewhere, still singing, very softly, the way somebody always had been in the Long Barn, the whole week. Ice didn't know who. He hoped they wouldn't stop.

And the grey lay on the floor behind the straw man, from the top of the barn to the line. A great soft fan of it, all across the stone, from wall to wall. The colour gone out of everything it touched. The empty sockets. The place where Hild's stool had been. The legs of the first table, at the top end, grey to the ankle.

And at the very top of the barn, in the recess in the end wall, the seed.

The grey had gone round the bottom of the recess. Not into it. Round it. Like water round a rock.

Ice looked at that for a long time. It was probably about a second.

Then he looked up.

***

The straw man had stopped.

It stood at the line, at the edge of the grey, a pace in front of the shields, which were a pace in front of him. Tall as the roof. Its head in the hoop up among the lamps on the chains, with the poppy crown on it gone white. Its arms out from its sides.

And it was looking down at him.

Not at the shields. Not at the line, or the seven hundred people on the tables behind him, or anything. Down. Straight down, past all of it, the way it had been looking for a week. The plaited face. The twisted straw eyes. The frost in them.

The arms came down.

Slowly. The long bundles of straw at the ends of them, the hands, if they were hands, coming down out of the lamplight, past the shields. Reaching. Not for the shields. Over them. For him.

He felt the thread go tight.

Thea's hand was still in his. He could feel her weight shift, beside him on the stone, the way it had shifted on the Salt, forward, toward the front, toward him, the ward already halfway into her hand. He felt it start.

He felt it stop.

He didn't look at her. Not yet. He let go of her hand. Gently. And stood up.

"Higu," said Ice.

***

She was on the end of the first table. Standing. With the rock against her chest and the chalk in her other hand and her face very pale in the lamplight. She looked at him.

She didn't say *what*. She didn't say *sit down*. She'd known him for four hundred and fifty-eight days in this life and longer than that before it, and she'd written his name on more rocks than anybody's. She looked at him, and he watched her see his face, and see what was in it.

"Where," said Higu.

It wasn't a question. She knew it was a question he could answer.

He told her.

"Out there," said Ice. "Me." He didn't look at the straw man. He looked at her. "It only wants me. It's been looking at me for a week. If it walks, it walks at me, and it'll walk through everybody to get there and the grey comes with it." He heard his own voice. It sounded very ordinary. "So I'm going to take it out. Through the doors. Away from the barn. Somewhere nobody else can stand." He took a breath. "I'm taking it off them."

The barn had gone quiet.

Not all of it. The horses. The child, still crying. But near the line, at the top end, round the first two tables, where the thirty-six were, and the gleaners who sat in on things, it had gone quiet. Everybody had heard.

"I'm asking," said Ice.

He'd never said it before. In either life. He'd never once stood in front of somebody and said *I'm asking*. It felt like nothing. It felt like stepping off a ledge.

Higu looked down at the rock in her arms.

At the bottom of it, in small capitals, in chalk, from four days ago: *ICE: NOT IN FRONT.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she put her sleeve over it, and rubbed it out.

She wrote something else. Ice couldn't see what from the floor. She turned the rock round so he could.

*ICE: OUT THERE.*
*EVERYBODY: THE DOOR.*

"That's just where," said Higu.

Her voice went, on *where*. She didn't fix it.

"How," she said.

He'd had three nights.

"Borne," said Ice. "I can't stand on the grey. Nobody can. Annet went through to the knee." He looked at the fan of it across the floor. "But I'm not standing on it if you're holding me. That's what it's for. *What you cannot carry, they will.*" He looked round. At all of them. At thirty-five faces at the top of the Long Barn in the lamplight. "I need you to hold me up. From the door. All of you. On the real ground. And not let go."

"Link," said Deston.

He was already up. Off the end of the second table, with his hand on his shirt pocket. "I'll link everybody. All of us. To you. Like the Long Night." He swallowed. "I'll give it to you. Whatever you need."

"The sill," said Maigo.

He was sitting against the wall where Gigi had put him, a day ago, after she pulled him out of the floor. He was still grey in the face. His leaves were still yellow. He got up anyway, slowly, with Gigi's hand under his arm.

"The doorsill," said Maigo. "It's stone. It's old. It's real, the grey's never been in a doorway. I'll root it." He looked at Ice. "There'll be something under the door. All the way. I'll make sure."

"You can't—" said Gigi.

"I'm a bit planted," said Maigo. "Remember."

Gigi looked at him for a second. Then she nodded, and didn't let go of his arm.

"Hearthwall at the sill," said Gabe. "Rachael."

"Yes," said Rachael.

"Nobody shoots," said Higu. Off the table, onto the floor, with the rock. "Nobody burns. Nobody hits it. It's not ours to hurt. It's theirs." She looked at the gleaners on the tables. "Everybody holds him. That's all. That's the whole plan."

Calvin held up his hand.

Not to vote. His left hand. Open. Palm out, toward Ice, in the lamplight. And on it, faint, faded, written in Ina's fingertip a long time ago on the Salt, and never washed off, a name.

"*Look at my back*," said Calvin. His voice was shaking. "That's what I always say. In the Fen. On the hill." He kept his hand up. "This time it's the other way round. I'm going to be at the door. You look at the door."

"I'll open the doors," said Tiger.

He'd already gone. Down the barn, between the tables, toward the far end, without waiting.

And Gabe.

Gabe came up beside him at the line. On his right. Where he always was. He had Hearthwall on his arm, steaming still, and he didn't put it down. He looked at Ice. Not at his face. At the side of his head, the way he'd looked at the side of his head on a barracks step on floor 10 with a shield across his right side.

"Dec," said Gabe.

"I know."

"You asked."

"I asked."

Gabe nodded. Once. His jaw was very tight.

"Then go on," said Gabe. "Go on, Dec."

He reached round behind him and took the lamp shield off Ice's back. The first one. The one he'd made on floor 5, dark grey Fen iron with a lamp in the middle and a rim the colour of old blood, the one Ice had been carrying for more than three hundred days and wouldn't let go of in the sea. He held it out. Ice took it. Put his arm through the straps.

"Bring it back," said Gabe. "I'm not making you another one."

"You said you would."

"I lied," said Gabe.

***

He turned round.

Thea was standing up.

She was a pace behind him. On the near side of the line. With the lantern in her hand, lit, the amber in it flat against the glass. She'd stood up when he did. He hadn't heard her. And she was standing there, with her weight on her back foot, very still, the way you'd stand on the edge of something if you'd decided not to go over it.

He looked at her.

He knew what he was looking for. He'd been looking for it on every floor since the pass. Since the Gate. Since the Salt, and the heron, and a light at her shoulder saying *don't*. He'd looked for it every time something came at the middle. Her weight, going forward. The ward in her hand. The step past his left shoulder. *The same ward.*

It didn't come.

He watched it not come. He watched her stand there with every line of her body wanting to go forward, toward him, toward the front, toward the thing reaching down over the shields with its long straw arms. He watched her hold it. Hold all of it. In one place. The way you'd hold a full cup across a room.

"Go," said Thea.

Her voice was perfectly level.

"Go," she said again. "And come back."

He couldn't say anything.

She lifted the lantern. A little. So the light went up between them.

"I'll be at the door," said Thea. "With this. All the time. So you know where it is." She held it there. "So you can find your way back."

Under his window, where only he and Alea could see, something moved.

He hadn't opened his window. It opened anyway. The way it used to on the Salt, on the hill. Just his name and his level, Voyager, Lv 73, and under it, in the grey that wasn't quite a colour, a line he'd seen before:

*Althea · Record 2 of 2 · Observing.*

And then the last word changed.

*Althea · Record 2 of 2 · does not match.*

*Don't*, said Alea. Very fast. Very quiet. In his ear. *Don't say it. Don't look at it. Not here. Go.*

He didn't say it. He looked at Thea, and she looked back at him, and he thought she knew anyway. Not the words. The shape. The way she'd known on a rock in the sea that it didn't know she knew.

He went.

***

He walked down the barn backwards.

It was the only way. He didn't know if it would follow him if he turned his back on it. He didn't want to find out. So he walked backwards down the main aisle of the Long Barn, between the long tables, with the lamp shield on his arm and his sword not drawn, facing up the barn, and the straw man came after him.

He made a light. He didn't decide to. It just came, the way it had come on the rope on floor 9, in the dark under the Down. *Beacon.* Mother Tallow's, from floor 2. A light in his left hand, low, amber, held up over the rim of the shield. *Hold it up, and everything in the dark will come to you instead.* He felt it go out of him toward the straw man like a hook on a line.

The straw man came.

Over the line. Over the chalk, where the shields had been; they'd moved, they were going down the sides of the barn now, along the walls, all thirty-five of them, fast, toward the doors, ahead of him. It stepped over the line, and the grey came up round its foot on the near side, on the stone where he'd been sitting for three days, and it came on. Door-slam. Door-slam. A pace at a time. Down the aisle after him. Between the tables.

And the tables were full.

Seven hundred people. Standing on them. Three deep, on the long tables either side of the aisle, all the way down the barn. Holding on to each other. Holding children up. Old men and old women and boys and girls and mothers, the whole of the Long Acre, in their nightclothes and blankets, standing on the tables in the lamplight, an arm's length from the aisle, and the straw man walking down the aisle between them.

Nobody ran.

He'd thought they would. He'd have understood it if they had. But they didn't. They stood on the tables, and watched it go past, tall as the roof, white with frost, with the grey coming up out of the floor under every footstep. And as it went past them, some of them put their hands out.

The children first.

A small girl on her father's shoulders, at the end of the second table, reaching out as it went past and putting her hand flat on the frozen straw of its leg. Just for a second. And taking it back. And her father not stopping her. Then a boy, further down. Then a woman with grey hair. Then more. All down the barn. People putting their hands out as it passed, and touching it, the frozen sheaves of its legs, its hips, as high as they could reach. For luck. The way they'd touched it every night for a week in the dark, sitting with it, without being asked.

The grey didn't touch them. It came up out of the floor. It didn't come up the tables.

He walked backwards down the barn with a light in his hand, and the straw man came after him, and the whole of the Long Acre touched it as it went by, and he had to stop looking at their faces, because he couldn't see properly any more.

*Keep walking*, said Alea. *Ice. Keep walking.*

He kept walking.

***

Tiger had the doors open.

The great cart doors at the end of the Long Barn, two of them, as tall as a house, oak and iron, that it took two horses to swing in summer. He had them both open, on his own, all the way. He was standing beside one of them with his shoulder against it, breathing hard, with snow coming in round him.

The cold came in.

It came in like a wave. Down over the sill and into the barn, and the lamps nearest the doors went sideways on their chains and flared. And outside, the night. Dark. Clear. Stars, more than he'd ever seen on floor 14, hard and white, all the way up. And under them, white. The lane, which wasn't a lane, going down from the doors. The Barn Field. The whole of the slope going down into the dark, smooth and white under the stars, perfectly still.

And under the white, nothing.

The thirty-five were at the door.

They'd got there ahead of him, along the walls, and they'd done it the way they did everything, without anybody saying how. Maigo was on his knees on the sill. The doorsill, a single long block of old grey stone worn into a dip in the middle by a thousand years of carts. He had his bare root-foot down on it, in the dip, and his eyes shut, and his ring round him gone thin and gold, and Gigi kneeling beside him with her hand on his back and her bucket by her knee.

Gabe was on the sill. Hearthwall flat on the stone beside Maigo, boss up, steaming in the cold. Rachael kneeling by it with her sleeves pushed up and her white arms bare.

And behind them, all of them. In a line. Back from the sill into the barn. One behind another. Each with a hand on the shoulder of the one in front, the way they'd stood on every stair since floor 1 when they counted. Deston in the middle of them, with his hand on his shirt pocket and his eyes shut and gold going out of him to everybody, thin threads in the lamplight. Calvin at the front, right at the front, behind Gabe, with his left hand up, open, palm out, toward the doors.

Thea was at the doorpost.

On the left. Against the oak. Where the cold came in. With the lantern held up in both hands, the amber in it lying flat on the glass, toward the night.

He walked backwards past her. Over the sill. Past Maigo and Gabe and Hearthwall. He felt Gabe's hand on his back for a second as he went by, flat, between the shoulder blades, and then not.

He stepped out of the Long Barn backwards into the snow.

***

His heel went through.

He knew it would. He'd seen Annet's leg go to the knee. He'd known, all three nights, sitting at the line, that the first step was going to be the worst, because the first step was the one where he'd find out if it worked. And his heel came down on the snow outside the doors, on the white smooth skin of it under the stars, and there was nothing under the snow, and his heel went through it like going through the surface of a pond—

And stopped.

Not on anything. On nothing. He felt it stop. He felt it the way you'd feel somebody catch you under the arms when you missed a step in the dark. Not hard. Not a jolt. Just hands. A lot of hands. Holding.

[ Borne ]
[ What you cannot carry, they will. ]

He stood on the snow.

Like a bird. Like something that didn't weigh anything. His boots on the white, a finger deep, not going through. The nothing under it, a hand's width under his feet, cold, grey, the bottom of a well somebody had painted. And him standing on top of it. Held.

He could feel them. All of them. Behind him, through the doors, in a line, on the real stone of the Long Barn's floor. Thirty-five people. He could feel exactly where each of them was, the way he'd felt every one of them through Sanctuary at the Gate. Gabe, big and banked and close. Maigo, faint and green and deep, down in the sill. It came over the sill.

One foot. The other. Over the stone where Maigo was rooted, over Hearthwall, and the grey didn't come up round its feet there. It couldn't. Maigo's eyes were shut and his face was screwed up like somebody holding a door against a wind, and the sill stayed stone.

Then it was out. Standing up, in the snow, outside the Long Barn, under the stars. Straightening. Tall. White. With the lamplight from the doors behind it lighting up the edges of it like a hedge at sunset.

And behind it, inside the barn, Hyvred shouted.

He heard her. Clear across the snow, high, from somewhere on the tables, with her glasses off. "*It's going!* The floor! It's *going out!* It's following him! It's *following him out!*"

He couldn't see. He didn't need to. He could see it in the snow at the straw man's feet. Where it stood, outside the doors, the snow round its feet was moving. Sinking. Very slightly. As if something under the snow were draining toward it from behind. From the barn. From the floor. All the grey that had come up out of the sockets and spread across the stone, going back. Out under the doors, under the sill, round Maigo, into the snow, toward the feet of the thing that had brought it in.

Like a tide, going out after the moon.

*It's taking it with it*, said Alea. *All of it. Out of the barn. After you.*

"Good," said Ice.

He walked backwards. Down the slope. Forty paces. Sixty. A hundred. Away from the barn, down the Barn Field, into the white, with the stars over him and his light in his hand and the nothing a hand's width under his boots. And the threads behind him, stretching, from the doors. He could feel them stretch. Thirty-five of them. Not thinner. Just longer. Like a rope paid out from a capstan.

The straw man came after him.

Door-slam, door-slam. Down the slope. Every footstep sinking into the snow and coming out grey. Away from the barn. Away from the doors and the people on the tables and the seed in its recess. After him.

At two hundred paces he stopped.

He didn't decide. He just knew. The way you know where the middle of a floor is. Far enough. Far enough that if it went past him it would have a long way to go back. Far enough that the doors were a rectangle of yellow light at the top of the slope, small, with shapes in it. Far enough that he couldn't see Thea's face. He could see the lantern.

He stopped. He turned the light down. He let Beacon go. It didn't need it any more.

He drew his sword.

It stopped too. Ten paces from him. Up the slope. Tall as a house, with the stars behind it and the window over its head, pale, steady, in the dark.
