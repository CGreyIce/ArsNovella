Drew carried the rock everywhere.

It was in his pack now. It had been since floor 8. A flat grey rock the size of a dinner plate, worn smooth on one side by a river nobody had named, and covered on that side in chalk. He'd found it on the hillside the first morning on floor 8, when Ice had woken up to thirty-five people and Drew had needed somewhere to write *NOBODY WATCHES ALONE* that wasn't a slate nailed to a wall in a town they were about to leave. He'd picked it up and put it in his pack, and it had never come out again except to be written on.

It was heavy. He'd stopped noticing.

On floor 2 it had been a slate, nailed to the wall of the trapper's hall in Larchwick. On floor 3, a slate by the smoking-hall door, with a note pinned to it. On floor 6, a slate at the top of a pit. On every floor there'd been something to write on, and Drew writing on it. Where everybody was going. Who was with who. What was true. *Facts go to everybody the same day.* Thirty-five people, and then thirty-six, and all of it in chalk, in his hand, so that nobody ever had to wonder.

He'd been the general since day 40. He'd been something like it since day 12, when he'd stood in front of a slate in a trapper's hall with four parties' worth of maps and thought: *somebody has to*.

Somebody always had to. It was usually him.

And on the morning of the two hundred and seventy-first day, on the fifth wall, when a tall grey thing with no face opened a book at the bottom of the slope and said *the count*, the general had frozen like everybody else, with his hand out in the middle of a sentence and his mouth open, and stayed frozen until a woman who said she wasn't brave said *breathe*.

He hadn't been able to look at Chi since. He wasn't angry with her. He was so grateful he could hardly stand it. That was the problem.

***

He wrote it on the rock that afternoon.

He didn't want to. He did it anyway. That was the rule. He'd made it himself, at the top of a pit, under the moon, with twelve other people: *facts go to everybody the same day*. The theory stayed with the club. The facts went on the rock.

And it was a fact. Thea had read it. Out loud. On the wall, with blood on her lip. Everybody had heard her.

He sat on his bunk in the barracks with the rock on his knees and a stub of chalk, and wrote it. Small. Square. The way the hold-folk carved names.

*DAY 271. DAWN. THE STEWARD.*
*CAME DOWN FROM THE GATE. NO FOOTPRINTS. NO FACE.*
*IT COUNTED US. ONLY US. 1 TO 36.*
*IRREGULAR. IN FRONT OF EVERYONE.*
*UNDER IT (THEA): ONE RECORD DOES NOT MATCH.*
*"THE COUNT WILL BE RECONCILED. AT THE GATE. AT THE DARK OF THE MOON."*
*BRENNA: IT CAME ONCE BEFORE. 312 YEARS AGO. COUNTED 30. "THE COUNT IS CORRECT."*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he got up and put it on the long table in the middle of the barracks, face up, where everybody would see it when they came in for supper. The way he always did.

***

They read it.

He watched them do it. One at a time, coming in out of the cold, stamping snow off their boots, stopping at the table. Reading. He watched their faces.

Calvin read it first, out loud, the way he read everything, and stopped halfway down. "*One record does not*—" He looked up. "What does that mean?"

Nobody answered him.

Hana read it with her crown in her hands and put the crown down on the table very carefully, next to the rock, as if she'd forgotten she was holding it. Dennis read it over her shoulder and said nothing at all, which Drew had never seen Dennis do. Salty read it twice with his lips moving and then looked across the room at Yan. Yan didn't read it. Yan was sitting on his bunk with his hands in his lap, looking at nothing, and Drew thought, from his face, that Yan had seen something on the wall that morning that wasn't written on any rock. Some light. Doing something.

Deston read it standing up, with his hand flat on his shirt pocket. When he'd finished he looked at nobody. He went and sat down on his bunk.

Ina read it, and looked at Winter across the room, and Winter looked back, and Ina nodded once, very slightly, and looked away. As if something had just been confirmed that he'd decided not to ask about.

And then, one by one, all of them, they looked up from the rock and across the barracks.

At Ice.

He'd come in last. He was standing just inside the door with snow on his shoulders and the lamp shield on his back. Gabe beside him. On his right. Ice had stopped in the doorway when he saw everybody at the table, and he hadn't come any further in.

He stood there and let them look.

Drew watched him do it. He'd watched Ice do a lot of things in two hundred and seventy-one days, most of them from a long way off. He'd never watched him do this. Stand in a doorway with thirty-four people looking at him, after a rock had told them the tower had a record of him that didn't match, and not move. Not run. Not explain. Not look away.

The barracks was absolutely silent.

"I know," said Ice.

His voice was very steady. Drew didn't know how.

"I know you want to ask," said Ice. "I know what it looks like. I know." He paused. "Not yet. I'm sorry. Not yet." He looked round at all of them. "Trust me. That's all I've got. I know it's not enough."

Nobody said anything.

Then Calvin, at the table, with the rock in front of him, said: "Okay."

Ice looked at him.

"Okay," said Calvin again. He shrugged. Not carelessly. "You said trust. You always say trust." He looked down at the rock. "It always works. Mostly."

Somebody laughed. Not much. Hako, probably. And then somebody else. And the room moved again, slowly, the way the wall had moved that morning when Chi said *breathe*. People sitting down. People taking their coats off. Somebody putting a pot on the stove.

Ice stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then he came in, and sat down on his bunk, and Gabe sat down beside him, and neither of them said anything.

Drew picked up the rock off the table. He held it in his hands. It was very heavy.

***

The Steward came back the next morning.

And the next. And the one after that.

Every dawn. Down the pass on top of the snow, the way it had come the first time, very straight, with its book against its chest, leaving nothing behind it. It stopped at the foot of the slope. It opened its book. *The count.* And everybody on the wall froze. And it counted. One to thirty-six. Only the climbers. Landing on each of them like a stamp coming down on a page.

And every morning, Chi said *breathe*. Slowly. A little sooner each time. And they came out of it.

And every morning, Drew froze.

He tried not to. He stood on the parapet at dawn with his buckler on his arm and his bladed knuckles on and the rock in his pack, and set his feet, and told himself *not this time*. And the Steward said *the count*, and the cold came up out of his middle and went into his arms and locked. Every time. *Fourteen.* He felt it land on him. *Fourteen.* Like a hand picking him up and looking at him and writing on him and putting him down.

And every morning, at *thirty-six*, he felt it land along the wall like a hammer, and saw the window stay in front of Ice's face a little longer than it had the day before.

He hated it more than anything that had happened to him on any floor. Not the fear. He'd been afraid before. He'd been afraid on floor 2, in the Fold, when the Bellwether screamed and he'd frozen with his knuckles up in front of a thing with a bell round its neck, and Chi had said *talk to each other slowly*, and he'd come out of it. He'd been afraid on floor 6 running across the glass. He was afraid every night on the wall.

It was the freezing. The general, standing there with his hand out, stuck, while the thing counted his people. Every morning. In front of all of them.

On the fourth morning, when it was gone, he walked down off the wall without saying anything to anyone and went to find somebody who could tell him why.

***

He found Old Sten.

He hadn't been looking for him. He'd been walking, mostly. Through the fourth level, in the snow, past the long halls. And in the biggest of the long halls, the one where the hold-folk ate, they'd pushed the tables back against the walls, and the floor in the middle was full of children.

Thirty of them. Maybe forty. Small ones, five and six, and bigger ones, ten, eleven, all in grey wool, all barefoot on the warm stone of the floor. And they were falling over.

On purpose. In rows. They stood, and then they fell. Forward, or backward, or sideways, rolling, slapping the floor with their palms as they hit it, and coming back up, and standing. And falling again. And an old man was walking up and down the rows, very slowly, with his hands behind his back.

He was the oldest man Drew had seen in the Bastion. Small, for a hold-folk man, and bent, and bald as an egg, with a thin white wisp of beard down to his chest. He wore grey like everybody, and he was barefoot like the children, and he didn't carry a shield. Drew had never seen a hold-folk adult without a shield. He walked up and down the rows of falling children with his hands behind his back and said, every so often, in a voice like a door creaking, *again*.

Drew stood in the doorway and watched for a long time.

"You'll be the one who froze," said the old man.

He hadn't looked round. He was standing in the middle of the floor with his back to the door, watching a small girl fall over sideways and get it wrong.

"Everybody froze," said Drew.

"Everybody froze," the old man agreed. "But you're the one who minded." He turned round. His eyes were grey and very pale, almost white, like the sky near the ground. "Brenna told me. The climbers' general. Froze with his hand out. Minds it." He looked Drew up and down. "Sten. I teach the empty hand."

"The what?"

"The empty hand." Sten gestured at the children. "Before a child in this pass holds a shield, they learn to stand with nothing. To fall, and get up. To breathe. To be hit, and not fall. To be afraid, and not freeze." He looked at Drew. "First you learn to hold yourself. Then you can hold something else."

Drew didn't say anything.

"Take those off," said Sten.

He was looking at Drew's hands. At the bladed knuckles. Drew had forgotten he was wearing them. He always wore them. On the wall, in the barracks, in the long halls. They'd been on his hands since floor 1.

"Why?"

"Because you can't learn anything with your hands full," said Sten. "Nobody can. Take them off. And take off that." He nodded at Drew's back. At the pack. "There's something heavy in it. You walk like a man carrying a stone."

Drew stood in the doorway.

"It's a rock," he said.

"Then put it down."

***

He took the knuckles off. That was easy, or easier than he'd thought. He unbuckled them and put them on a bench by the wall, and his hands felt strange without them. Light. Cold. Bare.

He put the pack down. Next to the bench. With the rock in it.

He didn't take the rock out.

Sten watched him not take it out, and didn't say anything.

Then he put Drew on the end of a row of children, a grown man in a navy track jacket with red stripes down the sleeves, barefoot on the warm stone between a boy of about eight and a girl of about six, and said *fall*.

Drew fell.

He fell badly. He'd been a fighter for two hundred and seventy-five days and a general for two hundred and thirty-five and he fell over in a long hall like a sack of grain, and the six-year-old beside him laughed so hard she fell over too.

*Again*, said Sten.

He did it all morning. Falling. Getting up. Breathing, the way Sten said: in through the nose, slowly, all the way down into the stomach, and hold it, and out through the mouth, slower. Standing with his hands open at his sides while a ten-year-old with a padded stick came at him and tapped him on the shoulder, and the chest, and the face, and he had to stand there with nothing in his hands and not flinch.

He flinched every time.

"Your hands," said Sten. "Every time it comes, your hands go up. Looking for something."

"For a weapon. For the buckler."

"For something to hold," said Sten. "That's what fear wants. Something to hold on to. You saw it on the wall. The Steward's fear. It needs something to grip." He tapped Drew's open palm with one bony finger. "If your hands are full, it holds your hands. If your hands are empty, it has nothing to hold."

Drew looked at his own hand.

"And if your *head's* full," said Sten, more quietly, "it holds that." He looked at Drew for a long time with his pale eyes. "What's in the bag, general?"

And a window came up in front of Drew's face. Small and blue, in the warm air of the long hall, with forty children falling over round him.

***

He told Ice that evening. Not because he had to. Because he wanted to, and it was the first time he'd ever wanted to tell Ice anything before he told the rock.

They were on the barracks roof. It was where people went now, on floor 10, when they wanted to say something; the warmth of the Breath came up through the chimney and you could sit against it in the cold. Ice had been up there on his own. Drew had sat down next to him without asking.

"A monk one," said Ice, when he'd read the window. Very quietly.

"You knew."

"I didn't know." Ice was looking at the window, not at him. "I didn't know it'd be here. Or now." He paused. "I knew you'd end up with your hands empty, one day. That's all."

Drew looked at him sideways.

He wasn't in the club by accident. He'd been at the top of the pit on floor 6. He'd asked *is it bad?* and Higu had said *I think it's sad*. He knew, as well as anybody, what it meant when Ice said a thing like *I knew you'd end up*. He didn't say anything about it. He'd learned not to.

"*Put down what you carry*," said Drew.

"Yeah."

"Sten says it's the rock." Drew put his hand on the pack beside him. "Not the knuckles. The knuckles came off in about four seconds. The rock." He was quiet for a moment. "I can't."

Ice didn't say anything.

"It's not a rock," said Drew. "You know that. It's—" He stopped. "It's where everybody is. Who's with who. What's true. It's thirty-six people. In chalk. I've been carrying it since day 12. If I put it down, who—" He stopped again. "Somebody has to."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true."

"No," said Ice. "It's always been you." He was quiet. "That's not the same thing."

Drew looked at him.

"On Earth," said Ice. "That tournament. The long weekend. You ran it from your bedroom. Brackets and rules and a channel and forty people shouting at you and you on your own at three in the morning fixing a seed somebody'd got wrong." He almost smiled. "Me and Yan won. Yan carried. And at the end everybody logged off, and you were still there. On your own. Typing *thanks everyone* to an empty room."

"You stayed," said Drew.

"I stayed."

"I always wondered why. You barely knew me."

"Because nobody else did," said Ice. "Nobody ever thanks the guy running it." He looked out over the edge of the roof, at the city going down the pass in the dark, the lights in the long halls, the fires on the walls. "You've been running it for two hundred and seventy days, Drew. All of it. On your own. With a rock in your bag." He was quiet. "And you've been asking somebody to thank you every single time, and making a joke of it, so nobody notices you mean it."

Drew didn't say anything for a long time.

"Who do I give it to?" he said.

"I don't know," said Ice. "That's not mine. That's the how." He paused. "But I know who's been drawing every floor since floor 1, and putting the big trees on a map so we'd know where we were when nothing else stayed still."

***

Higu was in her round house on the far side of the barracks, the little one she'd found on the second day and filled with maps. She was sitting on the floor with her board on her knees, drawing the pass. The five walls. The Gate, up at the top, where nobody had been. She looked up when he came in.

He took the rock out of his pack.

He'd never done that. Not for anyone. He held it in both hands. It was very heavy. The chalk on its face, layer on layer, wiped and rewritten a hundred times. *NOBODY WATCHES ALONE. ICE: WITH SOMEONE. ALWAYS. UP BEFORE DARK. IF THE DOWN SPLITS US. DAY 271.*

"Hold this," said Drew. "Just till tomorrow."

Higu looked at the rock. Then at him. She didn't ask why. That was Higu. She thought before she spoke, and sometimes she thought so long that you'd already got your answer before she said anything.

She put her board down. She held out both hands.

He put the rock in them.

It went out of his hands, and he felt it go. Not the weight. Something else. As if somebody had been standing on his back for two hundred and thirty-five days and had just stepped off. He stood in Higu's round house with his hands empty and couldn't breathe for a second.

"Okay," said Higu. Holding it. "Till tomorrow."

"Somebody has to—"

"I know," said Higu. "Me. Till tomorrow." She put the rock down on her map, on the pass, between the fourth wall and the fifth. Flat. Face up. "Go and stand in front of it, Drew."

***

He went out of the gate of the fifth wall at dawn on the two hundred and seventy-sixth day.

Not alone. *Nobody watches alone.* Chi came with him to the gate and stood in it, in the arch, with Waddles at her feet and her shield on her arm, ready. And the whole line was on the parapet above him, climbers and hold-folk, shields up, watching. And Thea with the lantern. And Sten, of all people, the old man, up on the wall in his bare feet with his hands behind his back.

But Drew went out alone. Down the slope. Into the snow. Thirty metres. To the place at the foot of it where the Steward stopped every morning, in the middle of the pass.

No knuckles. No buckler. No pack. No rock.

He stood there with his hands open at his sides and his feet bare in the snow, because Sten had said *bare*, and it was so cold his feet had stopped hurting about a minute after he started walking. In a navy track jacket and nothing else that mattered.

He breathed. In through the nose. Slowly. All the way down. Hold it. Out.

The sun came up over the low end of the pass behind him. Gold on the snow.

And the Steward came down.

***

He watched it come. That was the first thing. Every other morning he'd been on the wall with his hands full, looking at it over a buckler, waiting for it to say *the count*. This morning he watched it come down the pass toward him on top of the snow, tall and grey and very straight, with its book, and he didn't hold anything.

It stopped. Three metres from him. Closer than it had ever stopped. It stood there with its white face bent over its book, and for a moment it didn't open it.

As if it had noticed something in the way.

Then it opened the book.

"*The count*," said the Steward.

It came.

Up out of the middle of him, the way it always came. The cold. Going out into his arms, his legs, his hands. Looking for something. To grip. To lock. He felt it go into his hands, the way it went into them every morning, and look for the buckler, and the knuckles, and the strap of his pack, and the weight of a rock against his back.

And there wasn't anything.

His hands were open. Empty. Bare, and cold, and holding nothing. And the fear went into them and looked, and looked, and found nothing to hold on to. And went on. Through. Out the ends of his fingers into the snow. Like water going through a hand held open under a tap.

He breathed. In. Hold. Out.

He didn't freeze.

"*One*," said the Steward. Somewhere behind him, on the wall, it landed on somebody. "*Two. Three.*"

He stood in the snow with his hands open and felt each number go past him to the wall, landing. Stamp. Stamp. Stamp. And felt everyone on the wall frozen. He could feel it. Behind him. Thirty-four people and a line of hold-folk, all locked, all held, the way he'd been held every morning for five mornings.

"*Twelve. Thirteen.*"

And it came to him.

"*Fourteen*," said the Steward.

It landed. He felt it. Like being picked up. A hand reaching into the middle of him, where he kept himself, picking him up between finger and thumb, writing a number on him.

*Fourteen.*

And he opened his mouth, with his hands open at his sides in the snow, and said:

"*Drew*."

***

The Steward stopped.

It didn't look up from its book. But it stopped. The next number didn't come. It stood there three metres from him with its pen, or whatever it was, over the page, and nothing happened. For a breath. Two.

"*Drew*," he said again. Louder. Slowly. The way Chi would have said it. "My name's Drew. Not fourteen. *Drew*."

He didn't know why he said it. It just came. He thought about it afterwards, a lot, and the only thing he could think was the walls. Every wall in the Bastion, the inside of every tunnel, floor to ceiling, ten thousand names, carved, square, with a line under each one. A whole people who'd spent nine hundred years making sure that nobody who stood on their wall was ever just a number.

"*Fifteen*," said the Steward.

And from the wall behind him, somebody said:

"*Higu*."

He turned his head. He could move it. He looked up at the parapet. And there was Higu, at the top of the wall, in the line, with her bow in one hand. She'd been frozen. She wasn't now. Her mouth was open. She'd said it.

"*Sixteen*."

"*Xola*," said Xola. Not quite steady. But said.

"*Seventeen*."

"*Chi*," said Chi, from the gate. Slowly. Perfectly calm. And the word went out from her along the wall, the way her *breathe* went out every morning, and Drew felt it land on people. Felt them hear it. Felt the frozen line start to move.

"*Eighteen*."

"*Thea*."

"*Nineteen*."

"*Hana*." And then, from somewhere further along, a man's voice, laughing, wet: "*Alice*." Jared.

"*Twenty*."

"*Milli*."

It went along the wall. Every number the Steward said, somebody said a name back. Over it. Through it. Loud. Some of them shouted. Calvin, at *twenty-six*, so loud it echoed off the cliffs on both sides of the pass: "*CALVIN. IT'S CALVIN.*" Azac, at *nine*, too late, because Azac had been frozen when nine went past, saying *Azac* anyway, out of order, and then everybody else who'd been missed saying theirs too. *Gabe. Deston. Rachael. Dennis.* Out of order. All at once. A wall of names. The way a wall of names was supposed to sound. Thirty-five voices coming down off the parapet of the fifth wall into the snow, all of them saying who they were.

And the Steward went on counting through it. Steadily. As if it couldn't hear. *Thirty-one. Thirty-two.*

It could hear. Drew was sure of it. He was three metres from it, and he could see its hand on the page. Every time a name came down off the wall, the hand stopped, for half a heartbeat. And then went on.

"*Thirty-five*," said the Steward.

"*Hyvred*."

And then it paused. The long pause. The one it took every morning.

The whole wall went quiet.

"*Thirty-six*," said the Steward.

It landed. Drew felt it land. Up there, on the parapet, past the gate, at the place in the line where it always landed. Like a hammer.

And nobody said anything.

Drew turned round in the snow and looked up at the wall. At the place. Ice was standing there. In the line. With his lamp shield up and Gabe's warm shield across his right side. Not frozen. He'd never been frozen. Standing perfectly still with the window in front of his face, *Irregular*, small and blue, and his mouth shut.

He wasn't going to say it. Drew could see he wasn't. Whatever he was, whatever the record said, he wasn't going to stand on the wall and say a name into that.

And then thirty-five people said it for him.

Not all the same. That was what Drew remembered, afterwards, more than anything. They didn't say the same word. Half the wall said *Declan*. Deston and Gabe and Rachael and Dennis and Calvin, Xav and Malcolm and Rui Xiang and Azac and John, all at once, from all along the line. And the other half said *Ice*. Everybody else. Thea and Higu and Chi and Hana and all the rest of them. At the same moment. Two names, one on top of the other, coming down off the wall into the snow. *Declan. Ice.* Loud. Not shouting. Just said. The way you say the name of somebody standing next to you.

The Steward's hand stopped on the page.

It stayed stopped. A long time. Longer than it had ever paused on any number. Drew stood three metres from it in the snow with his hands open and watched its white face, bent over its book, with no expression on it, because it didn't have anywhere to put one.

Then it closed the book.

It didn't say anything. Not *the count will be reconciled*. Not anything. It closed the book and held it against its chest, very straight. And turned. And walked back up the pass, on top of the snow, leaving nothing behind it.

Drew watched it go.

He read them standing in the snow. He couldn't feel his feet. He read them again.

Then he walked back up the slope to the gate, slowly, where Chi was standing in the arch with Waddles, and she looked at him, and didn't say anything, and he didn't either. He walked past her through the gate and up the stair onto the parapet, barefoot, and the line was standing there. Climbers and hold-folk. Looking at him.

He didn't know what to say. He'd always known what to say. That was the job.

"Somebody—" he started.

"Thank you, Drew," said thirty-five people.

All at once. Before he'd finished. From all along the wall.

He stood at the top of the stair with his hands empty and his feet bare and looked at them, and didn't say anything at all, because he couldn't.

***

Higu gave him the rock back at noon.

She came to find him in the long hall, where he was sitting on a bench by the wall watching Sten's children fall over, with a blanket round his feet that Dennis had made him put on. She sat down next to him and put the rock on the bench between them.

"Till tomorrow," she said. "It's tomorrow."

He looked at it. The chalk. *DAY 271.*

He didn't pick it up.

"You could keep it," he said. "A bit longer."

Higu looked at him for a long time.

"How long?" she said.

"I don't know." He looked at his hands. Empty, in his lap. "A day. A week. Turns. You on mornings and me on evenings." He was quiet. "Somebody has to. It doesn't have to always be me."

Higu thought about it. For quite a long time. The way she did.

"Okay," she said. "Turns."

She picked the rock up and put it on her knees. And then, after a moment, she took a stub of chalk out of her pocket, and wiped one corner of the rock with her sleeve, and wrote, small, in her own neat hand:

*DAY 276. THE STEWARD COUNTED. WE SAID OUR NAMES.*

And under it:

*DREW STOOD IN FRONT OF IT WITH NOTHING.*

And under that, after another moment:

*SOMEBODY THANK HIM.*

She showed him. He looked at it for a long time.

Across the hall, Ice was standing in the doorway with Gabe, looking at them. He didn't come over. He just stood there, and caught Drew's eye, and nodded. Once. The way you'd nod to somebody at the end of a long weekend, at three in the morning, in an empty room, after everybody else had logged off.

Drew nodded back.

On the floor of the long hall, a six-year-old fell over sideways and got it right for the first time, and Sten, walking up and down with his hands behind his back, said, in his creaking voice:

"*Good.*"
