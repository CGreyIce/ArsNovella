Winter liked facts.

She'd liked them before the tower. She liked them more after. Facts didn't move. You could put your weight on them. A fact was a fact on floor 1 and on floor 17 and it didn't care whether you were frightened, and it didn't turn into somebody else's street while you were looking the other way.

She kept them. Not in a book, like Chris, or on a rock, like Higu. In her head. In rows. The way she kept arrows in a quiver: counted, straight, points down.

Fact: they'd left Turnstead at noon on the eight hundred and seventh day through a gate that hadn't existed the day before.

Fact: the road on the other side was white, and dusty, and went up a long low hill between hedges, and over the top, and on, as far as she could see. And further.

Fact: it was summer.

That one had hit everybody, at the top of the first hill. After the grey of Turnstead and the dark of the Hollow and the ice of fifteen. It was summer on eighteen, a proper one, hot and blue and loud with larks, and the hedges either side of the road were white with something like elderflower and smelled of it, and the fields beyond the hedges went away over the rolling country in squares of green and gold and the faint blue of flax. Poplars in lines along the edges of things, very straight, very tall, shivering silver when the wind went through them. And the road, going on, white, through all of it, over every hill, round every hedge, into the haze.

Calvin had stood at the top of the first hill and said *oh* at a normal volume. Rachael hadn't told him to shut up.

Fact: she had thirty-one arrows. Float iron, Mairead's heads, Keddle goose. She'd had forty on the fifteenth day after the jam and she'd used nine in Turnstead. She counted them every morning.

Fact: Ina had walked beside her all afternoon and not said anything, and that was the best thing about the afternoon.

Fact: there was a stone at the side of the road every so often, waist high, grey, with a number cut into it.

She counted those too.

***

The first one said *70*.

She noticed it because it was a number and she noticed numbers. A grey stone at the edge of the road, with a flat face toward the road and *70* cut into the face, deep and old and worn smooth at the edges. A mile, she thought, or more, and then another one. *69*.

"Mile markers," said Chris. Beside her. Writing it down. "Counting down. To something."

"To what?"

"To wherever the road goes." He looked ahead. At the white road going over the next hill. "Seventy of something."

"Seventy miles."

"Or seventy days. Or seventy bridges." He wrote. "I don't know yet. I want that on the record."

"*God*, you're annoying," said Winter.

He grinned. He liked it when she said that. She'd been saying it for two years and he'd grinned every time, and she'd never once told him why she kept saying it, and that was a fact too, one she kept at the back.

There were people on the road.

Not many. A few. Going the same way they were, ahead, small, in ones and twos. Walking slowly, the way people walk who've been walking a long time and are going to be walking a long time more. With staffs. Every one of them with a staff. And round their necks, on cords, something that caught the light.

They passed an old man by the *66* stone. He was sitting on it, resting, with his staff across his knees, and he looked up at thirty-six people and a goat and a dog and a Rimehound coming down the road toward him and didn't seem surprised at all.

The thing round his neck was a cord. Plain, brown, thick. With knots in it. Three knots.

"Walking the Road?" said the old man.

"Yes," said Xav. "I think so."

The old man nodded as if that were the most natural thing in the world. "Good weather for it." He looked at them, the whole long line of them, coming past. "You'll want the Lark, tonight. At Fallow. By the fifty-nine." He touched the cord at his neck. "And the first bridge the day after."

"What's at the bridge?" said Chris.

The old man looked at him for a long time. At the notebook in his hand.

"The toll," said the old man. And smiled. And didn't say anything else.

***

The Lark at Fallow was a long low inn by the *59* stone, with a thatched roof and a yard full of swallows and a sign over the door with a bird on it that might have been a lark or might have been a very optimistic pigeon. It was full of pilgrims.

That was what they were. Winter worked it out in the first ten minutes. Thirty or forty of them, in the long room and the yard, eating and drinking and mending their boots. Old and young. Every one with a staff, and every one with a cord round their neck. Some cords had one knot. Some had two, or three. One young man had none at all and kept touching his bare cord as if he were checking it was still there.

They were walking the Long Road. All of them. To the end.

"What's at the end?" said Winter.

She asked the old woman at the end of the table. She'd picked her on purpose; she'd looked round the room and found the oldest person in it, because the oldest person in a room usually had the most facts and the least patience for the ones that weren't.

The old woman was very old. Small and brown and creased like a walnut, with white hair cut short and very bright black eyes. She had a staff of dark wood worn pale at the grip. And a cord round her neck.

Six knots.

"The last bridge," said the old woman. "At the end of the road. Over the gorge." She looked at Winter with her bright eyes. "And then whatever's over it. Nobody knows. Nobody comes back."

"Then why walk it?"

The old woman laughed. A short dry bark. "You've a mouth on you."

"I like facts."

"Good." The old woman put down her cup. "I'm Sorrel. I've walked this road eleven times. Thirty years. I've a cord with six knots on it and I've never once tied the seventh." She touched it. "Sit. You'll want to know about the bridges before you get to one. Most people don't. Most people find out on the bridge, and some of them go in the river."

Winter sat.

So did Chris, beside her, with his notebook. And Ina, on her other side, without a word. And then Niko, and Maigo, and more of them, crowding in round the end of the long table in the Lark, the way they always did when somebody was about to say how a floor worked.

Across the room, she saw Ice. Sitting on a bench by the wall with Thea, a bowl of something in his hands, not eating it. Looking at the old woman. Listening.

He didn't come over. He didn't need to. He listened from where he was.

That was new, still. She'd made a fact of it, on fifteen. *He doesn't know this one either.* It still didn't sit right in her head, in the row with the others.

"Seven bridges," said Sorrel. "Between here and the end. Every river the road crosses, there's a bridge, and every bridge has a toll. You can't go round. The rivers are too deep and too fast. You pay, or you go back."

"Pay what?"

"Something true." Sorrel's bright eyes went round the table. "That's the toll. Every bridge. Something true." She held up one finger. "The first bridge: something you've never said out loud. To anybody. Ever."

Nobody said anything.

"You go up onto the bridge," said Sorrel. "On your own. To the top, the crown, the middle. And you say it. Out loud. To the river." She touched her cord. "And if it's true, and you've never said it, the bridge holds, and you walk down the other side, and the keeper ties you a knot."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the other side of the bridge isn't there." Sorrel's face didn't change. "You'll see. From the crown. The far half of it. Stone. And you put your foot on it, and it's water."

Chris was writing very fast.

"Who hears it?" said Winter.

That was the fact she wanted. The one under all the others. She'd seen it go through every face round the table the moment Sorrel said *out loud*: not *what will I say*, but *who'll hear me*.

Sorrel looked at her for a long moment. Then she smiled. All the creases in her face going at once.

"Nobody," said Sorrel. "The river. That's all. The rivers here are loud, every one of them. You stand on the crown and you could shout and nobody on the bank'd hear a word." She touched her ear. "And the keepers are deaf."

"Deaf?"

"Every one. Born that way. That's how they're chosen. There's a house at the end of the road where the deaf children go, and when they're grown the ones that want to come down and keep a bridge." She picked up her cup. "Nobody on this road has ever heard anybody else's toll. Not in the life of the road. That's the first thing everybody wants to know. You're quicker than most."

Winter didn't say anything.

She looked across the room. At the bench by the wall.

Ice had gone very still. With the bowl in his hands. His face doing nothing at all.

Beside him, Thea had put her hand on his knee.

Winter looked away. She had a fact for that too. She'd had it a long time. She kept it at the very back, behind the arrows, where she kept the ones that weren't hers to use.

"Seven bridges," said Chris. Very quietly. Looking at his notebook. "What's the seventh?"

Sorrel put her cup down.

"The last bridge," she said. "The one I've never paid." She touched the cord at her neck. The six knots. "That one doesn't ask for something you've never said."

"What does it ask for?"

Sorrel looked at him for a long time.

"What you've kept," said Sorrel.

***

Nobody looked at Ice.

That was the thing she'd remember about that night. Thirty-four people round the end of a table in an inn called the Lark, and not one of them looking at the bench by the wall. Carefully. All together. The way they'd carefully not looked for two years. The way you don't look at a man's hands when he's put them in his pockets.

She felt it go round the table. The same thought. In thirty-four heads. *What you've kept.*

She looked at Ina.

He was looking at the table. At the grain of the wood. His hands flat on it, pink still, healed from the jam, with the faint pale marks across the knuckles where the black had been. His mask was pushed up on his head. His face was very calm.

She'd made a rule, on nine. *The day it's a fact, I'll tell Ina.* She'd told the club. She'd told Ina, even, that she'd made the rule. And Ina had said *thank you*, and *it's your worst quality and your best*, and then *if it's something people are keeping for a friend, you don't pick the lock. You wait on the step until somebody opens the door.*

He'd been on the step a year and a half.

It wasn't a fact yet. She'd told herself that on fifteen, at the top of the stair, when Ice said *I don't know*. She'd told herself that on sixteen, when he said *mine* to a stray. She'd told herself that on seventeen, when he looked across a square at Rachael and said her name like he'd carried it through something worse. A theory with a lot of evidence was still a theory. That was a fact.

She didn't believe it any more. Not the bit about it still being a theory.

She looked at Ina's hands on the table and didn't say anything.

***

The first bridge was at the *57* stone. The Willow Bridge. It had willows.

A river, fast and green and loud, coming down out of the hills from the north and going under the road in a long narrow gorge between stone banks, and over it, a bridge. Old. Grey stone. One high arch, steep, so the road went up it like going up a roof and came down the other side. Willows on both banks, trailing in the water. And on the near bank, a little stone house, and a man sitting on a bench outside it.

The keeper.

He was about fifty. Brown, quiet, with a grey beard trimmed close and very clear grey eyes, and he watched them come down the road toward him the way Ottilie had watched her street. Steadily. Not missing anything. He had a bundle of brown cord on his knee and a little knife.

He didn't say anything. He couldn't hear anything to answer. He looked at them, at the cords they didn't have, and got up, and went into the stone house, and came out with thirty-six lengths of plain brown cord over his arm. And gave them out. One each. Without a word.

The goat got one. The keeper looked at the goat for a long moment and then tied a cord round its neck, very seriously, and the goat ate the end of it.

Then the keeper sat back down on his bench and looked at them.

And waited.

The river was very loud. Sorrel had been right. Winter stood on the bank and couldn't hear Chris, who was right beside her and talking. She could see his mouth. She could see him say *who's first*. She couldn't hear it.

Nobody moved.

Then Tiger did, because Tiger was first out of every door.

He walked up the steep arch of the bridge, alone, with the cord round his neck, to the crown. And stood there. In the middle. With the green river roaring under him and the willows trailing and the far half of the bridge going down in front of him to the far bank. And he said something. Winter saw his mouth move. Short. And his tail went very still behind him.

He looked at the far half of the bridge for a long moment.

Then he put his foot on it.

It held.

He walked down the far side, and the keeper, who'd been watching from the bench, got up and walked across the bridge behind him and tied a knot in Tiger's cord on the far bank. One knot. Neat. Tight.

Tiger looked at it. Then back across the river at the rest of them.

Then he grinned. And gave them a thumbs up. And sat down on the far bank with his back against a willow and his face turned away from the bridge, so whoever came next would know he wasn't looking.

***

They went one at a time.

All afternoon. Thirty-six people, one at a time, up the steep grey arch to the crown, alone. You couldn't hear any of them. You could only see.

She watched. She couldn't help it. She didn't watch their mouths; she looked away when they spoke, everybody did, it was the one thing they all agreed on without saying. But she watched the rest.

Calvin took the longest. He stood on the crown for nearly a quarter of an hour, with his mouth opening and shutting, starting and stopping, and everybody on the near bank very carefully not looking and all of them looking. And then he said something very short, and his whole body went still, and he walked down the far side without looking at the far half of the bridge first, as if he didn't care whether it was there. It was there. And Rachael, who'd gone before him, was standing at the bottom of it on the far bank with her hands in her pockets, waiting.

Hako said one word. She saw it. One. And walked down.

Thea stood on the crown with the lantern in her hand and the cloak off it, the amber light lying across the green water, and said something with her face turned up to the sky. And then looked down at the river for a long time. And then walked down, and the lantern leaned back across the river behind her, toward the near bank.

Toward Ice.

He went near the end.

Winter didn't look at his mouth. She looked at his feet. At the way he walked up the arch, which was the way he walked up every stair, and every slope, and every hill on every floor: steady, not fast, the weight forward, the way somebody walks who's walked a very long way. He stood on the crown. Alone. With the river roaring under him. The little lantern on his belt.

He stood there for a long time.

Longer than Calvin. She didn't count. She tried not to.

And she saw his hand come up. Not to his mouth. To his belt. To the little lantern. And rest on it.

And then he said something. Short. His face didn't change at all.

He walked down the far side. It held. The keeper tied his knot.

And then he did a thing she hadn't seen anybody else do. He turned round on the far bank and looked back up at the crown of the bridge. At the place he'd stood. For a long moment. The way you'd look at a door you'd just closed behind you, to make sure it was really shut.

Then he sat down beside Thea.

***

Winter went second to last.

She'd meant to go earlier. She'd stood on the bank all afternoon with the fact she was going to say ready in her head, in a row, straight, and every time it was nearly her turn she'd stepped back and let somebody else go. She didn't know why. She'd never once in her life been slow to do a thing she'd decided to do.

She went up the arch.

It was steeper than it looked. Stone, worn into a dip in the middle by a very long time of feet. She went up it with her bow on her back and the thirty-one arrows and the brown cord round her neck, and the river got louder, and louder, until it was the only thing, and then she was at the top.

The crown.

It was very quiet on the crown. That was the strange thing. All that roar, and up here it went so loud it went quiet, the way a big enough noise stops being a noise and becomes a place. She stood in the middle of it. The far half of the bridge went down in front of her to the far bank. Thirty-four people sitting on the grass over there, every one with their back half turned. Ina among them. Not looking.

She'd had a fact ready.

A good one. True. Never said. *I'm not brave. I'm angry. I've been angry since the white room and from the outside it looks like brave.* She'd been carrying it for two years. She'd been going to say it.

She opened her mouth to say it.

And something else came out.

"I trust them," said Winter.

To the river. She heard her own voice, very small, inside the roar.

"All of them. All thirty-five. Thirty-six." She was looking at the far bank. At the backs. "I've never trusted anybody. In my whole life. Not once. Not properly. Not my— Not anybody." Her voice did something she didn't like. "And I trust thirty-six people. I'd stand next to any one of them. I'd turn my back on any one of them. And it frightens me more than anything that's ever happened up here. More than the Moth. More than the Grey."

She stopped.

The river roared.

She hadn't known that was the fact. She'd thought it was the angry one. She'd been carrying the angry one around for two years, polishing it. And the bridge had asked for something true she'd never said, and her mouth had gone straight past it to this one, which she hadn't known she was carrying at all.

She looked at the far half of the bridge.

She put her foot on it.

Stone.

She walked down. The keeper came across behind her and tied the knot. He did it very carefully, with his clear grey eyes on her face, and when he'd finished he patted the cord, once, where it lay against her collarbone. The way you'd pat a horse.

He couldn't have heard. Nobody could.

She went and sat down on the grass. Beside Ina.

He didn't look at her. He was looking at the willows. His hands in his lap. After a while, without turning his head, he moved his hand, a little, on the grass between them, so it was next to hers. Not touching. Next to.

She didn't move hers away.

***

Chris was last.

He always was, at things like this. He watched everybody else do it first. He wrote it down. He made a column. And then, when he had all the facts, he went.

She watched him go up the arch. Notebook in his coat. Brown cord round his neck. His left arm, the one the hound had had on fifteen, swinging a bit stiff still.

He stood on the crown.

And she broke the rule.

She didn't mean to. She looked at his mouth. She'd been looking at everybody's feet all afternoon, and backs, and hands, and never once at a mouth, and she looked at Chris's. Because she'd known him two years. Because she'd sat across a fire from him on nine and helped him start a page with *THE ONES BEFORE* at the top. Because she'd watched him write *HOW?* in a grey notebook from Quill's on floor 1 and carry it to the bottom of his pack and leave it there, for eight hundred days, with lines left empty at the bottom of the page, and then two, and then one.

She couldn't hear. She couldn't read lips. She'd never been able to.

She saw him say it.

Not long. Not a speech. Not a list or a column. Six words, maybe. Seven. Very plainly. The way you'd say the answer to a sum you'd done in your head so many times you didn't need to work it out any more.

And then his face went white.

Not frightened. She knew his frightened face. This was the other thing. The face of a man who'd said something out loud for the first time and heard how it sounded.

He stood there for a long time.

Then he looked at the far half of the bridge, and it was stone, and he walked down it. And the keeper tied his knot. And he came and sat down on the grass on her other side, not Ina's side, and didn't say anything at all.

She didn't say anything either.

After a long time, Chris said, very quietly, looking at the river:

"I've never said it out loud before."

"I know you haven't," said Winter.

He turned his head and looked at her.

She looked at the willows.

"You looked," said Chris.

"I can't read lips."

"I know you can't." He was quiet. "You looked anyway."

"Yeah," said Winter.

He didn't say anything for a while. Then he took the notebook out of his coat. Not the fifteenth, or whichever he was on now. He went into his pack, the way she'd seen him do on the crown of the Spindle on floor 6, down past the food and the spare shirt to the very bottom, and took out the first one. The grey one. Wrapped in oilcloth. From Quill's.

He unwrapped it. He opened it to the page.

*HOW?*

She could see it, sideways, from where she sat. His capitals. The list going down the page. Twenty-two lines, she knew, though she'd never counted them over his shoulder. *Why noon?* near the top. And near the bottom, smaller, newer: *Day 525. "I came down once. To here." Gull Island.* And under that: *Day 640. The window said she'd died. She hadn't. Somebody got something wrong. Somebody who wasn't there.*

And under that, one line. Empty.

Chris looked at it for a long time.

He didn't write anything.

He closed the notebook. He wrapped it back up in its oilcloth. He put it back at the bottom of his pack.

"Not yet," said Chris.

Winter didn't say anything.

She thought: *Fact. He knows. He's known for a long time. He said it out loud to a river today for the first time. And he still won't write it down.*

She thought: *Fact. Neither will I.*

She looked across the grass at the bench under the willows on the far bank, where Ice was sitting beside Thea with his hand resting on the little lantern on his belt and his face turned up to the sky, and the light in the lantern humming something she couldn't hear over the river. He wasn't looking at them. He hadn't looked at the bridge once since he came down.

Fact: they had six more bridges.

Fact: the last one wanted what you'd kept.

Fact: she trusted thirty-six people, and it was the most frightening thing she'd ever said, and she'd said it to a river, and the river had let her across.

She didn't put that one in the row with the others. She kept it somewhere else. Somewhere nearer the front.
