In two nights on the north tree, Winter threw a hundred and six nut shells at bough-folk children, and hit every one.

It wasn't hard. They were very quick, but they always came the same way: along the big branch from the bridge, low, in a giggling crowd, toward the small brown shape sitting against the trunk with leaves on its head. And she was sitting a little way back on the branch, with her bow across her knees and a bag of nut shells in her lap, and she had nothing else to do.

*Tok.* A shell off the top of a moss-haired head. The child stopped, and looked round, and saw her, and her eyes, one blue and one pink, and her horns. And went back along the branch a good deal faster than it had come.

The ones who came back a second time got two.

She didn't mind sitting up. She'd sat up nine hours under an overhang in the sleet on floor 5 for Ina, and this was a warm branch in summer with a bag of nuts. What she minded was the other thing. The thing that happened every night, round about midnight, that nobody else on the branch could see except her, because nobody else was watching Maigo's face.

The sawyers came.

You heard them first. Everyone in Highcrook heard them. Far down, in the dark, at the foot of the north tree: *rrrrrrr*. Stop. *Rrrrrrr*. And Maigo, sitting rooted against the trunk with his eyes shut behind his sunglasses and his foot gone into the bark, didn't wake up. He didn't move at all. But every time the sawing went, his face did.

A twitch. At the corner of his mouth. Like somebody asleep having a bad dream. *Rrrrrrr.* A twitch. *Rrrrrrr.* And again. All night. Every stroke.

She'd watched it for two nights, from four metres away, with her bow across her knees, and the sawyers fifty metres down in the pitch black where she couldn't see anything to shoot at. And she'd thrown nut shells at children instead. Because there was nothing else to throw anything at.

She didn't like that. She didn't like it at all.

***

He woke up on the third day, when Ice came with soup. And in the evening, in the round woven house the five of them shared on the north side of the drum platform, he told them.

He told it badly. He kept stopping. He'd never been a good teller of things, Maigo, even in summer, even with his leaves out. He sat on the floor with his back to the wall and his knees up and his root-foot very carefully tucked under him, as if it was sore, and he said *rings*, and *fire*, and *the leaves closed*, and *cones*, and *a thousand years*, and *lights*. And then *counting*. And then he stopped for a long time and said *the Youngest*. And then he said what it wanted.

Nobody said anything.

Chris had his book open on his knee and his pencil had stopped in the middle of a line. Niko had his red book shut in his lap with both hands flat on it. Ina was sitting by the round window with his mask hanging round his neck on its cord, and he'd gone very still. Winter watched him go still.

"Fire," said Chris, eventually.

"At its foot," said Maigo. "Like the last time. That's all. It's not—it doesn't want *healing*. It's never wanted healing. Linden said it sounds like asking. It's been asking for that." He pressed the heels of his hands into the sunglasses. "Three hundred years. And every bough-person who ever listened to it thought it was saying *help*."

"It *is* saying help," said Niko quietly.

"Not the kind they've got."

Chris wrote something. Then he looked up, and across the room, at the only one of them who hadn't moved.

"Ina," he said.

"I heard," said Ina.

He didn't say anything else. He sat by the window with the foxfire coming in blue over one side of his face, and the white hair going lavender at the ends, and the single purple horn, and looked out at the dark. His hand had come up, without him noticing, and closed round the cord of the mask at his throat.

Winter knew that hand. She'd seen it on floor 5, on the edge of an island, the evening the sky went the colour of a bruise.

"Burr's going to say no," said Winter.

"Burr," said Ina, still looking out of the window, "is going to say a great deal more than no."

***

Burr said a great deal more than no.

He said it on the drum platform the next morning, in front of all of them, with his arms folded and his big eyes gone very wide and very bright and not blinking. He said it fast and low and rough, the way he said everything, and he didn't stop for a long time.

"*Fire*," said Burr. "*Fire*. Under the seven. Under Highcrook. Do you know—" He stopped. He unfolded his arms and spread them, both hands, the whole one and the two fingers and a thumb, out at the platforms, the bridges, the woven houses hanging in their hundreds in the green light, the children on the railings, the old ones in the doorways. "Do you know what this is made of? All of it? Every house. Every bridge. Every rope. Every *child* in it is asleep on a floor made of *wood*, in a house made of *wood*, hanging off a *tree*." He was breathing hard. "One spark. One. And there's no Highcrook by dark. There's no *seven*. That's what fire is, up here. That's the end of everything. We tell it to our children before we tell them their own names."

"It would be in the Down," said Chris. "At the foot. Not up here. Fifty metres—"

"The *Down*," said Burr, "is under the *seven*."

"I know. I know. I'm just saying—"

"*No*," said Burr.

He looked at Maigo. And his face changed. Not much. It went from furious to something that was nearly as bad, which was sorry.

"I'm sorry, little tree," said Burr. "I am. I believe you. If Linden says you heard him, you heard him. If he's asking, he's asking." He shook his head. "He can ask for another three hundred years. There's no fire under the seven. Not while I'm the ropes."

He turned and went back to his drum.

Maigo stood on the platform with his hands in his hoodie pocket and didn't say anything at all. Winter looked at him. Then she looked at Ina.

Ina was looking at Burr's back. Not angry. Thinking.

***

That night, she stopped throwing nut shells.

She'd decided it in the afternoon. She'd sat on the edge of the drum platform with her legs over the drop and looked down through the gap in the lower branches at the dark mouth of the cave at the foot of the north tree, for a long time, and worked it out. Fifty metres, near enough. Straight down. Through a gap in the branches about as wide as a door. No wind, under the leaves. Never any wind. Just the drop.

It was a long shot. It wasn't an impossible one.

It only needed light.

"Tansy," she said, in the evening.

Burr's rope-girl was sitting on a handle of the drum with her legs swinging. She looked round, and saw who it was, and saw Winter's eyes, and her horns, and went slightly warier, the way the children all did now.

"I need light at the bottom," said Winter. "At the foot of the north tree. Tonight. Not fire. Yours." She pointed at the foxfire jar hanging off the drum's frame, the cold blue-green lump of rotten wood in it, glowing. "As many of those as you've got. On ropes. Down to the moss. Can you do that?"

Tansy looked at her for a long time with her big shining eyes.

"Why?" she said.

"Because my friend's face moves every time they saw," said Winter. "And I'm sick of it."

Tansy looked at Maigo, at the end of the branch, sitting against the trunk with Ina beside him. Not rooted, tonight. Just sitting. With his hand flat on the bark.

She got off the drum handle.

"Burr," she shouted, across the platform. "I'm borrowing jars."

***

They came about midnight. They always did.

She was lying on her stomach on the lowest branch of the north tree she could get to, where it went out sideways from the trunk under the drum platform, broad as a road. Bow in her hands. Arrows in a row on the bark beside her, where she could get them without looking. Chris was beside her with his shield, which wouldn't do anything at fifty metres but which he'd brought anyway, because he was Chris. Niko on her other side. Up on the branch behind them, Maigo, sitting against the trunk with his hand on the bark. Ina next to him.

Below, the jars.

Tansy had found thirty. She'd let them down on ropes from the platforms and the branches, one after another, slowly, all round the foot of the north tree, and now they lay there on the moss and among the roots, in the dark, fifty metres down, glowing. Small and cold and blue-green. Like a ring of drowned stars. You could see the foot of the trunk by them now, faintly, for the first time. The roots like walls. The Grow round them, ferns and briar, already lifting and leaning in the dark. And the cave.

It was bigger than it had looked by day. Much bigger. A great black mouth in the side of the north tree, as high as a house, going back into the wood. The jars by its edge lit the inside of the lip, pale and fresh and splintered, where it had been sawn.

And far off, in the dark, north: *rrrrrrr*.

"Here they come," said Chris, very quietly.

She didn't answer. She was breathing. In, and out, slow, the way she did before anything long. Every archer had something. Hers was to stop thinking about the shot and think about the string. How it felt on her fingers. Where her elbow was. Nothing else.

*Rrrrrrr.* Closer. Stop. Closer.

The first one came into the light.

***

It came out of the ferns on the far side of the ring, feelers first, the way they did. Glossy. Black and brown, gone blue-green in the foxfire. It stopped at the edge of the jars and its feelers went round, slowly, tasting the air. Then it came on. Across the moss, through the light, toward the cave.

She shot it.

From fifty metres up, straight down, through the gap in the branches. The arrow went down into the dark and she lost it for a moment and then saw it hit, right in the soft grey joint behind the head, where Ice had said. Where they all knew now.

*Hoarfrost. What you hit stays where it is.*

The frost went out from the arrowhead across its back, white, fast, like breath on cold glass. It stopped. Mid-step. Frozen to the moss with one leg still raised, its feelers out stiff in front of it like two white sticks.

"Oh," said Niko, softly. "*Oh*, Winter."

"That's one," said Winter. She was already nocking the next.

They kept coming. That was what they did. They didn't stop because the one in front had stopped. They came round it, and past it, into the light, and toward the cave, and she shot them. One. And another. Between strokes of her own breath. The frost going out from each one white across the dark, and each one stopping where it was, so that after a while the foot of the north tree had a ring of white shapes round it, standing in the blue light among the jars, quite still, like statues somebody had left out in the snow.

Seven. Eight.

She didn't kill them. She couldn't, at fifty metres, through that much shell, with one arrow each. But she didn't need to. *What you hit stays where it is.* And they stayed. And not one of them reached the cave.

Behind her, very softly, Maigo let out a breath.

She heard it. She didn't turn round. But she heard it, and she knew what it was. It was the breath of somebody who'd been waiting for the next stroke of a saw, and it hadn't come.

***

And then the old one came.

They felt it before they saw it. Everybody did. A sound out of the dark, north, that wasn't *rrrrrrr*. It was lower. Slower. Rougher. A sound like a saw so old and so worn its teeth had gone round. *Rrrrrrrrrrrr.* And the ferns at the edge of the light didn't part for it. They went down. Flat. All at once, in a long line, like grass under a cart.

"Burr told me about this one," said Chris. His voice had gone very calm, which meant it wasn't. "He said it's been coming since before he was born. They call it Greyjaw."

It came into the light.

It was twice the size of the others. More. Not a pony. A cart horse. Its shell wasn't glossy any more. It had gone grey with age, dull, scarred, chipped, like an old pot, and there was moss growing on its back. Actual moss. Its feelers were broken off short, both of them, stumps. And its jaws.

Its jaws were enormous. And worn. The saw-teeth along the inside edges had been ground down over who knew how many years of wood until they weren't teeth at all. They were a long, smooth, wavy edge. Like an old bread knife.

It didn't stop at the jars. It didn't taste the air. It went straight across the light, between the frozen shapes of the others, not looking at them, straight at the mouth of the cave. Because it knew the way. It had been going that way every night for longer than Burr had been alive.

She shot it.

The arrow hit the grey back and the frost went out across it. And it didn't stop. It slowed. The frost went white over the moss on its shell and over its back and down its legs, and it went on, slowly, through it, cracking it, like something walking through a frozen pond. Into the mouth of the cave.

Behind her, Maigo made a sound.

She shot it again. Higher. In the back of the head. The frost went over its head and it slowed again, and went on. Its great worn jaws opened. Wide. She saw them, pale in the jar-light, at the back of the cave, against the fresh wood. Opening.

*Rrrrrrrrrrrr.*

Maigo cried out.

Not loud. A short sharp sound, like somebody who'd put their hand on a stove. She heard him hit the bark behind her with his back, and Ina say his name.

She stopped thinking about the string.

***

She didn't shoot it again in the back. That was what she'd been doing, and it didn't work. It was too big. The frost went over it and it walked through it like a frozen pond, because there was too much of it to stop.

*What you hit stays where it is.*

So she'd hit the bit that mattered.

She waited. Two breaths. Three. Down at the back of the cave, the great jaws went side to side, slowly, *rrrrrrrrrrr*, and on the branch behind her Maigo made the sound again, smaller, through his teeth. And on the next stroke, as the jaws came round to the left, wide, for the next bite of the wood, she saw them. Both of them. Open. Sunk into the pale fresh wood at the back of the cave, a hand's width deep. Where the saw went in.

She shot the wood.

Not the sawyer. The north tree. The pale splintered wood at the back of the cave, right between the old worn jaws, where they were biting.

The frost went out from the arrowhead into the wood. Into the cut. Into the jaws, from the inside. White, fast, round both of them where they were sunk in, like ice round a stick in a pond.

And the jaws stayed where they were.

Greyjaw heaved. She saw it. The whole great grey back of it bunched and pulled. And its jaws didn't move. They were frozen into the north tree. In the cut they'd made. Stuck. It pulled again, and its legs scrabbled on the floor of the cave, and its head didn't come back an inch, because the frost had it, and the frost was in the wood, and the wood was the north tree, and nothing on this floor was going to move the north tree.

She lowered the bow.

Behind her, Maigo had gone quiet.

"Chris," said Winter.

"Yeah," said Chris. He was already standing up. "Yeah. I know."

***

Burr let the rope down himself.

He didn't say anything about it. He'd come down to the lowest branch at the first arrow, and he'd been crouched at the edge of it with his arms folded, watching the white shapes come up one after another in the jar-light, and saying nothing. And when Chris stood up with his shield on his arm, Burr looked at him. Then at the cave. Then he got up, and went to the nearest rope post, and threw a coil off it, and let it go.

It went down into the dark.

"You've got till the jars go dim," said Burr. "Then I pull, whoever's on it." He looked at Chris. "It's night, walker. The Down's awake."

"I know," said Chris.

"Then go," said Burr.

Chris went down first. Niko after him. Winter lay on her stomach on the branch with an arrow on the string and watched them go down the rope into the blue light, small, getting smaller. She could hear the Grow from here. The ticking, coming up, the way it did at night, like rain starting. Down there, at the edge of the jar-light, the ferns were lifting.

They hit the moss. They ran. Across the ring, between the frozen white shapes of the others, to the cave.

She watched them go in.

She couldn't see much. The jars at the lip of the cave lit the edges, and the rest was dark. She saw Chris go in with his shield up and stop. She saw him go still. Completely still, the way he went still sometimes, in the middle of things, for no reason anybody could see from outside, with his head a little on one side.

*Breakpoint. Everything stops. Look at all of it.*

She'd seen him do it on floor 1, under a scream. She'd heard him say what it was like. *Like a pause button on the whole world. And I can walk round it and look.*

Down in the cave, Chris looked. A long time, for him. Two seconds. Three.

Then he said something. She couldn't hear what. She saw Niko move. Two steps right, and down, low, under the frozen jaws, under the grey head, to the side of it where the old shell met the neck. And put his sword in. Up. Under the plate. Into the place Chris had found, that nobody had found in all the years it had been coming to the north tree, because nobody had ever made it stand still long enough to look.

Greyjaw shuddered. All of it. Its legs went out sideways on the floor of the cave, and its back sagged, and it hung there by its frozen jaws from the wood it had been sawing since before Burr was born.

And was dead.

"*UP*," shouted Burr. "*UP. NOW*."

The ferns were at the edge of the cave. She saw them. They'd come in from all round the ring in the time Chris had been looking, and they were waist high now, round the frozen shapes, round the jars, and closing. Chris and Niko came out of the cave at a run. Through them. Niko's foot caught in something and Chris hauled him up by the collar without stopping, and they hit the rope, and Burr's people on the drum hauled, all six of them, and they came up out of the blue light into the dark very fast, swinging.

The ferns closed over the place where they'd been standing.

Winter let out her breath. She hadn't known she was holding it.

***

In the morning the Grow had gone over all of it. The jars were under it, and the frozen white shapes of the sawyers were under it, mounds, already green. All except the cave. The cave was too deep in the tree for the Grow to get into. And at the back of it, in the pale fresh wood, hanging by its jaws, there was a great grey shape that wasn't going anywhere.

Burr looked at it from the drum platform for a long time.

"Well," he said.

That was all he said. Then he turned round and looked at Winter. He looked at her for a long moment with his big eyes, and she looked back with hers, one blue and one pink, the way she looked at everybody.

"Tansy," said Burr. "Give the horned one her jars back. She's to have them whenever she wants."

Tansy grinned at her from the drum. Winter didn't smile back. She nodded. It was the same thing, from her.

And on the branch by the trunk, with his hand on the bark, Maigo said, to nobody in particular:

"It's quiet."

***

The club met that night.

Not all thirteen. Most. In the round house on the far side of Highcrook that Higu had claimed for her maps, because it was the biggest and furthest from anywhere, and the bough-folk didn't go out on the far bridges at night. They sat in a ring on the floor in the foxfire with Chris's book open in the middle, at the second page. *THE ONES BEFORE.* In Chris's handwriting, underlined twice, and under it, three weeks of everything they knew.

There wasn't much.

*Thirty. 312 years ago. Lights at their shoulders. None had a fox. Came down the falls in autumn. Stayed a winter. Laid the fire. Went up in spring. None came down.*

*Their head counted them out loud every morning on the stair.*

*One laughed. Like him. Went up first. Talked to everyone. Sat on his own every evening and looked up.*

That was floor 8. That was Sefa. That was all of it.

"Floor 9," said Chris. He turned the page. "Two things. Maigo's, and Winter's."

He read Maigo's. The rings. The fire. The cone, and the Youngest. He read it slowly and carefully, the way Maigo had told it, and when he got to *counting every morning*, Hako made a small sound and Kei put a hand on his knee.

"They were here," said Thea, quietly. "At the foot of the north tree."

"Yeah."

"They made a fire. And a tree came up." Thea was looking at the page. "That's—"

"That's a thing they left," said Hako. "On purpose or not. They left a tree."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"And Winter's," said Chris.

She'd asked Burr that afternoon. On the drum platform, after the cave, while he was still being nearly polite to her. She'd asked him one question, the one that had been bothering her since the first night, since a basket came up out of the dark and a small grey man with two fingers caught the rim and moved his lips.

"Why do you count?" she'd said.

Burr had looked at her as if she'd asked why he breathed.

"Walkers count," he'd said. "Everybody knows that. When walkers come up a rope, you count them. That's what they do. They count their own. We learned it from them." He'd shrugged. "Long time ago. Before my grandmother's grandmother. *Nobody left down.* That's a walker saying. We just say it."

She told them. In Burr's words, as near as she could.

The round house went very quiet.

"*Nobody left down*," said Drew, eventually. Very low. "That's—"

"Somebody said that," said Winter. "Three hundred years ago. At the bottom of a rope. Every morning. Enough times that a whole town learned it, and kept it, and taught it to their children for three hundred years after he'd gone." She looked at the page. "Their head. The one who counted. He said *nobody left down* every morning, and the bough-folk thought it was just a thing walkers said."

"Like Sefa," said Thea. "*As if he was afraid one would be gone.*"

"Yeah."

Xav, at the back, against the wall, hadn't said anything all night. He said something now. Just one word. Under his breath.

"Declan," said Xav.

Nobody asked him what he meant. Nobody had to. They'd all heard Ice in the basket, on the first night, saying it back to Burr. *Nobody left down.* As if he knew the words. As if he'd heard them before.

He probably had, thought Winter. He'd probably heard Burr say it in exactly that voice ten years ago, on this platform, and said it back then too.

She didn't say that. Nobody did.

"So what happened to them?" said Drew. "After."

"I don't know," said Chris. He shut the book. "That's still the question. We know they came here. We know they counted. We know they made a fire and something grew. We know they went up." He put his hand flat on the cover. "We don't know anything after that. Not one thing."

"Is it bad?" said Drew.

He'd asked it on floor 5, about the first page. *Is it bad?* And Higu had said *I think it's sad*.

Nobody answered him this time.

***

Winter waited until the end. Until they'd said everything about the ones before that there was to say, which wasn't much, and Chris had written it, and people were starting to get up.

"I've got something," she said. "Not about them."

They stopped.

She didn't like talking in front of people. She did it anyway, when it mattered. She'd done it on floor 2, in a trapper's hall, to make Deston tell Gabe. She sat with her arms folded and her back very straight, and looked round the ring of them in the foxfire.

"Maigo and Ina," she said. "My party. They don't know."

Nobody said anything.

"Maigo sat in a tree for three days," said Winter. "He heard a thousand years. He found out about the ones before before any of us did. He's going to keep finding things out, on this floor, because he's a tree, and trees remember. And Ina reads my face like a book." She stopped. "And neither of them knows there's anything to know. They don't even know there's a *page*. They're walking round in a room where everybody else has already heard the joke."

"Winter," said Thea gently.

"I know. I know the rule. It stays with us. Till we can ask him. I voted for it." Winter's arms tightened. "I'm not going to tell them. Not what. I swore. I'm not breaking it." She looked at Thea. "But I'm going to tell Ina there's something. That's all. That there's a thing some of us know that he doesn't, and it's not mine to tell, and I'll tell him the day I can." She looked round at all of them. "Because that's what I'd want. If it was me. I'd want to know that much. I'd want to know there was a door, even if nobody'd give me the key."

Nobody spoke for a while.

"I'm telling you first," said Winter. "So nobody in this room finds out after."

Thea looked at her for a long time. Then she nodded.

"Okay," said Thea.

"Okay," said Chris.

Drew rubbed his face. "Okay. Fine. Okay." He looked at Hako. "Is that okay?"

"That's fair," said Hako. "That's actually really fair."

And then Winter looked at the back of the room. At Xav, against the wall, with his arms on his knees. He was looking at the floor.

She waited.

"Then Gabe," said Winter. "If I'm telling Ina. By my own rule. Gabe should know there's a thing. Before Ina does. He's the one we swore for."

Xav didn't look up.

"Gabe already knows there's a thing," said Xav.

The round house went very still.

"He's known since floor 8," said Xav, to the floor. "Since the jetty. Maybe before. He watches Declan look at things. He watches us not look at each other." He was quiet for a moment. "He hasn't asked. Not me. Not anybody."

"Why not?" said Winter.

Xav looked up then. At her. And his face did something she'd never seen it do.

"Because he's waiting for Declan to tell him," said Xav. "That's why. He's not going to ask any of us. Not ever. He's going to wait for Dec." He looked down again. "And I swore. On floor 6. The day it's a fact, from me, first. So I'm waiting too."

Nobody said anything after that. There wasn't anything to say.

***

She found Ina on the long bridge to the Youngest, very late, alone.

He was sitting halfway along it, where it sagged lowest, with his legs through the rope rail and his arms folded on the top rope, looking at the young tree in the dark. It wasn't lit. There was no town in it. It just stood there on its own a little way off from the seven, black against the faint blue of the foxfire behind them, with its crown that didn't quite reach.

She sat down next to him. Legs through the rail. The bridge swung, a little, and settled.

"Can't sleep?" she said.

"I've been thinking about Burr," said Ina. "And about fire." He didn't look at her. "And about a tree that's been asking for one thing for three hundred years, and everybody thought it meant something else."

"You're going to do it," said Winter.

"I'm going to *ask*," said Ina. "It's not mine to do. It's his town." He was quiet for a moment. "But I'm going to ask very well."

She almost smiled.

They sat there. Below them, very far, the Down ticked. North of them, in the dark, the Youngest stood and didn't do anything.

"Ina," she said.

"Mm."

"There's a thing."

He turned his head and looked at her.

She'd thought about how to say it all the way across Highcrook. She'd thought about it in the round house and on the bridges and on the long dark walk out along this one. In the end she just said it, the way she said everything, which was the only way she knew.

"Some of us know something," said Winter. "About Ice. About all of it. Not all of us. Some. It's not mine to tell you, and I'm not going to. I swore." She looked at him. "But I'm telling you there's a thing. So you know. So you're not the last one standing in the room when everyone else already knows what's in it." She stopped. "That's all I've got. I'm sorry it's not more. I'll tell you the day I can. The day. Not the day after."

Ina didn't say anything for a long time.

He looked at her. In the dark, on the bridge, with his teal eyes and his horn and his mask hanging at his throat. She'd thought he might be angry. He had every right to be. She'd have been. She'd have been furious.

He wasn't. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her on floor 5, at the bottom of a ladder in the sleet, at dawn, coming down after a night on the Kitecliff with a child's head on his knee all night, and finding her still there.

"Thank you," said Ina.

"That's it?"

"That's it." He turned back to the Youngest. "I suspected there was something. I'm not a fool. I've watched thirteen people not look at each other for three months." He was quiet. "I didn't know you'd noticed I'd noticed."

"I notice everything."

"I know. It's your worst quality." He almost smiled. "And your best."

"Aren't you going to ask?"

"No," said Ina.

She looked at him.

"If it's something people are keeping for a friend," said Ina, "you don't pick the lock. You wait on the step until somebody opens the door." He leaned his chin on his folded arms on the rope. "I'll wait. Thank you for telling me there's a step."

She sat there next to him on the bridge in the dark for a long time after that. Neither of them said anything else. She didn't need to. It was the quietest she'd felt in months.

Out in the dark, beyond the end of the bridge, the Youngest stood on its own. Three hundred years old. Come up out of a fire that somebody had lit at the foot of a tree one night, and left, and never come back to see.
