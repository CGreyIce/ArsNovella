Thea had been reading the words under the words for two hundred and seventy-six days, and she'd learned that most of them were boring.

That was the secret nobody believed. They thought Source was like reading minds. It wasn't. It was like reading the back of a form. Under every window there was another line, grey, small, and most of the time the grey line said things like *base value 12, modified* or *cooldown shared with* or *see also*. She'd read thousands of them. She'd learned the tower's grammar the way she'd once learned to read code: the boring parts first, until she could see where the interesting parts were by the shape of the gaps round them.

There'd been four interesting ones, in two hundred and seventy-six days. Four times the grey line under a window had said something that wasn't boring.

*Held: Tier trial required*, on floor 1, beside a blank name at the top of the rankings. *Hidden class: Paladin*, on floor 3, under the word *Knight*. A black bar, on floor 6, where something had been crossed out so she couldn't read it. And on the fifth wall, five days ago, with her body locked and her nose bleeding, under a word she'd been waiting a hundred and twenty days to read:

*One record does not match.*

She'd read it every morning since. Every dawn, on the wall, frozen, while the Steward counted. *Irregular*, in front of every face, and under it, grey, the same line. *One record does not match.* She'd read it until it didn't hurt to read any more. Until her nose stopped bleeding. Until she could read it, and then read it *again*, a second layer, under the grey.

There wasn't a second layer. She'd looked. There was the word, and the line under it, and then nothing. Like the bottom of a well.

*One record does not match.*

On the morning Drew stood in the snow with nothing in his hands and thirty-five people said a name, she'd been reading it again. Out of habit. And when they said *Declan* and *Ice* together, both at once, from all along the wall, she'd seen the grey line under *Irregular* do something it had never done.

It had flickered.

Just once. Like a candle when a door opens. The way her own ball of light had flickered on floor 6, when she'd asked it a question it didn't want to answer.

And then the Steward had closed its book.

***

"Babe," said Xola. "You're doing the face."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing *the* face. The reading face. You've been doing it at that wall for twenty minutes." Xola sat down next to her on the step of the barracks, in the cold sun, with Squiddles riding on top of her head like a purple hat. "There's nothing written on that wall. It's a wall. It's just a normal wall. Squiddles checked."

Thea looked at the wall. Xola was right. It was the side of the long hall opposite, grey stone, with icicles on the eaves.

"Sorry," she said.

"Don't be sorry. Be *here*." Xola leaned on her. Shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd leaned on Thea for years. "Talk to me. You haven't talked to me in five days. You've talked to the wall."

Thea didn't say anything for a while.

"It flickered," she said.

"What did?"

"The line. Under *Irregular*. When everybody said his name." Thea pressed her hands together between her knees. "The first morning, I could barely read it. Now I can read it like a sign. And it's never changed. It's always the same. *One record does not match.* And then when everybody said *Declan*, and *Ice*, it flickered. Like it didn't know what to do with that."

"Okay," said Xola slowly. "And that's—good? Bad?"

"I don't know." Thea looked at her hands. "I don't know what anything means, Xo. I've got a theory. I've had it since floor 6. I've had a *feeling* since the white room. And now I've got a fact, finally, a real one, out of the tower's own mouth. And it says exactly what the theory says. And I still don't *know*." She swallowed. "And he said *not yet*. And I said okay. On a bridge. And I meant it."

Xola didn't say anything. She leaned harder.

"And it's going to *reconcile* him," said Thea. Very quietly. "At the dark of the moon. At the Gate. That's what it said. And I don't know what that means either. Except I do."

***

Xola took her up to the barracks roof, after that, because that was where people went on floor 10 when they wanted to say things. They sat against the chimney in the warmth of the Breath, with the city going down the pass below them and the fifth wall at the top, and the Gate past it, somewhere, up the throat of rock on the high side where none of them had been.

"I'm going to say something," said Xola. "And you're going to let me."

Thea looked at her.

"You're in love with him," said Xola.

Thea didn't say anything.

"Babe. I'm not asking. I'm *telling*. I've known since floor 6. I knew in that cistern, with your head in Gigi's lap and your shoulder open, when you woke up at three in the morning and the first thing you said was *did he get out*." Xola's voice had gone gentle, which on Xola was rare enough to be frightening. "Not *am I okay*. Not *where's Gigi*. *Did he get out.*"

"I don't remember that."

"I do." Xola pulled her knees up. Squiddles slid down onto her shoulder and settled there, unbothered. "And then floor 7. The bridge. And then floor 8, every single morning, you sitting at the back of the room with the lantern where he can see it. And floor 9." She was quiet. "You walked straight at him through the Down in the dark with the lantern leaning so hard it was lying down. Like you'd done it before."

Thea didn't say anything.

"So I'm telling you," said Xola. "Because somebody should. And because I'm your best friend, and I introduced you, actually, *I* did that, I put you in that call, it was the best thing I ever did—"

"*Second* best," said Thea.

Xola stopped.

"You said," said Thea. "On Earth. *Best thing I ever did was bring him into that call. Second best was you.*" She almost smiled.

"And you always said it passed," said Xola. "Him liking you. You always said that. *It passed.*" She looked at Thea sideways. "For him, Thea. You never once said it passed for you."

The wind came down the pass from the high side, over the roof, and didn't touch them in the lee of the chimney.

"It didn't," said Thea.

She hadn't said it out loud. Ever. Not to Xola, not to herself. She heard it come out of her in the cold and sat there with it.

"It didn't pass," she said. "It went somewhere. I was busy. I had exams. I meant to call more. And then the calls thinned out, and I told myself that was fine, that was just how it went. And then a white room. And a blank name at the top of the rankings." She pressed her hands together. "And I knew. On the first day. Before anybody. Not who it was. Just that whoever it was had already lost something here. And I couldn't stop thinking about him. And I thought it was the mystery. For about a hundred days. I really did think that."

"Babe," said Xola.

"And then on six he stopped," said Thea. "With his hand out. A few centimetres from my shoulder. Shaking. With a fresh scar on it. And he didn't touch me. And I thought—" She stopped. "I thought: *oh. It's not the mystery.*"

Xola put her arm round her.

They sat there.

"I'm not going to tell him," said Thea, after a while.

"I know."

"Not now. Not with the Gate. Not with *reconcile*. He's got enough." She leaned her head on Xola's shoulder, next to Squiddles. "He's got a whole tower looking at him. He doesn't need me looking at him too."

"Babe," said Xola. "You've been looking at him for two hundred and seventy days. He knows."

Thea didn't answer that. She didn't have an answer.

***

Ice told them about the Gate that evening.

Higu asked. Of course she did. They were all in the barracks, all thirty-six, after supper, with the stove going and the shutters closed and the wind outside, and Higu got up with the rock in her hands, because it was her evening for it, and put it on the long table, and said:

"Seven days to the dark of the moon. The Steward said the Gate." She looked down the table. "Ice. You say where. Tell us what's there."

Thea watched him. She always did. He was sitting at the far end with Gabe on his right and Deston on his left, which was where they'd sat every night since the Long Night, and he looked at Higu for a long time.

"It's a gate," he said. "At the top of the pass. Past the fifth wall. About a day up, through the high side." He was quiet. "Two doors. Big. As tall as the walls. Iron, or something like iron. Grey. And in front of the doors there's a thing."

"A thing," said Higu.

"The Gatekeeper." He looked at the table. "It's—you'll see it. It's big. It doesn't fight like the others. It doesn't really fight at all, at first. It just stands in front of the doors and lets you come." He stopped. "It only opens for a group."

"A group," said Chris. Writing.

"All of you. Together. It counts you through. Like the Steward. You go through together, holding on to each other, and it lets you." He paused. "If anybody's left on this side. Anybody. On their own. The doors shut. On all of you. And you have to come back down and start again."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"All or none," said Drew, quietly, from halfway down the table. He didn't have the rock. Higu had it. His hands were on the table in front of him, open.

"All or none," said Ice.

"That's not so bad," said Calvin. "We do that anyway. That's literally our whole thing. Nobody left down."

"Yeah," said Ice.

He didn't say anything else. And Thea, at the other end of the table, watched him not say it, and felt the room feel it too. One by one. The way the wall had felt the Steward stop at thirty-five, every morning, and wait, and say thirty-six like a hammer.

"It's going to try to make us leave one," said Thea.

Everybody looked at her.

She hadn't meant to say it. She said it anyway. She was looking at Ice, down the length of the table, past thirty-four faces, and he was looking back.

"*The count will be reconciled*," said Thea. "*At the Gate.* It counts us through. It only opens if we're all there. And one record doesn't match." She swallowed. "So it's going to try to make the count match. And there's only one way to make thirty-six records match thirty-six people when one of them's wrong."

The barracks was absolutely silent. The stove ticked.

"You take the wrong one out," said Thea.

Nobody moved.

At the end of the table, Ice was very still. He didn't look away from her. He didn't say *yes*. He didn't say *no*. He sat there with Gabe's shoulder against his on one side and Deston's on the other, and looked at her, and she saw in his face that she was right, and that he'd known since the first morning on the wall, and that he hadn't been going to say it.

"Okay," said Gabe.

He said it quite loudly. Everybody looked at him.

"Okay," said Gabe again. "So it tries to leave him." He put his hand flat on the table. "Then the doors shut. On all of us. Because we're not going through without him." He looked down the table. "That's what *all or none* means. Right? It means none. It means it can try whatever it likes, and we sit down in front of its stupid door and wait."

"Gabe," said Ice.

"Shut up, Dec. You don't get a vote on this one."

"We voted on five," said Calvin. Very quickly. "Nobody fights alone. If it happens, everybody comes." He looked round. "My hand was last."

"We voted on nine," said Higu. She put her own hand on the rock. "*If the Down splits us, Ice stands still and makes a light. Everyone comes.*" She looked at Ice. "This is the same thing. With a door."

"Thirty-six," said Newish, from somewhere. "Or nothing. I'll count it. I'll count it at the stupid door if I have to."

"Nobody left up," said Aleaka. "Burr said. That's the other half."

One by one, down the table. Not a vote. Nobody called it. People just said it. Hana, with her crown in her hands. Yan, with his eyes shut. Salty, *obviously*. Deston, very quietly, with his hand on his shirt pocket: *all of us*. Rachael: *none*, and then, after a moment, *obviously none*. Azac, under his breath: *and so our heroes sat down in front of the door*. All the way down. Thirty-five people. Saying it the way they'd said his name on the wall.

Ice sat at the end of the table and listened to them.

He didn't say anything. He couldn't, Thea thought. She'd seen him not be able to before. On a jetty, on floor 8, with tears all down his face. On a heap of green in the Down, with Gabe in front of him. On the barracks step at dawn with his forehead on Gabe's shoulder.

He put his face in his hands. Just for a moment. And then took them away again, and looked down the table, at all of them.

"Okay," he said.

That was all. *Okay.* Like on floor 8. Like on floor 7. Like the first thing he'd ever said to Gabe, in a whisper, after a hundred and fifty-three days.

Higu wrote it on the rock. Under *DAY 276*. Small and neat.

*THE GATE. DARK OF THE MOON.*
*ALL OR NONE.*
*NONE.*

***

He found her on the roof, late. She'd known he would.

She was sitting against the chimney with the lantern beside her, where she'd sat with Xola that afternoon. She heard him come up the ladder. She knew it was him before she looked; she'd always known. Since the white room. Since before she knew who he was.

He sat down. Not next to her. A little way off. With the chimney between them and the lantern's light on both their faces.

They didn't say anything for a long time.

"You worked it out," he said, eventually.

"It wasn't hard."

"No." He was looking up the pass, at the dark where the Gate was. "I should've said."

"You said *not yet*," said Thea. "On the bridge. I said okay." She looked at the lantern. "I'm still saying okay. I didn't ask you anything tonight. I told them what I thought. That's not the same."

"I know."

"I'm good at the difference."

He almost smiled. She saw it in the lantern light.

"Thea," he said.

"Mm."

"I want to ask you something."

She went still.

He'd never asked her anything. Not on this floor or any other. He'd answered. He'd said *not yet*. He'd said *you'll be the first*. He'd stood in front of her on floor 6 with a scar on his hand and not touched her. He'd never once asked her for anything.

"Okay," she said.

He didn't say it straight away. He sat there with his back to the chimney and his knees up, looking up the pass, and she watched him try to find the words, and not find them, and try again.

"At the Gate," he said. "Whatever it does. Whatever it tries." He stopped. "Don't step in front of me."

Thea didn't move.

"Promise me," he said. Very quietly. "That's all. That's the only thing I'll ever ask you. Whatever happens up there, whatever it comes for, don't—" His voice went. He made it come back. "Don't get between it and me. Please."

She looked at him.

At the side of his face in the lantern light, turned away from her, looking up the pass. At his hands, locked together round his knees so tight the scar on his left palm had gone white. At the whole of him, holding very still, the way you hold still when you're waiting for something to land.

*I think last time it was me*, she'd said to Xola, on floor 6, in the dark. Not knowing what she meant. *I think last time I was the one who got in the way. And it went through. And he was standing behind me, and he watched.*

She'd said it about a feeling. About nothing. About a theory with no facts in it at all.

She looked at his face now, and knew it wasn't nothing.

"I can't promise that," said Thea.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"I can't," she said. Her voice was very steady. She didn't know how. "You asked me not to ask you. On the bridge. And I haven't. Not once. Not even tonight." She held his eyes. "So don't ask me that. That's the deal. You don't ask me that, and I don't ask you what the first time was."

He looked at her for a very long time.

His face was doing something she'd never seen it do. Not on any floor. It was the face of somebody standing at the edge of something, looking down, who has just understood that the person next to him has seen exactly what he's looking at.

"Thea," he said.

"That's the deal," said Thea.

She picked up the lantern. Not to go. Just to hold it. On her knees. The amber coming up through the glass, his light, the one she'd carried since floor 3, warm on both their faces.

"I'll be careful," she said. Softer. "I'm always careful. I'll stand where Drew tells me. I'll keep the light up. I'll do everything you'd want." She looked at the lantern. "But if it comes for you, Ice. If it's you it wants. Don't ask me to stand there and watch."

He didn't say anything.

He couldn't, she thought. He sat on the roof of the barracks in the cold with the Gate up the pass in the dark and looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't say anything at all.

Then, after a long time, very slowly, he moved. Along the chimney. Until he was sitting next to her. Not touching. Close enough that she could feel him there, the way she'd felt his thread pointing at her since the clock room on floor 7, steady, the way a compass points.

They sat there. With the lantern between them on her knees.

"That's not a fair deal," he said, eventually. Very quietly.

"No," said Thea. "It's not." She leaned, a little. Just enough that her shoulder was against his. "It's the one you get."

He didn't move away.
