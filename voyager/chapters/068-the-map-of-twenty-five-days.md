Higu drew the map by moonlight, because the moon on floor 6 was the only thing on the whole floor that didn't hurt to look at.

She'd known that since the first night. They'd walked from the first water cache to Deepwell in the dark, thirty-five of them in a line across the Glass. And somewhere around midnight the moon had come up over the eastern dunes, huge and white and real, and the whole Waste had changed. The glass that threw the sun back at you like knives all day caught the moonlight and held it. It went silver. Soft. Every dune glowing faintly from inside, as far as you could see. Like walking across the bottom of a sea that had turned to light.

Chi had found her standing still in the line, looking up, with her face wet. Chi hadn't said anything. She'd just taken Higu's hand and walked with her, slowly, until Higu could walk on her own again.

Higu had always been a moon person. On Earth, on stream, she'd been the moon, more or less. A soft white light on a dark page, quiet, a little far away. People came to her streams to be calm. She'd liked that. She'd liked being the thing people looked up at when they wanted the noise to stop.

On floor 1 there'd been no moon. On floor 2 there'd been a painted one, a flat white disc on the painted ceiling. She'd looked at it every night anyway. On floor 4, nothing. On floor 5, the first real one, coming up over the Reaches, and she'd cried.

On floor 6, the moon made the desert into something you could bear.

So she drew the map by it.

***

It was her idea. That was the strange part. Higu didn't usually have ideas in front of people. She had them quietly, and then mentioned them to Drew, and Drew had them out loud.

But on the hundred-and-twenty-seventh evening she found herself standing in the round room on the third ring. Drew and Chris were talking about the bowl, and Hyvred was trying to describe the trails again, *it's like a web, it's all over the place, he came in from everywhere*, with her hands. And Higu said, out loud, to all of them:

"Somebody should draw it."

They looked at her.

"The trails," said Higu. "All of them. Every time he came in and turned round. Where from. How many times." She was calm. She was always calm, but she found she was very calm now, the way she got when she'd seen the shape of a thing before anybody else had. "Hyvred can see them. Nobody else can. When Hyvred's not standing there, they're gone. Nobody else will ever know what he did here." She looked at Hyvred. "Unless somebody draws it."

Hyvred stared at her.

"I'll come," said Chris, at once. He already had his notebook out.

"I'll come," said Drew. "Nobody goes alone. And somebody needs to carry the water."

So the four of them went. On the hundred-and-twenty-eighth evening, at dusk. Higu, with a roll of paper she'd bought from Ostrel's wife and a stick of charcoal she'd borrowed from Bee. Hyvred, to see. Chris, to write it all down. And Drew, to carry the water and keep them from walking into anything.

***

They started at Deepwell. At the lip of the pit, by the top of the bucket chain, where the path came in from the east.

"It's thickest here," said Hyvred. "The trail. It goes in and out of the pit. In and out. He came back for water every few days." She turned slowly. "And here, from the west, from the stair door, the first day. Once. And then later, once more, going west again—" She stopped. "That's the caches. That's him carrying the water out. The night before we came."

Higu drew.

She'd never been much of an artist. Not like Bee. But she'd always been good at shapes. At seeing where things were in relation to other things. She'd been the strategist on floor 1, the one who looked at the hills and said *if we go round that way the sheep can't see us*. On floor 2 she'd mapped the north slope for Drew's board. She sat on the lip of the pit in the dusk with the paper on her knees and drew Deepwell. A circle. And the line to the stair door going west. And four little marks along it, for the caches. And from the pit, going east, a line.

Then another line beside it. And another.

"How many times?" she said.

Hyvred looked east, along the path into the Glass.

"I can't count them," she said. "They're on top of each other. It's like a road."

Higu drew a road.

***

They walked east through the night, following it. Hyvred in front, reading the light. Chris beside her, writing. Drew behind with the water. Higu last, drawing, with the paper on a flat board she held against her arm, under the moon.

At the cistern, they stopped for the day. They slept at the bottom of it, in the cool, where he'd slept, all four of them. And Higu lay awake for a while looking up at the round mouth of the white sky, thinking about a man lying here alone for twenty-five days looking up at the same circle.

That night, they went to the bowl.

And Higu drew the web.

It took her the whole night. Hyvred walked her round the rim of the bowl, slowly, all the way round. A kilometre across, three kilometres round. And at every point on the rim where a trail came in, Hyvred stopped, and pointed. *Here. Down this way. In. Two hundred metres. Then back.* And Higu stood at the rim and looked down into the silver bowl in the moonlight and drew the line. In, and back.

From the west, where the cistern road came in: Hyvred counted eleven. Eleven times in, and back. "Some of them in the day," she said. "Some at night. The night ones are different. Bluer. And smaller. Much smaller." She was quiet. "He tried it at night and it was worse."

From the north: three. From the south: three.

And from the east, on the far side of the bowl, from the dunes beyond it, where Higu could see nothing at all but glass going away to the edge of the world: one.

"From *there*?" said Drew. "What's out there?"

"Nothing," said Hyvred. "Nothing at all. That's two nights round, the long way, through dunes nobody walks. Just to come in from the other side." She swallowed. "He was trying to find a way round. Any way round."

Higu drew it. One long line, from the far edge of the paper, curving round the whole bowl, two nights' walk, and coming in from the east.

And stopping at two hundred metres. Like all the others.

She looked down at what she'd drawn.

It was a bowl. A circle. A ring of black rocks in the middle, the Still Water. And all round it, coming in from every direction, from the west and the north and the south and the far east, lines. Eighteen of them. Every one of them stopping at the same distance from the middle. Not at the water. Not at the rocks.

At a ring. An invisible ring, two hundred metres out from where the hole had stood.

"That's not right," said Chris slowly. He was looking over her shoulder at the paper in the moonlight. "If it was guarding the water, they'd stop at different places. Wherever it was standing. It was in front of the water, wasn't it? Between us and it?"

"It was in front of *us*," said Higu.

Chris looked at her.

"When we came in," said Higu. "From the west. It was between us and the water. Because we came from the west." She put her finger on the paper. On the line from the far east. "When he came from the east, two nights round, the long way, it was between *him* and the water from the east side. It moved." She traced the ring of stopping points. "Wherever he came from, it was in front of him. Exactly two hundred metres from wherever it was standing."

The four of them stood on the rim of the bowl in the moonlight and looked at the map.

"It wasn't guarding the water," said Higu quietly. "It was guarding *him*. Keeping him out. Wherever he went."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Like it was made for him," said Hyvred, very softly.

***

And then there was the last line.

Higu drew it last, at dawn, because she'd been saving it. She didn't know why. It felt like the kind of line you drew last.

From the north rim. Hyvred walked her to the place, behind a spur of black glass on the edge of the bowl, where he'd crouched. "He sat here," said Hyvred. Her voice had gone thin. "For hours. With his back to the bowl. Look—" She pointed at the glass behind the spur. "His back was to it. He wasn't watching. He was sitting with his back to it and his eyes shut, while we came in from the other side."

And then down. One line, straight, from the spur of black glass, down the north slope into the middle of the bowl. Straight. No turning. No stopping at two hundred metres. Right through the ring that had stopped him eighteen times, as if it wasn't there. To the place where Thea had fallen.

And then back up, the same way, fast.

And then, a little later, east, round the rim. And down from the east, again straight through the ring, to the water's edge. And a long time sitting there. And then, at noon on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, out into the middle of the blue.

Higu drew all of it. The straight line down from the north, through the ring. She made it darker than the others. She didn't decide to. Her hand just did it.

Then she sat on the rim with the map on her knees in the first grey light and looked at it.

Eighteen lines, coming in from everywhere, stopping at the ring.

One line, coming straight through it.

"He couldn't get past it," said Higu. "Twenty-five days. Eighteen tries. From every direction. Day and night. Two nights round, the long way, through nothing, just to try from the other side." She touched the dark line with one finger. "And then he got past it in about ten seconds. Running. With his head down."

"Because of the lantern," said Chris.

"Because of the lantern," said Higu. She looked down at the line. "Because she was there. Inside the ring. Holding it."

Chris looked at the map for a long time. Then he took out his notebook and wrote something in it, small, and closed it.

***

Hyvred read the water at noon on the hundred-and-twenty-ninth day, and Higu learned something she hadn't expected to.

They went down to the Still Water through the ring, the four of them. There was nothing there now to stop them. Just the gold bowl in the white sun, and the shards of broken glass lying where the hole had been, and the ring of black rocks, and the blue.

The Prism was in the water.

A new one. Floor 6 had made another, the day after he'd killed the first. The way bosses did when people stayed on the floor. It sat in the middle of the Still Water, a great clear many-sided thing the size of a house, and the white noon sun poured down into it. And lines of light came out of it. Thin white beams, sweeping slowly across the bowl. Where they touched the glass, the glass ran like water.

And on the shore, half in the blue, there was the old one.

Broken. Lying on its side in the shallows, cracked through, dull, its faces gone cloudy. A dead thing of glass the size of a house, with the water lapping at it. Floor 6 didn't take things away.

"He fought it at noon," said Hyvred.

She was standing at the edge of the ring of black rocks, out of reach of the beams, looking at the water. Her eyes were moving.

"In the middle of the day," she said. "With the sun straight down into it. With all those beams." She pointed. "He went into the water there. Up to his waist. And walked out. Into the middle of them. I can see it, his light, all the way out. And it doesn't go round them. It goes *through*. Straight through the middle of where the beams were." She swallowed. "It's burned. His light. All along the way out. Like it was standing in a fire."

"Why noon?" said Chris.

Nobody answered.

"You'd do it at night," said Chris. He'd stopped writing. He was frowning at the Prism and its beams. "Anybody would. It uses the sun. No sun, no beams. You wait till dark and walk out there and it's just a big glass thing sitting in a pond." He looked at Hyvred. "Why would anybody do it at noon?"

And Higu, who didn't usually have ideas in front of people, found herself having one.

"Because it's the hour nobody would choose," she said.

Chris looked at her.

"He's done everything the obvious way," said Higu slowly. She was working it out as she said it, the way she worked out the shape of a hill. "Every floor. Floor 1, the Bellwether, he cut the strap. Floor 4, the Kiln, he stood in the fire. The way it *works*. The way you'd find, if you knew." She looked at the beams sweeping the water. "And then floor 5, two weeks, two shields. And Nan Gully said he went up and down that needle for two weeks fighting the bird, and Pell taught him to lean. Like he had to learn it new." She looked at Chris. "And then this. Noon. The worst possible hour. The one nobody would ever pick."

"Like he's—" Chris stopped.

"Like he's *avoiding* the obvious way," said Higu. "On purpose. Like the obvious way stopped working, somewhere around floor 5. Or like he's afraid it will." She was quiet. "Like somebody's watching how he does things."

Nobody said anything for a long time. The beams swept slowly across the Still Water.

Chris wrote it down. Higu saw what he wrote, over his shoulder. Two words, under all the others on the page that said *HOW?* at the top.

*why noon?*

***

They walked back to Deepwell through the nights of the hundred-and-twenty-ninth and the hundred-and-thirtieth. On the second night, somewhere in the middle of the Glass, under the moon, Drew dropped back to walk beside her.

He didn't say anything for a while. Drew could do that. People thought of him as the one who talked, the general, the voice. But he could walk beside you for an hour and not say anything at all, and it was never awkward.

"Gu," he said at last.

"Mm."

"Floor 7," said Drew. "When we go up. What do we do?"

She looked at him. He was looking ahead, at Hyvred and Chris walking in front of them, small against the silver glass.

"I've been general for ninety days," said Drew. "Since floor 3. And I've spent all ninety of them chasing him. One floor behind. Every floor. And every time we get close, he goes up." He was quiet. "And on six we got closer than we've ever been, and Thea nearly—" He stopped. "I don't want to do floor 7 the same way. I don't know what else to do."

Higu thought about it. She thought about it for a long time, walking, the way she thought about everything. With the moon on the glass all round them and the map rolled up under her arm.

"We stop chasing him," she said.

Drew looked at her.

"Every time we come up behind him," said Higu, "he runs. Not *from* us. I don't think it's from us. I think it's ahead of something. Something he can see and we can't." She looked down at the map under her arm. "But the closer we get, the faster he goes. Like he's trying to keep a whole floor between us and whatever it is."

"So?"

"So we stop running after him," said Higu. "We go up ready. All of us. Together. Not in a hurry. Not chasing. Ready." She was calm. "And we let him see us be ready. Floor by floor. Until he understands he doesn't have to be a floor ahead."

Drew walked beside her for a while without saying anything.

"And if he still won't stop?"

Higu thought about a dark line on a map, going straight down a slope through a ring that had stopped him eighteen times. Toward a woman lying on the glass, holding a light.

"Then he'll stop," she said, "when he needs us. The way he needed the lantern."

Drew didn't answer that. But after a while he put his hand on her shoulder, briefly, and then took it away. And they walked on through the silver, under the moon, toward Deepwell.

***

They came over the lip of the pit on the morning of the hundred-and-thirty-first day, and went down to the round room on the third ring. And Higu stopped in the doorway.

Thea was sitting up on the cushions by the wall, with the lantern beside her and her shoulder loosely bandaged. Xola was asleep against the wall. Gigi was cross-legged on the floor nearby, braiding something.

And Xav was sitting beside Thea's cushions.

Not talking. Just sitting. On the floor, with his back to the wall and his hands on his knees, the way he always sat. As if he'd been there a while. Higu could tell he had. He had the stillness of somebody who'd been waiting in one place for a long time and was prepared to wait longer.

Thea wasn't talking to him either. She was looking at the lantern. But every so often her eyes went sideways, to him, and away again.

Higu didn't know what that was. She didn't ask. She went and knelt down in front of Thea instead, and unrolled the map on the floor between them, in the grey light coming down the pit.

Thea looked at it.

Higu watched her face. Watched her eyes go over the circle of the bowl, and the ring of black rocks, and the eighteen lines coming in from every direction and stopping. And then the one dark line from the north. Coming straight down. Straight through. To a small mark Higu had drawn in the middle, where Thea had fallen.

Thea put her finger on the dark line. Very lightly.

"Eighteen times," said Higu quietly. "From everywhere. He couldn't get past it. Twenty-five days." She put her own finger beside Thea's, on the place where the line went through the ring. "And then once. In your light."

Thea didn't say anything for a long time.

Beside her, on the floor, Xav had turned his head. He was looking at the map too. At the dark line. He looked at it for a long while with his calm, still face.

Then he looked at Thea.

"When you want to," said Xav quietly. "You said. On four. On the terrace."

Thea looked at him.

"I want to," said Xav.
