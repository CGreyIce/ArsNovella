Xav had a rule.

*Wait as long as you need. And then move once.*

He'd said it to Dec on the edge of the fountain in Evermorrow, a year ago, on the night of the thousand words. And Dec had said *you taught me that*, and Xav had said *half of it. Somebody else taught you the waiting*. And Dec had looked at the water and said *yeah. Somebody else did.*

The waiting was the half Xav had always had. He'd been good at it since he was a child. He'd sat at the edge of things his whole life, in every group he'd ever been in, and watched, and not moved, and people had thought he was slow, or shy, or not paying attention. He was paying more attention than anybody. He was waiting for the moment. And in nine years of knowing Dec, since the first week at poly, he'd never once moved early.

The moving once was harder. He'd only ever done it once.

On floor 10. In the hay hall over the stables at the Bastion, with thirteen people in a circle, he'd said *I'm going to tell Gabe what we think. So he's not last.* And then he'd gone down to the Breath and told him. That was his once. He'd spent it.

He'd been waiting again ever since.

Not for anything he could name. For Dec, he supposed. For the day Dec stood up in front of all of them and said it, whatever it was, the whole of it, the thing Xav had sworn on a stair to put down in one place first and had. The thing the club had on Chris's second page. The thing Thea carried the way she carried the lantern.

He didn't know if that day would come. He'd decided he could wait for it either way.

***

The stair down from 8 came out through the clock.

Three hundred and forty steps. The first time, going up, Dec had counted out loud for the first time in this life, and Deston's voice had caught on sixty-four, and there hadn't been a gap. Going down, Newish counted, and Thea sang, and Xav walked in the middle of his party with Gabe in front of him and Rui Xiang behind and listened to the count go down instead of up, and thought it sounded different. Not sadder. Just going the other way.

At the bottom, a door. A round one, made of the clock's own face.

Tiger opened it. From the inside, it was a disc of old painted iron on a great hinge, with the numbers backwards, and when it swung open the light came in through the twelve.

They stepped out into the clock room.

Xav remembered it. Everybody did. The slow wooden ticking. The iron wheels, man-high, turning. The great wheel in the middle that the Hour had tried to push backward every midnight for a hundred and fifty-three days, and the long arm of it, and the short blunt one that had broken two of Dec's ribs. Dec on the floor at one minute past midnight, laughing, with the moon outside the window not moving.

The wheels were turning. Forward. Slowly.

Dec stopped in the middle of the room and looked at the clock face from the inside. At the place where the hands met, in the centre, on the back of the dial.

There was a crack.

Xav saw it too. A long thin line across the centre of the painted iron, from the inside, where something had gone through it a hair from twelve. Somebody had mended it. A strip of brass riveted across the back, neat, careful, holding the two halves of the crack together. But you could see it. The line where the dial had been struck.

Dec looked at it for a long time.

"They mended it," said Gabe. Beside him.

"They mended it," said Dec.

He didn't say anything else. He reached up and put his hand flat on the brass strip. Just for a second. The way he'd put his hand on a lot of things, on the way down. A gauntlet in the snow. A wall with a name on it. Then he took it away, and they went down the ladder to the ringing chamber.

***

The ropes were new.

Not the ropes. The grips. Thirty-six ropes hanging down through thirty-six holes in the ceiling from thirty-six bells in the belfry above, in a circle, round the room, the way they'd been the night everybody pulled. Each with a soft woollen grip at the height of a hand, so the rope wouldn't burn your palms.

The grips had been grey. Xav remembered. All of them, the same grey wool.

They weren't grey now.

Every one was different. Red. Blue. A green like moss. Yellow, striped with brown. One that was a terrible orange. Knitted, every one, by a different hand; you could see it in the stitches, tight on some, loose on others, one with a hole in it where somebody had dropped a stitch and gone on anyway. Thirty-six grips, thirty-six colours, on thirty-six ropes in a circle.

Calvin went round them touching every one.

"Somebody *knitted* these," said Calvin.

"Everybody," said a voice from the stair.

The caretaker. Old, small, in a leather apron, with an apple. He'd been eating an apple the first time they climbed the tower, sitting on the bottom step, and said *nobody ever does*. He was eating an apple now. He was standing on the stair down from the ringing chamber looking up at thirty-six people who had come out of his clock.

"Nobody ever comes *down* the clock," said the caretaker.

"We do now," said Tiger.

The caretaker looked at them. At all of them. Slowly. And Xav saw it happen in his face. Not the floor-1 thing, the blank kind smile of somebody who'd never seen you before and never would again. The other thing. The thing real people did. Recognising.

"You're them," said the caretaker. "The ones who rang it. On the night." He put the apple down on the step. "I remember. I *remember*." He seemed astonished by it. "I had my supper, and I went home, and I came back in the morning and it was the next day and the ropes were all still swinging." He looked at the grips. "Everybody in town made one. For this year. One each. For the night." He looked back at them. "You'll want to talk to the baker."

***

Evermorrow was the same, and wasn't.

Red roofs. The river bend. The old stone bridge. The market square with the fountain in the middle and the clocktower on one side with its four faces and its open belfry. Lantern Street going off the corner of the square, narrow and leaning. It was morning, mid-morning, and it was a market day, and the square was full of stalls and people and noise.

But not the same stalls.

Xav had only been in the loop for a day and a half, the first time, before the bells. He'd still learned it. Everybody had. The dog that took the sausage. The cabbage cart man whistling off-key at the same corner. Mrs Penn and her knee at ten past nine. The same day, every day, a hundred and fifty-three times, and you could set your watch by the dog.

There was a dog. Brown and white. It was lying in the sun by the butcher's stall with its chin on its paws. The butcher was cutting something. He looked down at the dog, and the dog looked up at him, and the butcher sighed, and cut off the end of a sausage, and threw it. The dog caught it. The butcher went back to his cutting.

Nobody shouted.

"He gives it to him now," said Rui Xiang. Quietly. Beside Xav. Rui Xiang read people the way other people read weather, and he'd read the butcher. "Every day. You can tell. He's stopped minding."

The cabbage cart man was in a different place. Whistling a different tune. Still off-key.

And on the far side of the square, at a stall with a white awning, a small sharp woman in a white cap was arguing with somebody about the price of a wheel of cheese.

Dennis stopped dead.

***

Xav watched it from the fountain. It wasn't his. It was Dennis's. But you watched.

Dennis went across the square to the cheese stall. Not fast. He walked the way he walked into a market, which was the way a cat walks into a room with a mouse in it, slowly, interested, taking everything in. Rachael went with him, a pace behind, in her tanner's gloves. Dennis stopped at the stall and waited for the cheesemonger to finish with her customer.

She finished. She turned round.

She looked at him.

"*You*," said the cheesemonger.

"Me," said Dennis.

"*Six and a half*," said the cheesemonger. She said it like an accusation. "With chutney. You got me down to six and a half. I *never* go under seven. I told everybody. My sister doesn't believe me."

"You said that last year," said Dennis.

"I *meant* it last year." She folded her arms. "And then you went off with a wheel on credit and said *tomorrow*, and it *was* tomorrow, and you never came back." She looked at him with her small sharp eyes. "A year. A whole wheel. Do you know what that is on my book?"

"Eight coppers," said Dennis. "Less the half you knocked off for the chutney. Plus a year." He was taking his purse out. "I worked it out. On the stair. I'll pay interest."

"I don't *want* interest."

"I'm paying interest," said Dennis. And counted it out on the board of her stall. Coppers. One at a time. Slowly. Saying them under his breath. Xav could see his lips moving from the fountain. He'd been doing sums for five hundred days. He'd done this one more times than any of them.

The cheesemonger looked at the coins.

Then at Dennis.

Then she took her book out from under the counter, a fat greasy thing with a string round it, and opened it, and found a page, and ran her finger down it to a line. And took a stub of pencil from behind her ear, and drew a line through it. Neat. Straight. From one side to the other.

"There," said the cheesemonger. "Paid."

Dennis looked at the line.

He'd said it on the day after the bells, the first time. *The tab grows.* And cried, in the square, because a cheesemonger remembered him and gave him a wheel on credit, and that meant there was a tomorrow. Xav hadn't been there for that. He'd heard about it.

He was here for this.

Dennis stood at the cheese stall and looked at a line through a name in a greasy book, and his face did something, and Rachael put her gloved hand on his back.

"It's closed," said Dennis. His voice was strange. "The tab. It's the first one I've ever closed."

"Well, don't make a habit of it," said the cheesemonger. "I'll have nothing to talk about." She looked at the coins. At him. And then, gruffly, she cut a wedge off the wheel in front of her, a big one, and wrapped it in a cloth and pushed it across the board. "On the house. For coming back." She sniffed. "Nobody comes back."

"We're going down," said Dennis. "To the bottom. There's somebody there we owe."

The cheesemonger looked at him for a long moment.

"Then you'd better pay *her* too," she said. "With interest."

"That's the plan," said Dennis.

***

Dec went to the bakery alone.

He'd said so, on the stair. Not to anybody. To the stair, under his breath, the way he said things now. And Higu had heard, and written it on the rock at the bottom, small, in the corner where the things that belonged to somebody went. *ICE: THE BLUE DOOR. ON HIS OWN. A ROSEMARY LOAF. DON'T SAY ANYTHING.*

Merle had said it. On the evening they left, at the blue door, with flour on her hands. *Come back one day. In a long time. Years. When I've forgotten what you look like. Walk in through that door and buy a rosemary loaf, and don't say anything. And surprise me.*

So he was going to.

The thirty-five waited at the corner of Lantern Street. Not in the street. At the corner, in the square, where you could see the blue door and the bow window but not be seen from them. Xav was at the front, with Gabe. He didn't know how he'd got to the front. He thought Gabe might have pulled him.

Dec walked up Lantern Street on his own.

Narrow. Leaning. Cobbles. The smell came out to meet him halfway: bread, and rosemary, and an oven roaring. Xav saw his step change when it reached him. Just slightly. Then he went on.

He stopped at the blue door.

He stood there for a moment with his hand on it. The way he'd stood with his hand on the brass strip in the clock. Then he opened it, and the little brass bell on its curl of iron rang over his head, and he went in.

The door shut behind him.

Gabe made a sound.

"We're not going to watch," said Xav.

"We're *obviously* going to watch," said Gabe.

They watched.

***

You could see in through the bow window from the corner of the street if you went three steps out into Lantern Street and stood against the wall of the house opposite. Gabe went three steps. Xav went with him. And then, because they were who they were, everybody else did, and thirty-five people stood against the wall of a house in a narrow street in Evermorrow, very quietly, looking through a bow window full of loaves.

Merle was behind the counter.

The same. Flour to the elbows. Dark hair tied up in a cloth. Freckles. She was turning to the shelf behind her for something, and she turned back, and there was a customer at her counter.

Dec put a coin on the counter. He reached over to the basket by the window, where the rosemary loaves were, the ones she made every morning, the ones he'd bought every day the first time and eaten whole on the fountain's edge while she laughed at him. He took one.

He didn't say anything.

Merle looked at him.

Xav saw it. Through the glass. Her face. It went still, the way a pond goes still when something big moves under it. She looked at him standing at her counter with a rosemary loaf in his floury— no. His hands weren't floury. That was the first time. His hands were just his hands. With the two lines round the left wrist. Holding a loaf.

She didn't say anything either.

She stood very still for a long time.

And then she put both her hands over her eyes.

"*Don't*," said Merle. Xav could hear it through the glass, just. "Don't. Don't let me count."

Dec didn't say anything. He stood there holding the loaf.

"I said I didn't want to know," said Merle, with her hands over her eyes. "I said don't come back and tell me whether everybody's all right. I *meant* it. I wanted to not know. That was the whole—" She stopped. "And there are people at my window. I can see them. I saw them before you came in. There are a *lot* of people at my window."

Thirty-five people against the wall of the house opposite went very still.

"Don't let me count them," said Merle.

Nobody moved.

Then, slowly, through her fingers, she counted.

Xav watched her do it. Her eyes going along the faces at the window, one at a time, between her fingers. Her lips moving. He saw her get to the end of the row. He saw her go back and start again, the way Burr had, and Aldo, and the cheesemonger, the way everybody seemed to, on the way down. As if once wasn't enough to believe.

She took her hands away from her eyes.

She looked at Dec.

"Thirty-five," said Merle. "Out there."

She looked at the rosemary loaf in his hands.

"And you," said Merle. "Thirty-six."

And then she came round the counter so fast she knocked the basket of rolls off the end of it, and they went everywhere, all over the floor of the shop, and she didn't stop, and she put her arms round him, and the flour went up off her apron in a cloud, and he went back a step, the way he'd gone back into the flour sacks the first time, except this time it was because somebody had hold of him instead of because somebody had hit him.

Through the glass, Xav heard her.

"*Years*, I said. *Years*. When I'd forgotten your *face*."

"It's been a year," said Dec. Muffled. Into her shoulder.

"I said when I'd *forgotten*—"

"Have you?"

"*No*. You absolute *idiot*. *No*."

***

She let them all in after that.

She didn't ask anything. That was the thing Xav noticed most, afterwards. Thirty-six people came into her shop, which didn't fit thirty-six people any more than the Spoon on floor 1 had fit thirty-five, and stood among the shelves and the loaves and the rolls on the floor that Calvin was picking up, and she looked at every one of them, every face, and didn't ask a single thing.

Not where they'd been. Not what was up there. Not why Dec had two lines round his wrist, or why Rachael wore gloves indoors, or why Yan had a scythe now instead of a stick, or why Thea and Dec were holding hands and Thea's face was doing something halfway between angry and something else.

"Don't tell me," said Merle, when Calvin opened his mouth. "Don't. I mean it." She was crying a bit. She didn't seem to mind. "I've had a whole year of not knowing things. Do you know what that's *like*? I wake up and I don't know what the weather'll be. I don't know who's coming in. Mrs Penn's had six different things wrong with her since spring and I didn't know about *any* of them till she told me." She laughed, wet. "I don't want to know where you've been. I don't want to know where you're going. You're all here. That's what I wanted to know and I didn't want to know it and now I do, and that's *enough*."

"We're going—" said Calvin.

"*Calvin*," said nine people.

"I didn't *say*," said Calvin.

Merle looked at him. Then at all of them.

"Tomorrow's bread," said Merle, and went to the shelf, and started taking it down.

***

Gabe went to the kitchen door.

Xav saw him go. Through the crowd in the shop, past the counter, round the end of it, to the doorway at the back where the kitchen was. Orange light from the oven. Flour in the air. The big wooden table. And against the wall, by the door, sacks of flour. Stacked. A lot of them. In the place where, five hundred and fifteen days ago, a man had gone back into them in a white cloud with a red mark on his jaw, laughing.

Gabe stood in the doorway and looked at the sacks.

Xav went and stood beside him. He didn't know why. He'd been at the back of the shop, the first time, behind thirty-four people. He'd only heard it. *ONE WORD. A hundred and fifty-three days.* And then the sound of somebody going into flour sacks, and Calvin making a kettle noise in the doorway.

"That's where," said Gabe.

Dec had come up behind them. Xav didn't see him come. He was just there, on Gabe's other side, looking at the sacks too.

"That's where," said Dec.

They stood there. Three of them. In the kitchen doorway. Looking at sacks of flour.

"Good punch," said Dec.

"It was a *great* punch."

"It was a good punch."

"I've thought about that punch every day for a year," said Gabe. "It was the best thing I ever did."

"Not in my kitchen," said Merle, from behind them, with an armful of loaves. "Not again. I said lean on it."

"I'm leaning," said Gabe.

He was. Xav could see it. His shoulders were loose. His hands at his sides. He was standing in the doorway the way he'd stood on Lantern Street on the night before, with his hand flat on the frame, and let go.

Then Gabe turned round. Not to the sacks. To Dec.

"Dec," said Gabe.

"Yeah."

"Are you ok?"

He said it plainly. Out loud. In the kitchen doorway, in front of Xav and Merle and whoever else was close enough to hear, which in a bakery with thirty-six people in it was everyone. Xav felt the shop go quiet behind him. Not all at once. The way a room goes quiet when the people nearest the door stop talking and the people behind them notice.

*Just tell me youre ok. One word. Please.*

Gabe had sent that on the second day. In a whisper, from floor 1. And Dec had carried it for a hundred and fifty-one days and answered it, finally, at dawn, from the other side of a blue door, with one word. *ok.* And then opened the door and stood in the flour and Gabe had hit him, and held him, and said *you're not ok*, and Dec had said *no*.

Dec didn't answer straight away.

Xav watched him not answer. He'd watched him do it on the way down, more and more. Somebody asked him a thing, and he didn't say *fine*. He stopped. He looked inside himself first, the way you'd look in a pot to see if it was done.

"Yes," said Dec.

Gabe looked at him.

"Actually?" said Gabe.

"Actually." Dec looked at his own hands. "Not all of me. My hands hurt in the mornings. Rachael says a finger at a time, and it's a finger at a time. And Thea's still a bit angry." Behind them, somewhere in the shop, Thea made a sound that wasn't quite a word. "And there's a lot of floors. And something at the bottom I need to get to before it's too late." He looked up. "But yes. I'm ok. You asked for one word. That's the word. And it's true this time."

Gabe didn't say anything.

He sat down. On the flour sacks. Heavily. The way a man sits down at the end of a very long day. A little puff of flour went up round him. He sat on the sacks in the kitchen doorway with his big hands on his knees and looked at the floor, and his shoulders, which Xav had watched for five hundred and fifteen days, came all the way down.

"Took you long enough," said Gabe. To the floor.

"Five hundred days."

"Took you five hundred days."

"It took me a lot longer than that," said Dec.

Quietly. Not to Gabe. To nobody, really.

Xav heard it.

He didn't move. He didn't look at Dec. He stood in the kitchen doorway with his hands at his sides and felt it land in him, the way things landed in him, and go where it went. *A lot longer than that.* Not five hundred days. More. A lot more. Xav knew how much more. Or he thought he did. He'd sat in a hay hall with twelve other people and put it into words, once, and taken it down to the Breath, and told Gabe. *All thirty-six, the first time. Ten years. Maybe all of us. The top, alone. A choice. Back to the first minute so he could run.*

He'd kept that in one place. He'd sworn to.

He kept it there now.

Gabe, on the flour sacks, had heard it too. Xav saw him hear it. Saw his head come up an inch, and his eyes go to Dec, and stay there for a long moment. Gabe, who'd known Dec since he was thirteen, and had been told what the club thought on a forge floor on floor 10, and had said *you didn't tell me anything I didn't know, mate, you just said it out loud*.

Gabe didn't say anything.

He reached up from the flour sacks and put his hand on the back of Dec's neck. The way he'd done at the tower door, the first time, after the separate sorry. And left it there.

"Yeah," said Gabe. "I know, mate."

***

Merle told them about the bells at dusk.

They were round the fountain by then. All of them, on the edge and the cobbles, the way they'd been on the evening of the thousand words, with Merle's baskets of tomorrow's bread going round. The square emptying. The lamps coming on. The clocktower over them, with its four faces and its open belfry, and somewhere up in the dark, thirty-six bells.

"It's a year on Thursday," said Merle.

She was sitting on the church steps, where she'd sat the first time. With her arms round her knees.

"Since the night," said Merle. "The night it was tomorrow." She looked up at the belfry. "We're ringing them. All of them. At midnight. The whole town. Every year, from now on. We decided in the spring. We're calling it Tomorrow Night." She made a face. "The Dawley boy named it. Nobody could think of anything better."

"Who's ringing?" said Calvin.

"Everybody. Anybody. We need thirty-six." Merle laughed. "We've been practising for a month. In the ringing chamber. Every evening. Thirty-six of us on the ropes, the smith and Mrs Penn and the caretaker and the Dawley boy and me and everybody, and old Hobbes who can't hear, and we've *never once* got it. All at once. Not once. Somebody's always late. Or early. Or Mrs Penn lets go to tell you about her knee." She shook her head. "It's *hard*. Thirty-six people pulling at the same time. I don't know how you did it."

"Newish," said Higu.

Newish, on the edge of the fountain, sat up a little.

"She counts," said Higu. "We pull on *one*."

Merle looked at Newish for a long moment.

Then she looked at all of them. At thirty-six people round her fountain, eating her bread. And Xav saw her decide something. He saw it the way Rui Xiang would have, in her face, the moment of it.

"Then you'll ring," said Merle. "On Thursday. You'll have to." She stood up. "It's yours. It always was. We were only practising."

"It's yours," said Dec. From the fountain. "The town's. We just rang it once."

"You rang it *first*," said Merle. "That's different." She looked at him. "Don't argue with me in my own square. You're *on a rope*. All of you." She looked round. "And me."

"That's thirty-seven," said Dennis. Automatically.

"*Dennis*," said Rachael.

"There are thirty-six ropes," said Dennis. "That's all I'm saying. It's just a fact."

Merle looked at him. Then she laughed. Out loud, with her head back, on the church steps, in the lamplight, the way Xav had heard her laugh once before, at one o'clock in the morning on the first real night, when the clock struck and it didn't jump.

"Then somebody'll have to work it out," said Merle. "Won't they."

***

Xav sat on the fountain edge with Dec, late.

Everybody else had gone in. The inn on the far side of the square. Thea had gone, last, after a long look at Dec that Xav didn't try to read, and Dec had nodded, and she'd gone. It was just the two of them, on the cold stone edge, with the water running behind them and the clocktower over them and the lamps going down one by one round the square.

They'd sat here on the night of the thousand words. Xav had said *you picked it for me*, about the name, *Voyager*, the one thing only Xav would understand. And Dec had said *so somebody would*.

"You heard," said Dec.

Xav didn't pretend he didn't know what.

"I heard."

"In the kitchen. *A lot longer than that.*" Dec was looking at the water. "I didn't mean to say it."

"I know."

"You didn't say anything."

"No," said Xav.

Dec was quiet for a long time.

"You've been waiting," said Dec. "Since ten. Since before ten. I can see it on you. Like somebody standing at the edge of something." He looked at Xav sideways. "You don't have to. Wait. For me. It's not your job."

"It's not a job," said Xav. "It's just what I'm good at."

"I know." Dec almost smiled. "I know it is."

"I'm not waiting for you to say it," said Xav. He found, saying it, that it was true. He hadn't known until now. "I thought I was. For a long time. Since the Breath. I thought I was waiting for the day you stood up and told all of us. The whole thing." He looked at the clocktower. "I'm not."

"What are you waiting for?"

Xav thought about it. Properly. The way he thought about everything, before he moved.

"For you to not need to," said Xav.

Dec looked at him.

"You said it in the kitchen," said Xav. "*A lot longer.* You didn't mean to. You didn't stop yourself, either. And then you said you were ok and it was true." He looked at his hands. "A year ago you'd have said *fine* and gone out the back door. And I'd have waited. And it would have been the right thing." He shrugged. "I don't think I need to wait for you to tell us. I think you're telling us. A bit at a time. A finger's width. The way Rachael does her hands."

Dec didn't say anything for a long time.

"That's not moving once," said Dec at last. "That's the opposite of moving once."

"I know," said Xav. "It's somebody else's way." He looked at the water. "I'm learning that too."

They sat there.

The clock over them struck midnight. Xav counted the strokes, the way everybody in Evermorrow still did, out of habit, a year on. Twelve. And the world didn't move. The moon stayed where it was. The minute hand went past the twelve, and on.

Dec let his breath out. He'd been holding it. Xav heard him.

"Still," said Dec. Very quietly. "Every time."

"Every time," said Xav.

Then, because it was a year and a bit since a man had come out of a kitchen covered in flour, and because he'd been good at waiting his whole life and had just found out he could stop: "Go to bed, Dec. Thursday's a long way off. Somebody'll have the door."

"You?"

"Off to one side," said Xav. "John's teaching me."
