He couldn't kill the table. So he carried water.

It was the only thing he could think of to do. He'd lain at the bottom of the cistern for a night and a day after Drew's post about hidden quests, staring up at the stone lip, going round and round it. *It'll show them what they want.* He couldn't get past it. He couldn't break it. He couldn't warn them without going down there, and he couldn't go down there. And in two weeks, maybe less, thirty-five people who'd never seen floor 6 were going to come out of the stair door from the Spindle into the middle of the day, the way he had. The way they all had, the first time.

And Gigi had stopped sweating by the afternoon, the first time.

So on the hundred-and-seventeenth day he walked back to Deepwell, and bought twelve water skins from Ostrel, and filled them.

"Twelve," said Ostrel, slowly, at the top of the bucket chain. He looked at the white helm and the barrel lid, and the line of skins hanging from Ice's shoulders like a fisherman's catch. "That's a lot of water for one man."

"It's not for me."

Ostrel ladled out the last one and didn't ask.

"There'll be thirty-five people," said Ice. "Coming up the stair. Two weeks, maybe less. They'll come in the day, because they won't know." He took a pouch off his belt. It was heavy. He put it on the ledge of the booth. "The first cup's free, for anybody who walks in alone in the day. That's what you said."

"That's what I said."

"They won't be alone. There'll be thirty-five of them." He pushed the pouch across. "So that's for thirty-five first cups. And thirty-five second ones. In case."

Ostrel looked at the pouch. He didn't open it. He looked at Ice for a long time with his slow brown eyes.

"Yours?" he said.

Ice thought about how to answer that.

"Mine," he said.

Ostrel nodded slowly, and put the pouch under the ledge.

***

He bought onions in the market on the second ring. Little bitter desert onions, no bigger than a thumb, that Ostrel's wife grew in a tray of wet sand in the shade at the bottom of the pit. He bought a dozen. He sat on a ledge on the third ring, outside the room with the round window where he'd lived the first time, and peeled them.

He did it properly. Skin off in one long curl, the papery outer layer, then the tough one under it, all the way round without breaking. The way Mother Crane had shown him at dawn on the first day of the first run, in the back of the Copper Spoon, with her big floury hands over his. *You do it properly, love, or you don't do it.* He'd done it properly ever since. He'd done it on floor 2, under a pine, with frozen hands, and left the skins in a little pile that Tiger had found on the sixth day and put in World. He'd done it every time he cooked, in both lives.

He kept the skins. He ate the onions.

Then he cut twelve thin strips from the papery skins, and tied one round the neck of each water skin, just under the cork. A thin brown curl, like a ribbon on a present.

"They'll know," said Alea quietly, watching him tie the last one.

"Tiger will. And Hana. She's got one in her crown." He tied the knot. "Anybody who sees an onion skin on floor 6, in the middle of the Glass, tied to a full water skin—" He stopped. "They'll know it's water they can drink."

"They'll know it's *you*."

"They already know it's me." He hung the last skin on his shoulder. "This just tells them it's safe."

That night he walked out west from Deepwell along the line he'd come in on, the line from the stair door. Anybody coming up would walk it, because there was nowhere else to walk. Every few hours, in the shade of the black glass rocks that stood up out of the dunes like broken teeth, he stopped and dug a hole in the cool sand on the shady side. He put three skins in it, and covered them. And on top, on the rock, at head height, where anybody coming along the line would see it, he scratched an arrow pointing down with the point of his sword.

Four caches. Three skins each. The last one an hour's walk from the stair door.

He walked back to the cistern in the morning, in the heat, with nothing on his shoulders at all, and lay down at the bottom of it and slept for the first time in three days.

***

They came on the hundred-and-twentieth day, in the afternoon.

He was at the bottom of the cistern when it happened. Awake, lying on his back, looking at the circle of white sky above the stone lip, the way he'd been lying every afternoon for nineteen days. Waiting for the heat to go down.

And something woke behind his breastbone.

He sat up so fast he hit his head on the cistern wall.

It was one. Then two. Then more. Small, fine, so far away he had to stop breathing to feel them, threads running out of him west across the whole of the Glass Waste. Two days' walk away, to a speck of rock on the western edge where a stone arch stood over a door. Waking, one after another, as people came out of it into the white light. Swinging. Settling. Pointing.

*Calvin.*

*Rachael.*

He sat at the bottom of the dry cistern with his hand pressed flat on his chest, and counted. He couldn't stop himself. He never could. *Three. Four. Five.* Deston. Azac. Xav. Gabe, pulling harder than the others, the way he always did. Then on. Yan's party. Hana's. Hyvred's. Chris's.

*Thirty-four.* Thea.

*Thirty-five.* Drew. Last.

Thirty-five threads, running out of him across the Glass, so long and fine they were almost nothing.

And none of them going out. Not when he breathed. Not when he moved. Not when he climbed out of the cistern into the white afternoon and stood on the broken wall of the old waystation, looking west across the glittering dunes toward a horizon he couldn't see. They stayed. All of them.

On his floor.

For the first time since the white room, they were on his floor, and he wasn't leaving it.

"*Ice*," said Alea. Very small, on his shoulder.

"I know."

"You can't go up. The Prism's alive. The stair's shut. You can't—"

"I know, Alea."

"They're on your floor," said the fox. "They're on your floor and you can't go anywhere."

He stood on the broken wall with the sun on his back and thirty-five threads running out of his chest, and couldn't tell whether what he felt was the happiest he'd been in two lives or the most frightened.

Both. It was both.

***

They walked in the day. Of course they did. Nobody had told them.

He felt it. All afternoon. Thirty-five threads, two days west of him, moving across the Glass in a long slow line in the worst of the heat. He could feel the speed of them. Slower than walking pace. Slower every hour. He knew exactly what it looked like. He'd been in that line, the first time. Thirty-four people walking into the white with their heads down and their mouths shut, because if you opened your mouth the heat came in. Somebody would be saying *it's fine, it's not far*. It was always far.

Around the middle of the afternoon, one of the threads changed.

It didn't go red. Not quite. It went *warm*. A thread that had been fine and cool and steady went thin and hot, like a wire with too much current in it. And it flickered. Once. Twice.

*Gigi.*

He was off the wall before he'd thought about it. He was running. West, across the dunes, in the heat, in the helm, with the barrel lid banging on his arm. Running across two days of glass toward a thread that was flickering—

"*Ice.*" Alea, in front of the slit. Not flaring. Just there. "*Ice. Two days. It's two days. You'll be dead of the heat before you're halfway.*"

He stopped. He stood on the top of a dune in the white glare with his chest heaving. The thread flickered in him.

"*She's not alone*," said Alea. "*Look. Feel it. She's not alone.*"

He felt it.

Another thread had come close to Gigi's. Right up close, as close as two threads could be without being the same thread. And then the two of them started moving together. Slowly. Slower than the rest. As if one of them was carrying the other.

*Hyvred.*

He knew. He knew it the way he knew the Glass. The first time, Hyvred had carried Gigi on her back for six hours to Deepwell, both of them sunburned so badly they'd peeled for a week. And now, two days west of him, the same two threads were moving together across the Glass, slow, one carrying the other.

And then a third thread came close. And a fourth. And the hot flickering wire of Gigi's thread went steadier. Cooler.

He didn't know what they were doing. He couldn't know. Kei, maybe, with something out of his apothecary bag. Xola, with water. Bee, drawing something. Someone was doing something. Gigi's thread stopped flickering. It stayed warm, but steady.

He sat down on the top of the dune in the heat.

"She's not alone," said Alea again, very gently.

"I know."

"The first time, Hyvred carried her six hours on her own. This time there's four of them round her."

"I know, Alea."

He sat there and held his breath and felt them. Thirty-five threads, moving west to east across the Glass, slow, in the heat. And four of them close together round a fifth, carrying it.

And then, about an hour later, the line stopped moving.

All of it. All thirty-five threads, stopped, in one place. Not scattered. Not falling. Stopped together, at one spot on the line from the stair door to Deepwell. An hour's walk from the arch.

He held his breath.

The threads didn't move for a long time. A long, long time. And then they started moving again. And they were different. Steadier. All of them. Even Gigi's. Cooler, the way a thread gets when someone's had water.

He'd scratched an arrow on the rock there. Pointing down.

He lay back on the top of the dune in the glare with his arm over the slit of the helm, and laughed. It came out cracked and dry and sounded terrible.

"*They found it*," said Alea. She was laughing too, the dry little laugh she had. "*Ice. They found the first one.*"

"They found the first one."

"There's an onion skin on every cork." Her voice did something. "Tiger's going to *cry*."

***

He felt them reach the second cache at dusk, and the third in the middle of the night. They'd started walking in the dark, after the first one. Somebody had worked it out. Somebody always did. Chris, probably, writing it down. *Walk at night.* The line moved faster in the cool. Steadier. All night.

They reached Deepwell on the morning of the hundred-and-twenty-first day. He felt all thirty-five threads stop on the lip of the pit, together. Then start going down. Slowly. Ring by ring, into the shade, toward the black water at the bottom.

He imagined Ostrel in his booth at the top of the bucket chain, looking at thirty-five people coming over the lip of the pit, dusty and burned and walking. Ladling out the first cup. Not taking any money.

He imagined somebody asking why. And Ostrel saying, in his slow voice, *it's paid*. And somebody asking *by who*. And Ostrel looking at them with his slow brown eyes.

He lay at the bottom of the cistern that day and didn't sleep at all. He just lay there with his hand on his chest and felt them. Thirty-five threads, half a night's walk west of him, at the bottom of a pit in the desert, in the shade, by the water. All together. All still. Resting.

He'd never felt anything like it. Not in either life.

On his floor. All of them. Safe.

***

On the hundred-and-twenty-second day, at dusk, ten of them left.

He felt it start. He'd been half asleep at the bottom of the cistern. He woke because something had changed, the way you wake when a sound stops. Ten threads had separated from the rest. They came up out of the pit, ring by ring, and over the lip, and out onto the Glass in the dusk. Heading east.

Toward him.

He climbed out of the cistern and stood on the broken wall in the last of the light and held his breath, and felt them, one by one. He could tell. He'd always been able to tell, since the House of Ash. Every thread had a name.

*Thea. Drew. Higu. Xola. Chi.*

*Hyvred. Gigi. Bee. Hako. Kei.*

Two parties. Ten people. Walking east from Deepwell in the dusk along the line he'd walked, the line he'd walked back and forth for twenty-five days. Between Deepwell and the cistern and the bowl. Again and again and again.

"Hyvred," said Alea quietly. "She's following your trail."

"Yes."

"It'll be bright. You've walked it a dozen times. It'll be like a road."

"Yes."

"Ice." The fox was very still on his shoulder. "It goes to the bowl. Your trail. It goes into the bowl and back out, over and over. Every time you tried. It goes right up to—"

"I know where it goes, Alea."

He stood on the wall of the old waystation and felt ten threads coming east across the Glass in the dark. Following his light. Walking, in the cool, the way he'd taught them without meaning to, by leaving water in the shade with onion skins on the corks.

Straight toward the cistern. And past it, into the bowl. Toward the table.

*It'll show them what they want.*

"Go," said Alea. "Ice. Go and meet them. Before the bowl. Stop them at the cistern. Tell them."

He didn't move.

"*Ice.*"

"If I go down there," he said. His voice wasn't working properly. "If I stand in front of ten of them in the dark and say *don't go into the bowl*. With my face. With my voice. Gabe's not there, but Hyvred is, and Thea is—" He stopped. "It's over. All of it. The whole thing. They'll know. And I'll have to tell them how I know what's in the bowl, and how I know it's new, and—"

"*So what?*" said Alea.

He looked at her.

"So *what*, Ice?" The fox had stood up on his shoulder, small and furious and bright in the dark. "So they know. So it's over. So you have to tell them. *So what?* Thirty-five people said *including you*. Thea put a full stop on it. There are ten people walking toward that bowl, Ice. Thea's one of them. Hyvred. Gigi—" She stopped. "You said it on the terrace. On four. *Close enough that if something goes wrong.* Well, it's going wrong. And you're close enough."

He stood on the wall.

The threads came east.

He didn't go.

He'd think about that for a long time, afterwards. On a lot of nights. That he stood on that wall in the dark, with ten threads coming toward him and Alea shouting at him, and didn't go. For a hundred and twenty-two days he'd been standing in front of things so nobody else had to. And when it came to standing in front of *them*, with his face, and saying *wait*—he couldn't do it.

He told himself they'd stop at the cistern. That they'd find his camp and stop for the night. That it would be morning before they went into the bowl, and he'd think of something by morning.

They didn't stop at the cistern.

***

He felt them reach it a little before dawn, while he was crouched on the north rim of the bowl in the dark, waiting. He'd left the cistern an hour before they got there. He'd taken everything, his water and his bedroll and the helm. He'd gone north, round the rim, to a place where a spur of black glass stood up on the edge of the bowl. From there he'd be close enough. Close enough to—he didn't know what. Close enough.

The ten threads stopped at the cistern. For a long time. He imagined them climbing down into it. Finding the cool. Finding the marks in the sand where a man had slept for twenty-five days, and the scorch of a cooking fire. Hyvred, standing in the middle of it all with her eyes wet, reading the light on every wall.

Then they started moving again.

Toward the bowl.

The sun came up. The real one, the one that hurt, white over the eastern rim. And in the bowl below him, he knew without looking, the gold canopy would be catching it. The long table. The benches. Thirty-five people at it, laughing, passing bread. And one empty place.

He didn't look.

He couldn't. If he looked at the bowl he'd see it. And if he saw it, he'd walk. So he sat on the north rim behind the spur of black glass, in the heat, in the white helm, with his back to the bowl and his eyes shut. And felt them coming.

All morning.

Slowly. Following his trail in, the way he'd gone in, from the west. Down over the rim. Onto the glass of the bowl. Into the place where the air stood still in the middle of the moving heat.

He felt them stop.

All ten threads. At once. Stopped, on the glass. About two hundred metres in from the western rim. Where the gold canopy would be, if you were looking. Where the table would start to be close enough to hear.

They didn't move for a long time.

He sat with his eyes shut and his hand pressed flat on his chest and held his breath, and felt ten people standing on the glass in the bowl. Looking at something.

He knew what.

He'd known since the bottom of the cistern. He knew what Gabe's face did when Gabe looked at him across a fire. He knew what Hyvred's light looked like when it went small and frightened. He knew what Thea put at the end of a sentence. He knew what thirty-five people wanted, more than anything in the world, because they'd told him every night for a hundred and twenty-three days. In World, in whispers, in *including you*.

They were standing in the bowl on floor 6, looking at the Wanting. And the Wanting was showing them him.

Then the threads moved.

All at once. Fast. Ten threads, surging forward across the glass toward the middle of the bowl. Running. *Running*, the way you run toward someone you've been looking for for a hundred and twenty-three days, when you finally see him.

Nine of them.

One thread didn't move.

It stayed where it was, on the glass, two hundred metres in, while the other nine ran. It stood absolutely still. Then it moved too. Not toward the middle. *After* them. Fast. Faster than the others. As if it was trying to get in front of them. As if it had seen something they hadn't.

He knew whose it was. He'd have known it in the dark, in his sleep, at the bottom of the world.

*The lantern.*

She had the lantern. She was carrying the lantern, the way she carried it everywhere. And the lantern made lies look like what they were. She'd looked at the table, and the lantern was in her hand, and she'd seen it. Not him. Not a long table. A hole, shaped like what you wanted most, standing still in the middle of the moving air. *Eating you.*

And nine people she loved were running into it.

Her thread got in front of theirs.

And went red.

***

He didn't remember standing up.

He was on his feet, on the rim of the bowl, with the helm on and the slit dark. The barrel lid was on his arm and his sword was in his hand, and he was moving. Down. Over the rim, onto the slope of glass, running. Not toward where the table would be. He didn't look. He looked at the ground, at the glass going past under his boots. He followed the threads in his chest. Ten of them, nine together in the middle of the bowl, and one in front of them.

Red. Red as a coal. Pulling at him like a hook.

"*Ice,*" said Alea. She was on his shoulder, holding on, small and bright. "*Ice, you'll see it. You'll see it, you'll stop, you can't—*"

"*Then don't let me stop.*"

He ran.

Across the glass of the bowl, in the white sun, in the heat, with the dunes going past under his feet. Down the slope toward the middle, toward ten threads and the red one. He didn't look up. He didn't look at the canopy or the table or the faces or the empty place on the bench. He looked at the glass, and he listened to the thread. Red, and getting redder, the way a coal gets redder when you blow on it.

He heard them before he got there. Not the laughing. Not the table.

Shouting. Real shouting. Ten real voices on a real floor, frightened, in the white light. Somebody screaming a name. Somebody screaming *Thea*. And under it, all round it, very close now, the sound of a long table full of people he loved. Laughing. Passing bread. Calling him to sit down.

"*Don't look*," said Alea. "*Ice, whatever you do, don't look up—*"

He ran toward the red thread with his eyes on the ground and his sword in his hand, and didn't look up.
