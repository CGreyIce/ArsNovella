Dennis had a graph.

He'd started it on the stair down from 14. Not on paper. In his head, where he kept all the good ones. Time along the bottom, in days since the bell. Up the side, a number between nought and ten, which was how angry Thea was with Dec, estimated, by Dennis, from the outside, with error bars.

Day one: ten. Obviously. She'd put the lantern down by the doorpost and taken his hand and her face had been the most frightening thing Dennis had seen on fourteen floors, and he'd seen the Quiet. Ten.

Day two: ten. The *I know why* speech. Rachael had been in the barn for it and reported back. *It's the one you get.* Ten.

Days three to seven: nine and a half, nine, nine, nine and a half (the soup day; complicated), nine. Xola's days pulled it down a bit. Shared anger, Dennis had noticed, went further and weighed less. That was a whole other graph.

The walk down the valley: eight. Holding his hand all three days. Singing on the stair, still angry. Eight.

Tell: seven and a half. Rachael said Thea had nearly smiled on the Tellhouse steps when Mags changed the rhyme, and kept it for a moment before she remembered. Dennis had asked how long a moment. Rachael had said *Dennis*. He'd put it down as two seconds and moved the point.

It was a nice curve. It was coming down. Not fast. Not in a straight line; there were bumps. But down. He thought it had a half-life of about nine days, if you squinted, and he'd told Dec that on the stair from the well-house, quietly, somewhere around the two hundredth step.

"It's not a curve," Dec had said.

"Everything's a curve, Dec."

"Not this."

"Especially this," said Dennis. "This is the most curve thing I've ever seen."

Dec had made a sound in the dark that might have been a laugh. Ahead of them on the stair, Thea had turned her head half round, and they'd both shut up.

***

The stair came out in the Mast.

Dennis knew it would. He'd been there when they went up. The hold of a ship standing upright on its keel in the salt, a thousand years old, dry as the inside of a whale, with a plain round-topped door at the far end and four hundred and twenty steps going up behind it. They'd climbed out of the hold by the rope ladder on the morning after the rain, with Tiger first and Dec not first, and the sound had come in at the top of the stair.

They climbed out the same way. Down, this time. Up the ladder from the hold, over the hatch, onto the deck, and down the old rope ladder on the outside of the hull, rotted, that Hako had tied a new rope to on the way up, so it held.

Dennis came down the ladder and stepped off onto the Salt.

He stood there for a while.

It was wrong. That was his first thought. Not wrong like the Grey. Wrong like a sum you've done a hundred times coming out with a different answer. He'd walked across this for thirty-five days, west, and he knew it the way you know the walls of a room you've been ill in. White. Flat. Glittering. The glare coming up from below, so bright you wore your hood down over your eyes. No wind, no cloud, no shade. No sound. Your own voice going out of your mouth and dying at arm's length like into a cupboard. And under the crust, a hand's depth down, things that came up when you made a new noise.

It wasn't white.

Not all of it. It was white the way a field is white after a frost that's starting to go. White in long patches, grey-white, cracked, and between the patches the crust had gone dull, and between that, in the low places where the rain had stood and dried, there was a colour. Faint. A haze, low down, close to the ground, all the way to the end of the sky.

Green.

Not much. Not grass. A green you only saw if you weren't looking straight at it, like the green on a wall where there's been damp. It lay across the Salt in a long soft wash, thicker in the dips, thinner on the rises, and it went east as far as he could see.

And there was wind.

He felt it on his face. A breeze. Coming across the Salt from the west, from the Edge, from the white beyond the sky, and it moved his hair, and it moved the old salt-stiff sail on the crosstree of the Mast over his head, and the sail creaked.

He heard it creak.

"Oh," said Dennis.

His voice went out across the Salt. It didn't stop at arm's length. It went.

Behind him, coming down the ladder, Rachael stopped with her foot on the last rung and looked out at it with her gloved hands on the rope.

"It's green," said Rachael.

"It's green."

"It wasn't green."

"It rained," said Dennis. He'd been there. They'd all been there. The first rain on the Salt in a thousand years, the evening after the heron, drumming on the shields, and Ina laughing with his pots filling, and the ridges that had pointed at Thea for a month going soft and running together. "It rained once. And then we left." He looked at the haze. "And it kept going without us."

***

The heron was still there.

What was left of it. It lay on its side on the salt a hundred metres from the Mast, where it had fallen on the morning of the three hundred and fifty-sixth day with Gigi's thorns round its neck and Thea's line through its eye. Dennis remembered it as a hill. A heron the size of a tower, salt-plated, with legs like masts and a heart that beat once an hour. Then, after the rain, blue-grey and ordinary and dead.

It was bones now.

Long, pale, curving. The ribs of it standing up out of the salt in a row like the ribs of the boats it had kept. The great beak lying out along the crust, longer than a man, bleached. Feathers in the salt round it, grey, the colour of old paper, half buried.

And birds.

Dennis didn't see them at first. He heard them. A small sound. A chirp, and another, and then a lot, all at once, like somebody shaking a bag of seeds. And then he saw them. In the ribs. Small brown birds, dozens of them, going in and out between the bones, sitting on them, building something in the angle where a rib met the spine. Nests. Little cups of dry grass and feathers. The heron's own feathers, Dennis realised. They were building with its feathers.

He stood and looked at that for a long time.

The heron had kept things. That had been the whole of it. Birds, with their wings out, lying in the salt round its feet. Boats. A raker's sled with its strap still on. Kept, for a thousand years, so they could never be anything else.

And now there were birds nesting in its ribs.

Yan came and stood beside him.

"It's not there any more," said Yan. Looking at the bones. At the place in the ribs where the heart had been. "It went. After the rain. I watched it go." He looked at the birds. "They're all lit. Every one. Tiny. Like sparks." He sounded pleased. "There weren't any when we came."

"Where did they come from?"

"No idea," said Yan. "Does it matter?"

Dennis thought about it.

"No," he said.

***

Higu took the rock out at the foot of the Mast.

She did it every floor. You came out of a stair and she sat down on whatever there was, and took out the rock, and wiped it, and drew the line, and said *Ice. What do you remember?* And Dec told her, and she wrote it on the left. And then they went and found out what was true and she wrote that on the right. Three columns now, since 14. REMEMBERED. TRUE. NOW.

She sat on a coil of the old rope at the foot of the hull and drew the lines and looked up.

"Going down," said Higu. "Twelve. What do you remember?"

Dec opened his mouth.

He didn't say anything. Dennis watched him not say anything. He watched Dec look at the Salt, at the green, at the bones with the birds in them, at the Mast going up over his head with its one mast and its stiff sail creaking in the wind. He watched his face try to find something.

"Nothing," said Dec.

Higu looked at him.

"I never came back down," said Dec. Slowly. As if he was hearing it for the first time. "The first time. We went up. We never came back down. Not once. Not one floor." He looked at the rock in her lap. At the left-hand column, empty. "I don't know what's on twelve going down. I've never seen it."

Nobody said anything.

Higu looked at the rock for a long moment. Then she picked up the chalk and drew, in the left-hand column, at the top, where the first thing he remembered always went, a short straight line. Nothing else. Just a line.

"Then it's new for you too," said Higu.

Dennis did the sum. He couldn't not. Four hundred and seventy-one days since the white room. And in all of them, on every floor, Dec had known something. Where the stair was. What was under the crust. Which bell. Which step rocked. What the guardian did. Even when the tower changed things, even when nothing was where he'd left it, he'd known what had been there before. He'd always been a floor ahead, even when he wasn't.

He wasn't now.

For the first time since a white room, Dec knew exactly as much about where they were going as Dennis did.

Dennis looked at his face.

He'd thought it would be frightened. It wasn't. It was something Dennis didn't have a column for. Like somebody who's been carrying a very heavy pack up a very long hill and has just been told the pack's empty, and has been empty for a while, and they can put it down.

"Okay," said Dec.

"Okay," said Higu, and wrote *NOW: GREEN. WIND. BIRDS IN THE BONES.* in the third column, and put the rock away.

***

Gigi asked Thea at the first green.

It wasn't far. The last one she'd planted, on the night before the heron, was a stone's throw from the Mast. Dennis remembered it as a hand high. Three red-tipped stems and a bud, in a thumb-high ring of thorns, in a hollow in the crust. Gigi hadn't planted another after the heron. She'd said that was the end of the road.

It was waist-high now.

A bush. Low and spreading, with dozens of stems, every one of them fat and jointed like a string of beads, red at the tips, green underneath, glossy. And the thorns round it weren't a thumb-high ring. They were a hedge. Knee-high, dark, close, going right round in a circle, with a gap on the east side where you could step in. And beside the gap, a stone. A flat grey stone, set upright in the salt, the size of a hand. With marks pricked into it, small, blue-black, in a row.

Gigi stood in front of it and didn't say anything.

She'd been the first off the ladder after Dennis. She'd walked straight here, without asking anybody, and stood in the gap in the hedge, and now she was crouched down by the bush with both hands on the salt beside its roots, very still, the way she went still when she was listening to something. Maigo had come and stood behind her. He didn't crouch. He put his bare root-foot down on the crust beside the hedge, just for a second, and took it up again.

"It's not salt underneath," said Maigo. "Not all salt. It's wet. Down there. Sweet." He sounded astonished. "It's gone *down*. All of it. The rain. It's down in the old water, and the old water's come up." He put his foot down again. "Gigi. It's a whole hand higher than it was."

Gigi didn't answer. She was looking at the stone.

"What's that?" said Calvin. Too loud. On the Salt you didn't use to be able to be too loud. Now you could.

"Rakers," said Gigi. "It's raker writing." She touched it. "They've been here. Somebody's walked out from Seep. All the way here." She traced the pricked marks with her fingertip. "They write on the things they hold. When they can't say it." She looked up. "Somebody's been looking after it."

She stood up.

And turned round, and looked along the line of them, all thirty-six, standing on the Salt at the foot of the Mast with their packs on. Not at all of them. At one.

Thea was at the back, with Dec. Holding his hand. With the lantern in her other.

"Thea," said Gigi.

Thea looked at her.

"Will you walk it with me?" said Gigi. "The road. Going back." She swallowed. "I want to show you all of them. Every one. I want to—" She stopped. "I want to walk it with you. That's all. I'd like you to."

Dennis didn't know everything about Gigi and Thea. Nobody did, except the two of them, and maybe Xola. He knew there'd been something, on Earth, before the white room. He knew it had been bad. Not shouting bad; the other kind. The quiet kind, that goes on for a long time. He knew that on floor 6, in a cistern under a glass city, Gigi had sat all night with Thea's head in her lap while Thea's fever turned, and said *I'm angry at you for going, and you were right to go, both*. And that on the Salt, on the first night of the green, Gigi had taken the lantern out of Thea's hands and said *I've got it. For a bit. You can put your hands down*.

He knew that Gigi didn't ask people for things.

He watched Thea look at her.

And then Thea looked down at her own hand. The one holding Dec's. She'd been holding it since the doorpost. Fourteen days. She'd let go to wash, and to eat if she had to, and once, in Tell, to take a plait of straw from an old woman and button it into his vest. That was all.

She let go.

Not fast. She opened her fingers, and his hand came out of hers, and she looked at him. Dennis couldn't see what was in her face. Dec could. He nodded. Just slightly. *Go on.*

Thea went.

She walked down the line, past all of them, with the lantern, to where Gigi was standing in the gap in the hedge. And stopped in front of her.

"Yes," said Thea.

***

So Dennis walked with Dec.

That was how it went, every day, for the next eight. Thea and Gigi out in front, a long way out, at the head of the line, with the lantern. Walking the road. And the rest of them strung out behind, with the sleds, the way they'd crossed going west, except slower, because nobody was in a hurry and nobody had to walk soft. And at the back, Dennis, and Dec.

He hadn't planned it. He'd just found himself there on the first afternoon. Dec had been walking on his own at the back of the line with his hand empty, and Dennis had looked at him walking on his own with his hand empty, and dropped back.

"I'm not keeping you company," said Dennis. "I'm collecting data."

"Okay."

"For the graph."

"Okay."

They walked.

The road was easy to follow. You couldn't miss it. Going west, it had been one green point a night, a day's march apart, each one a hand high in a thumb-high ring of thorns, with nothing in between but white. Now there was something in between. The green haze lay thickest along the line of them, like a path worn across a field. A soft green ribbon, a few metres wide, going east across the Salt from plant to plant. As if the plants had been talking to each other under the crust all summer. As if they'd been reaching.

Maigo said they had. "Roots," he said. "All the way. Under. Holding hands." He said it as a fact. He'd stopped being astonished by the second day.

And every plant had a stone.

Every one. A flat grey stone set upright in the salt beside the gap in the hedge, with raker marks pricked into it. Different marks on every stone. Gigi read them with her fingers, though she couldn't read raker writing, and Dennis watched her do it from the back of the line, every morning, crouching by the stone in the low sun with Thea standing beside her holding the lantern, Gigi's hand on the marks and her head down, as if she could hear them.

"What's she doing?" said Dennis, on the third morning.

"Listening," said Dec.

"To a stone?"

"To whoever pricked it." Dec watched them. "She's like that. She always was. She'd read the backs of seed packets out loud on Earth. The bit about how to sow them. She said the people who wrote them were trying to tell you something and nobody ever listened."

Dennis looked at him.

He did that. Dec did. Said a thing like that, about Earth, about somebody, a thing you didn't know he knew. And then went on walking as if he hadn't. Dennis had noticed it on every floor since 1. He'd never written it down. He didn't need to. It was just Dec. He knew things about people the way some people knew things about weather.

"Did she?" said Dennis.

"Every packet."

Dennis didn't ask how he knew. Nobody asked. He walked on.

***

On the fourth day there was water.

They'd been carrying it. Ina's still had made it on the way west, a cask a day off the brine with his fire and Winter's frost and a lot of patience, and they'd brought casks down from Tell on the sleds because they didn't know. They didn't need them.

At the eighth plant there was a well.

Not a big one. A hole in the salt the width of a bucket, with a ring of salt blocks round it, cut square, the way the rakers cut them, and a stone lid. And a bucket. On a rope. Hako lifted the lid and dropped the bucket and it went down a long way and hit something with a sound like a dropped glove, and he pulled it up, and it was full.

Kei tasted it. Kei tasted everything first.

"It's sweet," said Kei. Surprised. "It's *sweet*. It's the old water. The bottom of the sea." He tasted it again. "It's better than Tell's."

"The rakers," said Gigi.

She was standing at the plant. The eighth one. A bush with grey-green leaves like little spoons and a hedge round it, and a stone with marks on it, and now a well. She looked at the well for a long time.

"They followed it," said Gigi. "The road. They walked out from Seep and found the plants and they knew. Plants need water. If there's a plant, there's water. So they dug." She put her hand on the ring of salt blocks. "They dug a well by every one they could. Because something grew there." Her voice did something. "They dug a well because I planted a thing."

Thea put her hand on Gigi's back.

Dennis saw it from the back of the line. Flat. Between the shoulder blades. The way you'd steady somebody on a ladder. Gigi didn't turn round. She just stood there with her hand on the well and Thea's hand on her back, and after a while she nodded, and they went on.

***

He asked Dec about the numbers on the sixth day.

Not about anything. Dennis didn't ask about anything. He'd decided that on floor 3, when Deston read the note out, *be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor*, and he'd looked at Deston's face and seen Deston decide not to ask, and decided the same. Since floor 3. He'd seen things since. Everybody had. He'd put them on a graph he didn't look at very often, in a part of his head he didn't go into much.

This wasn't about anything. This was about the door.

"You said every number," said Dennis. "On fourteen."

Dec looked at him.

"I was at the door," said Dennis. "In the line. Behind Rachael. My hands on her shoulders. She was on her knees at the sill with her hands on Hearthwall, and I had my hands on her shoulders, and every time it hit you I felt it go through her into me." He looked at the green ribbon going east. "And then at the end, when you had the knot, when the winter came up the threads, I felt that go through her too. Into the shield. And into me." He swallowed. "She took more than her share. At the sill. She always does. She took a lot more."

"I know," said Dec.

"I'm not saying it so you'll feel bad."

"I know."

"I'm saying it because you said every number. All night. Up a hill. So we'd know." Dennis looked at his own hands. "So I'm saying mine."

Dec waited.

"Her hands," said Dennis. "Kei says a finger. Each time. Of her. It comes back, but slower than it goes. She told you that, in the Assize." He stopped. He'd done this sum a hundred times, at night, on the stair, in Tell. He'd never said the answer out loud. "I counted how many times. Since floor 4. Since Calvin's arm. Every time she took something. Every frost and every grey and every burn and every bit of salt off Jared's arm. I've got all of them. I count things. You know I count things."

"I know."

"I'm not going to say the number."

"Okay."

"It's not a good number, Dec."

They walked. The sled runners hissed on the crust ahead of them. Somewhere up at the front of the line, very faint, Thea was saying something to Gigi, and Gigi laughed. Out loud. On the Salt. Dennis heard it come all the way back down the line on the wind.

"Then we don't let her take more than her share," said Dec.

"You try stopping her."

"I know," said Dec. "I'm not going to stop her. I couldn't." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm going to make it so there's less to take."

Dennis looked at him.

"That's what fourteen was," said Dec. "At the end. With the knot. I couldn't carry it. So it went up the threads. Into all of you. A bit each." He looked at his own hands. At the two lines round his left wrist. "A bit each is better than a lot for one. Azac knew that on ten. I didn't." He stopped. "So next time, there's more of us at the sill. And she takes a bit. Not all of it. A bit."

"She'll still take more than a bit."

"Yes," said Dec. "And you'll have your hands on her shoulders. And you'll tell me the number. And I'll tell you mine." He almost smiled. "And then we'll both know. And that's better than one of us knowing."

Dennis walked for a while without saying anything.

"That's very good maths," he said at last.

"I had a good teacher."

"Who?"

"You," said Dec. "On floor 1. With the wool. *If we kill thirty sheep a day, that's ten copper.*" He looked at Dennis sideways. "You were wrong, by the way. It was nine."

"It was *ten*."

"Nine. The weaver took one for the bag."

Dennis opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

He hadn't told anybody about the weaver taking one for the bag. He'd been annoyed about it for a week, on floor 1, and he'd never told anybody, because it was a stupid thing to be annoyed about. One copper. He'd counted it himself, standing in the weaver's shop on the high street with the coppers in his palm, and come out one short, and been embarrassed, and never mentioned it to a soul.

Dec had been a floor ahead by then. On his own. Nowhere near the high street.

Dennis put it on the graph. The one he didn't look at very often. Then he looked at Dec, walking beside him on the Salt with his hand empty and his face open and not at all careful, the face he'd had on the Mast when Higu drew a line in the left column, the face of a man who'd been told the pack was empty.

He didn't ask.

"Ten," said Dennis. "I'm putting it down as ten."

"It was nine."

"I'm putting it down as ten, Dec, and that's the end of it."

Dec laughed. Properly. Out loud, on the Salt, in the wind. It went up the line ahead of them. Dennis saw Thea's head turn, right up at the front, and look back, a long way back down the line, at the sound.

She didn't come back. She was with Gigi. But she looked for a long moment, with the lantern in her hand.

Dennis moved the point on the graph. Seven.

***

They reached the first green on the eighth day.

Dennis knew it before they got there, because of Gigi. She'd been walking faster all morning. Pulling ahead. Thea keeping up with her, the lantern swinging. And then, at about noon, Gigi stopped dead on the green ribbon in front of them and stood there, and Thea stopped beside her, and the whole line stopped behind them, and Dennis came up from the back with Dec to see.

It was a tree.

Not a big one. Not like the trees on floor 9. But a tree. Taller than Salty, which wasn't saying much, but taller than Gabe, which was. A small grey-trunked tree with a crooked spreading top, the leaves on it small and silver-green and thick, like something that had learned to keep water in. And round it, a hedge. Not a ring. A hedge. Chest-high. Dark, thorned, thick as a wall, going right round in a circle with a gap on the east side. And beside the gap, a stone. And a well.

And people.

Rakers. Five of them, sitting on the salt in the shade of the tree, the only shade on the whole of the Salt, with their cloths down off their mouths and their pale squinting eyes turned up at the line of thirty-six coming along the road. And one of them standing up. Thin. Brown. Deeply lined. With her hand up, palm out, and on her palm, a row of tiny blue-black marks.

Tullah.

"*You came back*," said Tullah.

She said it out loud. On the Salt. In a hoarse voice that didn't get used much. And it carried. Dennis heard it from the back of the line. He saw the other rakers flinch, all of them at once, the way you flinch when you drop a plate, and look at the crust round their feet. And nothing came up.

Tullah didn't flinch.

"We didn't think you would," said Tullah. "Nobody comes back from the west. The old ones said." She looked at them. At Gigi, at the front. "The rain came. After you went west. The first rain. And then the well went up. Two hands. Not one. *Two*." She held up two fingers. "And then the green. We didn't know what it was. We'd never seen it. Old Henna said it was the salt going bad. And then my girl walked out on her own to see where it came from, and found the first one. Here. A little thing. In a ring of thorns." She looked at the tree. "It was this high." She held her hand at her knee. "In the summer."

Gigi was crying.

Not loudly. She was just standing on the road with her face wet, looking at the tree. At the hedge. At the gap in the hedge on the east side, and what was inside it.

Dennis could see it from where he was. Round the foot of the tree, in the salt, where the trunk came up out of the ground. Woven into the bottom of it. Brown, brittle, crooked, the stalks gone grey with a summer of sun, but still there, still a ring, round the trunk like a collar. Held up off the ground by the roots that had grown through it.

A crown. Of grass.

From floor 6.

"I made it on six," said Gigi.

She didn't say it to the rakers. She didn't say it to anybody. Thea was standing beside her, and Dennis saw Gigi say it without turning her head, to the tree, and Thea hear it.

"In the Glass," said Gigi. "After the bowl. The day it showed me you." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I made it out of the grass on the bowl's edge, the only grass on the floor, because I didn't know what else to do with my hands. And I carried it for six floors. And I put it round the first seed here because it was the only thing I had that had ever grown." She was looking at the crown in the roots. "And it's keeping it. Look. The tree's keeping it. It grew up through it and it's holding it up."

Thea didn't say anything.

"I used to blame you for everything," said Gigi. Quietly. Still to the tree. "I know you know. I said it on nine. I'm not saying it again for that." She swallowed. "I'm saying it here. Where something grew. Because I wanted to say it once somewhere it wasn't true any more."

Thea put the lantern down.

On the salt. Very carefully. Beside the well. Dennis had seen her put it down like that once before, by a doorpost, fourteen days ago. Then she turned round and put both her arms round Gigi.

Gigi made a sound. And held on.

Dennis looked away. Everybody did. Thirty-four people on the Salt looking at the sky, at the sled runners, at their own feet. Tullah looked at the rakers and the rakers looked at Tullah. Xola, near the front, had both her hands over her mouth and Squiddles had gone a pale soft pink he'd never seen before.

Beside him, Dec was very still.

Dennis looked at him sideways. He couldn't help it. He was a counter. He counted faces.

Dec was looking at them. At Gigi and Thea, under a tree that had been a seed, with a crown in its roots. And his face was doing the thing it did. The face Dennis had seen on him at the Mast, when Higu drew the line. The face from the stair at Tell when Aldo held up the handkerchief. The one you'd have for somebody you'd lost, who'd come back. Except it wasn't one somebody. It was two. And it wasn't that they'd come back.

It was that they'd found each other.

Dennis didn't put that one on any graph. Some things you didn't.

***

Tullah's girl was called Wyn.

She was one of the five under the tree. Younger than the rest, maybe twenty, with a raker's cloth round her neck instead of her mouth and her mother's squint. She'd found the first green. She'd walked out from Seep on her own, three days, following a rumour of colour on the crust, and found a thing a knee high in a ring of thorns, and sat down by it for a day, and walked home and told her mother. And the next week they'd come out with a saw and cut blocks and dug the first well.

"It was her idea," said Tullah. "The stones. She said if somebody planted it they'd come back for it. And if they came back they'd want to know who'd been looking after it. So we wrote it. On the stones. Who'd been."

"What do they say?" said Gigi.

Tullah looked at the stone by the gap. At the row of pricked marks.

"That one says *Wyn*," said Tullah. "And *Henna*, the next. And *Orrin*. And *Tullah*." She shrugged. "They all say who'd been. That's all. Names."

Gigi crouched down by the stone and put her hand on it.

Dennis saw Tullah watch her do it. Watch the girl who'd planted the road kneel down on the salt with her fingers on the names of the people who'd kept it. And he saw Tullah do something then. She looked across the green at her daughter, sitting under the tree in the shade. And lifted her hand. Not palm out. Not to sign. Just lifted it, a little, the way you'd lift a hand to wave.

And opened her mouth.

"*Wyn*," said Tullah.

Out loud. On the Salt. Across the crust, in the wind, in her hoarse old voice that didn't get used much, to her daughter, ten paces away.

Wyn's head came up.

Every raker under the tree went still. Dennis saw them. All five. Frozen, the way the rakers froze on the salt when somebody dropped something, waiting for the crust to break and the needles to come up. They looked at the ground round Tullah's feet. They looked at Tullah.

Nothing came up.

Tullah looked at her own palm. At the row of tiny blue-black marks pricked into the skin, her daughter's name, so she could say it to her on the salt. She'd been saying it to her on the salt for twenty years, Dennis thought, with her hand. Holding it up. Across the white. *Here. This is you. I can't say it, but here it is.*

"*Wyn*," said Tullah again. Louder.

"*Mum*," said Wyn.

She said it out loud too. Startled. As if it had come out of her by itself. And then she put both her hands over her mouth, and looked at the crust, and nothing came up, and took her hands away, and laughed. And got up and came across the green to her mother and stood in front of her, and the two of them looked at each other, two rakers on the Salt with their cloths down, in the wind, saying each other's names out loud for the first time in their lives.

Dennis had his hand over his own mouth. He didn't know when he'd put it there.

Rachael came and stood beside him. In Rafe's gloves. She didn't say anything. She put one gloved hand on his arm, just above the wrist, and left it there.

He put his hand over hers.

***

Seep was two days on.

The town on the rock, the salt-block houses, the low wall. The well at the north end. Tullah took them to it and let down the bucket, and it came up full a lot quicker than it had gone down on the three hundred and nineteenth day, and she said *two hands* again, and held up two fingers again, and the whole of Seep stood round the well in the evening and watched the climbers drink from it, as if that were the thing they'd been waiting to see.

The rakers talked outdoors now. Not much. Not loud. They'd signed on the salt for longer than anybody could remember and you didn't stop a thing like that in a summer. But they talked. On the rock, at the wall, at the well, and once or twice, carefully, out on the crust, with everybody stopping to look at their feet afterwards. And Old Henna, who'd said the green was the salt going bad, stood at the wall with her saw, the one with her mother's and her grandmother's words down the back, and read it with her thumb, and then said the words out loud. Every one. For the first time.

Dennis didn't know what they meant. He didn't need to.

On the last morning, at the door, at the low hump of grey rock at the edge of the Salt where they'd come out on the three hundred and nineteenth day with Calvin's sunburn and the glare coming up from below, Higu took out the rock. And in the third column, under *NOW*, she wrote, small:

*THE ROAD: WELLS. STONES. NAMES.*
*TULLAH SAID WYN.*

And under that, after a moment, because she'd seen it too:

*GIGI + THEA: THE TREE.*

She didn't explain it. She didn't have to. She put the rock away.

Thea was holding Dec's hand again.

She'd come back on the ninth morning, at Seep, on the rock. Walked down the line from Gigi at the front, and stopped in front of him, and held out her hand without saying anything, the way she'd held it out on the stair from 14. And he'd taken it. And she hadn't let go since.

Dennis looked at her face. He was a counter.

Six, he thought. Maybe five and a half. Bumpy. But down.

He'd tell Dec it was a curve. Dec would say it wasn't.

It was.
