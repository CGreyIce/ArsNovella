title: The Morning She Gets Up

Chi had been calm at him for sixty-six nights, and on the sixty-sixth morning he finally fell asleep.

She'd thought she'd feel it like a door shutting. Or like a weight coming off. She'd sat on the end of the chest in the little room under the eaves with her cup gone cold in her hands, across from him, every hour of her shifts and most of the hours that weren't, being calm, as hard as she could, at a man in a chair. She'd thought that when it stopped being needed she'd *know*.

She didn't. He just stopped.

It was the grey part of the morning, an hour after the turn. The candle in the window had burned down to a stub and Thea had lit a new one off it from the Spoon's own candle box without asking anybody. The lantern sat on the sill beside it. Kei was asleep on the floor by the chest with his book open on his chest and Dose curled on the book, the first time Chi had seen either of them asleep since the Long Barn. Rachael had gone down to let Calvin hold her hands and hadn't come back up; Chi had heard her laugh, once, through the floor, which was a sound Chi hadn't known Rachael could make. Xola was asleep sitting up against the wall with a cloth still in her hand. Maigo had gone down the stair barefoot with his one leaf and lain down on the kitchen floor at the bottom of it like a tree that had been felled.

And the old woman in the bed was asleep. Properly. Wet with sweat, all over, her hair stuck to her forehead, her mouth a little open, breathing slow and deep and even, the way you'd breathe if you'd been carrying something very heavy up a very long stair and had just put it down at the top.

Her hand was still holding his.

He'd been sitting in the chair with his head down on the quilt beside her hand since the turn. Chi had watched him. She'd watched his shoulders, the way you'd watch a sea for the turn of the tide. Held. Held. Held. And then, at some point between one breath and the next, while Chi was looking at the candle, they weren't held any more.

She looked back, and he was asleep.

Just like that. With his face on the patched quilt and his hand in the old woman's hand and his mouth a little open too, like hers. Two people asleep in a small hot room, breathing the same slow way.

Chi put her cup down very, very carefully on the chest.

She got up. She took the blanket off the end of the bed, the second one, the one Rachael had put on the old woman on the first night when the shivering started and nobody needed now. She put it over his shoulders. He didn't wake. She tucked it in round him the way you'd tuck in a child, which she'd never done for anybody, and which she found she knew exactly how to do.

Then she sat back down on the end of the chest.

She didn't need to be calm at him now. He was asleep. She'd done it. Sixty-six nights. She'd promised on six, at noon, in the cistern, with the sun pressing on everything. *I'll be calm at you. Every night.* And he'd said *please*. And she'd done it.

She found that her hands were shaking.

***

They'd shaken on six, too. After. She'd told him then. *I was terrified.* He'd said *it never sounds like it when it works*.

It hadn't sounded like it now, either. Nobody had known. Not Thea, not Higu. Not him. Sixty-six nights of sitting across a room from a man who was frightened all the way down, and being calm at him, steady, warm, like a wall in the sun, so that he could lean on it without knowing he was leaning. And every one of those nights, inside, where nobody could see, she'd been as frightened as he was. Of this room. Of this bed. Of the day that was nothing on the calendar to anybody but him and Thea, coming closer, one number at a time.

She'd been calm at him with everything she had. And now he was asleep, and there was nobody to be calm at, and all of it came up at once.

She sat on the end of the chest in the grey light and shook.

She didn't make a sound. She'd learned that a long time ago, before the white room, before any of it. You shook quietly. You let it go through you and out, like weather. You waited for it to stop.

A hand took hers.

She looked up. Thea had come round the end of the bed. She didn't say anything. She sat down on the chest beside Chi, close, and took her shaking hand in both of hers, and held it in her lap. And looked at the candle. Not at Chi. The way Chi had looked at him for sixty-six nights. Steadily. At something else. So you didn't have to be looked at.

Thea was being calm at her.

Chi hadn't known Thea could do that. Then she thought, of course she could. Thea had been doing it for him for a year and a half. Of course she could.

"It worked," said Chi. Very quietly. "Didn't it."

"It worked," said Thea.

"It never sounds like it."

"No," said Thea. "It never does."

They sat on the end of the chest together, holding hands, and watched a candle in a window, and two people sleep.

***

The old woman woke at noon and wanted to know who'd been in her kitchen.

"Thirty-six people," said Kei.

He'd woken an hour before her and sat up on the floor and looked at her and burst into tears, silently, and then wiped his face and checked her pulse and her forehead and written it all down, twice, and then sat on the chest with his book on his knees and Dose round his neck, waiting. He'd been the first thing she saw when she opened her eyes.

"*Thirty-six*," said Mother Crane. Her voice was a croak. She tried to sit up. She got about an inch. "Thirty-six people have been in *my* kitchen?"

"For three days," said Kei.

"*Three days?*" She stared at him. Then at the room. At Xola asleep against the wall with her cloth. At Chi and Thea on the end of the chest. At the man asleep in her husband's chair with his face on her quilt and her hand in his. She looked at him for a long time. She didn't take her hand away.

"What happened?" she said.

"You had a fever," said Kei. "The bad one. The winter one. In old people."

"I've never had a fever in my *life*."

"You had one now," said Kei. "You had it for three days. It broke this morning."

Mother Crane looked at him for a long time with her small sharp eyes. They weren't too bright any more. They were just her eyes.

"I remember," she said slowly. "Sitting down. My feet. And then I was hot." She frowned. "And then I don't remember." She looked at the window. At the candle and the lantern on the sill. "What's that? Why's there a candle in my window in the daytime?"

"So you'd know somebody was keeping one," said Thea.

"I'm *awake*. I can see you're keeping one. You can put it out now."

"Not yet," said Thea.

Mother Crane looked at her. Then at Kei. Then down at the man in the chair, asleep on her quilt, holding her hand.

"Has he been there the whole time?" she said.

"Yes," said Chi.

She hadn't meant to answer. It just came out. The old woman looked at her.

"The whole time?"

"He did three hours," said Chi. "Like everybody. Kei made a rota. A hundred and eight hours, three each." She swallowed. "And then he didn't go. He did his three and went downstairs and sat on the bottom step, and every time you asked for him, he came back up. You asked a lot."

"I *asked* for him?"

"*Where's the onion boy*," said Kei. "About every hour. For three days."

Mother Crane looked at the man in her husband's chair.

Her face did something. Chi couldn't have said what. Chi had spent a long time watching faces, on six, on fourteen, on every floor. She'd never seen one do quite this. It was like watching a wall decide whether to fall down, and decide not to, and lean, very slightly, instead.

"Well," said Mother Crane at last. Croaky. Cross. "He'll have a crick in his neck. Sleeping like that."

She didn't let go of his hand.

***

She wasn't allowed up for three days.

Kei said so, and Rachael said so, and when Mother Crane said that nobody told her what to do in her own house, Gabe came up the stair and stood in the door, ducking under the beam, and looked at her, and said, "We do now," in a voice that wasn't unkind at all. And she looked at him for a long time, the biggest man she'd ever had in her house, filling her bedroom door with his wolf coat and his warm shield on his back, and said, "Hm," and lay back down.

So she stayed in bed, and they came up to see her. All of them. One at a time, or two, up the fourteen steps behind the stove, to sit on the end of the chest or the floor and tell her things. Newish made her laugh, at least once an hour, on purpose, and said it was medical. Hana sat with her for an afternoon and did her hair, very gently, and Mother Crane let her, and said nobody had done her hair since her mother, and then said Hana was too pretty to be a Climber and should settle down, and Hana said she had, she was married, and Mother Crane said *good*, and asked to meet him, and Milli came up the stair pink to the ears.

And Calvin came up with a list.

He'd written it on the back of a page of Chris's notebook, in his big untidy hand, and he sat on the floor by the bed and read it to her. Every name. Everybody who'd come to the step of the Copper Spoon in three days, in the snow, with something in their hands. The egg girl, with eggs. The miller, with flour, and his father, with nothing, who'd just wanted to stand there. The woman from the tannery, with goose fat. The smith, with a trivet.

"The *smith*?" said Mother Crane. "That trivet? He's been making that trivet since his wife died. Twenty years. He never finished it."

"He finished it," said Calvin. "It's on your stove. Gabe says it's a good trivet."

Mother Crane looked at the ceiling.

"Quill," said Calvin. "With a notebook. A blank one. He said it was for writing down. When you're better."

"*Quill*."

"I know."

"Quill's never given anybody anything in forty years."

"I know. Malcolm told you. You were asleep." Calvin went on down the list. A man with a cheese, whose name was Ott and who'd come in from a farm up the valley because he'd heard. Children. Lots of children. And a hundred people Calvin didn't have names for, who'd just stood in the street. For three days. In the snow. Looking at her window.

He got to the end. He folded up the page.

Mother Crane didn't say anything for a long time. She was looking at the ceiling. At the beam over her bed. Chi was on the chest. She saw the old woman's eyes go wet, and saw her blink it away, fast, furious, like somebody brushing a fly off.

"It's the fever," said Mother Crane. "Makes your eyes run."

"Yeah," said Calvin.

"Don't look at me."

"I'm not."

"You *are*."

"I'm looking at the trivet," said Calvin, who was looking right at her. "In my head. It's a really good trivet."

Mother Crane laughed. A wet croak of a laugh, with her eyes running. And put her hand out, without looking, and found Calvin's head, where he was sitting on the floor by the bed, and put her hand on it, the way you'd put your hand on a dog. And left it there.

"The egg girl," she said. "Did you get her name? Malcolm said—"

"Elsie," said Calvin.

"Elsie." She said it slowly. "Forty years. On that corner. Every morning. *Eggs, fresh eggs.* And I never once asked her." She shut her eyes. "You'll take her something. From me. When I'm up. A loaf. The good one, with the new starter."

"Okay."

"And tell her she's to come in. Not stand on the step. *In*. And sit at my table."

"Okay," said Calvin. His voice had gone funny.

Mother Crane opened her eyes and looked at him sideways.

"And you," she said. "*Feet*."

"I'm not even *at* the—"

"I know what you're thinking about doing with them."

***

On the second day she found out about the table.

Chi was there for it. Everybody was there for it, more or less; they'd all come up the stair when Calvin went up, because they'd all known Calvin was going to say it eventually and nobody wanted to miss it. Chi was wedged in the corner by the chest with Waddles on her lap. Waddles had brought a fish up the stair that morning, a small silver one from the river, and laid it on the quilt by Mother Crane's hand, very carefully, and looked at her. And Mother Crane had looked at the fish, and at the penguin, and said, "Well, you can't leave it *there*," and then, "Thank you," and Waddles had sat on the end of the bed with his chest puffed out for an hour.

Calvin had been sitting on the floor by the bed telling her about the street again. He'd done it every time he came up; she seemed to like hearing it. And somewhere in the middle of it he said, "And then I went under the table, and—"

"Under my *table*?"

Calvin stopped.

"What were you doing under my table?" said Mother Crane.

Calvin looked round at all of them. At Thea, on the chest. At Ice, in the doorway; he'd been in the chair, and then Kei had made him go down for an hour, and he'd gone down and come straight back up and stood in the door instead, which he said didn't count as the chair. At Yan, who was on the floor at the top of the stair, outside the door, with his back against the frame. He'd come up the stair on the morning after the turn and not come in. He sat in the doorway, now, when he came. Just outside. With the Edge behind him on the landing.

"Ada," said Calvin.

The room went very quiet.

Mother Crane looked at Calvin. Her face went completely still.

"Who told you that?" she said. Very quietly.

"The table," said Calvin.

"The *table*?"

"Underneath. There's names. Carved. All down the middle. All the women who kept the Spoon." Calvin swallowed. "Edith and Agnes and Rosa and a lot of others. And at the end, by where you sit, *Ada*. And next to it, really big, *Tam*." He paused. "And he drew a boot."

Mother Crane didn't say anything.

She lay in her bed under the eaves and looked at Calvin, and didn't say anything for so long that Chi felt Waddles shift on her lap, uneasy. Then the old woman's mouth moved. And then it moved again. And then she laughed. Not the croak. A real laugh, from somewhere deep, so sudden and so loud that Kei jumped and Dose hissed. She laughed until she had to hold her chest, and her eyes ran, and she didn't say it was the fever this time.

"That *man*," said Mother Crane. "That *man*. I told him. I *told* him. It's for the keepers. It's for Mother Cranes. You carve your name when it's yours, under the table, where nobody sees, and you don't tell anybody, it's not for showing off. And he said *I'm keeping it too*, and I said *you're keeping nothing, you're putting your boots on it*, and he went under there with his big knife when I was asleep." She wiped her eyes. "He never told me he'd drawn a boot. Twenty-three years. I never looked. You don't look. Nobody looks under a table."

"Calvin does," said Rachael, from the stair.

"Calvin *would*." Mother Crane looked at him. Something in her face softened all the way, like bread rising. "Nobody's called me that since him," she said. "Since Tam. Twenty-three years. Nobody else ever knew it."

"Sorry," said Calvin.

"Don't be sorry." She looked away from him. At the doorway. At the man standing in it.

"Somebody said it," said Mother Crane. "In the night. I heard it. I don't remember anything else, I don't remember sitting down, I don't remember three days. But I remember that. Somebody said it. Close. In the dark." She looked at Ice in the doorway. "Like at the onions."

Ice didn't say anything.

"It was you," said Mother Crane.

"Yes," said Ice.

She looked at him a long time.

"You said I had to be here," she said. "I heard that too. You said I had to be here when you brought my pot back. Because it was open. On the tab." She frowned. "Was that you?"

"Yes."

"Hm." She looked at him, in her doorway, under the beam. At his face, which Chi could see from the corner was doing the thing it had done when he looked at the lantern on the bench on three. "Well," said Mother Crane. "You were right. It is open." She settled back on the pillow. "You'd better bring it back, then. Hadn't you."

"I will," said Ice.

"When?"

"When you're up."

"I'm up *now*."

"You're not," said Kei, Rachael, Gabe and Thea, all together, and Mother Crane glared at all of them, and then at the ceiling, and then, very quietly, to nobody, smiled.

She didn't say his name. She didn't ask it. Chi waited for her to; she thought everybody did. The old woman lay in her bed with her name said out loud in her room for the first time in twenty-three years, by a man she'd only ever called *the onion boy*, and she looked at him in her doorway for a long time, and she didn't ask.

"Onion boy," she said instead. "Go and see to my stove. Somebody's let it go too low. I can feel it from here."

He went and saw to the stove.

***

On the fourth morning, she got up.

Kei said she could. He said it at six, with his hand on her forehead and his book open on his knee and Dose saying something in his ear that made him nod. Rachael said she could. Gabe, in the doorway, said she could, as long as somebody held her arm, and then made it clear that the somebody was going to be him.

So she got up. And she came down the stair.

Fourteen steps. Chi was at the bottom of them. Everybody was. Thirty-five people in the kitchen and the long room of the Copper Spoon at six in the morning, all on their feet, not saying anything, looking at the top of the narrow stair behind the stove.

She came down on Gabe's arm. In her apron. She'd made Hana help her put it on; she'd said she wasn't coming down her own stair into her own kitchen without her apron, she'd sooner die, and then looked at everybody's faces and said *well, not literally, obviously*. She was slow. She held the rail with one hand and Gabe with the other, and she took the steps one at a time, and her knees shook, and she said a word on the sixth step that made Newish whistle.

Ice was in front of her. Going down backwards. One step ahead, the whole way, with his hands out, not touching her, just there. The way you'd go down in front of somebody on a ladder.

Kei counted her breaths. Chi saw his lips move.

Twenty-two.

At the bottom she stopped, and looked at her kitchen. At her stove, with the smith's trivet on it, and the good pot. At the bread on the board, which Thea and Merle's starter and Gabe and a great deal of shouting had made the day before and which was, Hako said, *technically* bread. At the onion sack. At the chimney corner with Hild's plait on the shelf, and Tansy's bead on its nail, and Joan's apple gone a bit wrinkled in the window. At her long table. At thirty-five people standing up in her cookhouse, looking at her.

"Well, sit *down*," said Mother Crane. "What are you all standing up for? You look like a funeral."

Nobody sat down.

She looked at them. All of them. And then she let go of Gabe's arm, and walked, by herself, slowly, holding onto the edge of the long table, to the kitchen end of it. To her place. Across from the onion sack. And sat down on the bench, the way she'd sat down on the six hundred and ninth day to laugh at Newish and not got up.

She put her hand flat on the table.

It didn't shake.

Chi heard somebody behind her make a sound. She didn't look round to see who. She thought it might have been everybody.

***

He made her eggs.

He'd been going to all along. Chi knew because she'd seen him take the little twist of cloth out of his vest pocket at five, before anybody was up, and look at it for a long time by the stove. Grey-green herb, dried, that smelled of rain and pepper. From a kite-person on five, on the crown of the Spindle, who'd said *you can't thank people up here, it just blows away*. *For her eggs. The morning she gets up.*

Her hens had laid. All nine. For the first time since the eve of the six hundred and ninth day. Aleaka had come in from the yard at dawn with her hands full and said so, slowly, with an expression on her face like somebody who'd been told a secret. Elsie had brought more, at first light, to the back door, and come in, and stood in the kitchen holding her basket, and not known what to do, until Mother Crane, from the stair, had said *Well, sit down, girl*, and she'd sat down at the long table, very straight, for the first time in her life.

He made the eggs the way, Thea said later, he'd made them on five, on a rock behind a ferry-woman's hut, every night for two weeks, alone, before any of them came up. Beaten, in a pan, in butter. A little salt. And the herb, crumbled in at the end, so it didn't cook too long.

He put the plate in front of her.

Chi saw the window come up in front of his eyes. She couldn't read it from where she was. She didn't need to. She saw him read it, and close it, and not say anything. Later Thea told her what it said.

*Kitecliff eggs. Sure-footed. Four hours.*

Mother Crane looked at the plate. Then at him.

"What's in that?" she said. "That green."

"Rain," said Ice. "And pepper. A herb. It only grows on one cliff, on five. A man who flies gave it to me. On the way down." He paused. "He said, *for her eggs. The morning she gets up.*"

Mother Crane looked at the eggs for a long time.

"A man who *flies*," she said.

"On a kite."

"Hm." She picked up her fork. She ate a mouthful. She chewed it, slowly, with her small sharp eyes on him the whole time, the way she'd watched him make broth on the first morning, saying nothing, which was worse than shouting.

"Well," said Mother Crane. "That's not bad."

She ate the whole plate. And then she held it out for more. And he made more. And she ate that too, and wiped the plate with a heel of the technically-bread, and pushed it away.

"Too much salt," she said.

And then she stood up. By herself. Off the bench, with one hand on the table. And she didn't sway. She stood there at the kitchen end of her long table and looked at her stove.

"Sure-footed," said Thea, very quietly, beside Chi. "Four hours."

Mother Crane walked to her stove. Four steps, steady. She opened the firebox door and looked in. She shut it.

"Who's been feeding this?" she said.

"Me," said Ice.

"Too much wood," said Mother Crane. "You'll have the chimney on fire." She took the poker off its hook. "Out of the way."

He got out of the way.

***

He slept for fourteen hours.

He did it at the long table. He sat down on the bench at the kitchen end, after the eggs, after she'd shouted at him about the wood, because she told him to sit down, and put his arms on the table to rest his head on them for a minute, while she banked the fire. And he didn't lift his head again till the next morning.

Chi watched it happen. She was on the bench by the fire at the far end of the room, where she'd sat every night for sixty-six nights. Out of habit. With Waddles on her lap, and her cup. She saw his head go down on his arms. She saw his shoulders go. She saw his breathing change, slow and deep and even, the way the old woman's had changed in her bed on the morning of the turn.

She didn't need to be calm at him. She was anyway. She couldn't help it. It was just what she was, now.

Mother Crane came out from behind the stove. Slowly, with the poker still in her hand. She looked at him, asleep at her table with his face on his arms. She looked for a long time.

Then she put the poker down. And went to the bench by the door, where there was always a blanket, because somebody was always asleep on that bench with their boots on. And brought it back, slowly, a step at a time, and put it over his shoulders. And tucked it in. The way Chi had tucked the other one, upstairs, on the morning of the turn. The way you'd tuck in a child.

She stood there with her hand on his back.

"Onion boy," she said. Quietly. To his back. Not to wake him.

She looked across the room. At Chi on her bench by the fire. As if she'd known all along that Chi was there.

"What's wrong with him?" said Mother Crane. Low. "Really."

Chi thought about it. She thought about the cistern on six, at noon, with the sun pressing on everything, and a man saying *I'm frightened. Of the Spoon. Of being on time and it not mattering.*

"He was frightened," said Chi.

"Of what?"

"Of this," said Chi. "That he'd get here on time. And it wouldn't matter."

Mother Crane looked down at him. Asleep at her table. With her blanket on him and her hand on his back.

"It mattered," she said.

"I know," said Chi.

"You tell him," said Mother Crane. "When he wakes up. You tell him from me." She took her hand off his back. "It mattered."

And she went back to her stove.

***

Thea put the candle out that night.

It had burned in the window under the eaves for six nights. Mother Tallow's first, down to a stub, and then six more out of the Spoon's box, each lit from the last, the flame never going out, the way Mother Tallow had kept hers on the pass since she was nine. Thea had lit every one. Thea went up the stair at dusk on the sixth night, when Mother Crane was asleep in her own bed again, and came down with the stub of the last one in a dish, and the lantern in her other hand.

She put the dish on the shelf above the stove. By the black tab book, and the salt.

Mother Crane, who wasn't asleep, who'd come down the stair behind her in her nightcap and her husband's boots because she couldn't sleep with people in the house and never could, looked at the stub on her shelf.

"What's that for?"

"So you know somebody kept one," said Thea.

Mother Crane looked at the stub for a long time.

"I'll keep it," she said. "There. On the shelf." She sniffed. "Next to my pot. When it's back."

She went to her place at the long table and sat down, across from the onion boy, who was asleep again, at the table, on his arms. She sat and looked at him a while. Then she took her little knife out of her apron pocket and pulled the onion sack over, and started peeling. Quietly. In the candlelight. Not to wake him.

Out in the street, the goat had gone home. Aleaka had walked him back to his bridge that morning, slowly, the whole way, through the empty square and past the corner where Elsie no longer stood saying three words to the air, and down the stream path in the painted snow. He'd lain down under his arch. He'd watched her go back up the path. And then, she said, he'd come out, and stood on top of his bridge, in the middle, and watched her till she was out of sight.

And the grey dog had gone back to the inn step. And the hens had laid. And the people in Crooked Street had gone home, all of them, a hundred small floor-1 lights, a little brighter than they'd been, Yan said, than they'd ever been, all going home through the snow in different directions, choosing their own ways.

Chi sat on her bench by the fire with Waddles asleep on her lap and watched Mother Crane peel onions at her long table by one candle across from a man asleep on his arms.

She wasn't being calm at anybody. She didn't need to.

She was just calm.
