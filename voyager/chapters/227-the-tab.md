Dennis had kept a tab since floor 1, and he'd never once seen a tab like Turnstead's.

He kept them in a little book. Not Cursiffe's kind of book; a cheap one, from the stall by the Copper Spoon on the first week, with a cover that had come off on four and been sewn back on by Salty with wire. In it, every debt. Who owed who, for what, since when. *Calvin, 11 (eggs, floor 1, the bet about the hen).* *Hako, 3 (the knife sharpening, Tell).* *Dec, 2 loaves + a pot (Mother Crane, floor 1, day 1. Hers, not mine. Paid, day 643, with interest she didn't want).*

Two years of it. It wasn't about the money. There wasn't any money; there'd hardly ever been any money. It was about knowing. Who'd given what. Who'd been given. What was still open. Dennis had always thought that was the most honest thing you could know about people, and the best: not what they said, but what they'd done for each other, written down, waiting.

He'd closed one, once. A cheese. In Evermorrow. *Six and a half.* The first tab in his life he'd ever closed. He'd cried in front of a cheesemonger.

Turnstead's tab was the worst he'd ever seen.

He found it out by asking. That was what he did, in a new town. You went round. You found the markets and the stalls and the people with ledgers, and you asked what things cost and what people owed and you learned a place by its debts. It had worked on every floor. He started on Turnstead on the third day, with the book in his hand, going street to street, sideways, the way everybody walked, with half his eye on the cobbles in case they went.

He asked the baker on Ottilie's corner. A big floury man called Hollis with a voice like a cart going over gravel. *Anybody owe you? Anything outstanding?*

Hollis laughed. Then he stopped laughing.

"There was a woman," said Hollis. "Came every week. Seventh day. A brown loaf and a twist. On tick, always. Paid at the end of the month, regular as the drip, twenty years." He was looking at his street, not at Dennis. "Last month of it she didn't pay. Then she didn't come."

"What happened?"

"Her street went." Hollis shrugged. "In the Loose. Nine years back. Fever in the house, everybody sick, nobody watching. You hear about it."

"What was her name?"

Hollis opened his mouth.

He stood behind his counter in the warm floury dark with his mouth open and his eyes on his street. And didn't say anything. For a long time. His face did something Dennis had never seen a face do. Like a man reaching into a pocket he knows he put his keys in, and finding it empty, and putting his hand in again, and again.

"I don't know," said Hollis. Very slowly. "I don't— Twenty years. Every week. Brown loaf and a twist." His big floury hand was flat on the counter. "I can see her. Grey coat. A limp on the left. She'd say *the usual* and I'd say *the usual it is*." He was quiet. "I can't remember her name."

Dennis wrote it down.

*Hollis the baker. A woman, grey coat, limp (left). Brown loaf + twist, every 7th day, 20 years. 1 month unpaid. Street into the Loose, 9 yrs. NAME: —*

He looked at the dash for a long time.

***

It was the same everywhere.

He did eleven streets in two days. He asked everybody who'd talk to him, and in Turnstead that was more people than you'd think, because nobody could leave their step and so everybody was glad of somebody coming to them. And every one had a line for his book.

A cobbler who'd mended a man's boots on tick for six years. The man's street went. The cobbler could tell Dennis the size of his feet and the way he walked over on the outsides of his heels and the exact shade of brown of the leather. Not his name.

Two old sisters on the Wall Road whose third sister's house had been on the corner, before the corner went. They argued for an hour about whether she'd been the eldest or the youngest. They could both remember the dress she was married in. Neither could remember what they'd called her.

A boy of about ten, one of the Breck boys, who said his uncle had owed him a whistle. A carved one. Promised for his birthday. And then the uncle's lane had gone into the Loose, on the night of a storm when the rain was so loud nobody could hear the street creak. The boy could remember the whistle. Exactly. What wood. What it would have sounded like.

"What was your uncle called?" said Dennis.

The boy looked at his street.

"I don't know," said the boy. "I called him Uncle." He was quiet. "Everybody else called him something. I can't remember what." He looked at Dennis sideways. "Is that bad?"

Dennis didn't know what to say. He wrote it down.

*Breck boy (younger). Uncle. A whistle, carved, promised. Lane into the Loose, storm. NAME: —*

By the end of the second day he had forty-one lines. Every one with a dash at the end where the name should be.

He sat on the step of the sixth house in the Crook on the third evening, on watch, facing west, with the book open on his knee, and looked at forty-one dashes, and felt sick.

"That's not a tab," said Dennis. Out loud. To nobody.

"What isn't?"

Rachael. On the step of the seventh house, facing north. She didn't turn round. She never did on watch. Rachael was the best watcher of the thirty-six; Ottilie had said so, sideways, on the second day, and Rachael had pretended not to hear and gone very slightly pink.

"This." He held the book up, though she couldn't see it. "I've been going round. Asking what the Loose owes. Streets, houses. I thought it'd be streets and houses. I thought it'd be a sort of— a list. Of what got lost." He looked at it. "It's names."

"Names?"

"Everybody who went into the Loose. When their street went. Nobody can remember their names." He heard his own voice go up. "Not one. Forty-one people and I've got forty-one dashes. They remember the coat. They remember the limp. They remember what bread she had on a seventh day for twenty years. They don't remember her *name*."

Rachael didn't say anything for a while.

"That's not a street going," said Dennis. "That's a *person* going. All the way. Out of everybody's head." He looked at the book. "The Loose doesn't just take the street. It takes them. Who they were. It leaves the—" He couldn't think of the word. "It leaves the receipts. And takes the name."

"Receipts," said Rachael. Quietly.

"That's all anybody's got left. What they owed. What they were owed. The debts are still there. The names aren't." He shut the book. "That's not a tab. That's theft."

Rachael was quiet for a long time.

"Ask Pask," she said finally. Still not turning round. "About his wife."

***

He asked Pask.

He went down the narrow lane to the forge on the fourth morning, in the grey, with the book. Pask was at the anvil. *Tink.* Working, in the day, with his door open and his eye on his lane. Sleeping at night now, since Salty. You could see it on him. He looked ten years younger and about a hundred years old.

"The climber with the book," said Pask. Without looking up. "Ottilie said you were going round."

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

"Your street. The Smithy. It went when your wife died. At the burying." Dennis had the book open. "What was her name?"

Pask's hammer stopped.

It stayed stopped. In the air over the hinge on the anvil. Pask stood there with it raised and his eyes on the lane and didn't move.

"I've been trying," said Pask.

Very quietly.

"Eleven years," said Pask. "I've been trying for eleven years. Every night. In the chair. Watching the lane. Trying." The hammer came down, slowly, and rested on the anvil without striking. "I can see her face. Clear as you. I can hear her. She used to laugh at me for leaving the bellows open. I can tell you every dress she ever had." He looked at the lane. "I stood at her grave and said her name. I know I did. Everybody did. And then I came back and the street was gone, and the name was gone with it."

Dennis didn't say anything.

"Forty years married," said Pask. "And I call her *her*. In my head. *Her*." He put the hammer down. "I don't sleep in the chair because of the lane. Not all of it. I sleep in the chair because if I lie down in the bed I reach for her and I can't—" He stopped. "I can't say what I'm reaching for."

He stood at the anvil with his old thin hands flat on it.

Dennis looked at his book. At the forty-one dashes.

He wrote it down. Carefully.

*Pask. His wife. Forty years. She laughed at him for leaving the bellows open. The Smithy into the Loose, at her burying, 11 yrs. NAME: —*

Then, under it, he did something he'd never done in the book before. He drew a line down the right-hand side of the page. A column. The kind you'd put an amount in. And at the top of the column he wrote:

*OWED.*

And beside Pask's line, in the column, in the space where a number would go, he wrote:

*her name.*

Pask was looking at him. Sideways.

"What's that?" said Pask.

"It's on the tab," said Dennis. His voice wasn't steady. "You're owed it. It's written down. It's on the tab." He shut the book. "I don't let tabs go. Ask anybody. Ask Dec. He owed a woman on floor 1 two loaves and a pot for six hundred days and I didn't let it go."

"What happened?"

"He went back down and paid it."

Pask looked at him for a long time.

"With interest," said Dennis. "She didn't want the interest."

Pask made a sound. It might have been a laugh. It might not.

"Get it back, then," said Pask. To his lane. "If you're going to put it on a tab."

He read it twice. Then he laughed, out loud, on watch, which you weren't supposed to do, and Rachael on the next step said *Dennis* without turning round.

"Sorry," said Dennis. "Sorry. It's just—" He looked at it. "It's a tab. My hard one's a *tab*."

"Course it is."

"*Call it in.*" He looked at the forty-one dashes. Forty-two, with Pask's. "How do you call in a name?"

Rachael didn't answer for a long time.

"You'd have to know who's got it," she said.

***

They had the council in the Crook, on the fifth evening, all thirty-six, on the steps, six watching and thirty talking, the way they did everything now.

Dennis read out the tab.

All forty-two lines. He hadn't meant to. Xav had said *what have you got* and Dennis had opened the book and started reading, and once he'd started he couldn't stop. The baker's woman with the limp. The cobbler's man who walked over on the outsides of his heels. The third sister. The uncle with the whistle. Pask's wife, who laughed at him for leaving the bellows open. Forty-two people, and every one of them ending in *NAME: —*, and he said the dash every time. Out loud. *Name: nothing.*

By the end nobody was saying anything.

"The Loose takes the name," said Dennis. "When it takes the street. It takes who they were. Out of everybody's head." He shut the book. "That's what's in the middle. I think. Not just streets. Them."

Yan, on the step of the fifth house, with Working On It across his knees, had his head on one side.

"*A light-shaped place*," said Yan. Quietly. "Where one should be and isn't." He was quiet. "I thought it was one light. Missing. Very big." He looked toward the middle of the town, over the roofs, though you couldn't see anything from the Crook. "It's not one. It's all of them. Everybody it took. It's the shape of where they should be."

Nobody said anything.

"Ruth went in," said Cursiffe. "*GOING IN. 30.*" He had the ink book open on his knee. "And they came out. They were cried on 22." He looked at Dennis. "And they were still thirty. On the wall. Every night. They never forgot each other."

"*Watch each other*," said Tiger. "*That's the whole trick.*"

"So we go in," said Xav. He stood up. The way he did. When everything had been said. "Not to look. We've looked. We go in, all of us, and we keep it." He looked round. "Ottilie said. You keep what you watch. Nobody's ever watched the middle. In the whole life of this town. They watch their streets and the middle goes." He was quiet. "So we watch the middle."

"All of us," said Higu. Already drawing. "In a ring. Round the outside of it. Facing in. And every one of us watching the one on our left."

"*Cover the one on your left*," said Azac. Very quietly. From the step of the second house. "*Let the one on your right cover you.*"

Overlap. Everybody knew it. Everybody had stood in Azac's line on ten.

"Never all blink," said Rachael.

Dennis looked at her. She was on her step. On watch. Facing north. Not looking round. She'd said it to the street.

"Never all blink," said Xav. "Tomorrow."

***

Dennis went to the edge first.

Not into the Loose. Not the middle. Just the edge. The day before. Because he wanted to see it before he went into it with thirty-five people and a ring, and because Tiger was going anyway, on the rope, to find the way, and somebody had to go with Tiger, and Dennis was the one who'd asked.

Dec held the end of the rope. He always did now. At the top of the alley off Ottilie's street, with Thea against his shoulder and Salty at Pask's anvil with the hammer. *THUD.*

"Bring it back," said Dec. To Dennis. As he went past.

"Bring what back?"

"Whatever you find." Dec looked at the book in Dennis's hand. "You've got a tab. Put it on the tab."

Dennis stopped.

He looked at Dec, at the end of a rope, at the top of an alley, not coming in. Holding the end. Dec, who'd owed Mother Crane two loaves and a pot for six hundred days and gone back down fourteen floors to pay it.

"You remember that," said Dennis. "The pot."

"I remember all of it," said Dec. Quietly. "Every line. You read them to me on fourteen. Three times." He almost smiled. "You don't let tabs go."

"No."

"Then don't let this one go," said Dec. "Go on."

Dennis went.

***

The middle was a square.

He'd known it would be something. He'd thought a pit, or a tower, or a ruin. Something you could point at. They came through the Loose behind Tiger on the beat, hand over hand on the rope, through streets that rewrote themselves between every *THUD*, past Ruth's courtyard, where the stray still hung, and through the other door, the one the arrow pointed at, and on. And the streets got stranger. And stranger. Doors on the second floor. Windows on the ground. A house standing upside down on its roof with its chimney in the cobbles.

And then they stopped. All at once. Like a sentence that had run out of words.

And there was the square.

Huge. Empty. Paved in grey flags, every one the same, going away from them flat and still for two hundred paces, three. And round the edge of it, on every side, houses. Tall and narrow and grey like every house in Turnstead.

With no doors.

He saw that second. Every house round the square faced in. Every one had windows, rows of them, three and four floors of windows, all looking in at the empty square. And not one had a door. Not one. Just wall where the door should be, smooth grey stone, and windows above it looking in.

It was still. That was the first thing, really. He'd been in the Loose for an hour on the beat and everything had been moving, sliding, rewriting itself, and here nothing moved. Nothing creaked. Nothing leaned. It was the stillest place he'd ever stood.

And it was quiet in a way that made his ears hurt. *THUD*, said Salty's hammer, far back, very faint, through the Loose. And here, it didn't hold anything still. There was nothing here that needed holding.

He stood at the edge of the square with the rope round his wrist and Tiger beside him and looked at it.

"There's nothing there," said Tiger. Very quietly.

"Don't look at it," said Dennis.

He didn't know how he knew. He just did. You didn't look straight at things in Turnstead. You looked sideways. Ottilie's way. With the edge of your eye. Resting it there.

He looked sideways.

There was something in the middle of the square.

At the edge of his eye. Not when he looked; when he didn't. Tall. Thin. Grey. Standing in the centre of the flags, very still. The shape of a person. Not a person. A gap. Like the place on a wall where a picture's been taken down, Yan had said, and the paint is different, a square where it was. The shape of somebody who should be there and wasn't. Very tall. Taller than the houses, maybe, or maybe not; it was hard to tell; it was hard to tell anything about it, because the moment he tried to look at it properly it wasn't there.

He looked away. Sideways again.

There.

The window opened in the corner of his eye, the way they did, grey. Beside the shape that wasn't. Exactly where he could see it without looking.

"Tiger," said Dennis. Not moving his eyes. "Do you see—"

"Yes," said Tiger. His voice was strange. "Sideways. Yes."

"It's not doing anything."

"No."

"It's just standing there."

"Yeah," said Tiger. "Like it's waiting."

Dennis stood at the edge of the still square with the window in his eye and the thing that wasn't there in the middle of it, and felt the tab in his coat pocket. Forty-two lines. Forty-two dashes.

*That's who's got it*, he thought. *That's where they went. That's what's got their names.*

He'd known where a debt was, every time in his life. You found the person who owed it. You went and stood in front of them. You said: *you've got something that isn't yours*.

He'd never stood in front of anything like this.

***

He did a stupid thing.

He knew it was stupid even while he did it. He'd think about it for a long time after. He did it because he was Dennis, and because the thing in the middle of the square was standing there with forty-two names and doing nothing, and because doing nothing was the one thing he'd never been able to stand.

He made a Double.

*What you show them is somewhere it isn't. They believe it for as long as you do.* He'd made forty on a cliff on ten. He'd been making them since floor 1: a Dennis, standing somewhere Dennis wasn't, so whatever was looking for Dennis looked there instead. It was the most natural thing in the world to him. It was how he fought. *Look over there.*

He made one. A Dennis. Ten paces out into the square. Standing on the grey flags with a little book in its hand.

He didn't think. He just wanted to see what the Unseen would do.

It saw.

He felt it see. Not the shape in the middle; that didn't move. Something else. A sort of turning, in the whole square. In the windows, all those hundreds of windows looking in. As if every one of them had shifted its attention, all at once, very slightly, to the Dennis standing ten paces out on the flags.

The Dennis nobody was watching.

Because that was what a Double was. That was the whole trick of it. It was a Dennis that wasn't there. Everybody looked at it, and nobody watched it, because there was nothing to watch, and the real one was somewhere else.

The Double went grey.

He saw it go. From the edges in. The colour going out of it, the way colour goes out of a sky. The coat, the hair, the hand with the book. Grey. And then thinner. And then not a Dennis at all. Just a shape. A Dennis-shaped place, on the flags, where a Dennis should be.

And he felt it.

Not in the Double. In himself. A pull. Somewhere behind his breastbone, where Bearing's thread went to Dec. A sort of slipping. As if somebody had got hold of a corner of something in him and was pulling it, very gently, out.

He couldn't remember his sister's name.

He stood at the edge of the square and reached for it, and it wasn't there. His sister. Who didn't believe him. Who he'd told about the cheese, in his head, a hundred times, *six and a half, you never believed me*, starting every time with her name—

He reached for the name. Where it always was. At the front of the sentence.

The front of the sentence was empty.

He couldn't—

"*DENNIS*."

Tiger had him by the collar.

Not his name. His collar. Hauling him back. Off the edge. And Tiger's face, right in front of his, with the eyes very wide and the tail straight out behind him like a bottlebrush.

"*Dennis*," said Tiger again. "*Dennis. Dennis. Look at me. You went grey. Your face went grey. Look at me—*"

He looked at Tiger.

And the Double, out on the flags, went out. Like a candle. Gone. Not even a shape.

And the pull stopped.

And the name came back.

He stood there with Tiger's fists in his collar and his heart going like a drum and said it. Out loud. The whole of it. His sister's name. All the syllables of it. Three times. Into Tiger's face. Like a man counting his fingers after a door's slammed on them.

Tiger didn't let go of his collar.

"Don't do that again," said Tiger. "*Ever*."

"No."

"You made a you. And nobody was watching it. And it took it." Tiger's voice shook. "And then it started on *you*."

"I know."

"I was watching you. I was watching you the whole time. That's the only reason—" Tiger stopped. "*Watch each other.* Ruth said. On the wall. It's not a saying. It's not a nice thing on a wall. It's—" He couldn't finish.

"It's the rule," said Dennis.

"It's the *only* rule."

Dennis stood at the edge of the still square and breathed. Out in the middle, sideways, at the edge of his eye, the shape that wasn't stood where it had always stood. It hadn't moved. It didn't need to.

It had got a Double. It had nearly got a name.

He took out the book with shaking hands and opened it, and under forty-two lines, on a new line, he wrote:

*The Unseen. Took a Double (mine). Nearly took D's name. Got it back (Tiger watching).*

And in the *OWED* column, beside it:

*nothing. Paid in full.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked up the page. At the forty-two dashes. At Pask's line. *OWED: her name.*

*You'd have to know who's got it*, Rachael had said.

He knew now.

***

They came back out on the beat. Hand over hand. Tiger in front, not letting go of the rope, and not letting go of Dennis's sleeve either, the whole way, though Dennis didn't say anything about it.

Dec was at the top of the alley. Feet in the same place. Rope round his wrist.

He counted them. Dennis watched him do it.

"Two," said Dec.

And then his face did something, looking at Dennis. His head tipped a fraction toward his own shoulder. The way it did.

"You got hurt," said Dec. Not a question.

Dennis stared at him.

"Not hurt." He didn't know how to say it. "It took a Double. And then it started taking— my sister's name. For a second. I couldn't—"

"I felt it." Dec's hand had gone tight on the rope. "Bearing. *When they are hurt.*" He looked at the alley. "It felt like your thread going thin. Like it was being let out." He was quiet. "I didn't know what it was."

"It's that," said Dennis. "That's what it does. It doesn't hurt you. It lets you out. Of everybody."

Dec didn't say anything.

Then he did a thing Dennis had never seen him do. He let go of the rope with one hand, and reached out, and took Dennis by the wrist. Two fingers and a thumb. Round the bone. The way Burr had taken him on nine.

"Dennis," said Dec. Quietly. Holding his wrist. Looking at him. "Dennis."

"I know my name."

"I know you do. I'm just saying it." Dec didn't let go. "In case."

Dennis stood at the top of the alley with Dec's fingers round his wrist and the book in his other hand and couldn't say anything for a while.

Behind Dec, on the step of the nearest house, Rachael was on watch. Facing the alley. Not turning round. She'd been there the whole time, he realised. Watching the alley. Watching the rope. Watching.

She didn't say anything.

But that night, on the step of the sixth house in the Crook, on watch, facing west, he heard her on the step of the seventh say, very quietly, to her street:

"*Dennis*."

Once. Like checking.

He didn't turn round. He was on watch.

"Yeah," he said. To his street. "Here."
