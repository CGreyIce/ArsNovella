Floor 4 was red, and it was silent, and it had killed Rachael and Dennis on the thirtieth day of the first run. Ice had been standing twenty metres away.

He came out of the door at the top of two hundred and four steps into the evening of the eighteenth day. The first thing he did was stand completely still and listen.

Nothing.

That was the thing about floor 4. That was what had got them, the first time. Every floor before it had been loud. Floor 1 had birds and sheep and wind in the grass. Floor 2 had wolves howling down the valley, and icicles chiming, and the creak of snow. Floor 3 had the croak of things in the reeds, and the whine of flies, and water. You got used to the noise. You stopped hearing it. And then you came out of a door onto floor 4 and it was *gone*, and you didn't notice it was gone, not for a while. Not until it was too late.

He noticed now.

The door had opened onto a terrace of red stone, high up on the side of a canyon. Below him, the canyon went down and down in great steps: terraces, stairs, walls, roofs, all cut out of the same dark red rock. A city. A whole city, carved into the walls of a canyon, going down to a dry river at the bottom, and up to a plateau at the top. Every window was empty. Every doorway was dark. Nothing moved.

The sky over it was low and dark red, the colour of a coal going out, and there was no sun on any rail that he could see. Just a red, even dimness, like dusk that never finished. Dry wind came up the canyon from somewhere below, carrying dust. It smelled of iron and dust, and under both of them something old and dry and sweetish, the smell of a place where a great deal had died a long time ago and nobody had cleaned up.

"I hate it here," said Alea, very quietly.

"I know."

"I hated it the first time. I hated it every time we came back through it. I hate it now."

"I know," said Ice. "Me too."

He'd never told anybody the whole of it. Not the first time, not after. He'd told it in pieces round fires, the way you tell things that hurt: a bit at a time, leaving gaps. Nobody had ever asked for the gaps. They'd all been there.

It had been the thirtieth day. They'd been on floor 3 a week, in the Fen, at level eight or nine. They hadn't known about the wall at ten, or the trials, or anything. They'd been tired of the swamp and the heat and the wisps. And somebody had said, at the long table in the smoking hall, *let's just go up*. Nobody remembered afterwards who'd said it. Everybody had agreed.

They'd come up the stair into the Red City at dusk, thirty-six of them in a long line. And Calvin had gone first.

Of course he had. He was the scout. He was fast, and outgoing, and adventurous, and it had been his job since floor 1. He'd come out of the door at the top of the stair and looked at the red canyon and the silent city. He'd laughed, delighted, the way he laughed at everything new. And he'd gone running down the first long stair to the first square below, calling back up over his shoulder: *come on, come on, it's amazing, there's a fountain*.

He'd been talking the whole way. Calvin always talked. That was the thing.

The square was about twenty metres across, with a dry stone fountain in the middle and red walls on all four sides, four storeys high, full of empty windows. Calvin had stopped by the fountain and turned round in a circle, looking up at the windows, and called up to the rest of them on the stair: *guys, guys, you have to see this*.

And something had come down off the roof behind him.

Ice had seen it from the stair. He'd been about halfway down. He'd seen it come over the edge of the roof like a shadow coming loose: tall and thin and grey and very fast, falling four storeys, with no sound at all. No sound when it landed. No sound when it moved. It had reached Calvin in two long silent strides, and Calvin hadn't heard it, because he'd been talking. Because nobody could hear it. Because it was the Quiet, and it was the only thing on floor 4 that didn't make a sound, and it hunted anything that did.

It had taken his left arm off at the elbow before he'd finished turning round.

Rachael had been right behind Ice on the stair. She'd gone past him. Ice still didn't know how; she'd never been fast. She'd gone past him down the stair and across the square to Calvin, with her staff, without a word. Dennis had gone after her, shouting her name. Shouting. Because Dennis always talked too, and he was frightened, and he was shouting at her to stop.

The Quiet had turned toward the shouting.

Ice had been twenty metres away. Halfway down a stair. He'd been running, with his shield, as fast as he'd ever run. And it hadn't been fast enough. It hadn't been anywhere near fast enough.

By the time he got to the square, Calvin was lying by the dry fountain with Rachael's hands still clamped over the end of his arm. She'd held the bleeding while she died, and stopped it, and saved his life. And she was lying across him with her staff under her. And Dennis was by the wall where he'd been running to her.

And the Quiet was gone. Back up a wall, over a roof, without a sound.

Nobody had ever killed it, the first time. They hadn't been able to find it. It had been the thing on floor 4 that everybody was afraid of, all the time, for the six weeks it took them to get through the Red City. Everybody moving in silence. Nobody talking. Nobody laughing. Calvin, with one arm, never once talking again above a whisper on that floor. And when they finally went up the stair to floor 5, the Quiet was still out there somewhere, on a roof, listening.

Ice stood on the red terrace at the top of the stair, on the eighteenth day, with the dry wind coming up the canyon, and looked down at the first square. Twenty metres across. A dry stone fountain. Four storeys of empty windows.

"It's still there," said Alea. "It's always there. It never leaves this district. It's—Ice, it's an elite. It's level twenty-two. You're seventeen."

"I know."

"You can't hear it. Nobody can hear it. You'll never know where it is until it's—"

"I know, Alea."

The fox was quiet.

"You're going to kill it," she said.

"Yeah."

"Before they come up."

"Before they come up," said Ice. "Before anyone comes down that stair talking."

***

He didn't go into the square. Not that night. Not for a week.

He'd learned a few things in ten years, and one of them was that you didn't go looking for the thing that had killed your friends on the first night, out of grief. You went looking for it when you knew exactly how to kill it. And until then, you didn't make a sound.

So he didn't.

He moved through the Red City in silence, on the higher terraces away from the first square, with strips of cloth wound round his boots and the shield's rim wrapped so it wouldn't ring. He killed what he had to. Dust-hounds, the colour of the rock, that hunted in packs along the dry river. Long-necked carrion birds that sat on the empty rooftops in rows and came down screaming on anything that bled. They all made noise. They all died making noise. And every time something died near the first square making a lot of noise, he stood very still afterwards and watched the rooftops.

He saw it on the third day.

Not *it*. Its movement. He was on a terrace a long way above the first square, crouched behind a broken wall, watching. Below him, a pack of dust-hounds had cornered something in the square by the dry fountain and were killing it, very loudly. And over the edge of the roof on the far side, a little puff of red dust lifted and drifted down. Nothing else. Just dust. As if something had shifted its weight on the parapet.

Then the dust-hounds were dead. All five of them. In about the time it took him to blink. And there was nothing in the square but a tall grey shape, standing very still among the bodies with its head tilted, listening. And then it was gone, back up the wall, without a sound.

He'd watched it the whole time. He hadn't heard a thing.

But he'd seen the dust.

"Dust," said Alea, that night, in the empty room on the high terrace where he'd made his camp. "When it lands. Or moves. On the roofs."

"It's silent," said Ice. "It's not weightless."

"So you watch the dust."

"I watch the dust." He was turning the little cracked Bellwether's Bell over in his fingers, on its leather loop. "And this."

*Taunt reaches further. Rings once for each enemy that turns.*

"It doesn't make a sound," said Ice, slowly. "But it *turns*. Everything turns when it's hunting. When it hears something and decides to come for it. It turns its head." He held up the bell. "And when it turns toward me, this rings."

The fox looked at the bell for a long time.

"You'll have a warning," she said. "One ring. And the dust."

"One ring and the dust," said Ice. "That's more than Calvin had."

***

He did it on the eighth day on floor 4. The twenty-fifth day of the climb. At dusk, because that was when it had happened the first time.

He went down the long stair to the first square on his own. He walked out into the middle of it, past the dry fountain, and stood there, twenty metres across, red walls on all four sides, four storeys of empty windows. He took the cloth off the rim of his shield.

He stood still and looked up at the rooftops. All four sides. Watching the parapets.

Then he took a breath, and did the thing he'd done in the Fold on the first night, and in every doorway of every dungeon for as long as he could remember. He brought the flat of his sword down on the rim of his shield as hard as he could.

*CLANG.*

It went off the red walls like a gunshot. It bounced from wall to wall, round and round the square, and up, and out over the silent city. The loudest sound on floor 4 in a very long time.

He stood absolutely still.

Nothing.

The echo faded. The dry wind came up the canyon. On a roof somewhere, a carrion bird shifted and went quiet.

And then, on his belt, very small, very clear, the little cracked bell rang.

*Once.*

It had turned.

He didn't move. He didn't look round. He kept his eyes on the parapets, all four sides, and waited. His heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth. Somewhere up there, on one of those rooftops, something tall and grey and silent had heard the clang. It had turned its head, and was coming.

He couldn't hear it. Nobody could.

He watched for the dust.

*Left.* A tiny puff, on the parapet of the west wall, drifting down. Then nothing. *Then.*

It came down the wall.

It didn't jump this time. It came down headfirst, like a spider, four storeys of sheer red stone, in a long fluid silent rush, so fast it was almost a blur. Tall. Thin. Grey as ash. Limbs too long, too many joints. And where a face should have been, nothing at all. A smooth grey blank, tilted toward him. Listening.

It hit the ground at the bottom of the wall without a sound and came across the square at him.

He'd known it was fast. Watching it kill five dust-hounds in a blink, he'd known. Knowing wasn't the same.

It reached him in three strides. He got the shield up on instinct, on the side where the dust had been, and something hit it so hard his whole arm went numb to the shoulder. He went back two metres across the stone. There'd been no sound. Not even when it hit the shield. The Vigil Shield had taken a blow that should have rung like a bell, and the Quiet had taken the sound out of it, somehow, the way it took the sound out of everything it touched.

It was gone before he could swing. Round to his left. He didn't see it go.

But he saw the dust.

A swirl of red dust off the ground to his left, where its long feet had touched. He turned and swung, and the plain Knight's sword went into something and came out again. It made no sound. But a long thin line of grey opened along the Quiet's side. It *staggered*. And for a fraction of a second it stood in front of him, tall and thin and faceless, and he could see it properly.

It had no jaw. No mouth. Nothing on the front of its head at all, only that smooth grey blank. But in the middle of its chest, under the thin grey skin, there was something moving. Something beating. Very fast.

Its heart.

The only thing in it that made any sound at all. And it was tucked away inside where nobody could hear it but the Quiet itself.

It came again. It hit him again. He felt something give in his ribs, and the bar at the edge of his vision dropped, and then rose again, slowly. The ember behind his breastbone was working, mending, from the inside, without his having to ask. *Paladin.* He'd have laughed if he'd had the breath.

"I can see the dust," said Ice. He stood up straight. He didn't put the shield up. "And I don't need to see it. It's coming to me."

He took a breath. And he did the only other thing he could think of. The opposite of the clang.

He went completely silent.

He stopped breathing. He stood absolutely still in the middle of the square. He let the light in his chest go down, all the way, until he was just a man standing in the dusk, making no sound at all.

The Quiet stopped.

He felt it stop. Somewhere in front of him, a few metres away, tall and grey in the red dusk. It had been coming toward the sound of him, his breathing, his heart, the scrape of his boots. And now there was nothing. No sound. And it didn't know where he was.

It tilted its blank head. Listening. It took one step, slowly, silently, toward where he'd been. Then another. Close. Very close. Close enough that he could see the thin grey skin of its chest moving with that fast hidden heartbeat.

He thought about Calvin by the dry fountain, with no arm. About Rachael, with her hands clamped over the end of it, holding the bleeding while she died. About Dennis, shouting her name.

He moved once.

Point. Not low, not under the jaw, because there wasn't one. Straight in. Into the middle of the chest, where the heart was. Where the only sound in the Quiet had been beating, all this time, hidden away where nobody could hear it.

The sword went in.

And for the first and last time, the Quiet made a sound.

***

It wasn't loud. It was a small sound, a long thin breath going out, like a door closing in another room. The tall grey shape folded down onto the stones of the square by the dry fountain, slowly, joint by joint, the way a crane folds. And it lay there, and the fast hidden beating in its chest slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

The square was very quiet. The ordinary kind of quiet. The kind with wind in it.

He stood over it for a long time.

Then he sat down on the edge of the dry fountain. Right where Calvin had been lying, the first time. He sat there with his sword across his knees, in the red dusk, and looked at the square, and breathed, and let the ember mend his ribs from the inside.

"It's done," said Alea, very softly.

"Yeah."

"It's dead. It can't—nobody's ever going to come down that stair and—"

"I know."

The fox appeared on the rim of the fountain beside him, small and teal and white in the red dusk, and looked at him with her two white pinprick eyes.

"Calvin's going to come down that stair one day," she said. "Talking. The whole way. And nothing's going to come off the roof."

Ice didn't say anything.

"And Rachael's going to come down after him," said Alea, "and tell him to shut up. And Dennis is going to be right behind her, complaining about the stairs."

He put his hands over his face.

He sat on the edge of the dry fountain in the first square of the Red City, where his friends had died the first time, and cried. Properly. For the first time since the hillside on floor 1, the first morning, when he'd sat down in the grass and shaken all the way down. He cried for Rachael and Dennis, who weren't dead. And for Calvin's arm, which wasn't gone. And for the thirty days of the first run, and the six silent weeks after, when nobody on floor 4 had laughed.

Alea sat beside him and didn't say anything. She'd learned that.

When he was done, he wiped his face on the sleeve of his white shirt, which was filthy now, red with dust. He looked up at the stair going up the canyon wall, to the door at the top, to two hundred and four steps, and floor 3, and thirty-five people in a swamp.

"Let them come down talking," he said.
