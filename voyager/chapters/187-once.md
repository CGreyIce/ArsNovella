Chris started a new notebook on the stair.

He hadn't meant to. The old one had two pages left, and he'd been saving them for floor 5, for no reason he could have explained, except that floor 5 was where the notebooks had stopped being a habit and started being the point. But somewhere around the hundred-and-fortieth step down from the Glass, in the dark, with Newish counting at the front of the line and Thea singing somewhere ahead of him, he'd found he didn't want to write floor 5 on the last two pages of anything. So he'd stopped on the step, and Maigo had bumped into the back of him, and he'd got the new one out of his pack by feel, and opened it on his knee, and written at the top of the first page in the dark without being able to see it:

*FLOOR 5. DAY 525. DOWN.*

It was his fifteenth. Fourteen of them were in the bottom of his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, in order, the first one at the very bottom. That was the one from Quill's on floor 1, grey, soft at the corners now, with his own handwriting in it from before he knew anything. He hadn't opened it in months. He knew roughly where everything in it was anyway. That was the other thing about writing everything down. After a while you didn't need to read it. It was just in you, in order, like a stair.

"Two hundred and seventy," said Newish, at the front. "Two hundred and seventy-one. Somebody's stopped."

"Me," said Chris. "Sorry."

"Write faster," said Winter, behind Maigo.

He put the notebook away and went on down.

At the bottom, Tiger put his hand flat on the door first, the way he did now. He held it there a moment, feeling for whatever he felt for. Then he pushed. And the wind came up the stair at them.

Chris had forgotten the wind.

Not forgotten. He'd written it down. *Day 95: the wind came down the stair at us. Cold, wet, smells of rain.* He could have told you which page. But the page didn't have the wind in it. The page didn't have the way it went straight past you up the stair into the dark behind, as if it had been waiting at the bottom for a year and a half for somebody to open the door and let it in somewhere. Cold. Clean. Smelling of rain and salt and, very faintly, of fish. After eight days of the Glass it was like being slapped awake by somebody who liked you.

Calvin, three people ahead of him, made a noise. Not a word. Just a noise, the kind you make getting into cold water.

Then they came out onto the crown of the Spindle in the first light, all thirty-six of them, one after another, and stood there.

***

The sun was coming up out of the cloud.

That was the first thing. It was the only thing, for a while. The floor of cloud a long way below them on every side, white and slow, going pink at the eastern edge, then gold, and then the rim of the sun coming up out of it like something being lifted out of milk. The light came across the cloud all at once and hit the islands. Hundreds of them. Little ones and big ones, rocks and grass and roped-down towns, hanging in the air at every height, with the thin dark threads of the bridges going between them in every direction and white water falling off their edges into nothing. All of it went gold together. It went gold from the top down. The Spindle first, because it was highest, so they were standing in sun before anything else was. Then the Kitecliff, off to the north-west, white cliffs going suddenly bright. Then Tether, the big island in the middle of the lower Reaches where the seven bridges met, its roofs and its roped chimneys and its hundred little kites.

Chris had seen it before. On the ninety-sixth day, from the little island at the top of the floor-4 stair, with the other thirty-four, crammed onto a rock the size of a house. He'd written it down. He knew exactly what he'd written, because it was the shortest entry in the whole first notebook. Two words. *First sun.* And then nothing, for the rest of the page, because he hadn't been able to think of anything else to put.

He got the fifteenth notebook out now, on the crown of the Spindle, in the wind. He held it against his knee so the pages wouldn't go. He wrote *Second sun.* And then he stood there with the pencil over the page for a while, the way he had the first time.

There were kites in the sky.

Seven of them. High. Higher than anything. Long grey kites with something hanging under each one, going round the Spindle in a wide slow ring in the morning light. Round and round, not flapping, never still. One of them, the smallest, kept breaking out of the ring and turning a loop, and going back in, and then doing it again, as if it couldn't help it.

"Pell," said Hyvred, beside him, softly. "That's Pell's lot. They're up."

"They're always up," said Ice.

He was at the edge of the crown, with Thea. He hadn't come out into the middle with everybody else. He'd come out of the arch and gone straight to the eastern edge and stood there with his back to the sunrise, looking the other way. South. Over Tether, over the bridges, at something out past the town that Chris couldn't make out from where he stood. A white rock, maybe. A crooked one.

"Always?" said Hyvred.

Ice didn't answer.

Chris looked round the crown. He'd been here once before as well. Day 110, the day the thirty-five killed the Squall, the first and only time. There'd been two dead ones on the crown that day already, huge and grey and half-spread, the ones Ice had left. Then there'd been three.

The three were still there. What was left of them. Bones, mostly. Long white bones with the wind going through them, a great keel of a breastbone, hooked beaks the size of doors lying open on the rock as if they were still surprised. But no feathers. Not one. The rain-coloured feathers, the soft grey-white ones that the wind had gone with, the thousands of them. Somebody had come and taken every single one.

He wrote that down too. *Three Squalls. Bones. No feathers. Somebody took them.* And under it, smaller: *No fourth.*

***

Higu had the rock out before anybody had finished looking at the sky.

She did it at the foot of every stair. She'd done it at Tell and the Salt and the Sea and the Bastion, at Highcrook and Wendmere and Evermorrow and the Glass. She sat down on the bottom step, or the nearest thing to one, and took the flat grey stone out of her pack, and wet her chalk. Three columns. *REMEMBERED. TRUE. NOW.* She'd drawn them on the Long Acre with her thumb, the night they decided to come down, and she'd kept the rock ever since. It had eight lines in the left-hand column now, one under the other, short and straight. Eight floors. Eight lines. Nothing written beside any of them.

"Ice," said Higu. She didn't look up. "What do you remember?"

"Nothing," said Ice. "I never c—"

He stopped.

Chris was writing. He'd been going to write the line. He'd written it eight times; he wrote it the way you write the date. *Higu: what do you remember. Ice: nothing, I never came down.* He'd got as far as *Ice: nothing* when he heard the gap, and looked up, and the pencil stayed where it was.

Ice was still at the eastern edge, looking south. He hadn't turned round. The wind had his hair. Thea was holding his hand, the way she held it on every stair, and she hadn't let go when they came out, and Chris saw her fingers move. Just once. A small tightening, the way you'd squeeze somebody's hand in a dark room so they'd know where you were.

"No," said Ice. "I did."

Nobody said anything.

Thirty-five people on the crown of the Spindle in the first sun, with the wind going round them, and seven kites going round above them, and nobody said a word. Calvin had his mouth open to say something about the wind and he shut it. Newish had stopped counting. Higu's chalk was over the rock and it stayed there.

"Once," said Ice.

He turned round. He looked at Higu, not at anybody else. He looked like a man reading something off a page he'd only just found in a book he'd owned for years.

"I'd forgotten," he said. "I came down once. To here. I said I never came down, at every one of them, and I meant it. I didn't remember." He was quiet a moment. "I don't remember the floors. Any of them. The way down. I didn't look at any of it. I was looking at somebody's back. The whole way down. In case he—"

He stopped again. He didn't finish that one.

"Gull Island," he said instead. "That's what I remember. That's all."

Higu looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at the rock. She drew the short line in the left-hand column, the way she had eight times, under the other eight. And then, beside it, for the first time since the Long Acre, she wrote something. Small, careful letters, in chalk, on a grey stone, in the wind.

*GULL ISLAND.*

"Do I change the others?" she said.

"No." Ice shook his head. "They're right. I don't remember any of it. That's still true."

Higu nodded slowly. She left them.

And Chris, standing in the middle of the crown with the fifteenth notebook open against his knee, found that he wasn't looking at Ice any more. He was looking at Niko.

Niko was by the arch, with his pack still on. He was looking south as well. Past Tether, past the bridges, at the crooked white rock that Chris still couldn't make out. He wasn't looking at Ice. He was very carefully not looking at Ice. It was the most careful not-looking Chris had ever seen anybody do, and he had been watching the thirty-five carefully not look at Ice for most of a year and a half. Niko's face didn't do anything at all. His thumb was on the hilt of his sword, on the dull place near the guard where it always went. It stayed there.

*Somebody's back*, Chris thought. *The whole way down. In case he.*

He didn't write that.

He closed the fifteenth notebook. He knelt down on the crown of the Spindle, in the sun, and opened his pack, and went down through it to the very bottom, past the food and the spare shirt and the oilcloth, to the first one. The grey one from Quill's. He didn't have to look for the page. It fell open there. It always had.

*HOW?*

In his own capitals, from the fourteenth day. And under it the list, the one he'd started on floor 1 and taken to Thea on floor 5, that went most of the way down the page and then stopped, because he'd moved it to other notebooks under other names after that, and never come back here. *The note, before anybody saw floor 1. The chapel already kept. The lantern left ready. A day early. The Paladin by day 18. He fights things he's never seen like he's seen them.* Twenty-two lines. Three left at the bottom of the page, empty, for more than four hundred days.

He wrote on the first of them, small, so he'd have room.

*Day 525. "I came down once. To here." Gull Island.*

Then he looked at the two lines that were left. And he didn't write anything on them. He shut the grey notebook and wrapped it in its oilcloth, and put it back at the bottom of his pack, where it lived.

When he stood up, Higu was writing in the middle column. Calvin had finally said it.

"It's *windy*."

"That's true," said Higu.

*WIND*, in the middle. Underlined once. And the sun came the rest of the way up out of the cloud, and the kites came down.

***

They came down the way the wind came, all at once and from everywhere.

One moment the seven of them were going round above the Spindle in the wide high ring. The next, the ring had tightened, and dropped, and they were coming in low over the crown with the sound of canvas, like sheets on a line in a gale, and the thirty-six ducked without meaning to. Seven long grey kites came round the crown at head height, once, twice, and then one after another they turned into the wind, stalled, and dropped onto the rock as lightly as gulls landing.

Kite-folk. Chris had seen them twice before, and only at a distance, on the Kitecliff. Close, they were stranger and finer than he remembered. Tall, and very thin, with long arms and high narrow shoulders. Small grey feathers grew along the backs of their arms, across their shoulder blades, up the backs of their necks to the white down they had for hair. Their eyes were large and dark and wide-set. They wore long grey coats slit for the feathers, and the kites were strapped along their arms, so that when they stood on the rock with their arms down the kites folded behind them like great grey cloaks.

The kites weren't plain grey any more.

That was what Chris saw first, and wrote, later, in three underlined words. They'd been grey canvas, when he saw them last. Now every one of them was feathered. Edged and backed and sewn all over with long soft grey-white feathers, rain-coloured, the colour of weather. Thousands of them. Each kite had been made new.

The oldest of the seven came forward. Chris knew who it was from Ice's description, given once, over a fire, a long time ago, when Hyvred had asked. A face like old paper. A voice like wind in grass. *They*, Ice had said, when he talked about Pell. *I couldn't tell. It doesn't matter, up there.*

Pell looked round at the thirty-six. They took their time. Their dark eyes went from face to face, unhurried, the way you'd look at a sky to see what it was going to do. Then they found Ice, at the eastern edge, and stopped.

"You came down," said Pell.

"Yes."

"Nobody comes down." Pell said it without any surprise at all. As if they were saying it was windy. "My grandmother said one day somebody would. She was wrong about most things. I'll have to tell her she was right about one." They looked at the bones on the crown. Then up, at the kites on their own people's backs. "It stayed dead."

"Since the last time?" said Higu.

"Since the last time." Pell nodded at the bones. "Three. Then none. We waited for it every morning, the first month. Then we stopped waiting." They looked at Ice again. "We've flown every day since. Four hundred and fifteen days. I counted. I didn't mean to. You start counting a thing like that and you can't stop."

"I know," said Ice.

"Yes," said Pell. "I thought you might."

The smallest of the seven had come up beside them. Not small, really. Taller than Chris already. A boy, or nearly not one, with his arm-feathers all the way in now, grey and sleek, right down to the wrist. The one who'd been turning loops. He was staring at Ice with his whole face.

"You're the one who leaned," said the boy.

"Brin," said Pell.

"He is, though. Pell. He is." The boy didn't take his eyes off Ice. "You fell off the post four hundred times. I counted that too. I was *little*."

Ice looked at him. Something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"You've got your feathers," he said.

"I've had them since the summer." The boy held up his arms, both of them, kite and all, so the feathered kite opened out behind him in the wind, rain-coloured, and the wind nearly took him off his feet. He leaned. Not even thinking about it. Leaned into the gust like you'd lean on a wall, and stayed exactly where he was. "I can go to Tether and back before breakfast. My great-grandmother could do that. Pell says."

"She could," said Pell.

"I *can*."

Chris wrote it down. *Brin. Tether and back before breakfast.* He didn't know why. It felt like something that should be written down by somebody.

"The feathers," said Hyvred. "On the kites. Are they—"

"Its." Pell looked at the bones. "We took them in the spring. When we knew it wasn't coming back. They're good feathers. They knew the wind better than we did." They paused. "It seemed a waste. To leave them."

"It's beautiful," said Hyvred.

"Yes," said Pell, as if she'd said it was windy, and turned back to Ice.

"There's somebody on floor 1," said Ice. He said it without being asked. Chris had seen him do that more and more, since the Long Barn. Just say the thing. "An old woman. With an inn. She's going to be ill this winter. We're going down to sit with her."

Pell looked at him a long time with those large dark eyes.

Then they reached into their long grey coat and brought out a little twist of cloth, tied with grass. They held it out. Ice took it. Chris could smell it from where he stood, even in the wind. Rain and pepper.

"For her eggs," said Pell. "The morning she gets up."

Ice stood there with it in his hand.

"Thank you," he said.

"You can't thank people up here," said Pell. "It just blows away." But they looked pleased, in a dry, papery way. "Go on down. We'll come with you."

***

They went down the Spindle the way Ice had gone up it, the first time, by the spiral path. A ledge cut round and round the outside of the needle from the crown to the foot, five hundred metres, with iron rings sunk into the rock at the inside edge every few steps for your hands, and nothing at all at the outside edge. You went round, and round, and down, holding the rings, and the wind came at you from a different side every time you turned the corner.

Chris had been frightened of it the first time. He remembered being frightened. He'd written it down on the evening of day 110, in his bunk at the inn, with his hands still not right: *the path. I didn't look down once. Niko did and laughed. I don't understand Niko.*

He wasn't frightened of it now. He noticed that, on the third turn, and stopped for a moment with his hand on a ring to be sure. No. He wasn't. He'd been up nine floors since, and down nine, and he'd stood on the Grey with nothing under the snow, and the path was just a path. He went on.

And the kites came down with them.

All seven, the whole way. They didn't land again. They went round the Spindle as the thirty-six went round it, a little out from the rock, a little below, so that whenever you looked down past your own boots, past the edge, past the long fall to the cloud, there was a kite there. Grey and feathered. Leaning on nothing. Keeping pace. Round and round, the whole five hundred metres, all morning, not saying anything. Just there. In case.

Nobody said anything about it. Nobody needed to. Chris wrote one line, on a ledge at the halfway point where the whole line stopped for water.

*The kites came down with us. All the way. In case.*

Winter read it over his shoulder. She'd been reading over his shoulder since floor 1. He'd stopped minding about four days into it.

"They're showing off," she said.

"They're keeping us company."

"Same thing, for them." But she was watching the boy, Brin, going round under the ledge in a long slow curve with his feathered arms spread. She liked the cold. The wind up here was the coldest thing since the Grey, and she had her face up into it with her eyes half shut and her silver hair going everywhere. "I'd do it," she said. "If I could. I'd never come down."

"I know."

"Don't write that."

He didn't.

Maigo was behind him with his sunglasses on. He'd had them on since the Glass and showed no sign of taking them off; you couldn't see his eyes, but he was smiling at the rings. Maigo always liked iron that had been somewhere a long time. Ina was at the back of the party, with his oni mask pushed right up on the top of his head and his hands in the rings and his face turned out to the sky, very quiet. And Niko was in front of Chris, between him and Maigo, and Niko hadn't said a word since the crown.

He climbed down fine. He held the rings fine. Every time the path turned south, and the crooked white rock came round into view beyond Tether, his head turned toward it, and stayed turned until the path took it away again. Every time. Chris counted. Eleven times, on the way down.

He didn't write that either. He was starting to have a lot of things he didn't write.

***

They came into Tether in the early afternoon over the long north bridge, and the town came out to look at them.

It was the way Chris remembered it, and it wasn't. The roofs were still roped. Every house in Tether was tied down, from the roof to iron rings in the rock, from door to door, from chimney to chimney, the whole town laced up like a boot, so that when the big winds came nothing went anywhere. The kites still flew from the roofs, long thin many-coloured ones, rattling and humming. The streets still smelled of tar and salt fish and woodsmoke. There were still gulls on every rope.

But the kites on the roofs had feathers on them too. Not many. A few on each. Grey-white, sewn in along the edges. As if the whole town had heard what the kite-folk had done with the Squall and wanted a bit of it for itself.

And the people stood in their doorways, holding on to their ropes, and watched thirty-six climbers come in off the north bridge, and nobody shouted.

Chris had got used to the shouting. *THEY CAME BACK*, all the way down. Orlo on the Wellgate in Tell, dropping his broom. Tansy in the treetops. Nearly every floor had shouted. Tether didn't. Tether was a town that lived in the wind, and it didn't shout, because the wind took shouting and threw it into the cloud. It just watched them come. A woman with a basket of fish on her hip. An old man mending a rope. Two children holding on to the same doorframe. And then, as they passed, one after another, the people of Tether lifted a hand off their rope, just for a moment, and put it back.

That was all. One hand. Off the rope and back.

"Oh," said Bee, very quietly, behind him.

Chris wrote it down.

The gulls found Aleaka before they were halfway down the main street.

Forty of them, maybe more. They came off the roof ridges and the ropes all at once in a white screaming cloud and dropped round her on the cobbles, on her shoulders, on Sleet, who sat down and endured it with the look of a dog who had been through this before. They screamed at her. They walked over her boots. One of them sat on her head.

"You remember me," said Aleaka, slowly, delighted. "You *remember* me."

"They're gulls," said Newish. "They don't remember anything. They just like you."

"Same thing," said Aleaka, and walked the rest of the way to the ferry with a gull on her head and the rest of them following her in a long white shouting train, and the whole town of Tether, for the first time, laughed.

***

Nan Gully was at her winch.

She'd been at her winch, Chris thought, for most of her life, and she'd probably be at it the day she died. The chain-ferry ran from the edge of Tether out across the air to a far blue island, a wicker basket big enough for two on an iron cable, and Nan Gully sat on a stool beside the great iron wheel that wound it, in four shawls, with a pipe. She was smaller than he remembered. Walnut-brown, and thinner, and slower. There was a gull living on her hat. It might have been the same gull. It looked at them with the same expression.

She didn't get up. She looked at them come along the edge toward her, all thirty-six, and took the pipe out of her mouth.

Then she sang. Not loudly. The wind took most of it, and she let it. But Chris knew the words. He'd written them down the first week, in the first notebook, and he knew they were five generations old, from her nan's nan, and that she'd never once in her life written them down herself.

*They came up the stair with their lights in their hands,*
*and they asked for the way to the top.*
*They went on to the sky, and they never came by,*
*and we never knew where they'd stop.*

She stopped. She looked at them, standing in a crowd at the edge of Tether in front of her winch.

"Song never says anybody came back down," she said.

"It does now," said Calvin.

"Does it." Nan Gully put her pipe back in her mouth and looked at him over it. "Who are you?"

"Calvin."

"Calvin." She considered him. "No. Doesn't scan."

Dennis had pushed through to the front. Chris had known he would. Dennis had been on floor 5 for twenty-five days, the first time, and he'd haggled with Nan Gully on eleven of them, and lost every one, and had talked about her for a month after with the respect of one professional for another.

"What's the fare?" said Dennis.

Nan Gully took the pipe out again and looked at him for a long moment.

"You're the one who haggles."

"I'm the one who haggles," said Dennis. "What's the fare? To the blue island. And back."

"You don't want to go to the blue island. Nobody wants to go to the blue island. There's nothing on it but sheep."

"I'm not going. What's the fare?"

She told him. It was two coppers. It had been two coppers the first time, and Dennis had tried to get it to one and a half for most of a month.

Dennis opened his purse. He counted out seventy-two coppers, one at a time, into her brown palm, and closed her fingers over them.

Nan Gully looked at the coppers. Then at him.

"That's thirty-six," said Dennis. "There and back. For us." He opened the purse again. And he counted out sixty more, one at a time, slowly, into her other hand. "And that's thirty. There and back."

"Thirty what?"

"Thirty of them." Dennis nodded at the sky. At the Spindle. At the top. "The ones in your song. That went up and never came by. I heard there were thirty." He did up the purse. "It's in the second verse. They never paid the ferry. Somebody should."

Nan Gully sat on her stool with her hands full of copper and didn't say anything at all.

The gull on her hat looked at Dennis. Nan Gully looked at him. Behind Dennis, thirty-five people stood in the wind and didn't make a sound. Chris didn't write anything. He couldn't have. His hands had stopped.

"No tab," said Dennis. "I don't do tabs any more."

"He doesn't," said Rachael. "It's a whole thing. Don't ask."

Nan Gully closed both hands. She put the copper away somewhere in the four shawls, slowly, all of it. Then she looked up past Dennis, through the crowd, and found Ice. She didn't have to search. Chris thought she'd known exactly where he was standing since they came along the edge.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"Did you tell him yourself?"

Ice looked at her. Then, without turning his head, he reached out behind him with one hand. Gabe was there. Gabe was always there. He took Ice's hand without looking, the way they did now, and stood there holding it, enormous, in the wolf-fur coat, slightly bewildered.

"Yes," said Ice.

"Tell me what?" said Gabe.

"Lean," said Ice.

Nan Gully looked from one of them to the other for a long time. At the hands.

"Good," she said. "I'd have been cross."

And she put her pipe back in her mouth, and sat back on her stool, and looked out at the sky, and started humming. Not the old verses. Something else. A tune without words yet. Trying it out.

Chris got the notebook out. He didn't have anything to write. He held the pencil over the page and listened to an old woman at a winch at the edge of the sky, making a new verse for a song that was older than anybody she'd ever known, and he understood, for the first time since the white room, that he couldn't write down everything. Some things you had to just hear.

***

They slept in the inn. The same inn. The same long room with the lamps and the tables and the kites humming on the roof, and the slate by the door that the innkeeper used for ferry times and the price of fish, where Drew had once written *HIDDEN QUESTS. IF IT'S STRANGE, WRITE IT HERE.* It had been wiped since. Of course it had. It said *GULL EGGS 3 FOR A COPPER* now, and *FERRY STOPS AT DUSK*.

Ice didn't come in.

He sat outside on the rock at the south edge of the town, where you could see across the two bridges to Gull Island, and watched it. Thea sat with him. After a while Gabe went out and sat on his other side. After a longer while Chris went out too, with a lamp, and sat a little way off with his notebook on his knee, and didn't pretend he wasn't watching, because nobody would have believed him.

Gull Island was a crooked white tooth of rock two bridges south, sticking up out of the cloud. White with gulls. On the very top, above the nests, a small dark shape. The tower. You could just see it, in the last of the light, squat and round.

Ice was looking at the top of it. At the chimney.

There was no smoke.

There couldn't have been, Chris told himself. Not that you'd see. The wind on the bridges this evening was the kind that laid the kite-strings flat and made the roped houses creak. You could have had a bonfire on the top of Gull Island and the wind would have taken the smoke sideways into the cloud before it was a hand above the chimney. You couldn't tell anything from here. You couldn't tell at all.

He wrote, very small: *Can't tell from here.*

Ice didn't move. He didn't say anything. He sat with his arms round his knees, the way he'd sat at the bowl on six, with his eyes open, looking. Once his lips moved. Chris couldn't hear it. Thea could. She didn't say what it was.

When it was dark, Higu came out with the rock under her arm.

"Morning," she said. Just that. Not an order. A where.

Ice looked at the dark where the tower was. Then at her.

"Morning," he said.

Higu nodded and went back in. Chris wrote that down. *Higu: morning. Ice: morning.* It was the first time Chris had ever seen Ice agree to wait for something he wanted to go and see. On floor 1, on floor 3, on every floor all the way up, he'd have gone across those bridges in the dark. Alone. Without telling anybody.

He didn't go. He sat on the rock all night with his eyes open, and Thea asleep against his shoulder, and Gabe asleep against the other one like a fallen wardrobe. And in the morning, when Chris came out with the first light, Ice was in exactly the same place, and his face was grey, and he stood up as soon as he saw Chris, as if he'd been waiting for permission.

***

Higu sent six of them.

Chris's party, all five, because it was Niko's, and the book was Niko's, and *it counts from here*, Higu said, the class, whatever the old woman had for him, if she had anything. It counts from where it opens. *Five*, she said, and looked at Niko the way she'd looked at the rock on the crown. *Do it on five. We'll wait. Four days. I've counted.*

And Aleaka, because the gulls were going anyway, and so was she.

And Ice. Higu didn't send Ice. Ice went. Thea went with him, because his hand was in hers, and that was where his hand went now, on stairs and bridges and anywhere else that went down.

The first bridge was long and swung in the wind, and they went across it single file with their hands on the rail-chains. The gulls went over them in a white river, screaming, toward the island. On the second bridge the smell hit, the way it had the first time, and Winter put her sleeve over her nose and said exactly the same thing she'd said the first time, word for word, which made Maigo laugh. Ina put his mask down. Niko walked in front. He walked a little faster than anybody else, and nobody asked him to slow down.

Halfway across the second bridge, Chris could see the top of the island properly.

The tower. The slate roof. The single door.

The clear ring round the door, where the gulls didn't nest.

It was swept.

He saw it, and let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding. Swept clean. Bare grey rock in a perfect circle, without a feather on it, without a gull on it, with the nests coming right up to its edge all round and stopping as if at a wall. Somebody swept that every morning. Somebody had swept it this morning.

But there was no smoke.

It was a still morning. The first still morning since they'd come down. The wind had dropped in the night and the gulls were the only thing moving, and the kites on Tether's roofs behind them hung slack on their strings. On a morning like this, smoke went straight up. You'd have seen it from Tether. You'd have seen it from the Spindle.

There was no smoke from the chimney.

Ice stopped.

Right in the middle of the second bridge, on the planks, over two hundred metres of nothing. Thea stopped with him because her hand was in his. Chris, behind them, nearly walked into them. And Ice stood there and looked up at the chimney with no smoke coming out of it, and his face stopped. Chris had seen it do that once before, in the Long Barn, at the first snow. Like a clock stopping.

"Go on," said Ice.

Niko had stopped too. A little way ahead, at the foot of the island, where the path started up between the nests. He turned round.

"It's yours," said Ice. His voice was perfectly level. "Go on, Niko. I'll be here."

Niko looked at him for one second. Just one. The first time he'd looked straight at Ice since the crown. Then he turned and went up the path.

***

Chris went to the foot of the path with the others and stopped there. It wasn't his. He knew that much.

Niko went up alone. Between the nests, with the gulls screaming at him from every ledge, up the path cut in the white rock, small and straight-backed with his sword at his hip and his pack on his back. He didn't hurry. He went up the way he went up everything, steadily, patiently, one foot and then the next. He came out at the top into the swept ring. He crossed it. He stood in front of the door.

He knocked.

Nothing.

He knocked again. Chris heard it from the foot of the path, small and flat over the gulls.

Nothing.

Niko stood there for a moment. Then he put his hand on the latch, and the door swung in, and he went inside.

He was in there a long time.

Chris stood at the foot of the path with Winter on one side and Maigo on the other and Ina and Aleaka behind, and counted. He couldn't help it. A minute. Two. The gulls went on screaming. The cloud went on lying there far below, white and slow. Behind them, on the second bridge, Ice was standing where he'd stopped, with Thea's hand in his, and hadn't moved.

Three minutes.

Niko came back out into the doorway.

He stood there and looked down the path at them. And then past them, at the bridge. At Ice. And he shook his head.

Not *no*. Chris saw that, even from there. It wasn't that kind of shake. It was smaller and stranger than that. It was the shake you give when you've gone into a room for somebody and they aren't in it. *She's not here.* The fire out. The chair empty. The bed made. Nobody.

Behind Chris, on the bridge, Ice sat down.

He didn't fall. He just sat. His legs folded and he sat down on the planks in the middle of the second bridge, two hundred metres above the cloud, with his back against the rail-chain and Thea's hand still in his, so that she came down with him onto her knees. He sat there with his face completely still and his eyes on the tower, and didn't make a sound. Thea let go of his hand, and put both hers on his face, and turned it toward her, and said something. Chris couldn't hear what. Ice didn't seem to hear it either. His eyes went past her, back up to the empty door.

*In her chair*, Chris thought. He didn't know where the thought came from. He hadn't written it anywhere. It was just there, in the shape of the way Ice was sitting. *Waiting for something.*

And then, from somewhere below them, from round the side of the island where the white cliff dropped away into the cloud, a voice said:

"Get out of my house."

***

She came up over the edge of the cliff on a rope.

Hand over hand. There was a rope there, Chris saw now, tied off to an iron ring at the edge of the swept circle, going down over the lip of the rock into the nests below. And up it, hand over hand, slow and steady and absolutely furious, came a very old woman with a long white braid down her back and a basket on her shoulders. She came up over the edge on her knees, and then onto her feet, and stood up straight as a post on the rim of her own swept ring, with gull feathers in her hair and gull muck on her coat and a basket full of eggs on her back, and looked at Niko standing in her doorway.

She was older. Chris could see it from the foot of the path. She was thinner, and the cliff of her face had more lines in it, and her shoulders under the basket were the shoulders of somebody who had been carrying things up ropes for a very long time. But she stood the way she'd stood the first time. As if nothing in the world had ever made her bend. One eye milky. The other one sharp and grey and fixed on Niko like the point of a sword.

"Huh," said Hessa.

Niko stood in the door of her tower and didn't say anything at all.

"You came down," said Hessa.

"Yes," said Niko.

"I was getting eggs." She swung the basket off her back and set it down on the swept rock, carefully, the way you'd set down something that had taken all morning. "I get eggs in the morning. Every morning. I light the fire when I get back. Any fool knows that." She looked at the open door behind him. "And then I come up the rope and there's a boy in my house."

"Sorry," said Niko.

"You should be." She looked at him for a long time with her one good eye. Then something in the cliff of her face shifted, a little, the way it had once before, the first time, at the door. "You're taller."

"I'm not."

"You stand taller, then. It's the same thing, from where I'm standing." She sniffed. "You took your time."

And then she looked past him. Down the path. At the seven of them at the foot of it, and the gulls, and the bridge. At the man sitting on the planks in the middle of the second bridge with his back against the chain.

She looked at him for a long time.

Ice got up. Chris didn't see him do it. One moment he was sitting, and the next he was standing, with Thea's hand in his again, and he came along the bridge and off the end of it and up through them, past Chris and Winter and Maigo, past Ina and Aleaka, and up the path between the nests, not fast. Thea came with him. She had to. He didn't let go. He came up into the swept ring and stopped on the edge of it, and stood there in front of the old woman with the basket of eggs at her feet.

"You," said Hessa. "Barrel lid."

"Hello," said Ice.

She looked at him. She took her time. Her sharp grey eye went over his face slowly, the way it had gone over Niko's on the first day, from the top down. It took in the scar across his left palm, that hadn't been there the first time, and the girl holding his other hand. And it came back to his eyes and stayed there.

"You've stopped looking at me like that," said Hessa.

"Like what?"

"Like you'd been to my funeral."

The gulls screamed. The cloud lay below. Chris stood at the foot of the path with his notebook shut in his hand and didn't open it.

"Yes," said Ice.

That was all. Just *yes*. Chris turned it over afterwards and couldn't make it come out one way or the other. *Yes, I've stopped.* Or *yes, I had.* He thought, maybe, it was both. He thought Hessa thought so too. She looked at Ice a moment longer, with her one eye, and nodded, very slightly, as if he'd answered something she hadn't asked out loud. Then she bent down and picked up her basket.

"Well," she said, to Niko. "Have you written it?"

Niko was still in the doorway.

"No," he said.

"Good," said Hessa. "Neither did I." She came across the swept ring toward him, with the eggs, and stopped in front of him, and held the basket out until he took it. "Come in. Light my fire. Wipe your feet." She looked over her shoulder, down the path, at the rest of them. At Chris. "All of you. Not the gulls."

She went in.

Niko stood in the doorway holding a basket of gull eggs. He looked down at Ice, standing on the edge of the ring. Ice looked back. Neither of them said anything. Then Niko turned and went in after her, and a moment later Chris heard the scrape of a flint, and then another, and then a third.

And a thin grey line of smoke came up out of the chimney into the still morning, straight up, the way smoke does when there's no wind, and went on going up, higher than the tower, higher than the island, until you could have seen it from Tether. Until you could have seen it from the top of the Spindle.

Ice stood and watched it go up.

Chris opened the notebook. He wrote one word, and underlined it twice, the way Higu would have.

*ALIVE.*
