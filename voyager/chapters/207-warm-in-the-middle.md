Tuli talked to Ina because he didn't talk back.

He worked that out by the middle of the first morning. They were walking down the river on the ice in a long line, the seven of them: Chris at the front with his shield on his arm and his notebook in his coat; Sabe beside him with her black pole, putting it down every twenty steps, *tock*, and listening; Niko; Maigo, with his sunglasses on against the glare and his four leaves; Winter; then Tuli; then Ina, at the back. And Tuli, who had decided by the end of the first hour that Chris asked too many questions, and Niko was too quiet in the wrong way, and Maigo was strange, and Winter was frightening, had dropped back to walk beside Ina.

And talked.

About the ice. About her gran. About the Float, and the summer moorings, and a boy at Keddle called Ennis who could stand on his hands on a moving raft and was, in Tuli's opinion, an idiot. About her pole, which had been her mother's, who'd gone through the ice four springs ago at the Narrows and not come back up. She said that last part the same way she said the part about Ennis. Flat. Fast. Looking straight ahead.

Ina didn't say anything.

He didn't say anything to the part about Ennis, or the pole, or the mother. He walked beside her on the ice in the cold gold morning with his hands in his pockets and his mask pushed up on his head, and listened. That was all. And every so often he'd turn his head and look at her, so she'd know he was still listening. And she went on talking.

At noon they stopped, and Sabe put her pole down and listened for a long time, and Tuli went and sat next to her gran. Winter dropped back to walk beside Ina instead.

"She's told you about her mother," said Winter.

"Yes."

"She hasn't told Chris. Chris asked her about the pole, an hour ago. Where she got it. She said *it's a pole*." Winter was looking ahead at Tuli's back. "She told you."

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't." Winter glanced at him. Her silver hair was blowing everywhere. She liked fifteen. It was the first floor since fourteen's snow that was cold enough for her, and she'd been almost cheerful for a week, which on Winter looked like being slightly less sharp. "That's why."

They walked.

"You do that," said Winter. "With everybody. You did it with Calvin on twelve. With me." She was quiet. "People tell you things."

"I don't know why."

"Because you're on the step," said Winter. "And everybody knows you're on the step. And nobody on the step ever asks." She looked away. "So they tell you instead. To see if you'll keep it."

Ina thought about that.

"I'll keep it," he said.

"I know you will," said Winter. "That's the other reason."

***

The Narrows were black.

He saw them first on the second morning, coming round the last bend of the valley in the low sun. The hills on either side, which had been low and brown and soft all the way down, got higher. Steeper. Then not hills at all. Cliffs. Black rock, wet and cracked, going up out of the river on both sides like the walls of a house, higher than the Spindle on five, closer and closer together, until the whole mile-wide white road of the frozen river ran in between them and narrowed and narrowed and went through a gap.

A quarter-mile. Haddo had said. It looked less. It looked like a door.

And in the door, the ice.

It wasn't flat any more, the river. Not here. Here, in the last mile before the gap, the ice had come up. Piled. Slab on slab, broken and tilted and heaved, grey and white and black, every piece the size of a house, a field, a barn, thrown up on each other in a long jagged wall right across the river from cliff to cliff. Already. Before the first word. Sabe said it did that every spring, a bit; the ice from upstream pushed down all winter, slow, and piled at the gap. *This is nothing*, Sabe said. *This is the doorstep. Wait till she wakes.*

They camped on a ledge in the left-hand cliff, high up, above the wall of ice, where a long-ago rockfall had left a shelf of stone big enough for seven and a fire. You could look down from it into the Narrows. Into the gap. You could see the piled ice, and beyond it, through the door, the river going on below, flat and white, toward a far brightness that Sabe said was the summer moorings, the reed-bays, where the Float went every year if it went anywhere.

Sabe put her pole down on the rock of the ledge.

*Tock.*

Rock, not ice. It shouldn't have told her anything. She tipped her head anyway, and listened, with her milky eyes shut, for a long time. Then she held the pole out to Ina.

He looked at her.

"Put your hand on it," said Sabe. "Low. Near the rock."

He put his hand on the pole, near the iron point, where it met the stone.

And felt it.

Not a sound. He couldn't have heard it with his ears. It came up through the rock of the cliff, and the iron point, and the willow, into his palm. Slow. Deep. Enormously slow. A long pause. And then something, very far down, very big, going *once*. Like a door closing in a house the size of a mountain. And then a long pause again.

And then *once*.

He took his hand off the pole.

"That's it," said Sabe. "That's what we hear. From the Float. From Keddle. Gran heard it from a day off." She took the pole back. "It's slow. It's asleep." She looked down into the Narrows, at the piled wall of ice. "It's under that."

"It's a heart," said Ina.

Sabe looked at him.

"It's a heart," he said again. He didn't know how he knew. He'd felt a lot of things under his palms. Salt and snow and a frozen straw man's neck, a man-sized patch of winter ground over Gaffer Tobin's grave. Maigo's foot, on the Salt, when he went too deep. He knew what was alive under his hands and what wasn't. "It's asleep. But it's a heart."

Sabe looked at him for a long time with her milky eyes.

"Gran said that," she said. "Her gran said it to her. *It's a heart.*" She looked down at the ice. "Nobody believed her."

***

Chris did his columns by the fire that night. He did them every night; it was how he slept. *THE NARROWS. THE JAM. THE BREAKER (?). WHAT WE KNOW.* The last column was short. *It piles every year. Some years it holds too long. It has a heart. (Ina, Sabe's gran.) You let it go. (Sabe.) Early. Bit at a time. Nobody's ever done it.*

"That's not much," said Niko. He was reading over Chris's shoulder. He read over everybody's shoulder; he always had.

"It's what we've got," said Chris. "Higu says that's what a map is."

"It's a map of nothing."

"It's a map of what we know," said Chris. "We don't know anything. So it's a small map." He looked down at the ice. "We'll make it bigger."

Ina sat at the edge of the ledge with his legs over the drop and looked down at the wall of ice.

It was very quiet, at night, at the Narrows. Not like the Float. The ice didn't talk here the way it did up the valley, all the time, ticks and creaks. It was too piled. Too packed. It just sat there in the dark between the black cliffs, and every so often, from somewhere deep in it, a long low groan came up. And under the groan, if you put your hand on the rock of the ledge, the heart. *Once.* A long wait. *Once.*

He put his hand on the rock and left it there.

He was good at waiting. He'd been on the step since floor 9. Since before. He'd sat on the step with Hana and Maigo and Calvin and listened to people not say things for two years and never once picked at any of it. He didn't mind. He'd always been like that, since he was small. People told you things if you waited. Things told you things.

The heart went *once*.

He waited.

And something else came up through the rock into his palm. Not the heart. Something under the heart, or round it. Faint. Very faint. He almost didn't feel it.

Warm.

He took his hand off the rock and looked at it.

Then he put it back. And pulled his mask down.

He didn't do that lightly. Everybody knew. *Not unless someone would die.* That had been his rule since floor 5, since a hard trial on the Kitecliff in a gale, where he'd kept the kite-folk warm until morning with a fire they were afraid of. The grey oni mask with its horns and its long carved grin turned his fire white-blue and made people step back. It made Tuli, across the fire, sit up and stare. He pulled it down anyway. Because with the mask down he could feel heat better. Further. Through things.

He put his palm flat on the rock and let the fire go down into it. Not much. A thread of it. White-blue. Into the stone of the cliff, and down, through the rock, toward the ice, toward the heart, feeling for heat the way you'd feel for a draught in a dark room with the back of your hand.

Cold. The rock. Colder. The ice, where the cliff met it. Cold all the way down, cold as the bottom of a well, cold as fifteen, cold as the inside of the world, the way Ice had said the river was cold when he went under it after Calvin. Cold, and cold, and cold.

And in the middle of it, deep under the piled wall, where the heart was: warm.

Not much. A small warm place. Like a hand cupped round a candle in a cold room. Like the one warm stone in a hearth after the fire's gone out. In the middle of all that cold. Beating. *Once.*

He sat on the ledge for a long time with his mask down and his hand on the rock.

"Ina," said Winter. Behind him. Quietly. She'd seen the mask come down. "What is it?"

He took his hand away. He pushed the mask up.

"It's warm," he said. "In the middle."

Winter looked at him.

"The jam," said Ina. "The Breaker. Whatever it is. It's all cold. Everywhere. Except in the middle. Round the heart." He looked down at the ice in the dark. "It's warm in the middle."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know." He thought about it. "It means it's alive. All the way through. Not just a heart." He looked at his palm. "Things that are alive are warm in the middle. Even in winter. Even trees. Maigo says."

Maigo, by the fire, opened his eyes. He'd been asleep, or nearly.

"They are," said Maigo.

***

The first word came at night.

They felt it. Ina was asleep, for once, with his back against the cliff and Winter asleep a little way off and Tuli between them, rolled in her furs like a caterpillar. He felt it in the rock at his back before he heard anything. A shock. Up through the cliff, through the ledge, into his spine. The whole Narrows jumped. Stones came down off the cliff above them and clattered off the ledge into the dark.

Then the sound arrived. From far up the valley, miles. A crack like the sky splitting. And behind it, rising, filling the valley, rolling down toward them between the hills: a roar.

Everybody was on their feet. Sabe was at the edge of the ledge with her pole down on the rock and her whole body bent over it, listening. Tuli was beside her.

"She's awake," said Sabe. "The first word."

"She's *early*," said Tuli. Her voice cracked. "Gran, she's—"

"I know."

The roar came down the valley. Ina could see it now. In the dark, by starlight, a long way up the river: the ice moving. All of it. The whole white mile-wide road of it, from bank to bank, as far as he could see, heaving and breaking and turning over, slabs the size of fields rising up on edge and going over and down, the sound of it like every door in the world slamming at once. Coming down the valley. Toward the Narrows. Toward the door.

Toward the wall of piled ice under the ledge.

"Back," said Chris. "Everybody back. Against the cliff. *Now*."

They got back against the cliff.

The ice hit the jam.

Ina would never be able to tell it, afterwards. Not properly. Calvin asked him, on sixteen, and he tried, and stopped. It was a sound and a weight and a shaking that went on and on, the whole of the river's winter coming down at once and hitting the wall of piled ice in the Narrows and going *up*. Slabs riding up over slabs. Ice the size of houses going up into the dark, higher than the ledge, higher than the cliffs, and falling back. The cliffs themselves shaking. The ledge under them shaking. Stones coming down. Spray, freezing as it fell, coming down on them like hail. Tuli screaming, not in fear, Ina thought, but at it, the way you'd scream at a storm. Winter's hand on his arm. Maigo with his bare feet flat on the ledge and his eyes shut and roots going down into the rock, holding on.

And then it stopped.

Not stopped. Held. The roar went on, up the valley, behind. But here, at the Narrows, the wall of ice had stopped moving. It stood there in the dark. Huge. Higher than it had been, twice as high, three times, a jagged mountain of broken ice from cliff to cliff, packed and piled and groaning. Holding everything behind it. The whole river, backed up, pressing.

And holding it, something moved.

***

He saw it because he was looking.

Everybody else was looking up the valley, at the roar. At the river behind the wall, rising; you could see it rising, already, a dark line of water coming up along the foot of the jam. Ina was looking down. At the wall itself. At the ice right under the ledge.

At the place where the ice met the cliff.

Something was in the crack.

Something long and pale and enormous, the colour of old ice, the colour of the floe-hounds, nearly white. It came out of the piled jam where the ice met the black rock of the cliff, and went *into* the cliff. Into a crack in the rock. Deep. Like an arm going into a sleeve. Like fingers going into a gap in a wall to hold on. It was as thick as a tree. Thicker. And it was moving. Very slowly. Tightening.

He looked along the cliff.

There was another one. Further down. Going into the rock of the left cliff the same way. And across the gap, on the right cliff, in the starlight, three more. Five. Five great pale arms, or roots, or fingers, or something he didn't have a word for, coming out of the middle of the jam and going sideways into the rock on either side of the Narrows. Holding. Holding the whole mountain of ice in the door. Holding the whole river behind it.

And in the middle, in the heart of the jam, deep down, under the piled slabs, through a gap where the ice was black and clear, he saw it.

A shape. Huge. Pale. Curled. Like something very old asleep in a bed of ice, with its arms out sideways into the rock, holding on. He couldn't see its face. He didn't know if it had one. He could see, in the middle of it, through the black ice, a darkness. Round. Slow. Going *once*.

And over it, in the air above the Narrows, the window came up.

Nothing else. No level. Nobody could see a level. Chris was standing at the edge of the ledge with his notebook in his hand and his mouth open, looking at the window, and Ina saw him look for the number, under it, where the number always was. And not find one.

Ina pulled his mask down.

He knelt at the edge of the ledge and put his palm flat on the cliff, near the top of the nearest pale arm where it went into the crack, and let the fire go down. Further than before. White-blue, through the rock, down to the arm. Feeling.

The arm was cold. Colder than anything. Cold as the inside of the river.

He followed it. Down, along it, into the jam, toward the middle. Cold, and cold, and cold.

And then warm.

The middle. The curled shape. The heart. It was warmer now. Much warmer than before the first word. Not a candle in a cold room any more. A whole fire. Burning, deep in the jam, in the middle of all that ice, the way a fire burns in a stove when somebody's flung the door open. Burning hard. Holding.

And under the heat, under the heart, under everything, faint, through the rock, through his palm, through the fire, something that wasn't a sound and wasn't a feeling and that he'd never once in his life felt from anything that wasn't a person.

It was frightened.

***

He took his hand off the cliff.

He sat back on his heels at the edge of the ledge, with his mask down and his hands in his lap, and didn't say anything for a long time.

"Ina," said Winter.

He pushed the mask up.

"It's frightened," he said.

They were all looking at him. Chris. Niko. Maigo, with his eyes open now. Winter. Sabe, with her pole. Tuli, with her face white in the starlight.

"It's holding on," said Ina. Slowly. Finding it. "That's all it's doing. It's not angry. It's not—it doesn't want to drown anybody. It's holding the river. Because the river's coming. All at once. All of it. And it's—" He looked down at the jam. "It only knows how to hold. All of it, or none of it. It doesn't know how to let a bit go. So it holds on to everything. Till it can't." He swallowed. "And then it lets go of everything. All at once. Because that's the only other thing it knows."

Nobody said anything.

"It's warm in the middle," said Ina. "Because it's alive. And it's frightened. And it's holding on as hard as it can." He looked at his hands. "That's all."

Sabe was looking at him. Her milky eyes were wet.

"Gran said that," said Sabe. Very quietly. "Gran said it doesn't want to drown us. She said it just doesn't know how to let go a bit. Only all or nothing." She looked down at the jam. "Nobody believed her. They said it was a gran's story. They said it was a monster in the ice and it drowned people because it was a monster." She put her pole down on the ledge. *Tock.* "She'd have liked you."

Tuli was staring at him.

"My mother," she said. "Four springs. It took her. At the Narrows. When it let go." Her voice was very flat and very fast. "You're saying it didn't *mean* to."

Ina looked at her.

He didn't say *I'm sorry*. He'd never been good at that. He didn't say *it didn't mean to*, either; he didn't know if it was true, and you didn't say words over something unless they were true. That was the only rule he'd ever had about words.

He said the only true thing he had.

"I'm saying it was frightened too," said Ina.

Tuli looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned round, and sat down at the edge of the ledge with her back to all of them and her pole across her knees, and looked down into the Narrows at the jam and the five pale arms in the rock and the darkness in the middle going *once*. And didn't say anything.

After a while Ina went and sat down next to her.

Not close. About the width of his mask. He didn't say anything. He just sat there. So she'd know he was still listening.

And after a long time, without turning her head, Tuli said, very quietly, to the dark:

"She'd have said put your hand on it. Mum. She always said put your hand on things. Not your pole. Your hand." She was quiet. "Gran says that's how she went through. Putting her hand on things."

Ina didn't say anything.

"Can you show me?" said Tuli. "What you did. With the fire. Through the rock." She still didn't look at him. "I want to feel it. That it's frightened. I want to *know*."

Ina looked at her. A girl of fourteen, at the edge of a ledge in the dark, with her dead mother's pole across her knees.

He took her hand. Carefully. The way you'd take the hand of somebody stepping onto a boat. He put it flat on the rock of the cliff, by his own. Palm down. And he pulled his mask down, and let the fire go into the rock, just a thread, white-blue, round her hand and under it, so she'd feel what he felt. Down. Through the cliff. Down the pale arm. Into the jam. To the middle.

He felt her feel it.

Her hand went still under his. Her breath stopped. Then started again, raggedly.

"Oh," said Tuli.

That was all. She didn't say anything else. She left her hand on the rock with his beside it for a long time, while the river roared up the valley behind the jam and the water rose and the heart in the middle of the ice went *once*, and *once*, and *once*, faster now, frightened, holding on.

***

At first light, Chris called them together.

The jam stood in the Narrows in the grey dawn like a mountain range somebody had built in a night. Higher than the ledge. Ice heaped on ice, slabs standing on end, glittering, groaning, with the five pale arms going into the cliffs on either side. Behind it, up the valley, the river had become something else. Not a road of ice any more. A lake. A long dark lake of water and broken ice, rising, already over the reed-beds along the banks, already a hand higher than it had been at midnight. You could see it rise if you watched one rock long enough.

"How long?" said Chris.

Sabe put her pole down. Listened. Lifted it.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe. Before it's too heavy. Before it can't hold any more and goes. All of it." She looked up the valley. "The Float'll be up by then. Riding. Coming down. They have to. When the water's this high there's nowhere else to go." She looked back at the jam. "If it goes when they're close behind it—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

"Three days," said Chris.

He had his notebook open. The fifteenth. He looked at the page for a long time. At *WHAT WE KNOW*, and the short list under it. *It piles every year. Some years it holds too long. It has a heart. You let it go. Early. Bit at a time. Nobody's ever done it.*

He wrote something under it. Ina saw him do it.

*It's warm in the middle. It's frightened. It only knows all or nothing.* And under that: *(Ina.)*

Then Chris shut the notebook.

"Then we've got three days," said Chris, "to teach it a bit at a time."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know how," said Chris. "I want that on the record. Before we start. I don't know how. Nobody here knows how. Sabe doesn't. Her gran didn't. Ice doesn't. Ice said *I don't know* on the cliff, on the first day, and Higu wrote it down." He looked round at them. At his party. At the old woman with the pole and the girl with her mother's. "So we're going to work it out. The way we always have. Columns. Everything. All of it." He looked down at the five arms in the rock. "Five places it's holding. One heart. Three days. And we're going to watch it, and listen to it, and write down everything it does, and find out how to make it let go of one thing without letting go of everything."

"And if we can't?" said Niko. Quietly.

Chris looked at him.

"Then we'll know that," he said. "And that's something too."

Winter laughed. Short and sharp. The first time Ina had heard her laugh since fourteen.

"*God*, you're annoying," said Winter. "Fine. Columns."

***

Ina wrote something that night, too.

He didn't, much. Chris wrote everything; Ina had never seen the point of writing anything down twice. But that night, on the ledge, with the jam groaning below and the water rising up the valley behind it and Tuli asleep beside her gran with her hand flat on the rock, as if she were still feeling it, he took the stub of a pencil out of his pocket and the back of a scrap of birch-bark Higu had given everybody, and wrote:

*It's warm in the middle.*

He looked at it for a while.

Then he wrote, under it, slower, the way you'd say words over something when you weren't sure yet if they were the right ones:

*It's holding on because it's frightened. That's not a small thing. That's the whole thing.*

*You don't fight a thing that's frightened.*

*You sit with it. Until it isn't.*

He looked at the words a long time. They weren't his. He didn't know whose they were. Gaffer's, maybe. Ice's, maybe, from a long way off, a thing Ice had said once that Ina hadn't heard but had felt, somehow, the way you feel a heart through rock. They were just true. That was the only rule he had.

He folded the bark up small and put it in his pocket.

On the step, he thought. With the rest.
