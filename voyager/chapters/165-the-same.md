It started snowing at about three in the afternoon on the day after harvest-home, and Niko was the first one to see Ice's face when it did.

He hadn't meant to be. He'd been sitting on the step of the Long Barn's third door with the red book open on his knees, in the thin cold sun, reading. Not the spell pages. The back pages. Hessa's pages, from floor 5, the ones in her young hand, the drawings of swords with lines of tiny writing down the blades. He'd read them so many times he didn't need to any more. He read them anyway. It was a thing to do with his hands on a day when there was nothing to do with his hands. The harvest was in. Every field. There was nothing left in the Long Acre to cut or bind or carry, and seven hundred people were sitting about in the lane under the Long Barn's walls in the cold, wrapped in blankets, talking quietly, waiting to go home.

Ice was sitting on the wall at the end of the lane, a little way off, with Thea.

They weren't doing anything. They were sitting on the wall with their shoulders together, looking down the valley, the way they did at the end of every day now on every floor. Thea had the lantern in her lap, not lit. Ice had his hands between his knees. They weren't talking.

And the first flake came down between them.

Niko saw it. A single one, very large, very slow, turning over as it fell, out of a sky that had been pale blue all morning and had gone, without anybody quite noticing, the colour of old pewter. It came down between their two heads and landed on the wall between them and sat there.

Ice looked at it.

Niko had seen a lot of things on Ice's face. In the first week, on floor 1, when Ice was still a name at the top of a list. On floor 5, by the forge, with his eyes shut. On the Salt, with the pot of earth. He'd seen it go white and soft and blank and terrible and, once, at the Gate, completely still in a way that had frightened Niko more than anything the tower had ever sent.

He'd never seen it do this.

It didn't go anything. It just stopped. The way a clock stops. Ice sat on the wall with his hands between his knees and looked at a snowflake on a stone, and his face stopped, and stayed stopped.

Then another one came. And another. And the sky let go.

***

It snowed all afternoon.

Not gently. Not like the first snow on floor 10, coming down off the high side in a thin grey veil you could see through. This came down thick and soft and straight, enormous flakes, so many that you couldn't see the hills on the other side of the valley after ten minutes and couldn't see the end of the lane after twenty. It settled on everything. On the walls. On the blankets round seven hundred people's shoulders. On the carts and the forge and the backs of the horses, who stood in the lane with their heads down and their eyes half shut and let it. On the stubble of the Barn Field, which went white in a quarter of an hour, and then on the field below it, and the field below that, and all the way down.

And on the Grey.

That was what Niko watched. He stood at the top of the lane with the red book shut under his arm and snow building up on his shoulders and watched the snow come down on the Grey.

It was down there, below the last barn field. You could see it from the lane, until the snow took the view. A long flat colourless stain, from the last hedge all the way down the valley as far as you could see: grey stubble, grey hedges, grey trees, grey fields, perfectly still, with nothing on them. The frost hadn't settled on it. The dew hadn't. The rain at Haverley hadn't. Nothing settled on the Grey.

The snow did.

It came down on the grey fields the same as it came down on everything else. And it stayed. And it lay. And in twenty minutes, from the top of the lane, you couldn't tell where the Grey stopped and the white began. It was all white. The whole valley. The barn fields, which had been gold yesterday, and the grey below them, and all the way down to wherever Haverley was, under the snow, by its river. White, from the Long Barn's walls to the end of the world.

Niko stood there and looked at it, and felt something go cold in him that wasn't the snow.

He didn't know what it was. Not yet.

Behind him, somebody said, "Oh."

It was Higu.

She was standing in the lane with the rock in both hands, flat side up, the way she held it when she'd just taken it out and was about to write something. She wasn't writing. She was looking down the valley. At the white.

Then she looked down at the rock.

Niko went and stood beside her, because he wanted to see what she was seeing. And Higu, without looking up, turned the rock a little, so he could.

Two columns. The line down the middle, and the arrow across it, from the morning at the old barn. And on the left, in her small neat capitals, from the first evening on floor 14, from the shade of the barn wall, everything Ice had said:

*WINTER.*

*FIELDS: BARE. ALL IN.*

*HAVERLEY. ANNET. TITHE BARN. WINTER: INDOORS.*

*LONG BARN. 3 DAYS N. STRAW MAN (WALKS IN WINTER).*

And on the right, everything that had been true instead. *SUMMER. (END OF.) HARVEST: NOT IN. NOBODY INDOORS. BARROW-BULLS. THE GREY.*

Higu looked at the right-hand column for a long time. Then she took out the chalk, and, very slowly, under everything else on the right, she wrote:

*WINTER.*

She stopped.

She looked at the left-hand column. At *FIELDS: BARE. ALL IN.* And she wrote that too. On the right. Underneath. In exactly the same letters.

*FIELDS: BARE. ALL IN.*

Then she stood there with the chalk in her hand and didn't write anything else, and Niko looked at the two columns, side by side, with the line down the middle, and the top of the right one was different and the bottom of it was the same as the left. Word for word. Higu's hand. Both sides.

"It's caught up," said Higu.

Very quietly. Nobody else heard. Niko didn't think she'd meant him to.

"It's made it the same," she said. "That's what the Grey was. It wasn't eating the fields. It was making them what he remembered." She looked down the valley at the white. "And now it's put the snow on it. So it looks like it." She swallowed. "So it *is* it."

Niko didn't say anything. He looked at the wall at the end of the lane.

Ice was still sitting there. With the snow on his shoulders and in his hair, a white crust of it on his knees. Thea had put the lantern down and both her hands on his arm. He hadn't moved since the first flake. He was looking down the valley at the white, at the barn fields and the Grey under them and all of it gone the same, and Niko knew, the way you know when somebody you've known for years is looking at a thing they've seen before, that he was looking at the place he'd come into on the first day of the first time.

*We came out of that door in the snow*, Ice had said. *Everything was white. The fields were bare. All of them.*

It was.

***

The gleaners didn't go home.

They'd meant to. That was harvest-home: you ate, you danced, you slept in the Long Barn, and in the morning, the morning after, every village packed its carts and walked back down the valley to its own barns, which were full, and its own houses, which had been empty for weeks, and shut its doors, and had the winter.

They'd been packing all day. The carts were in the lane. Haverley's at the front, because Haverley was furthest down and had the longest to go.

Annet Sallow stood at the head of the lane in the snow with her stick and looked down the valley for a long time.

Then she walked down it.

Not far. Fifty paces. Down the lane, past the last cart, out into the white of the Barn Field, which had been stubble yesterday and was snow now, a hand deep and getting deeper. And down to the bottom of the Barn Field, to the hedge. The last hedge. The one the Grey had stopped at the night before, when there was nothing left above it to take.

Niko went after her. He didn't know why. Several people did. Cobb. Osric of Ashwell with his long stick. Chris, who went everywhere anything was being found out, with his notebook. Maigo.

Annet stood at the hedge. The snow was on it, thick, white, like the snow on every other hedge. She put her hand out and brushed the snow off the top of it.

Grey underneath.

Grey leaves. Grey twigs. Perfectly still, under the snow, the way they'd been under the sun. Nothing on them. The snow had sat on top, and they were still grey under it.

She stepped through the gap in the hedge, into the field below.

Niko saw her foot go down. Into the snow. It went in, the way your foot goes into snow, a hand deep. And then it went on. Not into the snow. Through the snow, and into the grey underneath, and *on*. Her boot went in to the ankle. To the shin. Her whole weight went forward onto it as if she'd stepped onto a stair that wasn't there, and Cobb caught her arm, and she came back, hard, through the gap, and stood there on the white of the Barn Field with one leg grey to the knee.

Not hurt. Not cut. Grey dust, all down her boot and her skirt to the knee, very fine, running off it like flour.

She looked down at it. She looked through the gap at the place she'd stepped.

There was a hole in the snow there. Her footprint. And at the bottom of it, where the ground should have been, there was nothing. Not dark. Not deep. Grey. Like the bottom of a well somebody had painted grey and forgotten to put a bottom in.

"There's no down," said Maigo. Very quietly. Behind her.

Annet didn't turn round.

"There's no *ground*," she said.

"That's what I said." Maigo had taken his shoe off. He was standing in the snow of the Barn Field on his root-foot, with his toes just in the earth under the white, the way he did now. He'd gone very still. "It's not *ground*, down there. It's a picture of ground. You can walk on the snow if you're careful, I think. If you don't push. The snow's real. The snow's on top. But under it—" He looked through the gap. "Under it there isn't anything to stand on."

Nobody said anything.

Annet looked down the valley. At the white, going on and on, down to where Haverley was, by its river, under the snow. Three days' walk. With nothing under the snow to stand on.

"Then we don't go home," she said.

She said it the way she said everything. As if it was the weather. As if it were just a thing that was true and you put it on the stick and got on with it.

She turned round. She walked back up the Barn Field through the snow to the lane, with her leg grey to the knee and the dust running off it, to where seven hundred people stood by their carts with the snow on their blankets, waiting to go home.

"Unload," said Annet Sallow. "We're staying in."

Nobody argued. Nobody asked why. They'd all seen. They unloaded the carts, slowly, in the snow, and carried everything back in through the four great doors of the Long Barn, and the horses went in too, into the far end, by the straw man, where there was room, and the doors were shut behind them one by one.

Indoors. All of them. With the doors shut. In the winter.

Niko stood in the lane in the snow and looked at the last door closing and heard Ice's voice in his head, from the first evening, as clearly as if he were standing beside him.

*It was the middle of winter and everybody was indoors, all the time, with the fires going and the doors shut.*

He went in.

***

Gaffer Tobin was still in his chair.

Nobody had moved him. That was how it was done, Cobb said: you didn't move them till they were going. He'd sat all night at the head of the first table, where he'd stopped, with his blanket over his knees and his stick across them and his hands on the stick, with his eyes shut now; Annet had shut them, at dawn. And all day people had walked past him, gleaners and passers, the whole Acre, packing, and nobody had touched the chair, and everybody, going past, had put a hand out, very lightly, and touched the stick. Just the stick. For a second. Then gone on.

Niko had done it too. He hadn't known why. He'd seen Calvin do it, and Rachael, and Ice, and he'd gone past and put out two fingers and touched the old pale wood, and it was warm from the lamps, and he'd gone on.

"Where do you bury them?" said Chris.

He asked Annet. At the end of the afternoon, inside, by the first table, in the lamplight, with the snow coming down outside the high slit windows and the doors shut and the barn full of the sound of seven hundred people settling in for a winter they hadn't meant to have. Chris always asked. That was Chris. If there was a thing nobody had found out yet, Chris asked it, kindly, the way you'd ask somebody the time.

Annet looked at the old man in the chair.

"At home," she said. "At the bottom of the first field. Where the harvest starts. Every village does. So they're there for the next one." She looked at her stick. "He'd go at the bottom of Gorse Piece. By the old barn. With his brother, and his father, and all of them." She stopped. "In the spring. When the ground was soft. We'd keep him in the cold till then. In the tithe barn. That's how it's done, in a hard winter."

Nobody said anything.

Gorse Piece was grey. Under the snow, three days down the valley, past nothing to stand on. The tithe barn at Haverley was full of harvest and shut and there was no way to get to it.

"There's no Gorse Piece," said Annet. Very quietly. "Is there."

"No," said Chris.

She stood there for a while.

"Then he goes here," she said. "In the barn fields. Under the barn. Where he saw it in." She looked round, at the barn, at the doors shut on the snow. "Nobody from Haverley's ever gone in the barn fields. It's not ours. It's everybody's." She looked at Osric, who was standing at the next table, with his long stick. "Is that allowed?"

Osric looked at her for a long moment.

"He saw it in," said Osric. "The whole Acre's harvest. Not just Haverley's. He came up the whole valley on a cart to see it." He lifted his stick, the long one, an inch. "If anybody's allowed, it's him."

"Then he goes here," said Annet.

"The ground's frozen," said Cobb. "It went hard this afternoon. You'll not get a spade in it."

"I'll get a spade in it," said Gabe.

"You'll not."

"I've got arms," said Gabe.

And Ina, at the end of the table, who'd been sitting with his mask down and his back to the wall, not saying anything all day, which was Ina, looked up.

"I'll warm it," said Ina.

Everybody looked at him.

"The ground," said Ina. Smooth. Quiet. The voice that made people listen. "Where you want him. I'll warm it. Not burn it. Just warm it. Like a hearth stone." He looked at the old man in the chair. "So it's soft. So it's the spring for him. Just for an hour."

Annet looked at him for a long time.

Niko knew what she was looking at. He'd seen the gleaners look at Ina all month, at the white fire on his hands when he'd dried the stooks at Brightwater after the frost, very carefully, a little at a time, not touching. The gleaners didn't like fire near the harvest. Nobody did. But Ina had never once lit anything that wasn't his to light. On floor 8, in the house for lights, he'd refused to light the hearth the ones before had laid, three hundred and twelve years before. *It's not for us*, he'd said.

This was for somebody.

"Yes," said Annet.

***

They buried Gaffer Tobin at dusk, in the snow, at the top of the Barn Field, against the wall of the Long Barn where the wind didn't reach.

Not everybody went out. It was too cold, and there were too many of them, and the snow was still coming down. But a lot did. Haverley, all of it. Annet. Cobb. Ezra. Wren, in three blankets, holding Tiger's hand. The two lads who'd carried his chair every day of harvest, carrying it again, with him in it, out of the third door and across the snow, very slowly, to the place. And the thirty-six, all of them, because nobody had to ask.

Ina knelt in the snow at the place and put his hands flat on it.

Niko watched him do it. The mask was up, pushed back on his head, so his face was bare, which it almost never was in front of strangers. His hands went white-blue. Not flame. Not even light, really. A shimmer in the air over his palms, the way the air shimmers over a road in summer. And the snow under his hands went soft, and then wet, and then ran away from his fingers in little streams, and under it the frozen earth went from grey-white to dark, from hard to soft, the colour of earth in spring, steaming very slightly in the cold, in a long patch the size of a man.

It took him a long time. He didn't hurry. Nobody asked him to.

When he stood up, his face was grey with tiredness. Winter put her hand on his back without looking at him and kept it there.

Gabe dug it. Gabe and Salty, and then Azac, in turns, with the gleaners' spades, in the soft dark earth that Ina had made, while it was still soft. Not fast. Properly. Square. The way the gleaners showed them.

And then they put Gaffer Tobin in it.

Not in a box. There wasn't time to make one, or wood to make it from, and that wasn't how it was done in the Acre anyway, Cobb said. In his blanket. In his clean white shirt buttoned to the neck. With a sheaf from the Barn Field on his chest, the last field, heads up, and his hands folded on it. And his stick. Annet laid it beside him, along his side, the whole length of him. The pale peeled hazel, with the notches on it. Seventy-four harvests and this one. She'd cut the last notch that morning and crossed it.

"Has anybody got words?" said Annet.

She said it to the gleaners. To Haverley. Standing in the snow round the edge of the grave with the lantern light on their faces. And the gleaners looked at each other, and at the grave, and nobody said anything, and Niko understood that this was the bit that was usually somebody's. Somebody old. Somebody who'd been at a lot of these. And they'd all been at a lot of these, every gleaner here, and every one of them was standing there with their mouth shut.

It was Gaffer Tobin's bit. He'd done the words. For twenty years. From his chair.

Nobody had ever thought who'd do his.

"I'll say something," said Ina.

He said it very quietly. He was still grey with tiredness, swaying a little, with Winter's hand on his back. He looked at Annet, not at anybody else.

"If that's all right," said Ina. "I'm not from here. I didn't know him. I only saw him in his chair." He paused. "But somebody should. And I've—" He stopped. Something went across his face, very fast, that Niko didn't understand. "I think I'm the one who does this. I don't know why I think that. I just do."

Annet looked at him for a long moment.

"Go on," she said.

Ina looked down into the grave.

He didn't take a breath. He didn't think about it. Niko had heard Ina talk for years, on calls, on Earth, in that smooth deep careful British voice that made you want to listen to whatever it was saying even if it was the weather. He'd never heard him do this. It just came out of him, very quietly, into the snow, the way water comes out of the ground.

"He watched," said Ina.

"That's all he did, at the end. He sat at the top of the field in the cold and he watched it come in. Every stroke. Every sheaf. He couldn't hold a blade any more and he couldn't bind and he couldn't walk the rows. So he watched. And when a field was in, he lifted his stick, so the people bringing it in would know somebody saw.

"That's not a small thing. That's the thing. Everybody here's brought a field in with somebody watching. That was him.

"He said he'd see it in, and then he'd stop. And he did. He saw all of it. Every village. The last field. The last cart. Two people with tails tying one band." His voice didn't change. "He didn't leave anything in the field. Not one thing. It's all in. It's under a roof.

"So he can put it down now. The stick. The watching. All of it. Somebody else'll lift it, next year. Somebody here." He looked round, once, at the faces in the snow. "You'll know who. You'll see them watching.

"Go on, then," said Ina. To the grave. Very softly. "It's in. You saw it. We'll keep the winter."

That was all.

He stood there. He didn't say anything else. Winter's hand was still on his back.

And Niko, who'd been watching Ina, because you always watched whoever was talking, turned his head without meaning to and looked across the grave.

At Ice.

He was standing on the far side, between Thea and Gabe, with the snow in his hair. And he was looking at Ina. Not the way you look at somebody saying words at a grave. The way you look at somebody who's just handed you a thing you dropped a long time ago and never found. His lips were moving. Very slightly. Without a sound. Going over it. *He watched. That's not a small thing. It's in. We'll keep the winter.* The way you go over something you're trying to learn by heart, so you'll have it, so you won't lose it again.

His face was wet. It might have been the snow.

Ina looked up from the grave and saw him.

Niko saw it happen. He saw Ina see Ice mouthing his words. He saw Ina's face, still bare, with no mask, go very still, the way it went still when something landed in him that he was going to put somewhere and keep. And Niko remembered, because he was in Chris's party and Chris's party had been on the Salt, and on the Salt Ina had sat by the only fire on the floor and written thirty-five names on Calvin's palm with his fingertip. And Ice had sat with him at dawn and said something to him, quietly, that Ina had told nobody and Niko had only heard about from Winter, who'd only heard half.

*I never once remembered what you said. Only that it was right.*

Ina didn't say anything. He looked at Ice across the grave for a long moment. Then he nodded. Once. Very slightly. The way you'd nod to somebody who'd said *thank you* in a room too loud to hear it.

And Ice nodded back.

Then the gleaners came forward, one at a time, and each of them put a handful of the dark soft earth that Ina had made in on top of Gaffer Tobin, and his sheaf, and his stick. And then the thirty-six. And then Gabe and Salty filled it in, properly, the way the gleaners showed them, square, while it was still soft. And by the time they'd finished, the snow was already settling on it.

Yan stayed at the edge of it after everybody else had gone in.

Niko went back for him. Because it was dark, and snowing, and Yan was standing there with Working On It upright in the snow beside him and Suzu round his neck, looking at the grave with his head on one side, the way he looked at things.

"Yan," said Niko. "Come in. It's freezing."

"It went up at the band," said Yan.

Niko stopped.

"His," said Yan. "His light. Last night. In the barn. I saw it go." He didn't look round. "When the knot held. When everybody said *aye*. He lifted his stick, and it went up. Straight up. Quick as anything. Like the ones in Tell, when they hear their names." He tipped his head the other way. "It didn't need walking. It didn't need its name said. It just went. When the knot held." He sounded interested. Pleased, almost. "I've never seen one go up *at* something before. They always go up because something's stopped. His went up because something *started*."

Niko looked at him.

"Did you tell anybody?"

"No," said Yan. "Why would I?" He turned round, finally, and looked at Niko, with his pale cyan eyes and the snow in his silver hair. "Nobody asked me." Then he picked up Working On It and walked back to the barn through the snow, humming, and went in.

Niko stood by the grave for a moment longer.

Then he went in too.

***

He couldn't sleep.

Nobody could, much. Seven hundred people and a dozen horses in a barn the size of a hill, in the snow, with the doors shut, and the lamps turned down, and the wind getting up outside and going round the walls with a long low sound like something breathing. People talked, in the dark, in low voices. Somebody was crying, a long way off. Somebody else was singing to a baby. The horses shifted at the far end and blew.

At the far end, past the horses, against the end wall, the straw man.

Niko lay in the straw near the middle of the barn with the red book under his head and looked at him.

You couldn't see much. The lamps were down, and the far end of the barn was very far, and very dark. But there was a lantern on a hook by the horses, turned low, for whoever was watching them, and in its light you could see the shape of him. Tall as the roof. The straw arms out from the crosspiece. The round head in the iron hoop, with its crown of poppies gone dark in the lamplight. The plaited face.

Niko had looked at the face a lot, yesterday. At the wedding. Standing at the back, with Chris, while Hana and Milli tied the band. He'd looked at it because there was something about it he couldn't place, and he didn't like not being able to place things. It wasn't the eyes, twisted straw, or the mouth, a line. It was the plaiting. The way the old women had done it.

He'd watched them do it, in the afternoon, while the field was being cut. Old women from every village in the Acre, sitting on the barn floor round the hoop with their laps full of straw, plaiting the face. And they hadn't talked about it. They talked about everything else. They talked about the barley and the frost and whose daughter was walking out with whose son and who'd cheated at the hook-throwing the year of the big rain. But not the face. They'd plaited the face in silence, all of them together, and they'd all done the same thing. The same pattern. Over, under, a twist, a turn, two over, a knot. Over and over. Round and round the hoop. As if they'd learned it from their mothers, who'd learned it from theirs, and nobody had ever once asked what it was.

He'd asked one of them. An old woman from Lammas with no teeth. *Why that pattern?*

She'd looked at him as if he'd asked why the sun was round. *It's his face*, she'd said. *That's what his face is.*

Niko lay in the straw and looked at it in the dark, and thought about Hessa.

About her red book. About the back pages, in her young hand, the drawings of swords with tiny writing down the blades. About floor 12, and the rakers on the Salt, who wrote on the things they held, and read them with their thumbs, because out loud the salt would hear you. About his own blade, by his side in the straw, with the first line of his first spell punched down it two letters a night, that he read in the dark without a sound.

*Write a spell nobody wrote*, the window had said. The third one. On the Salt.

He looked at the plaited face. Over, under, a twist, a turn, two over, a knot. Over and over. Round and round.

It looked like writing.

Not letters. Not any letters he knew. But it went the way writing goes. In lines. In a pattern that came round and came round, the way a sentence comes round when it's a thing you say every year. The same words. Over and over. A thousand years of old women plaiting the same sentence into the same face and not one of them knowing they were writing anything.

He sat up.

He didn't know why. Something had moved.

Not him. Not the horses. Something at the end of the barn. He looked. The lantern by the horses, turned low. The horses, asleep, one of them with its head hanging. The end wall. The straw man.

Nothing.

He lay down again.

He lay there for a long time, with the red book under his head, looking at the dark shape against the end wall. Tall as the roof. Arms out. Head in the hoop. Not moving.

At some point he must have slept. Because the next thing he knew it was grey, and cold, and the high slit windows in the walls were white with snow-light, and somebody was standing over him.

It was Chris. With his notebook.

"Niko," said Chris. Very quietly. "Come and look at this."

***

He followed Chris down the barn.

Between the sleepers, in the grey light, stepping over people. Past the tables. Past the horses, who were awake now, and restless, shifting, rolling their eyes, the big brown Haverley mare backing against her rope. To the end wall. To the foot of the straw man.

Maigo was already there. Standing on the barn floor in front of the iron frame, barefoot on his root-foot, with his pixel sunglasses pushed up on his forehead, which he never did, looking up.

"I measured it," said Chris. "Last night. Milli told me. He said the head had turned. A finger. He said he measured it at the wedding and it was a finger to the left after." He held up the notebook. "So I measured it. At midnight. Before I went to sleep. From the post there to the tip of the nose." He pointed at a post, against the side wall, with a nail in it. "With a bit of string. And I wrote it down."

"And?"

"And it's two fingers now," said Chris. "Not one. Two."

Niko looked up.

The straw man's face looked down at them. Tall as the roof, in the grey snow-light from the windows. The two twisted eyes. The mouth. The plaited pattern going round and round. And the face was turned. Not straight down the barn at the doors, the way the old women had tied it. To the left.

Toward the middle of the barn. Toward where the thirty-six slept.

"And that," said Maigo.

He pointed. Not at the face. Lower. At the straw man's chest. At the place in the middle, where a heart would go, where old Ezra had climbed up the frame two nights ago and tied in the neck. The plaited gold figure, the last of the last field, that Milli had cut.

It was white.

Frost. All over it. A thick white fur of it, on every ear, every stalk, every twist of the plait, as if it had been left out all night in a hard winter. And nothing else in the barn had frost on it. Not the straw man's arms. Not his head. Not the beams, not the horses, not the seven hundred people asleep in the straw. Just the neck. Just the heart.

"It's cold," said Maigo.

He'd put his root-foot down. Into the earth floor of the barn, at the foot of the frame, his toes going soft and in. His face had gone very still behind the pushed-up sunglasses.

"Not the barn," he said. "*Him*. There's something in him. I can feel it. Down through the frame, into the floor." He swallowed. "It's like the Grey. A bit. But it's not the Grey. The Grey's got no down. This has. This has *so* much down. It's the harvest. All of it. The whole Acre. He's full of it." He stopped. "And something's putting the winter in."

Niko looked at the frost on the neck.

"From where?" said Chris.

Maigo didn't answer for a long time.

"From the same place as the Grey," he said at last. "I think. From whatever made the fields match." He looked up at the plaited face. "It's making him match too."

*In winter*, Ice had said, on the first evening, in the shade of the old barn. *It walked.*

Nobody said it out loud. Nobody had to.

Behind them, at the near end of the barn, somebody opened one of the great doors a crack, to look out at the morning. And the snow-light came in, white, cold, and the wind with it. And it went the whole length of the barn, over seven hundred people, and touched the straw man.

And the straw man's arm moved.

Not much. Not a reach. Not a gesture. The right arm, the bundle of straw bound with plaited bands that went out from the crosspiece where a shoulder would be. It lifted. A finger's width. In the draught.

And didn't come down when the draught stopped.

Chris wrote it down.
