Hana had stopped in the middle of a word, and she still didn't know why.

She thought about it for three days. On the deck, in the wind, with the *Patience* running south over water so deep it was purple, and the hum coming up through the planks under her feet day and night now, low and steady and gentle, from somewhere ahead and below. She thought about it in the hammock at night with Milli's arm under her head and her tail over both of them. She thought about it while she was doing other things, which was how she thought about everything that mattered.

She'd been about to charm Marrit.

That was the plain fact of it. She'd stood on the quarterdeck and opened her mouth and felt her voice start to go warm, the way it did, like honey going into tea. It had been the quickest way. Marrit was frightened and stubborn and in the way, and Hana could have made her want to say yes in about the time it took to say her name. She'd done it a hundred times. On every floor. To guards and clerks and stallholders and a man on floor 5 who wanted three times too much for a boat. She wasn't ashamed of it. It was a skill. It was *hers*. You didn't apologise for being good at something.

And she'd stopped.

She'd looked at Marrit's hand on the wheel, at the tide-cord wound so tight round the brown fingers that the knots had gone white, and something in her had just—stopped. Halfway through the name. As if she'd walked into a glass door.

"Because she's real," said Milli, on the third night, in the hammock, when she finally said it out loud.

Hana looked at the dark.

"The clerk on floor 2 was real," she said.

"Was he?"

She thought about it. The clerk on floor 2. In the hall by the gate. He'd said the same thing every time she went in. *Name and business, please.* The same words, the same voice, the same tired little smile. She'd charmed him on the third day to get a room upgraded and he'd said *of course, madam* and smiled exactly the same smile and she'd thought, at the time, nothing at all.

"He said the same thing every time," said Hana slowly.

"Marrit doesn't," said Milli.

"No."

"Marrit has an uncle who went over the side when she was six," said Milli. "And a grandfather who's dying. And pitch on her elbows." He was quiet. "And she asked Thea if we were real. At the Teeth. She didn't know if she was on the right side of the line." He shifted under her head. "You heard that. You went quiet for an hour."

"I know."

"So you couldn't," said Milli. "Not to her. Not to make her want something she was frightened of for a reason." He said it calmly, rationally, the way he said everything, like a measurement. "The higher you go, the more real it gets. Ice says. Higu says. Your charm doesn't get weaker. It's just that what it's *on* gets heavier."

Hana didn't say anything for a long time.

"I don't like that," she said eventually.

"I know," said Milli. "That's why you stopped."

***

Ice went hunting on the islands.

There were small ones, south of the old route, scattered across the purple water like crumbs: humps of black rock and white sand and scrubby pale trees, with things living on them that came out to look at the *Patience* when she anchored. The singer led them from one to the next. Not on purpose, Aleaka said. It just went where it was safe. And the ship followed, and stopped where there was fresh water, and the parties went ashore in the boats to fill the barrels and level.

And Ice went off on his own.

Hana watched him do it. From the deck of the *Patience*, on the second afternoon, at anchor in the lee of an island Calvin had named Sock, because of its shape. She watched him go up the white beach with his shield on his back and his sword on his hip and his cloak blowing, and into the scrub, alone. And she watched Thea and Xola get out of the boat behind him, and wait, on the beach, a long way back, until he'd gone a hundred metres. And then follow.

Not with him. Behind.

She'd watched them do it every day since Hythe. On the Shoals. On the island called Sock. On the one after. Thea with the lantern. Xola with Squiddles on her head and her gills spread for the salt. Walking a long way behind him, the way the rule said. *Somebody watches. Nobody lifts a hand unless he goes down.*

Hana thought it was the most romantic thing she'd ever seen in her life.

She'd never say so. Thea would kill her. But she stood at the rail with her chin on her hands and watched Thea, who had stepped round him at the Gate with a wall of light, walk a hundred metres behind him up a beach on purpose, and not catch him up, and not call his name, and not lift a hand. Every day. Because that was what he needed. Because somebody had to watch, and it was going to be her.

"You've got the face," said Jared, beside her.

"What face?"

"The face you had at the tarn." Jared leaned on the rail. "When you saw them. Like you already knew."

Hana didn't say anything.

"Did you?" said Jared. Lightly.

She thought about it.

"I knew she'd been looking at him since floor 2," she said. "Everybody knew that. I knew he'd been looking at her since before the white room." She smiled at the beach. "That's not knowing. That's just *seeing*, Jared. You can see things without knowing them."

"Can you?" said Jared.

She looked at him.

He was looking at the beach too. At Ice, small and dark, going into the trees. At Thea and Xola following. His heavy-lidded eyes were very steady.

"I can see that he put the wax in without being told," said Jared. "Milli saw it. I saw it. Two balls, two ears, didn't look. Like he'd done it a hundred times." He paused. "I can see that. I don't know it."

"Then don't," said Hana.

Jared looked at her.

"He's going to tell us," said Hana. "One day. Or he isn't. Either way it's his." She took her chin off her hands. "Thea knows. You can see that too. Look at her. She's carrying something. She carries it the way she carries the lantern." She was quiet. "And she's not putting it down for anybody. So it's not mine to pick up."

Jared didn't say anything for a while.

"That's what Ina said," he said eventually. "On nine. Winter told me. *You wait on the step.*"

"Then I'm on the step," said Hana. "With Ina. It's a nice step." She smiled. "There's room."

***

On the fifth day after the answer, the hum stopped.

It was the middle of the afternoon. Hot. The wind had been going down since noon, slowly, the way the tide went out: so slowly you didn't notice it until it was gone. The sails had gone from full to soft to slack. The *Patience* was barely moving, rolling gently on a long glassy swell with her canvas hanging off the yards like washing, and everybody on deck was lying about in the shade of it, half asleep.

And the hum stopped.

Hana felt it go. Everybody did. They'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a clock, and then it wasn't there and the silence where it had been was so loud that she sat up on the coil of rope where she'd been dozing with her heart going and her ears flat before she knew why.

At the stern, Aleaka had stood up.

She was at the rail. She'd been asleep against it with Sleet, in the shade of the mizzen. Now she was on her feet with her hands on the wood and her face turned down to the water, and Sleet's ruff was going white.

Not slowly, this time. All at once. Like a window icing over in a single breath.

"Leaka?" said Hana.

Aleaka didn't answer. She was listening. Hana could see her doing it. Her fox ears forward, rigid. Her whole body leaning out over the rail, toward the water, toward something.

And then the hum came back.

Except it wasn't the hum.

It came up through the deck so hard that the planks buzzed under Hana's feet. Not low and gentle. High. *Loud.* A long rising cry out of the deep, rising and rising and breaking at the top into something ragged, and then again, and again, faster, the same shape over and over, the way the song had been on the first night but *faster*, frantic, like somebody shouting the same word and nobody turning round.

Every tide-folk sailor on the ship came to their feet.

"*Not that way*," said Aleaka. Her voice was shaking. "It's saying *not that way*. It's saying it over and over. It's—" She turned round. Her face had gone grey under the freckles. "Hana. It's saying it to *us*."

"We're not going anywhere," said Newish, from the deck. "There's no *wind*."

"I know," said Aleaka. "I *know*. That's not—"

"*Captain!*"

Kip. The ship's boy. Twelve years old. Up the mainmast, in the crosstrees, where he'd been sitting all afternoon with his legs dangling, keeping the lookout nobody had needed since the singer started leading them. He was standing up on the crosstrees with one hand on the mast and the other pointing.

West.

Hana went to the rail.

Everybody did. She found herself between Milli and Jared, with Newish squeezing in underneath her arm and Aleaka at the stern behind them, and they all looked where the boy was pointing. Out to the west. Across the glassy purple swell, toward the sun, which was low now and gold, and laying a long gold road across the water toward them.

And at the end of the road, near the horizon, the water was wrong.

It was smooth. That was the first thing. The sea everywhere else was rolling, long and glassy, with the swell going through it. And out there to the west, in a wide band right across the horizon, it was smooth. Flat. Like a mirror. Like a pond on a still day. With the gold of the sun lying on it in a long bright smear.

And it was moving.

Not toward them. Not away. *Round.* Very slowly. You could see it if you looked at the edge of the smooth band, where it met the rolling water: the swell running into it and being turned. Bent. Drawn sideways. All in one direction. Left to right. Slowly. As if the whole of the western sea was a great wheel, miles across, lying flat on the water, turning.

"*No*," said Marrit.

She was at the wheel. Her face had gone the colour of the barnacles at the Teeth.

"That's not," she said. "That's not there. That's never been there. That's four days north. It's always been four days north. Off the old route. *Everybody* knows where it is. You keep the Long Islands on your right and you never see it. You never—" She stopped. Her tide-cord was round her fingers. She was looking at it. At the knots. At the way the cord was hanging.

It wasn't hanging straight.

It was hanging *west*. Very slightly. A finger's width off true. As if something was pulling on it.

"That's the Undertow," said Marrit. Very quietly. "That's the edge of it. And we're in the current."

***

They tried everything.

Hana would remember it afterwards as a list. The way Higu would have written it on the rock, if anybody had had time. She'd remember each thing they tried and how long it took to fail.

The sails, first. Marrit had every scrap of canvas the *Patience* owned set, sheeted in, trimmed, every rope hauled, to catch any breath of wind there was. There wasn't any. The sails hung. The sea was a mirror. The sun went down a finger's width toward the smooth band in the west, and the *Patience* went with it.

The boats, second. Both of them. Lowered over the side with eight climbers in each on the oars, and lines run from them to the bow, to tow. Gabe and Salty and Azac and Hako and Xav and Deston and Calvin and Rui Xiang in one; Jared and Milli and Winter and Ina and Tiger and Wendy and Drew and Chris in the other. Rowing. South. Away. With their backs bent and the lines coming taut and creaking. Newish on the bow of the *Patience* throwing *one* at them by name, at no regular time, to keep them together without a beat for anything to hunt.

The *Patience* moved. A little. South. A ship's length.

And the current took two ship's lengths back.

The rudder, third. Jared's rudder. On Jared's irons. Marrit had the wheel hard over, both hands, her whole weight on it, and the rudder held. It held perfectly. The irons didn't give. The pintles didn't shift. Jared, in the boat, rowing, looked back over his shoulder at the stern of the ship with his jaw tight, and Hana saw his face, and knew what he was thinking. *I made it a rudder it can't take.*

And it didn't take it.

It didn't need to. A rudder needs water going past it to steer. There wasn't any. The *Patience* wasn't sailing. She was being carried. The rudder held, and turned, and did nothing at all, because the whole sea was going the same way the ship was.

*It'll take something else*, Milli had said, in the lean-to.

*It took the wind*, Hana thought.

"It moved," said Chris. Up on the quarterdeck, with Higu. He was looking at the smooth band in the west with his face very white. "Marrit said four days north. Always four days north. Off the old route." He swallowed. "It *moved*. It's been told where we're going. The singer said *go round*. So it came round."

"Like the light," said Higu. Calm. Not calm. "On the Teeth."

"Like the light."

"It changes what *we* do," said Higu. "Not just him." She looked down the deck. At Ice. "Floor 8. The Cataract. The count. It learned the count from us. And now it's learned the *singer*."

Ice didn't say anything.

He was at the rail amidships with his hands flat on the wood and Thea beside him, looking west. At the smooth band. At the slow turning. At the gold road of the sun lying on it, bending, being drawn round, the whole road curving into the wheel like a ribbon going round a spindle.

His face was very white.

Hana looked at it.

And she thought: *he's never seen this either.*

***

By sunset they could see the middle.

It took Hyvred to see it first. She went up the mainmast to the crosstrees beside Kip, and stood there with one hand on the mast and her eyes doing the thing, and was quiet for a long time. And then she said, very calmly, in a voice that came down to the deck like a stone dropped down a well:

"It goes down."

Everybody looked up.

"In the middle," said Hyvred. "Miles away. Five. Six. The water goes round faster and faster, closer in, and then in the middle it just—" She stopped. "Goes down. There's a hole. A hole in the sea. It's dark. I can't see the bottom. I can't see anything at the bottom." Her voice was very steady. "There's things going round it. Wood. Weed. A whole tree. I watched it go round three times, and then it went in, and it didn't come up."

Nobody said anything.

"And there's something in it," said Hyvred.

"What?" said Drew.

"I don't know." Hyvred was very still. "Something in the hole. Down at the bottom where I can't see. It's not like the Rimed. It's not empty." She was quiet. "It's *full*."

At the stern, Aleaka made a sound.

Hana turned round. Aleaka was standing at the rail with both hands on it and her eyes shut and tears coming out from under her glasses, and her mouth was moving. And her window was open in front of her. Hana could see the glow of it, faint, over the water.

"Leaka," said Hana. And went to her.

"It's calling," said Aleaka. Without opening her eyes. "The thing in the hole. It's *calling*. I can hear it. With Heed. I couldn't before." She swallowed. "I can hear what it means."

"What does it mean?"

Aleaka opened her eyes.

"*More*," she said.

Hana felt the hair on her tail stand up.

"That's all," said Aleaka. "That's all it says. Over and over. Not like the singer. The singer's got so much in it. Sad, and sorry, and *not that way*, and the small one. This hasn't got anything. Just *more*. *More. Come. Down. More.*" She was shaking. "It's calling everything. The wood. The weed. The tree. Us. It's not even calling *us*, Hana. It doesn't know we're us. It's calling *everything*, all the time, and it never stops, and it never gets enough." Her voice cracked. "It's been calling forever. And the singer's been calling back *not that way* for hundreds of years, over the top of it, so ships would hear the singer and not *this*."

The *Patience* went round. A finger's width. West.

"And the wax," said Aleaka. "The wax stopped them hearing both."

***

They held a council on the quarterdeck in the last of the light, because there was nothing else to do.

The boats had come in. There was no point rowing. They'd rowed until Salty's hands bled and Rachael made him stop, and the *Patience* had gone two ship's lengths west for every one they'd pulled her south. So they came in, and climbed up the side, and stood on the deck in the dusk with their hands raw, looking at each other.

The smooth band was closer. You didn't need Hyvred now. You could see it from the deck. The swell ran into it from the south and turned. The gold had gone off it. It was grey now, and flat, and enormous, and turning, and from somewhere far out in the middle of it there came a sound. Not the singer. Not anything you'd call a sound. A sort of hollow roar, very low, very far off, like the noise inside a shell when you hold it to your ear.

And under them, through the planks, the singer, still crying *not that way, not that way*, over and over, frantic.

"How long?" said Drew.

"Till we're in?" Marrit was at the wheel still. She hadn't left it. She was holding it as if holding it would do something. "The edge is the slow part. The edge is days, sometimes. Ships have come out of the edge." She swallowed. "Not out of the middle. Nobody's come out of the middle."

"How long till the middle?"

Marrit looked at the tide-cord on her fingers. At the angle of it.

"Two days," she said. "If the wind doesn't come back."

"And if it does?"

"Then we sail out," said Marrit. "But it's not going to. Is it." She looked at Ice, of all people. As if she knew. As if she'd worked out, somewhere between the Teeth and here, which of the stair-folk to look at when the sea did something it had never done. "It took the wind."

Ice didn't answer.

"So we need to get out of the current," said Higu. Calm. "Before the middle. We need to get the ship out of the edge and into still water. South. Where the singer is."

"We tried that."

"We tried rowing," said Higu. "We tried sailing. We tried steering." She looked round at them all. "What else is there?"

Nobody said anything.

Then Hana heard herself say:

"It wants."

They looked at her.

She hadn't meant to say it. It had just come out, the way Aleaka's words came out, before she'd finished thinking them. She was standing at the quarterdeck rail with her crown on and her hands on the wood, looking at the grey turning water to the west, and she'd been thinking about it for an hour. Ever since Aleaka said it. *More. Come. Down. More.*

"The thing in the hole," said Hana. "Leaka says it calls. It calls *more*. It wants everything to come down." She turned round. "It *wants*."

"So?" said Newish.

"So anything that wants," said Hana, "I can make want the wrong thing."

***

The quarterdeck went very quiet.

She saw them work it out. The ones who'd been there. On floor 8, on the shelves above the big pool, with the Great Fall coming down white and a blind thing in the water that couldn't see her. *It doesn't see. It never saw any of it.* And she'd taken her crown off, and found the one thing it could feel, the slow enormous pulse of the falling water, and made it love that instead. *Make them love the wrong thing.*

It had gone head first into the base of the fall and been pounded on the rocks.

It had wanted to.

"The Cataract was a fish," said Deston slowly. "A big one. But a fish. This is—" He looked west. "This is the *sea*, Hana."

"This is a thing in a hole that wants more," said Hana. "Leaka can hear it. If Leaka can hear what it wants, I can change what it wants." She was surprised how steady her voice was. "Not for long. Not much. I don't know if I can hold something that big at all. But if I can make it want the bottom—"

"The bottom?" said Chris.

"Its own bottom. Its own dark. Down. Instead of *in*." She turned to Aleaka. "Leaka. Does it want things to come *in*, or come *down*?"

Aleaka shut her eyes. Listened. Her window glowed.

"In," she said slowly. "And then down. Both. It pulls them in, round, to the middle. So they go down." She opened her eyes. "It wants them to come to it."

"Then I make it want to go to *them*," said Hana. "Down. Its own way. Away from the edge. Into its own dark." She looked at the turning water. "If it's looking down, it's not pulling in. Just for a moment. And the edge goes slack."

"And then what?" said Higu.

"And then we need to already be moving," said Hana. "South. Hard. All at once. The moment it goes slack."

"With no wind," said Marrit.

"With the boats. And the singer." Hana looked down at the water off the stern. At the dark under it, where the frantic hum was coming from. "Leaka. Can you ask it? The singer. When the edge goes slack, can it push us?"

Aleaka looked at her for a long moment.

Then she went to the stern rail and put both her hands on it, cupped, and shut her eyes.

Nobody moved. Hana watched her. Watched her lips move. Watched the window glow over the water. Watched Sleet press against her legs, and Tuppence go bolt upright on her shoulder, and the frantic crying under the deck change. Slow. Go quiet. For a moment, nothing.

And then come back. Lower. Steadier. A single long note, held.

Aleaka opened her eyes. They were wet.

"It's under us," she said. "Right under. It's been under us the whole time. Trying to hold us back." She swallowed. "It's tired. It's so tired, Hana. It's been pushing against the current since noon." She wiped her face. "It says yes. It says it'll push. When it goes slack. It says it's been waiting a very long time for something to come out of the edge."

"Then it needs to know when," said Drew.

"Newish," said Higu.

Newish was sitting on the quarterdeck steps with her arms round her knees. She looked up.

"*Finally*," she said. "Somebody's realised I'm useful." And stood up.

***

It was Drew for where and Newish for when. The way it had been since floor 8.

Drew laid it out on the quarterdeck in the dusk with his hands, the big signs, *hold* and *up* and *back*, and let the rest come from everybody else. The boats, both, back in the water, every rower in them, lines taut to the bow, ready to pull. Not now. Not yet. At the one. The singer, under the stern, ready to push. At the one. Marrit at the wheel, hard over south. At the one.

And Hana, on the quarterdeck, at the rail, with her crown off.

And Aleaka beside her. To hear it. To tell her what it wanted, while she was holding it. To tell the singer.

"How long do you need?" said Drew. To Hana. "Holding it. For the edge to go slack enough. For us to get out."

Hana didn't know.

She said so. She stood at the rail with the grey turning water going on and on to the west and that hollow roar coming from the middle of it, and the crown in her hands, and said it honestly: "I don't know, Drew. I don't know if I can hold it at all. It's not a fish. It's—" She looked at it. "I don't know how long I can hold something that big."

"Four seconds."

Ice said it.

He was at the foot of the quarterdeck steps, with Thea. Hana hadn't known he was listening. He said it the way you'd say a number you'd always known. Without thinking. Without looking up. Flat and quiet and certain, the way he'd said *it's water, floor 11, a lot of it*.

"You held it four seconds," said Ice.

Everybody looked at him.

He looked up.

And Hana watched it land on him. What he'd said. She watched it go through his face like a crack going through ice: the words reaching him a second after they'd left his mouth, and his face going white, very slowly, from the top down, the way the Last Light had come apart from the top down at the Teeth. Thea, beside him, had gone absolutely still.

*You held it.*

Not *you can hold it*. Not *you'll need*. *You held it.*

Hana stood at the rail with her crown in her hands and looked at him.

"When?" she said.

She didn't mean to. It came out the way things came out of Aleaka, before she'd thought them. Very quietly. Just the one word.

Ice didn't say anything.

He stood at the foot of the steps with his face white and his hands at his sides and looked at her. And his face did something she'd never seen it do. Not on any floor. Not in the white room. Not at the tarn. It was the face of somebody watching a door close on something they'd been trying for a very long time to keep on the other side of it.

And under it, she saw something else.

He was looking at her the way you look at someone you've lost.

She didn't know how she knew that. She just did. She'd seen that face on other people. At the edge of things. She'd never once seen it pointed at her.

The quarterdeck was silent. Thirty-five people, and Marrit, and the hollow roar from the west, and the singer's single long note under the deck. Waiting.

Hana looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked away.

"Four seconds," she said. To Drew. Steadily. As if he'd said *you can hold it four seconds* and nothing else. As if that was all she'd heard. "Okay. Four seconds. I can do four seconds."

She heard Ice breathe out.

She didn't look at him. She heard Jared, beside her, very quietly, say "*Alice*," and that was all. Just her name. The way he said it on the shelf above the Cataract, through his teeth, with a thorn in the roof of a monster's mouth. And then nothing.

Nobody asked.

Nobody on the whole ship.

It's not mine, Hana thought. *It's not mine to pick up.* And put her crown down on the rail, and put her hands on the wood, and looked west.

***

She reached for it.

It was like putting her hand into the sea. Not the warm green sea of the Shoals, or the clear pools Xola had floated in. The deep. The black. She reached out from the quarterdeck with whatever it was she reached with, the thing she'd had since the first day, the thing that found what people wanted and turned it, gently, like turning a face toward a window. And it went out from her across the grey turning water and *down*, and down, and found nothing, and nothing, and kept going.

"Leaka," she said. Through her teeth.

"I'm here." Aleaka's hand was on her arm. "It's to the left. Down and left. The middle. It's— *more*. It's saying *more*. Can you feel it?"

She could.

Oh, she could.

She found it the way you find a sound in the dark: not with her eyes, with everything else. Down. In the middle. In the hole in the sea where Hyvred couldn't see the bottom. Something huge. Not a shape. A *want*. Enormous and blind and endless, like a mouth that had been open for a thousand years and had never once closed, pulling, pulling, at everything there was. *More. Come. Down. More.*

It didn't have anything else in it.

That was the thing that frightened her. The Cataract had wanted. But the Cataract had wanted a beat, a count, Newish. It had wanted *something*. This didn't want anything. It just wanted. *More.* It would have wanted the whole sea. It would have wanted the sky. It would have wanted the stars, if it could have reached them.

And she had to make it want something else.

*What do you love?* she thought at it. The question she always asked. The first one. The one under all of it. *What do you love? What do you want more than this?*

Nothing. It wanted nothing more than this. It only wanted more.

*Then want down*, she thought. *Want your own dark. Want the bottom. Want to go home.*

She took everything she had, and turned it.

***

It was like turning a mountain.

She'd never felt anything like it. Not the Cataract. Not the boatman on floor 5. Not the thousand small easy turnings she'd done every day of her life since the white room without thinking, a clerk's frown into a smile, a guard's no into a yes. Those had been like turning a face toward a window. This was like putting her shoulder against the side of the world and *pushing*.

It didn't want to turn.

It wasn't fighting her. It didn't know she was there. It was just *heavy*. All that want, all that endless pulling, going one way, the way it had gone forever, and she was one cat-girl on the deck of a ship with her crown off, pushing it the other way.

Something gave.

Not much. A little. Like a door that's been stuck for a hundred years moving a finger's width on its hinges. She felt the want, down in the dark, *waver*. Just for a moment. Just very slightly. Turn. Away from the edge. Away from the ship, and the wood, and the weed, and the whole grey turning sea. Down. Toward its own dark. Toward the bottom.

*Home*, she thought at it. *Go home.*

"*Hana*," said Aleaka. Very far away. "It's— it's *listening*. It's turning. It's—"

She heard Ice say *one*.

Under his breath. At the foot of the steps. Very quietly. She shouldn't have been able to hear it. She had cat ears and she was holding a mountain and she heard it anyway.

*One.*

The current went slack.

She felt it. Everybody felt it. The *Patience* had been leaning very slightly west all afternoon, against the pull, the way a tree leans in a wind, and she stopped leaning. Straightened. The tide-cord on Marrit's fingers swung back to true. The grey water at the edge of the wheel stopped turning, just for a moment, and lay still.

*Two*, said Ice.

"*ONE*," said Newish.

To everybody.

***

She didn't see it. She was holding the mountain. She heard it.

She heard the oars go into the water at once, both boats, every rower, at the same moment, like one hand. She heard the lines to the bow go taut with a crack like a whip. She heard Marrit shout something in tide-folk and the wheel spin. She heard the singer, under the stern, *rise*.

And the *Patience* moved.

Not drifted. *Moved*. Like a cart when somebody puts their shoulder to the back of it. The whole ship lurched forward under her feet, south, hard, so hard that people on the deck staggered and grabbed for ropes, and the water at the stern came up in a great white swell where something vast and pale had pushed against the hull from below with its whole length.

*Three*, said Ice.

The want was turning back.

She felt it. Down in the dark. The mountain shifting under her shoulder. Not fighting. Just *heavy*, going back the way it had always gone, the way water goes back downhill. *More. Come. Down. More.* She held it. She held it with everything. Her nose was bleeding. She could taste it. Her hands on the rail had gone white and she couldn't feel them.

*Home*, she thought at it. *Go home. Please. Just a little longer. Go home.*

*Four*, said Ice.

And she let go.

***

She came back to the deck on her knees.

She didn't remember kneeling. She was just there, at the quarterdeck rail, on her knees on the planks, with blood running out of her nose onto her collar and her crown lying on the deck beside her where it had fallen off the rail, and Aleaka's arms round her, and Milli's, and somebody's hand on the back of her head.

The ship was moving.

She could feel it. Under her knees. Not turning. Not drifting. *Moving*. South. With the boats still pulling at the bow and the singer still pushing at the stern and the water going past the hull for the first time since noon, going past, rushing, so that the rudder could bite.

"*Out*," Marrit was shouting. From the wheel. Somewhere far above her. "*We're out, we're out of the edge, we're—*"

And then the wind came back.

Hana felt it on her face. Cool. From the south. A breath. And then more than a breath. The sails above her, slack all afternoon, filled with a sound like a door slamming, one after another, mainsail, foresail, mizzen, all at once, and the whole ship leaned over and *went*.

She knelt on the deck with her nose bleeding and laughed.

She couldn't help it. She laughed so hard she couldn't breathe, with Aleaka holding her and crying at the same time, and Milli's hand on the back of her head, and Newish somewhere saying *four seconds, FOUR SECONDS, she held the SEA for FOUR SECONDS*, and Calvin in one of the boats screaming something completely incoherent, and all of it, everybody, the whole ship, laughing and shouting and the sails full and the wind cold and the grey turning wheel of the Undertow falling away behind them to the north-west, smaller and smaller, until it was just a smooth band on the edge of the dusk, turning.

"You held it," said Milli. Into her hair. Very quietly. Under the noise.

"I held it."

"Four seconds."

"Four seconds," said Hana.

She looked up.

Over Milli's shoulder. Over Aleaka's. Past all of them. Down the deck, to the foot of the quarterdeck steps.

Ice was still there.

He hadn't moved. He was standing at the foot of the steps with his face white and his hands at his sides, and Thea beside him with both her hands round his arm, and he was looking at Hana on her knees with blood on her collar and her crown on the deck.

He wasn't laughing.

He was crying. Not much. Not so anybody would notice who wasn't looking. Just his face, wet, very still, looking at her the way you'd look at somebody who'd come back. Who'd done the thing, and let go, and was still there afterwards. On her knees. Laughing.

*Four seconds was how long*, Hana thought, and didn't know where the thought came from, and let it go.

She smiled at him.

The way she'd smiled at the tarn. Small. Private. *I'm on the step.*

And after a moment, very slowly, Ice smiled back.

***

The singer stayed with them all night.

They sailed south in the dark with the wind steady and the stars out, and the hum came up through the deck again, low and gentle, the way it had been before. Not *not that way*. Something else. Aleaka said it was tired, and glad, and something she didn't have a word for, and then she fell asleep against the stern rail in the middle of saying it, with Sleet on her feet and Tuppence in her collar, and nobody woke her.

Hana sat on the coil of rope by the foremast with a cloth pressed to her nose and her crown in her lap and Milli beside her, and didn't sleep.

"Do you want to know?" said Milli.

Late. Near midnight. The ship was quiet. He said it so softly that nobody else could have heard it, even with cat ears.

Hana thought about it.

She thought about *you held it four seconds*. About *when*. About his face going white from the top down. About the way he'd counted, *one, two, three, four*, under his breath at the foot of the steps, as if he'd counted it before. As if he'd stood somewhere, once, and counted four seconds while she held something, and those four seconds had been the most important four seconds of his life.

About the way he'd looked at her afterwards. Like somebody who'd come back.

"No," said Hana.

Milli looked at her.

"I want him to be all right," said Hana. "That's what I want." She took the cloth away from her nose and looked at it. Still bleeding. A bit. "I don't need to know where he got four seconds from. I need him to stop looking like that."

Milli didn't say anything for a long time.

"He stopped," he said eventually. "When you smiled. I saw. He stopped."

"Good," said Hana.

She put the cloth back. She leaned her head on his shoulder. Her tail came round both of them, the way it always did.

Across the deck, by the mainmast, Corran was asleep in his nest of sailcloth. He'd slept all through it. Through the hum stopping, and the edge, and the boats, and the four seconds, and the wind coming back. Maigo was sitting up beside him, awake, the way he'd sat up every night since the slip, with his hand resting very lightly on the old man's blanket.

Hana watched them.

The old man was breathing. Slowly. Very slowly. With long gaps between, so long that every time, Hana found herself waiting for the next one. And every time it came.

She watched Maigo watch him, and wait, and every time, when it came, breathe out.

*He's not ill*, Rachael had said. *He's just old.*

The *Patience* ran south under the stars with the hum coming up through the deck, and the grey wheel of the sea turning somewhere behind them in the dark, calling *more*, to everything, forever. With something in the hole at the bottom of it. Something full.

And a small pale one, a long time ago, gone round, and in, and down.

Hana looked north-west, where it was.

*We'll be back*, she thought at it. Not a charm. Not anything. Just a thought, from a cat-girl on a coil of rope with a bloody nose. *We're going to have to come back. You know that.*

The sea didn't answer.

But under the deck, very faintly, the singer's hum changed. Just slightly. The way Aleaka said it changed when it heard you.
