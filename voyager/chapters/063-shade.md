Bee's wings didn't like floor 6. She found that out in the first ten minutes.

They were good wings. Big, clear, veined like leaded glass, folded flat down her back most of the time. They'd been her OC's wings for years before the white room. Then she'd woken up in the white room with them actually *on* her, and they'd buzzed when she was nervous, and she'd hated them for a week, and then she'd loved them. She'd never flown with them. They weren't for that. But on floor 1 they'd caught the breeze off the hills and hummed, and on floor 5 they'd caught the wind off the Reaches and nearly taken her off a bridge twice, and she'd laughed both times.

On floor 6, they cooked.

The sun came through them like a magnifying glass. By ten minutes out of the stair door she could feel it, a hot line down each vein, as if somebody were drawing on her back with a soldering iron. She pulled her cloak up over them, and then she was too hot under the cloak. She took it down and they cooked. She put it back up.

"Bee," said Hyvred, beside her. "Stop doing that. You're making me hot just watching."

"*You're* making *me* hot."

"I'm not *doing* anything."

"You've got horns. Horns are hot. It's science."

Hyvred laughed. It came out dry, and then she stopped laughing very quickly, because laughing let the heat in.

Nobody talked much after that.

***

The Glass Waste went out in front of them in every direction, white and glittering, and they walked into it in the middle of the afternoon. They didn't know any better.

Bee would think about that, later. How stupid it had been. Thirty-five people walking in a long line across a desert of crushed glass in the worst heat of the day, because the door had opened onto the afternoon and that was when they'd arrived. The white sky. The sun that you couldn't find without turning your whole head, and then wished you hadn't. The ground throwing it all back up into your face, so that you were cooking from above and below at once, like toast. The mirages. A lake. Trees. A tower. All of it shimmering on the horizon and none of it getting any closer.

Calvin, at the front, had stopped saying *look at my back* about an hour in. He just walked, with Rachael's hand on his collar.

Bee walked with her eyes half shut and her wings under her cloak. She thought about water. It was the only thing anybody thought about. Everybody had skins from Tether, and everybody had been drinking from them since the door, and the skins were getting light. You could feel them getting light on your hip. And the line was getting slower.

About two hours in, Gigi stopped sweating.

Bee noticed because Gigi had been complaining. Steadily, for two hours, in the blunt bratty way she had, about the heat and the glass and the sun and the stupid floor and the stupid tower and whoever had designed it. It had been the only sound in their part of the line. And then it stopped.

Bee looked round.

Gigi was walking. Still walking. But her face had gone a strange colour, flushed and dry at the same time. Her silver hair hung limp. The little blue flowers she kept pinned in it had gone brown. And she was walking very carefully, the way you walk when you're not sure where your feet are.

"Gigi," said Bee.

"I'm fine."

"You stopped complaining."

"I'm *saving* it."

Gigi's knees went.

She didn't fall over. She just sort of sat down on the glass, all at once, as if her legs had decided for her. Hyvred was there before Bee was, crouching, her hand on Gigi's face.

"She's not sweating," said Hyvred. Her voice had gone sharp. "Kei. *Kei.* She's dry. She's hot and she's *dry*."

Kei came back down the line at a run, with Dose round his neck. He knelt and put two fingers on Gigi's neck and his other hand on her forehead and looked at her eyes. His face went very calm, the way it did when something was bad.

"Heat," he said. "Too much. Her body's stopped trying." He was already in his bag. "Salt. Water. Shade. Now. We need to get her cool and we need to keep walking, because there's no shade for—" He looked round at the white. "There's no shade."

"I'll carry her," said Hyvred.

"Hyv—"

"I'll *carry* her." Hyvred was already pulling Gigi's arms over her shoulders. She stood up with Gigi on her back, staggered once, and stood. "Go. Kei, salt. Somebody give me water."

Xola was there, from Thea's party. She came running back up the line with water moving round her fingers in little curls, the way it always did, and put her hand on the back of Gigi's neck. The water went into Gigi's skin like it was being drunk. Gigi made a sound.

"Shade," said Kei again. "She needs *shade*."

There wasn't any. There was the white sky and the white sun and the glittering glass in every direction, and nothing taller than a man for miles.

Bee took out her sketchbook.

***

She'd had it since floor 1. A little leather book with blank pages, from a stall in Hearthstead, and a stick of charcoal. On floor 1, she'd found out that if she drew something in it and *meant* it, really meant it, it came off the page. Not for long. Thirty seconds, maybe. A drawn sun that kept you warm. A drawn wall that a sheep bounced off. A drawn rope that held for exactly as long as she could keep picturing it.

She'd never drawn shade.

She drew it now, walking, in the glare, with sweat running into her eyes. A parasol. A big one, like the ones at the beach, with ribs and a fringe. She drew it fast and badly, and meant it with everything she had. She tore the page out and held it up over Hyvred's head.

The parasol came off the page.

Big and pale and slightly wobbly, the way all her drawings were, with charcoal lines still showing at the edges. It opened over Hyvred and Gigi like a flower. And under it there was shade. Real shade, a dark round shadow on the glass, moving with them.

Gigi opened her eyes.

"Oh," she said weakly. "Oh, that's nice."

The parasol lasted thirty-two seconds. Then it went back into charcoal dust and blew away.

Bee drew another one.

She drew them for two hours. Walking, one after another, as fast as she could. Parasols, awnings, a tree once, which didn't work as well. A cloud, which worked better than she'd expected. Thirty seconds of shade, then the dust, then another. Her hand cramped. The charcoal wore down to a stub and she started on the second stick. Kei walked beside Hyvred feeding Gigi salt and sips of water. Xola walked on the other side with her hand on Gigi's neck. Hyvred walked in the middle with Gigi on her back and didn't say a word, and didn't stop.

Four of them round Gigi. And Bee, drawing.

"Stop drawing umbrellas at me," said Gigi, on about the fortieth one. Her voice was stronger. "It's embarrassing."

"Shut up and be in the shade," said Bee.

Gigi shut up and was in the shade.

***

About an hour from the door, the line stopped.

Not because anybody fell. Because Tiger, out on the edge of it, had seen something on a rock and run to look. The rock was a big black spur of glass sticking up out of the dunes. There was something scratched into the side of it, at head height, on the shady side. An arrow. Pointing down.

Tiger dug. With his hands, in the sand at the foot of the rock, in the shade, fast. Bee, coming up with her charcoal stub and her sketchbook, saw him stop. He'd found something. He pulled it up out of the sand.

A water skin. Full. Fat and heavy and cool from the sand. And another under it. And a third.

And round the neck of each one, tied in a little bow just under the cork, there was a strip of something brown and papery.

Tiger held one up. He looked at the bow round its neck.

And Tiger, who'd run laps round an island in the sky at sunrise, who'd never stopped moving in his life, who'd found a pile of onion skins under a pine on floor 2 on the sixth day and posted *hey voyager. you peel onions really well*, sat down in the sand at the foot of the rock with the water skin in his lap. And cried.

He didn't say anything. He just held the skin and cried, with his tail flat on the sand and his ears flat on his head, and the strip of onion skin round the cork shaking in his fingers.

Hana went to him first. She knelt down in the sand beside him and looked at the onion skin, and put her hand up, without seeming to know she was doing it, and touched the front of her crown. Where, inside, tucked in, there was another one. From the same hands.

"He peeled them properly," said Tiger. His voice was wrecked. "Look. All the way round. In one go. He always—" He couldn't finish.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Drew came down the line and looked at the three skins, and the arrow on the rock. He looked out across the white Glass in the direction they were walking.

"There'll be more," said Drew. "If there's one, there'll be more. Along the way we're going." He turned round. "Everyone drinks. Gigi first. Then we wait here, in the shade of this rock, till it's dark. And we walk at night." He looked at the arrow. "That's what this means. He's telling us. Walk at night."

They waited in the shade of the rock till dark. Gigi drank first. Then everybody. And when the sun went down, the Glass stopped glittering, and the air went from an oven to merely hot. They walked at night, under stars, in a long line. Every few hours there was another black rock with another arrow scratched in it, and another three skins in the sand, with onion skins on the corks.

Four of them. Twelve skins. Exactly enough to get thirty-five people to Deepwell, if they were careful.

Tiger carried the empty ones. All twelve. He wouldn't let anybody else.

***

Deepwell was a hole in the world with a town in it, and at the top of it, in a little stone booth by a chain of buckets, there was a big slow man with a ladle.

"First cup's free," he said, before anybody could ask.

He ladled them out. One after another, thirty-five cups of black cold water from the bottom of the pit, onto the ledge of his booth. He didn't take any money. He didn't look at anybody's face for very long. When they'd all drunk, he ladled out thirty-five more.

Dennis, who couldn't help himself, said, "Why's it free?"

"It's paid," said the man.

"By who?"

The man looked at Dennis with slow brown eyes, for a long time. Then he looked past Dennis, at Gabe, standing at the back of the crowd with two shields on his back. The man looked at him the longest.

"Man in a white helm," he said. "Walked in alone in the day, three weeks back, near enough. Said thirty-five were coming." He ladled out another cup. "Said they were his."

Gabe stood very still.

"Which way did he go?" he said. His voice had gone rough.

The man looked east. Out across the Glass, toward the far rim of the pit, and the white beyond it.

"East," said the man. "Most days. Comes back for water, every few." He put the ladle down. "Hasn't been in for four."

***

Hyvred saw it at the bucket chain, an hour later. Bee was with her. They'd gone up to the lip of the pit to look at the Glass in the morning light, because Hyvred had wanted to, and Bee went where Hyvred went.

Hyvred stopped. She was looking at the stone lip of the pit, by the top of the bucket chain, where the path came in from the east.

"It's here," she said.

"What is?"

"His light." Hyvred's eyes were moving, the way they did, reading something on the air that nobody else could see. "It's all over this path. In and out. In and out. Over and over." She turned round in a slow circle. "Bee. It's *fresh*. Some of it's from days ago. Some of it's—" She stopped. "It's not on the stair. Not going *up*. It's going *east*. And coming back. And going east again."

"He's still here," said Bee.

"He's still *here*." Hyvred's voice cracked in the middle. "Chris was right. On the stair. He hasn't gone up. He's on this floor. With us. *Right now*."

Bee looked east, across the Glass, at the white.

Somewhere out there, a man in a white helm who'd left them water with onion skins on the corks was walking. In and out. Over and over.

"Something's stopped him," said Bee slowly. "That's what Chris said. Something on six."

Hyvred didn't say anything. She just stood on the lip of the pit and looked east, with her golden horns catching the morning, and her eyes wet.

***

They argued about it all of the hundred-and-twenty-first day. In the shade, on the third ring, in a big round room that Ostrel's wife rented them for the floor.

Gabe wanted to go. Of course Gabe wanted to go. He stood in the middle of the room with two shields on his back, his own and the other one, the one with the lamp on it, and said *I'm going* in a voice that meant it.

And then he stopped. In the middle of saying it. Bee watched it happen.

"No," said Gabe. "No. I'm not."

Everybody looked at him.

"If he sees me," said Gabe slowly. As if he was working it out as he said it, and hated every word. "If he sees me coming. He'll go. He'll go up, like he always does. Four floors, he's always been one ahead. And now he's not, and something's stopped him, and if I go out there with my big stupid face and he sees me coming—" He swallowed. "He'll find a way. He'll get up the stair somehow, just to get away from me. Because he's not ready. He's never been ready."

The room was very quiet.

"So somebody else goes," said Gabe. "Somebody he won't run from."

Drew looked at him for a long time.

"Ten," said Drew. "Two parties. On the slate, so everybody knows. Hyvred's, because she can see it. Mine, because I'm the general and because Thea's got the lantern." He looked round. "If he's out there, ten's enough to find him. Not so many he panics."

"I'll come," said Gigi, from the corner. She was sitting up on a pile of cushions, pale, with a cup of water in both hands. Kei had said she shouldn't walk for a day.

"You'll stay," said Kei.

"I'm in Hyvred's party. Hyvred's party's going." Gigi's chin came up, the bratty way. "I was *carried* here. By Hyv. For hours. I'm not sitting in a hole while she goes out there." She looked at Hyvred. "I'm coming."

Hyvred looked back at her. Then she nodded.

"Then we go at dusk," said Drew. "Tomorrow. And we walk at night."

Gabe went over to Hyvred. Bee saw him bend down and say something to her, very low, with his hand on her shoulder. Hyvred's face did something. She nodded.

Later, Bee asked her what he'd said.

"He said, *tell him*," said Hyvred. "And then he couldn't think what. He stood there for ages. And then he said, *tell him I'm still here.*"

***

They left on the hundred-and-twenty-second day, at dusk. Ten of them. Out over the eastern lip of the pit onto the Glass, as the sun went down red and the white went gold, then grey, then dark.

Hyvred went first. She didn't need John's lights or Thea's lantern or anything. She walked across the dark Glass like somebody walking down a lit street, following something only she could see. Thea walked just behind her, with the lantern, its amber light falling on the glass. Then Drew. Then the others. Higu with her bow, silent, looking at the stars. Xola. Chi, without Waddles, who'd been left in Deepwell in a bucket of water with strict instructions. Hako, with his spear, cheerful, the way he was even when things weren't. Kei, with Dose. Gigi, walking on her own feet, pale and stubborn. And Bee.

"It's like a road," said Hyvred, about an hour out. Her voice was very quiet. "He's walked it so many times. Over and over. Back and forth. It's like a road made of lamplight."

They walked all night. Bee watched the stars come out over the Glass, the real stars, too many, and thought about the man who'd walked this road over and over in the dark, alone, with the same stars over him.

A little before dawn, they came to the cistern.

***

It was a ruin. Four broken walls of black glass round a square of sand, and in the middle, a round stone mouth going down into the ground.

Hyvred stopped in the gap of the broken wall. She didn't go in. She stood there with her hand on the black glass and looked at it all for a long time, her eyes moving.

"He lived here," she said.

Bee went past her and looked down into the cistern.

It was a dry pit. Round, stone-lined, five metres deep. At the bottom, in the dark, there was sand. And in the sand, even Bee could see it, without Afterglow, without anything: the shape where somebody had lain. A long hollow, pressed into the sand by night after night. A ring of blackened stones where a small fire had been, over and over. A little pile of something by the wall that turned out, when Kei climbed down and looked, to be onion skins. Peeled properly. In long curls.

And on the sand, near the hollow, a dark stain. Old. Dried brown.

Kei crouched down by it and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"Blood," he said, quietly. "Not much. A cut. A deep one." He looked up at them, at the top of the cistern. "On six, it stays."

"His light's small here," said Hyvred. She'd come to the edge of the cistern and was looking down into it, and her voice had gone very thin. "All over the walls. The bottom. Everywhere. It's the smallest I've ever seen it. Smaller than behind the wall on four." She put her hand over her mouth. "Bee. He's been *frightened* here. For weeks. Lying at the bottom of a hole in the desert being frightened."

Bee took out her sketchbook.

She didn't draw anything that would come off the page. She just drew, the way she'd always drawn, since she was small, when something was too much to look at and she needed her hands to do something. The broken walls. The round mouth. The hollow in the sand at the bottom. The pile of onion skins. She drew it fast and careful in the grey light before dawn. Thea stood at the edge of the cistern with the lantern held out over it, so that its amber light fell down into the dark onto the place where he'd slept. Nobody said anything.

"His trail goes on," said Hyvred, after a long time. She'd turned east again. "From here. Into—" She stopped. "There's a bowl. A big one. In the dunes. His trail goes down into it. And comes back. And goes down. And comes back." Her voice went strange. "Over and over. A dozen times. More. He keeps going in and turning round."

"Something's in there," said Higu.

It was the first thing Higu had said all night. She was standing at the eastern wall of the cistern, looking out across the dark Glass toward where the dunes dipped down, with her pale lilac eyes half shut and calm.

"Something he can't get past," said Higu. "That's what that is. That's somebody walking up to a door again and again and not being able to open it."

***

They went down into the bowl in the middle of the morning, after Kei had made them all drink and sit in the cistern's shade for an hour.

Bee would remember that, afterwards. That it was beautiful. The sun was high and white over the eastern rim and the whole bowl was lit gold. It was a great shallow dip in the dunes, a kilometre across, the glass of it catching the light like a bowl of honey. And in the middle, far off, in a ring of black rocks, there was a pool. Round, still, and impossibly, shockingly blue. Like a piece of a sky from somewhere else, dropped in the desert.

"Oh," said Xola softly. "Oh, that's *pretty*."

They followed Hyvred down the western slope onto the floor of the bowl. Following the road made of lamplight that only she could see. In, toward the blue. One hundred metres. One hundred and fifty.

At two hundred, Hyvred stopped.

"It stops," she said. Her voice was very odd. "Here. His trail. It comes this far and it—it doesn't stop. It *turns*. Every time. Right here. Every single time." She turned round slowly. "This is where he turns round."

Bee looked past her. Toward the middle of the bowl. Toward the blue water and the ring of black rocks.

The air was moving. The way air moves over hot glass, all of it rippling, shimmering, the heat coming up off the bowl in the early sun. Everywhere except one place. In the middle distance, between them and the water, there was a place where the air wasn't moving. A patch of stillness in the middle of all the shimmer, like a gap in a curtain.

And then there was someone standing in it.
