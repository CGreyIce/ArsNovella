He woke up and didn't know where he was.

That had happened before. Hundreds of times. Ten years of waking up on floors he'd never slept on, in camps and caves and ruins and the bottoms of holes, with his hand already going for his sword before his eyes were open. He knew how to wake up not knowing where he was. You didn't move. You listened. You counted what you could hear and what you couldn't. You found the door.

He didn't move. He listened.

Water. A great deal of it, going a long way down, somewhere below. Close. A steady endless roar, so big it was almost a silence. Rain on stone, light, stopping. Wind. A bird.

And breathing. Lots of it. All round him.

He opened his eyes.

Stone. A low ceiling of old flagstones, a little above his face. Three stone walls. And the fourth side open, onto grey light and wet grass and a hillside going down. Against the wall beside him, leaning where he'd left it, a round shield. Dark grey, with a lamp in the middle.

And behind his breastbone, before he'd even thought to look for them, thirty-five threads.

Not stretched across a floor. Not running up a hill road or across a desert. *Here*. All round him. Within a few metres. Thick as ropes. Every one of them slow and soft and settled, the way threads went when people were asleep.

None of them was red.

He lay there and felt them. He didn't move. He'd slept. He'd actually slept. All night, without waking once, without dreaming. He hadn't done that since a stone room in Hearthstead on the first night of the first run, ten years and a hundred and fifty-four days ago. He'd forgotten what it felt like to wake up from it. His whole body felt strange. Heavy and loose, like a rope somebody had finally let go of.

He turned his head.

***

Gabe was asleep.

He was sitting at the open side of the shelter, exactly where he'd sat down last night. His back was against the end of the wall and his legs were out on the wet grass and the shield was across his knees. His chin was on his chest. The wolf coat was pulled round him, beaded with rain. He was snoring very gently. One hand had slid off the shield in his sleep and was lying on the grass beside him, palm up.

And against his shoulder, a little to one side, John. Asleep too. His head on Gabe's arm, his mouth open. No mote. It had gone out in the night.

*Off to one side.*

He looked at the two of them for a long time.

They'd sat up all night. Or most of it. He knew they had, because he'd fallen asleep to them talking, low, and Gabe humming. Somewhere near dawn, they'd lost. Both of them. Sitting up against a wall in the rain, watching a door so he wouldn't have to.

He didn't mind that they'd fallen asleep. He found he didn't mind at all. He'd watched doors for so long. It turned out that what mattered was that somebody had sat down there and meant to.

And past them, on the grass, there was someone else awake.

Thea was sitting a little way down the slope. She had her knees up and the lantern on the grass beside her, still lit, its amber pale in the grey morning. She was looking down the hill, at whatever was below. She'd been there all night too, he thought. Keeping the light.

She turned her head and looked at him.

She didn't say anything for a moment. She just looked at him, lying in the stone shelter with his eyes open. Then:

"Go back to sleep," said Thea.

"It's morning."

"So?"

He looked at her.

"It's barely morning," said Thea. "Nobody's up. Gabe's asleep. The rota hasn't even been invented yet." She looked back down the hill. "Go back to sleep, Ice."

He lay there for a moment longer.

Then he shut his eyes. And went back to sleep. For an hour. Because she'd said so.

He'd think about that afterwards. That he'd done it. That somebody had told him to and he'd let them.

***

When he woke up the second time, the whole hillside was awake, and Calvin was shouting about the leaves.

"The leaves are *changing*," Calvin was saying. "Look. Look at them. They're *yellow*. They're going *yellow*."

Ice came out of the shelter, and stopped.

The hillside went down from the stair door in a long wet slope of grass and rock to the edge of a cliff. And beyond the edge of the cliff, below, was the Cataracts.

He'd forgotten. He'd seen it before, the first time, and he'd forgotten how big it was. A river. A huge one, wider than any river on any floor below, coming out of a gorge in the hills to the west. And then falling. Not once. Over and over. Down a vast staircase of lakes, one below another below another, each one a great still pool of green-grey water, each one spilling over its lip into the one below in a long white fall. Down and down and down, a dozen falls, two dozen, going away into mist. The roar of it came up the cliff like weather. Spray hung over the whole valley in a white haze, and the morning sun was coming through it. Everywhere he looked, there were rainbows. Not one. Dozens. Little ones and huge ones, arching over the falls and through the spray, coming and going as the light moved.

And all along the edges of the lakes, on the slopes going down, trees. Willows, and something like oaks, and tall thin pale-barked trees he didn't have a name for. And their leaves were turning.

Gold. Yellow. Red at the tips. A whole valley of trees going slowly into autumn, under rainbows, beside falling water.

"It's autumn," said Higu.

She was standing at the edge of the cliff with Chi, looking down at the trees with her pale lilac eyes. She sounded very calm and very far away.

"It was spring on seven," she said. "On five there wasn't any season. On six there was only hot." She looked at the gold leaves. "The floors have their own years."

Ice stood at the top of the hill and looked at the valley going into autumn.

He'd known that. He'd known it for ten years. Every floor had its own seasons, its own year, turning on its own. You could leave a floor in spring and come back to it two years later and find it in winter. It was one of the things that had made it feel real, the first time, on the higher floors. That time went on everywhere, without you.

He'd forgotten that the first autumn was here. On eight. He'd forgotten what everybody's faces had looked like, the first time they saw the leaves change.

He looked at their faces now.

***

They ate Merle's bread for breakfast, on the hillside, in the spray.

It was a day old. Yesterday's tomorrow's bread, Dennis called it, which made Malcolm groan and Tiger laugh so hard he choked. It was still good. They ate it sitting on the wet grass in a big loose circle, all thirty-six, with the rainbows coming and going over the valley below.

And then Drew stood up, and found a flat grey rock by the stair door, and took a stick of chalk out of his pocket.

"Rota," said Drew.

Everybody looked at him.

"Gabe's rule," said Drew. He was writing on the rock. "From last night. *Nobody sleeps with nobody watching.*" He looked up. "It's a good rule. I'm making it everybody's."

He wrote it at the top, in his big square letters.

*NOBODY SLEEPS WITH NOBODY WATCHING.*

And under it:

*NOBODY WATCHES ALONE.*

"Pairs," said Drew. "Every night. Two-hour watches. Everybody's door, not just one. Wherever we're sleeping, somebody's awake. Two somebodies." He looked round at them. "I'll do the list. Everybody takes a turn. Including me. Including Gabe, even though Gabe already took the whole of last night and fell asleep on John at about four."

"I did *not*—"

"You did," said John. "I've got a dead arm."

"Including," said Drew, "him."

He turned and looked at Ice.

Ice opened his mouth.

"I can take a watch," he said. "I've watched—" He stopped. He'd been about to say *I've watched doors for ten years*. He felt the words come right up to the edge of his teeth. "I've watched a lot of doors," he said instead. "I'm good at it. I can take a whole night. Let somebody else sleep."

"You can take a watch," said Gabe.

Everybody looked at Gabe. He was sitting on the grass with his own shield across his knees and the Kiln-heart Iron in its pouch at his belt, glowing faintly in the grey light. He didn't look up from the bread he was eating.

"You can take a watch," said Gabe again. "You're not taking one alone."

"Gabe—"

"*Nobody watches alone.*" Gabe pointed at the rock with a piece of crust. "It's on the rock. Drew wrote it. It's a rule now." He finally looked up, and his face was very steady. "You did a hundred and fifty-three nights on your own, Dec. You don't get to do any more."

Nobody said anything.

Drew wrote, under the rest, in smaller letters:

*ICE: WITH SOMEONE. ALWAYS.*

Ice looked at the rock.

Then he looked round at all of them. At thirty-five people sitting on a wet hillside in the spray, with day-old bread in their hands, watching him. Waiting to see what he'd say. Waiting, he realised, to see if he'd fight it.

Six weeks ago he'd have fought it. A month ago he'd have nodded and done it anyway, alone, at three in the morning when everybody was asleep.

"Okay," he said.

Gabe looked at him for a long moment, as if checking. Then he nodded, and went back to his bread.

***

Higu was the one who thought of the other thing.

She came and sat down beside him on the grass when Drew had finished the rota and everybody was packing. She didn't say anything for a while. She was good at that. She looked down at the falls, and the rainbows, and the gold trees.

"You know where things are," she said eventually. "Don't you. On this floor."

He looked at her.

"You knew the city on seven," said Higu. "You knew the stair on six. You knew the bird on five, until it changed." She was still looking at the falls. "You know what's down there."

He was quiet for a second.

"Some of it," he said.

"And the tower watches what you do," said Higu. "You said so. In the square. It changed the bird because of how you fought the Kiln. It made the thing in the bowl out of—" She stopped. "Out of something. It counters what you do."

"Yes."

"So here's what I think," said Higu. Calm. Slow. The way she'd said *we stop chasing him* to Drew on the Glass. "You tell us where things are. What's there. What it does. Where the city is, where the bad thing is, which way the path goes." She turned and looked at him with her pale eyes. "And then *we* decide how to do it. Not you. All of us. Together. Some way you've never done it."

He stared at her.

"If we do it your way," said Higu, "it'll learn your way. Again. The way it learned the stoop and the fire." She looked back at the falls. "If we do it a new way. A way none of us has ever done it, not even you. Then it's new. It's never seen it. It can't have learned it."

He sat on the wet grass and looked at her for a long time.

"That's—" He stopped. "Higu, that's really good."

"I draw maps," said Higu.

"It's a firewall," said Chris, who'd come up behind them with his notebook. He'd clearly been listening. "That's what that is. You don't let the thing that's watching see how you'd normally do it. You put a layer in between." He was writing. "Ice is the documentation. We're the implementation."

"Chris," said Higu, "nobody knows what that means."

"*I* know what it means."

Ice laughed. He couldn't help it. He sat on the hillside and laughed, and Higu almost smiled.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. There's a city at the bottom of the falls. About two days down. The river-folk live there. They're old. They live a very long time. They're kind." He looked down at the biggest of the falls, the one in the middle of the staircase, where the white water went down a hundred metres into a great green pool. "And there's something in that one. In the big pool. A serpent. It's the floor guardian." He looked at Calvin, who had wandered over. "Don't swim near the big one."

"Noted," said Calvin.

"I mean it, Cal."

"I said *noted*." Calvin grinned. "I'm the scout. I note things." He looked down at the falls. "Okay. Big one, no swimming. City at the bottom. Kind fish people." He turned round and looked at all of them. "*Look at my back.*"

***

He walked in the middle.

It was the hardest thing he'd done in days. Harder than the bells. They went down from the hillside in a long line, onto a stair cut into the rock beside the falls, wet and slippery and steep, with the spray coming over it in sheets. Calvin first, with Rachael. Drew last. And Ice in the middle, between Gabe and Thea, with the lamp shield on his back. Every single part of him wanted to be at the front.

He could see the stair ahead. He knew it. He knew where it narrowed, and where a section had fallen away the first time and they'd had to rope across. He knew where the moss was worst. He knew all of it, and Calvin didn't. Calvin was up there at the front of the line, *looking*, the way a scout looked at a path he'd never seen, testing every step.

His foot kept wanting to go faster.

"Your foot's tapping," said Alea.

She was on his shoulder, small and teal and white in the spray. Since the bakery, he'd stopped minding whether she was on his shoulder in front of people. He'd decided, somewhere on the stair from Evermorrow, that if they couldn't see her, it didn't matter. If they could, it didn't matter either.

"It's not."

"It is. You keep doing a little hop. Like a dog that wants to go out."

"I'm walking down a stair, Alea."

"You're walking down a stair *in the middle*," said the fox. "For the first time since the white room. I'm allowed to enjoy it."

He didn't answer. He kept walking. In the middle.

At the narrow place, where the stair had fallen away, Calvin stopped. Ice saw him stop. He saw him look at the gap, three metres of nothing with the falls roaring below it. And he felt his whole body go forward, *I know this, I know how*—

Gabe's hand landed on his shoulder. Heavy. Not pushing. Just there.

He stopped.

Up at the front, Calvin looked at the gap for a long time. Then he turned round and called back down the line, loud over the water. "Rope! Anybody got rope? Jared? Salty?" And Jared came up the line with a coil of it, and Salty, and the two of them and Calvin worked out how to get across. It wasn't the way they'd done it the first time. The first time Ice had jumped it, and then thrown the rope back. This time Salty planted himself on the near side like a mountain and Calvin went across hanging from the rope with Salty taking his whole weight, and then tied it off on a spike of rock on the far side.

It took twenty minutes longer than his way would have.

Nobody fell. Nobody got hurt. And when Ice went across the rope himself, hand over hand, with Gabe behind him and Thea in front, he looked down at the falls going away below. He thought: *it's never seen that. It's never once seen Salty stand there like a mountain and take Calvin's weight.*

"Firewall," said Alea, quietly. And laughed.

***

They camped that night on a broad ledge beside one of the lakes, halfway down. The trees here were gold and red, and leaves came down on the water and floated there, turning slowly. The falls above and below roared. Drew read out the rota from the rock in his head, because he'd memorised it, and nobody argued.

Ice's watch was the second one. Midnight to two. With Rachael.

They sat at the edge of the ledge, by the dark water, with the sleeping camp behind them and the falls roaring below. Rachael didn't say anything for the first hour. She sat with her staff across her knees and her frown on and watched the dark. He watched it too. It was easy, being quiet with Rachael. She'd never once in her life said anything she didn't mean, and she didn't believe in saying things to fill a silence.

In the second hour, she said something.

"You feel it twice," said Rachael.

He looked at her.

"When you heal yourself," said Rachael. She didn't look at him. She was looking at the water. "With the ember. Or whatever it is. You feel the cut. And then you feel it close, slowly. And then you get the echo." She was quiet. "I told them. On five. All of them. When we worked out what it costs you."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"You told them," he said at last.

"On five. In the inn, at Tether. After Milli." Rachael's frown went deeper. "Somebody said, *at least he can heal himself.* As if that made it fine. And I said it doesn't. And I told them. About the echo. About it being twice." She swallowed. "I'd never told anybody before. About mine."

He looked at her. At the healer who didn't cry, and didn't hug, and had held Calvin's broken arm together with cold water on the first day and felt the break in her own.

"Rachael," he said.

"So here's the thing," said Rachael. She turned and looked at him then, straight, the way she looked at everything. "When you're hurt. On this floor, or the next one, or any of them. You don't do the ember first. You don't do it on your own, twice, in the dark, so nobody sees." Her voice was very flat. "You tell me. And I do it. And *I* get the echo. Once." She looked at him. "That's what I'm for."

"You'd feel it—"

"Once," said Rachael. "Instead of you feeling it twice. That's better maths." She turned back to the water. "That's not a request, by the way."

He sat at the edge of the ledge and looked at the back of her head for a long time.

Somewhere behind him, in the sleeping camp, he felt the threads. Thirty-five of them. Thirty-three asleep. One, Thea's, half awake, he thought, turning over. And one right beside him. Rachael's. Steady. Clean. The colour of cold water.

"Okay," he said.

Rachael nodded once, without looking round.

"Good," she said.

***

At the end of the watch, when Rachael had gone to wake Dennis and Tiger for the next one, he sat on his own for a minute. He wasn't watching anything. He'd been relieved. He was allowed to sleep. He just sat there, for a minute, by the water.

And opened his window.

[ Voyager · Lv 45 · Tier 4 trial required to advance · Experience held ]

The little grey padlock. Patient.

"When?" said Alea.

He looked at it.

"In the city," he said. "Near the water." He didn't know why. It just seemed right. "When we're all there."

"*All*," said Alea.

"All."

"That's new," said the fox. "You've never wanted anybody near for a trial. Not once. Not in either life."

He closed the window.

"Everything's new," he said.

He went back to the camp. He lay down in his bedroll between Gabe and Deston, on the ledge by the lake. Dennis and Tiger were sitting up at the edge of the water with their backs to everybody, arguing quietly about something, keeping the watch.

He shut his eyes. He slept.
