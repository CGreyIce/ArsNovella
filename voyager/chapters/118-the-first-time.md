"There were thirty-six of us," said Ice.

He said it to the water. He couldn't say it to her face. Not the first part. He sat on the rock with his hands round his knees and looked at the tarn, at the sky in it, the green and violet moving slowly over the black, and said it to that.

"The first time. Thirty-six. In the white room. We all woke up together. At the same time. In our circles." He breathed. "Nobody ran."

Beside him, Thea didn't move.

"The lights came up out of the floor, and said *welcome, player*, and we all stood there like idiots and looked at each other. Gabe found me in about four seconds. He grabbed me by the shoulders and shook me and said *Dec, are you seeing this*. Calvin screamed. Dennis asked if anybody else's light was saying it was a system and could he speak to a manager." He almost smiled. "And then the lights picked nine of us. Not seven. Nine. And said *choose*. And everybody picked fours. Parties of four. Not five."

"Who did I pick?" said Thea. Very quietly.

"Xola. Higu. Chi."

She breathed in.

"Same as this time," she said. "Nearly."

"Nearly. Drew was a head, the first time. He had his own." He looked at the water. "I was a head. I had Deston. And Gabe. And Xav."

He felt her look at him.

"And we went out of the arch," he said. "Together. All thirty-six. And it took us ten years."

***

He told her about the first floors.

He hadn't meant to start there. He'd thought, all day, walking round the tarn, that he'd start at the end. With the thing she'd stepped in front of. He'd thought that was what mattered. But he sat on the rock in the starlight and found that it came out from the beginning, the way a story does when you've never once told it to anybody all the way through.

Floor 1, in the spring, with the sheep and the Bellwether and a stone room in Hearthstead where he'd lain awake the whole first night, the way everybody had. Mother Crane, who fed all thirty-six of them on credit for a month and taught him to peel onions on the fourth morning, and shouted at Calvin for putting his feet on the table. Floor 2 in the snow, with the Old Stag, which had taken them nine tries. Floor 3, the Fen, where the wisps lied, and they hadn't had a lantern, and they'd gone round in circles in the reeds for two weeks and come out the other side at Lv 8 because nobody could stand it any more.

"Lv 8," said Thea. "And four wanted eighteen."

"Nobody knew. We didn't know anything. Nobody did. We'd all read the stories. Half of us had read the stories for years. Gabe and Deston and Calvin, they'd read every one. *Transmigrated to another world.* They thought they knew how it went." He shook his head. "None of it helped. Reading about something isn't the same as standing in it."

"No," said Thea.

"And then we went up to four."

He stopped.

She didn't say anything. She waited. She'd always been good at that. He'd forgotten how good. On Earth, on a call at two in the morning, when he'd stop in the middle of something, she'd wait. Not say *go on*. Just be there, on the other end, breathing, until he did.

"We were Lv 8," he said. "Some of us 9. We were tired of the swamp. We'd been in it two weeks and it felt like a year. Everybody wanted to go. Nobody knew floor 4 was where the tutorial stopped." He was quiet. "We came out of the door at the top of two hundred and four steps into the red city. All thirty-six of us. And Calvin ran ahead."

Thea's hand went to her mouth.

"Into the first square," said Ice. "Talking. The way he does. *Look at this, look at this, guys, look*—" He stopped. "And there was a thing in the square. We called it the Quiet, after. It doesn't make any noise. It hunts noise." He looked at his hands. "It took his arm. His left one. At the elbow. And Rachael went past me to get to him, and I didn't see her go. And she got to him. And she held his arm while it—" He stopped again. "And Dennis ran to her. Shouting her name. By the wall."

Thea didn't say anything. Her hand was still over her mouth.

"That was the first two," said Ice.

***

The aurora moved over them. Very slowly. Green, going to violet at the edges, rippling, the way a curtain moves in a window when somebody's opened a door somewhere else in the house.

He told her the rest of four. The six weeks in the red city afterwards. Two graves in the red stone below the first square, because floor 4 didn't take things away. Calvin with one arm, at the back, never first again, never above a whisper. All of them, thirty-four, coming up the stair to floor 5 in the dark, and sitting on an island the size of a house in the middle of the sky, not talking.

"That's why the note," said Thea.

"That's why the note."

"*Be careful. Train longer on the third floor.*"

"I wrote it on the back of Deston's card in the white room with my finger," he said. "In the first minute. The cards take a fingertip as ink, for the first minute. I wrote it and put it under his hand." He swallowed. "He was asleep. He looked about thirteen. I wanted to wake him up so badly. I wanted to wake all of you up."

"Why didn't you?"

He didn't answer for a long time.

"Because if I'd woken you up," he said, "I'd have been one of thirty-six again. In a room. With everybody grabbing my shoulders. Going out of the arch together, the same way, at the same speed, into the same floors." He looked at the water. "And I knew where that went. I'd been there. I'd been there for ten years." He breathed. "I needed to be ahead. Not with you. Ahead. A floor ahead. Fast enough to get to four before any of you. Fast enough to kill the Quiet on my own, before Calvin ever got into that square."

"You did," said Thea. "Kill it."

"Day twenty-five."

"And Calvin's got both his arms."

"Calvin's got both his arms," said Ice. And his voice went, there, on the rock, the way it had gone on the jetty when he looked at Calvin's arms for the first time. "And Rachael carried the cold out of Dennis's arm on ten and cried. And Dennis ran to her, *shouting*. And this time he didn't—" He stopped. "This time they're both still here."

Thea put her hand on his arm.

Not on his hand. On his forearm. Just above the wrist. Lightly. The way she had once before, on the seventieth floor, a long time ago, gone before he could think about it.

She didn't take it away.

***

"I said them all once," he said. After a while. "Every one. On four. In the House of Ash. There's a carving over the door. You read it, the first time. *It's where they came to say things.*" He was quiet. "I knelt there and said every name to the dark behind the grille. Where, and when, and what I'd been doing, and what I hadn't. All thirty-five of you. Some of them took a minute. Some took an hour." He looked at her hand on his arm. "I'll tell you all of them. Every one. I promise. Every floor. Every name." He swallowed. "Not tonight."

"Okay," said Thea.

"Tonight I want to tell you the end."

"Okay," said Thea again.

"And something on nine."

She looked at him.

"I got lost," he said. "The first time. On nine. In the Down. On my own, on the seventh day, because I thought I'd be faster. Three days. The sawyers took my shield to pieces." He breathed. "And on the third night I saw a fire. On the ground. In the dark, at the foot of the seven. And I walked to it, because it was the only thing in the forest that didn't move."

"Gabe," said Thea.

"Gabe. Three nights. With a heap of wood. Burr told him he was mad." He laughed, a little, and it hurt. "And I never asked him about it. Not once. Not in ten years. And then on nine this time I asked him to make a fire if I'm ever late. And he said *every night*. And he doesn't know why I asked. He's guessed. I think he's guessed. But he doesn't *know*."

Thea's hand tightened on his arm.

"I wanted you to know that one," he said. "Because it's his. And because it's the first time somebody stood still and made a light so I could find my way back. And I didn't know that's what it was for ten years."

***

He told her about the middle.

Not all of it. He couldn't. Not tonight. The shape of it. Ten years, floor by floor. The long climb. People going. One at a time. Not all at once, after four; slowly. A floor, and then nothing for a while, and then another floor. Ninety floors of it. He didn't say who. She didn't ask. He felt her not ask. She sat beside him with her feet in the cold water and her hand on his arm and listened to the shape of it the way you'd listen to someone describe a house you'd lived in and couldn't remember.

And then he told her about them.

Not the floors. Them. The two of them. The first time.

"On Earth I liked you," he said.

He felt her go still.

"You knew," he said. "You always knew. On the calls. A year of them. You singing and me playing and both of us pretending it was just that." He looked at the tarn. "And then it passed. For me. The way those things pass. You had exams. You meant to call more. I meant to call more. And then a white room."

"I know," said Thea. Very quietly.

"And then up here," said Ice, "it came back."

The aurora rippled over the tarn. Green. Violet. A long slow fold of blue at the edge, like the hem of something.

"So slowly I didn't see it," he said. "Ten years. It came back the way a tide comes in. You don't notice it. You look up and it's round your ankles." He swallowed. "On this rock. The first year. The tenth floor. You were sitting right here, where you are now, with your boots off and your feet in the water. Singing. To nobody. That song. The one from the end of the calls."

Thea's breath went out of her.

"I stood up there," he said. He pointed. Up the slope behind them, in the dark, at a place in the grass he could have walked to with his eyes shut. "Where you couldn't see me. I listened to the whole thing. And I nearly came down. I nearly sat on the grass next to this rock and said something. I stood up there with my weight on one foot for about ten minutes, deciding." He was quiet. "And then Gabe shouted for me about the tents. And I went."

"Ice," said Thea.

"*There'll be time*, I thought. *Once we're out.*" He laughed. It came out completely wrong. "That was the first year. There were ninety floors after that. There was a dinner on forty-three that neither of us called anything. There was your hand on my arm on seventy." He looked down at it, on his arm now. "Right there. That exact place. You took it away before I could think about it. And I never said anything. And neither did you. For ten years. Because there'd be time."

Thea didn't take her hand away.

"There wasn't," said Ice.

***

He told her the end.

He didn't look at her for that part. He couldn't. He looked at the sky.

The fire on the ninety-ninth floor. All of them who were left, round it. Twelve. Doing the maths, the way they'd done it a hundred times, and it always coming out the same. Six in front. Six behind. Close the doors. Gabe and Xav and Gigi and Azac and Cursiffe and Ina at the gate, holding everything the tower had left to throw at them, so the other six could get through. Gabe's voice, through twenty metres of black iron, shouting at nothing in particular, the way he shouted at a forge when the steel wouldn't take, the last thing on the other side of the doors.

And the six of them in the room. Him and Deston and Yan and Hana and Drew.

And her.

"Deston went first," he said. "Nearest the door. With his hands out. Holding us together. Like always. I felt his gold go out of me about ten seconds after he went." He was quiet. "Yan, against the wall. Suzu on his chest. She went a heartbeat after him. Hana charmed it. For four seconds. Nobody had ever charmed it. Four seconds was how long it took me to cross the room." He breathed. "Drew made it stand. Once. For half a second. With his bare hands. He'd stopped needing a blade somewhere around the ninetieth floor."

"*Make it stand*," said Thea. Almost silently.

"Yeah," said Ice. "I saw his window tonight. I nearly lost it."

He stopped.

The aurora moved. The tarn moved under it. The reeds. Somewhere a long way off, round the far side of the water, thirty-four people were still singing the hold-folk song with all the verses, loudly and badly, so nobody would hear.

"You were behind me," he said. "Where a mage belongs. Most of the fight. And then in the last ten seconds you weren't."

Thea's hand on his arm had gone very still.

"You saw it coming before I did," he said. "You always did. You always saw everything before I did. You stepped round me. And you put a ward up. A wall. Of light. Rewritten. With text going across it so fast I couldn't read it." He swallowed. "Tonight. On the pass. That ward. I'd seen it once. Once, in ten years. In the last ten seconds of everything."

He made himself look at her.

She was looking at him. In the green light. With her eyes very wide and very steady and wet.

"It went through the ward," said Ice. "And through you. Instead of me. And that's the only reason I was close enough to finish it."

Thea didn't say anything.

"You said something," he said. "As you went down. Three words. Maybe four." His voice had gone almost to nothing. "And I didn't hear it. I was too busy. I was killing it. It was too loud and I was too far away and I was *busy*, and I didn't hear it." He looked at her. "I killed it. And everybody was dead. And I sat on the floor, and I closed your eyes. And I said *sorry, I didn't catch that*. To nobody. Because there was nobody left."

***

She was crying.

Not loudly. Not the way he'd ever seen her cry. She was sitting beside him on the rock with her hand on his arm and her feet in the cold water and the tears running down her face, steadily, in the light of the aurora, and she wasn't making any sound at all. She was just looking at him.

"And then a window," he said. Quieter. He had to finish. If he stopped now he'd never start again. "With two buttons. *Go home. Go back.* Alea said it was older than the thing on the throne. She'd had a soul for two years by then. I'd given her one on eighty-eight."

He stopped.

Because Alea had moved.

He felt her decide to. On his shoulder. He'd felt her do it before, twice, for Thea: once on floor 6, for one second, in a bowl of gold glass, and once on floor 7, on purpose. And again on the barracks step, sitting on the lantern. Small, every time. The size of his hand. A little pale thing you could mistake for a reflection.

She came down off his shoulder now. Onto the rock. Onto the flat grey stone between them, beside the lantern. And she wasn't small.

She'd been making herself small for two hundred and eighty-five days. He'd never asked her to. She'd just done it, from the first minute in the white room, so that nobody would look twice. So that nobody would ask what she was. She'd folded herself down to the size of a hand and sat on his shoulder where nobody could see her and said *your foot's tapping*, for nine floors.

She let it go.

She sat on the rock between them the way she'd sat on the black stone of the hundredth floor, beside his leg, saying *your health's at four percent*. The size of a cat. Made of light. Pale teal at the edges and almost white at the centre, with one long tail curled round her feet, and two pinpricks of brighter white for eyes. When she moved, she left a faint shimmer on the air, like heat over a road. The aurora went over her and through her and she glowed under it, softly, like a lamp somebody had turned up.

Thea stopped breathing.

She looked at the fox. The fox looked at her. Neither of them moved.

"Oh," said Thea. Very quietly. "You're *bigger*."

"This is what she looks like," said Ice. His voice had gone. "She's been making herself small. Since the white room. So nobody would ask."

Alea didn't say anything. She sat between them on the rock with her tail round her feet and looked at Thea, steadily, with her white eyes. The way she'd looked at things for ten years. The way she'd learned, a long time ago, which things not to look at, and had looked at them anyway.

"She was there," said Ice. "All of it. Every floor. She was a ball of light for eight years that said *welcome, player* and *quest updated*, the same as yours. And then on eighty-eight I gave her a soul, and she was *her*." He swallowed. "And at the end, on the floor of that room, with all of you, she was the one who said *go back*. She said *I think souls go where they're carried. If you go, I'm coming. I'm not asking.*" He looked at the fox. "She's the only thing that came back with me. Everything else stayed up there. My levels. My class. My shield, in two pieces. All of it. Except her."

Thea looked at Alea for a long time.

Then, very slowly, the way you'd hold your hand out to a cat you didn't know, she took her hand off his arm, and held it out. Palm up. On the stone. Between them.

Alea looked at the hand.

And then she got up, and walked across the rock, two steps, three, leaving a shimmer on the air behind her. And put her forehead down into Thea's open palm.

Thea made a sound. Ice felt it go through her. Through her thread, close as a rope.

"She's warm," said Thea. "She's *warm*. Like holding your hand over a lamp."

"She's always been warm," said Ice.

"*Tell her*," said Alea, very quietly, in his ear, without lifting her head out of Thea's hand, "*I was there. Tell her I saw her step round you. Tell her I saw the ward.*" A pause. "*Tell her I've been waiting two hundred and eighty-five days to say hello properly.*"

He told her.

Thea was crying again. She didn't seem to notice. She sat there on the rock with her feet in the cold water and a fox made of light with its forehead in her palm, and looked at it, and said, in a voice that barely worked:

"Hello, Alea. Properly."

Alea lifted her head. She looked at Thea for one more long moment. Then she turned round, and walked back across the rock, and lay down on the stone beside the lantern, curled, with her tail over her nose and her eyes open, between the two of them. Not small. Not hiding. Just there.

Thea put her hand back on his arm.

"Go on," she said. "I'm here. She's here." She swallowed. "Go on."

"And home was a room in Malacca, and the same night, the same minute, and a group chat with thirty-five people in it who'd never send another message." He breathed. "If I went home, you didn't."

"So you went back," said Thea.

"I went back."

"To the white room."

"A minute before anybody woke up," said Ice. "I stood in the middle of the ring and looked at every face. All of you. Asleep. In your circles. The way you'd been on the first morning, ten years before. Not knowing anything." He looked at her. "Yours longest."

She made a sound. Small. In her throat.

"And then I wrote on Deston's card," he said. "And I ran."

***

He told her why. All of it. The tower. How it changed things. Floor 5, where the Squall had learned to come at whoever stood still, because the first time he'd beaten it standing still. The Wanting, built out of what he'd confessed on four. The white Quiet in his trial, built out of the same. *It changes what I do the old way.* Every floor. The thing behind the windows, watching him do it.

"And then it started watching all of you," he said. "On seven. *Irregular*, on every face. And I thought: if I say it, out loud. To anybody. If it hears me say it—" He stopped. "I didn't know what it would do. I still don't. But it builds things out of what I say. It always has. And I didn't want it building anything out of *this*. Out of you."

"So you said *not yet*," said Thea.

"So I said *not yet*. And *trust*. And *let it look*." He looked up at the sky. "And tonight Alea can't open a single window. There's nothing here. Nothing watching. For the first time since four." He was quiet. "She thinks it's one night."

"It's enough," said Thea.

He looked at her.

"And tonight on the pass," he said, "I read under my own window. I've never been able to do that. That's yours. I could read it tonight. *One record does not match.* And under it." He swallowed. "*Record found: one of two.*"

Thea went white.

"It knows," said Ice. "It's got two of me. The one from the white room. And the other one. From ten years. From the hundredth floor." He looked at his hands. "It's known since four, I think. Since I told the dark everything. And tonight it asked me where the other one came from."

"And you said *them*."

"I said *them*." He looked round. Across the tarn. At the fire on the far shore, small and orange in the dark, and the shapes round it, and the song coming over the water. "Because that's where. That's the only answer I've got. I came from them." He turned back to her. "And I came back for them. All of them."

He stopped.

He'd got to the end. He'd told her the whole shape of it. The first time, and the end, and the window, and the white room, and the running. He'd told her more in an hour on a rock than he'd told anybody in two lives, more than he'd told the dark in the House of Ash, because the dark hadn't had a face, and she did.

There was one thing left.

He'd been going to say it first. He'd thought, all day, that he'd start with it. And he'd found he couldn't. He'd had to tell her everything else, all of it, the whole ten years, so that when he said it she'd know exactly what it weighed.

"Thea," he said.

"I know," she said.

He looked at her.

"I came back for all of them," said Ice. "I did. That's true." He swallowed. "But I looked at your face longest. In the white room. Both times." He made himself keep looking at her. "I loved you. The first time. For years. And I never said, because there'd be time. And then there wasn't." His voice went, and came back. "And I came back, and you were there, alive, in your circle, and you didn't know me. Not like that. Not any more. You were younger than I remembered and you'd meant to call more. And I loved you anyway. Every floor. From a floor away. I loved you on two and on four and on six with my hand out and on seven on a bridge and on nine in the dark with the Grow over me and you walking straight at me with the lantern lying down." He was crying now. He let it. "I love you. Now. This time. I'm sorry it took two lives to say it."

***

She didn't say anything for a long time.

She sat on the rock beside him with her feet in the cold water and her hand on his arm, and the aurora going over them both, green and violet and slow, and looked at him. And he looked at her. And the tarn under them held the sky, and the two of them sitting on a rock in it, very small.

Then she took her hand off his arm.

He felt it go. He'd felt it go once before, on the seventieth floor, a long time ago, before he could think about it.

She put it on his face instead.

On his cheek. Her palm. Warm, in the cold. The way you'd put your hand on somebody's face to make them look at you, when they were already looking.

"You said three words," said Thea. "Maybe four."

He couldn't speak.

"You don't know what I said," said Thea. "The first time. You didn't hear it."

"No."

"I don't know either," she said. "I don't remember. I wasn't there. Not this me." She was crying, and smiling, at the same time, the way he'd only ever seen people do at the very end of something or the very start. "But I know what I'd say."

He looked at her.

"If I was stepping round you," said Thea. "In the last ten seconds. If I'd seen it coming, and I knew. If it was the last thing I was ever going to get to say to you, after ten years of not saying it." Her thumb moved on his cheek. "I know *exactly* what I'd say, Ice."

And she said it.

"I love you," said Thea.

And then, after a moment, because she was Thea, and could be mean when she needed to be:

"*Idiot*."

***

He laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him on the rock in the dark, under the aurora, with her hand on his face and the tears running into her fingers, the way it had come out of him on the jetty on floor 8 at Calvin's arms. Cracked and stupid and enormous. He laughed until he couldn't breathe.

"That's four," he said, when he could.

"*Maybe four*, you said."

"I did say maybe four."

"So it was four," said Thea. "That's what I said. The first time. I'm telling you. That's what it was." She leaned her forehead against his. "You just didn't hear the last one."

He shut his eyes.

He'd been trying to work out what it was for ten years and two hundred and eighty-five days. Every night. On every floor. In the white room, with her asleep in her circle. On floor 6, with his hand out. On the bridge on seven, when she'd said *what was the first time* and he'd said *not yet*. He'd tried every word in the world. He'd never once tried *idiot*.

It was so obviously right that he started to laugh again, and she started too, and they sat there on the rock with their foreheads together, laughing, crying, in the green light, with the cold water round her feet and the lantern on the stone between them.

And then she kissed him.

Or he kissed her. He was never sure, afterwards, and neither was she, and they argued about it, later, on a great many floors, and never settled it. On the rock. At the edge of the tarn. Under the aurora, at the top of the tenth floor, on the one night in two lives when nothing in the tower was watching.

It wasn't like he'd imagined. He'd imagined it a lot, for ten years, from a long way off. It was better. It was warm, and wet, and she was laughing into it, and her hand was still on his face, and his was in her hair, in the loose ponytail, in the pink and blue streaks. It went on for a long time.

When it stopped, she didn't move away. She stayed where she was. Forehead against his. Breathing.

"Ten years," she said.

"And two hundred and eighty-five days."

"You *idiot*," said Thea, with enormous tenderness. "You *absolute*—"

"I know."

"You could have *said*. On Earth. On the calls. You could have said *anything*—"

"I know."

"And you stood up *there*." She pointed up the slope, without looking. "With your weight on one foot. For ten minutes. While I sang."

"Gabe shouted about the tents."

"I'm going to kill Gabe."

"He doesn't know," said Ice. "He wasn't there. Not this him."

"I'm going to kill him anyway," said Thea. And then she laughed again, wet, into his collar, and put her arms round him on the rock, properly, all the way round, the way nobody had held him since a bakery on floor 7 with flour in the air. And held on.

***

They stayed on the rock a long time.

The aurora went on. It got brighter, for a while, around what he thought was midnight. Great slow curtains of green and violet and a cold pale blue, folding over the whole sky above the peaks, so bright that the sea of cloud below the valley's lip glowed with it. And then it got fainter, slowly, the way a fire gets lower. And they sat in it, close, with her head on his shoulder and his arm round her, and talked.

Not about the end. About small things. She asked questions. Little ones. *Was I happy, the first time?* And he told her: sometimes. On forty-three she'd laughed so hard at something Calvin said that she'd fallen off a bench. On fifty-something she'd taught Waddles to bow. She'd read the tower the way she read it now, words under words, and found things nobody else ever found, and been unbearable about it for days. *Did I still sing?* Not often. Here, once, on this rock. A few other times. Never when she knew anyone could hear.

"I'll sing for you," said Thea. "Now. Whenever you want."

"You sang on the step."

"I'll sing on every step," said Thea. "On every floor. Every night, if you like." She was quiet. "I meant to call more."

"So did I."

"We've got time now," said Thea. "Haven't we. This time."

He didn't answer straight away. He thought about *record found: one of two*. About a thing with no face standing in the snow on the other side of a gate, asking him where he came from. About ninety floors.

"I don't know," he said. Honestly. "I don't know how much. Nobody does."

"No," said Thea. "But we're not waiting for it any more."

"No," said Ice. "We're not."

"Then don't waste it," said Thea. She didn't lift her head off his shoulder. "Don't come back under half."

He was quiet.

"Xola said it on nine," said Thea. "At the top of the rope. I heard her. And you said *I won't*, and she said you don't get to say *won't*."

"I don't do it on purpose," he said, after a while. "The head. Not healing. Not looking." He stopped. He'd never said this to anybody. Not in either life. "Ten years at the front of a line. If the front flinches, everybody flinches. If the healer's looking at me, she's not looking at the one beside her. So I stopped. One fight at a time. I don't know how to start again."

"Then don't start," said Thea. "Just heal sooner. That's all. A bit. So I don't have to stand on a root a mile away and learn your head."

"You've learned it."

"I'm still learning it. I'd rather not have to." Her hand found his on the cold stone. "Try."

He thought about the root. About Xola's water, thrown, not poured. *Under half.* About Thea at the top of the rope, the angriest he had ever heard her, in either life.

"I'll try," he said.

"Good," said Thea. "That's the first thing you've ever said to me about it that wasn't *fine*."

On the stone beside the lantern, Alea stirred.

"Can she hear me?" said Thea. She'd felt him turn his head. "Alea?"

"She can hear everything," said Ice.

Thea lifted her head off his shoulder and looked at her. At the fox curled on the stone beside the lantern, the size of a cat, pale teal and white, watching them both with her pinprick eyes. She hadn't made herself small again. She hadn't gone anywhere. She'd been lying there since the window, between them, glowing softly in the aurora like a lamp turned low.

"Thank you," said Thea. "For staying with him. Both times."

Alea didn't say anything for a long moment.

"Tell her she's welcome," said Alea, very quietly, in his ear. "Tell her I liked her the first time too." A pause. "Tell her she *was* unbearable on fifty-something, for days, and she was right."

Ice told her.

Thea laughed. And then cried again, a little. And then put her head back on his shoulder.

***

Somewhere very late, the singing at the fire stopped.

He heard it go. Thirty-four voices on the far side of the tarn, getting fewer, getting quieter. Somebody laughing. Then just one voice, the last verse. Newish, he thought, on her own, getting it right for once, slowly. And then nothing.

And then, across the water, in the dark, very small, a light.

Not the fire. A different one. White. Rising. A mote. John's. Going up from the shore into the dark over the tarn, slowly, and stopping. Hanging there in the air above the far side of the water, like a lamp in a window. Steady. Not coming any closer. Just there.

*Off to one side*, he thought.

And beside it, a second light. Gold. Thin. A thread. Deston's, he thought, going up out of the dark by the fire and across the water, not to him, just out, the way you'd hold a hand out palm up on a jetty. And then another light, orange, low, at the water's edge. A fire. A small one. Somebody had built a second fire, right at the edge of the tarn, on the near shore, where it would be the first thing anybody saw if they came back round the water in the dark.

Gabe.

"They're making us a light," said Thea. Very quietly. Watching it.

"Yeah," said Ice.

"So we can find our way back."

"Yeah," said Ice. And couldn't say anything else.

They sat on the rock and looked at it for a long time. Three small lights on the far side of a tarn on the top of the world, under the last of the aurora, in the dark of the moon. Waiting. Not coming closer. Not asking anything.

"In a bit," said Thea.

"In a bit," said Ice.

She took her feet out of the water. Finally. They were white with cold and she didn't seem to care. She pulled her knees up and leaned into him and put her cold feet against his leg, on purpose, and laughed when he flinched.

And he put his arm round her, and his cheek on the top of her head, and they stayed where they were, a little longer, on the rock, by the water, where he'd stood on the slope once with his weight on one foot and decided there'd be time.

There was.
