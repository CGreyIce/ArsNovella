Rui Xiang couldn't sleep on floor 4. There was nothing to listen to.

He lay on his back on the stone floor of the big empty hall on the east side of the first square, with his head on his pack and a knife turning slowly over his knuckles. He listened to thirty-four people trying to sleep. Nobody was snoring. Nobody was talking. On floor 3 there'd been frogs all night, and the reeds, and water slapping under the walkways, and the eel-woman banging pots in the smoking hall. Here there was just breathing, and the occasional rustle of somebody turning over. And under it, behind it, around it, the silence of the Red City. It sat on the roof of the hall like a weight.

Drew had put them all in one room. "Everyone. One hall. Two on watch at the door, change every two hours. Nobody goes out alone." He'd said it in the voice he used when he'd already thought of everything and was daring you to find what he'd missed. Nobody had argued. Nobody had wanted to be anywhere else.

The light didn't change. That was the other thing. It was the middle of the night, by the rankings, and the light coming in through the empty windows was the same dull red it had been at noon. The same red it would be at dawn. Bee had asked, quietly, around what should have been dusk, whether it was ever going to get dark. Nobody had answered her.

Rui Xiang turned the knife over his knuckles and looked at the ceiling and thought about the thing by the fountain.

***

He hadn't looked at it for long. He'd looked at it long enough.

Calvin had stopped talking. That was what Rui Xiang remembered most. They'd been coming down the long stair in a line behind him, listening to him the way Slipstream wanted them to. *Look at my back, nobody run, there's a fountain.* And then he'd stopped. Right in the middle of a word. And all thirty-five of them had come down off the stair into the square and stopped too, in a ring, and looked at what Calvin was looking at.

Long. Grey. Folded down on itself by the dry fountain like a dead spider, all knees and elbows. A head with no face. A hole in its chest.

"It didn't go to light," John had said, very quietly.

That was when Rui Xiang had understood what was wrong with it. On floor 1 the sheep had gone to light. On floor 2 the wolves had gone to light. On floor 3 the eels and the wisps and the Lanternjaw had gone to light, every one of them, a soft little burst of brightness and then nothing, and you got used to it. You stopped thinking about it. You hit something until it went to light, and then it was gone, and that was killing.

This one hadn't gone anywhere. It was lying there. It had been lying there a long time. The dry wind had got into it and made it stiff and brown. When the wind gusted, a flap of grey skin on its arm moved, very slightly, and Bee had made a small noise and turned round and put her face in Hako's shoulder.

"The dead stay," Kei had said. He'd crouched down beside the hole in its chest, close, the way a doctor looks. "On this floor. Look. They don't go anywhere." He'd looked at the hole for a long time. "Somebody stabbed it. Once. From the front. Right in the heart."

Nobody had said who. Nobody had needed to.

And Rui Xiang had looked up.

He couldn't have said why. His eyes just went there, the way they did when he walked into a room in a game and his hands already knew where the enemy would be. Up. To the roofs. To the four storeys of empty windows and the parapets along the top, red against the red sky. Whatever that thing on the stones was, it had been tall, and thin, and made for climbing. It hadn't lived down here in the square. It had lived *up there*.

And somebody had gone up there after it. Alone.

***

He gave up on sleeping around two in the morning. He got up, quietly, and stepped over Malcolm, who was lying flat on his back with his arms folded and his eyes open, staring at the ceiling.

"No," said Malcolm, without moving.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're going out."

"I'm going *up*." Rui Xiang crouched down beside him. "On the roofs. Just round the square. I want to see where it lived."

"That's how people die in films."

"It's dead."

"*That* one's dead." Malcolm turned his head and looked at him with his face of quiet doom. "How do you know it's the only one?"

Rui Xiang thought about it. "I don't," he said. "That's why I'm going to look."

Malcolm stared at him for a long moment. Then he sighed, unfolded one arm, and touched two fingers to the back of Rui Xiang's hand. Something warm went into it, then something warm again on top of the first. Two blessings. Never one.

"In case the first one fails," said Malcolm, and folded his arm back up.

Xav was on watch at the door, sitting cross-legged against the frame with his sword across his knees, beside Hyvred. He didn't look surprised to see Rui Xiang. Xav never looked surprised.

"Roofs," said Xav. It wasn't a question.

"Roofs."

Xav looked at him for a second. The calm, even look that always made you feel he'd already played out the next three moves and just wanted to see if you'd find them.

"Take one of John's lights," he said. "Stay where I can see the light. If it goes out, I'm coming up."

"It won't go out."

"Then go once," said Xav. "Look. Come back."

***

The walls of the Red City had been made for climbing. Rui Xiang had seen that the moment they came down the stair. The red stone was soft, carved, worn by centuries of wind into ledges and hollows and grooves, and every house had its windows set in deep frames you could get a hand into. He went up the side of the hall in about thirty seconds. Hand, hand, foot, window frame, ledge, parapet, roof. John's little light came bobbing up after him like a firefly on a string and hung over his shoulder.

He crouched on the flat roof of the hall and looked out over the first square.

It was smaller, from up here. Twenty metres. A long way to run across, on the ground. Nothing at all for something tall and thin dropping off a roof. He could see the dry fountain, and the grey shape lying beside it, and the long stair going up to the high terrace, and the door at the top. He could see the hall door below him with Xav and Hyvred sitting in it, Xav's face turned up toward the light.

He waved once. Xav lifted a hand.

Then he started reading.

He'd always been able to do it. Not the way Xav read a room, the whole of it at once, where everybody would move before they moved. Rui Xiang read *movement*. Where something had been. How fast. What it had touched. In every game they'd ever played, when the round was over and everybody else was arguing about what had happened, Rui Xiang had already known. He'd watched it happen. He'd seen the line that someone ran, and the corner they'd cut, and the half-second they'd stopped to look.

The dust told him first.

It lay on everything up here. A thin, even, red skin of it, on the roofs and the parapets and the ledges, the same colour as the stone. Wherever anything had touched it, it remembered. A bird's three-toed feet. The drag of a wing. And on the south roof, on the far side of the square, a pattern he didn't understand at first: long smooth sweeps along the top of the parapet, as if something had laid its whole length down on it and slid. Again and again. A track, worn into the dust of the parapets, going all the way round the square on the roofs. The thing's road. The way it had gone round and round, up here, for a very long time, looking down.

He followed it, on the roofs, with the light bobbing over his shoulder. Round the south side. Round the west. He stepped over the sweeps without touching them. He didn't know why. It just felt like the kind of thing you didn't walk on.

On the west roof, he found where it had been hurt.

The dust there was wrong. Scattered. Kicked up in a spray across the roof, the way it goes when something lands hard. On the parapet there was a dark stain gone brown with weeks of dry wind. And a little way along, caught on the rough stone at the edge of the parapet, a few threads. Rui Xiang bent down and picked them off the stone and held them up to John's light.

Cloth. Plain grey-brown cloth, the coarse kind they'd been buying in every market since floor 1. A few threads, frayed, as if they'd been dragged over the edge of the stone.

He turned them over in his fingers. Then he looked down at his own boots.

Then he looked at the dust on the parapet again, properly, crouching with his nose almost on it. And in the middle of the spray, among the long sweeps of the thing's road, there were footprints. Not boots. Something flatter, softer, with no heel and no tread. As if somebody had wound strips of cloth round and round their boots, so tight and so thick that the sole wouldn't make a sound on the stone.

"*Oh*," said Rui Xiang softly, to nobody.

He wrapped his boots.

***

He sat down on the west roof with his back against the parapet and the threads in his hand, and thought about it.

A thing that hunted from the roofs. A thing that hadn't made a single sound in the time it took them to come down the stair. He'd been listening, and Rui Xiang had very good ears. Nobody on the stair had heard it, because it had been dead for weeks, but they wouldn't have heard it alive either. He knew that. He could feel it. This was a floor where things didn't make sounds. And a thing that made no sound would be very, very good at hearing them.

It hunted by sound. It had to. Look at the floor. Look at the road it had worn round the square on the roofs, round and round, waiting for anything down there to make a noise.

So you couldn't make one.

Rui Xiang looked down at the square, at the dry fountain and the grey shape beside it, and saw something he hadn't seen from the ground. Lying on the stones a little way from the body, where somebody had dropped it, there was a small dull shape. Round. Metal. He knew what it was before John's light reached it. He'd seen Kei pick it up that afternoon and turn it over and put it down again very carefully, as if it might go off.

A bell.

A small bronze bell. In the quietest place Rui Xiang had ever been.

*Somebody rang it.*

He saw it. The way he'd always seen things, after the round, when everyone else was arguing. He saw a man come down into the square, alone, with his boots wrapped in cloth and a bell in his hand. He saw him ring it once. One clear note going up off the stones into all that silence, round the four storeys of empty windows, up to the roofs. And he saw the thing on the roofs lift its faceless head.

And then the man had gone silent. Completely silent. Standing in the middle of the square, holding his breath, waiting for something he couldn't hear and couldn't see to come down off a roof at him.

"How did you know where it was?" said Rui Xiang, out loud, very quietly. "You can't hear it. You can't—"

He stopped.

The dust.

He looked at the parapets. All round the square, every parapet had a skin of red dust on it, thin and even. The thing's road ran along the top of them. When it moved, the dust moved. A little puff of it, lifting off the edge of the roof and drifting down. Not a sound. Just dust.

*He watched the dust.*

He stood in the middle of the square, silent, with the bell ringing out round the walls. He watched the tops of the parapets. When the dust moved on one of them, he knew which way it was coming. And then it came down off the roof, four storeys, fast as anything Rui Xiang had ever seen. And the man was waiting for it with a sword.

*Once. From the front. Right in the heart.*

Rui Xiang sat on the west roof with his knife still in his hand and felt the hair stand up on his arms.

He was the assassin. That was what he was. That was the thing he'd picked on the first day in the class hall without even having to think about it, because his hands had wanted it. Daggers, and shadows, and getting there first, and one hit. He was good at it. He was the best in the group at it, everybody said so, and he was level twenty-two, the highest of all thirty-five of them, and smug about it. Calvin had asked him how in World four times.

He sat on the roof and thought about standing in the middle of that square with a bell in his hand, waiting for that thing to come down off the roof. He knew, very clearly, the way you know things in your hands before your head, that he couldn't have done it. Not at level twenty-two. Not at twenty-five. Not with all thirty-five of them standing behind him.

Whoever had done this had gone in silent. Alone. Against something that heard everything. And it had been, Rui Xiang thought, looking at the hole in the grey shape's chest from four storeys up, a perfect kill. An assassin's kill. The kind he'd been trying to make for sixty-six days, and never quite had.

And it had been made by a tank.

***

He went round the rest of the square on the roofs, slower now, and found where the man had waited.

It wasn't over the square. It was higher. On the terrace above the north side, a long way up, behind a low broken wall where a house had fallen in. From there you could see the whole square, every roof, every parapet. And behind the wall, in the dust, there was a hollow.

Not a footprint. A hollow. Pressed into the dust in the shape of somebody sitting, knees up, back against the broken stone. The dust round it was disturbed in a ring, the way dust gets when somebody shifts and settles and shifts again, for hours. More than hours. The hollow was deep. There were more threads caught on the wall behind it, and a little pile of something that had been bread, once, gone hard as stone.

Rui Xiang crouched by it for a long time.

He'd sat here. For days. Behind a wall, watching a square, waiting. Watching the dust. Learning it. Learning exactly how it moved, and how fast, and where it came down. Before he went in.

*He waited a week.*

In every game they'd ever played, in every round, for as long as Rui Xiang had known him, Declan had gone in first. Every door, every corner, every fight. Shield up, straight in, *go go go*, while the rest of them were still getting ready. It had driven Xav mad for a year. *Wait*, Xav would say. *Wait. Then move once.* And Declan would laugh and go in anyway, and usually it worked, and sometimes it didn't. And then, slowly, over a year or so, Rui Xiang had noticed him start to do it. Stop at a door. Wait half a second. Then go.

Half a second. That was what Declan had learned from Xav. Half a second at a door.

Not a week behind a wall.

Rui Xiang put out a hand and touched the edge of the hollow in the dust, very lightly, without disturbing it.

*Who taught you to wait like that?*

***

He was still crouching there when someone came up the wall behind him.

He had a knife up and turned before he'd thought about it. Then he saw the golden horns and the long red-streaked hair and the bow on her back. He let out a breath.

"You walk *loud*," he said.

"I'm not the assassin," said Hyvred. She hauled herself over the broken wall, breathing hard. Her hands were scraped. She'd climbed. "Xav said you'd found something. You stopped moving. He said if you stopped moving that long, either you'd found something or you were dead, and he'd rather I checked than him."

"I'm not dead."

"I can see that." She stopped. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking past him, at the hollow in the dust behind the wall.

Her face did something Rui Xiang had seen it do once before. In the ascension room on floor 1, when she'd looked at a bench.

"Oh," she said softly.

"You can see it."

"I can see *him*." She came and knelt down beside the hollow, the way she'd knelt by the door on the terrace that afternoon. She didn't touch it. Her eyes were moving, the way they did when she was using Afterglow, reading something on the air that none of the rest of them could see. "It's all over here. He sat here a long time. Days. It's soaked into the wall like—like a lamp left on in one room. You can still see the shape of him." She swallowed. "He was frightened, here. You can tell. The light goes small when people are frightened. It goes in on itself."

Rui Xiang looked at the hollow.

He'd never thought of Declan as frightened. Not of anything. Not once.

"There's light all over these roofs," said Hyvred. She turned round, still kneeling, and looked out over the square, and her eyes went along the rooftops the way his had. "Old, most of it. Weeks. It's fading, like the trail on floor 2. There, where the thing went down. And on the west roof, a lot of it, and in the square by the fountain—" She stopped. "He sat by the fountain after. For a long time. It's all in one place, there, on the edge of it. Like he sat down and couldn't get up."

Rui Xiang thought about the thing coming down off the roof. About the hole in its chest. About sitting on the edge of a dry fountain afterwards, next to it, for a long time.

"And there's this," said Hyvred.

She'd stopped looking at the square. She was looking north. Up, along the high terraces on the north side of the canyon, the ones above the first square, going down-canyon toward the bottom of the city. Her face had gone very still.

"What?"

"It's new." Her voice had gone strange. "Rui Xiang. It's *new*. It's bright. It's from—" She stood up. She put her hand on the broken wall to steady herself. "It's from today."

He stood up too, and looked where she was looking. He couldn't see anything. Roofs, red, going down in steps toward the dry river, empty. But he knew how to look at a line, and he looked at the line she was following with her eyes. Along the top of the north terraces, roof to roof to roof, keeping high, above the long stair. Above the square. And then on, down-canyon, along the roofs, away.

It was exactly the line he'd have taken. If he'd wanted to follow thirty-five people down a stair without them seeing him. Above them. On the roofs. Where he could watch every roof edge round the square, at the same time as he watched the people.

"He was here," said Hyvred. "Today. While we came down the stair. He was up *there*, on the north roofs, going along above us, the whole way down." Her voice cracked. "He walked us down, Rui Xiang. He walked us down to the square. On the roofs. And then he kept going."

Rui Xiang stood behind the broken wall in the silence of the Red City and looked at the empty roofs.

He thought about Calvin at the bottom of the stair, stopping in the middle of a word. About all thirty-five of them standing in a ring round the fountain, looking down. About himself looking up. Looking up at the roofs, the way his eyes always did, for whatever lived up there.

He'd looked straight at that line. He was sure of it. He'd looked up and his eyes had gone along those roofs and there'd been nothing there. And all the time, somewhere along it, somebody had been crouching behind a parapet with his boots wrapped in cloth. Not making a sound. Watching the roofs over thirty-five people's heads, so that nothing could come down off them.

He'd been *looking* for him. And he hadn't seen a thing.

"The door," he said. His throat had gone tight. "On the terrace. This afternoon. You knelt down."

"There's a place by the door." Hyvred's eyes had gone wet. "By the wall. Where he sat. Every evening, I think, for weeks. The light's gone right into the stone. Like a stain. You could put your hand in the shape of it." She wiped her face with the back of her scraped hand. "I told Gabe. And Thea came. I didn't—I didn't know what else to do. I couldn't *not*. Not after floor 2. Not after Deston and the pocket." She looked at him. "And then this afternoon I saw it was fresh. The trail going away from the door. Down the far stair. And I thought, he can't have been here *today*. He can't have. He wouldn't have been that close and not—"

She stopped.

"He was that close," said Rui Xiang.

"He was that close."

They stood behind the wall together and looked at the line along the north roofs, going away down the canyon in the red.

"Where does it go?" said Rui Xiang.

Hyvred's eyes followed it. Down. Roof by roof, terrace by terrace, along the north side of the canyon, toward the bottom of the Red City. Toward the dry river, and the big black building cut into the canyon floor at the bottom, with a chimney going up through four hundred metres of rock.

"Down," she said. "All the way down."

***

They climbed down off the north terrace and went back across the square, the long way round, not touching the grey shape by the fountain. Neither of them said anything. Rui Xiang went in front, without thinking about it, the way he always did, eyes on the roofs.

Xav was standing in the hall door. He'd seen the light coming back, and seen them both, and stood up. He looked at Rui Xiang's face, then Hyvred's. He didn't ask anything. He turned round and went back into the hall without a word, and they followed him in.

He went straight to Drew. Drew was asleep on his back with his buckler for a pillow, and Xav put a hand on his shoulder, and Drew was awake at once, the way generals have to be.

"Roofs," said Xav quietly. "Rui Xiang and Hyvred found something. I think we need to hear it now. Not in the morning."

Drew looked at Hyvred's face for a moment. Then he sat up.

"*No more pocket*," he said. It wasn't a question. It was the thing they'd all agreed, on floor 3, when Deston read the note out loud in the smoking hall. Rui Xiang had been there. They all had.

"No more pocket," said Hyvred.

"Then wake Deston. And Thea." Drew was already on his feet, pulling his knuckles on. "And—"

He stopped. They all did.

Gabe wasn't asleep.

Rui Xiang didn't know why he'd thought he would be. Gabe hadn't slept properly since the white room. He was sitting against the far wall of the hall in the wolf coat, in the red light, with his knees up and his arms round them, and the big sacking-wrapped shape propped against the wall beside him. He'd been watching the door. He'd watched them all come in. And now he was looking at Hyvred, and at Rui Xiang, and at their faces. He didn't move.

"Where?" said Gabe.

His voice was very quiet. On floor 4, it carried to every corner of the hall. Rui Xiang heard people waking up. Somebody sitting up in the dark. Thea, by the window, with the little lantern already lit in her lap, as if she'd been awake too.

Hyvred looked at Gabe for a long moment. Then she lifted her arm and pointed. Not at the door. Not at the stair, or the terrace, or the roofs.

Down.
