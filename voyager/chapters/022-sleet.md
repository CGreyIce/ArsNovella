Things had been following Aleaka since the first morning, and she'd stopped pretending it was a coincidence.

It had started with the penguin. Not her penguin; she didn't have one. In the white room, while everybody else was waking up and panicking, a small, round, yellow penguin had waddled away from the girl with the heart-shaped tuft and come straight across the floor to Aleaka, and stood on her foot. Just stood there. Looking up at her. Its owner had spent ten minutes trying to get it back. Every time she picked it up and carried it away, it put its head down and waddled straight back to Aleaka's foot.

"I'm so sorry," the girl had said, very calmly, for the fourth time. "He doesn't usually do this."

"It's quite all right," Aleaka had said, and meant it.

Then in Hearthstead it had been the hens. Mother Crane's hens, in the yard behind the Copper Spoon, who'd followed her down the lane in a line on the second morning, clucking, until Mother Crane came out with a broom. Then a big grey dog from the inn, who'd slept outside her door three nights running. And on the green slopes of floor 1, the lambs. Not Woolbacks, but the small ones, the young ones, which didn't fight. They'd trotted after her across the fields like ducklings and stood round her in a ring while she shot their parents.

That one had been very hard. She'd cried about it, privately, behind a wall.

"You've got a thing," Jared had said, on the fourth day, watching three lambs follow her up the high street. "Like a skill. Or a curse."

"It's not a curse."

"Leaka. There's a goat behind you."

She'd turned round. There was a goat behind her. It looked very offended, as if somebody had once punched it in the face and it hadn't forgotten. But it was following her anyway, at a careful distance, like a sulking cat.

"It's not a curse," she'd said again, with slightly less certainty.

***

The pup was on the tenth day, on floor 2, in the snow.

They'd been hunting Rimehounds in the high woods above Larchwick all morning, the five of them. They'd learned the wolves by now: how they circled, how they came from where you weren't looking. Hana could do something with her voice now that made one of them turn on another. Jared held the front like a wall. Milli was so fast and precise with his knives that he'd started to look bored. Newish shouted counts. Aleaka had her bow.

She was a decent shot. Not like Wendy, who'd come down to the trapper's hall last night with a bag of ugly black arrows and shown them all a knot in a log she'd hit three times at sixty metres. But decent. She'd killed four wolves that morning. Each time, afterwards, she'd stood over it in the snow for a moment and said *sorry* very quietly, in her head, so nobody would hear.

The last pack came out of a gap in the rocks above them. Five of them. And when the fight was over, and the five white shapes lay still in the trampled snow, and everybody was breathing hard and checking themselves for bites, Aleaka heard it.

A small sound. From the gap in the rocks.

She went up to look. She didn't think about it. Nobody stopped her, because nobody else had heard.

It was a den. A hollow under a slab of grey stone, lined with old fur and pine needles, out of the wind. In the very back of it, pressed against the stone as far as it could go, there was a pup.

It was white, like the others. Fluffy, the way only very young things are fluffy, all fur and feet. Its eyes were the same pale ice-blue as the grown wolves', but enormous in its small face. It was shaking. It was looking at her.

"Oh," said Aleaka. "Oh, no."

***

"No," said Milli.

He'd come up behind her and seen it. Then they'd all come up, and now they were standing in a half-circle round the gap in the rocks in the snow, looking at the pup, and the pup was looking at Aleaka.

"Leaka," said Milli, in the calm, rational voice he used to reduce terrible things to steps. "It's a Rimehound. That's a monster. In a few weeks it'll be the size of a big dog, and in a few months it'll be one of *those*." He nodded down at the five white shapes in the snow. "It'll go round behind people and bite them."

"It's a baby," said Aleaka.

"It's a monster baby."

"It's *shaking*, Milli."

"I know it's shaking. I can see it shaking. That doesn't make it not a monster."

"We killed its family," said Aleaka. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, stumbling, the way her words always came out when they mattered. "We came up the hill and we killed its whole— and now it's on its own. In a hole. In the snow. And you want to—"

"I don't *want* to," said Milli. "I'm saying what's sensible."

"Can I say something?" said Newish.

Everybody looked at her.

"It's a game, right?" said Newish. She was standing with her staff over her shoulder and her chin up, the way she always stood when she was about to start an argument for fun. "That's what everybody keeps saying. Malcolm said it on the first day. *They're NPCs. It's a game.* So it's a monster in a game. It's code. It's a little white bit of code in a hole." She paused. "So why does everybody look like they're about to cry?"

Nobody answered.

"Right," said Newish. "So that's settled, then. It's not a game." And she sat down in the snow with her staff across her knees, as if she were waiting for somebody else to sort out the details.

Aleaka knelt down in front of the den.

She didn't reach in. She'd read somewhere, a long time ago, that you didn't reach in. You let them come to you. So she knelt in the snow in front of the gap in the rocks with her hands open on her knees and her glasses fogging with her own breath, and she waited.

"Hello," she said. Softly. Slowly. "I'm Leaka. I'm very sorry about your family. I'm not going to hurt you. I don't think I could. I've been crying about lambs all week."

The pup looked at her.

"You can stay in there if you like," said Aleaka. "Or you can come out. It's up to you. I'll wait."

She waited. Behind her, she could feel the others waiting too. Jared had put his hand on Milli's shoulder. Hana had gone very quiet.

The pup came out.

It came very slowly, on its huge clumsy feet, belly low to the stone, shaking all the way. It came out of the den into the light and stopped a hand's width from Aleaka's knee, and smelled her fingers, a long careful snuffle. Then it lay down in the snow with its chin on her boot, the way the penguin had stood on her foot in the white room, and closed its enormous eyes.

Aleaka read it twice. Then she took her glasses off and wiped them on her scarf, very carefully, because they'd fogged up, and that was the only reason her eyes were wet.

"Well," said Jared, eventually. "That's not a curse."

"No," said Aleaka. Her voice did something. "No, it isn't."

Milli let out a long breath.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. But when it's the size of a horse and it eats Newish, I said so."

"I'd like to see it try," said Newish, from the snow.

***

Larchwick did not like the pup.

The frost-bearded woman at the gate took one look at the small white shape trotting at Aleaka's heels and reached for the axe at her belt. It took Hana nearly five minutes, leaning in and speaking softly, to make her put it down. The trapper said flatly that nobody kept a Rimehound and nobody ever had, and a Rimehound wasn't a dog, and it would turn on her the first time she turned her back. The trapper's wife said nothing. She just put a bowl of scraps down on the floor of the hall by the fire, and watched the pup eat every scrap and then fall asleep in the bowl.

"You can't keep a Rimehound," said the trapper again.

"I'm not keeping her," said Aleaka. "She's keeping me."

She'd known it was a girl. She didn't know how. She just knew. The way she'd known which of the lambs would follow her and which wouldn't, and that the big grey dog at the inn was sad.

Yan's party came in from the cold an hour later, and it went about as well as anything involving Suzu ever did.

The white snake was round Yan's neck as usual, scarf-sized. She lifted her head the moment they came through the door. Her bright green eyes went straight across the hall to the bowl by the fire, and the small white thing asleep in it, and she *hissed*. A long, cold, flat sound, the kind that makes the hair on your arms stand up even when you know it's coming from a scarf.

Sleet woke up, saw the snake, and got behind Aleaka's boots so fast she left the bowl spinning.

"Suzu," said Yan mildly.

The snake said something to him. Aleaka couldn't hear it; nobody could but Yan. But she saw Yan's mouth twitch at the corner.

"She says it's a wolf," said Yan.

"It's a baby," said Aleaka.

"She says she's aware it's a baby. She says it's a baby wolf, and she doesn't care for wolves, and she'd like it noted." He paused, listening. "She also says it's very small and she won't eat it."

"That's very generous of her."

"She'd like that noted as well."

Then Yan came over and crouched by the fire, slowly, the way he did everything. He looked at Sleet, peering out from behind Aleaka's boots, for a long time. Too long. He wasn't looking at the pup's face, or her feet, or her fur. He was looking somewhere in the middle of her chest, with his pale cyan eyes slightly unfocused, the way you'd look at a lamp through a curtain.

"What?" said Aleaka.

Yan didn't answer straight away. When he did, his deep voice was very quiet, as if he were telling her something he hadn't decided to tell anyone yet.

"She's alive," he said. "Properly. All the way through."

"I know she's alive. She's eating my boot."

"No," said Yan. "I mean—" He stopped. He seemed to change his mind about what he'd been going to say. "I mean you did the right thing. That's all."

He stood up and went back to his party. Aleaka watched him go, and then looked down at Sleet, who had decided the boot was safe after all and was chewing the laces with great concentration.

*Properly,* she thought. *All the way through.* She didn't know what he'd meant. She had the feeling, very strongly, that he'd meant something.

***

She couldn't think of a name.

She sat by the fire that evening, with the pup asleep in her lap, still in the bowl, which she hadn't had the heart to take her out of. And she went through every name she'd ever liked. None of them were right. The pup was too small for big names and too fierce for silly ones. And every time Aleaka thought she had one, the same name came up underneath it instead, like a song you can't stop humming.

*Alea.*

She knew where it came from. Of course she did.

She'd known Ice for less than a year. That was the thing nobody ever believed, when she said he'd been one of her closest friends. Less than a year. But some people you knew for ten years and never really met, and some people you met once in somebody's stream chat and by the end of the week it felt like you'd known them forever. He'd been the second kind.

They'd called, every once in a while, late at night, about nothing. He'd been in her chat constantly. And they'd flirted. They'd both known it didn't mean anything; she was aromantic, and he knew that, and it had never once made him weird about anything. It was just a game they played, a rhythm. She'd worked out very early that he liked being teased, talked down to, put in his place. So she did exactly that, every chance she got. And he went along with it, meekly, *yes ma'am, sorry ma'am*. And then, just when she thought she'd won, he'd turn it round on her by telling her she was cute. Just like that. Out of nowhere. And she'd lose completely and have to mute herself.

And he named everything Alea.

Every pet. Every companion. Every horse, every dog, every little floating fairy in every game he'd ever played. All of them Alea. It had been a joke among the people who knew. There weren't many: her, and Gabe, and Deston, and Xav. She'd found out in the first month and asked him about it on a call, deliberately, smugly, because she'd thought she'd caught him.

*Alea,* she'd said. *Ale-a. Ale-aka. Is that after me, then? Is it? Have you been naming everything after me?*

*Obviously,* he'd said, immediately. *Every single one. Trust.*

*You've been doing it since before you met me.*

*Time is a construct, Leaka.*

She'd laughed so hard she'd had to mute.

He'd never actually told her where it came from. She'd asked twice more, and both times he'd made a joke and changed the subject. That was what he did with things he didn't want to talk about. She'd decided, in the end, that it didn't matter. Some names you just like the shape of.

She looked down at the pup asleep in the bowl in her lap.

*If he were here,* she thought, *he'd call you Alea. He wouldn't even think about it. He'd pick you up and say "hi, Alea" and that would be it.*

She found she couldn't do it. It wasn't hers. It was his. It belonged to a man on floor 3, alone, at level ten, with a name that wasn't his name. Somewhere up there he'd have his own Alea by now. She'd have bet her glasses on it. A horse or a dog or a little floating something, called Alea, because he couldn't help it. And she wasn't going to take that off him, even here, even from a floor away.

"Not that one," she said to the pup. "That one's taken."

The pup twitched its ear in its sleep.

Outside the hall, through the small window by the fire, the snow was falling. It wasn't the soft white floating snow of the afternoon any more, but the hard, half-frozen, slanting kind that comes before a storm, rattling on the shutters like thrown sand.

"Sleet," said Aleaka.

The pup opened one enormous ice-blue eye and looked at her.

"Sleet," said Aleaka again, more certainly. "Because you're small and cold and you come down sideways when nobody's expecting you, and you sting." She stroked one huge clumsy paw. "That's you. You're Sleet."

The pup closed her eye again and went back to sleep in the bowl.

***

Hana sat down beside her later, when the fire had burned low and most of the others had gone to the bunk room. She didn't say anything at first. She just looked at the pup, and at Aleaka, and at the iron rose in her own crown, which caught the firelight.

"You were thinking about him," said Hana.

"How did you know?"

"You had the face. The Ice face." Hana tilted her head, her one visible violet eye soft. "The one you used to get on calls when he said something stupid and you were trying not to laugh."

Aleaka was quiet for a while.

"He named everything Alea," she said finally. "Did you know that?"

Hana blinked. "No."

"Every companion. Every pet. In every game. Since forever. I asked him if it was after me." She smiled a little. "He said *obviously*. And *trust*."

Hana laughed, quietly. "That's so him."

"It is so him." Aleaka looked down at Sleet. "So if he's up there, on his own, I think he's got one. I think he's got something with him called Alea. A pet or a horse or something. I hope so." She stroked Sleet's ear. "I didn't want to take the name. In case he needed it."

Hana didn't say anything for a long moment.

"That's the nicest thing I've ever heard," she said.

"It's a bit stupid."

"It's a bit stupid," Hana agreed. "It's still the nicest thing I've ever heard."

They sat together by the fire, the two of them, with the pup between them in a bowl and the sleet rattling on the shutters.

"When we find him," said Aleaka, "I'm still going to talk down to him for an hour. I said that on the first day."

"You did."

"And he's going to let me."

"He will," said Hana.

"And then," said Aleaka, "I'm going to ask him what his Alea is. And I'm going to make fun of him for it. A lot." She paused. "And then I'm going to introduce him to Sleet."
