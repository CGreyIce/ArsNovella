Every morning for two hundred and twenty-three days, Gigichi had opened her watch, and every morning it had said nine minutes past two.

She didn't mind any more. She'd minded on floor 2, a lot, alone, with her thumb on the lid. Now it was just a thing she did. You woke up. You opened the watch. Nine past two, the second hand stuck halfway between the four and the five, the little gold leaf on the lid worn smooth where her thumb went. Home, holding its breath. Fine. You shut it and got up.

On floor 9, she'd started checking something else as well.

She didn't tell anybody that either. She lay in her heap of blankets in the hanging house she shared with Hyvred and Bee, with the foxfire jar gone dim by the door and Bee's wings twitching in her sleep beside her, and she opened her watch, and shut it. And then she rolled over onto her stomach and put her face to the gap in the woven floor, where the withies didn't quite meet, and looked down.

You couldn't see it from here. It was fifty metres down and round the side of the north tree. You couldn't see anything from here but green going down into dark.

She looked anyway.

***

The ash was going.

Maigo told them on the two hundred and twenty-third morning, at breakfast, on the drum platform, with his nut bread in both hands and his antlers drooping very slightly, which on Maigo meant he was upset.

"It's coming back," he said. "The Grow. At night."

"I thought it couldn't cross the ash," said Hako.

"It can't." Maigo put the bread down. "But it's eating the edges. Every night. A finger. Two fingers." He held his small bark-brown hand up, finger and thumb apart. "The ash is going into the ground. It's just dirt again at the outside. And the Grow comes up to wherever the dirt is." He looked at the bread. "Four more nights. Maybe five."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ina could—" said Hyvred.

"No," said Ina, from the other end of the platform, before she'd finished. Not unkindly. "Not that close. It's a seedling. It'd cook."

"And Burr said not all at once," said Chris.

"Burr said not all at once," agreed Ina.

Gigi was looking at her own hands.

She hadn't meant to. They were just there, in her lap, on the black of her skirt. Ordinary hands. Rings on three fingers. A smear of nut oil. And under the skin, if she let herself feel it, the thing she'd had since floor 2: a sort of warm thread running down each arm into the palms, that went out of her when she wanted it to, into the ground, into whatever was growing there, and *pulled*.

*Bramble. Grow it where you need it. It doesn't let go.*

She'd held two wolves off Bee with it on floor 2. A ring of thorns, out of nothing, out of the snow. She'd grown it round sawyers' legs in the log, and round the legs of a shardback on floor 6, and once, very stupidly, round Hako's ankle, by accident, on a bridge. It came when she called it. It did what she said. It always had.

And on the first day on floor 9, at the door, she'd put her hand on a fern and *asked* it, and it had ignored her.

Not resisted. Ignored. The way a crowd ignores one person shouting. She'd pushed, and it had gone on doing what it was doing, shouting its own one word, *more*, *more*, as if she wasn't there at all. She'd taken her hand off it and not said anything to anybody. Maigo had said it was awake. Maigo had said it was *fast*. Maigo hadn't said it felt like putting your hand on something that already belonged to someone.

She'd felt that. She hadn't liked it.

"I could do it," said Gigi.

Everybody looked at her.

"Not the Grow. It won't listen to me. It's—" She stopped. "It's not mine. But mine is." She turned her hand over. "I could put a hedge round it. Bramble. A ring. Outside the ash. Then it's my thorns the Grow has to get through, not dirt."

Maigo looked at her for a long time through his sunglasses.

"It'd fight it," he said. "Every night."

"I know."

"You'd have to grow it back every morning."

"I know, Maigo."

"Every morning," said Maigo. "Not most. *Every*."

Gigi looked at him. Small and brown and serious in his orange hoodie, with his leaves drooping.

"Then I'll go down every morning," she said.

***

Burr said she could have a rope at first light, every day, as long as she was up by the time the gold went, and as long as she didn't go alone.

"Nobody goes down alone," said Burr. "Walkers taught us that." And he looked at her for a long moment with his big shining eyes, the way he'd looked at the sawyer in the cave. "And no fire."

"No fire," said Gigi. "Just thorns."

Burr grunted, which meant yes.

Hyvred went with her the first morning. They came down the rope in the grey light, with the Down cold and wet and ticking softly round them the way it did at dawn, and went round the foot of the north tree to the place by the cave where the ring of stones was. Where Ina's fire had been, and the ash, and the cone, and the four small leaves.

It wasn't four leaves any more.

Gigi stopped at the edge of the stones and looked at it.

It came up to her knee. Three days, and it came up to her knee. A thin red-brown stem as straight as a pencil, and leaves all the way up it, a dozen at least, small and dark and glossy. At the top, a tight green bud, like a fist. Standing in the middle of the ring of stones in a circle of grey ash that wasn't a circle any more, because at the edges, all round, the Grow had come in. Fern-tips. Briar. Thin and pale and creeping, a finger's width into the grey, right round. Like a tide coming in over sand.

"Oh," said Hyvred softly. "Look at it."

Gigi was looking at the Grow.

She knelt down at the edge of the ash. She put her hand flat on the bare earth outside the ring, where the fern-tips were. And she called it.

It came the way it always came. Out of her palm, down into the ground, warm, pulling. And then up. A thorned stem out of the dark earth, then another, then a dozen, brown-gold and thick and hooked, the colour Hyvred said her magic was, coming up between the pale shoots of the Grow and shouldering them aside. Climbing over each other. Weaving. Thickening. She walked it round. On her knees, one hand on the ground, round the outside of the ring of stones, slowly, all the way. And behind her hand, as she went, a hedge came up out of the Down. Knee high. Then waist high. Dense and dark and thorned, closed all round the seedling and its ash like a fence round a field.

She sat back on her heels when it was done. She was breathing hard.

Outside the hedge, the Grow touched it. She felt that. The fern-tips came up against her thorns and leaned on them, and pushed, the way they pushed on everything. And her thorns didn't let go.

A window came up in front of her face. Small and blue, in the grey light.

[ Hidden quest: The Garden (1 of 3)
Keep one that cannot keep itself.
Come back every day. ]

She read it twice.

"Gigi?" said Hyvred.

"It's a quest," said Gigi. "A hidden one." She looked at the seedling through the gap in her hedge. It didn't do anything. It was a stick with leaves on. "It says come back every day."

Hyvred crouched beside her and read it over her shoulder. Gigi felt her go still.

"Every day," said Hyvred.

"I heard."

"Gi." Hyvred's voice had gone careful, the way it went when she was trying not to say something. "You know what that's going to be. That's not a fight. That's not one day of anything. That's—"

"I know what it is," said Gigi.

She did. She knew exactly what it was. She'd known as soon as she read it. It was the one thing she'd never been any good at, in her whole life, on Earth or anywhere.

Showing up.

***

She went down the next morning. And the next.

It was worse than she'd thought. Every morning she came down the rope in the grey and went round the north tree, and her hedge was torn. Not cut. *Torn*. As if something had leaned on it all night with its whole weight, the way the Down leaned on everything, and pulled and pulled at it until her thorns had given way in places, and the Grow had come through in thin pale fingers. Into the ash. Toward the seedling. Never all the way. But closer.

And every morning she knelt and put her hand on the ground and grew it back.

It took longer each time. It took more out of her. The Grow didn't care how tired she was. It had all night, every night, and the whole of the Down behind it, and she had one pair of hands and an hour in the grey before Burr's people started looking at the light at the tops of the trees. By the third morning she was shaking when she sat back on her heels. By the fourth she had to sit on the stones for a while before she could stand up.

"You're doing it the hard way," said Hyvred, on the third morning, watching her.

"It's the only way."

"I know. I'm just saying it out loud. So somebody has." Hyvred sat down on the stones next to her. "You can't miss one, Gi."

"I know."

"Not one."

"I *know*, Hyv."

Hyvred looked at her sideways, through her glasses, with the little red mark in her golden eye that went sharp when she was really looking at something.

"You used to miss things," said Hyvred. "On Earth. You know you did. You'd say you'd be there and then you'd—"

"I know," said Gigi, much more quietly.

Hyvred didn't finish. She put her arm round Gigi's shoulders instead, and they sat there on the stones in the grey with the seedling in its ring of thorns, and the Down ticking, until the light at the top of the trees started to go from grey to green.

***

On the fourth morning, there was somebody else at the rope.

Gigi came along the branch to the drum platform in the dark before first light, with her cardigan pulled round her and her hair still tangled, and Thea was sitting on the edge of the platform with the lantern beside her.

Just sitting there. In the cold. With her knees up and her hands round them, and the amber light coming up warm through the blue of the foxfire. She looked like she'd been there a while.

Gigi stopped.

"What are you doing here?" she said.

"Coming," said Thea.

Gigi stood there in the dark with her hands in her cardigan sleeves and looked at her.

They hadn't been alone together since floor 6. Not really. Since a cistern at the bottom of a desert, with Thea's head in her lap all night and the fever going, and Thea saying *I should have stayed up. Some nights*, in the dark, very quietly, with her eyes shut. And Gigi saying *I'm still angry at you*. And Thea saying *me too. At me.* And their foreheads together, for a long time.

And then nothing. Not nothing. They sat near each other at meals. They talked, in the club, about the ones before, about him. Thea had passed her the salt on floor 8 and Gigi had said thanks. It was fine. It was *fine*. They were people who'd been best friends once and were being very polite about the fact that they weren't, and it had been fine for a hundred days, and Gigi had hated every single one of them.

"Hyvred's coming," said Gigi.

"Hyvred's asleep," said Thea. "I asked her if I could have this one." She looked at the lantern. "She said yes. She said it very fast."

Gigi didn't say anything.

"You need somebody with you," said Thea. "That's the rule. Nobody goes down alone." She looked up at Gigi in the dark. "I can hold a lantern. I'm told I'm good at it."

Burr's people were coming along the branch to the drum, quick and quiet, yawning. Somebody was uncoiling a rope.

"Fine," said Gigi.

***

Thea didn't talk, the first morning. She held the lantern while Gigi knelt and grew the hedge back, and she watched, and she didn't say a word. When it was done and Gigi was sitting on the stones shaking, Thea passed her a waterskin without being asked. Gigi drank. They went back up.

She came the second morning too. And the third.

Gigi started to notice things. She didn't want to, but she did.

She noticed that Thea held the lantern low, at the level of Gigi's hands, so she could see what she was doing, and not up where it would have been easier. She noticed that Thea had worked out on the second morning which bits of the hedge got torn worst, which was the north side, toward the Down, and stood there. She noticed that Thea's free hand kept going to her left shoulder, the way it did, to the scar, and then away again.

She noticed that Thea didn't ask her for anything. Not once. Not to talk. Not to forgive her. Not to say any of the things they'd been not saying for a hundred days, or for the long time before that, on Earth, when the messages took a week to come back and then didn't come back at all.

She just turned up. Every morning. In the dark, on the edge of the platform, with the lantern.

It made Gigi furious.

She didn't know why. She knelt in the ash on the sixth morning with her hands on the ground and her thorns coming up brown-gold between her fingers, and she was so angry she could hardly see. At the Grow. At the hedge. At her own hands, which were shaking already, before she'd even started. At Thea, standing there behind her, holding the light low, not saying anything.

"Why are you doing this?" she said.

She didn't turn round. She didn't stop growing the hedge.

Thea didn't answer for a long time.

"You know why," said Thea.

"No, I don't. I genuinely don't. You've got your own stuff. You've got the club. You've got—" She stopped. She didn't say *him*. "You don't have to stand here every morning holding a lamp over a stick."

"I know I don't have to."

"So why—"

"Because you said *staying up*," said Thea.

Gigi's hands stopped.

"In the cistern," said Thea. "When you had my head on your knee. You said it showed you me. On six. The thing that shows you what you want. You said it showed you me *staying up with you. Like before*." Her voice was very even. "That's what you wanted most. On the whole of floor 6. Out of everything." She paused. "Not him. Not home. Me. Staying up."

Gigi didn't say anything. She was looking at her hands on the ground.

"I didn't, on Earth," said Thea. "Stay up. Not enough. I know I didn't. I was always too tired, or too busy, or I'd say *tomorrow*, and then it was tomorrow and I'd say it again." She was quiet. "And you stopped coming. And I told everyone it was because you didn't pull your weight. I told *myself*. For ages." The lantern moved, very slightly. "I was right. You didn't. But I didn't either. I just didn't say that part."

The Down ticked round them, in the grey.

"So I'm staying up," said Thea. "That's all. It's morning, so it doesn't really count. But it's what I've got." She paused again. "I'm not asking you for anything. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I just wanted to be somewhere you could see me doing it."

Gigi knelt in the ash with her hands on the ground.

She didn't know what her face was doing. She was glad Thea was behind her. She was glad of the dark, and the hedge, and the seedling standing in front of her, taller than her knee now, with its fist of a bud at the top, not caring about any of it.

"I'm still angry at you," said Gigi. It came out very thick.

"I know."

"I've been angry at you for ages. It's—I'm *good* at it. I know how to do it. I know where everything is." She pressed her hands into the earth. "Bee says I never let go of anything."

"Bee's right," said Thea. "You never did. That was the best thing about you."

Gigi laughed. She didn't mean to. It came out of her wet and furious and sudden, on her knees in the ash, and she put her arm up over her face and laughed into her sleeve, and couldn't stop.

Behind her, Thea didn't move. She just held the lantern low, where Gigi could see her hands, and waited.

***

The seedling grew.

That was the thing about it. Whatever else happened, it grew. Floor 9 didn't make anything wait for growing, and it didn't make the seedling wait. Knee high on the fourth morning. Thigh high on the fifth. On the sixth morning the fist of the bud at the top had opened, and there were new leaves coming, paler, softer, and a second stem had come off the first, low down, the beginning of a branch.

Bee drew it every day.

From above. From the edge of the lowest platform, lying on her stomach with her wings folded flat and her sketchbook propped on the boards, looking down through the gap in the branches. Every morning, while Gigi and Thea were down there. She drew the seedling and the hedge and the ring of stones and the mouth of the cave and the Down round it, all of it, carefully, the same view every day, and wrote the date in the corner.

"Why?" said Gigi, on the sixth evening, when Bee showed her.

"Because it's growing," said Bee, as if that were obvious. "You draw things that are growing. So you can see them do it."

She turned the pages. Six drawings. Six mornings. The seedling in each one a little taller, and the hedge round it a little more torn on the north side each morning before Gigi got there, and the Grow outside it in pale scribbled lines of charcoal, leaning in.

Gigi looked at them for a long time.

"It looks like it's getting bigger," she said.

"It is getting bigger. That's what I'm *saying*." Bee shut the book. "Also your hedge looks stupid on the north side. You should do it thicker."

"I *know*."

"Okay. Just checking."

Maigo came down on the seventh morning. He'd come most mornings, for a while, at the end, after Gigi was done. He sat on the stones and put his bare root-foot on the ground outside the hedge, not rooting, just resting it there, the way you'd rest your hand on somebody's back.

"It likes you," said Maigo.

"It's a stick."

"It's a tree." He looked at the seedling through his sunglasses. "It can feel you come. Every morning. The hedge. It's—" He stopped, the way he did when he didn't have the word yet. "It's not frightened at night. Because you'll come back."

"It's a *stick*, Maigo."

"It's going to be the biggest thing on this floor," said Maigo. "After its mum." He looked at the north tree, going up beside them into the green, enormous. "She's watching you, you know. Linden says. The north tree. Every morning. She's watching what you do."

Gigi didn't know what to say to that. She grew the hedge back. Thea held the lantern.

***

On the eighth morning, the hedge wasn't torn.

She came round the foot of the north tree in the grey with Thea beside her, and stopped.

It was whole. All the way round. Every thorn of it. Not one place on the north side where the Grow had come through in the night. And outside it, the Grow wasn't leaning on it any more. That was the strange thing. Every morning for seven mornings it had been pressed right up against her hedge, fern-tips flattened on the thorns, pushing. This morning it wasn't. It was there, pale and thin, all round. But it had stopped a hand's width short.

As if there was something there it didn't want to touch.

"Gi," said Thea quietly.

Gigi knelt at the edge of the hedge and put her hand on the ground outside it.

She felt it at once. Not her thorns. Something else. Under the earth, under the ash, under the stones and the edges of the hedge and the place where the Grow had stopped. Spreading out in every direction from the foot of the seedling, fine and white and thin, a hundred of them, a thousand. Roots.

Its own roots.

They'd gone out under her hedge in the night, past it, out into the Down. A hand's width beyond the thorns, all round. And the Grow had come up to them in the dark, the way it came up to everything, and touched them, and *stopped*. The way it stopped at the ash. As if they were the same thing. As if a great tree's roots, even a small one's, were a place the Grow didn't go.

It could keep itself.

She knelt there with her hand on the ground. She didn't know how long. Thea didn't say anything.

And she understood what she had to do next. She didn't want to. She understood it anyway, the way you understand something you've been not understanding on purpose for a long time.

*It doesn't let go.*

Her thorns were still there. All round it. Thick and dark and hooked and holding, because that was what they did, because that was what she did. She'd grown them round it and they'd held, eight nights, and they'd go on holding as long as she let them. And now, inside them, the seedling's roots were coming up against them too. Pressing on them. From the inside. Wanting out. Wanting room.

The hedge wasn't keeping the Grow out any more. It was keeping the tree in.

"Oh," said Gigi. Very quietly. "Oh, you're kidding me."

"What?" said Thea.

"I've got to let go of it."

She heard her own voice. It sounded young. It sounded the way it had sounded on Earth, sometimes, very late at night, when there was nobody to hear it.

"It doesn't need it," she said. "It doesn't need the hedge. It doesn't need me. It can do it itself now. And the hedge is in the way." She pressed her hand flat. "I've got to let go."

Thea crouched down beside her. She put the lantern on the ground between them. She didn't say anything. She didn't touch her.

Gigi shut her eyes.

She felt for it. The warm thread down her arm, into her palm, into the ground, into the thorns. All of them. All round. Holding. She'd never once in her life let it go on purpose. It went when she was done with it, when she got tired, when she stopped thinking about it. She'd never just *opened her hand*.

She thought about all that time of not answering. About being good at being angry, about knowing where everything was. About the nights she'd said she'd be on and wasn't, and the nights Thea had said *tomorrow*, and the long, long time of both of them being right.

She opened her hand.

The hedge let go.

She felt it go. All at once, all round. The thorns going loose. Softening. Not dying. Going back. Down into the dark earth they'd come up out of, slowly, the way a held breath goes out of you. The brown-gold of them fading. The hooks unhooking. And inside the ring, the seedling's roots felt them go, and spread out into the space where they'd been.

When she opened her eyes, there was no hedge. There was a ring of stones, and some grey ash, and a young tree as tall as she was, standing in the middle of the Down on its own, with its leaves moving very slightly in no wind at all. And all round it, a hand's width out, the Grow, pale and thin, stopped.

[ Hedgerow
Grow it round what you love. It holds while you're gone. ]

[ The Garden (2 of 3)
Pull up one that was never planted. ]

She read them. She read the last one twice.

Then she put her face in her hands.

She wasn't crying. She told Thea that afterwards, and Thea said *okay*, and didn't argue. She was just sitting on the stones at the foot of the north tree with her face in her hands, for a while, breathing.

"Gi," said Thea, after a while.

"Mm."

"You came back every day."

"I know."

"Eight days. Not one missed." Thea's voice did something. "I've never seen you do that. Not once. Not for anything."

Gigi took her hands away from her face.

"Neither have I," she said.

They sat there. The tree stood in front of them, taller than either of them, with the light coming down green through the leaves of the north tree onto its leaves. Thin. Young. Nothing yet. Going to be enormous.

Gigi took her watch out of her pocket.

She didn't know why. She just did. She opened it, the way she did every morning, with her thumb on the worn leaf. Nine minutes past two. The second hand stuck between the four and the five.

"What time is it?" said Thea.

"Nine past two."

"Still?"

"Still," said Gigi. "Always." She looked at it. "Every morning. I check it every morning. I've never told anybody that."

Thea looked at the watch in her hand. Then at her.

"Then nothing's happened at home yet," said Thea. "Has it."

"No."

"So we've got time."

Gigi looked at her for a long moment. At Thea, crouched in the grey ash with the lantern beside her and her hand on her shoulder, on the scar, where she'd taken something for Gigi on floor 6 without even stopping to think about it.

"Yeah," said Gigi. "I suppose we have."

She shut the watch. She put it away. And then she leaned over, the way she hadn't in a very long time, and put her forehead against Thea's, the way they had in the cistern, and stayed there.

Thea didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Above them, a very long way up, on the edge of the lowest platform, somebody was drawing.

***

She told the party that evening, about the third window. *Pull up one that was never planted.*

Maigo went very still when she said it.

"Mine says find its root," he said. "*Something grows here that was never planted. Find its root.*" He looked at her across the platform, small and brown in the foxfire, with his leaves gone stiff. "That's the same thing."

"I know," said Gigi.

"Yours says pull it up."

"I know, Maigo."

Hako, who'd been eating, put his bowl down.

"Okay," said Hako. "So what wasn't planted?"

Nobody answered him. But Gigi was looking out over the edge of the platform, down, at the dark going away under the seven in every direction, ticking. At the Grow. At all of it, every fern and briar and pale shoot of it, from here to the far side of the floor, shouting its one word.

It had never listened to her. Not once. Not on the first day at the door, and not on any morning since. Because it already belonged to somebody.

"That," said Gigi.
