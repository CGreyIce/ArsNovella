John had been watching the Countinghouse door for five nights, and nothing had come out of it that hadn't gone in.

He did it from the roof. Xav had asked him to, the morning after Rui Xiang went in alone: *keep a light on it*. So he did. Every night, on his watch and off it, he sat on the lead with his back against the chimney and sent one small mote across the Wellgate, and parked it in the dark under the eaves of the Tellhouse portico opposite, where it could see the Countinghouse steps, and nobody looking up from the street would see it. And he watched through it.

That was what the motes were. A little bit of him, sent ahead. *See what it sees.* He'd had the skill since floor 1, and it was still the best thing that had ever happened to him. He'd spent his whole life off to one side. In the corner of a screen, once, a long time ago, in a small room in Singapore. A shoulder and half a face and a voice saying the wrong thing at the wrong moment. And now he could send that corner anywhere he liked.

So he sent it across the road, and sat, and watched the clerks go in.

They went in at dusk. One at a time, from every corner of the Wellgate, walking up the four plain steps and through the plain door without knocking, and the door opening for each of them and shutting behind. He'd counted. A hundred and twelve, the first night. A hundred and twelve the second. And at dawn they came out again, one at a time, and walked back down to their corners, and stood.

Never anybody else. Tull hadn't come back.

"It's like a hive," he said to Xav, on the third night. "In at dusk. Out at dawn. Same number."

"What do they do in there?"

"Write," said John. "Rui says. All night."

"Write *what*?"

John didn't know. Nobody did. That was the point.

***

Thea asked him on the fifth evening.

He was in the copy-room yard, at the pump, washing his face. She came down the stable stairs with the lantern, on her own, and stood beside the trough while he dried his hands, and waited till he'd finished.

"John."

"Thea."

"Your motes." She didn't look at him. She was looking at the yard gate, and the Wellgate past it, and the Countinghouse across the road. "Can they go in?"

He knew what she meant before she'd finished.

"Through the door?"

"Under it. Round it. Anywhere." Her voice was very steady. "Rui Xiang said there's a book. On a lectern. At the far end. He couldn't get close enough to read it." She turned her head and looked at him. "Could a mote?"

John didn't say anything for a moment.

He'd known Thea since floor 1. Not well. Thea's party and Xav's had never had much to do with each other until floor 3, and by then Thea had already been the one who read things, and John had already been the one off to one side. They'd sat at the same fires. They'd been in the same white room, the first morning, thirty-five of them in a ring round an empty circle. But he'd never had a conversation with her on her own, he realised. Not one.

And she was asking him to send a bit of himself into the Countinghouse.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I can try."

"Will it hurt? You?"

He thought about it. Nobody had ever asked him that.

"Sometimes," he said. "If they go out. It's like pinching a candle. Just for a second."

Thea looked at him for a long time.

"Then don't," she said. "If it's—"

"I'll try," said John.

***

He did it from the roof, with Thea beside him.

He sent the mote he'd parked under the Tellhouse portico across the last of the road, low, under the level of the steps. Up the steps. To the door. And he looked through it, the way he always did: not with his eyes, exactly, but with the part of him that was out there, a tiny white light in the dark, looking at a plain grey door from a hand's width away.

There was a gap at the bottom. A finger's width. Grey light coming out of it.

He went under.

The passage. Ten paces of grey stone. Rui Xiang had described it, and it was exactly as he'd described it, and that was somehow worse. And then the hall.

John had never seen anything like it. He'd seen a lot of things through motes. Roofs and cellars and the insides of hollow trees and the far side of the Steward's ring on floor 10. But the hall went back and back under its grey ceiling, further than the building, further than the hill, with the grey light coming from nowhere, and the rows and rows of clerks at their desks with their grey pens moving, all at once, with the sound of it like rain. And the mote, a tiny white thing, went along the top of the pillars, high up, in the grey, the only thing in the whole hall that had any colour.

He felt Thea, beside him on the roof, holding her breath.

"There's a lot of them," he said. Out loud, so she'd know. His own voice sounded far off. "Hundreds. They're writing. It's—it's further than it should be. Rui was right."

"The book."

"Going."

The mote went along the pillar-tops. Over the desks. Toward the far end, where the grey light was brightest. And there it was. Raised up on its step. The lectern, tall and grey, and on it the great book, open, bigger than a door, with a tall clerk standing at it, turning a page.

He brought the mote down. Slowly. As close as he dared. Over the clerk's shoulder.

And looked at the page.

It was grey. All of it. Grey paper, grey writing, lines and lines of it, very small. He could see it was writing. He could see the lines. But he couldn't read it. It was like trying to read a page under water, or in a dream. The letters were there and wouldn't be letters. They slid. Every time he looked at a word it was a different word, or not a word, or nothing.

"I can't read it," he said. "Thea. It's there. It's writing. But it won't—it moves. When I look at it."

"Does it move for the clerk?"

He looked. The tall clerk turned a page. Its eyes went along the lines, steadily, left to right, the way you read.

"No," said John. "It's reading it fine."

Thea was quiet.

And then every pen in the hall stopped.

He felt it before he heard it. Through the mote. A silence so sudden it was like a hand over his mouth. Hundreds of pens, still. And every clerk at every desk lifting its head.

Not toward the door.

Up.

At the mote.

Hundreds of faces with nothing in them, turned up toward one small white light at the top of a pillar. And the tall clerk at the lectern, last, slowly, raising its head from the book.

It looked at the mote.

And the mote went out.

John made a sound. He didn't mean to. On the roof, with his back against the chimney, his whole body jerked, the way it does when you pinch a candle with wet fingers and get it wrong. A small sharp nothing, behind his eyes, and then dark.

"*John*—"

"I'm okay." He was holding his own head. "I'm okay. It went out. It just went out." He breathed. "It saw it. It looked straight at it."

Thea had her hand on his arm. He didn't know when she'd put it there.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I shouldn't have—"

"It's fine." He breathed again. The dark behind his eyes was going. "I've had worse. A Squall ate one on floor 5." He took his hands away from his face and looked at her. "It's a book, Thea. A real one. It's there. And the clerk can read it, and I can't."

She looked across the Wellgate at the Countinghouse door.

"Then somebody who can read things will have to go and read it," she said.

He knew who she meant. He'd known since floor 3. Thea read things. Under the windows. Under the words. She read what wasn't meant to be read.

"You'll need a light," he said.

She turned and looked at him.

"In there," said John. "It's all grey. Grey light from nowhere. You won't be able to see what's real and what's not." He nodded at the lantern beside her on the lead, glowing its deep warm amber. "That shows things how they are. But it's not enough on its own. You'll need something that can go ahead." He shrugged. "Off to one side."

Thea looked at him for a long time.

"Will you come?" she said.

"I'm not in your party."

"I'm not asking your party," said Thea. "I'm asking you."

John looked across the road. At the plain grey door. At the place under the portico where his mote had sat for five nights, empty now.

"Best seat in the house," he said.

***

They came up onto the roof on the seventh night. Gabe and Thea. Together.

John was already there. His watch, midnight to two. He had a mote out over the Wellgate, low, the way he always did now, and he was sitting with his back to the chimney, and he heard them on the ladder, and knew who it was by the way Gabe climbed. Heavy. Careful. Hearthwall bumping on the rungs.

Gabe came up first. Then Thea, with the lantern. They stopped at the top of the hatch and saw him, and Gabe's face did something.

"John," said Gabe.

"I'll go." John was already getting up. "I'll take the ladder. Rui can—"

"Stay."

John stopped.

Gabe was looking at him. Not unkindly. In the way Gabe looked at people when he'd decided something.

"You were always there," said Gabe. "In the corner. On the streams. Every night. Off to one side." He sat down on the lead, with his back to the parapet, and set Hearthwall beside him, warm. "You might as well be here."

John looked at Thea.

Thea looked back at him. And then, very slightly, she nodded. And sat down on the lead a little way from Gabe, with the lantern between them.

So John stayed. He sat back down against the chimney, a little way off. Off to one side. And watched.

***

For a long time, neither of them said anything.

Below them the Wellgate was dark and empty, with a clerk on every turn of it, standing under the dead lamps. Across the road, the Countinghouse with its door shut. Above, the stars. Real ones, Chris said, or near enough that it hurt.

"I said I'd tell you," said Thea at last. "What I need. At the Reckoning."

"You did."

"I need you to look at the screen."

Gabe turned his head.

"When they read it," said Thea. She was looking straight ahead, at the dark, at nothing. "The second hand. Every line. Everyone in that hall is going to look at him. Thirty-five of us. Half of Tell. And some of them are going to look at me." She didn't say why. She didn't need to. "Because the clerks always look at me. Because Rui Xiang knows I know something. Because I'll be standing next to him and I'll know what it means, and everybody else will be finding out, and they'll look at the one person whose face isn't changing." She stopped. "And so will the thing behind the screen."

Gabe was very quiet.

"And if you look at me," said Thea, "they'll all look at me. If you look at him, they'll look at him. You're the biggest one there. You're the one on his right. Wherever you look, they'll look." She turned her head, finally, and met his eyes. "So look at the screen. Whatever it says. Don't look at him, and don't look at me. Look at the Magistrate. Like a wall."

Gabe looked at her for a long moment.

"Covers the one beside you," he said.

"Yes."

"Okay." He looked down at Hearthwall, beside him, with its three flames glowing very faintly. "I can do that. I'm good at being looked at. I'm big."

Thea laughed. Just a little. Wet.

John sat against the chimney and didn't move.

***

"Can I tell you something?" said Gabe.

Thea looked at him.

"Not about the thing." Gabe held up his hands. "I don't want to know about the thing. I've never wanted to know about the thing. I want to tell you something about *him*." He was quiet. "Because you know the thing. But I've known him since he was thirteen."

"I know."

"So." Gabe looked at the dark. "He's never once said *it hurts*. Not once, in his whole life. Not at thirteen. Not ever. He'd fall off something and get up and say *it's fine*. He'd—" He stopped. "You know. You've seen it. Everybody up here has. It's the only thing he's ever said about himself. *It's fine.*"

Thea didn't say anything for a moment.

"He said it," she said.

Gabe turned his head.

"On the Shoals," said Thea. "On floor 11. On the rock in the sea. He started to say *fine*, and he stopped, halfway, and he said *it hurts*. To me." She was looking at the lantern. "And to Malcolm. Five days ago. At the pump. He held his arm out and asked. Malcolm told me. Malcolm said he did two, and Ice said *I just never stayed still long enough to notice*."

John watched Gabe's face.

He'd seen it do a lot of things. On floor 2, by a cold fire, holding a little heap of onion skins. On floor 7, in a bakery, with flour in the air and his fist going back. On floor 10, on the barracks step, with Hearthwall on his arm and his forehead against Declan's shoulder. He'd been off to one side for all of them.

He'd never seen it do this.

It went quite still. And then something came up into it, slowly, from a long way down, and stayed. Not pain. Not quite. Something like what happens to a man's face when somebody hands him a thing he lost a long time ago and had stopped looking for.

"He said *it hurts*," said Gabe.

"Yes."

"To you."

"Yes."

Gabe looked at her for a long time.

"What did you *do*?" he said.

"I asked him to try," said Thea. "That's all. I asked him. Under the aurora. *Heal sooner. A bit.*" She looked down at her hands. "And he said he would. And he has."

"Twenty-three years," said Gabe. Very quietly. Not to her. To the dark. "I've known him twenty-three years, and I've never once heard him say it."

"You made him a fire," said Thea.

Gabe shook his head. "That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same." Thea turned round on the lead to face him. "Gabe. You made him a fire every night. You stood on his right on every wall since floor 10. You forged a shield that covers the one beside you and you gave it to him on a step and you didn't ask him anything. You're the reason he knows how to let anybody stand next to him at all." Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren't. "I just asked him to try. You're why he could."

Gabe didn't say anything.

John watched him not say it. Off to one side.

Then Gabe put his hand on the lead between them, palm up. The way Deston did, on a jetty on floor 8.

Thea looked at it.

Then she put hers in it.

***

They sat like that for a while. Not talking. John sent his mote up a little higher, over the Wellgate, without quite meaning to, so its light came down very faintly on the roof. The way he'd done once over a tarn at the top of the world, for two people on a rock.

"I don't want to know what's in the book," said Gabe, eventually.

"I know."

"But I want you to know I know there's a book." He didn't let go of her hand. "And whatever's in it, I've already decided."

Thea looked at him.

"Decided what?"

"That it's him." Gabe shrugged, the way he shrugged about everything that mattered most. "Whatever it says. However many lines. Whatever the clerk reads out, in front of whoever. It's still Dec. It's still the kid who counts flat on the eights. It's still the idiot who let go of my arm in the Down so the thorns wouldn't go through it." He was quiet. "That's what the Magistrate asks, isn't it. Malcolm says. *Who writes him.* Write him as you know him." He looked at the screen of stars over the Assize, a long way up the hill. "So I know what I'm writing. I've known since I was thirteen."

Thea's hand tightened in his.

"I'm going to read it," she said.

Gabe turned his head.

"The book. Before the Reckoning." She was looking across the road at the Countinghouse. "I can't decide anything if I don't know what it says. What they're going to read out. Every line. I need to know first. So I can—" She stopped. "So I can decide what to do."

"Not alone," said Gabe.

"Not alone." She almost smiled. "With my party. Drew. Higu. Xola. Chi. They're my party, Gabe. They'll come." She nodded toward the chimney, without looking. "And John's light. He's coming."

Gabe looked over at John for the first time since he'd sat down.

"Are you?"

"Best seat in the house," said John.

Gabe looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded. Once.

"And Dec?" he said, to Thea.

"Ice waits on the step," said Thea. "Like at the Tellhouse. He's getting good at steps."

"*He's* getting good at steps." Gabe let out a breath that was nearly a laugh. "He's never waited on a step in his life."

"He has now," said Thea. "He's waited on two."

***

Declan came up the ladder at about half past one.

John heard him. Light. Quick. The cord on his wrist catching once on a rung. He came up through the hatch and stopped at the top of it, the way Gabe had, and saw them. Gabe and Thea, sitting on the lead with the lantern between them and their hands not quite apart yet. And John, off to one side, against the chimney.

He didn't say anything.

He came across the roof and sat down between them. Just sat. On the lead, with his back to the parapet and his knees up. Gabe moved over without a word, so there was room on his left, the way he always did, so he could be on Declan's right. Thea moved the lantern so it was on Declan's other side, by her.

Nobody said anything about what they'd been saying.

Declan looked from one of them to the other. Gabe was looking at the stars. Thea was looking at the lantern.

"Okay," said Declan, after a while. Quietly. As if somebody had told him something, and he'd decided not to ask what.

"Okay," said Gabe.

They sat there. The three of them. Gabe on his right with Hearthwall. Thea on his left with the lantern. The fire and the lamp. In the dark, on a lead roof over a stable, in a city that had decided to write them down.

John looked at them for a long time.

He'd spent a whole life in the corner of the frame. He'd thought, on floor 1, in the white room, with an empty circle in the middle of thirty-five people, that it was where he belonged. Off to one side. Watching other people be in the middle of things. And he'd thought that was a smaller thing to be, and he'd been happy with it anyway, because the view was so good.

He didn't think that any more.

Somebody had to be off to one side. Somebody had to be the one who saw it. Who could say, afterwards, *I was there, and this is what it looked like*. That was what the Tellhouse was. That was what Cursiffe was. That was what the Magistrate was going to ask, in less than three weeks, in front of the whole of Tell: *who writes him*. Who saw him. Who was there.

He knew what he'd write.

He sent the mote up. Higher. Above the three of them on the parapet. Not far. Just high enough to hang there in the dark over their heads, small and white and steady, like a lamp in a window. The way he'd hung one over a tarn.

Declan looked up at it.

Then he looked across the roof at John. In the corner. Against the chimney.

"Off to one side?" said Declan.

"Best seat in the house," said John.

And Declan smiled. Not much. But all the way. The way John had seen him smile a thousand times, a long time ago, from the corner of a screen in a small room in Singapore, when he looked up from whatever he was doing and saw that somebody was still there.

***

He wrote it down that night, after his watch, by the one lamp. Not on a slate. In the back of the little book where he kept the count of his motes, the way Chris kept notes and Cursiffe kept everything. Just to see what it looked like.

*John. Off to one side.*

*I've watched him for a year and a half from closer than anybody except the people next to him. He never once looks at himself. Not in a fight, not on a wall, not on a step. He's always looking at one of us. Making sure. Counting. Seeing who's still there.*

*He let go of Gabe's arm so the thorns wouldn't go through it. He stood still for eleven hours so we could find him. He asked Malcolm to heal his arm.*

*He looked up at a light and smiled because somebody was still there.*

*That's him. I was there. Best seat in the house.*

He read it back.

Then he closed the book, and lay down on his blanket, and for the first time in a long time, before he slept, he didn't send a mote anywhere at all.
