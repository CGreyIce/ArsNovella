Hana had a rose in her crown, and she didn't know where to put the doubt.

The rose was easy. Jared had given it to her on the fifth night at the Copper Spoon, across the long table, without a word, the way he did things he cared about. It was a small, rough thing of black iron, the size of a thumb, its petals curled in on each other, still faintly warm from the forge. She'd looked at it. Then she'd looked at him, and said, "It's ugly," and he'd said, "I know," and she'd pinned it into her crown, right at the front, among the gold beads. It had been there ever since.

Milli said it made her look like a queen in a war.

The doubt was harder. The doubt didn't have anywhere to go.

***

They cleared the Fold on the ninth morning: the fourth party to do it, after Deston's and Yan's and Thea's. They did it cleanly, more or less, because there was a guide now, Deston's four lines in World. Everybody had read it a dozen times, and Jared had learned it by heart.

It wasn't the first time Hana had seen the ram. But it was the first time she'd seen it look at her.

She'd done it on purpose. The guide said *have something it can hit instead*, and Hana had decided, on the walk up the valley, that the something could be its own attention. When the Bellwether swung round on Milli at the end of its second charge, she'd stepped out from behind Jared's shield and caught its yellow eye. She'd said, in the voice she used on her streams when chat was being difficult, the soft, leaning-in one: "*Hey. Not him. Look at me.*"

And it had. For two whole seconds, a ram the size of a horse had stood in the middle of the Fold with its head tipped toward her, listening, as if she'd said something interesting. Two seconds. Milli had got the strap the rest of the way through. It had been the most frightening thing she'd ever done, and also, she was a little ashamed to admit, the most fun.

Then it had screamed, and come round on its curve, and Jared had held it, and Newish had shouted a count, *one, two, three, GO*, and somehow all of them had moved on the *go* at once. And it was over, and they were sitting in a row against the cracked stone, the fourth party to clear floor 1.

Fourth. Behind Voyager, who'd done it alone on the first day.

She'd opened the rankings while she was getting her breath back. She hadn't meant to. She did it all the time now, the way you touch a sore tooth with your tongue.

Level ten. Floor three. Since last night.

The second window had come at supper. She'd been at the long table at the Spoon when it opened in the corner of everybody's eye at once: *Floor 2 has been cleared*. She'd watched Gabe put his spoon down and not pick it up again. She'd watched Deston put his hand flat on his shirt pocket. She'd watched Xav look at nothing for a long time.

And she'd thought, as she'd been thinking for eight days, every time that name went further up the list: *Is it really him?*

***

The thing was, she'd *seen* him.

That was what made it worse. Gabe had seen him for a second through rising light. Yan had seen him for half a second, past a snake. Hana had seen him from three metres away, in the clear white light of the Threshold, sitting bolt upright, wide awake. He'd been standing by the arch with one hand half raised, and he'd looked straight at her, and she'd watched him know her.

Three metres. She could have got up and touched him.

And every day since, the three metres had got longer.

Not the distance. The certainty. She'd woken up on the second morning sure. By the fourth she'd been nearly sure. By the sixth she'd found herself lying awake in the bed she shared with Milli in the inn on the high street, going over it frame by frame, the way she'd go over a clip of a stream to see what had actually happened.

She'd been asleep two seconds before. She'd sat up all at once, disoriented, in a body that wasn't hers, in a room she'd never seen. The light had been strange. Everything had been strange. She'd seen a young man in a black vest with a teal streak in his hair. And she'd known Ice's little drawing for years, from every emote and profile picture he'd ever made. Of course her brain had put his face on the nearest stranger. That was what brains did.

That was what she told herself at three in the morning.

Then morning would come, and she'd remember his face. Not the drawing. His *face*, looking at her. The way it had done something when he saw her, something that wasn't in any drawing. Like a man seeing someone he'd lost, alive again.

Then she'd be sure.

Then the name would go up the list, *Voyager, Voyager, Voyager*, and she'd stop being sure again.

***

"Say it," said Jared.

They were going up the stair. A hundred and twelve steps, in single file, in the dark, with Jared behind her and Milli in front, and Newish at the very front, loudly complaining about stairs. Aleaka was somewhere at the back, talking softly to nobody.

"Say what?"

"The thing you're thinking. You've been thinking it so loud since breakfast I can hear it from here." Jared's voice was easy in the dark. "Say it."

Hana climbed three more steps.

"If it were Ice," she said finally, "he wouldn't do this to Gabe."

Jared didn't answer straight away.

"Think about it," said Hana. It came out faster now, the way things did once she'd started. "Think about who he is. He's the one who notices. When somebody's had a bad stream, he's in their chat drawing cats until they laugh. When somebody's sad in a call, he goes quiet and asks them a question about something else, and then another, until they're not sad. He's never, *ever* been the one who leaves people hanging. Not in all the years I've known him." She stopped on the stair. "And Gabe's been sitting at that table for eight days with a whisper open that says *sent*. Every single morning. Ice would never. Ice would rather die than let Gabe sit there like that."

The stair was very quiet.

"So either it's not him," said Hana. "Or it is him, and something's very wrong."

She hadn't meant to say the second half. It was the half she'd been not saying since the second morning.

Jared put his hand on her shoulder, lightly, from the step below.

"Or," he said.

"Or what?"

"Or it's him. And nothing's wrong with him. And he's got a reason that's bigger than Gabe sitting at a table." He was quiet for a moment. "You know what he'd say if somebody asked him to choose between making one person sad for a while and getting thirty-five people killed?"

Hana didn't answer.

"He'd say *trust*," said Jared. "And then he'd go and do the sad one. And he'd hate himself for it the whole time. And he'd do it anyway."

She stood on the stair in the dark.

"You don't know that," she said.

"No," Jared agreed. "But I knew him. Same as you." He took his hand away. "If you saw Ice, you saw Ice. I said that on the first day. I'm still saying it. I'll take you over a light bulb."

"And over a ranking?"

"Over a ranking," said Jared. "Over a name. Over all of it." A pause. "Keep climbing, Alice. Newish is going to start a fight with the stairs."

From somewhere far above, Newish's voice came echoing down: "*Who builds a hundred and twelve stairs? Who? Who sat down and drew this and said, yes, perfect, a hundred and twelve?*"

Hana laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. She climbed.

***

Floor 2 was snow.

She'd known it would be. Everyone did. Yan's party had come up three days ago, and Wendy had put one line in World the first night: *bring a coat. bring two coats. bring the person you love most and lie on them.* But knowing it and walking out into it were different things. Hana came out of the door at the top of the stair in a lavender off-shoulder top and a pleated skirt, into a wind that came straight off a mountain, and she said a word she'd never once said on stream.

"Same," said Milli, through his teeth.

They went down to Larchwick at a run, and halfway there the wolves found them.

There were four. White on white, coming out of the trees at the edge of the slope, low and fast, ice-blue eyes. One of them came at the front, snarling, making a great deal of noise, while the other three went round.

"*They go round!*" Jared shouted, getting his shield up. "Tiger said! Watch your back!"

Hana watched her back. She saw one of them coming round Milli's left side, low through the snow, and Milli hadn't seen it. Milli was looking at the one in front, the way everyone always looked at the one in front. And there was no time to shout, and nothing in her hands but a slim staff she'd barely learned to use.

So she did the only thing she'd ever been really, truly good at.

She looked at the wolf, and she leaned toward it, a little, the way she'd lean toward a camera on a bad night when chat was full of people being cruel to each other. And she said, very softly, in the voice that made things feel like their own idea:

"*Oh, not him. He's mine. Look at your friend instead. Look how close he is. Isn't he in your way?*"

The wolf stopped.

It stopped in the snow, a metre from Milli's leg, and turned its head. Not toward her, toward the wolf beside it, the one coming round the other way. And for three long seconds it looked at its packmate with an expression she'd only ever seen on a cat looking at another cat across a sofa. Then it went for its throat.

The two of them went over in the snow in a white tangle of teeth. And the pack, the whole circling, careful, clockwise pack, came apart in confusion. Jared and Milli and Newish had them down before they'd worked out what had happened.

Hana stood in the snow with her staff in her hand and her heart going like a drum.

"Honey," said Milli, staring at her. "What did you *do*?"

"I asked nicely," said Hana.

She didn't say that it had felt exactly like being sure. That for three seconds, the wolf had believed her completely. She wondered, very briefly, standing in the snow, what it would be like to believe something that completely again.

Then the wind came down off the mountain, and they ran the rest of the way.

***

Yan's party was in the trapper's hall by the well, round the big fire in the corner, with their furs steaming. When Hana came in with snow in her hair and her ears flat and her crown crooked, Cursiffe stood up.

He didn't say anything. He was Cursiffe; he rarely did. He just stood up from the bench by the fire, very straight in a heavy fur coat over his indigo jacket, his round wire glasses fogging slightly in the warmth. He looked at her, and at Jared behind her, and at Aleaka coming in last with her tail full of snow. And his sly half-smile went very slightly wider than she'd ever seen it.

"Ah," he said. "You came up."

"We came up," said Jared, and crossed the room and put his arms round him. Cursiffe bore it with great dignity and didn't let go first.

Hana hadn't realised, until that moment, how much she'd missed him. Her group, her circle, five people who'd known each other for years before any of this, and the white room had split them, four and one. Cursiffe had gone with Yan, the last pick, and she'd watched him go and told herself it didn't matter. They'd see each other at the Spoon. They'd see each other every day.

They hadn't, of course. Yan's party had gone up to floor 2 on the sixth day, and she hadn't seen him since.

"You look like a bear," she told him.

"I am a bear," said Cursiffe. "It's the only way to survive. You'll be a bear by tonight. The trapper's very reasonable if you talk to him for long enough."

"How long?"

"About twenty minutes," said Cursiffe, "of very calm, very boring conversation, until he gives up."

***

They bought furs. They sat by the fire. They traded news, the way the parties always did now when they met: who'd cleared what, who'd levelled, who'd learned what, who'd been hurt. Salty told them about the wolves and how they went round. Wendy showed them her ugly black arrows. Cursiffe, when asked, very calmly took the little glass vial of ink from round his neck and showed them how it froze.

And Tiger took something out of his pocket and held it out to Hana on his palm.

"Here," he said. "You should see this. You saw him."

It was an onion skin. Frozen stiff, papery and pale, peeled off in a single perfect piece.

"Found it at a camp," said Tiger. "First day up here. East of the stair, under a big pine. Somebody slept there on their own and peeled wild onions." He grinned a bit, not quite his usual grin. "*Properly*. You know. Like the old lady at the Spoon said. *Top and tail, keep the root on.*"

Hana took it. It weighed nothing. It crackled faintly in her fingers.

She'd heard Mother Crane say it at the long table on the first night. *Your onion boy. Peeled them right, which nobody does.* She'd heard the description that went with it: black hair in his eyes with a bit of blue in it, black waistcoat thing, all pockets. *Sad round the eyes. The kind of sad that makes a joke so you won't notice it.*

And now this. Under a pine, on a mountain, on his own.

*Is it really him?*

She looked at the onion skin for a long time. Then she looked up at Yan, sitting across the fire with Salty's little iron box in his hands and the white snake round his neck. He was watching her. He'd been watching her since she came in, she realised, with that calm half-lidded look of his.

"You saw him too," she said.

"For half a second," said Yan. "Past a snake."

"Are you sure?"

Yan thought about it the way he thought about everything, slowly, all the way down.

"On the second morning," he said, "I told everyone I was less sure. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"I lied." His deep voice was very quiet. "I wasn't less sure. I was scared. They feel the same from the outside. They're not the same." He looked at the onion skin in her hand. "I think you're scared too, Hana."

She didn't say anything.

"Suzu saw him," said Yan. "She says it was him. She says he looked back." He paused. "She says he looked at me like I look at my mother's door when I go out and she's asleep."

Hana's throat did something.

She knew what Yan meant. Everybody in Yan's circle knew about his mother, the way you know things about people you've been in calls with for years. She knew what he meant by that door.

*Like a man seeing someone he'd lost, alive again.*

She closed her hand round the onion skin, very carefully, so it wouldn't break.

***

That night, in the bunk room at the back of the trapper's hall, in a stiff new fur that smelled of somebody else's animal, Hana lay awake beside Milli and didn't go over it frame by frame.

She'd decided not to. Somewhere between the onion skin and Yan's mother's door, she'd decided. She wasn't going to replay three metres in a white room until it wore out. She'd done that for eight nights and it hadn't made her any surer, only more tired.

Instead, she thought about the rest of it. The onion boy. The coat he didn't have and needed. The camp on his own under a pine. The rankings, *Voyager · Lv 10 · Floor 3*, every morning and every night, one name at the top of a list, far ahead of everybody. Nobody behind him, nobody with him. And Gabe at the long table with the whisper window, and *sent*, and nothing.

*Or it's him. And nothing's wrong with him. And he's got a reason.*

"Milli," she said into the dark.

"Mm."

"If you had to do something that would hurt me. A lot. For a long time. To keep me alive. Would you do it?"

Milli was quiet for long enough that she thought he'd gone back to sleep. Then he turned over, and she felt him looking at her in the dark.

"Yes," he said. "Obviously."

"And you'd let me be angry at you."

"You'd be angry at me anyway. You're angry at me now. For something. I don't know what yet." His voice was the calm, rational one, the one he used to reduce frightening things to steps. "But yes. I'd do it. And then later I'd explain. And you'd still be angry. And I'd let you."

Hana lay there for a while.

"Okay," she said.

"Is this about him?"

"Yes."

Milli was quiet again.

"Then he's an idiot," he said finally, "and I like him a bit more than I did."

She laughed, very quietly, into the fur, so as not to wake Aleaka in the next bunk. Then she reached up in the dark and touched the iron rose in her crown, which she'd hung on the bedpost, and the frozen onion skin, which she'd put inside the crown for safekeeping, because it was the safest place she had.

She wasn't sure. She didn't think she was going to be sure for a long time.

But she was done being scared of it.
