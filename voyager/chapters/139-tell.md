The first thing Yan saw was the lights.

Not the faces. Not the sky, or the walls, or the hill. The lights. Tiger opened the door at the top of the four hundred and twenty steps, and the sound came in, and behind the sound, through the gap, before Yan's eyes had even got used to the day, there were *lights*. Dozens. Hundreds. More than he could count, more than he'd ever seen in one place in his life, all of them at once, all of them moving.

He stopped on the step. Wendy walked into his back.

"Yan—"

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. Just—"

He didn't know how to finish it. He went on up the last step, and through the door, after Tiger.

It was a small round room. That was the first strange thing. Not a hillside, or a ledge, or a hump of rock at the edge of something. A *room*. Round, and stone, and so low that Salty, coming through behind him, ducked without being told. There was a well in the middle of it, a wide stone ring with a heavy wooden lid on it and a bucket on a chain hanging over it from a beam, and a smell of wet stone and old rope. Light came in through a doorway on the far side. Bright, ordinary, yellow daylight, with dust floating in it.

And noise. Through that doorway. The noise of a great many people, and hens, and somebody shouting about cheese, and wheels on stones, and a dog.

And then, all at once, as Tiger stepped out through the doorway into the light, none of it.

The noise stopped. Not the way the Salt had stopped it, swallowing it at arm's length. It stopped the way a room stops when somebody walks into it that nobody expected. One voice, and then the next, and then the next, going out like candles down a table, until the only thing left was the hens.

Yan went out after Tiger, into the light.

*Yun*, said Suzu, round his neck, very softly.

"I know," said Yan.

It was a market. A big one. A square of uneven grey stones with a fountain in the middle that didn't work and had a cat asleep in it, and stalls all round under striped awnings: cheese and eels and cabbages and hens in wicker cages and bolts of brown cloth and copper pans and a man with a tray of pies. Tall narrow houses on every side, leaning together over the square with their upper floors jutting out, and washing strung between them, and window boxes. Streets going off between the houses, and every street going *up*.

And people. A great many people. Hundreds. Standing still, now, all of them, with their baskets and their purses and their children, looking at the well-house. At Tiger, standing in front of it in the sun with his hands loose by his blades and his tail very still. At Yan, coming out behind him with a scythe on a pale ash haft and a small white snake round his neck.

And their lights.

He'd been on twelve floors. He'd seen the lights of a great many things. The first villagers on floor 1, small and the same and hardly there. The creatures on six, coals going out red to grey. The river-folk, burning low and long, deep down under the breastbone, like lamps turned down for the night. The tide-folk the same. He'd learned to see a crowd as a field of small steady flames, and to look past them to the faces.

He couldn't look past these.

They were *bright*. That was the first thing. Not low. Not long. Not turned down to last. Bright, and quick, and up near the surface, the way his friends' were. The way his own was, he supposed, though he'd never seen it. And every one of them was different. Not a little different, the way the river-folk's were a little different, a shade warmer here or steadier there. *Different*. A big red one that guttered. A small hard white one like a needle. One that was a mess of three colours fighting. One so wide and soft it was hard to see where it stopped. And they were all doing something. Flaring, now, as the strangers came out of the well-house. Leaning back. Shrinking down. Some of them leaning *towards*.

They looked like people. That was the thing. That was what was making his throat close. They looked exactly like people.

"Yan," said Wendy, behind him, low. "Are you all right?"

"Yes," said Yan. "They're— Wendy, they're like *ours*."

She was quiet a second.

"All of them?"

"All of them. Every single one."

***

They came out of the well-house one at a time, because the door was too low and narrow for two, and the market watched them do it.

Salty, with the greatsword on his back. Wendy, with her bow. Cursiffe, last of Yan's party, blinking in the light with his book already half out of his shirt. Then Hako with the rope. Then the rest. Chris's party, and Xav's, and Hana's, and Deston's. Thea's, without Thea. Hyvred's. Every one of them ducking under the lintel and straightening up and stopping dead, the way Yan had, at the sight of the square.

Yan stood a little to one side of the door and watched the market watch them.

It didn't run. That was what he kept noticing. A crowd that size, faced with thirty-odd armed strangers coming out of a well-house that, judging by the faces, nobody had ever come out of before: on any floor below this one, it would have run. Or screamed. Or done exactly what the people on floor 1 had done, which was nothing at all, and gone on selling cabbages as if the climbers weren't there.

It didn't do any of those. It *chose*. He could see it choosing. Each light on its own.

A woman at the front with a basket of eggs on her arm didn't move an inch. Her light was a small, hard, bright yellow one, and it didn't move either. A man by the pie tray took three quick steps back and his light went up in a flare of orange and stayed there. A young man in a leather apron put his hand on the knife at his belt. A mother pulled two children behind her skirts, and her light went wide, round them both, like a cloak. An old man on a bench by the dry fountain didn't even stand up. He just looked, with his chin on his stick, and his light, which was very low and very old and nearly the colour of the stones, didn't change at all.

And a little girl, somewhere in the crowd, behind somebody's legs, began to sing.

*Thirty up the well,* she sang, high and thin and pleased with herself, *and thirty up the—*

"*Shush*," said somebody. "*Mags*."

She shushed.

Yan turned his head and looked at the well-house behind him.

Just inside the door, low down on the inside of the wall, near the floor, where a child would sit, there were scratches in the stone. He'd seen them coming out and not seen them. Short straight lines, in rows. Very old. Worn so smooth by so many years of hands and shoulders and feet that you'd have had to be looking for them to see them at all.

He didn't mean to count them. He just did. It was a thing he did now. Three hundred and eleven names on a ship, one at a time; you got into the habit.

Thirty.

[ Floor 13 · Tell ]

It came up in front of all of them at once. Everybody read it. Nobody said anything.

Somewhere at the back, Calvin said, "*Tell?*" quite loudly, and Rachael said, "Shut *up*."

The well-house was at the bottom of a hill. Yan saw that now, as the last of them came out and the market began, very slowly, to breathe again. The square was the lowest thing there was. Everything else went up. The streets went up between the leaning houses in steps and slopes and stairs, and above the houses there was a wall, old and grey and patched, with a gate in it, and above the wall more houses, and another wall, and more houses, ring above ring, all the way up a great round green-and-grey hill to the top. And at the very top, white in the sun, a long pale hall with a square bell tower at one end and a flag on it.

On the left, where the hill had been cut away, you could see inside it.

That was the strange thing about the hill. Somebody, a long time ago, had dug into its side, or a river had, and left a cliff. And the cliff wasn't earth. It was walls. Old walls, packed one on top of another, with floors between them and the ends of rooms and the round mouths of drains and here and there a doorway, half buried, opening onto nothing. Layer on layer. Brown, then grey, then a band of black like burning, then a pale one, then brown again. All the way up to the bottom of the houses of the city that was there now, sitting on top of it all like the last coat of something.

"It's a *cake*," said Tiger.

"It's a book," said Cursiffe, behind Yan, very quietly. "Shut sideways."

Higu had come out with the rest of Thea's party, with the rock under her arm.

She'd wiped it at the bottom of the stair. Yan had watched her do it in the hold of the old ship, both sides, while everybody was climbing the ladder. She stood it up against the dry fountain now, next to the cat, who opened one eye at it and closed it again. She took the chalk out of her pocket and drew the line down the middle. Then she looked round for Ice.

He'd come out with Thea, in the middle, the way he'd gone up. He was standing in the sun just outside the well-house door with his helm under his arm, looking up at the hill. At the hall on the top of it. With a face Yan had learned, over two floors now, to recognise. Not frightened. Not sad, exactly. The face of somebody looking at a picture of a house they used to live in.

"Ice," said Higu.

He looked round.

"Go on," she said.

He didn't need to ask what she meant. He came over to the rock, and stood by it, and said it the way he always did now. Plainly, in order, all at once, like reading off a list.

"It's called Tell," he said. "The city. It's built on top of itself. There are older ones underneath. They keep their records in them." He glanced at the cliff. "There was a market. It was market day. It didn't stop. Nobody stopped. A teller came running down the hill with two books under his arm. An old man. Aldo. He was crying. He said, *Climbers. In my life*." Something moved in his face and went. "They wrote us in. Two hands. His, and one of the grey ones. They put us in a house at the bottom of the hill road, the Climbers' House. Everybody was kind. Nobody asked us anything."

Higu wrote. Small, quick capitals, down the left. *TELL. MARKET DIDN'T STOP. ALDO RAN. 2 HANDS. CLIMBERS' HOUSE. KIND.*

"There's an archive," said Ice. "In the hill. They wouldn't let us in." He stopped. "Somebody tried every morning. For a week." And he looked, for a second, not even a second, at Cursiffe.

Then away.

Yan saw Cursiffe see it. Cursiffe didn't move. But his light, which was ink-dark and very still, and had been for as long as Yan had known him, apart from the edges, which were brighter now than they'd been on floor 1, did something it almost never did. It went, very slightly, *in*. Like somebody closing a hand round something.

"There's a court at the top," said Ice. "The Assize. The stair's under it. We were here seven weeks." He looked up at the hill again. "It was autumn. The first time. The leaves were turning on the hill road."

It was not autumn. It was the end of spring. There were window boxes full of something blue on every sill, and the trees Yan could see between the houses on the second ring were the heavy dark green of nearly summer.

*ARCHIVE: NO*, Higu wrote. *STAIR UNDER ASSIZE. 7 WKS. AUTUMN.*

On the right, she didn't write anything yet.

***

The market bell rang while she was writing. Not the big bell up on the hall. A small one, somewhere close, on a pole above the eel stall, rung hard and fast by a boy hanging off the rope with his whole weight. And people moved aside, at the far corner of the square, and the watch came.

There were eight of them. Grey-blue coats, round steel caps, long pikes held upright, and not one of them, Yan thought, had expected to be doing this when they got up that morning. Their lights were all over the place. One was so frightened it was nearly white. They came out into the open stones and stopped about twenty paces off and lowered the pikes, not quite all at the same time, and the man at the front, who had a red sash and a big grey moustache and a light like a banked kitchen fire, opened his mouth to say something.

"Oh, put those *up*, Wat, for pity's sake," said a woman's voice behind him. "They came up the well. They're climbers. You can see they are."

And a woman came out from between the pikes, pushing one of them aside with the flat of her hand as she passed it, like a branch.

She was about forty, and thin, and quick, and out of breath. She had a long brown coat with ink all down the front of it, and ink to both wrists, as if she'd dipped her hands, and a pen behind each ear, and a big leather satchel on a strap across her chest that bumped her hip as she walked. She'd run. You could see she'd run. All the way down whatever street she'd come down, and her light, which was a clear quick blue-white like the flame on a stove, was still going hard from it.

She stopped in front of them, and looked at them. All of them. Her eyes went along the line, the way Wenna's had on the rock at the edge of the sea, counting.

"Well," she said. "There's a lot of you."

"Thirty-six," said Drew.

"I can count, love. I'm a teller." She pulled the satchel round in front of her and started undoing the buckle. "Hawise. Teller Hawise Brand, of the Tellhouse. I'll be writing you in."

"Where's Aldo?" said Ice.

He hadn't meant to say it. Yan saw that. It came out of him the way *It was four days north* had come out of him on the deck of the *Patience*, before he'd heard himself.

Hawise looked up at him from the buckle. Her eyes were very sharp.

"Aldo Pellow? Old Aldo?" She looked at him a second longer. "He's up the Wellgate. In his chair outside his door, where he always is." She got the buckle undone. "He heard the bell. Sent his grandson down to the Tellhouse to say climbers had come up the well. I said, *aren't you coming, Aldo?* And the boy said his gramps said, if climbers wanted Aldo Pellow, climbers knew where he lived." She took a big flat book out of the satchel, bound in brown leather, and a little horn of ink. "How do you know Aldo?"

"I don't," said Ice.

Hawise looked at him for a moment longer. Then she looked down at her own inky wrists, and back at him, and said nothing at all, and opened her book.

On the rock, on the right-hand side, Higu wrote: *ALDO DIDN'T RUN. ("COME TO ME.")* And under it: *HAWISE.*

Ice looked at it for a long moment.

Yan watched his light while he did. Ice's light was the hardest one Yan had ever tried to look at. Not because it was bright, though it was. It was the *one*: the one he'd meant on the deck of the *Patience*, when he'd told Salty his was the brightest thing on the ship apart from one. Because it was *old*. It was the only light Yan had ever seen that was old and bright at the same time. Like the river-folk's, deep, and turned down for the long night, and like his friends', quick and up near the surface, both at once, as if somebody had put two lamps in one glass.

It didn't flinch at *ALDO DIDN'T RUN*. It went, very slowly, a little warmer. As if something about an old man who wouldn't run down a hill for anybody was the best news Ice had heard in a while.

***

The grey one came down the hill road while Hawise was inking her pen.

Yan didn't see where from. One moment the bottom of the street at the top corner of the market was empty, and the next there was somebody walking down it. Not hurrying. A tall person in a long plain grey robe, with the hood down, carrying a big book on the crook of the left arm the way a woman carries a baby. Walking straight down the middle of the street, and the people in the street stepping out of the way without looking at it, the way you step round a puddle.

It came into the market, and the crowd moved for it in the same way, without looking. It came across the stones to the well-house, and stopped beside Hawise. Not close. A little apart. And opened the book on its arm.

It had a face. Yan had been half expecting it not to, after the Steward, but it had. An ordinary face. Pale, and plain, and not old and not young, the sort of face you'd forget while you were still looking at it. It didn't look at any of them. It looked at the page.

And its light—

*Yun*, said Suzu. Sharply.

"I see it," said Yan.

It was small. Grey-white. Very steady. Exactly in the middle of the chest, the way the first villagers' lights had been on floor 1, before anything on any floor had started being real. Not dim, exactly. Just *flat*. Like a picture of a light.

He looked past it, up the hill road. There was another grey robe standing at the corner of the street up there, by a water trough, that hadn't been there before. And another, on the other side of the square, under the eel stall's awning, with a book on its arm. And one at the top of a flight of steps between two houses. He hadn't seen any of them come.

All of their lights were the same. Not like each other. The *same*. As if somebody had lit one small grey candle and put it in all of them.

"Hawise," said Wendy, beside him, quietly. She'd seen him looking. "Who's that?"

"Clerk of the Count," said Hawise, without looking up. She'd dipped her pen. "From the Countinghouse. Up the Wellgate." She said it the way you'd say *the tax man*. "Nothing's told in Tell unless two hands write it the same. That's the law. Mine's one. Theirs is the other, for climbers. The Count's always written the climbers." She looked up then, at the clerk, and something went across her face that Yan's eyes caught in her light before it got to her face: a quick small flinch, blue going thin. "They don't usually come *out*, mind. Not down here. They stay up the Wellgate and they count things. But they've been standing on the corners since the start of spring. Every square in Tell. Just standing. Nobody likes it." She looked back at her page. "And then you lot come up the well."

*Since the start of spring*. Yan thought about the start of spring. Spring had started the night of the Gate. The dark of the moon, and the Steward walking down the slope with its book, and *one record does not match*.

And then on the Salt, at the dawn council, Thea, quite calmly: *I think it's noticed somebody's been reading it. And now it's reading me back.*

He didn't know what it meant. He saw Thea, by the well-house door, not looking at the clerk at all. Very carefully not.

"Name," said Hawise, to Tiger.

They did it on the well's lid. Hawise put her book down flat on the wood, and stood at it with her pen, and each of them came up in turn and said their name, and she wrote it. In a quick, round, brown hand, with a little flick on the end of every word. And beside her the clerk stood with its book open on its arm and wrote in that, with a grey pen, and when they'd both done, Hawise leaned across and looked at the clerk's page and looked at hers, and said, "Told," and the next one came up.

"Tiger."

"Told."

"Suzuyun." Yan stopped. "Yan. It's Yan. Both."

Hawise looked at the clerk's page. "Says both here too. Told."

The clerk didn't look up. Its pen moved in small even strokes, grey on grey. Yan watched it. It didn't hesitate on anything. It didn't seem to listen, even.

"Calvin," said Calvin, when it came to him, loudly, the way he said everything.

Hawise wrote.

Calvin peered over the edge of the clerk's book. He was the only one who'd tried. The clerk didn't seem to mind or notice.

"Hey," said Calvin. "Hey, it wrote mine before I said it."

Nobody said anything.

"It *did*," said Calvin. "I watched it. It was already done. I hadn't even—"

"Calvin," said Deston.

"I'm just *saying*—"

"Told," said Hawise, and looked at Calvin over the top of her book with sharp, tired, not unkind eyes, and Calvin closed his mouth and went back to the others with his fist closed, the way it had been for nearly three weeks now, and didn't open it.

It went on. Thirty-odd names. Yan stopped hearing them after a while and just watched the lights. Hawise's, working away, steady and blue. The clerk's, flat and grey and not moving. And the market's, all round, settling. The pie man's had come back down out of its flare. The egg woman's hadn't moved all along. The young man with the knife had taken his hand off it. They were still there, all of them, a ring of them at a careful distance. But they were watching now, not getting ready.

He saw a woman decide.

She was at the front, near the egg woman. Middle-aged, plump, with a basket of apples. Her light was a round, warm, uncertain orange, and it had been leaning back, and back, the whole time, like somebody leaning away from a fire. And then Tiger, who'd been told first and was standing about by the well-house door with nothing to do and his tail going slowly round his ankle, looked at the apples. Just looked. And the woman's light *stopped*. Yan saw it stop. It hung there. Leaning neither way. For about the time it takes to breathe in.

Then it leaned forward. Just a little. And steadied. And stayed.

And the woman took an apple out of the basket, and held it out at arm's length, in Tiger's direction, without coming any closer.

Tiger looked at it. Then he went and took it, very carefully, and said "thank you" in the smallest voice Yan had ever heard him use, and went back to the door, and held it in both hands and didn't eat it.

Yan found that he'd stopped breathing, to see it better.

*I saw her decide*, he thought. *I saw it happen. Inside her. Before she did it.*

He'd never seen that before. Not on any floor. Not in anybody. Not even in his friends, because with his friends you never saw it, it was always already decided by the time it got to their light, they were too fast. But she'd been slow enough to see. She'd stood in front of a stranger with a tail and a scythe and a sword and thirty-five friends, and decided, on her own, all the way down, to give him an apple.

*Yun*, said Suzu, against his ear.

"I know," he said. "I know."

***

"Althea," said Thea.

Hawise wrote. The clerk wrote.

And then the clerk stopped writing.

Its pen came to the end of a word, and lifted, and stayed lifted, a finger's width off the page. And for the first time since it had walked into the market, it raised its head from the book, and looked at somebody.

It looked at Thea.

It didn't do anything else. It didn't say anything. It just looked, with that plain, forgettable face, and that flat grey light, for a long time. Long enough for the market to notice. Long enough for Hawise to stop with her own pen in the air and glance sideways at it, frowning.

Gabe moved. Yan didn't see him decide to. He was at the back of the group, and then he was beside Thea, a step in front of her and to the left, with Hearthwall on his back, not looking at the clerk. Just *there*. And Yan saw, past Gabe's shoulder, Ice, by the well-house wall, and Ice's light had gone very bright and very still all at once, the way it went at the rail of a ship, and Ice himself hadn't moved at all.

Hadn't moved. Was standing quite still by the wall. With his hands open.

The clerk looked down again, and finished the word.

"Told," said Hawise, slowly. She looked at Thea. Then at the clerk. Then at her own page. "Told," she said again, more briskly, and put a little tick in the margin. "Next."

The last one was Ice.

He came up to the well's lid with his helm under his arm, and Hawise looked up at him over her book, and he said, "Ice."

Hawise wrote it.

The clerk's pen was already moving. Yan watched it. It went along, grey, even, small, and then it went on. And on. Longer than anybody else's had. A whole line, and a second line, and then the clerk lifted the pen off the page, and was still.

Hawise leaned across to look.

"That says Declan," she said.

"That's my name too," said Ice.

"Two names is fine. Lots of people have two names. Your friend there has two names." She was still reading. "But it says—"

She stopped.

Yan saw it in her light first. Her light was quick and blue and clean, and it went, all at once, *thin*. Pulled in, the way a flame goes thin in a draught. Her face didn't change for a second after that. Then it did. She leaned closer to the clerk's page, and read something on it again, and then she looked up, at Ice, and her face had gone the colour of the well's stone.

"Told," said the clerk.

It was the first word it had said. Its voice was quite ordinary. Flat. Not loud.

"What?" said Hawise.

"Already told."

"He's only just—he came up the *well*, he's been here as long as it's taken me to—" She stopped herself. Yan watched her stop herself, all the way down, the way he'd watched the apple woman decide. Her light pulled in hard and steadied. She looked at the clerk's page one more time. Then she put her own pen down, very carefully, across her book. And closed it.

"Then it's two hands that don't agree," she said.

The market heard.

Yan didn't know what it meant to them. He saw that it meant something. A sound went round the square, not a word, just a lot of people breathing in at once, and the ring of lights all round the well-house drew back. Not far. A step. But all of them, together. The egg woman's light, which hadn't moved for anything, moved.

"What does that mean?" said Drew.

"It means it's for the Assize." Hawise was rummaging in her satchel. "It means the Magistrate says which hand's true. At the Reckoning. Not me." She came out with something. A length of thin grey cord. "Hold your wrist out, love."

Ice held his wrist out.

She tied the cord round it, quickly, in a knot Yan didn't know. A neat small hard knot with two short ends. She didn't look at his face while she did it.

"It means you're not told," she said, to the knot. "Not yet. Not till the Magistrate says. It doesn't mean you can't go where you like. You can go where you like. It means people will know." She pulled the knot tight. "Keep it where they can see it. It's worse if they think you're hiding it."

"What did it say?" said Gabe.

He said it quite quietly. He was still standing by Thea.

Hawise looked up at him.

"Disputes aren't said in the street," she said. "That's the law too. He'll hear it at the Assize, the same as everybody." She looked back at Ice. Something in her face that Yan couldn't read, but could read in her light, which had come back from thin and was now doing something odd and wavering, back and forth, like somebody trying to decide which way to walk round a puddle. "Not before."

Ice looked at the cord on his wrist.

"Okay," he said.

That was all. *Okay*. Yan watched his light. It hadn't done anything. That was the thing that Yan would think about, that night, on the roof. Hawise had looked at a page and gone white, and the whole market had stepped back, and somebody had tied a cord round Ice's wrist that meant *not yet*, and Ice's light hadn't done anything at all. It had gone on being what it was. Old, and bright, and very still. Like somebody who'd known that page was there all along.

"Do mine," said Gabe.

He'd held out his wrist. Right over the well's lid. Hawise looked at it.

"It's not a ribbon," she said. "It's the law."

"Then I'll dispute something."

"You can't just—"

"Can I be disputed?" said Calvin, from the back. "I'll *lie*. I'm a great liar."

"You're a *terrible* liar," said about six people at once.

"*Where he goes,*" said Drew, over them, not loud, but in the voice that had been telling thirty-five people where to stand for three hundred and fifty-seven days, and they stopped, "we go. Whatever it is. That's not a dispute. That's just how it is."

Hawise looked at him. Then at all of them. Then at the clerk, who had closed its grey book and was standing a little apart with it on its arm, looking at nothing.

"You're already in," she said. "You came up the well. You're in Tell. Thirty-five of you are told, and one isn't, yet, and that's all it means." She started putting her things back in the satchel. "Now. Where are you going to sleep."

It turned out that was a question.

"The Climbers' House," said Ice, before he could stop himself. Then shut his mouth.

Hawise looked at him again, with that sharp look.

"The Climbers' House is Widow Rennet's now," she said. "Since her husband. It's a lodging. Bottom of the Wellgate." She buckled the satchel. "And you can go and ask her, if you like, but she was in the market just now, and I saw her face when I tied that cord, and I'll tell you for nothing she's not having a corded man under her roof. Nor his friends."

"Then where?" said Hako.

Hawise was quiet for a moment. Her light did the puddle thing again. Back, and forth.

Then it stopped. Leaned. Steadied.

"There's a copy-room," she said. "Over the Tellhouse stables. Long and empty. Nobody's copied anything in it since the new presses went in at the top of the hill. It's got a floor and a roof and a pump in the yard." She swung the satchel back round behind her. "The Remembrancer'll have my hide."

"Why are you helping us?" said Wendy.

She said it the way Wendy said things. Straight, and not unkind, and wanting an answer.

Hawise looked at her.

"Because I wrote you," she said. "You're in my book now. In my hand." She tapped the satchel. "That makes you mine, near enough. Thirty-five of you, anyway."

"And him?" said Wendy.

Hawise glanced at Ice. At the cord on his wrist.

"Him too," she said. "Till the Assize says different. That's what the cord means. Not *no*." She turned to go. "*Not yet*."

***

The copy-room was long and low and smelled of old ink and dry horse, and there were thirty-six of them in it by dusk, and nobody minded.

Yan went up on the roof.

You could get out onto it through a hatch at the end, up a ladder that Salty tested with his whole weight and pronounced "probably fine", and the roof was flat lead with a low parapet round it, and from it you could see the whole of the lower town spread out down the hill below. The market. The well-house, small and round in its corner. The streets. And the plain beyond the bottom wall, green and wide and going on for ever in the evening, with roads across it, and farms, and woods, and far off a line of hills going blue.

Wendy came up after him. She didn't say anything. She sat on the parapet beside him with her bow across her knees, which was how Wendy sat when she was being company.

The sun went down behind the hill. The city came on.

Not the lamps. Though the lamps came on too, window by window, yellow, up and up the hill, ring above ring. Yan didn't really see the lamps.

He saw the lights.

All of them. The whole city. Tens of thousands. More. More than he'd known there were in the whole tower. Every house full of them. Every street. Bright and quick and every one different, red and white and blue and gold and three colours fighting and wide and soft and small and hard, moving behind the walls, moving in the streets, leaning together over tables, flaring up in quarrels, going small and close and quiet in the upper rooms where people were putting children to bed. The whole hill was full of them, from the bottom wall to the pale hall on the top, as thick as stars.

He couldn't speak for a long time.

"Yan," said Wendy, eventually, carefully.

"They're alive," he said. "All of them. They're all *alive*, Wendy."

She didn't say anything. She just moved her hand along the parapet until the edge of it was touching the edge of his.

He looked for the grey ones. He found them. Here and there, among all that colour, small and flat and grey and still. At street corners. On steps. Standing in the dark, not moving, with nobody near them. Like the little marks you'd put in the margin of a page to show where to look.

Then, from somewhere up the hill, in the middle of the city, a bell rang. Not the big one on the hall. A smaller one, lower, three slow strokes. And the city went quiet.

Not all of it. Not like the market. A hush that went out from somewhere up the hill and spread, street by street, the way a wind goes over a field. People in the streets below stopping where they were. A man at a window putting down a jug.

And a voice came down. A long way off. Clear, and slow, and carrying, the way a voice only carries in a city at dusk when everybody has stopped to let it.

It said a name.

Yan didn't catch it. Something like *Jonet*. And then something else, a house, a street, *of the Tanners' Row*. And then a silence. And then another name. And another silence. Three of them.

"What is that?" said Wendy.

"I don't know," said Yan.

But he did. He knew before she asked. Because on the third name, down in the lower town, in a narrow house near the bottom wall, two streets from the market, he saw a light go up.

It had been lying very low and very still in an upstairs room, with three or four others round it, close round it, the way his friends had sat round Corran's bunk. And when the voice on the hill said the name, it rose. Straight up. Off the bed, and through the roof, and up into the dark over the city, quick and sure and clean as a thread, the way the lights had gone up on every floor he'd ever seen, before the river, before the sea. Not hanging. Not sinking. Not waiting for anybody to walk it anywhere. As if it had heard its name, and known the way, and gone.

"They say them," said Yan. "Every one. Every night. Everybody who died that day." His voice had gone quick, the way it went when something clicked. "Wendy, nobody *here* goes without a name. Nobody. They write them all down. They say every one of them out loud."

He wasn't sad. He noticed that, the way he'd noticed it on every floor. Beside him Wendy had gone very still, and her light had gone small and bright the way it did when she was trying not to cry, and all he felt was the click of a thing fitting into place. He thought about Annis, with no boat and a needle-case made of a seal's tooth, who'd have gone down into the dark under the sea for good if one old man hadn't bothered. He thought about Corran, who had bothered, *put me at the end, two Rs*, and whom he'd liked. He thought about three hundred years of tide-folk sinking into the dark because everyone who loved them was too frightened to say a word. Frightened of a *word*.

And here was a city that had simply worked it out. Somebody stood on a step at the end of every day and said them all, and they went.

*That's how*, he thought. *That's the whole trick.*

Wendy didn't say anything. She left her hand where it was.

*Yun*, said Suzu, after a long time. And put her small white head against his cheek. *You've got your planning face on.*

"I haven't got a planning face."

*You've got a planning face*, said Suzu. *I love it. It frightens Wendy.*

The hatch behind them creaked.

It was Cursiffe. He came up the last two rungs of the ladder slowly, the way he did everything, and stood on the lead roof in the dark with his book under his arm and one hand flat against the front of his shirt. Over the pocket. Where he'd kept the stone leaf since floor 5.

He didn't look at the city. He didn't look at the lights, which of course he couldn't see. He didn't look at either of them.

He was looking at the roof. At his own feet.

"Cursiffe?" said Wendy.

"It's hot," said Cursiffe.

He said it quietly, the way he said everything. But his voice was not quite steady.

"It's been warm since the well-house. It got hot when that bell went." He pressed his hand flatter against the pocket. "It's never been this hot. Not at the Prism. Not on the sea."

"Which way?" said Yan.

Cursiffe was quiet for a moment. He turned a little, one way, then the other, the way he'd turned on the deck of the *Patience* with the leaf in his palm, like a man holding a compass.

Then he stopped turning, and looked straight down. At the lead under his feet. At the copy-room under that, and the stables, and the yard, and the hill.

"Down," he said.
