Hyvred had only ever seen backwards.

She'd never said it like that, to anybody. She'd only worked it out on floor 9, in Highcrook, lying on a platform a hundred metres up a tree with her glasses on her chest. Everything she saw, with her glasses off, had already happened. Where light had been. Where somebody had stood, and what they'd felt while they stood there. Where a hit had landed, like chalk. A footprint of warmth on a step where somebody had stopped to count. The whole floor lit up with what it remembered.

Remembered. That was the word. Not *knew*. Not *was going to*. She could look at a field on floor 2 and see where a wolf had died in it, three days before. She could look at a man and see where he'd been hurt, and how long ago, and how often. She could look at a lantern and see that it was two things, a lantern and him, because he'd been in it so long.

But she couldn't look at anything and see what it was going to do.

Nobody could. She'd thought that was just how it was. You saw backwards, if you were lucky, and you guessed forwards like everybody else.

She found out on the four hundred and fifty-sixth morning that she'd been wrong.

***

She went up on the roof at first light, because the barn was too full.

Not of people. Of everything else. Seven hundred people and twelve horses and the whole of a harvest stacked against the walls to the beams, and every one of them leaving a mark on the air, all the time, warm, overlapping, the way people do. And the straw man at the top of it all. She'd been looking at the straw man in pieces for six days, a bit at a time, the way you'd look at the sun. She couldn't look at the whole of it any more. Not first thing. She needed to look at something with nothing in it first.

There was a ladder up the inside of the end wall, by the doors, to a hatch in the roof where the Acre's boys went up in summer to mend the stone slates. She'd found it on the night of harvest-home, when they were throwing hooks at the neck and she'd decided the roof was the best place to throw from, and Annet had said it didn't count.

She went up it with her glasses in her pocket and pushed the hatch open and climbed out onto the ridge.

The cold hit her like water.

She sat astride the ridge in the snow with her knees gripping the stone and her breath going up white and let it hit her. She liked it. She'd liked it since floor 2. On Earth there'd been no snow where she lived, ever, and she hadn't known you could be cold all the way through like this and still be all right. She sat there till her eyes stopped watering. Then she took her glasses out of her pocket and held them in her lap and looked.

***

The whole of the Long Acre was white.

She'd seen it from up here on the first day of the snow, eight days ago, with her glasses on. Just white. White fields, white hedges, white hills on both sides, the river a dark line down the middle of it, and all of it going away south under a pewter sky to wherever Haverley was. Beautiful. Quiet. Like a picture of winter on a card.

With her glasses off it was nothing like that at all.

The snow had its own light. Faint, cold, blue at the edges, the way frost has on a window. Everywhere. Lying over the whole valley like a sheet over furniture in a room nobody lives in. That was the first thing.

And under the snow there was nothing.

Not dark. She knew dark. Dark had things in it. Dark was just light turned down. This wasn't dark. This was where the colour had gone out of the world and not been replaced with anything. The Grey. Under the white sheet of the snow, all the way down the valley, every field it had taken, every hedge and stook and orchard and blackbird's nest, a long shape of nothing the shape of the Long Acre, with the snow lying over the top of it like a skin over a hole.

She'd seen it the first time, on Old Barn Piece, on the morning of the four hundred and sixteenth day, and hadn't been able to look at it for more than a few seconds at a time. Nobody had. You looked at it and your eyes slid off.

She made them stay on it now. She looked along it.

The villages were down there. Little clusters of shapes under the snow along the river, every few miles, empty. She could see the warmth that had been in the houses, fading. A hearth, still faintly red in its stone, a week after somebody last sat by it. A bed with the shape of two people in it, gone pale. A cradle. She looked away from the cradle.

On the east ridge, the barrows. A long line of green mounds under the snow, with their dark doorways. And in each one, a lump of slow warm red, low down, like a coal banked under ash for the night. The barrow-bulls, asleep. They slept in the winter. Cobb had said. They'd go down into the barrows when the frost came and not come up till the gorse flowered. She could see them breathing. Very slowly. Once a minute, a faint swell of red in each mound, and then down.

And far away, at the very bottom of the valley, where the hills came together, the old barn. A small square shape at the edge of what had been Old Barn Piece. And in the end wall of it, a point.

Not much. Tiny, from here. But bright. A thin hard thread of something that wasn't any colour she had a word for, going down into the ground. She'd seen it on every floor. Every stair. It was the one thing the tower made that looked the same everywhere, the colour of a door. And between it and her there were three days of walking across the snow over nothing.

She looked down. At the barn under her.

The Long Barn stood on its rise at the head of the valley, where it had always stood, with the lane going down from its doors, and the Barn Field round it, and the last hedge.

The last hedge was gone.

She looked for a long time to make sure. Eight days ago the Grey had stopped at the bottom of the Barn Field, at the last hedge, because there was nothing left above it to take. She'd seen it. A line of nothing along the bottom of the white field, with real stubble above it. Real ground. Brown and gold under the snow with old roots in it and worms asleep.

There was no real ground any more.

The Grey had come up the Barn Field in the night. Under the snow. All of it. All the way up the rise to the barn, to the walls, to the foot of the walls. She could see it there, under the snow, pressed against the stone all round the outside of the Long Barn like water pressing against the side of a boat. Like the sea round the Stair at Hythe at the top of the tide, with the island just sticking up out of it. The lane was gone. The ground under the doors was gone. The only real ground on the whole of floor 14, except the barrows and the old barn, was the floor of the Long Barn, inside the walls, and the barn itself, standing on nothing in a sea of white.

Maigo had said, on the second day, *it's pushing at the footings, like water at a door*.

It wasn't pushing now. It was leaning.

Hyvred sat on the ridge in the snow with her glasses in her lap and her breath going up and understood that it had done this since the vote. Since the ring at the line, and thirty-five hands, and *down, all of us*. Eight days it had sat at the last hedge, waiting. And then, in one night, this.

*He's in a hurry now*, Hild had said. Hako had told them.

She put her glasses on.

She climbed down the ladder, fast, and went to find Higu.

***

Higu didn't say anything when Hyvred told her.

She listened with the rock in her lap. Then she got up and went to the third door and opened it a crack, and looked out at the lane, which was white and smooth and looked exactly like a lane under snow, and shut it again, and came back and sat down.

She wrote on the rock. In the new column. The third one. *NOW.*

*THE GREY: AT THE WALLS. ALL ROUND.*

And then, smaller, under it:

*SINCE THE VOTE.*

"Okay," said Higu. "Thank you."

That was all. Hyvred loved her for it. She didn't know if Higu knew how much.

***

She looked at the straw man properly at mid-morning, because she couldn't not any more.

She went and sat on the end of the second table, behind the line, beside where Ice was sitting on the stone. He looked up at her when she sat down. He'd been there all night again. She could see it on him. Not tiredness. She could see tiredness, it was grey-yellow and sat on the shoulders. This was something else. She'd tell him later, if he asked. He didn't ask. He said *morning, Hyv*, and she said *morning*, and he looked back up the barn.

She took her glasses off.

She'd been looking at it in pieces. A bit at a time. The frost on the chest. The iron foot. The face. She let it come all at once now, the whole of it, top to bottom, and held on to the edge of the table.

He was gold.

Not the gold of straw. Not the gold of anything she'd seen. He was every gold there was. He was every field in the Long Acre, and every field was its own gold, and she could see them all, bound on, sheaf on sheaf, from his feet to his head. The Long Meadow at Haverley, a pale, clean, early gold like morning. The barley from the Barn Field, darker, like honey in a jar with the light behind it. Ashwell's orchard grass, gold-green. The Lammas top fields, red-gold, the colour of the old woman's hands. A hundred golds. Two hundred. Every one of them the colour of somebody's summer, of a line of mowers going swish-step down a field in the heat, of a band twisted round an armful and tucked, of a stook stood up against the light. All the people who'd cut it. All the hours. All of it bound up together into the shape of a man as tall as a roof.

The whole year. Hild had said. *He's got all of it in him.*

And through all the gold, threads.

Hundreds of them. Fine, warm, every colour. Going into him from everywhere. From the stools and blankets and places on the floor round his feet where somebody had sat with him in the night. From the children's handprints on the frozen straw of his legs, small and bright, where they'd touched him for luck. From Ina's two hands on his neck, a white-blue patch like a handprint on a cold window, twice a day for six days, layered. From the line of shields, where Hako and Salty and Gabe and the rest had stood. From all over the barn. Every village. Every gleaner who'd come up in turns through the long nights, without being asked, and sat. Every one of them had left a thread. And they all went into him, and held.

*The keeping.* She hadn't known it would look like anything. It looked like this.

And under all of it, the winter.

It wasn't white, to her. It was what was under the white. Grey-blue, the tower's kind, a cold wet colour like ink. It was in him. All the way through. It had come up from his feet first, Winter said, and Hyvred could see that it had. It was thickest low down, in the legs. In the iron. It had gone up through him slowly the way ink goes up a strip of blotting paper, and spread, and now it was everywhere. Not instead of the gold. Over it. Through it. The gold was still there, all of it, every field, but it was going under, the way the green had gone under on floor 9. And the threads were going in, all of them, warm, holding, and the grey-blue was coming up from below, and there was more of it coming up than there was warmth going in.

Less out than in.

She looked down at his feet.

At the left foot, the free one. The iron upright, standing on the stone a hand's width out of its socket. And the socket. The square hole in the flagstone where the frame's foot had been set, a long time ago, with lead, by somebody's great-great-grandfather.

At the bottom of the socket, there was nothing.

Grey. Not grey-blue. Grey. The Grey. The same nothing that was under the snow outside, all the way down the valley. A patch of it, at the bottom of the hole in the floor of the Long Barn, the size of her palm. And a thread of it going up out of the hole and into the iron foot and up the leg, into the straw. Into him.

Or out of him. Down. She couldn't tell which way it was going. It was like looking at a river and not being able to see which way the water ran.

Chris had said it. On a bit of string, with a nail in a post. *The Grey can't come under a roof. So it's coming in through him.*

He was the door.

***

"Hyv," said Ice.

She'd been holding on to the table. She hadn't noticed. Her knuckles had gone white on the edge of it, and he'd seen.

"There's a hole," said Hyvred. "Under the left foot. In the socket."

He didn't say anything. He waited.

"The Grey," said Hyvred. "It's in the floor. Just there. In the hole. A palm." She swallowed. "It's going into him. Or out of him. Into the floor. I can't tell which."

She didn't lie to him. She'd said she wouldn't, on the Salt, to Thea. *I won't lie to him. If he asks.* He hadn't asked. She told him anyway.

"How big?" said Ice.

"A palm."

"How fast?"

"I don't know. I've only just seen it." She looked again. "It was in the socket last night. I'd have seen it if it was bigger. I wasn't looking there." She made herself say it. "I'll know by tonight."

"Tell Higu," said Ice.

"I will."

He looked back up the barn. At the iron foot. At the hole in the floor that he couldn't see, a hand's width from it, at the bottom of a square of shadow. His face didn't do anything. But Hyvred had her glasses off, and she saw what his light did, and she put that away to think about later.

She went to tell Higu.

Higu wrote, in *NOW*: *THE SOCKET. THE GREY. A PALM.*

***

It was Gigi who said the thing about the seed.

Hyvred didn't think about the seed. She didn't know about seed. On Earth she'd bought bread in bags. She'd learned to bind on the Acre, badly, and to carry, and to sleep in straw, but nobody had told her about the seed, and she'd never thought to ask what was in the sacks.

There were sacks. At the top of the barn. Behind the straw man, against the end wall, in a long low recess in the stone that looked as if it had been built for them. Twelve of them, maybe fifteen. Fat, tied at the neck, with marks painted on them. A bell for Haverley. An apple for Ashwell. Something like a beehive for Lammas. She'd seen them on the night of harvest-home, when the old women were building the straw man and the men were carrying the sacks in from the carts and stacking them in the recess, carefully, more carefully than they'd carried anything. She'd thought it was flour.

Gigi came down the barn from Maigo with her bucket, at the hour, and stopped by the table, and looked at Hyvred's face.

"What," said Gigi.

"The socket," said Hyvred. "The Grey's in it."

Gigi looked up the barn. At Maigo, sitting rooted in the frost at the straw man's feet, by the right foot, with his eyes shut and his gold ring round him gone thin and his leaves yellow at the edges. At the left foot, a pace and a half from him, and the hole.

She went white.

"He's right next to it."

"His root's in the gap by the right foot. Not the left. It's not near his root." Hyvred looked, to make sure. "Not yet."

Gigi didn't say anything. She put the bucket down. She looked past Maigo, past the feet, at the end wall, at the recess.

"Oh," said Gigi. "Oh, the *seed*."

Hyvred looked at her.

"The sacks," said Gigi. "At the back. That's not flour, Hyv. That's the seed corn. That's next year." She was speaking fast, the way she did when it was plants. "Every village keeps back the best of the harvest. The fattest ears. They don't eat it. They don't eat it even if they're starving. You keep it through the winter and you plant it in the spring and that's the next harvest. That's all there is. If you lose your seed you haven't got a next year." She looked at the recess. "Annet told me. On the first week. They keep it at his back. All winter. Where he can see the doors and nobody can get at it without going past him." She put her hand over her mouth. "Hyv. Look at it. Look at it properly. Tell me it's all right."

So Hyvred looked.

She looked past the straw man, past the gold and the grey-blue and the threads, past the iron feet and the hole, to the recess in the end wall and the fat tied sacks in it with their painted marks.

And saw something she had never seen.

***

The sacks were glowing.

Not the way things glowed when people had been near them. There was some of that. Warm handprints on the necks where somebody had tied them, on the painted marks where somebody had painted them. That was backwards. That was ordinary.

This wasn't that.

Inside the sacks, every grain had a light in it. A tiny one. Gold. Folded up small, the way a leaf is folded up small in a bud. And when she looked at one grain, just one, she could see what the light in it was.

It was a field.

Not a field that had been. A field that hadn't been yet. Small, folded, faint as breath on a window, but there. Green, first. A haze of green on brown earth, in a thin spring sun. Then taller. Then gold. A line of mowers she'd never seen, in a summer that hadn't happened, going swish-step down it. A band twisted round an armful. A stook. All of it. Folded up in one grain, waiting.

Next year.

She was seeing next year.

She'd never seen forward. Not once. Not on any floor. Everything she saw had already happened, and she'd thought that was the rule, that the world only remembered. And it turned out it didn't. It turned out the world was also *going to*. It just kept it somewhere small. In a seed, at the back of a barn, in a sack with a bell painted on it.

Hyvred screamed.

In perfect silence, with her mouth wide open and her hands flat on the table and her eyes streaming. Gigi grabbed her arm. Ice was on his feet; she didn't see him get up. Bee came running down the barn from somewhere with charcoal on her fingers. Hyvred couldn't stop. She sat on the end of the table and screamed at the seed corn of the Long Acre without making a sound, and cried, and couldn't explain, and didn't want to.

"Hyv," said Bee. "Hyv. *Hyv*. What."

"It's *next year*," said Hyvred. "It's in there. I can *see* it. It's a *field*. Every one. Bee. I've never seen *forward*."

Bee looked at her. Then at the sacks. Then back at her. And then she sat down on the table next to her, and put her arm round her, and held on.

"Okay," said Bee. "Okay. That's good. That's a good one."

"It's the best one," said Hyvred.

And then she looked at the straw man again, with her face wet, because she'd just understood something, and she had to see if she was right.

She was right.

It was in him too.

Every sheaf. Every ear on every sheaf of him, all the way up. Each one with its own tiny folded field in it. Not as bright as the seed. Fainter. But there. The whole of next year, a hundred golds of it, folded up in the straw of a man as tall as a roof.

*In the spring each village ploughs a piece of him into the first furrow of its first field*, Hild had said. *So it goes back in.*

So the year goes back in.

She looked lower. At his legs. Where the grey-blue was thickest, where it had come up from the iron first.

In his legs, the little folded fields were going out.

Not all at once. One at a time. A grain here. A grain there. She watched one. A tiny gold spring in a tiny folded field, and the grey-blue round it, and the gold going thin, and thinner, and then not gold at all. Not anything. A grain of straw with nothing in it.

She didn't scream at that.

***

Annet came when Gigi fetched her.

She stood at the line with her stick, beside Higu, and listened to Hyvred tell it. Hyvred didn't tell it well. She'd never been able to say the things she saw. But she said *there's next year in the sacks*, and *there's next year in him*, and *it's going out of him from the feet up*, and *the Grey's in the socket, a palm, behind him, a pace from the sacks*. And Annet's face went still and stayed still all the way through.

"You can see that," said Annet.

"Yes."

"Next year."

"Yes."

Annet looked up the barn for a long time. At the straw man. At the recess. At the sacks with the bell and the apple and the beehive on them.

"We've never moved the seed," said Annet. "Never. Not in a thousand years. You keep it at his back. That's the keeping. He keeps the winter, and the seed's behind him, and nothing gets to it that hasn't gone past him first." She closed her hand on the stick. "That's what he's for."

"Can we move it?" said Higu. "Now. Out of the recess. To the other end. Away from the hole."

"You'd have to go past his feet," said Annet. "Both of them. There's no other way in." She was looking at the left foot. "And past the hole."

Nobody said anything.

"I'll do it," said Salty, from the shields. "I'll carry them. Two at a time. It's fifteen sacks, it's eight goes—"

"No," said Higu.

"I'm *strong*—"

"I know you're strong. No." Higu was looking at the iron foot. At the hole. "Nobody goes past his feet. Not with the Grey in the floor. We don't know what it does if you step on it from inside. Annet put her leg in it to the knee from outside and got grey dust. We don't know what it does in here. Under a roof." She shook her head. "Not yet. Not till we know."

"Hild's past his feet," said Hako, quietly.

They all looked. The old woman on her stool, right at the bottom of the straw man, a pace from the left foot. She hadn't moved in six days except to sleep, and she slept there.

"Hild," said Higu. "Will you come back? Behind the line. Please."

Hild didn't look round.

"No," said Hild.

Higu shut her eyes for a second. Then she opened them, and picked up the chalk, and wrote on the rock, in *NOW*, very small:

*THE SEED. BEHIND HIM. NEXT YEAR.*

And under it, smaller still:

*NOT JUST US.*

***

They brought Maigo out at dusk.

The hole was the size of a plate by then. Hyvred looked every hour. A palm at mid-morning. Two palms by noon. By the time the high windows were going dark it was a plate, at the bottom of the socket and spilling over the edge of it, onto the flagstone round it, a soft grey ring on the stone in a fan of white cracks. Not fast. But not stopping.

It was a hand's width from the right foot's flagstone. From the gap where Maigo's root went down.

"Out," said Higu. "Maigo. Now."

Maigo opened his eyes.

He'd been rooted for six days. Hyvred could see it on him. His colour had always been green, faint, steady, with the bright points of his leaves up on his antler. It was thin now. Like a lamp with the wick turned right down. His gold ring round him, the light he kept round himself when he was rooted, was a thread. And his leaves weren't bright points. They were dull yellow, curling at the edges.

"Not yet," said Maigo.

"*Maigo.*"

"If I come out," said Maigo, "there's nothing under him. On this side. I'm holding the floor under his right foot. I can feel it. It's real. It's still real. If I come out, it's just a floor."

"It *is* just a floor," said Gigi. She was kneeling beside him. She'd been kneeling beside him since noon. "It's a floor, Maigo. It's not you."

"It's a bit me."

"You're not *planted*."

"I'm a bit planted," said Maigo. He sounded very tired. "After six days. You'd be surprised."

Gigi looked at him. And Hyvred saw her decide something. She saw it in her colour, brown-gold, thorn-coloured, going suddenly bright, the way it went when she reached for a seed under the salt.

"You weren't," said Gigi. "Planted. Nobody planted you. You fell." She put both her hands on his root-foot, where it went down into the gap between the stones. "What was never planted, you can pull."

"Gigi—"

"I'm *pulling you*," said Gigi, "you stupid deer."

And she pulled.

Not hard. Hyvred had seen her pull a seed up out of thirty rings of salt on floor 12 with a nosebleed and her whole face gone grey. This wasn't like that. It was gentle. It was the way you'd take a hand off a rail when somebody's been holding it too long and can't let go. Gigi's warmth went down into the gap, round Maigo's root, and held it, and lifted.

He came out with a small, soft sound, like a cork.

He fell sideways into her lap. She caught him. He lay there with his eyes shut and his root-foot pale and wet and curled up like a fist, and his leaves shaking.

"Ow," said Maigo.

"I know."

"You called me a deer."

"You *are* a deer."

"I'm a *person*," said Maigo, with his eyes shut, "with *antlers*," and Gigi laughed and cried at the same time and held on to him, and Kei was there with his fingers on Maigo's wrist and Dose round his neck and his mouth in a line, measuring.

Behind them, the right foot's flagstone was just a flagstone. Hyvred looked. It was real, still. Stone and lead and the iron going down into it. But it was just stone now. Nobody holding it.

Two bolts down at the right foot. Two up.

At about the time the bells would have gone, the third one came up.

***

She took the late watch at the line with Thea.

They'd done it before. On the Salt, on the sea, on the second night of the snow, when she and Thea had come to take the third door at midnight from Winter and Ina. It was Higu's rota. Two by two, nobody watching alone. She didn't think Higu put them together on purpose. She thought Higu probably did.

The barn was dark. The lamps were down. Seven hundred people asleep in the straw behind them, the horses quiet for once, the wind on the roof. In front of them the line, and the shields, Gabe and Azac, their backs and their rims touching. Past them, the fifteen paces. Hild on her stool, asleep sitting up with her chin on her chest. The straw man.

And Ice, on the line. Where he'd been for three days. Awake.

Thea sat down on the end of the second table, beside Hyvred, with the lantern in her lap. She didn't go to the top of the barn. She'd stopped going, since the line. She sat as close to it as the line let her, which was here, two metres behind Ice and a little to the left.

They didn't say anything for a long time.

"What does he look like?" said Thea.

Very quietly. Not looking at Hyvred. Looking at the back of Ice's head.

Hyvred had known she'd ask. Thea had asked her before. On floor 6, the first time. On the Salt. She couldn't see what Hyvred saw, and she never pretended she could, and every few floors she asked, the way you'd ask somebody who'd been in a room you couldn't go into what it was like in there.

"Amber," said Hyvred. "Same as always. Same as the lantern."

"I know that bit."

"I know you do."

She looked at him. She let her eyes go to him and settle.

She'd seen him on every floor. She'd seen him in the bowl on 6 go enormous, standing over Thea, like an armful of dry wood going up, and then small, so small she'd nearly lost him, when he stood up and walked away. She'd seen him on the rope on floor 9, a light on the end of a rope, going up. She'd seen him on the hill at Tell, on Gabe's shield, grey to the chest, and the gold going up through the shield into all their hands. She'd written on a slate in a hall on floor 13 that he was the brightest thing she had ever seen, and she saw everything, and it was true, and she'd written it in capitals so nobody could argue.

He wasn't enormous now. He wasn't small.

"He's steady," said Hyvred. "He's never steady. He goes up and down. All the time. Like a fire somebody keeps opening the door on." She tried to find it. "He's not doing that. He's like a fire somebody's decided to keep in. All night. On purpose. They've banked it up and shut the door on it and they're going to sit there and keep it in till morning, whatever happens." She stopped. "That's what he looks like."

Thea didn't say anything.

"He's decided something," said Hyvred.

"Yes," said Thea.

"You know what."

"No." Thea looked down at the lantern. The amber in it lay flat against the glass, toward him. "Yes. I know what kind."

Hyvred looked at her.

Thea's own colour was what it always was. Not a colour. Lines. Fine bright lines of light all over her, moving, the way text moves in a window when you scroll. *You look like a page*, Hyvred had told her once, on floor 6, and Thea had said it was the nicest thing anybody had ever said to her. She looked like a page now. But the lines weren't scrolling the way they usually did, all over the place, every way at once. They were all going one way. Slowly. Steadily. Like somebody writing one long sentence, a word at a time, and not stopping.

"Whatever it is," said Thea, "I'm not going to step round him."

She said it to the lantern.

"Hyv. Whatever he does. When it comes. I'm not going to step round him." Her voice was very level. "I'm going to stay where I am. And let him. And be there when he gets back." She was quiet for a moment. "That's what I'm deciding. While he decides his."

Hyvred didn't know what to say. She didn't know what it meant. She knew it meant everything.

"Don't tell him I said that," said Thea.

"I won't lie to him," said Hyvred. "If he asks."

"I know." Thea almost smiled. "He won't ask. He knows. He just doesn't know he knows."

***

At about three Hyvred saw the thread.

She'd been watching the hole. The grey plate at the left foot, spreading over the rim of the socket onto the floor, slowly, in a soft ring, like damp coming up through a wall. She'd been watching it for so long that her eyes had gone tired, and she let them go, the way you let your eyes go when you've been reading too long, and they went where they wanted.

Up. Up the left leg, the grey-blue in the iron and the straw. Up past the thighs, the body, the frost on the chest. To the neck. The knot at his heart.

She'd been there, at harvest-home. When Milli cut it, at the end of the Barn Field, with forty hooks in the stubble round it, and shouted *I HAVE IT*. When Ezra climbed the frame and tied it into the straw man's chest where a heart would go. And when Hana and Milli stood in front of him with a sheaf held up between Jared and Aleaka, and one band, and twisted it each from their own end, slowly, so slowly, and tied it together in one knot, and it held. The old women had put that sheaf in too, after, when the dancing was over. Up the frame, into his chest, bound, right against the neck. Hyvred had watched them do it. *That's where the wedding sheaves go*, Hild had said. *So the keeping's got something to hold.*

It was the brightest thing in him.

Even now. Even with the frost on it. Even with the grey-blue all the way up through the rest of him. The knot at his heart was gold. The plain clean gold of a band tied slow round a sheaf, by two people, in a barn full of people who meant it. Ina's warmth on it, white-blue, twice a day. Gaffer Tobin's light had gone up from it. Yan had seen it. *Because something started.*

And out of it, there was a thread.

She hadn't seen it before. She didn't know if it had been there before. It was very thin. Thinner than any of the hundreds of threads going *in* to him from the gleaners and the children and the shields. And it wasn't going in.

It was going out.

Out of the knot at his heart, down through the dark, across the fifteen paces of frost, past Hild asleep on her stool, between Gabe's shield and Azac's, where their rims touched, through the gap you couldn't see. Over the chalk line.

To Ice.

Not grey. Not grey-blue. Not the tower's kind at all. Hyvred looked so hard her eyes hurt. It was gold. The Acre's gold. The same gold as the knot. The same as the threads from all the people who'd sat with him in the night. It went out of the straw man's heart and across the barn and into the chest of the man sitting cross-legged on the line, and it was so thin it was hardly there.

But it was there.

"Thea," said Hyvred.

Thea looked at her.

"There's a thread," said Hyvred. "From him. From the knot. The band. To Ice." She couldn't stop looking at it. "It's not the grey. The grey's looking at him like—" She didn't know. Like somebody who hurt it. Hild had said. *The way you'd look at somebody who'd hurt you, a long time ago, somewhere you can't remember.* "That's still there. In the rest of him. But this isn't that. This is gold. It's coming *out*. From the heart." She swallowed. "It's like the threads going in. From everybody who sat with him. Except the other way."

"What's it doing?" said Thea.

"I don't know." Hyvred looked. She looked the way she'd looked at the seed, and the folded fields. "It's—" She knew this one. She'd seen it on the Salt every night for a month. "It's like Gigi's plants. On her road. When they want water. There's a thread from each one back to her, and it goes like this. Thin. Pulling. Not hard." She stopped. "It's asking him."

"Asking him what?"

Hyvred didn't answer.

She didn't need to. She could see Thea already knew. She could see it in the lines of her, still going one way, a word at a time.

*When the straw man walks, there's winter walking in him*, Ezra had sung, at the council, in an old cracked voice, a verse nobody sang except by the fire in a hard winter. *Keep him, and he'll keep you.*

The grey wanted him because he'd burned it, once. Somewhere neither of them could remember.

The gold wanted him to keep it.

On the line, Ice lifted his head.

Hyvred saw him do it. She didn't know if he'd felt it. She didn't know if he could feel a thread that thin. But he lifted his head, slowly, and looked up the barn, past the shields, past Hild, all the way up the white body to the face at the top, and the face was looking down at him, and they looked at each other. Just looked. The two of them. The straw man and the man who'd burned it, the first time, in a lane, in the snow, two days of fire.

His light didn't go up. It didn't go down. It stayed exactly where it was. Banked. Shut in. Kept.

At the right foot, the fourth bolt made a sound.

Not *tik*. Lower. Longer. A deep strained groan of iron in stone, the sound a ship makes when the wind gets into her all at once. All four bolts at the right foot. Three up. And the last one, the only one still down, holding the whole of the straw man to the floor of the Long Barn by one foot and one finger of iron, singing with it.

Gabe's shoulders went up. Azac's did. Hild woke, and lifted her chin off her chest, and didn't get up.

The grey at the left foot had reached the edge of the flagstone. It was at the gap between that stone and the next. Hyvred watched it find the gap, and go into it, the way water finds a crack.

"It's tonight," said Hyvred. "Or tomorrow."

Thea didn't say anything.

She got up off the table. She didn't go past the line. She didn't go round Ice. She went and sat down next to him, on the cold stone, on the near side of the chalk, on his left, close enough that their shoulders touched. With the lantern in her lap.

He didn't look round. He put his hand out, without looking, the way Gabe did, and she took it.

Hyvred watched them.

Two lights, side by side on a chalk line on a stone floor in a barn on an island of real ground in a sea of nothing. The lantern, amber, his. And him, amber, banked, kept. And her, a page, every line on it going one way. And from the heart of the straw man at the top of the barn, across all of it, a thread of gold so thin it was hardly there, asking.

She thought about putting her glasses on.

She didn't. She wanted to see. Whatever was going to happen, she wanted to see all of it, every bit, backwards and forwards. She wanted to be able to say, after, that she'd seen it. That she'd been there, and looked, and not looked away.

So she sat on the end of the table in the dark with her glasses in her lap and her eyes wide open, and watched him keep the fire in till morning.
