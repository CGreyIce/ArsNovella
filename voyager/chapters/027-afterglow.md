Hyvred still cried at sunsets, and she'd decided not to be embarrassed about it.

They weren't even real sunsets. She knew that. Everybody knew that by now; it had gone round the Copper Spoon in the first week, the way things did. The sun went along a rail. The clouds all moved at the same speed. The sky was a ceiling painted so well it had weather. And every evening, when the sun slid off the western end of its rail and the whole painted sky went orange and pink and gold behind the hills, Hyvred stopped whatever she was doing and watched it, the *whole* of it, edge to edge. And her eyes filled up.

On Earth she'd had a circle.

That was the only way she'd ever been able to explain it. Everybody else saw a whole world, and she saw a small bright circle in the middle of a lot of dark, and she moved the circle around, stitching things together, like reading a map through a straw. She'd posted a clip once, years ago, on her stream, of what it looked like from inside. A camera view with everything black except a little round hole in the middle. People had been kind about it. One person in chat had been very funny about it, without realising. He'd said it looked like playing a game with the lights off and a torch, and asked what game it was. Then somebody had told him, and he'd gone completely silent. Then he'd apologised for about ten minutes in capitals, and she'd laughed so hard she'd had to stop the stream.

That had been Ice. She'd known him four months. She'd thought he was the coolest person she'd ever met.

Now she had the whole sky, every evening, edge to edge. And she cried at it, and Bee stood next to her and watched for both of them, the way she had on the first morning in the white room. And Hyvred didn't care who saw.

***

She had more than the sky, too. She'd started noticing that on the third day.

It wasn't just that she could see everything now. She could see things nobody else could. Small things, at first. When Kei healed somebody, she could see the healing go in: a soft green glow, like light through leaves, sinking into the hurt place and spreading. When Gigi called up roots out of the ground, she could see them coming before they broke the surface, a faint brown-gold stir under the soil. When the Woolbacks came over a wall, she could see their little dull red sparks before she could see the Woolbacks.

And she could see where things had *been*.

That was the strange one. That was the one she hadn't told anybody about yet, not even Bee. When Kei healed Hako's arm on the fourth morning and then walked away, there was a faint green smudge left in the air where his hands had been. It was like the warm spot left on a chair, and it stayed for most of an hour, getting fainter. When Gigi's roots went back into the ground, they left a brown-gold shimmer on the earth for a day.

Light remembered where it had been. And she could see it remembering.

There'd been one, at the Copper Spoon, that she couldn't explain.

It was on the fifth morning, at breakfast. She'd been sitting at the long table next to Bee, eating Mother Crane's porridge and looking at everything the way she looked at everything now, greedily, all at once. And at the far end of the table, by the kitchen, where Mother Crane kept the big sack of onions on the end of the bench, there'd been a smudge.

Not green, not brown-gold. Something else. Warm, amber, faint as a breath on glass. On the bench, just about where a person would sit if they were peeling onions with their back to the room.

She'd stared at it for so long that Mother Crane had come down the table and asked if there was something wrong with her porridge.

"No," Hyvred had said. "Sorry. There's—who sits there? At the end?"

Mother Crane had looked at the end of the bench. Then at her.

"Nobody sits there," she'd said. "That's where I keep the onions." She'd considered. "Except the onion boy. First morning. Before you lot came. He sat there and peeled half a sack." She'd sniffed. "Why?"

"No reason," Hyvred had said.

She'd looked at the smudge every morning after that. It got fainter every day. By the ninth morning it was gone, and she'd felt, absurdly, as if somebody had left the room without saying goodbye.

She hadn't told anyone. She hadn't known what to tell them. *I can see where a stranger sat to peel some onions a week ago* wasn't a sentence that made sense, even here. But she'd remembered the colour. Warm. Amber. Like a lamp in a window on a winter night. Nobody else's light had ever looked like that.

Her left eye did most of it. The one with the little red mark in it, the reticle, a fine crosshair set right in the golden iris. When she really looked at something, the mark locked on. It held, and steadied, and everything inside its circle went sharp and bright and *close*, like looking through a scope. On Earth she'd seen the world through a torch. Here she saw it through a scope. She thought, sometimes, that Ice would have found that very funny, and then very cool, in that order.

***

The Fold was on the eleventh day.

They did it well. Better than well. They were the fifth party, and by then there was a guide in World: Deston's four lines, Calvin's *under the jaw*, and Thea's long careful post about the strap having its own health and the scream making you *Afraid*. It said Afraid lasted as long as you held onto it. And there was a short, odd line from Chi, which said only: *talk to each other slowly when it screams. it helps.*

Hyvred cut the strap herself.

She hadn't planned to. Hako was holding the front with his spear and his shield, grinning the whole time like a man on a fairground ride. Gigi had a wall of thorns up round the far stones so the Woolbacks couldn't get in. Bee was drawing something in the air with her finger, a net, glowing faintly honey-yellow, that held for three seconds and then frayed. And Kei was keeping everybody standing. And Hyvred, on a rock above the Fold with her bow, looked at the strap.

The reticle locked on.

The strap was black leather, a hand wide, already frayed a little from the stuns where Hako had got a spear-thrust in. Inside the reticle's circle it was so sharp she could see the individual cracks in the leather, where it had gone stiff and old. She could see exactly where it was thinnest. A little spot, the size of a fingernail, right under the ram's left ear.

She put an arrow through it.

Then another, through the same hole. Then another. The fourth one went in and the strap came apart, and the bell fell off, and everybody in the Fold turned round and stared up at her on her rock.

"*FOUR ARROWS!*" shouted Hako, delighted. "*SAME HOLE!*"

"I know," said Hyvred, who was as surprised as anybody. "I know, I *know*, I was there—"

The Bellwether screamed. She felt the cold go through her, the *Afraid*, and she thought of Chi's line in World. *Talk to each other slowly.* So she did. She sat on her rock with her whole body locked up and said, very slowly, out loud, to nobody, "It's okay. It's just loud." Down below, Kei said it too, in his calm voice. Then Gigi, through her teeth. And the cold went out of them like water out of a sink.

Then the ram came round on its curve, and Hako met it with his shield and his whole cheerful weight, and that was that.

***

It was afterwards, in the quiet, that she saw it.

They were sitting in a row against the stones, the five of them, the way every party did after the Fold. Hako was lying flat on his back laughing at nothing. Bee was drawing a very small, very rude picture of the Bellwether in the dirt with a stick. Gigi was picking thorns out of her own sleeves. Kei sat with his eyes closed and the little green snake round his wrist, tasting the air.

Hyvred was looking at the split stone.

It had a lot of damage on it now. Every party had broken a bit more off it. There was a great split up the middle from root to crown, a slab knocked off the top, a dozen fresh scrapes and gouges. But that wasn't what she was looking at.

On the grass in front of it, about six metres out, there was a light.

It was faint. So faint that if she hadn't spent nine days learning what faint things looked like, she'd never have seen it. A small warm patch, like the warm spot on a chair. But it wasn't green, like Kei's healing, or brown-gold, like Gigi's roots, or the soft white of Deston's *Bless* or the clean blue of Rachael's heal. She'd seen all of those, on the people who'd come through the Fold before her. She'd seen their leftovers on the stones every time she came up here to look.

This one was gold. Not bright gold. *Warm* gold, amber, the colour of a lamp in a window on a winter night.

She'd seen it before. Once. On the end of a bench, by a sack of onions.

And it was old. Days old. Older than anything else in the Fold. Somehow it was still there.

She got up without saying anything and walked over to it.

The reticle locked on, and the warm patch went sharp. It wasn't a patch. It was several, close together, overlapping: the warm spot where somebody had stood, and stood, and stood. And in the middle of them, very faint, the shape of something like a hand. Pressed flat. As if somebody had stood here, a long time ago, and put their hand on their own chest, over and over.

She turned round slowly.

There was more. Now that she knew what she was looking for, she could see it. Little patches of that same warm amber all over the Fold: here by the cracked stone, there in the middle where the cropped grass was scarred, and one big one right up against the stone itself, the first one, the one with the split. A trail of them, leading from one to the next, the way footprints lead.

And then a line of them going up the slope at the back of the Fold, to the door in the hill.

"Hyv," said Bee, behind her. "What are you looking at?"

Hyvred didn't answer for a moment.

"Bee," she said. "Has anybody got a light like a lamp? Any of the parties? Anybody's magic we've seen, is it this colour?"

Bee came over and looked at the grass where Hyvred was looking, and saw grass.

"I can't see it," said Bee, bluntly. "You know I can't see it. What colour is it?"

"Like a lamp," said Hyvred.

***

She followed it to the door in the hill.

The others came after her, because she didn't stop to explain, and because they were used to her by now. The ascension room was round and dry, with a stone bench round the wall and a fire-pit in the middle, full of the old ashes of five parties' fires. And on the stone bench, by the wall, near the fire, there was the biggest patch of amber light she'd seen yet.

Somebody had slept there. A long time ago, on the first night, before anyone. Somebody with a lamp inside them had sat on that bench with their back against the wall and slept. The warm spot was still there, faint as breath, a person-shaped glow on the old grey stone.

And beside it, on the bench, a smaller one. Round. Cat-sized. Curled up, like an animal sleeping.

Hyvred looked at the small one for a long time.

"Kei," she said.

Kei came and stood beside her. He couldn't see it either; she knew that. But he was the calmest person she knew, and the most analytical, and he listened properly when you told him things.

"There's a light here," she said. "Where somebody slept. A long time ago, the first night. It's gold, like a lamp. Nobody else's light is this colour." She swallowed. "And there's a little one next to it. Like a cat, curled up. Or a—" she didn't know what. "Like something small, that he had with him."

"He," said Kei quietly.

"He." She heard herself say it. "It's him. It's Voyager. Ice. It's *him*. He sat here the first night. I'm looking at where he sat."

Kei looked at the empty bench for a while.

"Can you see where he went?" he said.

Hyvred turned round and looked at the stair at the back of the room.

There it was. Up the stair, one step at a time, faint as anything. A trail of small warm gold marks, one on every step. Like somebody climbing, slowly, and touching each step with something warm as he went.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice did something strange. "Yeah. I can see where he went."

***

They didn't climb that night. They'd planned to. Hako was all for it. But Gigi said, in her blunt, flat voice, that if they went up to floor 2 in the dark after a boss fight, they'd freeze to death on the stairs, and it'd be stupid. And Gigi was usually right about things being stupid. So they slept in the ascension room, all five of them, round the fire-pit. Hyvred lay awake on the floor, looking at the warm gold person-shape on the bench above her head.

It was very faint. In a few more days, she thought, it would be gone.

She'd met Ice in person exactly never. She'd known him four months, in calls and in chat, through Maigo. She'd thought he was cool. She'd thought he was funny. She'd been a bit dazzled by him, to be honest, the way you get dazzled by people who seem completely at home in a world you're still learning to see. And then he'd apologised for ten minutes in capitals because he'd made a joke about her eyes without knowing. She'd decided that was the thing about him she liked best. That he'd been so horrified. That it had mattered to him that much.

And now here was where he'd slept. On a stone bench, on his own, on the first night, with something small curled up beside him. And then he'd got up and climbed the stair, alone, touching each step with light.

She didn't cry. She was surprised. She'd cried at every sunset for eleven days. But she lay on the floor of the ascension room and looked at the faint gold shape of where he'd been, and she didn't cry at all. She just felt very still inside, the way you feel when you've found something you didn't know you were looking for.

***

In the morning they climbed the stair. Hyvred went first, because she could see the steps.

Every one of them had a little warm mark on it. Faint, fading, but there. A hundred and twelve of them.

"Are you counting?" said Bee, behind her, somewhere around fifty.

"No," said Hyvred. "He was."

Bee didn't say anything for a few steps.

"How do you know?"

"Because they're all the same," said Hyvred. "Every step. Same place, same size, same warm. Like he put his foot down exactly the same way every time, on purpose. Like he was saying a number on each one." She didn't know how she knew that. She just did, the way she knew which way a wolf would go before it went. "Nobody climbs stairs that carefully unless they're counting."

"Or unless they're very tired," said Bee.

Hyvred thought about that. She thought about the bench at the bottom, and the small curled-up glow beside the big one.

"Both," she said. "I think both."

At the top there was a door, and cold coming round its edges, and she opened it, and the wind hit her like a wall, and the world beyond was white. Pines and snow and a frozen river and a town in a stockade, far below. Hana's party had put it in World days ago: *bring a coat. bring two coats.* Hyvred had brought one, from the tailor in Hearthstead, and she was immediately furious that it wasn't two.

But she didn't look at the view for long.

She looked down. At the snow at her feet, in front of the door. And there, through the reticle, locked on, sharp and bright and close, there it was.

A line of warm gold footprints in the snow.

They were very faint. Snowed over, days old, almost gone. But they were there. They came out of the door she was standing in and turned left, east, along the edge of the trees. They went away along the treeline in a long, steady, unhurried line, as if whoever had made them had known exactly where he was going and had never once stopped to look round.

She could see them going all the way to a great dark pine, far along the edge of the wood. And past it. And up.

"Oh," said Hyvred.

Bee came out of the door behind her, and stopped, and looked at Hyvred's face.

"You can see him," said Bee.

"I can see where he *went*," said Hyvred. "Bee. I can see the whole way. All of it."
