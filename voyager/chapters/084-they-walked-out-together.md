Merle gave them bread for the road. Of course she did.

Thirty-six rosemary loaves, wrapped in paper, in two big baskets on the step of the blue door on Lantern Street, in the evening. She'd baked them all afternoon, on her own. She wouldn't let him help. *You've got packing to do*, she'd said, which wasn't true, he had almost nothing to pack. *Go away. I want to make these by myself. I want to know they're the last ones I'll make before I don't know what happens next.*

She stood on the step in her apron with her floury arms folded and watched them take a loaf each. One by one. Thirty-five people filing past the blue door in the dusk, saying *thank you* and *thank you, Merle* and, from Dennis, *what do I owe you*. And Merle saying *nothing* and *nothing* and, to Dennis, *double*.

He was last.

He stood on the step with his loaf in one hand and the lamp shield on his back. Merle looked at him.

"Well," she said.

"Well."

"Don't come back and tell me how it goes," said Merle.

He looked at her.

"I mean it," she said. "Don't come back down that hill in a week and tell me what's up there and how you did and whether everybody's all right. I don't want to know." Her mouth twisted. "I've known everything for a hundred and fifty-three days. I want to not know something. I want to wake up tomorrow and not know where you are." She was quiet. "That's the best thing anybody's ever given me. Not knowing."

"Okay," said Ice.

"Good."

"Not ever?"

Merle looked at him for a long moment.

"Come back one day," she said. "In a long time. Years. When I've forgotten what you look like. Walk in through that door and buy a rosemary loaf, and don't say anything." Her eyes were very bright. "And surprise me."

He didn't trust his voice. So he put the loaf down on the step and hugged her instead. Floury apron and all. She held on very tightly.

"Lean on it," said Merle, into his shoulder. "Whatever it is. Up there. Don't punch it."

"I know."

"You don't. But you will." She let go, and stepped back, and wiped her face, and left a long white streak of flour across it. "Go on. Your friends are waiting. All thirty-five of them." She almost laughed. "*Like the bells.*"

He picked up his loaf. He went down the step. At the end of Lantern Street he looked back.

She was standing in the blue doorway, in the last of the light, with the bell over the door and the smell of rosemary coming out round her. She lifted one floury hand. Not a wave. Just lifted it.

Then she went in, and shut the door. And he heard, very faintly, the bell.

***

Gabe caught him at the bottom of the clocktower.

The others had gone in already. He could hear them on the stair inside, going up, a long line of voices and boots. Thirty-four people. Drew had said *everybody in by eleven, we go up from the clock room at half past*. Ice had stopped at the tower door to look back at the square one more time. At the fountain, and the church steps, and the mouth of Lantern Street. And Gabe had stopped too.

They stood there, in the doorway, in the dark.

"You owe me one," said Gabe.

"I know."

"Separately. You said."

"I know, Gabe."

Gabe didn't say anything. He waited. Big and broad in the tower door, in the wolf coat, with his own shield on his arm and the Kiln-heart Iron in a pouch on his belt, glowing faintly through the leather like a coal.

Ice paused.

He didn't mean to. It was just there now, the pause. He'd been doing it for a hundred and fifty-four days. He'd done it at the edge of a stone cell and the edge of a cistern and the edge of a kitchen door. He supposed he'd do it for the rest of his life.

"Sorry," he said.

Gabe looked at him.

"For all of it," said Ice. "For the white room. For the stair. For making you sit on a step in a furnace hall till it closed, and on the edge of a pit for two and a half days, and on a—" He stopped. "For every morning. For a hundred and fifty-three mornings. For not saying one word, when you asked for one word." His voice had gone. "I'm sorry, Gabe. I'm so sorry."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long time.

"Accepted," he said.

Just that. Flat. The way he said things. And then he put his big hand on the back of Ice's neck, heavy and warm, the way he had since he was thirteen, and left it there.

"You're sleeping tonight," said Gabe.

"On eight?"

"On eight. I don't care where. I don't care if it's a hole in the ground or a ditch or a tree. You're lying down and you're shutting your eyes." Gabe's hand tightened. "And I'm sitting outside it. All night. So you know somebody's watching the door."

"Off to one side," said a voice from the stair behind them.

John. Sitting on the bottom step, in the dark, with a little white mote floating over his shoulder. He'd been there the whole time.

"I said I'd sit next to him," said John. "Off to one side. Best seat."

Ice looked at the two of them. Gabe in the door. John on the step.

He didn't say anything. He couldn't. He just nodded.

"Good," said Gabe. "Now get up the stairs before Drew comes down and gives us all a speech."

***

The clock face was a door now.

That was where the stair was. He'd half known it. The Hour had come out of the clock face every night for a hundred and fifty-three nights, out of the place in the middle where the hands met. And when it was gone, the clock face wasn't a window any more.

It stood open.

The great round window of thick old glass, with the shadows of the hands on it, had swung inward on hinges nobody had ever seen, like a door. And behind it, where there should have been the night air over the square and a long drop to the cobbles, there was a stair. Stone. Narrow. Going up. Into a dark that wasn't the dark of the sky over Evermorrow.

Thirty-five people stood in the clock room among the brass pieces of the Hour, looking at it.

"Okay," said Calvin. He'd gone to the front. Of course he had. He stood at the foot of the stair, in the round open doorway of the clock face, with Rachael beside him. "Okay. Look at my back. Everybody. Whatever's up there." He paused. "Nobody runs."

Then he looked back. Not at Drew. Over everybody's heads, at Ice, in the middle of the line, between Gabe and Thea.

"You're not going first," said Calvin.

"I know," said Ice.

"I'm going first."

"I know, Cal."

"You're in the *middle*."

"I'm in the middle," said Ice.

Calvin looked at him a moment longer. Then he grinned, the old grin, the delighted one. And turned round. And went up.

***

They counted the steps.

All of them. Out loud. Together. They'd done it on every stair since floor 3, because Hyvred had said once that he counted, on floor 1, alone. And somehow after that they all had. A long low chorus going up the dark stone ahead of them and behind them, bouncing off the walls. *Ten. Eleven. Twelve.*

He counted with them.

He'd never done that before. Not once. Not in this life. Every stair since the white room, he'd climbed on his own, counting under his breath, with Alea on his shoulder. A hundred and twelve. A hundred and sixty. Two hundred and four. Two hundred and forty-four. Two hundred and eighty. Three hundred and twelve. Alone.

*Thirty. Thirty-one. Thirty-two.*

He counted out loud. In the middle of the line. With Gabe's voice beside him, loud and off-key, and Thea's behind him, quiet and steady. And all the others going up ahead, Calvin's at the front louder than anybody. And Drew's at the back, calm, the voice of someone who'd counted a lot of things in his life and was happy to count these.

*Fifty. Sixty.*

Up ahead, a few people in front of him, he heard Deston's voice falter.

Just for a second. Deston, counting steadily, like everybody else, and then a little catch in it, on *sixty-four*. And Deston turned round on the stair. In the light from John's motes. And looked back, down the line, past the people between them.

At him.

Ice didn't know why. And then he did.

On floor 2, on the stair up from the Old Stag, all thirty-five of them had counted together for the first time. And Deston had heard a gap in it, where a thirty-sixth voice should have been. He'd put one line in World that night: *there's a gap when we count.* Alea had read it to him weeks afterwards, on the floor of the empty room in the Red City, and he'd had to ask her to stop for a while.

He was listening for it now.

Ice counted louder.

*Sixty-five. Sixty-six.*

Deston looked at him for a moment longer, on the stair, in the light. Then he turned round and went on up. And his voice came back into the chorus, steady, and didn't catch again.

There wasn't a gap.

***

*Three hundred and thirty. Three hundred and thirty-five.*

The light was changing. Not John's motes. Something else, coming down the stair from above. Grey. Cool. A smell came with it. Rain on stone, and something green and wet, like the hillside above Evermorrow on the first morning, but different. Colder. Older.

*Three hundred and forty.*

There was a door.

Calvin stopped in front of it. Everybody stopped behind him. Thirty-six people on a stair in the dark, between one floor and the next. Not one floor ahead. Not one floor behind. All on the same step, more or less, all at once.

"Okay," said Calvin. Very quietly. "Okay."

He put his hand on the door.

And Ice felt, behind and below him, very far away now, down three hundred and forty steps and through a clock face and a clock room and the stone of a tower, something he couldn't have felt with anything but his whole body. A tower in a valley. A clock in a tower. The great iron hands on its four faces, moving round, past eleven, toward twelve.

And not one of the thirty-six of them on that floor any more.

Somewhere far below, the great bell would strike. Twelve times. And then, after it, another minute. And another. And in the morning, there'd be a day after the day after. A baker would come downstairs in a house on Lantern Street with a blue door. She wouldn't know what was in the window, or what the weather was going to do, or who was going to come in at ten past nine. Or what had happened to thirty-six people who'd gone up a stair in the night.

She wouldn't know any of it.

He hoped she'd be glad.

Calvin opened the door.

***

It was night on floor 8. That was all he let himself see, the first night.

A dark hillside, going down. Wet grass, cold. A sky full of stars, real ones, too many, and a wind coming across the hill from somewhere far off, carrying rain. And close to the door, a little stone shelter, three walls and a roof, the kind that stood by the stair doors on half the floors he'd ever been on. Somewhere below, out of sight, water. A lot of it. The sound of it going a long way down.

He knew what was down there. He knew every floor from here to the top. He didn't let himself think about any of it.

They came out of the door one by one, the way they always did. Calvin. Rachael. Dennis. Deston. And he felt them. Every one. Thirty-five threads coming through the door around him, onto the cold grass, under the stars. Thick as ropes, all within a few metres. Not one of them pointing anywhere but here.

None of them was red.

[ Floor 8 ]

That was all the window said. Nothing else. He looked at it for a moment, waiting for something more. A flicker. A word.

Nothing came.

Drew came out last. He stood by the door and looked round at all of them, spread over the dark hillside, and counted with his eyes, the way he always did.

"Thirty-six," said Drew.

Nobody said anything. Then several people laughed.

"Right," said Drew. "Nobody goes anywhere tonight. We stay right here by the door. We sleep. In the morning we look." He looked at Ice. "And *you* sleep."

"He's sleeping," said Gabe. "I've already said."

"Then he's sleeping."

***

They put him in the stone shelter.

He tried to argue. He said somebody else should have it, Merle's bread had been heavy and Calvin's feet were wet. Nobody listened. Gabe walked him to the door of the little shelter with a hand on the back of his neck, the way you'd walk a drunk friend home. And he went in, because there was no point doing anything else.

It was small. Three walls and a roof and a floor of old flagstones, with somebody's ancient fire-ring in one corner. He rolled out his bedroll. He took the lamp shield off his back and leaned it against the wall, where he could see it. He lay down, in the cloak, with his head on his rolled-up spare shirt.

Outside, through the open side of the shelter, he could see the hillside and the stars, and thirty-five people bedding down on the wet grass. Rolling out blankets. Talking low. Somebody laughing. Somebody, Hyvred probably, saying *I can see all your lights, they're all so bright*, and somebody telling her to go to sleep.

And at the open side of the shelter, right at the edge of it, with his back to the wall and his legs out, Gabe.

Sitting down. In the wolf coat. With his own shield across his knees, and the Kiln-heart Iron in its pouch on his belt, glowing through the leather like a banked fire. Facing out. Facing the hillside and the dark.

Beside him, a little to one side, John. With a mote floating over his shoulder, small and white, lighting the side of Gabe's face.

And a little way past them, on the grass, sitting with her knees up and the lantern beside her, Thea. Not close. Not far. Keeping the light.

He lay in the shelter and looked at the three of them, with the stars behind.

"Gabe," he said.

"Go to sleep."

"I'm going."

"You're *talking*."

"I just—" He stopped. "Thank you."

Gabe didn't turn round.

"Go to sleep, Dec," he said. His voice was rough. "I've got the door."

***

He didn't think he'd sleep. He'd lain awake on four floors with nobody watching the door, waking every hour, listening. He'd lain at the bottom of a cistern in the desert for twenty-five nights and in the middle of a ring of ropes for twenty-six. He didn't know how to sleep any more. He'd forgotten.

He lay on his back in the stone shelter and felt the threads.

Thirty-five of them. All round him. A few metres away. Not one of them on another floor. Not one of them a hill road or a pit or a bowl of glass away. Here. On the grass. Within reach. He could feel them settling, one by one, the way threads settle when people fall asleep. Getting quieter. Calvin's, still racing a bit. Rachael's, beside it, steady. Deston's, warm. Thea's, just outside, amber at the edge, pointing straight back at him the way his pointed at her.

Gabe's, at the door. Huge and steady and awake.

Outside, very quietly, Gabe had started to hum. Not well. Gabe had never been able to sing. Something slow, that Ice knew. It took him a moment to place it. A school hall. A second row. A choir, a long time ago, singing it at a concert, while three boys stood in a row trying not to laugh. And after a minute, from somewhere out on the grass in the dark, another voice joined in. Low. Deston's.

Ice lay in the shelter and listened to them.

Alea came and sat on his chest. Small and teal and white in the dark, with her tail curled round her feet. She didn't say anything for a while.

"Sleep," she said.

"I'm trying."

"No you're not. You're listening to them." Her two white pinprick eyes were very soft. "You can listen tomorrow. They'll still be here tomorrow."

He looked at her.

"*They'll still be here tomorrow*," said Alea again. Very gently. "That's what tomorrow means now. Go to sleep, Ice."

He shut his eyes.

And somewhere between the second line of the choir song and the third, with Gabe's off-key voice and Deston's steady one, and thirty-five threads within reach, and a fox on his chest, he slept.

He didn't dream.
