The floe-hounds came up from underneath.

He'd been waiting for them from the side.

That was the whole of it, afterwards, when he lay in Sabe's house with his leg wrapped in fish-oil and Xola's cold water and Thea not speaking to him for an hour. He'd gone out onto the ice on the second morning alone, the way he went out on every floor, to find something above his level and fight it on his own, at six times, with Thea and Xola following a hundred metres behind on the ice and not lifting a hand unless he went down. He'd heard Sabe say the word *hounds* at the stove the night before. *Hounds on the ice, this side of the Narrows. Don't let them hear you.* And he'd thought *wolves*.

He'd thought: long grey shapes, in a line, silent, coming at you from the side you're not watching. He'd thought: the second night on fifteen, the first time, Gabe's shield hand. He'd thought he knew.

He'd walked out a mile onto the river in the low gold morning with his shield on his arm and his eyes on the white flat distance either side, watching for movement along the pressure ridges. Watching the sides.

The ice under his feet went *tock*.

Just that. Small. The way Sabe's pole went *tock* when she put it down. Except nobody had put anything down.

He looked down.

Under his boots, through the ice, through a hand's depth of clear black ice he hadn't noticed was clear, something long and pale was looking up at him.

***

*Floe-hound*, said Alea. *Lv seventy-four. There's—Ice, there's three. Under. They're under—*

The ice in front of him exploded.

Not the ice. A crack. A dark line he'd stepped over without thinking, that had been a dark line in the ice for as long as he'd been on it. It burst open upward in a fountain of black water and broken plates, and something came up out of it at him so fast he only saw it as a shape. Long. Pale grey, nearly white, the colour of the ice. Smooth, like a seal. A long narrow head with no ears and small black eyes set very far forward, and a mouth that opened wider than the head, wide as a door, lined with short curved teeth like a fish's. It came up out of the crack on four short flat limbs that weren't legs or flippers but something between, and it didn't land on the ice. It landed on *him*.

He wasn't there.

He'd moved before he'd thought, the way he always moved. Early, not fast. He'd felt the ice go *tock* and his body had already been going sideways, off the line of the crack, before the water came up. The thing's jaws closed on the place his chest had been. He came round behind it on the ice with the shield up and his sword already moving and cut it across the back of the neck where the smooth grey went thin—

—and it wasn't thin. It was thick. Fat. Blubber under the smooth hide, a hand deep, and his sword went in and stopped, the way it would in a sack of wet sand. The thing turned its head all the way round on its long neck and looked at him with its small black eyes and opened its mouth.

Behind him, the ice went *tock*.

He turned.

He was too slow. Not because he was slow. Because he was looking at the sides. Because on fifteen, the first time, things came from the sides, and his whole body was waiting for the sides, and when the second hound came up through the ice behind him through a crack he'd never seen, from straight *underneath*, his body was turning the wrong way.

It hit him in the back of the left leg.

He felt the teeth go in. Short and curved. Through the leather and the cloth and into the muscle above the knee, and then the pull. Not a bite. A drag. Back. Toward the crack it had come out of. Toward the black water.

He didn't make a sound. He never did, in the moment. Something went through him and out, and he didn't look down, and he didn't stop moving.

*Seventy-one*, said Alea. Very clear. Very calm. The way she called it on every floor. *It's got your leg. It's pulling. Ice, it's pulling you to the water—*

He put the edge of the lamp shield down on the ice like a brake and drove it in. The Fen iron bit. The Kiln rim bit. He stopped moving backward. The hound pulled. Something in his leg went wrong. He drove his sword down behind him without looking, two-handed, into whatever was on the end of his leg, and felt it hit something hard, a skull, and slide off.

*Sixty-three.*

The first one was coming round. On the ice now, out of its crack, low and fast and flat, the way a seal moves on land but faster, much faster, its belly sliding on the ice and its short flat limbs pushing. Coming at his front while the second one had his back.

And the third one wasn't anywhere.

*Under*, said Alea. *The third one's under. I can't see it. I can't—Ice, I don't know where it is. I don't know what it does. I've never—*

*Sixty*, she said.

He healed.

He'd promised. Thea, on eleven, after the tow, in the rain. *Look after yourself. For me.* At sixty, every time, he healed. And he said it out loud, so whoever was watching would know.

"It hurts," he said. Clearly. Not loud. Across the ice, to where he knew Thea and Xola were. "Sixty. Leg. Three of them. I don't know what they do."

Mend Self came up in him like warmth. The bite in his leg closed round the teeth that were still in it. He turned, finally, the right way, and brought the shield round and down, hard, edge first, on the long neck of the thing holding his leg, where it came up out of the crack. The neck was thick too. But it wasn't as thick as the back. The hound let go.

He stepped back. Off the crack. Onto ice he couldn't see under. He stood with his shield up and his leg going warm and wet and his sword out and two pale things on the ice in front of him, low, swaying, with their small black eyes on him and their door-wide mouths open, and a third one somewhere under his feet that he couldn't see and didn't know.

He didn't know anything.

Not where they'd come from next. Not how they moved. Not where they were thin. Not whether they'd go for his legs or his throat or back down into the water. Not what *tock* meant, or where the cracks were, or whether the ice he was standing on was a hand thick or a finger.

He'd fought on fourteen floors. In two lives. He'd fought things the size of barns and things that weren't there and a straw man full of winter and a wall of grey that wanted to cross him out of a book. He'd never once, not since the Bellwether on the first morning of the first time, stood in front of something and not known what it was going to do.

He stood on the ice in the gold morning with his leg bleeding and two floe-hounds swaying in front of him.

And he did the only thing he could think of.

He stopped watching the sides. He stopped watching *them*. He stood very still, on the ice, with his weight on both feet, and he listened.

***

It was the stupidest thing he'd ever done in a fight. He knew it while he did it. You didn't stand still in front of two things with mouths like doors. You moved. Early. That was the whole of him; that was what he was. *Never where it lands.*

He stood still.

And the ice went *tock*. Under his right boot. A small sound. And then a long thin creak, running away from his right foot toward the left, under the ice, the way a crack runs.

The third one.

Under him. Moving. Going from his right to his left. He felt it go through his boots before he heard it. He stood still and felt it come round, under the ice, slowly, looking for him, and stop. Just behind his left heel.

*Tock*.

He moved.

Not sideways. Forward. Toward the two on the ice in front of him. Straight at them. And behind him, where his left heel had been, the ice burst open and the third hound came up out of the black water into the empty air and closed its mouth on nothing.

The two in front weren't ready for him coming at them. Nothing on any floor was ever ready for that. He went between them. He put the shield edge into the nearer one's face, where the small black eyes were, and the sword into the other one's open mouth, all the way, two-handed, and twisted. The mouth was the thin place. The only thin place. He found it because it was open.

The second hound went still on the end of his sword.

The first one, with his shield in its face, reared back and came down at him, and he wasn't there, and it hit the ice. He came round. He went for the mouth, because the mouth was the thin place. It didn't open its mouth. It had learned, in the time it took to watch its sister die. It went down. Into the crack it had come out of. Gone.

He stood still again.

He listened.

*Tock*. Under the ice. Behind him. Then a creak, running left. Then nothing. Then *tock*, much further off. Then a creak, going away. Two of them. The first one and the third one. Going away under the ice, toward the middle of the river, toward the deep water, fast.

They'd gone.

He stood on the ice for a long time with his sword in the mouth of a dead floe-hound and his leg bleeding and his heart going, listening to them go.

*One*, said Alea. Very quietly. *You killed one. Two went.*

"I know."

*You stood still.* She sounded almost frightened of it. *You never stand still.*

"I listened."

She was quiet for a moment.

*I'm writing that down*, she said. And then, after a pause, in a voice he'd never heard her use, small and careful, like somebody opening a new book and not wanting to crease the spine: *First entry. Floor fifteen. Day seven hundred and nineteen. Floe-hounds come from underneath. They're thick everywhere but the mouth. When the ice goes* tock *under your foot, move.*

He pulled his sword out of the dead hound's mouth.

*I don't have anything else*, said Alea. *That's all I've got. For fifteen. That's the whole log.*

"It's a start."

*It's a start*, she said.

***

Thea didn't speak to him for an hour.

She'd come out across the ice at a run when he went still, with Xola beside her, and stopped twenty metres off, because that was the rule, you didn't lift a hand unless he went down, and he hadn't gone down. She'd stood there with her hands in fists and watched him stand still in front of two floe-hounds with his leg in the mouth of a third, and not lift a hand. Then she'd watched him walk back across the ice toward her limping, with his leg bleeding into his boot.

She hadn't said anything. She'd looked at the leg. Then at him.

"Sixty," she said. "You said sixty."

"I said sixty."

"And *it hurts*."

"And it hurts."

"And *I don't know what they do*." Her voice went. "You said *I don't know what they do*."

"I didn't."

"I know you didn't. I *heard*." She turned round. She walked back across the ice toward Sabe's ladder. Xola looked at him and shook her head and put her hand out, and the cold water came out of her palm and went into the bite on his leg like ice going into a burn, and he made a sound for the first time that day.

"Icey," said Xola. Quietly. Without looking up from the leg. "She's not angry."

"She's angry."

"She's frightened." Xola finished the leg. She stood up. "You've never said *I don't know* in a fight before. Not once. Not on any floor. She's heard every number you've ever said. Fifteen, on the Grey. She heard that." Xola looked at him. "She's never heard *I don't know what they do*."

He looked across the ice at Thea's back, going away.

"Neither had I," he said.

***

Calvin went through the ice the next morning, and it was his fault.

He'd said white.

That was all. That was the whole of it. Deston's party was going out across the river to the Float, the raft-town frozen into the ice out in the middle, a mile off, where Sabe said there were people who'd want to know about the thaw coming and might know things about the Narrows. Calvin was first. Calvin was always first. *Look at my back.* Rachael's fist in his collar. And Ice was coming back from the other direction, from the far bank, with his leg stiff and Thea a hundred metres behind him, and he met them on the ice halfway.

"Which way?" said Calvin. Out of habit. He always asked. On every floor, for two years, at every crossing, Calvin had asked *which way*, and Ice had told him, and Calvin had gone that way, first, talking, and nobody had ever gone wrong.

Ice looked at the ice between them and the Float.

There was a band of dark ice going out toward the raft-town. Black, almost. Clear and dark, like the ice the hound had looked up at him through. And beside it, a long smooth band of white. Thick-looking. Snow-white. Solid. Like the ice on the Mere on floor 2, like the snow on the Long Acre, like every safe white thing he'd ever walked on in fourteen floors.

"White," he said. "Stay on the white. The dark's thin."

Calvin went onto the white.

Behind Ice, from the bank, a voice screamed: "*NOT THE WHITE*—"

The white gave way.

Not cracked. Gave way. The whole band of it, a cart-width, five metres along, went soft under Calvin's boots like wet sugar and folded down into black water, and Calvin went with it. Straight down. No sound. Rachael's fist in his collar went with him, and Rachael's arm to the elbow, and Rachael was on her face on the edge of the hole with her arm in the water and her fist still closed, screaming his name.

And then her fist came up empty.

The collar had torn.

Ice was already moving.

He didn't decide. He never did. He was across the ice and past Rachael and over the edge of the hole into the black water before anybody else had finished turning round. He heard Thea scream his name from a hundred metres off, once. Then the water closed over his head.

***

It was the coldest thing he'd ever felt.

Not the Grey. Not the snow on fourteen, or two, or the frost going up his arms to the elbow. Those had been cold the way a floor was cold. This was cold the way the inside of the world was cold. It went into him through every part of him at once, through his clothes and his skin and his mouth and his eyes, and his whole body seized, the way a fist closes, and for a second he couldn't move anything at all.

He was under the ice.

He could see it. Above him. A ceiling of grey-white, lit from above by the morning, with the hole a bright ragged shape a little way off. And below him, black. Nothing. The river going down to wherever the river went. And somewhere in the black, Calvin.

*Ice*, said Alea, very far off, very small, *Ice, the cold, it's—your hit points are—*

He didn't listen. He kicked. Down. Into the black. Toward where Calvin must have gone, down and back, with the current, which was pulling, he felt it now, a long slow pull under the ice toward the middle of the river. He kicked with it.

A hand.

He felt it before he saw it. Cold fingers, brushing his face, in the black. He grabbed. He got a wrist. Calvin's. He knew it the way he'd know Calvin's wrist in any dark on any floor; both wrists, both hands, both arms, the ones Calvin had because in this life nothing had come off the roof.

He pulled Calvin up against him. Calvin wasn't moving much. He turned, in the black, with Calvin's weight on his arm, and looked up for the hole.

It wasn't there.

The ice was. Grey-white ceiling, lit from above, the same everywhere. The current had pulled them. He didn't know how far. He didn't know which way. The bright ragged shape of the hole was gone. He was under the ice of a river a mile wide with Calvin on his arm and his lungs starting to go and he didn't know where the hole was.

He didn't know.

*Ice*, said Alea. *Ice, I don't know where it is. I don't know where it is, I can't—*

And through the water, faint, from somewhere off to the left, a sound.

*Tock*.

He turned his head.

*Tock*. Again. From the left. And above. Small and sharp, the sound of something hard coming down on ice. The sound Sabe's pole made. Somebody was up there, on the ice, somewhere off to his left, putting a pole down. Again. And again. *Tock. Tock. Tock.* Steady. Like a heartbeat. Like somebody counting. Like a bell.

He kicked toward it.

He didn't think about it. There wasn't anything left to think with. He kicked toward the sound with Calvin on his arm and the black closing in at the edges of his eyes, and the *tock* got louder, and louder, and then there was a shape above him in the grey ceiling, a bright ragged shape, and the *tock* was coming from right beside it, and something long and pale came down through the hole into the water in front of his face.

A pole. With an iron point. Somebody was holding it.

He grabbed it.

***

They pulled him out. All of them. He didn't remember most of it. Gabe's hand in his collar, then under his arm. Somebody taking Calvin. Rachael's voice, high and terrible, saying *give him to me, give him to me*, and then not saying anything, which was worse, and then saying *he's breathing, he's breathing, he's breathing*. Kei, somewhere. Xola's hands on his face, and then not cold water coming out of them for once but warm. Thea.

Thea was holding his head in her lap on the ice.

She wasn't saying anything. She was holding his head and looking down at him, and her face was white, and wet, and doing nothing at all.

He turned his head. On the ice, a few metres off, was the pole. Long and pale, willow, with an iron point, lying where it had been dropped. And beside it, on her knees on the ice, breathing hard, with her hood thrown back and her black braids frozen, a girl.

Fourteen, maybe. Fifteen. Short and broad, like all of them, in grey fur. A flat brown face burned dark by the snow-glare, and eyes so angry they could have lit a fire.

She'd been the voice on the bank. *NOT THE WHITE.* He knew it now.

"*You said white*," said the girl.

He looked at her.

"I heard you," she said. "From the bank. *Stay on the white. The dark's thin.*" Her voice cracked. She was very young. "*Who told you that?*"

He opened his mouth. His teeth were going so hard he couldn't make anything come out.

"The *white*'s rotten," said the girl. "In spring. It's *snow*. It's snow that froze. It's got air in it. It goes first." She was shaking too. From the cold, or from something else. "The *black*'s new. The black's strong. It's clear because there's nothing in it but ice. *Everybody knows that.*" She stared at him. "You're a *climber*. Gran said climbers know *everything*."

"Not this," said Ice.

It came out of him in pieces, through his teeth.

"Not this one," he said. "I didn't know. I was wrong."

The girl looked at him.

Across the ice, Calvin was sitting up. In Rachael's arms, which nobody had ever seen and nobody ever mentioned afterwards. Wrapped in three coats. Blue at the lips. Coughing up river. Rachael had both his hands in hers and her forehead on his shoulder and she was shaking so hard Calvin was shaking with her.

Calvin looked across the ice at Ice.

"You were *wrong*?" said Calvin. Through his teeth.

"I was wrong."

Calvin stared at him for a long moment. Then he started laughing. Coughing and laughing at once, blue, in three coats, with Rachael's forehead on his shoulder.

"*Huh*," said Calvin. "That's new."

***

Her name was Tuli. She was Sabe's granddaughter. She'd had her pole since the autumn, which meant this was her first thaw with a pole of her own, which meant, she told him, in the tone of somebody explaining something very obvious to somebody very stupid, that she'd never once been allowed to walk out on the ice in spring without her gran beside her, and today she had, because she'd heard a climber on the river saying *white*, and she'd known.

"You *knew*," said Ice.

He was sitting by Sabe's stove in every fur she owned, with his leg up and his teeth finally stopped, with Thea beside him not letting go of his hand, and Calvin across the stove in the same state with Rachael not letting go of either of his.

"Course I knew," said Tuli. "I've *listened*." She was sitting on the floor with her pole across her knees, glaring at him. "Gran's made me listen every day since I could walk. Every day. Even in summer when there's no ice. She makes me put my hand on the river." She sniffed. "You don't listen. I saw you yesterday. Out there. You were watching. Watching *sideways*. Like the ice was going to come at you from the *side*."

"I thought it would."

"Why?"

He didn't answer that.

"*Tock*," said Tuli. "That's what you followed. Under the ice. I was doing it so you'd follow it." She looked away. "Gran says that's what you do. If somebody's under. You put your pole down and you keep putting it down, and you don't stop, and they come to it. Because it's the only sound under there that's somebody." She was quiet. "I've never done it before. Not for real."

"You did it right," said Ice.

Tuli looked at him.

"You did it right," he said again. "I followed it. I didn't know where the hole was. I didn't know anything. I just heard you."

Tuli went red under the snow-burn. She looked at her pole.

"Teach me," said Ice.

The whole room went quiet. Sabe, at the stove, stopped stirring. Thea's hand moved in his.

"Teach me to listen," said Ice. "The ice. The way your gran taught you. I don't know any of it. I don't know the white from the black. I don't know what *tock* means. I know—" He stopped. He'd been going to say *I know fourteen floors*. "I don't know this," he said. "Teach me."

Tuli stared at him.

"You're a *climber*," she said.

"I'm somebody who doesn't know," said Ice. "On this one. Same as you, the first thaw."

Tuli looked at her gran.

Sabe, at the stove, didn't look round. She went on stirring.

"He went in after the other one," said Sabe, to the pot. "Without knowing. Under the white." She tasted the spoon. "Fool thing to do. Only fools and mothers." She put the spoon down. "Teach him, Tuli. He'll listen. He's had the fear put in him."

***

So Tuli taught him.

Every morning, for four days, at first light, on the ice below Sabe's ladder. She gave him a pole. Not a good one. An old one, cracked, with a bent iron point, that had been Sabe's before Sabe's was Sabe's. She made him put the point down. She made him stand still. She made him listen.

He'd never felt so stupid in his life.

*Tock*, went the pole. And he'd feel it come up the willow into his hands, a small sharp thing, and he'd have no idea what it meant. And Tuli would put her own pole down a metre off, *tock*, and tip her head, and say *thick*, or *thin*, or *new*, or *rotten*, or *don't*, without even looking. And he'd stand there. And feel nothing.

"You're *thinking*," said Tuli, on the second morning, disgusted. "Stop thinking. You're trying to remember it."

"I'm trying to learn it."

"You can't learn it. You can only hear it." She put her pole down. *Tock*. "Every time's the first time. Gran says. You don't remember what thick sounds like. You listen to what *this* sounds like. *Now*."

On the third morning, he heard it.

Not much. One thing. He put the bent old pole down on the ice below Sabe's ladder in the grey light, *tock*, and felt it come up the willow into his palms, and it was different. From yesterday. From the place a metre to the left. It was lower. Rounder. It went down further before it came back. It felt, in his hands, like putting your foot down on a stair you'd thought was there and finding that it was.

"Thick," said Ice.

Tuli put her pole down beside his. *Tock*. She tipped her head.

"Thick," she said. Grudgingly.

He put the pole down a metre to the right. *Tock*. Higher. Shorter. A sort of hiss at the end of it, like something running away under the ice. Like the creak he'd heard on the first night and not known.

"Not that," he said.

Tuli looked at him.

"*Not that*," she agreed. And for the first time, very slightly, at the corner of her flat brown face, she nearly smiled.

That night he wrote it down. In his diary. Not in Alea's log; Alea was writing her own. He sat by Sabe's stove with Thea asleep against him and wrote, in the small careful hand he'd used for the note in the white room:

*Black is strong. White is snow and rotten in spring. Grey is worse. Tock that goes down and comes back round is thick. Tock with a hiss is not that. Floe-hounds come from underneath. Their mouths are the thin place. If somebody's under, put a pole down and don't stop.*

He read it back.

It was the shortest page he'd ever written about a floor. Fourteen floors in two lives, and he'd had pages. Books. Ten years of Alea's logs and his own head. This was eight lines.

It was the only page in the whole of the Spire, he thought, that he'd ever written himself, about a place, the first time he'd seen it. Like everybody else.

*I've got four*, said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. *Entries. I've got four now.* A pause. *Tuli's better than me.*

"Tuli's better than both of us."

*Yes*, said Alea. And then, after a while, almost cheerful, the way she'd been on floor 1 the first time, before there was anything to be sad about: *I like her.*

***

On the fourth evening, Thea sat with him on the bottom rung of Sabe's ladder, the way she had on the first night, with the lantern low, and the river talking in the dark in front of them.

He could tell most of it apart now. The tick. The creak. The crack and the hiss. The low groan under everything. He still didn't know what most of it meant. But Tuli said that was all right. Tuli said she didn't either, not really, not the way Gran did, and Gran said she didn't either, not the way *her* gran had, and her gran had said that nobody had, ever, and that was why you listened every day.

"You were wrong," said Thea.

"I was wrong."

"You've never been wrong before." She wasn't angry any more. She was looking at the river. "Not in front of me. Not once. Not since the white room."

"I've been wrong a lot," said Ice. "In the other life. All the time. For ten years. I was wrong about nearly everything." He looked at the ice. "I just knew what came after. This time. That's not the same as being right."

Thea was quiet.

"Calvin," she said.

"I know."

"You said white. And he went on the white. Because you said."

"I know."

"Because he's always gone where you said. For two years. Every crossing. *Which way.* And you always knew." She turned her head. "And he'd do it again. He told Rachael. Tonight. By the stove. Through his teeth." Her voice went soft. "*I'd still go where he says. Next time I'll also listen.*"

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

"That's what it is now," said Thea. "That's all. You say, and they go. And they *also* listen. And so do you." She put her hand on the back of his neck. "That's not worse. Ice. That's just—more people knowing things. Than one."

He looked at the river.

Out in the dark, far up the valley, something boomed. Long and low and rolling. Closer than it had been on the first morning. He felt it come up through the ladder and the bank and the ice and into his bones.

And then, a long way off, from the direction of the Float out in the middle of the river, very faint, he heard it.

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

Dozens of them. Poles going down on the ice in the dark, all over the raft-town, all at once. Everybody on the river listening to the same thing at the same time.

He didn't know what they were hearing.

But he put his hand flat on the cold bottom rung of the ladder, and shut his eyes, and listened with them.
