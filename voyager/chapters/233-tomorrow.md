The windows had been coming in for eight days.

Ice had watched them come. On the Long Road, between the black bridge and the End, in the evenings at the inns and in the long hot afternoons on the white dust, one after another, the way rain comes in across a field: a drop, then another, then all of it. Thirty-five people who'd been sitting on a window since the Hollow, or since the Thaw, or since Turnstead, looking at it, closing it, saying *not yet*. And then the road had asked them for something true at every bridge, six times, and something in all of them had come loose.

He'd seen it in the World. He always looked, now. He'd never once looked in the first life; he'd thought the World was for other people. Now he read every line.

The End was the last inn on the Long Road, and nobody in it was going back.

He understood that the moment they walked into the yard. The other inns had been full of pilgrims going on, with a knot or two or three, talking about the next bridge, mending boots, eating. The End was full of pilgrims who'd stopped. Forty or fifty of them, sitting in the yard and on the walls and in the long low white house, all with six knots, all looking the same way. Down the slope, past the *1* stone, at the bridge.

Some of them had been there a long time. You could tell. An old man in the corner of the yard with a beard to his belt and a cord with six knots worn so smooth they'd nearly gone back into the cord. A woman in her forties who'd planted a little garden by the well, beans, which meant she'd been there at least a summer. A boy, younger than Tuli, sitting on the wall with his knees up, staring at the tollhouse, who'd come with his father, the innkeeper said, three years ago, and his father had paid and gone over, and the boy hadn't.

They were waiting. For the thing they'd kept to get light enough to say.

Sorrel walked into the yard of the End with her new staff, a hazel one Salty had cut and Jared had shod with iron at the Plough, and stood there with her six knots and looked round at all of them.

Then she went and sat on the wall next to the boy.

She didn't say anything to him. She just sat. About the width of a staff away. The way Ina sat with people. The way Maigo had sat with her.

After a while, the boy looked at her cord. At the six knots, worn, eleven times over.

"You've been before," said the boy.

"Eleven times."

"Did you go over?"

"Not yet," said Sorrel.

The boy looked at the tollhouse.

"Me neither," he said.

They sat on the wall together, an old woman and a boy, and looked at the bridge, and didn't say anything else.

***

He went out at dusk to level.

He always did. Every evening, on every floor, for two years, the way he'd done it in the first life except not alone now. Out to the edge of whatever was there, with a sword and his own two hands and a little bit of himself held back for healing. Thea came, with the lantern. Xola came, with Squiddles, because Thea had asked her to on fourteen and she'd never once stopped.

He didn't hunt far. He didn't need to.

The gorge was right there.

Past the last of the hedges, past the *1* stone, the ground just stopped. A lip of short grass and white rock, and then nothing. The gorge went down further than he could see, into the mist, and the mist was gold. Thick and soft and full of the last of the sun, filling the gorge from edge to edge like milk in a bowl, so you couldn't see the bottom or the far side or anything at all except the bridge going out into it, arch after pale arch, until it was gone.

And things lived in the mist.

The innkeeper had said. *Don't go to the edge at dusk. Not for long. The kites come up.* And the old man with the beard had laughed, and said, *let the young ones find out*.

Ice went to the edge.

He stood on the lip of white rock with Tuli's pole in one hand and his sword in the other and the gold mist going down in front of him forever, and Gabe's terrible lantern on his belt, humming. Thea a hundred paces back, by the *1* stone, with the lantern open, the amber leaning at him across the grass. Xola beside her.

He waited.

He'd never been patient. Not in two lives. He'd always gone. Early. First. Toward the thing.

He'd learned a lot on the Long Road about standing still.

The kites came up out of the mist at the moment the sun went off the top of it.

***

There were seven.

He saw the first one as a shadow. A darker place in the gold, rising, very fast, from somewhere far down. Then it broke out of the top of the mist and he saw it properly. Pale. Long. Wings out either side wider than the bridge, thin and stretched like the sails on Corran's ship, the gold light shining through them so he could see the bones. A narrow head like a heron's, all beak, and under it, folded up against a narrow body, two legs ending in talons the size of his hands.

It went up. High. Riding whatever was coming up out of the gorge, the warm air, up and up over the edge of the cliff in a long rising spiral, very fast, silent. And turned. At the top of the spiral. And looked down.

At him.

Then there were seven.

They came up out of the mist one after another, spiralling, pale, enormous, wings full of gold light. And turned at the top. And folded.

And dived.

He'd fought a lot of things that came from above. On twelve, a heron the size of a tower, with Thea's ward through its eye. You didn't fight things that came from above by being where they were going.

*Early, not fast.*

He moved before the first one had finished folding.

Not far. A step and a half to the left, along the lip, toward a lump of white rock that stood up out of the grass like a tooth. He didn't know why there. He knew. The first kite came down out of the dusk like a thrown spear, beak first, wings back, so fast it whistled, at the place where he'd been standing.

It hit the grass. Talons first. Where he'd been. Hard enough to tear up a square yard of turf.

He was a step and a half to the left. He put Tuli's pole into the ground and went round it, the way you'd go round a post on a corner, swinging, and came down on the far side of the kite with his sword already going, and the edge went into the long pale neck behind the head where it was thin.

It came apart like wet paper. Like the moths. The whole pale stretched thing folding in on itself on the grass at the edge of the gorge, wings collapsing.

He didn't look at it. The second was already coming.

He went right. Back past where the first had landed. Two steps. The second hit the rock tooth he'd been standing beside, beak first, and the beak went into the rock and stuck, and it hung there for a breath with its wings beating, and he cut it at the neck, and it folded.

The third came from behind.

He didn't see it. He didn't need to. He'd been fighting for two lives and some part of him knew where things were going to be before the rest of him had worked it out, and it said *behind*, *low*, and he went down. Flat. On his face in the grass. And the kite went over him so close he felt the wind of its wings on the back of his neck and its talons went through the air where his shoulders had been.

And caught his arm.

Not the shoulder. The arm. The left, trailing, as he went down. One talon, the size of his hand, through his sleeve and into the meat of his forearm, and the kite going up again with its wings beating, and his arm going up with it.

He went up with it.

Not far. A foot off the ground. Two. Dragged up off the grass by one arm with the kite's talon through it and the kite beating up, up, toward the edge, toward the gold mist and the long fall, and his weight pulling against it.

*Seventy*, said Alea. On his shoulder. Very calm. *Ice. Seventy-two.*

"Seventy-two," he said. Out loud. Through his teeth.

He didn't know if anybody heard. He said it anyway. Every number. Out loud. Every time.

He brought his sword round with his right arm, up, over his own head, and put it into the kite's leg above the talon.

It let go.

He fell. Six feet, onto the grass, on his back, hard. The air went out of him. The kite went up, screaming, the first sound any of them had made, and wheeled, and came back down at him while he was on his back with no air in him and his left arm not working.

*Sixty-four.*

"Sixty-four." He couldn't get it out. He got it out. "Sixty-four. It hurts."

He rolled.

Off his back, onto his side, toward the gorge. Toward the edge. Because it was coming from the land side and the edge was the last place it would think he'd go. And it hit the grass where he'd been lying, and he was on his knees at the very lip of the gorge, with the gold mist going down forever a hand's width from his knee, and he put his sword into its neck from underneath, from below, kneeling.

It folded.

He knelt at the edge with his left arm hanging and his breath not coming and four more pale shapes spiralling up over the edge of the cliff in the dusk, turning at the top, looking down at him.

*Sixty*, said Alea.

He'd promised. On eleven, after the tow, with Thea and Xola both shouting at him. On fourteen, in the healing, every day. Not at twenty. Not when he'd decided he'd had enough. At sixty. Every time.

He put his right hand on his chest. Mend Self. Plain.

It went in. Like a hot needle sewing. The arm closing. The echo on top of it, the arm hurting again from the inside as it closed, the way it always did. He knelt at the edge and let it.

*Sixty-eight*, said Alea.

"Sixty-eight." He said it louder. To the dusk. To the *1* stone, a hundred paces back, where there was an amber light leaning at him across the grass. "Sixty-eight. It hurts."

He heard Xola swear.

He didn't look round. He didn't need to. He heard her coming. Running. Across the grass. Not fast; Xola was never fast. But coming. Swearing the whole way. *You absolute— on your KNEES at the EDGE you absolute*—

And then he did a thing he'd never done in a fight in either life.

He lifted his left hand. The bad one. And held it out behind him, toward the sound of her coming, without looking round.

"Xola," said Ice. "Can you?"

She stopped.

He heard her stop. In the grass, behind him, a few paces off. The swearing stopping. Everything stopping.

Then her hands were on his arm. Cold, wet, Xola's water, out of nothing, running over the torn sleeve and the arm under it, and closing it. It still hurt, closing. Healing always did. But slower. Gentler. Not his own hot needle shoving it shut mid-fight, the way he'd done it alone on every floor. Somebody else's hands, agreeing with it.

"*Can you*," said Xola. Behind him. With her hands on his arm. Her voice very strange. "Mid-fight. You asked me mid-fight. You've never asked me mid-fight. You've never asked *anybody* mid—"

"I know."

"Two *years*—"

"Behind you," said Ice.

She ducked. She'd learned that too. Without looking. And the fifth kite went over both of them, low, and he came up off his knees under it with his arm working and put the sword through its wing, and it went sideways over the edge into the gold mist and down and didn't come up.

The last two turned at the top of their spiral, and looked down at a man and a woman at the edge of the gorge, back to back now, her hands up with water running off them and his sword out. And something in the long narrow heads decided.

They went back down into the mist.

Five kites on the grass behind them, folded, pale, already going thin and grey the way things did when you killed them.

He looked at it and closed it.

Thea came across the grass from the *1* stone with the lantern. She didn't say anything either. She sat down on his other side, with her feet over the edge too, and put the lantern down on the white rock beside her. The amber light lay out across the gold mist and leaned at him.

Thea turned her head when it did that. She always did.

"He asked," said Xola. To Thea. Across him. Not looking at either of them. "Mid-fight. He held his hand out behind him without looking and said *can you*."

Thea didn't say anything for a while.

"I heard," said Thea. Quietly.

"*Mid-fight*, babe."

"I know." Thea looked at the gorge. "I heard all the numbers, too."

Xola made a sound. Then she put her face down on Ice's shoulder, the way she never did, and stayed there, and Squiddles climbed off her collarbone onto his arm and sat on the place where the talon had been, cold and damp, as if checking.

"I'm not making it weird."

"You're making it a *bit* weird."

"Sorry."

"Don't say sorry." She didn't lift her head. "Say a number."

"Ninety," said Ice.

Xola laughed, wet, into his shoulder. "*Ninety*. You absolute— You're at *ninety*."

"Ten to go."

"To what?"

He didn't answer that.

He looked out at the bridge going into the gold mist, and didn't answer, and Thea's hand found his on the white rock between them.

***

Xola went in when it was dark. She said she was going to check on Kei, which meant she was going to cry somewhere, and she knew he knew that, and she went anyway.

He stayed on the edge with Thea.

The mist had gone from gold to grey to silver, now. There was a moon. He hadn't noticed it come up. It was over the gorge, high and nearly full, and the mist under it was white and soft and very still, and the bridge went out into it, pale, arch after arch, the way a road goes into snow.

And at the near end of the bridge, the tollhouse.

Small. Stone. Very old. He could see it from where they sat; it was only two hundred paces down the slope from the lip. A little square house with a slate roof and a chimney with no smoke and a door. Shut. No window that he could see. No keeper on a bench. No light.

*That one isn't deaf*, Sorrel had said. *That one hears everything.*

"Tomorrow," said Thea.

"Tomorrow."

She didn't say anything else for a while. She held his hand on the white rock.

He'd thought about it for eight days. Since the Lark, since Sorrel said *what you've kept* and thirty-four people carefully didn't look at him. He'd thought about it on every bridge. On the Willow Bridge he'd stood on the crown with his hand on the little lantern and said a thing he'd never said out loud, and it had been true, and it hadn't been *that*. On the black bridge, with thirty-three people below him with their hands over their ears, he'd said a thing he'd worked out and never said, and it had been true, and it hadn't been *that*.

He'd been saving it.

He hadn't known he was. He'd known on the black bridge. Standing on the crown in that silence, looking down at thirty-three people with their hands over their ears so they wouldn't hear him, he'd known he was saving it for the end. For the one that asked for it. For the one that heard.

"It hears," said Ice.

"I know."

"Sorrel said. Everything. The keepers on the road are deaf. That one hears." He looked at the tollhouse. "And it asks for what you've kept."

"I know."

"I've only got one thing I've kept."

Thea didn't say anything.

"I told you," said Ice. "Under the aurora. On ten. I told you the shape of it. And bits. Since. On stairs. At night. Names." He looked at the moon. "I've told you more than I've ever told anybody. In two lives. I've told you more than I've told Alea, and she was there."

*Thank you very much*, said Alea. On his shoulder. Very quietly. *I'm right here.*

"Sorry."

*I'm joking. Mostly.*

"I haven't told you all of it," said Ice.

Thea's hand didn't move in his.

"I know," said Thea.

He looked at her.

She was looking at the bridge. At the tollhouse. Her face in the moonlight doing nothing at all.

"I've always known," said Thea. "There's a part you keep back. Even from me. Even on the stairs. You tell me the shape and you tell me the names and you tell me what happened on a floor when I ask." She was quiet. "And there's a part you don't. A part you hold. The way you used to hold everything, before the jam. I can feel it. Like a stone in a pocket. You put your hand on it sometimes when you think nobody's looking."

He didn't say anything.

"I've never asked," said Thea. "Not once. Because it's yours. Because you'd have told me if you could. Because I thought, maybe, it's the bit that's too heavy to lift up and say out loud. Even to me."

"It is," said Ice.

He hadn't known that until he said it.

"It's not secret," he said. "Not from you. There's nothing in it you don't know the shape of. It's just—" He stopped. He didn't know how to say it. "It's all of it. The whole thing. Start to end. Every floor. Every day. Everything I thought. Two lives of it. I've told you bits, the way you'd show somebody a few pages of a book. I've never once said all of it. Out loud. In one go. To anybody." He looked at the tollhouse. "I don't think I could. To a person. I don't think a person could stand there and hear it."

Thea was quiet for a long time.

"Then tell it to the Toll," said Thea.

He looked at her.

"It hears," said Thea. "And it doesn't tell. That's what Sorrel said, isn't it. It keeps what you've kept. Every pilgrim who's ever gone over that bridge has stood in that little house and told it the heaviest thing they had, and it's never told anybody. Not once. In the life of the road." She looked at him. "That's what it's for, Ice. It's not a person. It's not me. It's not Gabe. It's a thing that's built to hear all of it. All the way through. And keep it. And not need to do anything about it."

He didn't say anything.

"You've carried it on your own for two lives," said Thea. "And then you told me bits, and that helped. I know it helped. I've watched it help." Her hand tightened. "Tell it all of it. Once. To something that can stand to hear it. And then it won't be just you that's kept it."

"And you?"

"I'll be outside," said Thea. "On the step. With everybody." Something happened at the corner of her mouth. "It's a very crowded step."

He laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him like a cough, on the edge of a gorge in the moonlight, and Thea laughed too, quietly, with her head on his shoulder.

On his belt, the little light hummed. Her song. The middle wrong.

They both stopped laughing.

"It still gets that bit wrong," said Thea.

"Every time."

"I'll teach it," said Thea. Very quietly. Into his shoulder. "Some night. When we're up."

"Up where?"

"Wherever it is." She lifted her head and looked at the bridge. At the white mist under the moon, and the pale arches going out into it, and the far end that nobody had ever seen. "Wherever that goes."

***

They walked back up to the End at midnight.

The yard was dark, and most of the pilgrims were asleep, rolled in blankets on the warm stones, or inside. But not all. Sorrel was still on the wall. The boy beside her, asleep now, with his head on her shoulder. She was sitting very still so she wouldn't wake him, looking down at the tollhouse in the moonlight.

She looked at Ice as he came through the gate. Sideways. With her bright black eyes.

"Tomorrow?" said Sorrel.

"Tomorrow."

She nodded. Slowly. She looked at the boy on her shoulder. At his cord, round his thin neck. Six knots.

"Me too," said Sorrel. "First thing." She was quiet. "Before I lose my nerve."

"Is it light enough?"

Sorrel looked at him for a long time.

"The one with the leaves says so," she said. "The quiet one. He said the road's been carrying it with me." She looked down at the tollhouse. "I've been sitting here all night trying to feel if it's true."

"Is it?"

"I don't know," said Sorrel. "That's the thing. I don't know if it's light enough. I won't know till I'm in there." She smiled. All the creases going at once. "Thirty years and I still won't know till I open my mouth."

He looked at her. An old woman on a wall at the end of the world with a sleeping boy on her shoulder and a cord with six knots, worn smooth.

"Me neither," said Ice.

Sorrel looked at him for a long moment more. Then she nodded, as if he'd told her something she'd been waiting a long time to hear from somebody.

"Go to bed, then," said Sorrel. "Both of you. Tomorrow's a long day."

They went in.

He lay down on the floor of the long room at the End, between Thea and the wall, with Gabe snoring three blankets away and Tiger's tail across his feet and thirty-three more people asleep all round him, every one of them with six knots on a brown cord round their neck. And Gabe's terrible lantern on the floor by his hip. Humming.

He didn't sleep.

He lay there and went through it. All of it. Start to end. The way you'd go through a list before a long journey, checking you had everything. The white room. Floor 1. The first time. Every floor. Every name.

He'd never once in two lives gone through all of it, start to end, in one go. Not even in his own head. He'd always stopped. Somewhere. The pass, usually. Or earlier. He'd get to a place and his mind would go round it, the way water goes round a stone, and come out the other side, and go on.

He didn't go round anything.

He went through all of it. In the dark. On the floor of the last inn on the Long Road. With his hand on the little lantern on the floor beside him and Thea's breathing beside him and thirty-four people asleep who'd never asked.

It took until dawn.

*Ice*, said Alea. Very quietly, at the end. When the window over the door had gone grey. *You did it. All of it. You didn't stop once.*

"I know."

*How do you feel?*

He thought about it.

"Ready," said Ice.
