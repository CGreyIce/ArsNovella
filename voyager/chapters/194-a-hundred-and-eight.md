Kei watched her breathe.

He couldn't help it. It was what his head did now with everybody, but with her it did it all the time, from the moment he woke on the floor of the class hall in the grey painted morning to the moment he lay down again at night with Dose cold round his neck and his book on his chest. He watched her breathe on the stair behind the stove, coming down at first light, and counted the breaths it took her to get from the top to the bottom. Eleven, the first morning. Eleven the second. Twelve on the fifth, which kept him awake half the night until on the sixth it was eleven again and he realised she'd been carrying a basket of wet washing on the fifth.

He watched her colour. The red in her cheeks from the stove, and how long after she stepped away from the stove it took to go. He watched her hands, knotted and floury and quick, for a tremor, and found none. He watched her eat, which she did standing up, in bites, between doing other things, as if eating were a chore she'd have given to somebody else if she could. He watched her cough, once, on the fourth morning, when the smoke from the stove went the wrong way, and his whole body went cold, and then she said a word about the stove that would have made Newish proud and kicked it and the smoke went the right way and she didn't cough again.

He wrote all of it down. Twice.

On the seventh day she caught him.

***

He was sitting at the long table with his book, by the window, where the light was best. It was mid-morning. The Spoon was half empty; most of them were out, at the mill, at the class hall, at the smith's where Gabe and Salty and Jared had taken over the forge from an old man who'd been so astonished to have help that he'd sat down on an anvil and not got up again. Mother Crane was at the stove. Kei was watching her lift the big kettle. Both hands. A small grunt. Was that new? He didn't have a measure for her grunt from before. He wrote *grunt (kettle)* and put a question mark.

"Are you a doctor?"

He looked up. She hadn't turned round. She was pouring the kettle into the porridge pot with her back to him.

"Nearly," said Kei.

"What's nearly?"

He thought about it. "I know what's in things," he said. "And how much."

"Hm." She put the kettle down. She turned round, and wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at him with her small sharp eyes. "Well, stop it."

"Stop—"

"Looking at me. You've been doing it for a week. Every time I bend down, every time I lift something, there you are, with your little book, writing." She folded her arms. "It's rude. If you want to look at something, look at the soup."

Dose, round his neck, made a sound that Kei had only heard her make twice before, both times at Hako. It was a laugh.

*She's got you*, said Dose. *Look at the soup.*

"Sorry," said Kei.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Mother Crane jerked her chin at the kitchen. "If you're going to sit in my cookhouse looking at me all day you can do it from over here, with a knife. There's turnips."

So he did it from over there, with a knife.

It was better, after that. Much better. He could watch her all he liked from the kitchen, because in the kitchen everybody watched everybody, it was how you didn't get burned. She showed him where the turnips went and how she wanted them cut, which was differently from how Ice cut them and differently again from how he'd cut them at home on Earth, and she shouted at him when he did it wrong, and he did it right. And while he cut, he watched her. And while she stirred, she watched him watching her. And neither of them said anything about it again.

On the third day of turnips she stopped stirring and said, without turning round:

"What's in things, then?"

"Sorry?"

"You said you know what's in things. And how much. What's in this?" She held the spoon up. "Go on."

He looked at the pot. He smelled it.

"Barley. Mutton. Onion. Turnip, because I cut it. Thyme. Too much salt." He paused. "And something else. Something bitter. Right at the back."

Mother Crane looked at him over the spoon.

"Willow," she said. "A bit of bark. I put it in the winter pots. For the old men's knees. They come in here with their knees and their backs and they won't go to anybody, so I put it in the pot and say nothing, and they go home and their knees are better and they think it's my mutton." She sniffed. "It's not my mutton. Nobody knows that. You're the first one who's ever tasted it."

Kei put his knife down.

"How much?" he said.

She told him. He wrote it down. Twice.

"You're a strange one," said Mother Crane. "Why'd you want to know that?"

He didn't answer straight away. He looked at the pot, and the bitter at the back of it, and at her hands on the spoon.

"Because it's the right amount," he said.

***

He had the willow already. He'd carried bark from Larchwick since floor 2, topped up on every floor that had willows, dried and shaved and wrapped in waxed cloth. But hers was better. Floor 1 willow, from the trees along the river below the mill, where the bark came off in long pale strips that smelled green even dried. He went out with a knife on the ninth day and cut fresh, and dried it in the warm corner by her stove, and she watched him do it and didn't say anything, because she'd decided on the third day of turnips, he thought, that whatever he was doing was his business and she'd find out soon enough.

He had the river root. Ma Yarrow's, fresh from under the ice on two, washed and dried in long pale fingers in a cloth.

He had the honey. Lammas honey, from fourteen, dark and thick, in a jar Salty had sealed with wax.

He had the jar. Small and brown, with a cork in it, and a label in his own square separate letters. *FOR MOTHER CRANE.* And under that, smaller, *Maigo grew it*. He kept it in the inside pocket of his coat, against his chest, where Dose slept, and he took it out once a day and looked at it, and put it back.

Willow bark. River root. Honey. And a tiny dose of the dark blue flower.

Three days. And somebody sitting with her, day and night.

That was the whole of it. It was written twice on the inside back cover of his book, the pencil gone soft from five hundred days of carrying, the second copy squarer than the first. And under the second copy, in black ink, from a night on fourteen when he'd lain awake doing sums: *her?* And under that, from a dawn in the Long Barn: *yes*.

He had all of it. He'd measured all of it. He'd made the willow and the root into a decoction a dozen times, at a dozen strengths, and drunk them himself, and written down what each one did to his pulse and his sweat and his sleep. He knew the honey. He knew the root.

He didn't know the flower.

***

*No*, said Dose.

Kei felt it in his collarbone before he heard it, a sort of hiss under the word, the voice like a page turning in a library where somebody had just dropped a book.

*No. Absolutely not. You are an idiot. I have always known you were an idiot. I have said so. On every floor. But I did not think you were* this *kind of idiot.*

"I have to know where the edge is."

*You know where the edge is. Ma Yarrow told you. It's written down. Twice.*

"She told me the dose. For an old woman. For the fever." He had the jar open on his knee. It was late, the thirteenth night, and he was sitting on the bottom step of the stair behind the stove, the narrow one that went up to her room, because it was the only place in the Spoon where nobody was asleep. "She didn't tell me what it *does*. What too much feels like. Where it stops being the cure." He looked at the dried flowers in the jar. Dark blue, gone almost black, curled like little hoods. "The difference between a poison and a cure is how much. I've said that for years. I've never once not known where the line was. Not with the adder. Not with anything. I had the venom, I knew the venom, I knew exactly how much."

*You have never once given a cure to somebody who mattered this much*, said Dose.

He stopped.

*That's what this is*, said Dose. Quieter. *Not the measure. You know the measure. You're frightened. And you want to be frightened of something small, that you can hold, that you can put on your own tongue and feel go numb and say* there, *that's what it is, now I know it. Instead of the big thing. Which you can't hold.*

He didn't say anything.

*It's not the amount that frightens you*, said Dose. *It's the room. It's being in the room.*

He looked at the jar for a long time.

Then he took one flower out of it, very carefully, with the tips of his fingers in his cloth, and broke off one petal. One. Dried, dark blue, the size of his smallest fingernail. And with the point of his little knife he scraped off a speck of it, a grain, less than a grain, the amount you'd lose if you sneezed. And he put it on the tip of his tongue.

*Idiot*, said Dose, and put her head down on his collarbone, and stayed there, very close.

It didn't taste of anything at first. Then it tasted bitter. Then it didn't taste of anything, because his tongue had stopped. It went out like a lamp. Not pain. The opposite. A cold, spreading quiet, from the tip of his tongue back, into his lips, into his gums, into his cheeks, and then a tingle in his fingers, both hands, as if they'd been out in the snow on two without gloves. His heart went once, hard, and then slow. Slower. He counted it. He sat on the bottom step of Mother Crane's stair in the dark and counted his own heart with his numb fingers on his own wrist and wrote the numbers down in his book with a hand he could barely feel.

Then he wrote, under them, in letters that wandered: *That's a grain. A grain is the edge.*

And then he wrote it again.

***

He couldn't talk the next day.

His tongue came back in the morning, slowly, thick and stupid in his mouth, and by noon he could make the right shapes but not the right sounds. Hako found him at the long table at breakfast, trying to ask Mother Crane for salt and producing a noise like a goose. Hako looked at him. Hako looked at the jar, which Kei had forgotten to put back in his coat.

"You didn't," said Hako.

Kei made the goose noise.

"You *tasted* it." Hako sat down opposite him. "The *poison*. The *flower*. The one Ma Yarrow said not to touch with your *hands*. You put it in your *mouth*."

Kei held up one finger and thumb, very close together. *A grain.*

"Classic," said Hako. And then, a moment later, much more quietly, looking at him across the table with something in his face that wasn't a joke at all: "You absolute idiot. Classic."

Rachael found out by lunch. Rachael found out about everything by lunch. She came into the Spoon with her gloves off and her face like a slammed door and took his face in both her hands and looked at his tongue and his eyes and felt his pulse with two fingers and didn't say anything for a long time. Then she let go.

"How much?"

He held up the finger and thumb. *A grain.*

"Did you write it down?"

He nodded. He opened the book. She read it, upside down. The numbers, the wandering letters. *A grain is the edge.*

"Twice," said Rachael.

He nodded.

Rachael looked at the page for a while longer. Then she sat down next to him on the bench. She didn't touch him again. She didn't do that, unless she was mending.

"You're an idiot," she said.

He made a noise that was meant to be *I know*.

"I'd have done it too," said Rachael. "If it was mine." She looked at the jar. "Don't do it again."

He shook his head.

"And next time you're frightened," said Rachael, "come and find me. Before you put something in your mouth. I'm frightened too. It's better with two."

He looked at her. She was looking at the jar, not at him. Her gloves were on the table beside her hands, and her hands were very still.

He nodded again.

Mother Crane, at the stove, had heard every word. Kei knew she had. She didn't turn round. But at supper his bowl had no salt in it at all, and a great deal of honey, which was what you gave somebody whose mouth had gone wrong, and she put it down in front of him without looking at him and said, "Idiot," exactly the way Hako had.

***

The sick started coming in the third week.

He didn't know how they knew. He hadn't told anybody in Hearthstead he was nearly a doctor. But floor 1 was a small floor, and the egg girl had a mouth on her now, apparently, and by the middle of the third week there were people at the Copper Spoon's back door in the mornings. In the yard, among the hens. Standing very still in the painted snow, the way floor-1 people stood, as if they'd been placed there. The miller's father, with a cough that rattled. A woman from the tannery with a burn on her arm she'd been hiding under her sleeve for a month. A boy with a fever. Ordinary fever, the bright fast kind children got and shook off in two days; Kei knew it the moment he put his hand on the boy's forehead, and his whole body unclenched so hard he had to sit down on the step.

They didn't ask. That was the strange thing. They just came and stood in the yard. And when Kei went out, they looked at him. And then one of them, the first morning, the miller's father, said in a voice like a rusty hinge, very slowly, as if he were reading it off something:

"Is it you? That knows what's in things?"

Kei looked at him.

"Yes," he said.

"I've a cough," said the miller's father. And then stopped, as if that were as far as the words went, and stood there.

So Kei did the cough. He did it with willow and honey and a pinch of Ma Yarrow's root, the weakest of his twelve decoctions, and Malcolm stood beside him and did whatever it was Malcolm did, the blessing that made things go easier, that he never quite explained, always with a second one behind it in case the first one slipped. Rachael did the burn. Xola did the boy, with cold water on a cloth, sitting on the back step with him in her lap, singing something under her breath that Kei didn't know. They did it every morning after that. By the end of the third week there were a dozen a day.

"You're turning my yard into a sickroom," said Mother Crane.

She said it standing in the back door with her arms folded, looking at the miller's father on her bench with Kei's hand on his chest, and the tannery woman on the step with Rachael's gloved hands on her arm, and three hens on the tannery woman's head.

"Sorry," said Kei.

"Don't be sorry." She looked at them all a while longer. "Nobody ever came to anybody, here," she said. "Not with a cough. Not with anything. You got a cough, you kept it. You got old, you got older. You got a fever, you went to bed." She was quiet. "That's how it was. Always. My mother. Her mother. Nobody ever *asked*."

"They're asking now," said Kei.

"They are." She sniffed. "I don't know what you lot did. Coming down. But they're asking." She turned round to go back in. Then she stopped, in the door. "I'll do soup," she said. "For the yard. They'll want something hot. Coming out in this." And she went in.

Kei looked at the miller's father. He looked at the yard. At the hens, and the painted snow coming down in rows, and the people of Hearthstead sitting on Mother Crane's step being mended, who a year and a half ago had stood on corners saying the same three words to the air.

*They choose things now*, said Dose, very softly, round his neck. *Yan says. He's right. Look at them choosing.*

He looked.

***

The rota went up in the fourth week.

Drew did it. Of course Drew did it. Drew had a slate and a piece of chalk and a look on his face, and he came into the Spoon one evening after supper and went to the pantry door, the inside of it, where Mother Crane couldn't see from the stove, and started writing.

Kei had done the sum on fourteen. In the Long Barn, at the line, with Ice beside him on the floor and the straw man looking at them both. He'd said it out loud then. *Nobody's got three days to sit with an old woman. I did the sum. Thirty-six people. Three days. We've got a hundred and eight.*

He'd meant hours. Ice had known he meant hours. Ice had put his hand over his face.

Drew wrote it at the top of the pantry door in his square capitals. *WINTER WATCH.* And under it, a grid. Seventy-two hours, three days and three nights, in blocks of three. Thirty-six names. Every name once.

It took the whole evening, because everybody had an opinion. The healers at night, Kei said; the nights were the bad part, Ma Yarrow said, the third night most of all; so the third night was Kei and Rachael and Malcolm and Xola, and Maigo, if Maigo had leaves by then. Somebody who could cook in the mornings; Ice. Somebody who could make her laugh in the afternoons, said Newish, and put her own name down before anybody could argue. Calvin wanted the first night so he could be first; Rachael put him on the second so he'd have to wait. Gabe took the slot nobody else wanted, the one at four in the morning on the second night, and said it was because he was up then anyway. Thea took the one before dawn on the third. Chi took the one after Ice, wherever Ice's was, so she could sit across the room and be calm at him while he did it, and didn't explain, and nobody asked.

And then they got to Ice.

He'd been sitting at the long table the whole time, at the kitchen end, with his back to the pantry door, peeling an onion very slowly. He hadn't said anything. He hadn't turned round. When Drew got to the last empty block and said "Ice," he put the onion down.

"All of it," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"I'll do all of it," said Ice. "The whole three days. I'll be there anyway. Put me in every one."

"No," said Kei.

He hadn't known he was going to say it. He'd never said *no* to Ice. Hardly anybody had. Thea had. Gabe had, in the Long Barn, at the vote, *you don't get a vote on this one, mate*. Higu had, in her way. Kei hadn't. He'd measured Ice for four hundred and fifty-four days, and then for a hundred and twenty more, and he'd never once told him no.

Ice looked round at him.

"It's a sum," said Kei. "That's the whole point of it. That's why I did it. Nobody's got three days. *You* haven't got three days. Ma Yarrow said it takes somebody sitting with her day and night, and I thought, that's not one person. That can't be one person. One person can't do that. One person tries to do that and on the third night, when it's worst, when it matters, they're asleep in the chair. Or they're so tired they can't see her. Or they're so frightened they can't think." He was surprised how steady his voice was. "So I did it with thirty-six. A hundred and eight. Three each. That's the measure. That's the right amount."

"Kei—"

"You've got three hours," said Kei. "Like everybody. That's what I worked out. That's what I've got. You do your three, and then Chi does hers across the room from you, and then you go and *sleep*, and somebody else sits with her, and she's not on her own for one second of it, and neither are you." He stopped. He found his hands were shaking. He put them flat on the table. "That's the remedy. All of it. Not just the flower. The flower's the easy bit."

The Spoon was very quiet. Mother Crane, at the stove, had stopped stirring. She wasn't looking at them. She was looking at the pot. But she'd stopped.

Ice looked at Kei for a long time.

Kei didn't know what his face was doing. Hyvred would have known. Hyvred would have seen his colour. He thought it was probably something like the colour of somebody standing at the edge of a hole nobody had dug yet, and being told by somebody else that they'd dig it with him, and that he'd only have to hold the spade for three hours.

"Okay," said Ice.

Just that. Very quietly.

"Okay," said Kei.

Drew wrote it. One block. Three hours. *ICE.* On the first night, at the beginning, because that was where Ice would want to be, and Kei had told Drew that too, privately, on the stairs, because a measure you didn't let somebody keep wasn't a measure, it was a punishment. And right after it, *CHI*.

And at the bottom of the door, in very small letters, in the corner, Drew wrote: *SOMEBODY THANK KEI.*

Hyvred, behind him, read it out loud. And then said, "Thank you, Kei." And then everybody did. All of them. Round the Spoon. Thirty-five people, one after another, and Kei sat at the long table with his hands flat on it and his face going hot and didn't know where to look, so he looked at the soup.

Mother Crane, at the stove, said, without turning round, "What's he done?"

"Sums," said Hako.

"Hm," said Mother Crane. And then, after a moment: "What's the watch for? On my pantry door?"

Drew didn't miss a beat. "Winter," he said.

"Winter," said Mother Crane.

"It's a long one. We thought we'd take turns."

Mother Crane looked at the pantry door, at the grid of names, at *WINTER WATCH* across the top. She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at Kei. Then she turned back to her pot.

"Well, nobody's sitting up with me," she said. "I sleep like the dead."

Ice put his hand over his face.

***

Hyvred found him on the back step that night.

He'd gone out to breathe. The yard was empty, the hens asleep in their house, the painted snow coming down in its rows over the river and the mill and the dark shapes of the willows. It was very cold. He sat on the step with his coat round him and the jar against his chest and Dose curled tight on his collarbone, and Hyvred came out and sat down beside him with her glasses on, which meant she was looking at things.

"You were very brave," she said. "In there."

"I wasn't."

"You were. I saw. Your colour went all—" She made a shape with her hand, upward. "Like a lamp somebody turned up. And then it stayed up. Even when he looked at you." She was quiet. "Nobody stays turned up when he looks at them like that. Except Thea."

Kei didn't say anything.

"Do you want to know what colour she is?" said Hyvred.

He looked at her.

"Mother Crane," said Hyvred. "I've been looking. I've been looking since the door. I didn't tell anybody because I didn't know if it'd help or make it worse." She looked up at the window above the kitchen, small and dark under the eaves. "She's warm. Kitchen-coloured. Like the inside of an oven when the bread's nearly done. Steady. It doesn't flicker." She was quiet. "It's very strong, Kei. For somebody her age. It's the strongest old light I've seen on any floor."

Kei let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the Long Barn.

"And him?"

Hyvred didn't answer for a while.

"He's like somebody holding a match in the wind," she said. "With both hands. All the time. Even asleep." She took her glasses off. "He's been like that since the door. He won't be anything else till it's over."

"I know."

"So we hold the wind off," said Hyvred. "That's all. That's the hundred and eight." She put her glasses in her pocket. "Yan's got a skill like that. Shelter. Did you know? He cups his hands round a light and the wind can't get at it. His hands go white."

"I knew."

"He hasn't been in her room. He won't go near the stair." Hyvred looked at the dark window. "I asked him why. He said, *wherever I stand, there's an edge. I'm not putting one by her bed.*" She was quiet. "I think that's the nicest thing I've ever heard him say."

They sat on the step. The snow came down in rows.

***

On the twenty-sixth night Kei gave Rachael the second copy.

He'd copied it out again, on a clean page, the squarer one, every letter separate, and torn the page out of his book, which he'd never done before, and folded it in four. He found her on the bench by the fire at the end of the Spoon, with Calvin asleep against her shoulder, and he held it out.

She took it. She unfolded it and read it. Then she looked up at him.

"In case one of us gets wet," said Kei.

Rachael looked at him for a long moment.

"Statistically," she said.

"Statistically, one of them was going to."

She folded it again, in four, along his folds, and put it inside her glove. The left one. Against her palm.

"I'll be in the room," she said. "The third night. With you."

"I know. I wrote it."

"I know you wrote it." She looked at the fire. "I'm frightened."

"Me too."

"Good," said Rachael. "Then it's two."

He went and lay down in the class hall with the jar against his chest and his book on top of it. He didn't sleep. He opened the book in the dark and turned to the inside back cover, by feel, and found the place under *yes* with his thumb.

He wrote a number there. In the dark. He couldn't see it. He knew what it said.

*609.*

*It's the right amount*, said Dose, very softly, against his neck. *You've measured everything there is to measure.*

"It's not enough."

*No*, said Dose. *It never is. That's not the amount's fault.*

"Then whose is it?"

Dose was quiet so long he thought she'd gone to sleep.

*It's who's in the room*, she said at last. *That's the rest of it. That was always the rest of it. You knew that on fourteen. You did the sum.*

He lay in the dark with the book on his chest and thought about thirty-six names on a pantry door.

*A hundred and eight*, said Dose. *Go to sleep.*

He went to sleep.
