Floor 3 smelled like a pond, because it was one.

Ice came out of the door at the top of a hundred and sixty steps into air so warm and wet it was like walking into a mouth. After eight days of snow it was almost unbearable. He stood on the top step in the white Hartshorn Cloak with the sweat coming out on his face, and looked.

The Fen.

It went on forever. Flat, green-brown, under a low, soft, yellowish sky that wasn't a sky, hazed with heat. Reed beds taller than a man, in great rustling blocks. Channels of black water winding between them, still as glass. Islands of tussock and mud. Trees standing up out of the water on their own, grey and dead and drowned, with moss hanging off them in long beards. Clouds of small flies. The croak of something enormous a long way off. And over everything, everywhere, the smell: green and rotten and sweet, like flowers left too long in a vase.

The sun was going down its rail. In the dusk, out over the water, the lights were coming on.

Not stars. Not yet. Low lights, out over the black channels, pale and soft, the size of a fist. Drifting. Bobbing slowly over the water, just above the surface, the way a lantern bobs when somebody carries it along a path. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Out across the whole width of the Fen, as far as he could see, little pale lights drifting through the dusk, as if a great many people were out on the water carrying lamps.

There was nobody on the water.

"Don't look at them," said Alea.

"I know."

"I mean it, Ice. Don't look at them for long. You know what they do."

"I *know*, Alea."

He looked at them anyway. Just for a moment. One of them, the nearest, was bobbing along a channel about fifty metres away, pale and soft and warm. As he watched, it seemed to slow, and turn, and drift a little toward him. As if whoever was carrying it had seen him on the steps and wanted to say hello.

He looked away.

***

The first time, the wisps had taken four of them in the first week.

Not killed. Taken. That was the word they'd used afterwards, round the fire, very quietly. You'd be walking along a raised path through the reeds in the evening, and a light would come drifting along the channel beside you. And it wouldn't look like a wisp. That was the trick. It would look like *home*.

A window, lit up yellow, at the end of a dark street. The glow of a phone screen on a bedside table in a dark room. A porch light. The light under a door, the one you'd stood outside a hundred times as a child, waiting to be let in. Whatever light you most wanted to walk toward. And you'd walk toward it, off the path, into the reeds, into the water. And the water in the Fen was black and deep and full of things. And by the time you realised your boots were wet, you were up to your chest.

Four of them, in the first week. Calvin, then Hako, then Bee. And Thea.

He'd been the one who'd gone in after Thea.

He remembered it very exactly. It was the fourth night. She'd been walking ahead of him on the reed path, quiet. And then she'd just stepped off it, into the reeds, toward the water, with her hands at her sides and her face very calm. He'd shouted her name and she hadn't turned. He'd gone in after her. The water had been warm, like a bath, and black, and up to his waist, then his chest, and she'd been in front of him walking deeper. He'd got her by the back of her jersey and hauled. She'd fought him. She'd actually fought him, hard, all the way back to the path, with her eyes on the light.

Then the light went out, and she'd stopped fighting. She'd sat on the path in the mud, soaked, shaking. And she'd looked at him with her blue eyes, and said, very quietly, *it was home. The light that's always on when I'm coming back late.*

He'd sat down in the mud next to her. He hadn't said anything. There wasn't anything to say. They'd sat there until the shaking stopped.

That was year one. Floor 3. It had been the first time he'd thought, sitting next to her in the mud, *oh. Oh no. Not again.*

He'd been right. It had been again. It had taken ten years and ninety-seven floors, and then she'd stepped in front of something on the hundredth, and it had ended.

"Ice," said Alea.

"I'm fine."

"You're standing on the top step with your eyes shut."

"I'm fine," he said again, and opened them, and went down into the Fen.

***

He didn't go to the town.

There was a town; there always was. A village of reed houses up on stilts, in the middle of the Fen, joined by walkways of planks and rope. Eel-fishers in flat boats, smoke from a hundred little cookfires, voices across the water at night. Reedmoor. He'd spent a long time there the first time. He knew the eel-woman who sold the best smoked eel and the old man who mended punts, and the girl who'd shown them which paths through the reeds were safe.

He didn't go. Larchwick had been easy to avoid: one gate, one road, a stockade. But Reedmoor was all walkways and windows and people who sat out on their platforms in the evening, watching the water. If he walked into Reedmoor, everybody would know there'd been a Climber there before the rest. And thirty-five people would arrive a few weeks from now, and somebody would say *oh, another one*. And somebody would ask *what did he look like*, and somebody would tell them.

*One's a warning. Two's a pattern.* He'd left a coat. He'd left onion skins in the snow by accident. He'd lit a tower that the whole of floor 2 could see. He was running out of things he could afford to leave.

So he made a camp on a tussock in the reeds a long way from anywhere: an island of mud and roots and long grass about the size of a room, above the waterline. He cut reeds for a bed and lit a fire with the flint from his vest. He hung Mother Crane's empty broth pot over it and caught fish in the channel with a line Alea talked him through tying. The fish were long and eel-like and tasted of mud. He cooked them with the last of the wild onions from floor 2.

He didn't answer that.

Alea didn't push. She lay down by the fire, very small, with her chin on her paws, and looked at him with her two white pinprick eyes.

***

He was still capped. *Lv 10, held.* He'd been capped since the fifth day.

He knew what was coming. He'd been waiting for it since the chapel. The Testament had three lines about the third light, and he'd read them two thousand times. Every time he read them, he'd thought: *this is the one I couldn't have done. Even if I'd found the book in time. Even if I'd been here in the first week.*

*Where all the lights lie, the third light is kept.*
*Follow no light but your own.*
*The Knight who keeps it does not carry the light, nor stand in it. He is it.*

He'd never understood the last line. He'd thought he did, on the floors after, when he'd read it in the dark: *he is it*, like a metaphor, like a song. But he'd learned something on floor 1, in a doorway, at seven percent health. Things in the Testament that sounded like songs usually weren't. They were instructions, written by somebody who'd known exactly what they meant and had no other way to say it.

*Where all the lights lie.* The Fen. Hundreds of lights, every one of them a lie.

*The third light is kept.* Somewhere out there, in the middle of the Fen, among all the false lights, there was a true one. Or there had been, once.

He knew where. He'd seen it the first time, from a distance, without knowing what it was. A tiny island right in the heart of the Fen, where the water was deepest and the wisps were thickest. Nobody had ever gone near it, because you couldn't get there without walking through the wisps. On the island was the top of a tower, half drowned, grey stone sticking up out of black water like the last tooth in a jaw.

"When?" said Alea.

"When the trial opens."

"It's open now. It's been open since you came up the stair. You're at ten. You can do it any time."

"I know."

"Then *when*?"

He looked at the fire for a long time.

"When I'm ready to walk past her," he said.

***

He spent four days getting ready.

Not levelling; he couldn't level. Everything he killed went into the pool, *held*. There was a lot to kill on floor 3. Things in the black water that came up under you and dragged you down: long, grey, sinewy things with too many teeth, which the Reedmoor fishers called mirelings. Herons as tall as he was, grey and silent, that stood in the reeds on one leg and struck with beaks like spears. Leeches the size of his forearm. He killed them all, steadily, methodically, the way he'd killed sheep and wolves. He kept his back to something. He watched where things would be, not where they were. And every night he went back to the tussock and cooked fen eels with onions, and ate, and looked at the wisps.

The mirelings were the worst. On the second afternoon one of them nearly had him.

He was crossing a channel on a fallen tree, a grey drowned trunk lying half in the water from one tussock to the next, the way he'd crossed a hundred channels the first time. Halfway over, the water beside the trunk went flat. Not still: *flat*, the way water goes when something big is right under the surface and has stopped moving.

He knew that flat. He'd known it for ten years. He still wasn't fast enough.

It came up out of the black water in a single grey whip of muscle, as long as two men, all sinew and teeth. It took him round the ankle and pulled. He went off the trunk sideways into water that was warm as a bath and black as ink, and down, and the light went green and then went out.

He didn't panic. He'd panicked the first time, and it had nearly killed him. This time he let it pull him down. He let the water close over his head and counted. *One. Two.* And on *three*, when it had to turn to drag him deeper, he got the edge of the Vigil Shield round and down and into the place where its head met its neck, as hard as he could, in the dark, by feel.

It let go.

He came up gasping beside the trunk with the shield still on his arm, and hauled himself out onto it. He lay there on the grey wood, in the hot wet air, coughing up a great deal of the Fen. The mireling came up once more, a long way off, and looked at him with small yellow eyes, and decided he wasn't worth it.

He lay there until he stopped coughing. Then he got up and crossed the rest of the trunk very carefully, watching the water for flat.

He made himself look at them a little longer every night.

The first night, he looked for a few seconds. They drifted. They bobbed. One came a little toward him across the water, and he looked away.

The second night, he looked for a minute. And one of them, slowly, as he watched, stopped being a pale fist-sized ball and started to be something else. Square. Yellow. A lit window, in a building he couldn't see, at the end of a street he couldn't see. His mother's window. The one in the flat in Singapore, years ago, before she was ill. He'd come home late from school and seen it lit up from the bottom of the road, and known she was in.

He looked away.

The third night, he looked for longer. And this time it wasn't his mother's window. It was a white room. Thirty-six circles on the floor. Thirty-five people asleep, and the light from all their rising pillars, and a man in a doorway, standing very still.

He looked away fast.

The fourth night, he didn't look away.

He sat on the tussock by the fire with his hands round a bowl of eel and onion. *Resist Lure.* He watched a wisp come drifting toward him across the black water, and watched it change.

It was a ward.

A wall of rewritten light, standing up in the air. White, shot through with lines like code, like a page whose source you could read. A ward he hadn't known she could make. And behind it, very faint, just the edge of a black-and-pink-and-blue ponytail, turning.

He sat very still.

He didn't move toward it. He didn't move at all. He sat on the tussock with the bowl in his hands, and the ward hung there over the black water, and he looked at it. He looked at it the way he hadn't let himself look at anything since the white room: properly, all of it, for as long as it took. And he let it hurt exactly as much as it hurt.

It was a long time.

Then the wisp drifted on, along the channel, pale and soft. And after a while it was only a light again, bobbing away into the dark among all the others.

Ice put the bowl down. His hands were shaking.

"That one," said Alea, very quietly. "That was the last one, wasn't it."

"Yeah."

"The one where she stepped round you."

"Yeah."

The fox was quiet for a long time.

"You didn't follow it," she said.

"No."

"Are you ready?"

He looked out at the Fen. At all the lights, hundreds of them, drifting out over the black water in every direction. And somewhere in the middle of them, so far away he couldn't see it, there was a drowned tower, with a lantern that had been dark for a very long time.

He thought about Thea in the mud on the reed path, ten years ago. *It was my mum's kitchen.* He'd sat next to her until she stopped shaking. Then for ten years, without either of them ever saying it, he'd been the one who walked next to her on every path, at every edge, at every bit of dark water. Just in case.

And on the hundredth floor, she'd stepped round him. Into the one place he couldn't follow.

"Tomorrow night," he said.

***

He opened the logs that night. He hadn't meant to.

Alea had kept everything since the Fold, as she'd promised: World, whispers, all of it, waiting. He'd been not reading it for twelve days. Gabe's whisper sat at the top of his window every morning, *sent*, and he read that, because Alea had made sure he would. The rest he'd left alone, the way you leave a room in a house.

But he was sitting on a tussock in the Fen, and he'd just watched Thea's ward drift away over the water. And he thought, quite suddenly, that he wanted to know if she was all right.

"World," he said. "From the floor 2 clear. Just skim it."

Alea opened it. He scrolled.

*bring a coat. bring two coats.* Wendy. He laughed. *WOLVES GO ROUND. WATCH YOUR BACK.* Tiger. *heavy arrowheads fix drift. ask me.* Salty.

He sat on the tussock and looked at those two lines for a long time.

"She's reading the windows," said Alea quietly. "Already. The way she did on floor fifty."

"Yeah."

"She's faster this time."

"She's got a reason to be," said Ice. It came out strange. He cleared his throat. "She's looking for something."

He closed the log. He didn't read any more. He didn't need to. He sat by the fire and looked out at the Fen, at all its false lights. And for the first time in twelve days, he let himself think about the end of it. Not the hundredth floor. The other end. The end where they were all upstairs and safe, and he could stop, and answer the whisper. And somebody with a black-and-pink-and-blue ponytail, who read windows like code and had found a padlock in a week, would look at him. And he'd have to find a way to say all of it.

He didn't know how. He had a long time to work it out.

Tomorrow night, he'd walk past her.
