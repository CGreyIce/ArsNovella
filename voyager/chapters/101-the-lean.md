Bee had drawn the Down thirty-one times, and she only noticed on the thirty-first.

She'd started on the first evening. She always did. A new floor, a new view, the sketchbook out before she'd even put her pack down. On floor 8 she'd drawn Wendmere hanging across the falls until her hand cramped. On floor 9 she'd lain on her stomach on the edge of the drum platform with her wings folded flat and drawn the Down: the dark going away under the seven, the great trunks going down into it, the green, the ferns. Then she'd drawn it again in the morning, because it had changed. Then again in the evening. Because it was the floor where things changed when you weren't looking, and if you drew it every time you looked, you'd catch it doing it.

That was the idea. She hadn't caught anything. It just changed. The paths went somewhere else every night, the fern-gaps moved, the hollows filled. It was like drawing the sea. You could draw it a thousand times and none of them would be wrong and none of them would be the same.

And then for eight mornings she'd drawn the seedling, from exactly the same place, at exactly the same time, while Gigi knelt in the ash and grew her hedge back and Thea held the lantern. And on the evening of the eighth, after Gigi said *that* and looked out over the edge of the platform at all of it, Bee had gone back to the hanging house and taken every drawing of the Down she'd done on floor 9 out of the book, and laid them out on the floor in rows. Thirty-one of them. In the foxfire.

And looked at them.

It was the ferns.

She'd drawn them the same way every time, without thinking. Quick lines. Charcoal. A stroke for each frond, curving, the way they curved. She'd never looked at which way they curved. Why would you? Ferns curved. They curved toward the light.

Except there wasn't any light. Not down there. Not the kind that comes from one side. There was only the green coming down from straight above, the same everywhere, all day.

And in every drawing, all thirty-one, every fern she'd ever put down leaned the same way.

***

"South-west," said Bee.

She'd got Hyvred up. Hyvred was sitting cross-legged on the floor in her nightshirt with her glasses on and her hair everywhere, looking at the drawings. Gigi was awake too, because Gigi had been awake anyway, lying on her back in the blankets staring at the woven ceiling the way she had since the seedling.

"Every one," said Bee. "Look. This one's from the first night. This one's from the drum platform, this one's from the bridge to the Youngest, this one's the seedling, the eighth morning. Every single fern. It's like—" She stopped. She didn't have a word. She put her finger on the paper. "It's like hair. Round the crown of your head. You know how it all goes round one place? Or iron filings. Round a magnet."

Hyvred leaned forward. Bee saw the little red mark in her golden eye go sharp.

"It moves," said Hyvred, after a long time.

"What?"

"Look at the first ones, and the last ones." Hyvred put one finger on the first drawing and one on the thirty-first. "The lean. It's not quite the same way. It's moved. A little."

Bee looked. Hyvred was right. On the first night the lines had all leaned a hair more to the south. On the thirty-first, a hair more to the west. As if the thing they were leaning toward had moved, over thirty days, a little way.

She'd drawn it every time. She'd drawn it moving. And she'd never once seen it.

"Okay," said Gigi, from the blankets. Very quietly. "Okay. That's horrible."

"It's not horrible," said Bee. "It's *useful*." She sat back on her heels. "If they're all pointing at something. And I draw them from somewhere else—"

"Then you get another line," said Hyvred slowly.

"And where the lines cross—"

"That's where it is."

Bee looked at the thirty-one drawings on the floor. Thirty-one mornings and evenings, not looking.

"I need to go and draw it from somewhere else," she said.

***

She drew it from four places the next day.

The drum platform, again, to be sure. The far end of the long bridge to the Youngest, with Tansy hanging off the rail beside her asking what she was doing every ten seconds. The highest platform on the east tree, which was so high it swayed, and Bee's wings hummed the whole time without her asking them to. And the end of the high way going south-west out of Highcrook, a branch as wide as a road that went out from the seven over the Down toward somewhere else, as far as Burr would let her go on it, which was a quarter of a mile.

Every time, she lay on her stomach and drew the Down under her, the ferns, quickly, without thinking about it, the way she always had. And then she looked at which way they leaned.

Higu put them together in the evening.

She had her map of the ceiling spread across the floor of the round house on the far side of town, the one she'd claimed for maps. All the great trees, as far as she could see from Highcrook, and the high ways between them. And she took Bee's five drawings and worked out where each one had been drawn from, and drew a line on the map from each place, in the direction the ferns leaned.

Five lines. Going out from Highcrook across the map, south-west, not quite parallel.

They crossed.

Not exactly. Not at a point. In a small rough place, a long way south-west, past the edge of anywhere the bough-folk built, between three great trees Higu had drawn very small because they were so far off. A day out, if you walked. If you could walk straight in the Down, which you couldn't.

Higu put her pencil down on it.

"There," she said.

Bee looked at the place. It was just a blank bit of map. Between three trees.

"What's there?" she said.

Nobody knew. Then Hyvred went to look.

***

She went up the east tree at dusk, to the high platform that swayed, with her glasses off.

Bee went with her, because Hyvred didn't go anywhere high without Bee if she could help it, and because Bee wanted to see Hyvred see it. They stood at the rail at the very top of Highcrook, as high as the bough-folk had ever built, with the leaves of the great canopy close over their heads like a ceiling you could touch, and the Down so far below that it was just dark.

Hyvred looked south-west.

Bee watched her. She'd watched Hyvred look at things for two hundred days now, on this world, and she still didn't know what it was like. Hyvred had tried to tell her. *Magic's got a colour. Everything that's been done leaves a mark.* Kei's healing was green, Gigi's thorns brown-gold, Bee's own drawings honey-yellow, which Bee had liked very much. And Ice's was amber, like a lamp in a window on a winter night, and nobody else in the world had that one.

Hyvred looked for a long time.

"It's grey," she said.

"What is?"

"All of it. The Grow." Her voice had gone flat and careful. "I've seen it every day. It's always been a sort of grey. Faint. Like dust on a window. Everywhere. I thought that was just what it looked like." She was quiet. "It's moving."

"Moving where?"

"There." Hyvred lifted her hand and pointed, south-west, at nothing, at the dark. "All of it. Very slowly. Like dust going down a drain. All of it is going the same way." She stopped. "Bee. It's not going *out* from there. It's going *in*."

Bee looked south-west. She couldn't see anything. Dark and more dark.

"And there," said Hyvred, "where it all goes. There's nothing."

"What do you mean, nothing?"

"I mean there's no colour. No light. Nothing." Hyvred's hand had started to shake a little. She put it down on the rail. "It's a hole. Like on six. The thing in the bowl. Like wallpaper where a picture's been. It's—" She stopped. "It's the same."

Bee stood beside her at the top of the world and didn't say anything for a while.

She remembered floor 6. A bowl of gold glass in the noon light, and *the lamp's on, I left the lamp on*, and a hole in the shape of a person with glittering edges. She'd been the one who'd seen her own front door.

"The same as the Wanting?" she said.

"The same *kind*," said Hyvred. "Not the same thing. The same kind of thing."

The tower's kind, thought Bee. She didn't say it.

***

Maigo said it was a sower.

He said it that night, on the drum platform, in front of everybody. He'd gone and sat with his hand on the north tree for an hour while Hyvred was on the high platform, not rooted, just touching, the way Linden did. *Heartwood. It will tell you what it remembers, in its own time.* And when he came back his leaves had gone stiff and dark, and he sat down in the middle of all of them with his knees up and didn't say anything for a long time.

"It came down," he said, eventually.

Everybody waited.

"The great trees remember it," said Maigo. "Not when. Trees don't do when. But before the bough-folk. A long way in. Hundreds of rings. Before there was any Grow at all." He pressed his hands together. "The Down used to be open. Under the leaves. Moss and old leaves and roots. Dark. Quiet. The way under a forest is supposed to be. And then something came down."

"Down from where?" said Chris.

"I don't know. Up. It came down out of the leaves and knelt on the ground, a long way that way." Maigo pointed south-west without looking. "And it put its hands in the earth. And the Grow came up out of its hands." He was quiet. "The trees didn't like it. They don't like anything fast. They felt it come up all round their roots and they didn't have any way to say so." He looked at his own hands. "It's been there ever since. It never moved. Not once. Until a month ago."

"A month ago," said Higu.

"When we came," said Maigo.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"It was never planted," said Gigi, very quietly. "That's what it means. Something grows here that was never planted. The Grow wasn't planted. It just came out of something's hands."

"Find its root," said Maigo.

"Pull it up," said Gigi.

They looked at each other across the platform, the tree and the girl who could tell trees what to do.

And then, because that was the rule now, everybody looked at Ice.

***

He'd been sitting on a coil of rope at the edge of the platform the whole time, with his bad leg out in front of him, listening. Bee had been watching him. She watched people when she was drawing them in her head, and she'd been drawing him in her head on and off for three weeks, since she'd drawn him asleep. He'd gone very still when Hyvred said *hole*. He'd gone stiller when Maigo said *it came down*.

"You say where," said Higu.

"I can't," said Ice. "I told you. Nothing stays where it was."

"We know where it is," said Higu, patiently. "Bee found it. So tell us what."

He looked at her for a long time.

"It's tall," he said. "It kneels. It's white. Grey-white. Like the Quiet. It hasn't got a face." He stopped. "Its arms go into the ground up to here." He touched the inside of his own elbow. "It doesn't move. Not when you come. It just kneels there with its hands in the ground and the Grow comes up all round you."

Chris was writing.

"And how does it die?" said Hyvred.

"It burns," said Ice.

He said it too fast. Bee saw him hear it. She saw his face do the thing it had done at the top of the stair, on the first day, when Higu asked him where the boss was. Just for a second.

"It should burn," he said. More slowly. "I think. It's a plant. Mostly."

Chris didn't look up from his book. But Bee saw his pencil stop for a moment, and then go on.

She didn't say anything. Nobody did. She'd sat at the top of a pit on floor 6 with twelve other people, under the moon, and heard Thea say *regression*, and she'd kept it the way they'd all kept it, carefully, like a hot cup. *It burns.* As if he'd seen it. As if he'd stood in front of it once, in a life nobody else remembered, and watched it go up.

*It should burn,* she thought. *But you're not sure any more. Because things don't stay the way you remember them. Not on this floor. Not any more.*

"Fire, then," said Chris. And wrote it.

***

They went the day after next.

Not walking. You couldn't walk straight in the Down. They went by the high way, the long branch-road going south-west out of Highcrook toward the far towns, with three of Burr's people to guide them and Tansy, who hadn't been asked and came anyway. It went from tree to tree, the high way. Along great branches as wide as streets, and across rope bridges where the branches didn't meet, and down ladders and up ladders, and once along a branch so narrow the bough-folk walked it without hands and the thirty-six went along it on their knees. Bee's wings hummed the whole way. She couldn't stop them. Every time she looked down, the Down was a very long way below, and dark, and moving.

It took most of the day. By early afternoon they were past the last of the platforms anybody had built in a hundred years, and the guides were whistling to each other nervously, and the branches were old and furred with moss and creaked when you trod on them. And then the high way ended. At a great tree with nothing in it. A branch going out over the Down and stopping.

"That's as far as we go," said the oldest of Burr's people. "Nobody goes further. Nobody has for a long time." He looked down. "Your place is under there."

Bee looked down.

And saw it.

She didn't need Hyvred. She didn't need Higu's map. Below them, a long way down, through a gap in the lower branches, the Down was green and dark and moving, the way it always was. And then, in the middle of it, it stopped.

A circle. Perfect. Thirty metres across, maybe. Bare. Not a fern, not a briar, not a blade of anything. Just earth. Black earth, dry, cracked. With the Grow standing round the edge of it on every side so thick and high it was like a wall round a garden. And every fern on that wall, every stem, every briar, leaning in.

And in the middle, kneeling, something white.

***

They went down on ropes. All thirty-six, in the green light, onto the black earth inside the circle. Nothing stopped them. The Grow stood round the edge, leaning in, ticking, and didn't come over.

Bee stood with her sketchbook in her hand and looked at it.

It was exactly what Ice had said. That was the first thing. Tall, even kneeling. Taller than the river-folk standing, by a lot. Thin. Thin as a young tree, all the way up, with long thin arms and a long thin back bowed forward over its knees. Grey-white, smooth, like old bone, with moss grown on its shoulders and the back of its head in soft green patches, as if it had been kneeling there so long the forest had started to think it was a stone. Its arms went down into the black earth in front of it, both of them, up to the elbow. Where they went in, the ground was cracked in long lines going out across the circle, like the cracks round a stake that's been driven in.

It didn't have a face. Its head was bowed. Where a face should have been there was just more of the smooth grey-white, curving, nothing.

It didn't move. It didn't look up.

A window came up in front of Bee's eyes. And everybody's, she knew. She saw them all stop.

***

The white fire went out of his hands across the black earth and hit the Sower in the back, and it moved.

Not toward them. Down.

It happened so fast Bee nearly missed it. One moment it was kneeling with the white fire pouring over its bowed back and the moss on its shoulders going up in a puff of sparks. The next, its arms bent. And it went *down*. Into the earth. Through the cracks round its arms, as if the black ground were water and it had just let itself sink. Its back. Its head. Gone. In the time it takes to drop something.

The fire burned on the bare earth where it had been, white, on nothing.

And the Grow came over the wall.

All of it. All round the circle at once, the way it had come up at the log. Fern and briar and root and sapling, pouring in over the bare black earth from every side, ticking so loud it was a roar, and splitting as it came. Bee saw it split. She'd been told about this. She'd been in it once already, at the log. She still couldn't believe it. The walls came up between them in lines, in curves, fast as falling, and they came up *between parties*. Round Hana's five. Round Yan's. Round Deston's. Round—

"*Bee*," said Hako.

He had her by the arm. Hyvred was beside her, and Gigi, and Kei with Dose up and hissing. Five. A wall of briar going up all round them, dark red, thorned, closing over their heads. A room. A small one. Smaller than Ice had said the rooms were at the log. Small enough that Hako couldn't lift his spear.

Through the wall, somewhere, very close, she heard Drew shout: "*ICE. LIGHT. NOW.*"

And through the gaps in the briar, the amber came up.

She saw it. Warm and gold and steady, going up somewhere off to the left through the green, the way it had gone up in the dark on the night of the split. Ice, standing still. Making a light. Exactly as Drew's rock said.

*Anyone who can see you can find the way.*

But they could barely see it. They were in a room of thorns so close and thick that the amber came through in specks, like light through a sieve. And the way wasn't opening. There wasn't room for it to open. The walls were too close, and still closing, pressing in, and Hako had his shield up against the north side of it and his whole weight behind it and it was still coming.

"Cut it," said Hyvred.

"It grows back *twice*—"

"I *know*, cut it anyway—"

"Gi," said Kei. "Your hedge. Can you—"

"It won't *listen* to me." Gigi had both hands flat on the ground. Bee could see the brown-gold of her thorns coming up between her fingers and being swallowed, instantly, by the dark red of the Grow. "It's not mine. It's *his*. It's got its hands in *all* of it—"

The wall touched Bee's wing. She felt it. A thorn, through the thin clear skin, a bright line of pain.

She opened her sketchbook.

***

She didn't think about it. That was the only way it ever worked. If she thought about it, she drew it badly, and then it came out badly, and a badly drawn thing was worse than nothing. She'd drawn a sun once on floor 2 when she was frightened and it had come out cold.

She didn't think. She drew a door.

On a clean page. In the specks of amber coming through the briar. A rectangle. Tall. A plain wooden door, the kind on a garden shed, planks and a crossbar and a latch. Four lines and a latch. She did it in less time than it took Hako to shout her name. She drew it as if it were set in the wall of briar right in front of her, flat against the thorns, and she drew the latch last, and she drew it *up*, open.

The page went honey-yellow.

And in the wall of thorns in front of her face, there was a door.

A real one. Wood. Grey and weathered, with a crossbar and an iron latch, set into the briar as if somebody had built the hedge round it a hundred years ago. Exactly where she'd drawn it. Exactly as she'd drawn it, with the knot in the third plank that she'd put in without meaning to.

"*Bee*," said Hyvred.

"Thirty seconds," said Bee. "Maybe. *Go*."

Hako opened it.

On the other side, there was a path. Narrow, through the Grow, going away toward the amber. And it was *open*. The way the paths had opened on the night of the split. Straight at the light. Because now they could see it.

"Go, go, *go*—"

Kei went first, with Dose. Then Gigi, with her hands still shaking. Hyvred. Hako held the door. He stood with his back to it and his shield across the gap and his spear arm in the doorway, holding it open, the way he'd stood over Thea and Gigi on floor 6 like a door himself.

"*Bee*," said Hako.

She was still looking at the page. At the door she'd drawn, glowing honey-yellow in her lap. She could feel it. She could feel exactly how long it had left, the way you can feel a breath running out.

"Bee, *now*—"

She went through. Hako came after her.

Behind them, the door went. She didn't see it. She heard it. A soft sound, like a page turning, and the briar closing over the place where it had been with a dry rush, ticking.

***

The path went straight to the light.

They came out of it into a space in the Grow where everybody else was coming out too, from every side, out of seven paths, toward the middle. Toward Ice, standing in it with his left hand over his head and the amber pouring out of it, gold on everybody's face. Not moving. Exactly where he'd been standing when the walls came up. Waiting.

He looked at them as they came out. At Hako first. At her. His eyes went to the sketchbook in her hand, still faintly yellow, and to the line of blood on her wing.

"You drew a door," he said.

It wasn't a question. She didn't know how he knew. She didn't ask.

"Yeah," said Bee.

He looked at her for a moment longer. Then he nodded, once.

They went up the ropes from there. Burr's people had seen the light from the end of the high way, the way Highcrook had seen it on the night of the split, and let them down in the right place. The Grow was everywhere below them, pouring over the circle, over the black earth, until you couldn't tell where it had been. Ina's white fire was gone under it. The Sower was gone under it.

Hyvred stood at the end of the high way with her glasses off and looked down at it for a long time. Bee stood beside her.

"Where did it go?" said Bee.

Hyvred didn't answer for a while.

"It's still in the ground," she said. "I can see where it's been. Under the Down. It's like—" She stopped. "It's like a line of nothing. Going through the grey. Like a fish under ice." She turned her head. Slowly. Following something. "That way."

She was looking north-east.

Back the way they'd come. Along the high way. Toward the seven.

***

Maigo felt it arrive at midnight.

Bee was awake. She'd been awake since they came back up the ropes at dusk, sitting on the drum platform with her sketchbook in her lap and the page with the door on it, which had gone back to being just a drawing. Grey charcoal. A shed door with a knot in the third plank. She kept looking at it.

Maigo was sitting against the trunk of the north tree, where Linden sat in the daytime, with his hand on the bark.

And at midnight his leaves went stiff, all at once, and he made a sound.

"Maigo?"

"It's here," said Maigo.

He didn't open his eyes. His hand was pressed flat on the bark, the fingers spread, and his whole small body had gone tight, like something bracing.

"Under us," he said. "Under the seven. It's—" He stopped. "It's in the roots. The old ones. The great ones. It's put its hands down in between them." His voice was very small. "It's never been here. It never moved, in all those rings. It came down there and it knelt and it stayed. And now we've been, and burned it, and it's come here."

"Why?" said Bee.

Maigo opened his eyes. He looked at her through his sunglasses. And past her, over the edge of the platform, down. Toward the foot of the north tree. Toward a ring of stones and some ash and a young tree, taller than Gigi, standing on its own in the dark.

"I don't know," he said. "But it's right underneath her."

Down below, in the dark, the Grow at the foot of the seven had started to tick louder.

***

Ice found her on the platform an hour later.

He'd been on the rota, the midnight-to-two, with Kei. He came and sat down on the boards a little way off from her, carefully, with his bad leg out, and didn't say anything for a while. Below them, the ticking. Louder than it had been on any night since they came. All round the foot of the seven.

"Burr's doubled the watch," he said.

"I know."

"Gigi wanted to go down."

"I know. Thea sat on her."

He almost laughed. He didn't. They sat there.

"You drew me," he said, after a while. "When I was asleep. You never said why."

Bee didn't apologise. She didn't apologise for drawing things. You couldn't draw anything if you apologised for it.

"You looked different," she said.

"How?"

She thought about it.

"Younger," she said.

He didn't say anything to that.

She looked at him sideways. At the black hair falling over his eyes, the teal streak in it. The vest. The white shirt, filthy. The face. She'd seen it in the corner of Hyvred's streams, years ago, for four months, a name in a chat and a voice in a call. Funny, apparently. Everybody said he was funny. She'd never really talked to him. On the first morning, when everybody worked out he'd run off on his own a floor ahead of them, she hadn't been upset. She'd felt bad about that afterwards, a bit, when everyone else had been so upset.

"You hardly knew me," said Bee.

He looked at her.

"No," he said.

"Four months. On Hyv's streams. You said about nine words to me."

"Ten," said Ice. "I think it was ten."

"You put my name down anyway," said Bee. "On four. In that hall. Hyv said. All thirty-five. You put my name down with everybody else's." She looked at him. "Why? You didn't know me."

He was quiet for a long time.

"You were in the white room," he said.

"That's it?"

"That's all it takes."

Bee looked at him. And for a moment she thought about the round house on floor 6, and Thea in the dark, and the word, and all of it. A white room. A long time. Thirty-five names. Somebody who'd had ten years to forget a girl he'd said ten words to, and hadn't.

She didn't say any of that. She'd promised. They'd all promised.

She looked down at her sketchbook instead. At the door, with the knot in the third plank.

"Okay," she said. "Then I'm going to draw you properly. Awake. Doing something."

"Like what?"

"I don't know yet. Something stupid. You'll do something stupid. You always do, apparently." She shut the book. "You owe me. I drew a door to get to your light today."

"I know," said Ice. "I saw."

Down below, the ticking went on. Louder. Under the seven, in the roots, something with no face was kneeling with its hands in the dark, right underneath a young tree that a girl had come back to every morning for eight days.

Bee opened her sketchbook again, to a clean page, and started drawing the Down. From the drum platform. Exactly where she always drew it from.

Every fern she put down leaned toward the foot of the north tree.
