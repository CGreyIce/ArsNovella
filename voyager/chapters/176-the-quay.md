Azac had told a lot of stories about Dec, and never one with an ending.

That was the thing about Dec. On Earth, back home, a long time before any of this, it had been the two of them, mostly, in every group they were ever in. Dec in front. Azac talking. Dec would do the thing, whatever it was, the stupid brave thing or the clever quiet thing, and Azac would tell it afterwards, to whoever was there, and make it bigger, and put *and so our hero* at the front, and Dec would go red and tell him to shut up.

He'd been telling stories about Dec since floor 1. Out loud, under his breath, to himself, on stairs. *And so our hero punched a goat.* *And so our hero peeled half a sack of onions without being asked.* *And so our hero sat down in the snow.* He'd told them on the well-house steps in Tell to anybody who'd listen, and in the crypt under the Assize to the whole Bench.

And every one of them had a hole in it.

Not a hole you could see. A hole like a missing page. He'd get to the end of a story about Dec and know there was a bit he didn't have. Why Dec ran ahead, on floor 1. How he knew where the stair was. What he'd been looking for, reading the names on the Long Wall so fast. Why he looked at Calvin's arms like that. There was always a page missing, somewhere in the middle, and the story went on round it, and you could tell it was missing by the way the words went round.

On floor 14, standing at the doors of the Long Barn with his hands on Gabe's shoulders and the winter coming up the threads, Azac had heard Dec say every number.

All night. Out loud. Up a hill.

He'd thought about that for twenty days. On the stair, in Tell, on the Salt. He'd thought: *that's the first time. That's the first time I've ever had all of it.*

He didn't know yet what to do with that. He thought he might find out.

***

The stair from the Salt came up under the Last Light.

Four hundred and twelve steps. Up, this time, because floor 11 was above floor 12 and you went up to go back. Newish said that was cheating and counted them anyway. At the top there was a door, and Tiger opened it, and there was a cellar.

A real one. Low, stone, smelling of tar and lamp oil and the sea. Barrels. A coil of rope. Shelves of tins. A ladder going up to a square of light. And from up the ladder, a voice.

"*Who's that?*"

Old. Sharp. A woman's.

"Climbers," said Higu. "Coming up from below. Thirty-six."

There was a long silence from the top of the ladder.

Then a face looked down through the square of light. Small, brown, wrinkled as a walnut, in a black knitted cap, with an unlit clay pipe in the corner of its mouth. It looked down at them, all thirty-six, packed into a cellar under a lighthouse at the edge of the world with their packs on, and its eyebrows went up and up until they disappeared under the cap.

"Fifty years," said Wenna of the Light. "Fifty years I've kept this rock. And my husband before me, and his mother before him. And in all that time, nobody ever came up out of my cellar." She took the pipe out of her mouth and pointed it at them. "Nobody ever *went down* it either, till you lot."

"Sorry," said Higu.

"Don't be sorry. Come *up*. You're letting the cold in."

***

The house was the same.

Azac had been in it twice. Once on the evening of the three hundred and eighteenth day, wet, after the Undertow, with the whole of the *Patience*'s crew and Wenna counting faces like sheep through a gate. And once the next morning, before they went down. He remembered it the way you remember a room in a story. The low white house against the foot of the tower. The blue door. A rush chair by the stove with one leg shorter than the others. A blue enamel kettle, chipped at the spout. Shells on every shelf. A ship in a bottle on the windowsill. Low beams that Gabe hit his head on, both times.

Gabe hit his head on one again.

"*Every* time," said Gabe.

"Mind the beams," said Wenna, who'd watched him do it.

And the step.

The fourth step from the blue door, going down to the landing. Azac remembered it because Thea had done something strange on it, the first time, on the real one: stepped on it, on purpose, hard, so it rocked, and looked at nothing, and Azac had thought *that was for something* and never found out what.

Dec stepped over it.

Azac saw him do it. On the way out of the door, with Thea's hand in his, going down to look at the landing. He lifted his foot at the fourth step without looking down and stepped over it, the way you'd step over a sleeping cat, onto the fifth. Like a thing his feet knew.

And Wenna, in the doorway behind him, laughed.

A short dry bark of a laugh, with the pipe in her teeth. "You needn't," said Wenna. "It doesn't rock any more. The boy fixed it."

Dec stopped.

He looked back at the step. Then he stepped back up onto it. Carefully. With his weight. It didn't move. He stood on it and it was just a step. Stone, worn in the middle, solid.

"Kip," said Wenna. "Fixed it with a wedge of slate and some lime. In the summer." She jerked her pipe at the tower. "He keeps the shutter with me now. Since you went. Marrit brought him out on the second run and he wouldn't go back." She sniffed. "Twelve. Can't sit still. Knows every star." She looked at Dec standing on the fourth step, very still, looking down at it. "What? It's only a step."

"Nothing," said Dec.

But Azac saw his face. It was the face from the Mast, Dennis said, when Higu drew a line in the left-hand column. The face of a man finding out that a thing he'd been carrying for a long time had been put down while he wasn't looking.

He didn't know what it meant. He put it somewhere. He'd tell it later. Maybe.

Thea, holding Dec's hand, was looking at the step too. And then at Dec. And Azac saw her almost say something, and not.

***

Kip came down from the tower at dusk, with soot on his nose and the shutter-key round his neck on a string, and stopped dead in the door of the house when he saw them.

"You came *back*," said Kip.

Everybody kept saying that.

"Marrit's due," said Kip, when he'd got over it, which took about four seconds, because he was twelve. "Tomorrow. Or the day after. If the singer's let her." He looked at Wenna. "She comes every big tide. Since you went. Seventeen days out and seventeen back." He was very proud of it. "On the deep road. Nobody ever went on the deep road before. Now she goes on it every month."

"Brings me bread," said Wenna. "Takes my shells to sell in Hythe, the fool. Nobody buys shells in Hythe. Hythe's made of shells."

"They do buy them," said Kip. "She says they buy them because they're from the *Light*."

"Then they're fools too."

Azac sat by the stove and listened to them argue and thought, *that's a story*. An old woman alone on a rock for fifty years, and a boy who wouldn't go home. He didn't tell it. It wasn't his. He just listened, and put it with the others.

That night, in the tower, he heard the light go round. Three short and a long. And the long one wasn't the same length every time.

He asked Kip about it, in the morning.

"That's me," said Kip. "When it's me on the shutter. I can't hold the long one as long as she can. My arm gets tired." He looked embarrassed. "She says it doesn't matter. She says the sea knows it's somebody."

***

The *Patience* came on the second evening.

The singer came first. Aleaka heard it. She'd been sitting on the landing all afternoon with her feet over the edge and her ears up and Heed beside her, and at about four she stood up very fast and said "*Oh*," and everybody looked at her. And then, a long way out, in the grey water east of the rock, something lifted. Long. Pale. Grey-white like old bone. A ragged fin along its back with the low sun coming through it. And it hummed.

Azac felt it in the rock under his feet.

*You came*, Aleaka had said it said, the first time. On the night of the three hundred and first day, roped round the waist at the rail with Jared holding the line. *You came.*

She didn't say anything now. She stood on the landing with her ears flat and her face wet, and hummed back.

And behind the singer, on its wake, with her two patched sails and Corran's eleven years of oak and pitch, the *Patience*.

Marrit was at the wheel.

She brought the ship in to the landing on the last of the light the way she brought her in everywhere, close, fast, with about a hand's width to spare, and Pell threw a line and Hako caught it. And Marrit looked down off the quarterdeck at thirty-six climbers standing on the landing of the Last Light with their packs on, and Azac watched her face do what everybody's face had done on the way down.

"You came back," said Marrit.

"Everybody says that," said Calvin.

"Everybody's *right*," said Marrit. "Nobody comes back. Not from the Edge. Not from anywhere." She came down the ladder to the deck, and across, and stood at the rail. Her left arm was bare to the elbow. Wound round it, from wrist to elbow, brown to black, the top one faded, eleven cords. Corran's. No knot on any of them.

And no burn on the back of her hand.

Azac saw Higu look. He saw her look at the back of Marrit's left hand, where in Dec's left-hand column, on the first day on floor 11, there had been a burn, *from a lamp, as a girl*. And there wasn't. There never had been, this time. Higu didn't take the rock out. She didn't need to. She'd written it on the first day. *NO BURN.* She looked at it now, the plain brown back of Marrit's hand on the rail, and Azac saw her mouth move.

*Still.*

***

They sailed on the morning tide.

Seventeen days. East. On the deep road, with the singer under the hull, the way they'd come. Azac had been sick the first time for two days. He wasn't now. He'd got his sea legs on floor 11 and it turned out you kept them, the way you kept a song.

It was winter at sea. That was new. They'd sailed west at the end of spring, with long blue days and the singer humming under them through the warm nights. Now the days were short and grey and the sea was the colour of slate, and the swell came from the north, long and heavy, and there was ice on the rigging at dawn. Ottery made soup. It was very good soup. Dec said so, and Ottery looked at him the way people looked at Dec when he said food was good, as if he knew something about soup that nobody else did, and gave him seconds.

On the eighth evening, Marrit stopped the ship.

Azac didn't know why at first. They were nowhere. Grey water, all round, to the end of the sky. No rock. No mark. The singer had gone quiet under the hull. Marrit brought the *Patience* round into the wind and let her lie, and the sails went slack, and the whole ship went still on the swell.

Then Azac knew.

The evening turn. The eighth day east of the Edge.

Corran.

They'd put him over the side here. On the evening of the three hundred and eighth day. Sewn into the *Patience*'s new spare foresail with a Hythe ballast stone at his feet that Kip had chosen, and Kip ringing the bell once, and Marrit breaking the custom of three hundred years by saying his name out loud over the water. *Corran Patience. You can hear it if you like. I don't care. It's his.*

Marrit didn't know the place. Azac was sure of that. There was nothing to know it by. But the singer had stopped humming, and Marrit had stopped the ship.

She came down off the quarterdeck to the rail. She stood there with her bare left arm on it, the cords on it, and looked down at the water.

"Corran Patience," said Marrit.

That was all. She didn't say anything after it. She didn't have to. Pell, behind her, said it too. And Ottery. And Jago from the wheel. And then Yan, who'd walked Corran out along the shelf at the Last Light, last, at the end, because Corran had asked to be put at the end.

"Two Rs," said Yan.

Marrit laughed. Wet. "Two Rs," she said.

Under the hull, very low, the singer hummed. Once.

Then the wind came back into the sails, and Marrit went up to the wheel, and they sailed on.

***

On the eleventh night, Pell asked for a story.

The tide-folk liked stories. They'd found that out on the way west. Azac had told them on the deck at night, with the singer under them: floor 1, the goat; floor 5, the Kitecliff; Highcrook's rope. Pell had listened to every one with his arms folded and his face like a rock and at the end of each one said *that'll do*, which Kip said was the highest thing Pell ever said about anything.

"The one from up the top," said Pell. "Where you've been. You came down from somewhere. You've all got a face on you like you've come down from somewhere." He nodded at Dec, sitting against the mainmast with Thea beside him. "Him especially. Looks like a man who's been carried a long way."

"I walked," said Dec. "Most of it."

"Then tell me how he walked," said Pell. To Azac.

Azac looked at Dec.

He'd never asked. In all the years, back home, and on fourteen floors, he'd never once asked Dec if he could tell a story about him. He'd just told it. That was the deal. Dec did the thing and Azac told it and Dec told him to shut up.

He asked now. With his face. Across the deck.

Dec looked back at him. For a long moment. Then he nodded.

"It's yours," said Dec. "Go on. It's all true."

***

So Azac told it.

Not all of it. Not the way it had been. You couldn't tell a thing the way it had been; that wasn't what telling was. He told the tide-folk the bones of it, on the deck of the *Patience* in the dark, with the swell under them and the singer humming and the lantern lit by the mainmast.

A barn at the top of a valley, and a straw man made by old women, that the winter got into. A line chalked on a floor. A man who asked, for the first time in his life, to go out on the snow where nobody else could stand. And the doors, and the people at the doors with their hands on each other's shoulders, holding him up. And two hundred paces of white over nothing, in the dark, and a thing as tall as a house. And a bell that was an oven.

And the numbers.

He told the numbers. Every one. He'd heard every one, through Gabe's shoulders, at the door, and he'd kept them, the way Kip kept stars. *Eighty-eight.* *Seventy-four, my hand.* *Sixty, it hurts.* He said them the way Dec had said them, flat, out loud, up the hill. And as he said them he felt something he'd never felt telling a story about Dec.

There was no hole.

There was no page missing in the middle. There was no bit where the words had to go round something. He was telling a story about Dec that was all there, every bit of it, from the line to the doors, because Dec had stood on the snow in the dark and said every bit of it out loud so that it would be.

*He made it so I could tell it*, Azac thought. In the middle of a sentence. *That's what that was. He made it whole.*

He got to *thirty*.

He got to the part where the line went forward a step and Calvin went to his knees at the sill and the foot came up. He said it. And he got to—

Thea stood up.

She did it quietly. She didn't say anything. She let go of Dec's hand and got up from beside the mainmast and walked away up the deck, to the bow, in the dark, and stood there with her back to all of them, looking out at the sea.

Azac stopped.

Xola got up. She'd been sitting on a coil of rope with Squiddles on her knee. She got up and went after Thea, up the deck, and stood beside her at the bow. Not touching her. Just there.

Azac didn't know what to do. He looked at Dec.

Dec was looking up the deck at the two of them at the bow. His face was very still.

"Go on," said Dec. Quietly. Without looking away from them. "Finish it. It's yours. She knows how it ends. She was there." He swallowed. "She just doesn't want to hear that number again. That's all. That's allowed."

So Azac finished it.

He said *fifteen*. Quietly. Not the way Dec had. He couldn't say it the way Dec had. And then he went on, quickly, past it, because it was a story and stories go on. Getting up. The knot. The winter going up the threads, a bit each, into everybody. Rachael and the steam. The light coming. *Bettris, the oven.* A whole valley saying *not yours*. And the ground coming back. And a man walking two hundred paces uphill in the snow on his own two feet, and going down on one knee halfway, and getting up, and saying so.

"*And so our hero*," said Azac, at the end, because he always did, "*came in when the bell went.*"

Nobody said anything.

The singer hummed under the hull. The sails creaked. Up at the bow, Thea and Xola stood looking out at the dark.

"That'll do," said Pell, after a long time. His voice was rough.

***

Thea came back.

Azac watched her. Down the deck from the bow, in the dark, with Xola a pace behind. She stopped in front of the mainmast where Dec was sitting. She looked down at him. Azac couldn't see her face; the lantern was behind her. He could see Dec's.

She sat down. Beside him. Where she'd been.

And held out her hand.

Dec took it.

She didn't say anything to him. She looked across the deck at Azac instead.

"You got the numbers right," said Thea.

Her voice was perfectly level.

"He said them out loud," said Azac. "I just had to listen."

Thea looked at him for a moment longer. Then she nodded. Once. And looked away, at the sea, with her hand in Dec's.

Later, much later, when most of the deck was asleep and the swell had gone long and slow and the singer had stopped humming, Azac woke up on his coil of rope and looked across at the mainmast.

Thea was asleep.

Not sitting up. Not against the mast. Against Dec. Her head on his shoulder, her hand still in his, the lantern turned low in her lap. Asleep, properly, the way you only sleep against somebody when you've stopped being on guard. And Dec, awake, sitting very straight so as not to move her, with his eyes open, looking at the dark sea.

Azac hadn't seen her do that since the Long Barn. Not once. Five weeks of nights, against a doorpost and on stairs and on the Salt and in Tell, and she'd slept sitting up every night, on her own, beside him. Holding his hand. Not leaning.

She was leaning now.

Dec saw Azac looking. He didn't move his head. He just looked at him, across the deck, in the dark.

Azac didn't say anything. He lay back down on his rope and looked at the stars, which Kip would have known the names of.

*And so our hero*, he thought, *didn't move all night.*

He didn't tell that one. It wasn't his.

***

Hythe on the seventeenth day.

The horseshoe harbour. The houses on stilts with their rope walkways. Nets. The stone wall across the mouth with the squat bell tower. The tide out, the harbour bare mud, boats on their sides. Winter, now: smoke from every chimney, ice in the ruts on the quay. And the whole town on the wall when the *Patience* came in on the evening tide, because Marrit had been gone seventeen days and they always came to see her in, and because, on the deck, there were thirty-six people nobody had ever expected to see again.

Corran's bucket was still on the slip.

Azac saw it from the deck. Upside down at the top of the slipway where the *Patience* had been built, where an old man had sat every day for eleven years and watched her grow. Nobody had moved it. There was frost on it.

Marrit saw him looking.

"Nobody sits on it," said Marrit. "Nobody's said anybody can't. Nobody does." She looked at it a moment longer. "I do. Sometimes. Of a morning."

They slept in the long loft. Corran's gift, for pulling on the launch. Ebba came up with bread, and her baby on her hip, the girl born on the turn of the evening tide on the first night they ever came to Hythe, seven months old now and fat and furious, and Calvin held her and she screamed and he gave her back very fast. Old Sab the midwife looked at Rachael's hands and said "*Hm*" and gave her a pot of something that smelled of goose fat and wouldn't say what it was for.

And at the evening turn, Marrit went out on the quay.

***

Azac had heard about it. On the *Patience*, from Pell. He hadn't understood.

He understood when he saw it.

The quay at the evening turn. The tide coming back in, fast, the way it did at Hythe, a mile of mud going under in an hour. The small bell in the tower ringing once, for the turn. The sky going dark over the sea to the east. And at the end of the quay, where the stone went out into the water, Marrit. Standing. With her left arm out over the water, bare, and Corran's cords on it.

Saying names.

Out loud. One after another. Not loud. Steady. In the voice you'd use to say a thing across a kitchen. *Treeve Fulmar.* *Morwen Gull.* *Bryok Gull.* *Old Hedra.* *Elowen Petrel.* *Kitto Sanderling.* Three hundred and eleven names. Corran's. Every one he'd said through the night of the three hundred and seventh day on the deck of the *Patience* with Cursiffe writing them down. *Jory Kittiwake.* *Ysella.*

And she wasn't alone.

That was the thing. That was what Azac hadn't understood from Pell. When Corran's gran had said her names on this quay, a long time ago, she'd been alone, and they'd called her touched. When Marrit started, on the evening she came home from the Edge, she'd been alone too.

She wasn't now.

There were people on the quay. Behind her. Tide-folk, in their winter coats, standing on the stones in the dusk. Twenty. Thirty. More. Old men and young women and children holding their mothers' hands. And every so often, when Marrit said a name, somebody behind her said it too. Under their breath. Or out loud. Their own name. The one they'd knotted onto their own cord, at the wrist, and never said.

And then, when Marrit stopped for breath, somebody else said one. A name Marrit didn't have. A young man, at the back, with his cord held up in his fist. Then an old woman. Then a girl, about Kip's age, in a voice that cracked. Names nobody in Hythe had said out loud for a hundred years. Going out over the water at the evening turn, one after another, while the tide came in.

Azac stood at the back of the quay with the others and couldn't say anything at all.

*Nobody goes without a name*, Yan had said, on 13. About Tell.

Yan was beside him. Watching the water. Watching something go up off it, Azac supposed, that only Yan could see.

"Are they—" said Azac.

"Some," said Yan. Quietly. "Not all. Some of them have been down there too long." He watched. "But some." He smiled a little. "That one. The girl's. Just now. Straight up. Like a thread."

***

Marrit finished at full dark.

Corran's names, and then everybody's. And then she stopped, and the quay went quiet, and the tide was nearly in. And she turned round, on the end of the quay, with her bare arm, and looked at the thirty-six standing at the back.

"And these," said Marrit.

And she said their names.

All of them. Thirty-six. The way she'd said them on the landing of the Last Light, on the evening of the three hundred and eighteenth day, as they went down into the cellar one at a time. A new tide-folk custom, Pell had said. She'd made it up on the spot. *So the sea knows they came.*

She said them now. On the quay at Hythe. To the sea. Out loud.

*Higu.* *Drew.* *Thea.* *Xola.* *Chi.* She knew all of them. She'd learned them on the way west, at the wheel. *Gabe.* *Deston.* *Rachael.* *Dennis.* *Calvin.*

"*Azac*," said Marrit.

And thirty tide-folk on the quay behind her, who'd never met him, who didn't know him, who'd been saying their own dead into the dark for an hour, said it after her.

*Azac.*

He'd never heard that. In his whole life. On Earth or on any floor. His name, in a story he wasn't telling. Said out loud by people who didn't know him, to the sea, because somebody had decided it should be. Not *and so our hero*. Not anything. Just his name. Going out over the water at the evening turn in thirty voices, as if it mattered that he'd been there.

He found he was crying. He didn't stop.

Marrit went on. All of them. And last, the way they'd gone down into the cellar, last of all, after Gabe:

"*Ice*," said Marrit. And then, because she'd heard it on the landing: "*Declan*."

*Ice*, said the quay. *Declan.*

Azac looked at him.

Dec was standing at the back, by the wall, with Thea. She had his hand. He wasn't crying. He was just standing there with his face open, looking at the end of the quay, at a woman with eleven cords on her arm saying his name to the sea. And Thea was looking at him, not at Marrit, with a face Azac had never seen on her.

Not angry.

He didn't know what it was. Something under the angry, that had been there all along. Something the angry had been standing in front of, the way a shield stands in front of a man.

She saw Azac looking. And it went back.

But he'd seen it.

***

They went up the cliff path in the morning.

Round the back of the Stair, the island they'd come out on, on the two hundred and eighty-sixth day, into the sound of the sea. The path cut into the rock. The high ledge with no rail, the sea far below. And in the cliff, a door.

Marrit came with them, to the top. She didn't have to. She stood on the ledge in the wind with her arm bare and looked at them go in.

"Back to the stair," said Marrit. "Out of the stair and back to the stair." She looked at Dec. "That's a song now. Kip made it up. On the second run. It's a bad song."

"Everybody's songs about us are bad," said Calvin.

"That's because nobody knows how they end," said Marrit.

She looked at them. At all of them. Thirty-six, on a ledge, with their packs on. And she said their names again, very quickly, under her breath, the way you'd count sheep through a gate. Azac saw her lips move. All thirty-six.

Then she turned and went back down the path to Hythe, and her ship, and her quay, and the evening turn, and didn't look back.

Azac went last but one through the door. Gabe was last. He always was, going down.

On the stair, in the dark, four hundred steps, he started a story in his head. He didn't say it out loud. He didn't know yet how it ended. He just started it.

*And so our hero*, he thought, *had his name said to the sea.*

And then, after a few steps, because it was true, and because he'd learned on floor 14 that you could tell a whole thing if you said all of it:

*And so did I.*
