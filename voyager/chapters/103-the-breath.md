On every floor, the first thing Gabe did was find the forge.

He hadn't decided to. It had just turned out that way. On floor 1 it had been Ulla Dray's, in Hearthstead, with a hundred nails and a man's voice in his head saying *a shield that doesn't break*. On floor 3, a lean-to in Reedmoor with a smith who spat a lot. Floor 5, the Linkworks, with chain going out into the dark and Otta shouting. Floor 8, the trip hammer under the second arch, and a river-woman with burned white hands looking at the thing in his pack and saying *not here*.

*Higher.*

He'd carried it ever since. Since a bakery kitchen on floor 7, when Declan had put it in his lap and said *I wasn't putting this one on a bench*. An ingot the size of his hand, dark red, faintly warm, with something moving deep down inside it like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out. It lived at the bottom of his pack, against his back, and he could feel it there all day. Warm. Waiting. Through the Cataracts and the Deepwood and two nights holding a forest off a platform with his shield.

*That one wants a hotter fire than mine.*

He'd been looking for a hotter fire for eighty days.

***

They left floor 9 on the morning after the Sower, because floors put their guardians back if you stayed.

Burr took them out along the south-west high way himself, the whole length of it, to the empty tree at the end and the black circle under it where the Sower had knelt. Tansy came. She'd stopped asking whether she could come to things. Old Linden didn't; she said goodbye at the drum, to Maigo, with both her long hands flat on his bark for a long time and her blind eyes shut, and then she said something to him nobody else heard and he went quiet for an hour. Gigi went down the rope to the foot of the north tree at first light, on her own, with Thea at the top holding the lantern. She was down there ten minutes. When she came up she didn't say anything and Thea didn't ask.

The circle was just earth now. Black, and cracked, and bare. The Grow stood round the edge of it in its wall, chest high, and didn't lean in at all. And on the far side, in the trunk of the oldest of the three great trees round it, right in front of where the white thing had knelt for a thousand years with its back to it, there was a door.

Plain. Round-topped. Like all of them.

Burr let them down the ropes onto the earth. Then he came down himself, last, the way he'd taken to doing, and stood at the foot of the rope with his two-fingered hand on it and counted them. All the way to the door. His lips moving.

"Thirty-six," said Burr.

"Nobody left down," said Ice.

Burr looked at him for a long moment.

"Nobody left up, either," said Burr. "That's the other half. Walkers never say the other half." He folded his arms. "Come back."

Tansy was crying, furiously, behind him. Gabe saw Aleaka whistle at her, the three notes she'd learned, and Tansy whistle back, wet, and then turn round and shin up the rope so fast she was gone into the leaves before anyone could say anything.

They went through the door.

***

Three hundred and eighty-four steps.

They counted. They always counted now. Newish started it, and by the fourth step it was everyone, thirty-six voices going up in the dark, and Gabe counted with them and listened for the gap that had been there once, on floor 2, where a thirty-sixth voice should have been. It wasn't there. It hadn't been there since floor 7. He listened for it every time anyway.

Declan was two steps above him. Gabe could hear him counting. A bit flat on the eights, the way he'd always been, since the second row of a choir when they were thirteen and fifteen.

At the top there was a landing and a door, and they stopped, the way they did. Because of Higu.

"Go on, then," said Higu.

Declan looked at the door for a moment. Gabe watched his back. On floor 9, at the top of the stair, he'd stood there for a long time and then said *I don't know*, and Gabe had put his hand between his shoulder blades because there hadn't been anything else to do. He didn't stand there long this time.

"A fortress," said Declan. "Across a pass. A big one. Walls, one above the other, all the way up. There's a whole city in between them. People. They've held it for nine hundred years." He paused. "Against what comes down from the high side."

"What comes down?" said Calvin.

"Winter," said Declan. "That's what they call it. You'll see." He paused again. "It's cold. Colder than floor 2. Real cold. And the sky's real. Properly. All of it."

Chris's pencil.

"And the boss?" said Higu.

"Past the top wall. Out on the high side." Declan didn't turn round. "It's a gate. That's all I'll say. It's a gate, and you have to go through it together."

"Okay," said Higu.

"And under the city," said Declan, "there's a forge."

Gabe looked up.

Declan had turned round on the landing. He was looking down the steps at him, in the dark, past everyone else. With the lamp shield on his arm, the one Gabe had made for him without knowing it was for him, the Kiln-iron rim catching the faint light from the crack under the door.

"They call it the Breath," said Declan. "It's the hottest fire on any floor I've—" He stopped. "Any floor. It comes up out of the mountain." He looked at Gabe. "That's your forge, Gabe."

Gabe didn't say anything. He couldn't, for a second. He felt the ingot at the bottom of his pack against his back, warm.

"Open the door, Dec," he said.

***

It was cold.

That was the first thing. Not floor-2 cold, painted snow on a painted pass. Cold that went into your chest on the first breath and stayed there. Cold that made your eyes water and your nose run and your fingers go stupid. The air was thin. Gabe breathed in and didn't get enough, and breathed in again.

And the sky.

He'd thought he knew what sky was. Floor 5 had had a real sun. Floor 8 had had real rain. But this was the first time since the white room that he'd stood under a sky that went all the way up, and up, and didn't stop. Enormous. Pale blue going to deep blue going almost to black right at the top, so high and so clear you felt like you might fall up into it. And under it, mountains. All round. White and grey and black, sharp as broken glass, with snow on them that the wind took off the tops in long white flags.

They were standing on the low end of a pass. A long notch between two mountains, going up and away from them, narrowing. And across it, a little way up, there was a wall.

Grey stone. Huge blocks of it, fitted so close you couldn't have got a knife between them, going from one side of the pass to the other, from cliff to cliff, thirty metres high. With a gate in the middle, shut. And above it, further up the pass, another wall. And another above that. Five of them, Gabe counted, one above the other, climbing the pass like steps, each one higher and narrower than the last. And between them, cut into the rock of the mountains on both sides, crammed into every flat space and hanging off every ledge, a city. Grey roofs. Grey towers. Chimney smoke going sideways in the wind.

"Oh," said Calvin, beside him, very quietly. For once.

The window came up. Everybody's. He saw them all stop.

"One tenth," said Chris, slowly. He was writing. "One tenth of the—"

"Of a hundred," said Thea.

Nobody said anything for a moment. They stood at the bottom of the pass in the cold and looked at the walls going up it, five of them, and the sky over the top, and thought about that.

Gabe looked at Declan.

Declan was looking at the sky. Not at the walls. At the sky, going up forever over the top of the pass. With his face very still.

Gabe didn't know what he was thinking. He'd stopped pretending he always did. But he knew what that face looked like, from the second row, and from three in the morning in a call, and from the end of a jetty on floor 8. It was the face of somebody doing a sum and not liking the answer.

*One tenth.*

He went and stood next to him. On his right. He didn't say anything. After a moment, Declan's shoulder leaned against his. Just a bit.

***

The gate in the lowest wall opened before they got to it.

Not all the way. A door in the gate, man-high, and out of it came a woman with a shield. Tall. Taller than Gabe, which didn't happen much, and broad, in grey wool and grey leather and a grey steel cap, with a long grey braid down her back that didn't look like it had started grey. Her face was weathered and hard, with a white scar going through her top lip, and her eyes were grey too. Pale, like the sky near the ground. She looked at them. All thirty-six. For a long time.

"Climbers," she said.

Her voice was deep and flat and carried in the cold.

"Climbers," said Drew. "Yeah. Hi."

"We have it written," said the woman, "that climbers come. Up the stair, out of the low end. In the old books." She looked them over again. "Not in my life. Not in my mother's." She paused. "Thirty-six?"

"Thirty-six," said Drew.

The woman looked at them for a moment longer. Then she stood aside from the door in the gate.

"I'm Brenna," she said. "I keep the first wall. Come in out of the wind. It's going to snow."

They went in. Gabe went in near the end, with Declan and Thea and the lantern. And just inside the gate, in the dim of the long stone tunnel that went through the thickness of the wall, he stopped.

The tunnel walls were covered in writing.

Both sides. Floor to ceiling. Small and close and carved into the grey stone, line after line after line, going away into the dark ahead of them as far as he could see. Names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one cut neatly, carefully, in the same square letters, and after each one a little mark, a short line, like the line you put under something to show it's finished.

"The Long Wall," said Brenna, beside him. She'd seen him stop. "Everyone who's fallen holding this pass. Since the first stone." She put her hand flat on the wall beside a name, low down, near the floor, without looking at it, the way you'd touch a doorframe you'd touched a thousand times. "We carve them. All of them. We read them aloud on the longest night. Every one." She looked at him. "Nine hundred years. It takes all night."

Gabe looked at the names going away into the dark.

He'd seen dead things stay dead since floor 4. Hounds. Knappers. The Quiet. Sawyers going green under the Grow. He'd stood on the stair on floor 8 and watched a boat go over the Edge with a lamp at the bow. But he'd never seen anything like this. Tens of thousands. Every one with a little line under it.

Behind him, he heard Declan breathe in.

Gabe turned round. Declan was standing in the mouth of the tunnel with the snow starting to come in behind him on the wind, and he was reading the names. Fast. His eyes going along the lines. Along one, and down, and along the next. The way he'd read windows in the white room on day one, in half a heartbeat each. As if he was looking for something.

"Dec," said Gabe.

Declan stopped reading. He looked at Gabe. And his face did something Gabe didn't understand at all, and then put itself away.

"Sorry," said Declan. "It's—there's a lot of them."

"Yeah," said Gabe.

He didn't ask what he'd been looking for. He'd stopped asking things. On floor 8. On the jetty, or before it, he didn't know exactly when. He'd decided. Declan would tell him. When he was ready. Not before. Gabe would wait. He'd waited a hundred and fifty-three days for one word on floor 7, and he could wait again.

But he'd seen it. He filed it with the rest.

***

He found the Breath on the second day.

It wasn't hard. You could feel it from anywhere in the Bastion if you stood still. A warmth under your feet. In the stones of the streets, in the walls of the houses, in the steps cut into the rock between one level of the city and the next. The whole place was warm from underneath, the way a house is warm over a bakery. In the bitter cold, with the snow coming sideways down the pass, you could put your hand flat on any wall in the city and feel it.

He went down with Salty and Jared. The nail guild. Salty with Ressa's small old hammer at his belt, which he never took off now and never talked about. Jared with the thorn still bolted through the boss of his shield. Brenna had told them the way: down. Under the third wall. Through a door with a smith's mark on it, a hammer over a shield. And then down, and down.

It was a long stair. Cut into the mountain, going down in a spiral, with the warmth coming up it like breath out of a mouth. Every turn hotter. By the fifth turn they'd taken their coats off. By the tenth, Salty was sweating and swearing under his breath. And then there was light coming up it, orange and white, and a sound.

A sound like something enormous breathing.

In. A long low roar, rising. And out. Falling. And in.

They came out of the bottom of the stair into a cavern, and stopped.

It was huge. Bigger than the furnace hall on floor 4. A great round space hollowed out of the inside of the mountain, with a roof lost in the dark above, black with a thousand years of soot. And in the middle of the floor, a crack. Long and jagged, running across the rock from one side of the cavern to the other, as wide as a man was tall. And out of the crack came fire.

Not flames. Not a fire you'd make. A *sheet* of it, coming up out of the mountain along the whole length of the crack, blue at the bottom, deep and clear and blue as a flame on a stove, going to white above it, so bright it hurt, and then to orange where it licked up into the dark. And it breathed. That was the sound. It rose, roaring, a man's height, two men's height, and fell back, and rose. In and out. Slow. Like a sleeper.

All round the crack, at a distance, set into the floor, there were anvils. Dozens of them. Black iron, old, worn into dips in the middle by a thousand years of hammers. And at the anvils, people. Working. Hold-folk in leather aprons, with their sleeves rolled and their arms shining with sweat. The sound of hammers went round the cavern under the roar of the Breath, and every one of them was making a shield.

"Oh, my *days*," said Salty.

Jared didn't say anything. Gabe looked at him. Jared was looking at the Breath with his mouth open and his eyes wet, which on Jared meant he'd seen the most beautiful thing in his life.

Gabe felt the ingot at the bottom of his pack against his back.

It was hot.

***

The master of the Breath was called Geir, and he had one eye.

He found them before they found him. An old man, short and wide as a door, with a bald head shining in the firelight and a grey beard tucked into his apron so it wouldn't catch. His left eye was gone. Not covered. Gone, a long time ago, in a burn that had taken the eyelid and half the eyebrow and left the skin round it shiny and pulled. His forearms were the same. Burn on burn on burn, white and pink and old, like lace. Like Ressa's hands, Gabe thought. Like every smith who'd ever worked a fire too hot for skin.

He looked at them with his one eye. Grey. Like everybody's here.

"Climbers," said Geir. "Brenna said." His voice was a rasp, like a file on iron. "You're smiths."

"We're learning," said Gabe.

"Everybody's learning." Geir looked at Salty's hammer, at his belt. He looked at it for a long time. "That's a good hammer."

"It's not mine," said Salty. "It's lent."

"Hm." Geir looked at Jared's shield. At the thorn through the boss. He reached out and touched it with one thick burned finger. "That's a thorn."

"Roses have them," said Jared.

Geir almost smiled. Then he turned his one eye on Gabe.

"And you've got something in your bag," he said, "that's been getting hotter since you came in the door. I can feel it from here."

Gabe took his pack off. He knelt on the warm stone floor and undid it and reached right down to the bottom, past everything, and took it out.

The Kiln-heart Iron.

It wasn't dark red any more. It was red. Bright. Glowing in his hand like a coal, with the thing deep inside it moving, turning over, the way something turns over when it's waking up. It was hot. Not hot enough to burn. Hot like a living thing.

Geir went very still.

He looked at it in Gabe's hand for a long time with his one eye. Then he held out his own hand. Gabe put it in his palm. Geir closed his fingers round it, round the glow, and held it, and shut his eye.

"Where did you get this?" he said.

"A friend gave it to me."

"Where did *he* get it?"

"Floor 4. Out of a furnace. Out of the heart of a thing that was a furnace." Gabe swallowed. "He stood in its fire to get it. Twice. On his own."

Geir opened his eye. He looked at Gabe. Then past him, at the stair, as if he expected someone to come down it.

"I've seen one other piece of this," he said. "In my whole life. My master had it. *His* master gave it him. It sat in a box on that shelf for sixty years and nobody ever made anything with it, because nobody ever came to the Breath who knew what to make." He held it out. Gabe took it back. It was hotter. "One piece. You can make one thing with that. Folded into good iron, it'll make one thing, and that thing'll be what it is for the rest of time." He looked at Gabe. "What?"

"A shield," said Gabe.

"For who?"

"Me," said Gabe.

"No," said Geir.

Gabe stared at him.

"A shield's never for you," said Geir. He said it flatly, the way you'd tell somebody the sky was up. "Not here. Not on the wall." He jerked his chin upward, at the roof, at the Bastion above them, at the five walls. "You stand in a wall, boy. Shoulder to shoulder. Every shield in it covers half the man on its left. That's what a wall *is*. You hold your shield up and the half that sticks out past you covers the next man. And his covers the next. All the way along." He folded his arms. "Nobody covers himself. Nobody can. You cover the one beside you, and the one on your other side covers you. That's why it holds."

Gabe didn't say anything for a while.

The Breath roared up and fell back, behind Geir. In. Out.

"Then it's for him," said Gabe.

"Who?"

"The one who stood in the fire," said Gabe. "Dec. Declan." He looked down at the ingot in his hand, glowing. "He's a wall. He's always been a wall. On every floor. On his own. Nobody's ever been on his—" He stopped. "Nobody's ever covered him."

Geir looked at him for a long time with his one eye.

"Then you stand on his right," said Geir. "And you make a shield that covers him." He uncrossed his arms. "Can you fold?"

"I can learn."

"A hundred folds," said Geir. "Every one in the Breath. Every one hammered flat before it goes dull. Every one *right*, or the whole thing's wrong and you've wasted the only piece of this you'll ever see." He turned round. "Anvil nine. It's free. It was my master's. Don't drop anything on it."

***

He folded for four days.

He'd never worked like it. Not at Ulla Dray's, not at the Linkworks, not on the lamp shield on floor 5 when he'd sat up three nights with Jared fitting the rim. The Breath was so hot that you couldn't stand at the crack for more than the time it took to heat the iron, and then you ran it back to the anvil with tongs as long as your arm, and hammered, fast, before the colour went, and folded, and hammered again, and back to the crack. Over and over. The Kiln-heart in the middle of a bar of the Bastion's grey iron, folded in on itself, and out, and in again, so that every fold took a little of it and spread it through the whole. You could see it. Every fold, the grey iron got darker, and redder, and at the heart of the bar something glowed that didn't stop glowing even when it came out of the fire.

Salty worked the bellows when the Breath was low. Jared held. Geir stood behind him with his arms folded and said *no* a great deal. *No. Again. Too cold. No. Hit it like you mean it. No.* And, very rarely, once a day if Gabe was lucky, *hm*.

By the fourth day his arms were shaking all the time, even in his sleep. He'd burned both hands. Malcolm came down every evening and fixed them, in about ten seconds a burn, sitting on the warm stone with his tongue between his teeth. *You're a terrible patient,* said Malcolm. *You keep putting them back in the fire.*

He was on the thirty-first fold, on the afternoon of the fourth day, when Declan came down the stair.

***

Gabe didn't see him come. He was at the crack with the bar in the tongs, waiting for the colour, with the Breath roaring up and falling in front of his face and the heat coming off it like a wall. He only knew Declan was there because Jared said, behind him, "Oh, hey, man," and Gabe looked round.

Declan was standing at the bottom of the stair. Just standing there. With his coat over his arm because of the heat, in his white shirt, filthy, with the sleeves rolled, the way he'd been in a thousand calls and on a thousand screens. He wasn't looking at Gabe. He was looking at the Breath.

Then he was looking at Gabe.

And his face did a thing Gabe had only seen it do a few times in his whole life. Once on the end of a jetty, at Calvin's arm. Once in a kitchen full of flour, on floor 7, at Gabe himself. It went open. Soft. As if he were looking at something he'd thought he'd never see again.

"Hi," said Gabe.

"Hi," said Declan. He came across the cavern, slowly, and stopped a little way behind Gabe and to his left. Not too close to the Breath. He folded his arms. "Don't mind me."

"I never mind you."

"Liar."

Gabe grinned and turned back to the crack.

He worked. Declan watched. He didn't say anything, which was rare for him, and Gabe found he liked it. Having him there. Just standing behind his left shoulder in the heat and the roar, watching, while the iron came up through red to orange to the colour Geir called *straw*, and Gabe ran it back to anvil nine and hammered it flat and folded it, *thirty-two*, and back to the crack, and again. *Thirty-three.* It felt right. It felt like something he'd done before, though he never had. Somebody behind him, watching him make something. It felt like the most natural thing in the world.

*Thirty-four.* He hammered it flat. The colour was going. Straw to orange. Orange to red. Geir said the folds had to be quenched every tenth, quickly, in the trough of oil by the anvil, to set them. Thirty-four wasn't tenth. But the edge of the bar had gone wrong, a little, a ripple he didn't like, and he thought if he quenched it now it'd set before the ripple spread. He turned to the trough with the tongs.

"Don't," said Declan. "Not yet."

Gabe stopped.

"You always quench too early," said Declan. Easily. Not thinking about it. In the voice he'd used in a thousand calls, at three in the morning, for things that didn't matter. "Let it go dull first. All the way. You can see the ripple better when it's dull, and then you can hit it out. If you quench it now you'll lock it in." He paused. "You always want to set it before it's ready. Every time."

Gabe stood there with the bar in the tongs over the trough.

He didn't move.

The Breath roared up behind him and fell. In. Out. At anvil nine, Jared had gone still with his hands on the holding tongs. At the bellows, Salty had stopped pumping.

"What?" said Gabe.

He turned round.

Declan was standing exactly where he'd been. Behind his left shoulder, with his arms folded. And his face had gone white. Not pale. *White*, the way it had gone at the big pool on floor 8 when the Great Fall stopped. The way it had gone on the end of a rope over the Down, three days ago, when every fern on the floor turned round and looked at him.

"When have you ever," said Gabe slowly, "watched me forge, Dec?"

Nobody said anything.

The Breath went up. Came down.

"I—" said Declan. He stopped. "Salty. Salty said. That you—"

"I never said that," said Salty. Quietly. From the bellows.

Declan looked at Salty. Then back at Gabe. His mouth opened and shut.

"Lucky guess," he said.

Gabe looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, in the orange light, with the bar of grey iron going dull in the tongs in his hands. At the white face. At the arms folded so tight across the white shirt that the knuckles showed. At his oldest friend, his brother from a different mother, who'd been in the second row with him at thirteen and in his ear at three in the morning for eleven years since, who had never once in any of those years watched him make anything out of iron, because he'd never made anything out of iron until nearly two hundred and forty days ago, in a town called Hearthstead, on floor 1.

*You always quench too early.*

*Always.*

"Yeah," said Gabe. "Lucky guess."

He turned back to the anvil. He didn't quench the bar. He held it in the tongs over the anvil and let it go dull, all the way, the way he'd been told. Red to dark red to grey. And when it was grey he could see the ripple in the edge, clear as a crease in paper, much clearer than he'd been able to see it hot. And he hit it out. Three blows. Flat.

It was right.

Behind him, after a while, he heard Declan's boots go back across the cavern to the stair, and up it. Not fast. Not running. Just going.

Gabe didn't turn round.

***

Geir found him at anvil nine very late, when everyone else had gone up and the cavern was empty except for the Breath, breathing.

He was sitting on the warm stone with his back against the anvil and the bar across his knees. Thirty-four folds. It had gone dull hours ago. He hadn't put it back in the fire. He was just sitting with it.

Geir stood over him for a while. Then he lowered himself down onto the stone beside him, slowly, with a grunt, the way old men sit, and didn't say anything.

"Have you ever had somebody," said Gabe, after a long time, "know something about you that they couldn't know?"

Geir thought about it.

"No," he said.

"Me neither," said Gabe. "Until about three hours ago."

The Breath went up. Came down. The light went orange and white and orange across the black roof.

He didn't know what it was. He didn't have a word for it. He wasn't Chris, or Thea, or Hako; he didn't sit up at night in a ring with a notebook working things out. He'd never wanted to. He'd decided, a long time ago, on a jetty or before it, that he wasn't going to. That whatever it was, Dec would tell him. That he'd wait.

But he had a list. He'd been keeping it without meaning to, the way you keep stones in a pocket. He took it out now and looked at it, sitting against anvil nine.

A note on a nail on floor 3. *Be careful. Train longer.* Before anybody had any reason to be careful of anything.

A coat on a bench. *His* coat. Left for *him*, on floor 2, before Gabe had ever said a word about wanting one.

Onion skins in the corks of water caches on floor 6. Peeled properly. All the way round, in one go. The way only one person in the world peeled them.

A bell on floor 4, in a city with no dark, rung once, and a thing called the Quiet dead in the street, and Calvin with two arms.

The ropes in Highcrook. *You knew they'd come. You weren't sure, though.*

*If I'm ever late back. Anywhere. Any floor. Make a fire.* As if he were asking Gabe to do something he'd watched Gabe do before.

And *you always quench too early*.

*Always.*

He looked at the list for a long time.

Then he put it away, the way he'd put it away every time before. Back in his pocket. He was going to wait. He'd promised himself. On a jetty. In a basket. In a hanging house with Dec asleep and a door to watch. He wasn't going to ask anybody. Not Thea, who knew something; he'd seen her face. Not Hako, who'd said *ten thousand hours* once, on floor 6, and then never said anything again. Not Xav.

Especially not Xav.

Xav, who'd known Declan nine years and could read his face from across a room. Who'd stopped on *NOT THIS TIME* on Drew's slate on floor 6 and looked at Thea. Who'd been not looking at Gabe, very carefully, for a hundred days.

*You don't ask them,* thought Gabe. *You wait for him.*

He picked the bar up off his knees.

"Thirty-four," he said. To Geir. To the Breath. "I've got sixty-six more."

"Sixty-six," agreed Geir.

"He said let it go dull first."

"Your friend's right," said Geir. "You do quench too early. I was going to tell you tomorrow." He got up, slowly, with a hand on the anvil. "I've only known you four days."

He went off toward the stair. At the bottom of it he stopped and looked back with his one eye.

"How long's he known you?" said Geir.

Gabe didn't answer. Geir didn't wait for him to. He went up.

Gabe sat by anvil nine with the bar across his knees and the Breath going up and down in front of him, orange and white, in and out, like something very big asleep. And he thought about the answer.

*Since I was thirteen.*

And then, very quietly, in the place under everything where you put the things you aren't ready for:

*Longer than that.*
