On floor 6, Xola was the most popular person in the world.

She'd known she would be. She'd been dreading it a bit, all the way down the stair, three hundred and twelve steps with Newish counting and Thea singing and Squiddles asleep in the hollow of her collarbone like a small damp hat. She'd been dreading it since the Long Barn, really. Since the night Higu had drawn the floors going down on the back of the rock, one under the other like a ladder, and put a little sun beside the sixth. Not a nice sun. A sun with far too many spikes.

Tiger opened the door at the bottom. He stood with his hand flat on it first, the way he did now, and then he pushed, and the white came in.

It came in like a hand. Like somebody putting their whole palm over your face and pressing. The little stone arch at the foot of the stair stood half in the shallows of the Still Water, and the bottom four steps went down into it, and beyond the arch was the pool, perfectly round and so blue it looked painted, and beyond the pool was the bowl, and beyond the bowl was the Glass. All of it white. White sky, white sun, white dunes of crushed glass throwing the light back up from underneath so there was no shade anywhere, not even under your own chin.

"Nope," said Calvin, and walked backwards up three steps into the dark, and sat down.

Nobody argued. They'd learned that, on six, the first time round. They'd walked out into the middle of the day because nobody had told them not to, and by the afternoon Gigi had stopped sweating, and Hyvred had carried her the rest of the way. After that everybody walked at night. You didn't have to tell them twice.

So they sat on the stair.

Thirty-six people on the bottom forty steps, in the shade of the arch, the lowest of them with their boots off and their feet in the Still Water. It was cold. Impossibly cold, under that sky, like the pool had been brought up from the bottom of something very deep and set down here by mistake. Calvin put his feet in and made a noise nobody would ever let him forget. Then Rachael put hers in, and made the same noise, and pretended she hadn't.

And then, one after another, without anybody organising it, everybody came and found Xola.

Dennis first, with his water skin held out, already talking. "Not a full one. Half. I'm budgeting." Then Malcolm, with two. "One's a backup." Then Hako, who didn't say anything, just tipped his head forward so she could put a hand of cold water on the back of his neck, and sighed like a kettle coming off the boil. Then Bee, who wanted to know whether Xola could do a cloud. Then Calvin, from three steps up, who wanted to know whether she could do a cloud *with rain in it*.

"I'm a healer," said Xola. "Not a well."

"You're a well with opinions," said Hako, with his eyes shut.

She did it anyway. She always did. She went up and down the bottom forty steps with her palms running, and put water in skins and on necks and into the crook of Waddles's flipper, because Chi had brought him down the stair wrapped in a wet towel like a very small grumpy pudding and he was making his cross noise. She put her hand on the top of Thea's head and let the water run down into her hair. Thea closed her eyes and leaned into it.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome, babe."

Squiddles woke up, slid down her arm into the pool, and lay on the bottom step in two inches of impossibly cold blue water with every arm spread out, glowing a pleased, pale pink. Squiddles didn't come out for the rest of the afternoon.

***

Higu took the rock out on the step just above the waterline.

She did it every floor. You came out of a stair and she sat down on whatever there was and wiped the rock with her sleeve and drew the line down the middle and the other line across, and then she looked up and said, "Ice. What do you remember?"

He was sitting on the bottom step with his boots on and a brown clay crock in his arms. He looked out through the arch at the white.

"Nothing," he said. "I never came down."

Higu drew a short line in the left-hand column. That was all it ever was now, going down. A short line.

"It's hot," said Ice, after a moment.

"That's not remembering," said Higu. "That's true." And she wrote *HOT* in the middle column, under *TRUE*, in big letters, and underlined it twice.

Everybody laughed. Even Ice. And something in Xola's chest that had been held tight since the Long Barn let go a notch, the way a knot does when you finally find the right end to pull.

***

The crock was a problem.

Xola noticed it first, because she noticed everything about the crock. Everybody did. Ice had carried it in both arms against his chest all the way down from Evermorrow, the way you'd carry a sleeping baby or a bomb, and he looked at it every few minutes, and lifted the cloth on top and looked inside and put the cloth back, and his face did something small and worried every time.

It was Merle's mother. The starter. A year of tomorrows in a brown pot, for Mother Crane. *If it goes grey on top, it's hungry. If it smells like old socks, it's very hungry. If it's flat, it's sulking.*

It wasn't any of those. It was climbing out.

"Ice," said Thea, without opening her eyes.

He'd already seen. The cloth on top had gone up in a dome like somebody breathing in, and round the edge, where it was tied, a pale soft froth was coming through the weave and down the side of the crock in slow, fat, bubbling runs. It smelled sour and warm and alive. It smelled like the back of Merle's kitchen.

"It's doing something," said Ice.

"It's hot," said Thea.

"Merle didn't say anything about hot."

"Merle lives on a floor with weather."

He looked at the crock, then at Thea, then out at the white, and then, slowly, at Xola. Squiddles was still in the pool. Her hands were still wet. She knew exactly what he was going to say before he said it, because she'd known him for years, and his face had always done this. Gone very polite, the way it did when he wanted something and was going to ask for it as if he didn't.

"Xola," said Ice.

"No."

"I haven't—"

"I'm a healer. I'm not a cellar."

"Please."

She looked at him. At the crock. At the froth climbing slowly down the side of it onto his sleeve.

"Give it here," said Xola.

He gave it to her. She soaked the cloth from her palms until it ran, and wrapped it round the whole crock, and then she put both hands flat on the outside and thought about the bottom of a well. The real bottom. The black water at the foot of Deepwell, three hundred metres down in the dark, that had never once seen the sun. She thought about it hard. It went out of her hands and into the clay, cool, slow, and the clay drank it, and under her palms the crock stopped feeling like something with a fever and started feeling like something asleep.

The froth sank back. The dome went down.

Ice let out a breath he'd obviously been holding since Evermorrow.

"Thank you."

"Don't," said Xola. "It's not for you. It's for her." She gave him the crock back, cold and wet and quiet. "And for Merle."

"Okay."

"Every hour," said Xola. "On this floor. You're going to have to bring it to me every hour and say please."

"Please."

"Every *hour*, Icey."

"Please," said Ice, again, and almost smiled.

He sat back down on the step with the crock in his lap. She watched him lift the wet cloth, just a corner, and look inside, and put it back, and his face didn't do the worried thing. She sat down two steps up and put her feet back in the water.

After a while he said, without looking round, "Are you still—"

"No."

"You were. On fourteen."

"On fourteen you went out on the snow on your own and came back with your arms white to the elbow." She said it lightly. It didn't come out quite as lightly as she'd meant. "I was allowed."

"I know."

"Thea's at one."

"I know." He turned the crock a little in his lap. "Is she going to stay at one?"

"Thea's Thea. She keeps things." Xola leaned back on her elbows on the hot stone. "I don't. I'm not built like that. I get angry and I yell at you and I hold your stupid frozen hand for a week and then it's gone. I don't keep things."

"You don't keep things," said Ice. Slowly. Carefully. The way he said things now, since the Grey. Like he was checking the words one at a time before he put his weight on them.

"I don't keep things."

From four steps further up, with her eyes still shut and her head against the wall and the lantern beside her on the step, Thea said, "Yes you do."

"I don't."

"One thing," said Thea.

Xola opened her mouth, and shut it again.

She knew exactly what Thea meant. Of course she did. She'd known Thea for years and years and five hundred and nineteen days, and Thea had never once in all that time said a thing like that without meaning one particular thing by it.

She didn't say anything. Neither did Thea. Ice looked from one of them to the other, and was wise enough, for once in his life, to look back down at the crock.

***

In the late afternoon, Hyvred came down the stair and stood in the arch with her glasses pushed up on her head, looking across the pool.

"There are people," she said.

Everybody looked. Across the water, on the far western shore, where the bowl rose away toward the Glass, there were shapes. Low, pale, square. Tents. A thread of smoke going straight up into the white. And moving between them, very small, slowly, the way people move in a heat like this, figures.

"Twelve," said Hyvred. "No. Fifteen. Some of them are small." She tilted her head. "They're lit. All of them. Just ordinary lights. Just people."

"There were never people at the water," said Higu.

There couldn't have been. That was the thing about the Still Water. You couldn't come near it in the day. The thing that lived in it bent the sun into beams that turned dunes to running glass, and in the day the bowl had been a place where nothing walked.

It was still in the water. Both of it.

Xola could see them from the step, if she leaned out. Three great broken shapes in the shallows on the north side, where the pool met the glass. The first one was half sunk, its faces crazed white all the way through like ice on a puddle after somebody's stood on it, and that one was Ice's. He'd killed it at noon, alone, two days after the bowl. The other two lay further along, one on its side like a lantern somebody had dropped and one in pieces, and those were theirs. All thirty-five of them, twice, at dusk, because they weren't stupid.

There was no fourth.

"It didn't come back," said Kei, from the top of the group, quietly.

"On six it stays," said Higu. She didn't write it yet. She wanted to see the tents first.

***

The sun went down the way it always did on the Glass, all at once and then not at all. The white went yellow, and the yellow went the colour of the inside of a shell, and then the light was gone off the dunes and the whole bowl went blue, and then silver, because the moon was up, and on six the moon turned the Glass into something you could have wept at. Higu stood in the arch and watched it come up with her face gone soft. She was a moon person. She always had been.

They went out through the shallows round the edge of the pool, in a long line, with Thea's lantern at the front and Waddles shrieking with joy in the cold water and Gabe at the back.

The Prism's grooves ran away from the water in every direction. Xola had forgotten them. Long smooth channels cut down into the dunes where its beams had gone, fused into dark glass, a stride wide and as straight as anything, running out from the pool like spokes. The sand had blown over them and off again for a year and they were still there. Clean. Hard. And there were footprints in them. Dozens of footprints, going both ways.

"They walk them," said Higu. "They don't shift."

The camp was on the western shore. Five tents of pale cloth, pegged into the wet sand with glass stakes. A fire of something that burned blue. Water skins hung in a row on a line, sweating in the cool. And along the edge of the pool, where the sand was always wet, a long low bed of something green, in neat rows, little bitter spikes coming up out of the sand like a comb.

Onions.

Ice stopped.

Thea's hand found his without looking.

A man came out of the nearest tent. Thin, dark, burnt by the Glass all his life, with a ladle on his belt like Ostrel's. And behind him a girl about ten with her hair wet and plastered flat, who'd been swimming, and who stared at the thirty-six of them coming round the shore in the moonlight as if they were a herd of something nobody had ever seen.

"You'll be Ostrel's seventy," said the man.

"Thirty-six of us," said Dennis. "Seventy was cups."

"Seventy's what he calls you." The man looked them over. "Dov. From the fourth ring. This is Sadi. Say hello, Sadi."

Sadi didn't say hello. She was looking at Ice. Or at the crock in his arms, in its wet cloth, under Xola's last cool hand.

"What's in that?" said Sadi.

"Bread," said Ice. "Tomorrow's."

Sadi considered this, and accepted it, the way children accept anything said seriously enough.

Dov told them while the fire burned blue. The thing in the water hadn't come back after the last time. Nobody had believed it. Ostrel had said leave it a year and a day, the way you did with a well that had gone bad, and they'd left it a year and a day, and then at the turn of the year four families from the lower rings had walked out along the grooves with tents on their backs, at night, scared out of their wits, and sat down at the water. And in the morning the sun had come up and nothing had come out of the pool. Nothing at all.

"So we stayed," said Dov. "Fetch water back for the whole well. Six skins a night. Ostrel's wife sent the onions. Said if they'd grow anywhere, they'd grow here." He looked at the green rows. "They grow here."

"The children swim," said Sadi. "At night. It's warm at night."

"It's freezing," said Calvin.

"It's warm at *night*," said Sadi, with contempt, and went back in the water.

Higu sat down on the sand by the blue fire and took out the rock. Under *NOW*, in the third column, small, she wrote:

*THE PRISM STAYED DEAD.*
*PEOPLE AT THE WATER.*
*ONIONS IN THE SAND.*

She looked up at Ice. He was still looking at the onion bed. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Everybody who'd been on six the first time knew about the onions. A dozen bitter desert onions peeled properly on the third ring of Deepwell by a man in a white helm, and the skins tied round the necks of twelve water skins under the cork, and the skins buried in four holes along the line from the stair. Like a ribbon on a present. So you'd know it was safe.

"Okay," said Higu, and put the rock away.

***

Gigi dropped back when they started across the bowl.

Xola saw her do it. She'd been walking with Hyvred and Bee near the front, behind the lantern, with her silver hair tied up off her neck and her crooked dark-green crown on top of it, and she slowed, and slowed, and let the line go past her until Thea came level. And then she walked beside Thea for a while without saying anything. Xola was on Thea's other side. Ice was on the far side of Thea, with the crock, and Thea's hand in his.

"Will you come with me?" said Gigi, at last. "To the place."

She didn't have to say which place. There was only one place, in the bowl.

"Yes," said Thea.

Gigi nodded. Then, after a moment, she looked past Thea. At Xola.

"You too," said Gigi. "If you want."

Xola nearly missed her step.

"Why me?"

Gigi looked straight ahead, across the bowl, at the dark. "You were there," she said. "Both times."

Both times.

Xola knew which two. Gigi knew she knew. Neither of them said either one out loud, and Thea walked between them holding the lantern, looking at neither of them, the way you look at nothing when you're giving people room.

"Okay," said Xola.

***

Ice's feet stopped.

Xola didn't see it happen. She felt it, through Thea, the way you feel a rope go tight on the other end. Thea stopped too, because her hand was in his, and Xola stopped because Thea did, and Gigi stopped a step later and looked back.

He was standing quite still on the sand in the moonlight, about two hundred metres in from the west rim, with the crock in his arms, looking at his own boots.

"That's the ring," said Higu.

She'd come back down the line. She had the map in her head; she always had the map in her head. Eighteen lines coming into the bowl from every side, west and north and south and one from the far east, two nights round. Every one of them stopping here, at an invisible circle two hundred metres out from wherever the thing had stood. Eighteen times. And then the nineteenth, from the north rim, straight through.

"My feet stopped," said Ice. He sounded surprised. "I didn't tell them to."

"Do you want to come in?" said Thea.

He looked across the sand toward the middle, where the moon was coming off something small and sharp and glittering. Then he looked at Gigi.

"No," he said. "It's not mine." He let go of Thea's hand, gently, and sat down. Right there, on the line, cross-legged, with the crock in his lap. Facing in. With his eyes open.

The first time, Higu had said, he'd sat on a spur of black glass on the north rim with his back to the bowl and his eyes shut. Xola looked at him sitting on the sand, facing it, looking straight at where it had been, and something went through her that she didn't have a name for.

"I'll wait," said Ice. "Go on."

Gabe came down the line from the back, as he always did, and sat down next to him on the sand without saying a word, and put his elbows on his knees.

Drew sent everybody else on up to the west rim. "Go on. Go. Sit down somewhere and stop looking." They went, in a long dark line up the slope, and only Hyvred looked back, once, with her glasses pushed up on her head, at whatever she could see.

Then it was the three of them, walking in across the sand.

***

The shards were still there.

Of course they were. *On six, it stays.* A ring of them, about three metres across, in the middle of nothing. Real glass, fallen in a circle where the thing had shattered like a window, and they'd fallen point-down into the sand and stayed point-down, a year and a summer of wind and they hadn't moved. They stood up out of the bowl in the moonlight like frost on a field. Like teeth. Like the edge of something that had been a hole.

There was nothing in the middle. Just sand. Just the bowl, and the moon, and the far-off blue of the pool behind them, and the dark rim of the Glass all round.

Gigi stopped at the edge of the shards. Then she stepped over them, carefully, into the middle, and stood there.

Xola held her breath. She didn't mean to. She'd been holding her breath since the stair, if she was honest. Since the Long Barn and the rock with the spiky sun on it. Because she knew what had stood here, and what it had done, and what it had shown nine of them, and she'd been one of the nine. She'd run at it. She didn't remember deciding to. She remembered Ice in the dunes, the way he'd been on a call at four in the morning years ago, laughing at nothing, and she'd run, and then Thea was in front of her with the lantern, and then Thea was on the ground and there was a lot of blood.

Nothing came.

Gigi stood in the middle of the ring with her arms at her sides and waited. The wind moved her hair. Somewhere up on the west rim, Calvin laughed at something and Rachael told him to shut up. Nothing came.

"It was about here," said Gigi. She turned, slowly, on the spot. "It was standing about here. And it showed me you." She was looking at Thea. "Staying."

"I know," said Thea.

"I wanted to see what it'd show me now."

They waited. All three of them. Long enough that Xola started to feel stupid, standing in the moonlight in the middle of a bowl of glass waiting for a monster to come back so it could show somebody something. Long enough for that to turn into something else.

"Nothing," said Gigi. "There's nothing."

She laughed. It came out wrong, halfway to something else, and she put the back of her wrist against her mouth.

Thea put her hand up, without seeming to notice she'd done it, and touched her own left shoulder through her sleeve.

Xola knew exactly what was under there. A long pale raised line from the base of her neck, across the shoulder, down the upper arm. The thing's edge had gone through her in a tiny bright *tink* and she'd lain in a dry cistern all night with her head in Gigi's lap, and in the morning Xola had put both hands on it and pushed every bit of water she had into it, and it had closed, and it had stayed. A scar. The first one Xola had ever not been able to undo. She'd hated it for a week. Then she'd sat with Thea on a cushion in the round room in Deepwell and put her hand on it and said, *Okay. You can stay.*

"It's just a scar now," said Thea.

"It was always just a scar," said Xola.

"No," said Thea. "It wasn't."

***

Gigi took a breath.

"It showed me the wrong thing," she said.

She wasn't crying. She was doing the thing she did instead, which was standing very straight with her chin up and her arms tight, the way she'd stood in the white room on the very first morning, a little way off from the light, neither going toward it nor away.

"It showed me you staying. And I thought, that's it. That's what I want. I want her to have stayed. I want to have been right." She swallowed. "And I've carried it round ever since. Five hundred days. Up and up. I carried it on nine, when you came with the lantern every morning and I didn't know why. I carried it on twelve, on the road, when you walked with me." She looked at Thea, and her mouth did something complicated. "You walked with me nine days. And I kept looking at you. And you kept looking back down the line. Every hour. For him."

Thea didn't say anything.

"And I thought, it was *wrong*. It didn't show me what I wanted. It showed me what I used to want. Ages ago. Before." Gigi's chin came up a little further. "I don't want you to stay. I don't. I want you to go where you're going. I want you to have that." She didn't look back toward the line, toward the man sitting on the sand with a crock in his lap and Gabe beside him. She didn't need to. "I'm letting go. Properly. Not pushing. Not making you go. Just—letting."

"Gi," said Thea.

"Don't say sorry."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good." Gigi let out a breath that shook. "Good."

Then she put her hand up into her hair, under the crooked green crown, and took something out.

Xola knew what they were before she saw them. Forget-me-nots. A little bunch of them, brown now, so dry they were nearly paper, so thin the moon came through them. The ones Gigi had woken up with had gone brown on floor 1, on the third day, and there'd been a bank of them by the river in Hearthstead, and Thea had picked new ones and given them to her without a word. And Gigi had worn them every day since. Brown, then browner, then this. Five hundred days.

"Bee says I never let go of anything," said Gigi.

She knelt down in the middle of the ring of shards, and put the forget-me-nots in the sand, and pressed them in with her fingertips. And then she put her whole palm flat over them, on the glass sand, and closed her eyes.

Xola felt it. You couldn't not, standing that close. A warmth going down out of Gigi's hand into the ground, slow and certain, the way it had gone down into the salt on twelve. Down and down. *Plant it where nothing grows. It will take.*

When Gigi lifted her hand, there was blood on it. The sand on six was glass, and glass cut. A thin dark line across the heel of her palm, welling.

Thea moved.

Xola got there first.

***

She didn't decide to. That was the thing she'd think about afterwards, walking up the slope, and on the next night, and for a long time. She didn't decide anything. She just found she'd stepped over the shards and gone down on her knees in the sand in front of Gigi and taken Gigi's hand in both of hers, before Thea had finished taking her first step.

"Give me that," said Xola.

The water came out of her palms, cool, and ran over the cut, and the cut closed. It was nothing. It was the smallest thing she'd healed in five hundred days. It closed like a mouth.

She didn't let go.

Gigi was staring at her.

And Xola knelt in the sand holding Gigi's hand and thought about it, because there wasn't any getting out of thinking about it now. The months. Thea's face, at the end of it, with the shadows coming up under her eyes like water coming up through sand. The way Gigi had blamed her for everything. If Thea did well, it was at her. If Thea did badly, it was because of her. And Xola there every night, holding Thea up, for months, until Thea finally put it down and walked away and was right to.

Xola had never forgiven her. Not once. Not on Earth, and not in the white room, and not on floor 6 on a cushion in the round room in Deepwell, when she'd taken Gigi's hand second, after Thea's, because Thea had. She'd held on to it the whole way up. Because Thea forgave things too fast. Because Thea picked forget-me-nots for people by rivers without saying a word. Because somebody had to keep it. Somebody had to remember, on Thea's behalf, so it wouldn't just go.

Like Dennis's tab, she thought. A year on a slate in the cheesemonger's, in chalk, never closed.

Except Thea had closed hers. Ages ago. On nine, at the rope, in the dark, with the lantern held low over Gigi's hands. On twelve, walking the road. Thea had put hers down so long ago Xola couldn't even remember when.

And Xola had still been holding the other end. For nobody. For years.

"You don't have to," said Gigi.

"I know."

"You never—"

"I know." Xola looked at their hands. Hers wet, Gigi's dry, both of them full of moonlight. "I'm not saying it was fine."

"Don't."

"It wasn't fine. Any of it."

"I know."

"I held her up. For months. Every night."

"I know," said Gigi. Very quietly. "I watched you."

Xola's eyes stung. She let them. She cried easily; it was the main other thing about her.

"I've been angry at you the whole time," she said. "Since before. All of the tower. Every floor."

"I know."

"I'm putting it down."

Gigi's face did something Xola had never once seen it do.

"Not to one," said Xola. "I'm not Thea. I don't do ones." She sniffed, hugely. "All the way down. Done. Gone. I'm putting it down."

Gigi cried then. Properly, for the first time Xola had ever seen, with her chin up and her eyes open and the tears just going. And Thea knelt down in the sand on Gigi's other side and took her other hand, and then the three of them were kneeling in a ring of glass teeth in the moonlight in the middle of a bowl on the sixth floor of a tower, holding hands, and nobody said anything.

Squiddles, who had come up out of the pool at the camp under protest and spent the walk across the bowl sulking on Xola's shoulder, turned a deep, bright, astonished pink, and stayed that way.

It didn't hurt. Not even quite.

***

When Xola looked up, Ice was walking in.

She didn't know how long he'd been walking. She looked up because Thea did, and there he was, coming across the sand toward them in the moonlight with the crock held in both arms against his chest. Behind him, on the line, Gabe was still sitting with his elbows on his knees, watching him go.

He'd crossed the ring.

The line where his trail had turned round eighteen times. Where his feet had stopped tonight without him telling them. He'd walked straight across it, with his eyes open, and he didn't stop, and he didn't slow down, and when he got to the shards he stood at the edge of them, outside, and looked down at the three of them kneeling there.

"Nothing?" said Ice.

"Nothing," said Thea.

He looked at the sand under Gigi's free hand. At the little brown shapes pressed into it.

"What did you plant?"

"Forget-me-nots," said Gigi. Her voice was wrecked. She didn't seem to care.

Ice laughed. Not much. Just a breath, surprised, the way he'd laughed on the bridge in Evermorrow. "Here?"

"It'll take."

"I know it will."

Gigi wiped her face with the back of her wrist and looked up at him for a long moment. Then she said, "What did it show you? When you came for it?"

Xola went still. So did Thea.

Ice looked across the ring of shards, at the empty middle, for a while. Long enough that Xola thought he wasn't going to answer. Then he said, "A table."

"What table?"

"Mother Crane's. The long one, in the Spoon. Under the canopy." He shifted the crock in his arms. "Everyone at it. All of you. Gabe laughing. Deston with his cup. Dennis arguing about who was paying." His voice didn't change at all. "Alive. Passing bread."

*Alive.*

Xola heard it. She heard it go past, and she put it with all the other things, the way the club had been putting things since floor 2. She didn't ask. Nobody ever asked.

"And you?" said Gigi.

"There was a seat," said Ice. "I couldn't sit in it."

Gigi looked at him. Then, unexpectedly, she snorted.

"Well," she said. "That's where we're going."

He blinked. "Yes."

"So it's not a want any more, is it." Gigi got to her feet, still holding Xola's hand, pulling Xola up with her. "It's a plan."

Ice's face did a thing Xola had only seen it do three or four times, in two lives. On the quay on eleven. On the stair from fourteen. It went open, all at once, like a door somebody had finally found the right key for.

"It's a plan," he said.

***

They walked up to the west rim together. All four of them, and Gabe falling in behind as they passed the line, as if he'd been waiting for exactly this and nothing else. Thea had Ice's hand again. Xola had Gigi's. She hadn't let go of it. She found she didn't want to.

At the top of the slope, the thirty-one were sitting in a long line along the rim in the moonlight, pretending very hard not to have been watching.

Higu had the rock out on her knees.

She'd seen. Of course she'd seen; she was a Ranger, and she'd been watching the whole way with the moon behind her, and she missed nothing, ever. Under *NOW*, under the onions, she was writing.

*THE BOWL: NOTHING THERE.*
*GIGI PLANTED THE FORGET-ME-NOTS.*
*XOLA FIRST.*

"Don't write that," said Xola.

Higu didn't look up. She underlined it.

Xola looked back, once, from the rim. Down the long slope of the bowl to the middle, where the moonlight was coming off a ring of glass teeth on the sand. There was nothing there. Nothing standing, nothing waiting, nothing showing anybody anything. Just glass, and a ring, and in the middle of it, too small to see from up here, something pressed into the ground that hadn't come up yet.

It would, though. By morning. She knew it would.

"Come on," said Gigi, and pulled her hand. "Cistern by dawn. Ice says."

"Ice doesn't know," said Xola. "Ice never came down."

"I know it's half a night," said Ice. "I walked it eleven times."

"Each way," said Higu.

They went down the far side of the rim into the Glass in a long line, at night, the way you were supposed to, with the lantern at the front and the moon on everything and Waddles complaining. Xola walked with Gigi's hand in hers. Every hour, Ice brought the crock back down the line to her, and said please, and she put her hands on it and thought about the bottom of a well.
