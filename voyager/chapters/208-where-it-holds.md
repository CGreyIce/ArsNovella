Chris had never used Breakpoint on something that wasn't trying to kill him.

He'd thought about it. On fourteen, in the Long Barn, with the straw man at the end of the aisle and a string from a nailed post to its nose, measuring how far its head had turned in the night. He'd thought: if I stopped it, everything, just for a moment, and looked at all of it, would I see where it was going? He hadn't tried. You didn't use a thing like that for looking. It came up in fights. It came up when something screamed and the scream tried to put *Afraid* on him, and instead of being afraid, everything stopped, and he could look. *Everything stops. Look at all of it. Then let it go on.* It had come up first on floor 1, against the Bellwether, and he'd called *Niko, right, two steps* and *Winter, higher, under the ear*, and they'd done it, and the ram had gone down.

He'd never once used it to look at something just to see what it was.

He stood at the edge of the ledge on the left cliff of the Narrows in the grey morning of the seven hundred and thirty-first day, with the jam groaning below him and the lake rising behind it, and thought: why not?

"What are you doing?" said Winter.

"Looking," said Chris.

He opened his notebook. He put the pencil behind his ear. He looked down at the jam. And he did the thing.

***

Everything stopped.

He'd never been able to describe it properly. He'd tried, in the notebook, on floor 1, the first time, and crossed it out. It wasn't that time stopped. Things went on moving; he could feel them going on, somewhere, the way you can feel a clock ticking in another room. It was that *he* stopped. Stopped needing to keep up. For a moment, the length of a held breath, the world went on at its own speed and he didn't have to go with it. He could just look. At all of it. Every part. At once.

He looked at the jam.

It was enormous. He'd known that. He hadn't known it like this. From the ledge, normally, you saw the front of it, the slabs standing up, the glitter, the pale arms going into the cliffs. Now he saw all of it. The way you'd see a building if somebody took the front off it. Slab on slab on slab, going down under the surface of the water, going back up the valley under the lake, packed together in a great wedge that filled the Narrows from cliff to cliff and from the riverbed to higher than the ledge. And every slab was pushing on the next one. He could see it. Not see. Feel. Lines of push, running through the jam like cracks through glass, every slab leaning on the one in front of it, all of them leaning toward the door, all of the river's weight pressing down through them from behind.

And all the lines went to the arms.

That was the thing. That was what he'd needed to see. The push didn't just go to the door. It ran sideways through the jam, slab to slab, to the five places where the pale arms came out of the ice and went into the rock. Five arms. Five places. Every line of push in the whole jam ran to one of them. The arms were holding *everything*. Every slab. Every ton of the river.

But not equally.

He saw it. He'd never have seen it from the ledge; not in a year. The lines weren't the same. The arm on the upper left, nearest the ledge, where the jam was highest and the lake was pressing hardest, had more lines going to it than any of the others. A thick knot of them, like every road in a country running to one town. The arm below it, lower on the left cliff, had fewer. Across the gap on the right cliff, the three arms there: one high, with a lot; one in the middle, with less; and one low, right at the downstream edge of the jam, where the ice was thinnest and nearest the door, with hardly any. A few lines. A small section of the jam, a corner of it, the downstream right-hand corner, leaning on that arm and nothing else.

And in the middle, under all of it, the heart.

He looked at it. Through the slabs, through the black ice, through everything. Curled. Pale. With its five arms going out from it like the spokes of a wheel. And every line in the jam, every one, after it went to an arm, went *on*. Down the arm. Into the middle. Into the heart.

It was holding all of it. Through its arms. All of the river. Every ton.

He felt something then, in the stopped moment. He didn't know what. Something like the feeling you get looking at somebody carrying too much, up a stair, refusing to put any of it down.

Then the breath ran out, and it all went on.

***

He sat down on the ledge rather fast.

"Chris." Winter had his arm. "*Chris*. Your nose."

He put his hand up. It came away red.

"It does that," he said. "Sometimes. If I look too long." He took his hand away. "I've never looked that long."

Maigo was beside him already, with his hand on the back of Chris's neck, warm, the way Maigo's hands were warm. The bleeding stopped. A leaf on Maigo's head went yellow at the edge, and stayed on.

"What did you see?" said Niko.

Chris got the notebook off the ledge where he'd dropped it. His hands were shaking, a bit. He opened it to a new page. He drew a picture. He wasn't good at pictures; Higu was the one for pictures. But he drew the jam the way he'd seen it, from the front, with the five arms going out from the middle into the cliffs on either side like the spokes of a wheel, and the heart in the middle, and lines. Lots of lines. Running through the jam to the arms, and down the arms to the heart. He drew the lines thick for the upper left, thick for the upper right, thinner, thinner, and very thin, just a few, for the lower right, at the downstream corner.

He numbered them.

*1* for the upper left. *2* for the lower left. *3* for the upper right. *4* for the middle right. *5* for the lower right, the downstream corner, the thin one.

And under the picture, he wrote what he'd seen.

*It's not holding them all the same.*

*5 is holding hardly anything. Just the corner. If 5 let go, just the corner would go. A bit.*

*Then 4. Then 2. Then 3. Then 1.*

*Every line goes to the heart. The heart holds all of it. Through the arms.*

He looked at it. Then he turned the notebook round and held it out, so they could all see.

"It's an order," said Chris. "There's an order. Smallest first. From the bottom. From the door end." He put his finger on *5*. "If that one lets go, the corner goes through. On its own. A bit. And the rest's still held." He moved his finger. "Then the next. Then the next. Each one's a bit bigger. But each one's only a bit." He looked up. "That's how you let something go a bit at a time. You don't start with the thing it's holding hardest. You start with the thing it's barely holding at all."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Sabe was looking at the picture. She couldn't read. She looked at it the way she looked at ice, with her head on one side and her milky eyes narrowed.

"Five," she said. "That's the corner. By the door." She looked down into the Narrows. "Every year the corner goes first. A bit. Before the rest. Some years." She was quiet. "In the years it goes slow."

"And the years it doesn't?"

"The corner doesn't go," said Sabe. "It holds with the rest. And then everything goes."

Chris looked at his picture.

"Then we make the corner go," he said.

***

How was the next column.

*HOW.* He wrote it at the top of the page in his small square capitals, and looked at it, and thought, briefly, of a grey notebook from Quill's at the bottom of his pack, with a page in it headed the same way, from the fourteenth day of floor 1, with one line left on it. He didn't get that one out. That was a different *how*. He'd decided a long time ago that he'd know when it was time for that one.

This one was simpler. *How do you make an arm let go of a rock?*

They worked it out by the fire. All of them. Sabe and Tuli too. That was how Chris's party had always worked things out, since the white room: everybody said what they had, and he wrote it down, and then he looked at all of it at once, the way he'd looked at the jam, and saw what was there.

"Hot, then cold," said Winter.

They looked at her.

"The Prism," said Winter. "On six. Its faces. Ina heated them white, and I froze them, and they cracked. Hot, then cold. Things that are ice crack when you do that." She looked down at the pale arm going into the cliff below the ledge. "That's ice. It's an arm, but it's ice. Ina warms it where it goes into the rock. Where it's holding. Then I hit it. Hoarfrost. *What you hit stays where it is.*" She shrugged. "It cracks. It lets go of the rock."

"Or it just goes through the rock," said Niko. "Or it cracks and the whole arm comes off and the jam goes."

"Then we don't do it to the big ones," said Winter. "We do it to five."

Chris wrote it down. *Hot then cold. Ina, Winter. Crack it at the rock.*

"It'll need to know where," said Maigo. Slowly. He'd been sitting with his bare feet on the rock of the ledge for an hour, listening. "Where it's holding. It goes into the crack. But not all of it's holding. There's a place. Like a knuckle. Where it's gripping hardest." He looked at the cliff across the gap. "I can find it. If I can get my feet on the rock near it. The cliff'll tell me."

*Maigo. The knuckle.*

"It'll want a cut," said Niko. "As well. To be sure. If it cracks and doesn't let go." He put his thumb on his sword at his hip, on the dull place, and then on the letters on the back of the blade. "I can leave one. On the arm. At the knuckle. The night before. And call it home when Winter hits it. So it goes all at once. The crack and the cut." He looked at Chris. "So it's not two things. It's one."

*Niko. Wait for Me. A cut that waits at the knuckle.*

"And it'll want *when*," said Tuli.

Everybody looked at her. She'd been sitting next to Ina, close, the way she'd sat next to him since the night of the first word, with her mother's pole across her knees. She went red under the snow-burn and lifted her chin.

"You can't just do it," said Tuli. "Whenever. The ice has to be ready. The corner has to be heavy enough to go when it's let go of. If it's not heavy enough it'll just sit there. And then the arm'll grab it again." She looked at her gran. "And if it's *too* heavy, if you wait too long, it'll take the next bit with it. And the next." She swallowed. "You have to listen. For when it's ready. Like listening for thick." She put her pole down on the rock. *Tock*. "Gran can. I can, a bit. Through the pole."

Sabe looked at her granddaughter for a long time.

"She can," said Sabe. "More than a bit."

Chris wrote it down. *Sabe, Tuli: WHEN.*

He looked at the page. At all of it. *Ina, Winter: hot then cold. Maigo: the knuckle. Niko: the cut that waits. Sabe, Tuli: when.*

And himself. He looked for his own name, and it wasn't on the page, and he thought about that.

"And you," said Ina.

Chris looked up. Ina was looking at him across the fire, with his mask pushed up and his hands palm-up on his knees.

"You'll look," said Ina. "When it goes. All of it. At once. So you'll see if it's going wrong. Before any of us can." He was quiet. "That's yours."

Chris looked at him for a long moment.

Then he wrote it at the bottom of the page. *Chris: look at all of it.*

***

They did five on the morning of the seven hundred and thirty-second day.

It was on the right cliff. Across the gap. They couldn't get to it from the ledge; the jam was between them, a quarter-mile of heaved and broken ice, groaning. So they went over the jam.

Chris would never forget it. He wrote it down that night in more detail than anything he'd written since the Long Barn, and when he read it back, he couldn't believe they'd done it. Seven people, roped together, at first light, crossing the top of a jam of river ice the height of a church, slab by slab, with Sabe at the front with her pole going *tock* on every step and Tuli behind her going *tock* a step later, and both of them saying *here* and *not there* and *wait* and *now* in low voices, and everybody else putting their feet exactly where the two of them said. The slabs moved. Under their feet, some of them, a little. Groaning. Somewhere down in the jam, under them, the heart went *once*, and once, faster than yesterday, and they could feel it in their boots.

Nobody said anything. Not even Winter.

They got across. They came down off the jam onto a shelf of black rock at the foot of the right-hand cliff, a few metres above the water, just upstream of the door, where the lowest of the five arms came out of the jam and went into a crack in the cliff, as thick as a tree, pale, glistening, tight.

Five.

Maigo took his shoes off. He put his bare feet on the rock beside the crack and shut his eyes. Nobody spoke. After a long time he opened them and put his hand on the arm, about a metre into the crack, where the pale ice went into the black rock.

"Here," said Maigo. "The knuckle. It's holding here."

Niko had come the night before. Alone, with Winter watching from the ledge across the gap with her bow, and Ina beside her, ready. He'd gone across the jam in the dark with nobody but Sabe's pole sounding from the ledge to tell him where. Chris had hated it. Niko had said *it has to be the night before; it's called Wait for Me, it has to wait* and gone. He'd come back three hours later grey with cold and said *done*. Chris looked now at the place Maigo's hand was. In the dim, under the arm, against the rock, he could see it. A thin line of orange light, hanging in the air. A hand's length. Perfectly still. Humming, very faintly. Waiting where Niko had left it.

"Tuli," said Sabe.

Tuli put her pole down on the edge of the jam. *Tock*. She tipped her head. Her eyes shut. She listened for a long time.

"Not yet," she said.

They waited.

Chris stood on the rock shelf with his shield on his arm and his notebook in his coat and watched the arm, and the jam, and the water. It was the longest wait of his life. Longer than the Long Barn. The arm didn't move. The jam groaned. The heart went *once*. The lake, behind, rose. You couldn't see it rise, from down here, but you could feel it, in the weight of the air, in the way the whole jam leaned a little more every hour toward the door.

*Tock.*

"Not yet," said Tuli.

*Tock.*

Her face changed.

"*Now*," said Tuli. "Gran—"

"Now," said Sabe.

Ina pulled his mask down.

***

He put his hands on the arm at the knuckle, where Maigo's hand had been, and the white-blue fire went into it.

Chris had seen Ina's fire a hundred times. He'd never seen it like this. It didn't burn. It went *in*. Into the pale ice of the arm, white-blue, spreading out from Ina's palms in a slow bloom like ink in water, until the whole knuckle of the arm was glowing from inside, and the ice of it, where it met the rock, went from pale to clear to something like glass, and started to *steam*. A hiss. The ice expanding. Swelling against the rock that held it. Ina's face, under the mask, white with effort. Five seconds. Six.

"*Winter*," said Ina.

He took his hands off and threw himself sideways.

Winter put an arrow into the knuckle from three metres. Hoarfrost. The frost went out from the arrowhead into the glowing ice in a white star, and the heat met the cold, and the arm, at the knuckle, where it was holding, went *crack*.

Not loud. Small. The way a bone goes.

"*Niko*."

Niko put his thumb on the letters on the back of his blade. Chris saw his lips not move. *I'm here.*

The waiting cut came home. The thin orange line in the air under the arm went *into* it, all at once, along the crack Winter had made, the way a thread goes through the eye of a needle.

And the arm let go of the rock.

Chris saw it. In the grey light. He'd been ready. He'd told himself he'd be ready. He did the thing.

Everything stopped.

He looked at all of it. The arm, coming out of the crack in the cliff like a hand pulled out of a sleeve, slowly, glistening, the knuckle split. The lines in the jam, the push, the weight, running to *five* and finding nothing there. Running *on*. Not to *four*. Not to the heart. Down. Into the corner. The downstream right-hand corner of the jam, the small corner, the bit that had only been held by *five*. All the lines that had gone to the arm going into the corner instead, and the corner leaning. Leaning on nothing. Starting to go.

On its own.

He looked at *four*. The next arm up. Its lines hadn't changed. It was still holding what it held. He looked at the heart. The heart had felt it. He could see that. Every line in the jam went to the heart, and the heart had felt one of them go slack, and it was—

It was gripping. Harder. With the other four.

He saw it tighten. The other four arms, in the cliffs, going tight. Pulling. Holding everything else harder, the way you'd hold on tighter to the rest of something when a bit of it slipped out of your hand.

*It's frightened*, he thought. *Ina was right. It's frightened and it's holding on to the rest.*

But it wasn't letting go of the rest. It wasn't letting go of everything. Only the corner.

*A bit.*

The breath ran out. Everything went on.

***

The corner went.

It went slowly, the way something very big goes slowly. A section of the jam the size of a village, a whole corner of heaved and broken ice, groaned, and leaned, and slid. Forward. Through the door. Through the Narrows. Slabs the size of barns going end over end, down, off the front of the jam, into the open river below the gap, with a sound like a cliff falling into the sea. Spray went up a hundred metres. The rock shelf they were standing on shook. Chris was knocked flat. He heard Tuli shout. He heard Winter say a word.

And then it was gone.

Through the door. Down the river below the Narrows, a long tumbling grinding mass of ice going away toward the summer moorings, breaking up as it went, slowing, spreading. The water came after it. Not all of it. A tongue. A long dark tongue of the lake behind, coming through the gap in the corner where the ice had been, over the lip, down. Fast, and then less fast. And then steady. A river. Running. Through a small gap in the downstream corner of the jam, where nothing had run before.

The rest of the jam held.

Chris sat up on the rock shelf. He looked up at the jam. At the four arms still in the cliffs, tight, holding. At the heart in the middle, through the black ice, going *once*, *once*, *once*, fast. At the corner, where the corner had been, gone, and the water running through.

"She let a bit go," said Sabe.

She was on her knees on the rock with her pole flat on the stone in front of her. Her face was wet. She was laughing. Chris had never heard her laugh. It sounded like ice settling.

"Fifty-eight thaws," said Sabe. "Fifty-eight. I've never once seen the corner go on purpose." She looked up at the jam. "*She let a bit go.*"

Tuli was standing at the edge of the shelf with her pole in both hands, looking down at the water running through the gap. Her face was doing something Chris couldn't name. She put her pole down. *Tock*. She listened. She lifted it.

"It's lower," said Tuli. "The lake. Behind." Her voice was very small. "It's gone down. A hand." She looked at her gran. "*A hand*, Gran."

And then she turned round and threw her arms round Ina, who was sitting on the rock with his mask still down and his hands smoking, and held on, hard, and didn't say anything.

Ina sat very still. Then he put one hand on her back. Lightly. The way you'd put your hand on something you'd been asked to keep.

***

The hounds came out of the jam about a minute later.

Chris should have expected it. He'd written it down, on the first night: *Floe-hounds live in the jam. (Sabe.) They come out when it moves.* He'd written it and not looked at it again, because there'd been so much else to look at. And the jam had moved. A corner of it had gone. And out of the cracks in the face of the jam, out of the black gaps between the slabs, all along the front of it, they came.

Not three. Not six. Chris stopped counting at twenty.

Long and pale and smooth, sliding out of the cracks onto the slabs and down onto the ice and the rock, with their small black eyes and their door-wide mouths, coming the way they came, low and fast and flat. And not from underneath, this time. From above. Off the face of the jam. Down onto the rock shelf where seven people were sitting, stunned, at the foot of the right-hand cliff, with an old woman and a girl.

Chris was on his feet with his shield up before he'd thought about it.

He'd never been the best of them at fighting. He knew that. He wasn't Gabe or Azac or Salty, a wall. He wasn't Rui Xiang, fast, or Winter, sure. He'd been a Knight on the first morning because a Knight seemed sensible and a Guardian because somebody had to stand in front, and he'd stood in front, for two years, carefully, the way he did everything, watching. That was what he was good at. Watching. Seeing where things were going to go.

He did the thing again. He shouldn't have. His nose went the moment he did it, warm down his lip. He did it anyway.

Everything stopped.

He looked at all of it. Twenty-three hounds. Coming down the face of the jam. Where each one was. Where each one was going. Two going for Maigo, who was on his knees with his feet still on the rock and his eyes shut, too deep in the cliff to see them. Three for Niko. One, a big one, coming down a slab on the left, low, going for the edge of the shelf, for the place where Tuli was standing with her arms round Ina.

The breath ran out.

"*Winter*, left, the slab, the big one, it's going for Tuli. *Niko*, three, from above, behind you, two steps right. *Ina*, Maigo, two, his back. *Sabe*, down. *Down.*"

They did it. They'd always done it. *Niko, right, two steps*, on floor 1, against a ram. *Winter, higher, under the ear*. Two years of his voice in a fight saying where, and them going there, and he'd never once got it wrong.

Winter put the big one on the slab down with two arrows, frost and frost, and it stayed where it was. Niko went two steps right and turned and three hounds came down on the place he'd been and he cut the first one through its open mouth and left a line in the air behind him where the second one was going to be and it went into the line and the line closed. Ina got to Maigo with his hands already burning and put white-blue fire into the faces of the two coming at Maigo's back and they reared away screaming without a sound. Sabe went down flat on the rock with her pole over her.

And one came for Chris.

He saw it coming. He had his shield up. He hit it. Shield-slam, the one he'd learned on two, the edge of the shield into the face of the thing as it came up off the ice at him. It went back. Another came from the side. He turned. A third was already there. On his arm. His shield arm. Its door-wide mouth closed on his forearm below the shield, and the short curved teeth went in, and the drag started. Back. Toward a crack in the rock shelf. Toward the water.

He didn't scream. He was surprised, afterwards, that he didn't.

He looked at it. That was all he could think to do. He looked at the thing on his arm with its small black eyes, and its mouth open round his forearm, and he saw the thin place. Of course he did. It was the only thing he'd ever been good at. The mouth was open. The mouth was the thin place.

He put his sword in it.

The hound let go.

***

It lasted about four minutes. Chris wrote it down, after, to the second, because that was what he did. Four minutes and some. Twenty-three hounds, and seven people on a rock shelf, and an old woman and a girl. At the end of it there were eleven dead hounds on the rock and the ice, and the rest had gone back into the jam, into the cracks, out of sight, and the face of the jam was groaning and the heart was going *once*, *once*, *once*, very fast.

Nobody was dead.

Maigo had a long bite down his back that Ina had burned closed before it could bleed much, and two leaves gone. Niko had a cut on his face from a slab of ice, not a hound. Winter had shot every arrow she had and was sitting on the rock with her empty quiver across her knees and her hands shaking, very slightly, which on Winter was screaming. Ina's palms were blistered from the inside, from too much fire too fast; he held them up and looked at them and said *huh*, exactly like Gabe. Sabe was sitting up, furious, with her pole across her knees, saying things to the hounds that Tuli said she'd never heard her gran say in her life.

And Chris's arm was bleeding.

Badly. He sat on the rock and looked at it. Below the shield, where the teeth had gone in, the forearm was open in a row of short curved punctures, deep, and the blood was coming out of them in a way he didn't like. He looked at it with interest. He thought he was probably in shock. He wrote it down, in his head, the way he wrote everything. *Left forearm. Bite. Deep. Bleeding. I don't like the colour of it.*

Maigo crawled over to him on his knees, with his back burned closed and his face grey, and put both hands round the arm.

"Hold still," said Maigo.

"I'm holding still."

"You're *writing it down*. In your head. I can see you doing it." Maigo's hands went warm. "Stop it. Hold still."

Chris held still.

The warm went into the arm. The bleeding slowed. Stopped. The punctures closed, slowly, from the inside, the way things closed when Maigo did them, like something growing back. Another leaf on Maigo's head went brown and came off and fell onto Chris's knee.

"Sorry," said Chris. "About the leaf."

"They grow back," said Maigo. "You said that. On twelve. You wrote it down."

Chris looked at the leaf on his knee.

Then he looked up. At the jam. At the corner, gone, and the water running through the gap where it had been, steady. At the four arms in the cliffs, tight. At the heart, through the black ice, going *once*.

One. They'd done one. A bit. The smallest bit. And it had worked, and the lake behind had gone down a hand, and nobody had died. And the jam was still holding everything else, harder than before. And there were four arms left, each one holding more than the last. And the heart.

And two days.

He got his notebook out. His hand was steady now. He opened it to the page with the picture. The jam, the five arms, the lines. He drew a line through *5*. Neatly. Once. He wrote beside it: *Day 732. Let go. A bit. Lake down a hand.*

Then he looked at *4*. And *2*. And *3*. And *1*.

And at the heart, in the middle, with every line in the jam running into it.

He wrote, at the bottom of the page, under everything:

*The arms we can do. One at a time. We know how now.*

*The heart.*

He stopped. He looked at it for a long time.

*The heart: ?*

He didn't know. He didn't know how you made a heart let go of everything a bit at a time. He didn't think you could cut a heart, or crack it, or find its knuckle. He thought about Ina, on the first night, with his hand on the rock. *You don't fight a thing that's frightened. You sit with it. Until it isn't.*

He wrote that under the question mark.

Then he sat on the rock shelf at the foot of the right-hand cliff of the Narrows, with a leaf on his knee and his arm closed and blood drying on his lip, and looked at the question mark for a long time, and thought about a man on a ledge high above a frozen river on the first morning of fifteen, saying *I don't know*.

*He held on to all of it*, Chris thought. *For two years. Every floor. Everything he knew. He never put any of it down. Not a bit. Not till fourteen.* He looked at the heart in the ice. *All or nothing.*

*And then on fourteen, at the line, he asked. And on fifteen he said I don't know. And then he asked how.*

*A bit at a time.*

He didn't write that. That was a different *how*. That one went in a different notebook, on a different page, on the last line.

He thought he might know, now, what it was going to say.

Not yet.
