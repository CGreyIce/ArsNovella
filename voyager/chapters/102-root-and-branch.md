Hako had a rule about bad nights, and the rule was that they ended.

He'd had it since long before any tower. It wasn't clever. It was the kind of thing you'd put on a mug. But it had got him through some things on Earth, and it had got him through the floor-2 snow with frostvenom in his leg and Kei counting drops of poison into honey, and through floor 6 with the top of his shield gone and a hole in the shape of his friend standing in front of him. You held on. You did the next thing. And then it was morning, and it was fine. It was always fine.

He'd said that to Ice once, on Earth, in a call, at about four in the morning, after they'd lost something for the ninth time in a row. *It's always fine, right?* And Ice had laughed so hard he'd had to mute.

He thought about it on the night of the two hundred and thirty-fourth day, standing on the lowest platform of the north tree with his shield up against a wall of briar that was coming up the trunk toward him.

It wasn't fine.

***

It had started at dusk.

Maigo had said it would. He'd been sitting against the north tree since midnight the night before, with his hand on the bark and his leaves gone stiff and dark, not eating, saying very little. *It's under us. It's in the roots.* And all day the Down at the foot of the seven had been ticking louder than anybody had ever heard it, louder than at the log, louder than at the circle. Burr had doubled the watch, and then doubled it again, and the bough-folk had gone quiet in a way Hako hadn't seen before. No whistling. Children kept in. Old ones on their doorsteps looking down.

And at dusk, when the light at the tops of the trees went gold, the Grow had come up off the ground.

Not across it. *Up*. Up the trunks of the seven. All seven, at once, all the way round. Briar and fern and pale grasping root, climbing the bark of the great trees the way ivy climbs a wall, except fast, so fast you could watch it go, a man's height a minute, up into the dark of the lower branches.

The bough-folk had screamed.

Hako had never heard them scream. Not when the sawyers came. Not when walkers turned up in their baskets. They screamed now, the whole of Highcrook, from every platform and doorway, one high terrible sound. And Burr, on the drum platform with a knife in each hand, had gone white under the grey of his skin and said, very quietly, to nobody:

"It's never left the ground. Never. Not in any story. Not *once*."

That had been four hours ago.

***

Now it was at the lowest platform.

Hako stood on it with his shield up and his spear grounded and his back to the steps that went up to the drum, and watched it come over the edge. Fern-tips first, unrolling. Then the briar behind them, dark red, thorned, hooking itself over the edge of the boards and pulling. Then root. Pale. Thick as an arm. Feeling its way across the platform toward him like a hand across a table in the dark.

He put his shield on it.

It pushed. He pushed back. That was all you could do. You couldn't cut it; it grew back twice. Burr wouldn't have fire on the trunks, not up here, not with every house in Highcrook hanging over their heads, and nobody had argued. So you put your shield on it and leaned, and it leaned back, and nobody moved, and you held.

Chris was on the platform to his left, holding the next stretch of edge the same way. Azac to the right. Jared further round. Gabe and Salty were on the east tree. Drew had put tanks on every platform the Grow could reach, all the way round the seven, and everybody else behind them, and Burr's people up top hauling ladders up out of reach and cutting ropes that the Grow had got onto.

And Ice.

Hako could see him from here. On the drum platform, up the steps. Standing at the edge with his left hand on the rail, looking down. Not doing anything. He'd tried to come down. Hako had heard it. *Let me take a platform.* And Drew had said *no*, and Rachael had said *no*, and Gabe, from the east tree, had shouted *SIT DOWN DEC* so loud that the bough-folk on the next bridge had flinched.

So he was standing there. Watching thirty-five people hold a town up against a forest in the dark. With his hand on the rail so tight that Hako, forty metres away, could see the knuckles.

The root under Hako's shield leaned harder. He leaned back.

"Hako," said Chris, from the left, very calmly. "How long can you do this?"

"Till morning," said Hako.

"Are you sure?"

"No," said Hako. "But it's always fine."

Chris laughed. It came out a bit wrong. They held.

***

It stopped at first light.

Not all at once. The ticking went down, slowly, the way it always went down at dawn, and the Grow stopped leaning. It didn't go back. It just stopped where it was. On every trunk of the seven, up to the lowest platforms, a thick dark mat of briar and root and fern, clinging to the bark, motionless, as if it had been there for a hundred years.

Hako stood with his shield on it for a long time after it had stopped, in case. Then he sat down where he was, on the boards, and put his head back against the trunk, and shut his eyes.

His arms were shaking. He let them.

"It's morning," said Chris, beside him, also sitting down.

"Told you."

"You said you weren't sure."

"I wasn't." Hako opened his eyes. "It's still morning, though."

Up on the drum platform, Maigo was standing. He hadn't stood up in a day and a night. He was standing at the rail with his hand flat on the trunk of the north tree, looking down, past the mat of briar, at the foot of the tree. Where a ring of stones was. And some ash. And a young tree, taller than Gigi.

It was still there. Hako could see it from here, through the gap in the branches. Still standing. In a little circle of bare ground where the Grow hadn't gone. Everything else round the foot of the seven was buried in green, waist high, chest high, climbing. And in the middle of it, a young tree, with a hand's width of clear ground round it, all the way round, where its own roots were.

"She's holding," said Maigo. To nobody. His voice was very small. "She's holding on."

***

Higu called it at breakfast. Nobody ate.

"It came up the trees," she said. "It's never done that. It's never come here. It's never done anything but kneel in one place for longer than the bough-folk have been alive." She looked round at all of them on the drum platform, grey-faced, in the early green. "We burned it on Tuesday and it came here. To the one place on this floor we care about. And then it climbed up to get at us."

"Is it Tuesday?" said Calvin.

"I don't know, Calvin, it's an *expression*." Higu turned to Ice. "Where."

Ice had sat down at last. On the coil of rope, with his bad leg out. He looked like he hadn't slept. He hadn't.

"Under the north tree," he said. "In the roots. Maigo says."

"What will it do?"

"I don't know."

He said it simply. Hako watched him say it. On floor 8 he'd known everything. Where the city was, where the serpent lived, which pool. On this floor he'd known almost nothing, and said so, and every time he said so it had cost him something you could see.

"It never came here," said Ice. "It never moved. It never—" He stopped. "I don't know what it does now. Nobody does. That's yours."

"Okay," said Higu. "Then how."

***

They worked it out together. That was the thing Hako would remember. Not one of them had it. All of them had a bit.

Maigo had the first bit.

"It's not in its hands," he said. He'd been listening all night. "Its root. I thought it'd be its hands, because that's where the Grow comes out. But the trees—" He shut his eyes. "When it came down. The first time. It knelt. And its *knees* went in. Not its hands. Its hands came after. It knelt and its knees went into the ground and stayed there and became roots." He opened his eyes. "It's never stood up. Not once. Not since it came down. That's where it's planted. Its knees."

"So it's kneeling under the north tree now," said Chris.

"In the roots. Its knees in the old roots, right under the seedling. And its hands are out all round, under the seven, in every root it can reach." Maigo's leaves were shaking. "That's how it climbed. It's holding the trees from underneath."

Gigi had the second bit. She was sitting with her arms round her knees, very pale, and her watch open in her hand. She'd been holding it all night, on the platform behind Hako, while he held the root off the edge.

"*Pull up one that was never planted*," she said. "Mine says pull it up. Not cut. Not burn." She shut the watch. "If it comes up out of the ground, it's not holding anything any more. It's just a thing. And things burn."

"It sank last time," said Hyvred. "When Ina touched it. It went down into the ground like water."

"Then it can't be in the ground when Ina touches it," said Gigi.

"How do we get it out?"

Nobody answered.

Hako was looking at Ice.

He didn't know why. He just was. Ice was sitting on his coil of rope with his left hand in his lap, palm up, with the scar across it. And Hako was thinking about the night of the split, and the log, and a light going up in the dark, and Drew's rock. *Everything in the dark will come to you instead.*

"Beacon," said Hako.

Everybody looked at him.

"It's in the dark," said Hako. "Under the ground. Under the roots. That's dark, right? That's the darkest place there is." He looked at Ice. "*Everything in the dark will come to you.* The moths came. The sawyers came. Everything came. On the night of the split." He swallowed. "Would it come?"

Ice looked at him for a long time.

"It might," he said.

"It came up the trees to get to us," said Higu slowly. "It wants something up here. It wants *us*." She was looking at Ice now too. "Or him."

"So it comes up for the light," said Chris. "Its hands come up. Gigi gets hold of them."

"It'll just pull them back down," said Gigi. "It's strong. It's got the whole floor. I can't hold the whole floor."

"You don't have to hold its hands," said Maigo suddenly. "Its *hands* aren't its root." He'd stood up. "Its *knees* are. If it reached up. If it reached up high enough. If it wanted the light so much it *stood up*—"

He stopped.

Everybody had gone quiet.

"Then it'd pull its own root out," said Gigi. Very softly. "Itself. Out of the ground. Standing up."

"And then you've got hold of it," said Maigo. "And you don't let go."

"I never let go," said Gigi.

And then, last, because it was his, Burr had the final bit. He'd been standing at his drum the whole time, with his arms folded, listening, saying nothing, the way he did. Hako had forgotten he was there.

"How high," said Burr, "does it have to reach?"

They looked at him.

Burr unfolded his arms. He put his two-fingered hand on the rope on the drum beside him. The one that went down through the branches to the Down. The one that had hauled thirty-six walkers up out of the dark on their first night, and Chris and Niko out of the cave, and Ice out of the Grow on the morning after the split.

"I hold the rope," said Burr. "Always." He looked at Ice. "Put your light on the end of it. And I'll put it as high as you like."

***

Drew wrote it on the rock.

*THE SOWER. FOOT OF THE NORTH TREE. FIRST LIGHT TOMORROW.*
*ICE: MAKE A LIGHT. BURR: PULL.*
*GIGI: DON'T LET GO.*
*EVERYONE: COME.*

He put it in the middle of the drum platform, face up, where everybody could see it. Then he looked at Ice.

"Somebody thank me," said Drew.

Ice was looking at the rock. At the second line.

"You want me on a rope," he said. "Hanging. Over it. With a light."

"Yeah."

"Going up."

"Yeah," said Drew. "You don't go anywhere. You just go up." He paused. "Alea said that, apparently. On the night of the split. You told Thea. Thea told me."

Ice looked at Thea. Thea looked back at him, with the lantern on her knees, and didn't say anything.

Ice laughed. Very quietly, under his breath, the way he did on this floor.

"Thank you, Drew," he said.

***

They held the trunks through one more night. It was worse than the first. And at first light on the two hundred and thirty-sixth day, they went down.

Not by rope. The Grow had the bottoms of all the ropes. They went down the north tree itself, by the ladders and the mat of briar that had stopped where it was at dawn, climbing down through it with their weapons out, slowly, all thirty-six, and Burr's people above them letting down every foxfire jar in Highcrook on lines, so that the foot of the north tree came up out of the dark in a wide cold ring of blue-green light.

The Down was chest high. All round the foot of the seven, the Grow stood up like a field of wheat gone wrong, dark and thorned and leaning. Leaning in. Toward the north tree. Toward the young tree at the foot of it, in its hand's width of clear ground, with its leaves very still.

They came down into the clearing round the young tree, one after another, and made the ring.

Hako took his place in front of Gigi.

That was where Drew had put him. Gigi in the middle of the clearing, right beside the young tree, kneeling, with both hands flat on the ground. Thea behind her with the lantern and her ward. Maigo at the foot of the north tree, three metres off, with his bare root-foot in the earth and his eyes shut, rooted, listening. And Hako in front of Gigi, between her and wherever it came from, with his shield up and his spear in his hand.

"Don't let it touch her," Drew had said. "Whatever happens. She can't move. She can't defend herself. She's holding."

"I know," Hako had said.

"You're the door."

"I know, Drew."

Round them, the ring. Chris and Azac and Jared and Salty and Gabe with their shields facing out. The healers behind. The ranged on the roots, high. Ina on the north tree's lowest root with the mask already down, the white fire sitting in his hands like a held breath. Hyvred beside him with her glasses off.

And in the middle of everything, beside the young tree, on the end of Burr's rope, Ice.

He'd tied it round himself. Under his arms, round his chest, a plain knot that Burr had checked twice with his two fingers. The rope went up from him through the gap in the branches, up into the dark, to the drum, where Burr stood with his hands on it and his six on the handles behind him. Ice stood on the ground with the rope slack above him and his sword in his right hand and his left hand empty, and looked round at all of them. At the ring. At the Grow, leaning in, chest high, all round.

"Ready?" said Drew.

"No," said Ice.

"Good," said Drew. "Light."

Ice lifted his left hand.

***

The amber went up.

Hako had seen it twice now. Once through the gaps of a briar wall from far off, and once through the specks of a room of thorns. He'd never stood right next to it. It came out of Ice's palm and up his arm and out of the whole of him, warm and gold, and went up the trunk of the north tree, and out across the Down in every direction, over the leaning ferns, gold on everything. It lit Gigi's face, kneeling, very white. It lit the young tree. It lit Maigo, at the foot of the north tree, with his eyes shut.

And under Hako's feet, the ground moved.

It was like standing on a drum that someone had hit from underneath. One deep thud, up through his boots into his knees. Then another. Then the earth all round the young tree's clear ground split. In long lines. Cracking outward, the way the black earth had been cracked in the circle, round a thing driven into it.

And something came up.

A hand.

It came up out of the earth three metres from Ice's feet, between Hako and the young tree. White. Grey-white, smooth as old bone, wet with black earth. Long. Far too long. The fingers went on and on, jointed in too many places, thin as roots, and they *were* roots. At the ends of them, pale threads, trailing, ripped out of the ground. It came up out of the earth like something surfacing out of deep water, reaching. Toward the light. Toward Ice.

"*GIGI*," shouted Drew.

Gigi didn't shout anything. Hako heard her breathe in, behind him, a long breath, like somebody diving.

The bramble came up out of the ground round the hand. Brown-gold, thick, hooked, faster than Hako had ever seen it come. It went round the white wrist in one coil, two, three, like a rope round a post. And held.

The hand stopped.

It pulled. Hako felt it pull. Not through his hands. Through the ground. The whole earth under the clearing heaved, down, as the thing under it tried to drag its hand back into the dark. And Gigi made a sound. A short hard sound, through her teeth. And her bramble held.

*It doesn't let go.*

The second hand came up behind Hako.

***

He heard it before he saw it. A crack of earth, right behind him, between his back and Gigi. And he turned, and it was there. Coming up out of the ground beside her. Not reaching for the light. Reaching for *her*. The long white root-fingers curling over toward her bowed head where she knelt with both hands flat on the ground holding the first one.

Hako put his shield on it.

He didn't think. He was a door. He stepped in between and put the shield into the fingers as they came down, and they hit it.

It was like being hit by a tree.

Not a branch. A tree. The whole weight of something enormous coming down on the face of his shield, and the shield going back into his chest and the breath going out of him and his boots going back across the ash, a metre, more, ploughing. His arm went numb to the shoulder. He heard something in the shield go. Not break. *Go*. A crack, through the wood under the steel.

He didn't move off it.

He leaned. The way Ice had taught him, years ago, in a call, in a game he'd been terrible at. *No, Hako. Don't step back. Lean in. It can't push you over if you're already leaning.* He set his feet in the ash and leaned into the shield with everything he had, and the white fingers pushed, and he pushed back, and they stopped. Twenty centimetres over Gigi's head.

"*Hako*—" Thea, behind him, and her ward came up over his shield and Gigi's head together, pale and clear like glass.

"*Fine*," said Hako, through his teeth. "*It's fine*—"

The fingers came round the edge of the shield. One of them. Thin and white and fast, round the rim, and across his forearm, above the strap. It didn't cut. It *gripped*. And it was cold, colder than anything, and it pulled, and his arm went with it, and the shield started to come round.

Something hit the finger.

A dagger. Then another. Rui Xiang's, from nowhere, low, under the shield, into the joint where the finger bent. And an arrow behind them, frost going out white from the head of it across the white of the hand. *Hoarfrost. What you hit stays where it is.* Winter, from the north tree's root, high. The fingers stopped dead. Frozen to the rim of his shield. Not gripping. Just stuck.

"*Got it*," shouted Winter.

"Thank you," said Hako, very politely, to nobody, and leaned back into it.

Somewhere behind him Kei's hand landed on his back, flat, and something went into him. Green. Bitter. *Measure*. He felt his numb arm come back, all at once, the way your foot comes back after you've sat on it, and he could have cried, and he didn't, because he was busy.

***

And the Grow came.

He'd known it would. They all had. He saw it out of the corner of his eye: all round the clearing, the field of chest-high briar and fern lifting, ticking, coming in. Walls. Coming up out of the ground between them, the way they'd come up at the log and at the circle. Between Deston's five and Hana's. Between Yan's and Chris's. Splitting. Sorting. Seven rooms of five and one of one.

And bending.

He saw it happen. The walls came up between them and *bent*. Away from the light. As they rose they leaned, the way every fern on this floor leaned, and they leaned away from the amber pouring out of Ice's raised hand, and they couldn't close. There was nowhere for them to close. Every one of the thirty-five was standing in the light, looking at it. Every one of them could see him. And anyone who could see him could find the way, and the way was right here, and the walls came up and bent and opened and fell back, again and again, all round the ring, like waves on a beach.

"*IT CAN'T*," shouted Calvin, somewhere, delighted. "*IT CAN'T DO IT*—"

"*Hold*," said Drew. "*Everybody hold*—"

And under them, the earth heaved.

***

It was coming up.

Hako felt it under his boots. Not the hands. Something bigger. Under the ash. Under the clearing. Under the young tree. Something moving in the earth, rising, pushing the ground up from underneath so that it bulged and cracked all round him. The young tree swayed. Gigi cried out.

"*Maigo*," shouted Drew.

At the foot of the north tree, Maigo opened his eyes.

"*It's still kneeling*," he said. His voice cracked high. "*It's coming up but it's still kneeling. Its knees are still in. It's reaching. It wants the light. But it won't stand up, it won't let go of the ground*—" He pressed his hand flat on the bark. "*It needs to reach higher. It's not high enough*—"

Hako looked up.

At Ice. Standing in the middle of the clearing, on the end of the rope, with his left hand up and the light pouring out of it. Three metres from the white hand in Gigi's bramble, reaching for him, straining. Not reaching him.

"*Burr*," said Ice. Not loudly. Up. Into the dark, at the rope.

And Burr pulled.

***

Ice went up.

Slowly. That was the thing Hako would never forget. Not fast. Not a jerk. The rope went taut above him and he came up off the ground, a hand's width, two, his boots leaving the ash, and rose. Steadily. The way a lamp goes up a mast. With his left hand still lifted over his head and the amber still pouring out of it. Not moving. Not struggling. Not reaching for anything. Just going up, into the air over the clearing, turning very slightly on the rope, with the light.

*It doesn't go anywhere. It just goes up.*

And the ground under the young tree burst.

The head came first. Grey-white and smooth and faceless and enormous, the size of a cart, coming up out of the earth between the young tree and the north tree with black soil pouring off it, tipped back. Looking up. With no face. At the light. Then the shoulders. Thin and long and white with moss on them, the moss Ina had burned black three days ago. Then the thin bone-white chest. Rising. Reaching. Its one free arm going up, and up, the long root-fingers spread, straining for the light going up above it on the end of a rope.

It wasn't high enough.

It reached higher. Hako saw it reach. Its whole long body straining up out of the earth after the light, the way the ferns strained. The way everything on this floor strained. *More. Up. There.*

Ice went higher. Burr's people hauled.

And the Sower stood up.

***

Hako heard it. They all did. A sound like the biggest tree in the world coming out of the ground. A long tearing groan, from under everything. The earth all round the young tree heaved up in a great ring and split, and out of it came the Sower's knees.

They weren't knees. Not any more. They were roots. Two great thick knots of white root, where its legs went into the ground, with a thousand pale threads trailing off them into the dark. And they were coming *up*. Tearing out. Out of the old roots of the north tree, where they'd been sunk. Out of the dark under the seven where they'd been driven a thousand years ago and never moved. The Sower was standing for the first time since it came down. Standing up, on legs that had been roots for longer than the bough-folk had been alive, rising out of the ground after a light on the end of a rope.

"*GIGI*," screamed Maigo. "*NOW. IT'S UP. IT'S OUT*—"

Gigi let go of the hand.

Hako felt her do it. Behind him. The bramble round the white wrist went loose all at once, the way her hedge had gone loose on the eighth morning. And in the same breath, she put both hands flat on the ground again and *pulled*.

The bramble came up round the roots.

Not one coil. Not ten. Everything she had. Brown-gold and thick and hooked, out of the ground all round the Sower's torn-up knees, round them, through them, between the thousand pale threads, up its legs, binding. And then, at the base, where the roots had come up out of the dark, it went *down*. Into the hole. Into the earth. And wrapped round something there, and held it, and *pulled it up after*.

The Sower came out of the ground.

All of it. Every thread. Every root. It came up out of the earth under the north tree in a great tearing rush of black soil and white root, and Gigi's bramble had it, all of it, round the legs and through the roots and over the place where it had been planted, and it was standing in the clearing for the first time in a thousand years, swaying, reaching up after the light, and *nothing of it was in the ground at all*.

"*It's out*," said Gigi. She was on her feet. Hako didn't know when she'd stood up. Her hands were out in front of her, shaking, fingers spread, the brown-gold going out of them in streams. "*It's out, it's out, I've got it, I've—*"

And at the base of it, where its roots had come together, where its legs had been driven into the earth, Hako saw it.

***

A knot.

At the bottom of the Sower, where the roots met. Where it had been planted. Grey-white like the rest of it, but different. Thick. Twisted. A fist of root, the size of a man's head, and it was *moving*. Slowly. In and out. Like a breath. Like a heart.

"*Hyv*—" said Hako.

"*I see it*," said Hyvred. From the root of the north tree, with her glasses off and her golden eye with the little red mark in it gone sharp as a pin. "*It's not nothing. Everything else on it is nothing. That's not. That's the only thing on it that's anything*—" Her voice cracked. "*Hako, it's right there. It's right in front of you*—"

It was.

It was two metres in front of him. At the height of his chest. Where the Sower's torn roots came together, swaying, as the whole long white body above it reached up and up after the light. Unguarded. Because it had never needed guarding. Because it had been in the dark under the earth for a thousand years where nothing could ever reach it.

Hako looked at it.

He thought about a lot of things, very quickly. About floor 2, the snow, Kei's drops. About floor 6, a hole in the shape of a person, and his spear going through it like a doorway, and nothing there. About being slightly behind. About being the one who came last to everything, the one who didn't get it till it was explained twice, in every call, for years. About a voice in his ear at three in the morning, patient, saying *again*. And *no*. And *again*. Until he got it.

*Don't think about it. Look where it is. Put it there.*

He dropped the shield.

He'd never dropped his shield in a fight. Not once. Not on any floor. It went down onto the ash with the frozen white finger still stuck to its rim, and he had both hands on his spear, and he stepped in. One step. Into the swaying roots. And put the spear into the knot.

***

It went in like a spade into wet ground. Then it hit something. Then it went through.

The Sower screamed.

He'd never heard anything like it. Nobody had. It came out of the whole of it at once, out of every root and thread, out of the earth all round, out of the Grow, out of every fern and briar on the whole floor at the same time, a sound like every tree in the Down being torn up together. The ground shook. Highcrook shook above them. The young tree bent nearly flat.

The Sower came down. Its reaching arm. Its faceless head. Down out of the air, toward the earth, the way it had come down a thousand years ago, trying to kneel. Trying to put its knees back into the ground.

Gigi's bramble wouldn't let it.

"*No*," said Gigi. "*No. You don't get to go back down.*"

It hung in her thorns. Half kneeling. Its torn roots a hand's breadth off the earth, and not touching. Never touching.

"*INA*," shouted Drew.

The white fire came.

It came off the root of the north tree in one long blazing sheet, white as a star with blue at the edges, and it hit the Sower in the chest. And this time, it couldn't go anywhere. It couldn't sink. It was held up out of the ground in a net of brown-gold thorns, with a spear through its heart, and the fire went over it the way fire goes over dry paper, all at once, from the chest outward, white.

It burned.

It burned the way Ice had said it would, on the drum platform, too quickly, before he'd caught himself. *It burns.* It burned white and then grey and then black, its long arms, its faceless head, its thousand threads of root, curling up in the white fire like paper. And Gigi's thorns held it the whole time, and didn't burn, because Ina's fire burned only what he put it on and nothing else, and he'd put it on the Sower.

When it was done, there was nothing in Gigi's bramble but ash.

***

The ticking stopped.

Hako heard it stop. Everybody did. All of it. Every creak and tick and snap of the Grow, all round the clearing, all round the seven, out across the Down in every direction, as far as anyone could hear. It had been going since the first morning. Since the door. It had been going so long that nobody heard it any more. And it stopped. And the silence came in after it like water into a footprint.

He stood in it with his spear in his hand and the ash of the Sower drifting down past his face, and couldn't hear anything at all.

And then every fern in the Down turned.

He saw it. They all did. All round the clearing. All through the chest-high Grow, as far as the foxfire reached, and further, out into the dark. Every frond. Every briar. Every pale shoot. They turned. Not leaning. Not growing. *Turning*. All at once. Slowly. The way a crowd turns to look at something.

Not at the clearing. Not at Gigi, or at Hako, or at the ash.

Up.

At Ice. Hanging on the end of Burr's rope over the clearing, ten metres up, turning slowly, with his left hand still raised and the amber still pouring out of it.

Every growing thing on the floor looked at him. All at once. For one long moment.

Then it stopped.

Hako looked up at Ice. Ice was looking down. At the Grow. At all of it, turned toward him. His face, in his own light, had gone completely white. Hako had only seen it go that white once before. At the big pool on floor 8, when the Great Fall had stopped for a heartbeat.

Beside the young tree, Thea had the lantern up. She was looking at the ferns. Then up, at him.

"Not at us," she said. Very quietly. "That time. Not at all of us."

Ice didn't answer. Hako saw his lips move. He was talking to Alea. Hako didn't hear what Alea said back.

Then Ice lowered his hand, and the light went out, and Burr let him down, very slowly, to the ground.

***

She'd sat down when the Sower was gone. Just sat, where she was, beside the young tree, with her hands in her lap, shaking all over. Thea was beside her. Hako was on her other side, because he hadn't known where else to go, and because his legs had stopped working properly at about the same time hers had.

He saw her windows because she held them up for him to read.

Then he took his foot out of the ground, slowly, and came across the ash, and sat down on Gigi's other side, between her and Hako, without being asked, and leaned on her.

"It's gone," said Maigo. "The trees can't feel it. Anywhere. First time since—" He stopped. "Since before anything."

"Good," said Gigi.

Old Linden had come down. Of course she had. She'd made them let her down in a basket before the ash had stopped falling, and she was standing at the foot of the north tree now with her cheek on the bark and her blind eyes open, the way she always stood. And she was smiling.

"She's quiet," said Linden. "The north tree. Nothing under her. Nothing holding her. First time in my life." She pressed her hand flat. "She's just *standing*."

***

Ice came and sat down in front of Hako at some point. He didn't know when. Things had gone a bit loose. Ice crouched down on the ash in front of him with the rope still round his chest, untied, hanging, and looked at him.

"You dropped your shield," said Ice.

"Yeah."

"You never drop your shield."

"I know." Hako looked at it, lying on the ash a few metres off, cracked through the wood, with a frozen white finger still stuck to the rim, slowly going to ash like everything else. "It was right there. The thing. Right in front of me. And I thought—" He stopped. "I thought, *don't think about it. Look where it is. Put it there.*"

Ice looked at him.

"You said that," said Hako. "In a call. At three in the morning. When I was terrible at everything. You said it about forty times."

"Forty-one," said Ice. "I counted."

Hako laughed. He didn't mean to. It came out of him wet and stupid and he put his hand over his face.

"Is it fine?" he said, through his hand. "Ice. The ferns. The—all of them. Looking at you."

Ice didn't answer straight away.

"It's fine," he said.

Hako took his hand away from his face and looked at him.

"You're lying," said Hako.

Ice looked back at him for a long moment. In the grey light at the foot of the north tree, with the rope hanging off him and the ash on his shoulders.

"Yeah," said Ice.

He said it quite simply. Not sad. Not anything. Just true. And Hako, who'd said *regressor* out loud to Kei in a bowl of gold glass on floor 6, who'd seen a man move like ten thousand hours across a bowl of gold glass, who'd kept it, like everyone kept it, carefully, like a hot cup, sat in the ash and looked at his friend and thought:

*It's not looking at all of us any more. It's looking at you. Because you're the one who's been here before. And it knows.*

He didn't say it. He'd promised.

He said, instead: "Okay. Then it's not fine. But it's morning."

Ice looked at him. And then he laughed. Properly. The first time in a week.

"Yeah," said Ice. "Yeah, Hako. It's morning."

***

Burr counted them up the ropes.

He'd come down himself, the way he had on the morning after the split. He stood at the foot of the north tree on the Down, which he hated, in the quiet, with the Grow standing round him chest high and not ticking, and looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at the young tree, taller than Gigi, with its leaves moving very slightly in no wind at all. Then at the ash where the Sower had been.

"Thousand years," said Burr.

"About that," said Maigo.

"It came down. And knelt. And we never knew." Burr shook his head. "My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother. All of them. Living over it. Never knew it was there."

"You know now," said Gigi.

Burr looked at her. Then he looked at all of them.

"The way up," he said. "Where you're going. Linden's people had a story. That there's a door. Out where nobody goes." He jerked his chin south-west, toward the circle. "Where the white thing knelt."

"That's where it'll be," said Ice. Quietly. "In front of where it knelt."

Burr looked at him for a long time with his big shining eyes, the way he'd looked at him on the first night, deciding.

"You knew to stand in the middle of the seven," he said. "On your first night. Walkers don't know that."

"I was told," said Ice.

"By who?"

Ice looked at him. Then he smiled, very slightly.

"By you," he said.

Burr didn't understand that. Hako could see him not understand it. He stood there for a moment with his two-fingered hand on the rope, frowning, and then he grunted, and decided not to, and turned to his drum.

"Up," said Burr. "All of you. Before the gold." He took hold of the rope. "I'll count."

He counted. Hako heard him, all the way up, standing at the top by the drum as the baskets came up out of the quiet Down one after another into the green, his lips moving, the way they had on the first night.

"Thirty-six," said Burr, at the end. "Nobody left down."
