Lanthorn's weather was a held breath.

Rui Xiang had felt it since the eyes. Everybody had, he thought, but he was the one who felt weather, so he felt it the way you feel a pressure in your ears before a storm. Two hundred people in the roots round a black pool, and every one of them, since the fall Edda went, walking a little more quietly than they had. Looking down the Hollow a little more often. Touching their lanterns as they passed them, the way Hana touched the little one on her crown. Not afraid, exactly. Waiting. The weather of a whole town standing very still in a field with its face turned toward the place the thunder came from last time.

It didn't change. Fall after fall. The drip fell into the pool and Isel said the number, in her small flat voice, getting it right more often now, and the town hummed, and went on holding its breath.

He read the thirty-six too. He couldn't help that either.

Thea's weather had been the same for a long time. Since the aurora on ten. *Somebody carrying a full cup up a stair*, he'd thought of it, a long time ago, on a roof in Tell. A hundred days and more and she'd never spilled it. Now the stair went down, and it was dark, and the cup was a lantern wrapped in a cloak that she held against her chest everywhere she went and slept on and ate with in her lap. *A full cup, carried down a stair, in the dark.* She didn't look at anybody when she walked. She looked at the steps.

Gabe's was a wall. It always had been.

And Dec's—

Dec's had changed.

He'd been reading Dec's weather since they were seventeen. In a poly corridor, in a queue for a bus, in a canteen, at two in the morning on a call. It had always been the same, under everything, whatever was on top. A storm, a long way off, that never came closer and never went away. Something being held at arm's length. Something that would break, one day, all at once, and take everything with it.

It had broken on the jam. He'd felt it go. Thirty-five threads at once and one word, *help*, and then a long dark gap with Gabe shouting, and then *twenty-six, it hurts*.

And after that the sky had been clear.

Not empty. Not calm. Dec was still frightened of things; Rui Xiang could see it every time Dec looked at the bundle in Thea's arms. But it was a clear sky with weather in it, the ordinary kind, the kind that comes and goes. Not the storm. Not the thing held off at arm's length for twenty-odd years.

*Clear, after rain*, Rui Xiang thought. *Like the morning after a long night of it. When everything's wet and bright and you can see a long way.*

He'd never once in his life seen Dec's weather look like that.

He didn't tell anybody. It wasn't his to tell. He kept it, the way he kept everything.

***

"It came the next fall," said Corwen. "For the fire. When I was a boy."

They were in the forge. Most of them. The thirty-six didn't fit, so they were in the forge and outside the forge and on the walkway and up the ladders, the way they always were when they needed to talk. Corwen at his bench with his hands flat on it. Isel in the corner with Edda's old lantern, which she carried everywhere now, the way Thea carried hers. Xav sitting on an upturned crate with his elbows on his knees, the way he sat when he was waiting.

"The house caught on the twelfth drip," said Corwen. "By the sixteenth it was out. The whole street had come and put it out with buckets from the pool. And on the third drip of the next fall, the moths came. Not a few. All of them. And behind them, up the Hollow, the Moth." He was quiet. "It came for the light of the fire. The fire was out. It came anyway. It came for where the light had been."

Nobody said anything.

"That was a fall," said Corwen. "One." He looked at the bundle in Thea's arms. "It's been nine."

"Why hasn't it come?" said Malcolm.

"I don't know."

"Is it a bad thing? That it hasn't?"

Corwen looked at him for a long time. "I don't know that either."

"It's waiting," said Isel. From the corner. Without looking up from her lantern. "Gran said. When she was little, it waited outside a house where a man had a candle he wouldn't put out. It waited three falls. Till he opened the door." She turned Edda's lantern in her hands. "It's waiting till that one's open again."

Thea didn't move. Her arms didn't tighten on the bundle. Rui Xiang read her and there wasn't anything there to read, which was how he knew she'd heard it.

Xav stood up.

He didn't say anything at first. That was how Xav did things. He'd wait, in a room, in a fight, on a wall, longer than anybody could stand, until the thing had shown everybody what it was going to do, and then move. Once. It was the most frustrating thing about him and the best. On floor 10 he'd moved early once in nine years, and a boy had died, and he'd never moved early again.

"Then we go and look at it," said Xav.

Everybody looked at him.

"It knows where we are," said Xav. "It's seen the lantern. It's waiting. We don't know where it is, or what it is, or how big, or what it does when it comes. We know it ate a street." He looked at Corwen. "Nobody's ever gone down? Not ever?"

"There's nothing down there. The old fields. The old houses. Then the roosts. Then dark." Corwen shook his head. "Nobody goes past the roosts. Nobody's ever come back from past the roosts."

"Then we'll be careful at the roosts," said Xav.

Rui Xiang felt it before anybody said anything.

Not from Xav. From the corner by the door. From Dec.

He was sitting on the floor with his back to the wall and Tuli's pole across his knees and Gabe's terrible lantern on his belt, empty. And when Xav said *we go and look at it*, his weather moved. Rui Xiang felt it come up in him like the wind getting up before a squall, the old thing, the storm, rising: *me. Me first. I'll go. I'll go down alone, a floor ahead, first, in the dark, and look at it, and come back and tell you, and you won't have to—*

It came up. All the way. He saw Dec's hands tighten on the pole.

And then he watched Dec put it down.

It took about three breaths. He saw every one. Dec sitting against the wall with his face doing nothing, and the weather in him rising, and then going still. Not gone. Put down. The way you put down something heavy on purpose, slowly, so it doesn't break.

"Xav," said Dec.

Xav looked at him.

"Who's going?"

"Us," said Xav. "My lot. We've got John's lights. Rui Xiang's first down anything." He was quiet for a moment. "You want to come?"

Everybody went very still.

Dec looked at Xav for a long time. Then at the bundle in Thea's arms. Then at Thea, who was looking at the floor.

"No," said Dec. "I'll stay with the lantern."

Gabe turned his head and looked at him. Slowly. Like a man who'd been handed something he'd stopped expecting.

"Off you go," said Dec. To Xav. To all of them. "Go and look at it. Come back and tell me what's there." He leaned his head back against the wall. "I'll be here."

Rui Xiang read his weather. Clear. Frightened, a bit, round the edges. But clear.

*Off you go. Tell me what's there.*

Nineteen years and fourteen floors and two lives, if he was right about the two lives, which he was fairly sure he was though nobody had ever said it and nobody would. And Dec had never once said that to anybody.

***

They went down on the next fall. Five of them. Xav in the middle, because Xav was always in the middle. Gabe at the back, with Hearthwall. Malcolm beside Gabe, because Malcolm was always beside whoever was at the back. John off to one side.

And Rui Xiang in front.

He was always in front. Going down anything. Stairs, cliffs, the Wellgate in Tell, the inside of the Countinghouse. He didn't decide it. He just was. He went first because he could see what was coming, a breath before anybody else, the way he could see weather on a face. It made him early. It had never once made him wise.

The path went down past the roots of Lanthorn and out the far side and on, down the slope, under the white trees.

At first it was like the slope above. Pale moss, great paper-white trunks going up into the dark, strays drifting between them, fewer than above but still there. Their lights in their little lanterns on their collars, turned down to sparks, scattered to a hundred points. John's motes going ahead.

That was the thing about John on this floor. He'd been sending motes ahead for two years, *a little light, see what it sees*, and nobody had ever thought much about them. On sixteen, everybody thought about them. A mote was a light. Small and pale and on its own in the dark, the size of a walnut. It looked like a stray. It *was* a stray, nearly. And John sent them out ahead down the path in a long loose line, five, six, seven, a light every hundred paces, going down into the dark, and saw what they saw.

"Nothing," said John. Quietly. Walking off to one side, with his eyes half shut, the way he walked when he was looking through a mote. "Trees. Moss. More trees." A pause. "A wall."

"A wall?"

"Stone. Low. Old. Going along the slope." Another pause. "Another one. Below it. And another." He opened his eyes. "Terraces. They're terraces. Somebody built fields."

They came to them at the end of the second drip.

Edda had said it. *We had fields once. Before my gran. There was more light then.* He'd thought she meant it the way old people mean things. She'd meant it exactly.

Terraces, all down the slope, as far as the motes could see. Low walls of dry stone, built by hand, holding up long narrow strips of flat ground. The walls had held. The fields hadn't. There was nothing in them but grey moss, thick, springy, gone over everything like a sheet over furniture. And every so often, in the moss, a shape. A trough. The stump of a post. A wheel off something, half-buried.

And lanterns.

On posts at the ends of the terraces. On hooks in the walls. Hundreds of them, all down the slope, the way they hung in Lanthorn. Iron, woven, open. Rusted. Empty. Every one of them. Swinging very slightly in an air that didn't move.

"Oh," said Malcolm. Behind him. Very quietly.

They walked down through the terraces for most of a fall. Past houses, after a while. Not in roots, like Lanthorn. Built. Stone, with turf roofs gone to moss. Doors open. Nothing inside but moss and dark. A village. Then fields again. Then another.

The strays thinned. Then there weren't any.

He noticed the exact moment. They passed under a white tree with a great split in its trunk, and there was one stray drifting on the far side of it, high up, alone, the size of a plum. And then they walked on, and he looked back, and it hadn't followed. It hung there, at the split tree, the way something hangs at the edge of a place it won't go past.

After that, nothing. No lights but theirs. And John's, going on ahead, getting further apart.

"Rui," said Xav. From the middle.

"I know."

"How far?"

"I don't know." He looked down the dark slope. He'd never had to say that before. Going down anything, first. He'd always known, a breath early. "I can't see anything coming. There's no weather down here. There's nothing to read." He was quiet. "It's like the clerks. Like floor 1. Nothing."

"Then we go slow," said Xav.

They went slow.

***

They found the roosts on the third fall.

John found them, or his motes did. He stopped walking so suddenly that Malcolm walked into him. His eyes were shut. His face had gone grey.

"Ow," said John. Quietly.

Rui Xiang was beside him before he'd thought. "What?"

"A mote went out." John's hand had come up to his chest, flat, as if he'd been pinched there. "The far one. Seventh. It just— Something had it." His eyes were still shut. He was breathing carefully. "Like pinching a candle. With wet fingers. It's fine. It's—" He stopped. "Sixth's gone."

"*John*—"

"Pull them back," said Xav. Calmly. "All of them. Now."

"I'm pulling them." John's face twisted. "Fifth. Fourth. They're coming up the slope after them. They're *following*. The lights. They're following the lights up—"

"How many?"

John opened his eyes. He looked down the dark slope, toward where his motes had been.

"All of them," said John.

They came up the terraces out of the dark without a sound.

Not five. Not ten. A cloud of them, grey-brown, silent, wings open, coming up the slope between the white trunks in a long drifting wave, the way smoke comes up a hillside from a fire. Following John's motes. Every one of the three left, coming back up the slope toward John's hand as fast as he could pull them, and the moths behind them, close, closer, tongues uncoiling.

"*Go out*," said John. Through his teeth. To his motes. "Go *out*, go out, I'm pulling you, go—"

The last three went out. All at once. John went down on one knee on the moss with a sound like somebody who'd been hit.

And the moths, with nothing in front of them any more, hung there in the dark. Twenty paces off. Fifty of them. More. Turning. Looking. For the point.

Rui Xiang's spark was on his collar in Jared's lantern, scattered to a hundred points. So was everybody's. Five little iron cages, five small scattered lights in the dark, each of them nowhere and everywhere.

*A moth can't find the middle. It goes past.*

They didn't go past.

Gabe's shield was warm.

He hadn't thought of it. Nobody had. Hearthwall was always warm. It didn't glow, not to look at, it was just iron, kiln-heart iron, a hundred folds, with a heat in the middle of it that never went out. *It does not go cold.* And in the dark, in the cold of the bottom of the Hollow, with everything else scattered to points, it was the only warm thing for a mile. And the moths could feel it.

They turned toward Gabe.

"*Back to back*," said Xav.

***

He'd fought a lot of things in two years. They all had.

This was different, because there were too many, and because they made no sound, and because he couldn't read them. That was the worst of it. He'd fought wolves and men and things in the Down, and everything that fought had weather, a breath before it moved. You could see it coming. These didn't. There was nothing in them. They came out of the dark at you in perfect silence with nothing on their faces, because they didn't have faces, and they were on you before you'd seen them start.

So he didn't read them. He did what Dec did.

*Early, not fast.* That was what everybody said about the way Dec fought. Never where it landed. Not fast; early. And Rui Xiang had thought, the first time he heard it, *that's me*. That was what reading weather was. Starting early.

You couldn't start early on a thing with no weather.

So he started earlier.

He stopped looking at the moths and started looking at the gaps. The places they'd have to come through to get to Gabe. Between two trunks. Over the terrace wall. Down from above, out of the dark. And he went to the gaps before anything came through them. Every time. A breath before. Two. He didn't know what was coming. He just went where it would have to come.

A moth came over the terrace wall, wings open, and he was already there. His knife went into it below the head, where the furred body joined, and he twisted and pulled and it came down on the wall like a dropped coat.

Behind him, Gabe had Hearthwall up, and they were coming over it, over him, the way they'd gone over Jared's in Lanthorn, soft bellies brushing his hair, tongues reaching for the warmth. Gabe didn't swing it at them. He turned it. Edge up. And they came down onto the edge and it went through them like a spade through turf.

Malcolm behind Gabe. Hands up. Blessings going out of him, gold, on Gabe's arms, on Xav's blade, on John, who was up again, white, with a knife in his hand. Malcolm was saying something under his breath, the whole time, over and over. Rui Xiang heard it between moths. *Plan B. Plan B. Plan B.*

And Xav.

Xav in the middle of all of it, not moving.

Standing with his sword down and his eyes half shut, while moths came at Gabe and Rui Xiang and John on every side, and Malcolm's gold went past him, and he didn't move. Not a step. Not a twitch. Waiting. Rui Xiang had seen it a hundred times and it still made his teeth hurt. *Wait*.

A moth came at Malcolm's back. Malcolm didn't see it. Nobody did.

Xav moved.

Once. One step and one cut, from where he'd been standing to the place behind Malcolm where the moth would be, and the moth was there, exactly there, as if it had flown onto the sword on purpose, and it came apart. And Xav was still again. Sword down. Eyes half shut.

*Once.*

Rui Xiang went to the next gap.

He didn't know how long it went on. There was no drip down here. No count. Just the dark and the moths and the gaps and his knife. He went to the gaps. They came through them. He was there.

The fiftieth, or the sixtieth, came down out of the dark from above, straight at the back of Gabe's head, and he was already in the air. He'd gone up the terrace wall and off the top of it before it started its dive, and he met it in the middle of the dark with both knives, and they came down together onto the moss, him on top of it, and it didn't get up.

He knelt on it, breathing.

He looked at the window. He didn't stop moving. There was another gap.

***

They counted forty-one dead on the terrace when it was over, and stopped counting.

The rest had gone. Back down into the dark, the way they'd come. Not running; moths didn't run. They'd just turned away, all at once, the way they'd turned toward Gabe, and gone down the slope, silent, and were gone.

Nobody was hurt. Not badly. Gabe had a long scrape on his jaw where a wing had caught him, and it was full of grey dust, and Malcolm was picking it out with his fingers and swearing quietly. John was sitting against the terrace wall with his eyes shut and his hand flat on his chest, very white.

"I'm fine," said John. Before anybody asked. "It's fine. Seven. It's just a lot at once." He opened his eyes. "They followed the motes. Every one. They went straight for them. They didn't even look at us till the motes went out."

"I know," said Xav.

"They'd *follow* them. If I sent one away. Somewhere else. They'd go after it." John looked down the slope into the dark. "Like Hana's thing. Over the pool. Except real."

"I know," said Xav again. More slowly. And looked at John for a long time.

Rui Xiang wasn't listening. He was looking at the window.

It had changed while they were counting.

He read it twice. Then a third time.

*Get there first. Then wait for them.*

Behind him, somebody laughed. Gabe. Reading over his shoulder, with Malcolm's fingers in his jaw.

"Mate," said Gabe. "That's *you*."

"I know."

"That's literally the most you thing I've ever—"

"I *know*, Gabe."

*You always get there first*, Dec had said. At seventeen. Laughing. *You just don't know what to do when you're there.*

He'd been thinking about that for nineteen years.

His light was on his collar, in Jared's lantern, a spark. It didn't read the window out. It used to read them all out, in the voice, every one, the moment they opened. *Tier trial available*. It didn't now. It just hummed. Very quietly. One low note. As if it were reading over his shoulder too.

"Hard," said Rui Xiang.

He didn't even think about it. He'd never once in his life thought about it. Hard was the only one that said anything about him.

***

The bottom of the Hollow was on the fourth fall, and he got there first.

Of course he did.

They'd gone down from the terraces into a steeper place, where the white trees stood further apart and the ground under them was bare black rock instead of moss, and loose, broken stone that slid. Scree. A long slope of it, going down into the dark, with nothing growing on it at all. John had one mote out, only one, and kept it close, a few paces ahead of Rui Xiang's feet.

And then the roost above them woke.

He felt it. Not weather; there wasn't any. Just the air changing. Above them, in the dark, in the branches of the white trees, the undersides of the high leaves, he saw what he'd thought were leaves move. Hundreds of grey-brown shapes, hanging upside down, folded, like dead leaves on a winter hedge. Unfolding. All at once.

"Down," said Xav. "Down, go down, under the—"

He went down.

First. A breath early. He didn't think. He went down the scree slope fast, on his heels, half sliding, toward the bottom, to get under the trees, to get out from under the roost and find cover and hold it for the others. Get there first. That was what he did.

The scree went with him.

The whole slope. All at once, with a noise like a wave coming in on shingle, the loose black stone sliding under his feet and round his ankles and away, a river of it, going down. He went with it. He didn't fall. He'd never fallen, in two years, going down anything; he rode it, on his heels, knees bent, arms out, the way you ride a sand dune, down and down into the dark with the stone roaring round his legs.

And behind him, above him, the slope that he'd just come down slid away into a cliff of bare rock, smooth, too steep to climb. And then the dust came down over everything.

He came to a stop at the bottom.

On his feet. In the dark. In silence.

He turned round. Above him, the dust settling. A wall of rock where the slope had been. Somewhere up there, far up, the moths, and four people, and one of John's motes.

He couldn't see any of them.

*Nobody goes alone.* They'd made that rule on floor 1, all thirty-five, before they'd even known there'd be a thirty-sixth. Nobody goes alone. And he'd broken it once, on thirteen, going after Tull into the Countinghouse at one in the morning because he'd seen something coming a breath before anybody else and gone. He'd got there first. And he hadn't known what to do when he was there, and he'd stood in that grey hall with a thousand pens going and a thousand clerks not looking at him, and done nothing, and come back out. *I didn't know what to do when I got there.* And Xav had looked at him with his face doing nothing at all and said *that's new*.

He'd got there first again.

He turned round, and looked down, and saw it.

***

The slope ended in a lip. Rock. And past the lip, the ground fell away into a bowl.

Big. He couldn't tell how big. Not the dark this time. It had edges. He could see them, faintly, all round, far off: the rim of a great round hollow in the rock at the bottom of the Hollow, the way the inside of a cup has a rim.

And it had light.

Not much. Faint. Grey. Coming up out of the bottom of the bowl the way light comes through a curtain. Through cloth. A dim, soft, patched light, in rings and spots, that moved.

He lay down on the lip, flat, on the cold rock, the way he'd lain on roofs in Tell, and looked over.

The Great Moth was asleep in the bottom of the bowl.

He'd seen its eyes. They all had, from the pool. He hadn't understood how big the rest of it would be. It filled the bowl. It *was* the bowl, nearly, the way a cat fills a basket. Folded. Wings down over itself like a tent, like a hill of grey cloth, grey-brown and soft and patterned with rings, each ring the size of a house, dark, like eyes painted on a sail. Its body was under the wings somewhere, a long furred shape, and its head at the far end, down, with the great round eyes shut. And its tongue, coiled under its head, coiled and coiled, as thick as a tree.

And the light was coming from inside it.

Through the wings. From underneath. Faint, grey, soft, in patches and spots, moving. Not one light. Hundreds. Thousands. Moving under the cloth of the wings the way fish move under ice. Turning. Drifting. Bunching together and coming apart. Like the strays in the dark between the white trees, except under a sheet. Like a whole Hollow's worth of lights, folded up and put away.

And round the edges of it, moths. Small ones. Dog-sized. Coming down out of the dark from the roosts, one at a time, silent. Landing on the great folded wings. Lying flat. And as they lay there, he saw the faint glow in their furred bodies, the glow of whatever they'd drunk, fade. Go *down*. Into the great wing under them. And the lights under the wing get, very slightly, brighter.

He lay on the lip and understood.

*It came for where the light had been.*

They brought it here. Everything they drank. Every stray, every glim, every light that went out loud in the dark and every one they caught on a walkway. They brought it here and gave it to the thing in the bowl. And it kept them. Under its wings. All of them.

And he did a thing he'd never done before.

He read its weather.

He hadn't thought it would have any. The moths didn't. He'd been fighting things with no weather for four falls. But this did. He felt it come up off the bowl at him the moment he looked properly. Like heat off a road.

It was hungry. That was the top of it. A vast slow hunger, sleepy, patient, the hunger of something that has eaten for a very long time and never once been full.

And under the hunger—

He lay very still.

Under the hunger, it was a crowd.

That was the only word he had. The weather of a crowd. A station at rush hour, a stadium, a street on a festival night. Thousands of small weathers, all at once, all different and all the same, packed together so close they made one weather. And every one of them, every single one, was—

*Whose.*

He didn't have Heed. He didn't need it. He read it the way he'd read a face in a crowd at seventeen. Every light under those wings was asking the same thing the strays asked. *Whose. Whose. Whose. Whose are you.* Thousands of them. Under a sheet. In the dark. Asking, and asking, and nobody to answer, for longer than Edda's gran's gran had been alive.

His throat had gone tight.

*It's full*, he thought. *It's not one thing. It's full of them.*

***

The window was still open in the corner of his eye.

*Get there first. Then wait for them. Whatever comes, do not strike.*

He'd got there first.

And everything in him, every single thing, the whole of him that was early, that went to the gap before anything came through it, said: *do something*. He was at the lip of the bowl. It was asleep. Its head was at the far end, and its eyes were shut, and he could get there. He could get round the rim in the dark, silent, he was the quietest thing on any floor, and down, onto the wing, along the wing, across the furred back to the head. To the place under the head where the body joined, where his knife had gone into fifty moths on the terraces. And put it in. And find out.

*You always get there first. You just don't know what to do when you're there.*

He knew what to do. He'd always known. That was the trouble. He always knew what to do, a breath before anybody else, and he always did it, and he did it alone, because by the time anybody else knew, he'd done it.

Tamm, on the fifth wall, with his shield up.

The grey hall, and a thousand pens, and Xav on the steps with his face doing nothing.

He lay on the lip of the bowl and didn't move.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

***

He didn't know how long he waited.

There was no drip down here. He'd lost the count on the scree. He counted his own breath for a while, the way Milli counted heartbeats, and lost that too. He lay on the cold black rock with his chin on his hands and his knives under his chest and watched the bowl, and the lights moving under the wings, and the small moths coming down out of the dark one at a time to lie on the great wings and give it what they'd drunk, and didn't move.

A moth found him.

He felt it before he saw it. The air changing, above him. He didn't look up. He didn't move. He lay on the rock with his spark on his collar in Jared's little cage, scattered, and felt the moth come down out of the dark, slowly, wings open, hanging over him. A metre up. Less. He felt the faint stir of air off its wings on the back of his neck.

He could have had it. He knew exactly where it was. It was right there.

*Whatever comes, do not strike.*

He lay still.

The moth's tongue uncoiled. He heard it. A faint dry whisper, the only sound a moth ever made. It came down past his ear, feeling, looking for the point, for the middle of the light on his collar. He felt it touch the iron of the lantern. Tap. Slide. Tap.

His light went very still in the cage. He felt it. Not humming. Holding its breath.

The tongue went round the lantern. Looking for the middle. A hundred points of light, scattered, everywhere and nowhere.

It couldn't find it.

The tongue coiled back up. The moth hung over him a moment longer. Then it lifted, slowly, silent, and drifted away over the lip, down into the bowl, to the great wing, and lay down on it, and gave what it had.

His light, in its cage, started humming again. Very faintly. Very fast. The way your heart goes after.

*Thank you, Jared*, he thought. *Thank you, Corwen. Thank you, Corwen's mother.*

He didn't move.

***

The eye opened.

Not all the way. A slit. At the far end of the bowl, under the folded wings, in the great lowered head, one huge round eye opened a crack. The size of a door. Red-black, dull, like a coal nearly out. And the lights under the wings all moved at once, all of them, toward the head, the way fish move toward a shadow.

It wasn't looking at him.

It was looking up. Past him. Up the Hollow. Up the slope, past the scree, past the roosts, past the terraces and the dead villages and the split tree where the strays stopped, all the way up, through the dark, toward something it couldn't possibly see from here and could.

Toward a lantern wrapped in a cloak in a girl's arms by a black pool.

*It's waiting till that one's open again.*

He felt its weather change. The hunger going up, slowly. Not sleepy now. Patient. Interested.

And under it the crowd, all those thousands of small weathers, going up with it, the way a crowd goes quiet and then loud when somebody comes out onto a stage. *Whose. Whose. WHOSE.*

He lay on the lip.

He could stop it. Now. While it was looking the other way. He was here. He was first. Nobody else was. Nobody else knew what he knew. If he went now—

*Then wait for them.*

He shut his eyes.

He thought about Dec. In the forge, against the wall, with the pole across his knees. The old storm coming up in him all the way. *Me. Me first. Alone.* And Dec's hands tightening on the pole. And three breaths. And Dec putting it down.

*Off you go. Come back and tell me what's there.*

Nineteen years Rui Xiang had been reading that storm. He'd thought it was the most Dec thing about Dec. He'd thought it was who Dec was.

And he'd watched him put it down in three breaths. In a forge. Because somebody else could go.

*You always get there first.*

*Yeah*, Rui Xiang thought. *I always do. And then I do it. On my own. Because by the time you all get there I've already done it.*

*Just like you.*

He'd never once thought of it like that.

He opened his eyes. He didn't move. He stayed where he was, on the lip, first, with his knives under his chest and the eye open a crack at the far end of the bowl looking up the Hollow toward Thea, and the crowd under the wings asking, and he waited.

For them.

The mote came first.

A little light. Pale. The size of a walnut. Coming down out of the dark from somewhere off to the right, along the rim of the bowl, slowly, low, a few inches off the rock, the way you'd carry a candle through a house where people were sleeping.

It came along the lip to him and stopped. A pace away. It hung there in the air beside his face.

He looked at it. He couldn't help it. He smiled.

It went back the way it had come. Not fast. A pace. Then it stopped, and waited, the way he had. Then another pace.

Showing the way.

And along the rim, out of the dark, quiet, careful, one at a time, they came.

John first, off to one side, with his eyes half shut and his hand on his chest. Then Malcolm. And Xav. Last. Coming along the lip with his sword down.

He lay down beside Rui Xiang on the rock without a word. He looked over the edge into the bowl for a long time. At the great folded thing. At the lights under the wings. At the eye, open a crack, looking up the Hollow.

Then he looked at Rui Xiang.

"You waited," said Xav.

Very quietly. Not a question.

Rui Xiang didn't say anything. His throat wouldn't. He'd never once in nineteen years heard Xav sound like that. Xav, who waited for everything. Who'd waited nine years and moved early once and never moved early again. Who'd been waiting at two in the morning for Rui Xiang to come back out of a place he'd gone into alone.

"I got here first," said Rui Xiang. His voice came out rough. "Then I waited."

Xav looked at him for a long moment.

"Yeah," said Xav. "I can see."
