Tiger was always early.

He'd been early his whole life. Early to calls, early to everything, first in and then waiting for everybody else. On floor 1 he'd been first out of the Copper Spoon every morning and first to the south fields and first to the Bellwether's hollow. He'd been early so long that he'd stopped noticing it. It was just what his body did. Got there before he did.

Neth had noticed. *Quick ones always think they are late*, she'd said, on floor 8, with her paper-thin webbed fingers holding the net up in front of him, and he'd run straight into it. *It is not a thing you can be, in a net.*

He thought about that a lot.

He was thinking about it at dawn on the three hundred and eighteenth day, lying along the bowsprit of the *Patience* with his tail hanging down over the water and his chin on his crossed arms, looking at the thing that was going to try to kill them at noon.

It didn't look like anything. That was the worst part. From out here it was just a wide flat place in the sea, smooth as oil, turning. A mile off. Going round, slowly, left to right, all the way out to both sides of the world. And past it, in the middle of it, the rock and the tower, very small, with the light at the top gone pale in the sunrise.

The singer was under him. He couldn't see it, but he could feel it, through the spar, through the hull, a long low hum. It had been holding them all night. Marrit had the sails down and the *Patience* lying quiet, and something underneath, enormous and patient, keeping her out of the edge of the wheel the way you'd keep a child back from a road with your hand.

He had six hours to wait.

He'd never been so early for anything in his life.

***

At noon, Kip rang the bell.

Once. A thin clear note. Marrit at the wheel had said *now*, very quietly, and Kip had pulled the rope and then let it go and run, barefoot, to the foot of the mainmast, where Higu had told him to stand, with a quiver of arrows on each shoulder and his thin brown face set like a fist.

The turn.

Tiger was on the bowsprit still. Higu had said he could be. *Tiger on the bowsprit*, she'd said yesterday, at the council, and written it on the rock, and then looked at him. *Because you'll be there anyway.* Which was fair.

Below him, at the very front of the ship, where the bulwarks came together at the stem, Yan's party.

Salty in front. Right in the angle of the bow, at the rail, with his chipped greatsword in both hands, the one that was *serrated, that's a feature*, feet planted. Salty always went first. Behind him, Yan. Standing straight, with the cords on his left arm from wrist to elbow, black at the top, brown at the bottom, and in his right hand Working On It, the butt of the pale ash on the deck and the long curved blade high over his head, catching the noon sun along the grain. Suzu round his neck with her head up.

Wendy at the foot of the bowsprit. Right below Tiger. Bow strung, quiver at her hip, black heavy arrowheads, kneeling on the deck with one knee up. Wendy went for the eyes.

And Cursiffe at Yan's back, with a stack of river-ink pages in an oilskin pouch and one in his hand, and a pen behind his ear. Everybody on the ship had one of his pages. Tiger had his folded flat inside his shirt, against his chest, where he could feel it. One word on it, in black eel-oil ink that didn't run.

OPEN.

The rest of them were everywhere. Tiger could see all of it from the bowsprit. Azac and Jared and Hako along the port rail with their shields. Drew and Chi in the waist. The healers spread out, Rachael aft, Xola on the quarterdeck rail with her legs over the side, Maigo amidships. The archers in the tops, Hyvred at the main, Winter at the fore, Aleaka at the mizzen with her hands on the mast and her eyes shut. The mages on the quarterdeck where they could see, Thea and Ina and Bee and John, Hana with her crown off, Newish beside her. Deston at the foot of the mainmast with his hand on his shirt pocket, linking everybody, the faint warm thread of his mana running through all of them. The tide-folk who'd refused to go below, which was all of them, round the mainmast, in a tight knot.

And Ice in the middle.

By the mainmast. Not at the rail. Not in front. In the middle of the main deck, with the lamp shield on his arm, the one Gabe made him, and Gabe on his right with Hearthwall and its three warm flames. Standing where Higu had told him to. Where everybody could get to him and he could get to everybody.

Tiger looked at him for a second.

He'd never seen Ice stand in the middle of anything. Not once. Not on any floor. He'd seen him first, and alone, and in front, and a long way ahead, so far ahead that all you found was a camp under a pine tree where somebody had peeled wild onions properly, top and tail, keep the root on. He'd kept one of those skins. He still had it. In a pocket, in his pack, flat and dry and gone gold.

Ice was standing in the middle, with everybody round him, looking like it was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

"Ready," said Higu. From the quarterdeck. Not loud.

Yan breathed in.

"Annis," he said.

Out. Over the bow. Over the wheel of water. Not loud. The way he said it every morning, to the sea, before he started. Like saying good morning to somebody on a wall.

Nothing happened. Tiger hadn't expected anything to. That one was just for her.

Then Yan lifted his head, and his voice, and called.

"*Treeve Fulmar!*"

It went out across the sea like a bell.

And behind him, from the whole ship, every voice, forty-four of them, a beat late and ragged and not together at all:

"*Treeve Fulmar!*"

***

Tiger couldn't see what happened.

That was the thing about Yan's lights. Nobody could see them but Yan. Tiger knew about them now; Yan had told him on the second night after the old man died, on the bowsprit, in about four sentences, and Tiger had said *oh* and then *is mine all right?* and Yan had said *it sits still now* and Tiger had had to go and lie down. But he couldn't see them. He could only see Yan's face.

He watched Yan's face.

"*Morwen Gull! Bryok Gull!*"

"*Morwen Gull! Bryok Gull!*"

Yan's face changed. Something went across it. Not happy. Something bigger than happy, and sadder.

"Yes," said Yan. Quietly. Under the noise. To the sea. "Yes. Here. That's it."

"*Hedra!*"

"*Hedra!*"

"Yes," said Yan.

And on. He didn't stop. He didn't look at the cords. He had his left hand on them, his thumb going along the knots, and his eyes on the wheel of water, and he called them one after another, loud, clear, each one out over the sea, and the ship called them back. Tiger called them. He hadn't known he was going to until he heard himself. *Elowen Petrel. Ysella.* He knew them. He'd shouted numbers at Yan for six days, and the names had gone in without him noticing, and now they came out of his mouth on their own.

"Yes," said Yan, after every one. "Yes. Here."

And after about thirty, he said, in a different voice, "*Oh*. Oh, they're *coming*."

"What's happening?" said Wendy, below Tiger. Not looking round. Her eyes on the sea, an arrow on the string.

"They're coming *up*," said Yan. He was crying. He didn't stop calling. He said it between names, fast, as if he couldn't help it. "Out of the middle. Out of the dark. Every time I say one, one of them turns and comes *up*. *Kitto Sanderling!*" A breath. "They're coming to the ship. They're under us. All round. Like—" His voice went. "Like Hythe at night, Wendy. All the lamps on the harbour."

"*KITTO SANDERLING!*"

Marrit's voice was the loudest. From the wheel.

Tiger looked down at the water round the bow. Blue. Green. Clear. Nothing in it.

He believed it anyway.

"The water's slowing," said Hyvred, from the maintop. Very calm. "The wheel. At the edge. It's slowing."

It was. Tiger saw it. The smooth band of water a mile ahead, the edge of the wheel, where the swell had been bending sideways all morning and sliding round. The swell wasn't bending as much. It was bending less. A wave came in under the bowsprit and went on, straight, out onto the smooth water, further than any of them had gone, before it started to turn.

"It's letting go," said Aleaka.

From the mizzen. Her eyes shut. Her hands on the mast. Her voice very strange.

"It doesn't want to," said Aleaka. "It's *holding* them. And they're turning round and it can't hold them when they turn. It's—" She stopped. Her face went white. "It's frightened. It's never been frightened. It doesn't know what that is."

"*Dannet!*"

"*DANNET!*" screamed Kip, at the mainmast's foot. "*RED HAT!*"

"Keep calling," said Higu.

Yan kept calling.

A hundred. Cursiffe said it, behind him, very quietly, the way he said everything. *A hundred.* He was counting in the margins of a book in his head. A hundred and fifty. The wheel was turning slower and slower, and the swell was going straight across it now, almost to the middle, and the singer's hum under the ship had gone up, higher and higher, until it wasn't a hum any more but something like a note held by somebody who'd been waiting their whole life to sing it.

"*Tamsin Gannet!*"

"*Tamsin*," said Ottery, by the mainmast, and it wasn't a whisper.

Two hundred.

And the wheel stopped.

***

Tiger felt it more than saw it.

The whole sea in front of them went still. Not calm. Still. Like glass. Like the top of the weir at the moment the water goes over. A mile of it, flat and grey, all the way to the rock, not moving at all.

And then the middle of it began to rise.

Slowly. A swelling. A bulge in the grey, a mile off, out in front of the rock, as if something very big underneath had taken a breath. It went up. And up. The water slid off it. Grey, and then paler, and then not water any more.

"Oh," said Calvin, somewhere aft. "Oh, no."

It came up out of the sea like an island being born.

A dome. Smooth and enormous and pale, grey-white, the colour of the inside of a shell, wet and shining in the noon sun. It went on coming up. Higher than the masts. Wider than Hythe harbour. Tiger could see through it, a little, the way you can see into ice, and there were shapes in it, deep inside, dark, moving, that he didn't want to look at.

Round the bottom of it, where it met the water, a ring of eyes.

Milky. Round. Each as big as a cartwheel. Dozens of them. All the way round. Opening.

And from under the dome, out of the water all round it, the arms came up.

Long and flat and grey. Ribbons. Each one as wide as a road. Dozens of them, and they came up out of the sea in a ring round the dome and lay down on the water, all pointing the same way, round and round, and Tiger understood all at once what had been turning the sea. Not a current. Not a hole. *These.* Going round and round for three hundred years, stirring, with something in the middle that never stopped being hungry.

The window came up in front of his eyes. In front of everybody's.

"It's hungry," said Aleaka. Her voice had gone high and thin. "It's empty. It's never been empty. It's—" She opened her eyes. "*MORE. MORE. MORE.*"

Every arm turned toward the ship.

"*KEEP CALLING!*" shouted Higu.

"*Bastian Fulmar!*" shouted Yan.

And the arms came across the sea.

***

They were faster than horses.

Tiger was the fastest thing on any floor and he knew fast when he saw it, and these were fast. They came across the flat grey water in a long rushing ripple, a dozen of them, the ends lifting, flat and wide and dripping, and the sound of them was like a wave breaking that didn't stop.

The first one hit the port bow.

It came up out of the sea beside the ship and over the rail and down, flat, like somebody slapping a table, across the bulwark where Azac and Jared were. Tiger saw it hit. He saw Azac's shield take it and Azac go down, flat on his back, across the deck, three metres, his shield ringing like a bell. He saw Jared not go down. Jared set his feet and took the rest of it on the thorn-shield, the one with the rose inside the rim, and the deck groaned under him, and he *held*.

And then Salty was there.

Salty came round the angle of the bow with the greatsword already moving, low, flat, the whole of him behind it, and put it into the arm at the rail, edge first. Not a cut. A saw. The chipped edge, the serrated edge, *that's a feature*, went into the grey flesh and caught, and Salty *pulled*, the way you'd pull a saw through a log, and the edge tore along the arm, and something grey and thick and cold sprayed out over the deck.

The arm went back over the side so fast it took a piece of the rail with it.

"*That's a FEATURE!*" roared Salty, at the sea.

"*Jory Kittiwake!*" shouted Yan, behind him. Not stopping.

The second one went for the foremast.

It came up on the starboard side and reached, and wrapped. Round the mast, just above the deck, three times, the way a snake goes round a branch. And pulled.

The *Patience* heeled.

Tiger felt the bowsprit go out from under him sideways and grabbed it with all four limbs and his tail. The deck went over to starboard. Water came over the rail. People slid. Somebody screamed. The foremast groaned, a long deep wooden groan that went right through the ship, the sound of something Corran had made eleven years ago being asked to break.

"*HOLD HER!*" Marrit, at the wheel.

Hako got there first. With his spear, straight in, where the arm went round the mast, and again, a sound like a spade going into wet sand. Chi beside him with her sword, once, twice, a sound like an axe going into wet wood. And Ina, from the quarterdeck, white fire, a long bright line of it across the grey flesh, and the arm *shrieked*, it had no mouth and it shrieked anyway, and let go of the mast and went back over the side, smoking.

The ship came upright with a crash that threw Tiger half off the bowsprit.

He got back on.

"*Sennen Petrel!*" shouted Yan.

"*DAD!*" screamed Kip, from the mainmast. "*SENNEN PETREL!*"

And the third arm came over the middle of the ship.

Over the starboard rail, amidships, high, and down. Straight down. Onto the main deck. Onto the mainmast's foot, where the tide-folk were standing in their tight knot, Pell and Jago and Morva and the Lusk brothers and Ottery, and Kip with his quivers.

Onto Ice.

Tiger saw Ice see it.

He saw Ice's whole body start toward the rail, toward the arm, the way it had always gone, every floor, every fight, *first, alone, in front*. One step. Tiger saw the step.

And then Ice stopped.

He went down on one knee. In the middle of the deck. With the lamp shield on his arm, and Gabe on his right with Hearthwall up over all of them, and everybody round him.

And the light came up.

Tiger had heard about it. Sanctuary. Floor 8, the Templar trial, in the city by the water. *Kneel. Where you stand, they are safe.* He'd never seen it. It came up out of the deck round Ice like the light round a lamp, warm, gold, a dome of it, not very big, just big enough. Just big enough for everybody standing at the foot of the mainmast.

The arm came down on it.

And stopped.

A metre above Ottery's head. Flat. Like a hand slapping a pane of glass. The whole vast weight of it, the width of a road, hanging there in the air on nothing, on a little gold dome of light round a man on one knee, and the deck under Ice cracked, a long split in the planks right under his knee, and Ice didn't move.

Under it, Ottery looked up.

She looked up at the grey flesh of the thing hanging a metre over her face. And she put up one vast hand and laid it flat against the gold. From the inside. The way she'd laid it on the canvas over the old man's feet.

"*Ysella!*" shouted Yan.

And the arm went back.

Tiger saw Ice's face. Just for a second. On one knee in the middle, with the light round him and everybody under it, and his face wet. He hadn't gone to the rail. He'd wanted to. Every bit of him had wanted to. Tiger had seen the step.

He'd stayed in the middle.

***

The fourth arm got Kip.

Tiger saw it happen and didn't think. There wasn't time. The fourth one came up over the starboard bow, right by the foremast, and it didn't slap. It reached. The end of it curled, quick and clever as a hand, and went round Kip, who'd left the light, who'd been running for the foremast with a quiver for Winter in the foretop, because that was his job, that was what Higu had told him to do, and he was twelve and he did it.

It went round him and lifted.

Twenty metres. Straight up. Kip's legs kicking. A thin high scream.

And Tiger was already gone.

He didn't remember deciding. He never did. His body went, and he went after it. Off the bowsprit, onto the forestay, up it, hands and feet and tail, the rope singing under him. Off the top of the forestay into the air. Onto the arm.

It was like landing on a road. A wet, cold, moving road, grey and slick, going up into the sky at an angle. He landed on all fours and slid and drove both blades in, his two short blades, into the flesh, to stop himself. It shuddered under him. He pulled them out and ran.

Up. Up the arm. It was wide enough to run on. It was the stupidest thing he'd ever done. He didn't think about it. He ran up the moving grey road into the sky with the sea a long way down on both sides and the arm bucking under him like a horse that wanted him off, and he didn't come off, because he was Tiger, and he was the fastest thing on any floor, and he was not going to be late.

The end of the arm was curled round Kip like a fist.

Tiger got there. He didn't stop. He went past Kip's face, white, eyes like plates, and round, to where the arm thinned at the curl, where it was only as thick as a man, and put both blades into it, one each side, crossed, and *cut*.

Not a slow cut. Not Neth's knot. Just as fast as he had ever cut anything in his life.

The fist opened.

Kip fell.

Tiger heard himself shout. He saw Kip go down, turning over, twenty metres, toward the sea, too far, too high, and he couldn't do anything, he was on the arm, he'd cut and Kip had fallen and he couldn't—

Something went off the quarterdeck rail.

Lilac and pink. Fast. In a long flat dive that went out over the water like an arrow and in, with hardly a splash, exactly where Kip went in. Tiger saw the water close. He saw it open again, a second later, and Xola come up through it with Kip held against her, one arm round him, his face up out of the water, coughing.

"*Saltwater*, babe!" Xola shouted. Up at the arm. At Tiger. Laughing. "*I'm VERSATILE!*"

The arm whipped back.

It whipped back toward the dome so fast that Tiger lost his feet and went flat and slid, and drove the blades in, and held on, and the sea went past underneath him in a grey blur and the ship went away behind and the sky went round. It was going back. The arm, with him on it. Back to the thing in the middle. He couldn't jump. He looked down and there was only the sea, a long way down, grey and flat, and he hated water. He'd always hated water. He'd gone in once, on floor 8, at night, for Neth, and swallowed half the lake, and Salty had had to hold him at the post.

He held on.

And the arm, coming back to the dome, flicked, the way you'd flick something off your hand.

Tiger flew.

He went up. Over. The sky and the sea went round twice. And he came down on the dome.

***

It was cold.

That was the first thing. Cold through his clothes, through his skin, cold like the bottom of a well. He hit it on his side and slid, fast, down the curve of it, toward the water, and the ring of milky eyes round the bottom, and he drove the blades in and stopped, three metres from the nearest eye.

It looked at him.

He lay on his side on the dome of the Undertow, with both blades in it, and an eye the size of a cartwheel three metres away looked at him. Milky. Round. The pupil in it, if it was a pupil, very small and very black, turning to find him.

And then it burst.

An arrow. Black-headed. Heavy. Straight into the middle of it, from a quarter of a mile away, from the bow of the *Patience*, and the eye went in on itself like a wet paper bag and something dark poured out of it, and the whole dome shuddered under him.

Wendy.

He knew it was Wendy. Nobody else shot like that. He looked up the curve of the dome, back toward the ship, and he could see her, tiny, kneeling at the foot of the bowsprit with her bow up and another arrow already on the string.

The next eye went. And the next. One after another, round the ring, *thunk*, *thunk*, a quarter of a mile, like somebody knocking on a row of doors. The dome shrieked, everywhere, through his whole body, a shriek with no mouth.

Wendy went for the eyes.

"*Tiger!*"

Yan.

He could hear Yan. From the ship. Across all that water. Shouting. Not a name. His name.

"*TIGER! THERE!*"

He looked. He couldn't see what Yan was pointing at. He was a quarter of a mile away on a wet grey dome with his blades in it. But he could hear him. Yan's voice, cracked, carrying, the way he'd carried the names.

"*It's under you! Its own! Up and left! Ten metres! It's—*" A breath. "*It's black! It's the only one left that's black! THERE!*"

Its own light.

Tiger understood. Yan could see them. All of them. All the lights in the thing. And they'd been going, turning, coming up out of it, all the ones with names, two hundred and something of them, and the old ones with no names were still in there somewhere, and in the middle of all of it, the thing's own. Its own light. Black. Hungry. The only one left that was black.

Up and left. Ten metres.

He pulled the blades out and ran.

Up the curve of the dome, across it, slipping, catching himself, running, the cold coming up through his feet. The arms were coming back. He could feel them. Through the dome, through his feet, a shudder, a gathering. They couldn't see him any more, Wendy had most of the eyes, but they could feel him on it, the way you feel a fly on your neck. One came up over the top of the dome behind him, he heard it, the wet slap of it, and he didn't look back.

Ten metres. He stopped.

There was nothing there. Just the dome. Grey. Pale. He could see into it, a little. Shapes. Dark.

"*THERE!*" Yan, from the ship. "*You're on it! You're RIGHT ON IT!*"

Tiger put his hand inside his shirt and pulled out Cursiffe's page.

It was flat and dry and folded in four. He unfolded it. One word. Black eel-oil ink, that shed water, square and small and careful, the way Cursiffe wrote everything.

OPEN.

He slapped it down on the dome.

***

It opened.

The way the stone book had, on floor 5. The way the crab's claw had, on floor 8, under the falls, on Salty's sword. Tiger had been there for both of them. He'd seen a statue open its book because Cursiffe wrote a word on it. He'd seen a crab let go.

The dome opened under his hand like a keyhole.

A slit. Then wider. The grey flesh peeling back from the page in a long split as wide as a door, wet and cold and stinking of the bottom of the sea, and underneath it, down inside, a thing.

He didn't know what it was. It looked like a knot. A huge black knot, the size of a cart, of something like old tarred rope and something like stone, wound round itself, over and over, tight. Hard. Shining. It was the only thing in there that wasn't grey.

It was the only one left that was black.

He jumped down into the slit and put both blades into it.

They skidded.

They went in a finger's width and stopped, and skidded sideways, like a knife on a stone, and his hands went numb to the elbows with the shock of it. He tried again. The same. *Tock*. Stone. Like hitting a cliff.

"*It's like STONE!*" he shouted, to nobody, to the ship a quarter of a mile away.

The dome bucked.

An arm came over the edge of the slit above him, and down, and he threw himself flat against the knot and it went over him, so close he felt the wind of it, and slapped the dome on the other side.

He wasn't going to get out of here.

He knew it. Lying flat against the black knot in a slit in the thing, with the arms coming back. He was a quarter of a mile from the ship and the only way off was the sea and the sea was the thing. He'd come all this way. Fast. First. And he'd got here, and it was stone, and he was going to be—

*Quick ones always think they are late.*

He stopped.

He lay there against the knot, with his blades in his hands, and stopped. Everything stopped. He didn't know why. He thought about an old woman with thin white hair trailing in the water and swollen knuckles and paper-thin webs, holding up a net in front of him. *Sit.* He thought about the slow knot. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. Thirty-one times on the first morning. *If you move it before it has lain down, it has not got there. It is only passing through.*

He wasn't late.

He looked at the knot. Properly. With his face against it. He looked the way he'd looked at Neth's net, the way he'd had to learn to, slowly, one cord at a time.

It was cracked.

Not much. A hair. A line, down the side of the black, where the two blades had skidded. A thin pale line in the black. Where something had already gone in, a little, before it stopped.

*Wait.*

"*WENDY!*" Yan. From the ship. He sounded closer, somehow. He wasn't. "*WHERE TIGER IS! TWO METRES LEFT OF HIM! LOW! IT'S CRACKED! THERE!*"

Tiger heard it. He didn't understand it. Wendy couldn't see in here. Wendy was a quarter of a mile away and he was down inside a slit in the top of a dome, and there was no way she could—

*Don't watch*, Wendy had said, on floor 6, on the glass, with the Prism standing over them. And Yan had said, *I have to. Somebody should.* And she'd shot it. Without seeing. Where Yan said.

The arrow came down out of the sky.

He didn't see it come. He heard it. A thin high whistle, falling. And then *crack*, a sound like a tree splitting in frost, two metres to his left, low, exactly where the hair-line was, and he turned his head and there was a black-headed arrow standing in the knot, a hand's depth in, quivering. And the hair-line wasn't a hair-line any more. It had opened. A finger's width. A crack, in the black, going down.

He put both blades into the crack.

They went in.

Up to the hilts. Easily. As if they'd found a door. He felt the knot round them, cold and black and hard and *alive*, and he felt it feel him, and every arm on the thing came off the sea at once and came back toward the dome, and toward the slit, and toward him.

And he didn't turn the blades.

He held them. In the crack. With his whole weight on them. And waited.

Over. Wait.

He could feel the crack moving. Round the blades. Opening. Slowly. As the thing bucked and heaved and the arms came, the crack was going, down, further, on its own, the black splitting along itself the way ice splits on a lake in spring, from one hair-line, all the way across.

*Let it lie down where it is going before you move it again.*

The arms came over the edge of the slit.

He waited.

The crack went all the way down.

He turned the blades.

***

It broke.

The black knot came apart under his hands like a wet log hit dead centre with an axe, both halves falling away from each other, and from somewhere very deep inside it something went out, a long breath, a sigh as big as the sea, and every arm on the Undertow, every one of them, a dozen, two dozen, hanging over him in the air, went soft.

They fell. Slowly. Like wet sails. Into the sea, all round, with a sound like a long wave, and lay there.

The dome shuddered. Once. Everywhere.

And began to sink.

Tiger stood in the slit in the top of it, up to his shins in cold grey stuff, with his two blades in his hands and the black pieces of the knot falling away under his feet, and the dome going down under him. Slowly. The sea coming up the sides. Grey. Then blue.

He looked back at the ship.

A quarter of a mile. Tiny. All of them on it. He could hear them. He could hear them *shouting*, across all that water, every one of them, Hyvred in capitals and Calvin and Newish swearing at the sky and Salty, *Salty*, roaring something that wasn't a word.

The sea came up over his feet. Cold.

He couldn't swim. Not really. He could stay up for a bit, badly, swallowing half of it. He hated water. He'd always hated water.

He stood on the sinking dome and watched the sea come up his legs, and was not afraid, and didn't know why.

And then the singer came up underneath him.

He felt it before he saw it. The dome lurched, and lifted, and *rose*, and he went over on his back, and when he sat up the grey was falling away all round him in sheets and what was under him wasn't grey any more. It was white. Old white, like bone. Crusted. Barnacles under his hands and weed in long trailing beards and the warmth of it, so warm after the cold. A great ragged fin along the back in front of him with the sun shining through it.

The singer had come up under the Undertow and lifted it off the sea and shrugged it off its back like a coat, and caught him.

He sat on the singer's back with his blades across his knees and the dead thing sliding away into the deep on both sides.

It hummed. Through him. Through his whole body.

It sounded, Tiger thought, like somebody saying *there*.

***

It carried him back to the ship.

Slowly. Not in a hurry. The way you'd carry something you'd been asked to look after. It swam up alongside the *Patience*, so close the barnacles scraped her hull, and Marrit at the wheel was laughing and crying at once, and somebody threw a rope, and it was Salty, and Tiger caught it.

Salty pulled him up the side. Hand over hand. He came over the rail and Salty got him under the arms and pulled him the rest of the way and they both went over onto the deck, and Salty lay there on his back with Tiger on top of him, breathing.

"Got you," said Salty. "You *idiot*."

"Did Kip—"

"Kip's fine. Xola's got him. He's been sick twice and he wants to do it again."

Tiger lay on Salty.

"I wasn't late," he said.

"What?"

"I wasn't late," said Tiger. "I waited."

Salty didn't say anything. He just put his hand on the back of Tiger's head, between his ears, the way Neth had.

And Yan was still at the bow.

Tiger saw him over Salty's shoulder. Standing in the angle of the stem, with Working On It upright in his right hand and the cords on his left arm and Suzu round his neck. Not moving. Looking down at the sea. At the place where the Undertow had gone down. With a face Tiger had never seen on him.

"They're coming out," said Yan.

Quietly. To Wendy, beside him. Tiger heard it.

"The ones with no names," said Yan. "The old ones. Older than Corran. Older than his gran. Hundreds of them. They're coming up out of it as it goes down, and they don't know where to go, Wendy, they've never known, they're just—" He stopped. "They're following the others. The ones I called. Because the ones I called know where I am."

He swallowed.

"And there's a big one," said Yan. "Pale. Bigger than all of them. Coming up last." His voice went. "The singer's going to it."

Tiger turned his head.

The singer had gone down. Beside the ship. Down into the clear blue water, where the dark thing had gone, a long white shape going down and down into the deep until it was only a paleness, far below.

And then it came back up.

Slowly. And the hum, when it came, wasn't the hum. It wasn't low and it wasn't long and it wasn't asking. Tiger didn't have a word for what it was. Up on the mizzen, Aleaka had her face in her hands.

"It's not asking any more," she said. Through her hands. "It's not asking. It's just—" She couldn't finish.

She didn't need to.

***

They sailed to the Last Light at the slow speed of a ship in a light wind on a flat sea, with something enormous and white swimming alongside, and something else, that only Yan could see, all round them in the water.

"Like Hythe at night," Yan said again, at the bow. "All the lamps on the harbour."

The rock got bigger.

It was black, and tall, and steep on every side but one, the side facing them, where somebody had cut steps into it, long ago, up from a small stone landing at the water. On top, the tower. White. Round. Not very tall, close to. Thick. With a lamp-room at the top under a black iron cap, and a house built against the foot of it, low, white, with a blue door.

And past it, the Edge.

Tiger hadn't looked at it properly. He looked now. Past the rock, maybe a hundred metres, the sea just stopped. It ran on, flat and blue, toward the rock and round it and past, and then there was a line, dead straight, from one side of the world to the other, and past the line there was nothing but white. Not mist. Not cloud. White. Bright, like snow in the morning. The sea went over the line, smoothly, all along it, the way the lake had gone over the lip on floor 8, and you couldn't see where it went.

A shelf of black rock ran out from the foot of the tower toward it. Low. Flat. Under the water, ankle-deep, the sea running over it toward the line.

Like a causeway.

Tiger looked at Yan. Yan was looking at it too.

"Oh," said Yan.

***

She was on the landing when they came in.

An old woman. Small. In a shawl, and a black knitted cap, with a short clay pipe in her mouth that she wasn't smoking. She stood at the top of the stone steps with her hands on her hips and watched the *Patience* come in, and the white thing alongside, and didn't move, and didn't wave, and when Jago threw a line, she caught it, and made it fast to an iron ring, and looked up at them all.

"Well," she said.

Marrit was first down the side. Then Ice. Then all of them, one after another, onto the little landing, until it was full and they had to go up the steps to make room. The old woman watched them come. All of them. Tiger saw her look at each face as it came down the ladder, one after another, the way you'd count sheep through a gate.

"Fifty years," said the old woman, "I've kept this light."

She took the pipe out of her mouth.

"Fifty years, and my husband before me, and his mother before him. And nobody has ever come from that way." She pointed the pipe east, across the flat sea. "Nobody. Not once. And four nights ago a thing comes in the dark and sits itself down in front of my rock like a dog at a door, and turns the sea, and eats the light off the water so it doesn't go anywhere." She looked at the sea, flat and blue, where the wheel had been. "And today a ship comes in through it. And it stops." She looked at them. "And you're all stood there dripping on my steps."

Nobody said anything.

"Wenna," said the old woman. "Of the Light."

"Marrit," said Marrit. "Of the *Patience*. Out of Hythe."

"Hythe," said Wenna. As if it was the moon. "Hythe's two months round."

"Seventeen days," said Marrit. "Straight."

Wenna looked at her for a long time. Then at the singer, lying in the water off the landing like a reef. Then back at Marrit.

"I see," said Wenna. Slowly. "I see." She put the pipe back in her mouth. "Well. You'd better come in. The lot of you." She turned. "Mind the fourth step from the door. It rocks."

Tiger was behind Ice.

He was right behind him, going up the steps from the landing to the blue door. And he saw Ice step over it.

The fourth step. Before she'd finished saying it. Ice's foot came up and over and down on the third, not breaking his stride, the way you'd step over a puddle on a road you'd walked every day of your life.

Tiger looked at the fourth step.

It was just a step. Grey stone. Cut into the rock like all the others.

He didn't say anything. He was good at not saying anything, now. He hadn't been, before floor 8. He'd learned. *Sit.*

And behind him, Thea put her foot on it.

Squarely. Right in the middle. Tiger heard it. A small grinding knock, stone on stone, as the step tipped under her and back. She wobbled. Ice's hand came back without him turning round and caught her elbow.

"It does rock," said Thea. Mildly. To nobody. In the voice of somebody who'd never been here in her life.

Ice didn't say anything. He let go of her elbow. He went in through the blue door.

Tiger stood on the third step and looked at the two of them for a second, the one who'd stepped over and the one who'd stepped on, and didn't understand it, and knew he didn't, and went in.

***

Gabe hit his head on the beam.

Tiger saw that too. It was the first thing that happened inside. The house was low, built for a small woman and a small man and a small mother before them, and the beams across the ceiling were black and old and exactly at the height of Gabe's forehead, and Gabe walked in through the blue door and into the first one with a sound like a mallet on a post.

"*Ow*," said Gabe.

And Ice laughed.

Out loud. Suddenly. A real laugh, from somewhere right at the bottom of him. Tiger had heard it maybe three times since the white room. It came out of Ice like something escaping, and then it stopped. Just stopped. Ice stood in Wenna's kitchen with his hand over his mouth and his eyes very bright, and Gabe looked at him, rubbing his forehead, and didn't say anything at all.

"Mind the beams," said Wenna, too late. "Sit anywhere. There's no room. Sit anyway."

There was a kettle on the stove. Blue enamel. Chipped at the spout. Steam coming off it, and moving, drifting sideways in the draught from the door, curling up into the black beams.

Tiger saw Ice look at the kettle.

Just for a second. The way you'd look at somebody's face in a crowd and think you knew them, and then look away before they saw you.

Then he looked away.

***

Yan walked them out at dusk.

He didn't say he was going to. He didn't need to. Tiger saw him get up from the bench by Wenna's window, where he'd been sitting with Suzu round his neck and a mug of something he hadn't drunk, and pick up Working On It from where it leaned against the wall, and go out through the blue door, and everybody went after him.

Not everybody onto the shelf. Just to the edge of it. At the foot of the tower, where the black rock went out under the water toward the line. They stood along the edge of the rock, all of them, forty-four, and Wenna, with her pipe, at the back.

Yan's party went with him to where the shelf began.

Salty. Wendy. Tiger. Cursiffe. They stopped where the water started. Ankle-deep, running out over the flat black rock toward the white, fast, the way it had run over the causeway on floor 8. Tiger could feel it leaning. Even standing on the edge. Like a hand in the small of your back.

"Rope?" said Salty. Holding one.

Yan shook his head.

"It's not a thing you tie," he said. "Aud didn't."

He turned to Marrit.

She'd come down to the edge of the rock. She was standing beside Wendy, with her own cord round her wrist, and her face very still. Yan unwound Corran's cords from his left arm. One at a time. The brown ones at the wrist first, and up, and the black one last, from just under the elbow. It took him a long time. He'd got very good at putting them on. He wasn't used to taking them off.

He held them out to her.

"I've got them," he said. "All of them. Like you said."

Marrit took them. She held the tangle of tarred string in both hands and looked at it.

"Is he there?" she said.

"He's at the front," said Yan. "He came first. Before any of them. He's been waiting the longest." He looked out along the shelf, at the white. "He's going last. He asked."

Marrit's face did something. "*Put me at the end.*"

"Two Rs," said Yan.

She laughed. It came out broken. She stood there with her grandfather's cords in her hands and laughed and cried at the same time, and didn't wipe her face, and nodded.

And Yan walked out.

Pole and step. Pole and step. The butt of the pale ash down on the black rock under the water, and then a step, and then the pole again. The way Aud had walked. The way he'd walked on floor 8, out along the causeway under the fast water, with a boat beside him and a small blue light in it. The water leaned on him. Tiger could see it lean. It pushed at his ankles, it pushed at the back of his knees, it tried to take him, and he set the blade's heel down in a crack in the rock every few steps and leaned back on it, and it held, like an anchor, the way Aud's had. *It's not only for cutting.*

He didn't look at the Edge. Not until he was there. Aud had said.

Tiger watched him go. Out along the black shelf, getting smaller, a slim straight shape with silver-white hair and a long curved blade over his head, against the white.

And then he was there. At the end. At the lip, where the sea went over.

He stopped.

He stood there with the water running past his ankles and over the line, and the white in front of him, and Tiger couldn't see what he saw. Nobody could. But Tiger watched his face, as much of it as you could see from a hundred metres, turned a little to the side, and he saw the moment Yan stopped walking and started to wait.

*Wait. Until it lies down.*

Yan waited.

A long time. The sun went down into the white. The sky went red, and then gold, and the white went gold too. Behind them, up in the tower, Wenna's light came on. Three short. A long.

And Yan started to say them.

Tiger couldn't hear. Not from the rock. Not over the water. He could see Yan's mouth move. Slowly. One after another. And he knew what they were, because he'd shouted numbers at them for six days and called them across the sea at noon. He knew Yan was saying all of them. Every one. From the black cord to the brown. Not to call them, this time. To let them go.

The wind stopped.

Tiger didn't notice it stop. He just noticed, after a while, that it wasn't there. That the air was still. And that the water running past his ankles and out along the shelf had gone quiet, too, as if it was listening.

Out at the lip, Yan lifted the blade.

He held it out, low, over the water, at the very edge, where the sea went over into the white. He held it there.

He was saying a name. Tiger could see. A short one. He knew which.

*Annis.*

And then another, and another, a long time, and Tiger stood on the edge of the rock with the cold water round his feet and watched Yan's mouth, and thought, *Kitto*, when Yan's face went a certain way, and thought, *Jory*, and saw Marrit, beside him, with the cords in her hands, say it at the same moment, out loud.

And then Yan stopped.

He stood very still at the end of the shelf with the blade out over the white.

He said one more. Tiger saw it. He didn't need to hear it. He saw Yan smile, a little, the way you smile at somebody being stubborn at you, and say it.

And then he cut.

With the inside of the blade. Low. Across the water at the lip. One long stroke, from right to left, the way Aud had cut the line on floor 8. Not hard. Not fast. Like drawing a line under something. Like the last stroke of a pen.

Tiger didn't know what he cut. There was nothing there. There was water and air and the white.

He thought about it afterwards, a lot. On the stair. On the next floor. For a long time. And what he thought, in the end, was that Yan had cut the only thing still holding them, which was Yan. That three hundred and ten people and an old man and a great many others with no names at all had turned round when he called them, because he knew them, and come up out of the dark to him, and followed him all the way to the end of the world, because he knew them. And at the end of the world they were still holding on to him. Because they knew him. And somebody had to let go.

*Walk one to the Edge. Do not keep them.*

Yan lowered the blade.

The white was very bright. For a moment. Brighter than it had been. Brighter than snow. Like something very big had gone into it all at once.

And off the end of the rock, out at the lip, the singer had come.

Tiger hadn't seen it come. It was just there. Lying in the water beside the shelf, right at the edge, its whole enormous length along the line, white against the white. Its great eye, half clouded, half clear, turned toward the lip.

It sang.

Once. A long note. Not low. Not high. Tiger didn't have a word for it. It went out over the Edge into the white and didn't come back. And beside Tiger, on the rock, Aleaka sat down suddenly on the wet stone as if her legs had gone, with her hands over her mouth.

Then the singer turned. Slowly. The whole length of it. Away from the Edge. Back toward the sea, east, toward everything.

And swam away.

***

Yan came back along the shelf in the last of the light.

Pole and step. Slowly. He wasn't leaning so much. The water wasn't pushing so hard. He came up off the shelf onto the dry black rock, and stopped in front of the four of them, and stood there, with the blade in his hand and the cords gone and Suzu round his neck.

His face was wet. He was smiling.

"He said *fair*," said Yan. To Cursiffe. "At the end. When I said his name. He said *fair*." He laughed, and it broke in half. "I don't know how I know that. I just do."

Cursiffe didn't say anything. He took out his book. The clean one. He opened it to the last page, the one with *Three hundred and eleven* at the bottom and the ticks going right across, and under it, small and square, he wrote something. Tiger leaned over to see.

*Day 318. At the Edge, by the Last Light. Yan walked them. All of them. He cut at the lip. The singer sang. Corran said fair. (Yan.)*

Then he closed it and put it away and put his hand on Yan's arm, which Cursiffe never did, and left it there.

Salty put his arms round all of them at once. He was crying again. He didn't care.

Wendy said, "Don't," to nobody, about nothing, and put her face in Yan's shoulder.

And Tiger stood with them, in the middle of them, with his tail wound round Wendy's ankle, and looked back past them at the Edge, gone grey now in the dusk, with the Last Light going three short and a long over it, over and over, the way it had for fifty years, and the way it would.

Up in the tower, Wenna's hand was on the shutter. Tiger could see her. A small dark shape in the lamp-room, against the light. Working it. Three short. A long. The long one a little longer, once, and a little shorter, the next.

Behind him, Marrit had come to the edge of the rock with Corran's cords in her hands. She didn't tie a knot in her own. She looked at the cords for a long time. And then she wound them round her own left arm, from the wrist, the brown ones first, and up, the way he'd worn them. They didn't fit her. She didn't seem to mind.

*Keep an Edge*, Yan's window had said.

Tiger saw Yan look up at the lamp-room. At Wenna, at the shutter, keeping the light at the end of the world.

He looked for a long time.

He didn't say anything.

***

They went up the next day, just before noon, because the rule said a day and Higu wasn't going to argue with it.

The stair was under the house. Of course it was. Wenna took them down through a trapdoor in the kitchen floor, into a cellar cut out of the rock, past the coal and the oil-casks and a great many shells, to a door in the back wall that nobody had opened in fifty years. "My husband's mother said it went down to the sea," said Wenna. "She was wrong. It goes up." She looked at it. "I never went through. It's not mine."

Before that, there was the goodbye.

It took all morning. Tiger had thought it would be quick. It wasn't. It was the tide-folk, mostly, who didn't know how to say goodbye to people who were going up a stair under a lighthouse at the end of the world, and kept finding things to do instead. Pell checking every line on the *Patience* twice. Jago showing Calvin how to read the tide in a cord. The Lusk brothers arguing about whether their grandfather had been one man or two, and asking Cursiffe to write down that it was two, and then that it was one.

Ottery gave Salty a loaf. A whole loaf, still warm, out of Wenna's oven, which she'd apparently taken over at some point in the night without asking. She put it in his hands and looked at him and said, "*First*," and went back inside.

Kip followed Tiger round from dawn.

He didn't say anything for the first hour. Then, on the steps above the landing, he said, "Teach me to run."

"I can't," said Tiger. "I don't know how I do it."

"Then teach me the knot." Kip held out a cord. "The slow one. The one on the blade."

So Tiger sat on the steps of the Last Light with a twelve-year-old ship's boy and a piece of tarred cord and showed him Neth's knot. Over. Wait. Under. Wait. Through. Kip got it wrong eleven times. He did it too fast. Every time. Tiger watched him do it too fast and felt something in his chest that he didn't have a word for.

"Sit," said Tiger.

"I *am* sitting."

"No," said Tiger. "*Sit.*"

On the twelfth time, Kip's knot held.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked up at Tiger with his thin brown face, and Tiger put his hand on his head, between where his ears would be if he'd had ears like Tiger's, and said, "You're not late."

Kip didn't understand it. That was fine. Tiger hadn't either, the first time.

Marrit stood at the top of the landing steps with Corran's cords round her arm and watched them all go in. One after another. Through the blue door. She didn't cry. She'd done that. She said, to each of them, as they went past, their name. All thirty-six. She'd learned them. Tiger didn't know when. She said them out loud, over the water, one at a time, as they went in, and Tiger understood that it was a tide-folk thing now, a new one, that she'd just made, standing there.

"Tiger," she said, when he went past.

"Marrit," said Tiger.

"*Patience*," she said. "Marrit Patience. Say it all."

"Marrit Patience."

"That's right," said Marrit. "Don't forget it."

He wouldn't.

At the back, Yan stopped in front of her.

They looked at each other for a long moment. Then Marrit held up her left arm, with the eleven cords round it, and touched the black one, at the top, under her elbow.

"No knot," she said.

"No," said Yan. "You don't need one."

"I'll say them," said Marrit. "In Hythe. On the quay. Of an evening. To anybody who's passing." Her mouth went up at one corner, exactly the way the old man's had. "Like Gran."

"They'll call you touched," said Yan.

"Good," said Marrit.

***

The stair was four hundred and twelve steps.

Tiger counted. He always counted. He went up first, of course, because he always went up first, because his body always got there before he did. Up the narrow stone stair in the dark, round and round, the way they always went, with the cold coming down from above and the sound of the sea going away below, and the others coming up behind him, a long way behind, Salty swearing about his legs and Ice saying nothing and Calvin saying everything and somebody, Newish, counting, under her breath, the way Newish counted everything.

He got to the top first.

The door was there. The way the doors always were. Plain. Shut. With a line of light under it, very thin. White. Not sun. Something else.

He didn't open it.

He stood at the top of the stair with his hand flat on the door and his two blades on his back, and didn't open it.

Four hundred and twelve steps. He could hear them all coming. All of them. Thirty-five people coming up a stair in the dark, slowly, at their own speed, Salty and Wendy and Yan and Cursiffe and everybody, and Ice somewhere in the middle of them, where Higu had put him.

Tiger stood at the door, and waited for them.

He wasn't late.

He'd never been late. He knew that now. He'd only ever been early, and alone, and on his own at the top of things, waiting for everybody else.

So he waited. With his hand on the door. Until he heard Salty's breathing on the last turn of the stair below him, and Wendy behind Salty, and Yan behind her, with Suzu, and Cursiffe last, slow and careful and still there. Until he felt them, all of them, on the steps behind him in the dark.

Then he opened it.
