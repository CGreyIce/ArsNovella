Chris had written down everything since the white room, and he'd never once known which bits mattered until afterwards.

That was the point of writing down everything. He'd learned it a long time before the tower, from a kind of work that was mostly looking at things that were going wrong and trying to see why. You didn't decide in advance what was important. You couldn't. If you only wrote down the things you thought mattered, you'd only ever find the things you already knew. So you wrote down all of it. The weather. Who sat where at breakfast. What time the bell went. How many fingers to the left a straw man's face was turned, every morning and every night, with a bit of string from a post with a nail in it. And then one day you looked back over a hundred pages and something was there that you hadn't seen while you were writing it, because it was spread across a hundred pages and you'd only ever been looking at one at a time.

He'd done it on floor 1, with the Bellwether. On floor 5, with the hidden quests. On floor 9, with the club's second page, *THE ONES BEFORE*, by the fire in Highcrook. Every floor. A notebook a floor, near enough. He was on his fourteenth.

On the morning of the fourth day in the Long Barn, he sat in the straw by the east wall with the notebook open on his knees and read the last thing he'd written, by feel, in the dark, the night before, while Winter watched.

*Winter: write down that I was right to ask.*

*And that I'm sorry I asked it like that.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he got up and went to find Ice.

***

Ice was at the Lammas oven, with a ladle, the way he was every morning now.

There was a line. There was always a line. Seven hundred people with bowls, gleaners and passers, shuffling along the end of the first table in the grey light from the high windows, half asleep, and at the end of it Ice, with a cauldron of barley soup, filling every bowl, and looking, for a second, at every face before he did. Chris had watched him do it for three days and had written it down on the first morning. *He counts them.* He'd underlined it.

He got in the line. He didn't have a bowl. When he got to the front Ice looked at him, at his empty hands, and then at the notebook.

"Winter asked me to write something down," said Chris. "Last night. I thought you should see it."

He held the notebook out, open.

Ice looked at it. He didn't take it. He read it where it was, in Chris's hand, with the ladle in his.

He didn't say anything for a while. Behind Chris, somebody coughed. The line waited. The gleaners had got used to the passers stopping in the middle of things to look at each other. They didn't ask any more.

"She was right," said Ice.

"That's what she said."

"No." Ice put the ladle down on the edge of the cauldron. "She was right to ask. And she was right how she asked it." He looked up from the notebook. At Chris. "Somebody had to say it like that. Out loud. In front of them. So I'd have to hear it." He was quiet. "Tell her not to be sorry."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her I'd rather know." Ice picked the ladle up again. "Tell her that's the whole of it. I'd rather know."

Chris closed the notebook. He nodded. He went to go, and then he didn't, and he stood there at the front of the line with the notebook under his arm, and Ice looked at him, and waited, the way Ice had learned to wait somewhere on the way up.

"I've got a theory," said Chris.

Ice didn't move.

"I've had it since floor 5," said Chris. "Since the night we started the hidden page. It's in this notebook, and the nine before it. I've never written the end of it. I've never asked you." He held the notebook against his chest. "I'm not asking now."

"I know you're not."

"I'm just saying." Chris paused. He'd never once said this out loud to anybody but the thirteen, and only to them in the dark, in a ring, by a fire. "Whatever the first time was. Whatever it says in the book in Tell, on the left-hand page. Whatever that thing at the end of the barn thinks you did." He nodded down the barn, at the white shape against the end wall, at the far end, past the horses. "It's not looking at you."

Ice looked at him.

"It's looking at a line in a book," said Chris. "That's all. A line somebody wrote in the first hand. It's not the same thing as the man standing here with a ladle." He was surprised how certain he sounded. "The Magistrate said both hands stand. It didn't say they were the same hand. It said there were two, and they were both true, and neither of them got to cross the other one out." He shrugged, a little. "So it can look at the line all it wants. I've got a lot of pages that say you're a man who makes soup."

Ice didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then his mouth did something at the corner. Not a smile. Near one.

"You've written down that I make soup."

"Every day," said Chris. "Since floor 8. What kind. Who you gave the first bowl to. You always give the first bowl to whoever looks worst." He tapped the notebook. "It's a pattern. It's been a pattern for four hundred days. It's very consistent."

"Who'd I give it to today?"

"The old Lammas woman," said Chris. "The one on the stool. You took it down the barn to her yourself, before the line started." He paused. "You went right up to the end. Right up to it. Where it could see you."

Ice looked at the cauldron.

"She hadn't moved off that stool in three days," he said. "Somebody had to."

"I know," said Chris. "I wrote that down too."

***

There was a way to look at a problem. Chris had always had it, long before the tower. He'd thought everybody had it until he found out they didn't, and then he'd spent a lot of his life saying strange things in rooms and having people look at him.

You didn't look at the thing that was going wrong. Not first. You looked at everything round it. What was the same. What had changed. What had been true yesterday that wasn't true today, and the other way round. And you wrote it all down in columns, and then you looked at the columns, and somewhere in them, if you were patient, there was a line that didn't fit.

He did it in the straw by the east wall all that morning, with the notebook, and the rock, which Higu let him borrow.

*True:*

*The Grey doesn't go under roofs. (Annet, Cobb. Never once.)*

*The Long Barn is a roof.*

*The barn floor is ground. Real ground. (Maigo: "It's got a down. All of it. The Grey's pushing at the walls under the snow outside and stopping at the footings like water at a door.")*

*So the Grey can't get in here.*

*Something's getting in here anyway.*

*Only into him. Not the floor, not the walls, not the sheaves, not us. Just the straw man.*

*The straw man is made of the fields. (The last sheaf. The Barn Field. The neck Milli cut.)*

*He's the only thing in the barn that came in from outside after the harvest was in.*

He looked at that one for a long time.

*So the Grey can't come under a roof*, he wrote, slowly. *But he's the fields. And the fields are its. So it's coming in through him.*

He sat back.

It was the shape of something. He didn't know what yet. He could see the outline of it, the way you could see the outline of the ones before on the club's page long before anybody knew who Ruth was. A door. The tower couldn't get into the one place in the whole Acre that was still real, still standing, still full of people and harvest and warm. So it had found the one thing inside it that it could reach. And it was filling him with winter, a finger at a time, a night at a time, and turning his face round to look at the one man in the barn who, in the first record, had burned him.

*Making it match.*

He wrote it down. He underlined it.

Then, because he was Chris, and that was what Chris did when he'd found the shape of a thing, he went to find out what you did about it.

***

"Keep him," said Ezra.

They were round the first table again. Not a council. Just the people who'd been at the last one, and a few more, the thirty-six mostly, because by the fourth day in the barn nobody in the Acre did anything without the passers there. Chris had brought the notebook and the rock and read out his columns. Nobody had understood all of it. The gleaners hadn't understood most of it. But Annet had understood *it can't come under a roof, so it's coming in through him*, and so had Ezra, and so had the old Lammas woman, who'd been fetched off her stool again and was sitting at the end of the bench with her hands in her lap.

"The song," said Ezra. "*Keep him, and he'll keep you.*" He looked at Chris. "That's what it means. I never knew. Nobody did. But that's what it means. He's ours. He's the Acre's. He's the whole harvest, in one, and something's putting the winter in him." He put his old hands flat on the table. "So you keep him. You don't let it."

"How?" said Annet.

Nobody answered for a moment.

"Warm," said Ina.

He was sitting at the far end of the table with his mask on his shoulder. He'd got some of his colour back since the grave. Not all.

"The neck," said Ina. "Where Ezra tied it. That's where the cold started. That's where it's coming in. That's his heart." He looked down the barn. "If I put my hands on it. Not fire. Never fire. Just warm. Like the ground for Gaffer." He paused. "I can't do it for long. But it's something."

"It's something," said Chris, and wrote it down.

"Take it out," said Rachael.

Everybody looked at her.

She was sitting with her knees up on the bench, the way she always sat, with Calvin beside her and Dennis beside him. She didn't look up. She was looking at her own hands.

"The cold," said Rachael. "On floor 10. The Rimed's cold. It went into people's fingers and nobody could get it out. Ingrith couldn't. Kei couldn't. Healing couldn't touch it." She turned her hands over. "I could. With Burden. A finger at a time. I took it into me, and then I put it into Gabe's shield, because Gabe's shield doesn't go cold." She looked at Gabe. "You remember."

"I remember," said Gabe.

"So I'll take it out of him," said Rachael. "The winter. The way I took it out of Orm's fingers. And you hold Hearthwall, and I'll put it in." She looked down the barn at the white shape. "He's very big. It'll take a long time. It'll come out of me." She shrugged. "That's what it's for."

Calvin said "Rach—" and she said "Don't," without looking at him, and he didn't.

"Down," said Maigo.

He'd taken his sunglasses off. Altogether. He was holding them in his lap. Chris had never once seen him take them off in front of anybody, in four hundred days. His glowing white eyes looked very strange without them, and very young.

"He's full of harvest," said Maigo. "That's down. That's all down is, really. Something that grew, and got gathered, and is waiting to go back in." He looked at his root-foot. "The floor of the barn's got down. Real down. I can feel it from here. If I root at his feet, I can drink it up, and give it to him." He swallowed. "Taproot. It's what it says. *Drink from what's under you. Give it away.*"

"How long can you do that for?"

"I don't know," said Maigo. "I've never tried to give anything to something that big." He put his sunglasses back on, slowly, as if he needed them for the next bit. "Days. Maybe. If somebody keeps watering me."

Gigi, beside him, put her hand on his arm.

"Somebody'll keep watering you," she said.

Chris wrote it all down. *Warm (Ina). Out (Rachael + Hearthwall). Down (Maigo). Gigi waters Maigo.*

"And sit with him," said the old Lammas woman.

Everybody looked at her.

She hadn't spoken all council. She'd sat at the end of the bench with her hands in her lap, the way she'd sat on her stool, and looked at nothing.

"He's on his own," she said. "At the end of the barn. With the horses. Everybody's moved away from him." She looked down the barn. "He's never been on his own. Not in a thousand years. Every harvest-home, the whole Acre in front of him, all night, singing. And then all winter, in every village, there's a piece of him in the corner by the fire, and the children touch it for luck when they go past." She pressed her lips together over her gums. "He's not used to being looked at like that. Like something to be frightened of."

Nobody said anything.

"So you sit with him," said the old woman. "Day and night. Somebody always there. In front of him. Not frightened." She looked at Annet. "That's keeping. That's what you do with anything you're keeping. You don't leave it on its own in the dark."

Annet looked at her for a long time.

Then she looked at Higu.

Higu had the rock. She'd had it all council, on the table in front of her, with Chris's columns on it in her writing. She looked at it now. And then she wiped a space clear at the bottom, under everything, with the side of her hand, and wrote.

*THE STRAW MAN. NOBODY SITS ALONE.*

*INA: WARM.*

*RACHAEL + GABE: OUT.*

*MAIGO: DOWN. (GIGI: WATER.)*

*EVERYBODY: SIT WITH HIM.*

She stopped. She looked down the table. At Ice, at the far end, between Thea and Gabe.

And under it, very small, she wrote:

*ICE: NOT IN FRONT.*

Ice looked at it.

"It's looking at you," said Higu. Very level. "Since the wedding. Chris measured it. Every night. It's turning to you." She put the chalk down. "So you don't sit with him. You don't go near him. Somebody else takes him the soup." She held his eyes. "That's not a punishment. That's just where."

"I know," said Ice.

He didn't argue. Chris had thought he might. He'd thought, on any other floor, before floor 8 and the door rota, before *Kneel* and the Gate and Tell, Ice would have argued. He'd have said *it's me it wants*. He'd have said *I'll go*, and meant *I'll go on my own*.

He didn't. He looked at the rock for a long moment, at *ICE: NOT IN FRONT*, and nodded, once, the way he'd nodded at Higu on every floor since she started writing things down.

"Okay," said Ice.

And that was all.

***

They kept him.

Chris wrote it down. All of it. Every day, and every night, in his notebook, by the light of the horse lantern at the far end of the barn, sitting on the floor in front of the straw man on a folded blanket, because it was his turn, or because it wasn't his turn and he couldn't sleep.

It went like this.

Ina went first. Every morning, and every evening, at the bell; there was no bell in the Long Barn, but the gleaners knew when it would have gone, the way you know when a clock would have struck. Ina climbed the frame. The same way Ezra had, on harvest-home, up the straw man's bound body, which was cold now, cold enough to hurt your hands, frost on every stalk. And at the chest, where the neck was tied in, he put both his bare hands flat on the white plaited figure and held them there.

Warm. Not fire. Never fire. The white-blue shimmer in the air over his palms, the way it had been over Gaffer Tobin's grave. And the frost on the neck went soft, and wet, and ran, in little streams, down the straw man's body. A hand's width of it. Then a little more.

He could hold it for about half an hour. Then he'd come down, very slowly, grey, shaking, and Winter would be at the bottom of the frame with a blanket, and she'd put it round him without saying anything and walk him back up the barn to the straw, and he'd sleep for four hours.

Then Rachael and Gabe.

Gabe stood at the foot of the frame with Hearthwall held flat, face up, like a table, the fire boss in the middle of it glowing faintly, the way it always did. And Rachael put one hand on the straw man's frozen foot, where the frost was thickest, where the bolts were, and the other on Hearthwall's rim.

*Burden.* Chris had seen her do it on floor 10, at the house for the wounded. He'd seen her do it for the frostbitten fingers of a soldier called Orm who said *thanks for trying* and then cried when it worked. You could see it go. The white went out of the straw, out of the frozen iron, into her hand. You could watch it climb her arm, a white stain on her skin, up the wrist, up the forearm, toward the elbow, like frost climbing a window. And then out of her other hand, into the shield. And the shield took it, the way the shield took everything, and didn't go cold, and the white ran off the rim onto the barn floor as water.

She did it for an hour at a time. Then Calvin would make her stop. She'd sit on the floor with her back against Hearthwall and her arms white to the shoulder and her face grey, and not say anything, and Calvin would sit beside her and hold her hands in his, both of them, for as long as it took the white to go, and he wouldn't talk either. Calvin didn't talk. Not then. It was the only time Chris had ever seen it.

Then Maigo.

He rooted at the straw man's feet, in the earth floor of the barn, between the two iron uprights, with his back against the frozen straw and his root-foot down. All day. Every day. With his sunglasses on and his antlers up and his leaves, the three of them, the ones that had come back on the Salt, turned toward the straw man like faces toward a fire. Taproot. You couldn't see it go. You could only see that the straw round Maigo's back stayed gold, a little, a ring of it the size of a cartwheel, gold, not white, where everything else was frost.

And Gigi watered him. Every hour. With a cup. She sat on the floor beside him in her cardigan and held the cup to his root-foot and talked to him, very quietly, about nothing, about the Salt and the green and her watch, which still said nine past two, and the barley, and what he was going to grow on floor 1 when they got there. And he answered her sometimes. And sometimes he didn't, and she kept talking anyway.

And everybody sat with him.

That was the thing Chris hadn't expected. The old Lammas woman's thing. *Sit with him.* He'd thought it would be the thirty-six. Taking turns. Two at a time, like the door rota, nobody alone.

It wasn't the thirty-six. It was the Acre.

On the first evening, the old Lammas woman went back to her stool, at his feet. And sat there. And after a while, somebody brought another stool and sat down next to her. A woman from Hollin. And then a man from Brightwater, on the floor, with his back against a sheaf. And then a family from Middle Acre, the whole lot of them, the grandmother and the parents and four children, who sat in a row in front of the straw man and didn't do anything. Just sat. And the children, after a while, got up one at a time and went right up to the foot of the frame, very carefully, past Maigo, and touched it. The frozen straw. For luck. The way they would have touched a piece of him in the corner by the fire.

By the second night, the forty paces of empty floor in front of the straw man weren't empty. They were full. Gleaners, sitting. Every village. In turns, through the night, nobody alone. Not singing. Not talking much. Just there. In front of him. Not frightened.

Or frightened, and there anyway.

Chris wrote it down. *The Acre's sitting with him. Every village. Nobody asked them to.* And then, underneath, because it was true: *It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen.*

***

It wasn't enough.

He knew it wasn't by the second morning. He knew it the way you know, when you've been watching a thing go wrong for long enough, that the thing you're doing is slowing it and not stopping it. He had the numbers. That was what the numbers were for.

The head turned. Not a finger a night, now. Two fingers. Three. Chris measured it from the post with the nail in it, every morning and every night, and from Ice's place on the east wall, every morning and every night, and the second number got smaller and smaller, until on the morning of the sixth day in the barn the straw man's two twisted eyes were looking straight down the line of his string at the place in the straw where Ice slept.

Not near it. At it.

The frost came back. Every night. Ina took a hand's width off the neck in the morning, and Rachael took the frost off the feet, white to the shoulder, and in the morning it was back. Thicker. Further. Up into the head now. Into the hoop. The crown of poppies was white. The plaited face was white. The two twisted eyes, white, looking at Ice.

And the bolts.

There were four of them at each foot. Eight. Iron, as thick as Chris's thumb, holding the frame's two uprights into their sockets in the stone floor of the Long Barn, where they'd been held for longer than anybody in the Acre knew. Chris checked them every night with the lantern, kneeling at the foot of the frame between Maigo and the old Lammas woman.

On the third night, one bolt at the left foot was a hair proud. Winter had seen it.

On the fourth night, two. On the fifth, three.

On the sixth night, all four bolts at the left foot were up. Not out. Up. A finger's width each, standing proud of the iron, with fine dark cracks running out from them across the frosted stone of the floor like the cracks in ice on a lake when something heavy walks out onto it. And at the right foot, one.

"We're taking out less than it's putting in," said Chris.

He said it to Higu. At midnight, on the sixth night, sitting on the floor in front of the straw man with the notebook on his knees, among the gleaners sitting there with him. Higu was beside him, with the rock. It was their turn.

"I know," said Higu.

"It's not stopping. It's just slower. Ina's doing everything he can. Rachael's doing more than she should. Maigo's been rooted three days and his leaves are going." He looked at the notebook. At the numbers. "If it goes on like this, it'll be out of the floor in two days. Maybe three."

"And then?"

Chris didn't answer.

He looked up at the straw man. At the white face in the white hoop, looking past him, past the gleaners, down the barn, at the east wall.

He'd asked Ice. On the fifth night, on the late watch, at the third door. He'd asked him the only thing he'd let himself ask in four hundred days. Not *what was the first time*. He'd never ask that. Just:

*What did it do? When it walked?*

And Ice had looked out at the snow for a long time, and then he'd said:

*It came out of the barn. Through the doors. In the snow. It was dark. We were camped outside, in the lane, because the gleaners didn't want us in here, the first time.* A pause. *It came straight down the lane at us. Through the fires. It went for me. I was in front. I thought that was why.*

*And?*

*And we burned it*, said Ice. *Because it was that or nothing. It took two days. It kept getting up.* He'd been quiet for a long time. *I didn't know it was anything. I thought it was a thing the tower put there for us to fight. I didn't know it was theirs.*

Chris had written that down too. In the dark, by feel. *I didn't know it was theirs.*

"And then it comes for him," said Higu.

Very quietly. Beside him. She hadn't needed him to answer. She was looking at the straw man too.

"Yes," said Chris.

"And he doesn't burn it."

"No."

"And nobody else does either."

"No," said Chris. "No fire. We said."

"So what does he do?"

Chris looked at the notebook. At a hundred pages, and the thirteen notebooks before it, and everything in them. The weather. Who sat where. What time the bell went. How many fingers. Every pattern.

He didn't know.

That was new. He'd always known. Not the answer, always. But where to look for it. Which column. Which page. And he sat on the floor of the Long Barn at midnight with fourteen notebooks of everything that had happened since the white room and he couldn't find the page.

"I don't know," said Chris.

Higu looked at him.

"I don't either," she said. "That's two of us." She looked at the rock in her lap. At *ICE: NOT IN FRONT*. "That's all I've got. Where. Not how."

"Maybe that's enough," said Chris. "For now."

"Maybe," said Higu.

They sat there.

***

Kei came to sit with them at about two.

He didn't say anything. He sat down on the floor on Chris's other side, with Dose round his neck and his book in his lap, the way he always had it. He didn't open it. He sat there with his hand flat on the cover.

Chris had seen him do that a lot, the last few days. Since the evening on the Acre, at Haverley, in the tithe barn, when Ice had stood up and said *there's somebody on floor 1 I want to see before the winter*, and sat down. Kei had turned his book to the back cover that night and looked at it for a long time, and not slept. Chris had written that down. *Kei. Back of his book. Didn't sleep.* He hadn't known why. He still didn't.

Kei looked up at the straw man.

"It's winter," he said. Very quietly. Not to anybody in particular. "On floor 1."

Chris looked at him.

"Floor 1's the same as here," said Kei. "The year. Isn't it. Hyvred said. The floors have their own years, but they go the same way, mostly, down the bottom. If it's winter here—" He stopped. "If it's come early here."

He didn't finish.

He sat there with his hand on the cover of his book. And Dose, round his neck, lifted her head and looked at him, the way she did when she was saying something only he could hear. And Kei listened. And his face did something Chris couldn't read.

"Not now," said Kei. To the snake. Very quietly. "Tomorrow. When Hyv's awake."

Then he got up, and went back to his place in the straw, and lay down, and Chris knew without looking that he didn't sleep.

He wrote it down. *Kei: "It's winter. On floor 1." Not now. Tomorrow. When Hyv's awake.*

He underlined *tomorrow*.

***

It happened at about four.

He'd nearly gone to sleep. Sitting up, on the floor, with the notebook on his knees and his chin going down. Higu had gone, at three, and Thea had come, with the lantern, and sat on his other side. It wasn't her turn. She didn't seem to care about turns at the far end of the barn. She came most nights now. She sat as close to the straw man as anybody, closer than Ice was allowed, and held the lantern up a little, so the amber light went up the white body toward the white face. Not saying anything. Just holding it there.

Chris had asked her why, once. On the fourth night.

*Because it's looking at him*, Thea had said. *And I'd like it to have to look past me to do it.*

He'd written that down.

She was holding it up now. And the straw man's face was looking past her, past the lantern, past the gleaners on their stools, down the barn, at the east wall. And Chris's chin was going down.

And the floor cracked.

Not a sound. He felt it. Through the stone, through the blanket he was sitting on, into his bones. A long low crack, like ice on a lake when the whole sheet of it shifts, the kind you feel in your teeth before you hear it.

He was awake.

So was everybody at the far end. Every gleaner on every stool and every blanket, sitting up, all at once, the way the field had gone still at the bell. Maigo, rooted at the frame's feet, made a sound. Gigi said *Maigo* and had his arm. The horses, against the side wall, screamed. All of them. The big brown Haverley mare reared against her rope and came down and reared again.

Chris looked at the left foot.

The four bolts weren't proud any more. They were out. Not in their sockets. Lying on the frosted stone of the floor, all four of them, sheared clean through, their heads still on, their shanks snapped off flush with the iron, bright and new and grey where they'd broken. And the iron upright of the left foot wasn't in its socket. It was standing on the floor. Beside the socket. A hand's width to the left of where it had been for a thousand years.

It had stepped.

Not walked. Stepped. One foot, a hand's width. Toward the middle of the barn.

The cracks ran out across the floor from the empty socket. Dark, fine, branching, going out across the stone in a fan, toward the gleaners, toward Chris and Thea, toward the middle of the barn. And frost along them. White. Spreading. Running out across the floor of the Long Barn along every crack like frost along the lead of a window.

Nobody moved.

Chris looked up.

The straw man's face was looking down the barn. At the east wall. White. Crowned in white poppies. Two twisted eyes.

And above it, in the dark under the beams, where there was nothing, a window came up.

He'd seen a thousand windows. Every one of them since the white room. He'd never seen one come up over something that wasn't a thing the tower had sent. Over something that had been made, by hand, by old women from every village in the Acre, sitting on a barn floor plaiting a face in silence the way their mothers had.

It came up anyway. Pale. Steady. Over the white head, in the dark.

[ The Last Sheaf · Floor Guardian ]

Thea didn't move. She held the lantern up. The amber light in it was leaning so hard toward the east wall it lay flat against the glass.

"Chris," she said. Very quietly. Without looking round. "Wake Higu."

"Yes."

"And Hyvred."

"Yes."

"And don't wake him." She didn't need to say who. "Not yet. Let him sleep. He hasn't slept properly since the snow." She held the lantern steady. "If it's coming, it can come past me first."

Chris got up.

He didn't run. You didn't run, in front of something like that. He walked, quickly, up the barn, through the gleaners, who'd started to stand, slowly, all of them, and not run either. Past the tables. Up the long dark length of the Long Barn toward the east wall and the thirty-six asleep in the straw.

Halfway there, he looked back.

The straw man hadn't moved again. One foot out of its socket. The other still held, by three bolts and one that was up. Its arms lifted. Its white face turned down the barn. The window over its head, pale, steady, in the dark.

And at its feet, on the frosted floor, among the cracks, the old Lammas woman. On her stool. She hadn't stood up. She was sitting there with her hands in her lap, right in front of it, looking up at its face.

Not frightened.

Or frightened, and there anyway.

Chris wrote it down. Walking. By feel. In the dark.
