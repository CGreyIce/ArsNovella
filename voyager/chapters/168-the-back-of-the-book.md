The difference between a poison and a cure is how much.

Kei had said it for years before he meant it. He'd said it on stream, on Earth, in the voice you use for a thing that sounds clever, a thing you read somewhere once and never had to test. Then he'd woken up in a white room, and on the fourteenth day of floor 2 something pale had come up out of the snow like a whip and gone into Hako's calf. And Kei had knelt in the snow with a vial of blue venom in one hand and a spoon in the other and found out he'd meant it all along.

Since then he'd measured everything.

Not on purpose. It was just what his head did now. How much sleep Maigo needed for every leaf he put out. How much salt was in the water on floor 12 before it stopped being water. How long Rachael could keep her hands on something before they went white, and how long after that before they stayed white. How much honey for a burn. How much pine for a cut. How much of a person you could give away before there wasn't enough left to give. On floor 6, on the lip of the cistern, with Thea's fever turning in the dark behind him, he'd sat and wondered how much one person could carry before it stopped being a cure.

He still didn't know. He'd been watching Ice for four hundred and fifty-four days, trying to find out.

He'd been awake for three nights when the floor cracked.

***

He wasn't asleep. He'd stopped pretending to be, round about one. He lay in the straw by the east wall of the Long Barn with his book on his chest and his hand flat on the cover, the way it had been for most of the last three days, and Dose round his neck, cool and heavy, not asleep either.

Two hours ago he'd sat on the floor by Chris at the top of the barn and looked up at the straw man and said *it's winter on floor 1*. Out loud. He hadn't meant to. And Dose had lifted her head, and said, in the dry old voice that only he could hear, the voice like a page turning in a library nobody had been into for a thousand years:

*It's her. The one you wrote it for. Tell them.*

And he'd said *not now*. *Tomorrow. When Hyv's awake.*

Not because he didn't believe her. Dose was rude, and lazy, and vain about her scales, and had once refused to speak to him for two days because he'd called her *pretty* in front of Hako. But she'd never once told him a thing that wasn't so. He believed her. That was the trouble. Believing was a feeling, and a feeling wasn't a measure, and you didn't give anybody anything on a feeling. Not a drop. Not half a drop. You measured first. You made sure. And there was only one person in the Long Barn who could make him sure, and she'd been asleep since midnight with Bee's head on her shoulder.

So he'd lain there and waited for her to wake up.

At about four, the floor cracked.

It came through the straw under him before it came through the air. A long hard split, *crack*, like ice going on a lake, and he felt it in his back, in his teeth, run the whole length of the barn and out under the walls. And then the horses at the far end screamed. All of them. Twelve horses, all at once, the high terrible sound horses make when they don't understand something, and the thump and scrape of hooves on stone as they went back against the far wall.

All down the barn, people sat up.

Kei sat up. Dose's head came off his shoulder and stood up straight in front of his face, tongue out, tasting.

*Oh*, she said. *There he goes.*

He looked up the barn.

It was dark. The lamps on the chains were turned right down, the way they were every night now, to save the oil. But there was light at the top of the barn, at the very end, by the straw man. Amber. Steady. Held up. And above it, over the straw man's white head, in the dark under the beams, something pale and square that hadn't been there when he lay down.

A window.

He couldn't read it from there. He didn't need to. He'd seen enough of them. He'd seen them come up over a heron the size of a tower and over a tide of salt and over a robe as tall as the windows in a hall on floor 13. He knew the shape of a window that came up over something you were going to have to fight.

Chris was coming down the barn. Not running. Walking fast, between the sleepers and the ones sitting up, with his notebook in one hand, the way he always had it. He went past Kei without looking at him and crouched by Hyvred and shook her shoulder.

Hyvred came up out of sleep all at once, the way she did, with her hair standing straight up on one side and her glasses already in her hand. She didn't put them on. She looked at Chris. She looked past him, up the barn, with her bare eyes. And Kei watched her face change.

"Oh," said Hyvred. "Oh, no."

Higu was already up. Kei didn't see her get up. One moment the place by the east wall where she slept with the rock was a shape in the straw and the next it was empty, and Higu was walking up the barn in her socks with the rock under one arm and a stub of chalk in her other hand.

And beside Kei, three places down, in the long row of the thirty-six against the wall, Gabe was awake. Up on one elbow. Not moving. With his hand flat on the arm of the person next to him.

Ice woke anyway.

Kei had seen him wake a hundred times. He woke the same way every time, in both lives probably, though Kei only knew about the one. All at once. No middle. Asleep, and then not, with his eyes open, as if he'd been waiting the whole time just under the surface. He didn't sit up. He lay there for a moment with Gabe's hand on his arm and looked up the barn at the window.

Then he got up.

He didn't hurry. He walked up the barn in the dark in his shirt with no sword and no shield, between the tables, through the gleaners, who'd all started to stand now, slowly, a whole village at a time. Gabe got up behind him and walked on his right. And Kei got up too, without deciding to, and went after them, because whatever was happening was happening to one of them, and you went.

Higu got there first.

She was on her knees on the stone in the main aisle, between the end of the first table and the end of the second. Fifteen paces from the straw man's feet. Kei counted them later. She had the chalk on the floor and she was drawing a line. A long, straight, careful line across the flagstones, from the leg of one table to the leg of the other, the whole width of the aisle, with her tongue between her teeth. She didn't look up when Ice reached her.

She finished the line. She sat back on her heels. She looked at it.

"There," said Higu.

Ice stopped with the toes of his boots on the near side of it.

He looked down at it. He looked up the barn, past it. At fifteen paces of frosted stone. At the old Lammas woman on her stool, right at the bottom, at the straw man's feet, with her hands in her lap. At Thea, standing beside her, holding the lantern up. At the iron frame going up into the dark, wrapped in gold straw gone white with frost, with its arms out and lifted and its head in the hoop as big as a cartwheel. At the left foot of the frame, where the iron upright went down into its stone socket, and wasn't in it any more. Out. A hand's width. Standing on the floor beside the hole it had come out of, like a man who'd taken one step out of a doorway and stopped.

And the face. The plaited face with its twisted straw eyes, at the top of all of it, in the dark under the window. Looking straight down the barn. Straight down Chris's string. At him.

"There," said Ice.

That was all.

He sat down. Right there, on the near side of the chalk, on the cold stone, with his legs crossed, facing it. Gabe stood behind him with his arms folded. Nobody said anything.

On the rock, three days ago, Higu had written *ICE: NOT IN FRONT* in small letters at the bottom, and said *that's not a punishment, that's just where*, and Ice had said *okay*. Now it was on the floor.

Kei looked at the line. At the fifteen paces of stone on the other side of it. At the iron foot standing a hand's width out of its socket.

He couldn't help it. It was what his head did. A hand's width, in six nights of bolts coming up. Fifteen paces. A pace was about seven hands, if you were Kei, and the straw man's paces would be longer than his. If it went a hand a night, it would be a hundred nights to the line. If it went two—

*It doesn't walk in hands*, said Dose, round his neck. *Stop it.*

He stopped it.

Behind him the shields were coming. He heard them before he saw them, the scrape of shield rims on stone, the particular sound of people who carried heavy things all day putting them down carefully. Gabe went past him, and Azac, and Jared with his thorn-shield, and Salty, and Chris with his notebook stuffed into his belt, and Drew with nothing in his hands at all, and Chi. And Hako. Hako, last, with his spear over his shoulder and his shield on his arm and his hair flat on one side from the straw, who stopped beside Kei for half a second and said, very quietly, "Classic," in a voice that meant it wasn't.

They went past the line. Ice didn't. They spread out across the aisle a body's length in front of it, between the chalk and the straw man, and grounded their shields, one beside another, rims touching. A wall. Facing up the barn.

Ice sat behind it.

***

It didn't move again.

Kei sat on the end of the second table and watched it until the high windows went grey, and it didn't move. The window over its head stayed. *The Last Sheaf.* He could read it now, from here. *Floor Guardian.* The frost stayed. The foot stayed out of its socket, a hand's width, with frost all down the iron and a fan of cracks across the stone round it, white lines in the white.

At first light, Higu tested it.

She did it the way she'd done it on the Salt, with Thea and the heron. Quietly, and without saying what she was doing until she'd done it. She asked Ice to stand up. He stood up. She asked him to walk, slowly, along the near side of the line, all the way to the east wall, and then back, all the way to the table on the other side. He did. And the whole barn watched the straw man's head follow him.

Not fast. A finger's width at a time. With a sound. A long, low creak of frozen withy bands taking a strain, from the hoop, up in the dark. When he stopped by the east wall, it stopped, looking at him. When he walked back, it came back. When he sat down again at the middle of the line, it was looking at the middle of the line.

Higu didn't write anything down. She didn't need to.

"Okay," she said. "Sit."

He sat.

Then the bell went. There wasn't a bell. There hadn't been a bell in the Long Barn since harvest-home. But it was the time of morning when the bells in the villages would have gone, if anybody had been in the villages, and Ina got up from where he'd been sleeping sitting up against a pillar with Winter's blanket round him, and went up the barn with his mask pushed up, and climbed the frame.

He'd done it twice a day for four days. Kei had watched every time. He went up the iron like a ladder, past the shields, past the old woman, who didn't look round, past the frozen legs and the white chest, to the neck. The plaited gold knot where a heart would be, white with frost. And he took his gloves off, and put both bare hands on it, and held them there.

Kei could see the shimmer from the table. White-blue. Ina's fire, not the kind that burned. The kind that warmed. A shiver of it in the air round Ina's hands like heat over a summer road.

Half an hour. Then Ina came down, slowly, grey in the face, and Winter met him at the foot of the frame with the blanket and walked him back to the pillar, and he slept.

Kei measured it, because he couldn't not. Ina's half hour had melted a hand's width of frost off the neck. Kei had watched. And by the time Ina was asleep against the pillar, the hand's width had a skin of white on it again. Thin. But there.

*Less out than in*, Chris had written. Chris was right.

Kei sat on the end of the table with his hand on his book and looked down the barn, to where Hyvred was sitting on a sack by the east wall with her glasses in her lap and Bee asleep against her, awake now, looking up the barn with her bare eyes.

He got up.

***

"I know," said Hyvred, before he'd sat down.

She didn't look round. She was looking up the barn. Her eyes were doing the thing they did with her glasses off, going everywhere at once and then settling, slowly, like birds coming down on a field.

"You've been sitting up with that book for three nights," said Hyvred. "You light up like a lamp when you're thinking. It's very annoying to sleep next to."

"Sorry."

"You're not." She moved over, a little, on the sack, without disturbing Bee. He sat. "What."

He held the book on his knees. He didn't open it.

"Can I ask you something," said Kei. "About Haverley. The tithe barn. When he stood up."

Hyvred was quiet.

"Yes," she said.

"Did you have your glasses off?"

"I always have them off in the evenings now. Since the Salt." She turned her head and looked at him for the first time. "You know that."

"I wanted to measure it."

"I know."

"What colour was he?" said Kei. "When he said *her*."

Hyvred didn't answer.

Bee shifted against her shoulder, and murmured something, and was still. Up the barn, a horse blew out through its nose, long and shaky, and somebody went to it. The window over the straw man's head was very pale in the grey light from the high windows. Hyvred looked at it.

"You know how he goes," she said at last. "When he looks at one of us sometimes. Like he's seeing somebody he lost, and they've come back. Gigi's green, on the Salt. Gabe at the forge on ten. Calvin's hands, any time Calvin does anything with both hands."

"Yes."

"It wasn't that one."

Kei waited.

"It was the other side of that one," said Hyvred. She was frowning. She hated not having the words. She'd told him once, on floor 9, that she spent half her life now seeing things there weren't words for, and the other half trying to find them, and she never knew which half was worse. "You know at Gaffer's grave. When Ina said the words. And Ice was saying them after him. Under his breath."

"Yes."

"He went like that." Hyvred looked at her hands. "Like somebody at a grave. Except the grave wasn't there yet. Like he was standing at the edge of one nobody's dug."

Kei sat very still.

"How sure are you," he said.

"I don't do sure." She said it without heat. It was just true. "I do what's there."

"That's sure."

"Yeah," said Hyvred. "Yeah, I suppose it is."

He opened the book.

He didn't open it at the front, where the recipes were, two hundred pages of them, salves and draughts and poultices from fourteen floors of grandmothers. He opened it from the back. The way he'd been opening it for three nights. To the inside of the back cover.

Hyvred looked down at it. She didn't read it. She didn't need to read it. He could see her looking at it the other way, the way she looked at everything with her glasses off, at where his hands had been on it and what he'd felt while they were.

"There's one old woman on floor 1," said Kei.

"Don't," said Hyvred.

He didn't.

She put her glasses on. Kei had seen her do that before. It was the thing she did when she didn't want to see anything else for a minute. Glasses on, and the world was the world. She sat there with them on and looked straight ahead at nothing, at the end of a table in a barn.

"Mother Crane," said Hyvred.

***

Gigi came at the next hour with her bucket.

She'd been doing it for four days, every hour, day and night. Up the barn to the straw man's feet, where Maigo sat rooted in the frost with his bare root-foot down through a gap between the flagstones and his gold ring of light round him, and his eyes shut, and his leaves gone yellow at the edges. A cup of water for Maigo. A cup down the gap for the root. A few words to him about the green, and her watch, and what he'd grow on floor 1 when they got there, if they got there. And then back down the barn to the trough by the horses to fill the bucket for the next hour.

She saw them on the sack, Kei and Hyvred and Bee and the book, and changed direction without seeming to.

Bee was awake by then. And Hako, who'd come off the line at the change, with his shield on his back and his spear over his shoulder and frost on the front of his shield where it had been facing the straw man for three hours, thick enough to write in. He sat down on the floor in front of the sack with his back to the table leg and his legs out, and looked at Kei's face, and then at the book.

"Okay," said Hako. "What."

So Kei showed them.

The inside back cover. His handwriting, from floor 2. Small, in pencil, gone soft and grey with four hundred days of being carried, in a bag, through snow and fen and glass and salt and the sea. Written out twice. The first one in his ordinary hand, neat. The second underneath it, squarer, every letter separate, the way you write a thing when you want to be sure you'll be able to read it later in bad light.

*Winter fever (deep, chest). Ma Yarrow.*
*Willow bark, for the heat.*
*River root (grows by the frozen river), dried + powdered, for the chest.*
*Honey.*
*Blue flower (dark). A VERY LITTLE. Too much stops the heart. Exactly enough keeps it going.*
*3 days to make + give. A bit at a time. Day and night.*
*Somebody has to sit with them the whole time.*

And under the second copy, in different ink, newer, blacker, a single word with a question mark that he'd written on the night of the four hundred and sixteenth day, in the tithe barn at Haverley, after Ice had stood up and sat down again.

*her?*

Nobody said anything.

Gigi read it twice. Her lips moved on the second time, the way they did when she read seed packets. Bee read it once and then looked up at Kei's face, with the look she had when she was going to draw something, and didn't. Hako read it and then looked at the ceiling, at the dark beams going up into nothing, for a long time.

"You wrote it on two," said Gigi.

"Day fifteen. The day after the adder."

"For her."

"I didn't know why," said Kei. "I just did. It felt important." He looked at it. "She said it took the old ones every year. In the second half of the winter, when they'd been cold too long. And it took three days, and you couldn't leave it with anybody. You had to sit with them. Day and night." He swallowed. "She said that's why it doesn't save many. Not because it doesn't work. Because nobody's got three days to sit with an old woman."

Round his neck, Dose lifted her head.

*He knew*, she said.

"She says I knew," said Kei.

"Classic Dose," said Hako. Quietly. And then, after a moment, much more quietly, to the ceiling: "Classic."

***

They went through it the way they went through everything. Kei's way. Item by item.

He had the willow bark. He'd had it since floor 2. He'd used some on Hako, that day in the snow, and he'd been topping it up ever since, on every floor that had a willow, which was most of them. There'd been willows on the Acre, down by Haverley's brown river. He had a twist of it in waxed paper in the bottom of his bag and he took it out and showed them.

He had river root. A pinch. The last of what Ma Yarrow had given him, four hundred and thirty-nine days ago, dry as dust in a little twist of cloth, and he didn't know if it was still good. Roots kept. Mostly. It was enough for a day. Maybe. Not three.

Honey was easy. The Acre had bees. Lammas had a whole hillside of hives, the old woman had said, and they'd brought jars up to harvest-home, and some of them were still full.

"And the flower," said Bee.

"I haven't got the flower."

"At all?"

"She had it in a jar. Ma Yarrow. On a shelf by the stove. She never said what it was called. She said it grew on the north side of two, under the pines, in summer, and you picked it and dried it." He shut his mouth. Then: "On Earth there's a flower like it. It's dark blue. It's one of the worst poisons there is. You can get it through your skin if you pick it without gloves." He looked at the page. *A VERY LITTLE.* "That's why it works. Same as the adder. It's the how much."

"So we go to two," said Hako.

"Two's on the way to one," said Gigi.

"Everything's on the way to one," said Bee. "If you're going down."

Nobody said anything to that either.

Kei turned the book a little in his hands. Then he said the part he'd been measuring for three nights, the part that had kept him awake. Because you said all of it. You didn't leave the bad part out because it was bad.

"He said midwinter," said Kei. "In the tithe barn. On floor 1. About the six hundred and tenth day. And Drew said a hundred and ninety-two days. That was thirty-seven days ago." He didn't need to do the sum. He'd done it a hundred times. "A hundred and fifty-five. And Ma Yarrow said the second half of the winter. So it's after midwinter. Or round it." He stopped. "But it's come early here. The frost. The swallows. The snow. Gaffer said eight days, maybe ten, and then it just *came*. And Hyv said once the floors go the same way down the bottom. Mostly. So if it's come early here—"

"It didn't," said Hyvred.

They looked at her.

She'd taken her glasses off again. She was looking up the barn. At the straw man, and the window, and the line of shields, and the person sitting cross-legged on the stone behind them.

"It didn't come early," said Hyvred. "It came when he remembers."

Kei looked at her.

"It's making it match," said Hyvred. "Thea said. On the first day of the grey. He remembered it in winter, the first time. Bare fields. All in. Doors shut. So it brought the winter. To match." She was speaking slowly, finding it, the way she did. "It's not early. It's not anything. It's just making it *when he remembers*. So on floor 1—"

She stopped.

Kei felt it go into him. Cold. Not a feeling. A measure. The last number falling into place in a sum he'd been doing for three days, and coming out right, and the right answer being the worst one.

The tower wasn't going to bring it early.

It was going to bring it exactly.

"On floor 1 it'll be exactly when he remembers," said Kei.

Hyvred nodded.

"Then we have to be exactly too," said Gigi.

"Then we'll be early," said Hako. He said it with his eyes still on the ceiling. "We're good at early. Rui Xiang's always early. We'll get Rui Xiang to do it."

Bee laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, wet, and she put her hand over her mouth. Hyvred put her arm round her.

"Take it to Higu," said Hyvred. To Kei. "Tonight."

"Not yet," said Kei. He shut the book. "First I'm showing him."

***

Ice had made the soup.

Kei hadn't thought he would. Not today. But he had. He'd got up off the stone at the usual time and gone to the Lammas oven, which was behind the line, against the long wall, and made barley soup in every pot the way he did every morning, with the last of the barley and the last of the onions he'd carried since Hythe and salt and water, and filled seven hundred bowls. Kei had watched him. He'd looked at every face for a second before he filled the bowl, the way he always did. Counting. And he'd given the first one to the old Lammas woman, again, and taken it to her himself, right up to the line, and stopped there, and held it out across the chalk. And Hako had come back from the wall of shields and taken it from him and carried it the rest of the way.

Now the cauldron was empty, and Ice was sitting at the line again. In the middle. Cross-legged, facing up the barn, with his hands in his lap.

Kei went and sat down next to him.

On the near side of the line. On the cold stone. Ice looked at him, and then back up the barn. Neither of them said anything for a while.

It was quieter, here. Seven hundred people, and the horses, and the wind on the roof, and none of it seemed to reach this bit of floor in front of the line. The shields were a body's length ahead of them, their backs, the rims touching. Jared and Chi on this watch. Past them, the frost. The old woman on her stool. The iron foot out of its socket. And, at the top, the face, looking at the man next to him, with the window over its head.

"It's looking at you," said Kei.

"Yes."

"Does it—" He stopped. He didn't know how to ask it. He didn't want to ask it. "Is it all right? Sitting here."

Ice thought about it. Kei watched him think about it. That was new, too, since the Salt, since the hill on floor 13. Ice used to answer *fine* before you'd finished asking. Now he stopped and looked inside himself first, as if he was checking a pot.

"It's cold," said Ice. "Here. More than anywhere else. And it's—" He looked at it. "It's heavy. Like somebody looking at the back of your neck. All the time. I can feel it when I'm asleep." He paused. "It hurts a bit. Not anywhere. Just a bit."

"Okay."

"I'd tell you if it was more."

"I know you would," said Kei. And found, to his surprise, that he did.

He opened the book. From the back. He held it out between them, open at the inside of the back cover, on his knees, where Ice could see it.

He didn't say anything. He'd thought about what to say for three nights, and none of it was right, and in the end he'd decided that the page could say it. That was what he'd written it down for.

Ice looked at it.

Kei watched him read it. He read it the way he read everything, all at once and then again, slowly. His eyes went down the first copy. Then down the second. Then they stopped. At the bottom. On a word in black ink with a question mark after it.

He didn't move. He didn't do anything at all. He sat on the stone with his hands in his lap and looked at the page on Kei's knees, and Kei, who'd measured everything since floor 2, found that he couldn't measure this. Whatever was happening in Ice's face, in the stillness of it, went off the end of anything he had to measure it with.

"You wrote it twice," said Ice.

His voice was very ordinary.

"In case one got wet."

Ice laughed. It wasn't really a laugh. It was a breath that went the wrong way. He put the back of his wrist against his mouth for a second, and took it away.

"That's very you," he said.

"Statistically, one of them was going to get wet."

"Did it?"

"No."

Ice nodded. He was still looking at the page. At the second copy, the square one, every letter separate. He put his thumb out toward it and stopped it, just short of the paper. Just over the word *her*. He didn't touch it.

"Ma Yarrow," said Ice.

"You know her."

He didn't answer that. He said, "Three cardigans."

"Four, by the end of the week," said Kei. "She put another one on every time somebody left the door open."

"Hako."

"Hako."

Ice nodded again. Kei watched his throat move.

"She said it doesn't save many," said Kei. "Not because it doesn't work. Because nobody's got three days to sit with an old woman." He looked at the page. He'd been going to say the next bit carefully. He found he couldn't say it carefully. "I did the sum. Thirty-six people. Three days. We've got a hundred and eight."

Ice put his hand over his face.

He didn't make any sound. He just sat there, on the cold stone at the line, with the straw man looking at him from the top of the barn and seven hundred people eating soup he'd made behind him, and his hand over his face, and his shoulders not moving at all, as if he was holding them still with everything he had.

Kei didn't touch him. He didn't know if he was allowed. He sat next to him with the book open on his knees and waited, the way you waited by a bed. You sat. You didn't leave. That was the job. Rachael had said that to Malcolm once, on floor 10, and Malcolm had told Kei, and Kei had written it down at the front of the book, on the first page, above all the recipes. *The job's being there. The rest is just stitches.*

After a long time Ice took his hand away.

"I didn't—" said Ice.

"You didn't ask," said Kei. "I know. I'm not asking either." He shut the book. Gently. He kept his hand on the cover. "I'm just telling you what I've got."

Ice looked at him.

"I've got the bark," said Kei. "A day's worth of root, maybe, if it's still good. Lammas has honey. I haven't got the flower. Ma Yarrow's got the flower, or she did on day sixteen. The root grows by the river on two. So it's two, and then one." He made himself say the last of it. "And I can make it. I can make it in my sleep. I've made it in my head every night for three nights. But I can't sit with her for three days on my own. Nobody can. That's the bit." He looked at the line on the floor. "That's the bit that isn't mine."

"Kei," said Ice.

He didn't say anything after that. He just said his name, the way you'd put a hand on somebody's arm.

"Thank you," said Ice.

***

Thea had come down from the top of the barn.

Kei didn't see her come. She'd been sitting by the old woman with the lantern, the way she had every night for four nights, and then she wasn't, and she was standing just behind them on the near side of the line, with the lantern in her hand, looking down. At Ice's face. At the book on Kei's knees.

She crouched down.

She didn't say anything at first. She looked at the book, shut, with Kei's hand on the cover. And then she looked at Kei.

He'd always been able to read Thea. Not the way Hyvred read people, or Rui Xiang. He read her the way you read a patient. Small things. The colour round the mouth. How fast the breath. Where the eyes went and how long they stayed. He'd sat with her through a fever on floor 6 and he knew her face asleep and awake and somewhere in between, and he looked at it now, crouched on the stone of the Long Barn with the lantern, and saw a thing in it he'd never seen.

She knew.

Not *knew* the way the club knew things, from slips and stones and evidence written on the second page of Chris's notebook. *Knew*. All of it. Whatever it was. The whole of whatever Ice was carrying, she was carrying it too, and she'd been carrying it for a long time, and he'd just put a book into the middle of it, and she was looking at him like—

He didn't know what like. He'd never had anybody look at him like that.

"Thank you, Kei," said Thea.

Her voice went, on his name. She didn't try to fix it.

"It's just a recipe," said Kei.

"It's not," said Thea. "It's not just anything." She put her free hand on his shoulder, and left it there a moment. "Nobody's got three days. That's what she said?"

"Ma Yarrow."

"She's wrong," said Thea.

She got up. She didn't go back up the barn. She sat down on Ice's other side, on the cold stone, at the line, with the lantern in her lap. The amber light in it was leaning. Kei had noticed that, the last few days. It always leaned toward Ice; everybody knew that, it was his light, it had been his since the Fen. But now it leaned so hard it lay flat against the glass on his side. As if it was trying to get closer.

The three of them sat there.

Up the barn, the straw man looked down at them. The frost on its face was very white in the light from the high windows. The window over its head said what it said.

Round Kei's neck, Dose shifted, and settled, and said, in the voice like a page turning:

*Hm.*

Kei looked down at her.

"Is that *told you*?" he said. Under his breath. "On floor 2. When you said *hm*. Was that *told you*?"

*Hm is always told you*, said Dose. *You just never ask what it means.* She put her head back down on his collarbone. *Ask your reeve. Tonight. While the wheat's still warm.*

***

He found Higu at noon, at the end of the first table, with the rock.

She was sitting where she always sat now, at the end nearest the line, where she could see the shields and the straw man and the whole of the barn at once. She had the rock in her lap, wiped clean on the right-hand side, and she was writing something small at the bottom of the left, very carefully, in chalk. She didn't look up.

"Higu," said Kei.

"Mm."

"Can we have it tonight." He held the book against his chest. "The thing you said we'd have when the wheat's in."

Higu stopped writing.

She looked up at him, and then down the barn, at the line of thirty-six sleeping places against the east wall, and the long table, and the sheaves stacked against the far walls to the beams, the last of the harvest of the Long Acre, every ear of it. In. All of it. She looked at that for a long moment.

"The wheat's in," said Higu.

"Yes."

"Then it's tonight." She went back to the rock. "Bring your book."

He went back to his place by the east wall and sat down in the straw. And then, because he'd always written down the things that were true, even when he didn't know yet which bits mattered, he opened the book at the back, and found the pen, and under the word in black ink with the question mark after it, very small, he wrote:

*yes.*
