Tiger was first out of every door.

He'd been first out of every door since the white room. Not because anybody told him to. Because his legs got there before the rest of him had finished deciding. On floor 1 he'd been out of the white room and halfway down the hill before Deston had finished reading the first window. On floor 12 he'd climbed the Mast's rope ladder before Hako had finished tying the new rope to it. On floor 13 he'd opened the top door and the sound had come in.

On the stair down from 14 he'd said *I'll open the doors. Going down too*. And then he had. Every one. Tell, the well-house, the cellar under the Last Light, the cliff on the Stair, the black ice at the far side of the Gate, which hadn't been a door until they'd counted it into being one, and he'd stood in the gap while it opened anyway, first, because his legs had.

The door at the bottom of the Bastion's stair was a door in a tree.

He knew it would be. He'd come up through it. A door in the trunk of the oldest of the three great trees that stood round a circle in the Down, a day's walk south-west of the seven, right in front of where the Sower had knelt with its back to it for a thousand years. He'd stood in that circle on the morning of the two hundred and thirty-sixth day and watched a thing as big as a hill stand up out of the ground for the first time, with its knees torn out of a tree's roots, and Hako put a spear through its heart.

He put his hand on the door.

And waited.

It was a thing he'd learned. On floor 8, from Neth, with a net. *Quick ones always think they are late. It is not a thing you can be, in a net.* He'd learned to tie a slow knot, a finger's width at a time, waiting for it to lie down where it was going before he moved it again. Thirty-one times the first morning. Then it held. He hadn't stopped being quick. He didn't think he ever would. But he'd learned to wait with his hand on a door until the others were behind him.

"Go on," said Higu, from the dark behind him.

He opened it.

***

It was autumn in the Down.

He hadn't known trees that size could have an autumn. He'd thought they'd just be green forever, the way they'd been: towers of bark going up into a roof of leaves so high it was like looking up at weather, and the light coming down through it in long gold-green shafts.

The shafts were gold now. Just gold.

And it was snowing leaves.

He stood in the door in the tree and looked up. They were coming down out of the roof. Slowly. Turning over and over as they fell, a long way, the way snow falls when there's no wind. Leaves the size of shields. Leaves the size of the sail on the Mast. Gold and copper and brown, drifting down through the shafts of light into the Down, a hundred of them, a thousand, so slowly it was like they were deciding where to land.

One came down in front of him. He put his hand out. It landed on his palm and lay there, as long as his arm, dry and warm, with veins in it like the rivers on Higu's map.

"*Oh*," said Calvin, behind him. "Oh, it's *autumn*."

"The floors have their own years," said Higu. She'd said that on floor 8, the first morning, when Calvin shouted that the leaves were going yellow. She said it again now, and her voice did something on it. "I'd forgotten that."

The Down was quiet.

Not the old quiet. Tiger remembered the old quiet. The Grow, the undergrowth, ticking and creaking all the time, so that if you stopped walking you could hear it. Growing. Visibly. A frond unrolling while you watched. Waist-high over your footprints in twenty minutes at dusk, and the paths moving every night, so that the only thing you could steer by was the big trees, because they didn't move.

The Grow was still there. Ferns, briars, moss. Waist-high, mostly. He walked out into it and it was just ferns. It didn't tick. It didn't creak. A frond brushed his arm and stayed where it was. He looked back at his footprints and they stayed where they were.

"It's just plants," said Maigo. Barefoot, beside him, with his root-foot down on the moss. Wonder in his voice. "It's asleep. Like real plants. For the winter." He listened. "It's not shouting. It's not even talking. It's just *asleep*."

Tiger looked at the footprints. Still there.

He could have run. He wanted to. It was a day's walk to the seven and he could have done it by noon on his own, the way he'd have done it on floor 1, out of the door and away and back before anybody missed him. He felt his legs want to.

He walked.

Beside Higu, at the front. With the leaves coming down.

***

They got to the seven at dusk.

The ring of the seven biggest trees in the Down, leaning together at the top, with Highcrook in their crowns. Platforms on the great branches, rope bridges between them, round woven hanging houses like nests. And foxfire. In jars. Everywhere. Cold blue-green, a thousand points of it, all the way up the seven into the dark, coming on one at a time as the light went.

Tiger stood at the foot of the north tree and looked up and felt something in his chest he didn't have a word for.

He'd been here. On the way up. Thirty-odd days of it. He'd run every bridge in Highcrook in the first week and nearly fallen off two of them and been shouted at by a very small girl with three beads in her moss. He'd held a platform edge with his blades all night when the Grow came up the trunks. He'd stood in the Down in front of the seven on the last morning and watched Gabe make a fire on the moss at dusk, for the third night running, for no reason anybody would say.

He hadn't thought he'd ever stand here again. Nobody had. You didn't go back down. That was the whole thing about a tower.

"*Walkers!*"

A whistle, high, from somewhere above. Then another, from the next tree. Then a lot of them, all at once, going round the seven from branch to branch like birds waking up. Tiger knew three of them. Aleaka had taught him. That one meant *look*. That one meant *come*. That one meant—

"*WALKERS!*"

Not a whistle. A voice. Small. Furious. From a platform about forty metres up the north tree, where a very small figure was leaning so far out over the edge that Tiger's stomach went cold.

"*YOU CAME BACK!*" yelled Tansy.

And then she was coming down.

Not by the rope. Not by the basket. Down the trunk. Head first, the way the bough-folk climbed, with her long fingers and her long toes in the bark, fast, faster than Tiger had ever seen anything climb, so fast that bark came off in pieces under her hands. Forty metres in about the time it took him to breathe twice. She came off the bottom of the trunk onto the moss in front of him and stood there with her big dark eyes shining green in the dusk and four beads in her moss, not three, and her fists clenched.

"Nobody comes back," said Tansy. "Burr *said*. He said walkers go up. He said that's what walkers *do*. He said *don't get used to them, Tansy*." She looked at Tiger. Then at all of them. Thirty-six. In the Down, at dusk, in the leaves. "You came *back*."

"We're going down," said Tiger.

Tansy looked at him.

"*Down*?"

"To the bottom," said Tiger. "Floor 1. All of us."

Tansy opened her mouth. Shut it. Opened it again.

"Nobody goes *down*," said Tansy.

"We do now," said Tiger.

She looked at him for a long time with her eyes shining. Then she turned round and put her fingers in her mouth and whistled. One long one, up the north tree, high and hard. Tiger didn't know that one.

From the top of the tree, a long way up, a rope came down.

***

Burr was at the drum.

The rope-master. Old, his grey face lined like bark, his moss nearly white, a coil of rope on each shoulder, forearms like roots. Two fingers and a thumb on his left hand. He'd been the rope-master of Highcrook for longer than anybody alive, and his father before him, and he'd hauled the thirty-six up the north tree in baskets, three at a time unless one of them was Salty, every evening for thirty-odd days, and counted them up every night.

He hauled them up now.

He didn't say anything until the last basket. He worked the drum, with two of his boys, the way he always had, slow and steady, and the baskets went up the north tree in the dusk with the foxfire coming on round them, and he didn't say a word. Tiger was in the first basket. He watched Burr's face the whole way up and it didn't change.

When the last basket came up, with Gabe in it, and Salty, which wasn't allowed, Burr locked the drum. He stood up. He took one of the coils of rope off his shoulder and held it in his hands.

And counted them.

He did it the way he'd done it on the way up. Every night. Every one. His lips moving, his eyes going over them, standing on the drum platform in the foxfire with the dark coming up from the ground below.

"Thirty-six walkers," said Burr. "Good." He looked at the coil in his hands. "Nobody left down."

He'd said that on the first night. And every night after. *Nobody left down.* It was the thing he said. It was what the rope was for.

Then he looked at them again. Longer.

"Tansy says you're going down," said Burr.

"Yes," said Higu.

"To the bottom."

"Floor 1."

Burr was quiet.

"When you went up," said Burr. "On the last morning. At the door in front of where it knelt. I said something." He turned the coil over in his hands. "*Nobody left up, either. That's the other half. Walkers never say the other half.*" He looked at them. "I said *come back*."

"We remember," said Higu.

"Walkers never come back," said Burr. "That's the other half nobody says. You go up, and you're gone. Like a leaf. Like a rope you let go of." He was looking at the coil. "My father said it. His father. Three hundred years. *Nobody left up. That's the other half.* We said it at the door every time, and it never meant anything, because nobody ever came back down to make it true."

He stopped.

He put the coil back on his shoulder.

"You came and said it," said Burr. "With your feet." He looked at Tiger, of all of them. Tiger didn't know why. "That's the other half. That's the first time anybody ever said it." His voice went rough. "Walkers never listen. I said that too. Every day." He sniffed. "I was wrong about that."

Nobody said anything.

Then Ice came forward. Out of the middle of them, with Thea's hand in his. He stopped in front of Burr on the drum platform, and Tiger saw him look at Burr's left hand, the two fingers and the thumb, the way he looked at things he'd known a long time.

"Thank you for the rope," said Ice.

Burr looked at him.

"Which time?" said Burr.

"All of them," said Ice.

Burr looked at him a while longer. The old green eyeshine in the dark. Then he reached out with his left hand, the two fingers and the thumb, and put it on Ice's arm. Just above the wrist. Where the lines were.

"You were the light," said Burr. "On the end of it. I remember." He took his hand away. "I always hold the rope. You put the light on the end, and I put it as high as you like." He jerked his head at the platforms. "Go on. Eat. Tansy's mother's made nut bread. There's grubs. The big one'll want grubs."

"I *do* want grubs," said Hako, from the back.

***

They stayed two nights.

Tiger ran every bridge in Highcrook on the first morning. Of course he did. He'd meant not to. He'd meant to walk, and wait, and be the sort of person who didn't run every bridge in Highcrook on the first morning. He got out of the hanging house at first light and the bridges were there, all of them, swaying in the gold light between the seven, and his legs went.

Tansy caught him on the third.

She came out of nowhere. Off a branch above the bridge, dropping, landing on the ropes in front of him so the whole bridge bucked, and then running. Ahead of him. Backwards. Facing him. On a rope bridge forty metres up, running backwards, grinning, with her four beads swinging.

"*Race you*," said Tansy.

"Where to?"

"The Youngest!"

She turned round and went.

He'd raced her on the way up. Eleven times. He'd lost all eleven. He was the fastest of the thirty-six and he'd lost to a bough-girl half his height eleven times, on bridges, because she didn't run them, she *flowed* them, and he'd never once worked out how.

He chased her across the seven. Bridge to bridge. Platform to platform. Round the east tree, along the long branch, across the swaying bridge to the south tree, up a ladder, along another. She was always ahead. Not far. Just enough. Looking back, grinning. He felt his legs go faster and faster and the bridges blur and the wind in his ears and the old thing come up in his chest, the thing he'd had since the white room, the thing that said *faster, faster, you're late, you're late*—

And on the long bridge to the Youngest, he stopped.

He didn't decide to. He just did. In the middle of the bridge, forty metres up, with the whole thing swaying under him and Tansy at the far end, turning round, surprised. He stopped and stood there, holding the ropes, breathing.

*Quick ones always think they are late.*

He wasn't late. There was nothing to be late for. Tansy was at the end of the bridge and the Youngest was behind her and the sun was coming up through the gold roof of the Down and the leaves were falling, slowly, all round, a long way down. Nobody was waiting for him. Nothing was going to happen if he got there second.

He walked the rest of the bridge.

Tansy watched him do it with her mouth open.

"You *stopped*," said Tansy, when he got there.

"Yeah."

"You never stop."

"I do now," said Tiger. "Sometimes."

She looked at him with her big green-shining eyes. Then she sat down on the platform at the foot of the Youngest, with her legs over the edge, and patted the boards beside her. He sat.

They didn't race again. They sat on the edge of the Youngest's platform with their legs over the drop and watched the leaves come down. After a while she showed him her four beads. The first three were from before. The fourth was new. Red. A seed, polished, with a hole through it.

"From her," said Tansy. And pointed.

***

The young tree was at the foot of the north tree.

Tiger had seen it come up. Everybody had. On the morning after the Sower, at the north tree's foot, in the ash of a little orange campfire that Ina had lit in the Down against Burr's rules, a seedling. The Youngest's sibling. It had come up out of the ash knee-high by the fourth morning, thigh-high by the fifth, a first branch by the sixth, taller than Gigi by the eighth. And then they'd gone up.

It was taller than the hanging houses now.

He looked down at it from the Youngest's platform. Not a seedling. A tree. A young one, slim, pale-barked, with its first real branches going out in all directions like a child holding its arms out, and its leaves going gold like everything else. Growing out of the moss at the north tree's foot, in the shelter of the great roots. And round its foot, on the moss, in a ring, foxfire jars. Dozens of them. Lit, even in the day.

And people.

Bough-folk. On the ground. In the Down. Sitting on the moss round the young tree, in the morning, a dozen of them, old ones and children, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

Tiger stared.

"They go down now?" he said.

"Since the spring," said Tansy. As if it were nothing. "The Grow doesn't move. Not since. The paths stay. So we go down. In the day. To sit with her." She was looking at the young tree. "Linden goes every day. Old Linden. She used to sit with the north tree. With her cheek on him. Seventy years. And then he stopped asking, after the big thing, and she didn't know what to do. So now she sits with *her*."

"What does she hear?"

Tansy thought about it.

"Linden says the north tree used to sound like asking," said Tansy. "All the time. For three hundred years. Like somebody asking something and never getting an answer." She swung her legs. "And she says the young one sounds like talking. Just talking. Like somebody telling you about their day."

Tiger looked down at the young tree with the old woman sitting at its foot. He couldn't see from here, but he knew which one she was. The blind one, with thin white moss, sitting with her cheek on the pale bark.

"Can I have it?" said Tansy.

He looked at her.

"The apple," said Tansy. "In your pack. I can smell it." She was looking at his pack. "It smells like honey-pears but sharper."

He'd forgotten. Joan's apple, from the shop with the green shutters. In his pack, wrapped in a bit of cloth, since Tell. He'd taken it out and looked at it most nights. He hadn't eaten it.

"No," said Tiger. "Sorry. It's for somebody."

"Who?"

"Somebody on floor 1. An old woman." He thought about how to say it. "She fed us. The first night. All of us. And she said we'd come back down. And we didn't. Not for a long time." He looked at the leaves. "So we're going down. And I'm bringing her an apple."

Tansy looked at him for a long time.

Then she reached up and pulled the red bead out of her moss. The new one. The fourth. The polished seed from the young tree. She held it out to him on her long-fingered palm.

"Then give her this," said Tansy. "From me. Tell her it's from the young tree. Tell her it talks."

He took it.

He didn't know what to say. So he didn't say anything. He put it in his pack, next to Joan's apple, in the cloth, carefully, the way you'd tie a slow knot.

"She'll like it," he said.

"Course she will," said Tansy. "It's *red*."

***

Gabe made a fire that night.

On the moss. At the foot of the seven. In the Down, after dark, which you did not do. Tiger watched him go down the rope at dusk with an armful of fallen branches and a bag of the big dry cones from the Youngest, and Burr let him. Burr worked the drum himself. He'd said, after the Sower, that he'd allow cone-fires under his terms, and his terms were: in the ring of jars, never more than a man's height, and somebody up top watching all night.

Burr watched all night.

Tiger watched too. From the drum platform, beside the old rope-master, on the boards with his legs over. He didn't know why. He'd gone to sleep in the hanging house and woken at midnight and come out and Burr was there with the drum locked, looking down, and Tiger had sat beside him and neither of them had said anything.

The fire was small. Orange. Ordinary. On the moss inside the ring of foxfire jars at the north tree's foot, beside the young tree. And sitting at it, three people.

Gabe. Ice. Thea.

Tiger couldn't hear them. It was forty metres down. He could see them in the firelight, small, the way you see people from a high window. Gabe feeding the fire. Ice beside him, on his right, which was the wrong way round; Gabe always stood on Ice's right. Not at a fire, apparently. And Thea on Ice's other side, with her head on his shoulder.

She did that now. Tiger had noticed. Since the sea. She'd slept against him on the deck of the *Patience* on the eleventh night, Azac said, and she hadn't stopped. She was still angry. Everybody knew she was still angry. Dennis had a graph. But she slept on his shoulder now, and leaned on him at fires, and the angry seemed to be somewhere else. Behind. Like a sword you carry on your back instead of in your hand.

"He made a fire there," said Burr. Quietly. Beside him. "The big one. Three nights. When you came up. Against my rules."

"I know," said Tiger. "I was here."

"Never said why," said Burr. "I asked. He said *somebody might need to find their way back*." He looked down at the small orange light. "Nobody was lost. Not then. Thirty-six walkers every night. I counted."

Tiger didn't say anything.

He'd been in the club since floor 6. Since Hyvred's colours and Kei's word, in a cistern, and Hako saying it out loud for the first time. He knew what the club thought. He'd heard Thea's version from the pit on floor 10, from Xav: all thirty-six the first time, ten years, a choice at the top, back to the first minute so he could run. He knew there was a first time. He knew Ice had been lost somewhere, once, on some floor, in the dark. And somebody had made a fire.

He didn't know it had been here. He didn't know anything. He just knew that Gabe was sitting at a fire on the moss at the foot of the seven, the same place, and Ice was beside him, and that Ice had his head tipped back looking up at the seven and the foxfire and the dark, the way you'd look up at a thing you'd looked up at once before from the wrong side.

Down at the fire, Ice said something.

Tiger couldn't hear it. He saw Gabe turn his head. Saw him look at Ice for a long time in the firelight. And then Gabe laughed. Out loud. Tiger heard that. It came all the way up the north tree, Gabe's big laugh, forty metres, in the dark, and the foxfire jars on the platforms round them seemed to flicker with it.

Burr looked down at it.

"Good," said Burr. To nobody. And didn't explain.

***

They left on the third morning.

Burr let them down the rope, three at a time, unless one of them was Salty, the way he'd let them up. And at the bottom, on the moss, he counted them. All thirty-six. Every one. Then he came down himself. Burr, the rope-master, who never went down.

He came down the rope on his own, hand over hand, the two fingers and the thumb, slow, and stepped off onto the moss at the foot of the north tree, in the Down, in the morning, with the leaves coming down round him. He stood there for a moment as if he'd forgotten what the ground felt like.

Then he walked with them. To the door.

It was a day's walk north-east. The door in the root-wall of a tree, where they'd come up from floor 8, on the two hundredth day, out of a stair behind a waterfall. Burr walked the whole way. Tansy came too, though nobody had said she could, swinging along beside Tiger and not racing him. And three of Burr's boys. And, at the back, carried in a basket between two of them, because she was blind and very old and had insisted, Old Linden.

At the door, Burr counted them again.

"Thirty-six walkers," said Burr.

He stopped. He looked at the door. At the coil of rope on his shoulder.

"Nobody left up," said Burr. "That's the other half."

He said it the way he'd always said it, Tiger thought. The way his father had said it, and his father's father, at every door, for three hundred years, when it never meant anything.

It meant something now.

"Nobody left down," said Higu. Back to him.

Burr looked at her. And something in his old bark face moved that Tiger had never seen move.

"Walkers never say the *first* half," said Burr. "That's mine."

"We know," said Higu. "We're saying it back."

Burr didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he took the coil of rope off his shoulder. The left one. The older one, dark with three hundred years of hands. And held it out.

Not to Higu. To Tiger.

Tiger looked at it.

"You're the quick one," said Burr. "You go first. Every door. I've watched you." He held it out further. "Take it down with you. To the bottom. Wherever you're going." He looked at the coil. "Somebody down there might need a rope. You put a light on the end. And you hold it."

"I'll—" said Tiger. He didn't know what he'd. "I'll bring it back."

"No," said Burr. "You won't." He put it in Tiger's hands. "You'll go up. That's what walkers do." He almost smiled. "And then one day you'll come back down again. And you'll give it to somebody. And they'll say *nobody left up*. And it'll mean something."

Tiger held the rope.

It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. Dark. Smooth. Warm, from Burr's shoulder.

Tansy, beside him, was crying, and pretending she wasn't, and hitting him on the arm.

"You *stopped*," said Tansy. "On the bridge. Don't forget you stopped."

"I won't."

"You'll forget."

"I won't forget," said Tiger. "I stopped."

Old Linden, in her basket, had her cheek against the bark of the door-tree. Listening. She lifted her head as they went in, one at a time, and turned her blind face toward the sound of them, and said something very quietly that only the trees heard.

Tiger went last but one. Gabe was last; he always was, going down. Tiger stood in the doorway in the root-wall with Burr's rope over his shoulder and Joan's apple in his pack and a red bead from a young tree next to it, and looked back.

Burr was standing on the moss with his two fingers and his thumb on Tansy's head, and his empty shoulder, and the leaves coming down.

He lifted his hand.

Tiger went in.

He didn't run down the stair. Three hundred and sixty-four steps. He walked. Beside Newish, who was counting, and didn't need him to be first.

At the bottom, there'd be a waterfall. He'd open the door.

He'd wait first.
