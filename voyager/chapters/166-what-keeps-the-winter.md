Winter liked the snow.

She'd always liked it. It was one of the things people got wrong about her, the way they got most things wrong: they saw the silver hair and the horns and the eyes, one pale blue and one pink, and the way she didn't smile at people she didn't know, and they decided she was cold. And then they decided that being cold was a sad thing, a thing that had happened to her, that she'd want fixing. And then they were surprised when she didn't.

She wasn't cold. She just liked the cold. They were different.

She liked the way snow made everything quiet. She liked the way it showed you where things had been, every footprint, every bird, every place somebody had stood and thought about something and walked on. She liked that you couldn't lie in it. If you'd been somewhere, it said so.

She stood at the third door of the Long Barn on the morning after the burial, with the door open a crack and the cold coming in round her, and looked at the snow on the Barn Field, and for the first time in her life didn't like it at all.

It was lying to her.

***

It lay over everything. The barn fields. The hedges. The lane. The grave against the barn wall, which was just a long low hump now, like a sleeping dog under a blanket. All of it white, smooth, sparkling a little in the grey light, a hand and a half deep and still coming down in small hard flakes out of a low sky.

And under it, the Grey.

You couldn't see it. That was the lie. If you stood at the door and looked down the valley, it was just snow, all the way, the most ordinary winter morning in the world. White fields and white hedges and the long white valley going away into the falling snow. You'd have walked out into it. You'd have thought you could.

Annet had walked out into it. Winter had heard. She'd seen Annet's leg afterwards, at supper, grey to the knee with that fine dust, and the dust had come off in the water when Rachael washed it but Annet had sat very still the whole time with her face doing nothing.

*There's no ground*, Annet had said.

Winter looked at the snow lying over nothing and didn't like it.

"You're letting the warm out," said somebody behind her.

It was Ina. Of course it was. With his mask on his shoulder, not his face, and his hands in the sleeves of his long indigo coat. He still looked tired. He'd looked tired since the grave. Ina didn't get tired from fighting, or walking, or anything ordinary. He got tired from giving things away. And he'd given the ground under Gaffer Tobin an hour of spring, and it had taken something out of him that hadn't come back yet.

"There isn't any warm," said Winter. "It's a barn."

"There's seven hundred people," said Ina. "That's warm."

She looked at him. Then she shut the door.

***

It was very strange, being shut in a barn with seven hundred people.

She'd thought it would be loud. It was, at first. The first night, the night of the burial, everybody had talked, all at once, the way people do when something's happened that nobody understands. But by the second day it had gone quiet. Not silent. Just low. Seven hundred people in a barn the size of a hill, with the doors shut on the snow and the lamps turned down to save the oil, sitting in the straw in their villages, in little clusters, talking in low voices, mending things, sleeping. Children playing very quietly in the aisles between the tables, the way children play when they've been told to and know why. Somebody always singing, somewhere, very softly.

The harvest fed them. The Barn Field's harvest, the last field, all of it stacked against the long walls of the barn in sheaves, and the gleaners from Brightwater had a quern, and the women from Lammas had brought an oven on a cart for harvest-home, a squat iron thing like a beehive, and by the second morning there was bread. Not much. Enough. You couldn't go out, so you couldn't hunt, so there was no meat. Just bread, and what was left of harvest-home, and the Ashwell apples that the old women rationed out one at a time from a sack, very carefully, the way you'd ration something you weren't going to get any more of.

Ice cooked.

Of course he did. On the second morning he went to the women at the Lammas oven and stood there until they gave him something to do, and by noon he'd found a way to make the Barn Field's barley into a thick soup with nothing in it but barley and water and salt and an onion he'd had in his pack since Hythe, and it was the best thing anybody in the barn had eaten since harvest-home, and he made it in his own big iron pot, and then in somebody's cauldron, and then in all of them.

Winter watched him do it. She'd watched him do it on every floor. He didn't talk while he cooked. He didn't look at anybody. He just stood at the pot with a spoon, tasting, adding salt a pinch at a time, the way you'd do a thing you'd done in a lot of kitchens. And when it was ready he stood at the end of the line with the ladle, and every single person who came past, gleaner or passer, he looked at for a second before he filled their bowl. Not long. Just a second. Like he was counting them.

She knew what that was. She'd seen Higu do it with the rock.

*He's counting who's here*, she thought. *Every time. On every floor. Making sure.*

She didn't say anything. It wasn't hers.

***

The straw man's arms were both up by the second evening.

Not up. Not reaching. Just lifted. A hand's width, both of them now, the long bundles of straw going out from the crosspiece where shoulders would be, raised a little, the way you'd raise your arms if somebody was about to put a coat on you. They'd been down, at harvest-home. The old women had tied them down, with bands, to the iron. Winter had watched them do it.

The bands were still tied. She'd gone and looked. She'd stood at the foot of the frame in the half-dark at the end of the barn by the horses, who hated it now and wouldn't stand still, and looked up. The bands were tied, round the iron, round the straw. Tight. And the arms were up anyway. The straw had moved inside the bands.

And the head was three fingers to the left.

Chris had measured it. He measured it every morning and every night, with his bit of string from the post with the nail in it, and wrote it in his notebook, and showed it to Higu, and Higu put it on the rock. Three fingers. Toward the middle of the barn.

And the frost on the neck had spread.

It had been just the neck, the first morning. The plaited gold figure tied in where a heart would go, white with frost when nothing else was. Now the frost had gone out from it. Into the straw of the chest round it. A white patch the size of a dinner plate, then the size of a cartwheel, spreading out over the straw man's body from the middle, slowly, the way frost spreads across a window. By the second evening his whole chest was white.

Nobody went near him any more except the thirty-six. The gleaners had moved away from that end of the barn. Village by village, quietly, without anybody saying so, until the last forty paces of the barn floor in front of the straw man were empty except for the horses, who couldn't leave, and the old Lammas woman with no teeth, who wouldn't.

She sat on a stool at his feet. All day. With her hands in her lap. Looking up at him.

Winter went and sat on the floor beside her, on the second evening, because she wanted to know why.

The old woman didn't look at her for a long time.

"I plaited his face," she said at last. "Sixty years. Every harvest-home. My mother before me. I've done his face sixty times." She looked up at it. At the twisted straw eyes, turned now three fingers to the left, looking at something in the middle of the barn. "He's never looked away from the doors. Not once. Not in sixty years. They tie him looking at the doors, so he can see the winter coming, and keep it out." She pressed her lips together over her gums. "He's not looking at the doors."

"What's he looking at?"

The old woman didn't answer that.

"He's cold," she said instead. "He's never been cold. He's made of harvest. Harvest's warm. You put your hand in a stook in the middle of the night and it's warm inside, like a sleeping animal." She looked at the white on his chest. "He's cold all the way through."

***

The council was on the third day.

It wasn't called a council. Nobody called it anything. It just happened, the way things happened in the Long Barn now: somebody went and sat at the end of the first table, by the place where Gaffer Tobin's chair had been, and somebody else came and sat with them, and then a few more, and then it was a council.

Annet. Cobb. Osric of Ashwell with his long stick. Old Ezra. The Reeves of Middle Acre and Lammas and Brightwater and Hollin, two men and two women, none of whom Winter had learned the names of. The old Lammas woman, who'd been fetched from her stool. And the thirty-six, because by now the gleaners didn't do anything without the passers sitting in on it, and the thirty-six didn't do anything without Higu and the rock.

Higu put the rock on the table.

The gleaners looked at it. They couldn't read it. Nobody on this floor could read their letters. But they'd learned what it was, the way they'd learned what Newish's count was: the thing the passers looked at when they wanted to know what was true.

"What does it do?" said Annet. "When it walks."

She said it to the old Lammas woman. To Ezra. To the gleaners. The oldest people in the Acre.

Nobody answered.

"There's a song," said Ezra at last. Slowly. He was the oldest binder. He'd plaited the neck. "My grandmother's grandmother sang it. Not at harvest-home. Not ever at harvest-home. In the winter, if it was a hard one, by the fire." He looked down at his hands on the table. "*When the straw man walks, there's winter walking in him. Keep him, and he'll keep you.*" He stopped. "That's all I've got. That's the only verse anybody remembers."

"Keep him how?"

Ezra shook his head.

"Nobody knows," said the old Lammas woman. "Nobody's ever had to. He's never walked." She looked down the barn, at the white figure against the end wall, with its arms up. "He's never once in a thousand years done anything but stand there and keep the winter out."

"And in spring?" said Chris. Gently. "What happens to him in spring?"

"We plough him in," said Annet.

Everybody looked at her.

"The first day of the spring ploughing," said Annet. "Every village. We take him down off the frame, and we cut him up, and every village gets a piece of him, and we plough it into the first furrow of the first field." She looked at her stick, which was on the table in front of her. "So the harvest goes back in. So there's a next one. He's the last of the year, and he keeps it all winter, and in spring you put him back where it came from."

"You never burn him," said somebody. Quietly.

It was Ina.

Annet looked at him for a long moment.

"No," she said. "You never burn him. Burn him and you burn the keeping. Burn him and there's no next one." She said it quite flatly. "That's not a song. That's just true. Everybody knows that."

Winter wasn't looking at Annet.

She was looking across the table at Ice.

He'd gone white. Not the way he went white at the Gate, or on the Salt, slowly, from the top down. All at once. Like a lamp blown out. He was sitting between Thea and Gabe at the far end of the table and he'd gone completely white, and he wasn't moving, and he was looking at the rock.

At the left-hand column. Winter didn't need to read it. She knew what it said. She'd been there, the first evening, in the shade of the old barn, when he said it.

*LONG BARN. 3 DAYS N. STRAW MAN (WALKS IN WINTER). GUARDIAN. BURNED. STAIR UNDER FLOOR.*

*Burned.*

"Ice," said Winter.

She hadn't meant to say it. It just came out. That was a thing about her too that people got wrong: they thought she was careful, because she was quiet. She wasn't careful. She just didn't talk unless she had something to say. And when she had something to say, she said it.

He looked at her.

"You said," said Winter. "On the first night. It walked. In the snow. And you burned it." She didn't look away. "It took two days."

The whole table went still.

The gleaners didn't know what she meant. Not really. They'd heard *burned* and they'd gone stiff all down the table, every one of them, the way you'd go stiff if somebody said the worst word in your language at your table. But they didn't know what *on the first night* meant, or when, or how. They looked at her. Then at him.

He didn't look away either.

"Yes," he said.

"What happened?" said Winter. "After."

"I don't know."

"What happened to *them*?" She tipped her head at the gleaners. At Annet. At Ezra. At the old Lammas woman with her hands in her lap. "After you burned him. In the spring. Did they have a spring? Did they have a next one?"

"I don't know," said Ice. Very quietly. "We went up the stair."

Nobody said anything.

Winter heard Gabe, at Ice's right, breathe in. She saw his hand go flat on the table. She saw Thea, at Ice's left, not move at all. She saw Higu, with the rock, close her eyes.

And she saw Ice not do anything about it. Not explain. Not say *I didn't know*. Not say *it was coming at us*, which it must have been, it was a floor guardian, it must have been coming at thirty-four people in the snow. Not say any of the things anybody would have said. He sat there, white, with his hands on the table, and let it be what it was.

"So you don't know," said Winter, "if you starved them."

"No," said Ice.

***

It was Annet who spoke first. Of course it was.

She didn't look at Ice. She looked at the rock, for a long time. At the letters she couldn't read. Then at the gleaners down the table. Then, finally, at Winter.

"You keep saying *first*," she said. "All of you. Since the old barn. *The first time.* He knew my name before I said it. He knew about the straw man. He knew where we'd put the chair." She tapped her stick on the table. Once. "I've never asked. Nobody tells you a lot, I said, and nobody did, and I didn't ask." She looked at Ice. "I'm still not asking."

Ice looked at her.

"I don't want to know what it means," said Annet. "I've got seven hundred people in a barn and a winter that came a month early and fields with no ground under them and a straw man who's looking the wrong way. I've got no room for *what it means*." She paused. "But if somebody burned him once, somewhere, some time, and they don't know what happened after—" She stopped. "Then this time they'll want to know before they do anything. Won't they."

"Yes," said Ice.

"Good," said Annet. "Then nobody burns him."

"No fire," said Ina.

He said it the way he'd said it on floor 8, at the hearth the ones before had laid, three hundred and twelve years before. Very quiet. Not a request.

Annet looked at him. And then at Ice. And Ice nodded. Once.

"No fire," said Ice.

***

That night she went out into the snow with Ina.

She hadn't meant to. She'd been on the late watch at the third door with him, ten till midnight; Higu's rota still ran in the Long Barn, the same as everywhere, two by two, nobody watching alone. And at about eleven, with the barn asleep behind them and the snow stopped, finally, and the sky clearing, she'd opened the door and gone out.

He came with her. He didn't ask why.

It was very cold. The coldest yet. The kind of cold that's so still it rings. The stars were out, thousands of them, enormous, hard and white over the black shapes of the hills that had closed in round the head of the valley. There was no moon. The snow lay over everything, the barn fields and the lane and the grave and the whole long valley going away south, blue-white in the starlight, smooth, untouched, sparkling. Her breath went up in front of her in a long white plume and hung there.

She walked down the Barn Field. To the last hedge.

Ina came behind her. Not close. A few paces. The way he always walked behind anybody he was looking after, as if he just happened to be going that way too.

At the hedge, she stopped. And took her bow off her back. And took an arrow out of her quiver.

"Winter," said Ina.

"I'm not going to shoot anything."

"You've got an arrow in your hand."

"I'm going to *look*," said Winter.

She knelt in the snow at the gap in the hedge, where Annet had stepped through. The hole was still there, her footprint, half filled with new snow. And at the bottom of it, where the ground should have been, grey. Like the bottom of a painted well.

She put the arrow on the string. Not drawn. Just on. And put the point of it down into the hole, into the grey, very gently, and let Hoarfrost go.

*What you hit stays where it is.* That was what it said, her skill, on her window, on floor 1. Frost went out from the arrow into whatever it touched and held it. She'd held Rimed with it, on the walls of the Bastion, and a crack in the Prism's face, and once, on the Salt, a saltmouth under the crust while Hyvred told her where. The frost went out from the point of the arrow and into the thing, and the thing stopped.

The frost went out from the point of the arrow into the grey.

And didn't go anywhere.

It went out. She felt it go. The cold, out of her, down the shaft, out of the point. And then it just didn't land. It went into the grey and kept going, the way water goes into sand, the way a word goes into a room where nobody's listening. She pushed more into it. Then more. Her hand went cold to the wrist on the shaft.

Nothing froze.

Nothing changed. The grey at the bottom of the hole stayed grey, and still, and nothing at all, with her frost going into it and into it and not stopping anywhere because there was nothing there for it to stop *on*.

She took the arrow out.

The point was grey. Not frosted. Grey. Like the dust on Annet's leg. She looked at it in the starlight, and then she wiped it on the snow, and it came off, and the steel was clean underneath.

"There's nothing to hold," she said. "Is there."

"No," said Ina.

"It's not that it won't freeze. It's that there's nothing there to be frozen." She stood up. She looked down the valley. At the snow, lying over it, smooth and white and lying. "It's not ground and it's not *not* ground. It's just—" She stopped. "It's where the ground was. Like a word somebody's rubbed out and you can still see where it was on the page."

"Yan said blank," said Ina.

"Yan's right. Yan's usually right about horrible things." She put the arrow back in her quiver. "That's what makes him horrible."

Ina almost laughed. He didn't, quite.

They stood at the hedge in the cold for a long time, looking at the valley. Neither of them said anything. That was the best thing about Ina. You could stand next to him and not say anything for an hour and it was never once the wrong kind of quiet.

Then Winter said, "I told you something. On floor 9."

Ina didn't move.

"On the bridge to the Youngest," said Winter. "I said some of us know something. About Ice. And it's not mine to tell you. And I'd tell you the day I could." She looked at the snow. "The day. Not the day after."

"I remember."

"It's not the day," said Winter.

"I know."

"But I saw his face. At the grave." She didn't look at him. "When you said the words. He was saying them after you. Under his breath. Like he was learning them." She stopped. "And you looked at him and you knew something. I saw you know it."

Ina was quiet for a long time.

"I didn't know anything," he said at last. "I've said words over a grave once in my life. Two nights ago. For an old man I'd seen in a chair." He looked at his hands, in the sleeves of his coat. "But when I started, it was like going down a stair in the dark in a house you've lived in. Your feet know where the steps are. You don't." He was quiet. "I don't know why. I don't want to know why. Not yet."

"That's the step," said Winter.

"That's the step," said Ina. "There's a lot of us on it now. Hana. Maigo. Calvin, probably. Me." He almost smiled. "It's a nice step. There's room."

"I'm not on it."

"No," said Ina. "You know. You're on the other side of the door." He looked at her. "That's harder, I think. Knowing, and not saying. Being on the inside of it."

Winter didn't say anything for a while.

She'd never thought about it like that. She'd thought about it as a thing she was carrying for Ice, the theory, the thing the thirteen knew that the others didn't. A weight. Something she'd put down the day she could. She'd never once thought that the ones on the step, waiting, not asking, were carrying something too.

"I'm not going to tell you," she said. "Not tonight."

"I know," said Ina. "I didn't ask."

"You never ask."

"No," said Ina. "That's what the step's for."

She looked at him. In the starlight, with the snow behind him and the mask on his shoulder and his face bare and tired and kind.

She didn't like men much, as a rule. She never had. They weren't bad, mostly. They just took up a lot of room, and talked when they didn't need to, and wanted things from you that you hadn't offered. She'd learned to keep a wall up with most of them and not feel bad about it.

Ina had never once taken up more room than he needed.

She sat down. In the snow. At the edge of the Barn Field, by the last hedge, with her back to the Grey and her knees up.

After a moment, Ina sat down beside her.

They sat there on the edge of the snow over nothing, the two of them, not saying anything, with the stars going round, until the cold got too much, and Ina put his bare hand out flat between them on the snow, palm up, and a small white-blue warmth came up off it, not fire, just warm, like a hearth stone. Enough for two people to put their hands near. They put their hands near it.

"Don't tell Calvin," said Winter.

"About what?"

"That I sat down."

"I'd never," said Ina.

***

They went in at midnight, when Thea and Hyvred came to take the door.

The barn was dark. Seven hundred people asleep. The lamps all out but three: one by each door, low, and the one on the hook by the horses at the far end. She went in and along the wall toward her place in the straw, past the sleeping villages, stepping over people, quietly, and she didn't mean to look at the far end.

She looked.

The straw man stood against the end wall in the light of the horse lantern. Tall as the roof. Arms up a hand's width. Head in its hoop, with its crown of poppies gone black. Chest white with frost.

And the frost had gone further.

Up. From the chest. Into the shoulders, into the base of the arms, into the neck of the straw where the hoop sat. White, all of it, sparkling a little in the lantern light the way the snow outside had sparkled, the way nothing else in the barn did. And down. Into the long bound bundle of the body. Into the base of the frame, where the iron uprights went into their sockets in the floor. White. Frost on the iron.

The horses were awake. All of them. Standing pressed against the far side wall as far from the frame as their ropes would let them go, with their eyes showing white and their ears flat back, not making any sound at all.

And the old Lammas woman was on her stool, at his feet. Awake. With her hands in her lap. Looking up.

Winter went down the barn to her.

She didn't know why. Her feet just went. Past the last villages, into the empty forty paces in front of the frame, cold, colder with every step, as if she were walking toward an open door in a storm. Ina came behind her. Not close. A few paces.

The old woman didn't look round.

"He's looking at you," she said.

Winter stopped.

"Not you," said the old woman. "Him. Your one. The one who burned him." She lifted one thin hand out of her lap and pointed. Not at the straw man. Across the barn. Into the middle of the floor, where the thirty-six slept in a long row against the east wall, between two villages, in the straw. "He's been turning toward him since the wedding. A finger a night. Now it's more."

Winter looked up.

The plaited face. The two twisted eyes. She'd looked at it every day for three days and every time it had been turned a little more to the left, and Chris had measured it, a finger, two fingers, three, and she'd thought it was turning toward the thirty-six. Toward all of them. Toward where the passers slept.

It wasn't all of them.

From here, from right under it, at its feet, she could see where the twisted straw eyes were looking. Down the barn. Across it. To a place on the east wall, in the straw, in the dark, where somebody was lying on his side with his back to the wall and his black hair in his eyes and a lamp shield propped beside him and Gabe on one side and an empty space on the other where Thea would be when she came off the door.

It was looking at Ice.

"How do you know," said Winter, "he burned it."

The old woman looked at her for the first time. Her eyes were very pale, and very old, and wet.

"I don't know anything," she said. "I'm an old woman who's plaited a face sixty times." She looked back up at it. "But I know his face. I know every turn of it. And it's never once in sixty years looked like that." She was quiet. "It's looking at him the way you'd look at somebody who'd hurt you. A long time ago. Somewhere you can't remember."

Winter didn't say anything.

She looked up at the plaited face in the lantern light, and the frost spreading down the iron into the floor, and the arms up, and the old woman on her stool, and behind her, Ina, very still.

And she heard it.

Not with her ears. The way she'd heard the Rimed on the walls of the Bastion, coming, before anybody else did. Something in the air, very low, too low to be a sound. Like the creak of a branch under snow, just before it goes.

From the frame.

She looked down. At the base of it. At the iron upright on the left, where it went into its socket in the stone floor of the barn, bolted down, four great iron bolts the size of her thumb that had held the straw man's frame to the floor of the Long Barn for longer than anybody knew.

The frost was thickest there. White fur on the iron. On the bolts.

One of the bolts had moved.

Not much. A hair. Less. She'd never have seen it if she hadn't been kneeling in the snow an hour ago looking at a hole with nothing at the bottom. But she'd been looking at things all night. And she saw it. The head of the bolt, very slightly proud of the iron round it, where it had been flush. And a hairline crack in the frost on the stone round its foot, a fine dark line, running out from the iron across the floor.

The straw man was pulling on its feet.

***

She didn't shout. She didn't wake anybody. There wasn't anything to wake them for. It wasn't walking. It was standing against the end wall with its arms up and its frost spreading and one bolt a hair out of its socket.

She went and woke Chris.

Because Chris would measure it. And because Chris would write it down. And because if you woke Chris, Chris would know who else to wake, and in what order, and how, kindly, the way he asked people the time.

Chris sat up in the straw with his hair everywhere and his notebook already in his hand.

"The bolt," said Winter. Very quietly. "On the left. At the bottom. It's come up."

Chris looked at her.

Then he got up, and found his string, and went down the barn in the dark in his socks to the frame, and knelt at the foot of it, at the left upright, by the old woman on her stool, and looked at the bolt for a long time with the lantern held close.

He wrote it down.

Then he came back. He didn't wake anybody else. He sat down in the straw beside Winter, near the east wall, and looked down the length of the barn at the white shape against the end wall, and then sideways, at Ice, asleep against the wall a few places along, with Gabe's arm across his chest.

"It's looking at him," said Winter.

"I know," said Chris. "I measured it from his place tonight. Before I went to sleep. From the post to the nose, and from the nose to him." He opened the notebook and showed her, in the dark, though she couldn't see it. "It's not turning left. It's turning *to* him. It's been turning to him since the wedding. I just didn't know where to measure from."

Winter looked at Ice.

At his face, asleep. Which was a thing she'd almost never seen. He didn't sleep in front of people much. He hadn't on any floor she could remember, until floor 8, until the door rota and Gabe at the open side. He was asleep now, on his side, with his back to the wall and his hand on the strap of the lamp shield, and his face did nothing. Just a face. Tired. Younger than it was in the day.

*So you don't know if you starved them.*

*No.*

She'd said that to him. At the table. In front of everybody.

She'd been right to. She thought she'd been right to. She'd ask it again. Somebody had to ask it, and she was the one who asked things, and he'd known that and let her.

But she looked at his face, asleep, with a straw man at the end of the barn turning to look at him in the dark, and she thought about thirty-four people in the snow, the first time, with a thing as tall as a roof coming at them, and nobody to tell them what it was. And Ice in front. Because he was always in front, the first time. Everybody knew that. Everybody had worked out that much, even the ones on the step.

And thirty-four people burning it, because it was that or die.

And then going up the stair, and never once coming back down to see.

*Ten years*, she thought. *He's been carrying that for ten years. Whatever it was. And he never even knew it was a thing to carry until an old woman told him on the third day in a barn.*

"Chris," she said.

"Mm."

"Write down that I was right to ask."

Chris looked at her in the dark.

"And write down," said Winter, "that I'm sorry I asked it like that."

Chris didn't say anything for a moment. Then he opened the notebook again and wrote something in it, very small, by feel, in the dark.

"Done," said Chris.
