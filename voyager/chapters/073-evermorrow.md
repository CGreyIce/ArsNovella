He came out of the door at the top of three hundred and twelve steps into grass.

Real grass. Long and green and wet with dew, up to his knees, soaking through his trousers in the first ten steps. He stopped in it. He stood on a hillside in the grey before dawn with the white helm in his hand and the grass soaking him, and breathed.

It smelled of grass. Of wet earth, and something flowering somewhere, sweet and green. Of a river. Of woodsmoke, coming up from below, the morning kind, somebody lighting a stove. After twenty-nine days of glass and white sky and air that smelled of nothing at all, it hit him like a wave. He had to put his free hand on his knee.

"Ice," said Alea, on his shoulder. Very softly. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're gasping. Breathe *properly*."

He breathed properly. In, and out. Wet grass. Woodsmoke. A river.

The sky over him was going pale. Blue. A real blue, deep, with a few long thin clouds in it going pink along their bottoms. Somewhere close, a bird started singing. Then another. Then, all down the hillside, in bushes he couldn't see yet, dozens of them, all at once, the way birds do at the edge of dawn.

He sat down in the wet grass.

He hadn't meant to. His legs did it. He sat in the grass on the hillside with his scarred hand open on his knee and the helm in his lap, and listened to the birds. Below him the valley came up out of the dark as the light grew. Green. A river going through it, silver. Fields. Hedges. Trees. And in the middle of it all, where the river bent, a town.

Red roofs. Chimneys with smoke going straight up. A bridge. A market square, and in the middle of the square a tower. A clocktower, tall and square and old, with a clock face on each side and an open belfry at the top.

*Evermorrow.*

"It's the same," said Alea quietly.

"It's the same."

He'd loved this floor. That was the thing. Of all hundred, if anybody had asked him, the first time, which one was the kindest. And they had, round fires, on far floors, years later, when kind things were a long way behind them. He'd always said seven. Evermorrow. Thirty-four of them had come up out of the Glass burned and thirsty and half mad from the glare. They'd walked down this hill into this valley in the morning and stood in the river up to their waists for an hour without saying anything.

They'd stayed five weeks. Nobody had died. Nobody had even been badly hurt. The boss had been a great bronze thing in the belfry of the clocktower, the Bell-keeper, slow and heavy and patient, and they'd beaten it on the third try without losing anybody. It had been the floor where people had started laughing again, after the Red City.

He sat on the hill and watched the sun come up over Evermorrow.

He went down at full light. The road went down the hill between hedges full of white flowers, and at the bottom it crossed the river on an old stone bridge and went into the town.

It was market day. He remembered that too. The first time they'd come down on a market day, and the whole square had been full. Stalls, and awnings, and people shouting prices, and children underfoot, and a dog stealing sausages. It was full now. He walked into it and stood at the edge of the square and let it go round him. Noise. Ordinary noise. People buying onions and arguing about cheese.

He'd forgotten what an ordinary morning sounded like.

He bought a shield first. He needed one. The smith had a stall at the corner of the square, a big red-faced man with a leather apron, and on the wall behind him there was a round shield of oak and iron. Plain. Solid. Better than a barrel lid by a long way.

"Best in the valley," said the smith. "Twenty silver."

He paid it. He didn't haggle. He buckled it onto his left arm, over the empty straps where the Vigil Shield had been. It sat better than Otta's had. Not like the chapel shield. Nothing would ever be like the chapel shield. But it sat.

"It's not a barrel lid," said Alea.

"It's not a barrel lid."

"I'm almost sad."

Then he went to Lantern Street.

He hadn't decided to. His feet took him there. Out of the square on the east side, past the well, down a narrow street of tall leaning houses with their upper floors nearly touching across the top. To a little shop halfway down, with a blue door and a bow window full of bread. Loaves and rolls and plaits, golden and brown. And a smell coming out of the open door that made his knees go again.

*Rosemary.*

He stood outside the blue door with his hand on the frame.

The first time, he'd come here every morning for five weeks. The first morning by accident, following his nose. Every morning after that on purpose. The baker had been a woman about his age, with flour up to her elbows and dark hair tied up in a cloth and freckles, and a way of talking to you while she did six other things. She'd had a rosemary loaf. He'd bought it every morning and sat on the edge of the fountain in the square and eaten the whole thing at once. And she'd laughed at him for it every morning when he came back for another one.

Her name was Merle. He'd never told anybody that he remembered her name.

He pushed the door open.

***

She was behind the counter, with flour on her arms and her hair tied up and her back to him, pulling a tray of rolls out of the oven. She turned round with the tray in both hands when the bell over the door went.

And stopped.

It was a small stop. Half a second. She looked at him. At the white cloak, the new shield, the helm under his arm. At his face. And something went across her face that he didn't understand. Something careful. Like somebody looking at a word they thought they knew and finding it spelled wrong.

Then it was gone, and she put the tray down.

"Morning," she said. "You're new."

"I'm new," said Ice.

"Thought so. I know everybody." She wiped her hands on her apron. Her eyes went over him again, quickly, as if she were checking something. "What'll it be?"

"Rosemary loaf."

She looked at him a moment longer.

"Course it is," she said, and turned to get it.

He paid. She wrapped it in paper. Her hands were quick and floury and sure, the way they'd always been. She didn't say anything else. Neither did he. He couldn't. He took the loaf and went out of the blue door, and down Lantern Street to the square, and sat down on the edge of the fountain in the morning sun. And ate the whole thing at once, the way he always had.

It was exactly the same. Exactly. Warm and dense and salty, with the rosemary in it, and the crust that cracked.

He sat on the edge of the fountain and cried a little, with his mouth full, and didn't care who saw.

***

It rained at three.

A short sharp shower, out of a sky that had been clear all day. It came across the valley from the west in a long grey curtain. He watched it come from the steps of the old church on the corner of the square, out of the way. It lasted about ten minutes. Everybody in the market put their awnings up at once and swore and took them down again after, and the square smelled of wet stone and wet cabbage.

He remembered that too. Not this one. Just rain. The first time, on seven, it had rained most afternoons, a little. Ordinary weather. He'd loved it.

And at dusk, the clocktower rang.

He was on the church steps when it happened. Eating an apple from a stall, watching the market pack up in the gold light. The bell rang the hour. Six. One deep slow note, rolling out over the square and the red roofs and the river. Then another. He'd heard it ring every hour, all day, without thinking about it.

Six strokes. The same bell. One bell.

He looked up at the tower. At the belfry, the open arches at the top, with the low gold sun shining straight into them from the west.

And stopped chewing.

There were bells in the belfry. He could see them now, with the sun behind them, in silhouette. Hanging in rows from the beams, under the roof. Not one. *Rows* of them. Big ones and small ones, crowded together, dark shapes against the gold. Some of them moving very slightly in the evening wind. Just hanging there, silent, round the one great bell in the middle that had just rung six.

He counted. He couldn't help it. He always counted.

Thirty-six.

"Alea," he said.

"I see them."

"There was one. The first time. One bell. The Bell-keeper rang it on the hour. That's all there was."

"I know," said the fox. Her voice had gone very quiet. "I've got it logged. One bell." She was looking up at the belfry with her two white pinprick eyes. "That's new, Ice."

He stood on the church steps with the apple in his hand and looked at thirty-six bells hanging in the last of the light.

*Thirty-six.*

"Huh," he said. "Weird number."

It came out of him before he'd thought. Something Deston had said, on floor 1, at the top of a stair, about a hundred and twelve steps. *Huh. Weird number.* He didn't know why it came out now.

Alea didn't laugh.

***

He slept in a hayloft at the edge of town, the way he'd slept the first time, the first night. The farmer let him for a copper. He lay in the hay with the new shield beside him and listened to the town go quiet, and the river, and an owl. Real night. Real dark. Stars through a gap in the boards.

At midnight the clock struck twelve. He counted, half asleep.

And on the twelfth stroke, something happened that he didn't understand.

The world *moved*.

That was the only word for it, afterwards. He was lying in the hay with his eyes half shut, and the twelfth stroke rang. And everything lurched. Not the hayloft. Not him. Everything *else*. The dark through the gap in the boards went light. Not slowly. All at once, between one breath and the next, like somebody had pulled back a curtain. The stars went out. The sky went grey. Then pale. Then gold. And birds were singing, all down the hillside, the way birds do at the edge of dawn.

He sat up in the hay.

It was morning.

He'd heard twelve strokes, and it was morning. No night. No hours between. Midnight, and then dawn, as if someone had cut out the middle and stitched the ends together.

"*Alea*," he said.

"I felt it." She was on his chest, very small, very bright. "Ice. I felt it. I don't—I've never—"

"What happened?"

"I don't *know*."

He went down the ladder and out of the barn into the morning. Wet grass. Birds. The smell of woodsmoke coming up from the town, the morning kind, somebody lighting a stove. Exactly like yesterday.

*Exactly* like yesterday.

***

He knew by the time he got to the square. He'd known, really, on the road. The same hedges, white with flowers. The same mist on the river. The same cart coming over the bridge with the same load of cabbages and the same man walking beside it whistling the same tune, off-key in the same place.

The market was setting up. The same stalls in the same places. The same arguments about the same cheese. And at the corner of the square, the same dog, brown and white with one ear up, slid out from under a table and took a sausage off the butcher's stall and ran. The butcher shouted the same thing after it, the same word, in the same voice.

Ice stood at the edge of the square and watched it happen.

He'd seen that dog do that yesterday. That exact sausage. That exact word.

He went to the smith.

The red-faced man was at his stall, in his leather apron, setting out his wares. And on the wall behind him, in the same place, there was a round shield of oak and iron.

The same shield. Ice looked down at his own arm. At the round shield of oak and iron he'd bought yesterday, buckled on over the empty straps. Then up at the one on the wall. The same shape. The same grain in the wood. The same little dent in the iron rim, low on the left.

"Shield?" said the smith. He'd seen Ice looking. He nodded at the one on the wall. "Best in the valley. Twenty silver."

"I bought it yesterday," said Ice.

The smith looked at him. Then at the shield on Ice's arm. Then at the one on the wall. His face went puzzled, and then polite, the way people's faces go when somebody says something that doesn't make sense and they've decided not to mention it.

"That's a fine shield you've got," he said. "Didn't get it from me, though. I'd remember." He smiled. "Twenty silver, if you want a spare."

Ice stood there.

"It's the same day," said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. "Ice. It's the same *day*."

***

He went to Lantern Street.

He didn't run. He walked. He walked down the narrow street between the leaning houses with his heart going so hard he could feel it in his throat. He stopped outside the blue door with the bow window full of bread. The same loaves. The same plaits. The same smell.

He pushed the door open. The bell went.

She was behind the counter, with flour on her arms and her hair tied up and her back to him, pulling a tray of rolls out of the oven. She turned round with the tray in both hands when the bell went.

And looked at him.

She didn't stop, this time. She didn't do the careful thing. She just looked at him, standing in her doorway in the white cloak with the shield on his arm, for a long moment. And then she put the tray down on the counter very slowly, and wiped her hands on her apron, and didn't take her eyes off his face.

"You came in yesterday," said Merle.

Ice didn't move.

"You bought the rosemary loaf," she said. Her voice was very steady. The kind of steady you hold on to with both hands. "You didn't say anything. You took it and went out and sat on the edge of the fountain and ate the whole thing at once. I watched you through the window." She swallowed. "You cried a bit. With your mouth full."

He couldn't say anything.

"Nobody comes in twice," said Merle.

Her hands had gone still on the apron.

"Nobody," she said. "Do you understand? Nobody's come in twice. In a hundred and twenty-six days." Her voice cracked very slightly, and she held on to it. "Every morning, it's this morning. Every morning I pull these rolls out of this oven and that bell goes. And it's the same people, buying the same bread, saying the same things. Mrs Penn and her knee. The Dawley boy and his penny. And not one of them has ever, *ever* remembered that they were here yesterday." She looked at him. "And you walk in. Twice. And you look at me like you know exactly what day it is."

Ice stood in the doorway of the bakery on Lantern Street with the bell still trembling over his head.

"A hundred and twenty-six days," he said.

"A hundred and twenty-six." She didn't look away. "Since a morning in the spring. I woke up and it was market day. And then I woke up and it was market day again. And it's been market day ever since." Her mouth twisted. "I know the dog's going to take the sausage. I know it's going to rain at three. I know Mrs Penn's going to tell me about her knee at ten past nine, and I know exactly what I'm going to say back, because I've said it a hundred and twenty-six times." She stopped. "I stopped telling people. About the fortieth day. They thought I was ill. Then they thought I was mad. Then they forgot, the next morning, and I had to start again." She looked at him. "So I stopped."

*A morning in the spring. A hundred and twenty-six days.*

Ice did the sum. He didn't need to. Alea had done it already. He felt her go very still and very cold on his shoulder.

*Day one.*

The morning the thirty-six of them had woken in the white room. The morning he'd opened his eyes a minute before everybody else and run.

That was the morning Evermorrow had started to repeat.

"Do you believe me?" said Merle.

Her voice was very small, suddenly. All the steadiness gone out of it. A woman with flour on her arms, standing behind a counter in a bakery on a street she'd walked a hundred and twenty-six times, asking the first person in four months who'd walked in twice.

Ice looked at her.

He thought about a white room, and a minute, and thirty-five sleeping faces. About ten years, and a hundred floors, and a room at the top where everybody he loved had died. About a fox with a soul and a tower that had asked him if he wanted to go home. About standing in front of Mother Crane on the first morning with his heart in his mouth, and her not knowing him. About Hessa, looking at him like a stranger. About Nan Gully's song. About every single person on every single floor of this tower who'd looked at him and not known what he'd seen.

About being the only one who remembered.

"Yes," he said. "I believe you."

Merle looked at him for a long time.

Then she sat down. Right there, on the floor, behind the counter, with her back against the oven and her floury hands in her lap. And she put her face in her hands. And cried, for the first time, Ice thought, in a hundred and twenty-six days. Like somebody setting down something very heavy that they'd been holding up so long they'd forgotten they had arms.

He went round the counter, and sat down on the floor beside her, with the new shield on his arm and the rosemary smell all round them, and didn't say anything at all.

On his shoulder, Alea had gone so still she was barely there.

"Ice," she said. In his head. Very small. "Ice. *Look* at her."

He was looking at her.

"That's—" Alea stopped. "That's what you look like," she said. "From the outside. That's what you've looked like. For a hundred and twenty-six days."

He didn't answer. He sat on the floor of the bakery beside a woman who remembered, and let her cry. Outside, through the bow window, the brown and white dog went past with a sausage in its mouth, exactly on time.
