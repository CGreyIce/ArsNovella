They carried him all the way down the hill.

He told them he could walk. By the time they came out of the great doors of the Assize into the square, into the late afternoon, with the whole of Tell pouring out round them, he could. The grey was gone. His legs were his. He could feel every finger. He told Gabe, and Gabe said *I know*, and didn't put him down.

So they carried him. On Hearthwall. Down the Assize steps, and across the square, and down the Wellgate. Past Aldo's row of narrow houses. Past the third wall and the second. Past the Countinghouse, with its plain grey door shut. Past the Tellhouse, where Askew stood on the top step with her stick and a bundle of slates in her arms, and watched them go by, and didn't say anything at all. Down every flight of steps and every slope, in the gold light, with half of Tell walking alongside, and the other half at their windows.

He lay on his back on the warm iron and looked up at the sky over Tell going gold, and let them.

Calvin talked the whole way. Nobody told him to stop.

***

They put him down in the copy-room yard, by the pump. Gabe set the shield down on the stones as if it were full of water, and stood up, and his legs went, and he sat down very suddenly next to it, and laughed.

"Twenty-three years," said Gabe, to the sky. "Twenty-three years I've been trying to carry *something* of yours. Anything. Your bag. Your coat. Your—" He put his hands over his face. "You let me carry the whole *you*."

Ice sat up on Hearthwall. Slowly. His back was warm all the way through.

"You carried me," he said. "All of you."

"*Three metres*," said Calvin, from the trough, where he'd collapsed. "It was *three metres*, Dec. It felt like a *floor*."

"It was a floor," said Ice.

Nobody said anything to that. They sat round the yard in the gold light, all thirty-five of them, on the stones and the trough and the steps, with their legs not quite working and their hands not quite steady, and looked at him sitting up on the shield. And he looked back.

He'd never once in either life sat in the middle of them and let them look.

He did now.

***

Rachael's hands were pink by dusk.

She sat on her stool by the pump with them palm up on her knees, the way she'd sat with them on floor 10, after the fifth wall, and looked at them. White to the elbow, in the great hall, with the grey. And then flat on Hearthwall's boss, under his back, with the warmth running into them, the whole way down the hill.

He sat down on the trough beside her.

"How are they?"

"Pink." She flexed them. "It was the same as the Rimed. The cold. Except it wasn't cold. It was—" She stopped. "It was like being crossed out. Like somebody running a line through you. I felt it go up my fingers and I thought, *this is what the clerks feel*. All the time. A line, going up."

He didn't say anything.

"It's gone now," said Rachael. "Hearthwall. It doesn't go cold." She looked at her pink hands. "I keep finding out what I can carry."

They sat there.

"Is it safe yet?" said Rachael.

He knew what she meant. He'd known since she sat down. The roof, forty days ago, with her shoulder against his. *Not yet. Not while you're wearing that. Not while something up there is looking at Thea.*

He looked at his left wrist. At the place where the cord had been. There was a faint grey line round it, like a mark where a bracelet's been.

"No," he said. Honestly. "Nearly."

Rachael looked at him for a long time.

"Okay," she said. "Nearly's good." She put her pink hands back on her knees. "I'll ask when it's not nearly."

"I know."

"I'm not a hugger."

"I know."

She leaned her shoulder against his. Just for a second. Then took it away.

***

Tell came to the copy-room that night.

Not all of it. But a lot. They came through the yard gate in ones and twos after dark, with lamps, and didn't come in, mostly. They stood in the gate and looked at the climbers sitting round the yard, and said something, or didn't, and went away. Nell from the half-door, with a basket of buns, which she gave to Tiger without a word. The knife-grinder, who shook Rui Xiang's hand. Wat and Edith, arm in arm. Wat looked at Ice for a long time and said, "Never been any trouble," as if it were a verdict, and Edith hit him.

Joan Tull came with a key.

She came in through the gate and walked straight across the yard to where Tiger was sitting on the stable step with Nell's basket of buns, and held it out. A big iron key, old, on a loop of green ribbon.

"The shop," she said. "With the green shutters. I opened them this afternoon. First time in four winters." She was crying and didn't seem to have noticed. "I want you to have a key. All of you. Any of you. Whenever you're in Tell."

Tiger looked at the key. Then at her.

Then he reached into his shirt and took out an apple.

It was very wrinkled now. Fifty days. Brown at the stalk. Soft. He'd carried it every day since the Lantern Market, since the first morning, when a woman had stood at the front of a crowd that was stepping back and decided, all the way down, to hold one out to him.

He looked at it for a long moment.

And then he ate it.

All of it. Slowly. Core and all. Sitting on the stable step in the lamplight, with Joan Tull standing in front of him crying and the whole copy-room watching. He ate it the way you'd eat something you'd been saving for exactly the right time.

When he'd finished, he took the key.

"Thank you," said Tiger, in the smallest voice Ice had ever heard him use.

Joan laughed. Wet. And went back out of the gate.

"Hamo," said Hawise, later, from the step, when somebody asked. She'd come down with Alys an hour after dark and sat on the edge of the trough with her cousin beside her, in grey, with no book, holding her hand. "Hamo Tull. The grey got him too. In the hall. When it came across the floor. He was on the front bench, and it came up his legs, and he couldn't move." She was quiet. "And nobody said his name."

The yard was very quiet.

"Joan did," said Hawise. "In the end. She was at the table with me, and she looked round, and saw him, grey to the chest. And she said *Hamo*. Out loud. Like you'd say it across a kitchen." She looked at her hands. "And it stopped."

"She saved him," said Calvin. Slowly. "The man who stole her shop."

"She chose to," said Hawise. "That's what Tell is." She looked at Alys, beside her. "That's what Tell's always been. We just forgot, for a while."

Alys didn't say anything. She hadn't said much all evening. She sat on the trough in her grey robe with her hand in Hawise's and her eyes going slowly round the yard, at the lamps and the faces and the shield on the stones, the way you'd look round a room you'd been in once as a child.

She'd remembered her name. And Hawise's. And that she'd wanted, once, more than anything, to be a teller. She didn't remember much else yet. Hawise said that was all right. Hawise said there was time.

"Askew says she can come back to the Tellhouse," said Hawise. "To the door-roll. When she's ready. Beside Orlo." She looked at Cursiffe, sitting against the wall with his VOYAGER book on his knee. "Somebody's got to be the second hand. Now we're losing one."

Cursiffe looked up.

"Water Street," he said. "I haven't finished—"

"Orlo will finish it," said Hawise. "He's been practising. You taught him." She almost smiled. "And you can't stay. You know you can't. You've got a stair to climb."

Cursiffe looked at her for a long time. Then down, at the iron key on its cord round his neck. Askew's key.

"She said *till it's told*," he said.

"It's told," said Hawise. "You told it."

***

The Countinghouse door stayed shut.

He watched it from the roof, the next night. The clerks had gone back in at dusk. He'd counted them. A hundred and eleven. Every one of them coming up the Wellgate from every corner, up the four plain steps, through the door that opened for them.

But they'd walked differently. He'd watched. Not much. But their heads had been up. Not down. And at the top of the steps, one or two of them had stopped. Just for a second. And looked back. Down the Wellgate. At the city.

Then gone in.

"Do you think it's over?" said Thea, beside him. "For them?"

"No," said Ice. "I don't think it's over for anybody."

"But it's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," he agreed.

She had the lantern on her knees. The light inside it was steady. Leaning toward him, the way it always did now. But not lying flat against the glass. Just leaning. The way a plant leans toward a window it knows is there.

"The book," said Thea. "In there. On the lectern. It's got three hands on it now. On the left. Mine. Yours. And the Count's." She looked across the road. "*Witnessed.*"

"I know."

"It'll be there. Forever. Whatever happens. Whatever floor we're on. Whatever the tower does." She was quiet. "The first year. Thirty-four. All of them. In Aldo's hand. Told."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"You said them," he said. "In the hall. All of them. While they were carrying me."

"Yes."

"In order. The left page."

"Yes."

"You've never done that. Said them all. Together." He looked at her. "I've only ever given you one a night."

Thea looked at the lantern.

"I know," she said. "And I'll still take one a night. Every name. Not tonight." She looked up at him. "I just wanted them to hear it once. All together. Out loud. In a room full of people saying their names." She was quiet. "So they knew they were there too. On the left. While everybody said the right."

He put his hand over hers, on the lantern.

"And the thirty-four who were there before," he said.

Thea's hand went still under his.

"You heard that."

"I heard it."

"I said it very quietly."

"I was very close," said Ice.

She laughed. Short. Wet. And put her head on his shoulder, there on the lead roof, with the lantern between them, and the Countinghouse across the road, and the whole of Tell going to sleep below them, ring below ring, light by light.

***

He went up to Aldo on the last morning.

On his own. Not to the bottom of the steps. All the way up. To the green door with the paint coming off in curls, and the rush chair, and the old man in it with the rug and the writing-board and his bright bird's eyes, who'd watched him come up the last flight the whole way.

"Seven days," said Aldo.

"Yes."

"Better than forty-six."

"Yes." Ice sat down on the stool. Davy had put it out. "We're going up. Today. The stair under the Assize. It opened. Behind where the screen was. A door."

"I know. I heard." Aldo looked at him. "The whole of Tell knows. There's a crowd in the square already. They're going to watch you go." He paused. "Nobody watched the thirty go. It's in the Rolls. They went up in the autumn, and the Rolls just say *went up*. Nobody came."

"I know."

"Well." Aldo moved his knotted hands on the rug. "This time they're coming. I'm coming. Davy's going to push me. I don't run any more, but I can be pushed." He looked at Ice. "I'll be at the door. On your left."

Ice looked at him for a long time.

He thought about an old man running all the way down a hill, a long time ago, in an autumn that had happened somewhere else. Crying. *Climbers. In my life.* And bringing honey. And a book of the Rolls to show them their names. Every evening for seven weeks. Asking nothing.

And waving them off, at the door under the Assize, on the last morning. He'd forgotten that. He'd forgotten it completely until now. Aldo at the door, on his own, in the cold, waving a handkerchief. The last person in Tell who'd seen them. The only one.

He'd never told anybody. He'd never once thought about it in ten years.

"You waved," said Ice.

Aldo looked at him.

"Somebody waved," said Ice. "At the door. A long time ago. With a handkerchief. In the cold." He looked at his hands. "He was the only one there."

Aldo didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he reached into the pocket of his coat, under the rug, and took out a large white handkerchief. Folded. Very clean. And held it up.

"I bought a new one," he said. "Yesterday. In case."

***

The whole of Tell came to the Assize to watch them go.

The square was full again. Not the way it had been for the Reckoning, with everybody quiet and their account books under their arms. Noisy. Loud. Children on shoulders. People on the window ledges of the narrow houses. The Low Town in their market clothes, and the third ring and the fourth, some of them, with their shutters open for the first time in fifty days. Nell. Bertha, floury. The knife-grinder. The lamplighter. Rafe Collier, standing at the back with his arms folded, who didn't say anything and didn't leave.

The door was at the back of the great hall. Where the screen had been. The screen was still there, carved floor to ceiling, with the new line along the bottom, pale against the dark. But at its middle, where there'd never been a gap, there was a door now. Plain. Wooden. Round-topped. Open. With a stair behind it, going up.

Of course it went up.

Askew was at the door, with her stick. Hawise beside her, and Orlo, and Alys in grey with no book. Ivo Rook. Joan, with the shop key on its green ribbon round her own neck now, because Tiger had given it back to her and said *keep it for us*, and she'd cried again.

And Aldo, in his rush chair, on the left of the door. With Davy behind him. And a new white handkerchief in his lap.

They went up the way they always went up. Drew's order. Higu with the rock under her arm, front side up, two columns and no line. Tiger first, because Tiger was always first now, and waited at the top. Then the parties. Hana's, with Hana's crown on, and Milli and Jared and Aleaka and Newish. Chris's. Yan's, with Cursiffe last, slow, with an iron key round his neck that Askew had told him to keep anyway. Xav's. Hyvred's. Deston's, with Calvin talking.

Thea's. With Thea.

And him. In the middle.

And Gabe, last. With Hearthwall.

He stopped at the door.

Askew looked at him. Up and down. The way she'd looked at Cursiffe across her desk, every morning, saying *good morning, no*.

"Told," she said.

"Told," said Ice.

He looked at Hawise. At Alys beside her, who looked back at him with Hawise's eyes and something moving in her face, a long way down, like weather. At Orlo, very tall, with ink on his nose. At Joan with her key.

At Aldo.

Aldo didn't say anything. He sat in his chair on the left of the door with his writing-board on his knees and looked up at Ice with his bright bird's eyes, and lifted the handkerchief.

And waved.

Not much. Just once. A small white flag, in the warm air at the top of the hill.

Ice went through the door.

***

The stair went up.

Narrow and stone and round, the way it always did. Up into the dark, with a cool air coming down it from somewhere a long way above. Behind him, through the door, the noise of the square. The whole of Tell, shouting. Names. He could hear them. *Calvin. Tiger. The big one. The one with the lantern.* Getting fainter as he climbed. And under them, very faint, high and thin, children.

*Thirty up the well and thirty up the stair—*

He climbed.

They counted. The way they always counted, since the first stair. Newish started it, under her breath, and then everybody. *One. Two.* Thirty-six people counting out loud on a stone stair going up in the dark, their voices going ahead of them and coming back.

He climbed in the middle.

Thea a step above him, with the lantern. Gabe a step below, with Hearthwall. He could hear them both counting. He could hear Calvin, a long way up, counting louder than anybody. Hyvred in capitals. Maigo a beat behind. Higu, very precise. Drew, saying *somebody thank me* at the hundreds.

And somewhere round the two hundreds, in the dark, behind him, under the counting, very low, he heard Gabe start to hum.

He knew it. Of course he knew it. He'd known it since he was thirteen.

He'd stood in the second row of a choir, once, at thirteen, a long time ago, in another world. With Deston beside him. And he'd sung it flat on the eights, every single time, and never once fixed it.

Gabe was humming it.

And above him, a few steps up, he heard Deston pick it up. Low. Under the counting. The way he'd picked it up in the second row.

And then he heard himself.

He hadn't decided to. It came out of him on the stair in the dark, between *two hundred and forty* and *two hundred and forty-one*. The tune. Not loud. Just there. Under the counting, with Gabe's humming below him and Deston's above. Flat on the eights.

He didn't fix it.

Thea, a step above him, turned her head. He couldn't see her face. He could see the lantern. The light in it, leaning back toward him down the stair.

She didn't say anything. She just started to sing it too. Quietly. A line behind. Getting the eights right.

And then, round the next turn of the stair, somebody else. And somebody else. People who didn't know the words, humming. People who didn't know the tune, counting louder to cover it. Calvin, a long way up, singing completely the wrong song at the top of his voice, and Rachael telling him to shut up.

He climbed in the middle of all of it.

He wasn't in front. He hadn't been in front for a long time. He wasn't a floor ahead, or a step ahead, or alone on a ledge in the wind with a door shut behind him. He was in the middle of a stair full of people, singing flat, with somebody on his left and somebody on his right and somebody who knew, a step above him, with the light.

In the second row.

*Four hundred and twenty-two.*

*Four hundred and twenty-three.*

*Four hundred and twenty-four.*

And the top. A landing. A door. The way it always was.

Tiger was at it. Of course he was. With his hand flat on the wood, and his tail round his ankle, waiting. He waited until the last of them came up the last turn into the faint light under the door. Gabe, last, breathing hard, with Hearthwall.

Then he looked at Higu.

Higu looked at the rock. Two columns, side by side, with no line between them. She looked at Ice.

"Go on," she said. "Tell me what you remember."

He looked at the door.

"I'll tell you what I remember," he said. "And then you can tell me what's true."

"Deal," said Higu.

Tiger opened the door.

And the light came in.
