He cooked before dawn, behind Nan Gully's hut, in the dark, with the kites humming on the roofs overhead.

Salt fish, flaked. Two gull eggs, beaten. And a handful of a small grey-green herb that grew in the cracks of the Kitecliff and nowhere else, which Pell had given him on the last evening without saying what it was for. It smelled of rain and pepper. He'd tasted a leaf of it raw, and his feet had felt strange, as if they knew where the ground was going to be before he put them down. He cooked it into the eggs with the fish, in his one pan, over a fire the size of his hand. When it was done he ate it standing up, and read the window.

[ Kitecliff eggs · AGI +3 · Sure-footed · 4 hours ]

"Sure-footed," said Alea, from his shoulder. "Pell knew."

"Pell knows everything. Pell just doesn't say any of it."

"I like Pell," said the fox. "Pell's like me."

He laughed. It was the first time he'd laughed in a while. He washed the pan, strapped Otta's barrel lid onto his arm, and went out across the dark bridges toward the Spindle with the sky going grey in the east.

***

He reached the crown as the sun came up.

He'd timed it. He didn't know why, exactly. He just wanted to be on the crown when it came up, the real one, out of the cloud at the edge of the world. So he came up over the top of the spiral path onto the bare flat rock just as the first gold edge came over the horizon. The whole Reaches lit up round him, island after island catching fire. And there, off to the north, coming round the needle on its endless circle, the Squall.

It saw him. He watched its head turn.

He walked out into the middle of the crown.

He didn't brace. That was the first thing, and the hardest. Every part of him wanted to plant his feet and put his shoulders forward and get the shield up and *hold*, the way he'd held in front of everything for ten years. He felt it in his knees, his back, his arms. The old shape of himself, wanting to stand like a wall.

He didn't. He stood in the middle of the crown with his feet on the rock, and let the wind have him.

It came from the west, hard, and he leaned into it. It dropped, and he felt it go half a heartbeat before it went, and came back with it. It came from the south, gusting, and he went with it a little, and back. His body moved the whole time, small, constant, never stopping. His feet barely moved at all. A kite on a line. A boat on water.

The Squall's shadow went over him.

It didn't stop.

"*It's not stooping*," whispered Alea.

"Not yet."

It came round again. Lower. He felt its wind before it got there, a great push of air coming ahead of it round the needle, and he leaned on that too. It went over him, close, the feathers of its belly a grey blur a few metres above his head, and on. It hadn't stooped. It couldn't. There was nothing still on the crown for it to choose.

It came round a third time, lower still. On the fourth, it came in so low that its wingtip went over the crown at head height, and he leaned away from it, and it missed him by an arm's length. And as it went past, its legs were *there*. Hanging under it, the great scaled feet, the talons half-curled. In reach.

He cut.

He didn't plant his feet to do it. He let the wind of its passing take his weight. He went with it, sideways and up, and the sword went out at the end of his arm in a long flat arc into the back of its right leg, just above the scaled foot. He felt it bite. He felt it go through feather and skin and into something tough beneath. Then the Squall was past and climbing, screaming, and something dark and warm was spattering down onto the rock in a line across the crown.

Blood. Real blood. It hit the stone in fat red drops and didn't go anywhere.

"*You hit it*," said Alea.

"I hit it."

"You hit it and you're still moving—"

"I know. Don't talk. I'm listening."

He was. Not to her. To the wind. To where it was coming from and where it was going and what it was going to do next. *Stop thinking about what it's going to do. Feel what it's doing.* The Squall went up and round the needle, screaming, and he could feel its anger in the air, the wind of it coming round the rock in hard unsteady bursts. It came back low. He leaned. He cut again as it passed: the same leg, higher, a long gash through the feathers of the thigh. It screamed.

It went up.

And didn't come round.

***

He felt the change before he saw it.

The wind stopped coming round the needle. It started coming *down*.

He looked up. The Squall was directly above the crown, high, its great wings spread wide, hanging there in the morning sky. It wasn't circling. It was beating its wings. Huge slow strokes, each one longer than a house. And with each stroke a column of air came down onto the crown like a hand pressing on a table.

It hit him from straight above. There was no leaning on it. You couldn't lean on something that came from straight above. It pushed him down, down, his knees bending, his shoulders going down. He couldn't sway. He couldn't move with it. It was just weight, pressing down, harder with every stroke. It was pinning him to the rock, as still as a stone.

*Still.*

"*Ice*," said Alea. "*Ice, it's making you still, it's—*"

He knew. He felt it. Pressed down onto one knee in the middle of the crown, held there by the wind of its wings like a mouse under a hand. Holding still, because there was nothing else he could do. And above him, the Squall stopped beating its wings, and folded them.

It came down.

He'd seen it stoop on the seventy-first day. He'd thought nothing in the world could be faster. He'd been wrong. It came down out of the sky like a thrown spear, a grey streak, so fast the air screamed round it, talons out, straight at the one still thing on the crown.

He waited.

He didn't move. Every part of him was screaming to move, to roll, to get the shield up, to run. He held still, in the middle of the crown, pressed down on one knee. And watched it come, and waited. *Wait. Wait. Then move once.* Xav, at seventeen, in a game, at three in the morning: *Wait. Then move once. It's enough.* Declan, laughing, going through the door anyway.

*The first time, it pulled out.* Forty-one stoops, and it had pulled out at the bottom of every one, and climbed. It had never committed. It had never landed. Because pulling out was safe.

*This time it lands.* Because something had watched him stand still in a fire, and given it a rule. And a thing that lands has to land *somewhere*.

At the last instant, when the talons were three metres away and the wind of it was flattening him to the rock, he moved once.

The down-draft had stopped when it folded its wings. The pressure was gone, all at once, the way a gust drops. He felt it go, half a heartbeat before it went, the way Pell had taught him. And he went with the drop. Sideways. A long, low lean, the whole of his weight thrown out to the left along the line where the wind was going. Not a roll. Not a dodge. He didn't plant a foot to push off. He just stopped being where he'd been, the way a kite stops being where it was when the line goes slack.

The Squall hit the crown where he'd been.

It hit it with everything it had. All the speed of the stoop, all the weight of a bird the size of a barn, talons first, straight into solid rock. The whole crown jumped. Stone came up in a fountain of chips and dust. And for one heartbeat the Squall was on the ground, legs folded under it, wings flung out, its head jarred sideways by the shock of its own landing.

Stunned. On the rock. Right there.

He was already moving.

He came in low, round behind it, along the line of its right wing. Its right leg was in front of him: the leg he'd cut twice, the scaled foot clenched on the rock, blood running down the back of it in a dark sheet. He found the place he'd cut first, just above the foot, where the great tendon ran. And he put the sword through it.

He felt it part.

The Squall screamed. It wasn't like the other screams. It was high and cracked and terrible, the scream of something that had never once in its life been hurt, finding out. It tried to stand. Its left leg pushed. Its right leg didn't. It lurched sideways, one wing slamming down onto the rock to catch itself. It tried to beat its wings and go up, the way it always went up. And it couldn't. A bird has to push off. It had one leg to push with, and a crown of bare rock under it, and its wings beat and beat and it rose a metre and came down crooked.

The wind of its wings hit him like a wall. He leaned on it. He went with it, and back, and didn't fall.

"*Its leg*—" Alea was shouting. "*It can't take off, Ice, it can't—*"

"It'll try the edge."

It did. It started dragging itself across the crown toward the drop. Hopping on its good leg, wings half open, beating, every beat throwing a gust across the rock. If it got to the edge it could fall off into the air and open its wings and be gone. And it would come round again, and again, until it was healed, and every time it would be angrier.

He got in front of it.

He didn't stand there. He couldn't stand there. Nothing stood in front of that thing and lived. But he was there, between it and the edge, moving, swaying in the wild wind of its wings, never still. And the Squall stopped dragging itself toward the edge, and looked at him.

One yellow eye. The size of a plate. Looking at him the way it had looked into the crack on the seventy-fourth day. A hawk, deciding.

It struck.

The beak came at him like a pickaxe. He got the barrel lid up. The beak hit it dead centre. The tarred oak split from rim to rim with a crack like a gunshot, and the chain-iron bands sprang loose. The beak came on through the split, and the edge of it went across his forearm, and he felt it open the skin from wrist to elbow.

He didn't brace. He went with the blow, down and sideways, under the head.

And he was under its jaw.

*PSA*, said Calvin, in World, on the first night, eighty days ago, in capitals. *STAB THE SHEEP UNDER THE JAW.*

He drove the sword up.

Under the hooked yellow beak, where the feathers of the throat were thin and grey and there was no bone at all. Up and in, with all his weight behind it, the way you'd drive a post into soft ground. It went in to the hilt.

The Squall went very still.

It stood there on the crown of the Spindle on one leg, with its great wings half open, and the sword in its throat, and the morning sun on its rain-coloured feathers. And it looked at him. Just for a moment. Not like a monster. Not like a picture. Like something that had owned the whole sky for longer than anyone could remember, and had just found out it didn't.

Then it folded down onto the rock, slowly, wings first, and lay there.

He sat down on the crown beside it.

He didn't mean to. His legs just stopped. He sat down hard on the rock with his back against the Squall's great grey flank, which was still warm, and put his head back against the feathers, and breathed. His forearm was open from wrist to elbow and bleeding into his lap. The ember was already working on it. He let it. The barrel lid lay in two halves on the rock in front of him.

"Twelve silver," he said.

Alea made a noise that might have been a laugh. She came and sat on his knee, small and teal and white in the morning sun, and looked at his arm, and then at his face.

"It landed," she said. "On purpose. It *made* you still so it could land on you. And then it landed on nothing."

"Yeah."

"The first time, it pulled out. Every time. And you couldn't touch it, except its legs, for a second, at the bottom." Her voice was strange. "And it learned. It learned to land. Because of the Kiln. Because you stood still in the fire." She was quiet. "And landing's what killed it."

He thought about that. About a stone room on floor 4, and a voice saying *and you*. About a bird that had been made, sometime in the last three weeks, to land on whoever held still.

"It learned what I did," he said slowly. "The first time. Standing. Bracing. Taking it." He looked at the broken barrel lid. "It didn't learn what I'd do."

"Because you didn't know yet."

"Because I didn't know yet." He looked at his forearm, closing slowly under the ember. "Pell knew. Nan Gully knew. *Doors come off.*" He breathed out. "It's been watching me be a wall for eighty days. So it made itself into something that kills walls."

"So don't be a wall," said Alea.

"So don't be a wall."

The fox looked at him for a long time.

"I don't know what you are, then," she said. "If you're not a wall. You've been a wall for ten years."

He didn't know either.

***

He opened the reward window and took out the helm.

It was white. That was the first thing. White steel, so pale it was almost silver in the morning light, smooth, unmarked. Closed all the way round. No face, just a narrow slit across the eyes, dark, like the gap of a door left open a crack. A ridge ran up the middle from the brow to the crown, like the keel of a boat. It was much lighter than it looked. When he turned it over in his hands, the wind on the crown stopped tugging at it.

He put it on.

The world went narrow: a slit of sky and rock and the dead grey bird beside him. His own breathing was loud. And his feet were suddenly, completely still on the rock. Not braced. Just *there*, as if the wind had decided not to bother with them any more. It blew round him and he didn't sway.

"Oh," said Alea.

He turned his head and looked at her through the slit.

"Nobody would know you in that," she said quietly. "Not even Gabe. Nobody would know who you were."

He looked at her through the narrow dark slit for a long moment.

Then he took it off. He held it on his knees, white and smooth and faceless, and looked at it.

"No," he said. "They wouldn't."

He put it in his inventory. Not at the very back, where the Kiln-heart Iron was. Near the front. Where he could reach it.

***

The kite-folk came at noon.

He was still on the crown. He'd meant to go down, but his legs hadn't agreed, so he'd sat with his back against the dead Squall and eaten the last of the Kitecliff eggs cold out of the pan and watched the Reaches. The sun climbed. The wind came and went. Off to the northwest, on the top of the Kitecliff, the great white rock with its round stone huts, something moved.

A shape went off the edge of the cliff.

Grey. Wide. It dropped, and his heart dropped with it. Then the wind caught it, and it stopped dropping, and swung out over the gap in a long slow curve, rising. A kite. A great grey kite twice the size of a man, with someone hanging from it by the arms. Gliding.

Then another went off the cliff. And another.

Seven of them.

They came out across the Reaches in a long line, gliding, and they didn't stay low. That was the thing. Pell had said *never far, never high*. *If you go high, it sees you.* They went high. They found the wind coming up off the islands and rode it, up and up, in great slow spirals, higher than the Kitecliff, higher than anything except the Spindle. And then they came toward the Spindle. Toward the needle in the middle, where the bird had circled since before Pell's grandmother's time.

They came round it. All seven, in a ring, round and round the crown, the way the Squall had. Only slower, and lower, and laughing. He could hear them. The wind carried it, high and thin. The boy was in front, on a kite too big for him, with his patchy new arm-feathers, turning loops, *shrieking* with it.

Pell came down last.

They came in over the crown on their great grey kite and landed on the rock a few metres from him. Light as a gull. They unbuckled the wing straps from their arms and stood there, tall and thin, with the feathers along their arms lifting in the wind. They looked at the dead Squall for a long time.

Then they looked at him. At the split barrel lid, and his healing arm, and him, sitting there against the bird.

"You leaned," said Pell.

"I leaned."

The old face did something strange. In a person, it might have been a smile.

"My grandmother," said Pell, "flew from the Kitecliff to the Spindle and back before breakfast. She told me about it when I was the boy's age. I never believed her." They looked up at the six kites going round and round the needle, high in the blue, the boy still turning loops. "I believe her now."

He looked up at them too.

"It'll come back," he said. He had to say it. "Tomorrow. If I stay on this floor. The way they always come back."

"We know."

"I'm going to stay. For two weeks. Maybe more." He swallowed. "My friends are coming. Thirty-five of them. I need to be here when they come up. So it'll come back tomorrow, and the next day, and you won't—"

"Then we have today," said Pell.

He stopped.

The old kite-person looked at him with their large dark bird's eyes. The wind lifted the feathers on their arms.

"My grandmother had a hundred years of sky," they said. "I've had none. The boy's had none." They looked up at the kites. "Today, the boy has sky. That's a day more than I had at his age." They looked back at him. "You think one day is nothing, because you've had so many. Up here, a day of sky is a thing you tell your grandchildren about."

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

"And when your thirty-five come," said Pell, "and kill it again, we'll have another one." They turned back to their kite and began buckling the straps onto their long arms. "And another. Every time somebody kills it, we'll have a day." They looked at him over their shoulder. "Tell your friends to kill it often."

Then they stepped off the edge of the crown into the air, and the wind took them, and they went up.

***

He went down the Spindle in the late afternoon, slowly, without a shield, holding the iron rings with both hands. The kite-folk were still in the sky above him, all seven, going round and round. They stayed up until the sun went down into the cloud, red and real, and then they glided home to the Kitecliff in a long line through the dusk, one after another, like birds going to roost.

The Squall lay on the crown behind him, and didn't go anywhere. It would come back in the morning. A new one, out of wherever it came from. And the dead one would still be lying there beside it. On floor 5, the dead stayed.

He stopped halfway down, on the ledge where the steep bridge met the needle, and sat with his legs over the drop and thought about it.

*It learned what I did.*

Every floor he'd ever cleared, he'd cleared the first time's way. The Bellwether. The Old Stag. The Lanternjaw. The Quiet. The Kiln, twice, standing in the fire. He'd done them all the way he remembered them, because he remembered them, and because remembering was the only advantage he had.

And something had been watching. Not the future. Him. What he did, and how. And then it had gone up a floor and changed the next thing to kill the way he did things.

"It doesn't know the future," he said to Alea. "Does it."

"I don't think so." She was sitting on the ledge beside him. "It didn't change the gale-kites. Or the rock-gulls. Or the bridges. Just the Squall. Just the boss. And only the part of the boss that—" She stopped.

"That I'd have used," said Ice. "The stoop. The way I took it the first time. That's the bit it changed."

"It's not reading your memories," said Alea slowly. "It can't. I'd know. It's reading what you *do*. Here. This time." Her tail went round her feet. "And then guessing."

"Then every floor I clear the old way," said Ice, "I'm teaching it."

The fox didn't answer.

He sat on the ledge with the dusk coming up out of the cloud below him. He thought about thirty-five people who'd come up the Spindle in two weeks to fight a bird. A bird that now landed on whoever held still.

Thirty-five people who'd learned to fight from him. On Earth, in games, for years. Who'd learned to tank from watching him tank. Who stood like he stood.

Gabe stood like he stood.

Gabe stood exactly like he stood. Feet planted, shoulders forward, shield up, braced, a wall. Declan had taught him, years ago, in games on school nights. *You stand like this. You don't move. Nothing gets past you. That's the job.* And Gabe had stood like that in front of everything ever since. Floor 1. Floor 2, holding the drowning Old Stag's head up by its antlers. He'd have stood like that in front of the Kiln, if they'd let him.

He'd stand like that on the crown of the Spindle, under the bird.

And it would come down on him, and land, and lift him, and take him to the edge.

Ice sat on the ledge very still, the way he'd sworn on the crown that morning he'd never sit again.

"I have to tell them," he said.

"You can't whisper," said Alea. "You said. If you answer anything, Gabe comes up the stairs."

"I know."

"You can't post in World."

"I know, Alea."

He sat a while longer. Below him, through the dusk, across the gaps between the islands, the lamps were coming on in Tether. Hundreds of little yellow lights, laced together by ropes. On the edge of town, by the winch of the chain-ferry, a single lamp in a hut. An old woman with a pipe and four shawls and a gull on her hat, who'd told him on the first day that the song never said anybody came after.

"Not me," he said. "Somebody else."

He got up, and went down the rest of the needle in the dark, holding on to the rings. Nobody crossed a chain bridge in the dark on floor 5. He crossed four.
