Rachael didn't think anybody would come.

She didn't say so. She stood with the others at the foot of the Tellhouse steps in the grey before the opening bell on the three hundred and seventy-seventh day, with her arms folded and the thread on her wrist, and didn't say anything at all. But she thought it.

People said things on steps. She'd seen them. On the Assize steps, yesterday, in the sun, with Azac still shaking and Calvin hanging off his neck: *where do we go, can anybody, does it have to be one you know*. People said all sorts of things when they'd just watched a page stand up out of a Roll. And then they went home, and slept on it, and got up, and remembered that the Bench's crier still stood in the Lantern Market every noon, and there was still a clerk on every corner, and Hamo Tull still had a chain round his neck and a long memory. And they thought, *well, somebody else will*.

She'd been a healer for three hundred and seventy-seven days. She knew what people promised on the day, and what they did on the day after.

So she stood at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded, and waited for nobody.

Hawise was at the door-roll desk, inside the great door, with her pens. Orlo was at the second desk, beside her. Askew was on a stool behind them both, with her stick across her knees and her walnut face giving nothing away. And on the other side of the Wellgate, on the steps of the Countinghouse, a clerk of the Count stood with its book on its arm, looking at nothing.

Across the road, against the wall of the Tellhouse, a little apart from everybody, Dec stood with Thea, with his sleeve pulled down over the cord.

The bell went.

Nobody came.

The Wellgate was empty. Grey light, and cobbles, and a cat. Rachael watched the cat cross the road from one doorway to another and thought, *there*. That's it. That's about what I expected.

Then, from the bottom of the hill, round the turn below the first wall, she heard something. A creak. And a voice, cross and dry and carrying.

"*Mind the step, Davy. Mind the—I said MIND the—*"

And round the corner, up the last of the slope towards the Tellhouse, came a rush chair. Carried between two men, one at each side, like a sedan. One of them was a young man with a sulky face. The other was the watch captain, Wat, red in the face, with his moustache going up and down. And in the chair, with a rug over his knees and his bright bird's eyes fixed on the Tellhouse door, sat Aldo Pellow.

They set him down at the foot of the steps. Aldo looked up at the door. At Hawise inside it, at her desk.

"Well?" he said. "Am I first? I meant to be first."

Hawise had stood up behind the desk. She didn't say anything. She just nodded.

"Good," said Aldo. He looked round at the climbers on the steps. His eyes found Calvin, and stopped. "Come here, then, lad. Carry me up. I'm not doing those steps on my own. I'm eighty-seven."

***

He wasn't first for long.

Rachael didn't see where they came from. She'd been watching Calvin and Wat carry Aldo up the Tellhouse steps in his chair, with Aldo telling them both how to do it, and when she looked back down the Wellgate, there were people on it.

Not many. A dozen. Twenty. Coming up from the Low Town in ones and twos, in their market clothes, with their hands in their pockets and their faces set, the way people walk when they've made up their minds about something on the way and don't want to be talked out of it. And behind them, more. And more. Until the line went down the Tellhouse steps and along the wall and round the corner, out of sight.

Rachael stood at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded, and looked at it.

"Huh," she said.

Beside her, Dennis made a small noise that might have been a laugh.

"People said things on steps," he said. "Didn't they."

"Shut up, Dennis."

***

It went on all morning.

Hawise did it at the door-roll. The way it had been done in Aldo's grandfather's grandfather's day, she said, near enough, though she'd had to look most of it up in the night. The citizen came up to the desk and gave their name. And said which climber. And the climber came up. And the citizen said, out loud, so the door-roll heard it, *I write this one as I know them*. And wrote them. On a slip of the Tellhouse's cream paper, in their own hand, however they liked. A name and a line. And Orlo wrote the same, at the second desk, in the second hand. And Hawise compared them. And said *told*. And the slip went into a new Roll on Askew's knee, which Askew had begun that morning and labelled, in her own hand, on the leather tag: *The Host's Hand. The climbers. The three hundred and seventy-seventh day.*

Aldo wrote Calvin. He did it from his chair, with the slip on a board on his knees, in a hand that shook so much the letters went up and down like a fence on a hill. *Calvin. Loud. Came up the hill. Took him fifteen days.* Orlo wrote the same. Hawise said *told*, and her voice cracked on it, and Calvin burst into tears in the middle of the Tellhouse door and had to be taken outside by Deston.

Joan Tull wrote Tiger. *The one I gave an apple to. He hasn't eaten it.* Tiger stood very still at the desk with his tail wrapped round his ankle and his face doing something complicated, and when it was told, he put his hand in his shirt and took out a small, slightly wrinkled apple, and showed it to her. And Joan laughed, and then had to sit down on the step.

Wat wrote Drew. *Keeps them in order. So do I.* Drew looked at the slip for a long time. "Somebody finally thanked me," he said. Nobody laughed. He hadn't meant it as a joke.

Wat's wife wrote Thea. She was a small, fierce, grey-haired woman called Edith with a basket on her arm, and she marched up to the desk and said, before anybody could ask, "The one the clerks look at." Hawise went still. Edith didn't. "Somebody ought to write the one the clerks look at," she said. "So they know she's *ours* now." And wrote her, in a hard square hand, and Orlo wrote the same, and it was told, and Thea stood at the desk with her lantern and looked at the slip, and then at Edith, and didn't say anything at all. Across the road, against the wall, Rachael saw Dec close his eyes.

The old porter with no teeth, whose door had squeaked since he was a boy, came out of his cubby by the great door and wrote Salty. He did it with a face like thunder. *The one who took my squeak.* Salty looked at it and laughed so loudly that Askew told him to be quiet.

The man from the Cloth Hall wrote Hana. He came up with a length of green wool folded over his arm, and gave it her before he wrote anything, and couldn't look at her, and wrote, *I took it back. I'm sorry.* Hana held the wool against her chest and said, very gently, "That's not a line about *me*," and he went red to the ears and asked for another slip, and wrote something else, which he wouldn't let anybody see but Orlo.

Ivo Rook, the long-faced old Alderman, came at mid-morning, in his black but without the chain, and wrote Azac. *I would like to hear how it ends.* Askew wrote Cursiffe herself, from her stool, without getting up. "I'm not having my teller held," she said, to nobody, and that was all. A woman whose husband Hyvred had found in a tavern on the second ring wrote Hyvred, with her mouth very tight, and then, at the bottom, under the line, added *thank you* in very small letters, and then crossed it out, and then wrote it again.

Two old sisters from the Low Town who'd had dead window boxes for nine years wrote Gigi and Maigo, one each, and argued about which. Mothers wrote Kei, and Malcolm, and Xola, and Bee. A fishwife wrote Hako, who'd carried her water. A cooper from Cooper's Lane wrote Jared. Hawise's cousin's old neighbour wrote Yan. A boy of about fourteen, whose father had come with him to make sure it counted, wrote Winter, because she'd shot a pigeon off his roof that had been there a month, and he'd thought that was the best thing he'd ever seen.

And at the end of the morning, near the end of the line, flushed and floury to the elbow, in an apron, with her sleeves rolled up and her jaw set, came Bertha the baker.

She stood in front of the desk with her red arms folded, and didn't look at anybody.

"Which one wanted the pie," she said.

Dennis, on the step, went very still.

Bertha looked round. Found him. Stared at him.

"It was me," she said. "The flour. On your step. Every morning. Don't you tell anybody."

"I'll tell *everybody*," said Dennis.

Bertha's face went, if anything, redder. She snatched up a slip and wrote on it so hard the pen went through the paper. Orlo wrote the same, carefully, with a hole in it, to match.

"Told," said Hawise.

***

It wasn't all the city. Rachael knew that.

She'd stood on the Tellhouse steps all morning, watching, and she'd seen it. The line was long, but it was the Low Town, mostly. The market. The coopers and the fishwives and the old women with the half-doors. Up the hill, on the third ring and the fourth, behind the clean narrow houses, the shutters had stayed shut. Wace the moneylender had stood across the Wellgate for an hour with his arms folded, watching, and then gone away. And on the Countinghouse steps, opposite, the clerk had stood all morning with its book open on its arm, and written, and written, and written. Every name. Every slip. She'd watched its pen move.

People had chosen. Some of them had chosen this. A great many hadn't.

But thirty-five had. By noon there were thirty-five slips in Askew's new Roll. One for every one of them. Thirty-five climbers, told by Tell, in the host's hand, and not held by anybody.

And there was a thirty-sixth slip.

Rachael didn't know about it until noon. She was standing at the top of the steps, by the door, with the sun coming over the Countinghouse roof, and she saw Hawise, at the desk, with a separate sheet. Not a slip. A long sheet of the cream paper, on its own, to one side. And Hawise was writing on it, small and fast, a name on every line, and there were a lot of lines.

"What's that?" said Rachael.

Hawise didn't look up. "Hands," she said.

"For who?"

Hawise's pen stopped. She looked across the road. At the wall of the Tellhouse, and the man standing against it with his sleeve pulled down.

"They keep asking," she said. "All morning. Half of them. They come up and write their climber, and then they say, *and the corded one*. Can I write him too." Her voice was very steady. "And I tell them no. He's for the Magistrate. Nobody can write him till the Reckoning. And they say, *then put me down for the Reckoning*." She looked at the sheet. "So I started putting them down."

Rachael looked at the sheet.

She couldn't read it upside down, not properly. But she could see it was full. Both sides. And Orlo had started a second one.

"Who?" she said.

Hawise looked at the list. Read it out, not loudly, running her inky finger down it.

"Nell Fother, the half-door. Joan Tull. Aldo Pellow, first, before he'd even written your loud one, I had to put him down before he'd let me do anything else. Wat. Edith. The porter. Bertha." She turned the sheet over. "Ivo Rook. Hawise Brand." She didn't look up at that one. "Orlo Fenn. The cooper from Cooper's Lane, and his brother. Both the old sisters."

She stopped. Ran her finger down a little further. And then she was quiet for a moment.

"And Rafe Collier," she said.

Rachael knew the name. She'd had to think, for a second. Then she knew it.

***

He'd come to the pump on the third day of giving.

A big man, in a leather apron, from the Tanners' Row. He'd stood at the yard gate for a long time before he came in. She'd seen him there and gone on with what she was doing, which was a girl with a split lip, and when she'd finished the girl, he was still there. So she'd looked at him properly.

She'd known him at once. Not his face. The apron. Wendy had told them all about it, that night, in the copy-room, in Wendy's flat careful voice: a big man in a leather apron who'd stepped out of a doorway on the Wellgate and shouted *corded* and thrown a lump of dried mud with a stone in it, and it had hit Dec on the back of the shoulder, hard, and Dec hadn't turned round.

*You couldn't tell*, Wendy had said. *I saw it hit him. And I couldn't tell.*

He'd stood at the gate with his right hand held out in front of him in a rag, and he hadn't said anything, and she'd seen that the rag was wet through, and what the wet was, and that the hand inside it was a tanner's hand that had gone into the lime pit.

So she'd said, "Come here, then," and he'd come.

It had been a bad one. Lime burns always were. The whole palm, and the inside of the fingers, down to the red. She'd taken it. All of it. It was what she did. She'd held his hand in both of hers on her knee by the pump, and closed it, and he'd watched her the whole time with his jaw clenched, and he hadn't said thank you. He'd just looked at her. And then at the stable door, and the copy-room window over it.

And then for ten minutes after he'd gone, she'd sat on her stool with her own right palm burning, the whole palm, the inside of the fingers, down to the red. The echo. The same place. The way it always was.

She'd never told anybody who he was. Not Dennis. Not Dec.

***

"He wrote you," said Hawise.

Rachael looked at her.

"This morning. Early. Before Aldo was even in the door." Hawise reached under the desk and took out a slip. One of the cream slips, like all the others, already told, in two hands. "He didn't want to wait in the line. He asked could he do it first and go. He said he had a vat to see to." She held it out. "I was going to give it you at the end."

Rachael took it.

It was written in a big, rough, square hand that didn't write much. The letters were very carefully made, and pressed hard, as if whoever had written them had been afraid they'd come out wrong.

*The healer at the pump. She knew who I was.*

Rachael stood at the top of the Tellhouse steps with the slip in her hand.

She stood there for a long time.

"He asked to write the corded one first," said Hawise quietly. "I said no. So he wrote you. And then he asked could I put him on the list for the Reckoning. And I said yes." She paused. "And then he said, *tell him I'd have written him. If they'd let me. Tell him I'd have written him first*."

Rachael didn't say anything.

She folded the slip in half, very carefully, and put it in her pocket.

Then she went down the steps, and across the Wellgate, to the wall of the Tellhouse, where Dec was standing with Thea.

He looked at her as she came. He knew. She could see he knew; he'd been watching the door all morning. He'd watched every slip.

"Rafe Collier," said Rachael. "The leather apron. The one who threw the mud." She looked at him. "He'd have written you. He asked to. First. Before anybody. Hawise says to tell you."

Dec didn't say anything.

But she saw his face. And she'd only seen it do that once before. At dawn, on the fifth wall, at the end of the longest night, when he'd come up the stair and found her with her forehead on Gabe's shield and her arms going pink and Dennis holding her hand. When he'd looked at them all like somebody who'd been holding something up for a very long time, and had just watched somebody else put it down.

He looked like that.

Then he looked across the road. At the Tellhouse door. At Hawise's desk, and the long sheet of cream paper, and Orlo's second one beside it. Full.

"Okay," he said. And his voice did something on it. "Okay."

***

She went up on the roof that night because she'd decided to ask him.

She'd decided on the steps. With the slip in her pocket. She'd been deciding for a long time, if she was honest. Since floor 10. Since before. Since floor 1, if she was really honest, and Rachael tried to be.

Since Calvin's arm.

It was the first thing she'd ever healed. On floor 1, in the first days. Calvin's left forearm, laid open, and she'd put her hands on it without knowing what she was doing, and it had closed. And then, twenty minutes later, walking back, she'd felt it. In her own left forearm. In the same place. The whole cut, again, after. For twenty minutes. And she'd never told anybody.

She'd felt every one since. Every wound she'd ever closed, again, after, in herself, in the same place. She knew what that was like. She knew it better than anybody.

And she'd watched Dec, for a year, and seen him do it.

Not with wounds. With *things*. He'd look at something, a door, a bridge, a face, Calvin's arm on a jetty, Dennis's arm in the snow, and she'd see it go through him. Again. After. In the same place. As if he'd felt it once already, a long time ago, and was feeling the echo of it now. *You ran to her. Shouting.* As if he'd seen Dennis run to her before, shouting, and it had been the last thing—

She'd stopped there, every time. For a year.

She'd put it next to the other things. The note on Deston's card, *train longer on the 3rd floor*, eight words in a fingertip of grey. A man who knew where the doors were. A man who'd asked for an old teller by name before the teller's book was even open. *It was four days north*, on a ship, about a thing none of them had ever seen. *Already told*, from a grey clerk at a well, about a man who'd been in Tell for as long as it took Hawise to open her book.

She'd put it all next to the echo. *Again. After. In the same place.*

And she'd nearly had it. For a long time. And then, sometime on this floor, she'd stopped nearly having it, and just had it.

She was going to ask. She'd decided.

He was on the roof. On his own. Thea had gone down an hour ago, with Xola, and Rachael had watched her go and thought Thea had looked at her on the stairs as if she knew exactly where Rachael was going, and had let her go anyway. He was sitting on the parapet at the far end, looking down at the Low Town going to bed, light by light, with his sleeve pushed up and the grey cord on his wrist where anybody could see it, now that it was dark.

She sat down on the parapet a little way from him. Not touching. Rachael didn't touch people unless she was healing them.

He didn't look round.

"You're going to ask me something," he said.

"Yes."

He nodded, slowly. Still looking at the town.

She looked at him. Properly. The way she'd look at a patient. The way she'd been looking at people for three hundred and seventy-seven days, to see where it hurt before they told her, because they never told her.

And she saw it.

His head tipped. A little way. Slowly. Towards his left shoulder. The thing he did, the only thing he ever did, when the fox of light that only Thea could see told him a number. She'd seen it on floor 9, on the rooters, on the day Deston's party watched. *I couldn't tell you were hit*. She'd seen it four times that day, and the fourth one slower. She'd seen it on the Salt, at the end, kneeling under the beak with Gabe in front of him. And she'd seen Thea learn it. Watch for it. The slow one. The bad one.

It was slow now.

Nobody had hit him. He was sitting on a roof. Something had said something to him. And it was the slow one.

And she saw his shoulders. The way they set. The way his hands, on his knees, closed, and then very carefully opened again. She'd seen that ten thousand times. In the line at the pump. On the stool in the house for the wounded. In a field on floor 1, in Calvin, before she'd touched his arm.

It was the thing people did when they knew you were about to touch the bad place.

He was bracing.

He'd answer. She thought he would. She didn't know how, or what. But she thought, looking at him, that if she asked, he wouldn't lie to her. He'd turn round on the parapet and look at her, and either he'd tell her, or he'd tell her he couldn't. And either way he'd have to choose. Here. Tonight. With a cord on his wrist and the Reckoning coming and a clerk on every corner of the city writing down every time somebody was kind, and Hamo Tull's long memory, and the thing in the tower that had looked at Thea at a well.

And whatever he chose, he'd carry that too.

On top of everything else. Whatever *everything else* was.

She thought about her window. On floor 10. On the fifth wall, in the snow, with Dennis's arm going white.

*Carry what you cannot heal. Do not put it down until it is safe.*

"I'm not going to," said Rachael.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"Ask," she said. "I was going to. I came up here to. I've had it for—" She stopped. "A long time. Since Calvin's arm. I think I've got it."

He didn't say anything. His face had gone very still.

"I'm not going to say it," said Rachael. "And I'm not going to ask. Not yet." She looked at the cord on his wrist. "Not while you're wearing that. Not with the Assize coming. Not with a thing in grey on every corner writing down when people are kind to us. Not while something up there is looking at Thea." She looked back at him. "It's not safe. You don't put it down till it's safe. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine." She held up her right hand, palm out, in the dark. The window wasn't there, but he knew what she meant. "It's on my window. *Carry it somewhere it can be put down*. That's what you do. You carry it there. And you don't put it down in the middle of the road just because somebody asked you to."

He looked at her hand. Then at her.

"So you carry it," said Rachael. "Wherever it is you're carrying it. And when you get there, somewhere safe, then I'll ask. And you can put some of it on me. If you want." She put her hand down. "I'm good at carrying."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"What if it's never safe?" he said.

He said it very quietly. Not like a question. Like something he'd been asking himself for a long time, on a lot of roofs.

"Then I'll carry not knowing," said Rachael. "I've done it since floor 1. I'm used to it." She shrugged. "It's not heavy. Not compared."

He laughed. A short, cracked breath, through his nose, looking down at his hands.

Then he stopped.

His head was still tipped. A little way. She watched it come slowly upright again.

"Dec," she said.

"Mm."

"Does it hurt?"

It was the only question she'd let herself keep. The healer's one. The one she asked everybody, before she touched them, even when she already knew.

He was quiet for a long while.

"Yes," he said.

"Every day?"

"Less." He looked at the Low Town. At the lights going out, one by one, down the hill, towards the Lantern Market and the well-house in its corner, small and round. "Less than it did. A lot less. This year." He was quiet. "Today, less."

"Okay," said Rachael.

She didn't touch him. Rachael didn't touch people unless she was healing them.

She sat on the parapet beside him for a while, a little way off, in the dark, with her hands on her knees, and Rafe Collier's slip folded in her pocket, and the city going to sleep below them.

And then, after a while, because she was a healer, and because she'd decided, and because it was dark and nobody could see, she moved along the parapet until her shoulder was against his. Just her shoulder. Just leaning. Not much.

He went very still.

"Don't get used to it," said Rachael.

"I won't."

"I'm not a hugger."

"I know."

They sat there. Down in the Low Town, a door shut. Somewhere a dog barked once and stopped. Far below, on the corner by the eel stall in the Lantern Market, a small grey light that only Yan could have seen stood quite still, and if it wrote anything down, nobody saw it.

"Rach," he said, after a long time.

"What."

"Thank you."

"Shut up, Dec," said Rachael. And didn't move her shoulder.

***

She told Dennis in the morning. Not what she'd worked out. That she hadn't asked.

He was in the yard, early, with the ham, cutting it. He looked at her for a long time when she told him.

"Did you want to?" he said.

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"Not yet," said Rachael. "Not no. Not yet."

Dennis went back to cutting the ham. He did it very carefully, the way he did everything that mattered.

"That's very Tell of you," he said.

She looked at him.

"It's what Hawise said," said Dennis. "When she tied the cord. *Not no. Not yet*." He put a slice of ham on a piece of bread and held it out to her. "Everybody in this city's waiting for something to be safe enough to say."

Rachael took the bread.

"Eat it," said Dennis. "Don't hold it."

She ate it.
