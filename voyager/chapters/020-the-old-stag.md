Every morning on floor 2, the first thing Ice saw when he opened his eyes was Gabe's whisper.

Alea had done exactly what she said she would. She'd pinned it at the top of his window, above the level and the health bar and the little tab of logs, where it was the first thing that loaded. Small and plain, with the word *sent* underneath it, and *please* at the bottom.

It hurt every single time. She'd said it was supposed to.

On the eighth morning, he read it sitting on the threshold of a tower at the top of a mountain, with snow melting off his boots and a new ember in his palm and a candle in his pocket. Then he got up, and took the dead hunter's coat back from Mother Tallow, who'd folded it on the bottom stair with great distaste, and went down the far side of the pass.

***

The Mere was in a hanging valley above the head of the river, half a day's walk north of Larchwick. That was another reason he'd gone up the pass first: from the top, he could come down on it from above, the long way round, without going anywhere near the town.

Yan's party was in the town. They'd come up the stair on the sixth day; the rankings said so, and so did World. They'd had a bad first fight with the hounds. Tiger had put it in World afterwards, in capitals, as a warning to everybody still below: *WOLVES GO ROUND. WATCH YOUR BACK.* And late last night, Salty had posted a single line, which was the most Salty had ever posted about anything in either life: *heavy arrowheads fix drift. ask me.*

"Tiger worked out the circling," said Ice. He was smiling. He couldn't help it. "Not Wendy. Tiger. It went differently."

"And Salty's making Wendy arrowheads," said Alea. "From the forge in Hearthstead. He's a Blacksmith."

"Of course he is." He'd been one the first time too. He'd made Wendy arrowheads the first time too, on floor 4, after. When it was already too late for the first two people they'd lost. "Of course he is," he said again, more quietly.

He came over the last ridge in the early afternoon, and there it was below him.

It was a lake. A perfect round lake in a bowl of grey cliffs, frozen solid from shore to shore, white and smooth and gleaming under the low grey ceiling like a plate of bone. On the far side, where the cliffs went straight up, there was a doorway cut into the rock. Plain and square, at the top of a short flight of steps, with its door shut.

And in the middle of the ice, standing perfectly still, was the Old Stag.

***

It was white. All of it. It was as big as the Bellwether had been, bigger, as tall at the shoulder as a horse and longer, and its coat was the white of old snow, faintly grey at the edges. Its antlers were the thing. They went up from its head like a winter tree, branching and branching, a great crown of bone easily as wide as Ice was tall. Ice hung from every tine of it: long, clear, dripping icicles that chimed very faintly against each other in the wind, like a curtain of glass beads.

It wasn't doing anything. It was standing in the middle of the frozen lake with its head up, looking at nothing, breathing slow clouds of steam.

"Two above," said Alea.

"Solo."

"On the eighth day." A pause. "I'm logging it."

"You're always logging it."

"Because you keep *doing* it." The fox's voice went quieter. "Ice. The first time, this took the thirty-six of us four tries. Drew nearly drowned. Kei went through the ice on the second try and we only got him out because Salty went in after him and held him up by the collar for a full minute while everyone else pulled. You remember."

"I remember."

"I just want it in the log that I said it."

"It's in the log."

He went down to the shore. He stood on the very edge, where the snow met the ice. He took off the dead hunter's coat and folded it, and put it on a rock, because he'd need to turn fast and the coat would slow him down. The cold came through his shirt like knives. He drew his sword and settled his shield and looked out across the ice.

It wasn't all the same. That was the thing. That was what had taken the first thirty-six of them four tries to see. From the shore, the ice looked smooth and white and perfect, all the way across. But if you looked, really looked, with your head tilted to catch the grey light just right, there were places where it was darker. Patches. Some no bigger than a table, some as big as a room, scattered across the lake, where the white went faintly grey, like the shadow of a cloud.

Thin ice. Where the springs came up from underneath.

He'd drawn a map of them the first time, after Kei went through. He'd drawn it on the back of a quest note in the ascension room that night, with his hands still shaking. He'd kept it for years, until it fell apart. He didn't need it now. He looked at the ice and the map was there in his head, every patch exactly where he'd drawn it, and the nearest dark one was forty metres out, a little to the left of the Stag.

He walked out onto the ice.

***

It heard him at twenty metres.

The great white head came round, slowly, the antlers swinging, all the icicles chiming at once, a sound like a chandelier in an earthquake. The Old Stag looked at him with its eyes, which were pale blue, like the hounds', but deep and old and very calm. And it lowered its head.

He didn't need to wake it. It had been awake the whole time. It had just been waiting for somebody to come.

It charged.

It didn't charge like the Bellwether, head down, a straight line, stupid. It came across the ice like something that had done this a thousand times. Its antlers were lowered and spread, so the whole crown of bone and ice was a wall coming at him three metres wide. There was nowhere to go left, and nowhere to go right, because the antlers were left and right.

He went down.

Flat, on his back, on the ice, shield over his chest. The Stag went over him. He felt the wind of it, saw the white belly and the great hooves go past above him, heard the icicles chime. One hoof came down a hand's breadth from his head and cracked the ice in a white star.

He rolled up. It was already turning, at the far end of its run, slowing, swinging round.

"Ice," said Alea. "Patch is behind you. Fifteen metres. Left."

"I know."

He backed toward it. Not running. Walking backwards, shield up, watching the Stag come round. When he felt the ice change under his boots, a very faint give, a softness like stepping onto a frozen puddle, he stopped. He was standing right on the edge of the dark patch. One more step back and he'd be on it.

The Stag lowered its head again.

He waited. Wait, wait, move once.

At four metres he went sideways. Not left or right: forward and to the side, diagonally, past the edge of the antlers, so close a tine scraped his shield with a shriek of bone on iron. And the Old Stag, which had been aiming at the place where he'd been standing, came on at full speed.

Straight onto the dark ice.

It went through.

There was a crack like a cannon, and then a great black hole in the white with water fountaining up out of it. The Stag was in the hole, chest-deep in black freezing water, its front hooves scrabbling at the edge of the ice. The edge broke under them, and broke again, and it couldn't get out.

He went in on it while it was in the water. Not too close; the edge was breaking. He hit it on its neck, on its shoulder, anywhere he could reach that wasn't antler. He got four hits in before it got one hoof up on solid ice and heaved itself out in a great surge of water and steam. It came up streaming, shaking itself, ice already forming on its white coat.

He was already backing away toward the next dark patch.

***

He did it three more times.

It wasn't the same each time. The Stag learned. The second time, it slowed before the dark ice and nearly stopped, and he had to Taunt it, the little cracked bell ringing once on his belt, to make it come on. The third time it went wide, round the patch, and he had to run, actually run, across the open ice to the next one, with the antlers coming up behind him like a falling tree. The fourth time it stamped.

He'd forgotten the stamp. Not forgotten. He'd known it was coming, the way you know a step is there in a dark house, and it still caught him. The Stag reared up and brought both front hooves down on the ice, and a line of spikes went out from the point where they landed. Jagged white spikes of ice, waist-high, shooting up through the surface one after another in a straight line, fast, straight at him.

He got the shield down in time. The last spike came up under it and threw him four metres backward across the ice. He landed on his shoulder, and something in it went *crack*, and the world went white for a second.

***

The Old Stag stopped in the middle of the ice and threw back its head and bellowed.

It wasn't the Bellwether's scream. It was deeper and longer and older: a sound like wind going over the mouth of a cave, going on and on. And as it bellowed, the air over the Mere began to move. It thickened. The snow that had been falling gently out of the low grey ceiling all day stopped falling and began to *blow*. Round and round, faster and faster, in a great slow whirl centred on the Stag, until the whole Mere was a wall of white.

He couldn't see the shore. He couldn't see the cliffs, or the door. He couldn't see the dark patches on the ice; he couldn't see the ice under his own feet.

He couldn't see the Stag.

[ Whiteout ]

The first time, this was where Drew had nearly drowned. Thirty-six people in a whiteout on a frozen lake, unable to see each other or the ice or the Stag. Calling to each other through the roar, getting it wrong, running onto the dark patches themselves, going through. Drew had gone into the black water in the middle of the Mere, and it had taken them nearly five minutes to find him by his shouting.

Somewhere in the white, very close, he heard the icicles chime.

He stood very still. He listened. *Left. No. Behind.* The chime moved. It was circling him, the way the hounds circled. Somewhere in there, unseen, it was choosing which way to come.

And he understood, standing there in the white roar with his shoulder aching, that it didn't matter. He didn't need to see it.

He needed it to see him.

He raised his left hand.

He held it up, high, over his head, palm open, the way he'd held it over the beacon cradle on the pass, the way the stone knight held his lantern out over the aisle toward the door. The ember in his palm woke. The light came into his hand from nowhere, from himself, from the beacon on the mountain he'd lit last night, and his hand glowed gold. A small, steady, warm gold, in the middle of a roaring white storm on a frozen lake.

*A lantern is for others to see.*

The chiming stopped.

Somewhere in the white, the Old Stag had turned its head toward the light.

He felt it look. He felt the weight of its attention land on him, all of it, the way the Rimehounds' attention had landed on him at the door of the tower. It knew exactly where he was now. It could see nothing in the whole white world but the small gold light in his raised hand.

So it would come straight at it.

He took two careful steps backward, with the light held high. He felt for the ice under his boots. Softer. The give. A dark patch, under the snow, that he couldn't see. But it was there, exactly where he remembered it, the biggest one on the Mere, right in the middle, where Drew had gone through ten years ago.

He stopped right on its edge. He held the light up.

Out of the white, the Old Stag came.

He didn't see it until it was three metres away. Then it was there all at once, enormous, its antlers spread, its head down. It was coming straight at the light as if nothing else in the world existed.

He moved once.

He dropped the light. He closed his hand, and the gold went out like a lamp being covered, and he threw himself sideways onto the solid ice. And the Old Stag, aiming at a light that was suddenly gone, at a man who was suddenly somewhere else, came on at full speed onto the dark ice in the middle of the Mere.

It went through.

***

It was deeper here. Much deeper. It went in up to its neck, its great crowned head above the black water, its hooves finding nothing underneath. The antlers caught on the edge of the hole, the whole wide crown of them braced across the broken ice. For a moment it hung there, held up by its own antlers, the icicles chiming wildly as the ice cracked and shifted round it.

Its throat was right there. White, exposed, stretched out over the edge of the hole as it fought to hold its head up out of the water.

He put the sword in.

It didn't bellow. It made one long, low sound, like a breath going out of a very large house, and its eyes, deep and old and pale blue, looked at him, very calm. Then the light went out of them, quietly, the way lights go out in the Spire. The antlers slid off the edge of the ice. The Old Stag went down into the black water of the Mere, slowly, crown last, all the icicles chiming one last time as it sank.

The whiteout stopped. Just like that. The snow that had been roaring round the lake fell straight down, all at once, and lay still. The cliffs came back, grey and close. The doorway on the far side stood at the top of its steps, and as he watched, its door ground slowly open.

It was long, white-grey, light as paper, with a clasp at the throat carved from pale bone in the shape of a single antler tine.

He put it on. The cold that had been eating into him through his shirt for an hour went away. Not slowly. All at once, the way it goes when you step into a warm house out of a winter street. He stood on the ice of the Mere in a white cloak and breathed, and for the first time in eight days he wasn't cold.

"It suits you," said Alea.

"You say that about everything."

"Everything suits you. You're very lucky."

He went back across the ice to the shore, carefully, round the dark patches. He picked up the dead hunter's coat from the rock where he'd left it, and looked at it for a while: grey wolf-fur, enormous, stiff with age, smelling of smoke and wolf and now of Mother Tallow's tallow.

*The first time, Gabe got this coat.*

He carried it across the Mere and up the steps and through the open door in the cliff, into the ascension room of floor 2. It was round and small and dry, with a stone bench and a fire-pit and a stair going up at the back, the same as all of them. He folded the coat neatly, and put it on the stone bench by the door, where the next person to come in would see it first.

"Ice," said Alea.

"It's not a hint."

"It's a coat."

"It's a coat," he agreed. "That's all it is. It's not a note, it doesn't say anything. It's just a coat somebody left. Climbers leave things. It's the most normal thing in the world."

"Gabe's going to know you left it."

"Gabe's going to know *somebody* left it. Somebody ahead." He looked at it, folded on the bench. "And he's going to be cold, coming up that mountain. And he'll put it on. And Xav will be furious, because Gabe got the coat again."

Alea didn't say anything for a while.

"I'm logging that," she said finally, "as a gift. Not a thread."

"Thanks."

***

He didn't sleep in the ascension room this time. He sat by the fire-pit without lighting it and ate the last of a hare he'd cooked on the pass. Then he went to the bottom of the stair, and looked up.

He'd counted the last one. He'd count this one. He'd count all of them now, every stair on every floor, out loud, on his own, for Gabe, who'd counted them the first time for thirty-six people.

He took out Mother Tallow's candle and looked at it. Pale and smooth and perfect. *A light for the road, in case nobody's keeping one for you.* He didn't light it. He put it back in the vest pocket, behind the zip, next to the blank white card from the Threshold.

Then he put his foot on the first step.

"One," he said.

He climbed. A hundred and sixty steps, it turned out. He'd forgotten that too. At the top there was a door, with warm air coming round the edges of it, wet and heavy and green, smelling of rot and flowers and still water.

Floor 3.

He stood there with his hand on the door. At the top of every window that anyone could see, a name sat at level ten and didn't move.

Sooner or later, thirty-five people were going to come up this stair. He was going to make sure that when they did, they found a floor they could stay on.

He opened the door.
