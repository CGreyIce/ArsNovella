Rui Xiang read people the way other people read weather.

He'd always been able to. He didn't know how. He'd never been able to explain it to anybody, and after a while he'd stopped trying. You looked at somebody's face and you just knew. Not what they were thinking. That was different. What was coming. The way you look at a sky in the afternoon and know it'll rain by four, without being able to say what it was about the sky that told you.

At seventeen he'd picked his best friend of nine years out of a crowd in four minutes from the way the back of his head was holding itself. On floor 10 he'd known Xav was going to move early a second before Xav moved, and not been quick enough to stop him, and a boy called Tamm had died on the fifth wall with his shield up because Rui Xiang had been in the wrong place for one second. He carried that. He'd stood beside Ansa in the fifth wall's tunnel a few days ago while she put her hand on her son's name and said *ask me in the spring*.

He read people. It didn't make him fast. It made him early.

*You always get there first*, Dec had said to him once, at seventeen, laughing. *You just don't know what to do when you're there.*

He'd thought about that for nine years. He'd thought about it on fourteen floors. He thought about it the morning after Tomorrow Night, on the old stone bridge in Evermorrow, with the river going under him high and brown after a night of rain, watching Thea's face.

***

He hadn't meant to be on the bridge.

He'd come down to the river at first light because he couldn't sleep, and because the inn was full of seventy people who'd rung bells till one and eaten bread till three and were now asleep on every bench and table and floor, and the noise of seventy people sleeping was a lot. He'd come down to the river and sat on the bank under the first arch of the bridge, with his back against the old stone and his knife in his hand. Not to use. Just to hold. It was the only way he knew to hold anything still.

And then two people had come out onto the bridge above him and sat down on the parapet.

He knew who before he looked. He knew from the way the footsteps stopped. Two sets, one heavier, both slow, both stopping at the same moment, in the middle of the bridge, the way people stop when they've been walking somewhere together without saying where.

Thea and Dec.

Rui Xiang stayed where he was.

He couldn't hear them. Not properly. The river was too loud, high and brown after the rain. He could hear voices, not words. He could have moved. He thought about it. He thought about John putting the light out, last night, in the ringing chamber, for ten seconds. *Some things aren't for the camera.*

He didn't move. But he didn't listen, either. He looked at the river. And every so often, because he couldn't help it, because it was what his eyes did, he looked up at the bridge, at their faces, and read the weather.

***

He'd been reading Thea's for sixty days.

Everybody had, a bit. You couldn't not. Since the Long Barn. Since a lantern put down very carefully by a doorpost, and a hand taking a white hand, and a face that Rui Xiang had only ever seen once before on anybody, on Ansa, in a tunnel, the morning after the first night.

Dennis had a graph. Rui Xiang didn't hold with graphs. Graphs were what you made when you couldn't read the sky. But he'd looked at Dennis's graph, on the Salt, when Dennis showed everybody, and he'd thought it was about right. Ten, at the doorpost. A long time at ten. Then nine. Then the bumps. Eight in the valley. Seven in Tell. Six on the Salt, when she walked the road with Gigi and came back and held out her hand. Five at sea. Four at Wendmere. Three when she laughed at Yan's soup. Two last night, Dennis said, at the bells. One and a half when she let go of her rope.

Dennis was wrong about last night. Rui Xiang had been on the rope next to Calvin, across the ring. He'd seen her face when she let go of the rope and stood in the gap and looked at Dec.

That hadn't been a number at all.

It had been weather changing. All at once. The way it changes on a hill, sometimes, in the afternoon: you're standing in grey and rain and then the cloud just goes, off the top of the hill, in about a minute, and there's the sun, and everything's wet and shining, and you can't remember what grey looked like.

He'd seen that on her face. For about ten seconds. Before John put the light out.

He was looking for it now. On the bridge. In the morning.

***

They sat on the parapet the way they'd sat on it a year ago.

He knew that because Hyvred had told him, once, on floor 10, in the hay hall, when the club was going through everything anybody had ever seen. *On the bridge in Evermorrow, the day after the bells. The same distance apart as on the terrace on six. She held out the lantern and said it's yours. He said keep it. Then she said what was the first time. And he said not yet. And she said ok.*

They weren't the same distance apart now.

There wasn't any distance. She was sitting with her shoulder against his, on the cold stone, with the river going under them, and the lantern on the parapet beside her, lit, in the daylight, the way she always kept it now.

Rui Xiang read her face from below.

The angry was still there.

Not much. He'd known it would be. It was a long way back, behind everything else, like a cloud on the far side of a valley. But it was there. He could see it in the set of her mouth when she wasn't talking. In the way her hand, the one not holding his, kept going to the left side of her own chest, to the scar on her shoulder from the glass on floor 6, and resting there, the way it did when she was holding something.

She said something.

He couldn't hear it. He saw Dec's face as she said it. Dec's face did a thing it didn't do often, which was look surprised. And then something else, which was laugh. Rui Xiang heard that, over the river. Short. Out loud. Surprised into it.

Thea hit him on the arm.

Not hard. The way you hit somebody who's laughed at the wrong thing at exactly the right moment. And then she said something else, and it was longer, and her face wasn't laughing, and Dec stopped laughing too and listened.

Rui Xiang watched her face while she said it.

And saw the weather.

Not changing. Rui Xiang had expected it to change. He'd come down to the river at first light with his knife and some part of him had been waiting, he realised, for the sun to come out on the hill, the way it had in the ringing chamber. For the angry to go.

It didn't go.

It stayed exactly where it was. Far back. Small. A cloud on a hill on the far side of a valley. And she looked at it, Rui Xiang thought, while she talked. As if she could see it too. As if she was pointing at it. *That. That one. That one's staying.*

And the rest of the sky was clear.

All of it. Everywhere else. The whole of her face, apart from that one cloud on that one hill, was the clearest weather Rui Xiang had ever seen on anybody.

He understood.

It wasn't weather. Not that bit. Weather goes. That bit wasn't going anywhere. That bit was a mountain. It would be there every morning when she looked out of the window for the rest of her life, with a little cloud on the top of it, and she'd know it, and it would be fine, because it was hers, and because the rest of the sky was clear.

He looked down at his knife.

He held it very still.

***

They came off the bridge after a while. Rui Xiang heard them go. He didn't look up.

He sat under the arch with the river going past and thought about the boy called Tamm.

He'd tried to read it, after. On the fifth wall. What he could have seen. What the sky had been doing in the second before Xav moved early and the Rimed reached over the shields onto a seventeen-year-old's chest. He'd gone over it a hundred times on the barracks roof. A thousand. He'd been sure, for a long time, that there was something he should have read and hadn't. Some cloud he'd missed.

He'd told Ansa that, in the tunnel, a few days ago. Under his breath, while she had her hand on her son's name. *I should have seen it.* And Ansa had taken her hand off the stone and put it on his arm, the way you'd put your hand on somebody's arm to stop them walking into a road, and said: *You think you read weather. You don't. You read people. Weather doesn't care if you read it. People do.* And then: *He was looking at the snow. That's what Malcolm said. He liked the snow. He was looking at it.* And then: *Ask me in the spring.*

He thought about Thea's face on the bridge.

*That one's staying.*

He thought maybe Tamm was his. The cloud on his hill. Not a thing he'd missed. Not a thing he could have read. Just a thing that was there, on the far side of a valley, every morning, that he'd know, for the rest of his life.

And the rest of the sky.

He put his knife away.

He went up the bank, and back across the square, to the inn, to wake people up.

***

Dec was in the bakery.

Rui Xiang found him there at mid-morning, when he went to tell him Higu wanted everybody at the fountain by noon. The blue door. The bow window. The little brass bell over the door ringing as he went in.

Dec was at the counter. With Merle. And on the counter between them was a crock.

A brown clay crock, the kind you keep pickles in, with a cloth tied over the top and a string round it. Merle had both hands round it. She was looking at Dec the way you'd look at somebody who'd asked for something very odd.

"Your *mother*?" said Merle.

"The starter," said Dec. "The mother. The one you start the dough from. Every day." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the crock. "Some of it. If you can spare it. In that."

Merle looked at him for a long moment.

"It's a year old," she said. Slowly. "This one. I started it the first morning. The first real one, after the bells. I threw the old one out because it was yesterday's and yesterday's had gone, and I started a new one. This one." She put her hand flat on the cloth. "It's had a year of tomorrows. Every day. I've fed it every day for a year." She looked at him. "Nobody's ever asked for my mother."

"Somebody makes bread," said Dec. "At the bottom." He stopped. "She'll know what to do with it."

Rui Xiang stood in the door and read Merle's face.

She wanted to ask. He could see it. It was right there, in her mouth, in the way her hand tightened on the crock. *Who? What bottom? Where are you going?* Every question in the world. All of them, right there.

She didn't ask.

He watched her not ask. He watched her decide, with her hand on the crock, the way he'd watched Burr decide, and Aldo, and the cheesemonger. The way you watch somebody choose not to know something because not knowing is the thing they want most.

"Feed it," said Merle. "Every day. Flour and water. Same as you'd feed a dog. Don't let it get cold." She untied the string and checked the cloth and tied it again. "If it goes grey on top, it's hungry. If it smells like old socks, it's *very* hungry. If it's flat, it's sulking." She pushed it across the counter. "It's tomorrow's. All of it. Tell her that."

Dec took it.

Both hands. The way Thea had taken the plait off Hild. The way Tiger kept Joan's apple. He held the crock against his chest, and looked at Merle over the top of it.

"I will," said Dec.

"And don't tell me who," said Merle.

"I won't."

"Or how it goes."

"I won't."

Merle looked at him a moment longer. Then she reached over the counter and brushed something off his cheek. Flour. There wasn't any. She did it anyway.

"Come back," said Merle. "Another time. Years, this time. *Proper* years. Buy a rosemary loaf. Don't say anything." Her eyes were very bright. "Surprise me again."

***

Rui Xiang walked back to the fountain with him.

He didn't say anything about the crock. Dec carried it in front of him in both hands, like something that might spill, which Rui Xiang supposed it might.

"Thea's still angry," said Rui Xiang.

He hadn't meant to say it. It came out. It was the kind of thing that came out of him sometimes, when he'd read something and been sitting on it too long.

Dec looked at him sideways.

"A bit," said Rui Xiang. "Not much. It's not going to go."

Dec didn't say anything for a few steps.

"How do you know?" said Dec.

"I was under the bridge," said Rui Xiang. "This morning. I didn't listen. I couldn't hear. I just—" He shrugged. "Her face. It's a cloud on a hill. Far away. It's staying." He looked at the crock. "The rest's clear. All of it. I've never seen anybody that clear."

Dec walked for a while.

"She said that," said Dec. "On the bridge. Nearly exactly that." He was quiet. "She said she was going to be a bit angry about fifteen for the rest of her life. That's the bit that stays. And the rest was—" He stopped. He didn't finish it.

"I know," said Rui Xiang. "I saw."

"You always see."

"I always get there first," said Rui Xiang. "You said."

Dec looked at him. And Rui Xiang read his face, because he couldn't not, and saw the thing go over it that went over it sometimes, when somebody said something Dec had said to them a long time ago. Like a man looking at a photograph.

"At seventeen," said Dec. "*You always get there first. You just don't know what to do when you're there.*"

"Yeah."

"I was wrong," said Dec. "About the second bit."

Rui Xiang stopped walking.

"You know what to do," said Dec. He'd stopped too, in the middle of the square, with a crock of a baker's mother in his arms. "You've always known. You get there first and you *wait*. You sit under a bridge and you don't listen. You stand next to a woman at a wall and you hold your knife still. You knew what to do on ten." He looked at Rui Xiang. "Somebody being in the wrong place for one second isn't not knowing what to do. It's just a second."

Rui Xiang didn't say anything.

He didn't know how Dec knew. Dec had been on the wall that night, ninth along, and said *sorry, RX* afterwards in the tunnel, with his hand on Rui Xiang's shoulder. But not the second. Not the barracks roof and the thousand times. He'd never told Dec any of that. He'd told Xav, once, and Ansa, a few days ago, under his breath.

He read Dec's face.

It was clear. That was what he saw. Not the old way. Not the floor-1 way, the white-helm way, the face of a man a floor ahead holding everything so still it hummed. Clear. With a few clouds on a few hills. Rui Xiang could see them. A lot of them. More than anybody he'd ever read. A whole range of them, a long way back, on the far side of something. More hills than one person should have.

But the sky was clear.

"Okay," said Rui Xiang.

***

They left at noon.

The whole of Evermorrow saw them out. Not to the door. The door was on the hillside above the valley, at the top of three hundred and twelve steps, in the long wet grass where Dec had sat down on the first morning and listened to the birds. But to the edge of town. To the old stone bridge. Everybody. The butcher and the dog. The smith. The caretaker with an apple. Mrs Penn, telling Rachael about a new thing with her hip. The cheesemonger, who gave Dennis a whole wheel and said *on credit* and Dennis said *I'll be back* and she said *I know you will, that's why it's on credit*.

The Dawley boy ran beside Tiger all the way to the bridge, asking him about the rope over his shoulder. Tiger told him it was for somebody at the bottom.

Merle stood on the bridge.

In the middle. Where Thea and Dec had sat that morning. With flour on her arms and her hair tied up and her face wet. She didn't come any further.

"I'm not walking you to the door," said Merle. "I'm not watching you go up the hill. I did that once." She looked at all of them. "Go on. Go. I don't want to know which way you're going."

"Down," said Calvin.

"*CALVIN*," said about fifteen people.

"I didn't say *where*," said Calvin.

Merle laughed. On the bridge. With her head back. Then she put her floury hand up, the way she'd put it up at the blue door a year ago, and held it there.

"Thirty-six," said Merle.

She didn't count. She just said it. As if she knew.

Then she turned round and walked back across the bridge into town, toward Lantern Street and the blue door, and didn't look back, and Rui Xiang read the back of her head the way he'd read the back of his best friend's head in a crowd at seventeen, and saw that she was crying, and that she was happy, and that she was going to go into her bakery and feed her mother and start tomorrow's bread, and not know.

***

The hillside was wet.

Long grass up to the knee. Birdsong. Woodsmoke from the valley behind them. The river going round its bend below, brown and high. And in the hillside, at the top of the long slope, the door.

Rui Xiang got there first.

He didn't mean to. He just did. He walked up the hill the way he walked everywhere, not fast, early, and when he got to the top he was at the door and everybody else was still in the grass below him.

He stopped.

He stood at the door with his hand on it. Not opening it. That was Tiger's. Tiger opened the doors now, going down; he'd said so on 14. And Tiger waited first, with his hand on them, before he opened them; he'd learned it from somebody on floor 8. Rui Xiang stood at the door with his hand flat on it and waited.

For Tiger. For the others. For the long line of them coming up the hill through the wet grass. Calvin and Rachael. Dennis with a wheel of cheese. Yan with a scythe. Gigi and Maigo, Maigo with a new leaf. Hana and Milli. Higu with the rock. Tiger with Burr's rope. Thea with the lantern, and Dec beside her with a crock of a baker's mother held in both arms against his chest, and a twist of straw in his vest, and two lines round his wrist.

He waited until they were all there.

Dec came up last but one. Gabe was last. He always was, going down.

Dec looked at him. At Rui Xiang, standing at the door with his hand on it, first, waiting.

"You got there first," said Dec.

"I know what to do now," said Rui Xiang.

Dec almost smiled.

Tiger came up beside them and put his hand on the door next to Rui Xiang's. And waited too. Just for a moment.

Then he opened it.

Three hundred and twelve steps. Going down. Newish counted. Thea sang.

At the top of the stair, before he went down, Thea stopped. She turned round, on the first step, with the lantern, and looked at all of them. All thirty-five, on the hillside, in the wet grass, in the sun.

"For the record," said Thea.

Everybody stopped.

"I'm still angry," said Thea. "About one. It's staying at one." She looked at Dennis. "You can stop doing the graph."

Dennis opened his mouth.

"It's staying at *one*, Dennis," said Thea.

"Noted," said Dennis.

Thea nodded. Once. And turned round and went down the stair, with the lantern, singing, and Dec went down after her, with the crock, a step behind, the way he always was now. Not in front.

Rui Xiang read the back of her head as she went.

Clear. Everywhere. Except one small cloud, on one far hill, that was staying.

He thought that was probably the best weather there was.
