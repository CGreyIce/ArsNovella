Rui Xiang read people the way other people read weather. He'd always done it. He couldn't stop.

It wasn't a skill, or it hadn't been, on Earth. It was just the way his eyes worked. He'd sit in a room and watch where people's weight went. Who leaned toward who. Who stood with their back to a wall and who stood with their back to the door. Whose hands stopped moving when somebody else came in. He'd done it at seventeen in a polytechnic classroom, on his first day, and picked out in about four minutes which loud boy at the back was going to be his friend for the next nine years. He'd been right.

On floor 4 he'd read a city's roofs and found where a man had sat for a week without moving. On floor 10, he'd been reading the same three people for four days, and he didn't like what he was reading at all.

Gabe had stopped asking things.

Not out loud. He talked. He talked as much as ever, at meals, on the walls, in the long cold evenings in the barracks the hold-folk had given them on the second level. But since the afternoon he came up from the Breath with his hands burned and his face a strange flat colour, he hadn't asked Declan a single question. Not one. Not *where are you going*, not *you okay*, not *what's that*. And he'd started standing on Declan's right. Every time. On the walls, at the long tables, in the street. Always on the right, a little behind, the way you'd stand if you were holding a shield and the person on your left needed half of it.

Salty and Jared had come up from the Breath that afternoon too. They hadn't said what happened. Jared had told Hana something, low, at dinner, and Hana had gone very still and looked down the table at Declan for a long time. That was all Rui Xiang knew. That was enough.

And Declan had gone quiet in a way Rui Xiang had only seen once before, in a kitchen on floor 7, with flour in his hair and his jaw going purple.

And Xav.

Xav, who Rui Xiang had known for nine years. Who never moved before he was ready. Who'd been carrying something for a hundred days with both hands, very carefully, the way John had said once about Declan. You don't answer whispers when you're doing that. You'd drop it.

He'd watched Xav carry it. He hadn't said anything. It wasn't his to say.

On the fourth night, on the fifth wall, Xav moved too early.

***

The fifth wall was the top.

You went up to it through the whole of the Bastion. Out of the barracks on the second level, up the long stair cut into the cliff to the third wall, through its gate and its tunnel of names, up through the streets of the fourth level, where the forges were and the stables and the long halls where the hold-folk ate together in hundreds. And up again. Past the last houses, the last chimneys, onto bare rock. To the fifth wall, the highest and narrowest of them, where the pass came down out of the mountains on the high side into a throat of rock you could have shouted across.

It was the coldest place Rui Xiang had ever stood. The wind came down the pass from the high side all the time, never stopping, hard and dry and full of ice that stung like sand. The snow up there didn't fall. It went sideways. The sky over the high end of the pass was grey and low and moving fast.

"First watch of winter," Brenna had said, that morning. "Tonight. On the fifth." She'd looked at the thirty-six of them in the barracks, with her grey eyes. "It's yours if you want it. We'd be glad of you." She'd paused. "It's not a kindness, asking. It'll come tonight. It always comes on the first night of the snow."

"What will?" Calvin had said.

"Winter," said Brenna.

Fifteen of them went. Drew chose. Xav's party, all five. Deston's. And Hyvred and Winter for the high shots, and Declan, because Declan was going wherever there was a wall and nobody had been able to stop him, and Gabe, because Declan was going.

And Thea, at the end, with the lantern. Nobody had chosen her. She'd just come.

***

The hold-folk held the fifth wall in a line.

Rui Xiang had never seen anything like it. He'd seen fights. He'd seen thirty-five people kill a serpent and a sower and a bird the size of a house. But they'd always fought the way his friends had fought on Earth, the way he still fought: in pieces, everybody doing their own bit, fast, a name called out, a gap found, a knife put in. The hold-folk didn't fight like that at all.

They stood along the top of the wall shoulder to shoulder. Two hundred of them, maybe, in the dusk, in grey, with their shields. Big round shields of grey iron, every one of them chipped and dented and patched, every one of them made in the Breath. And they held them up so that each shield overlapped the next. Not touching. Overlapping. Every shield covering the left half of the man beside it. All the way along the wall, from one cliff to the other. Like the scales on a fish.

"It covers the man on your left," said Gabe, beside him, low. "Geir says. Nobody covers himself."

Rui Xiang looked along it. At the line of grey shields in the dusk, all overlapping, and the hold-folk behind them with their faces set and still, and nobody talking. Two hundred people, every one of them trusting the person on their right with half their body.

He'd never stood in anything like that. He wasn't built for it. He was built for the gap in it.

The climbers went in the line where Brenna put them. Gabe and Azac in the line itself, with their shields, among the hold-folk, which Gabe looked like he'd been born for. Declan in the line too, on Gabe's left. With Gabe on his right. Rui Xiang saw Gabe set himself there, and lift his old shield, and set its edge so that it covered Declan's right side, the way the hold-folk did. Saw Declan feel it. Saw him turn his head a little and look at the edge of Gabe's shield across his own arm, and look away.

Xav was behind them, in the second rank. Malcolm behind Xav, with two blessings ready. John on the stair with his motes. Hyvred and Winter on the tower at the end of the wall, high. Thea at the back of the parapet with the lantern, where Brenna had told her the light would help.

And Rui Xiang where he always was. Not in the line. Behind it. Loose. Watching for the gap.

"There's a boy," said Brenna, coming down the line past him in the dusk with her shield on her back. "Tamm. Third on your right. First winter." She didn't look at him. "If he goes, I'd be glad if somebody was near."

Rui Xiang looked.

Third on his right, in the line, a boy. Seventeen, maybe. Tall like all of them, but thin with it, the way you're thin at seventeen before the rest of you's caught up. A grey cap too big for him. A shield too big for him. He was standing in the line with it up, overlapping the man on his left, exactly right, and his knuckles on the strap were white, and he was looking out over the parapet at the high side of the pass with his mouth set in a line, the way you look at something when you've decided you're not going to be frightened of it.

He'd caught Rui Xiang's eye that afternoon, in the barracks, spinning a knife over his knuckles. And come over. And asked, very shyly, how he did it. And Rui Xiang had shown him. Over the first knuckle, under the second, over the third, catch. Tamm had dropped it eleven times and laughed every time and on the twelfth he'd got it, and his whole face had lit up.

"I'll be near," said Rui Xiang.

***

It came at full dark.

You heard it first. Everything on every floor, you heard first. The wind changed. That was the first thing. It had been coming down the pass from the high side all evening, hard and steady and full of ice, and then it dropped. Not stopped. Dropped. Went low and quiet, the way the air goes before a storm. And in the quiet, from up the pass, somewhere in the dark and the sideways snow, there was a sound like ice cracking on a pond.

*Crack.*

Then another.

Then a lot of them, close together, coming down the pass. *Crack. Crack-crack. Crack.* Like somebody walking out across a frozen lake that wasn't quite thick enough.

All along the wall, the hold-folk set their feet.

John sent a mote out. It went up over the parapet and out into the dark of the pass, white, and lit the snow coming down, and the rock, and the throat of the pass going up into the mountains.

And the things coming down it.

They were tall. Taller than the hold-folk. Taller than the wall was thick. Shaped like people, more or less, but long, too long, in the arms and the neck. And they were made of snow. Packed hard, grey-white, and ice, clear and blue in places like the ice at the bottom of a glacier, built up round something. Round armour. Old armour. Grey, rusted, a helmet here, a breastplate there, a gauntlet on the end of an arm made of ice. As if somebody had taken the armour off a dead army a long time ago and left it out in the snow, and the snow had built itself up round it into the shape of the men who'd worn it.

Where their faces should have been, under the old helmets, there was nothing. Just the dark inside the helmet. Hollow.

"Rimed," said Brenna, on the wall. Very quietly. Not to anybody. "Here they come."

On the tower at the end of the wall, Hyvred made a sound. Rui Xiang looked up at her. She had her glasses off, and her face had gone white in John's mote-light.

"There's nothing in them," she said. Loud enough to carry. "Nothing. Like the Sower. Like the Wanting." Her voice shook. "They're the same kind."

Nobody answered her. There wasn't time.

They came at the wall.

***

They didn't run. They walked. The ice in them cracked with every step, *crack, crack*, and they came down out of the dark and the snow in a crowd, fifty, sixty, more, and when they got to the foot of the wall they didn't stop. They put their hands on it. Their long ice hands, with the old gauntlets on, and the ice of their fingers went into the cracks between the stones like roots, and they climbed.

Straight up. Thirty metres. Fast.

"*Shields*," said Brenna.

The first of them came over the parapet.

Rui Xiang saw it happen twenty metres down the wall. A long grey-white arm coming up over the top, the old gauntlet, the ice fingers reaching. And the hold-folk in the line there didn't flinch and didn't step back. They leaned. The whole line leaned, shield on shield, into the thing coming over the top. And it hit the overlapping shields and couldn't find a gap, because there wasn't one, and a hold-folk man behind the line put a long spear up under its arm into the hollow inside its breastplate, and twisted.

The thing came apart. Like a snowman hit with a shovel. Ice and snow going everywhere, and the old armour clattering down the face of the wall into the dark.

"In the chest," said Brenna. "There's a stone. Black ice. Break it."

They came over all along the wall.

Rui Xiang moved.

***

He was good at this. He'd always been good at it. Not standing. Moving. Finding the gap. He went along behind the line like water along a gutter, low and fast, and everywhere a Rimed came over the parapet and the line held it, he was there, under the arm, under the edge of the old breastplate, knife up into the hollow. Twice he felt the black stone in there, cold, so cold it burned through his glove, and broke it, and the thing came apart in a shower of snow on top of him.

It was cold. The ice in them was cold. Where they touched you, it went into you. One got a hand on his shoulder for half a second before Azac's shield knocked it off, and the cold went down his arm like water and his fingers wouldn't close for a minute after. He didn't stop.

Ahead of him, in the line, he saw Declan.

Declan was holding. Not like on floor 9, a light in the dark. Like on floor 4 must have been. Like a tank. Shield up, overlapping the man on his left, a hold-folk woman with grey braids, his feet set, his sword going out low and coming back. And on his right, Gabe. With his old shield's edge across Declan's right side, covering it. Every time a Rimed came over the top at the place where they were standing, it came down on two shields that overlapped, and couldn't find the gap, and Declan's sword went up into it.

They didn't say anything to each other. They didn't need to. Rui Xiang watched them for one second, two, in John's white light and the sideways snow, and they moved like one thing. Like they'd been doing it for years.

*For years*, thought Rui Xiang, and filed it.

Then he heard Xav move.

***

You couldn't hear Xav move. That was the thing about Xav. He waited. He'd always waited, since the first day of poly, in every fight, in every game, at every door. Waited for the thing in front of him to commit, all the way, and then moved once. *Wait. Then move once. It's enough.* He'd taught Declan half of it. Somebody else had taught Declan the rest.

Rui Xiang heard him move now, because he moved too early.

A Rimed came over the wall in front of the second rank, in a gap where a hold-folk man had gone down with frost on his face, and Xav went for it. Before it had its feet. Before it had committed to anything. Before it had done anything at all but get its long arm over the parapet. He went in fast, his sword out, the way Declan used to go in at seventeen, before Xav ever taught him anything.

And the Rimed wasn't committed. So it moved. Its arm came round, the old gauntlet, the long ice fingers, and caught Xav across the chest and threw him back into the second rank like a sack.

Rui Xiang was there before he hit the stones. He didn't think. He went under the Rimed's other arm, up into the hollow of the breastplate, and found the black stone, and broke it, and the thing came down on both of them in a heap of snow and old iron.

"*Xav*." Malcolm. Already there. Hands already on Xav's chest, where the frost had gone in through his coat in the shape of five long fingers, white. "*Xav*, don't move—"

Xav was looking at the sky. His face had gone a strange colour. Not the cold. Something else.

"I moved early," he said.

"I saw," said Rui Xiang.

"I never move early."

"I know."

Xav shut his eyes.

Malcolm's healing went in, warm, slow, ten seconds, twenty. The white marks on Xav's chest went pink. Xav breathed. And over Malcolm's bent head, his eyes opened again, and found Rui Xiang's, and Rui Xiang saw what was in them.

Then Brenna shouted, very loud, from further down the wall:

"*TAMM*—"

***

He was third on the right.

Rui Xiang had said he'd be near. He'd been near. He'd been near for an hour. And then Xav had moved too early, and Rui Xiang had gone, and he hadn't been near.

He turned round and saw it. A Rimed, a big one, over the parapet right on top of the line where Tamm was standing. The boy in the grey cap too big for him, with the shield too big for him, overlapping the man on his left, exactly right. Holding. Leaning into it, the way he'd been taught, with his face set. And the Rimed's long arm coming down over the top of the shields, not at the shields, *over* them, the way the hand had come round Gabe's shield on floor 9, the long ice fingers reaching down behind the shield, into the line. Onto Tamm's chest.

And closing.

Rui Xiang was there in four steps. He went up the Rimed's arm like a ladder and put his knife into the hollow from above, down through the neck of the old breastplate, and found the black stone, and broke it. It came apart. Snow and ice and old iron, everywhere, and the hand on Tamm's chest came apart with it.

Tamm went down.

He went down slowly. Onto his knees, behind his shield, in the line. And then sideways. The shield still on his arm. Still up. Still overlapping the man on his left.

Rui Xiang was on his knees beside him in the snow. He didn't remember getting there.

The front of Tamm's coat was white. Frost. In the shape of a hand, long fingers, spread across the middle of his chest. And under it, Rui Xiang could feel it through his glove when he put his hand there, there was nothing. No heat. Cold going in and in, and nothing coming back out to meet it.

"*Malcolm*—" said Rui Xiang. "*Malcolm, here*—"

Malcolm came. He came fast, with his hands still glowing from Xav. He knelt in the snow and put his hands on the white frost on Tamm's chest and the healing went in, warm, the way it always went in.

And it didn't go anywhere.

Rui Xiang watched Malcolm's face. He watched him feel it. The healing going in, warm, and there being nothing for it to agree with. Nothing trying to close. Nothing trying to do anything. Malcolm's face went from frightened to something else, very slowly, and his hands stayed where they were for a long time after there was any point.

Tamm was looking up. At the snow coming down, sideways, out of the dark, in John's white light. His mouth was open a little. His eyes were open. He still had his shield on his arm.

He'd been seventeen. He'd learned to spin a knife over his knuckles that afternoon. On the twelfth try.

***

The Rimed stopped coming a little before midnight.

They didn't retreat. They just stopped. The ones still on the slope below the wall stood where they were in the dark, and the cracking went quiet, and then the wind came back, hard, down the pass from the high side, full of ice. And when John sent a mote out, they were gone. Nothing on the slope but snow and scattered armour and the marks of long fingers in the cracks between the stones of the wall.

"That's the first night," said Brenna. She was standing over Tamm. "It'll be back. Every night it snows, now. Till spring." She looked down at the boy. "They always take one on the first night. Always. Nine hundred years."

Nobody said anything.

Two hold-folk came with a long board, and lifted Tamm onto it. Gently. Still with his shield on his arm. They didn't take it off. And they carried him down off the fifth wall, along the parapet, and down the stair, and the line opened for them as they went, and every hold-folk man and woman on the wall turned their head as he passed, and put a fist to their chest. Just once.

Rui Xiang followed them down. He didn't decide to.

***

They carved him into the wall that night.

Not the Long Wall at the bottom of the pass. The fifth wall had its own tunnel through it, its own names, and they went in there in the middle of the night with a lantern and a chisel, and a woman who was the carver for the fifth wall knelt on the stone in the light and cut his name into an empty place on the tunnel wall. Low down. Near the floor. Below a long line of other names.

TAMM, SON OF ANSA. FIFTH WALL. FIRST NIGHT.

And then a short line under it. The line you put under a thing to show it's finished.

Rui Xiang stood at the back and watched her do it. It took a long time. Every letter. Square and careful and deep, the chisel going *tik, tik, tik* in the stone, and nobody saying anything. The hold-folk who'd carried him stood with their fists to their chests the whole time. Brenna stood with her shield on her back and her eyes shut.

On floor 8 the dead had gone over the Edge in boats. On floor 9 the Grow had covered them by morning. On floor 4 they'd stayed where they fell, and the thirty-five had dug two graves that weren't for anybody, in the red stone below the first square, because it was the only thing anybody could think to do.

Here, somebody knelt in the middle of the night and cut your name into a wall. So that it would still be there in nine hundred years. So that somebody would read it out loud on the longest night, every year, forever, in a list that took all night to read.

He'd never seen anything so sad in his life. Or anything so kind.

When the carver had finished, and stood up, and the hold-folk had gone, he was still standing there. Looking at the name. At the line under it.

Somebody else was there too.

***

Declan was standing a little way along the tunnel, in the dark past the lantern, reading the wall.

Not Tamm's name. The others. All of them. The way he'd read the Long Wall at the bottom of the pass on the first day. Fast. Along a line and down to the next and along. His eyes going over the names in the lantern light, hundreds of them, the way they went over a window, in half a heartbeat each.

As if he was looking for one.

Rui Xiang watched him do it for a long time. Long enough to be sure. Then he said, quietly, so it wouldn't echo:

"What are you looking for?"

Declan stopped reading.

He didn't turn round straight away. He stood there with his back to Rui Xiang and his hand flat on the stone beside a name, the way Brenna touched the Long Wall.

"Nothing," he said.

"You're reading every name."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

Declan was quiet for a long time.

"Making sure," he said.

"Of what?"

Declan turned round. In the lantern light his face was very tired, and very young, and there was snow melting in his hair.

"That none of them are ours," he said.

Rui Xiang looked at him.

*Ours.* As if one of them might be. As if there was any way in the world a name in a wall that had been here nine hundred years could be one of the thirty-six who'd come up a stair four days ago. As if he'd thought, for a second, that it could be.

"Why would they be?" said Rui Xiang.

Something went across Declan's face. Rui Xiang read it. He couldn't help it. He'd been reading Declan's face since they were seventeen, in a classroom, in calls, across a hundred late nights. He knew all of it. And he read this one, and didn't know what it was, and that frightened him more than anything on the wall had.

"They wouldn't," said Declan.

And then, before Rui Xiang could say anything else, very quietly, the way he'd said it once in a bakery on floor 7 with Rui Xiang's knife going still over his knuckles:

"Sorry, RX."

Rui Xiang's hand stopped. He hadn't known he'd been spinning the knife. He looked down at it.

"For what?"

"For not being near," said Declan. "He was third on the right. I was ninth. I couldn't—" He stopped. "I heard Brenna ask you. To be near. I heard you say you would."

"I was near," said Rui Xiang. "Then Xav moved early."

"I know."

"I went to Xav."

"I know," said Declan. "You were right to."

He came back along the tunnel. He stopped beside Rui Xiang in front of Tamm's name, fresh, the chips of stone still on the floor under it. And stood there. Not saying anything.

"He learned to spin a knife this afternoon," said Rui Xiang. "Over his knuckles. He dropped it eleven times."

Declan looked at him.

"Twelfth try," said Rui Xiang. "He got it on the twelfth."

He didn't know why he said it. It didn't matter. It was the only thing he knew about him. Declan didn't say anything. He just stood there beside him in the tunnel in the lantern light, in front of a name, and after a while put his hand on Rui Xiang's shoulder, and left it there.

***

He found Xav on the barracks roof, near dawn.

He'd known he would. Xav went up when he was carrying something. He'd done it at poly, on the roof of the engineering block, in the rain, the night before every exam he was going to do well in. He was up there now, on the flat roof of the barracks on the second level, sitting against the chimney with the warmth of the Breath coming up through it into his back, with his coat open and the pink marks of five long fingers still on his chest under his shirt.

Rui Xiang sat down next to him. The roof was warm. The sky over the low end of the pass was going grey.

"You moved early," he said.

"I know."

"You never move early. Not once. Not in nine years."

"I know, Rui Xiang."

They sat there. The snow had stopped. The wind was still coming down from the high side, over the top of the Bastion, hard and cold, but it came over them and didn't touch them, in the lee of the chimney.

"You're holding something," said Rui Xiang. "With both hands. You've been holding it for a hundred days."

Xav didn't say anything.

"I don't want to know what it is," said Rui Xiang. "It's not mine. I'm not asking." He looked at his knife. He spun it, once, over the first knuckle, under the second, over the third. Caught it. "But you've been holding it so tight you can't use your hands for anything else. And tonight you moved early, and somebody else had to catch you, and while they were catching you they weren't where they'd said they'd be."

Xav turned his head and looked at him.

"That's not on you," said Rui Xiang. "I want to say that. Tamm's not on you. A Rimed killed Tamm. Nine hundred years of them. Brenna said they always take one." He spun the knife again. "But you're going to drop it, Xav. Whatever it is. You're going to drop it in the middle of something worse than tonight. And somebody's going to be third on the right."

Xav was quiet for a very long time.

"I promised somebody," he said at last, "I'd put it down in one place. One place. First. Before anywhere else." His voice was very low. "And the place isn't ready. And I don't know if it ever will be. And every day I don't, the thing I promised gets more wrong."

Rui Xiang thought about Gabe. Standing on Declan's right, on the wall, with his shield's edge across Declan's side. Not asking anything. Not for four days.

He didn't say it. He didn't need to. He saw Xav think it too.

"Then put it down," said Rui Xiang. "In the place. Even if it's not ready." He put the knife away. "Before it puts you down."

Xav didn't answer. He sat against the warm chimney with his hand flat on his chest, over the pink marks, and looked at the grey coming up over the low end of the pass.

A window came up in front of Rui Xiang's face. Small and blue in the grey light.

He looked at the little padlock beside the forty-five for a long time.

The first of them to it. He'd been first to twenty-five, too, on floor 4. Declan had told him once, at seventeen, laughing, after the hundredth time Rui Xiang had beaten one of his scores by one point and then lost the next forty: *you always get there first, RX. You just don't know what to do when you're there.*

He shut the window.

Beside him, Xav got up. Slowly. Stiff from the cold and the frost and the long night. He stood on the roof with his coat open and looked down, over the edge, at the street below. At the door of the barracks. Where, in a minute or two, Gabe would come out, the way he did every morning now, before anybody else was up, and go down. To the Breath. To anvil nine.

"Thanks, Rui Xiang," said Xav.

He didn't go down yet. He stood there. Waiting.

Rui Xiang watched him wait. And for the first time in nine years, he couldn't tell what Xav was waiting for.
