Ina had a rule about the mask, and the rule was: not unless someone would die.

He'd made it on floor 2. He'd broken it once, on floor 2, for Winter. And since then he'd worn the thing pushed up on top of his head like a pair of sunglasses, where it could see the sky and nobody could see it. He pulled it down for smells and for very bright light and, once, on floor 4, for the dust. He told himself those didn't count.

They didn't. He knew what counted.

What counted was the fire.

***

The trial came on the hundred-and-fourteenth day, in the evening. The sky over the Windward Reaches had gone a colour he hadn't seen on floor 5 before.

He was sitting on the edge of the island outside the inn with Winter, watching it. She was cleaning her bow and he was pretending to read and they were both, really, watching the sky. It had gone the yellow-grey of an old bruise all along the western edge, low, over the cloud. The wind had dropped right away to nothing, which on floor 5 never happened. The gulls had gone quiet. All forty of Aleaka's, on the inn's roof ridge, in a row, not screaming.

"That's weather," said Winter.

"That's a great deal of weather," Ina agreed.

Down in Tether, people were tying things down. More than usual. Doubling the ropes on the roofs, taking the kites in, carrying chairs and baskets and washing indoors. Nan Gully had taken the chain-ferry basket in off the cable for the first time since they'd come, and lashed it to the winch post with three ropes. Otta's apprentices were running from bridge to bridge with coils of chain.

"Storm," said a fisherman, going past them with a net. He didn't stop. "Cold one. Comes off the cloud once a season. You lot want to be inside by dark."

Ina opened his window to check the time, and the padlock was there beside his twenty-five, where it had been for six days. He looked at it the way he'd been looking at it all week: sideways, without quite meeting its eye.

He asked for the trial. He didn't know why then, of all times. Something about the sky.

[ Tier 2 trial · Mage
Easy: Keep a fire alight in the rain.
Normal: Keep a fire alight through the storm.
]

[ Hard: Onibi
The fire they're afraid of. Tonight, on the Kitecliff.
Keep them warm until morning. Alone.
]

He read the last line four times.

"Winter," he said.

She looked over at his window. She read it. Her face, which was usually doing whatever it felt like, went very still.

"*Alone*," she said.

"Yes."

"We voted on that. Eight days ago. All thirty-five."

"We voted on *fighting* alone." He looked at the window. "It doesn't say fight. It says keep them warm."

"Keep *who* warm?"

He looked northwest. Across the Reaches, past a dozen islands, the Kitecliff stood up white against the bruise-coloured sky, with its round stone huts on top, roped down, small as teeth from here.

"Seven people," said Ina, "with feathers and hollow bones, on top of a cliff, in a cold storm that comes once a season."

Winter looked at the Kitecliff for a long moment.

"They'll freeze," she said.

"Yes," said Ina. "I rather think that's the point."

***

Drew didn't like it. Ina hadn't expected him to.

They had it out in the inn's long room, fast, because the light was going and the wind was starting to come back from the wrong direction. Drew stood at the slate with his arms folded. Ina stood in front of him with the trial window still open in the air between them, *Alone* in it like a stone in a shoe.

"Nobody goes alone," said Drew. "We said."

"It's not a fight. It's a fire."

"It's a cliff in a storm."

"It's a trial," said Ina. "If I take somebody, it fails. If I don't take it at all, those seven people are on that cliff tonight anyway, trial or no trial, and the fisherman says it's a cold one." He kept his voice level. That was his whole trick, really, always had been. You said the true thing calmly, in a nice voice, and people listened. "I'm a fire mage, Drew. I'm the only one who can do it. I'm asking you to let me."

Drew looked at him for a long time.

"Then somebody sits at the bottom of the ladder," he said. "All night. Close enough to hear if you shout. Not in the trial. Just there."

"Me," said Winter.

Nobody argued with her. Nobody ever did, when she used that voice.

***

The storm hit while they were still on the second bridge.

It came off the cloud from the west like a door slamming. One moment the air was dead and yellow. The next it was full of sleet, coming sideways, so cold it hurt, so hard you couldn't see the next bridge post. The chains screamed. The planks bucked. Ina went down onto his knees with both arms round the rail chain and the sleet hammering on his back. Winter beside him, her silver hair whipping round both of them like a flag. They didn't speak. There was no point. They went hand over hand along the rail chain on their knees, feeling for the gusts the way Gabe had taught them, and they didn't let go.

It took them two hours to reach the foot of the Kitecliff. It should have taken one.

The chain ladder went up into the dark. The sleet was coming down it like water down a drain. At the top, very faint, there should have been the glow of the kite-folk's hearths through the hut doors. Small, sunken fire pits, down in the floor, out of the wind. Ina had seen them on the day Dennis took the fish.

There wasn't any glow.

"Ina," said Winter.

"I see it."

"There's no light."

"I know." He took hold of the first rung. It was so cold it burned. "They've gone out."

She grabbed his arm.

"Ina." She had to shout it, right in his ear, over the storm. "*The mask.*"

He looked at her. Her face was white with cold and sleet in her eyelashes. She had that look on her face she'd had on floor 2, when he'd put the mask on and the fire had gone white and blue and burned the wolf off her.

*Put it on more. I'd rather look like the bad guy and be alive.*

"I know," said Ina.

He went up.

***

The village on top of the Kitecliff was dark and screaming.

Not people. The wind. It came across the cliff top flat and hard, full of sleet, and went round the round stone huts howling. It tore at the turf roofs. It had already torn the kites off half their poles and thrown them God knew where. The ropes holding the huts down were singing like wires. And every hut was dark.

He found them in the biggest one. The one in the middle. He pushed the door open against the wind and fell inside, and the wind came in with him and went round the room like an animal. In the dark he heard something. A small, high, frightened sound. And then a dry old voice that said, very calmly, "*Shut the door.*"

He shut it. He put his back against it.

It was black. Absolutely black. He could hear them breathing, though. Shallow, fast. Too fast. The breathing of people who were very cold and going colder. And under it, a sound like teeth chattering. He could smell wet feathers and smoke and old cold ash.

He held up his hand and made a small flame.

Orange. Ordinary. The fire he'd had since floor 1, the one he could do in his sleep. It came up out of his palm the size of a candle and lit the room.

Seven of them. Huddled against the far wall in a heap, all wrapped together in the long grey coats and each other's arms, feathers soaked flat. The hearth in the middle of the floor was a sunken pit full of black wet ash. The storm had come down the smoke-hole and drowned it. The boy was in the middle of the heap, the one who did loops. His lips were blue. His eyes were half shut.

And they were all looking at Ina. At the flame in his hand. With their large dark bird's eyes very wide.

They were afraid of it.

He saw that at once. Not afraid of *him*, not yet. Afraid of the fire. The way you'd be afraid of fire if you were made of feathers and hollow bone and lived your whole life on a cliff in the wind, where one spark in a dry turf roof could take the whole village. Their hearths were small and sunken for a reason. They'd built their whole lives round keeping fire *small*.

And they needed it. Now. All of it. Or the boy was going to die before morning.

"Pell," said Ina.

The old one was at the edge of the heap, nearest him, with one long arm round the boy. The dark eyes looked at him over the boy's head.

"You're the fire one," said Pell. "From the thirty-five."

"Yes."

"Your knight leaned." The old voice was steady. Only steady. "What do you do?"

Ina looked at the flame in his hand. Small. Orange. The draught from the smoke-hole was already bending it nearly flat. He fed it, and it grew a little, and the draught caught it and bent it again, and a gust came down the hole, and it went out.

Dark.

The boy made a sound.

***

He tried it the ordinary way first. He was always going to.

He knelt by the sunken hearth and scraped the wet ash out with his hands, and found the driest of the turf they kept stacked by the wall. He lit it, orange, careful, small, the way he'd lit a hundred fires on a hundred evenings since the white room. He fed it. It caught. It grew. Light came up into the room, warm and orange, and the kite-folk at the wall shrank back from it, all seven, as one, wings tight.

And then the storm came down the smoke-hole and put it out.

He lit it again. Bigger. It went out again. He lit it again and held his hands over it, shielding it, the way Yan said he shielded people's candles. It lasted a minute. Then a gust came down the hole like a fist and blew the turf apart and scattered sparks across the floor. The kite-folk screamed, a high terrible bird's scream, all of them at once. One of the old ones beat out a spark on the edge of his coat with his bare hand, frantic.

Dark again. Cold again. Colder. Every time it went out, colder.

[ Trial · failing ]

He knelt in the dark by the wet hearth with his hands burned and shaking.

He knew. He'd known on the bridge. He'd known when Winter grabbed his arm. He'd known since floor 2.

The mask was on top of his head. Pushed up, the way it always was, grey, cold, the cords hanging down behind. The face on it looked up at the black roof. Red-rimmed eyes. A wide fanged grin. The face of a thing that ate children in old stories, on the other side of the world from here.

When he wore it, the fire changed. It went white, and blue at the edges. It burned hotter than anything he'd ever made, and it didn't go out. Not in wind. Not in rain. It hadn't gone out on floor 2, in a blizzard, on a wolf. It had burned straight through the snow like the snow wasn't there.

And when he wore it, people looked at him differently.

Not his friends. Not really. Winter had looked at him on floor 2 and said *put it on more*. But other people. Larchwick's dogs, barking and backing away. A child in Larchwick, on floor 2, who'd seen him come back from the wolf with the mask down, and screamed, and run. A trapper who'd stepped back. The way the whole room went quiet for half a second when he walked in with it on, before they remembered it was him.

He'd spent his whole life being liked. That was his trick, his only real one. A good voice, the right words, a face people wanted to listen to. He'd talked his way into rooms and out of trouble since he was small. He made people comfortable. That was who he was.

And the mask took his face away and gave him one that made people afraid.

*Not unless someone would die.*

The boy, in the dark, by the wall, had gone quiet. That was worse than the crying.

Ina reached up and pulled the mask down over his face.

***

The fire came out of him white.

He didn't light it. It *came*, the way water comes out of a broken pipe. Up out of his chest, down his arms, out through his palms into the sunken hearth, and it was white. White as a star, with blue round the edges, so bright the whole round hut leapt up out of the dark at once. Every stone of the walls, every rope in the roof, every feather on the seven people against the wall. It didn't flicker. It didn't bend. The storm came down the smoke-hole like a fist and hit it, and it didn't even move. It stood up out of the wet ash of the hearth like a white flower, and the sleet coming down the hole hissed into steam a metre above it and never touched it at all.

Heat came off it. Real heat. Ina felt it on his hands, through the mask, on his chest.

And the kite-folk screamed.

All seven. The worst sound yet. They went back against the wall so hard the stones grated, wings up, arms over their faces. The boy turned his face into Pell's coat and screamed and screamed and wouldn't stop. And Ina knew without having to look why.

It wasn't the fire. Or not only the fire.

It was him. Kneeling over a white blaze, in the dark, in a grey mask with red-rimmed eyes and a wide fanged grin, and his horn coming up out of the top of it, purple in the white light. A demon at a fire. A thing out of a story.

He was what they were afraid of. Exactly what. Fire, and a monster's face.

[ Keep them warm until morning. ]

He stayed where he was.

***

It was the longest night of his life. He'd thought, on the bridge, that nothing could be longer than the bridge. He'd been wrong.

He knelt by the white fire and held it. That was all. He didn't talk. He'd thought, at first, that he could talk them round. That was what he did, wasn't it, talk people round, the nice voice, the right words. He'd said, *It's all right, it's me, I'm not going to hurt you, I'm here to help*. The voice had come out through the mask strange and muffled and wrong, and the boy had screamed louder. So he'd stopped. He knelt there, in silence, with a monster's face, and held the fire, and let them be afraid of him.

The storm went on. Hour after hour. It tore a whole section of turf off the roof at one point. Sleet came pouring in, straight down onto the white fire, and hissed away to nothing, and the fire didn't even notice. It hammered on the walls. It screamed in the ropes. And inside the round stone hut it was warm. For the first time all night, it was warm. The heat went out from the white fire across the floor and up the walls and into the heap of wet grey people against the far side.

They didn't come closer. Not at first.

He held it.

He thought about things, to pass the time. He thought about Winter, at the bottom of the ladder, in this. He hoped she'd found the overhang. He thought about floor 2. The wolf, and the blizzard, and the first time the fire had gone white, and the child in Larchwick who'd run screaming. He'd gone back to the inn that night and taken the mask off and hung it on the bedpost and lain looking at it for hours. *That's not my face. That's not me.*

He thought about Voyager.

He couldn't help it. Everybody did, these days. Ina hadn't been one of the ones who talked about him much. He'd met Ice through Yan, years back. They'd been friendly for a long time before they'd been friends. Ice had always thought he was cool, and said so, often, in a way that had made Ina feel slightly more like he was. He'd been on the edge of all of it. Not Gabe, not Deston. Not the ones who sat in the worn place.

But he thought about him now. Kneeling in a mask, holding a fire that frightened the people it was saving. He thought about a man on four floors, alone, standing in the Kiln's fire, killing a bird on a needle. Leaving things for them on benches. Never letting them see his face. Being, for a hundred and fourteen days, a name at the top of a list that half the group was a little afraid of. *How is he so strong. How did he know. What is he.*

And doing it anyway.

*I'd rather look like the bad guy and be alive.*

He held the fire.

***

The boy came first.

It was somewhere past the middle of the night. Ina didn't know when. He'd stopped feeling his knees a long time ago. The boy had stopped screaming hours before and gone quiet against Pell's coat, and Ina had been afraid for a while that the quiet meant something worse. Then he'd seen the small chest going up and down, and the lips weren't blue any more. They were pink. Warm.

And then the boy lifted his head off Pell's coat and looked at him.

At the mask. At the white fire. At Ina, kneeling there with his hands out over it.

He looked for a long time. Ina didn't move. He didn't speak. He'd learned that, tonight, if nothing else. Don't talk. Don't move. Just hold it.

The boy got up.

Pell's arm tightened round him. The boy said something, very soft, in a language of clicks and whistles Ina didn't know. Pell said something back. The boy said it again.

Pell let go.

The boy came across the floor of the hut. Slowly. On hands and knees, his patchy new feathers dragging. He stopped when he was close enough to the fire that the white light was full on his face. Then he looked up at Ina. At the red-rimmed eyes of the mask, and the fangs, and the purple horn.

And then he came round the fire, and sat down against Ina's side, under his arm, close as a cat. And put his head on Ina's knee.

He was asleep in about a minute.

Ina knelt there with a child of a people who'd never seen him before asleep on his knee, warm, alive. He held the fire, and couldn't see anything for a while, because there was water inside the mask and nowhere for it to go.

By dawn they were all round him. All seven. In a ring round the white fire, close, wings loosely folded, warm. The oldest of them sat on his other side with a hand on his shoulder, very light, feathers against his sleeve. Pell sat across the fire from him and watched him over it the whole time, with their large dark bird's eyes. Pell didn't say a word.

The storm blew itself out a little before sunrise. Ina felt it go. The wind dropped, and the ropes stopped singing, and the sleet stopped hissing down the smoke-hole. The silence afterwards was enormous.

Grey light came in through the gap in the turf roof.

He let it go. The white fire sank down into the hearth and went out, gently, like a lamp turned down. The grey morning was the only light in the room. The boy on his knee stirred and didn't wake.

He reached up and pushed the mask back on top of his head.

The cold dawn air touched his face. His own face. Wet.

Across the dead hearth, Pell looked at him.

"Your knight leaned," said Pell. Their dry voice was very quiet, so as not to wake the boy. "On the wind. Never still. Never fighting." They looked at the mask on top of his head, the grin turned up to the broken roof. "You burn."

"Yes," said Ina. His voice came out hoarse. "I suppose I do."

"With a face the boy will have dreams about," said Pell.

"Yes."

"He'll dream it kept him warm," said Pell. "That's a good dream." They looked at Ina a long moment. "You were frightened of it too. All night. I watched."

"Yes," said Ina.

"And you kept it on."

Ina looked down at the boy asleep on his knee.

"Somebody told me," he said, "that she'd rather I looked like the bad guy and was alive." He swallowed. "I didn't understand it, then. I thought she meant me. Alive." He looked at the cold hearth. "She meant everyone else."

***

Winter was at the bottom of the chain ladder when he came down. Of course she was. She was sitting under a little overhang in the rock with her knees up and her bow across them. She was soaked through, white with cold, with sleet in her silver hair and her black horns. She hadn't moved all night. She stood up when she saw him and looked at his face.

At the mask, on top of his head. At his eyes.

"It's on top," she said.

"It came off at dawn."

"It was on all night." It wasn't a question. She'd seen the light. Of course she had. A white light through the turf roof on top of the Kitecliff, all night, in a storm, so bright it must have been visible from Tether. "I watched it. The whole time. It never went out."

"No," said Ina. "It doesn't, now."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something Winter never did. She stepped forward and put her arms round him, briefly, hard, soaking wet, and stepped back again.

"Good," she said. Bluntly. As if she'd just said something about the weather. She shouldered her bow. "Breakfast. I'm *freezing*, Ina, I've been sat in a crack in a rock for nine hours, you owe me eggs."

They walked back to Tether across the bridges in the first sun, with the Reaches steaming round them, every island wet and shining. Halfway across the second bridge he realised he'd pulled the mask down over his face again without thinking, against the glare of the low sun off the wet planks. He didn't push it back up.

At the inn, Chris was at the long table with Thea and Hyvred, the three of them bent over two notebooks. They had the look of people who'd been up all night too. Kei was beside them with Dose on his shoulders, and Hako, leaning back in a chair with his arms folded. They stopped talking when Ina and Winter came in. Then Chris looked up and saw the mask, and the mask's grin, and Ina's eyes behind it. His face changed.

"You passed," said Chris.

"I passed."

Chris looked at him for a second longer. Then he looked at Winter, who'd gone straight past him to the kitchen to shout about eggs. Then back at the notebooks on the table, and at Thea. Something passed between them that Ina didn't follow.

"Winter," called Chris, toward the kitchen. "When you've eaten. We need you."

"What for?" Winter's voice came back, through a door, already chewing.

Chris glanced at Thea. Thea, after a moment, nodded.

"Club meeting," said Chris.

Ina stood in the doorway with the mask down over his face, and looked at the five of them at the table, and the two notebooks. *Club.* He didn't ask. He was too tired. He went and sat at the far end of the table by himself, in the sun, with the mask on, and nobody in the room flinched.

That was new, too.
