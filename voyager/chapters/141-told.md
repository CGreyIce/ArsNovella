Cursiffe wrote it down before he slept, because that was the rule.

Not in the ink book. In the other one. The one with VOYAGER on the cover, that had nothing in it he hadn't seen or heard himself, or been told by somebody who had. He sat on his blanket at the end of the copy-room, under the one lamp they were allowed in a building full of Rolls, with the iron key on its cord round his neck and the eel-oil ink, and wrote small and square.

*Day 357. At the rock in the Lantern Market, before the teller came. Ice: "There's an archive. They wouldn't let us in. Somebody tried every morning. For a week." He looked at me while he said the last part. Less than a second. (Seen.)*

*Day 357. At the well's lid. The grey clerk wrote his line longer than anybody's. Two lines. Hawise read it and went white. The clerk: "Told." Hawise: "What?" The clerk: "Already told." (Heard. Everyone did.)*

*Day 359. On the Wellgate. A man threw mud with a stone in it. It hit him on the back of the left shoulder, hard. He didn't turn round. (Wendy. Yan. Wendy says you couldn't tell.)*

*Day 362. At dusk, after the door-roll. He stood at the bottom of the Tellhouse steps and looked up at me writing at the door. He didn't come up. (Wendy. She says she'd never seen that face on him. She says it was like watching a door you've seen stay shut a great many times start to move.)*

*Day 362. In the copy-room. When I asked Thea for the lantern, he said: "I'll be on the step." (Heard.)*

He read them back. Then he turned back through the book and read the others. All of them. The way he did, most nights now. From floor 6. From *Hana: "When?"* and *two months, every night* and *it was four days north* and *the fourth step*, all the way to tonight.

They pointed the same way. They'd been pointing the same way for a long time. Like iron filings on a page with a magnet underneath, every one of them lying down in the same direction, so that you could see the shape of the thing underneath without ever seeing the thing.

*Somebody tried every morning. For a week.*

Cursiffe had been to Askew's desk every morning for four days.

He sat with that. With the pen in his hand.

He didn't write anything else. He'd made himself a rule, on the ship, in Ottery's galley: you didn't write what you'd worked out. You wrote what you'd seen. What you'd worked out was yours, and it might be wrong, and if it was right it wasn't yours anyway. It was Ice's. And Ice hadn't said it.

He closed the book, and put it inside his shirt, next to the leaf.

The leaf was so hot now he'd had to wrap it in a handkerchief. He lay down with his hand over it and felt it through the cloth, all night, like a second heart.

***

They went down at the opening bell.

The Reading Hall was full again. Word had got round about that too. Tellers at the long tables pretending to read, and not reading. Hawise at the end of the nearest one, with her arms folded. Salty and Tiger by the great door, which didn't squeak. Gabe at the table closest to the back, with Hearthwall leaning against the bench beside him and his chin on his fist. And at the very back of the hall, as always, a grey robe with a book on its arm.

Askew was waiting at her desk with her stick. Orlo was beside her with a satchel over each shoulder, full of paper and ink and pens, so excited he couldn't stand still.

Cursiffe took the key from round his neck, and put it in the lock of the black door, and turned it.

It turned as if it had been oiled yesterday. The door swung inward on silent hinges, and there was a stair behind it, going down. Stone, and narrow, and cut straight into the hill. And a smell came up it that Cursiffe knew at once, from every cellar and every old library he'd ever been in, on any floor or before. Dust, and dry, and paper.

There was a step just inside the door, before the stair began. A wide flat stone landing, worn hollow in the middle.

"He can sit there," said Askew, behind him. "The step's the hall's. Not below it." She looked at Ice. "Not one foot below it."

"No," said Ice.

He sat down on it.

He'd come in with Thea and Wendy and Yan, and he'd said nothing at all since the copy-room. He sat down on the worn stone with his back against the door frame and his knees up and the grey cord on his wrist, and Wendy sat down beside him with her bow across her knees, and that was the step taken.

Thea held up the lantern.

It came on as she lifted it. It always did. Not lit, because there was nothing in it to light. It just *was* on, all at once, the way your eyes are open. A deep warm amber glow from inside the thick pale glass, spreading out round her in a soft circle, onto the stone and the stair and the bottom of the black door, and onto Ice's face, where he sat at her feet looking up.

They didn't say anything to each other. Cursiffe was right beside them and they didn't say anything at all.

Ice's hands were on his knees. Open. Cursiffe saw them close, slowly. And then, slowly, open again.

Thea went down first, because she had the light.

Then Askew, with her stick. Then Orlo. Then Yan, with Suzu round his neck, and nothing in his hands; Askew had made him leave Working On It against her desk, *nothing with an edge below the First Wall either*, and he'd done it without a word.

Cursiffe went last.

As he stepped past him onto the first stair, Ice spoke. Quietly. Not to anybody else.

"You got in," he said.

Cursiffe stopped, on the first stair, and looked back at him.

Ice was looking at the iron key in Cursiffe's hand. Not at his face. At the key. With an expression Cursiffe didn't have a word for and didn't try to find one for.

"Yes," said Cursiffe.

Ice nodded, once.

"Good," he said.

Cursiffe went down. He didn't write it. He didn't need to. He knew he'd remember it exactly.

***

The stair went down a long way, and then it stopped being a stair and became a street.

That was the first thing Cursiffe hadn't expected. He'd thought it would be cellars. Rooms. A library, with shelves. It wasn't. At the bottom of the first stair there was a doorway, and through the doorway there was a *street*. Narrow, and cobbled, and roofed over by the hill, with old houses down both sides of it, two and three storeys high, with their doors gone and their windows empty, going off into the dark beyond the lantern's light. A whole street. Under the city.

"The First Wall," said Askew. Her stick tapped on the cobbles. "This was Tell, once. The Tell before this one. Two hundred years ago, they built over it, and kept the Rolls in it."

Cursiffe looked in at the first doorway they passed. The room inside, which had been somebody's front room, was lined from the floor to the ceiling with roll-cases. Every wall. Pigeonholes full of rolls, tagged, in rows. And the room behind it the same. And the stairs up to the next floor had cases on them, step by step, like books on a staircase in a house where nobody has room.

"Every house is a year," said Orlo. He couldn't keep it in. "Or two, if the year was quiet. That's my great-great-grandad's year in that one, probably. I came down here once with my master when I was twelve. Only to the First Wall. Nobody comes past the Second except the Remembrancer, and she never does."

"I'm doing it now," said Askew.

"Yes, Remembrancer."

They went down. Through the First Tell, to the end of the street, where another stair went down through the floor of the last house. And at the bottom of that, another street, older. The houses lower, the stones bigger, the doorways rounder at the top. The Second Wall. And then a third stair, steep and worn, into the Third Tell, where the houses were low and the doorways round and the stones of the walls the size of sea chests.

At the bottom of the first slope, the walls went black at the foot. A band of it, the height of a man, running along under both sides of the street, under the doorsteps, under everything, as if the city had been set down on top of something scorched.

"The Fire," said Askew, as they passed it. "Four hundred years. The Tell under this one burned in a night, Rolls and all, and they built the Third on the ash." She said it without any feeling at all, the way you say *it rained on Tuesday*. "There's nothing below that. Nothing that's kept."

Cursiffe looked at the black band going past at the height of his knees. A layer. A whole city, burned, and built over, and kept.

*It's a book*, he'd said, at the market, looking at the cut side of the hill. *Shut sideways*.

He hadn't known how true it was.

***

Yan didn't say anything until the Third Wall.

He walked just behind Thea, in the edge of the lantern's light, and looked at everything, and said nothing, and Cursiffe walked behind him and watched him look. Yan in the dark was a different person from Yan in the light. Cursiffe had noticed it on the Salt. His eyes went somewhere else.

"What can you see?" said Cursiffe, at the bottom of the third stair.

Yan stopped.

"Nothing," he said. "There's nothing down here. Not anything. No lights at all." He was quiet a moment. "It's the emptiest place I've ever been. Emptier than the Salt. The Salt had things under it." He looked round, at the dark doorways. "This is just *kept*."

"Not even a mouse?" said Orlo, interested.

"Not even a mouse." Yan looked at Askew. At Orlo. At Cursiffe. "Just you." And then at Thea. Or not at Thea. At the lantern in her hand.

"And that," he said.

Thea stopped walking.

"That's got one in it," said Yan. "Did you know? A light. In the lantern. It's always had one, I think. I've seen it before, but you never see it properly in the day." He was looking at the glass. "It's not like ours. It's not like the city's." He looked up at her face. "It's like his."

Thea stood very still in the middle of the street under the hill, with the lantern held out in front of her.

"I know," she said.

That was all she said. *I know*. Not *how do you know*, or *what do you mean*. And she started walking again, and the light went with her, and Yan went after it.

Cursiffe stood for a moment longer at the bottom of the stair.

He didn't write it. He didn't have a free hand. But he put it away, carefully, the way you'd put away a page you meant to copy out later, word for word.

***

Water Street was in the Third Tell, at the bottom of a long slope, and it was wet.

Not now. It hadn't been wet for two hundred years. But you could see where it had been. A line ran along both walls of the street at the height of Cursiffe's chest, dark brown, wavering, perfectly level, from one end of the street to the other, and everything below the line was stained and everything above it wasn't. The cobbles were furred with a pale dry crust. The air had a smell in it that the streets above hadn't had, under the dust. Old water. Old rot.

"The Wet Year," said Askew. "The river came in under the hill in the spring thaw, two hundred and ten years ago, and found Water Street. It was a street called Water Street. Nobody thought anything of the name." She tapped the furred cobbles with her stick. "By the time they got down here it was up to the second floors. They pumped it out for a month. Six years of Rolls. Three hundred and fifteen years ago to three hundred and ten, by our count." She looked down the street, into the dark beyond the lantern. "Mush."

She said it without any feeling at all. Cursiffe thought that was probably because she'd felt it all a long time ago, and finished.

Orlo had stopped talking.

They went into the fourth house. It had been a narrow house, three floors, with a little round window over the door. Inside, the front room was lined with roll-cases like all the others. But the rolls in them weren't rolls any more.

They were bricks.

Grey, swollen, misshapen bricks, fused into their pigeonholes, the paper and the spindles and the leather tags all run together into one solid lump in each hole, wrinkled and cracked and dry as old bread. Hundreds of them. A whole room of them. A whole street.

"The index says," said Askew, and took a small roll out of her coat, and unrolled it a little way, and held it to the lantern. "*Climbers, told, thirty. The spring of the year three hundred and twelve before this one. Left Roll and Right Roll. Water Street, the fourth house, the second case, the top shelf*." She rolled it up again. "That's what the index says. That's all anybody's known for two hundred years. That they came, and they were told, and it's in a brick on Water Street."

Cursiffe looked at the second case. At the top shelf.

Two bricks, side by side. Left and right.

The leaf in his shirt was so hot now that he could feel it through the handkerchief and through his shirt and through the skin of his chest. He put his hand in and took it out, handkerchief and all, and unwrapped it on his palm.

It was glowing. Very faintly. A dull warm red at the edges, like a coal you'd blown on once. And the words on it had changed.

He'd carried it since floor 5. It had said *Write what you saw. Keep it true*, and then on floor 6 *Read what remains. Write how it was*. He'd read it every morning. He knew every scratch on it.

It said something else now. And a window came up over it, in front of his eyes, the way they always did, and said the same.

Thea, beside him, was looking at the leaf. She didn't say anything.

Askew couldn't see windows. But she was looking at the leaf on his palm, glowing red in the amber light.

"Well," she said, quite quietly. "That's what a leaf wants."

***

He took the two bricks down himself. They came out of the pigeonholes with a dry grinding sound and were lighter than he'd expected, like pumice. He set them side by side on the sill of the little round window, under the lantern, and looked.

Mostly, they were nothing. Grey crust and cracks. But at the edges, where the rolls had been tightest wound, where the paper had been folded deepest into itself, the outer turns had dried and curled back, like the edge of a burnt page. And on the curls, here and there, there were words. Brown, faded, a few at a time. A word. Half a word. A line.

Two hands. Two different hands. The Left Roll in a long narrow sloping hand, the Right in a squarer one. And because the bricks had drowned differently, rolled differently, curled differently, the words that had lived on one were not the words that had lived on the other.

He read them all. He wrote down every one, in his book, with the eel-oil ink, exactly where it was on the curl. Every word. Every half-word. Every mark.

On the Left Roll:

*…up the well at noon in the…* and *…thirty, and were…* and *…a house at the foot of the…* and *…the stair under the Assize…*

On the Right Roll:

*…Lantern Market…* and *…by the Count's clerk and…* and *…asked the way to the Assize…* and *…autumn…* and, on a curl of its own, very clear, as if the water had chosen to leave it: *…One of them laughed…*

And at the very bottom of both. On the last curl of each, where the end of each roll had wrapped round the spindle. A line, in the teller's hand, small: *In climbers' marks. Not told.* And beside it, on each, something else.

Not the tellers' hands. Not any hand he'd seen in Tell. Round, and quick, and slanting the other way. A line of it. Mostly gone, on both. On the Left Roll, the first two marks of it, and one near the end. On the Right Roll, three marks in the middle, and two at the very end.

"Climbers' marks," said Orlo, peering. "That's what they called them. Nobody could read them." He sounded almost reverent. "My master said they were just patterns. That the climbers couldn't write, and made patterns instead, to be polite."

Thea had gone very still beside him with the lantern.

"They're letters," she said.

Orlo looked at her.

"They're *letters*," said Thea. "That's a T. And an H. That's—" She leaned in. Her voice had gone odd. "That's an R. That's how *we* write."

Nobody said anything for a moment, on Water Street, in the dark under the hill.

"Can you read it?" said Cursiffe.

Thea looked at the curls. At both of them. For a long time.

"No," she said. "There isn't enough. There's a T and an H at the start. Then nothing. Then *ft do* in the middle, on the other one. Then an R near the end, and a *th*." She shook her head. "It's a line. And then something at the end. A name, maybe. I can't—there isn't enough of it to *guess*."

"No," said Cursiffe. "There isn't."

He turned to Askew.

"I need everything else," he said. "Everything that's left. Anything that remembers it. Not just the Rolls."

***

Orlo sang it.

Cursiffe hadn't known he was going to ask until he asked. But Tiger had come back up into the copy-room the day before with a skipping rope and a song, and sung it at everybody until Hyvred threw a boot, and Cursiffe had written it down without thinking, because he wrote everything down. And standing on Water Street with two drowned bricks and a line of climbers' marks, he remembered the first words of it, and his hand went still.

"The skipping song," he said to Orlo. "The one the children sing in the yard."

Orlo looked at him blankly. Then, slowly, went pink.

"*Thirty up the well*?"

"All of it. Please."

Orlo looked at Askew. Askew was looking at Cursiffe, with an expression Cursiffe couldn't read.

"Sing it, boy," she said.

So Orlo Fenn, the fastest pen in the Tellhouse, stood in a drowned street under three dead cities and sang a skipping song, self-consciously, in a light pleasant tenor that went a bit flat on the high bits.

*Thirty up the well and thirty up the stair,*

*thirty in the Rolls and thirty in the air.*

*Thirteen, twenty-two, thirty-one, forty,*

*fifty-two, sixty-one, seventy-four,*

*eighty-eight, ninety-nine,*

*nothing, nothing—*

*out.*

He stopped. The last word went off down the street into the dark, and didn't come back.

"That's all of it," said Orlo. "You jump in on *thirty*, and if you're still in at *out*, you've won."

Cursiffe had written it down as he sang. He looked at the numbers on the page.

"What are they?" he said. "The numbers."

"Nothing," said Orlo. "They're just the song. Everybody knows them. My nan knew them. They don't *mean* anything."

"They mean something," said Askew.

Everybody looked at her.

She was leaning on her stick, in the edge of the lantern-light, with her walnut face very still.

"They're the cries," she said. "Every child in Tell knows the cries and not one of them knows it." She looked down the street, up the slope, towards the dark mouth of the next street over. "Orlo. Bell Street. The house with the bell over the door. The Reckoning Rolls, from three hundred and nine down to three hundred. And the one after. Bring them here."

***

They were dry. Bell Street was above the line. Orlo came back with an armful of them, breathing hard, rolls in good brown leather with their tags still on, and Askew unrolled them one at a time on the windowsill beside the bricks and found the place in each and held it to the lantern, and Cursiffe read.

The same line, once a year, at the Reckoning. In two hands.

*At the Reckoning, on the Assize steps, the Count's clerk cried: the climbers stand on the fortieth.*

*At the Reckoning, on the Assize steps, the Count's clerk cried: the climbers stand on the fifty-second.*

*… on the sixty-first.*

*… on the seventy-fourth.*

*… on the eighty-eighth.*

*… on the ninety-ninth.*

And then the next year. The same place in the Roll. The same two hands.

*At the Reckoning, the Count's clerk went up the Assize steps and opened the book and stood, and cried nothing, and came down.*

And the next. *Nothing cried.*

And the next. *Nothing cried.*

"Every year," said Askew. "Every Reckoning since. Three hundred years. A clerk goes up the Assize steps at the Reckoning and opens the book and stands there, in front of the whole city. And says nothing. And comes down." She rolled the last Roll up again. "When I was a girl, people still went up to watch. Nobody does now. It's just a thing the clerks do."

"The first three," said Cursiffe. "Thirteen, twenty-two, thirty-one."

"Drowned," said Askew. "Water Street. Three hundred and twelve, three hundred and eleven, three hundred and ten." She looked at the windowsill. At the bricks. "But every child in Tell knows them. Every child in Tell has known them for three hundred years. Jumping in and out of a rope." Her voice did something then, for the first time. Very slightly. "We kept them. We just didn't know we had."

Cursiffe stood and looked at the windowsill. At everything on it. At his book.

Two bricks. A handful of curled words in two hands. A teller's note, *not told*. A line of climbers' marks with most of it gone. A skipping song. Six dry Rolls and a long line of *nothing cried*. And, in his book, from the first day, in Yan's words, written down that night: *thirty scratches inside the well-house door, low, near the floor. Very old. Somebody counted.*

*Read what remains*, the leaf had said, on floor 6. *Write how it was.*

And now: *Tell again what was lost.*

"Orlo," he said. "I'm going to write it. How it was. From this. Everything that's here, and nothing that isn't." He looked at Orlo. "Will you write it too? On your own. Without looking at mine."

Orlo stared at him.

Then he unslung one of his satchels, and took out a roll of paper, and a horn of ink, and sat down on the dry furred cobbles of Water Street with the paper on his knee.

"Plain form," he said.

"Plain form," said Cursiffe.

***

They wrote it on the floor, a pace apart, with their backs to each other, in the lantern-light.

It took a long time. Cursiffe didn't hurry. He wrote the way he always wrote, small and square, every letter properly made. He didn't put in anything that wasn't on the windowsill or in his book. He didn't put in *seemed*, or *perhaps*, or *I think*. When he came to a gap, and didn't know, he didn't fill it with what it was probably. He left it.

Thea held the lantern high between them, so the light fell on both pages and neither of them could see the other's. Askew sat on the bottom step of the fourth house with her stick across her knees and her eyes shut. Yan stood in the doorway looking out at the dark of Water Street, where there was nothing, and was quiet.

When they'd both finished, Askew read them.

Side by side, on the windowsill. The way she'd read the door-roll. One line of Orlo's, one line of Cursiffe's.

"*In the spring of the year three hundred and twelve before this. At noon. Thirty came up the well into the Lantern Market.*"

"*In the spring of the year three hundred and twelve before this. At noon. Thirty came up the well into the Lantern Market.*"

"Told."

"*They were climbers. They were written in by a teller and by the Count's clerk, in two hands. They were told.*"

"Told."

"*They were given a house at the foot of the Wellgate. It was after called the Climbers' House.*"

"Told."

"*They asked the way to the Assize. One of them laughed.*"

She stopped there, for a moment. Just a moment.

"Told."

"*They stayed until after the Reckoning. At the Reckoning, the Count's clerk cried from the Assize steps: the climbers stand on the thirteenth.*"

"Told."

"*In the autumn they went up the stair under the Assize. Before they went, one of them wrote at the end of the Roll, in climbers' marks. The tellers wrote beside it: not told.*"

"Told."

There was one line left on each page. Askew looked at it on Orlo's, and then on Cursiffe's. They were the same. They were both nearly empty. Orlo had copied the climbers' marks off the curls as exactly as he could, the T and the H, the *ft do*, the R, the *th*, each in the place where it had lived, with long blank gaps between, like a line of footprints in snow with most of the snow gone. Cursiffe had done exactly the same.

"Two hands," said Askew, "that agree."

She looked up at Cursiffe.

"But it isn't told," she said. "Is it. It's what's left. It's not what it *said*."

"No," said Cursiffe.

He took the leaf off the windowsill.

It was very hot now. Glowing. He could feel the heat of it on his face. He'd been carrying it since the bottom of a bridge on floor 5, two hundred and forty-six days, and it had never once told him what it was for.

He put it down flat on his own page. At the end of the last line. On the empty place after the last mark, where the name would have been.

And he waited.

He'd done this once before. On floor 6, at the edge of the Still Water, writing Voyager's path through the beams from Hyvred's account. He'd written it true, and for one second, the ink had moved. A tiny figure in a cloak, walking through the lines. He'd told nobody. He'd told himself it was the sun.

There was no sun on Water Street.

The ink moved.

It started at the first mark. The T. The ink of it went soft and wet again, as if he'd only just written it, and shone in the lantern-light. And then the H beside it. And then, from the end of the H, a line ran out. Thin and brown and quick. Not his hand. Not Orlo's. Not any teller's. Round, and slanting the wrong way, and *fast*, the way a person writes who's in a hurry and has done this a hundred times. It ran across the gap where the snow had been. It joined up with the *ft do*. It went on. It found the R, and went round it, and on, and found the *th*, and stopped. And then a full stop. And then a long dash.

And then, after the dash, where the leaf lay, it went *under* the leaf, and Cursiffe lifted the leaf away with shaking fingers, and it was there.

The whole line. In a hand that had been dead for three hundred years.

*Thirty. Nobody left down. — Ruth*

Thea made a sound.

Not a word. A sound, very small, behind her hand.

Then the ink went on moving.

It wasn't the line any more. The line was done; it stayed, wet, shining. It was the rest of the page. All the plain-form lines Cursiffe had written, small and square, from *in the spring* to *not told*. The letters loosened. They didn't smudge. They *loosened*, the way a crowd loosens when it starts to walk. And for as long as the ink was wet, Cursiffe watched them stand up off the page.

Tiny. Brown. No bigger than a fingernail. Figures. Coming up out of the line that said *up the well*, one after another, in a line. Out into the line that said *Lantern Market*, and along it. Up the line that said *the Wellgate*. Past *the Climbers' House*. Thirty of them. He counted. He couldn't not. And at the line that said *the stair under the Assize*, they went *up*. Up the margin of the page. In single file. Small and brown and walking, the way people walk when they've a long way to go and know it.

Near the end of the line, one of them stopped.

At the very top of the margin, at the edge of the page. It stopped, and stood, a little apart from the others, and tipped back its tiny brown head, and looked up.

Then it went over the edge after the rest, and was gone.

The ink lay down. It was letters again. Small and square, his own hand, exactly as he'd written it. Dry.

All except the last line. That was still wet. *Thirty. Nobody left down. — Ruth*. Shining in the lantern-light, in a round quick hand that slanted the wrong way.

Thea was crying. Not loudly. She was holding the lantern up, very steady, and the tears were just running down her face.

"Write it," said Cursiffe. "Please. You can read it. You write it too. Under mine."

She looked at him. Then she gave the lantern to Yan, and knelt down on the cobbles, and Orlo gave her his pen, and she wrote it. Under Cursiffe's. In her own hand, which was not round and not quick but tidy and upright, and nothing like the other one at all.

*Thirty. Nobody left down. — Ruth*

Askew looked at it for a long time.

At the two lines, one under the other. Different hands. The same words.

"Two hands," she said. "That agree."

She put one finger on the wet line. Very gently. As if to see if it was real.

"Told," said the Remembrancer of Tell.

He read them standing on Water Street with the leaf in his hand. It had gone cool. All at once, the way a coal goes out when you close your hand round it. Just a thin warm stone leaf again, grey, the size of his palm. He turned it over. The words had changed back.

*Write what you saw. Keep it true.*

He was still looking at that when the sound began.

It started on Water Street. All round them. A dry, soft, rushing sound, like a great many birds taking off at once, very far away. And Cursiffe looked up and saw the bricks.

Every brick in every case in the fourth house. Every one. And through the doorway, across the street, in the house opposite, every one in that. All the way down Water Street, into the dark. Six years of drowned Rolls. Their curled edges were lifting. Very slightly. All together, like the gills of something breathing. And on every one of them, on the grey crust, for one breath, there were words. Brown, and clear, and whole. Whole lines. Names. Dates. *A daughter, to the wife of a carter on the Rope Walk.* *A house sold.* *A quarrel settled.*

For one breath. Six years of Tell. Told again.

Then the edges lay down, and the words were gone, and they were bricks.

Askew had sat down. Cursiffe hadn't seen her do it. She was sitting on the bottom step of the fourth house with her stick fallen across her feet and both her hands over her face.

"Listen," said Orlo.

The sound hadn't stopped. It had moved. It was going *up*. Away from them, up the long slope of the Third Tell, and up the stair through the black band of the Fire, and on up, faint, rushing, like wind in a chimney, through the Second Wall, and the First, up and up through the hill.

Towards the hall.

***

They came up into the Reading Hall into noise.

Every teller in it was on their feet. Some of them were crying. Some of them were shouting, the way you shout in a place where you've never once been allowed to raise your voice, without knowing you're doing it. Rolls were open on every long table, and on the floor, and hanging from the shelves where people had pulled them out and not let go. An old man at the table nearest the door was holding a roll in both hands and saying, to nobody, over and over, "My mother's name. It was gone. It was gone. It came back. It came back and now it's gone *again*—"

Hawise was standing in the middle of the hall with a single sheet of paper in her hands, looking at it, and not moving.

"Every Roll in the hall," said Orlo, beside Cursiffe, in a whisper. "Every one that's lost anything. Faded. Torn. Wet. For one breath."

Cursiffe looked past them all, down the length of the hall, to the great door.

The clerk was standing there. In its long plain grey, with its plain face. But its book wasn't on its arm any more. It was holding it against its chest. With both arms. Wrapped round it, tight, hard, the way you'd hold a door shut against a wind that was trying to open it.

It didn't move. Its face didn't change. It just held the book shut.

"It's never done that," said Orlo, very quietly. "Never. Not once. Not in my whole life."

Cursiffe didn't say anything.

He turned round. Back to the black door. To the step.

Ice was sitting on it. Where he'd left him. Back against the door frame, knees up, the grey cord on his wrist. But his eyes were shut. And his head was on one side. Not a lot. Tipped, slowly, a little way over, towards his left shoulder, the way you'd tip your head to hear somebody standing just behind you who was speaking very low.

Wendy was beside him, on the step, where she'd been. She was looking at him. And Gabe was standing now, by the table, with one hand flat on Hearthwall, looking at him too.

"He's been like that since the books opened," said Wendy. Very quietly. To Cursiffe, as he came up the last stair. "Like somebody's talking to him."

Thea came up the stair behind Cursiffe. Yan had given her back the lantern at the bottom. She came up the last step into the light of the hall, with the amber glow round her, and her face still wet.

Ice opened his eyes.

He looked at her.

Cursiffe was one step below her. He'd stopped. He was close enough to touch them both. And he would remember it afterwards, every word, exactly, the way the leaf had told him to remember things: what he saw, and kept true.

Ice's face was quite white. Not the way it went white at the edge of things, from the top down. A different white. Like somebody who's been sitting a very long time in the cold, and has just been handed something warm, and doesn't know yet whether it's going to hurt.

He said four words.

Quietly. Not to the hall. Only to her. But Cursiffe was one step away, and he heard them, and he heard *how* Ice said them, and he would think about that for a long time afterwards.

Because he didn't say them the way you say a thing like that. Not the way you say it when it's yours.

He said them the way you read something out.

Slowly. Carefully. Word by word, as if each one was written down somewhere in front of him and he was reading it off the page, and was afraid of getting one of them wrong.

"I love you," said Ice. "Idiot."

Thea stopped.

Completely. On the top step, with the lantern in her hand. Cursiffe saw her stop. Saw it go through her, all the way down, from her face to her feet. Her free hand came up and went over her mouth.

And then, behind it, she laughed.

Once. Through her fingers. Wet.

"I told you," she said.

Ice laughed too. Or started to. It broke in the middle and turned into something else, and he put his head down on his knees, and Thea put the lantern down on the step beside him, carefully, and knelt down, and put her forehead against the top of his head, and stayed there.

The hall had gone quiet around them. Not because it was watching. It hadn't noticed. It was full of tellers crying over their own lost lines. Nobody was looking at two climbers on the step at the back but Wendy, and Gabe, and Cursiffe.

Gabe looked at them for a long moment. Then he turned round, deliberately, and put his back to them, and looked down the hall, all the way to the great door. At the clerk with its book held shut. And didn't take his eyes off it.

Cursiffe went up the last step, past them, quietly, and stood in the hall.

*I told you*.

It was a strange thing to say back. To *that*.

He thought about it. He couldn't not. He stood in the middle of the Reading Hall with the cool leaf in his hand and the iron key round his neck and a new window still hanging, faint, at the edge of his eyes. *What happened, and is kept anywhere, you may tell again*.

*Anywhere*.

He looked at Ice, with his head on his knees on the step, and Thea's forehead on his hair.

And he stopped thinking about it. On purpose. All at once, the way you put a pen down.

It wasn't his.

***

Drew wrote it on the slate that night, in the copy-room, for all of them. Because it was a fact, and facts went on the slate.

He wrote in big square capitals, the way he always did, and the copy-room watched him do it. Thirty-six people on blankets and benches, with grey threads on thirty-five wrists and a grey cord on one.

*THE ONES BEFORE.*

*THIRTY. UP THE WELL. SPRING, 312 YEARS AGO.*

*TOLD. (2 HANDS.)*

*ONE OF THEM LAUGHED.*

*CRIED AT THE RECKONING: 13. 22. 31. 40. 52. 61. 74. 88. 99.*

*THEN NOTHING. EVERY YEAR SINCE. (THE CLERK STILL GOES UP.)*

*THIRTY. NOBODY LEFT DOWN. — RUTH.*

He stepped back from it.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Cursiffe watched them read it. That was what he did. He watched faces. Not lights; he couldn't see lights. Faces.

He watched every one of them get to *ONE OF THEM LAUGHED*, and stop, and not say anything, and look, just for a moment, at Ice.

He watched Calvin get to the last line.

"*Nobody left down*," said Calvin. "That's Burr's thing. That's the walkers' thing. From floor 9. They said they got it from—" He stopped. "That was *her*?"

"She counted them," said Drew.

He was standing in front of the slate with the chalk still in his hand. He hadn't moved.

"She counted them," he said again. Quieter. "The tree. On floor 9. Maigo said it felt the same short sounds in the same order. Every morning." He looked at the slate. "That was her. Counting. Every morning. Thirty." He put the chalk down on the ledge of the slate, very carefully. "Thirteen floors. All thirty of them. And she wrote *nobody left down*."

Maigo, on his blanket, put his hand flat on the floor.

"It was," he said slowly. "It was the same sounds. Every morning. In the same order." He looked at Drew. "Somebody counting."

Cursiffe watched Ice.

Ice was reading the line of numbers. He'd read the rest. He'd read *ONE OF THEM LAUGHED* and not looked away from it, and not said anything, and now he was reading the numbers. Slowly. Thirteen. Twenty-two. Thirty-one. Cursiffe watched his eyes go along the line.

They got to the end. *99*.

And stopped there.

Ice's face didn't do anything. It never did. He was sitting with his back against the end wall, next to Thea, with the cord on his wrist, and his face didn't move at all.

But his head tipped.

A little way. Over towards his left shoulder. The way it had on the step, in the hall. Slowly. Very slowly. As if somebody very close to him had said something very low, and it had taken him a long time to hear it all.

Thea's eyes went to him. Not to his face. To the tip of his head. Cursiffe saw her see it, and saw her not move.

"Is it bad?" said Drew.

He'd asked it before. On floor 9. Nobody had answered then.

Ice was quiet for a long time.

"I don't know," he said.

Cursiffe had heard Ice say *I don't know* a great many times. On every floor. About the tower, and where things were, and why. Usually it was the kind of *I don't know* that had a door shut behind it.

This one didn't. Cursiffe would have put money on it. He'd have put the iron key on it. It was the first time he'd ever heard Ice say *I don't know* about the tower and mean it all the way down.

"Ruth," said Yan.

He'd said it to the air. Not loudly. To the dark over the copy-room, the way he said *Annis* to the sea every morning. The way a light you know by name will hear you say it, and turn.

They all waited.

Yan was quiet a long time. Then he shook his head.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing turned." He looked at the slate. "That doesn't mean anything. It doesn't mean no. It just means she's not *here*."

"Not no," said Hyvred, softly, from her blanket. "Just not here."

***

Askew had given him the key to keep.

She'd done it at the top of the black stair, in the hall, after, when the tellers had stopped crying and started, being tellers, to write down what had happened. She'd come up to him slowly with her stick and looked up at him with her walnut face, and her eyes had been red, and she hadn't seemed to mind that anybody saw.

"Six years," she'd said. "On Water Street. Six years of Tell, in bricks. For one breath I saw every line of them." She'd held out her hand, and he'd put the key in it, and she'd closed his fingers back round it and pushed it back to him. "You'll want to be a teller longer than a day."

"How long?" Cursiffe had said.

"Till it's told," said the Remembrancer.

He sat with it now, on his blanket at the end of the copy-room, with the VOYAGER book open on his knee and the eel-oil ink, late, after the others had gone quiet. The key on its cord round his neck, and the leaf, cool, in his shirt pocket, saying *Keep it true*.

He wrote.

*Day 363. Water Street. The Archive, three of three. Tell again what was lost. I wrote it in the plain form. Orlo wrote it too. Two hands. The ink moved and finished the line. "Thirty. Nobody left down. — Ruth." Thea wrote it again under mine. Askew: "Told." (Seen. Thea, Yan, Orlo, Askew.)*

*Day 363. When the Echo went up through the hill, every lost line in the Reading Hall came back for one breath. The clerk at the door held its book shut with both arms. (Seen. Orlo: "It's never done that.")*

*Day 363. Ice was on the step. Wendy says his head had been on one side since the books opened, like somebody listening. When Thea came up the stair, he said four words to her. I heard them. I am not writing them down. They're hers.*

*She said, "I told you."*

He looked at that for a long time.

Then he wrote one more line.

*Day 363. At the slate. 99. His head tipped, slowly. Drew: "Is it bad?" Ice: "I don't know." (Heard. He meant it.)*

He read it all back. From floor 6. From the very first page. All of it. The iron filings, lying down in their lines, every one of them pointing the same way, at the shape of the thing underneath.

He knew what the shape was. He'd known for a while. He'd known, if he was honest, since the galley of the *Patience*, and probably before.

He didn't write it.

He put the pen down, and blew on the page until it was dry, and closed the book, and put it inside his shirt, next to the leaf.

*Write what you saw*, the leaf said. *Keep it true.*

He had.

The rest was Ice's.
