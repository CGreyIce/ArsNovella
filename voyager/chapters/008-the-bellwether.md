The valley got narrower toward its head, the way valleys do, and darker.

The sun had gone off the end of its rail behind the western hills. In the Spire it didn't set so much as slide off the edge of the world. For a while afterwards the sky stayed a deep, clear blue, and then the stars came on. Not one at a time, as real stars did, but all together, as if somebody up there had found the switch. The first time, it had made Wendy laugh out loud. Ice remembered that. She'd laughed, and then she'd stopped laughing very suddenly, because she'd understood what it meant, and Salty had put his arm round her.

He walked up the last stretch of the valley alone, under the switched-on stars, with a shield on his arm.

"Health?" he said.

"Sixty-eight percent. Mend Self's ready. You should eat."

"I know."

He stopped at the last dry-stone wall before the head of the valley and sat on it. He unwrapped Mother Crane's cloth. The broth he'd made in her kitchen was cold now, in its little lidded pot, a skin of fat on top. He drank it straight from the pot, all of it, and the window that opened afterwards was small and very pleased with itself.

"It's not very good broth," said Alea.

"It's my first broth."

"I know. I'm just saying. Mother Crane would have said it."

"She did say it. She said I wouldn't poison anyone."

"That's a low bar."

"That's the bar," said Ice, and put the empty pot back in the cloth with the loaves, the half and the whole. He didn't eat the bread. He'd thought he would. When he got the cloth open and saw them there, one and a half loaves of Mother Crane's bread, he found he didn't want to. He wrapped them up again and put them where he wouldn't land on them.

Then he stood up, and checked the straps on the shield, and went over the wall.

***

The head of the valley was called the Fold.

He didn't know if anyone in Hearthstead called it that. That was what the thirty-six of them had called it, ten years ago, because Calvin had said it on the second day and it had stuck. It was a round hollow at the very top of the valley, where the hills closed in on three sides, and the grass in it was short and very green, cropped down to nothing. Seven standing stones stood round the edge of it in a ring, each taller than a man, grey and leaning slightly outward, as if the hollow were a bowl and they were its sides. Behind them, in the hillside at the back, a plain stone doorway was cut into the hill. Its door was shut.

The ascension room. The way up.

And in the middle of the hollow, asleep, the thing that kept it shut.

It was a ram. That was the only word that fitted, though it was the wrong word. The thing lying on the cropped grass in the middle of the Fold was the size of a cart-horse, its fleece a dense, matted grey-black, like a storm cloud that had been rolled in mud. Its horns curled round three times on each side of its head, thick as a man's thigh at the root. Round its neck, on a strap of black leather as wide as Ice's hand, hung a bell. A big bronze one, green with age, with a crack running up one side from the lip.

It was breathing slowly. Every breath, the bell moved, very slightly. It didn't ring.

Ice stood at the edge of the Fold, between two of the standing stones, and looked at it for a long time.

The first time, it had taken all thirty-six of them, two hours and three tries. Thirty-six level-fours and fives against one ram, with nobody knowing what they were doing. Calvin had got trampled. Gigichi had got stuck up a standing stone. Gabe had broken his shield on its head and spent the whole second try fighting with half a shield and his elbow. It had been the most terrifying thing any of them had ever done. Afterwards, sitting in a row against the stones with the bell lying cracked in the grass and their windows chiming, they'd laughed until they cried, because they'd all thought they were going to die, and none of them had.

Then they'd gone up the stairs and found out what the Spire actually was.

"Solo," said Alea, quietly. "Two levels under. Inside the first day. Ice, nobody's ever—"

"I know."

"I'm not trying to stop you. I'm logging it." A pause. "I just want it in the log that I said it."

"It's in the log."

He drew the sword. He settled the shield. He took a breath, the long slow kind he'd taken outside a hundred boss rooms, and let it go.

Then he walked into the Fold, straight across the cropped grass toward the sleeping Bellwether. When he was ten metres away he stopped, and put the flat of his sword against the rim of the shield, and hit it. Once. Hard.

*Clang.*

The Bellwether opened its eyes.

***

They were yellow, like the goat's, with the same flat, sideways pupils, and the same expression of deep and personal offence. It got up the way big animals get up, front end first, heaving, and the bell round its neck swung once and rang: a deep, cracked, bronze note that rolled round the Fold and off the stones and out over the valley.

Up on the slopes behind him, in the dark, something answered it with a bleat.

"Two Woolbacks," said Alea. "North-east. Thirty seconds."

"I know."

The Bellwether lowered its head.

The trick with the Bellwether, the first time, was that nobody had known there was a trick. They'd stood round it in a crowd hitting it, and it had gone through them like a cart through a hedge, and Calvin had gone flying. And then, the third time it charged, it had missed whoever it was aiming at and gone head first into one of the standing stones. It had just stood there for three seconds, swaying, with its eyes crossed, while thirty-six people hit it with everything they had.

Ice put himself between the Bellwether and the nearest stone.

Not too close. Six metres out, so it would have to build up to him. He set his feet. He lifted the shield a little, sword back and low.

The Bellwether's hooves scraped the grass. Once, twice.

It came.

It was faster than anything that size had a right to be, a grey-black wall of wool and horn, and the ground shook as it came. Ice held still. He held still the way Xav used to hold still in every fight they'd ever had, which had driven Ice mad for years until he'd finally understood it: wait, wait, and move once.

Four metres. Three.

He went left.

Not far. Just enough. The horns went past his right shoulder with a sound like a gust of wind, close enough to catch the rim of his shield and spin him half round, and then there was a *crack* behind him like a tree coming down.

He turned.

The Bellwether was standing with its forehead against the standing stone, legs splayed, swaying. Its eyes had gone very slightly crossed.

[ The Bellwether · Stunned ]

He didn't hit its head. Everybody had hit its head the first time, because it was the biggest, most obvious thing to hit, and it hadn't mattered at all. He went for the strap.

The black leather band round its neck was as wide as his hand and as thick as his thumb, old and stiff and greasy. On the third try, ten years ago, Hako had stabbed at the Bellwether's neck with his spear, missed the neck, and caught the strap instead. It had parted halfway. After that the bell had rung differently, and the Woolbacks had stopped coming. Nobody had noticed why until afterwards. Ice had noticed. Ice had spent an evening by the fire thinking about it and writing it down, back when he'd still thought writing things down would help him do better next time.

He cut the strap. Two strokes, sawing, as hard as he could, before the three seconds were up.

The leather split a third of the way through.

The Bellwether shook its head and came off the stone, and the bell swung and rang.

***

The first two Woolbacks came over the north-east edge of the Fold at the same moment the Bellwether turned round to look for him.

He used Taunt on them. He felt the hook go out and catch, and both of them came for him instead of milling round the hollow. Then he had two Woolbacks and a cart-horse-sized ram all wanting him, which was where he wanted them, and also the worst place in the world to be.

He put his back toward the nearest stone and fought.

Woolbacks he knew. Step left, look for the second, point low under the jaw. He took the first one as it came in, the sword going under its chin just as it had all afternoon. Then he turned his shoulder and took the second one on the shield, and it hit like a sack of wet sand. His feet went back two metres through the grass. He used Guard. The shield held. He pushed it off and put the sword through it.

The Bellwether was coming.

He hadn't set up. He was wrong-footed, half turned, with a dead Woolback dissolving at his feet, and it was coming straight at him across the hollow, already moving fast.

He didn't have time to go left properly. He went anyway. The horn caught the edge of his shield and the shield caught his arm, and he went up, actually up, off his feet, and over, and landed on his back on the cropped grass three metres away, with the stars spinning.

The Bellwether had missed the stone. It was turning round in a wide circle at the far side of the Fold to come again.

"Mend Self," said Alea.

"I know." He rolled onto his knees. He put his left hand flat on his own chest, over his heart.

There was no lantern here. No beam of gold through a high window. Just the dark and the stars and the Fold and the ram coming round. For one horrible second he thought: *it only works there. It only works in the chapel.*

Then he felt the ember.

It was in his palm. The one from the lantern, small as a seed, that had sunk into his skin and stayed. He'd half forgotten it. It was warm. It was always going to be warm. *The Knight does not carry the light*, said the Testament, *he stands in it*. And he'd been carrying a little of it, all this time, in the hand he'd just put on his heart.

He stood in it. He let it reach him.

The hand closed in his chest.

[ HP 58% ]

"Okay," said Ice, breathing hard, and got up. "Okay. Okay. Okay."

***

He did it right the second time.

He let the Bellwether come round, and put himself six metres out from a stone again, and set his feet, and waited. Wait, wait, move once. It went into the stone with a crack that he felt in his teeth. He was on the strap before it stopped swaying, sawing, two strokes, three—

The bell rang. Two more Woolbacks.

He took them. It cost him: one of them got its head into his hip before he could get the point in, and he went down on one knee and came up again with his leg screaming. The broth was ticking him back up a percent at a time, and every time he got near the ram, it knocked him back down.

[ HP 34% ]

The third charge he got wrong. Not badly: he went left, but late, and the horn took the shield on the inside of its curve and ripped it half off his arm, and the Bellwether went past him and hit nothing. It turned round at the end of the Fold in a long, angry circle, shaking its head.

The fourth he got right.

Crack. Stone. Swaying. And this time he didn't saw. He didn't have the strength left for sawing. He put the point of the sword through the frayed place in the strap where he'd already cut it two-thirds through, and wrenched upward with both hands and all his weight behind it.

The strap parted.

The bell fell off the Bellwether's neck and hit the cropped grass with a dull, final *bonk*. It rolled away down the slope of the hollow, the crack in its side catching the starlight. It didn't ring. It would never ring again.

The Bellwether made a sound.

He'd forgotten that sound. He'd spent ten years forgetting it on purpose. It wasn't a bleat. It was a scream, high and furious and far too big. All seven standing stones seemed to hum with it. Up on the slopes, every Woolback that had been coming to the bell stopped where it was in the dark, and turned, and went away.

"Phase two," said Alea, completely unnecessarily.

***

Without the bell it was faster.

Without the bell it *turned*.

That had been what killed the first try, ten years ago. Everybody had learned *dodge left and let it hit the stone*. Then the bell came off, the ram stopped running in straight lines, and started running in curves. It bent its charge round you when you dodged, the way a dog bends round a corner. And people dodged left, and it came round left after them, and that was the end of the first try.

It came at him across the Fold in a long, curving arc, low and fast, the grass tearing up behind it.

He didn't dodge left. He didn't dodge at all.

He'd learned this one on the hundredth floor, from the thing on the black glass throne. When something can follow you wherever you go, the only place it can't follow you is *forward*.

He ran at it.

Three steps. On the third, he dropped. Shoulder down, shield up and angled, the way Gabe used to take a door. The Bellwether's head came down to meet him, and its horns caught the angled shield, and the whole weight of it went up and over. Its forelegs went over him. He felt the wind of its belly, smelled wet wool, felt a hoof slam down a hand's width from his ear. And then it was past, behind him, going full tilt with nothing to stop it, head down, at the stone he'd put himself in front of.

Crack.

Not the stone, this time. The stone cracked, a great split running up from the base, and leaned, and stayed standing. The Bellwether stood against it with its legs braced, swaying.

[ HP 12% ]

He didn't remember getting up. He was up. He was walking across the cropped grass with the shield hanging off his arm by one strap and the sword in his right hand. The world had gone very quiet and very clear, the way it did sometimes at the bottom of a fight, as if somebody had turned off every sound but his own breathing.

The Bellwether turned its head toward him.

Its eyes weren't crossed any more. It was coming out of it. One more second.

Point, low, under the jaw, where the wool was thin.

He put the sword in.

***

It didn't fall over the way things fall in stories. It stood there for a while with his sword in it. Then its legs folded under it, front first, the way it had got up, and it lay down on the cropped grass of the Fold as if it were going back to sleep. He pulled the sword out. It sighed once, a long breath through the nose, and the light that ran through every living thing in the Spire went out of it, quietly, the way a lamp goes out.

Ice stood over it. He didn't sit down. He was afraid that if he sat down he wouldn't get up.

Then every window he had opened at once.

[ Floor 1 has been cleared. ]

That one was different. He felt it go. It didn't open just in front of him, small and private. It went out from the Fold like the bell's note, out over the dark valley, down to Hearthstead and its red roofs and the lamps in its windows, to thirty-five people who'd been in this world for less than a day and were, at that moment, he supposed, sitting down to eat.

He knew what it would look like to them. A small, plain window in the corner of everyone's eye, all at once, saying *Floor 1 has been cleared*, a few hours after they'd first touched a sword. And no name. Nothing to say who.

He didn't open world chat. He didn't want to see it.

"Ice," said Alea.

"Yeah."

"You did it."

"Yeah."

"Solo, two under, fifteen hours and forty-one minutes. Inside the first day." Her voice was doing something he didn't think she'd meant it to. "In the whole history of the Spire, Ice. In all the floors I can read, all the logs, every Climber who ever came out of that hill. Nobody. Nobody's ever—"

"Alea."

"I'm *logging* it."

He laughed. It came out shaky. He looked down at the Bellwether lying peacefully on the grass, at the cracked bell lying further down the slope where it had rolled, and at the sword in his hand with the blood on it, and he started to shake again, very slightly, all the way down, the way he had on the hillside that morning.

Behind him, in the hill at the back of the Fold, the stone door of the ascension room ground slowly open.

And in front of him, the last window opened. It was bigger than the others, gold at the edges, and it was in no hurry at all.
