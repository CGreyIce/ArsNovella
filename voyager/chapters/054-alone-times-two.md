Milli didn't sleep the first night on floor 5. Nobody did. But Milli had a reason, and his reason was asleep on his shoulder.

They were all on one rock. That was the first thing about floor 5. They'd come up two hundred and forty-four steps in the dark. Calvin had opened the door, and the wind had come down the stair at them, cold, wet, smelling of rain. And they'd come out one after another onto a flat-topped island about the size of a house, with a stone hut round the door and grass bent flat by the wind. At the edges, there was nothing at all.

Nothing. Just air. And a long, long way below the air, a floor of cloud, silver in the starlight, going out in every direction for ever.

"Nobody," Drew had said, very calmly, "goes near the edge."

There were bridges. Even in the dark you could see them: long dark lines going out from the island across the gaps, sagging, swaying, toward other islands at other heights. Chains. Drew had looked at them for a long time and said, "Nobody crosses those in the dark either." Nobody had argued. So all thirty-five of them had sat down on an island the size of a house, in a heap, backs against the hut and against each other. Somewhere between a campfire and a lifeboat.

Hana had fallen asleep about an hour before midnight, against his shoulder, with her crown still on. The gold beads were cold against his neck. The iron rose was pinned in the front of it, the ugly one Jared had made on floor 1, and she'd never once taken it off. Milli didn't move. His tail had gone to sleep under somebody's leg about an hour ago and he still didn't move.

He looked at the stars instead.

They were real. He'd worked that out about ten minutes after they came out of the door. On floors 1 to 3, the stars had come on at dusk all at once, in rows, like lights in an office building. You stopped seeing them after a week. On floor 4 there hadn't been any at all. These were *everywhere*. No pattern. Too many. Some bright, some so faint you weren't sure. When the wind moved a cloud across them, they went out behind it and came back one at a time.

Milli was a rational person. He liked things to make sense. And sitting on a rock in the sky with his wife asleep on his shoulder, he found that he couldn't make any sense of how much he wanted to cry about some stars.

So he looked at the others instead. It helped. People made sense, mostly.

Higu was crying. Quietly, sitting a little apart with her knees up, looking at the moon. It had come up over the edge of the cloud an hour ago, huge and white and real. Higu, who'd looked at the painted moon on floor 2 every single night, and then had no moon at all on floor 4. Chi was sitting beside her not saying anything, which was the right thing.

Tiger had been round the edge of the island eleven times. Wendy had given up telling him to stop.

Gabe was asleep sitting up against the hut, in the wolf coat, with his arms round the big shield he'd made on floor 3 and still hadn't put a rim on. He'd fallen asleep with his mouth open about an hour ago, the first time Milli had seen him sleep since floor 4.

And Thea was standing at the eastern edge.

She'd been there since dusk. Milli had watched her go. She'd walked to the edge of the island that faced the middle of the sky, where far off, in the starlight, a single needle of rock went up out of the cloud taller than anything else. She'd stopped about a metre from the drop, with the little glass lantern in her hand. She hadn't moved since. Five hours. The lantern's light lay on the grass at her feet, amber, and she stood in it, looking at the needle.

Drew had gone and stood beside her once. He'd said something. She'd said something back. Drew had come away again and left her there.

Milli didn't know Ice very well. That was the honest truth, and he'd never pretended otherwise, not even on floor 1 when everybody else was so sure. He'd known him as the guy from Hana's streams, years back. The bingo guy. The one who did stupid things in the chat to make her laugh, and always managed it. They'd talked a few times, in calls, in the last year before the white room. Ice had been funny and loud and kind, the way people are when you only know them through a screen. Then he'd been a name Hana said in the white room, about a place where Milli hadn't seen anybody. Then he'd been at the top of the rankings in a tower in another world, and everybody had cried.

Milli hadn't cried. He'd been sad for Hana, and for Aleaka, and for Jared. He'd been sad watching Gabe. But he hadn't really known the man.

He was starting to think he'd have liked to.

***

The sun came up a little after five.

He felt it before he saw it. Everybody did. The sky behind the needle went pale, and then a line of gold came up along the edge of the cloud, thin, and then thicker, and the cloud caught it and went pink. Everybody stopped talking. Thirty-five people, who'd been murmuring and shifting and not sleeping all night, went completely silent all at once.

And then the sun came up.

Not along a rail. Not out of a seam in a ceiling. It just came *up*, out of the cloud, the top edge of it so bright that Milli had to look away. Slowly. The way it does at home, so slowly you can't see it moving. The light came across the cloud, and up the sides of a hundred islands Milli hadn't known were there. Islands everywhere, at every height, with chain bridges strung between them like thread. It came across the gap to their island, and onto the grass, and onto their faces.

It was warm.

It was the first warm thing that hadn't come from a fire or a spell or a heal in ninety-six days. It landed on the side of Milli's face and he felt it go through his skin, and he made a noise he'd never made before in his life.

Then everyone was making noise.

Hyvred first. Of course Hyvred first. She stood straight up and screamed at the sun, wordlessly, in capitals. Then Calvin, who threw both his arms up over his head and yelled *OH MY DAYS* so loudly that it echoed off the next island. Then Newish, swearing at the sun, creatively, at length, because it was beautiful and she didn't know what else to do with it. Then everyone. Tiger was going round the edge again, so fast that Wendy finally grabbed him by the tail and he went over sideways onto the grass laughing. Salty was crying openly into his huge hands. Chi was holding Higu. Drew had sat down on the grass with his back to the hut and his face up and his eyes shut, like somebody sunbathing, with tears coming out from under the lids.

And Hana woke up.

Milli felt her wake. She lifted her head off his shoulder and opened her eyes, and the sun was right there, coming up out of the cloud, gold, on her face. She didn't say anything. She just looked at it. With the light on her face, and her lavender hair going gold at the edges, and her crown catching it, every bead.

Milli didn't look at the sun.

He looked at her. The whole time. He'd look at the sun tomorrow. There'd be one tomorrow. That was the whole point. That was what had made him want to cry about the stars.

"Milli," she said, after a long time. Her voice was very small. "It's *real*."

"I know."

"It's warm."

"I know, honey."

She looked at him then. Saw him looking at her and not at the sun. She laughed, and it went wobbly in the middle, and she put her face in his neck.

At the eastern edge of the island, Thea was standing exactly where she'd stood all night, with her face turned up to the sun and her eyes shut. She was laughing and crying, both at once, the light all over her face. She wasn't wiping it away. She was just standing there and letting it happen, with the lantern hanging forgotten at her side.

***

The kites came about ten minutes later.

Jared saw them first. He'd been standing at the edge looking at the needle, the way he looked at things, steady and still, and he said, "Hey. Alice. Milli. Look."

They looked.

Out of the sunrise, from the north, something was coming across the sky. Seven somethings. Grey, wide, flat shapes, sliding through the air toward them in a long line, riding the wind. Kites. Huge ones. And under each kite, hanging from it by the arms, *a person*.

"*Nobody move*," said Drew.

Nobody moved. Thirty-five people stood on an island the size of a house, in the first real sun, and watched seven people fly.

They came closer. Close enough to see. Tall, thin people, with long arms and narrow high shoulders, in grey coats. And down the backs of their arms, where the coat sleeves were slit, *feathers*. Grey feathers, lifting in the wind. The kites went over the island, round it, once, twice, so close that Milli could see their faces. Old, mostly. Lined. Large dark eyes. Looking down at the thirty-five of them with an expression Milli couldn't read.

The smallest one was a boy. He was hanging from a kite too big for him, and as he went over the island he let go with one hand and *waved*. Then he turned a loop, a whole loop, over and over in the sunrise. He was shrieking with laughter.

Then the oldest one, at the back of the line, came in low over the island, lower than the others, and looked at them.

Not at all of them. Milli watched the dark eyes go over the island, person to person. Searching. They stopped on Gabe, by the hut, in the wolf coat. They stayed on him a moment. Then they moved on, and found Thea at the eastern edge with her lantern. They stayed on her too.

Then the old kite-person tilted their great grey kite and rose, and turned, and the seven of them went away north across the Reaches in a long line, toward a white cliff with round huts on top, and were gone.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Okay," said Dennis eventually. His voice was very high. "Okay. Birdpeople. That's fine. That's fine, that's normal, that's a normal thing to see before breakfast."

"They were *looking* for someone," said Hyvred.

"They were looking at Gabe," said Milli.

Everybody turned and looked at Gabe.

Gabe was standing by the hut with his rimless shield in his arms and the wolf coat on, staring after the kites, with his mouth open.

"*Why*," he said, "would birdpeople be looking for me."

***

They found out at four in the afternoon, in Tether, from an old woman with a gull on her hat.

It took them all day to get there. Three bridges. Drew made them cross one at a time, both hands on the rail chain, the whole way. On the first bridge, halfway across, the wind changed. There was no window. Calvin, who was in front, went down onto his knees on the planks with both arms round the rail chain and said *WHAT* very loudly. Behind him on the bridge, Rachael hung onto the back of his collar with one hand and the chain with the other and said nothing at all, with her eyes shut. When the gust had gone past, Chris took out his notebook and wrote something down. His hand was shaking.

"There wasn't a window," he said, at the far end.

"I noticed," said Calvin, from the grass, where he was lying on his back.

"There's always a window. For weather. Floor 2, the whiteouts, *two minutes' warning*. Every time." Chris looked at what he'd written. "That one just—happened."

"Like home," said Aleaka quietly.

Everybody looked at her.

"Weather doesn't send you a message at home," she said, in her slow, careful way, with Sleet pressed against her legs, shivering. "It just comes."

Nobody said anything to that. They crossed the next two bridges holding the rail chains with both hands.

Tether was a town tied down with rope. Every roof roped to the rock, every door to the next door, and kites on every house, humming in the wind. Gulls on every ridge. Dennis walked into the main street with his mouth open and said *there's a fish market* in the voice of a man who'd seen God. Then he went straight to it, and they lost him for an hour.

And at the edge of town, by a big iron winch with a cable running out from it across a gap so wide you couldn't see the other end, there was an old woman sitting on an upturned basket, with a pipe, and four shawls, and a gull on her hat. She watched them come down the street, all thirty-five of them. She didn't move.

When they got close, she started to sing.

*They came up the stair with their lights in their hands,*

*and they asked for the way to the top.*

She stopped. She took the pipe out of her mouth.

"You're them," she said.

Drew, at the front, opened his mouth.

"Thirty-five," said the old woman, before he could say anything. "Counted you coming down the street. He said there'd be thirty-five." She looked them over with sharp old eyes. "Which one of you's Dennis?"

There was a short silence.

Dennis, who had just arrived from the fish market with a parcel under each arm, said: "*What?*"

"You're Dennis." The old woman nodded as if something had been settled. "He said you'd haggle."

"*Who said*—"

"Your knight." She put the pipe back in her mouth. "With the light by his ear." She jerked her chin at a place just beside her own head, about the size of a small cat, where there was nothing. "Came through a month back. On his own. Paid double for the ferry. Cooked me fish behind my hut every night for a fortnight." She sniffed. "Too much salt."

Nobody said anything.

Milli felt it go through the whole group. All thirty-five of them, at once, a sort of shiver. He felt Hana's hand close on his arm. Beside him, Jared had gone very still.

"Nan Gully," said the old woman. "That's me. Ferry's double. For the ones before." She looked at Dennis. "You, I'd charge triple. But he said be nice." She looked away from Dennis, past Drew, over all their heads. Her eyes moved through the crowd, the way the old kite-person's had that morning, searching. They stopped.

"You," said Nan Gully. "In the coat."

Gabe was at the back. He'd been at the back all day, carrying the rimless shield, quiet. Now everybody turned round and looked at him, and the crowd moved apart without anybody deciding it should. There was a path, suddenly, from the back of thirty-five people to an old woman on a basket.

Gabe walked up it. Slowly. He stopped in front of her.

"You're Gabe," said Nan Gully.

"Yeah."

"He said you'd be wearing a wolf coat. Even though it's too warm." She looked at the coat, scorched all down the back. "He was right."

Gabe didn't say anything. Milli could see his hands. They were white on the rim of the shield.

"I've got a message for you," said Nan Gully. "He paid triple for it, so you're getting it word for word." She took the pipe out of her mouth and held it in her lap, and looked straight up at him. "*The bird comes down on whoever stands still. Don't stand still.*" She paused. "*Lean.*"

Gabe stared at her.

"*Lean?*"

"That's what he said. *Lean.* Said you'd know, or you wouldn't, and Pell could teach you." Nan Gully put the pipe back. "Pell's the old one who was looking at you this morning. Off the Kitecliff."

"What bird?" said Gabe. His voice had gone strange.

"The one round the Spindle." She pointed the pipe at the needle in the middle of the sky. "Big as a barn. Your knight's been up and down that needle for two weeks fighting it. Lost his shield to it the first time. Lost the next one too." She looked at the rimless shield in Gabe's arms. "Otta's been selling him barrel lids."

Gabe made a sound.

"There's more," said Nan Gully. "Take fish to the Kitecliff. And kill the bird often." She reached under her shawls and brought out something: a little twist of paper. "And this. For your eggs, the morning you go up the Spindle. He left it on my basket this morning with a note. Didn't sign it." She held it out. "Here."

Gabe took it. He held it in his big hand, a tiny twist of paper, like something that might break.

"*This morning*," he said.

Nan Gully looked at him for a long moment.

"He left this morning," she said, more gently. "Before I was up. Went over to the Spindle. Pell's lot were flying at sunrise, so the bird was dead." She paused. "He killed it at sunrise. So they'd be up there flying in it when you lot saw the sun for the first time." She sniffed. "He didn't say that. I'm old, not stupid."

The whole street had gone quiet. Even the gulls.

"And then he went up," said Nan Gully. "I'd guess. Up the Spindle. To the next one."

Gabe stood in front of her with the twist of paper in his hand.

Milli watched his face. He'd never seen Gabe's face do this. It wasn't crying. It was something before crying, or after it, or underneath it.

"He was here," said Gabe. "This morning. While we were—" He stopped. "On the *island*. He was right there, he was across the—and he—"

"He killed a bird for you," said Nan Gully. "So you'd see people flying."

Gabe didn't say anything at all.

Nan Gully looked at him a while longer. Then she said, quite quietly, so that only the front of the crowd could hear:

"I asked him if you were his."

Gabe looked at her.

"He said brother," said Nan Gully. "Not by blood. Since he was fifteen."

And Gabe sat down.

He just sat down. Right there, in the street, on the stones in front of the chain-ferry, in the wolf coat, with the rimless shield in his lap and the twist of paper in his hand. He sat with his head down and his shoulders shaking. Nobody said anything. Xav came through the crowd without a word and sat down next to him, close, shoulder to shoulder. Then Deston on his other side. Then Malcolm, and John, and Rui Xiang, round him in a ring on the stones. Nan Gully sat on her basket above them all, smoking her pipe, looking out at the needle in the middle of the sky.

Milli felt Hana's hand on his arm, tight enough to hurt.

He'd been right. He'd have liked to know him.

***

They spent four days learning floor 5.

Learning bridges, mostly. How to cross with both hands. How to feel a gust coming in the chain before it hit, the way the iron changed under your palm. Chris made a page in his notebook for every bridge they crossed and a mark for every gust, and after three days he had forty pages and no pattern at all. "There isn't one," he said, staring at it. "It's just weather."

Dennis took fish to the Kitecliff on the second day, with Aleaka and Newish, because Nan Gully had said to. The old kite-person, Pell, took the fish and looked at the three of them for a long time. Then they said one sentence: *bring the big one*. So on the third day Gabe went up the chain ladder, alone, with his rimless shield. He came back at dusk bruised from head to foot and wouldn't say what had happened. On the fourth day he went again. He came back that night with grass in his hair, and a look on his face Milli had never seen. Fierce and baffled and sore. He sat down at the long table in the inn in Tether, and said to nobody: "I've been standing wrong my whole life."

Aleaka made friends with the gulls. All of them. By the third day there were forty gulls following her round Tether, and Sleet was beside herself, barking at them, and the gulls didn't care. A fisherman offered to buy them off her.

Milli learned the gale-kites.

They were the things to hunt here. Flat grey creatures like rays, as wide as a door, that lived under the outer islands in great colonies, hanging upside down from the rock beneath. At dusk they came out. They rode the gusts up from under the bridges and hit you from below, out of nowhere. Hunters from Tether went out to the outer islands for them, along the long thin spans nobody else walked, because their skins were worth money and their meat was good. Milli liked hunting them. It was all speed and timing, and he was fast, and he had very good timing.

He was good at it. That was the thing. That was why, on the hundred-and-first day, he went first.

***

Drew's board in the inn had their party down for the outer islands. *HANA'S PARTY — KITE HUNT — THORNBACK — BACK BY DUSK*. Thornback was an island off the western edge, past the hunters' spans, a long hump of black rock with gorse on top and a colony of gale-kites underneath. The span out to it was the longest any of them had crossed: a hundred metres of sagging chain and old grey planks, swaying, over a gap so deep the cloud below it was just a pale smudge.

"I'll go first," said Milli, at the near end.

Hana looked at the span. Then at him.

"I'm fastest," he said. "I'll go across, check the far end's sound, and call you. One at a time. Same as always."

"Same as always," said Jared, steady. He was standing at the near anchor with one hand on the post. A great iron post sunk into the rock, with the chains wound round it, and rust on everything. "Go on, man. We got you."

Milli went.

He went across the way he'd learned. Both hands on the rail chain, feeling for the gusts, fast. The planks were old and grey and some of them bent under his feet. Halfway across, the whole span swung, slowly, a long lazy sideways swing like a hammock. He waited it out. Then on. At the far end, the planks went up a little slope onto the black rock of Thornback, and he stepped off onto the gorse and turned round.

"Sound," he called. "It's fine. Come on. One at a—"

The wind changed.

He felt it. They'd all learned to feel it, in four days, in the chain under their hands. But he wasn't holding the chain. He was standing on the rock at the far end, turning round, with his hand up to wave. He felt the air go still. All of it, all at once, the way it goes before something.

And then it came.

Not from the side. Not a gust. From *below*. Straight up out of the gap, up the side of Thornback, up past the span, like the whole sky had breathed in. It hit the span from underneath. The planks went up. The whole long hundred metres of chain and wood went up into the air like a rope somebody had flicked. It hung there for one long, impossible second, a great curve against the sky.

And at the near end, Jared's end, with a sound like the world tearing, the iron post came out of the rock.

Milli saw it go. He saw the rust come off it in a cloud, and the post come up out of its hole, and the chains go loose. He saw Jared throw himself backwards, away from it, onto the grass, with his arm round Hana's waist, dragging her with him. The span came down. The whole length of it, a hundred metres of chain and planks, came down out of the air and fell. Not into the cloud. It was still fixed at Milli's end. It swung down from Thornback's edge, under Milli's feet, all the way down, and hit the cliff below him with a long rolling crash he felt through his boots. Then it hung there, against the black rock, twisting, a hundred metres of ruined bridge dangling into nothing.

The near end was gone. The post was gone. Where the span had been, there was a hundred metres of empty air.

And Milli was on the wrong side of it.

***

He didn't move for a long time. He couldn't.

Across the gap, a hundred metres away, his party was on its feet. Jared, with Hana. Newish, with her hands over her mouth. Aleaka, on her knees at the edge with Sleet in her arms. Hana had pulled free of Jared and gone to the very edge, where the post had been. She was standing there in the wind with her crown on, looking across at him, and her face was white.

His whisper window came up.

[ Whisper · Hanarisu: milli ]

That was all. Just his name.

He typed back. His hands were shaking, so it took him three goes.

[ Whisper · Milli: im ok. im not hurt. im on the island ]

[ Whisper · Hanarisu: theres no bridge ]

[ Whisper · Milli: i noticed ]

He saw her laugh across the gap. It was a terrible laugh, wet and broken, and then she put her hand over her mouth. Jared came up behind her and put a hand on her shoulder.

[ Whisper · Jared: man. dont move. were getting help. stay put ]

[ Whisper · Milli: wasnt planning on going anywhere ]

He looked round. Thornback. A long hump of black rock, maybe two hundred metres end to end, with gorse on the top and nothing else. No trees. No huts. No other bridge. He walked the whole edge of it, slowly, to be sure. There was no other bridge. There had never been another bridge. Thornback was a hunters' island at the end of a hunters' span, and the span was hanging off the cliff under his feet.

The sun was going down.

He stopped walking and looked at it. It was low in the west, red, going down into the cloud. Real. Slow. Beautiful. Behind him, across the gap, Drew had arrived. Then Deston, Chris, half the group, coming at a run along the bridges from Tether because somebody had whispered. They stood on the far edge in a line and looked at him across a hundred metres of air.

Dusk on floor 5.

He knew what happened at dusk.

They'd come to Thornback to hunt gale-kites. Thornback had a colony of them. They lived under it, hanging from the rock in the dark beneath the island, hundreds, Nan Gully had said, it was the biggest colony on the western edge. And at dusk they came out. Up from under the edges, on the wind, to feed.

Milli looked down over the edge of Thornback at the rock beneath.

Something moved there. Grey. Flat. Then another.

"Oh," he said, out loud, to nobody. "Oh, that's *bad*."

***

The first ones came up about ten minutes after the sun went down.

They came up over the edge on the evening wind, three of them, rippling, flat grey wings as wide as doors. They came at him low across the gorse, the way they did, skimming. He'd hunted them for four days. He knew how they moved. He went sideways and low under the first one's wing, and his daggers went into its soft underside as it passed, one, two. It went down into the gorse and flopped there and didn't get up. The second one he hit from above, dropping onto it as it skimmed. The third went past him, turned, came back. He was ready for it.

Three dead gale-kites on the gorse. They didn't go to light. Floor 5. They just lay there, flat and grey, leaking.

A window came up.

[ Fighting alone · Experience ×2 ]

He stared at it.

He'd never seen that window before. None of them had. He'd been in a party of five since the white room. He'd never once, not once, fought anything without four people at his back.

*Experience ×2.*

He looked at his experience bar. It had jumped. Three gale-kites, at level twenty-three, and the bar had moved further than it moved for ten of them in a party. Further than it had moved all of yesterday.

Milli was a rational person. He liked things to make sense. And something in the back of his head, the part that was always quietly doing sums, began very quietly doing one.

More were coming up over the edge.

Six this time. Then, behind them, more. Ten. Twelve. He stopped counting. They came up over every side of Thornback at once, out of the dark under the island, grey and flat, rippling on the wind, filling the dusk. They'd smelled the dead ones.

He put his back to the biggest rock on the island, a spur of black stone about his height. He got his daggers up.

A second window.

[ Outnumbered · Experience ×3 ]

"*Seriously*," said Milli.

They came.

***

He didn't remember the next hour very well. He remembered pieces.

He remembered wings. Grey wings, everywhere, as wide as doors, coming at him from the front and the sides and above, skimming low over the gorse. Their edges were hard as leather and they cut. He remembered going under them, over and over. Low, sideways, under the wing, daggers up into the soft belly, out the other side before the next one came. Fast. Faster than he'd ever been. He was a cat. That was the thing he kept thinking. *I'm a cat. This is what cats are for.*

He remembered one catching him across the back with the edge of its wing as it went over, and the feeling of his leathers parting, and then his skin. He remembered rolling, and coming up, and putting a dagger into it as it turned.

He remembered his whisper window, at the top of everything, filling up. *milli. milli. MILLI.* Hana. He couldn't answer. His hands were busy. He typed *ok* once, with one thumb, between two gale-kites. Then he didn't have time to type anything else for a long time.

He remembered the gull.

It came out of the dark from the west, from the far side, low across the gap. A single grey gull. It came straight at him over the gale-kites, through the middle of them, and they didn't touch it. It flew right over his head and dropped something on the gorse at his feet. Then it wheeled away, screaming, back across the gap.

A line. A thin pale line, tarred, the kind fishermen used. It ran back from his feet across the gorse, over the edge of the island, out across the gap into the dark toward the other side.

[ Whisper · Aleaka: tie it to something. please. to the big rock. we can pull a rope across. then a bigger one ]

He tied it to the big rock. Between two gale-kites. With one hand, while he held a dagger in the other. He made a knot he hadn't known he knew. Then a gale-kite came over the rock at him and he went under it.

He remembered, at some point, the experience bar filling up, and up, and *up*, and then stopping.

[ Milli · Lv 25 · Tier trial required to advance · Experience held ]

And then still filling, behind the padlock. A second bar, faint, under the first, going up and up with every kill, banking, the way Chris had said Voyager's had banked on floor 2. *It's not wasted. It's banking.*

He remembered thinking, very clearly, with a gale-kite coming at his face: *oh. So that's how.*

***

It thinned out around midnight.

Not all at once. They came in waves, and the waves got smaller, and further apart, and then there was a long gap, and then three more, and then a very long gap. Milli stood with his back to the big rock with the tarred line tied round it, and a thicker rope beside it now, and a thicker one still, all running out over the edge into the dark. He couldn't remember tying the second and third. He must have. He waited for the next wave.

It didn't come.

He sat down.

The gorse round him was covered in them. Gale-kites, flat and grey, dozens of them, lying where they'd fallen, not going anywhere. Some of them still moving a little. The smell came up off them in the dark, fishy and coppery and raw. His back was wet. His arms were wet. He didn't look to see with what. He sat against the rock and breathed.

He opened his whisper.

[ Whisper · Milli: im ok ]

[ Whisper · Milli: really. im ok. they stopped ]

He sat against the rock on Thornback in the dark among the dead gale-kites, with the ropes running out from beside him across the gap toward a line of thirty-five people. And he waited for morning.

It came the way it had the day before. The sky went pale behind the needle. Then a line of gold along the cloud. Then the sun, real, slow, warm. It came across the Reaches and up the side of Thornback and over the gorse and the dead gale-kites, and onto his face.

He watched it come up. Alone. It was the second real sun he'd ever seen.

He thought, for no reason he could explain, about a man standing on a crown of rock in the middle of the sky yesterday, killing a bird at dawn. So that people he wasn't with would see somebody flying.
