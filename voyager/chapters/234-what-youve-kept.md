Sorrel went at first light.

He was awake. He'd been awake all night. He was sitting in the door of the End with his back against the frame and the little lantern in his lap when she came out past him with her hazel staff, and she stopped on the step and looked down at him.

"You look terrible," said Sorrel.

"I didn't sleep."

"No. Me neither." She looked at the yard. Grey, in the dawn, with the pilgrims asleep in heaps on the stones and the swallows starting. The boy was still on the wall, asleep sitting up, with his head against the stone where her shoulder had been. "He woke up in the night and asked if I was going today. I said yes. He said *are you frightened*. I said yes." She was quiet. "He said *me too*. And went back to sleep."

She looked down the slope. At the tollhouse. Small and grey and shut, at the end of the white road, with the bridge going out past it into the mist. The mist was white now, not gold. It would be gold again when the sun got on it.

"Thirty years," said Sorrel.

"Is it light enough?"

"I'll know in a minute." She looked at him. Sideways. With her bright black eyes. "Tell the quiet one with the leaves. Whatever happens. Tell him I went."

"I'll tell him."

She nodded. Then she went down the step, and across the yard, past the sleeping pilgrims, and out of the gate, and down the slope. Small. Brown. With her staff. Not fast. Not slow. The way somebody walks who's walked a very long road and is nearly at the end of it.

He watched her go. He couldn't not.

At the bottom of the slope she stopped at the door of the tollhouse. She stood there for a long time. He thought she might turn round. She didn't.

The door opened.

He didn't see who opened it. Nobody. It just swung in, slowly, on its own, into the dark inside. And she went in. And it shut behind her.

He sat in the door of the End and waited.

***

Other people came out while he waited. One at a time, quietly, in the grey. Maigo, barefoot, who stopped beside him and looked down the slope and didn't say anything. Then Ina. Then Salty, rubbing his face. Then more. Pilgrims too. The old man with the beard to his belt. The woman with the beans. They came out of the End and stood in the yard, and on the wall, and in the gate, and looked down at the shut door.

Nobody said anything.

It was a long time. Longer than any bridge. The sun came up behind them and got onto the top of the mist and turned it gold, the whole gorge going from white to gold in about a minute, like a bowl of milk with the light coming into it, and the bridge going out into the gold, pale, arch after arch.

The door opened.

Sorrel came out.

She stood in the doorway of the tollhouse with the gold light on her. Small. With her staff. And round her neck, on the brown cord, worn smooth over thirty years—

Seven knots.

He heard the yard breathe in. All of it at once. Fifty pilgrims and thirty-six climbers and a goat.

She didn't look up at them. She looked at the bridge. She stood at the foot of it for a long moment with her hand on the first stone of the parapet, the way you'd put your hand on the door of a house you were leaving.

Then she turned round.

She looked up the slope. At the End. At the yard. At the wall where the boy was. The boy was awake now. Standing on the wall. Very still.

Sorrel lifted her staff.

Not a wave. Just lifted it. Up. Over her head. To the boy on the wall. The way you'd lift a lantern to somebody across a dark field, to say *I'm here, this way*.

Then she turned round again and walked onto the bridge.

Out. Over the first arch. The second. Small and brown, with her staff, on the pale stone, getting smaller. Into the gold. The mist coming up round her ankles, her knees. She didn't look back again. She went into it the way you'd walk into a warm sea.

And was gone.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then the boy got down off the wall.

He didn't say anything to anybody. He walked across the yard, through the gate, and down the slope, quickly, as if he'd decided and wanted to get there before he undecided. And stopped at the door of the tollhouse. And it opened. And he went in.

He wasn't in there long.

When he came out he had seven knots, and his face was wet, and he didn't stop at the foot of the bridge at all. He went straight onto it. Running, nearly. After her. Into the gold.

Beside Ice, in the door of the End, Maigo made a sound.

Ice looked up at him.

"She said to tell you," said Ice. "That she went."

Maigo looked down the slope at the bridge going into the gold for a long time. His leaves moving in the morning wind.

"Light enough," said Maigo.

***

They went one at a time.

Nobody decided the order. Nobody needed to. Tiger went first, because Tiger was first out of every door, and he walked down the slope with his tail very still and stood at the tollhouse door, and it opened, and he went in.

Ice watched.

He didn't watch the door. He watched the people. Thirty-five of them, in the yard of the End, in the gold morning, waiting their turn, not talking. Every one of them with six knots on a brown cord and something they'd kept.

He knew what most of them had kept. Not the details. The shape. He'd watched them for two years. He knew which ones had kept a person, and which ones had kept a mistake, and which ones had kept a fear so old they'd forgotten it was there. He knew Rachael had kept a question. He knew Chris had kept a page.

He knew some of them had kept him.

He didn't know which ones. He didn't want to. He sat in the door of the End with the little lantern in his lap and watched them go down the slope one at a time, and in, and out, and every one came out with seven knots, and every one of them walked onto the bridge, onto the first arch, and stopped there.

And waited.

He didn't understand that at first. Tiger came out, with his seven knots, and walked onto the bridge, and Ice thought he'd go on, into the gold, the way Sorrel had. He didn't. He went to the top of the first arch and sat down on the parapet with his back to the mist and his face to the slope, and his tail hanging down over the pale stone, and waited.

Hako came out and went and sat next to him.

Then Salty. Then Bee. Then Calvin, who'd been in there longest so far, and came out silent, completely silent, with his face white, and walked onto the bridge without looking at anybody and sat down on the parapet next to Rachael, who'd gone before him, and Rachael didn't say anything, and didn't put her fist in his collar, and just sat with her shoulder against his.

They were waiting for everybody.

Of course they were.

He sat in the door and watched the first arch of the last bridge fill up with people. Sitting on the parapet. Standing. Leaning on each other. Thirty of them. Thirty-one. All facing back up the slope.

Rachael came out of the tollhouse and stood at the foot of the bridge and looked up at the End. At him. In the door.

For a long time.

He looked back.

She didn't say anything. She didn't come up the slope. She stood at the foot of the last bridge with seven knots on her cord and her hands in her pockets and looked at him, and he knew, the way he knew where all of them were, that she'd been in there and said the thing she'd been keeping for a year. The question. To the Toll. Instead of to him.

*Not yet.*

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded. Once. And turned round, and walked up onto the first arch, and sat down on the parapet next to Calvin.

He breathed out.

***

"I'll go last," said Ice.

He said it to the yard. To whoever was left. Thea, beside him in the door. Gabe, against the wall of the End with Hearthwall on his back. Xav, Chris, Winter, Ina, Yan, and the goat, which Aleaka had left with him because the goat wouldn't go down the slope without Ice, for reasons the goat hadn't explained.

Gabe looked at him.

"Course you will," said Gabe.

Not angry. He said it the way he'd say *it's raining*. A fact about him. And then he pushed himself off the wall and walked across the yard and down the slope without looking back, with Hearthwall on his back, and went into the tollhouse.

He was in there a long time.

When he came out he didn't go straight onto the bridge. He stood at the door of the tollhouse and turned round and looked up the slope at the End, the way Rachael had. At Ice in the door.

Then he lifted one hand. Not a wave. Flat. Palm out. The way you'd hold your hand up to somebody across a room to say *stay there, I'm coming back*.

Then he went onto the bridge, and sat down on the parapet at the very top of the first arch, in the middle, with a gap beside him. On his right.

Xav went. Chris. Winter. Ina. Yan, with Working On It upright beside him at the door, and Wendy on the arch already, standing up, her hand out toward the tollhouse door as if she could reach it from there. Yan was in there the shortest of anybody. Ice timed it without meaning to. He came out and stood at the foot of the bridge with his face doing nothing at all, and then he looked up at the End, at Ice, with his head on one side.

The way he looked at lights.

Then he went up onto the arch, and Wendy took the back of his coat.

The goat went. Aleaka came running back up the slope for it and the goat walked past her down to the tollhouse door on its own and the door opened for it, and Aleaka stood in the middle of the slope with both hands over her mouth, and the goat went in, and came out a minute later with the brown cord round its neck and seven knots in it. Nobody had tied them. Nobody had been in there with it.

Aleaka sat down in the grass on the slope and laughed until she cried.

And then it was Thea.

***

She didn't say anything.

She stood up from the step beside him with the lantern in her hand. Open. The amber light lying across the yard and the slope and leaning, the way it always leaned, at him. She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she bent down and put her forehead against the top of his head. The way Tegan had done to Malcolm in Turnstead. The way people did there. Very briefly.

And went.

He watched her walk down the slope with the lantern. Small, in the gold light. Not fast. And stand at the door. And the door opened. And she went in, and the amber went in with her, and the yard went a little greyer.

He sat in the door of the End on his own.

It was the first time he'd been on his own in a long time. He noticed it the way you notice a sound stopping. The yard was empty except for the pilgrims, on the walls and the stones, watching. Fifty of them. With six knots. Watching a man in a doorway with a lumpy little lantern in his lap.

*Ice*, said Alea.

"Yeah."

*You're on your own.*

"I know."

*You've never once been on your own on purpose. Not this time. Not since the jam.* She was quiet. *How does it feel?*

He thought about it.

"Like waiting at the top of a path," said Ice. "For somebody to come back up it."

The door opened.

Thea came out.

She stood at the foot of the bridge with the lantern held up and seven knots on her cord, and she didn't go onto the bridge. She turned round and looked up the slope at him. For a long time.

And the lantern leaned. All the way. Out of her hand. At him. So hard he thought for a moment it would pull out of her fingers and come up the slope on its own.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

He stood up.

***

The door opened before he touched it.

He'd known it would. He'd watched it open for thirty-six people and a goat. He still stopped. On the step of the tollhouse, in the gold morning, with the little lantern on his belt and Tuli's pole in his hand, and the bridge going out past his left shoulder into the mist with thirty-five people sitting on the first arch of it, every one of them facing the other way.

He'd seen them do it as he came down the slope. One after another. Turning round on the parapet. Their backs to the tollhouse. Their faces to the mist.

So whoever came next would know they weren't looking.

Thea at the foot of the bridge, with the lantern, facing the gorge. Gabe at the top of the arch with a gap on his right, facing the gorge. All of them.

He went in.

***

It was small. That was the first thing. Smaller inside than out, it felt like, the way a room does when it's very full.

It was very full.

Cords. On the walls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Hung on wooden pegs, from the stone floor to the low ceiling, every wall, so close together you couldn't see the stone behind them. Brown cords, every one, plain, the kind the keepers gave you at the Willow Bridge. And every one with seven knots.

Every pilgrim who'd ever crossed. He knew it the moment he saw them. Every one who'd ever stood in this room and paid. They'd gone out over the bridge into the mist with seven knots round their neck, and somehow, here, the cord was too. Hung on a peg. Kept.

The light was dim. Grey. Coming from nowhere in particular.

And in the middle of the room, at a plain wooden table, somebody was sitting.

Hooded. Grey. Very still. He couldn't see a face. He couldn't see hands, at first, until he saw them move. In its lap, a cord. Brown. Plain. And long. So long it went down off its lap onto the stone floor and coiled there, round and round, a great heap of it, like the rope on the deck of Corran's ship, more cord than any one person could ever use. And all along it, as far as he could see into the heap, knots.

Its hands were tying one. Slowly. As he came in.

He stood inside the door.

It didn't look up. It finished the knot. It laid the cord down on its lap. And then it spoke.

He'd thought it would have a voice. It didn't. It had voices. Very quiet, all at once, one on top of another, the way Maigo had said the road sounded through his feet. Thousands of people, a long way off, all saying the same thing at the same moment. Not loud. Close. Like somebody speaking in the next room with the door open.

"Sit down," said the Toll.

There was a stool. On his side of the table. He hadn't seen it. He sat on it.

"What have you kept?" said the Toll.

***

He told it.

He'd gone through it all last night. In the dark, on the floor of the End, start to end, for the first time in two lives, without going round anything. He'd thought that would make it easier. It didn't. It made it longer. He knew where all of it was now. He couldn't pretend any of it wasn't there.

He started at the white room.

The first one. A white floor, and thirty-six people in a circle waking up on it, and him at the edge of the circle, a little way off. That was the first thing he'd kept, and he said it first. That he'd been there and not really with them. That he'd stepped back, before, on Earth, from the two people he'd loved most, to keep himself in one piece, and gone to Yan's lot, and so in the white room he'd been in the circle and not in the middle of it, and from the first day he'd stood a little way off.

He told it floor 1. The first time. Mother Crane and the Copper Spoon, and a tab of two loaves and a pot that he never paid, because she died in the second winter of a fever while they were thirteen floors up, and nobody told them for a month.

He told it the pass. And the Down. And the Red City.

He told it four. Rachael and Dennis. Dennis running to her across a square, shouting. He told it exactly. He'd never told anybody exactly. Not even Thea; he'd given Thea the names and the floors and stopped. He told the Toll what he'd seen. What he'd done. What he hadn't. Where he'd been standing. How far.

He told it Calvin's arm.

He told it the gate. The six. The guardian's room. He told it every name. He'd set them all down once, palms up, a long time ago, and he set them all down again, here, in the grey, at a plain wooden table, with his hands flat on the wood.

He told it the floors after. The long years. The way the thirty-five became fewer, and the fewer stopped looking at each other, and he got stronger, and faster, and better, alone, always alone, a little way off, because that was where he'd always been, and because if you were a little way off it couldn't take you apart when the next one went.

He told it the hundredth floor.

He told it Thea. On the pass. Turning. Raising the ward. Stepping round him, past his left shoulder, the way he'd asked her never to and she'd done anyway. The thing going through the ward. Through her. Three words. Four. That he hadn't heard. That he'd found out, two years later, on another floor, in another life, from a friend's echo in a fox's logs, were *I love you. Idiot.*

He told it the white again. After. And waking up. On floor 1. A day ahead. Alone. Knowing everything.

He told it why he'd gone a floor ahead. Every floor. First. Alone. In the dark. Not to be a hero. Not to be first. Because if he was a floor ahead he could clear the thing before they got there, and if he cleared the thing before they got there, they'd come up the stair to an empty room, and nobody would die. And because if he was a floor ahead, he wouldn't have to stand in a room with them and know.

He told it the Grey. The jam. *Help.*

He told it the light on his belt.

It took a long time.

He didn't know how long. The light in the room didn't change. The Toll didn't move. Its hands lay still on the cord in its lap the whole time, and it didn't say anything, and it didn't stop him, and it didn't look away. He couldn't see its face. He knew it didn't look away.

Somewhere in the middle of it, he realised his face was wet.

He didn't stop.

***

And then he got to the end, and there was one thing left.

He'd known there would be. He'd felt it last night, going through it all in the dark. Right at the bottom. Under everything. The thing Thea had felt in him like a stone in a pocket and never asked about. He'd gone through every floor and every name, and it had been there the whole time, under all of it, and he'd come to the end and it was still there.

He sat at the table with his hands flat on the wood.

He'd never said it. Not to Thea. Not to himself, out loud, in two lives. He'd never even let it get as far as words in his own head. It had always stayed down at the bottom, heavy and smooth and shaped like a stone, and he'd walked round it.

"It should have been one of them," said Ice.

The Toll didn't move.

"Out of thirty-six," said Ice. His voice was very quiet. "Somebody came back. And it was me." He looked at his hands. "It should have been one of them. Any of them. Gabe. Thea. Rachael. Calvin, with both his arms. Any of them would have done it better. Any of them would have—" He stopped. "They were the ones who were good. They were together. They looked after each other. I was the one who went off. I was the one in the other group, a little way off. I didn't deserve to be the one who got to try again."

He'd said it.

He sat there.

"I've thought that every day," said Ice. "For two years. Every floor. Every time one of them said *thank you*. Every time Gabe put his hand on my neck. I thought: *it should have been you. You'd have done it better. You'd have got everybody through without going off on your own.*" He was quiet. "I've never told anybody that. Not Thea. Not Alea. Not anybody. That's what I've kept."

The room was very quiet.

The Toll didn't say anything for a long time.

Then its hands moved.

Not tying. Lifting. The cord in its lap. The long one, with all the knots. It lifted a length of it off its lap, a few feet, and held it up between them over the table, in the grey light. And he saw the knots. Close. Hundreds of them, along that one short length. And every knot—

He didn't know how he knew. He did.

Every knot was somebody who'd sat on this stool and said *it should have been somebody else*.

Hundreds of them. On one short length of cord. And the cord went down off the Toll's lap onto the floor and round and round in a heap the size of a cartwheel.

"You're not the first," said the Toll.

Very quietly. In all its voices.

"Most people who sit there have kept that," said the Toll. "Somewhere. Under everything else. *It should have been somebody else. I didn't deserve it. Somebody better should have had it.*" It laid the cord back down. "The ones who lived when the others didn't. The ones who got the house, the field, the second chance. The ones who came back." It was quiet. "I've heard it more than anything else in the life of the road."

He didn't say anything.

"I'll ask you what I ask all of them," said the Toll. "The ones who keep that."

"What?"

The hooded head tipped. Very slightly. Toward his belt. Toward Gabe's terrible lantern, and the light in it, humming its four notes in the quiet.

"Whose was it?" said the Toll.

He stared at it.

"The second time," said the Toll. "The going back. The trying again. You say it should have been somebody else's." Its voices were very gentle. "So whose was it? When it came. Whose was it to have?"

He couldn't say anything.

The little light on his belt had stopped humming.

He looked down at it. Gabe's lantern. Lumpy, lopsided, forty points of pale light through a bad knot, so you could nearly see the middle. Sitting very still. Turned up at his face. The way it had sat in the dark on sixteen, before he knew it was there. Waiting. Asking.

*Whose.*

And on sixteen, on the moss, with the tongue coming, he'd put his hand over it and said—

"Mine," said Ice.

Out loud. In the grey room. To the Toll. To the light. To himself.

"It was mine," said Ice. His voice broke right down the middle. "It came to me. Not to anybody else. Me. I didn't— I didn't ask for it, and I didn't deserve it, and it was mine anyway. It's *mine*."

The light on his belt hummed.

Four notes. The middle—

Right.

He heard it. The middle of the song. The bit it had got wrong every single time since sixteen, every night, on his belt, on her shoulder, trying. It got it right. Once. Clear. The whole of it. The way she'd sung it at two in the morning on the other end of a call a long time ago.

He put both his hands over his face and didn't make a sound for a long time.

***

When he took them away the Toll was tying a knot.

Not in its own cord. In his. The brown cord round his own neck, with six knots on it. He hadn't felt it reach across. He didn't feel it now. He just saw the grey hands, very close, at his collarbone, tying the seventh knot. Slowly. Carefully. And patting it, once, when it was done. The way the keeper at the Willow Bridge had patted Winter's.

"Paid," said the Toll.

He sat on the stool.

"That's the heaviest thing anybody's paid me," said the Toll. "In the life of the road."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." Its voices were almost amused. "That's what I'm for." It sat back. "Who else knows? What you told me. The whole of it."

"One," said Ice. "Most of it. Not that last bit."

"She'll know it when you're ready." It wasn't a question. "And the rest?"

"They worked it out. Most of them. The shape." He looked at the door. "They never asked."

The Toll was quiet for a moment.

"I don't tell," it said. "Not ever. Not what anybody pays. That's the whole of me." It folded its grey hands on the cord in its lap. "But I'll tell you one thing that isn't theirs. It's mine. I counted it."

"What?"

"Your name came through that door this morning," said the Toll, "thirty-five times."

He didn't understand.

Then he did.

He sat on the stool in the grey room with the seventh knot at his collarbone and the light on his belt humming the middle right, and couldn't move.

Thirty-five people. Every one of them in here before him. Every one of them sitting on this stool and paying what they'd kept. And every one—

He didn't know what they'd said. He'd never know. It wasn't his. *I worked it out and I never asked. I knew and I kept it. I saw him look at the Long Wall. I watched him count us. I'm on the step.* He didn't know. He'd never know.

But his name had been in all of them.

*Thirty-five times.*

"They've been keeping it too," said the Toll. Very quietly. "For you. All this time. Out there." It tipped its hooded head at the door. "It's not a stone you've been carrying, Voyager. Not on your own. Not for a long time. It's a wall. Thirty-six people holding one end of it each."

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

The Toll let him not say anything for a long time.

***

When he stood up to go, it spoke again.

"Thirty came this way once," said the Toll.

He stopped. With his hand on the door.

"A long time ago," said the Toll. "Thirty. Like you. From somewhere else. Up the stair from the town that forgot." Its voices were very quiet. "Every one of them sat there. Every one of them paid. The woman with the long *R* went last."

He turned round.

"Ruth," said Ice.

The hooded head didn't move.

"Did they—" He didn't know how to ask it. He'd never known. The cry on 22. On 31, 40, 52. On 99. Then nothing. Ever. "Are they— Did they get— What happened to—"

"Not mine," said the Toll. Gently. "I keep what's paid here. Not what happens after." Its hands lay on the cord. "They went over together. All thirty. That's all I have."

He stood at the door.

"Ask on twenty-two," said the Toll.

He looked at it for a long time. The grey hood. The grey hands. The cord in its lap with all the knots, and the heap of it on the floor, and the thousands of cords on the walls, every one with seven knots, every one kept.

"Thank you," said Ice.

"Go on," said the Toll. "They're waiting. They've got their backs turned so you'll know they're not looking." Its voices were almost fond. "You'd better go and make them turn round."

He went out.

***

The gold was very bright.

He stood on the step of the tollhouse with his eyes stinging and the seventh knot at his collarbone and Tuli's pole in his hand, and looked at the bridge.

Thirty-five people. On the first arch. On the parapet. Backs to him. Faces to the mist. Thea at the foot of it with the lantern, facing the gorge. Gabe at the top of the arch, in the middle, with a gap on his right.

Not one of them looking.

He walked to the foot of the bridge.

He stopped beside Thea. She didn't turn round. She was looking at the gold mist. The lantern in her hand was leaning so hard toward him it was nearly horizontal.

"Thea," said Ice.

She turned round.

She looked at his face. At whatever it was doing. At the wet on it, and the seventh knot, and the little lantern on his belt.

The light in it hummed. Her song. Four notes. The middle right.

Thea's face went completely still.

"It got it," she said. Very quietly. "The middle."

"Just now. In there."

She looked at the little lantern for a long time. Then at him.

She didn't ask what he'd said. She didn't ask anything. She put the big lantern down on the first stone of the bridge, carefully, and put both her arms round him, and her face in his neck, and held on.

Up on the first arch, he heard somebody turn round.

Then somebody else.

He didn't look. He held Thea at the foot of the last bridge in the gold morning and heard them turn, one after another, on the parapet, thirty-four people turning round to face the slope, and not one of them saying anything. And then footsteps. On the stone. Coming down.

Gabe.

He knew the footsteps. He'd known them since he was thirteen.

Gabe stopped beside them at the foot of the bridge. He didn't say anything. He put his big hand on the back of Ice's neck, and left it there, warm, the way Hearthwall was warm.

"Paid?" said Gabe.

"Paid," said Ice. Into Thea's hair.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

Gabe was quiet for a moment.

"Good," said Gabe. "Took you long enough, mate."

He didn't take his hand away.
