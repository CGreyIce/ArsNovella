The knapper took the first bite out of Salty's sword on the morning after the trial, and he heard it do it, which was the worst part.

He heard it over the falls. That was saying something. The ledges under the third fall were the loudest place he'd ever stood in his life. The water came down past them in a white wall three metres away, and the spray hung in the air so thick you breathed it, and the roar was so big that it stopped being a sound after a while and became a sort of pressure on your chest. You couldn't hear yourself think. You could hear somebody if they shouted right next to you, and nothing else at all.

He heard the knapper.

*Tack.*

A small, neat, horrible sound. Like somebody tapping a flint with a stone. And a chip came off the edge of his greatsword, a flake of good grey steel the size of a fingernail, and spun away into the spray.

*Tack. Tack.*

"*IT'S EATING MY SWORD*," he shouted, at nobody, because nobody could hear him.

***

The river-folk had asked for help two days before. Slowly. That was how they asked for everything.

An old eel-man called Mott had come to the house for lights in the evening and stood in the door for a long time, looking at the fire Ina had lit, before he said anything at all. Then he'd said that the knappers had come up the ledges again, as they did every autumn, and were stripping the eel traps below the third fall. And that the river-folk couldn't stop them, because knappers were quick and river-folk were not, and a knapper's small claw went through webbing like a knife through bread. And that the last people who'd stopped them were the last lights, three hundred and twelve years ago, and that he'd been very young, and had watched them do it from a balcony, and had always wanted to see it again.

Drew had written it on the rock. *KNAPPERS. THIRD FALL. YAN'S LOT.*

So here they were.

There were three of them. Crabs, Salty would have called them on Earth, if crabs were the size of a handcart and had shells like wet slate and eyes on long stalks that swivelled independently in two different directions at once. Each one had two claws. A big one, heavy and blunt, for crushing. And a thin, narrow one, serrated, like a long flint knife, that it used to chip things. Rock. Wood. Eel traps.

Swords.

The biggest one had its crushing claw closed round his greatsword.

It had come at him off the ledge the moment they climbed down. He'd taken it on the flat of the blade, the way he took everything, feet set and knees bent and the weight of the sword across his chest. It had stopped. He'd held. And then it had simply reached out with the big claw, very calmly, and closed it round his blade, and *held*. And brought the little claw up, and started *knapping*.

*Tack. Tack. Tack.*

Flakes came off the edge. He could see them go. He pulled. It didn't let go. It was like trying to pull a sword out of a door. His boots were sliding on the wet stone, backwards, toward the edge of the ledge and the white wall of the fall and the long drop to the pool below, and the knapper was walking him there, slowly, sideways, the way crabs walk, chipping his sword as it came.

*Tack.*

He let go with one hand and punched it in the eye.

It didn't have much effect, except that it hurt his hand. The eye went back into its stalk with a sort of offended snap and came out again.

"*Salty*." Tiger. Somewhere. A blur at the edge of his vision. "*SALTY, IT'S GOT A—*"

"*I KNOW IT'S GOT A*—"

An arrow went into the left eyestalk. Right at the base. Black shaft, goose feathers, one of his own heavy heads. The eye went out like a lamp. The knapper jerked, and the big claw loosened, a little, and he got his sword back a hand's width before it clamped again.

Wendy. Up on the rock above them, in the spray, with her bow. Her cardigan soaked black. Her silent headphones round her neck. Not even looking at him. Already drawing the next one.

*Tack.*

"*CURSIFFE*," he roared.

Cursiffe was behind him. On the ledge. With his little vial of ink and his brush. He'd been writing something on the knapper's shell since it grabbed the sword. Salty had seen the brush go out, quick and neat. And he'd seen the spray hit the letters and run them. The ink went down the slate shell in long dark streaks like tears, and whatever he'd written didn't hold, because it wasn't a word any more. It was just wet.

"*IT RUNS*," Cursiffe shouted. Calmly. Even shouting, he sounded calm. "*THE WATER. IT RUNS THE WORDS.*"

"*THEN DO SOMETHING THAT DOESN'T RUN*—"

"*I AM*."

He'd got another vial out. Not the blue one. A small clay pot, with a stopper, that Mott had given him the night before when he'd asked about ink. River-folk ink. Lamp-black and eel-oil, thick and shiny and stinking.

Tiger, going past at a speed that didn't make sense, sniffed the air on the way and went "*Oh, it's FISH*," in tones of deep betrayal.

Cursiffe dipped his brush in it and reached past Salty's shoulder and wrote, very small, very fast, on the knapper's big claw, right where it was closed round the sword. Three letters. Four.

The spray hit it. And ran off. The oil shed the water like a duck's back.

The letters stayed.

*OPEN*.

The claw opened.

It just *opened*. Like the stone book under the bridge on floor 5, the one with the keyhole in the cover. He'd been there. He'd tried a nail in the keyhole, and Tiger had tried a lockpick, and Wendy had said *things on statues don't open*, and Cursiffe had written that same word across it in wet ink and it had swung open in front of them all.

The knapper's claw swung open. And the knapper looked very surprised about it, as far as a crab can look surprised, which is mostly with the eyestalks. It had one left.

Salty got his sword back. All of it. And brought it up and over and down, two-handed, with his whole weight behind it, onto the joint of the little knapping claw, where it met the shell.

It came off.

It went spinning off the ledge into the white water and was gone, and the knapper reared up, and Tiger was underneath it.

Salty hadn't seen him go. Nobody ever saw Tiger go. One second there was a blur at the edge of the ledge and the next there was Tiger, flat on his back on the wet stone, *under* the knapper, with both short blades up. Right in the soft joint under the tail flap, where the shell didn't meet. The only place on it that wasn't slate.

The knapper stopped.

It stood there for a moment, over Tiger, very still. Then it folded down slowly onto him, and Tiger said "*nope*" and came out the other side of it, sideways, in a long wet slide that took him to within a metre of the edge, where Wendy had come down off her rock to grab the back of his coat.

"*Don't*," said Wendy.

"I didn't *do* anything."

"You were about to go over."

"I was about to *not* go over."

"That's what people say," said Wendy, "right before," and didn't let go of his coat.

***

The other two took longer.

The second one got Salty on the forearm. He saw it coming, the big claw swinging round low, and got the sword down, and was a quarter of a second slow, and it closed on his left arm above the wrist. Not all the way. The vambrace took most of it. But enough. He felt the bone bend. He felt the number in the corner of his eye go down fast, the way it had gone down in the gorse on floor 1, *seventy, sixty, fifty*—

And stop.

Yan's hands. Over him. Cupped, the way you cup a candle in the wind. Not touching him. Just there. Yan had come down the ledge in the spray without a sound, the way he moved, and knelt beside him, and put his hands round something over Salty's chest that Salty couldn't see. And the number stopped going down. Just stopped, at forty-eight, and stayed there.

*Shelter*. He'd felt it before. On floor 1, with the ram. It felt like somebody standing between you and a cold wind.

He looked at Yan's face. It had gone very still. Not the calm still. The other kind.

Wendy put an arrow in the second one's eye. Then the other eye. It let go of his arm. Tiger did the tail joint. Salty, one-handed now, broke its knapping claw on the rock with the flat of his sword because he was very, very annoyed with it.

The third one was the smallest.

It was backing away. Toward the water. With its little claw up, in front of its face, the way you'd put your arm up. And Salty saw Yan look at it. And lower his staff.

He didn't say anything. He just lowered it. And looked at it. At the small one, backing up the ledge, with its claw up.

Wendy looked at Yan. Then at the knapper.

She drew. She didn't hurry. She put an arrow through the soft place under its eyestalks, where Tiger had shown them it was, very cleanly, so that it went down all at once, without moving again.

She lowered her bow. She didn't look at Yan. She just stood next to him, in the spray, with her shoulder touching his.

Salty sat on the wet ledge holding his arm and watched them both, and didn't understand, and filed it away.

***

His greatsword looked like a saw.

He sat on a bench outside the house for lights in the afternoon, in the thin autumn sun, with the sword across his knees and his left arm still aching where Yan had mended it, and looked at it. The edge was chipped from hilt to point. Not dents. *Chips*. Little half-moon flakes knocked out of it all the way down, neat and even, the way you'd knap a flint arrowhead. Hundreds of them.

"It's ruined," he said.

"It's not ruined," said Wendy. She was sitting next to him with her knees up and her cardigan drying on the rail. "It's *serrated*. That's a feature."

"It's ruined, Wen."

"You can fix it."

"I can fix a nail," said Salty. "I can make a shield rim. I can make a hand-warmer. I can't fix *that*." He turned it over. The other side was the same. "That's proper smithing. That's—"

He stopped, because from somewhere deep underneath the city, under the stone arches and the stilts and the water, there was a sound.

*Thud*.

He'd been hearing it for five days. Everybody had. It went through the whole of Wendmere, through the floorboards of the house and the stones of the walkways and the water of the lake. Very deep. Very slow. Once every four seconds. You stopped noticing it after the first day, the way you stop noticing your own heart.

*Thud*.

"What *is* that," said Salty.

"Calvin thinks it's the Cataract," said Wendy. "Breathing."

"Calvin thinks everything's the Cataract."

*Thud*.

He got up, and put the sword on his shoulder, and went to find out.

***

It was a hammer.

It was under the second arch. Down a stair cut into the stone of the arch itself, inside it, wet and dark and roaring, to a long low room carved out of the rock at the very bottom, where the fall came down past one open side like a moving wall. And a waterwheel. A huge one. Black wood, green with weed, turning in the edge of the fall. And on the end of its axle, a great iron cam, that came round, and caught the end of a beam as thick as a tree. And lifted it. And let it go.

And on the other end of the beam, the hammer came down.

*THUD*.

It was the size of a barrel. It came down on an anvil the size of a bed. The whole room shook with it. Then the cam came round again, and lifted it, slow, slow, and let it go.

*THUD*.

And standing beside the anvil, with a long piece of glowing iron held in tongs, turning it a little between each blow, very slowly, was a river-woman.

She was old. Not as old as Sefa. Older than Oarin. Her skin had gone dark in the forge-light, like river stone, and she had her green hair tied up in a knot on top of her head, out of the way, and a leather apron on that came down to her ankles. Her webbed hands were burned. Old burns, white and shiny, across the backs of both of them, so many that the skin looked like lace.

She didn't look round. She turned the iron a quarter. The hammer came down. She turned it again.

Gabe was sitting on a stool against the wall. And Jared beside him. They'd clearly been there for some time. Gabe had the little leather pouch in his lap with the Kiln-heart Iron in it, glowing faintly red through the leather, the way it always did.

"Salt," said Gabe, without looking away from the anvil. "Sit down. Don't talk. She's been on that blade for eleven years."

Salty sat down.

"*Eleven years*," he said, after a while. Quietly.

"She told us," said Jared. Also quietly. "This morning. When we came in. She said, *I am Ressa. This is a blade. I began it eleven years ago. I will finish it in the spring.* And then she hasn't said anything since."

"Since *this morning*?"

"She talked to Gabe a bit."

"She looked at the Iron," said Gabe. He touched the pouch. "She looked at it for a really long time. Then she said, *Not here. That one wants a hotter fire than mine. Higher.*" He was quiet. "Then she went back to the blade."

*THUD*.

They sat there, the three of them, the Blacksmiths, the nail guild, on stools against the wet wall of a forge under a waterfall, and watched an old river-woman turn a piece of iron a quarter of a turn every four seconds. It got dark outside. The fall went from white to grey. Nobody said anything.

It should have been boring. Salty had never been any good at sitting still. He liked to *do* things. He liked to hit them, and see what happened, and hit them again. He'd learned to forge in Ulla Dray's black stone smithy on floor 1 in about three days, a hundred roofing nails and a burn on his thumb and done.

It wasn't boring.

He watched the iron. He watched how she turned it. He watched the hammer come down, and how the colour of the iron changed in the place where it landed. And after a while, he started to see it. What she was doing. Every blow was the same. But the iron wasn't. Every time she turned it a quarter, it was in a slightly different place under the hammer. A finger's width along. And then back. And then along. She wasn't hitting it. She was *walking* the hammer up and down it. Slowly. Four seconds a step. Eleven years.

"She's folding it," said Salty. Under his breath. "She's not shaping it. She's *folding* it."

Ressa turned her head and looked at him.

For the first time since he'd come in. A long, slow look, over the anvil, with her large dark eyes, while the hammer came down beside her.

"Yes," said Ressa. Her voice was rough, from the forge and the water. "Two thousand times. Each fold, half the iron, onto the other half. Two thousand." She looked at the sword on his knees. "What is that?"

He held it up.

She looked at it for a long time. At the chips all the way down. She didn't laugh. He'd half expected her to.

"Knappers," she said.

"Yeah."

"You killed them?"

"Three."

She looked at him a while longer.

"That is a sword made in a hurry," said Ressa.

"Yeah," said Salty. "It's what I do."

The hammer came down.

Ressa almost smiled. Almost. The corners of her wide mouth moved.

"Leave it," she said. "I will show you. Not today." She turned the iron. "Today I am folding."

***

The bells rang at dusk. Not real bells. The river-folk didn't have bells. A sound like bells. Long, low, humming notes, coming from all over the city at once, from every balcony and walkway and window, as if Wendmere itself had started to sing.

Ressa put down her tongs.

She'd never put down her tongs. Not once all afternoon. She put them down on the anvil, and stepped back, and the waterwheel kept turning and the hammer kept falling, *thud*, on nothing, on the empty anvil. And she went to the open side of the forge, where the fall came down, and stood there, with her burned hands folded in front of her apron.

"What is it?" said Gabe.

"Somebody is going over," said Ressa.

They went up the stair after her. Up through the inside of the arch, and out onto the walkway on top, in the cold grey dusk. And all across Wendmere, on every balcony, the river-folk had come out. Standing. Silent, except for that low humming note. And as Salty watched, they began to put out their lamps.

One by one. All over the city. Gold lights in the windows behind the water, going out. From the top of the falls, down. A long slow wave of dark, coming down the city toward the lake.

And on the lake, below the last arch, there was a boat.

A small one. Made of woven reeds, pale gold. With a single lamp at its bow, the only light left on the water. And lying in it, wrapped in a green net from head to foot, somebody very long and very still.

And walking beside it, there was an old woman.

She was walking on the water. That was what it looked like, from up on the arch. Then he saw the causeway. A line of flat stones, set in the lake, running out from the end of the last jetty across the flat green water, toward the far end of the lake. Where the lake *ended*. He'd seen it from the stair on the way down and not understood it. Past the city, past the last stilts, the whole lake simply stopped, in a long straight lip like the edge of a table, and poured over it in one smooth curve of green glass into white mist. Nobody in Wendmere had ever seen the bottom. They called it the Edge. The water was running over the causeway stones ankle-deep, fast, toward it. And the old woman was walking along them, slowly, in the running water, with a long pole in her hands, guiding the reed boat beside her.

She was very bent. Very thin. Her hair was white. And the pole in her hands was long, longer than she was, and had something on the end of it that caught the last of the light. A blade. Long and curved and thin, like a new moon. Set across the end of the pole.

"The edge-keeper," said Ressa, very quietly, beside him. "Aud."

Salty watched the old woman walk the boat out across the lake to the edge.

It took a long time. Everything did, here. The water ran over the stones round her ankles, white and fast, and she walked through it a step at a time, and the boat drifted beside her on its line, and the humming went on all over the dark city. And her arms were shaking. Even from up on the arch he could see it. The long pole trembling in her hands.

At the very end of the causeway, at the edge, she stopped.

She stood there for a long moment, with the water running past her feet and over the lip into the white. The boat swung out on its line beside her, over the edge, held. With its one small lamp.

Then she lifted the pole, and turned it, and the curved blade came down, and cut the line.

The boat went over.

It went over the Edge in one smooth tip, like a leaf. The lamp went with it. A little gold spark, falling, down into the white mist, and gone.

The humming stopped.

All over Wendmere, all at once, in the dark, the lamps came back on.

***

Yan was at the end of the last jetty when they came back.

Salty saw him from the walkway. Standing right at the very end, where Ice had sat for the trial. Staring out across the dark water at the place where the boat had gone over. Very still. The stillest Salty had ever seen him, and Yan was always still.

Wendy was beside him. A little way back. Watching him.

Salty went down.

He saw Yan's hands as he got close. They were cupped. Held out a little in front of him, at chest height, round nothing. The way he'd held them over Salty on the ledge that morning. And they were white. Bone white, right up to the wrist, the way they went when he'd used Shelter too long.

"Yan," said Salty.

Yan didn't look round. "It didn't go up," he said.

His voice was very low. Even for him.

"What didn't?"

Yan didn't answer.

Salty looked at Wendy. Wendy looked back at him, with her two different eyes, the brown one and the violet one, for a long moment. And then she did a thing she'd been doing for weeks, since floor 6, that Salty had noticed and not asked about. She looked at Yan. And then she looked away, and pressed her lips together.

He'd figured she was carrying something. She muttered when she carried things. She'd been muttering since the glass.

"Yan," said Salty. "Whatever it is. You can tell me. I'm not going to make it weird."

Yan was quiet for a long time.

Then he lowered his white hands, slowly. And turned round. And looked at Salty. Not at his face. Lower. At the middle of his chest. The way he looked at people sometimes, that Salty had always thought was just a Yan thing, like the slow voice.

"Everything's got a light," said Yan.

And he told him.

***

He didn't tell it long. Yan never told anything long. Candles. In everybody. Under the breastbone, since the white room. Big and small and different colours. In the people, and in the NPCs, the same, exactly the same, so that he couldn't tell them apart and had stopped trying. In animals, smaller, like coals. And on six, in the glass, the coals had started burning like people's. So that he didn't know what he was killing any more. And Wendy took the shots he couldn't.

Salty listened to all of it. He didn't say anything until Yan had finished.

Then he said, "Okay."

Yan looked at him.

"Okay," said Salty again. "That's—yeah. Okay." He thought about it. He thought about the small knapper on the ledge with its claw up in front of its face, and Yan lowering his staff, and Wendy drawing without hurrying, so it wouldn't move again. He thought about Wendy muttering since the glass. "That's why she's been taking your shots."

"Yes," said Yan.

"Good," said Salty. He looked at Wendy. "Good. That's—good, Wen."

Wendy didn't say anything. She just put her hand in his.

"Does mine look all right?" said Salty.

Yan looked at the middle of his chest for a moment.

"It's enormous," said Yan. "It's like a forge. It never keeps still."

Salty thought about that.

"Sounds right," he said.

And Wendy laughed, a short surprised laugh, the first one in what felt like days, and some of the stillness went out of Yan's face.

"So what didn't go up," said Salty.

Yan looked back out across the dark lake. To where the causeway ran out to the edge, under the water, invisible now.

"On every floor," said Yan. "When something dies. A thread goes up off it. Thin. Like smoke off a candle when you blow it out. It goes up and it goes—" He stopped. "Somewhere. It goes. Every time. Woolbacks, hounds, the Prism. It goes up."

"Okay."

"He didn't." Yan's voice had gone very low again. "The old man in the boat. His candle didn't go up. It stayed. In the boat, with him, under the net. Small. Sitting there. All the way across the lake." He was quiet. "Like it was waiting. Like it didn't know where to go. And she walked it. Aud. All the way out to the edge. And when she cut the line—"

He stopped.

"It went," said Yan. "Over. With him. Into the white. It knew where to go, then. She'd walked it there."

He looked at his hands. The white was going out of them slowly.

"I didn't mean to do it," he said. "Hold my hands up. I didn't even know I'd done it. It was just sitting there, in the boat, so small. And I wanted to keep the wind off it." He looked out at the edge. "There wasn't any wind. That wasn't what it needed."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"She's so old, Salty," said Yan. Very quietly. "Aud. Did you see? Her hands were shaking. All the way out."

"I saw," said Salty.

***

He went back to the forge after everybody was asleep.

He didn't tell Wendy. He didn't need to. When he got up off his bedroll in the dark she opened one eye, looked at him, and closed it again. Wendy knew where he went at night. Wendy generally knew everything.

The hammer was still going under the second arch. *Thud*. Every four seconds. Ressa was still there, at the anvil, turning her iron, in the light of the forge, as if she hadn't moved since dusk. Maybe she hadn't.

He sat down on his stool against the wall.

"The edge-keeper's blade," he said.

Ressa turned the iron a quarter.

"The long one," said Salty. "Curved. On the pole. Who made it?"

The hammer came down.

"I did," said Ressa. "A hundred and ninety-one years ago."

"How long did it take?"

"Eleven years."

He'd thought she'd say that. He sat on the stool and looked at the hammer and thought about it. About eleven years. About a boat going over a fall with one small lamp in it, and an old woman's arms shaking on a long pole. About Yan, standing at the end of a jetty with his hands cupped round nothing, looking at that curved blade catch the light, the way you look at something you didn't know you'd been waiting for.

Yan had carried a plain staff since floor 1. He'd never complained about it. Yan never complained about anything.

"Teach me," said Salty.

Ressa didn't look round.

"It will take you eleven years," she said.

"I haven't got eleven years. I've got about forty days, and then we go up." He leaned forward on the stool. "So teach me the first day. And I'll do the rest somewhere else. Badly. In a hurry. It's what I do."

The hammer came down. *Thud*.

Ressa turned her head and looked at him for a long, long time, over the anvil, with her large dark eyes. The way she'd looked at the Kiln-heart Iron. The way she'd looked at his chipped sword.

Then she reached down beside the anvil, slowly, and picked something up, and held it out to him across the forge.

A hammer. A small one. Old, the handle worn smooth and dark where hands had held it. Her hands. For a very long time.

He took it.

"First day," said Ressa. She turned back to the anvil. "Sit. Listen to the hammer."

"That's it?"

"That is the first day." She turned the iron. "You make things in a hurry, little light. You hit them to see what happens. First you will listen to how long it takes."

*Thud*.

Salty sat on the stool, under the second arch, inside the roar of the fall, with an old river-woman's hammer in his lap. And listened.

*Thud*.

Four seconds.

*Thud*.

He didn't tell Yan. He'd decided that before he'd even come down the stair. He'd tell him in the spring. Or on floor 10. Or whenever it was done, however badly, however much of a hurry.

*Thud*.

He sat there until the fall went from black to grey. He didn't move once.
