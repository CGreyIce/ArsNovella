Ina found it by accident, with his face.

He'd been kneeling on the salt with his hand flat on the crust round Maigo's foot. The third evening. The gold dusk, with Maigo hanging off his own root in Chris's arms and his roots gone down into the white too deep and too dry to come back. Ina had pulled his mask down without thinking. He always did, for the fire. The grey oni mask, with its horns and its long carved grin, that turned his Onibi white-blue and made people step back from him in the street. He'd pulled it down and put his hand on the salt and warmed it, very gently, round and round, the way you'd warm a frozen lock.

And the salt had steamed.

Not much. A thin white breath coming up off the crust where his fire went into it, where the brine under the skin got warm. It came up round his hand and round Maigo's foot and into his face. Into the mask. It was cold, the mask. It was always cold, whatever he did with his fire; he didn't know what it was made of, only that it never warmed. The steam came up and touched the inside of it, and stayed there.

He didn't notice. Not then. He was watching Gigi talk Maigo's roots out of the salt, one at a time, under her breath, *slowly, slowly, let them come*, and watching Maigo's face, and Chris's, and the one leaf.

He noticed afterwards. Much later. In the dark, by the sleds, when it was all over and Deston had done the thing with the threads and Maigo was asleep on the wood and everybody was sitting about on the salt too tired to move. He'd pushed the mask up onto his forehead, and a drop had run down from it, from the inside of the chin, onto his lip.

He'd licked it off. The way you would.

He sat there in the dark for a long time.

Then he pulled the mask off his head altogether and turned it over in his hands and looked at the inside. At the chin, where the steam had gone. There were more drops. Tiny. Clinging. He put his finger on one and put his finger in his mouth.

Not salt.

***

"Salt doesn't go up," said Ina.

He'd waited until morning. He always waited. He'd sat up the rest of the night with the mask in his lap, thinking about it, turning it over, making sure. Ina's two kinds of *no*, Winter called them: *I haven't thought about it*, and *I've thought about it all my life*. He didn't say yes either, not to anything, not until he'd thought about it for long enough. So he'd sat up.

In the morning he went and found Higu.

She was on the aft sled with the rock. She'd hardly slept either. Nobody had, much. On the right-hand side of the rock, under everything else, in her small square capitals, there was a new line from the night before: *WATER UNDER THE SALT. NEARLY FRESH. TOO DEEP.* And under that, the number she'd been crossing out and rewriting every morning since Seep. *3.*

"Salt doesn't go up," said Ina again, sitting down beside her. Close. So she could hear.

Higu looked at him.

"When it steams," said Ina. "When the brine gets warm, and steams. The steam's water. The salt stays behind." He held out the mask. "I put my fire into the crust last night. For Maigo. And it steamed, and the steam went in here. And it's fresh. It's on the inside of the chin." He paused. "I licked it."

Higu took the mask.

She turned it over. She looked at the inside of the chin. She put her finger on it and tasted it, the way he had, and her face didn't change at all, which was how Ina knew.

"There's no fire on the Salt," said Higu slowly. "That's why the rakers can't. Tullah said. There's nothing to burn. Nothing grows. Every bit of wood in Seep came up the stair or across the Salt from somewhere else, a hundred years ago, and they don't burn it. They'd sooner die." She looked at him. "There's no fire on this floor."

"There's mine," said Ina.

Higu didn't say anything for a long moment.

"It doesn't need anything," said Ina. "It never has. It doesn't eat wood. It doesn't need air, much. It burns as long as I want it to, and nothing can put it out but me." He looked out at the white. "On floor 9, Ice said it was the one fire on the floor nothing else could take. I thought he meant the cones."

Higu was looking at him very hard.

"So I warm the brine," said Ina. "Not boil it. Boiling's a noise. Just warm it, very slowly, so it steams. And it goes up. And it touches something cold." He held up the mask. "And it comes back down as water. Without the salt."

"Something cold," said Higu.

"Yes," said Ina.

They both looked across the camp at the same time. At the second sled, where Winter was sitting on the edge with her bow across her knees and her silver hair tied up under a cloth and her black horns poking through it, restringing an arrow, with her tongue between her teeth.

Winter looked up. She felt it, Ina thought. She always felt it when he was looking at her.

"*What*," mouthed Winter, across the camp.

Higu picked up the chalk.

***

It took them a day to make it work.

The first one didn't. They'd known it wouldn't. Higu said the first one never did, and Ina believed her, because she'd said it on every floor and been right on every floor. But they made it anyway, that afternoon, on the salt by the sleds, with everybody standing round in a ring, softly, watching.

Chris and Hako dug through the crust. A hand's depth, with the edge of a shield and a raker's salt-saw, slowly, the long steady hush that didn't go down. Under it, the slurry, grey and thick as porridge, wet. They scooped it out with a mug into one of Ice's cooking pots, the big iron one he'd carried up from floor 1 and wouldn't let anybody else wash. It came out grey and gritty. Brine.

Ina knelt and put his hand under the pot. Mask down. White-blue fire, very low.

And Winter held a shield over it.

Chris's. His long kite shield, tilted, so the low end hung out past the rim of the pot over an empty water cask. Winter had taken one of her Hoarfrost arrows and drawn its head slowly over the face of the shield, side to side, like somebody icing a cake, and the steel had gone white. Frosted. Cold enough to burn.

The brine warmed. And steamed. A thin white breath, up off the grey, into the frost.

And the frost ran.

Ina watched it. They all did. Thirty-five people, standing in a ring on the salt in the afternoon sun, very quietly, watching a shield. Watching the frost on the steel go soft where the steam touched it, and bead, and run. Down the tilt of the shield. To the low edge. And gather there, on the rim, a drop. Fat. Shining. Hanging.

And fall.

Into the cask. *Tik*.

"Oh," said Bee. Very small.

The drop sat at the bottom of the empty cask. One drop.

"That's one," said Winter.

"We need about four thousand," said Higu. "A day."

"That's fine," said Winter. "That's only four thousand." She drew the arrowhead over the shield again. The frost came back. "Do it again."

It didn't work, the first one. Not properly. Ina got the brine too hot, once, and it started to bubble, a small busy sound in the bottom of the iron pot, and the crust under the camp shivered and everybody went flat and nobody breathed and nothing came up, that time, but it was close. And the shield warmed too fast. Every few minutes, the frost was gone and the drops stopped and Winter had to do it again, and by the end of the afternoon her hands were shaking and white, and her lips were blue.

"It's coming out of you," said Kei, taking her wrist.

"I know," said Winter. "It's cold. It's always come out of me. That's what frost *is*." She took her wrist back. "Do it again."

By sunset they had a mug. One mug. In the bottom of the cask. Clear. Higu dipped a finger in it and tasted it and nodded and wrote on the rock.

Ina was so tired he couldn't feel his hand.

***

The second one worked.

Higu thought of it. She lay awake that night working it out on the rock in the dark by feel, and in the morning she came to Ina and Winter on the second sled and drew it in the salt with her finger.

Not one pot. Six. In a ring. Every pot of every kind on the sleds, Ice's iron one and his two copper ones and the rakers' brine pans from Seep, all of them. And in the middle, not a shield held over each, but all the shields together. Leaned in over the ring like the roof of a tent, the tops touching, the bottoms over a trough cut in the crust that ran round the outside of the pots and down to the casks.

"One fire," said Higu. "Under all of them. Low. In the middle." She looked at Ina. "Can you?"

"Yes," said Ina.

"One frost," said Higu. "On all of them. From the top, where they touch. It runs down." She looked at Winter. "Can you?"

Winter looked at the drawing for a long time.

"Not all night," she said. "Not on my own."

"No," said Higu. "Not on your own."

And she looked round, at the camp, the way she did when she was about to give everybody a job.

It took everybody.

That was what Ina remembered about the second one, afterwards, more than the water. That it took all of them. That nobody stood round in a ring and watched. Chris and Hako and Azac and Jared and Drew and Chi and Gabe dug, through the crust, slowly, a hand's depth, the long steady hush, all along the trough. Calvin and Tiger and Milli carried brine, soft-footed, a mug at a time, from the holes to the pots. The shields went up. Nearly every shield they had. Chris's kite and Azac's and Jared's thorn-shield with the rose inside the rim and Hako's and Drew's buckler and Chi's, leaned in at the top so they touched, a little steel tent in the middle of the white.

Not Hearthwall.

"It doesn't go cold," said Gabe, holding it. He looked at it. "Course it doesn't. Typical."

"It's a good shield," said Ina.

"It's a brilliant shield," said Gabe. "It's just no use for anything that wants it *cold*." He put it down on the sled. "I'll dig."

And the frost. Not just Winter. Winter at the top, where the shields met, with her arrowhead. But under her, holding the shields steady and taking turns, anybody whose hands could stand it, and the healers between them, Rachael and Kei and Xola, taking the cold out of people's fingers when it got too bad and putting it into themselves, the way it was on the Salt, and then Deston behind *them*, with his gold threads, so it didn't come out of anybody too much.

And under all of it, in the middle, on his knees on the salt with his mask down and his hand flat, Ina.

He kept it low.

That was the whole thing. That was what he'd been doing his whole life, he thought, kneeling there. Keeping it low. *Not unless somebody would die.* He'd kept it so low for so long, on so many floors, that he'd started to think low was all it was. A small fire, in his hand, that he didn't use. That made children run.

He fed it into the salt under six pots now, very slowly, very evenly, never enough to bubble. Just enough to steam. And held it there.

The steam went up.

It went up off six pots at once, white, into the steel tent over them, and touched Winter's frost, and the frost ran, and the drops came down the inside of six shields to six low edges and fell into the trough in the salt and ran round, and down, and into the cask.

*Tik. Tik. Tik.*

Not one at a time. Many. A little stream of them. Clear. Running.

Nobody said anything. You couldn't, on the Salt. But Ina looked up from his fire, through the eye-holes of the mask, and saw the whole camp standing round the steel tent in the dusk with their hands full of something, a mug, a shield-strap, a raker's saw, and every one of them looking at the trough.

At water. Running. On the Salt.

Higu wrote on the rock. Ina saw her do it. On the right-hand side. Under *3*.

*WATER: FIRE AND FROST. INA AND WINTER. AND EVERYBODY.*

Then she crossed out the *3*, and didn't write anything instead, and put the chalk in her pocket.

***

They walked by day and made water by night.

That was how it went, after. A routine, the way the rota had been a routine on floor 10, and the wax on floor 11. You got up at first light and loaded the sleds and walked west, softly, all day, into the white, with the sun coming up off it into your face. And at dusk you stopped, and dug the trough, and set the pots, and leaned the shields in, and Ina knelt in the middle, and Winter climbed onto a sled at the top and drew her arrowhead over the steel, and everybody took their turn, and by dawn there were four casks.

Not enough to be easy. Enough.

It came out of them. Of course it did. On the Salt, everything did. Ina got thin. He felt it. He'd never been big, but by the fourth night his wrists had gone to bone and his face in the inside of the mask, when he caught it reflected in a pot, was sharper than he remembered. Winter's hands were blue every morning. Kei made them both eat twice what anybody else ate and stood over them while they did it.

And Deston's threads ran through all of it, all night. Gold, in the dark. Thin. Warm. *What one of you has.* So that when Ina's fire went low because Ina was going low, he'd feel something come down the thread into him, a little, from somewhere, a cup of somebody, and the fire would steady.

He didn't know whose. You couldn't tell, down the threads. It was just everybody.

***

People came and sat with him.

He didn't notice it at first. You didn't notice things like that while they were happening. But somewhere around the third or fourth night, he looked up from his fire in the small hours, through the eye-holes of the mask, and saw that he wasn't alone in the middle of the camp.

It was the warmth. He understood that, after. On the Salt, at night, it was cold. Not floor-2 cold. A dry clean cold that came up off the white as soon as the sun went and got into your bones. And there was no fire on the Salt. Nothing to burn. No wood, no peat, no dung. Every night since the door, they'd lain in their bedrolls on the crust and shivered, and in the morning there'd been frost on the sleds.

Except here. In the middle. By the steel tent.

So people came.

Hana first. He remembered that. She came on the third night, in the middle watch, with her blanket round her shoulders and her ears flat against the cold, and sat down on the salt beside him without a word, close enough that he could feel her tail curl round against his back. And sat there. Watching the pots steam.

"Hello," said Ina. Very low.

"Budge up," said Hana.

"I'm on the salt. There's nothing to budge on."

"On the step," said Hana.

He looked at her.

She didn't look back. She looked at the fire under the pots, the white-blue glow coming up through the salt round his hand, and her face in it was very calm.

"I heard," said Hana. "About your step. Don't ask me who from. Things get round." Her mouth went up at one corner. "*If it's something people are keeping for a friend, you don't pick the lock. You wait on the step.*" She pulled the blanket tighter. "I'm on it. Since the edge of the Undertow. I've been on it for a month." She looked at him, sideways. "It's a nice step. There's room."

Ina didn't say anything for a long time.

"Four seconds," he said eventually.

"Four seconds," said Hana.

She didn't say anything else. She sat beside him until dawn with her tail round his back, and in the morning she got up and went to help load the sleds, and didn't mention it again.

The next night it was Maigo.

He came very slowly, with one hand on Niko's shoulder and the clay pot of earth in the other, and sat down on Ina's other side and put his root-foot in the pot, and his face, in the white-blue light, went soft. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Ina could see the bud on his antler, a little bigger now, pale and curled, catching the light from the fire.

But he looked across the camp, once, in the middle of the night, at the dark shape lying on the salt by Thea's sled. And then at Ina. And his face had something in it that hadn't been there before the dip in the salt. Something he'd found down there, Ina thought, and brought back up, and put away.

*You too*, thought Ina.

He didn't say it.

Then Niko. Who sat down on the end of a sled with his sword across his knees and his thumb on the line of tiny letters down the blade, and lit it, silently, just a little, just enough to see by, and read the red book by his own sword's light all night without making a sound. And Rachael, who didn't sit by anybody, ever, and didn't now, but stood at the edge of the warmth with her arms folded for an hour and then sat down, a careful polite ten centimetres away from everyone, with her hands in her lap. And Calvin, who couldn't sit still, and lay on his back on the salt with his head near Ina's knee and his eyes open, looking at the stars.

And Winter. At the top. On the sled, drawing her arrowhead over the steel. Looking down at all of them through the gap between two shields. Looking at him.

He looked back.

*It's your worst quality*, she'd said to him on floor 9, on the long bridge to the Youngest. *And your best.* She'd meant the waiting.

He'd thought, at the time, that she meant he'd be waiting on his own.

He looked round. At Hana with her tail. At Maigo with his foot in a pot. At Niko reading by his sword. At Rachael ten centimetres away from everyone. At Calvin's head by his knee. At all of them sitting on the salt in the cold, round the only fire on the floor, in the dark, waiting.

*Oh*, thought Ina.

It wasn't a step. It was a bench. It was a whole bench, and it was full, and he'd been sitting on the end of it since floor 9 thinking he was the only one there.

***

Gabe asked him on the fifth night.

He came and crouched by the fire in the small hours with Hearthwall on his back, the three flames on it glowing gold from inside, the only other warm thing on the Salt. He'd been out on the edge of the camp with Azac on the night watch. He crouched down beside Ina and held his hands out to the white-blue, the way you would to any fire, and didn't say anything for a while.

"He's out late," said Gabe eventually. Low.

Ina knew who. Ice was always out late now. Every night, as soon as the camp was set, he took his sword and his lamp shield and went out onto the white alone, and Thea went after him with her lantern shuttered and Xola beside her, a hundred metres back, the way it always was. Ice hunted the things under the crust. Alone. Softly. Times six. He came back before dawn, most nights, white with salt to the knees.

Tonight he hadn't come back yet.

"On floor 10," said Gabe, "I told him. I said, *I'll light a fire every night. If you're late. So you can see it.*" He looked at his hands in the firelight. "I've done it every night since. Every floor. On floor 11 it was a lamp on the stern. It's what I do." He was quiet. "There's nothing to burn here, mate. I've looked. There's nothing. I've been lying there every night watching the dark where he went, and I can't."

Ina looked at him.

"I can't keep it," said Gabe. "That's all. I said I would and I can't." He swallowed. "So I was going to ask."

"Yes," said Ina.

Gabe looked up.

"Every night," said Ina. "As long as we're on the Salt. I'll keep one up. On the side he goes out. Where he can see it." He looked at the pots, steaming in the ring under the shields. "This one's for the water. I'll do another. A small one. Just to see by."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long moment.

"It'll come out of you," he said.

"Everything does," said Ina. "Here."

Gabe looked at him. Then he reached out, the way Gabe did, and put his big burned hand on the back of Ina's neck, briefly, warm, and squeezed, and let go.

"Thanks, mate," said Gabe.

Ina put his other hand out flat on the salt. Away from the pots. Toward the west, toward the dark, where Ice had gone. And let a little fire go out of it. A little more. Not white-blue. He pushed the mask up for it, so it would be the other colour, his own, orange, the colour of an ordinary fire in an ordinary hearth on an ordinary night, because that was what Gabe meant. A small flame, sitting on the salt by itself, ten metres out, burning on nothing.

A light, in the dark. On the side he went out.

Gabe sat down beside him and watched it, and didn't go back to bed.

***

Calvin started shaking on the sixth night.

Ina didn't see it start. He heard it. Not the shaking; you couldn't hear anything on the Salt. He heard Calvin's breathing, which you could, just, because Calvin's head was by his knee again, the way it had been every night since the third, lying on his back on the salt looking at the stars. And the breathing went wrong. Fast. Short. In, in, in, and not out.

He looked down.

Calvin was shaking. All of him. Lying on his back on the salt with his eyes open and his arms clamped to his sides and his whole body going, like somebody very cold, except he wasn't cold, he was by the fire.

"Calvin," said Ina.

Calvin didn't hear. Or did, and couldn't answer.

Ina pushed his mask up. He kept his left hand on the salt for the water fire and his right for Gabe's, and he leaned over, so his face was close to Calvin's, close enough to be heard.

"Calvin."

"I can't hear anybody," said Calvin.

It came out in a rush. A whisper, because that was all there was on the Salt, but a whisper going so fast it fell over itself. "I can't hear anybody, Ina, I can't, there's nobody, I've been lying here every night and I can't hear *anybody*, it's not that it's quiet, it's that it's *nobody*, I can't hear anybody breathing or anybody snoring or anybody talking or *anything*, there's thirty-five people and it's like there's *none*, it's like I'm the only—" His breath went. "I've never been quiet. I've never been quiet in my *life*. I don't know how. I've been shouting since I was *born*, it's how I know where everybody is, if I shout and they shout back then they're *there*, and here I can't, I can't shout, and nobody can shout back, and I'm lying here and I can't tell if anybody's—" He stopped. He was crying. "I can't tell if anybody's *there*."

Ina looked at him.

Round the fire, people had woken. Hana, with her ears up. Rachael, her hands coming out of her lap. Deston, on his elbow, on the far side. Calvin was Deston's. Ina saw Deston start to get up.

He didn't wait for Deston.

He didn't know why. He didn't decide. He took his right hand off the salt, and Gabe's little orange fire went out on the white, and he took Calvin's hand.

The left one. The one that was nearer. He turned it over, palm up, in the firelight. And held it flat. And with the forefinger of his other hand, slowly, the way Tullah had shown them in Seep, the way the rakers did on the salt where they couldn't talk, he wrote on Calvin's palm.

Not a word. A name.

*Deston.*

Calvin went still. Not stopped shaking. Still, the way you go still when something touches you that you weren't expecting.

Ina wrote another.

*Rachael.*

And another.

*Dennis. Azac.*

He wrote them slowly. Every letter. On Calvin's palm with his fingertip, the way the rakers wrote on the things they held. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He wrote them all. Deston's party first. Then the rest. *Hana. Milli. Jared. Newish. Aleaka.* All of Hana's. All of Yan's. *Salty. Wendy. Tiger. Cursiffe. Yan.* Hyvred's. Xav's. His own. *Chris. Niko. Maigo. Winter.* One at a time, slowly, all the way down the list. Every one of them. And when he'd done one, he'd look up and find them in the dark, by the fire, and look at them, so Calvin would look, and Calvin would turn his head and see them. Lying there. Sitting there. Awake now, most of them, watching.

*Gabe.* By the fire.

Thea's party. *Drew. Higu. Chi.* By their sled. And then *Xola*, and *Thea*, and for those two he didn't look at the camp. He looked west. Past the shields. Out into the dark, toward the little orange fire burning on the salt, because that was where they were, a hundred metres behind somebody.

And then the last one that wasn't his. He wrote it slowly.

*Ice.*

He looked west again. For Calvin. So Calvin would look.

And Ice was there.

At the edge of the warmth. Just inside the dark, by Gabe's fire, white with salt to the knees, with his sword in one hand and his lamp shield in the other and his helm off. Back. With Thea and Xola behind him. Ina hadn't heard them come. Nobody had; nobody did, on the Salt. He must have been there a while.

Calvin looked. Ina felt his hand twitch.

Thirty-five names. He wrote his own last. *Ina.* On the heel of Calvin's hand. And then, after it, because it was true, a small mark. A line. The rakers' sign for *here*.

He closed Calvin's fingers over all of it. And held them shut.

"They're there," said Ina. "I've just told you who. All of them." He held Calvin's fist in both his hands. "You don't have to hear them. You've got them. In your hand. You can hold on to them."

Calvin lay on the salt with his fist shut.

He'd stopped shaking.

He lay there, breathing. In. And out. In. And out. And then he brought his fist up, slowly, and held it against his chest, the way you'd hold something small and alive that you'd caught. And shut his eyes.

"Okay," said Calvin. In a whisper. Normal. "Okay."

Across the fire, Deston had sat back down. He had his hand on his shirt pocket. He didn't say anything. He looked at Ina for a long time, and nodded, once.

And Ina looked up, past Deston, past the shields, into the dark at the western edge of the camp.

Ice was still there.

He hadn't moved. He was standing where he'd been, at the edge of the warmth, by the little orange fire. He must have seen it. All of it. From the first name.

His face.

Ina had seen Ice's face do a lot of things. On floor 9, at the foot of the north tree, crouched by a seedling in the ash. *I never saw one of these. Not once.* On every floor since, when the slips came and Ice heard himself and went white from the top down. He'd seen it, and noticed it, and put it away, and not picked at it.

He'd never seen it do this.

Ice was standing in the dark at the edge of the fire, looking at Calvin. At Calvin's fist against his chest. At Calvin, lying on his back on the salt, breathing, in and out, *stopped shaking*. And Ice's face was coming apart. Not slowly. All at once. Like something that had been holding for a very long time and couldn't any more. He stood there with his sword and his shield, and his mouth opened, and nothing came out, and he put the back of his sword hand against it, hard, as if to keep something in.

Thea was behind him. She came the last step. She didn't say anything. She put her hand flat on his back, the way she did, and stood there with him in the dark.

Ina looked at them.

At Ice, with his hand over his mouth, looking at Calvin as if Calvin had done something that Ice had watched happen once, a long time ago, somewhere else, and never thought he'd see again.

At Thea, looking at Ice, and then, very briefly, across the fire, at Ina. With an expression he couldn't read at all.

*There's something*, Winter had said. On the long bridge. *About Ice. It's not mine to tell you.*

He'd known that for more than a hundred days. He'd sat on the step and waited. He'd never once tried the lock.

He didn't try it now.

He looked at Ice in the dark with his hand over his mouth, and Ina understood only one thing, and it wasn't the secret. He didn't know what the secret was. He understood that whatever it was, whatever Ice carried, whatever had made a man carry a pot of earth for a hundred days and stand at the edge of a fire on the Salt looking at a frightened friend's closed fist like it was the most important thing he had ever seen, Ina had just done something that was part of it. Without knowing. By accident. The way you'd find water on the inside of a mask.

He didn't ask.

He put his right hand back on the salt, and let the little orange fire go out from it again, ten metres west, on the side Ice had gone out. Gabe's fire. Burning on nothing. In the dark.

Then he pulled the mask back down, and went back to the water.

***

Ice came and sat by him at dawn.

Ina had known he would. He didn't know how he'd known. He just had. He was sitting in the grey light in the middle of the camp with his mask pushed up and his hands flat on the salt, letting the fires go down at last, one at a time, the water fire under the pots and Gabe's fire out on the white, and everybody round him was asleep. Calvin, with his fist still shut. Hana, with her tail. Maigo. Niko, with his sword dark across his knees. Rachael, ten centimetres away from everybody. Gabe, sitting up against a sled, asleep with his mouth open and Hearthwall across his lap.

Winter, at the top of the steel tent, curled on the sled, asleep with her arrowhead still in her blue hand.

Ice sat down beside him. On the salt. Close enough to hear.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

The light came up. Grey, then white, at the edge of the world behind them, over the east, the way it always did. The casks on the trough were full. Four. Clear water. Higu would wake up and see them and write on the rock and not say anything about it.

"Thank you for the fire," said Ice eventually.

"Gabe asked," said Ina.

"I know," said Ice. "I saw it." He was quiet. "I could see it from a long way out. On the salt. A little orange one. On its own." He looked at the place on the white, ten metres west, where it had been. "I knew whose it was straight away."

"Whose?"

"Gabe's," said Ice. "It's always Gabe's." He paused. "And yours."

Ina didn't say anything.

They sat there.

"Calvin," said Ice. Very low. And stopped.

Ina waited.

He was good at waiting. It was his worst quality, Winter said, and his best. He sat with his hands on the salt and waited, the way Winter had once waited all night for him at the foot of a ladder on floor 5 in a sleet storm, the way he'd waited on a step since a long bridge on floor 9. And Ice sat beside him with his face turned to the east, and Ina could see him trying to say something. Could see it go up to his mouth and stop there. Twice.

"You always know what to say," said Ice.

Ina looked at him.

"I didn't say anything," said Ina. "I wrote it."

"Yeah," said Ice. And something happened to his face that was almost a laugh and wasn't. "Yeah. You did." He looked down at the salt between his boots. "I never knew the words. Any of them. I never once remembered what you said. Only that it was right. And that he stopped." He stopped himself. Hard. Ina heard it. Like a door shut on somebody's fingers. "Sorry. I'm tired. I don't know what I'm saying."

Ina sat very still.

*I never once remembered what you said.*

He'd never said anything to Calvin before tonight. Not like that. Not ever. He'd known Calvin three hundred and thirty-seven days, and he'd never once sat by him while he shook and said anything at all.

He sat with it.

And then he put it away. Carefully. The way Maigo had put something away, he thought, coming up out of the salt. The way Hana had, on a coil of rope, with her nose bleeding. *Four seconds. Okay.* He put it on the step beside him, where all the other things were, *never saw one, not once*, and *you always try it the first night*, and *I never once remembered*, and left it there. Next to Hana's. Next to Maigo's. On the bench.

He didn't pick at it.

"You're tired," said Ina. "Go to sleep."

Ice turned his head and looked at him.

For a long time. In the dawn. And Ina looked back, with his mask pushed up and his face, which people liked, which was a nice face, doing nothing at all. Just looking. Waiting. On the step.

"Ina," said Ice.

"Yes."

Ice didn't say what.

He didn't need to. Ina could see it. Whatever it was, it was right there, behind his teeth, and it was enormous, and it had been there for a very long time, and Ice was holding it with both hands so it didn't fall. And he wanted to put it down. Here. On the salt. Ina could see that too. He wanted to so badly that his hands were shaking on his knees.

And he didn't.

Not because of Ina. Ina understood that, as well. Something else. Something that was listening. Something Ice was always, always keeping it from, on every floor, even here, even on the Salt where nothing could hear anything at all.

"It's all right," said Ina.

Ice looked at him.

"Whatever it is," said Ina. "It's all right. You don't have to." He looked out at the white. "There's a step. There's a few of us on it now. Hana. Maigo, I think, since the salt. Calvin, probably, after tonight, though he doesn't know it yet." He paused. "We're not going anywhere. When you open the door, we'll be there. And if you never do, we'll still be there. That's what a step's for."

Ice didn't say anything.

He sat on the salt beside Ina in the dawn with his hands shaking on his knees, and then, slowly, they stopped. And he put his head down. On his arms. On his knees. And stayed like that, with his face hidden, and his shoulders going, without a sound, because there was no sound on the Salt, there was only the light coming up white over everything and the four full casks on the trough and thirty-four people asleep round the place where a fire had been.

Ina didn't touch him. Ina didn't do that.

He just sat beside him. On the step. Until the sun was up.

And when Thea woke, by her sled, and sat up, and looked across the camp, and saw them, she didn't come over. She stayed where she was. With her lantern by her head and her hands in her lap. And looked at Ina.

He looked back.

*Thank you*, she mouthed.

He nodded. Once.

Then he pulled his mask down over his face, so nobody would see what his face was doing, and sat there in the morning with Ice, and waited. He was good at it. He'd thought, since floor 9, that it was the loneliest thing in the world.

It wasn't. Not here. Not on this step.

It turned out it was the opposite.
