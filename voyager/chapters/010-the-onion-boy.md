By the end of the first day, Deston had learned three things about being a Cleric.

The first was that the staff was mostly for leaning on. The second was that his only spell, a soft white light he could put on someone's shoulder that made them feel slightly better about whatever had just hit them, was called *Bless*, and it cost about a fifth of everything he had. The third was that thirty-five people with twenty copper coins each, and no idea what anything cost, could not all sleep in one inn.

"Nineteen beds," said Dennis, coming back down the inn's front steps with the expression of a man who'd just argued with a landlord and lost on points. "Nineteen. In the whole place. And he wants three copper a bed, a *night*, and I talked him down to two, and that was *charming* him, Dest. You should've seen me. I was magnificent."

"I believe you."

"Two copper. Times nineteen. Times however long we're stuck in this—" Dennis waved his hand at the whole of Hearthstead, the red roofs, the white tower, the painted sky going gold at its western edge. "—*place*. That's money we don't have. And people need to eat. And I don't know what food costs. Nobody knows what food costs." He opened his inventory window and stared at it like a bank statement. "I have twenty coins and a bread roll I don't remember picking up."

"Climbers?"

They both looked round. It was the girl with the eggs. She'd stopped in the street with an empty basket now, looking up at the crowd of strangers on the inn steps with the bright, possessive interest of somebody who'd spotted them first and wanted it known.

"You want Mother Crane," she said. "Down the bottom of Crooked Street. The Spoon. She feeds Climbers." She considered. "She'll shout at you, though."

"Will she feed us if we can't pay?" asked Dennis, who believed in getting to the point.

The girl looked at him as if he'd asked whether water was wet.

"She's Mother Crane," she said, and ran off.

***

The Copper Spoon was a long, low room at the bottom of a crooked street, with a spoon painted on its sign and onions in the air. Thirty-five people did not fit in it. Thirty-five people went into it anyway, ducking under the low beam one after another, until every bench at the long table was full and people were sitting on the windowsills, on upturned crates, on the floor by the fire. Maigo sat on the hearth with his back to the warm stones like a cat.

At the far end, in an open kitchen, a short, wide old woman in a stained apron watched them come in with her arms folded.

She didn't look surprised. That was the first thing Deston noticed. Everybody else in Hearthstead had looked surprised in a pleased sort of way, the way you look at a circus. This woman watched thirty-five strangers in horns and robes and snakes pour into her cookhouse as if she'd been expecting them since breakfast and they were late.

"Thirty-five," she said, when the last of them had squeezed in. "Is it?"

"Thirty-five," said Deston. He'd ended up at the front, the way he always did, because somebody had to. "I'm sorry. We were told you—"

"Feed Climbers. I do. I know." She unfolded her arms and pointed a wooden spoon at him. "Mother Crane. Nobody starves on my floor, and nobody's starved in this family's kitchen since the Spire was young, and I'm not starting with you lot. It goes on the tab. You pay when you come back down." She turned round to the stove. "Bowls are on the shelf. Pass them down. *Don't* drop them."

They passed the bowls down. Nobody dropped them. It was very quiet for a minute, apart from the scrape of a ladle and thirty-five people being handed something hot.

Then Calvin put his feet up on the table.

He didn't think about it. Deston watched him not think about it: he leaned back on the bench, with a bowl of stew in his lap and the first warm food of a very long day inside him, and put both boots up on the corner of the long table, sighing like a man in his own living room.

"*Boots,*" said Mother Crane, without turning round from the stove, "*off my table.*"

Calvin's feet came down so fast he nearly went off the bench.

"Sorry!"

"Every one of you," said Mother Crane darkly to the stew. "Every Climber. Like my husband, God rest the pig."

Rachael laughed out loud. Deston hadn't heard her laugh like that in a long time. It was short and startled and came right up out of her, and she put her hand over her mouth afterwards as if it had escaped. Next to her, Dennis caught Deston's eye across the table with a look that said, very clearly, *I love this woman*.

***

It was halfway through the second bowl, when the room had warmed up and the noise had started to come back, that Mother Crane came down the table with a jug and stopped by Deston.

"You're in charge," she said.

"I'm— sort of. Not really. We've got seven of us, sort of in charge."

"You look in charge. You've got the face." She poured water into his cup without being asked. "Your onion boy came through this morning."

Deston looked up.

"Our— sorry?"

"Your onion boy. At dawn, before I'd got the stove hot. Stood in that door like he'd forgotten how doors worked." She nodded at it with the jug. "Then he came in and peeled half a sack of onions without being asked. Peeled them *right*, which nobody does. Then he asked me to teach him to cook, and I did, and he made a broth that won't poison anyone, and I gave him two loaves and sent him off." She sniffed. "He's on the tab. *The onion boy.* Wouldn't give me his name. You can tell him from me he owes me for two loaves and a pot."

The table had gone quiet around them. It went quiet the way a room does when people have heard something they want to hear the rest of.

Gabe was three places down. Deston didn't look at him. He didn't have to.

"What did he look like?" said Deston carefully.

"Young. Your age. Black hair in his eyes, with a bit of blue in it. Black waistcoat thing, all pockets, over a white shirt with the sleeves rolled up. Sword on his back. Knuckles all cut up, like he'd been punching a wall." She considered. "Polite. Sad round the eyes. The kind of sad that makes a joke so you won't notice it." She looked at Deston more sharply. "He said the rest of you were coming. *A lot of us*, he said. *About an hour.*" She poured the rest of the jug into Rachael's cup. "Didn't believe him. And then here you all came, up the high street, just about an hour after, near enough to the minute."

Deston sat very still.

*About an hour.* They'd been in the white room perhaps two hours. He'd left in the first minute. He'd come straight here, stopped to peel onions, and still known, almost to the minute, when they'd walk through the arch and down the hill.

"Where did he go?" said Gabe.

His voice was strange. Too level. Mother Crane looked round at him, and something in her walnut face shifted, as if she'd recognised something in his.

"Out the back," she said, more gently. "East. Toward the fields." She paused. "I asked him why he'd come on his own, before the rest of you. You know what he said?"

"What?"

"*Because somebody has to see what's up there before they go. So it's me.*"

Nobody said anything.

"I told him that was stupid," said Mother Crane. "Brave, but stupid. He said he'd been told." She tucked the jug under her arm. "You'll be wanting more bread," she said to nobody in particular, and went back up the table to the kitchen.

Gabe sat down. Deston hadn't realised he'd stood up. He sat down hard on the bench, and put both his hands flat on the long table on either side of his bowl, and looked at them.

"He punched the goat," Gabe said, quietly, to the table. "And then he came here and peeled onions."

"Gabe—"

"Who *does* that, Dest?"

Deston thought about it.

"Him," he said. "He does that."

***

The window came at the start of the third bowl.

It came to everyone at once. Deston would remember that afterwards: how there was no ripple, no one person seeing it first and then the next. Thirty-five windows opened in the same instant in thirty-five corners of thirty-five eyes, small and plain and polite, and the whole room stopped moving.

[ Floor 1 has been cleared. ]

Somebody dropped a spoon. It rang off the edge of a bowl and onto the floor, and nobody picked it up.

Then everybody talked at once.

"*Cleared?*"

"We haven't even *found* it—"

"What does that mean, cleared, cleared how—"

"Is that a joke? Is the window joking? Can it do that?"

"Floor one. That's *this* floor. Someone just—"

"Someone just beat the boss of floor one," said Kei, across the room, very calmly, in a voice that cut through all of it. The little green snake on his wrist had lifted its head. "On the first day. While we were choosing classes."

"That's not possible," said someone. "That's not— we're level two. I'm level *one*."

"Someone's speedrunning," said Jared, from Hana's end of the table, with something between disgust and admiration. "Someone's speedrunning the tutorial. Who speedruns the *tutorial*?"

"In the novels," said Calvin, who had gone very pink, "that's the main character. Bro. Dest. In every novel, the guy who clears the first floor on day one is the main character."

"Calvin."

"I'm just saying! It's a genre convention!"

At the window seat, Chris had opened something. He was poking at the edge of his window with one finger, the way the girl in blue had been doing all day, and a new panel had slid out from the side of it. A list.

"Rankings," he said. "There's a rankings tab. Hang on. Hang on."

Half the room crowded round him, or opened their own. Deston opened his. It took him a moment to find it, a small tab on the right-hand edge of the window he'd never touched, and when he pressed it, a long list unrolled down the air in front of him.

[ Rankings · Floor 1
1. — · Lv 6 · Knight
2. Rui Xiang · Lv 2 · Ranger
3. Hyvred · Lv 2 · Ranger
4. Salty · Lv 2 · Knight
]

And on down. Thirty-six lines.

The top line had no name. Just a short dash, where a name should be, and *Lv 6*, and *Knight*.

Everyone else in the room was level one or two.

"Thirty-six," said Calvin. He'd counted. Of course he'd counted. "There's thirty-six. In the list. Thirty-six lines." He turned to the light at his shoulder and asked it directly, in front of everybody, the way only Calvin could. "Who's number one?"

"I am not able to answer that," said his Support.

"There are thirty-five Players, right? You keep saying. So who's number *one*?"

"I am not able to answer that."

"That's the same sentence, bro. That's the same sentence you always say."

Gabe was typing.

Deston saw it out of the corner of his eye. There was a chat tab too, it turned out, next to rankings, and in the chat tab there was a channel called *World*, and Gabe had found it. He was typing in it with both index fingers, jabbing at letters that hung in the air, his jaw set.

The whole room saw it, because everyone was in World. A line appeared in thirty-five windows.

[ World · Gabe: DECLAN ]

Then another.

[ World · Gabe: DECLAN ITS YOU. I KNOW ITS YOU ]

[ World · Gabe: you punched a goat you absolute idiot ]

[ World · Gabe: ANSWER ME ]

Somebody laughed nervously. Somebody else said, "Who's Declan?" Somebody else said, "Ice. It's Ice." That was Hana, steady and clear, from her end of the table. And a girl's voice Deston didn't know said, "I thought that was a dream?" And then World filled up so fast nobody could read it.

[ World · Newish: lmao who
World · Calvin: GUYS THERES 36 IN THE RANKINGS
World · Hako: someone cleared floor 1 on day 1???
World · Tiger: is this an achievement can we get it too
World · Jared: Ice if thats you you owe me a postcard
World · Gabe: DECLAN
]

Gabe sent it again. And again. The same word, every few seconds, typed with two fingers.

Nobody answered.

***

Deston let it go on for a while. Then he went down the table and put his hand on Gabe's arm, the way he had in the white room, and said, "Okay. Okay. Hey."

Gabe kept typing.

"Gabe."

"He can see this. He's on the list. He's got a window like us. He can *see* this."

"I know."

"Then why isn't he—"

"Because he doesn't want to," Deston said quietly. "Or he can't. Or he's asleep. Or he's busy. He just killed a boss, Gabe. On his own. He might be lying on the floor somewhere bleeding."

Gabe's fingers stopped.

That had been the wrong thing to say, Deston realised, and also the right thing, because Gabe stopped typing. He sat back on the bench with both hands in his lap and looked at the rankings, at the dash at the top of them, with an expression Deston had last seen at the side of a hospital bed, years ago, when it wasn't anybody's fault.

"Level six," Gabe said. "We're two."

"I know."

"He's going to get himself killed, Dest."

"He might." Deston didn't lie to Gabe. He never had. It was one of the few rules he'd kept since they were thirteen. "Or he might know exactly what he's doing."

Gabe looked at him.

Deston didn't touch his shirt pocket. He'd been touching it all day, he realised; he'd caught himself at it a dozen times, his fingers going to the button as if to check the card was still there. He didn't do it now. But he thought about it. *Be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor.* Somebody who'd known about a third floor before any of them knew there were floors. Somebody who'd known, near enough to the minute, when they'd walk through an arch.

Somebody who'd just cleared the first floor of a hundred, alone, on the first day.

"He knows," Deston said, very low, so only Gabe could hear. "Whatever this place is, Gabe, he knows something about it. More than us. More than a guy who woke up a minute early should." He let his breath out. "So let's not get killed proving he's wrong."

***

Mother Crane had stopped at the end of the table with a fresh stack of bread in her arms. She'd been watching the room, all of it: the windows hanging in the air that she couldn't see, thirty-five people staring at nothing and shouting about it. She looked at Deston.

"Something's happened," she said.

"Someone cleared the floor," said Deston. "The— I don't know what to call it. The boss. At the top of the valley."

Mother Crane's eyebrows went up and stayed there.

"The Fold?" she said. "The ram?"

"I think so."

"On his own?"

"We think so."

"*Today?*"

"Yeah."

She stood there with the bread for a long moment. Then, slowly, she put it down on the table.

"Gran said some of them were like that," she said. "The old Climbers. In her stories. She said most of them came down the hill all together, frightened as rabbits, the way you lot did. But once in a long while, she said, one came down on his own, ahead of the rest. Like he already knew the way." She shook her head. "I always thought she made that bit up."

"What happened to them?" said Rachael quietly. "The ones who came down on their own?"

Mother Crane looked at her.

"Gran didn't say," she said. "She only ever told the bit where they came down." She picked up her empty jug. "Eat your bread."

***

Later, much later, when the Copper Spoon had emptied out and thirty-five tired people had gone off in twos and threes to nineteen beds and a great many floors, Deston stood outside on the step in the dark.

The stars were on. All at once, the way they'd come on at dusk, as if somebody had flicked a switch. He'd been the one standing in the street when it happened, and he'd watched every single one of them light up in the same instant, and he hadn't said anything to anybody, because he didn't want to be the one who said it out loud.

Gabe came out and stood beside him. He didn't say anything either. He'd stopped typing hours ago. He just stood there with his hands in his pockets and looked east, past the end of Crooked Street, past the last red roofs, to where the valley went up into the dark.

"Xav says he's tanking," said Gabe after a while.

"I heard."

"Going ahead to stand in front of whatever's coming. Like in the old raids. So we don't have to."

"Sounds like him."

"It's stupid."

"Brave, but stupid," said Deston.

Gabe made a sound that was almost a laugh.

They stood there. Up at the top of the valley, somewhere in the dark, Deston thought, there was a ram that had been killed today. And a man who'd killed it was lying down somewhere up there, maybe bleeding, maybe asleep. He'd peeled onions for an old woman at dawn, and told her it had to be him. *So it's me.*

"Gabe," he said.

"Yeah."

"We're going to train."

Gabe looked at him.

"Properly. All of us. We're going to get good at this. Not fast. Good. Whatever he knows, he told me to be careful. So we're going to be careful." Deston kept his eyes on the dark at the top of the valley. "And then we're going to catch up. And when we catch up, you can punch him."

Gabe was quiet for a long moment.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Yeah," said Gabe. "Okay. Careful." He took his hands out of his pockets, and looked at the rankings again, at the dash at the top. Then he closed it. "And then I'm punching him."

"Deal."

They went in.
