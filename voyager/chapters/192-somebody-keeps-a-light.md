Maigo felt the winter before he saw it.

He felt it on the stair. Somewhere around the eightieth step down from the Fen, in the dark, with Newish counting at the front and Thea singing and the warm wet air of three going thin behind them, he felt the cold come up the stair to meet him and get into his feet. Not the cold of the Grey, which hadn't been cold at all, really, just nothing. Real cold. Snow-cold. The kind that came up out of the ground through your soles and went looking for your roots.

He didn't have roots. Not really. Not in the way people meant. But he had something, since the white room, that went down when he stood still and drank from whatever was underneath, and gave it away. And it knew winter. It knew winter the way a tree knows it, in the wood, without being told. On the hundred-and-sixtieth step it went quiet in him, the way it always did on two, and he felt his leaves start to think about letting go.

*Not yet*, he told them.

They didn't listen. They never did. He'd stopped expecting them to on floor 1.

***

The stair came out in the ascension room in the far cliff above the frozen Mere.

It was the same room. Plain stone, cold, with the stair arch at the back and the door at the front onto a ledge above the ice, and a stone bench along one wall. Gabe stopped in front of the bench. He was wearing the coat. He'd worn it every day since he found it folded on that bench on the eighteenth day, grey wolf fur to the knee, smelling of smoke and old tallow; he wore it on the Glass, which nobody understood, and he'd worn it in the Fen two days ago and come out of the reeds steaming like a horse.

He stood in front of the bench and looked at it. Then he sat down on it. Right where the coat had been.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Everybody knew what he'd said the first time, coming down off the Mere soaking wet with the coat over his arm. *He left it for me.* Xav had said, *Of course you got the coat.* It looked like it had always been his. It still did.

Ice stood in the arch and looked at Gabe sitting on the bench in the coat.

Gabe looked up at him.

"Fits," said Gabe.

"I know," said Ice.

That was all. Gabe got up. And they went out onto the ledge above the Mere, into the cold, all thirty-six of them, and the cold went into Maigo like a hand into a glove, and three of his leaves let go at once and went spinning down past the cliff toward the ice.

He watched them go. He didn't say anything. He put his sunglasses on. On two, the snow was so white it hurt.

***

The Mere was frozen. Round and white in its bowl of grey cliffs, with the dark patches still in it where the springs came up under the ice, the ones that had drowned the Old Stag four times the first time and once the second. Nothing stood on it now. The Stag had gone to light, the way things did on the low floors, and nothing had come back. The ice was just ice. Clean and white and empty, with a few of his leaves lying on it far below like dropped coins.

Higu did the rock on the ledge, with her back against the cliff.

"Ice. What do you remember?"

"Four tries," said Ice. He was looking at the Mere. "It took four tries. The first time."

Higu wrote it. *FOUR TRIES.*

"And a chair," said Ice.

He didn't say anything else. Higu didn't ask. She wrote *A CHAIR*, small, and drew the line under it, and looked up for the middle.

Maigo said it. He hadn't meant to. It came out of him the way leaves come off a tree, because it was time.

"It's cold."

"That's true," said Higu.

*COLD.* She didn't underline it. She looked at him over the rock, at his sunglasses and the leaves in his hair that were already going brown at the edges, and something in her face went careful. Higu knew about trees. She knew about everything she'd ever drawn.

"How cold?" she said.

"Cold enough," said Maigo.

***

Larchwick was half a day south, down out of the hanging valley through the pines. It was the same too, mostly. Steep roofs heavy with snow, smoke from every chimney, the trappers' hall at the top of the street with the door-sized slate inside it that Drew had organised four parties on, on the twelfth day, and the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool and something roasting. People came to their doors to watch them come down the street. Somebody shouted. A child threw a snowball at Tiger, who threw one back and was immediately buried.

Ma Yarrow's cottage was at the bottom of the town, by the frozen river, with herbs hanging in bunches under the eaves and a green door. Kei went straight to it. He didn't say anything. He just walked down the street with his book under his arm and Dose round his neck and his face set, and Maigo went with him, because Kei had asked him to on the stair, without saying why, and Maigo had understood without being told.

And Ice went with them, because it was for her.

Ma Yarrow opened the door before Kei knocked. She was round and red-faced and wearing, Maigo counted, four cardigans. She looked at Kei. Her face went through surprise and pleasure and a sort of fierce tutting, all at once.

"*You*," she said. "The one who writes everything down twice."

"Ma Yarrow," said Kei.

"Look at you. You've *grown*." She looked past him. At Maigo, in his sunglasses, with his leaves going brown. At Ice. Her eyes stopped on Ice a moment, the way people's eyes did, these days, on every floor; as if they'd heard something about him and were checking it against what they could see. Then she stepped back. "Well, come in. Shut the door. Shut it *properly*, there's a draught off that river would take the skin off a—" She glared at the door. "There. In."

It was hot inside. Ma Yarrow's stove took up half the room, and the other half was shelves, floor to ceiling, jars and bottles and bundles and boxes, every one of them labelled in a round untidy hand. It smelled of a hundred things drying. Maigo stood just inside the door with his back against it and felt the heat get into him, a little, and his leaves stopped thinking about letting go for a moment.

Kei had his book open already. Not at the front. At the inside back cover, where the pencil had gone soft from five hundred days of carrying. The remedy, twice. The second copy squarer than the first, every letter separate. And under it, in black ink, *her?*, and under that, *yes*.

"Winter fever," said Kei. "The bad one. In old people. The one you told me. Willow bark, river root, honey, and the dark blue flower. Three days, and somebody with her day and night." He looked up. "I've got the bark. I've got honey. I've got about a day's worth of your river root, from the last time, and it's five hundred days old." He took a breath. "I haven't got the flower."

Ma Yarrow looked at him for a long moment.

"Who's it for?"

"An old woman on floor 1. She keeps an inn." Kei didn't look at Ice. "She's going to be ill. This winter. In the second half."

Ma Yarrow's eyes went to Ice again. Then back.

"*Going* to be."

"Yes."

She didn't ask how he knew. Maigo watched her not ask. People on two had always been like that; they asked about the weather and the price of hare and whether you'd eaten, and never about anything that mattered, because the things that mattered on two were mostly snow. She just turned round and went to the shelf by the stove, the one at head height, and reached up, and took down a small brown jar with a cork in it, and held it out to Kei.

He took it. He took the cork out.

It was empty.

"Midsummer," said Ma Yarrow. "The miller's girl. Took the last of it, and she lived, so I don't regret it." She folded her arms over the cardigans. "And it doesn't come up till summer. Winterhood. North side of the valley, under the old pines, in the shade, where the snow lies longest. It comes up when the snow goes, and flowers, and goes back down, and sleeps under the snow all winter like a bear." She looked at Kei. "You'll not find it now. Not a leaf. It's down there under three foot of snow, asleep. You'd have to wait for the thaw."

"When's the thaw?" said Ice.

He hadn't spoken till then. Ma Yarrow looked at him.

"Spring," she said. "Three months. Four."

Nobody said anything. Maigo watched Ice's face. It didn't stop, this time. It didn't do anything at all. He just stood there in the hot little room among the jars, very still, the way you stand when something heavy has been put down in front of you and you're working out how to pick it up.

*Second half of the winter*, Maigo thought. Ice had been counting since the Glass. Seventy-six days, two days ago. The thaw was a hundred, maybe more.

Kei was looking at the empty jar.

"Asleep," said Maigo.

They all looked at him.

He hadn't said anything since the ledge. He didn't, much, on two. Talking took something out of him, in winter, that he'd rather keep. But he'd heard it. *It's down there under the snow, asleep.* Not dead. Not gone. Asleep. Like a bear. Like a root. Like a tree in winter, with everything it was pulled down into the dark and folded up small and waiting.

He knew asleep.

"It's asleep," said Maigo again. "Not gone." He took his sunglasses off. Ma Yarrow looked at his eyes, which nobody on two had seen before, and went quiet. "Show me where."

***

She showed him in the morning.

North side of the valley, an hour's walk up out of Larchwick through the deep snow, to a long slope under the old pines. Great black-green trees, straight as pillars, so old and so close together that the snow under them lay in drifts and hollows and the light came down green and dim even at noon. It was the coldest place Maigo had ever stood in. Colder than the Grey, because the Grey had been nothing and this was something: deep, patient, real cold, the kind that had been lying here under these trees every winter for as long as there had been winters.

"Here," said Ma Yarrow. She'd come up in five cardigans and a shawl, breathing hard, with her face red as a coal. She pointed at a stretch of the slope under three of the oldest pines, where the snow lay smooth and deep and blue in the shade. "All along under there. It comes up in a carpet, in summer. Dark blue. Like the sky right before it's night." She looked at Maigo. "And don't you touch it. Not with your hands. It gets in through the skin. A leaf of it on your bare palm and you'll not feel your arm for a day."

"I know," said Kei. Dose, round his neck, had her head up, tasting the cold air. Kei listened to her for a moment. "She says it smells like the end of something."

"It *is* the end of something," said Ma Yarrow. "In the wrong amount."

Maigo walked out onto the snow under the three old pines.

He went in up to his knees, then his thighs. He didn't mind. He stopped in the middle of the blue stretch, where Ma Yarrow had pointed, and stood there under the old trees with the cold coming up into him from everywhere, and took his sunglasses off and put them in his pocket. And shut his eyes. And put his feet down.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

Not the putting down. He'd done that a thousand times. In the Old Wood on nine, in the green under Highcrook, on the Salt where nothing grew and he'd listened for three days to nothing until the nothing answered. He'd put his feet down at the straw man's feet in the Long Barn with the Grey pushing at the footings, and held a gold ring round the whole of the barn, and lost his leaves doing it, one a night, until Gigi said he looked like November.

This was different. This was winter.

In winter he slept. That was what he was, now. That was what the white room had made him, or found in him, or whatever it was it had done. A tree, in some part of him he didn't have a word for. And a tree in winter didn't listen, or drink, or give. It pulled everything it had down into the dark, into the roots, and folded it up small and waited. That was the whole point. That was what kept it alive. You didn't wake a tree in winter. You didn't wake anything in winter. The cold would get in where you'd opened.

On floor 3, the first time, in the trial, he'd chosen the easy one. On purpose. Everybody had looked at him. He'd said *a tree should know when to rest*. He'd believed it. He still did.

He put his feet down anyway, and went looking.

***

It took him the whole of the first day to find it.

Down. Through the snow, which was just cold, and the frozen crust of needles under the snow, which was just colder, and into the earth under that, where the old pines' roots went down like rivers, slow and black and enormous and asleep. He went past them carefully. You didn't wake those. They were older than anything on the floor, and if they woke they'd want to know why, and he didn't have a good enough answer for a tree that old.

And under them, between them, small and folded and very deep, he found it.

Thousands of them. A whole slope of little sleeping roots, each one curled up tight round a bud the size of a seed, with everything it was pulled down into it and wrapped up and shut. Waiting. They had no idea he was there. They had no idea of anything. They were asleep the way only something that has done this a thousand winters can be asleep: completely, trustingly, all the way down.

He stayed there with them until it got dark.

When he opened his eyes, Ina was crouched on the snow beside him with his mask pushed up and his palms on the snow, and the snow round Maigo's legs had gone soft and wet in a circle a metre wide, and there was steam coming off it.

"You were going blue," said Ina.

"I'm a tree."

"You were going blue at the edges." Ina didn't move his hands. "Like a tree that's going to lose a branch."

Maigo looked down. Half his leaves were gone. They lay round him on the wet snow, brown and curled, like somebody had swept up a corner of autumn and tipped it out here.

"Found it," said Maigo.

"I know. Your face did a thing." Ina looked at him. "Are you going to wake it?"

"Yes."

"Can you?"

Maigo thought about it. He was very tired. It was the kind of tired that wasn't in your arms or your legs but further in, in the place where the putting-down came from. The kind a tree must feel, he thought, in about the second week of October, when it knows what's coming.

"Don't know," he said.

Ina nodded, as if that was a fair answer. He kept his hands on the snow.

***

He stayed under the pines for ten days.

He didn't decide to. He just didn't come out. In the evenings they'd bring him food, and he'd eat it standing up, rooted, with his feet down in the dark among the sleeping buds, and then shut his eyes again. In the nights somebody sat with him. Always. He didn't ask them to. He'd have told them not to, if he'd had the words to spare. But he didn't, and they came anyway, in turns, through the long blue cold under the old trees, one after another, the way the whole of the Acre had sat with the straw man. *You don't leave it on its own in the dark.*

Ina, mostly, with his palms on the snow, keeping the ground round Maigo's legs soft so the cold couldn't get in where he'd opened. Gigi, with a skin of water she'd warmed inside her coat, watering him every hour and talking. About the green on the Salt, and her watch, which still said nine minutes past two, and what he was going to grow on floor 1 when this was all over. Kei, with his book, measuring something on a scrap of paper and crossing it out. Winter, who sat in the snow beside him with her bow across her knees and said she was only there because it was the one place on two cold enough to be comfortable, and stayed all night, and didn't say anything else. Niko, reading out loud to him from a book of floor 2 stories he'd bought in Larchwick the first time, quietly, in the dark, because Maigo had always liked to be read to and had never once said so. Chris, writing it all down.

And Ice. Every night, at some point. He'd come up out of the dark between the pines with a pot from Ma Yarrow's stove, and pour something hot into a cup and put it in Maigo's hand, and sit down in the snow.

He didn't say anything, most nights. He didn't need to. He just sat.

On the fifth night, Maigo had lost all his leaves but eleven. He knew it was eleven. He'd been counting them going.

That was the night he nearly stopped.

It wasn't a decision. It was the winter. It came up into him through the open place, the way Ma Yarrow had said it would, the way Ina's hands were trying to stop it, and it was so quiet, and so heavy, and so patient. It didn't fight him. It just waited for him to stop fighting it. *Rest*, it said. Not in words. In the wood. *You're a tree. It's winter. A tree should know when to rest.* And all the sleeping roots under him, thousands of them, curled up tight round their buds in the dark, were saying the same thing, very softly, all together, the way a whole slope of grass bends the same way in the wind. *Rest. Come down. Fold up. It's winter. Wake in the spring.*

He wanted to. He'd never wanted anything so much. He stood in the snow under the old pines with his eyes shut and felt himself start to go, slowly, down, the way the sun went down on its rail on three, all at once and then not at all.

"Maigotron," said Ice.

Maigo opened his eyes.

Ice was sitting in the snow in front of him. Close. Closer than he usually sat. He had his hand round Maigo's wrist, where it hung at his side, and his fingers were very warm. Maigo hadn't felt him take hold of it.

Nobody else said that. Nobody in the world. Not Gigi, who'd known him longest up here. Not Yan, who'd heard him say it to a cold fire on floor 2 and never told anybody. Only one person had ever called him that, on a hundred calls, for years, in the stupid voice he did when he was trying to make Maigo laugh and Maigo was refusing to. He hadn't heard it in that voice since the white room. He'd heard it said, once, on floor 2, the first time, by himself, alone, to a fire that had gone out.

"You're going," said Ice. "I can feel it. In the thread. You're going down."

"Winter," said Maigo. It was hard to talk. "Trees."

"I know." Ice didn't let go of his wrist. "You can stop. If you need to. You can stop right now and fold up and sleep till spring, and I'll carry you down to one on my back, and I'll sit with you every night till you wake up. I mean that." He was quiet. "I'm not asking you to do this."

"I know you're not."

"I'm never going to ask you to do this."

"I know." Maigo looked at him. At his face, in the dark, in the blue light off the snow. It was the face from the ledge above the Mere. The very still one. The one that had something heavy in front of it and was working out how to pick it up. "You'd find another way."

"I'd try."

"You'd find one. You always do." Maigo shut his eyes again. "You'd go out on the snow on your own. Or something. Some stupid thing. And Thea'd kill you."

Something happened in Ice's face. Maigo didn't see it. He felt it, through the hand on his wrist.

"Probably," said Ice.

Maigo stood there with his eyes shut.

*A tree should know when to rest.* He'd believed it. He still did. That was the thing. It wasn't wrong. It was just not the only thing a tree should know.

He thought about the onion skins. Left in a little heap by the cold fire under the great pine on floor 2, the first week, peeled the way only one person peeled them, where everybody would find them. He thought about the ash from Highcrook, from the edge, where it wasn't near the roots, carried in a pocket for two hundred days and given to him on the Salt so he'd have something to start with where nothing grew. He thought about an old woman on floor 1 with an inn, who'd fed thirty-five people on credit the first week without asking anybody's name, and given Maigo, specifically, because she'd noticed he wasn't eating, a bowl of something green with no meat in it, and stood over him till he finished it, and never said a word about it after.

He thought about a man who'd had ten years of her and lost her in the second winter, and was sitting in the snow at the bottom of the world holding his wrist.

He didn't know that. Not properly. He'd never been told. But he'd been on the step since the Salt, and the step had a very good view.

"Not yet," said Maigo.

He put his feet down again.

***

It came up on the tenth night.

He felt it first. Down in the dark, among the sleeping roots, one of them turned over. That was all. Like somebody in a bed, half waking, turning over, settling again. He held his breath. It was very hard to hold your breath when you were a tree.

Then another one turned over.

Then a third. Then a dozen, in a patch right under his feet, where he'd been pouring everything he had down into the dark for ten days. Not all of them. He'd never wake all of them; he'd never have tried. A dozen. A dozen little roots, curled round their buds, that had been asleep since the autumn and should have stayed asleep till spring, turning over in the cold dark and opening, very slowly, like a fist unclenching. And reaching up.

He opened his eyes.

Everybody was there.

He hadn't known. They'd come up through the pines in the night, all of them, the whole thirty-five, with Thea's lantern and Kei's book and Ma Yarrow in six cardigans and a blanket, and they were standing round the edge of the blue slope under the old trees in a ring in the snow, not saying anything, watching. Ina was still crouched beside him with his palms on the snow, and Gigi on the other side with her water skin, and Ice sitting where he'd sat for ten nights, in front of him, holding his wrist.

And in the snow between Ice and Maigo, a green point came up.

Thin. Sharp. Dark green, nearly black, the colour of the old pines. It came up through the soft wet snow Ina had kept open, slowly, the way the Youngest had come up out of the ash in Highcrook, the way Gigi's forget-me-nots were coming up, somewhere, in the bowl on six. It straightened. It opened into leaves, dark and deeply cut, like hands with too many fingers. A stalk came up out of the leaves. And at the top of the stalk, while they watched, in the blue cold of the second winter under three old pines on floor 2, the buds swelled, and split, and opened.

Dark blue. Hooded. Like little monks with their heads bowed. Like the sky right before it's night.

Then another. And another. A dozen of them, in a patch the size of a blanket, flowering in the snow.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Winterhood," said Ma Yarrow, at last. Very quietly. "In winter." She had both hands over her mouth. "Sixty years I've been picking that slope. I've never—" She stopped. "In *winter*."

Maigo looked at the flowers. He felt his last leaves go. All eleven. He felt them let go of him one after another and drift down onto the snow round his feet, brown and light and done, and he didn't mind at all.

"Don't touch them," said Kei. He was on his knees at the edge of the patch already, with his book open and a cloth and a little knife. "Not with your hands. Nobody. It gets in through the—"

Maigo bent down and picked one.

With his hand. His bare hand. He reached down into the patch and broke one dark blue stalk off near the root between his finger and thumb, and held it up, and looked at it.

"*Maigo*—"

"It's not skin," said Maigo.

He held his hand out to Kei, palm up, with the flower lying across it. His palm was bark. It had been bark since the white room. Rough and grey-brown and grained, like the inside of an old tree's elbow. The dark blue hood of the flower lay on it, and nothing happened at all.

Kei stared at it. Then at him.

Dose, round Kei's neck, put her head out over Kei's collar and looked at the flower on Maigo's bark palm for a long time, tasting the air. Then she said something. Kei listened.

"She says," said Kei slowly, "*that's the right amount.*"

Maigo laughed.

He didn't laugh much. Everybody knew that. He'd laughed on floor 5, on Gull Island, when Niko's blade lit for the first time, and everybody had turned round to look at him. He laughed now, in the snow, with no leaves left and the flower on his palm, and it came out of him rusty and surprised, and he swayed, and Ice got up off the snow and caught him.

"Okay," said Ice. He had Maigo by both arms. "Okay. You can stop now."

"Yeah," said Maigo.

"You can rest now. It's done. You did it."

"Yeah."

"Maigotron. Rest."

"Okay," said Maigo, and rested, standing up, with Ice holding him and the whole of the thirty-six standing round in the snow under the old pines in the lantern light, and the dark blue flowers open at his feet.

***

They dried it at Ma Yarrow's stove. Twelve flowers. Kei did it himself, with gloves and a cloth over his mouth and Dose telling him when, and he wouldn't let anybody else in the kitchen. When it was done he put it in her jar, the small brown one with the cork, and wrote on the label in his square separate letters, *FOR MOTHER CRANE*, and then, under it, smaller, *Maigo grew it*.

Ma Yarrow made him put half back in her own jar. "For the miller's girl," she said. "Next time. And whoever else." She looked at Maigo, asleep on a chair by the stove under four blankets with his feet in a bucket of warm water, which Gigi had said was the closest thing to soil she could think of. "Somebody'll need it."

The river root was easy, after that. The river ran frozen past the bottom of Ma Yarrow's garden, and the root grew along its bank under the ice, and Ina knelt on the bank with his mask up and warmed a long strip of it until the ice went soft and dark and the earth under it steamed, and Gabe and Salty dug, and Kei took it fresh, wet, out of the black mud, in long pale fingers. Ma Yarrow showed him how to dry it. He wrote that down twice as well.

So on the morning of the twelfth day on floor 2, Kei had everything.

Willow bark. River root, fresh. Honey from the Lammas hives. And a small brown jar of dried dark blue flowers, measured to the grain, with *Maigo grew it* on the label.

He sat on the edge of Ma Yarrow's table with the book open on his knees, at the inside back cover, and looked at the remedy for a long time. Then he took his pencil and, very carefully, beside each line, he made a small tick. Four ticks.

Ice watched him do it from the door. He didn't come in. He'd been doing that a lot, lately, Maigo had noticed, from his chair, half asleep: standing in doors. Not coming in. Letting the room be somebody else's.

"Kei," said Ice.

Kei looked up.

"Thank you."

Kei looked at him for a moment. Then he looked down at the book again, at the four ticks, and at *her?* and *yes* underneath.

"Thank Maigo," said Kei. "He grew it."

"I know who grew it," said Ice.

And he looked across the room at Maigo, under four blankets, with his feet in a bucket. Maigo looked back at him. Neither of them said anything. Maigo didn't need them to. He'd been on the step since the Salt. The step had a very good view.

***

They crossed the pass on the thirteenth day.

They'd seen the beacon every night from Larchwick. Every night of the ten under the pines, from the north slope, through the trees: a small orange light high up on the far side of the valley, on the pass, burning. Larchwick said it had burned every night since the eighth day the first time, when somebody had carried the old woman up her own tower in the Long Storm and lit it with his bare hand. Every night. Five hundred and some. The trappers took wood up to her now, once a week, on a sledge, because somebody had to, and because she'd sent word down the pass the first winter that if anybody let that fire go out before the climbers came back down she'd come down the pass herself and want to know why.

Nobody had let it go out.

Maigo walked it. He'd woken up on the morning of the thirteenth day with his feet still in the bucket and one leaf. One. Small and pale green, right at the top of his head, very new and very determined, like it had come up overnight out of sheer cheek. Gigi had nearly cried. He'd walked up the pass road with the others in the bright cold, slowly, with the one leaf, and Salty walking next to him in case.

The beacon tower stood at the top of the pass, round and grey, fifteen metres of old stone with the iron cradle on top. There was smoke going up out of the cradle into the blue, thin in the daylight. And beside the tower, the little stone hut. With a candle in its window, lit, at noon, for no reason anybody could see except that somebody had decided a long time ago that you kept a light.

The door was open.

She was sitting in her chair just inside it. In the doorway, in the cold, wrapped up in shawls, with her feet on a little stool and a blanket over her knees. Bent like a hook. Ninety-something; Maigo had lost count. Tiny and fierce, with bright black eyes like a bird's. She was looking down the pass road at them coming up it, thirty-six of them in a long line in the snow, and she didn't look the least bit surprised.

Ice stopped in the road.

Maigo, three people back, saw him stop. He saw his face. He'd said *a chair* on the ledge above the Mere. Just that. *And a chair.* And Higu had written it down small and drawn a line under it and not asked.

The old woman was sitting in her chair in her doorway, alive, with her black eyes snapping, looking at him.

"Well," said Mother Tallow. "*There* you are."

Ice didn't say anything. He couldn't. Maigo could see he couldn't.

"Took your time," said Mother Tallow. She looked past him, down the line. At all of them. "And you brought the lot. Good. I've been keeping that fire going for the best part of two years, I'd want more than one of you for it." She looked back at him. "Well? Come here. My eyes aren't what they were. Come where I can see you."

Ice went.

He walked up the last of the pass road to the door of the hut and stopped in front of her chair. And then he knelt down. In the snow, in the doorway, so his face was level with hers. Mother Tallow leaned forward in her shawls and looked at him for a long time with her bright black eyes, close, the way you'd look at somebody's face to see if they were the same person.

"You're thinner," she said.

"Yes."

"And older. Round the eyes."

"Yes."

"Hm." She looked at him a while longer. "You stood in my doorway, the first time. Like you'd forgotten how doors worked. And you split my wood with a sword, which is wrong, and carried me up my own tower, which is worse." She sniffed. "And you sat in that door all night with the dogs coming, and I sang, and in the morning you told me your name was Voyager."

"I know."

"That's not a name. I told you. That's a job."

"I know."

"So." Mother Tallow folded her hands on the blanket. "I've kept a light for you for the best part of two years. I'd like a name to go with it. A proper one."

Ice was quiet for a moment. Maigo saw him start to say *Ice*. He saw the shape of it.

"Ice," said Ice.

"That's not a name either," said Mother Tallow. "That's weather."

Somebody behind Maigo made a noise. It was Newish. Newish had her hand over her mouth and her shoulders were going.

Ice looked at the old woman in the chair. Something happened in his face. It wasn't the stopping, or the shutter coming open. It was smaller than that. It was the face of a man who'd said his name once, out loud, on his knees, on floor 4, to something that was listening, and had it be the last thing he set down. Maigo didn't know that. He'd never been told. But he'd been on the step a long time.

"Declan," said Ice. "My name's Declan."

Mother Tallow looked at him.

"Declan," she said. She tried it, the way you'd try a new boot. "There. Was that so hard?"

"Yes," said Ice.

She laughed. A dry cackle, like sticks in a fire. She reached out of her shawls and put one tiny hooked hand on his head, on his hair, the way you'd put your hand on a dog's head, or a child's.

"Everyone comes back down eventually," said Mother Tallow. "I told your friends that. The first ones. The lot with the loud girl." She didn't take her hand away. "I told them I'd keep it lit for you to see. When you came back down." She patted his head. "And here you are. Down."

"Here I am," said Ice. Into the snow.

***

She gave him a candle.

Of course she did. She had a box of them by the door, tallow, hand-dipped, a hundred at least. She'd been making them all winter for the window, she said, and she had more than she'd ever need now, and she'd heard from the trappers that he was going down to sit with an old woman on floor 1 who kept an inn.

"For her window," said Mother Tallow. She put it in his hand. "So she knows somebody's keeping one. When she's poorly. It helps, knowing." She closed his fingers over it. "It helped me."

Ice put it in his vest pocket. Maigo saw him do it. In the left pocket, where he kept her other one, the best one, that she'd given him the first time, the night of the Long Storm, *for your way up, in case nobody's keeping one for you*, and the bronze bell from seven, and the blank card from the white room. He'd never lit the first candle. Not since the Fen. He kept it. He put the second one beside it.

"I've still got the first one," he said.

"I should hope so," said Mother Tallow.

And Newish came up the road and stood in front of the chair with her hands on her hips, and Mother Tallow looked up at her, and they looked at each other for a long moment like two cats on a wall.

"*You*," said Mother Tallow.

"*You*," said Newish.

"Still got a mouth on you?"

"Bigger than ever."

"Good," said Mother Tallow. "Sit down. Tell me everything. Leave out the boring bits." She looked at Ice, still kneeling in the snow. "And you can go and split some wood. *Properly*. With an axe."

He went and split some wood. With an axe. Properly. Maigo sat on the wall of the beacon tower in the cold sun with his one leaf and watched him do it, and didn't think he'd ever seen anybody look so happy to be told off.

***

They went down the far side of the pass that afternoon, to the great pine.

It was where it had always been. Four hundred metres east of the stair down to floor 1, at the bottom of the long slope below the pass, alone in a clearing: a great black pine, bigger than the old ones on the north side, with snow on its branches like a coat and a ring of bare ground round its trunk where the snow didn't lie. Ice had camped under it the first night on two, alone, before any of them came up.

And the first week on two, Maigo had come here with Chris's party and found the cold fire ring, and the little heap of onion skins beside it, peeled the way only one person peeled them. And he'd put his hand on the pine's bark, and the pine had remembered.

He put his hand on it again now.

It was asleep. Of course it was. It was winter. But it was old enough, this tree, to remember things in its sleep, the way old people do. He stood with his palm on the bark and his one leaf in his hair and let himself go down into it, just a little, just the surface, not enough to wake it.

It remembered a very warm someone. Sitting against it all night, alone, with something small and warm beside him, a long time ago. Before the others came.

And it remembered thirty-six.

All at once. Not the first time; this tree didn't know there'd been a first time. This time. Tonight. Thirty-six warm someones, round its trunk in the cold, settling down in the bare ring of ground with their packs under their heads, talking. One of them a little warmer than the others. Sitting against the bark, right where the first one had sat, with somebody beside him holding a light.

Maigo took his hand away.

"What does it say?" said Gigi. She'd come up beside him. She always asked. She was the only one who did.

"It says it's full," said Maigo.

Gigi looked at the tree. Then at the thirty-six, settling round its trunk in the dusk, with Ice sitting against the bark where he'd sat the first night, the very first, with nobody, and Thea beside him with the lantern, and Gabe on his other side in the coat.

"Good," said Gigi. And checked her watch, out of habit. Still nine minutes past two. She put it away. "Good," she said again.

***

In the morning Ice made onion broth on the fire under the great pine.

He dug the onions himself from the south-facing bank where they'd always been, under the snow, wild and small and bitter. He made it the way he'd made it on the first night on two, alone, for nobody. And when he peeled the onions, he left the skins in a little heap by the fire. Maigo saw him do it, and saw him see himself doing it, and stop, and look at the heap for a long moment.

Then he left it.

"For whoever comes up?" said Tiger, who'd been watching too.

Ice looked at the heap. "For Gabe," he said. "Last time it was for Gabe."

Gabe, by the fire in the coat, didn't look up. "I know it was," he said.

They went down the stair to floor 1 at noon, on the fourteenth day on two. Tiger opened the door with his hand flat on it. Newish counted. Thea sang. Calvin went first, and Rachael's fist went into his collar on the first step.

At the top of the stair Ice stopped, and took Thea's hand, and looked back at the great pine and the little heap of onion skins by the cold fire, and the long white slope going up to the pass, where a thin line of smoke went up from a beacon that somebody was keeping.

"Sixty-three," he said.

Nobody heard it but Thea. And Maigo, who had one leaf, and good ears, and a seat on the step with a very good view.
