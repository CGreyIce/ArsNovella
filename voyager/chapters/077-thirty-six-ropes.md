Calvin had been a scout for a hundred and fifty-two days, and the first thing a scout learned was that the moment you heard something, you went.

He heard Dennis before Dennis got to the inn. Everybody did. *DREW*, up the street, across the square, past the fountain, in a voice that cracked in the middle like a boy's. Calvin was on his feet before the second *DREW*. He was at the inn door before the third. And Dennis came through it at a run with Rachael right behind him and nearly knocked him flat.

"He's *here*," said Dennis.

The whole long room of the inn went silent. Thirty-three people at the tables, at breakfast, with their spoons halfway to their mouths.

"Lantern Street," said Dennis. He was breathing like he'd run a mile. Which, for Dennis, he had. "The bakery. The blue door. There's a baker who remembers—she remembers every day, the same as us, and she said—" He stopped. He looked at Rachael. Rachael's eyes were red. Calvin had never seen Rachael's eyes red. "He's *there*, Drew. Behind the kitchen door. He laughed. I heard him laugh. I'd know that laugh anywhere, I heard it in—"

Gabe stood up.

Calvin saw him do it. At the end of the far table, in the wolf coat, with the two shields leaning against the bench beside him. He stood up so fast the bench went over behind him with a crash, and he was moving. Round the table. Toward the door. Toward Dennis. Toward the street.

"*Gabe*," said Drew.

Gabe didn't stop.

"Gabe. *Wait.*"

Gabe stopped. In the doorway of the inn, with one hand on the frame, the way Calvin had seen him stand at the bottom of a stair in a furnace hall on floor 4. His back was to all of them. His shoulders were heaving.

"There's more," said Rachael. Quietly. "Gabe. Listen. There's more."

He didn't turn round. But he didn't go.

"The baker stood in front of the door," said Rachael. "When Den tried to go through. She stood in front of it and folded her arms." Her voice was very steady and very low, and Calvin could hear what it was costing her. "She said he said *all of you*. Not one at a time. She said when we're all here, all of us, in one place. Not a floor away, not a street away. *All of us.*" She swallowed. "She said he'd waited a hundred and fifty days and he could wait one more, if it meant doing it right."

Gabe stood in the doorway.

Nobody said anything. The whole inn was holding its breath. Outside, in the square, the market was going on as if nothing had happened, the way it always did. Somebody shouting about cheese. A dog barking.

"*One more*," said Gabe.

His voice was very rough.

"One more day," said Rachael. "That's what she said."

Calvin watched Gabe's hand on the doorframe. It had gone white at the knuckles. He watched Gabe stand there, in the doorway of an inn in a town where every day was the same day, a few hundred metres from a blue door. He'd been chasing a name for a hundred and fifty-two days. He'd typed *DECLAN* into World until his fingers hurt. He'd sat on a lip of a pit in a desert for two and a half days. He'd sat on a stair in a furnace hall until it closed.

And he was being asked to wait one more.

Gabe let go of the doorframe.

He didn't turn round. He just stood there, with his hands at his sides, and Calvin saw his whole back move. One long breath, in, and out. And his shoulders came down. Not slumped. *Down*. Loose. The way Gabe stood now, since the Kitecliff. Swaying, very slightly, the way you sway when you're not bracing against anything.

"Okay," said Gabe.

He turned round. His face was wet. He didn't wipe it.

"Okay," he said again. "Tomorrow. All of us."

"Dawn," said Drew. He'd stood up too. "We go at dawn. All thirty-five. Together." He looked round the room. "Nobody goes to Lantern Street before then. Nobody. Not to look. Not to walk past. Not to stand outside the window." He looked at Gabe. "He asked for one more day. We give it to him."

Gabe nodded, slowly.

Then he went and picked his bench back up, and sat down on it, next to the two shields, and put his face in his hands.

Calvin went and sat next to him. He didn't say anything. He just sat there, shoulder to shoulder with Gabe, and after a minute Deston came and sat on Gabe's other side. And after another minute, Xav came and stood behind them, with his hands on his knees. Nobody said anything for a long time.

***

Calvin wanted to go anyway.

That was the truth, and he told Rachael so, an hour later, out in the inn yard by the well, because Rachael was the person he told the truth to.

"I'm not going," he said. "I'm not. I swear. I'm just saying I want to."

"I know," said Rachael.

"I'm the *scout*. That's the whole—" He threw his hands up. "That's my whole thing now. *The scout who brings everybody home.* I said it on floor 3. It's the skill. Go first, everybody follows, nobody gets left." He looked at her. "And he's *right there*. He's a street away. He's the one who's been left, Rae. For a hundred and fifty days. And I'm the scout, and I'm sitting in an inn yard."

"You're sitting in an inn yard," Rachael agreed.

"It's driving me *mad*."

"I know." She looked at him with her frown. Then, which she never did, she reached out and put her hand on his left arm. Just above the elbow. The arm the Woolback Ram had broken on floor 1, in two places, which she'd held together with cold water running along the bone. She did that sometimes now. Since floor 4. Since the first square, and the grey thing lying by the fountain, and her hand reaching out for his without her knowing why. She'd never explained it. He'd never asked.

"You bring everybody home," said Rachael. "That's the skill. Everybody." She looked at him. "That means all of us. Together. At once. Not you running ahead on your own and then the rest of us catching up." Her hand tightened on his arm. "He's been doing that. Running ahead on his own. For a hundred and fifty days. Look where it got him."

Calvin didn't say anything.

"Tomorrow," said Rachael, "you go first. Down Lantern Street. Everybody behind you. And you don't run." She almost smiled. "And then we all bring him home."

Calvin looked at her for a long time.

"Okay," he said. Quietly. For Calvin.

***

It was Hyvred who said they should look at the tower. In the afternoon, when everybody was going quietly mad in the inn.

"His light's all over this town," she said. She was standing at the inn window, looking out at the square, with her eyes moving. "Everywhere. Twenty-six days of it. The fountain, the church steps, the smith's stall." Her voice wavered. "There's a hayloft at the edge of town that's got one night in it. And Lantern Street, so bright I can't look at it." She turned round. "But the brightest bit's the tower."

Everybody looked at her.

"He goes up it," said Hyvred. "Every night. And comes down every morning. Twenty-six nights." She pointed out of the window at the clocktower in the middle of the square, with its four faces and its open belfry. "He sleeps up there."

"*Why*?" said Calvin.

"I don't know."

"Then we go and look," said Drew. "Not Lantern Street. The tower. He said all of us, at the bakery. He didn't say anything about the tower."

So six of them went. Calvin, because he was the scout and he'd go mad if he didn't. Hyvred, to see. John, with his lights. Chris, with his notebook. Thea, with her lantern. And Drew, because nobody went anywhere without the general any more.

The door at the bottom of the clocktower wasn't locked. An old man in a dusty coat was sitting on a stool just inside, eating an apple. He looked up at the six of them in his doorway. Polite. Puzzled. Blank.

"Help you?"

"Can we go up?" said Calvin.

"Don't see why not. Nobody ever does." He took another bite of his apple. "Mind the clock room. Noisy."

They went up. A narrow stone stair, round and round. Past a big room halfway up full of iron wheels and wooden teeth and a ticking so loud and slow you could feel it in your chest. The inside of a great clock face was a huge round window of thick old glass, with the shadows of the hands lying across the floor. Then up again.

Into the ringing chamber.

Calvin stopped in the doorway, and everybody stopped behind him.

***

It was a round stone room, not very big, with a slit of a window high up and a ceiling of heavy black beams. And from the ceiling, through holes in the beams, ropes. Hanging down into the room in a circle. Each one with a soft woollen grip at the bottom, worn smooth by hands, hanging at about the height of your chest.

Calvin counted them. He didn't decide to. He'd been counting things since floor 1, since a hundred and twelve steps.

Thirty-six.

And in the middle of the circle of ropes, on the stone floor, there was a bedroll.

Just that. A plain blanket, rolled out, with a rolled-up cloak at one end for a pillow. White-grey cloak, light as paper. The floor round it was swept. There was flour on the blanket. A dusting of it, white, where somebody had lain down at night with flour still on their clothes.

Nobody said anything.

Hyvred made a small sound. Calvin looked at her. She had her hand over her mouth and her eyes were going round the room, round and round, reading.

"Every night," she said, through her fingers. "Every night. Lying here. In the middle." She took her hand away. "His light's in all of them. All the ropes. He's—he's had his hands on every one. Over and over. Trying." Her voice cracked. "He's been up here every night for twenty-six days, trying to ring thirty-six bells on his own."

"*Why*?" said Calvin again. It came out of him louder than he meant. "Why would you—why would anybody—"

"Up there," said John quietly.

He'd sent a light up. One of his motes, a little floating point of white, drifting up through the dim air of the chamber toward the heavy black beam that ran across the top of the room, above the ropes. And it stopped there, hovering, lighting the wood.

There were words on the beam. Carved deep. Old.

Chris read them out. Slowly. In his bug-report voice.

"*When every bell is rung at once, the Hour may be struck.*"

Nobody moved.

"Every bell," said Chris. He was looking at the ropes. "At once." He turned in a slow circle, counting them again with his eyes. "Thirty-six bells. Thirty-six ropes."

"It's the boss," said Drew slowly. "Isn't it. The floor guardian. Whatever *the Hour* is." He looked up at the beam. "You can't hit it unless every bell's going at once."

"And he's one person," said Calvin.

His voice had gone strange.

"He's one person," he said again. "He's been coming up here every night for twenty-six days. With thirty-six ropes. And two hands." He looked at the bedroll in the middle of the circle. At the flour on it. "He can't do it. Can he. He *can't*. Not on his own. Not ever. It doesn't matter how good he is. It doesn't matter what he knows. He can't ring thirty-six bells with two hands."

Nobody said anything.

"He's stuck," said Calvin. "That's why he's still here. That's why he's not on eight. It's not that he waited for us." He stopped. "It's that he *can't go on without us*."

***

They went up to the belfry. Calvin went first. He always went first.

It was open on all four sides. Great arches, with the afternoon sky outside them and the whole valley spread out below. The red roofs, the river, the hill road they'd come down in the dark two nights ago. And under the roof, from the black beams, the bells.

Calvin stood in the middle of them and turned round slowly.

Big ones and small ones. Bronze and iron and one that looked like silver. Old, green, cracked. New, shining. Crowded together in rows round the one great bell in the middle, the one that rang the hours, huge and dark. Each with a rope going down through the floor to the room below.

He counted them anyway. He had to.

"Thirty-six," said Calvin.

He heard Chris's pencil stop.

"Thirty-six bells," said Calvin. He looked at all of them, one after another. "Thirty-six ropes. A floor you can't clear unless thirty-six people ring every bell at once." He turned round and looked at the others. At Drew, and Chris, and John, and Hyvred with her hand still over her mouth. At Thea, standing in the arch with the lantern, very still, looking at the bells. "Why thirty-six?"

Nobody answered.

"There's thirty-five of us," said Calvin. "The lights said. On the first morning. *There are thirty-five players.* Every single one of them said it, at Gabe, when he asked." He looked up at the bells. "So why would a tower make a floor with thirty-six?"

"Because there are thirty-six," said Thea.

Calvin looked at her.

She hadn't moved from the arch. She was holding the lantern in both hands, in front of her, the way she always held it. The afternoon light was behind her. Her face was very calm. The calm she had when she was reading something nobody else could read.

"The tower thinks there are thirty-six of us," said Thea. "Thirty-six players. It made thirty-six bells." She looked at the great bell in the middle. "The tower's never lied to us. Not once. Not on any floor. The bosses come back, the dead stay, the weather doesn't warn you. It's hard and it's cruel and it's never once lied." She was quiet. "Our systems have."

Calvin stared at her.

"Our *systems*," he said. "The lights?"

"On the first morning," said Thea. "In the white room. Gabe asked about the thirty-sixth circle. And thirty-five lights said *there are thirty-five players*. All at once. Calm. Certain." She looked at him. "And there weren't. There were thirty-six. Gabe saw him. Yan saw him. Hana saw him. He was standing right there."

"So they *lied*," said Calvin. "The lights. They lied to us. On the first morning."

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

Thea didn't answer for a long time.

"I don't know," she said at last. Very quietly. "Maybe because he asked them to. Maybe because they wanted to help him." She looked down at the lantern. "Maybe they've been helping him the whole time, and we just never noticed. The way we never noticed the tower counting."

Calvin stood in the middle of thirty-six bells in the afternoon wind and didn't know what to say. He looked at Chris, and Chris was looking at Thea with a very odd expression. He looked at Hyvred, and Hyvred was looking at the floor. He looked at Drew, and Drew had his general's face on, the one that was thinking three moves ahead and not saying any of them.

There was something in the room he didn't know about. He could feel it. The way you feel a draught from a door you can't see.

He didn't ask. He was the one who asked everything, directly, always. He'd asked every question that occurred to him on every floor since the white room. And he stood in the belfry and looked at Thea's face, and for the first time in a hundred and fifty-two days, he didn't ask.

He thought about a bedroll in the middle of thirty-six ropes, with flour on it.

"Then tomorrow," he said instead. "We go and get him. And then we come up here. All of us." He looked round at the bells. "Thirty-six hands. Thirty-six ropes." He swallowed. "And we ring every single one."

"At once," said John.

"At once."

***

They went back down at dusk. On the way past the ringing chamber, Thea stopped.

Calvin saw her do it. Everybody else had gone on down the stair. She was the last, with her lantern. She stood in the doorway of the ringing chamber and looked at the bedroll in the middle of the circle of ropes. The flour on the blanket. The rolled-up cloak for a pillow.

Then she went in.

Calvin waited on the stair. He didn't know why. He watched through the doorway as she walked into the middle of the circle of ropes and knelt down beside the bedroll. She put the lantern down on the stone floor beside it, and the amber light lay across the blanket. And she reached out with her right hand, slowly, and touched the cloak. Where his head would go.

Just touched it. For a moment.

Then she picked up the lantern and stood up and came out, and saw Calvin on the stair. She didn't say anything. Neither did he. They went down together, round and round, past the clock room with its slow loud ticking, to the bottom, where the old man on his stool had finished his apple and was looking at the door as if he'd forgotten why he was there.

***

That night, at midnight, Calvin was awake.

He sat at the window of the inn with his chin on his arms and watched the clocktower in the middle of the square. Its four faces lit from inside like moons. Its belfry dark. He could see the shapes of the bells up there against the stars, if he looked hard. Thirty-six.

Somewhere across the square, down a narrow street, behind a blue door, there was a man who'd been the only one awake in this town at midnight for twenty-six nights.

Calvin thought about that. About being the only one. About lying in the middle of thirty-six ropes every night with two hands and flour on your blanket.

The great bell rang. *One.*

He didn't look away from it. *Two. Three.* He counted. *Ten. Eleven.*

*Twelve.*

The world fell over. The stars went out. The sky went grey, then gold. The birds started.

Morning. The same morning, for the third time.

Calvin sat at the window with his chin on his arms and watched the light come up over Evermorrow, and didn't say *what*. He just watched it.

"Tomorrow," he said to the window. Very quietly. To nobody. Except it wasn't tomorrow here. It was never tomorrow here. It was always this one, again.

Behind him, in the inn, people were waking up. He heard Drew's voice, low. Rachael's. The scrape of boots. Thirty-five people getting up in the dark-turned-dawn, without anybody having to tell them.

"Then we'll make one," said Calvin.

He got up off the windowsill and went to find his boots.
