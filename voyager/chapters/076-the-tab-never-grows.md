Dennis had been in the middle of a sentence. That was what annoyed him most, afterwards. He'd been halfway through a really good point.

They were walking down the hill road in the dark. All thirty-five, in a line, the way they always did. The grass on either side was so wet and so green-smelling after twenty-four days of glass that half of them had already cried about it at the door. The town was down there in the valley, a scatter of yellow windows by a river, and a tower in the middle with a clock face lit from inside like a moon. Somewhere a bell had just rung the half hour.

And Dennis had been explaining to Rachael, at length, that the first thing they needed in a new town was a decent exchange rate. On every floor so far they'd been fleeced the first day, by people who could smell new money. If they could just *establish a baseline* before anybody else opened their mouths—

"Dennis," said Rachael.

"I'm just saying, if Calvin goes into the first shop he sees and asks how much something is, in that voice, they'll double it, they'll see him coming from—"

The bell rang. *One.*

"—the other end of the valley, and then we're stuck with whatever—"

*Two. Three.*

"Midnight," said Chris, somewhere behind them. "Huh. Thought it was earlier."

*Four. Five. Six.*

"—baseline, that's all I'm saying, we just need a *baseline*—"

*Ten. Eleven.*

*Twelve.*

And the world fell over.

***

That was how Dennis would describe it, afterwards, to anybody who'd listen, and to several people who wouldn't. *The world fell over.* Like missing the last step on a stair in the dark. Like a lift dropping a floor. Everything lurched. Not him. Not the road. *Everything else*.

The dark went white.

Not slowly. Not like dawn. One moment there were stars over the valley and yellow windows in the town and the moon-faced clock. The next, between one breath and the next, the stars went out like somebody had blown them out. The sky went grey. Then pale. Then gold, all along the eastern hills. And birds started singing. Everywhere, all at once, all down the hillside, in every hedge. The way birds do at the very edge of morning.

It was morning.

Thirty-five people stood stock-still on a hill road in the dawn, with their mouths open.

Then Calvin, at the front, with Rachael's fist in his collar, said the only thing anybody could.

"*WHAT*—"

It went down the whole valley. It probably woke the town. It definitely set off every dog.

***

Nobody panicked. That was the strange part. Dennis thought about it later. On floor 1 they'd have panicked. On floor 2, probably. But they'd done a hundred and fifty days now. They'd seen a sky that was a painted ceiling and a city where the dead didn't go anywhere. They'd seen a bird the size of a barn and people with feathers and a hole in the air that showed you what you wanted. Midnight turning into morning was, on reflection, not the worst thing.

Drew stood in the middle of the road with his hands on his hips and looked up at the gold sky for a long time.

"Right," he said eventually. "Okay. Midnight's dawn here. Noted." He looked round at all of them. "Everybody all right? Anybody missing? Anybody suddenly older?"

"I feel older," said Newish.

"You always feel older."

"*Chris*," said Drew.

Chris was already writing. "Twelve strokes," he was saying, to his notebook, very fast. "Twelve strokes and then morning. No transition. No night. It's like—it's like somebody skipped the night. Like a time skip in a—"

"In a game," said Hako, helpfully.

"I was going to say *in a bad save file*."

Dennis stood in the road and looked down at the town in the morning light. Smoke going up from chimneys, straight. A market setting up in the square. Somebody on the bridge with a cart.

"Well," he said. "At least the shops'll be open."

Rachael let go of Calvin's collar long enough to hit him on the arm.

***

He loved the market.

He couldn't help it. He'd loved markets since he was a kid. Pasar malams at home, the night markets, with the generator hum and the smell of satay and the stalls in rows under the bare bulbs. You walked through, and everything had a price, and every price was a conversation. He'd loved Hearthstead's market on floor 1. Larchwick's. The ghost market on the plateau in the Red City, with the woman who wrote her prices on a slate. Tether's fish market, which had nearly made him weep.

Evermorrow's market was the best one yet.

It filled the whole square, round the fountain and the clocktower. Stalls with striped awnings. Cheese. Onions. Bread. Pots and pans. Ribbons. A man selling nothing but knives. A woman selling nothing but honey. Children everywhere. A dog, brown and white with one ear up, sliding out from under a table with a sausage and running off, while the butcher shouted a word after it that Dennis filed away for later use.

He established a baseline. He went round the whole square, slowly, with Rachael beside him rolling her eyes. He asked the price of everything and bought nothing, and memorised all of it, and *then* he went back to the cheese stall.

The cheesemonger was a small sharp woman with a white cap, and she was good. Dennis could tell in thirty seconds. She asked eight coppers for a wheel of something hard and yellow that smelled like feet in the best possible way. He offered five. She laughed in his face. He got her down to six and a half, and a little pot of chutney thrown in, and walked away feeling like a god.

"You enjoy that too much," said Rachael.

"I enjoy it the correct amount."

"You were *sweating*."

"Because she was *good*." He held up the cheese. "Six and a half. And chutney. She'll tell her grandchildren about me."

Rachael's mouth did something at the corner that she would have denied was a smile.

It rained at three, briefly. They stood under the church porch on the corner of the square and watched it, all thirty-five of them, and Higu cried a bit, because it was real rain, and so did Xola, because Higu was crying. And at dusk the clocktower rang six. They found an inn on the far side of the square that would take thirty-five people and didn't ask questions. They ate. Dennis negotiated the room rate.

At midnight, half of them were awake to see it happen again.

Dennis wasn't. He'd gone to sleep at eleven, full of cheese, with Calvin snoring in the next bed. He woke up because the light had changed through the shutters, between one breath and the next. And because birds were singing. And because, from the next bed, Calvin had sat bolt upright and said, much more quietly this time, "*Oh, come on*."

***

It was the cheese that did it.

Dennis went back to the market the next morning, because of course he did. He'd seen a honey stall he wanted to work on. He walked into the square in the morning light with Rachael and a purse and a plan.

The dog came out from under the table with the sausage.

He watched it go. Brown and white, one ear up. The butcher shouted the same word after it. The *exact* same word. In the exact same voice.

Dennis stopped walking.

"Rae," he said.

"What?"

"That dog."

"What about it?"

"It did that yesterday." He was staring after it. "Same dog. Same sausage. Same table. Same word." He turned in a slow circle. The striped awnings. The man with the cart on the bridge. The woman with the honey. "Same *everything*."

Rachael looked round the square. Her frown came down slowly.

Dennis walked to the cheese stall.

The small sharp woman with the white cap was setting out her wheels. She looked up at him with a bright professional smile. No recognition in it at all. None.

"Morning," she said. "Something hard and yellow? I've a lovely one here. Eight coppers."

Dennis looked at the wheel. It was the same wheel. He'd swear it was the same wheel. The same rind. The same little nick in the side where somebody had dropped it once.

"I bought that yesterday," he said. "For six and a half. With chutney."

The cheesemonger's smile didn't change. Her eyes went polite. The way people's eyes go when you've said something odd and they've decided to be kind about it.

"Not from me, love," she said. "I'd remember a face like yours. Eight coppers."

Dennis stood there.

"Rae," he said again. His voice had gone a bit high. "Rae, she doesn't *remember*. She doesn't remember me. I spent twenty minutes on her. I was *magnificent*."

"Dennis—"

"It's the same day." He grabbed Rachael's arm. "Rae. It's the *same day*. Midnight goes straight to morning and it's the same *day*. The dog, the cheese, the honey, all of it. The whole town's—" He stopped. A thought had arrived. A terrible, wonderful, profoundly Dennis-shaped thought.

"What," said Rachael flatly. She'd seen his face.

"If it's the same day," said Dennis slowly. "Every day. And nobody remembers." He looked at the cheese. "Then if I buy something on credit. Today. And say I'll pay tomorrow."

"*Dennis*."

"Then tomorrow never comes." His eyes had gone very wide. "The tab never *grows*, Rae. It never even *starts*. Every morning it's back to zero. You could buy the whole market on tick and every morning the slate's clean." He stared out at the square. "This is either the best place in the world or the worst one."

Rachael looked at him for a long moment.

"It's the worst one," she said.

"I'm just *noting*—"

"It's the worst one," said Rachael again. Quietly, this time. She was looking at the cheesemonger, who'd gone back to setting out her wheels, humming. "She doesn't remember you. She doesn't remember *anything*. Not you. Not yesterday. Not whatever she did the day before that." Her frown had gone deep. "How long's it been like this? For her?"

Dennis didn't know. He looked at the cheesemonger, humming, setting out a wheel of cheese she'd set out yesterday, and the day before, and how many days before that.

He stopped thinking about the tab.

***

It was Rachael who wanted bread. Dennis would point that out later, too, to anybody who'd listen.

She didn't say why. She just said *bread* in the voice she used when she didn't want to talk about something. Dennis knew that voice. He'd known it since he was seventeen. So they asked the honey woman where the best bread in town was. The honey woman said *Lantern Street, the blue door, Merle's*, without having to think. So they went.

Out of the square on the east side. Past the well. Down a narrow street of tall leaning houses whose upper floors nearly touched across the top, so the street underneath was all cool shadow. Halfway down, a shop with a blue door and a bow window full of bread.

And a smell coming out of the open door that stopped Dennis dead in the street.

"Rosemary," said Rachael.

"Rosemary," said Dennis. He couldn't move for a second. "Rae. That smells like—"

"I know what it smells like."

He didn't know what it smelled like. Not really. Bread, and rosemary, and an oven. But it smelled like something he'd smelled before, a long time ago, somewhere warm. He couldn't place it. It made the back of his throat tight.

They went in. The bell over the door went.

There was a woman behind the counter. About their age, maybe a bit older, with flour up to her elbows and dark hair tied up in a cloth and a lot of freckles. She was pulling a tray of rolls out of the oven. She turned round with the tray in both hands when the bell went.

And looked at them.

Not the way the cheesemonger had. Not polite and blank and kind. She looked at Dennis, and then at Rachael, for a long moment. And her face did something. It wasn't surprise. It was something more like recognition. As if she'd been expecting them, and had been told what they'd look like, and was checking.

She put the tray down.

"You're new," she said.

"We're new," said Dennis.

"You were new yesterday, too," said the woman. "I saw you. All of you. Thirty-five, going down Lantern Street at seven in the morning like a parade, staring at everything." She wiped her hands on her apron. Her eyes were very steady. "Nobody's new twice. Not here. Not in a hundred and fifty-one days."

Dennis felt Rachael go still beside him.

"You remember," said Rachael. "Yesterday. You remember."

"I remember every day," said the woman. "Every one. A hundred and fifty-one of the same one." Her mouth twisted. "Everybody else wakes up and it's the first time. Every time. I wake up and it's the hundred-and-fifty-first."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The bread smelled of rosemary.

"And you remember too," said the woman. "Don't you. You lot. You came down the hill at half past eleven and it went morning on you. And you noticed. You *know* it's the same day." She looked at them, from one to the other. "I can see it on your faces. That's the face. I've seen it once before."

"Once," said Dennis.

"Once." The woman folded her floury arms. "Merle. That's me. And there's a knight who believes me."

***

Dennis would remember the next part for the rest of his life. He'd go over it a lot, afterwards, at night, the way you go over the moment just before something big happens, trying to see if you could have guessed.

He didn't guess. He just stood there in the bakery on Lantern Street with his mouth open.

"A *knight*," he said.

"A knight." Merle didn't look away. "Came down the hill twenty-six days ago. On his own. Walked in here and bought a rosemary loaf and went and ate the whole thing on the edge of the fountain." She was quiet for a second. "And then he walked in again the next day. And I said *you came in yesterday*. And he looked at me like—" She stopped. "And I asked him if he believed me. And he said yes."

Rachael had gone completely white.

"White cloak?" she said. Her voice was very small for Rachael. "Black hair? A—a bit of teal?"

Merle looked at her for a long time.

"He said you'd find him," she said. "All of you. He said one of you can see where he's been. And another one carries a light. And another one goes first and everybody follows." She was watching their faces. "He said thirty-five friends were coming up the hill."

Dennis couldn't say anything at all.

"He said you'd haggle," said Merle, to Dennis. And her mouth did something at the corner, quick, gone. "So that's double, for you. For the rosemary."

"*Why*—"

"He said that too."

From somewhere behind her, through the door at the back of the shop that led into the kitchen, there was a sound.

A laugh.

Short. Sudden. Quickly stopped, as if somebody had put a hand over their mouth. Somebody who'd been standing very close to the other side of that door, listening, and hadn't been able to help it.

Dennis stopped breathing.

He knew that laugh.

He'd known it since he was seventeen. He'd heard it across a hundred tables, in a hundred calls. He'd heard it in Malacca, the year before the white room, when all of them had gone over on the coach to see him. The whole lot of them, Deston and Gabe and Rae and Cal and him. And the door had opened. And there'd been that face. And then that laugh, exactly that one, sudden and helpless, before he'd even said hello.

Dennis took a step toward the counter. Toward the door at the back.

Merle moved.

She didn't hurry. She just stepped sideways, behind the counter, so that she was standing in front of the kitchen door, with her floury arms folded and her feet planted. She looked at Dennis. Not unkind. Not unfriendly. Just very, very steady.

"He said all of you," said Merle quietly. "Not one at a time."

Dennis stopped.

"He said when you were all here," said Merle. "All of you. In one place. Not a floor away. Not a street away. Not one of you, then another. *All* of you." She didn't move from the door. "He said he'd waited a hundred and fifty days, and he could wait one more, if it meant doing it right." She looked at Rachael, and something in her face went gentle. "He's been waiting a long time. Let him do it right."

Behind the kitchen door, there wasn't another sound.

Dennis stood in the bakery on Lantern Street with his hands shaking. He looked at the door. At the baker standing in front of it with her arms folded. At the thin line of light under it, where somebody was standing on the other side, very still, a metre away, close enough to touch if it weren't for the wood.

A hundred and fifty-one days.

Rachael put her hand on his arm.

"Den," she said. Very low. "Come on."

He looked at her.

"He said all of us," said Rachael. Her eyes were wet. Rachael didn't cry. Rachael had never cried, not once, not in nine years that Dennis had known her, not on floor 1 with Calvin's arm, not anywhere. Her eyes were wet. "So we go and get all of us. Properly." Her hand tightened on his arm. "We're not doing this one at a time. Not with him. Not after all this."

Dennis looked at the kitchen door one more time.

Then he turned round and went out of the bakery with Rachael, into the cool shadow of Lantern Street. The bell went over the door behind them. And they walked back up the street toward the square, fast, then faster, then running. Past the well and into the market, between the stalls, past the dog with the sausage and the cheesemonger humming. Dennis was shouting before they'd even reached the inn.

"*DREW!*"

He didn't buy the rosemary loaf. He realised that afterwards, too. He'd been charged double and he hadn't even haggled.

It was the first time in his life he'd walked out of a shop without trying.
