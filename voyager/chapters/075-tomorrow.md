Alea told him on the hundred-and-thirty-sixth morning, in the bakery, while he had his hands in the dough.

He'd known something was wrong with her for days. Since the bowl, really. She'd been quieter. Not the dry quiet she went into when she was thinking, or the soft one when she was letting him be sad. A different one. The quiet of somebody carrying something round in a coat pocket, the way he'd carried Thea's whisper for a day without opening it.

She was sitting on the edge of the floury table, small and teal and white in the heat from the oven, with her tail round her feet. Merle was out at the front, selling Mrs Penn a loaf and hearing about her knee. Ice was leaning on the dough. Push it away, fold it back, turn.

"Ice," said Alea.

"Mm."

"On six. In the bowl." She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the dough. "When you knelt down by her. When the thing was broken and you were kneeling there with your hand out."

He stopped leaning.

"I showed myself to her," said Alea.

He didn't say anything.

"On purpose," said the fox. Very small. "For one second. On your shoulder. I let her see me. I could have stayed hidden. I've stayed hidden from everybody for a hundred and thirty-six days, and ten years before that, and nobody's ever seen me but you. And I let her." She swallowed. "I let Bee see too. I know she drew me. Aleaka's got the page. I know. I meant to."

He stood at the floury table with his hands in the dough.

"Why?" he said.

Alea was quiet for a long time.

"Because you never let anyone see you," she said.

He looked at her.

"Not once," said Alea. "Not in two lives. Not the first time, either, not really. You'd talk to everybody, you'd make everybody laugh, you'd tank everything, and nobody ever saw what you were carrying. Not Gabe. Not Deston. Not *her*." Her voice had gone thin. "And this time it's worse. This time you've got a helm with no face and a name that isn't yours and a floor between you and everybody. You don't even let them see your *back*."

"Alea—"

"And she was lying there," said the fox, "with her shoulder open. Looking up at you. At a slit in a helm. And she'd just run into a thing that showed people what they wanted, and she hadn't wanted it. She'd seen through it. And she'd stepped in front of Gigi." Alea looked up at him with her two white pinprick eyes. "And I thought, she should get to see *something* of him. Something real. Just once. Just for a second." Her voice cracked. "So I showed her me. Because I'm the realest thing about you that there is."

He didn't say anything at all.

He stood at the table with his floury hands on the dough and looked at the small fox made of light. She'd been in his head for ten years and a hundred and thirty-six days. She'd logged every death and pinned every whisper and flared in his face in a bowl of glass to pull him back from a table. She'd sat on his knee in the dark at the bottom of a cistern, and in a ringing chamber, and on a terrace in the Red City.

*The realest thing about you that there is.*

"Did she see?" he said. His voice had gone somewhere small.

"She saw," said Alea. "She looked straight at me. For the whole second." The fox paused. "She didn't look surprised. She looked like somebody being *shown* something. Like she'd been waiting for it."

He went back to leaning on the dough. Push. Fold. Turn. He couldn't look at Alea. He didn't trust his face.

"You're not angry," said Alea. It wasn't quite a question.

"No," said Ice.

"You should be. It was your secret. Not mine."

"You're not a secret," he said. He kept his eyes on the dough. "You're you." He folded it again. "And I think—" He stopped. "I think you were right. I think somebody should have seen something real. Ages ago. And I wasn't going to let them."

Alea was quiet for a long time.

Then she came across the table, through the flour, leaving tiny fox-shaped prints that weren't really there. She sat down on the back of his floury hand on the dough, very light, the way she'd sat on his palm in the House of Ash while he said every name.

"Merle's laughing at you," she said. "Through the door. Because you've stopped kneading and you're just standing there."

He laughed. It came out wet. Out at the front, he heard Merle laughing too.

On the hundred-and-forty-sixth night, Alea read him World at the bottom of the clocktower.

He'd taken to sleeping there. In the ringing chamber, in the middle of the circle of thirty-six ropes, on his bedroll, with the new shield beside him and the moon coming in through the slit window. The caretaker never remembered him in the morning. Merle said it was morbid. He said it was the only place in Evermorrow that didn't reset round him, because nobody ever came up.

He laughed. He couldn't help it. *Nobody got burned.* At dusk. Not noon, the way he'd done it, walking through the beams with every step a guess. Not at night, the way they'd done it the first time. *Dusk.* With somebody reading the dead one and writing down the gaps so everybody could see them.

*Cursiffe.* He'd never found the statue the first time. Nobody had. The first time, Cursiffe had been a Mage his whole life, with ink that froze, and he'd died at the gate on a hundred with his pen still in his hand.

*Cursiffe read the dead one.*

"He's started the Archive," said Alea softly. "He must have. On five. *Wrote down the gaps, and everybody could see them.* That's Living Manuscript." She was quiet. "That's twenty-six, Ice. Twenty-six things that are different."

He lay in the circle of ropes and looked at the moon.

And then there was another post. Under Rachael's. Later. An hour later, by the log.

He read it.

He read it again.

He read it a third time, lying on his back on the floor of the ringing chamber with his arm across his eyes, so that Alea had to hold the window up over his face where he could see it.

*We're not chasing you any more.*

*Take your time.*

"They're not coming after you," said Alea. Very gently. "Ice. Did you see? They're not chasing. They worked it out. Higu worked it out. Every time they got close, you ran. So they've stopped." She was quiet. "They're just coming up. Ready. Together. At their own speed."

"I know."

"*Take your time*," said Alea. "He said take your time."

"*I know*, Alea."

He lay there with his arm over his eyes and didn't move for a long time. The great clock ticked in the room below. The ropes hung still round him in the moonlight.

A hundred and forty-six days. He'd been running for a hundred and forty-six days. One floor ahead. Always one floor ahead. So that nothing could get to them before it had got to him first. So they wouldn't see him. So he wouldn't have to stand in front of them, with his face, and not be able to say why he knew.

And they'd stopped chasing. They'd sat down together at the bottom of a pit in a desert and worked out that the faster they came, the faster he ran. And they'd decided, together, to slow down. For him.

*Take your time.*

He'd never once, in two lives, had anybody tell him he could take his time.

***

"Will it ever be tomorrow?" said Merle.

It was the hundred-and-forty-eighth evening. They were sitting on the edge of the fountain in the square, in the gold light, eating the rosemary loaf. His, today. She'd let him do the whole thing on his own, from flour to crust, and stood at the back of the bakery with her arms folded and only laughed twice. The market was packing up round them. The brown and white dog was asleep under the butcher's table, full of sausage.

She'd never asked before. In twenty-two days. Not once. She'd asked how to ring more bells and whether the Hour had a face and why he slept in a tower. But never that.

He looked at her. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the clocktower, at the thirty-six dark shapes hanging in the belfry against the sky.

"I'm not asking you to promise," said Merle. "I just want to know if you think it will." She turned the bread over in her floury hands. "I've stopped thinking it will. Around the sixtieth day. You can't keep thinking it will, every morning, and have it not." She was quiet. "But you came in twice. So I thought I'd ask. Once."

He looked up at the bells.

*Thirty-six.*

"Yes," he said.

Merle looked at him.

"Yes," said Ice. "It'll be tomorrow. I promise."

"I said you didn't have to promise."

"I know. I'm promising anyway."

She looked at him for a long time with her floury hands still on the bread.

"How?" she said. "You can't do it. You've tried every night for three weeks. You, me, the Dawley boy, Mrs Penn and her *knee*. Seven was the best we ever got. It needs thirty-six." She shook her head. "Where are you going to get thirty-six people who'll stand in a tower at midnight and remember they did it?"

Ice looked at the bells.

"I've got thirty-five friends," he said. "Coming up the hill."

Merle didn't say anything for a moment.

"Thirty-five," she said slowly.

"Thirty-five."

"And you."

"And me."

She looked up at the belfry too, at the thirty-six dark shapes against the gold.

"Like the bells," she said.

"Like the bells."

She sat on the edge of the fountain with her floury hands in her lap. Then she started to laugh, very quietly, the way people laugh when they've been holding something in for a very long time and it's started to come out sideways.

"You absolute idiot," said Merle. "Why didn't you *say*?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He'd never had an answer for that. Not to Gabe, not to Nan Gully, not to anybody.

He made the decision that night, at the bottom of the clocktower, in the middle of the ring of ropes. Except that it didn't feel like making a decision. It felt like noticing he'd already made it, some time ago, without telling himself. Like noticing you've already stood up.

"I'm staying," he said.

Alea looked at him.

"Here," said Ice. "On seven. I'm not going anywhere."

"You can't go anywhere," said Alea. "The Hour. You can't clear it. You can't get up the stair."

"I know. That's not what I mean." He looked up at the ropes. "I mean, when they come. When they come up the hill. I'm not going to hide. I'm not going to go round the back of the valley and sleep in a hayloft and watch them through a window. I'm not going to wear the helm." He swallowed. "I'm going to stay where I am. In Merle's bakery. On Lantern Street. With my face. And let them find me."

Alea didn't say anything for a long time.

"And when they find you?"

"Then they find me."

"And Gabe?"

"I'll answer him," said Ice. "When they're all here. All of them. In one place. When nobody's a floor away." He swallowed. "*Ok*. And then a thousand words. And then I'll let him hit me."

"You've said that before."

"I meant it before. I mean it more now."

The fox was quiet.

"And the rest of it?" said Alea, very carefully. "The House of Ash. The white room. The first time. Ten years." She looked at him. "Thea heard you. She said so. *I heard you.* She knows what *not this time* means, Ice. Or she's guessed. And the others will have guessed by now too, some of them. Chris. Kei. Hako, probably, he reads all those books." She was quiet. "They'll ask."

He sat in the circle of ropes for a long time.

"Not that," he said at last. "Not yet." He looked down at the scar on his palm. "They can have me. All of me. My face, my name, the onion skins, the barrel lids, all of it. I'll stand at a rope with them at midnight and ring a bell." He closed his hand. "But not the rest. Not the first time. Not who died, and where, and what I was doing when it happened. Not yet." He swallowed. "I set that down once, in a stone room, and the tower built a table out of it. I'm not handing it to them. Not till I know what it'll do."

"And Thea?"

He didn't answer for a long time.

"Thea," he said. Very quietly. "When she asks. Because she will." He looked up at the bells. "I'll tell her *not yet*. And then I'll tell her *trust*." He almost smiled. "And she'll be furious with me."

"She'll be furious," Alea agreed.

"And she'll wait anyway."

"Yes," said the fox. "I think she will."

He lay down on his bedroll in the middle of the circle of ropes with the shield beside him. He looked up at the dark beams of the belfry floor overhead, and the thirty-six bells beyond it that he couldn't see.

"Alea."

"Mm."

"If it's a trap. If the tower built all this to see who's on the thirty-sixth rope. If we all stand here at midnight and pull, and it sees me, properly, for the first time. With them."

"Yes?"

"Let it look," said Ice.

***

They came on the hundred-and-fiftieth night.

He was on the step of the blue door on Lantern Street when it happened. Merle had gone up to bed at eleven, the way she always did now. *If I've got to wake up in the same day, I'm at least waking up in my own bed.* He'd stayed down on the step outside the shop, with the warm smell of the oven still coming out through the door behind him and the narrow street dark and quiet in front of him. Above the leaning roofs, a slice of stars.

The clocktower rang the half hour. Half past eleven. One note, deep and slow and very old, rolling out over the town and up the valley toward the dark hills.

And something woke behind his breastbone.

He didn't move.

It was faint. Very faint. Far off, up the valley, on the hillside above the town. Where an old door stood in the rock at the top of a stair, the door he'd come out of himself twenty-four days ago into wet grass and birdsong. A pull. Small and fine, like a single thread drawn tight. Running from somewhere behind the ember in his chest, out through the dark, up the valley, up the hill, to the door.

*Calvin.*

Then the second. A hair to the left. *Rachael.*

He sat on the step of Merle's bakery with his hands on his knees and counted. He didn't decide to. He never did.

*Three. Four. Five.*

They were coming out of the door. One by one, into the night, onto the hillside, the way he had. Into the wet grass, and the smell of it. He knew exactly what it would be like for them. Twenty-four days on floor 6, and then grass. He felt the threads stop, one after another, just outside the door. Stand there. Not moving. Breathing it in.

*Twenty. Twenty-five. Thirty.*

*Thirty-four.*

He'd have known that one in the dark at the bottom of the world.

*Thirty-five.* Drew, last.

Thirty-five threads, running out from his chest across the whole of the dark valley, up the hill to a door. Fine as hair. Shivering a little, the way they did when people were laughing, or crying, or both.

None of them was red.

He didn't get up. He didn't go and hide. He didn't put the helm on, or go round the back of the town, or climb the tower to watch. He sat on the step of a blue door on Lantern Street with flour on his trousers and a scar on his palm, and felt thirty-five people standing on a hill in the dark, on his floor, smelling the grass.

"You're not going anywhere," said Alea. She was sitting on his knee. Small and bright in the dark.

"No," said Ice.

"First time," said the fox. "In a hundred and fifty days. They've come up a stair, and you're not going anywhere."

"No."

She didn't say anything else. She just leaned against his arm, very light, the way she did when she'd decided to stop arguing. They sat together on the step and felt them.

The threads started moving. Down the hill. Slowly, carefully, in a line. Calvin in front. Toward the road. Toward the river, and the old stone bridge, and the town. They didn't know it was midnight in half an hour. They didn't know what midnight did here. They were just walking down a hill in the dark toward the lights of a town.

He sat on the step and waited for them.

The great bell began to ring.

*One.*

He felt the threads stop. All thirty-five, on the hill road, halfway down. Listening.

*Two. Three. Four.*

Somewhere above him, in the clock room, iron wheels began to grind. The Hour, stepping down out of the face. Walking past nobody. Putting its long pointed arm against the wheel.

*Eight. Nine. Ten.*

"Ice," said Alea. "They don't know."

"I know."

"In two strokes they're going to—"

"I know." He was smiling. He didn't know why. He couldn't stop. "Let them."

*Eleven.*

*Twelve.*

The world moved.

And up on the hill road, thirty-five threads jolted all at once. Every one. Like thirty-five people missing a step on a stair in the dark. He felt it go through them, the lurch, the disbelief, as the night went white and then grey and then gold. The stars went out. The birds began to sing all down the hillside, all at once, the way birds do at the edge of dawn.

And then, a second later, very faint, all the way down the valley, carried on the morning air from the hill road to the step of a blue door on Lantern Street, he heard it. Calvin's voice. Thirty-five people's worth of noise, but Calvin's on top. Loud enough to wake the whole of Evermorrow, if Evermorrow hadn't already been awake for a hundred and fifty days.

"*WHAT*—"

Ice put his face in his floury hands and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

Behind him, in the bakery, the oven roared. Upstairs, Merle was waking up in her own bed, the same as every morning. In a minute she'd come down the stairs and pull a tray of rolls out of the oven and the bell would go over the door. And it would be the same day again. One more time.

He thought it might be one of the last.

"Welcome to Evermorrow," he said into his hands. To thirty-five people on a hill who couldn't hear him.

Alea laughed too. The dry little laugh she had. And then she said, very softly, so softly he almost didn't hear it over the birds:

"Tomorrow, then."

"Tomorrow," said Ice.
