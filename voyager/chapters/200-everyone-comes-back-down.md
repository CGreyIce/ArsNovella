He woke at her table.

He didn't know where he was, for a moment. That hadn't happened in a long time. He'd woken in a hundred places since the white room, in caves and cisterns and barns and the holds of ships, on salt and snow and glass, and he'd always known, before his eyes were open, exactly where he was and who was near and which way the door was. It was the first thing Alea had taught him, the first time, on floor 1. *Know the door before you know anything.*

He didn't know the door. He was warm. There was a blanket on his shoulders that smelled of woodsmoke and onions, and his face was on his arms, and his arms were on wood, old smooth wood worn soft by a great many elbows, and somewhere close there was a fire, and somebody was peeling something.

He lifted his head.

The Copper Spoon. The long table. The kitchen end. Grey painted light in the window over the stove, and snow coming down past it in rows. The fire banked low. And across the table from him, with the onion sack between them and a little knife in her hand and a heap of whole brown skins at her elbow, in her apron and her nightcap and her dead husband's boots, Mother Crane.

She didn't look up.

"You snore," she said.

He sat there. He couldn't say anything.

"Fourteen hours," said Mother Crane. She topped an onion. "Chi counted. That girl counts everything, like the doctor. You lot are all counters." She ran the knife down the side, along the grain, and the skin came off whole. "I've done half the sack. You can do the rest. My hands are tired." She put the knife down and looked at him for the first time. "Well? Are you going to peel, or are you going to sit there with your mouth open?"

He peeled.

She watched him. She didn't say anything. After a while she got up, slowly, with one hand on the table, and went to her stove, and put the kettle on, and stood with her back to him.

"Chi told you," she said. "Did she."

"Told me what?"

"What I said. Last night. When you were asleep." She didn't turn round. "I told her to tell you."

He looked across the room. Chi was asleep on the bench by the fire at the far end, curled up with Waddles on her feet. She'd been asleep, he thought, since about the time he had.

"No," he said.

Mother Crane was quiet for a moment.

"Well, I'll tell you myself, then," she said. "Since she's asleep, and you're awake, and I'm not getting any younger." She turned round. She looked at him across her kitchen, with the kettle starting to sing behind her, and her small sharp eyes on his face. "She said you were frightened. All this time. That you'd get here on time and it wouldn't matter."

He put the onion down.

"It mattered," said Mother Crane.

That was all. She turned back to the kettle.

He sat at her long table with an onion in his hand and a heap of skins at his elbow and didn't move for a long time. Then he picked up the knife again. Top and tail. Along the grain. Keep the root on.

The skin came off in one piece.

***

He stayed a month.

They all did. Nobody talked about it. Nobody put it to a vote, or wrote it on a slate, or drew it on Higu's rock. It was midwinter on floor 1, with the snow coming down in rows and the river black under its painted ice, and floor 2 would be worse, and the Long Storm would be sitting on the pass like a cat on a chair, and there was a cookhouse at the bottom of Crooked Street with a fire in it and an old woman getting her strength back who'd shout at you if you put your feet on her table. So they stayed.

He peeled her onions every morning. Before anybody was up. She came down the stair in eleven breaths, and then ten, and on the twentieth morning in nine, which she said was because she'd taken the stairs at a run to get away from Calvin's snoring. She talked about Tam. She talked about her gran, and her gran's mother, and the women whose names were carved under the table, Edith and Agnes and Rosa and Bess and Tilda and Mab; she knew stories about every one. She'd never told anybody, she said. Nobody had ever asked. She told him now, in the mornings, at the onions, and he listened, and said *yes* and *I know* and *I believe you*.

She didn't ask his name. He didn't offer it. He was the onion boy. He'd been the onion boy since the first morning, and he found, at the end of the first week, that he didn't want to be anything else to her. Not Voyager. Not Ice, love. Not Declan. *Onion boy. Peel. Onion boy, your knife's blunt. Onion boy, go and see to my stove.* It was a name somebody had given him in this life, not the other one. It was his.

Elsie came every morning now. She came in by the front door, not the back, and sat at the long table, very straight, at the end nearest the door, and Mother Crane gave her a bowl of porridge and a heel of bread and talked at her about eggs. At the end of the first week Elsie laughed at something Newish said. At the end of the second, she said something back, and Newish laughed, and Elsie went so red that Hako had to leave the room. She didn't stand on her corner any more. Somebody else's girl did, sometimes, with a basket. But not every day. And not saying the same thing.

The town came in. That was new. On floor 1, in the first life, in the month he'd lived here, nobody had ever come into the Copper Spoon but Climbers and the old men with their knees. Now they came. The miller and his father. Ott with his cheeses. The tannery woman. The old smith, who sat by the fire every evening and argued with Gabe about trivets and with Salty about hinges and with nobody about anything else. Quill, once, very stiffly, to see if the notebook was being used. It was. Mother Crane had started a new tab in it. *For whoever comes down next.* With Dennis's little pile of interest written at the top as *PAID IN ADVANCE*.

Kei's yard went on. Every morning, the people of Hearthstead in the hens, being mended, asking. Mother Crane did soup for it. She said it was the least she could do, since he'd turned her yard into a sickroom without asking, and Kei said *sorry*, and she said *don't be sorry, be useful*, and gave him turnips.

And every night, the table.

***

He'd seen it before.

On six. On the Glass. In the bowl, at the end of the canyon, at the heart of the Wanting, which had stood in the gold evening light and shown him the thing he wanted most in the world. Not a fight. Not the top. Not the first life fixed. A table. A long one, under a white canopy on poles. Mother Crane's long table from the Copper Spoon, set out in the bowl. And all thirty-five of them at it. Alive. Gabe laughing. Deston with his cup. Calvin with both his arms. Rachael. Dennis arguing about who was paying. Drew with his knuckles off. Thea at the end, turning to look at him, with a seat beside her, empty, for him.

He hadn't been able to hit it. Alea had dragged him out. He'd sat on the edge of the bowl for twenty days, and walked it eleven times each way, and never once been able to go in.

On the way down, a year later, he'd walked in across the ring at last, and stood in the middle of the bowl where it had been, among the shards of glass in the sand, and said *a table. Alive. There was a seat.* And Gigi had said *That's where we're going. It's not a want any more. It's a plan.*

It wasn't under a canopy. It wasn't in gold evening light. It was in a low dark cookhouse at the bottom of a crooked street, with the snow coming in every time somebody opened the door and the fire smoking when the wind went the wrong way and the benches too short for thirty-seven people, so that half of them had to sit on the floor and Bee had to sit on Hako. There was no white cloth. There were onion skins on it, and a burn mark where somebody had put a hot pot down forty years ago, and names carved underneath that nobody could see.

Gabe was laughing. At something Salty had said about a hinge. With his head back and his wolf coat over the back of the bench and Hearthwall leaning against the wall behind him, warm.

Deston was holding his cup. He held it the way he held everything, carefully, both hands, like it might be the last one. The note was in his shirt pocket. Ice could see the corner of it.

Calvin had both his arms on the table. And then his feet.

"*Feet*," said Mother Crane, from the stove.

Rachael was next to Calvin, with her gloves off, eating with her bare hands, which had gone back to their own colour in the second week. Dennis was across from her, arguing with Hako about who was paying, out of habit, because the tab was paid and there was nothing left to argue about. Drew had his knuckles off. He'd had them off since the Gate. He had his bare hands round a bowl.

And Thea was at the end. Next to him. Not across. Not waiting with an empty seat. Next to him, on the same bench, with her shoulder against his, and the lantern on the table in front of them, and her hand on his knee under the table, where nobody could see.

She turned and looked at him.

He couldn't say anything.

"This is the table," said Thea. Quietly. Only to him.

"I know."

"The one on six."

"Yes."

She looked down the long table. At all of them. At the cookhouse full of noise and smoke and onions and people, alive, all of them, every one, thirty-five and him and an old woman at the stove shouting. She looked at it for a long time.

"It's better," said Thea.

"It's better," he said.

He counted them. He couldn't help it; he'd done it on every floor since the white room, before he filled any bowl, before he let himself sit down. He looked down the long table and counted every face.

Thirty-five. And her.

And then he stopped counting. On purpose. He put his hand over Thea's on his knee, and let himself not count, for the first time in either life, and just looked.

Gigi, three down from him, was watching him do it. She caught his eye. She raised her cup a little.

"It's not a plan any more," said Gigi.

"No," he said. "What is it?"

Gigi looked down the table. At Bee on Hako's knee. At Maigo, with four new leaves, eating soup with his sunglasses on. At Mother Crane, who had come out from behind the stove with a ladle to hit Calvin with it.

"Supper," said Gigi.

Higu did the rock on the twenty-sixth night.

She did it at the long table, after supper, with everybody watching, because everybody always watched now. It was nearly full. Thirteen short lines down the left-hand side, one for every stair on the way down, with *EVERYTHING* beside the last. The middle column a list of true things, *HOT, WIND, CALVIN: TALKING, THERE'S A LANTERN, COLD, CALVIN: HUNGRY.* And the right-hand column, the *NOW*, written up the edge of the rock and over the top and down the back, in smaller and smaller letters, the whole long way down from fourteen. *THE CACHES ARE KEPT. THE KITES FLY. HESSA. ALIVE. THE SQUARE. EVERYBODY TALKING. THE QUIET. BURIED. THE THIRTY-SIX.*

There was one corner left. Bottom right. Just big enough.

Higu looked at it for a long time. Then she wet the chalk and wrote, in her smallest, squarest capitals:

*ALL OF US.*

And under it, smaller still, because there wasn't room for anything bigger:

*AND HER.*

She put the chalk down.

"That's the rock full," said Higu.

Nobody said anything. Mother Crane, at the stove, said, "What's that? A slate?"

"A rock," said Higu. "For writing down what's true."

"Hm." Mother Crane looked at it over the stove. "Well, it's full. Get another one."

Higu looked at the rock in her hands. Then, very slowly, she smiled. Higu didn't, much.

"Yes," she said. "I will. On the way up."

***

The window came on the thirty-first night.

He'd known it would. He'd known since a night on the Long Acre, in a barn, under the third beam, when he'd told Thea. *On the fourth week. At night.* He'd done the sum on the way down without meaning to, the way he did all the sums now. *Midwinter. A month late.* He'd known, for thirty-one nights, that it was coming. He hadn't told anybody but Thea. Thea had held his hand at the table every night for the last week.

They were at supper. All thirty-six, at the long table. Mother Crane at the stove with the ladle. Elsie at the end by the door. The old smith by the fire with Gabe. Calvin's feet were on the table. It was very loud.

And every window in the room came up at once.

Thirty-six of them. Small, quiet, in front of thirty-six pairs of eyes, all at the same moment, with the soft sound a window made that you stopped hearing after a week and never stopped hearing again. Everybody stopped. Spoons in the air. Calvin's feet still on the table.

It was World. The chat. Where everybody wrote. He'd read every line of it on every floor since the first night, and never once written anything in it.

There was a new line at the top.

It didn't have a name on it.

[ World · Floor 1. Midwinter. Mother Crane of the Copper Spoon has died. ]

***

Nobody moved.

The Copper Spoon had never been so quiet. Not on the six hundred and ninth day. Not on the third night. Thirty-six people at a long table, all looking at the same small window in front of their eyes, with their spoons in the air. Elsie, at the end, who couldn't see windows, looked round at all of them with her mouth open.

He read it.

He'd read it before. In the first life. On the Long Acre, under the third beam of a barn, in the dark, on the fourth week of the second winter, with everybody asleep round him in the straw. He'd read it and gone out into the snow and stood under a bare tree for a long time. *Floor 1. Midwinter. Mother Crane of the Copper Spoon has died.* The only line in ten years that nobody wrote.

He read it again now. Word for word the same.

It had come on time. Exactly. Thirty-one nights after midwinter, at supper. The tower had kept its schedule all the way to the end. The fever on the day. The news a month late. Every line of the first record arriving when it was supposed to, the way the snow had come on fourteen, the way the bolts had gone. It had sent the line. Because that was what happened next.

He looked up from the window.

Mother Crane was standing at her stove with the ladle in her hand.

She was looking at them. At thirty-six people frozen at her table with their spoons in the air, staring at nothing. At Calvin, with his feet on the table, staring at nothing.

"What are you all looking at?" said Mother Crane. "What's that face for? You look like a funeral." She pointed the ladle. "And *you*. *Feet*."

Calvin took his feet off the table.

He did it without looking away from the window. Slowly. Carefully. Like a man in a dream. And Mother Crane, who had been alive for sixty-something years and had never had a fever in her life until the six hundred and ninth day, stood at her stove and glared at him with her small sharp eyes, and banged the ladle on the side of the pot.

*Her* pot. Not the good one. That was on his pack, still. By the stove. Clean.

And Ice laughed.

***

He couldn't stop.

It came out of him the way it had at the wall in the Red City, at Dennis and the terrible wall. All at once, from somewhere so deep he hadn't known there was anything down there. He put his hand over his face. He laughed until he had to hold the table. Thea had both hands on his arm, and she was laughing too, and crying, and laughing, and he couldn't see anything for a while.

"What?" said Mother Crane. "What's funny? What's *wrong* with him?"

"Nothing," said Thea. She could barely get it out. "Somebody got something wrong."

"Who?"

"Somebody who wasn't here," said Thea.

Mother Crane looked at her. Then at him. Then at the rest of them, still staring, still frozen, at nothing she could see.

"You're all mad," she said. "Every one of you. Mad." And she went back to her stove.

He took his hand off his face. He looked at the window.

*Floor 1. Midwinter. Mother Crane of the Copper Spoon has died.*

No name on it. In ten years, in either life, the only thing in World that nobody wrote.

He'd never written anything in World. Not once. Not in the first life; he'd always meant to and never had, and by the time it mattered there'd been nobody left to read it. Not in this one. He'd read every line, on every floor, every night, and typed *ok* to Gabe on the second day and deleted it, and never once put a word where the thirty-five could see it. It had been his rule since the white room. *Even thanks is a thread.*

He put his finger on the window.

He wrote.

[ World · Voyager: no. she's alive. she's shouting at calvin ]

***

It went up under the other one.

Thirty-five people saw it come. He felt them see it. Every thread in his chest went taut at once, and then something else, something he didn't have a word for. They looked at the window. They looked at the name on the new line. *Voyager.* The first line in World with that name on it, on any floor, in six hundred and forty days.

And then they looked at him.

Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to.

Calvin wrote first. Of course he did. He wrote it with his feet back up on the table, and his finger jabbing at the air, and his tongue between his teeth.

[ World · Calvin: she is. at me. specifically ]

And then Dennis.

[ World · Dennis: tab's paid. she's alive. both true ]

And Kei, with his book open on the table in front of him at the inside back cover, at *her?* and *yes* and *609*.

[ World · Kei: she's alive. i measured ]

[ World · Hako: classic ]

[ World · Maigo: alive ]

[ World · Rachael: alive. her hands are warm. i checked ]

[ World · Malcolm: A is everybody ]

Nobody knew what that meant but Malcolm, and Malcolm didn't explain.

[ World · Gabe: shes alive mate. shes hitting calvin with a ladle ]

[ World · Suzuyun: kept ]

[ World · Aleaka: the goat says so too ]

[ World · Newish: she laughed at me today. twice. medically ]

[ World · Chris: day 640. she's alive. i wrote it down ]

And Chris got out the grey notebook from the bottom of his pack, the first one, from Quill's, and opened it to *HOW?*, and wrote on the first of the two lines that were still empty. Ice saw him do it. He didn't see what Chris wrote. He didn't need to.

[ World · Drew: SOMEBODY THANK KEI. and maigo. and yan. and calvin for going under the table. and everybody ]

[ World · Hyvred: she's kitchen-coloured ]

[ World · PenguChi: it mattered ]

And on, and on. All of them. One after another, down the window, under the line with no name. Thirty-five lines. *She's alive.* *She's alive.* *Alive. Shouting.* *Alive.* Xola, *alive, babe's crying, so am i.* Higu, *TRUE*. Ina, last but one, who never wrote in World: *okay*.

And last, the way she'd been last on every list since floor 1, the only one with a full stop:

[ World · Althea: Witnessed. ]

***

He looked at it.

Thirty-six lines. And above them, at the top, the one that nobody wrote.

It didn't go away. He'd half thought it would. That it would flicker, the way windows did on fourteen when the tower changed its mind, and fade, and be gone, and the World would be thirty-six lines saying *alive* and nothing else. It didn't. It stayed at the top. *Floor 1. Midwinter. Mother Crane of the Copper Spoon has died.* The first record, arriving on time, the way it always had, the way it always would.

And under it, thirty-six people saying no.

Both of them. Standing. Neither crossing the other out. The way the two pages stood in the great book in Tell, side by side, *the first year* and *the first year*, with Thea's *Told* in the margin of both.

*One record does not match*, said Alea. Very softly. On his shoulder. Where she'd been for six hundred and forty days. *It's never going to match now. Not this line. Never.*

"No," he said.

*Good*, said Alea. And then, after a moment, in a voice he'd never heard her use, small and fierce and wet: *Good.*

***

He brought the pot back in the morning.

He washed it first. At her pump, in the yard, in the grey painted light before anybody was up but the hens. Six hundred and forty days of it. The dent from the tree on nine. The scorch from Ina's fire on the Salt. The scratch on the lid from the bottom of the stair on fourteen, four hundred and twenty-four steps with it knocking against his hip. He scrubbed it with sand and rinsed it and dried it with a cloth, and he didn't get the dent out, and he didn't try.

He came in by the back door.

She was at the long table, with the onion sack and the little knife. He'd known she would be. She looked up at the pot in his hands.

He put it down on the table in front of her.

"I brought it back," he said.

Mother Crane looked at the pot. Then at him.

"Took your time," she said.

"Yes."

"It's dented."

"A tree fell on it. On nine."

"You said." She picked it up. She turned it over in her floury hands, the way she turned everything over, looking for what was wrong with it. She looked at the dent for a long time. At the scorch on the bottom. At the scratch on the lid. At six hundred and forty days of a man going up and down a tower with a pot on his pack.

"Hm," said Mother Crane.

She got up. Slowly, with one hand on the table. She took it to the shelf above the stove. The shelf with the salt, and the black tab book with its cracked spine, and the stub of a candle in a dish that a woman on floor 2 had sent down to burn in her window. She put the pot on the shelf beside the candle. She pushed it back a little, so it sat right. She looked at it there.

Then she took down the tab book.

She opened it on the long table, at the last page with writing on it. *CLIMBERS (THE LOT). FIRST WEEK. PAID.* And above it, on its own, from the first morning. *ONION BOY. 2 LOAVES. PAID. 1 POT (GOOD).*

She took the pencil from behind her ear. Beside *1 POT (GOOD)*, in her small hard capitals, she wrote *BACK*.

And underlined it.

She shut the book.

"There," said Mother Crane. "That's closed."

He didn't say anything. He didn't think he could.

She put the book back on the shelf, beside the pot. She looked at the two of them there, side by side, with the candle stub. Then she turned round and looked at him.

"Everyone comes back down eventually," said Mother Crane. "Gran said. They go up, and they come back down to eat." She folded her arms. "She said something else, too. I told you. On the first night you were back. About the one who comes down alone. Ahead of the rest. Like he already knows the way."

"You said she never said what happened to them."

"She didn't." Mother Crane looked at him a long moment with her small sharp eyes. "So I'll tell you what happened to this one. Since I'm the one who saw it." She sniffed. "He came back down with the rest."

***

They left two days later, on the six hundred and forty-third morning.

He'd known they would. Higu had drawn the floors going up on the back of a new rock, a flat grey one from the river below the mill, one over another like a ladder. One. Two. Three. All the way to fifteen, where the stair was waiting under the line on the Long Barn floor where he'd sat. They'd go up fast. By the roads they knew. Mother Tallow's beacon. Reedmoor's slate. The arrows on four. Gull Island and the kites. The caches on six, with onion skins on the corks. Merle's bells. Aud's jetty. Burr's ropes. The Bastion with *RACHAEL* on the Long Wall, even though she didn't fall. Marrit's quay. Gigi's road across the Salt, going green. Tell, with Hawise and Alys and Aldo on the door-roll. And the Long Acre, under its snow, with the straw man kept in the barn and a bit of him in a hundred chimney corners, waiting for the spring.

And then fifteen. And up.

Mother Crane didn't come out into the street. She stood in her kitchen with her arms folded and watched them go, one by one, out of her door, ducking under the beam. She shouted at every one of them by name. *Calvin, your bootlace. Hako, door. Salty, that hinge's yours now, I'll not have anybody else touch it.* She gave each of them a heel of bread out of a basket by the door, wrapped in a cloth, the new bread, with Merle's mother in it, because she said she wasn't having them go up there with nothing in their stomachs but fear.

He went last. Except for Thea, who waited for him at the door.

Mother Crane looked at him.

"Well," she said. "Go on. Get out of my kitchen."

He didn't move.

"You've got flour on your face," said Mother Crane.

He put his hand up. He did.

She gave him a heel of bread. Then she gave him another one. "Because I know you'll share," she said. And then, as he took them: "You'll pay me when you come back down."

He looked at her.

"I'll keep a tab," said Mother Crane. And smiled. With her whole walnut face. The first time, in either life, that he'd ever seen her do it all the way. "Go on, onion boy."

He went.

***

The goat was on his bridge.

Standing on top of it, in the middle, in the painted snow, the way he'd stood the morning they came down, watching them come up the stream path toward him. Old and grey-muzzled, with his chipped horn and his swayed back. Waiting.

Aleaka stopped.

The whole line stopped behind her. Thirty-six of them on the stream path below the Fold, with their packs on and their bread in their pockets, in a long line in the snow.

She went forward on her own, slowly. She stopped at the foot of the bridge. The goat looked down at her.

"I'm going," said Aleaka. "Up. All of us. A long way." She was quiet a moment. "There's a sea. On eleven. You'd like the sea. There's a thing in it longer than a ship, that sings."

The goat looked at her.

Ice watched from the path. He didn't know what Aleaka could hear. He'd never known. He'd seen her with the wrens on eight, and the singer on eleven, and the gulls on five; he'd seen the window come up in front of her face on the deck of the *Patience*, in the dark, with salt water running out of her hair. *The Call (3 of 3). Call one that cannot come.* She'd carried it for three hundred and forty days and never once said what she thought it meant.

He thought he knew now.

"I know you can't," said Aleaka. "Nothing can. Nothing leaves its floor. Not the wrens. Not Tuppence. Not the singer. You've been under that bridge your whole life. You're old. It's a long way. And it's not allowed." She looked up at him. "I know."

The goat didn't move.

"So I'm not asking you to come," said Aleaka. "I asked you every day. *Would you come.* And you said yes, and you said you can't, and you were right." She put her hand out. Palm up. The way you'd put it out to a bird. "I'm not calling you to come," she said. "I'm calling you to come *with me*. It's different. Coming is something you do. With is something we do." She swallowed. "I'll keep you. All the way. Wherever it is. You won't be on your own once."

The goat looked at her hand for a long time.

Then he came down off the bridge.

Slowly. On his old legs, one careful step at a time, the way you'd come down a stair you weren't sure of in the dark. Down off the hump of the bridge onto the path. He stopped in front of her. He looked at her hand. He put his grey muzzle in it.

And then he walked past her up the path.

Not fast. Not slow. Past the line of them, all thirty-six, standing very still on the stream path with their mouths open, up toward the Fold. Past Calvin. Past Gabe. Past Thea.

He stopped in front of Ice.

He looked up at him with his flat yellow eyes. Old, and tired, and very calm. The goat Ice had punched eleven times on the first morning of the second life, because he was in the way, and there was an hour, and a woman at the bottom of a crooked street who wasn't going to live to the spring.

The goat put his head down.

And butted him. Gently. In the stomach. Once. Not hard. Just enough.

Then he turned round, and went on up the path toward the Fold, with Aleaka beside him and Sleet at her heel, looking resigned.

Behind Ice, somebody started laughing. It was Hana. Then it was everybody.

"I think," said Thea, beside him, with her hand in his and her face wet, "you've been forgiven."

***

The goat walked up through the Fold, past the cracked stone where the Bellwether had stood. He walked across the frost to the round room in the hill. He walked into it. He stopped at the foot of the stair, a hundred and twelve steps going up into the dark, steep and narrow and worn in the middle by everybody who had ever gone up them, which had been, for three hundred years, nobody. And then thirty-six people in two days. And then, a year and a half later, thirty-six again.

He looked up the stair.

Aleaka stood beside him with her hand on his back.

"With me," she said.

And the goat put one hoof on the first step.

The windows came up in front of Aleaka's face. Ice saw her read them. Everybody did. She read them slowly, the way she did everything, and then she read them out loud, because she knew everybody wanted to know and she didn't think it was fair to make them wait.

"*The Call, three of three. Complete.*" She took a breath. "*Hidden class unlocked. Summoner.*"

"Summoner," said Calvin. "*Summoner*."

"*Aleaka*," Aleaka read. "*Level sixty-three. Tier four. Shepherd.* Shepherd," she said again, and smiled. "*Summoner.*" She read the last one. "*Follow. What you call, and keep, may follow you anywhere.*"

There was a short silence in the round room in the hill.

"Sixty-*three*," said Chris. "That's two. On *one*. You don't get anything thirteen floors under. It—"

"It counts from here," said Higu. She had the new rock under her arm. She was looking at the goat, standing on the first step with his old hoof on the stone. "Where it opened. It opened on one." She looked at Aleaka. "That's the lowest there is. That's lower than *Ice's*."

Everybody looked at Ice.

He was looking at the goat. Floor 1. The first floor. The best place there was, for a class to open. Better than the third, where his own Paladin had. Better than anything anybody had found, in either life, on any floor. A Summoner with a goat, on the first step of a hundred and twelve, with the whole of the Spire going up above them.

He started laughing again. He couldn't help it.

"Good," he said. "*Good*."

Aleaka went up the first step beside the goat. And the second. The goat came with her. Slowly. One careful step at a time. The first beast in the whole long life of the Spire to leave its floor.

***

Tiger went after them. He didn't need to open the door; the door was the round room, and it was open. Newish went after Tiger, counting. *One. Two. Three.* Calvin went after Newish, talking, and Rachael's fist went into his collar on the first step.

And then all of them. In a long line. Up into the dark.

He went last but one. Thea's hand in his. Gabe behind them, last-last, in the wolf coat, with Hearthwall warm on his back.

At the foot of the stair, Ice stopped. Just for a moment. He looked back through the round room's door, at the Fold going down grey-white in the frost, and the valley, and the stream with its empty bridge, and the fields. And Hearthstead, small and red and smoking at the bottom of it all, under its painted sky, with the snow coming down in rows. The white tower. The mill. And somewhere at the bottom of a crooked street, a low old building with a crooked chimney, smoking, and a copper spoon on a sign, green with age, turning a little in a wind it hadn't been told to have.

*Floor 1*, said Alea, on his shoulder.

"Floor 1," he said.

*We're going up.*

"We're going up."

*Together*, said Alea. *This time. All of us. Going up together.* She was quiet for a moment. *I didn't think I'd ever say that. Not once. Not in either life.*

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

Thea's hand tightened in his. Up ahead, in the dark, Newish was counting and Calvin was talking and Aleaka was saying *with me* to a goat on every step, softly, and somewhere among all of it, very quietly, Thea started to sing.

He went up.
