Tiger had always been fast, and he'd always been last.

Not last at things. He was first at most things. First to the door, first to the bottom of the stairs, first to say yes when somebody asked who wanted to come. But last to be *picked*. He'd been the last pick so many times in so many games that he'd stopped minding years ago. He'd decided, at some point in his teens, that being picked last just meant you had more to prove, and proving things was fun.

So when the white room had come down to two people without a gold mark, him and Cursiffe, he'd grinned. Of course it had. And then Yan had picked him, and said *sorry, that wasn't an insult*, in that slow deep voice like a cello, and Tiger had liked him enormously for saying it.

Now, five days later, on the morning of the sixth day, he was standing on a wall at the top of the valley, looking down into the Fold at a ram the size of a horse. He was bouncing on his toes.

"Don't," said Wendy.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're bouncing. You bounce before you do something stupid."

"I'm bouncing *thoughtfully*."

[ The Bellwether · Lv 6 · Floor Guardian ]

He'd read the guide. Everybody had. Deston had come back down from floor two the night before and sat at the long table in the Spoon with his whole party round him. He'd written it out in World, slowly and carefully, so that thirty people could read it in their own windows at once:

[ World · Deston: Bellwether, from what we learned. 1. It charges in straight lines at whoever it's angry at. Stand in front of a stone. It hits the stone, it's stunned for about three seconds.
World · Deston: 2. The bell calls more sheep. Every time it rings. Cut the strap. Takes a few stuns.
World · Deston: 3. When the bell comes off, it screams and starts CURVING its charges. Don't dodge sideways, it follows you. Have something it can hit instead.
World · Deston: 4. Don't go alone. Please.
World · Calvin: (he means me) ]

Number four had got a lot of laughing reactions. Tiger had noticed Deston didn't laugh.

"Okay," said Yan quietly, beside him. "Same as we said."

Same as they'd said. They'd planned it round Ulla Dray's anvil the night before, with Salty's new hand-warmer passing back and forth between them like a talisman. Salty would hold the front. Wendy would go for its eyes. Cursiffe would hit it when it was stuck. Yan would keep everyone standing.

And Tiger would cut the strap.

"In one stun," he'd said, last night.

"It took Deston's party three," Cursiffe had pointed out, mildly.

"Deston's party didn't have me."

"That," Cursiffe had said, after a moment's consideration, "is actually a very fair point."

***

Salty went down into the Fold and woke it up by throwing a stone at its head.

It wasn't elegant. Salty wasn't elegant. He didn't do the sword-on-shield thing; he didn't have a shield. He just picked up a stone the size of a fist and threw it. It bounced off the Bellwether's thick grey skull with a *tock*, and the ram opened its yellow eyes and looked at him with total, personal offence. Then it got up, heaving, front end first, and kept getting up until it was the size of a horse. The bell rang.

"Yeah," said Salty. "You."

It came at him.

He'd set himself in front of the split stone, six metres out, the greatsword held flat across his body like a door. He didn't try to dodge. Tiger watched him not dodge, and thought it was either the bravest or the stupidest thing he'd ever seen, and then understood it was neither. Salty had decided, three days ago in the gorse, that next time he'd stand right. So he was standing right.

At three metres he stepped aside. One step. Not a dive, not a scramble. One flat step, like a man stepping off a kerb. The ram went past him with its head down and hit the split stone at full speed.

*Crack.*

[ The Bellwether · Stunned ]

Tiger went.

He'd been working it out for four days, what his legs did now. On Earth he'd been quick in the ordinary way. He'd won races at school, and once caught a bus he had no business catching. Here, he was something else. When he wanted to go, he went. His body seemed to forget about the bits of running that slowed everyone else down: the pushing off, the getting up to speed. One moment he was standing on the wall, and the next he was across the cropped grass of the Fold. And the next he was on the ram's shoulder, one foot on the wool and one knee on its neck, with both knives in the black leather strap, sawing.

Left knife, right knife. Left, right. The same rhythm as Salty at the anvil. Tiger had sat in the forge doorway and watched for an hour and hadn't known at the time that he was learning anything.

*One,* said the voice in his head that counted these things.

*Two.*

The strap parted.

All the way. The bell dropped off the Bellwether's neck and hit the grass, and Tiger dropped off after it and rolled. He came up on his feet ten metres away with both knives in his hands and the biggest grin of his life on his face, just as the ram came out of its daze.

"*One stun!*" he yelled. "*ONE!*"

"*Tiger!*" yelled Wendy. "*It's going to—*"

The Bellwether screamed.

***

He'd known it was going to. He'd read the guide. *It screams and starts curving.* Reading it hadn't prepared him. The sound went through him like cold water, and all seven stones seemed to hum, and every Woolback on every slope turned round and went home. And the ram swung its huge head round and looked, with its yellow eyes, straight at the person who'd taken its bell.

Him.

"Oh," said Tiger. "Oh, *no*."

It came.

And Tiger, who was the fastest person on the floor, who'd got out of every tight corner in every game he'd ever played by simply being somewhere else before the trouble arrived, did the thing his whole body told him to do. He ran. Left.

It came round after him.

He'd *read* it. He'd read *don't dodge sideways*. It didn't matter. His legs did it anyway, because his legs had been dodging sideways for twenty-four years. The ram bent its charge round after him like a dog going round a corner, and he went faster, and it went faster, and he was running out of Fold.

"*Here!*" Salty shouted. "*Tiger! Here! Bring it here!*"

Tiger looked. Salty was standing in the middle of the open grass with nothing behind him. No stone. Nothing. Just Salty, feet planted, greatsword held flat across his chest. And behind Salty, Yan, with both hands out and cupped round nothing, his face already going grey.

*Have something it can hit instead*, the guide had said.

Salty was the something.

Tiger ran straight at him. At the last possible second, when he could see the whites of Salty's eyes, he went *past* him. Not left, not right. Just past, so close their shoulders nearly touched. And the Bellwether, curving after him at full speed, came round the corner of its charge and found, instead of Tiger, a short, broad, extremely stubborn man holding a sword like a door.

It hit Salty.

Tiger heard it. He'd hear it for the rest of his life. A sound like a car hitting a wall. He spun round, sure he'd see Salty in the air, Salty in the gorse, Salty at nine percent.

Salty was standing there.

His boots had dug two long furrows in the cropped grass, a metre and a half back from where he'd started. His arms were shaking. The greatsword was bent, actually *bent*, a long shallow curve in the middle of the blade. And the Bellwether stood against it with its head down and its horns locked on the steel, pushing, pushing, and not moving him another centimetre.

Behind him, Yan was on his knees in the grass with his hands cupped and white to the wrist.

[ Salty · HP 51% · Sheltered ]

"*Now,*" said Salty, through his teeth, to nobody in particular. "*Somebody. Now.*"

Wendy's arrow went into the ram's left eye.

Cursiffe's dart of force went into the right one.

And Tiger, who'd already turned round, who was already moving, went in under the ram's lowered head, under the locked horns, under the bent sword. He put both knives under its jaw where the wool was thin.

***

They sat in a row against the split stone afterwards, the five of them, like people on a bench at a bus stop.

Nobody said anything for a while. Yan had his hands wrapped round Salty's iron box, with a coal in it from the little fire Cursiffe had made with a flint and a great deal of patience, and his fingers were slowly going from white back to pink. Salty was looking at his bent greatsword across his knees with an expression of deep, technical interest.

"It held," he said. "It bent. But it held."

"You held," said Yan.

"The sword held. I just stood there." Salty ran his thumb along the curve. "I'll straighten it. Ulla'll show me. Maybe I'll make a better one."

"You'll make a better one," said Wendy, leaning on his other side. She was looking at him with the look again. The one from three days ago, over the wall. But less frightened now. Prouder, maybe.

[ Floor Guardian defeated · Suzuyun's party ]

"Second," said Cursiffe, reading it. "We're the second party."

"One stun," said Tiger. He couldn't stop saying it. "*One.*"

"You ran sideways," said Wendy.

"I *know* I ran sideways."

"The guide said *don't run sideways*."

"I know what the guide said, Wendy. My legs didn't read it." He tipped his head back against the cold stone and laughed. "But I brought it to Salty. That was good, right? That was good."

"That was very good," said Yan, in his slow deep voice, and Tiger felt it go through him like sunlight.

Cursiffe had his little book out. He was writing in it, as he'd been writing in it since the first morning, small and neat, with the stub of pencil he'd talked off Quill. Tiger leaned over to look, because he'd never been able to not look at things.

*Day 6. Bellwether. Strap in one stun (Tiger). Salty held the curve. Yan's hands: white to the wrist, four minutes. Eyes: Wendy left, me right.*

And under that, in the same small neat hand:

*Second party to clear Floor 1. The last two picks were in it.*

Tiger looked at that line for a while.

"You wrote that down," he said.

"I write everything down." Cursiffe didn't look up. "I told you. I'll know which bits matter later." He finished the line with a small full stop and closed the book. "I suspect that one will."

Tiger didn't say anything. He found he couldn't. He bumped Cursiffe's shoulder with his own instead, the way Salty did, and Cursiffe, who did not seem like a man who was bumped very often, adjusted his glasses and allowed it.

***

The stairs were a hundred and twelve steps, and Tiger ran up all of them, because he could.

He hadn't meant to count. He counted anyway. He was at the top long before the others, hanging on to the wooden door in the dark with his heart going like a drum, grinning at nothing, listening to the four of them come up below him. Salty's heavy boots, Wendy's light ones, Cursiffe's measured tread, and Yan's long slow stride, with the white snake round his neck.

"How many?" said Cursiffe, arriving.

"Hundred and twelve."

"Hm. Odd number."

"Odd is good," said Tiger. "Odd's interesting." And he opened the door.

The cold hit him like a slap.

Not a bad cold. A clean one, sharp and dry, that went straight up his nose and made his eyes water. Wind came through the door with it, smelling of pine sap and snow and something like iron. And beyond the door, under a low grey sky that wasn't a sky, there was a whole new world, white and dark green and very, very big.

Floor 2.

It was a forest. A mountain forest, pines going up and up and up on every side, black-green and heavy with snow. A long valley went away below them, white, with a frozen river winding through it like a silver ribbon. At the bottom of the valley, where the river widened, there was a town: wooden this time, not stone, all steep roofs and smoke and a stockade of sharpened logs round it like a fence of teeth.

It was snowing. Very gently, very slowly, big soft flakes drifting down out of the grey from a ceiling too high to see. Tiger put his head back and caught one on his tongue.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's real snow."

"That's a real *roof*," said Cursiffe, looking up with his head tipped back, "that's *snowing*." He sounded faintly offended by it, as if the Spire had broken a rule of good taste.

"Larchwick," said Yan, reading a small window that had opened at the edge of his vision. "The town. It's called Larchwick."

"We should go down," said Wendy. She had her arms wrapped round herself; she was shivering in her navy cardigan. "Before we all freeze to death on our first day on a new floor. That would be embarrassing."

"Yeah," said Tiger. "Yeah. You go down." He was already looking along the treeline. "I'll scout. I'll catch up. I'm fast."

"*Tiger.*"

"I'm a *Ranger*, Wendy. It's my *job*."

"That's what Calvin said."

"Calvin went up a valley on his own. I'm going along a treeline. Three hundred metres. Within shouting distance." He grinned. "I promise. Trust me."

Yan looked at him for a long moment.

"Three hundred metres," he said.

"Three hundred metres."

"And if anything moves that isn't you, you come straight back."

"Straight back," said Tiger, and went.

***

It wasn't three hundred metres. It was maybe four hundred. He'd tell Wendy three hundred later, and she wouldn't believe him.

He went along the edge of the pines where the snow was thinner, under the heavy branches, moving quickly and lightly and enjoying it. His new legs liked snow. They liked everything. And about four hundred metres along, under the biggest pine he'd seen yet, a great old tree with branches like a roof that kept the snow off the ground underneath, he found the camp.

It wasn't much. A ring of stones. A heap of grey ash in the middle, cold, with a crust of frost on it. A flat stone beside it, blackened with soot on one side, where someone had set a pot. A patch of flattened pine needles under the lowest branches, about the length of a person, where someone had slept. It was a few days old, he thought. The ash was completely cold, and frost had grown over everything in thin white feathers.

And in the snow by the ring of stones, a little heap of onion skins.

Tiger crouched down and looked at them.

They were wild onions, he thought. Small ones, the kind that might grow in a mountain forest under the snow. But the skins weren't the scraps you got when you tore an onion open. They'd been peeled off whole. Each one in a single papery piece, top and tail cut clean, the cut run down one side. Somebody had sat here by a fire in the snow, peeling wild onions very carefully, the right way, the way you'd only peel them if somebody had once taught you and you'd never forgotten.

He picked one up. It was frozen stiff. It crackled.

*The onion boy*, Mother Crane had said, at the long table on the first night. *Peeled them right. Which nobody does.*

Tiger looked at the frozen onion skin in his hand for a long time.

He'd known Ice for less than a year. He'd thought he was a cool dude. Funny, quick, good in a fight, always ready with a joke. He'd played with him maybe a dozen times. He didn't know him well enough to say *that's him* the way Gabe did, or Yan, with their whole chests. But he knew what a cold camp in the snow looked like. He'd built and lost a lot of houses in his life, in a lot of games. And this was what a camp looked like when somebody had slept there on their own and moved on before dawn, and hadn't looked back, because they couldn't afford to.

He put the onion skin in his pocket.

Then he opened World.

[ World · Tiger: found a camp on floor 2. east of the stair, under a big pine. cold, few days old. somebody peeled onions here. properly. ]

He hesitated. Then he typed one more line, and sent it before he could think better of it.

[ World · Tiger: hey voyager. or whoever. if you see this. you peel onions really well. ]

He waited. Nothing. Nothing in World, nothing in whispers.

A second later, a line came from someone else.

[ World · Gabe: where exactly ]

Tiger grinned, a bit sadly, and started typing directions.
