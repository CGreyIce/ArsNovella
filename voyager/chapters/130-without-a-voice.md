The first thing Niko noticed about floor 12 was that he couldn't hear himself breathe.

He'd come up the four hundred and twelve steps behind Chris and in front of Maigo, the way his party always climbed, and stood on the top step in the dark while Tiger waited at the door with his hand on it. And then Tiger had opened it, and the light had come in, white, so white that Niko shut his eyes, and the thirty-five of them behind Tiger had come out into it one at a time, blinking, onto a low hump of grey rock.

And it was quiet.

Not quiet like the sea at night. Not quiet like the Bastion's snow, which had a hiss in it if you listened. Quiet like the inside of a closed book. Niko stood on the rock with his hand over his eyes and listened, the way he'd learned to listen to Hessa's sword, and there was nothing. No wind. No water. No birds. Nothing at all, from anywhere, as far as the world went.

He breathed in. He couldn't hear it.

"Oh, I don't like this," said Calvin, beside him.

It came out wrong. Niko heard it, but only just, as if Calvin were speaking from inside a cupboard. The words came out of Calvin's mouth and fell down onto the rock between them and lay there. They didn't go anywhere. They didn't bounce off anything. There was nothing for them to bounce off.

"*Hello?*" said Calvin. Louder.

It went about as far as Niko's elbow.

"Calvin," said Deston. Very low. "Don't."

Niko took his hand away from his eyes.

It was white. All of it. Everything. From the foot of the grey rock they stood on, out and out, flat, in every direction, white, to the end of the sky. Flat like a table. Flat like a frozen lake, except that it wasn't frozen and it wasn't a lake. It glittered. The sun was on it, high, and the white threw the light straight back up into your face, so that you had to squint, and even squinting it hurt.

Under his boots, at the foot of the rock, where the white began, there was a crust. Like the top of a loaf. He crouched and touched it. Hard. Rough. Grainy. He put his fingertip to his tongue.

Salt.

The window came up in front of his eyes. In front of everyone's.

***

Ice told them on the rock.

He did it the way he'd done it on the ledge on floor 11, because that was the rule now, Higu's rule. *Tell us what you remember, and we'll find out what's true.* Except that on the ledge he'd stood up and talked and everybody had heard. Here, they had to come in close. All thirty-five of them, packed round him on the grey hump of rock in a tight ring, so close Niko could feel Maigo's bark against his shoulder. Ice talked low. You had to lean in.

Higu sat at his feet with the rock in her lap.

She'd carried it up. Four hundred and twelve steps, in her pack, a flat grey stone off the Stair on floor 11 with a chalk line down the middle. Hako had carried it for the last hundred because her legs had gone. She'd wiped the floor-11 side clean at the top of the stair with her sleeve, and written the new headings, and now she sat with the chalk.

"It's called the Salt," said Ice. "It was a sea. A long time ago. It dried. This is what's left."

He looked out at it. He had the Galeward Helm on, the closed white helm from floor 5 with the slit visor, for the glare, and Niko couldn't see his face. Only the slit. It made him sound further away than he was.

"There's a town on the edge," said Ice. "A day south. Seep. On a big rock like this one. The rakers live there. They cut salt and sell it, and they dig wells, and they sell the water. We bought water from them." A pause. "Then west. Thirty days. Across the middle. There's a ship."

"A *ship*?" said Hyvred.

"Standing up," said Ice. "In the salt. On its keel, like it's still floating. Bigger than the *Patience*. A thousand years old, the rakers said. It was in the sea when the sea was here, and the sea went, and it stayed. They call it the Mast." He stopped. "The stair's in its hold."

Higu wrote. *SEEP. 1 DAY S. RAKERS. WELLS. WATER.* And under it: *30 DAYS W. THE MAST. A SHIP. STAIR IN THE HOLD.*

"And there's something under the crust," said Ice.

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know what they are," said Ice. "We never saw one properly. Not all of one. They live under the salt. And they come up when you're loud." He turned the slit of his visor down to the white, at the foot of the rock. "Sound doesn't go anywhere up here. You've heard. It doesn't carry. But it goes *down*. Into the salt. And they hear it." He was quiet for a moment. "We found that out on the first night."

He didn't say how. Niko waited for him to say how, and he didn't.

"So," said Ice. "Walk soft. Don't shout. Don't drop things. Don't cast out loud." The visor turned, slowly, round the ring of faces, and stopped, for half a second, on Niko. "Any of it."

Higu wrote: *UNDER THE CRUST. LOUD = THEY COME UP.*

Then she put the chalk down and looked at the right-hand column, which was empty, and said, very low, "We'll find out."

***

They found out some of it before they'd even got off the rock.

Calvin's arms went red first. He'd pushed his sleeves up on the stair, because it had been warm in the dark, and by the time Ice had finished talking the skin on the backs of his forearms had gone the colour of a cooked crab. The white threw the sun back up at you from underneath. There was no shade. Not anywhere. Not one tree, not one rock taller than a man, not one cloud.

"It's fine," said Calvin. "It's nothing. It's a sunburn."

"Hold still," said Rachael.

She put her hands on his arms, flat, the way she did. Niko saw the heal go in. The faint warmth, the redness fading out of Calvin's skin from the middle outwards, like a stain lifting out of cloth.

And then he saw Rachael's face.

It had gone grey. Not white. Grey. She took her hands off Calvin's arms and looked at them, palms up, the way she'd looked at them over Corran on the deck. Then she sat down. Quite suddenly. On the rock. With her hands in her lap.

"Rach," said Deston.

"I'm fine," said Rachael. "Give me a minute."

"Rach."

"It came out of me," said Rachael.

Everybody went still.

She looked up at them. She didn't look frightened. She looked the way she looked when she was working something out. "That's never happened. Not with a heal. Burden's different. Burden's me *taking* something, on purpose. A heal always came from somewhere else. From the light. I just—" She opened and closed her hands. "I just open the door. And it goes through. That's all I do." She looked at Calvin's arms. Then at her own. "That one came out of *me*. I felt it go. Like giving blood."

Kei crouched down beside her. He put two fingers on her wrist and counted, under his breath, the way he did.

"Fast," he said. "Not bad. Fast." He looked at Maigo. "Try something. Anything. Small."

Maigo didn't say anything. He took off his left shoe, the way he did, and put his root-foot down on the salt at the foot of the rock, and closed his eyes.

Niko watched his toes. On floor 9 they'd gone soft, and then into fine white roots, and gone down into the ground like water going into sand.

They didn't go down.

They went soft. And stopped. On the crust. And curled back. Like a hand that had touched something hot.

Maigo opened his eyes.

"There's nothing," he said. Very quietly. He looked down at his foot on the white. "There's nothing *in* it. It's not even dead. Dead's something. Dead's still got things in it. This is—" He stopped. "It's just salt. All the way down. It doesn't give anything back."

He took his foot off the crust. The soft pale toes were crusted white at the tips. He brushed them with his hand, and Niko saw him wince.

And a leaf came off his antlers.

One. A small one, from near the top, on the left. It came off and fell, turning, and landed on the white beside his foot, and lay there. The edges of it had gone brown. Curled. Dry.

Maigo looked at it for a long time.

"The air's got salt in it," said Kei. Very quietly.

"Yeah," said Maigo.

Higu picked up the chalk, and on the right-hand side, the empty side, where nothing had been written yet, she wrote: *HEALING COMES OUT OF THE HEALER.* And underlined it twice. And then, under it: *NOTHING GROWS. (TRUE.)*

***

The book got warm on the way to Seep.

Niko felt it in his pack. A day's walk south along the edge of the white, single file, softly, everybody walking the way Ice had told them, like walking past a sleeping baby. Thirty-six people trying not to make a sound. You'd think it would be funny. It wasn't. It was the longest day Niko had ever walked, with the sun coming up off the salt into his face and the water in his skin going somewhere he couldn't get it back from, and nobody talking, and the crust crunching very softly under thirty-six pairs of boots, and every crunch going down.

Somewhere in the afternoon, he felt the warmth through the pack against his back.

He knew what it was. He'd been waiting for it for two hundred days. Since a round room on Gull Island with gulls screaming outside and an old woman with one milky eye putting a thin faded red book in his hands. *Two more. Not on this floor. Not on the next one, I shouldn't think. The book'll tell you where, when you're near enough.*

He didn't take it out. Not on the salt. You didn't drop things on the salt.

He took it out at Seep.

***

Seep was a rock.

The same grey rock as the one by the door, but bigger. A long low island of it, standing up a little out of the white, with a town on top. Not much of a town. Low square houses built out of blocks of salt, grey-white, like sugar cubes, with flat roofs and tiny windows and doors that faced away from the sun. A few dozen of them. A wall round the edge of the rock, low, also salt. And in the middle, a wooden frame over a hole, with a rope and a bucket. The well.

The rakers came to the wall to watch them come. Twenty or thirty of them. Thin, brown, sun-dark people with cloths wound round their heads and over their mouths so only their eyes showed. Pale eyes. All of them squinting. They stood along the wall and watched the thirty-six come up the edge of the rock, single file, softly, and didn't say a word.

One of them raised a hand. And moved it.

Not a wave. The fingers moved. Quick and precise. A shape, and another shape, and a third. And then the hand went down.

"Oh," said Bee, very quietly. "Oh, they *sign*."

They did. All of them. Out on the salt, on the edge of the rock, at the wall, the rakers talked with their hands. Never with their mouths. Niko watched them do it all that first evening, along the wall, in the doorways, at the well, quick and silent, a whole town talking in a language he'd never seen. He saw a mother sign something sharp at a child, and the child sign something back, and the mother's eyes crinkle above her mouth-cloth in a way that meant she was trying not to laugh.

Inside, they talked.

That was the rule, Niko learned, when the headwoman brought them in. You talked inside. On the rock, in the houses, with the doors shut. The rock didn't listen. The salt did.

"Stone's deaf," said the headwoman. Her name was Tullah. She'd taken her mouth-cloth down indoors and her face was thin and brown and deeply lined from squinting, and her voice, when it came, was hoarse, as if she didn't use it much. "Wood's deaf. Salt hears. Out there, you keep your mouth shut and you keep your feet soft, and you live." She looked round at them, packed into the biggest of the salt houses on benches along the walls. "Thirty-six. That's a lot of feet."

"We'll be soft," said Deston.

"You'll have to be." Tullah looked at Ice, in his white helm, by the door. "Which way?"

"West," said Ice. "To the Mast."

Tullah was quiet for a long time.

"Thirty days," she said. "Across the middle. Nothing out there. No rock. No well. Nothing to stand on that doesn't listen." She looked at the thirty-six. "You'll want water."

"We'll buy it," said Deston. "Whatever you can sell us."

"That's the trouble," said Tullah.

She took them out to the well. At dusk, with the sun going down red into the white in the west and the whole flat world turning pink and then grey. She let the bucket down. Niko counted. A long time. A very long time, before the rope went slack. And then she pulled it up, hand over hand, and it came up a quarter full.

"Three years," said Tullah. "It's been going down. A hand every summer. My mother's mother dug it, and it never went down in her life, and it never went down in my mother's, and it's going down in mine." She tipped the bucket into a stone trough, carefully, every drop. "We've got the other one at the north end, and it's the same. We can sell you some. We can't sell you thirty days. Not for thirty-six. Not for six."

Nobody said anything.

Ice was standing at the back. By the wall. Niko looked at him. He'd taken the helm off for the dusk, and his face was very still.

*We bought water from them.*

Higu, beside Niko, took the rock out of her pack. On the right-hand side, under the other things, she wrote: *SEEP. WELLS LOW. 3 YEARS.* And then, after a moment: *10 DAYS' WATER. MAYBE.*

She didn't look at Ice when she wrote it. Nobody did.

Niko opened the red book that night.

He did it in the corner of the salt house where his party had put their bedrolls, by a little oil lamp the rakers had given them, with Chris asleep beside him and Winter asleep beyond Chris and Ina awake against the wall with his mask in his lap. Maigo was outside. Maigo had gone out onto the rock and was standing on bare stone with his shoe off and his eyes shut, the way he'd stood every night on every floor since he could remember, and Niko didn't want to think about why.

He opened it on his knees.

He knew it by heart. He'd read it every night for two hundred days, the way you read the same book over and over when you're waiting for something. Thin, faded red covers, no title. Inside, a hundred or so pages of old brown ink in a small, square, careful hand, spells mostly, sword-forms, with little drawings in the margins of hands on hilts. And at the back, about thirty pages blank.

They weren't blank.

The first of them had writing on it.

He sat and looked at it for a long time. The same brown ink as the rest, but a different hand. Younger. Quicker. A little untidy, the letters leaning forward as if they were in a hurry. He knew that hand. He'd seen it on Gull Island, on a slate by Hessa's door, where she'd chalked the price of gull eggs. Older there. Shakier. But the same.

*Hessa*, he thought. *When she was my age.*

There was a drawing. A sword, upright, carefully done, the blade long and straight. And down the blade, along its whole length, from the hilt nearly to the point, a line of writing so small he had to hold the lamp right up to it to see that it was writing at all.

Under the drawing, five words.

*Here they hear you. So—*

And then a gap, as if she'd stopped, and thought, and then gone on. Smaller. Neater.

*—read where only it can.*

The window came up.

He sat there with the book on his knees and the window in front of his eyes and didn't move.

"Niko," said Ina. Very softly, from the wall.

"It's the next one," said Niko.

Ina didn't ask what it said. Ina never asked anything. He just looked at Niko's face for a long moment with his calm dark eyes, and nodded, and went back to looking at his mask.

*Read without a voice.*

Niko looked down at his sword, lying beside his bedroll. The plain knight's sword he'd bought in Hearthstead on the first day. Salty had re-edged it twice and told him both times to get a better one, and he'd said *later*, both times, and not meant it. The dull place near the hilt where his thumb went.

Every spell he knew, he read aloud. That was how it worked. That was how Hessa had shown him. Sword in one hand, book in the other, and you read the spell *to* it, out loud, and the blade listened, and lit. *Don't push. Read. To it.* He'd done it every day for two hundred days. On every floor. In the snow on the fifth wall, under his breath, into the wind. He'd never once lit the blade without saying something.

*Here they hear you.*

He thought about Ice, on the rock, turning the slit of his visor round the ring and stopping on him for half a second. *Don't cast out loud. Any of it.*

He shut the book.

***

He tried it that night.

He knew he shouldn't. He knew it the whole time he was doing it. He'd been told for as long as he could remember that he did things too early. Chris had said it, and Winter, and Ice, on a floor 5 island via an old woman on a rock, *always does things too early and then gets them right later, by yourself, patiently*. He knew. It didn't stop him. It never had.

He went out onto the rock.

Not far. Just to the wall at the west end of it, where the grey stone ran down under the white and the salt began. Maigo was at the other end, a dark shape against the stars with his antlers up, standing on stone. Nobody else. The rakers were in their houses. The thirty-six were asleep, or near enough.

He sat down on the wall with his legs over the salt side and the sword across his knees.

He didn't open the book. He knew the spell. The first one, the one with the little picture of a flame, the one he'd read to the sword in the doorway of the inn in Tether. He put his hand on the hilt. He put his thumb on the dull place.

And he read it. In his head. Not moving his lips. The way you read when you're reading to yourself.

Nothing.

The blade lay across his knees, plain grey steel in the starlight.

He tried again. Slower. Every word. Clear and careful in his head. Nothing. Again. He frowned, and pressed his thumb down on the dull place, and thought the words harder, which was stupid, he knew it was stupid, you couldn't think words *harder*.

Nothing.

*Don't push.*

He let out a breath. And without meaning to, without deciding, the way you'd hum without knowing you were humming, his lips moved. Just the first word. Barely a breath. Not even a whisper.

The blade lit. A thin line of orange, from the hilt, an inch up the steel.

And the wall under him shivered.

Not the wall. The salt. The white crust at the foot of the wall, a metre below his dangling boots. It shivered the way a pond's skin shivers when something moves under it. And then it went still.

Niko went still.

He sat on the wall with the orange line dying on his blade and his heart going so hard he could feel it in his ears. Not hear it. Feel it. There was nothing to hear.

The crust bulged.

Ten metres out. A long low swelling in the white, like a mole-run in a lawn, coming toward the wall. Quick. Straight. Toward him.

And another one. To the left. And a third.

He got his legs back over the wall. He did it without a sound, and it didn't matter. They'd heard. They'd heard one breath of one word, a metre down through solid salt, and they were coming.

The first one came up out of the crust at the foot of the wall like a swimmer coming up for air.

The salt broke in plates. Crust, shattering upward. And out of it, a head. Flat. Wide. Like a shovel. White. Not salt-white. A wet, soft, sickly white, like the underside of something that had never seen the sun, with salt crystals stuck all over it like frost. No eyes. None. Just a wide flat head the width of a cart, and a split all the way across the front of it, and the split *opened*.

Sideways. The whole front of the head came apart, left and right, like two hands opening, and inside it was lined with needles. Hundreds. Long, thin, white, packed close, like the inside of a sea urchin turned out.

It came up the wall.

Niko had his sword up. Unlit. He didn't think. He hit it. Straight down onto the flat of the head, as hard as he'd ever hit anything, and the blade bit, and stuck, and the head snapped round toward him, toward the sound of steel going in, and the needles came at his face.

Something hit it from the side.

A shield. Chris's. The whole weight of Chris behind it, out of nowhere, slamming into the side of the flat head and knocking it sideways off the wall, back down onto the crust, and Chris going down on one knee on the top of the wall with his shield up and his longsword out and his face, in the starlight, absolutely furious.

"*Niko*," said Chris. Not a whisper. Not a shout. A word, flat, hitting the stone between them.

"I know," said Niko.

"You *know*—"

The second one came up the wall to their left.

It got Niko's leg.

He felt it close. The needles, through his boot, through the leather, into his calf, a hundred of them at once, cold, and then hot, and then nothing. It didn't bite. It held. And pulled. Back, toward the salt, and he went down flat on the top of the wall with his leg going over the edge and his fingers on the stone, and he couldn't hold, there was nothing to hold—

And then it froze.

White. From the needles back. A rush of white frost running back along the flat head and down into the crust, freezing the soft wet skin into something like glass, and the pull stopped. Just stopped. Niko lay on the wall with his leg in the mouth of a thing that had turned to ice.

There was an arrow standing in the top of its head. Pale blue. Frosted.

Winter was on the roof of the nearest house. Barefoot. In her nightshirt. With her silver hair everywhere and her bow up and another arrow on the string, and her black horns against the stars.

She didn't say anything. She shot again. The third one, coming up the wall at Chris, took a frost arrow in the split of its face and stopped, halfway up, with its needles out, and went white.

The first one, the one Chris had knocked down, was coming back up.

Chris was ready. Niko saw him see it. Chris had a skill he called Breakpoint. *Everything stops. Look at all of it.* He looked at something, and he found the place where it would break. He'd found the gap under Greyjaw's plate on floor 9 that nobody had found in a hundred years. He looked at the flat white head coming up the wall, and Niko saw his eyes go to a place just behind it, where the head met the long pale body, and stop.

"*There*," said Chris.

And put his longsword into it.

Behind the head. Down. Where the white skin went thin over something soft. The sword went in to the hilt, and the thing shuddered all along its length, under the crust, a long ripple going out through the salt like a wave, and went limp.

Niko tried to get up. His leg wasn't there. He looked down at it. The frozen thing still had it. He couldn't feel anything below the knee.

"Don't move," said Winter. From the roof. "I'll have to break it. Don't move."

"I'm not moving."

"You're *always* moving."

She came down off the roof and across the rock, barefoot, silent, and knelt on the wall beside him, and took her knife out, and began, very carefully, to crack the frozen mouth open one needle at a time.

***

Maigo healed him.

Niko said not to. He said it twice. He said it a third time, lying on the bench in the salt house with his leg on a folded blanket and the blood coming through the bandage Kei had put on, and a dozen people round him, and Rachael on the next bench still grey, still sitting with her hands in her lap.

"I'm not asking," said Maigo.

"Maigo, the *leaves*—"

"I know about the leaves," said Maigo.

He put his hands on Niko's leg.

Niko felt it go in. He'd felt Maigo's heals on every floor, the way they always felt, like something growing. Like a root going down. Warm and green and slow, filling things up from underneath. This one went in the same way.

It just came from closer.

Niko watched it come. He couldn't not. He lay on the bench and watched Maigo's face, the bark on his cheeks, the antlers, the dark glossy leaves that had doubled on floor 9 in the summer and that he'd been so proud of, so pleased with, the way you're pleased with a new coat. He watched them while the heal went in.

Three of them went brown.

At the edges first. Then the whole leaf. Like watching a year go past in a breath. Brown, then curled, then dry. And then they came off. One. Two. Three. And fell, turning, and lay on the floor of the salt house by Maigo's feet.

Maigo took his hands away.

"There," he said. His voice was a bit hoarse. "That's the needles out. And the muscle. Kei can do the rest with his poultice. It'll hurt for a week."

"Maigo," said Niko.

"Don't," said Maigo. He was looking at the leaves on the floor. "Don't say sorry. You'd have done it. You'd have done it for me." He bent down, slowly, and picked the three leaves up, and held them in his hand. "They'll grow back."

"Where from?" said Kei. Quietly.

Maigo didn't answer that.

Nobody did.

***

Ice came in late.

He'd been out on the salt. Alone. With Thea a long way behind him, and Xola, the way it always was now, the rule. Hunting, at night, softly. Niko didn't know how you hunted things that came up at sound, alone, in the dark, on salt. He didn't want to know. Ice came in with salt on his boots and his armour and the white helm under his arm, and Thea came in behind him, and they stopped in the door.

Ice looked at Niko on the bench. At the bandage. At Maigo, sitting on the floor against the wall with three dry leaves in his hand.

His face did something.

"Mashi," he said.

Nobody else called him that. Nobody else ever had. Since Niko was seventeen, for eight years, in every call, and then not at all, for two hundred days, because Ice had been careful. Hessa had said. *He was very careful not to say it again.*

He said it now. Very softly. From the door.

"You always try it the first night," said Ice.

He didn't say anything else. He came across the room and crouched by the bench and looked at the bandage for a moment, and then put his hand on Niko's shoulder, briefly, the way you'd touch somebody to make sure they were really there, and got up, and went to sit by Maigo.

Niko lay on the bench.

*Always.*

He'd heard it. He'd heard Ice hear himself say it. He saw Ice's shoulders go tight, sitting down by Maigo. He saw Thea, at the door, close her eyes for one second, and open them.

He didn't ask.

He'd never asked. He'd thought about it, a great deal, for two hundred days, about an old woman on a rock saying *he looked at me like a man who'd been to my funeral*, about a nosebleed that only six people had seen. He'd thought about it, and then he'd decided something, on Gull Island, walking back across the bridges with the red book under his arm, and he'd never changed his mind.

*He asked her to wait. For me.* That was all he needed to know about Ice. Whatever else there was, that came first, and it was enough.

He lay on the bench and looked at the ceiling of the salt house, and his leg hurt, and he thought, *the first night. Always the first night.* And then, much more quietly: *so that's what I'm like.*

He found he didn't mind.

He just had to be patient about it.

***

He didn't try again for three days.

He watched instead. That was the thing he'd learned on Gull Island, that Hessa had taught him with forty failures and a real sword. Before you do a thing, look at it. Look at it for longer than you think you need to. He stayed in Seep with his leg on a blanket while the others haggled with Tullah over water and sleds, and he watched the rakers.

They worked on the salt every day. Out past the wall, in teams, cutting. Long flat saws with handles at both ends, and two rakers on each, and the saw going in and out through the crust with a soft, steady, grinding hush that Niko could only just hear from the wall and that, Tullah said, didn't go down. "It's a long sound," she said. "Same all the time. They don't come up for long sounds. They come up for *new* ones."

They signed to each other as they worked. Always signing. Never a word.

And on the second day he noticed the saws.

He'd been watching one team for an hour. An old raker and a young one, cutting a block. And the old one stopped, at the end of a stroke, and put the saw down on the crust, and ran her thumb along the back of the blade. Slowly. From one handle to the other. With her eyes shut. Like somebody reading.

Then she picked it up and went on.

He asked Tullah that evening. Inside. With the door shut.

"Her saw," said Tullah. She looked at him oddly. "It's got her mother's words on it. And her mother's mother's. Down the back." She shrugged. "We can't say things out there. So we write them. On the things we hold." She held up her own hand, palm out, and Niko saw, for the first time, that there were tiny marks pricked into the skin of her palm, blue-black, a row of them. "That's my daughter's name. So I can say it to her on the salt."

Niko looked at her palm for a long time.

*Read where only it can.*

He thought about Hessa's sword on the wall of the round room on Gull Island. The long one, above the three books. With writing down the blade. He'd looked at it, that day. He'd thought it was decoration.

He thought about the drawing in the red book. The sword. The line of writing so small he'd had to hold the lamp up to it.

He thought about his thumb. On the dull place near the hilt. Where it had always gone, without thinking, for three hundred days.

"Tullah," he said. "Have you got something I could write on steel with?"

***

Gabe lent him the punch.

Gabe had a set. Of course he did. He carried them in a roll of leather at the bottom of his pack, from the Breath, a dozen of them, from big to tiny. He unrolled it on the bench in the salt house and looked at it, and then at Niko, and then took out the smallest. A thin steel rod with a point like a needle.

"This one," said Gabe. "It's the one I did the inside of Hearthwall with." He held it out. "You don't hit it. Not hard. You lean on it. And tap. A hundred little taps. Every letter. It takes ages." He looked at Niko. "Mate, it takes *ages*."

"I know," said Niko.

"You're not good at ages."

"I know," said Niko. "I'm going to try anyway."

Gabe looked at him for a long time. Then he took a small hammer out of the roll, too, and put it in Niko's hand on top of the punch, and closed Niko's fingers round both.

"Bring them back," he said. "And don't do it on the salt."

So he didn't.

He did it on the rock. In the salt house, at night, by the rakers' oil lamp, with the sword across his knees and the red book open beside him at the page with Hessa's drawing. One letter a night, at first. Then two. Lean, and tap. Lean, and tap. A hundred little taps for every letter, the way Gabe said. The first line of the first spell, the one with the little picture of a flame, down the flat of the blade, starting at the hilt. Starting at the dull place where his thumb went.

It took him four nights to do one line.

He didn't hurry it. He wanted to. Every night he wanted to. Every night some part of him that had been in a hurry his whole life said *just do the rest of the line, just finish it, just try it now*, and every night he put the punch down after two letters and wrapped the sword up and went to sleep.

*Always does things too early. And then gets them right later. By yourself. Patiently.*

He'd heard that from Hessa, who'd heard it from Ice. On a rock. Before Niko had even got there.

*Then I'll get it right later*, he thought, on the fourth night, and put the punch down, and looked at the blade.

The line ran from the dull place near the hilt about a hand's length up the steel. Small square letters, punched into the grey, so small you could hardly see them. Like the line on Hessa's drawing. You couldn't really read it with your eyes, by lamplight. It was too small, and the steel too grey.

He put his thumb on it.

On the first letter. In the dull place. And moved his thumb, slowly, along the line, the way the old raker had moved hers along the back of her saw.

He could feel them. Every letter. Tiny dents in the steel under the pad of his thumb, the shapes of them, the edges. He could read it. With his thumb. With his eyes shut. The first line of the first spell, letter by letter, in the dark.

He didn't say anything. He didn't move his lips.

He just read it. To it. With his hand.

And the blade lit.

Orange. From the inside. From the dull place up, along the line, and on, past the end of the line, the whole length of the steel, steady, warm, the way it had lit on Gull Island when Hessa had stood back and said *took you less time than it took me*. No sound. No breath. No blood.

The salt house was very quiet. Niko sat with his thumb on the hilt and his sword lit across his knees and couldn't hear anything at all.

Across the room, Ina was awake against the wall, with his mask in his lap. Looking at the light.

He didn't say anything. He smiled. A little.

Niko put his thumb on the line again, and read it again, and the blade went on burning, silently, for as long as he read.

***

They left Seep on the three hundred and twenty-fourth day.

With water. Some. Ten days, if they were careful, Tullah said, and they'd be careful because they'd have to be, and after ten days she didn't know. With sleds. Long low wooden ones, rakers' sleds, on runners of bone, that you pulled yourself, by a strap across your chest, because there was nothing on the Salt to pull them. Not a horse. Not a dog. Nothing grew, and nothing that ate what grew.

Six sleds. Water on four. Everything else on two. Six people pulling, in turns, softly. The runners made a long steady hush on the crust that didn't go down.

Tullah stood on the wall of Seep and watched them go. She didn't sign goodbye. She held up her hand, palm out, with her daughter's name pricked into it in blue-black. And then turned and went in.

West.

Into the white. Into the sun, as it went over, and down.

***

They came up on the second night.

Not at Niko. At a water cask.

Somebody on the second sled, Dennis, tired, at the end of his turn on the strap, let the sled stop too fast, and a cask shifted on it and knocked against another. Not loud. A dull wooden *tock*. The kind of sound you'd never hear anywhere else in the world.

Here, it went down.

And Niko felt it come back up. Through the soles of his boots. The crust shivering. All round. Not three this time.

"*Down*," hissed Chris. "*Everybody. Down. Don't move.*"

The thirty-six went down flat on the salt round the sleds in the dark.

Niko didn't. He stayed on one knee. He had his sword out. He hadn't meant to draw it. His body had. And in the starlight, ten metres out, he saw the crust start to bulge, the long low swellings coming in toward the sleds from three sides, from four, quick and straight, toward the sound that had stopped.

Five. Six.

They came up round the sleds like swimmers.

The salt broke in plates all round them, shattering up into the starlight. Flat white heads. The splits coming open, sideways, needles. They came up and *stopped*. Swaying. Heads lifted. Turning, slowly, left and right, the way a dog lifts its nose. Listening. They'd heard the *tock*, and then nothing, because thirty-six people were lying flat on the salt not breathing, and they didn't know where it had come from.

One of them was two metres from Hana. Hana was lying on her face with her crown off and her hand over Milli's mouth.

Niko put his thumb on the hilt.

On the dull place. On the line. He read it. With his hand, in the dark, not a breath, not a sound, letter by letter, the way he'd read it every night for three nights since the first time in the salt house.

The blade lit.

Orange. The whole length. In the dark, on the salt, among the sleds, a long steady line of fire, the only light for a mile.

Six flat white heads didn't turn toward it.

They couldn't. They didn't have eyes. They swayed on their long pale necks, listening, for a sound that wasn't there. And the light burned in the middle of them, a metre from the nearest one, and it didn't know.

Niko got up.

Softly. The way Ice had said. The way the rakers walked. He took one step, and another, on the crust, toward the head beside Hana, with the lit blade low in his right hand and his left hand flat out to the side the way Hessa had taught him, for balance. He took a third step, and put the blade into it. Behind the head. Where Chris had put his. Where the white skin went thin.

It went in like a hot knife into butter. It didn't make a sound. The fire went in with it.

The saltmouth shuddered, once, all along its length, and went down onto the crust and lay there, and smoked.

The other five turned. To the shudder. To the ripple it made going down through the salt. Toward Niko.

He was already gone. Two steps left, softly, the way Tiger moved. They came at where he'd been. One of them put its whole flat face into the crust where his feet had been, needles first, and he put the burning blade down through the back of its head while it was still looking, and it didn't make a sound either.

And then Winter was up on one knee by the third sled, with her bow, and shot, and the frost arrow went in silently, and a third one went white.

And Chris was up, and found the place, and put his sword in it. And Ina, beside the water sleds, pulled his mask down and put his hand flat on the salt, and white-blue fire went across the crust like spilled oil, silently, and the fourth one burned.

The fifth went down under the crust. Gone. Back to wherever they went.

The sixth was in front of Niko. He could see it. A metre away. Swaying. Listening, with its split face open and its needles out, at the place where its friends had died, at the faint hiss of Ina's fire, at nothing.

He read the line again. With his thumb. And the blade, which had been dimming, flared up bright, and he stepped in and cut, low, across the neck, and it fell.

He stood there.

On the salt, in the dark, among the sleds, with thirty-five people lying flat round him and a sword burning in his hand. He didn't move. He listened. Under his feet, the crust was still. Not shivering. Not anything.

He took his thumb off the line, and the fire went out of the steel, slowly, from the point down to the hilt.

And the window came up.
