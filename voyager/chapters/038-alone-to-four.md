Lodestar was a skill that only worked for other people, and Ice found that funny for two whole days.

He tested it the morning after the drowned tower, on his tussock, with the Fen quiet and empty of wisps all round him. He did what the window said. He stood, and let the warmth in his chest come up, and his hands went faintly amber in the daylight. He felt it go out from him in every direction like heat from a stove, a long way, across the reeds and the black water.

Nothing happened.

"It's working," said Alea. "I can feel it. It's going out a long way."

"Then why can't I feel it?"

"Because it's not for you." The fox looked at him from the tussock with her head on one side. "*Anyone who can see you can find the way.* It's for them. Whoever's behind you. Whoever's lost." A pause. "You're not lost, Ice. You know exactly where you're going. It doesn't do anything for you at all."

He stood there glowing faintly on a mud island in the middle of a swamp, with nobody for kilometres. Then he started laughing and couldn't stop.

"I spent six years," he said, when he could, "reading about this class. Every night. And the one skill that's *named after me*, the actual name of the class, is the one I can't use."

"You can use it."

"On *who*?"

Alea didn't answer that. She didn't need to. They both knew who.

***

He was stronger. That was the other thing. He could feel it everywhere, in everything he did.

Six levels had come out of the pot all at once, a week of held experience, and his body had grown into them overnight like a plant in the sun. His sword arm was faster. His shield arm held longer. The mirelings in the channels, which had nearly drowned him four days ago, couldn't pull him under any more; he just stood there in the black water while they tugged at his ankle, and then he hit them.

And Mend Self was different.

It wasn't standing in a light any more. It wasn't the ember in his palm. The ember was inside him, behind his breastbone, and when he got hurt now, he didn't have to put his hand on his chest and ask. It just *happened*. Slowly, steadily, warmly, from the inside out, the way a fire warms a room. Cuts closed while he was still fighting. Bruises faded while he walked.

"Paladin," said Alea, watching a heron's spear-cut on his forearm close up as they crossed a channel.

"Paladin," said Ice.

He didn't think he'd ever get tired of hearing it.

***

The Lanternjaw lived in the Black Mere, at the foot of the hill where the stair to floor 4 was. He went there on the sixteenth day.

The first time, it had taken the thirty-six of them three weeks to even find the Lanternjaw. It hid. It lived at the bottom of a great round pool of black water at the foot of a low green hill. The only thing it ever showed above the surface was its lure: a light on a long stalk, bobbing just above the water in the middle of the pool. A warm yellow light, like a lantern hung in a window. It looked exactly like the wisps, at first. Then you looked longer, and it looked like something better than the wisps. Like the thing the wisps had been trying to be all along.

Bee had walked into the pool after it on the first try. Salty had gone in after Bee. The Lanternjaw had come up underneath them both, a mouth the size of a cart, and only Hyvred's arrows in its eyes had made it let go. Hyvred was the only one of the thirty-six who'd been able to look at the lure and see what it really was: a wet grey stalk with a glowing sac on the end of it, growing out of the head of something enormous and hungry under the water.

That was the trick, the first time. Don't look at the lure. Look at Hyvred. Do what Hyvred says.

He didn't have Hyvred.

He had himself.

He stood at the edge of the Black Mere in the late afternoon with his sword drawn and the Vigil Shield on his arm. He looked at the lure.

It was bobbing in the middle of the pool, warm and yellow, a lantern in a window. And as he looked at it, it changed. It became a window. A lit window, at the end of a dark street. His mother's window. Then it became a white room, with thirty-six circles on the floor. Then it became a ward, a wall of rewritten light with a ponytail behind it, turning.

He looked at it.

And this time, it didn't pull. Not the way the wisps had, two nights ago, on the reed path. He looked at it, and it looked like everything he wanted. And it also looked, at the same time, underneath, like what it was. A wet grey stalk. A glowing sac. Something enormous and hungry in the dark water, waiting.

He could see both. A light that lied, and the truth under it.

*He is it.* He'd become the light. And it was very hard, it turned out, for a false light to fool someone who was already a true one.

"Ice?" said Alea, carefully.

"I'm fine," said Ice. "I can see it. I can see what it is."

He went down to the edge of the water, and raised his left hand, and held it up.

***

He didn't use Beacon. He didn't need to. He just let the warmth in his chest come up all the way, the way he'd let it on the drowned tower, until he was glowing. His hands, his arms, the whole of him, a faint steady amber in the dusk at the edge of the Black Mere.

The lure went still.

Then it turned. The glowing sac on its wet grey stalk turned, slowly, in the middle of the pool, toward him. Toward the light.

The Lanternjaw ate lights. That was what it was. That was what the lure was *for*. You hung up a false light, and things came to it, and you ate them. And now, standing at the edge of its pool, there was a light brighter and warmer and *truer* than anything it had ever hung up in the dark. Something it had never tasted in its life.

It wanted him.

The water in the middle of the Black Mere began to rise.

It came up out of the pool like a hill rising. It was grey, wet and warty and enormous, a body like a great frog crossed with something from the bottom of the sea. A mouth that went the whole width of its head, full of long thin teeth like a comb. Small blind eyes. And the lure on its stalk above them, swaying.

It came across the Black Mere toward him at a speed something that size shouldn't have had, the water heaving.

He set his feet at the edge of the pool and put the shield up.

***

The first time, it had taken thirty-six people three tries and a whole day. Hyvred, calling out where the real body was. Wendy's arrows in its eyes. Salty and Gabe holding its jaws apart with their shields while everyone else hit it. Calvin nearly swallowed. Rachael healing until she fell over.

This time it was one man with a shield, standing at the edge of the water, glowing.

It hit him like a wave. The great wet weight of it came up out of the pool and the mouth opened, a cave of grey teeth. He got the shield into the corner of the jaw and turned, the way he'd turned the Bellwether's horns, and the mouth closed on nothing. And he was inside the reach of its head, under its chin, where the lure couldn't see him.

He hit it.

He hit it the way he'd hit everything on four floors: point, low, under the jaw. Into the soft grey place where the warty skin was thinnest. It made a sound like a drain unblocking and reared back, and he went with it, up the slope of its chin. One hand on the shield, one on the sword, the Mend working in him from the inside, closing the cuts its teeth had left on his arm.

It thrashed. It rolled. It went under the black water and took him with it, and the light went out, and he was in the dark, in the cold, in the Lanternjaw's pool with the Lanternjaw. He held his breath, and counted. *One. Two.* On *three* it had to come up for air, and it did, and he came up with it.

"*Ice*," said Alea. "It's going to do the dark."

"I know."

The Lanternjaw reared up in the middle of the pool. And its lure, swaying on its stalk above its head, went out.

And so did everything else.

***

It wasn't night. It was worse than night. The light went out of the air itself, out of the painted sky, out of the water, the way it goes out of a room when somebody blows out the only candle. The whole Black Mere, the green hill behind it, the reeds, everything, was gone into a dark so total that he couldn't see his own hand.

The first time, this was where it had nearly ended. Thirty-six people in the pitch black, up to their waists in the Lanternjaw's pool, unable to see it, or each other, or anything. Calling out. Panicking. Going under. Hyvred was the only one who could see in it at all, and she'd been screaming directions for three solid minutes until her voice went.

Ice stood up to his waist in black water, in total darkness, and listened.

The Lanternjaw was moving. He could hear the water. Somewhere close, heaving, circling, the way the hounds had circled, the way the Stag had circled in the whiteout. It was in no hurry. It had all the dark in the world, and it ate lights. In the dark, there was nothing to stop it eating him.

Except.

He was the light.

He hadn't stopped being it. The dark had come down over everything, but it hadn't come down inside him. The ember was still there behind his breastbone, warm. When he looked down, his hands were still faintly amber in the black, the only light anywhere. And it wasn't the kind of light a lure could hide. It was his. It came from inside.

The Lanternjaw ate lights. And there was exactly one light left in the whole of its pool.

It was coming for it. He could hear it coming.

He didn't run. He didn't dodge. He let the light come all the way up in him. Bright, brighter, the brightest he'd let it go, until he was standing in the Black Mere like a lamp in a dark house, amber, steady, impossible to miss.

*Come on,* he thought. *Come and eat it.*

The Lanternjaw came out of the dark and swallowed him whole.

***

It was very dark inside, and very wet, and it smelled like the bottom of everything.

For about a second, he thought that had been a mistake. The great jaws had closed round him with a sound like a cellar door. He was in the throat of the thing, squeezed, crushed, the shield jammed against his chest and the sword pinned at his side, and everything was moving and dark and hot.

Except him.

He was still glowing. In the throat of the Lanternjaw, he lit it up from inside like a paper lantern. He could see the grey walls of its gullet round him, ridged and slick, pulsing. And he could see, very clearly, from inside, exactly where the thin place was. Where the throat met the bottom of the jaw. The same place as always. The soft place, under the chin, just from the other side.

*Point. Low. Under the jaw.*

He got his sword arm free. It took everything he had. He turned the blade in the dark, the right way up, and put the point against the thin place from the inside.

He pushed.

***

The Lanternjaw sat down in the middle of the Black Mere, very slowly, the way a building sits down when its walls go. Its mouth opened. It let go of him.

He came out of it in a rush of black water and slime and landed on his back in the shallows at the edge of the pool, gasping, glowing faintly, still holding the sword. Behind him, very quietly, the great grey shape slid back under the black water and didn't come up again.

The light came back. All at once, the way it had gone: the painted sky, the reeds, the green hill, the water. As if somebody had relit the candle.

He lay in the shallows and looked up at the sky and breathed.

He hung it on his belt beside the little cracked bell, on the walk up the green hill to the door of the ascension room. It was small, glass and dark iron, with something faintly glowing inside that might once have been the Lanternjaw's lure. It gave off a soft warm light that made everything round it look slightly more like itself.

He looked at it for a long time.

"For them," he said.

"What?"

"This. When they get here. When they come through the Fen." He touched the little lantern on his belt. "The wisps. They'll be back by then. They come back. Whoever carries this, the lights stop lying." He looked down at the Black Mere. "I'll leave it. In the ascension room. When I go."

"Ice—"

"I know. Two's a pattern. Three's a—" He stopped. "It's a lantern. Climbers leave things. It's the most normal thing in the world."

"You said that about the coat."

"And it was true about the coat."

Alea didn't argue. He thought she probably agreed, and had wanted to hear him say it.

***

He waited two days in the ascension room at the top of the green hill. He hadn't planned to. He'd planned to go straight up, the way he always had, the way Maigo would have bet he would. But on the first morning he sat on the stone bench by the fire-pit, and asked Alea for the rankings. Not his. Theirs. And she showed him.

"Deston's still on one," said Ice.

"He went back down. On the fifth day. After he cleared the ram." Alea's voice was quiet. "He stayed down there. He's been on floor 1 the whole time, Ice. With Chris's party. Until they cleared it."

Ice looked at the line. *Floor 1: Deston's party.*

He knew why. He knew Deston. Deston wouldn't go up until everybody else had. He'd stay behind the last party, the way he'd stayed behind the last of them for ten years, the first time. Holding the ones at the back together. Not leaving until everyone was through.

*Be careful*, he'd written on a card with his fingertip. *Train longer.*

And Deston was being careful, in the most Deston way possible. By not going anywhere until he knew everyone else was safe.

So Ice waited.

He waited. He cooked, and sat on the bench, and looked at the stair. That evening, the rankings changed.

*All parties.* Everyone was off floor 1. All thirty-five, all on floor 2, in a wooden town at the bottom of a white valley, all at level ten. All waiting for their trials.

Ice let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding since the white room.

"Tomorrow," he said. "One more night. To be sure."

He slept on the bench by the fire-pit, and woke, and checked again. *All parties.* Floor 2. Still. He spent the day sharpening his sword and mending the shield's strap and cooking the last of the fen eels, and in the evening he stood up.

"Now," he said.

***

He left the lantern on the stone bench by the door, where the next person through would see it first. Lit, small and warm, making everything round it look slightly more like itself.

Then he went to the foot of the stair at the back of the room and looked up.

*Floor 4.*

He'd been thinking about it since the white room. Since before. Since a fire on the ninety-ninth floor, and a list he'd carried for ten years. Floor 4 was where the tutorial ended. Where the Spire stopped being kind. Where Calvin had gone ahead to scout, because he was fast, and it was his job, and the party had followed. Where Rachael and Dennis had died, the first ones, the first names on the list. And where Calvin had come out with one arm, and nothing had been the same after.

*Be careful. Train longer on the 3rd floor.*

He didn't know if they would. He'd written it on a card and put it under Deston's hand. He'd trusted Deston to read it, and believe it, and make thirty-four other people believe it too. He'd never be able to make them do anything. He could only go first and make sure, when they came up behind him, that what was waiting at the top of these stairs had already met somebody who knew how to stand in front of it.

He put his foot on the first step.

"One," he said.

He counted all the way up. Two hundred and four. It was dark and steep and narrow and went on forever. At the top there was a door, and round its edges, the air coming through wasn't warm or wet or cold. It was dry. It smelled of dust, and iron, and something underneath both that he'd smelled once before, on this exact step, ten years ago, and never forgotten.

He stood there with his hand on the door.

*Anyone who can see you can find the way.*

Down below, a long way down, thirty-five people were on floor 2, all together, all safe. And in a few weeks, or a few months, if Deston was as careful as Ice had always known he was, they'd come up this stair. They'd come behind him. And whatever was on the other side of this door, he'd have walked through it first.

He thought, for a moment, of a ponytail in the reeds, saying something he couldn't hear. *I'll hear it from you. The real one.*

He opened the door, and went up alone.
