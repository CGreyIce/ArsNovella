*It's always fine*, thought Hako, with the river up to his chest.

He didn't mean it the way people thought he meant it. He'd never meant it that way. People heard *it's always fine* and thought it meant *this isn't happening*, or *don't worry*, or *I'm not frightened*. It had never meant any of those. He was frightened all the time. He'd been frightened on every floor since the white room. He was frightened now, standing in the river under the cliff at Keddle with the water up to his chest and colder than anything he'd ever felt, colder than the Grey, colder than the frost on the straw man, so cold it had stopped feeling cold and started feeling like a hand closing round his ribs.

*It's always fine* meant *this way*.

You said it facing the way you wanted to go. Like pointing a spear. So that when everything else went sideways, you knew which way you were facing. *Bad nights end.* That was all. Whatever this was, it ended, and you'd be on the other side of it, and you said *it's always fine* to remind yourself which side that was.

He'd told Ice that once. On a call, at four in the morning, on Earth, after a very bad night. Ice had laughed so hard he'd had to mute himself.

He leaned on the ladder.

It was the only thing between him and the river. A long ladder of grey willow lashed with rawhide, one end on the rock at the foot of the cliff path, the other end on the porch rail of the last stilt-house in Keddle, and the river going between them under it, fast and black and full of ice. Hako was standing in the river. Holding the middle of it. With his shoulder under the third rung and his shield arm round the uprights and his spear in his other hand, because the ladder was too long, and too thin, and the river was too strong, and without somebody standing in the middle of it holding it up it went down into the water and the floes took it.

So somebody was standing in the middle of it.

On the porch of the last stilt-house, a woman with a baby in a sling was looking at the ladder. At the water going under it. At Hako, in the water, holding it.

"*Come on*," said Hako. Through his teeth, which wouldn't stop. "It's fine. *Come on*. Look at me. Not the water. It's always fine."

The woman looked at him.

Then she put her foot on the ladder.

***

It had started at dawn.

Hako hadn't slept. Nobody on the Float had slept, after the first word. They'd stood on the edge of the raft-town in the dark and watched the whole river break, from bank to bank, a mile wide, the ice going up on edge and over and down with a sound like every door in the world, and felt the Float lift under them, and settle, and lift. Haddo had said *cut loose, hold on, listen*, and they had, all night, with the ropes in their hands ready and the channels they'd cut round the Float's edge full of black water, and nothing had happened. The Float had floated. It had risen with the water, slowly, the whole town on its rafts, and the ice had ground past it in the channels, and nobody had been crushed.

And then the sun came up, and Hyvred, at the top of the mast with her glasses on, said: "Keddle."

The stilt-houses under the cliff. Where they'd slept the first night. Where Sabe lived, and Tuli, and twenty families, in grey wooden houses on long legs standing up out of the reed-beds along the bank, with ladders down to the ice.

The water was up their legs.

Not a bit. Most of the way. The lake behind the jam at the Narrows was rising, up the whole valley, the way Haddo had said it would, and the Float rose with it because the Float floated. Keddle didn't float. Keddle stood. On legs that had been driven into the riverbed fifty years ago and had stood through fifty thaws, and the water was up past the knees of them and rising, and in it, grinding past, slabs of broken ice the size of rooms. Hitting the legs. One after another.

As Hako watched, from the edge of the Float, a mile off, one of the houses at the downstream end of Keddle leaned. Slowly. Like somebody sitting down who isn't sure there's a chair. And went over into the river.

There was nobody in it. They found that out later. But they didn't know that then.

"How many people?" said Ice.

He was standing at the edge of the Float next to Hako, with Thea. He'd been standing there all night. He'd been facing down the river, toward the Narrows, where Chris's party was, with his hand on his chest the way Deston did when he felt the threads. Hako knew what that meant. Ice could feel them. Five threads, a day's walk off, at the foot of the thing in the window. Hako had watched him stand there all night, facing that way, and not go.

He'd turned round when Hyvred said *Keddle*.

"In Keddle?" said Haddo. "Eighty. Ninety." He had his pole down on the planks of the Float. His face didn't do anything. "Sabe's not there. Sabe's at the Narrows. Sabe's the one who'd have got them out early." He looked across the ice at the stilt-houses, leaning. "They'll stay. Without her. They'll stay till the porches go. They always think the legs'll hold. The legs held for Sabe's mother."

"And when the porches go?"

Haddo looked at him.

"Then the houses go," he said. "With them on the roofs."

***

Hyvred's party went. And Ice, and Thea. And Jared and Salty, who could carry ladders, and Xola, who could do things with water, and Kei.

They went across the ice. The ice was moving. Not fast; not like the night of the first word. But moving. All of it. The great broken plates of the river grinding past each other toward the Narrows, slowly, with black water opening and closing between them. A man from the Float went with them, an old ice-reader called Brann with a pole and one eye, because Haddo said nobody was crossing the river in the week she woke without somebody who could hear her.

"How do you cross moving ice?" Ice asked him. At the edge of the Float. Before they went.

Hako heard it, and turned his head. So did Kei. So did everybody who heard it. They'd all heard *I don't know*, on the cliff, on the first morning. They'd all heard about *white*, and Calvin, and the river. They'd heard Higu read out *HE ASKED HOW* from the rock by the fire in the meeting-house, and Calvin say *what, he asked how? Dec asked how?*, and Rachael hit him.

Hearing it was different.

Brann looked at Ice with his one eye.

"You don't," he said. "Not on purpose. You wait for two plates to touch. And then you step. And then you wait again." He put his pole down. *Tock*. "You don't cross the river. You let the river bring you across. Bit at a time."

"Okay," said Ice.

And when they went out onto the moving ice, Ice didn't go first.

Hako noticed that. He thought maybe he was the only one who did. Ice had gone first onto every floor since the white room. Onto every bridge. Into every room. *A floor ahead.* On the way down, on every stair, Calvin had gone first and Ice in the middle, but that was Drew's order and Ice had let it be. This wasn't an order. This was a mile of moving ice, with houses falling into the river at the end of it. And Ice stood at the edge of the Float and let Brann go first. And then Hyvred. And then Hako.

And then went.

Hako looked back at him, once, on the ice. Ice was stepping where Brann had stepped. Waiting where Brann had waited. Watching Brann's feet, and Brann's pole, and listening, with his head tipped, the way Tuli had taught him.

He caught Hako looking. He didn't say anything. But something happened at the corner of his mouth.

*Huh*, thought Hako. *Classic.*

He didn't say it out loud. It didn't seem like the right time.

***

The people of Keddle were on their roofs.

Some of them. Most of them were on their porches, with their poles, putting them down on the planks of their own houses, *tock*, and listening. As if the house could tell them something. As if the legs could. Hako saw an old man on a porch with the water a hand below his boards, putting his pole down, and tipping his head, and lifting it, and putting it down again, over and over, and not moving.

"He's listening for the legs," said Brann. Quietly. "To see if they'll hold."

"Will they?"

Brann put his own pole down on the moving ice. Listened. Lifted it.

"No," said Brann.

The cliff path came down to the river at the upstream end of Keddle. The same path they'd come down on the first morning, from the stair door, zigzagging down the cliff to the reed-beds. The reed-beds were gone. Under water. The foot of the path was a ledge of rock with the river going past it fast and black and full of ice, and the nearest stilt-houses were thirty metres off across that water. Then the next. Then the next. All down the bank. Twenty houses. Eighty people.

And between the foot of the path and the houses, nothing but the river.

"Ladders," said Salty.

He and Jared had brought four. Long ones, from the Float, willow and rawhide, that the Float used to get from raft to raft when the channels were open. They were long enough to reach from the rock to the first house. Just. If somebody held the middle.

Hako looked at the river.

Then he looked at Ice. Ice was looking at the river too. At the water and the floes. At the ladders. At the houses. At the people on the porches with their poles. And Hako saw him look, the way he'd seen him look at a hundred things on fourteen floors, working it out, *where*, *what*, the shape of it, a floor ahead—

And stop.

And turn to Brann.

"What do you do?" said Ice. "When the legs go. When it's like this. What do people here do?"

Brann looked at him with his one eye for a long time.

"Somebody stands in the water," said Brann. "And holds the ladder." He looked at the river. "Somebody big. Who doesn't fall over." He looked at the people on the porches. "And they come across one at a time. And whoever's holding it doesn't let go. Whatever comes down the river at him." He was quiet. "Sabe's father did it. In her mother's time. He held the ladder at Keddle for half a day. Then a plate came down."

He didn't say what happened then.

Hako looked at the ladders. At the river. At the houses.

*Somebody big. Who doesn't fall over.*

He'd been a tank since the first morning. Since the class hall, on floor 1, when the man behind the counter had looked at him and said *Knight?* and he'd said *the one that stands in front*. He'd stood in front on every floor. On three, in the channels, up to his waist, learning to be a line with the seven others, going under, again and again, until on the fortieth day Azac told a story and he stopped going under. On fourteen, on the late watch, with his shield white with frost from a straw man fifteen paces off. He wasn't the biggest of them. Gabe was the biggest. Salty. Azac. He was just one who didn't fall over.

"Me," said Hako.

Everybody looked at him.

"I'll hold it," said Hako. "The first one." He was already taking his pack off. "Gabe's not here. Salty can hold the second one. Jared the third." He looked at Ice. "You can hold the fourth."

Ice looked at him.

"You've never held a ladder in a river," said Ice.

"Neither have you," said Hako.

Ice didn't say anything for a moment. Then he laughed. Short. Surprised. The way Thea laughed now.

"No," said Ice. "I haven't."

"So I'll go first." Hako put his shield on his arm. "And you can watch how I do it. And then you'll know." He looked at the water. "That's how everybody learns everything. Apparently."

***

The cold was the thing.

He'd known it would be. Ice had gone into the river on the second day after Calvin and come out blue and not been able to stop his teeth for an hour. Hako had seen him. He'd thought he knew. He went down off the rock ledge into the water with the end of the first ladder on his shoulder and Salty holding the other end on the rock, and the river closed round his legs, and his waist, and his chest, and he didn't know anything at all.

It wasn't cold. That was the strange thing. It was past cold. It went into him so fast and so far that it didn't feel like cold. It felt like the whole of his body had been grabbed by a hand the size of a house and squeezed, once, hard, and not let go. He couldn't breathe. He couldn't think. His legs were gone. Not gone; he could see them, under the black water, standing on the riverbed. He just couldn't feel them. He was standing in the river on nothing.

*It's always fine*, he thought. *This way.*

He faced the stilt-house. The first one. Thirty metres off. He put the ladder up. Salty, on the rock, lifted his end. Hako walked out into the river with the middle of it on his shoulder, slowly, one step at a time, feeling for the bottom with feet he couldn't feel, until the far end of the ladder came down on the porch rail of the first house, and the near end was on the rock, and he was in the middle, chest-deep, with the ladder on his shoulder like a yoke, and the river going past him.

A plate came down.

He saw it coming. A slab of ice the size of a kitchen table, grey and rotten-edged, turning slowly in the current, coming down the river at him. At the ladder. At the place where he stood.

He put his shield out.

Not up. Out. The way you'd put a hand out to stop a door swinging. Edge first, angled, into the water in front of him. The plate hit the shield. He felt it in his shoulder, his back, his teeth. It turned. Along the shield, off the edge, past him. Down the river.

*Don't step back*, Ice used to say. *Lean in.* Forty-one times. On Earth. *If it's pushing you, lean in.*

He leaned in.

"*Come on*," he said to the house. Through his teeth. "One at a time. Look at me. Not the water."

***

They came across.

One at a time, the way Brann had said. On the ladder, over the river, with Hako standing in the middle of it chest-deep holding it up on his shoulder. A man with two children. An old woman who wouldn't let go of her pole, who came across with it held crossways in her teeth like a dog with a stick. The woman with the baby in the sling. A boy of about twelve who came across on his hands, upside down, walking on the rungs, and Hako would have shouted at him if he'd had the breath, and found out later his name was Ennis and he was, in Tuli's opinion, an idiot.

Every one of them looked at the water. Every one of them. On the second rung, or the third, they'd look down, at the black water going past and the ice in it, and freeze.

And Hako would say *look at me*. And they'd look at him. A man with a spear and a shield standing in the river up to his chest with his lips going blue, saying *it's always fine*. And something in their faces would go, *well, if he's standing in it*. And they'd come on.

That was it. That was the whole of it. That was what he was for.

Behind him, on the rock, he heard Bee.

Bee was drawing. On a strip of birch-bark, with a stub of charcoal, fast, the way she drew when it mattered. A sun. A small round sun with lines coming off it. She'd been doing it since the white room; her drawn suns kept people warm, about thirty seconds each, nobody knew why, she didn't know why, they just did. She finished one and leaned out over the edge of the rock and slapped it flat on Hako's back between his shoulders, on the wet leather, and he felt it go into him like a hand on a cold stove.

Thirty seconds.

Then she was drawing the next one.

"Keep *going*," said Bee. Her voice was shaking. "I've got about forty more bits of bark. Keep *going*, Hako."

"It's always fine," said Hako.

"*It's not*," said Bee. "It's *not* always fine, it's *never* always fine, it's the *worst* thing you *say*—" She slapped another sun on his back. "*Keep going*."

He kept going.

Gigi had her hands on the stilts. Hako saw her from the corner of his eye, when he could see anything. She'd gone out onto the first house's porch with the first family and stayed, kneeling on the porch at the edge, with her hands flat on the wood where the legs came up out of the water. And from her hands, down the legs, into the river, something was growing. Thorns. Long dark brambles, thick as rope, winding down the stilt-legs and round them, into the water, into the riverbed, and back up the next leg, and the next, binding them together. *Bramble. Grow it where you need it. It doesn't let go.* The house had been leaning. It stopped leaning.

Hyvred was on the cliff path above, with her glasses on. Watching the houses. Calling out, every few minutes, in a high clear voice that carried over the river: "*Fourth house, two still in it. Sixth, one, upstairs. The eighth's empty. The eighth's EMPTY. Skip the eighth.*" Colours. She could see who was in the houses by their colours, through the walls. She'd never once in two years got it wrong.

Kei was on the rock beside Salty with his hand on Salty's wrist, then Jared's, then on the back of Hako's neck when he leaned close enough, measuring. Counting something. Saying, every so often, flat and quiet: "*Hako. Ten more minutes. Then you come out.*"

And Ice was holding the fourth ladder.

Further down. Where the last houses were. In the river to his chest, the same as Hako, with the ladder on his shoulder and his lamp shield out edge-first into the current, and Thea on the rock behind him with the lantern, and the families coming across over his head one at a time.

He'd watched Hako. For the first ladder. He'd stood on the rock and watched Hako walk out into the river and put the ladder up and turn the first plate with his shield, and he'd watched how. Hako had seen him watching. He'd looked like Tuli looked when she was listening. Like somebody learning.

And then he'd gone and done the same.

Not better. Not a floor ahead. The same. Hako, between plates, between families, between Bee's suns, looked down the river at Ice standing in the water at the fourth ladder doing exactly what Hako was doing, the way Hako was doing it, edge-first, leaning in. And thought: *I showed him that.*

He'd never showed Ice anything. Not once. In two years. Nobody had. Ice had always known first.

*Classic*, thought Hako. And nearly laughed, and swallowed river, and coughed, and leaned in.

***

The last house was the trouble.

Ennis went back.

Hako didn't see him go. Nobody did. The last of the last house's family had come across Ice's ladder, the father, with a sack of something over his shoulder, and Hyvred on the path had called *the last one's empty, it's empty*, and Ice had started backing toward the rock with the ladder. And then Hyvred's voice changed.

"*No*—*no*, there's—*somebody's gone back in*—*the last house*—"

Ennis. On the porch of the last house. He'd come across on his hands, an hour ago, laughing. And gone back. Nobody had seen. Along the ladder from the house before, maybe, or across the roofs. He was on the porch of the last house in Keddle, the downstream one, the one nearest the open river, and he was going in through the door.

"*ENNIS*," screamed a woman on the rock. "*ENNIS, THE DOG*—"

The house leaned.

Gigi's brambles hadn't reached it. It was too far, too far down, and Gigi was on the first house, at the other end of Keddle. It leaned. Toward the river. Slowly, the way the first one had at dawn. The porch tipped. Water came over the edge of it.

Ice was in the river with the fourth ladder on his shoulder, halfway between the rock and the house. Ten metres from the porch. He looked at it. Hako saw him look. Saw his whole body go toward it, the way it always went, toward whatever was falling, *a floor ahead*, first—

And stop.

Ice turned his head. Up the river. To Hako.

Thirty metres off. In the water. With the first ladder.

Hako didn't know how he knew. He'd never known how Ice knew anything. But he looked down the river at Ice, at the fourth ladder, ten metres from a falling house with a boy in it, and Ice looked back at him. And Ice didn't go.

He held up the ladder.

He planted his feet. In the river, on the riverbed, chest-deep, and lifted his end of the fourth ladder off his shoulder, both arms, up, and swung it, slowly, the whole length of it, until the far end came down on the rail of the falling porch. And held it there. A bridge. From the middle of the river to the last house.

"*HAKO*," said Ice.

Not *I'll go*. Not *wait here*. Not a floor ahead.

*Hako.*

Hako let go of the first ladder.

Salty had it. Salty was already in the water behind him, taking the weight. Hako dropped his spear and swam. He didn't know he could swim in water that cold. He found he could, for about ten metres, if he didn't think about it and the person at the end of the ten metres was holding a ladder up out of the river for him. He got to Ice. He got his hands on the ladder. Ice was under it, chest-deep, holding it up, with his face white and his teeth going.

"*Lean in*," said Ice.

Forty-two.

Hako went up the ladder. Over the river. Over Ice. Onto the porch of the last house in Keddle as it went over, the water coming over the boards, the whole house groaning. In through the door. It was dark. Water to his knees, inside. Furniture floating. A boy, in the back room, on the bed, which was tipping, holding a dog. A big wet grey dog, terrified, that wouldn't come.

"*It's always fine*," said Hako. "Come on."

Ennis looked at him.

"It's *not*," said Ennis. "It's—the house is—"

"I know," said Hako. "That's not what it means." He held out his hand. "Come on. This way."

***

They came out through the door as the house went.

Hako with Ennis under one arm and the dog under the other, which the dog did not enjoy, onto a porch that was going over sideways into the river. He saw the ladder. Its end on the rail, still. Ice at the other end of it, in the water, holding it up. The rail went under. The ladder went with it, down, toward the water.

Ice held it.

Hako saw him do it. The ladder going down at the house end as the porch went, and Ice at the other end bending under it, all of him, shoulders, back, both arms, keeping his end up, so the ladder went down at a slope instead of into the river. A slope. Into the water. A long slide down a wet willow ladder into the black river, with a boy and a dog, toward a man standing in it holding the top of it up.

"*Slide*," said Hako, to Ennis.

They slid.

Into the water. Into the cold. Into the hand the size of a house. Down, under. He felt Ennis go out from under his arm and grabbed and got his collar. He felt the dog go. Then he felt a hand in *his* collar. Gabe's way. The way Gabe pulled people out of things. Not Gabe. Ice. Pulling him up, and Ennis with him, and the dog, somehow, swimming on its own, furious, and the ladder gone, and the house gone behind them into the river with a long tearing groan, and the four of them in the water together, and the rock thirty metres off, and Salty and Jared already in the water coming toward them with the first ladder.

"*It's always fine*," said Hako. To Ice. In the water. With his teeth going so hard he could barely get the words out. "*This way.*"

Ice looked at him. Blue. Shaking. Holding him up by the collar in a river on a floor neither of them had ever seen.

And laughed.

The same laugh. Hako knew it the moment he heard it. From a call, at four in the morning, on Earth, a long time ago, after a very bad night, when Ice had laughed so hard he'd had to mute himself.

"*Hako*," said Ice. "That's the most you thing anyone's ever said."

"Yeah, well," said Hako. "It's true."

***

They got everybody out. Every one. Eighty-seven people from twenty houses. Hyvred counted. Up the cliff path, to the ledge at the top where the stair door was, where Higu had drawn the first line on the new rock on the first morning; the whole of Keddle sitting on the cliff ledge in the cold sun, wrapped in furs, with their poles, looking down at the river where their houses had been.

Not all of them had gone. Gigi's brambles had held six. Six stilt-houses at the upstream end, bound together at the legs with thorns as thick as rope, standing in the flood, leaning, holding. The rest were gone. Fourteen. Into the river. Down toward the Narrows.

Sabe's was one of them.

Ice stood at the edge of the ledge with a blanket round him and his teeth finally stopped, looking down at the place where Sabe's house had been. Where he'd slept the first night. Where he'd sat on the bottom rung of the ladder with Thea and listened to the ice and said *I'm frightened*. Where Tuli had taught him *thick* and *not that*.

Gone.

"We'll have to tell her," said Thea. Beside him. "Sabe. And Tuli."

"Yes."

An old man from Keddle was standing next to them. The one who'd been on his porch at dawn, putting his pole down over and over, listening for the legs. He was looking at the river too.

"We'll put them back," he said.

Ice looked at him.

"In the summer," said the old man. "When she's gone down. We'll cut new legs. Drive them in. Put them back up." He put his pole down on the rock, *tock*, out of habit. "Every spring she takes something. Every summer we put it back." He looked at Ice. "That's all. That's how it's done."

"How do you drive in new legs?" said Ice.

The old man looked at him. Then at Hako, sitting on the rock beside Ice with Bee's last sun going warm on his back and Ennis's dog asleep across his legs, which nobody had asked it to do.

"Your big one'll know," said the old man. "He held the ladder."

Hako opened his mouth to say he didn't know how to drive in stilt-legs. He'd never driven in a stilt-leg in his life. He'd never held a ladder in a river, either, until this morning.

He shut it again.

"I'll find out," said Hako. "In the summer."

The old man nodded. He put his pole down, and listened, and lifted it, and walked off along the ledge to find his family.

Ice was looking at Hako.

"What?" said Hako.

"Nothing," said Ice. And then, after a moment: "Thank you. For showing me how."

Hako looked at him. At the man who'd gone a floor ahead of all of them for two years and known everything, sitting on a cliff in a borrowed blanket, thanking him for showing him how to hold a ladder.

"It's always fine," said Hako.

"I know," said Ice. "This way."

Bee, on Hako's other side, put her charcoal down, and leaned her head on Hako's shoulder, and burst into tears, and said it was the cold.
