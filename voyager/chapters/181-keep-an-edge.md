*I'll take the shots you can't*, Wendy had said, a long time ago, on floor 6. *And I'll do the minding.*

She'd meant it as a joke. Mostly. They'd been on the lip of a cistern in a city made of glass, and Yan had said, pleasantly, that everything had a light, the animals too, and that it didn't bother him. *I look after mine.* And she'd looked at him with his stick and thought, *somebody's going to have to mind about things, around you. And it's not going to be you.*

So she did. She minded. She minded about strangers he walked past, and people he said things to in that pleasant voice, and the soul of a man in an apron in Tell whose light Yan would always know, anywhere, in the dark. She minded so that Yan didn't have to, and Yan let her, and she'd never once been sure he knew she was doing it.

On the jetty at dusk on the five hundred and twelfth day he handed her Working On It without looking round.

"Hold this," said Yan.

She held it.

***

The city went quiet from the top down.

She'd seen it before. Everybody had, on the way up. The river-folk did it for every boat. A hum, first: one low note, from everywhere at once, from every balcony and walkway and window and from the water itself, where the river-folk slept among the stilts. You felt it in your teeth. And then the lamps. Hundreds of gold lamps behind the falling water, going out. One fall at a time. From the top of the staircase of falls, all the way down, a long slow wave of dark coming down the cliff toward the lake, until the only lights left were the stars and the water.

There was no boat tonight. There'd been no boats for the fourteen; they'd gone over the Edge months ago, empty, with the line cut too soon. There was only Yan.

The river-folk did it anyway.

They were all out. On the balconies. On the jetties. On the walkways over the water, standing, tall and thin and grey-green, with their big wet eyes turned to the last jetty and the causeway and the lip. Hundreds of them. And in the water among the stilts, more, with only their heads showing, their long hair spread round them on the surface. All of them humming.

Wendy had never heard the whole city hum before. Not like this. It wasn't a sound. It was the lake.

Under it, every four seconds, from the forge under the second arch, the hammer. *THUD.* Ressa had left it going.

The thirty-six were on the last jetty. Behind Yan. Not in a line. Just there. Sitting on the boards, standing against the posts, the way they'd been on the Barn Field's doorway, except nobody had their hands on anybody's shoulders, because there was nothing to hold up. Ice near the back, with Thea. Salty, very still. Tiger, sitting on the edge with his feet out of the water for once. Cursiffe with his book shut on his knees and his hand flat on the cover.

And at the very end of the jetty, past all of them, past Yan, with her feet in the water, a child.

Lune.

Wendy had seen her the night before. And that morning. And all afternoon. Sitting on the end of the last jetty with her long legs in the lake and her long wet hair down her back, looking out at the causeway. Every river-folk child in Wendmere was at home behind a shut door with its family, or on a balcony a long way back holding somebody's hand, because the water at the end of the jetty was full of hands. Lune was sitting in it.

Nobody had told her to move. Wendy thought maybe nobody could.

Thea stood up.

She had the lantern. She went past the others, along the jetty, to the post at the end where the causeway began. An old black post, taller than she was, with an iron cage at the top and a lamp in it, cold. Aud had lit it every dusk for a hundred and ninety-one years. It hadn't been lit since the spring.

Thea opened the lantern.

Wendy didn't know how she did it. It wasn't fire. Ina did fire. It was the light that was in the lantern, the amber one, the one that had been in it since the Fen on floor 3, Ice's. Thea held the open lantern up to the cold lamp in the cage and the amber went across. Like water finding its level. Out of the glass of the lantern and into the lamp, and the lamp came on.

Gold. Amber. Steady. At the end of the jetty, over the first stone of the causeway, over the dark water.

The hum went up.

All round the lake, from every balcony, the river-folk saw it come on, and the note they were humming lifted, a fraction, the way a voice lifts when it sees somebody it was waiting for coming up a road.

Thea closed the lantern. It was still lit. There was more in it than she'd given. There always was. She came back along the jetty and sat down by Ice, and took his hand, and didn't look at him.

Ice was looking at the lamp.

Wendy didn't know what was on his face. She didn't look for long. It wasn't hers.

***

Yan unwrapped the pole.

He'd carried it since floor 8. Since the jetty, a year ago, when Aud put it in his hands with her arms shaking and said *keep it, until somebody makes you your own*. Wrapped in oilcloth, under his pack, up six floors and down six. Wendy had seen him take it out on the *Patience*, once, at night, and look at it, and wrap it up again. He'd never used it, after Salty gave him Working On It. He'd just carried it.

He unwrapped it now. On the end of the jetty, under the lamp. Pale wood, worn dark where Aud's hands had been for a hundred and ninety-one years, and Hessel's before her, and Coll's. Longer than Yan by a head. And at the end, Ressa's blade. Curved like a new moon. With a ripple down the steel like water moving.

He stood with it for a moment.

Then he stepped off the jetty onto the first stone.

The water went over his boots. Ankle-deep. Fast. Wendy saw it lean on him. She'd walked the first few stones of the causeway once, on the way up, to see, and come straight back, because it was like a hand in the small of your back, pushing, all the time, toward the lip. Yan stood in it the way Aud had stood in it. Weight down. Pole set. The heel of the blade in the crack between the stones.

He didn't walk out.

He stood on the first stone and looked down. At the water beside his feet. At nothing, as far as Wendy could see. Just water, going over stone, under the lamp.

"Aud," said Yan.

Not loud. Pleasantly. The way you'd say hello to somebody on a walkway.

Wendy couldn't see anything. She never could. She knew what Yan saw, because he'd told her, the first time, on floor 1, on the third day, in a voice so ordinary she'd thought he was joking: *I can see people's lights, Wen. Little ones. In their chests. Like candles.* She'd minded about that too. For a long time. Then she'd stopped minding and just believed him.

"Hello," said Yan. To the water. "Just Aud. I know. Cursiffe asked. Sefa says you'll hit anyone who puts the pole in it." A pause. He tilted his head, the way he did when he was listening to Suzu. "You're very low. Lower than anyone. But you're steady." He smiled a little. "You always were."

The hum went on. The hammer. *THUD.*

"You'll want them first," said Yan. "The fourteen. You'd never go in front of the ones you were walking." He was looking down at the water by his feet. "So wait. You're last. I'll come back for you." He paused. "I know. *The young always cut too soon.* I'll wait. Somebody taught me a knot."

He lifted the pole.

He walked out.

***

Wendy watched him all night.

She didn't sit down. She stood at the end of the jetty, under the lamp, with Working On It in both hands, the long pale ash haft, Salty's scythe, and watched Yan go out along the causeway in the dark. Pole and step. Pole and step. The blade's heel going into the cracks, and holding, and lifting, and going in again further on. The water leaning on him. Him not leaning back. Just standing in it, the way Aud had stood in it, the way Neth had told Tiger a knot should lie: down, where it was going.

It took a long time to get to the lip. It was meant to.

At the lip, he stopped.

She could just see him. A dark shape against the white, where the lake ended in a long straight line like the edge of a table and went over into mist. The lamp at the end of the jetty threw a long gold road across the water to him and he was standing at the end of it. With the pole.

"*Brann*," said Yan.

His voice came back across the water. Not loud. It carried, over the lake, under the hum, the way voices carry on water at night.

*A light you know by name will hear you say it. It will turn.*

Wendy couldn't see it turn. She saw Yan turn. A little. Toward something on the water at the lip, to his left. She saw him look at it. And then she heard him say, in the same ordinary voice:

"*He sang to the eels.*"

And wait.

***

That was how it went.

All night. Fourteen times. Wendy stood on the jetty and counted. She didn't mean to; it was Newish's thing, and Higu's, and Dennis's, counting. But she stood there with Working On It in her hands and counted every one.

He'd say the name. Turn to it. Say the line from Cursiffe's book, the thing somebody had written or said in a doorway that morning, who they were. And then wait.

Sometimes not long. Wira, who had the best garden on the stilts, went almost as soon as he'd said *beans*. Wendy saw Yan watch something on the water, and lean the pole, and then sweep the blade low across the surface with the inside edge, the way Aud had cut the line, and straighten. And look at the next.

Sometimes a very long time.

Sile took an hour.

*Sile, who was not old. A fever. Her mother wrote it and then crossed it out and then wrote it again.* Yan said it, and turned, and waited. And waited. Wendy saw him stand very still at the lip with the pole for so long that she thought something had gone wrong. The hum went on. The hammer. Then he started to hum too. The city's note. The low one. She heard it come across the water from him, very soft, under the hum of the whole lake, and the lake heard it, and the note changed, all round, from every balcony, very slightly, to meet his.

He hummed for a long time.

Then he leaned the pole, and swept the blade low across the water, and straightened.

On a balcony on the third arch, somebody made a sound. A woman. Wendy didn't see who. She thought it was probably Sile's mother.

Ode, who'd woven her own boat and left it on the roof. Garrow. Lisk. Orla, who was very proud, and who Yan said, afterwards, went over the lip *like she was showing it how*. Tarrin. Nolle. Veyle. Ammet. Rhun. Dessa, the oldest after Sefa, who remembered the year the lake froze, and who Yan said turned round twice before she went, as if she were looking for somebody, and then went anyway.

He was so cool about it.

That was the thing Wendy minded, standing there. Not that he was doing it. That he was doing it so *well*, and so calmly, and that she could see from the jetty that he wasn't feeling anything much. Not for them. He stood at the lip and said each stranger's name in his pleasant voice and said the line about them that a stranger had told Cursiffe, and waited for exactly as long as it took, and cut, and turned to the next. Like somebody threading a needle. Like Ressa walking the hammer down a blade a finger's width at a time. He did it right. Every one. He did not once cut too soon.

He didn't do it for them.

He did it because they were in front of Aud, and Aud would have wanted them done first, and done right. And because that was what Aud had taught him on a jetty a year ago in four conversations and a pole. And because Aud was one of his. And everybody else was in the way.

Wendy minded. For all fourteen of them. Somebody had to. She stood on the jetty with his scythe in her hands and minded about every name, every *beans* and *she was very proud* and *crossed it out and wrote it again*, so that somebody on the jetty was minding about them while he walked them out.

She didn't think he knew. She'd never been sure he knew.

***

Mott was thirteenth.

"*Mott*," said Yan.

And waited.

He waited a long time. Wendy saw him turn to something at the lip, on his right, and stand there. And not cut. And not hum. Just stand, with the pole, in the gold road from the lamp, looking at the water.

"He won't lie down," said Yan. Across the water. Not to anybody. To the jetty in general. Pleasantly, as if reporting the weather. "He keeps looking back."

At the end of the jetty, Lune took her feet out of the water.

Wendy saw her do it. The child had been sitting there all night, with her legs in the lake, not moving, watching Yan. She pulled her feet up and knelt on the boards, at the very end, by the lamp post, and leaned out over the water toward the causeway.

And sang.

Not loud. Under her breath. Wendy could barely hear it. A thin wavery child's voice, going out over the water, not a tune exactly. Something you'd sing to yourself, so quietly that nobody would know it was singing.

*So the eels wouldn't know.*

Out at the lip, Yan turned his head. Looked back. Along the causeway, along the gold road, at the end of the jetty, at the child kneeling there singing. He looked for a long moment.

Then he looked back down at the water at his feet.

"There," said Yan. Softly. "He heard her."

He leaned the pole. He swept the blade low across the water.

Lune stopped singing. She sat back on her heels on the end of the jetty, and didn't cry, and put her feet back in the water.

Wendy looked at her. A small grey-green child, alone at the end of a jetty in the dark, with her feet in a lake everybody else in her city was afraid of, who'd just sung her great-great-grandfather over the Edge so quietly that the eels wouldn't know.

Out at the lip, Yan was looking at her too. Across the water. Wendy could see him. Not at her face. Lower. At the place under her breastbone.

He looked for a long time.

Then he turned, and started back along the causeway. For Aud.

***

He walked her the whole way.

Back along the causeway to the first stone, pole and step, against the water this time, which was harder; you could see it was harder. To the first stone, under the lamp. And stood there, beside the place where Wendy couldn't see anything, and said, "Aud. Your turn."

Wendy heard him say it. *Your turn.* The way you'd say it to somebody waiting at a counter.

And then he walked her out.

Slowly. Slower than any of the others. The whole length of the causeway, pole and step, the blade's heel in every crack, with something beside him that nobody on the jetty could see, that had walked this causeway every dusk for a hundred and ninety-one years with somebody else beside her. He didn't talk to her. Wendy thought that was right. Aud hadn't been a talker. *Keep it short.*

At the lip, he stopped. And waited.

And waited.

It was nearly dawn. Wendy could see the sky going grey over the falls behind her. The lamps were still out all over the city. The hum had never stopped. The hammer. *THUD.*

He waited longer than for Sile. Longer than for anyone.

"She's looking back," said Yan.

Across the water. Quietly.

"Not at me." A pause. "At the jetty."

Wendy looked at the jetty. At the lamp, and the thirty-five people on the boards, and at the end of it, at the very end, under the lamp, a small grey-green child sitting with her feet in the water. Looking back at the lip. Not frightened.

*Hessel took the pole off her*, Sefa had said, Cursiffe told them, *and made her sit on the end of the jetty every dusk with her feet in the water and feel him there. Every dusk. For a whole year. Until she wasn't frightened of the warm any more.*

"She wants to see who's next," said Yan.

He didn't say anything else. He stood at the lip with Aud's pole, in the grey light, and waited, while something at his side that only he could see looked back along a gold road at a child on a jetty.

Then it lay down.

Wendy knew, because Yan's shoulders moved. That was all. A small thing. The way your shoulders move when somebody you've been holding something up for finally takes the weight.

He leaned the pole. And swept the blade, very low, very slowly, across the surface of the water at the lip of the Edge. With the inside of the curve. The way Aud had. The way he'd watched her do it a year ago, from this jetty, for an old man in a reed boat.

And straightened.

***

The windows came up as the sun did.

Wendy saw them. Everybody in Yan's party saw them; they always had, since floor 1, each other's, hanging in the air. These came up over the lip of the Edge in the first light, over the white, one after another, pale.

On the jetty behind Wendy, somebody said *oh*. Several people. And Chris, who had his notebook out, the way Chris always had his notebook out, said, "Sixty-*three*?"

Wendy looked round.

"He was sixty-one," said Chris. "Last night. I wrote it down. You don't get anything five floors under. Nobody does. We've had nothing since 14." He was frowning at the window. "That's two levels."

"It counts from here," said Higu.

She was looking at the window too. Higu always looked at windows the way she looked at maps.

"The class," said Higu. "It opened here. On eight. So it counts from eight. For him. For that." She looked out at the lip, where Yan was standing. "Everything he did tonight, he did on the floor it opened on. It didn't count it as five under. It counted it as *here*."

"That's a mistake," said Chris.

"That's a loophole," said Higu. And then, after a moment, very quietly, the way she said things she was going to write on the rock: "The tower didn't think of that."

Nobody said anything.

Out at the lip, Yan was reading his windows. Wendy could see him. He read them the way he read everything, all at once and then again, and then he looked at the last one for a long time.

*Wherever you stand, there is an Edge.*

He didn't say anything. He turned round, and came back along the causeway in the sunrise, pole and step, with the water leaning on his back now and him leaning on the pole against it.

All along the lake, the lamps came back on.

From the bottom up, this time. Wendy had never seen that. They always went out from the top down and came back the same way. This time the lamp at the end of the jetty was the first, it had never gone out, and then the stilt houses nearest the jetty, and then the walkways, and then the arches, and then up the falls, fall by fall, a long slow wave of gold going *up* the cliff, until the whole staircase of falls was lit, in the sunrise, gold on gold.

And the hum stopped.

And Wendmere, all of it, every balcony and walkway and window, every river-person in the water among the stilts, made a sound Wendy had never heard river-folk make.

They laughed.

Not loudly. Softly. All of them, at once, a long low sound across the lake like water going over a weir. Relief. Wendy thought. Fourteen hands off your back, all at once, at dawn.

The hammer went on. *THUD.*

***

Ice gave him soup.

That was the first thing that happened when Yan stepped off the first stone onto the jetty. Ice was there. With a bowl. He'd gone back to the house for lights in the dark, at some point in the night, Wendy hadn't seen when, and come back with a pot and bowls, and he was standing at the end of the jetty by the lamp post with a bowl of something hot and he held it out.

"I don't look worst," said Yan.

He'd heard. Everybody had. Ice gave the first bowl to whoever looked worst. He'd done it on 14, every morning. It was a thing.

"You look like you were up all night," said Ice.

Yan looked at him.

Then he took the bowl. And stood there on the end of the jetty with Aud's pole in one hand and the soup in the other, in the sunrise, with the lamps of the whole city coming on behind him, and drank it.

"It's good," said Yan.

"I know," said Ice.

Thea, behind Ice, laughed. Out loud. Short. Surprised. As if it had come out of her by itself. And then didn't take it back. Dennis, somewhere further down the jetty, said *three*, under his breath, and Rachael said *Dennis*.

Wendy minded about that too. In a different way. A good one.

***

Lune was still at the end of the jetty.

She hadn't moved. All night. She was sitting under the lamp post with her feet in the water and her long hair down her back, looking at the causeway, where there was nothing now. Wendy knew there was nothing now. She could tell from Yan's face. He'd looked at the water by the first stone when he stepped off it, and there'd been nothing there, and something in his face had done a thing it almost never did.

He finished the soup. He gave Ice the bowl.

Then he turned round and looked at Lune.

Wendy watched him look. She'd watched him look at people for years. At their chests. At the place under the breastbone where the light was. She knew the look he had for strangers: pleasant, uninterested, like somebody glancing at a window to see if it's raining. And the look he had for his own. Gabe's. Salty's. Hers.

This wasn't either.

It was the look he'd had on the jetty a year ago, when Aud first came to the house for lights and he'd looked at her light and said *very low, but steady*. Interested. The way you'd look at a tool somebody had left on a bench, and see that it was a good one.

He walked to the end of the jetty and stood over the child.

Lune looked up at him.

"It's warm," said Yan. "Isn't it. Where they are. You said."

"Yes."

"Everyone else says it's like hands."

"It's not like hands," said Lune. "It's like the sun-pool."

Yan looked at her for a long moment. Lower. At the place under her breastbone.

"Your light leans toward it," said Yan. "The warm. Everybody else's in this city leans away. Like a candle away from a door. Yours leans toward it." He tilted his head. "Like mine."

Lune didn't say anything.

Yan held out the pole.

Aud's pole. Pale wood worn dark. Ressa's blade, curved like a new moon, with the ripple in it. Longer than he was by a head. Much longer than Lune. He held it out, across the end of the jetty, and didn't say anything kind, and didn't explain.

Lune looked at it.

"Keep it short," said Yan. "Don't look at the Edge until you're there. Wait. Until it lies down. Then cut." He was saying Aud's words, Wendy realised. Exactly. The ones Aud had said to him on this jetty a year ago. "The young always cut too soon."

"I won't," said Lune.

"You will," said Yan. Pleasantly. "The first time. Everybody does. Aud did. And then somebody will make you sit on the end of the jetty with your feet in the water every dusk for a year."

Lune looked at her feet. In the water. At the end of the jetty.

"I already sit on the jetty," said Lune.

Yan looked at her.

And for the first time in about a year, Wendy saw him surprised.

"Then you've done the year," said Yan.

Lune took the pole.

It was far too big for her. She held it in both her long webbed hands and the blade swayed over her head in the sunrise like a branch in the wind. She stood up on the end of the jetty, with the water running off her legs, and held it, and didn't put it down.

Yan looked at her holding it. He nodded. Once. The way you'd nod at a knot that had held.

Then he turned round and walked away.

***

Just like that.

Wendy watched him go. Back along the jetty, past the thirty-five, past Ice with the soup pot and Thea and Salty and Cursiffe with his book open now and his pen moving, toward the city and the house for lights and his pack and the stair up the falls. He didn't look back. He didn't say goodbye to the child. He didn't say *good luck*, or *you'll be fine*, or *I'm sorry nobody else will help you*. He'd found the one who'd do it right, and given her the thing, and said the words, and that was the job, and the job was done.

That was Yan. That was exactly Yan. He'd walk to the end of the world for his own, and he'd walked to the edge of it tonight for an old woman he'd spoken to four times, and he'd walk past anybody else.

Lune was standing at the end of the jetty with a pole twice her height, alone.

Wendy minded.

She went and crouched down in front of the child. On the wet boards, under the lamp. She gave Working On It to Salty first, without looking; she knew he'd be there. He was.

"He picked you," said Wendy, "because you're not frightened."

Lune looked at her.

"That's true," said Wendy. "He doesn't say things that aren't true. He only says them in a horrible voice." Lune's mouth moved. "But listen. You can be frightened too. Sometimes. That's allowed. Aud was. The first time. And if it's too heavy one night, you can sit down. Aud sat down. On the first stone. At the end." She swallowed. "Nobody'll think less of you."

Lune looked at her for a long time. The way the river-folk looked at things. Thinking.

"Is he always like that?" said Lune.

"Yes."

"Do you like him?"

Wendy thought about it. She thought about it properly, for once, the way the river-folk did, and didn't fill the space.

"Yes," said Wendy.

"Why?"

"Because he came back," said Wendy. "For her. For Aud. He doesn't come back for anybody. He walks past. Every time. He walked past fourteen people tonight who weren't his. He walked them out because they were in front of her." She looked at the causeway. "He came back down six floors for one old woman he talked to four times. Because she was his." She looked at Lune. "You don't get many people who'd do that. You get one. If you're lucky."

Lune looked at the pole in her hands.

"I'm his now," said Lune. "Aren't I. A bit. He gave me the stick."

Wendy didn't say anything. She didn't know. She didn't think anybody did, including Yan.

"Scythe," said Wendy. "He'll want you to call it a scythe."

"It's a *pole*."

"I know," said Wendy. "Call it a scythe anyway. It'll annoy him. Somebody should."

Lune laughed. Short. Like a child. And then Ressa was there, coming along the jetty slowly on her old knees with something long in her arms wrapped in leather, and Wendy got up and stepped back, because that was Ressa's, and not hers.

"When your arms are long enough, child," said Ressa. "Not before. Until then, this." She unwrapped it. A blade. Lighter than Aud's. Thinner in the back. Curved like a new moon, with a ripple down it like water. "I made it for the end of a keeper," said Ressa. "It'll do for the start of one."

Wendy went.

***

She caught Yan up on the stair.

The long wet stair up the cliff beside the first fall, going up out of Wendmere toward the falls above, and the walk up, two days, to the stair to floor 7. He was halfway up it with his pack on and his hands empty. She came up behind him with Working On It, which she'd taken back off Salty, and held it out.

He took it. He put it over his shoulder. He didn't say anything.

They climbed.

"You minded," said Yan. After a while. Not looking round.

Wendy stopped on the step.

He knew. Of course he knew. He'd always known. She stood on the wet stair with the fall roaring beside her and looked at the back of his head and felt something go through her she didn't have a word for, and didn't want one.

"Somebody had to," said Wendy.

"I know," said Yan. He kept climbing. "That's why I brought you."

She caught him up in three steps and hit him on the arm. Hard.

"Ow," said Yan.

And then, because he was Yan, and nothing was ever only one thing, he put his hand out behind him without looking, the way Gabe did, the way Ice had started doing, and she took it. And they went up the stair like that, out of the city, with the lamps all lit behind them in the morning and the hammer going under the second arch.

*THUD.*

At the top of the stair she looked back. Once.

At the end of the last jetty, under the lamp that Thea had lit with Ice's light, a small grey-green figure was standing with a pole twice her height, looking out at the causeway and the lip and the white, in the sun.

Not frightened.

Wendy minded about her the whole way up the falls. Somebody had to. And it wasn't going to be him.

But she thought, now, that it might be a bit him. Sometimes. Under the pleasant voice. Just enough to know who'd be minding for him.

That was enough. That was more than enough.
