The light came in gold.

Not the white of the Salt, or the grey of the Fells, or the clean cold blue of the sea at the edge of the world. Gold. Thick, late, heavy gold, the colour of light that has been lying on something warm all day. It came in through the door at the top of the stair and lay across the landing and up the first three steps, and with it came the heat, all at once, like opening an oven, and with the heat came the smell.

Straw. Cut straw, and dust, and something sweet and green underneath, like crushed stems. And a sound he couldn't place for a second. A long, dry, hissing whisper, going on and on, rising and falling, like the sea if the sea were made of paper.

Tiger went through first. He always did. He stopped one step outside, and his tail went straight up.

"*Oh*," said Tiger.

Then the rest of them, one at a time, out into it.

***

They came out of the end wall of a barn.

A small one. Old grey stone, a slate roof sagging in the middle like a tired horse, a wooden door in the end of it that hadn't been opened, by the look of the hinges, in a very long time. It stood on its own at the edge of a field. And the field was wheat.

He'd seen wheat. Everyone had seen wheat. Pictures of it. The side of a cereal box. He'd never stood at the edge of it when it was ready, at the end of a summer, with the sun going down. It came up to his chest. It went away from the barn in every direction except back, a whole sea of it, gold and bronze and tawny, heavy-headed, every stalk bent the same way under its own weight. And when the wind came across it, it moved. All of it. In long soft waves that ran away from him over the field and up the slope and away, one after another, and that was the sound. The whisper. Thousands and thousands of dry ears brushing against each other, all at once, for as far as he could see.

And he could see a long way.

The barn stood near the foot of a valley. He could tell, because behind it the land fell away a little, toward a river he couldn't see but could hear, and in front of it the valley went up. And up. And on. Between two long lines of low hills, blue-green in the evening, the valley ran away north, wide and shallow and slow, as far as his eyes would go. Fields. Hundreds of them. Gold and pale gold and brown where they'd already been cut, and green where something else was growing, all stitched together with dark hedges like a quilt somebody had been sewing for a thousand years. Orchards on the lower slopes, rows of round trees heavy with something red. Long dark lines of something tall on poles, on the warm side of the valley, that might have been hops or might have been vines. A river, glinting, going down the middle in no hurry at all. And villages. A cluster of grey roofs and smoke every few miles, each with a squat square tower in the middle of it, getting smaller, and smaller, and further away, until the haze at the top of the valley took them and there was just gold, going on.

No end. He couldn't see the end of it.

And over it all, the sky. A real one. Late-summer blue going pale at the edges, with a few high thin clouds in it like brushstrokes, and swifts. Dozens of them, high up, screaming, cutting across the gold in long black curves.

Behind him, thirty-five people stood in the doorway of a barn at the edge of a wheat field, and didn't say anything at all.

He felt them do it. All the threads at once, going warm. The way they'd gone warm on the ledge over the sea. Not frightened. Not ready for anything. Just stopped, the whole lot of them, in the evening heat, with the smell of the straw in their faces.

Maigo was the first to move. He walked straight past Ice, out of the shadow of the barn, to the edge of the wheat, where the earth was dry and cracked and pale, and stood there for a second. Then he took off his shoe. The one on his root-foot, the bandaged one, that he'd worn all the way across the Salt. He unwound the bandage slowly. And then he put his foot down into the earth at the edge of the field, and pushed, and his toes went soft and went in.

He made a sound. A small one. Like somebody getting into a hot bath after a very long day.

"Maigo?" said Chris.

"It's *full*," said Maigo. His eyes were shut. Both of his leaves, and the new third one, had turned toward the field like faces toward a fire. "It's all full. Everything. Every bit of it. It's all *done*." He breathed out. "Oh, it's so loud. Gigi. *Gigi*. Come and—"

Gigi was already there. On her knees at the edge of the wheat, beside him, with both hands flat on the cracked earth and her head down. She didn't say anything. Her shoulders were going.

Bee had her book out. Hyvred had climbed onto the low wall at the side of the barn and was standing on it with her hand over her eyes, turning slowly, looking up the valley.

"There's people," said Hyvred. "Everywhere. All the way up. Hundreds. In the fields. They're—" She stopped. "They're all *working*. Every field. All at once."

He looked where she was looking.

In the next field over, up the slope, beyond a hedge, the wheat was being cut.

He hadn't seen it at first because the hedge was in the way. But he could see it now, and hear it, under the whisper of the standing corn: a different sound. A steady swish, and a pause, and a swish, as regular as breathing. A line of people. Twelve of them, maybe more, in white shirts with their sleeves rolled, walking slowly up the field side by side, a little apart, a little staggered, like steps on a stair. Each of them swinging a long curved blade on a long crooked handle, low, flat, right to left, through the base of the wheat. All of them together. The blades went through the gold in one long silver flash and the wheat leaned and fell in a row behind them, and they stepped, and swung again. Swish. Step. Swish. Like one person breathing twelve times.

Behind them came others. Women, men, children, bent double, gathering the fallen wheat in their arms, twisting a few stalks into a band, tying it round the bundle, standing the bundles up in little tents of six or eight, heads together, all down the cut part of the field. Rows of them. Hundreds of little gold tents in the stubble, going away up the slope in lines, like an army camped for the night.

Somebody beside him made a sound.

It was Yan. He was standing at Ice's elbow with Working On It upright in his hand, the butt of the pale ash on the dry earth and the blade curving over both their heads, and he was watching the mowers. Not the people. The blades. With his head tipped a little to one side, the way he watched anything he hadn't seen before and wanted to.

"Theirs are shorter," said Yan.

"They're for wheat," said Wendy, behind him.

"I know what they're for." He didn't take his eyes off them. "I'm just saying. Theirs are shorter."

*He's going to ask one of them for a go*, said Alea, very quietly, in Ice's ear. *Before the week's out. Watch.*

Ice didn't answer. He was watching the line of mowers, too, and the little tents of wheat in the stubble, and the valley going on and on into the gold haze, and the swifts, and the light. He was trying to make it fit.

It didn't fit.

He'd been here. He'd come out of this barn. He'd stood about here, in the door, and looked up this valley. He knew he had. He remembered the barn, and the sag in the roof, and the hinges. He remembered the valley going away north between its two lines of hills to where you couldn't see the end.

He'd never seen it gold.

"Okay," said Higu.

She'd sat down in the shade of the barn wall, on the dry grass, with her back to the warm stone, and taken the rock out of her pack. Two columns. The line down the middle that she'd drawn on the morning of the Teeth and redrawn on every floor since. She set it on her knees and looked up at him.

"Ice," she said. "What do you remember?"

They sat down. All of them, round him, in the shade, the way they always did now: Gabe on his right with Hearthwall propped on the wall behind him, Thea on his left with the lantern in her lap, unlit, because it was evening and the evening was still bright. Thirty-four other people in a rough half-ring on the grass, with their packs off and their faces sunburnt already from five minutes of standing in the light, looking at him.

He looked at the valley.

"Winter," he said.

Higu wrote it down. *WINTER.*

"We came out of that door in the snow," he said. "The first time. Everything was white. The fields were bare. All of them. You couldn't see where one stopped and the next one started, except the hedges, sticking up out of the snow in lines." He looked at the wheat, chest-high, gold, moving. "There wasn't anything in them. It was all in. Months before."

*FIELDS: BARE. ALL IN.*

"Thirty-four of us. About six weeks after Tell." He kept his voice level. "There's a village. Down there, by the river, a mile up the valley, the first one. Haverley. They took us in. There was a woman who ran it, the way Brenna ran the Bastion, sort of. Annet. She didn't talk much. It was the middle of winter and everybody was indoors, all the time, with the fires going and the doors shut. They were kind. They let us sleep in their big barn, the tithe barn, in the straw. And then they mostly left us alone."

*HAVERLEY. ANNET. TITHE BARN. WINTER: INDOORS.*

"Six weeks," he said. "We stayed six weeks. It was cold. There wasn't much to hunt. Things in the snow at night." He shrugged. "Then we went up the valley. Three days, on foot, in the snow. There's another barn at the top. The Long Barn. Bigger than the tithe barn. Bigger than anything. The gleaners—" He stopped. "That's what they call themselves. The gleaners. They make a thing every harvest, out of the last of the wheat. A straw man. As tall as the roof. They put it in the Long Barn for the winter. And in winter—" He looked at the barn wall behind Higu's head. "It walked."

Higu stopped writing.

"That was the guardian," said Ice. "The first time. A straw man as tall as a barn, walking about in the snow at the top of the valley. We burned it." He was quiet. "It took two days. The stair was under the barn floor."

*LONG BARN. 3 DAYS N. STRAW MAN (WALKS IN WINTER). GUARDIAN. BURNED. STAIR UNDER FLOOR.*

"Anything else?" said Higu.

He opened his mouth.

He knew what else. He could feel it, sitting there behind his teeth, the way you can feel a stone in your shoe before you take your shoe off. He could feel the tithe barn round him, the way it had been, the cold, the dark, the smell of old straw and woodsmoke and thirty-four people sleeping. He could feel the east wall at his back. The third beam over his head.

"Nobody died," he said. "Here. The first time. Nobody."

*NOBODY DIED. (1st time.)*

"That's all," he said.

Higu looked at him for a moment, half-lidded, steady. Then she looked down at the rock. At the right-hand column, which was empty.

"Okay," she said. "So what's true."

She looked up. At the wheat. At the line of mowers in the next field, swinging and stepping, and the hundreds of little gold tents in the stubble behind them, and the swifts, and the sun going down huge and slow and orange behind the western hills.

She wrote, on the right, in her neat small capitals:

*SUMMER. (END OF.)*

*HARVEST: NOT IN.*

And under it, after a moment, smaller:

*NOBODY INDOORS.*

"That's a different floor," said Drew.

"It's the same floor," said Higu. "It's a different time of year." She put the chalk away. "We came early."

*Early*, he thought. And didn't say anything. And felt Thea, on his left, not look at him. Very carefully. The way she'd not looked east, every night, on the Long Water.

***

Somebody was coming across the stubble.

A woman. On her own, from the field where the mowers were, through a gap in the hedge, walking fast, with long strides, the way people walk who have a great deal to do and not enough light to do it in. She was about fifty, maybe more. Brown as a nut, the brown of somebody who'd been outdoors every day of her life, with deep lines round her eyes from squinting at the sun, and a broad straw hat on the back of her head, and a stick in her hand. A long hazel stick, peeled pale, with notches cut all the way down one side of it. She came straight up to the edge of the wheat in front of the barn and stopped, and looked at them. At all thirty-six of them, sitting in the shade of a barn that nobody ever came out of, with their packs and their weapons and their sunburn.

She didn't look surprised. She looked like somebody who'd been told it might rain, and it had.

"Passers," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"That's what you are," said the woman. "Isn't it. Out of the old barn. There's a song says you'd come back one day. It's a bad song." She looked them over again, one by one, quickly, the way you'd count sheep through a gate. Thirty-six. Her eyes went over him without stopping. "Well. You've come at a bad time. Or a good one. Depends." She tipped her hat back further with the end of the stick. "Can any of you hold a rake?"

"Sorry?" said Deston.

"A rake," said the woman. "Or bind. Or stook. Or carry. Or anything. I don't care what." She pointed back over her shoulder with the stick, at the line of mowers, the falling wheat, the people bent double behind them. "That's Gorse Piece. It's got to be down and stooked before the bell, and the bell's in an hour, and I've got eleven mowers and nine binders and a girl with a sprained wrist, and the rest of them are up at Long Meadow because Long Meadow's further on. So." She looked at them. "Passers or not. Can you bind?"

There was a short silence.

"I can learn," said Jared.

"Good," said the woman. "That's one." She looked at the rest. "Well?"

And Ice, who'd sat in the shade and said *winter*, and *indoors*, and *a woman who ran it*, looked up at her. At the stick. At the notches. At the hat. At the brown face, younger than the face he remembered by a whole season and a half, which had been grey with cold and shut, a face at a door with the fire behind it, saying *the barn's yours, there's straw*, and closing the door again.

"Annet," he said.

The woman looked at him for the first time.

"Annet Sallow," she said. "Reeve of Haverley. Who told you that?"

"Nobody," said Ice. "I'm sorry. I'll bind."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then, briefly, at the grey line round his left wrist, where his sleeve had ridden up. Then back at his face.

"Hm," said Annet Sallow. "Well. Come on, then. All of you. I'm not saying it twice. It's *harvest*."

And turned round and walked back through the hedge, fast, with her stick, and didn't look to see if they were following.

They followed.

***

He'd never bound a sheaf in his life. In either life.

It turned out you did it like this. You gathered up an armful of the cut wheat from the row the mowers left behind, heads all one way, butts all the other, as much as you could get your arms round. You pulled out a few long stalks from the bundle, twisted them together just under the heads, and made a band. You put the band round the middle of the armful, cross over, twist, tuck the end under itself, tight. And then you stood it up. And picked up another. And another. And when you had six or eight, you leaned them together, heads up, butts out, in a little tent, so the rain would run off them and the wind would go through them, and you went on to the next one.

It looked easy when the gleaners did it. They did it in about four seconds. A small girl of about ten, with a dirty face and straw in her hair and one wrist wrapped in a rag, did it in three, with one hand, while telling Tiger what he was doing wrong.

"*Twist*," she said. "No. *Twist*. You're not twisting, you're just *holding* it."

"I'm twisting," said Tiger.

"You're holding it in a twisty way," said the girl. "That's not the same." She took the sheaf off him, twisted the band, tucked it, stood it up, and handed it back. "There."

"What's your name?" said Tiger.

"Wren."

"I'm Tiger."

"I know," said Wren. "It's because of your tail." And went back to her own row, binding, without looking.

Tiger stood there holding his sheaf and looking after her with the face of somebody who has just met the fastest thing on a floor and it isn't him.

Calvin's first band snapped. So did his second. His third held, and he held the sheaf up over his head and shouted, and the nearest mower, a lean silent man with a long brown face, turned his head and looked at him, and Calvin put it down again very quietly and said, "Sorry." Jared was good at it straight away, the way Jared was good at anything with his hands. Salty and Gabe gave up on binding after ten minutes and started carrying, an armful of finished sheaves under each arm, up the rows to wherever the stooks were going, and the gleaners stopped making the stooks and let them, and after a while one of the old men started pointing, and they went where he pointed.

Thea bound next to him. She was bad at it. She was worse at it than he was, which he hadn't expected, because Thea was good at everything she decided to be good at. She kept getting the heads mixed up with the butts. On her fifth sheaf she stopped and held it up and looked at it the way she looked at a window she couldn't read.

"It's upside down," she said. "Isn't it."

"It's sideways."

"*How* is it sideways."

"I don't know," said Ice. "You've invented a new way."

She hit him with it. Not hard. The sheaf came apart all over both of them, and she started laughing, and so did he, in the middle of a field at the end of a summer, with straw down the back of his shirt and dust in his mouth and the swifts going over.

Behind them, the line of mowers went on up the field. Swish. Step. Swish. Like a clock.

Yan had stopped binding. He was standing at the end of the row with his sheaf forgotten in one hand and his eyes on the lean silent man, the one who'd looked at Calvin, who was at the end of the line, nearest to them, mowing. Watching how he did it. The swing from the hips, not the arms. The blade kept flat and low, an inch off the ground. The little step forward at the end of each stroke. The man felt him watching, the way people did. He finished his stroke and looked round, and saw Yan, and saw what was standing beside Yan, upright, with the pale ash haft and the long blade, and looked at it for a while.

"That's not for corn," he said.

"No," said Yan.

The man looked at Yan's face. He looked for a long time. Then he nodded, once, as if Yan had said something he agreed with, and turned back, and went on mowing.

*Told you*, said Alea.

***

The bell went at sunset.

It came from the village down the valley, by the river. A single bell, not big, not deep, a flat clanging iron note that came up the valley over the fields in the orange light and seemed to go into everything. One stroke. Then another. Then another.

And everybody stopped.

Not slowly. Not *finishing up*. Stopped. The line of mowers stopped in mid-stroke, every blade at the bottom of its swing, and straightened, and lifted the blades onto their shoulders. The binders stopped with sheaves half-tied in their hands and put them down, just where they were, on the stubble. Wren, three rows away, stopped with a band in her teeth and spat it out. The old man who'd been pointing at Gabe and Salty stopped pointing. All over the field, all over the next field, and the next, as far up the valley as he could see in the low gold light, people straightened up and stopped, and turned, and began to walk home.

Calvin was holding a sheaf. He looked at it.

"Put it down," said Annet Sallow, going past.

"It's nearly done. I've just got to—"

"Put it *down*." She didn't stop. She didn't raise her voice. "When the bell goes, the field's not yours. Leave it. It'll be there in the morning." A pause. "Or it won't. That's the field's business. Not yours."

Calvin put it down.

They walked down the valley in the last of the light with the gleaners. In a long straggling line, along the edge of the fields, through gaps in the hedges, on paths worn into the earth by a thousand years of people walking home at sunset, past the little gold tents standing in the stubble in rows. Nobody talked much. The gleaners were too tired. The thirty-six were too full of it: the heat, the gold, the dust, the smell, the sound, the swifts going round and round above them in the darkening sky, screaming, and the long valley going grey and blue behind them, and in front of them, by the river, the grey roofs of a village with smoke going up straight from every chimney in the still evening air and a squat square tower in the middle of it with a bell in the top.

Haverley.

He knew it. He knew it before they got to it. The bridge, two stone arches, low over a slow brown river with weed in it. The green, beyond the bridge, with a great oak in the middle of it, wider than a house. The houses round the green, stone and thatch, low, deep-windowed, with doors standing open now on the warm evening and voices coming out of them and the smell of bread. The tower with the bell. A smithy, at the corner, with the fire going. And beyond the green, at the far end, on its own, set back from the houses like something nobody quite dared to put near the rest:

The tithe barn.

It was enormous. He'd forgotten how enormous. It was the size of a church, a cathedral, a ship upside down. Long grey stone walls with narrow slits in them for air, and buttresses, and a roof of old stone slates going up and up into the dusk like the side of a hill, and a pair of doors in the middle of the long side big enough to drive a cart through with another cart on top. They were open. Inside, it was dark, and he could smell the straw from the green.

He stopped walking.

He hadn't decided to. His feet stopped on the green, on the short dry grass, about fifty metres from the doors, and the rest of him stopped with them, and Gabe, who'd been walking on his right, walked two steps on before he noticed and turned round.

"Dec?"

He didn't answer.

*Here*, said Alea. Very softly. In his ear. She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. She had the list. She'd always had the list.

He'd come out of that barn. In the dark. In the snow. Thirty-three people asleep in the straw behind him. He'd walked out of those doors into the snow on the green with nothing in his head at all, and stood under the oak, which had been black and bare then, and stayed there for a long time. And then gone back in, and sat down against the east wall, under the third beam, in the dark. And after a while, somebody had sat down beside him.

"Ice," said Thea.

She'd stopped too. On his left. She didn't touch him. She stood beside him on the green in the dusk, with the lantern in her hand, lit now, the amber light in it leaning so hard toward him it lay flat against the glass. And she looked at the barn. Not at him. At the barn.

He felt her see it. The way he'd felt her see the fourth step on the Teeth. She didn't know what it was. She knew it was something.

"Later," she said. Very quietly. Only to him. "Not now. Later."

He breathed.

"Later," he said.

Gabe was still standing two steps ahead of them, looking back, with Hearthwall on his back and the dusk on his face and the question in it that he never asked. He didn't ask it now. He looked at Ice for a moment, and then at Thea, and then he turned round and walked on toward the barn, and as he went he put his hand out behind him, back, the way you would for somebody coming down a ladder, without looking.

Ice took it.

He walked into the tithe barn at Haverley holding Gabe's hand, like a child, with Thea on his other side, and nobody said anything about it at all.

***

They ate on the green.

Everybody did. That was harvest, Annet said, slapping bread down on a long trestle under the oak: nobody cooked for themselves at harvest, there wasn't time, so everybody cooked for everybody, in turns, and ate outside, and went to bed. Tonight was Haverley's turn. There were six trestles end to end in the dusk under the great oak, with lamps hung from its lower branches, and benches, and the whole of Haverley on them, and thirty-six passers squeezed in between the gleaners wherever there was room. Bread. Great round loaves of it, brown, with the crust split. Cheese, hard and crumbling and sharp. A pot of beans with herbs in it. Butter. Onions, raw, sliced, in a bowl, which Ice looked at and didn't comment on. Apples, early ones, small and green-red and sour. And jugs of something cold and brown and cloudy that the gleaners drank and that made Calvin cough and go red and then ask for more, and that Maigo took one sip of, politely, and then passed on, and that Newish didn't touch, and nobody minded.

He sat at the end of a bench with his back to the oak and watched the barn.

He couldn't stop. He tried. He ate the bread, which was good, very good, better than anything since Merle, and he talked to the old man beside him, who'd been the one pointing at Gabe and Salty in the field and who turned out to be called Cobb and to have opinions about how Salty carried. But his eyes kept going back across the green to the great dark doors of the tithe barn, standing open, with the straw inside.

There was a chair by the doors.

He hadn't seen it when they walked in. It was in the shadow, to one side of the left-hand door, against the wall: an old wooden chair with a high back and arms, worn pale where hands had held it. A rush seat. There was an old man sitting in it.

Very old. Older than Corran. Thin as a stick, in a clean white shirt buttoned to the neck and a jacket over it in spite of the heat, with a blanket over his knees and a stick of his own, plain, leaning against the arm of the chair. White hair, thin, combed flat. A face like an apple that had been kept too long in a loft, all wrinkles and softness. He wasn't eating. Somebody had brought him a plate and it was on the ground by the chair. He was sitting there in the last of the light with his hands on his stick, looking out across the green, past the trestles and the lamps and the people, up the valley, at the fields.

Ice knew the chair.

He knew it the way he'd known the bridge and the oak and the barn. He'd walked past it every day for six weeks, in the snow. In and out of those doors. Every day. It had been there, against the wall by the left-hand door, with snow on the seat.

Empty.

He'd never once wondered why there was an empty chair by the door of a barn in the middle of winter. He'd walked past it every day for six weeks and never looked at it.

"Who's that?" he said to Cobb. Quietly.

Cobb looked.

"That's Gaffer," he said. "Tobin. My grandad's brother. Ninety-two." He tore a piece of bread in half. "He's seen seventy-four harvests in. Sat in that chair for the last twenty of them. Can't bind any more, can't walk the fields. Sits there and watches them come in." He chewed. "Says he'll see this one in. And then he'll stop."

"Stop?"

Cobb looked at him sideways. As if it was a strange question. As if anybody would know.

"Stop," he said. "That's how it's done. You see it in. The last one. And then you stop." He shrugged. "Not everybody gets to choose. Gaffer's choosing. Good for him." He held out the onions. "You want these? You've been looking at them."

Ice took an onion. He didn't eat it. He sat with it in his hand.

*He's choosing*, he thought. *And the first time, I walked past his chair for six weeks with snow on it. And never asked.*

He looked across the green at the old man in the chair. The old man was still looking up the valley. At the fields. At the little gold tents in rows on the darkening slopes. Counting them, maybe. The way Annet counted things, on her stick.

*The world doesn't wait for you*, he'd thought, on the Long Water. Corran on his bucket at the top of the slip. *It goes on.*

This was the other half of that.

It ended. The world went on, and things in it ended, on their own time, whether you'd got there or not. And if you came in the winter, all you saw was the chair.

"You all right, Dec?" said Gabe, from across the trestle. With his mouth full.

"Yeah," said Ice. And then, because he'd promised, on a rock in the sea, to say it when it was true: "No. Not really. Later."

Gabe looked at him for a long moment, chewing. Then he nodded, as if that was a perfectly good answer, and passed him the butter.

***

He had the late watch with Thea. Ten till midnight. Higu had put it on the rock without asking anybody, and nobody had argued.

They sat on the step of the tithe barn, outside, in the warm dark, with the lantern between them on the stone and the doors open behind them on thirty-four people asleep in the straw. The green was empty now. The trestles had gone. The lamps in the oak were out. There was a moon, nearly full, low and yellow over the eastern hills, and in its light the valley went away north, grey and silver, field after field, with the little tents of the stooks standing in their rows on the slopes like a sleeping army. The swifts had gone to bed. Something else was out, something small with soft wings that came and went at the edge of the lamplight. An owl called, once, a long way off.

The old man's chair was empty. Somebody had taken him in.

Thea didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did he.

Then she said, "Now?"

He looked at the lantern.

"Come inside," he said.

She looked at him. Then she picked up the lantern, and turned the shutter half down, and followed him.

Inside the barn it was very dark, and very high, and smelled of old straw and old wood and dust and thirty-four people. The lantern's light went up into the roof and didn't reach it. Somewhere up there, in the dark, there were beams. Huge ones, whole trees, going across from wall to wall, black with age. He didn't need to see them. He counted along the east wall in the dark, past the sleeping shapes in the straw. Hako, on his back with his mouth open. Rachael, curled small, with Calvin's boot in her ribs. Ina, sitting up against a post with his mask on, asleep, the way he slept. Past them. Along the wall. One. Two.

He stopped under the third beam.

There was straw there. Clean straw, a bit flattened, where nobody had chosen to sleep because it was too near the wall and the wall was cold at night. He stood looking at it in the half-light of the lantern.

"Here," he said.

Thea stood beside him. She didn't ask *here what*. She waited.

He sat down. In the straw, with his back against the stone of the east wall, under the third beam, where he'd sat in the dark with snow on his boots, and nothing in his head at all. The wall was warm. It had been in the sun all day. The first time it had been so cold it hurt your back through your coat.

She sat down beside him, with the lantern on her other side, and turned the shutter down further, so it was just a glow in the straw.

"This is where we heard," said Ice.

He said it very quietly. Not because of the tower. He'd stopped thinking about the tower, mostly, since the book in the Countinghouse. It had two records of him and two of her and three hands on the page and it had heard everything he had to say. He said it quietly because there were thirty-four people asleep round them and he didn't want any of them to wake up.

"Heard what?"

"Mother Crane."

He felt her go still.

"She died," he said. "The first time. In the second winter. On floor 1, in her bed over the kitchen, of a fever. While we were thirteen floors up." He looked at the dark in front of him, at the shapes of the sleepers in the straw. "Nobody told us. Nobody could. There wasn't anybody on floor 1 who'd know how to reach us, even if they'd thought to. It was a month before we heard. And then we heard here." He swallowed. "On the fourth week. At night. Everybody was asleep, mostly. And a window came up. In front of everybody at once."

"A window."

"In World," said Ice. "Where everybody writes. Except it wasn't anybody. There wasn't a name on it. In ten years that was the only time anything ever came up in World that nobody wrote." He could see it. He'd always been able to see it. "It just said: *Floor 1. Midwinter. Mother Crane of the Copper Spoon has died.* That was all."

Thea didn't say anything.

"I didn't—" He stopped. Started again. "I went out. Onto the green. It was snowing. I stood under the tree for a long time. I don't know how long. And then I came back in, and sat down here. Right here. And I didn't say anything to anybody. I didn't know how to. She'd been—" He stopped again. "She was the first person who was kind to me. In the first life. On the fourth morning. She handed me a knife and a sack of onions."

"I know," said Thea, very softly. "You said. Under the lights."

"And after a while," said Ice, "somebody sat down next to me."

Thea's breath went out of her.

"You didn't ask," he said. "You didn't say anything. You'd seen me go out, I think, and come back in. You came and sat down here, in the dark, next to me, with a lamp. And you stayed till it got light. And you never once asked me why." He looked at her, beside him, in the glow of the lantern. "I never told you. You never knew why."

Thea was crying. Quietly, the way she did. She didn't wipe it.

"I didn't know," she said. "That first time. I didn't know anything. I didn't know you were—" She stopped. "I didn't know anything. And I sat down next to you anyway."

"Yeah."

"Because you were on your own under a beam in the dark."

"Yeah."

She laughed. It came out wet. She put her hand over her face.

"I'm so *stupid*," she said. "I'm so stupid, both times. I just keep *sitting down* next to you."

"I know," said Ice. "It's the best thing that ever happened to me."

She took her hand away from her face and looked at him. And then she leaned sideways, slowly, until her shoulder was against his, and her head was on his shoulder, in the straw, under the third beam, against the warm wall.

"I'm sitting down next to you," she said. "Now. Before. This time it's before."

He shut his eyes.

They sat there.

After a long time, Alea said, *She'd like to know how long.*

He opened his eyes. Alea was on his knee. Not hand-sized. Not cat-sized either. Something in between, small enough to be nothing much in the dark of a barn if anybody woke, big enough for Thea to see her properly. Pale teal and white, curled on his knee with her tail round her feet, looking at Thea.

"She wants to know how long," said Ice.

"How long what?"

"Till the fever."

Thea lifted her head.

*The line came on the six hundred and forty-first day*, said Alea. *The first time. I've got it. It said midwinter.* A pause. *Floor 1's midwinter, its second one, is about the six hundred and tenth day. That's where the month came from. That's when the fever comes.*

Ice told her.

"It's the four hundred and tenth today," said Thea.

"Yes."

"So two hundred days."

"About."

Thea was quiet. He could feel her doing it. The sum. The way she'd done it for nine floors, under the windows.

"We came up thirteen floors in four hundred days," she said slowly. "Going down's quicker. Isn't it. Nothing's in the way. We know where every stair is." She was still working it out. "The floors are cleared. Mostly. We wouldn't have to fight anything we didn't want to. We know the way on every single one."

"It's still thirteen floors."

"Ninety days. Maybe. If we went straight." She looked at him. "And there's this one. And this one's not finished." She looked up, into the dark of the roof, where the beams were. "And it's harvest. They need everybody. You saw them. She said she's got eleven mowers."

"I know."

"So we'd have to do this one first. Properly. Not just get to the stair. Do it." She was thinking out loud now, very quietly, against his shoulder. "Help. And then go down. And then there's the winter. On every floor we go back through. And then floor 1."

"Thea."

"I'm just working it out."

"I know you are." He looked at the dark. "I can't ask them."

She went quiet.

"I can't," he said. "We've come up thirteen floors. It took four hundred days. We nearly lost—" He didn't say who. "Every floor. Every one. And now they're on fourteen, and they're nearly at sixty, and it's going to start getting harder, properly harder, and I'm going to stand up at the table and say *let's go all the way back down to the start. For somebody on floor 1 none of you can know why I want to see.*" He shook his head. "I can't ask them that. Not for one person. Not for her."

Thea didn't answer for a while.

"You're not asking them," she said at last.

"That's what I said."

"No. Listen." She sat up, so she could see his face. "You're not *asking* them. You're *telling* them. That's different. You've done it on every floor since the Teeth. You don't ask them *will you*. You tell them *where*. And then they decide how." She held his eyes. "That's Higu's rule. You say what's true. *There's somebody on floor 1 I want to see before the winter.* That's true. You can say that. You don't have to say why. They'll never ask why." She almost smiled. "They're very, very good at not asking why. They've had a lot of practice."

He looked at her.

"And then it's on the rock," said Thea. "And it's not yours any more. It's everybody's. And they'll decide what to do with it. They always do." She paused. "And if they decide to go on up, then you and I go down. On our own. With a lantern. Somebody always knows a doctor." She was quiet. "But they won't decide to go on up."

"You don't know that."

"I do," said Thea. "I watched thirty-five people sit down in the snow."

He didn't have anything to say to that.

*She's right*, said Alea. *You know she's right. You're just frightened.*

"I'm just frightened," said Ice.

"I know," said Thea. And put her head back on his shoulder. "Me too. Not tonight, though. Not about this. Tonight we're sitting here. Before."

He leaned his head on hers.

They sat under the third beam until the watch was over, and past it, and when Gabe came stumbling along the wall in the dark at midnight to take the next watch with Deston, and found them there, in the straw, by the east wall, with the lantern turned down to a glow, he stopped.

He looked at them for a while. At the place they'd chosen to sit, in all that barn, against the cold wall that wasn't cold tonight.

He didn't say anything. He turned round and went back to his place in the straw, and got Deston up instead, and the two of them took the watch at the door, and let them be.
