Kei had the map on his knees all night, with a cup on each corner, and he didn't drink from any of them.

He'd put them there at eight to hold it down. The wind came over the rim of the eye of the dome and pulled at everything, and his map was the only map of the road of lights there was. Four cups. Kotori had filled them before she went down to the banner room. She'd said *drink them, one an hour*, the way she said things now, without a sorry on the end. He'd said he would.

They'd gone cold by nine. He hadn't noticed.

He was the eye. That was what Kei had written on the list on the board by the south door, at the top: *Kei · Higu · Mango — the eye*. He'd put himself there because somebody had to tell everybody. And he'd put Higu and John beside him because they could see further than anyone, and he couldn't see at all. Not the way they could. He could only read what they saw, and write it down, and say it. That was what a map was. Somebody else's seeing, written down so other people could use it.

Higu was on his left with her lamp, sending her Lantern up the north road every few minutes, her palm out, the word under her breath. *Ahead.* John was on his right with his little round mirror, catching the light and throwing it. *Glint.* And between them Kei sat on the ledge and listened, and wrote, and every so often leaned forward and called it down the whole height of the dome. To the round town, and the call, and sixty people and Chernn.

He'd never shouted in his life before tonight. He'd shouted more tonight than in the rest of it put together.

***

He'd marked the road as it went dark.

He'd drawn it in November, a line of ninety-four dots from the round town up to the edge, and twenty-two more past the old hall. Every lamp. All night, as Higu and John called them out, he'd taken his pen and drawn a small cross through each dot. *Twenty-two's out.* A cross. *Twenty-one.* A cross. All the way down the line. Like counting the dead. He'd tried not to think of it that way and failed.

At ten past two he'd drawn the last cross, through the first waystone, the first mile. Tiger's.

*Not yet*, Tiger had said, a mile off in the dark. Kei had heard it on the call and put his pen down and put both hands over his face for a moment. Then picked the pen up again. Twenty minutes later, John called it, *first mile's out*. Kei shouted down the dome louder than he'd known he could. *Tiger, COME IN.* And then sat on the ledge and couldn't breathe until Thea's Turn and Gigi's thorn and Azac's *open* had let them in.

Ninety-four crosses, and twenty-two. A whole road, crossed out.

And then the round town, holding. He couldn't see the north door from the ledge, not properly; it was under the curve of the dome. But he could see the fire, the glow of it on the north road, and he could see the dark. The wave. The whole night, edge to edge, come down the road of lights to a door two shields wide. He watched it hit. He watched it not get through.

At five, something stood up over the middle of the door, as tall as the dome's first landing. A shape of black against the gold. He watched it lean down at the fire. Then he watched it fall sideways off its own leg, away from the door, onto the road. And the whole wave, edge to edge, all of it at once, leaned *back*. A pace. Less.

The window had come up on the call, the way big windows did. Everybody saw it.

"*Salty*," said Kei, out loud, to the map. He'd known Salty for years, long before booth sixteen at the Cradle Commons. "Oh, *Salty*."

He wrote it in the margin of the map, in very small letters, next to the round town. *104. Right Size.* It was the only thing he wrote that night that wasn't a road.

***

At twenty past six, Bee's voice came up the call from under the floor.

"*It's gone. The big one. Under. It went into the stream.*" A breath, ragged. "*He's at nine. He's sitting down. Hyv's with him.*"

Kei didn't know what *the big one* was. He'd find out later. A lake, Bee would say. The whole lake under the old stone, standing up. And Ice had gone up the north tunnel to meet it at the stream with Hyvred two paces behind him, and pulled it into running water. Kei didn't know any of that yet. He only knew that the thing he'd been most afraid of all night was *under*. Under the town, where nobody could see. With two spaces.

And it was gone.

"*Thank you*," called Kei, down the call, to the floor. "*Bee. Thank you.*"

"*Don't thank me*," said Bee. "*I drew walls.*" A pause. "*Six walls. All night. Seventy-seven per cent.*" Another pause, and her voice went odd. "*He went up the tunnel, Kei. With a lake coming. And Hyv went with him. She said nobody alone, and she went.*"

Kei wrote nothing. He sat on the ledge with his pen in his hand and looked down through the eye at the floor of the Hall, two hundred feet below. Bee's gold dots and silver lines on the old stone, sixty of them. The iron hatch under Ama Corrie's stair, shut. Ama Corrie at her counter with her lamp, not sleeping, the way she hadn't slept all night.

And one gold dot near the north door that wasn't anybody yet.

***

At half past six, John stopped breathing.

Kei heard it. They'd been sitting shoulder to shoulder on the ledge for ten hours; you heard each other's breathing. John had sent a glint up the north road, the way he'd been doing every few minutes all night, past the north door and the wave and the first mile and on. He'd gone still. Not the stillness of a scout looking. The other kind.

"Kei," said John.

"What?"

"The road." John's voice was very odd. "The far end. The edge. Something's—"

He stopped. Higu, on Kei's other side, had sent her Lantern up after his. Kei felt her go still too.

"*The lamps*," said John, and this time he said it loud. Down the dome, to everybody. "*They're coming back on.*"

Kei put his pen on the map.

"Where?"

"*All of them*," said John. "*Behind the dark. The whole road. I didn't— I wasn't looking behind it. I was looking at the front, all night, at where it was. I never looked back up the road.*" A breath. "*They're lit, Kei. The twenty-two. The old hall. The fourth ring, the third. They're lit. They're all lit again. Somebody's lit them.*"

"*One at a time*," said Higu. Her voice was shaking. She'd sent her Lantern up the line, further than John's glint. She could see from her light, the way he saw from his. "*From the north. Somebody's lighting them one at a time. Coming south. Down the road. Behind it.*"

Kei looked down at his map.

Ninety-four crosses, and twenty-two. A whole road, crossed out, from the top of the page to the round town.

He took his pen.

He started at the top. At the edge, at the twenty-second stone past the old hall, where Thea had set her lantern in September. He drew a small circle round the cross. Then the twenty-first. The twentieth. All of them. Then the old hall. Then the road, down from the top of the page, one dot at a time, the fourth ring, the waystones, the third ring. A circle round every cross.

*Lit again.*

He didn't know how many he'd done. His hand was going by itself. Higu was calling them out, beside him, one after another, faster than he could write.

"*Sixty-eight*," called Higu. "*Sixty-seven. It's still coming. It's not stopping.*"

***

He understood it at about a quarter to seven. He'd been doing the sum in the back of his head the whole time, the way Malcolm would have. Then it came out the other end and he sat back on the ledge.

"She's been walking all night," he said.

Higu looked at him.

"It's a day and a half," said Kei. "In here. The longest night. Thirty-five hours, for us. Eleven and a bit for them out there." He put his finger on the top of the map. "The road's ninety-four miles. A day's walk is twenty-three, if you walk eight hours. Four days. Thirty-one hours of walking." He moved his finger down the line. "You could walk the whole road in one longest night. If you started when it started. If you didn't stop."

"Behind it," said John slowly.

"Behind it," said Kei. "The dark came south all at once. Down the road. Putting out every lamp. And somebody was walking along behind it the whole time, all night, a mile back, two miles, relighting every one it put out." He looked at the circles on the map. "We were looking at the front. All night. Nobody looked behind."

"*Who*?" said Higu.

Kei didn't answer. He didn't know.

But he knew one thing. He'd known it since September, since a light past the last stone that didn't go out. Since Hako had stood at the bottom of the twenty-two in November and said *hello* into the black. And the light had set itself down, and sat beside itself, looking back.

*Being carried*, he'd written on the map, very small, by the gold mark.

"Higu," said Kei. "Can you see them? Not the lamps. Whoever's lighting them."

Higu looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned to the north and held her palm out, steady, and said the word again, slower. *Ahead*. And the soft gold light went out of her hand over the rim of the eye and up the north road, past the north door and the fire and the wave. It went low over the heather this time, not high. Low, the way you'd walk. Up the road. Up past the first mile, still dark, still crossed out. Past the second waystone, the third, the fourth.

To where the lamps began again.

Kei couldn't see from Higu's light. Nobody could but Higu. He watched her face instead.

He saw it change.

***

"*Oh*," said Higu.

She didn't call it. She said it very quietly, on the ledge, to nobody. Her palm was still out. Her eyes were open but she wasn't looking at anything on the roof. She was looking at what her light was looking at, eleven miles up the north road in the dark.

"What is it?"

"*Somebody*," said Higu. "*Somebody walking.*"

"Who?"

"*Small. Old. A coat. A long grey coat, with a hood. A stick. And a lamp.*" Higu's voice was very soft. "*She's holding up a lamp. Not a lantern. A little iron one. Like the old cages. With a star on top.*" A breath. "*She's stopped at a waystone. She's gone round the back of it. She's opened the cage. She's holding her lamp up to it. To the wick.*"

A pause.

"*It's lit*," said Higu. "*She's closed the cage. She's going on.*"

[ A lamp, north. Lit. ]

The window came up on the call. Everybody saw it. Kei saw the whole round town see it, from the roof. The wall at the north door, which couldn't look up, went still for one second. Down in the Hall, Ama Corrie put her lamp down on her counter.

"*She's walking*," said Higu. "*Slowly. She's very old, Kei. She's so old. She walks like Joss. She's leaning on the stick. She's—*" Higu stopped. Her voice went. "*She's been walking all night. You can see it. She's so tired. And she stops at every one. Every single one. She goes round the back and opens it and lights it and closes it and goes on.*"

Higu was crying. Kei didn't say anything. He wrote a circle round the next cross on the map. And the next.

"*Fifty*," said Higu, through it. "*Forty-nine.*"

***

The dark began to go at about seven.

He didn't know if it was the dawn coming, or something else. The wave at the north door had been thinning for an hour. Fewer of the big ones, fewer of everything. And now, as Higu called the lamps coming back on behind it, ten miles off, then eight, then six, the dark started to sink.

It was the way Hako had said the eleven had sunk, on the moor in November, at the third ring. *Too early to stay.* The whole of it, the wave, edge to edge, all down the north road from the north door to as far as anybody could see. It began to settle. Down into the heather. Into the ground, the way it had come up out of the still water. Slowly. Like a tide going out across sand.

"*It's going*," called John, from Kei's right. "*The dark. It's going down. At the door. Salty's three, Azac's, Thea's, they're— it's going down in front of them. Into the road.*"

Kei leaned forward.

He could see it from the roof. The gold of the fire in the north door, shining up the north road. And the road, under it, coming out of the dark. Grey. The heather either side. The first stones of the moor. The line of the road going away north, empty now, and clear, the way it had been every other night of the year.

The dark had gone down into it, like water into sand.

"*Four*," called Higu, beside him. "*Three. Two.*"

Kei looked at the map. Two crosses left, uncircled, at the bottom of the line. The second waystone, two miles out. And the first, the first mile. Tiger's. The last lamp on the road of lights, the one Tiger had kept for twenty minutes, at ten past two, saying *not yet*.

"*One*," called Higu.

And through John's glint and Higu's light, and on the call, and on every stream in the round town, Kei saw it come. A window.

She came down the last mile in eight minutes. Higu said afterwards she didn't know how. *She was so slow. She was so tired. She walked the last mile in eight minutes like it was nothing. Like she'd been walking toward it for three hundred years.*

Kei watched her come from the roof.

He didn't need Higu's light for this. The fire in the north door lit the road for a quarter of a mile. At seven fifty-five she came into the edge of it, out of the grey, onto the gold. A small figure in a long grey coat with a hood, very bent, leaning on a stick. In her other hand, held up, a small black iron lamp with a star on top. Its flame was steady and gold. It was the same gold as the point of light on the edge of the world that had sat there since September and never gone out.

The road was empty in front of her. The dark had gone into the ground. There was nothing between her and the north door now but a quarter of a mile of grey road, and the fire, and the wall.

She kept walking.

Kei realised he was standing up. He didn't remember standing. He was standing on the ledge of the eye of the dome, two hundred and twelve steps up, with the map in one hand and the pen in the other. Higu was standing beside him. And John. And Kei leaned out over the rim, which you weren't supposed to do, and shouted down the call, as loudly as he'd ever shouted anything.

"*ICE*."

A pause. Under the town, a long way down, he heard Bee shout too.

"*Ice. Come up. Come up now.*" He took a breath. "*There's somebody on the road.*"

And then, because she was a hundred paces from the wall now, and getting closer, and the wall was facing her with shields up and couldn't see who it was:

"*Thea. Gigi. Xola. The left.*" He swallowed. "*It's her. The carried one. Let her in.*"

He saw the wall move. From up here, it looked very small. Nine people in a door two shields wide, with a fire behind them. And the left side of the wall, Thea's tower shield, beginning to turn.

He'd tell it to the long table, afterward, the way he told things. Plainly, with the map on the table. He'd say he saw her get to the wall. He'd say he saw the left side open. He'd say he saw the small grey figure with the lamp go through the gap, past Thea's shield and Gigi's thorn and Xola glaring. Into the north door. Past the fire, and under the arch, and into the Hall of Roads. He'd say he saw her cross the floor, under the eye, two hundred feet below him. Small, bent, very slow again now, as if the last mile had taken everything she had. Leaning on her stick, across Bee's gold dots and silver lines, toward the north end of the room. Where Ama Corrie was coming round the end of her counter, with her lamp, faster than Kei had ever seen her move.

He'd say that was all he saw.

Because the sun came up.

It came over the east road the way it came every morning. Grey, and then pink, and then gold, sudden, all at once, the way the game did dawns. It came up over the rim of the eye. The light came down through the eye onto the floor of the Hall, two hundred feet below. It fell across the gold dots and silver lines, and the small grey figure crossing them. Across Ama Corrie reaching her. Across the gold dot by the north door that wasn't anybody yet.

And the egg took him.

***

He lay in it in his room in Australia, at eleven in the morning, with the summer coming in through the blinds, for a long time.

Then he got up and found his phone.

The forum had it already. Of course it did. A hundred streams in the round town, and every one of them had been pointed at the north door at seven fifty-eight. Somebody had cut it together. The small grey figure coming out of the edge of the dark into the fire's light. The wall opening on the left. The lamp going through.

The title said: *WHO IS SHE.*

There were nine thousand replies. Kei didn't read them. He read the other thing instead, the thing the studio put up every year after the longest night. The world ranking, *Unlit returned to the dark*.

Last year, Moonlight Harvest hadn't been on it. They'd kept Fernwick, not rank.

This year, at the top, in gold:

*1 · Constellation · Carrow Cross*

And under it, a long way under, Meridian, and Sable Court, and VANTA. And a hundred more.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he scrolled down past the ranking to the other list, the shorter one. The towns. Every town in the world that had been taken on the longest night, the ones whose lights had all gone out. Last year it had been forty-one. A whole page.

This year it was nine. All of them far away, on the other side of the map.

None of them on the seven roads. Not one. Not Brackenford or Quarrymouth or Merrowgate or Millrace or Briarwick or Elmcross or the fen. Not Emberly, with its forge. Not Grimmet, with a lamp in every shaft mouth. Not Tallow Green, with a candle in every window. Not anywhere a road of lights would have taken the dark *away* from.

Everything had come to the round town. And the round town had kept.

*So the rest may sleep.*

Kei sat on the edge of his bed in Australia with his phone in his hand and the summer outside, and found that he was crying, and let himself.

Then he opened the map. The real one, the paper one he kept in his room, the copy of the copy. He found the round town in the middle of it, and the north road, and the line of dots. And next to the gold mark past the twenty-second stone, where he'd written *being carried* in November, very small, he wrote one more word.

*Home.*
