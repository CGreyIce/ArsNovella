Milli had logged out on the bench on purpose, the Sunday before, so that when he came back in, he'd come in there.

Hana had done the same, on the Friday night. They hadn't planned it together. They'd each just done it, and found out afterwards.

So they came in together, at the third dawn, on the *Somewhere* bench at the eye.

He opened his eyes in the game and she was beside him. Already. On the left, where she always sat, with her shoulder against his. The way it had been on the bench in August, when they'd picked a day in four minutes. And on the longest night, by the well, when he'd said *this is somewhere* and not known he was going to.

She was looking at him.

"Hi," said Hana.

"Hi," said Milli.

He'd seen her all day, out there. It was still the strangest thing in the world to open his eyes in the game and find her beside him on a bench made of a pole that held.

"It's dark," said Hana.

***

It was.

The whole round town. He looked over the parapet of the eye, down the curve of the dome, at the round market. Not a light. Not one. The fountain, dark. The Hart, dark. The forty-one doors with their squares of red, dark. The chestnut stall, the blue door on Spoke Street, the windows. Every one of them.

The only light in the whole round town was Kei's lamp, at the end of the bench, beside them.

Milli had never seen it dark. Not even on the longest nights. On the longest nights it had been the opposite; every light anybody owned, lit, all night. Now there was nothing. Just the dark shape of the market under them, and the seven roads going out into the darker moor. And in the middle of the market, by the fountain, a long dark line going up. The middle pole.

He couldn't see anybody. But he could hear them.

He could hear sixty people being quiet. If you've ever heard it, you know. A room full of people all holding their breath at once has a sound. It came up the curve of the dome from the market like a tide coming in.

"Oh no," said Hana, very quietly, beside him. "Oh *no*."

"What?"

"They're going to do something," said Hana. "They've *planned* something." She had both hands over her mouth. "Leaka's not even here. Leaka's *out there*. Who's—"

From the bottom of the dark, in the middle of the market, by the pole, somebody said:

"*Now*."

Jared.

***

*BOOM*.

Milli felt it in the bench. In the dome under the bench, in the stone. A sound like a bell, but flat, and heavier. It came up out of the middle of the market and rolled round the round town and off every wall. And he knew what it was, because everybody did. It was Ice's shield. The flat of his own sword into it, the way he'd done it on the hill with nine people pulling. And at New Year with twenty on the ropes.

On the beat. All at once.

And on the beat, all at once, the roof came on.

He'd seen it before. Last year, from under it. At New Year, from under it, in the wind, holding a beam. He'd never seen it from above.

Six hundred and twelve lanterns, lit in one breath. Sixty people with sixty tapers, each at a lantern, each waiting for the boom. Red and gold over the round market, all at once, where a breath ago there'd been nothing. On the seven ropes from the green pole out to the seven road ends. On the three rings of rope round. The round town in the middle and the roads going out and the rings. The whole ceiling of the Hall of Roads, laid out flat in lanterns under the eye, below them, at the third dawn.

And at the very middle, round the top of the green pole, a ring of twelve. Brighter than the rest. Each one with two flames in it, side by side.

Two flames. One candle.

He heard Hana make a sound beside him that wasn't a word.

Then the round town looked up. All of it. Sixty faces, lit red and gold, under the roof, turned up at the eye. At the bench. At the two of them.

And sixty people said, all at once, as though somebody had told them when:

"*SOMEWHERE*."

***

He didn't remember much of the next minute. He remembered Hana holding on to his arm with both hands, very tight. He remembered that he'd stood up without meaning to, and that she'd stood up with him. He remembered looking down at the roof and thinking that he was never going to see anything like it again in his life. And then thinking that he would, because it was going to be there every year. And Jared had said so.

He remembered that he couldn't see properly, and that he didn't mind.

He remembered that somebody down in the market, very loudly, had started crying. Gabe. Obviously. And that somebody else had said *Gabe*, very patiently. Rae.

"They did this," said Hana. "They *planned* this. *All* of them. In the dark. For—" She stopped. "How long did they stand there in the dark?"

"I don't know," said Milli.

"How did they know when we'd *come in*?"

Milli thought about it. Then he looked at Kei's lamp, at the end of the bench. The only light. Small, gold, on a bench made of a pole that held, beside two people who'd just opened their eyes.

"Kei," he said. "He'd see the lamp. From the market." He looked at it. "He'd see us. Sitting up."

From the bottom of the dome, very small, in the middle of the red and gold, a figure by the pole raised a hand. Kei. With his map under his arm.

Hana put her face in Milli's shoulder.

***

They went down the two hundred and twelve.

He'd never noticed how long it was. Round and round, inside the dome. A hundred and six to the first landing, forty to the second, sixty-six to the bottom. Hana in front of him, going down fast, then slow, then fast. Stopping on the landings to wipe her face and saying *I'm fine, I'm fine*. Then going on.

At the bottom, behind Ama Corrie's counter, Ama Corrie.

She was standing there with her broom, the way she always was. The Roll was shut on the counter. She'd never once had it shut, in front of them, that Milli could remember.

She looked at them for a long time as they came off the last step.

"You're late," said Ama Corrie.

"We were out there," said Hana.

"I know where you were," said Ama Corrie. She looked at Milli. Then at Hana. Then at their hands, holding on to each other, the way she'd looked at Hyvred's hands round Ice's arm in January. "Hm."

Then she did a thing Milli had never seen her do.

She came round the counter. With her broom. She walked between them and the south door, slowly, the way she walked. She swept the floor of the Hall of Roads in front of them, from the foot of the stair to the south door. One stroke. Then another. A path. Not wide. Wide enough for two.

She stopped at the south door and stood aside.

"There," said Ama Corrie. "Go on."

Hana stared at her.

"*Ama Corrie*—"

"Go on," said Ama Corrie. "They've been standing in the dark for an hour. My lanterns are burning." She looked at the swept path, two strokes wide, across the floor of the Hall. "It's not mine to write," she said. "But it's my floor."

***

They went out through the south door under the roof.

Milli would remember that part. Coming out of the south door of the Hall of Roads onto the top of the south steps, under six hundred and twelve lanterns. Looking down into the round market. Red and gold, with the green pole in the middle and sixty people under it, looking up.

And at the top of the steps, to the left of the door, in her chair, under the grey blanket: Isa.

Suzu had carried her out. She was looking up at the roof. She couldn't see it. Not the lanterns, not from there. But she was looking at it, the way she looked at the gold point in the north.

"It's very bright," said Isa. "I can see that much."

"It's a roof," said Hana. Crying. "They made a roof."

"I know," said Isa. "My mothers made one every year. On the hill. Round the lamp." She turned her face toward Hana's voice. "It always fell down. The wind. There were never enough people to hold it." She was quiet. "Is there wind?"

Milli looked up. At the lanterns. At the green pole. At the ropes.

There wasn't. Not a breath. The lanterns hung perfectly still in their rings over the round market, the flames standing straight up.

"No," said Milli.

"*Hm*," said Isa. "Then it doesn't need holding." She folded her hands in the blanket. "That happens too. Sometimes."

They went down the steps.

***

Under the roof, nobody let them go anywhere for about an hour.

Gabe got to them first, crying, and lifted Hana clean off the cobbles. Then he put her down and tried to do it to Milli, and couldn't, and cried about that too. Rae came and took him away by the arm, patiently. Kotori gave them tea. Chernn stood beside Deston, under the ring of twelve two-wicked candles at the top of the pole. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Hana, and then up at the candles, and Hana burst into tears again.

Curse was holding a lantern.

Not one off the roof. His own, small, red, in his hand. With writing on it, in his small neat hand, black ink on red paper.

*LEAKA WAS HERE (OUT THERE)*

"She made me," said Curse. "Out there. Before she got on the plane. She said if she couldn't be under the roof, her name was going to be." He held it out. "It's a label. It's true."

Hana laughed so hard she had to sit down on the fountain rim.

The round town came too.

Not the guild. The town. Milli hadn't expected it. People who lived there, who'd been there before the guild and would be there after. They came out of their doors under the roof one at a time, the way the round town did everything. Slowly, as if they'd only just noticed.

The old woman from the blue door at the end of Spoke Street came first. Small, in a shawl, with a basket. She'd once given Leaka an apple, Leaka said, for no reason, and Leaka had never got over it. She came across the market under the lanterns, through the guild, which moved for her. She stopped in front of Milli and Hana and looked up at them. She held out two apples. Red, polished on her shawl.

"One each," said the old woman. "So you don't have to share."

Hana took hers and couldn't say anything. Milli took his.

"Thank you," said Milli.

"Hm," said the old woman, who'd clearly been talking to Ama Corrie. And went back across the market to her blue door, with the square of red on it that Curse had written her.

The chestnut man came with a paper twist of chestnuts. He didn't say anything. He just put it in Milli's other hand and went back to his stall. The dyer came, and the woman from the cookshop on Spoke Street where Xola worked the fires, with a fish pie still hot. And then, last, slowly, from the smithy behind the Hart, Old Joss.

He was very old. Ina did most of the work at his anvil now. His grandfather had kept the fire, a long time ago, when one of Isa's mothers came down the north road and bent a star in it. He came across the market on a stick under the lanterns and stopped in front of them. He took something out of his apron pocket.

Two hinges. Iron, black, small, the length of a hand. A pair. Each one with a star stamped in the middle, five lines, very small.

"For a door," said Old Joss.

"We haven't got a door," said Hana.

"You will," said Old Joss. "Everybody gets one eventually." He put them in Milli's hand, on top of the apple and the chestnuts. "A door needs two. One won't hold it. Two and it'll swing for a hundred years." He looked at them both, very old, very slow. "Mind you oil them."

"We will," said Milli.

Old Joss nodded and went back to his smithy. Milli stood in the middle of the round market under six hundred and twelve lanterns. In one hand, an apple, a paper twist of chestnuts, a fish pie and two hinges. In the other, Hana.

"We haven't got a *door*," said Hana again, very faintly.

"We will," said Milli. Which was Old Joss's. He'd borrowed it.

***

Ice was by the pole. With his shield, which he'd just used. With Hyv holding his arm with both hands. Hyv was saying something into his ear, and he was going pink, and she was looking extremely pleased. He looked up as they came over and didn't say anything for a moment.

"Alice," he said. Then, to Milli: "Milli."

"That was you," said Milli. "The boom."

"Jared asked," said Ice. "So everybody'd light at once." He looked up at the roof. "So you'd see it all at once. From up there."

"We did," said Milli.

"Good," said Ice.

Hyv, on his arm, looked at Milli and Hana for a long time. Edge to edge, the way she looked at everything.

"You look *so* happy," said Hyv. Out loud. "Both of you. It's *disgusting*. I love it."

"She says that about me," said Ice.

"I say it about *you* because it's *true*," said Hyv. "I say it about them because it's *also* true." She put her head on his shoulder. "Everybody's disgusting tonight. It's the best night of my life."

***

Jared was under the beam.

Milli found him there, near the end, when the roof had been lit an hour and people had started to sit down. On the trestles, on the fountain rim, on the cobbles. Under the lanterns, eating Maigo's noodles.

Jared was at the foot of the green pole, with his shoulder under the beam.

There was no wind. There'd been no wind all night. The pole stood straight up in the middle of the round market with six hundred and twelve lanterns on it. It didn't move. It didn't need a beam. It didn't need anybody.

Jared was under the beam anyway. With his shoulder against it, and his hand flat on the pole, and his eyes shut.

Azac was standing behind him. Not with his back against Jared's. Just there.

Milli went and stood beside him, under the beam, the way he had at New Year. And put his hands on it.

Jared opened his eyes.

"There's no wind," said Milli.

"I know," said Jared.

"It doesn't need holding."

"I know," said Jared. He didn't move. "I said I'd be under it. Every year." He looked up at the lanterns. "Didn't say only when it bent."

A minute later Hana came. She put her hands on the beam between Jared's and Milli's. Where she'd been last year, and at New Year. She didn't say anything. She just held it.

The three of them under a beam that didn't need holding, under a roof that didn't need holding, in the middle of the round market, at the fourth hour of the night.

Milli thought it was the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen anybody do.

***

At the end, they went back up to the eye.

Just the two of them. Everybody else stayed under the roof. They went up the two hundred and twelve, slowly this time, not stopping on the landings. They came out onto the ring at the top and sat down on the *Somewhere* bench beside Kei's lamp. Hana on the left, where she always sat. Her shoulder against his.

Below them, the roof.

Six hundred and twelve lanterns in rings over the round market. The green pole in the middle with its two-flamed candles at the top. Sixty people under it, small, red and gold, sitting on the cobbles. And past it, the seven roads going out into the dark moor, and the moor going out to the edges of the world. And north, at the bottom of the sky, a gold point. The hill, where somebody had set a stone three weeks ago so the way would still be there.

*Somewhere with a view.*

Hana didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said: "You said *this is somewhere*. By the well. On the longest night. Before I'd finished saying *one day*."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," said Hana. "It's the best thing anybody's ever said to me." She put her head on his shoulder. "It was an accident, wasn't it."

Milli thought about it. Properly. He did that, mostly; it was why he didn't say very much. When he did say something, it was usually because it had come out before he could stop it.

"Yes," he said.

"Do another one," said Hana.

He looked at the roof. At the round town. At the view.

"We're in it," said Milli.

He didn't know he was going to say it until it was out.

Hana lifted her head off his shoulder and looked at him.

"We're in the somewhere," said Milli. "We're not looking at it from a long way off. We're not saying *one day* about it. We're *in* it." He looked at the lanterns. "We were always going to be. We just had to sit down."

Hana looked at him for a very long time.

"That was another accident," she said.

"Yes."

"*Good*," said Hana. Which was Suzu's. And put her head back on his shoulder. And they sat on the *Somewhere* bench at the eye, in it, with the view, until the first dawn came up gold over the moor and put the lanterns out.
