Gigi had decided on the first day of the walk that Carrow Cross was going to be a disappointment, and it wasn't, and that was worse.

She came in on the west road on Friday evening, nine days out of Briarwick, with her hawthorn staff over her shoulder and her boots full of the Thornlands. The road came over a low ridge and there it was, below her in the dusk. A round town, built in rings, the roofs going down in circles to a round market in the middle. And in the middle of the market, a round hall of pale grey stone gone gold, with a dome on top. Seven roads came into the town from seven directions, across seven kinds of country, like the spokes of a wheel. She could see four of them from the ridge. Every one of them ended at a door.

"I didn't say I liked it," said Gigi. "I said it's nice. Those are different. Lots of things are nice and I don't like them."

She went down the ridge anyway, faster than she meant to.

***

Kei was on the steps of the Hall.

She'd known he would be. He'd been on those steps every night for two weeks, on the call. *Carrow Cross. Here.* She'd pictured the steps a hundred times on the walk. She'd been wrong about them. She'd pictured something grand, and they were just steps, worn in the middle by four hundred years of feet. Kei was sitting on the third one with a notebook on his knee and the green tie in his hair, looking up at her as she came across the round market in the last of the light.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm on time. Everybody else is late." She stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at the Hall. The seven doors. The dome. The lamp smoke on the stone. "It's round."

"It's round."

"Everything's round here. The town's round. The market's round. You can't get lost in a round town. That's boring." She sat down on the step below him, quite suddenly, because her legs had been walking for nine days. "Where is she?"

"South road," said Kei. "A day out. She stopped at a ford to draw something."

"Of course she did."

"She said to tell you she's sorry."

"She's not sorry. She's drawing a ford." Gigi took the gold pocket watch out of her coat, the one with the leaf on the lid, and opened it, and looked at it, although it didn't tell any time that mattered in here. It ticked. It always ticked. "She'll be later than a day. The ford'll have a ruin next to it, and she'll have to map the ruin, and then it'll rain. You watch."

"You've missed her," said Kei.

"I haven't *missed* her," said Gigi. "I talk to her every night. I talked to her this morning, for an hour, about a *ford*." She shut the watch. "You can't miss somebody you talk to every night."

Kei didn't say anything. He went back to his notebook. That was the thing about Kei. He didn't argue with you when you were wrong. He just let you sit there being wrong until you noticed.

She sat there being wrong for quite a long time.

***

The old woman came out of the Hall at nine to light the lamps over the doors.

She was small and sharp, with spectacles on a chain and a ring of keys, and she looked at Gigi on the steps the way Granny Sloe looked at a gap in a hedge. Measuring it. Deciding whether it was worth the trouble.

"You're the hedge one," she said.

"I'm Gigi."

"The one who thinks it'll fall down."

Gigi looked at Kei. Kei looked very hard at his notebook.

"It will," said Gigi.

"Everything does," said Ama Corrie. She reached up with a long taper and lit the lamp over the west door. "I've kept this Hall forty years. The roof leaks in three places. The north door sticks. The east steps are going. Every winter something falls off." She lit the next one. "I keep it anyway. That's what keeping is."

Gigi stared at her.

"That's—" she said. "That's what I *say*. About hedges."

"Then you'll know," said Ama Corrie, and went round to the next door without looking back. Gigi sat on the step with her mouth slightly open. She'd met exactly two people in her life who were ruder than her and right about it, and they were both a hundred years old, and she hated it. She liked them both enormously.

"I like her," she said to Kei, when the old woman had gone round the far side of the Hall.

"You don't like anything for the first ten minutes," said Kei.

"That's what *Gu* says."

"I know. I've heard her say it."

***

Higu didn't come the next day.

Gigi had known she wouldn't. She'd said so. The ford had a ruin next to it. Higu had said so on the call at six in the morning, apologetic and delighted, with the sound of water behind her. It was an old toll-house, half fallen in, with marks on the lintel. And then, at noon, it had rained.

"You said that would happen," said Kei.

"I always say what's going to happen," said Gigi. "Nobody ever listens, and then it happens, and then they say *you said that would happen*." She was sitting on the same step, in the rain, which had followed Higu up the south road to Carrow Cross. She'd been there since her café opened that evening. She'd been there for five hours. "I'm not waiting for her."

"You've been on the step for five hours."

"I like the step."

"You said the step was boring."

"I've changed my mind," said Gigi, "about the step."

Higu came up the south road at a quarter past midnight on Saturday, her time, Gigi's time. Nearly the last hour of the café.

Gigi saw the light first.

It came over the rise of the south road in the rain, small and warm and swinging. A brass lantern on the end of a crook. For a second Gigi thought it was just somebody with a lantern, a traveller, one of the four thousand. Then the light went *ahead*. It lifted off the end of the crook and went floating down the road on its own, a few feet above the cobbles, slowly, toward the town. Toward the round market. Toward the Hall. Toward the steps. Looking.

It found her.

It stopped about ten feet away and hung there in the rain, glowing, a small warm ball of light at the height of her face. And Gigi knew, the way she knew the weather, that on the other end of it, three hundred paces up the south road in the dark, Higu was seeing her through it. Sitting on a step in the rain, in a round market, after five hours, with a pocket watch in her hand.

"Don't," said Gigi to the light.

The light bobbed, very slightly. As if somebody on the other end of it was laughing.

Then it went back up the road, and Gigi stood up.

She didn't decide to. She just stood. And then she was walking across the round market in the rain toward the south road, not fast, she was *not* going fast. And then she was going quite fast. And then Higu came round the corner of the last house on the south road, and Gigi stopped dead in the middle of the street with the rain coming down on both of them.

She looked like herself. She looked exactly like the model Gigi had watched on a screen for five years, nearly six. The soft careful face, the white ribbon bow in her hair. Except she was here, in the rain, in a long grey coat gone dark at the shoulders, with a crook in her hand and a satchel of maps on her hip. And her boots were muddy to the knee from a ford.

They stood ten feet apart in the street and looked at each other.

"You're late," said Gigi.

"Sorry," said Higu. "There was a ruin."

"I know there was a ruin. You told me about the ruin. For an hour."

"It had marks on the lintel."

"I *know*."

They looked at each other some more. The rain came down. The lantern on the end of the crook hissed, very softly, where the drops hit the brass.

"Chi," said Higu.

She said it quietly. Not loud enough for anyone else in the street to hear, though there was nobody else in the street. But it was the first time she'd ever said it out loud anywhere that wasn't a private call at two in the morning. Gigi heard it go into her, the way the cold went into you on the longest night. All the way down.

"Gu," said Gigi.

And then she crossed the ten feet, which she would never have done on any other night of her life, because she was Gigi and Gigi didn't cross streets in the rain for anybody. She put her arms round Higu, maps and lantern and wet coat and all, and held on.

Higu made a small surprised sound, and then didn't make any sound at all. She put one arm round Gigi, the one that wasn't holding the crook. The lantern swung above both their heads in the rain.

"You're soaked," said Gigi, into her shoulder.

"It rained."

"I *said* it would rain."

"I know," said Higu. "You always say."

***

Kei had stayed on the steps. Gigi noticed later, and was grateful, and never told him.

When they came back across the market, he was sitting where he'd been sitting for two weeks, with the notebook on his knee and the lamps lit over the seven doors. He looked up at the two of them coming toward him in the rain. Gigi had her arm through Higu's, which she would deny forever. Kei smiled, the small careful smile of a man measuring a dose and finding it exactly right.

"Selwick," he said.

"Here," said Higu.

"Briarwick."

"Here," said Gigi. "Obviously. I've been here a day and a half."

"Carrow Cross," said Kei, and wrote something in the notebook. "Here." He looked up. "Two more in. Kite's three days out on the north road. Tobi's on his way from Brackenford with Hyv and Bee. Drew and Tiger leave Sevenlocks on Monday." He closed the notebook. "It's starting."

Higu wasn't listening. She was looking past him, up, at the Hall. At the dome, dark against the rain clouds, with the lamps glowing on its lower edge.

"Is that the ceiling?" she said. "Inside?"

"That's the ceiling."

"Can I see it?"

"It's half past midnight," said Gigi. "The café closes at one. You've walked nine days. You're soaking wet. You've been mapping a *ruin*."

"I know," said Higu. She didn't take her eyes off the dome. "Can I see it?"

Gigi looked at her. At her face, turned up toward the dome in the rain, with the lantern-light on it and the white bow drooping wet over one ear. At the look on it. Gigi knew that look. She'd been watching it for nearly six years, on a screen, every time Higu found something that had lines in it.

She sighed. It was a long sigh, with a lot in it.

"Ama Corrie locks the doors at midnight," she said. "Everyone knows that. She's very strict." She paused. "But she likes Kei. And the north door sticks."

Kei stood up.
