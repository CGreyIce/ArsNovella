Gigi had decided on the first day that the hedge was going to be a problem, and she'd been right, and it had been a problem every single day since.

It was the biggest hedge she'd ever seen. That was the first thing. She'd come out of her little wooden room with the rain on the window into a lane of low thatched cottages, and at the end of the lane, the hedge. Taller than the cottages. Twice as tall. A great dark green wall of thorn and briar and hawthorn and blackthorn and elder, so thick you couldn't see light through it. It curved away out of sight in both directions. It went right round. All the way round the whole village, in one unbroken ring, with the cottages and the green and the duck pond and the little church all inside it, like eggs in a nest.

"I've got a hedge," she'd said on the call. "A big one. A really, really big one. The whole village is inside it." And then, because it was true: "I don't like it."

"You don't like anything for the first ten minutes," Higu had said.

She'd liked it a bit by the end of the first day. She'd never told anyone that. She liked it a lot by the end of the first week. She'd told nobody that either.

***

The woman who kept the hedge was called Granny Sloe, and she was the most annoying person Gigi had ever met, including herself.

She was tiny, and old, and bent like a hawthorn that had grown up in the wind. She had a leather apron, a pair of thick leather gauntlets that came up to her elbows, and a billhook. A short heavy blade with a hooked end, on a wooden handle worn black. She walked the hedge every day, all the way round, which took most of the morning. She looked at it the way some people look at a rug that somebody's walked on in muddy shoes. Then she came back and sat on a bench outside her cottage and said rude things about the people who'd been born since the hedge was last properly laid.

"Laid," said Gigi, on the second day. She'd taken the staff off the rack in the wooden room, a short thick one of hawthorn wood with the bark still on it. She'd taken Herbalism and Alchemy, because she'd watched Ice make potions out of a swamp for a month and thought, quite honestly, *I could do that better*. And now she was standing in front of a bench being looked at by a very small old woman. "What does that mean? Laid."

Granny Sloe looked at her for a long time.

"Come here," she said, "and I'll show you, and you'll hate it."

She hated it.

***

You couldn't just let a hedge grow. That was the first thing. A hedge left alone went tall and thin and gappy at the bottom, all its strength going up into the light. In ten years you'd have a row of thin trees with holes between them a dog could walk through. In twenty, a fox. In fifty, the dark.

So you laid it.

You took a stem, a living one, thick as your wrist. With the billhook, low down, near the ground, you cut it. Not all the way through. Most of the way. A long sloping cut, until there was only a strip of bark and wood left holding it, a hinge, thin as a ribbon. Then you bent it over. Down, sideways, along the line of the hedge, nearly flat. And it didn't die. That was the thing. It should have, cut nearly through and bent nearly double, but it didn't, because the hinge was still alive. And in the spring it grew. Not up. *Out*. All along its length, from every bud, new shoots, straight up, thick and green and thorned. A hedge laid like that came back three times as thick as it had been. A wall of living thorn so dense nothing could get through it.

"You hurt it," said Granny Sloe, "so it'll grow back stronger. And you don't hurt it too much, or it dies. And you don't hurt it too little, or it doesn't bother." She handed Gigi the billhook, handle first. "Go on. That one."

Gigi looked at the stem. At the billhook in her hand. At the old woman, watching her with her small black eyes.

"What if I cut it too far?"

"Then it dies."

"What if I don't cut it far enough?"

"Then it doesn't bend, and you look stupid."

"What if I—"

"Girl," said Granny Sloe. "You can stand there all day thinking of what'll go wrong. You'll be right about all of it. It'll still need cutting."

Gigi cut it.

She cut it too far. It died. It went brown at the hinge by the evening, and Granny Sloe looked at it and said, "Hm," and Gigi wanted to throw the billhook in the duck pond.

She cut the next one not far enough, and it wouldn't bend, and she looked stupid. She cut eleven more on the second day and four of them lived.

On the fourth day, Granny Sloe sat down on her bench while Gigi was cutting. She watched for a long time without saying anything rude. That was so unusual that Gigi stopped and looked at her.

"What?"

"You expect it to go wrong," said Granny Sloe.

"Everything goes wrong."

"That's why you're good at it." The old woman nodded at the stem Gigi had just laid. It was bent over flat along the line of the hedge, alive, the hinge pale and clean. "Anybody can cut. A hedger who expects the worst lays it careful. Checks the hinge twice. Leaves a bit more than she has to." She sniffed. "The ones who think it'll be fine, they kill half of what they touch. Every hedge I ever laid that's still standing, I laid expecting it to fall down."

Gigi looked at the hedge. Then at Granny Sloe. Then back at the hedge.

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in this game," she said.

"It wasn't nice," said Granny Sloe. "It was true. Again."

***

The other thing came on the fifth day.

It came by accident, the way the good things in this game seemed to. She was laying a long stretch of hedge by the church, near where it was thinnest. She cut a stem, and bent it, and put her free hand flat on the hinge to hold it while she pegged it down. And she said, without thinking, quite crossly, because it kept springing back: "*Lie down.*"

It lay down.

Not because she was holding it. It lay down on its own. It bent the rest of the way, smooth and slow, and settled along the line of the hedge, flat. And all along its length, from every bud, as she watched, pale green shoots came out and went *up*. Six inches, a foot, two feet, thorned, thickening, in the space of a breath. Spring, the way Granny Sloe said it came, all at once, in her hand.

She took her hand off it very slowly.

She was unbearable for about an hour. Then she sat on the bench beside Granny Sloe with the staff across her knees, quite tired. She watched the new thorn she'd grown thicken in the late sun, and listened to her pocket watch tick in the pocket of her apron, and didn't say anything at all.

***

Briarwick had kept, on the longest night, the way Briarwick had always kept: behind its hedge.

She didn't talk about the night much afterwards. Other people had better stories. Ice on his plank, Hyvred on her roof, Higu calling the whole lake from the end of a jetty. Briarwick's story was just a hedge. The dark had come up out of the ditches and the ponds in the fields outside, as it came up everywhere. It had walked up to the great ring of thorn round the village, and found it laid. Three hundred years of hedgers had laid it. It had found no way in.

Except one.

There was a gap in the hedge where the old gate had been. It had rotted out of its posts years ago, and nobody had ever laid the gap, because Granny Sloe said you didn't lay a gate. You hung one. At midnight, when the dawn didn't come, the dark had come to the gap.

Gigi had stood in it with her hawthorn staff. Granny Sloe had stood behind her with a lantern, saying rude things to the dark. And Gigi had cut a stem from the hedge on one side of the gap, and bent it, and said *lie down*. It had grown across the gap like a door closing. When something long and wet and dark had leaned on it from the other side, it had held. And when it had leaned harder, it had held. And when it had leaned all night, it had held. It had held as long as she did.

She'd held until eleven in the morning, her time, when the sun came up over the Thornlands.

That was all. That was the whole story. A girl in a gap in a hedge, holding it shut, expecting it to fall down the whole time. It hadn't.

They told Kei on Wednesday. Kei didn't say anything for a long time on the call.

He'd been the one, Gigi gathered, who'd kept six people on a call all night through the longest night, in six towns, a long way apart. *Nobody hangs up.* Higu had heard about it on Maigo's stream. Gigi had heard about it from Higu, who'd told it twice, as if it were the most sensible thing she'd ever heard. Which it probably was.

He listened to all of it. The silver lines, and the three places in the lake, and the ditches round the hedge. He asked very quiet, very exact questions. *How far apart were the three places? Which way did the line run? North? Toward what?* Higu answered every one, carefully. Gigi answered the ones about the ditches, less carefully, and corrected herself twice.

"I'd like to see the map," said Kei at the end. "All of it. If that's okay."

"I'll send it," said Higu.

"And the ditches. If you can draw where they are." He paused. "Gigi."

"What?"

"You held a gap all night. On your own. Expecting it to fall down."

"Who told you that?"

"Higu." His voice was calm and low. Gigi had known him a long time, on and off, the way you knew people from other people's streams. She'd always thought he sounded like somebody who'd never once raised his voice in his life. "That's a very good thing to be able to do."

Gigi didn't say anything. She felt her face go hot and was very glad nobody could see it.

"There's a call," said Kei. "Every night. Six of us. Maigo and Salty and Wendy and Ina and Hako and me. We kept it open through the long night and we've sort of kept it open since. Nobody's ever hung up." A pause. "Would you like to be on it? Both of you?"

Higu said yes immediately. Higu said yes to the right things immediately and thought about everything else for a week.

Gigi lay on her floor in front of her fan and thought about it.

She thought about eight people on a call every night, a long way apart, in eight towns, keeping their own. It would go wrong. Of course it would go wrong. Somebody would fall out with somebody. Somebody would stop turning up. Somebody would get bored, or busy, or sad, and the call would go quiet. And one night nobody would be on it. She knew exactly how it would go wrong. She could see every way.

*Every hedge I ever laid that's still standing*, Granny Sloe had said, *I laid expecting it to fall down.*

"Fine," said Gigi. "But I'm not doing a roll call."

"You don't have to do a roll call," said Kei.

"Good."

"You just have to say *here*."

Gigi thought about that.

"Fine," she said.

***

On Christmas Day it was thirty-six degrees, and she logged in at seven in the evening, when the day had finally cooled down a little. She walked the hedge with Granny Sloe in the snow.

There was snow in Briarwick. There hadn't been, all week; it had been cold and grey and clear. But on Christmas Day it snowed, softly, on the thatched roofs and the green and the duck pond, which had frozen at the edges. It snowed on the great dark ring of the hedge, all the way round, until the whole village was white inside a ring of dark green thorn with white on top, like a wreath somebody had laid on the ground.

Granny Sloe walked it the way she walked it every day, slowly, all the way round. Gigi walked with her. She carried the billhook and a lantern. When they came to the old gate gap, where she'd held all night on Monday, the new hedge was there. The stems she'd laid and grown across it with a word. Thick now. Shoulder-high. Thorned. A living wall where the gap had been, with snow on top.

Granny Sloe stopped in front of it and looked at it for a long time.

"Huh," she said.

"Is that a good huh?"

"It's a huh." The old woman reached out with one gloved hand and touched a thorn, very lightly, the way you'd touch something to check it was real. "Three hundred years that gap's been open. Every hedger in Briarwick. My grandmother. My mother. Me. We all walked past it. *You don't lay a gate. You hang one.*" She sniffed. "Nobody ever thought to just lay the bloody thing."

She turned round and looked up at Gigi, very small, very old, in the snow.

"Merry Christmas, girl," she said, and walked on.

Gigi stood in front of the hedge in the snow. Her pocket watch ticked in her apron, and the call was open in her ear. Seven other people were somewhere far off in seven other towns, doing seven other things, saying nothing, being there.

"Here," she said, to nobody in particular.

"*Here*," said Higu, from Selwick, immediately.

And then, one after another, like lights coming on in windows along a road: "*Here.*" "*Here!*" "*Here.*" "*Here, merry Christmas!*" "*Here.*" "*Here*," said Kei, last.

Eight.

Gigi didn't say anything else. She stood in the snow and listened to them. For once in her life she didn't think about all the ways it might go wrong. Not for nearly a whole minute.
