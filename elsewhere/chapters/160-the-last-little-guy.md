"*Hana*," said Milli, in the corner of the screen. She was in her kitchen in the Netherlands with the evening light behind her. She had a mug in both hands, and she hadn't drunk from it in ten minutes. "Hana, did you know?"

"*No!*"

"Did Jared know?"

Hana looked at her messages. Jared was at work, in a cupboard probably. He'd sent her eleven messages in four minutes and every one of them was *yo*.

"I don't think anybody knew," said Hana. "Except Salty. Salty keeps saying *told you*."

"Told you *what*?"

"I don't *know*, Milli."

They watched the rest of it in silence. Not real silence. Hana kept making small sounds, and Milli kept saying *oh* under her breath. On the screen a small woman in plain black went down on one knee and drew to full and held. And the man Hana had known for years walked straight down the line of her arrow, and then wasn't there. And then it was over, and the number in the corner said two hundred thousand, and Hana put her phone face down on the duvet and lay there looking at the ceiling.

"Hana," said Milli's voice, small and tinny, from under the phone.

"Yeah."

"Everybody's there."

Hana turned the phone back over.

Milli was looking at her from the kitchen, eleven thousand kilometres away. Calm, the way Milli was calm. Her hair was falling out of its clip, and the mug was still full.

"Everybody," said Milli. "At that Hall. In that town. Did you see the crowd? Everybody we know. Thea's lot are in now, they went last month. Ice is there. Hyv. Gabe. Xav's lot. Kei. Higu. Everybody." She turned the mug round in her hands. "Except us. And Leaka and Curse."

Hana didn't say anything for a while.

"I know," she said.

"You've been saying *after the wall*," said Milli. "Since April."

"I know."

"How much wall is left?"

Hana thought about it.

"Not much," she said.

***

The wall had been under Quarrymouth since before anybody could say.

She'd found it on the third night in the game. She'd dug round a carved stone face with half a smile in the cliff above the harbour and found a door behind it. Then a tunnel behind the door. And in the tunnel, running along one side into the dark, a wall. It was carved all over with little figures, each one about as tall as her hand, walking in a line. Every one of them was holding a light.

She hadn't dug into it. Not properly. Not at first. She'd stood in the tunnel with her double-headed pick and looked at the first few figures in the light of Jared's lantern. She'd said, *I'm not touching it until I know how*. Because it was old, so old. Ten thousand years, maybe more. You didn't just go at a thing like that with a pick.

So she'd learned how. All spring and all summer. Excavation, one careful skill at a time. Brushes and picks and little scraping tools she'd made herself, at the Jewelcrafting bench, out of the stone she dug out. She streamed every day. She'd stopped streaming three days a week in April, when she got Partner. She'd written it in pen, and found she wanted to stream all the time after that. And every day she uncovered a bit more of the wall. A figure, or two, or six on a good day. The chat came for it. Ten thousand people, some nights, watching a girl with a brush in a tunnel take the dirt off something very small and very old.

*NEW LITTLE GUY*, she'd shout, every time one came clear. *He's got a HAT.*

Some of them had hats. Some had cloaks. Some carried packs. One carried another one on his back, which had made her cry on stream for ten minutes. They walked in a line, one behind the other, all in the same direction, deeper and deeper into the tunnel, into the dark. Every one of them held a little light up in front of them. A lantern, or a torch, or a candle in a cupped hand.

She'd counted them. Three hundred and eleven, by July. Three hundred and forty by the middle of August. And every night Milli sat on the ledge outside the tunnel mouth with her knees up and asked if it was closer.

*Is it closer?* That was a thing they'd said since the beginning. Since the eighth night, on the ledge, when Milli had said it about everything. About each other. About Singapore and the Netherlands. *It feels closer in here. Two feet every night. And eleven thousand every morning.*

Now she asked it about the wall. *Is it closer? The end?*

And Hana had said, every night, *closer*.

***

She went back into the egg at ten on Monday morning, and went down into the tunnel with Jared's lantern, and started on the last bit.

She knew it was the last bit. She'd known for a week. The tunnel had been going down, slowly, for three hundred feet. Last Thursday her brush had found the far wall. The end. Solid rock, unworked, where the tunnel stopped. And between the last figure she'd uncovered and the end of the tunnel there was about an arm's length of wall still under the dirt.

"Two little guys," said Jared, holding the lantern. He'd come down with her, the way he always did. He was the roof. He'd put beams in all the way along the tunnel, drift-log beams from the harbour that held. *A beam that holds*, the game called it. Hana thought it was the nicest thing a game had ever called anybody. "Maybe three. Then that's it, Alice."

"I know."

"You okay?"

"No," said Hana. "Yes. I don't know."

She took her smallest brush out of her belt. Milli's knife, which Milli had given her to use for the fine work, because it was sharper than anything Hana could make. Curse's fountain pen, which wasn't a tool. She'd brought it into the game as her keepsake, and she held it in her other hand when she did something that mattered. She didn't know why. It just felt right to hold it.

She started to brush.

The first one came out in about an hour. A little figure with a cloak, like a lot of them, holding a lantern up. She brushed the dirt out of the lines of its face very carefully. It was looking up, she saw. Not at the lantern. Past it. Up and ahead, at something none of the others were looking at.

"*New little guy*," said Hana, quietly. "He's looking up."

The second one came out an hour after that. It was holding the hand of the first one. Hana had to stop and wipe her eyes, because some of them did that, held hands, and she'd never once got used to it.

And then the last one.

***

She knew it was the last because there was only about a hand's width of dirt left before the rock. She brushed it so slowly that the chat went quiet. Eleven thousand people, not typing. She could feel Jared holding his breath beside her with the lantern. She brushed the dirt off the top of the last figure's head first. It had no hat.

Then its face. Looking up, like the one before.

Then its arm. Raised.

It wasn't holding a light.

All three hundred and forty-two of the others were holding a light. Every single one. A lantern or a torch or a candle in a cupped hand, held up in front of them into the dark. This one's arm was raised higher than any of them, all the way up, as high as it could reach. And in its hand, instead of a light, there was a pole. And on the pole, carved so small and so fine that Hana could hardly believe anybody had ever been able to carve it, there was a banner.

A little stone banner, hanging from its pole, rippled as if there were wind in the tunnel.

And on the banner, at the very top, there was a star.

Five points. Small. Like the stars cut into the waystones on the north moor that Higu had shown on her stream in June. Like the big gold one on the ceiling of the dome at the round town, above the north door, that every silver line ran toward.

Hana sat back on her heels in the dirt.

"Jared," she said.

"I see it," said Jared.

"That's—"

"I know, Alice."

And as she looked at it, something happened that she'd never seen happen on the wall in four months. The star on the little banner had been carved into the stone. But there was something in it. Something set into the carved lines, under the dirt, that caught the lantern light. As she brushed the last of the dirt away from it, very gently, it came loose. It fell out of the wall into her palm.

It was gold. A tiny gold star, no bigger than her fingernail, old and soft and worn the way old gold went.

Hana sat in the dirt at the end of the tunnel with a gold star in her hand and looked at the wall.

Three hundred and forty-three little figures, walking in a line, all in one direction, into the dark. Every one of them holding up a light. And at the very front, at the end, one with a banner with a star on it, holding it up higher than any of them. Looking up and ahead. At where they were going.

She'd watched Higu's stream in June. Everybody had. *The old guild went north. Three hundred years ago. With their banner. Into the Unlit Country. And didn't come back.*

"They didn't get lost," said Hana.

Her voice came out very strange. Jared looked at her.

"Everybody says they went north and got lost," said Hana. "Higu said. Kei said. They went into the dark and didn't come back, and nobody knows what happened." She held up the star. "But that's not—Jared, look at them. They're not lost. Nobody's lost. They're all holding lights. Every single one. They're *carrying* them."

Jared looked at the wall for a long time in the lantern light.

"Which way's north?" he said.

Hana got her map up in the corner of her eye. The little compass in the corner of it.

The line of figures walked straight north.

***

She came up out of the tunnel at the noon dusk and sat on the ledge outside it with her legs over the edge, in the sun. The whole of Quarrymouth was spread out below her, the sea quarry with its wooden stairs and pulleys and baskets. The harbourmaster's hat was hanging on the stone hand. Milli was sitting beside her. Milli had come up from the harbour when Jared messaged her *come up, now, she's found something*.

Hana held the star out in her palm. Milli looked at it for a long time.

"Is it closer?" said Milli, finally.

Hana laughed. She laughed and cried at once, with the gold star in her hand, on a ledge, in the sun.

"Yeah," she said. "The end. It's done. I've finished the wall."

"So it's *after the wall*," said Milli.

"It's after the wall," said Hana.

Milli leaned her head on Hana's shoulder.

"Then we go," said Milli.

Hana looked down at the star. Then at the sea quarry, where she'd woken up on a ledge in March and jumped onto Milli's back with her fist in the air, crying. *Same town.* Where she'd stayed five months, digging. Where she'd got Partner, and written it in pen.

"We're the last ones," she said. "Milli. Everybody else is there. Everybody. And we're the last ones. Us and Leaka and Curse."

"I know."

"I didn't mean to be the last. I just wanted to finish the wall."

"I know," said Milli. "You finished it." She took Hana's hand, the one without the star in it. "You're not the last. You're the ones who finished something first. That's different."

Jared came out of the tunnel mouth behind them with the lantern and a beam over his shoulder. He'd taken it down from the last stretch, because he said he wasn't leaving a beam where nobody'd need it. He looked at the two of them on the ledge.

"Y'all going?" he said.

"Yeah," said Hana.

"To the round town?"

"Yeah."

Jared put the beam down. He looked at the sea for a while.

"Good," said Jared. "I was gonna say. If you didn't. I was gonna say, Alice, we're the last ones." He sat down on Hana's other side. "But you said it first. You always say it first."

Hana took Curse's fountain pen out of her belt and the little notebook she kept in her pack. She opened it to a clean page and wrote, in blue ink, the ordinary size:

*Going.*

Then she showed it to the camera floating beside her, so the chat could see. And so, she knew, could Curse, wherever he was in the Riverlands, watching with his six other viewers and the inkwell held like a bird.

***

They left Quarrymouth on Tuesday at the first dawn.

The harbourmaster came out to see them off. He was an old NPC with a beard like a sea-wall, who'd said nothing to Hana for the first month and then, one morning, *you're the one in the cliff*. He took his hat off the stone hand where he always hung it, and put it on his head, which Hana had never seen him do.

"You found the end, then," he said.

"Yes."

"My gran used to say there wasn't an end. That they went on walking for ever." He looked at the cliff. "What's at the end?"

Hana showed him the star. He looked at it a long time.

"Huh," said the harbourmaster. "Well. Somebody knew." He put his hat back on the stone hand. "Go on, then. Mind the stairs."

They went up the wooden stairs out of the sea quarry for the last time. Hana first, with her pick. Then Milli with her knife and her skinning roll. Then Jared at the back with the lantern and the last beam over his shoulder, which he wouldn't leave behind.

The road went north and west along the cliffs, then turned inland up a river valley toward the Riverlands. Four days to Merrowgate, the map said. Then another six to the round town.

They walked it slowly. Hana didn't want to rush, for once. She'd been in a tunnel for five months, with a brush, and she'd forgotten what it was like to walk somewhere that wasn't down.

***

On the second night, by a fire on the river bank, Milli said, "When we do it."

Hana looked up. Jared, on the other side of the fire, very carefully became extremely interested in his boots.

They'd been saying *when we do it* for a long time. Since before the game. Since the first time Milli got on a plane to Singapore and they met at the airport, and Hana jumped on her back. They'd been saying it out there, in the real world, on calls, at eleven thousand kilometres. One day. When it was time. When the eleven thousand was zero.

"When we do it," said Milli, "I want it to be somewhere with a view."

Hana thought about it.

"In here?" she said. "Or out there?"

Milli smiled. She looked at the fire, and the river going past black in the dark, and the stars over the Riverlands. More of them than there'd ever been over Singapore or the Netherlands.

"Out there," said Milli. "In here we already are."

Hana didn't say anything for a while. She put her hand out on the grass between them, flat, and Milli put hers on top of it.

"Y'all are disgusting," said Jared, from the other side of the fire, to his boots.

"Thank you, Jared."

"I mean it with my whole chest."

"We know, Jared."

"I'm gonna be at it," said Jared. "Out there. Whenever. Wherever. I'm gonna be there with a beam." He looked up at last, across the fire, at both of them. "Somebody's gotta hold the roof up."

Hana laughed so hard she had to lie down in the grass.

***

They came into Merrowgate on Friday at the second dusk.

It was the first time Hana had seen it outside Leaka's stream. It was exactly as loud as Leaka. A river town of painted houses on stilts, all the colours you could think of. In the middle of the river, on an island where the water split, a fair that never stopped. A carousel playing seven tunes in the same order. A great wheel turning slowly against the evening sky. Jugglers. A woman somewhere shouting *apples!* every forty seconds. And, asleep in a cage by the carousel with his chin on his paws, a very old bear.

And on the dock, at the end of the footbridge across to the island, a girl with a half-moon axe taller than she was. Her streaming headset round her neck. She was jumping up and down.

"*HANA*," shouted Leaka. "*MILLI. JARRED.*" She said it the way she always said his name, with two r's, because that was how it was really spelled and she was the only one who bothered. "*JARRED, YOU BROUGHT A PLANK*."

"It's a *beam*," said Jared.

"*WHY HAVE YOU BROUGHT A BEAM*."

"Somebody's gotta hold the roof up," said Jared, and walked straight into her hug, beam and all.

And behind Leaka, on the dock, standing very still with an ash quarterstaff, was a tall quiet man in a plain coat. He had a small glass inkwell in one hand, held the way you'd hold a bird. He looked at Hana. At the pen at her belt. At the notebook in her hand.

"Did you write it in pen?" said Curse.

Hana held up the notebook. *Going.* In blue.

Curse looked at it for a long time.

"Good," he said.
