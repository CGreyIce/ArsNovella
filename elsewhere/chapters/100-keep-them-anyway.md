Kei had been drawing dots and lines since he was small. People, and the lines between them. Who knew who, who'd introduced who, who'd stand next to who at a gate. He'd never once drawn a dot for a building.

He drew one on Saturday, at the second dawn, on the ridge above the valley, before they went down. A small round dot, halfway up the silver line, between Carrow Cross and the gold star at the top of the paper. He drew a ring round it, the way it was on the dome. Then he sat and looked at it for a moment, because he didn't know who it was.

"Ready?" said Drew.

"Hm," said Kei, and rolled up the map.

***

They'd slept in a fourth ring at the foot of the ridge, twelve posts and a stone and a star, and Tiger had lit it, and nothing had come inside. In the morning they walked across the valley on the line of the last stones. Every hundred paces now, close together, as if whoever had cut them couldn't stop. They went up the hill to the south door of the hall.

It was smaller than the Hall of Roads. Kei had known it would be, from the ridge. Three doors instead of seven: south, east, west. The north side was a blank curved wall with no door in it at all. The dome had fallen in on the north side, so that half of the great round room inside was open to the sky. Grass grew on that half of the floor. A thin silver birch had come up through the flagstones near the middle and was taller than Salty.

The other half still had its roof. Under it, the floor was dry and the walls were dark, and there was a counter. A long curved stone counter, exactly like Ama Corrie's, in exactly the same place. A beam had come down across one end of it a long time ago and was still lying there, holding off the rain from the other end.

On the dry end of the counter, under the beam, was a book.

Nobody went near it at first. They stood in the south door, nine of them, and looked. Even Maigo didn't say anything. Kei heard Hako take a breath to narrate, and then let it out again without saying a word, which was the most frightening thing that had happened all week.

"Banner room," said Gigi, very quietly.

It was where it was in Carrow Cross. A small arched room off the west side. Kei went to it first, because he couldn't make himself go to the book yet.

Iron hooks in the wall. And a paler rectangle on the stone where something had hung for a long time and then been taken down. Inside the rectangle, faint round marks, in a pattern. He didn't need Higu's sheets. He'd swept the banner room in Carrow Cross on four Sundays now, with Ama Corrie's broom, and he knew every mark on that wall by heart.

It was the same pattern. The same stars. The ceiling, sewn onto a banner, hung on a wall, and taken down.

"They took it with them," said Gigi. "The banner. Wherever they went, they took it."

***

The book was the same as Ama Corrie's. Black, enormous, older than anything. It had been wet and dry and wet again for a very long time. The pages had swollen and stuck together into a block, and the bottom third of the block was soft and green.

Kei got his long knife out. Not the edge. The flat of the very tip, cleaned first. He and Higu stood at the counter for an hour and a half, and separated the pages one at a time. Higu held each one flat with two fingers while he slid the tip of the knife along under it. Like peeling a dressing off a burn. He'd done that too, for Ysolde Marr in Hallowell, on his first week, measured to the drop.

The first pages were names.

Hundreds of them. The ink had run in the water and most of them were gone, just grey clouds on brown paper where a name had been. But some were left. Kei read them out as they came. He didn't know why. It seemed like somebody should.

"*Corwen.*" A grey cloud. "*Merrin. Thessaly.*" Three clouds. "*Oduya. Bran. Little Bran*—" He stopped. Somebody had written *Little Bran* under *Bran* in a smaller hand. "Hm," said Kei, and kept going.

There was no name at the top. Where Ama Corrie's book had CONSTELLATION in black, this one had a grey cloud the size of his hand.

"The rain had it," said Higu.

"The rain had it," Kei agreed.

***

After the names came the maps.

Page after page. Not maps of roads, or towns, or rivers. Maps of the dark. Grey wash laid on with a brush, darker and lighter, across outlines of country he half knew. *Midwinter. Midsummer. The thaw. The longest night.* Where the dark lay thin and where it lay thick. Where it came up out of the ground and how far it went. Little arrows. Little notes in the margins in a dozen different hands.

*Comes up out of still water. Not out of running.*

*Comes up where the old roads cross.*

*Comes to light, and puts it out, and goes on.*

*Once in many years it comes south all at once. On the longest night. Keep the lamps.*

Kei stood at the counter and read it with his hands flat on the stone. Behind him, nobody said anything. They'd all been on the Long Night. They'd all held something in the dark until morning, in six different towns, without knowing why it was happening or when it would stop.

Somebody had known. Three hundred years ago, somebody had walked out into the dark with a brush and a pot of grey, and drawn it.

"They charted it," said Higu. "The *dark*. They didn't chart the land. They charted the dark."

***

Then a page with a heading.

The ink was better on this one. It had been near the top of the dry end of the block, and the water hadn't reached it. Somebody had written it carefully, in a plain upright hand, very like his own. Kei found he was holding his breath.

*What we left.*

Underneath was a list. Town names on the left, one line on the right for each. Not long lines. Instructions. The kind of thing you'd tell somebody you were leaving in charge of a house.

He read the first one, and stopped.

*Carrow Cross. Keep the Roll. Sweep the banner room on Sundays. Read the ceiling by the moon.*

Behind him, Gigi made a sound.

"She sweeps it on Sundays," said Kei. His voice sounded strange to him. "Ama Corrie. Every Sunday. Her grandmother did. Her grandmother's grandmother. Nobody ever told her why."

"And her grandmother shut the eye," said Higu, "because the rain fell on the Roll. And the moon stopped coming in. For sixty years." Very quietly. "So they kept the sweeping. And lost the moon."

"Oh, that's horrible," said Gigi.

Kei read on. He read them out loud, one at a time, the way he'd read the names. And one at a time, the people behind him went still.

*Selwick. On the longest night, light the water.*

Higu didn't say anything at all. On the longest night in Selwick every boat in the town had gone out on the lake with a lantern. She'd stood on the jetty and watched them.

*Briarwick. Lay the hedge. Dig the ditches outside it, so that what comes, comes there.*

"*Ditches*," said Gigi. "The ditches. Granny Sloe said they were dug the same year as the hedge. I said what if somebody dug them on purpose. I *said*—" She stopped. She put her hand over her mouth.

*Sevenlocks. On the longest night, open every lock and let the river run.*

Tiger, who was never quiet, was quiet.

*Emberly. Feed the forge till it roars, and let them come to the hill.*

"Ina's," said Maigo. "That's *Ina's*. He's going to lose his *mind*."

*Grimmet. A lamp in every shaft mouth.*

Salty didn't say anything. He never said anything. But Kei saw him reach out and put one finger on the line in the book, very gently, under the word *Grimmet*. And leave it there.

*Orchard End. Graft the old tree. Never cut it down.*

"The *OLD PIPPIN*," said Maigo, at enormous volume, and then, much quieter, "they told us. *Never cut it down.* Mother Quince says it to everybody. I thought it was just—" He stopped. "I thought it was just a thing old people said."

*Hallowell. Whatever you give, measure it to the drop.*

Kei read that one to himself first. Then out loud. Ysolde Marr, in the physic garden on his first morning, with the adder in a jar: *poison and medicine are the same thing, in different doses*. Three drops. He'd never once, in all those months, asked her who'd taught her.

*The Waymeet. Roll the stone against the gate on the longest night. If the gate is shut, decide. Every time.*

Nobody said anything.

Then Drew said, "That's Nell's."

He said it the way he said everything, flat and steady. But Kei had known Drew for a long time, and he heard what was under it.

"That's Nell's gate," said Drew. "That's our *gate*. That's—" He stopped. He looked at the floor. "I told you that. On the call. In January. When you asked who it wasn't for. I said Nell says *if the gate's shut, you have to decide. Every time*." He looked up at the book. "It wasn't Nell's."

"It was Nell's," said Kei. "They gave it to her. Three hundred years ago. And she kept it. And you brought it to us." He looked at the line. "It's ours now. It was always going to be somebody's."

*Fernwick. Cut the reeds in song, to keep the count. Set one to watch—*

The rest of the line was a grey cloud.

Rafi had come up beside him without Kei noticing. He was staring at the word *Fernwick*.

"The reed song," he said. "Sedge's song. She sings it on the steps when she cuts. She says it's for counting. On the longest night the whole town sang it, and the guy with the book counted it, and—" He stopped. "*Set one to watch.* Watch what?"

"The rain had it," said Kei.

He looked at the grey cloud for a long time. *Set one to watch.* He thought, for no reason he could name, of Ice on the steps of the Hall of Roads on founding night, looking north.

There were more. Dozens more. Towns he didn't know, towns nobody he knew had ever been to. Every one with a line. Every one a small, plain thing that somebody had been told to do, and had done, and had taught their children to do. Even after nobody could remember why. Higu copied every one. It took her the rest of the evening.

***

The last written page was near the soft green bottom of the block. Kei almost didn't get it apart. The tip of the knife went through a corner of it and he stopped and breathed and went round the other way. It came up whole.

The same plain upright hand as *What we left*. Shorter.

*We have charted the dark as far south as it comes. It does not begin here. It begins in the north, where the line goes. Tomorrow we go to see.*

*Keep the customs till we come back.*

And then, under it, in the same hand but quicker, as if it had been added at the last moment, at the door:

*If we do not come back, keep them anyway.*

The page after it was blank. So was the one after that. Kei separated six more, very carefully, all the way down to where the green made it impossible. Every one was blank.

He put the knife down on the counter.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The wind came in through the broken half of the dome and moved the birch. Somewhere outside, a long way off, something called once across the valley and didn't call again.

"It fell down," said Gigi.

Her voice was very small. Kei looked at her. She was standing at the end of the counter with her hawthorn staff in both hands, looking at the last page.

"It fell down," said Gigi again. "They went north and they didn't come back and the roof fell in and the rain got the name. It *fell down*. I said everything does."

"You did," said Kei.

"And they kept them anyway." She looked up at him. Her eyes were wet. Gigi's eyes were never wet. "Three hundred years. Every town. The ditches and the boats and the locks and the sweeping. Nobody knew why. Nobody came back to tell them. And they kept them *anyway*. And on the Long Night every single one of them *worked*." She laughed, a small cracked laugh. "That's how it stays up. You build it like it's going to fall down. And then it falls down. And it stays up anyway, because somebody kept the bits."

Higu went round the end of the counter and stood next to her. Not touching. Just there.

"Chi," said Higu.

"I know," said Gigi. "Don't."

***

At dusk Kei climbed up onto the north wall, where the dome had come down. He could stand on the broken edge of it and look north.

The line went on. He could see the stones going away down the far side of the hill in the last of the light, closer together still, every fifty paces now, as if whoever had cut them had been running. Then they went into the dark. The land north of the hall wasn't moor any more. It was something else. Flat and black and very still, going away to the edge of the world, with no light on it anywhere.

Higu came up beside him with her lantern staff and lifted her hand.

"*Ahead*," she said.

The light went out from the staff, north, over the stones, into the black. Kei watched it go, bright and small. It went a long way. Further than he'd ever seen it go. And then, quite suddenly, something closed round it, the way a hand closes round a moth. It went out.

Higu made a sound as if somebody had hit her. She sat down on the broken wall, hard.

"It's all right," she said, before he could say anything. "It's all right. It just *went*." She was breathing fast. "It went out. Something put it out. It was so *big*, Kei."

Kei looked north for a long time. At the stones going into the black, every fifty paces. At the place where Higu's light had been.

He knew what he was going to say. He'd heard somebody say it so many times since January that it had worn a groove in him.

"Not yet," he said.

Higu laughed, shakily. "You sound like *him*."

"I know," said Kei. "I hate it."

***

They took the book home.

Gigi said you didn't take a Roll; you kept it. And there was nobody left here to keep it, and there was somebody in Carrow Cross who'd been keeping one for forty years and sweeping a room on Sundays for no reason anyone had told her. So they wrapped it in Higu's oilskin and Salty's cloak and Kei's coat. Salty carried it, all four days, in both arms, the way you'd carry somebody asleep.

On the first night, in the ring, Kei messaged Ice.

He didn't send all of it. He sent one line, because one line was the only one that wasn't his to keep.

Ice was somewhere north of a fen, at the top of a road of stone herons. It was a long time before he answered.

That was all. Kei read it four times. From anybody else it would have been nothing. From Ice, Kei had learned, *thank you* with nothing after it meant *I know exactly what that is, and I'm not going to tell you, and it matters very much.*

He got his map out in the ring, by the light of twelve lanterns, with Rafi asleep against a post and Tiger telling Hako the story of the forge for the third time. He found the little round dot halfway up the line, the one he'd drawn on the ridge without knowing who it was.

He knew now. Not their name. The rain had that. But who they were.

He wrote beside the dot, very small, in his upright hand: *They kept them anyway.*

Then he drew the line on north from it, past the dot, toward the gold star at the top of the paper. Not solid. Dotted.

*Not yet.*
