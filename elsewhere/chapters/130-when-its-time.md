Higu had said *I'll tell you when it's time* to her chat in February, under a dome, with all the lamps out and the moon coming through the eye. Then she'd said it again in April, in a ring of iron posts on a moor. And again, and again, every time somebody asked, for four months.

Her chat had made it a joke. *When's it time, Gu.* *Is it time yet.* *She'll tell us when it's time.* Somebody had made a counter. Somebody else had made the counter into a song.

On the eighth of June, a Tuesday, it was time.

***

They'd voted. That was how Constellation did things, because Kei said that was how a guild did things if it was going to be the kind of guild worth being in. Thirty-one names on Ama Corrie's black book. Thirty-one people on a call, from five countries, on a Sunday night.

"It was never ours," Kei had said. "We found it. That's all. It belongs to every town on that list." He'd stopped. "I think we should tell them. I'm not going to decide that. We are."

Twenty-nine said yes. Wendy said *it's going to go badly*, and then *yes*. Gigi said *it'll fall down*, and then *yes*.

And Ama Corrie, who wasn't on the call because she wasn't a traveller, had said it in the Hall the next morning. She'd been at her counter with the old black book open beside her own, the one Salty had carried home in his arms, swollen and green, that she'd been drying page by page for two months.

"Somebody had better keep it," Ama Corrie had said. "And somebody had better tell it. I can do the first. I'm too old for the second." She'd looked at Higu over her spectacles. "That's you, I think."

***

She went up the two hundred and twelve steps to the eye at half past nine, her time, with all the lamps in the Hall below put out.

Kei came up behind her. So did Gigi, because Gigi always came up behind her, carrying a cup of something horrible and good for you. They sat on the narrow stone ledge inside the dome, under the open eye, the three of them. The sky was dark blue and the moon wasn't up yet.

Higu turned her stream on.

She started with the ceiling.

She waited, the way you had to, for the moon. At eleven forty it came up over the rim of the eye and poured down into the dome. All the silver lines came out on the painted ceiling at once, the way they had on a night in February when nobody believed Ama Corrie's grandmother. The whole map. Lines going out from the round town across the world, to a lake, a ring of hedge, a staircase of seven stars. To a crossroads, a hill with a fire. And every line, every single one, running north. To one big gold star above the north door, off every map.

She let the orb look at it for a long time. All of it but one small corner, low down, where nine little stars stood in a ring with one turned out. She kept the orb off that. She'd never been told what it was, and she'd promised herself never to guess out loud. She didn't say anything. Her chat didn't say anything either. Nine thousand people, then twelve, then twenty, all looking at a painted ceiling by moonlight, not typing.

"Somebody knew," said Higu, at last. "That's what I said, in February. Somebody knew. I didn't know who." She took a breath. "I know now."

She told them.

She told them about a guild. Not this one. An old one, from before. So old the rain had taken its name. She told them about a ruined hall halfway up the silver line, on a hill in a valley, with half its dome fallen in and a birch tree growing through the floor. She told them about a black book on a counter there, swollen with damp, and Kei's knife separating the pages one at a time like dressing a burn.

She held up her copies of the maps of the dark. Grey wash, by season, by hand. *Comes up out of still water. Not out of running. Comes up where the old roads cross. Comes to light, and puts it out, and goes on.*

"They didn't chart the land," said Higu. "They charted the dark."

And then the list.

She'd copied it all, in the ruined hall, by lantern. It had taken her the rest of the evening. She read it out now, slowly, from the sheets on her knee, under the moon. Town by town. The way you'd read the names of people who'd kept something for you without knowing your name.

"*Carrow Cross,*" said Higu. "*Keep the Roll. Sweep the banner room on Sundays. Read the ceiling by the moon.*"

Below her, at the counter in the dark Hall, Ama Corrie was listening. Higu knew she was. She'd swept the banner room every Sunday of her life.

"*Selwick. On the longest night, light the water.*"

That was Higu's town. She heard her own voice go a little.

"*Briarwick. Lay the hedge. Dig the ditches outside it, so that what comes, comes there.*"

Beside her on the ledge, Gigi didn't move.

"*Sevenlocks. On the longest night, open every lock and let the river run. Emberly. Feed the forge till it roars, and let them come to the hill. Grimmet. A lamp in every shaft mouth. Orchard End. Graft the old tree. Never cut it down. Hallowell. Whatever you give, measure it to the drop.*"

She read them all. Dozens. Towns she'd never heard of. Towns nobody she knew had ever been to. Every one with a line. Every one a small, plain thing that somebody had been told to do, and had done, and had taught their children to do, after nobody could remember why.

"*The Waymeet,*" said Higu. "*Roll the stone against the gate on the longest night. If the gate is shut, decide. Every time.*"

She heard somebody on the call she'd left open in her ear make a small sound. Drew. Drew, who'd brought that rule to them in January from an innkeeper called Nell, and found out in a ruined hall that it had never been Nell's.

"And *Fernwick*," said Higu.

She stopped. She looked at the sheet.

*Fernwick. Cut the reeds in song, to keep the count. Set one to watch—*

Then the grey cloud where the rain had been.

"The rain took the end of that one," said Higu. "We thought it was gone. And then a friend of ours went home to a fen, and asked an old woman. And the old woman knew it. All of it." She looked out through the eye at the moon. "Her mother used to say it at bedtime. Every night. And then blow the lamp out. She thought it was just a thing mothers said."

She read the whole line.

"*Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep.*"

Higu read that twice, and found she had to stop for a moment.

She didn't say where. She'd promised. *The line, yes. Not where.* She didn't know where; she'd never been told. She had a ring of nine stars drawn at the bottom of a sheet of paper and a guess, and she'd keep the guess.

***

Last of all, the last page.

She'd held it back. She held it up now, her copy, in the moonlight, so the orb could see it.

*We have charted the dark as far south as it comes. It does not begin here. It begins in the north, where the line goes. Tomorrow we go to see. Keep the customs till we come back.*

And underneath, in the same hand, quicker:

*If we do not come back, keep them anyway.*

"They didn't come back," said Higu. "The book's blank after that. The banner's gone. We think they took it with them. North." She looked up at the gold star on the ceiling, above the north door, off every map. "That was three hundred years ago. And for three hundred years, in every one of these towns, people kept them anyway. Without knowing why. Without anybody coming back to tell them. And on the longest night, in December, every single one of them *worked*."

Beside her, Gigi said, very quietly, not to the stream, "That's how it stays up."

Higu put her hand out on the stone ledge between them. Gigi's was already there.

*Everyone's going to go and find their town's.*

Higu looked at that for a long time, under the moon. Two hundred and eighty thousand people. More watching her than had ever watched anything she'd ever made. All of them, at once, starting to wonder what the old woman on the corner of their own town did every evening, and why.

She'd been a cartographer since the first week. Master Ennis, going blind in a ruined observatory on a lake, had taught her that a map was a promise. *You draw what's there so somebody else can find it.*

She thought: *this is a map.*

"Go and ask," said Higu, to the orb, to all of them. "Your town's got one. Every town's got one. Ask the old ones. Ask the ones who do the thing every evening that nobody understands. They'll say *it's just what we do*." She smiled. "Ask them anyway. Then keep it. Even if you don't know why."

She turned the stream off.

The moon was right over the eye now. The silver lines lay across the whole ceiling, running north. Below, at her counter in the dark, Ama Corrie had put her spectacles down on the black book and her hand flat on top of them.

Kei, on Higu's other side, let out a long breath.

"Hm," said Kei.

"*Hm*?" said Higu. "That's it? Three hundred thousand people, and *hm*?"

"It's a big *hm*," said Kei.

Gigi laughed. Short and surprised, like something breaking. Then she held out the cup she'd carried up two hundred and twelve steps.

"Drink that," said Gigi. "It's horrible. You'll need it. Tomorrow's going to be worse."
