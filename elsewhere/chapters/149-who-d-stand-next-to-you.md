Drew watched feet.

He'd told Kei that once, in the spring, when Kei was trying to work out what everybody in the guild was for. Tiger was the yes. Kei was the not tonight. Gigi was the *it'll fall down*. Wendy was the running toward a light. *And I'm the one who watches the feet*.

Kei had written it down. He wrote everything down. He hadn't understood it, and Drew hadn't explained, because it wasn't the kind of thing you could explain. It was just that people lied with their faces all the time and almost never with their feet. A man could stand at a gate and smile and say all the right words, and his back foot would be turned half toward the road, already leaving. A girl could stand there scowling with her arms folded, saying *I don't care either way*, and her toes would be on the very edge of the step, as close as she could get without asking.

Drew had learned the trick of it hosting tournaments, years ago, on the other side of a screen. You couldn't see feet through a screen. But you could see the same thing in how twenty players came into a lobby: who readied up at once, who typed too much, who went quiet. You could tell which ones were going to fall apart in the second round. It had never once been the ones who looked nervous. In the game, it turned out, the same thing lived in the feet.

He watched feet. He was watching six pairs of them now, and four hooves, come round the curve of the south road into the round market.

***

They came slowly. Hyvred had been right about that. Drew had seen a lot of people come up the south road to the gate in eight months, and most of them came fast. They came running at the end, or striding, or at least walking that bit too quickly that meant *please let this be the place*. Rafi had come up it at a run in June with his big sword banging on his back. The three muddy kids from the moor had come up it yesterday at a stumble, holding each other up.

These six came at a walk. An even, steady, unhurried walk, the pace of people who'd walked eight days and decided not to change it for the last hundred yards.

Drew looked at the one at the back first, because that was where the pace was coming from. Small, round-faced, with a long spear held upright like a staff and a patched cloak. PenguChi. Her feet were planted flat and easy. She wasn't going anywhere she didn't want to go, and she wasn't in any hurry to get there. Good.

Then the two in the middle. A boy with a bow and a lot of hair, talking. Drew couldn't hear what. And a girl with black cat's ears on her head and a staff, saying *mm*. Their feet were all over the place, in the way of people who were nervous and excited and hadn't decided which. They were walking slightly toward each other, so their shoulders touched every few steps. Xyro and Tif. Tif2. Ice's head mod, who'd counted every healer in a fen in December. Drew knew her name from the chat; he'd never seen her face. Good.

Then the tall quiet one with the glaive. Slep. His feet were the steadiest thing in the whole market. He walked like a man who'd already worked out where he'd stand when he got there, and was simply going to go and stand in it. His left hand hung loose by his side, and every few steps it came up and touched the back of the shoulder of the girl next to him. Then it went down again. Good.

Then that girl. Pink gills at her neck, wide open, fluttering. A trident over her shoulder. Xola. She was walking at the very edge of her pace, right at the front of her own feet, leaning a little forward. It wasn't hurry. It was the way you walk when you're half a step ahead of somebody so that whatever's coming hits you first. Her eyes were going all over the steps. Every face. Looking for one.

Drew saw her find it. Saw the gills flare. Saw her feet not change at all.

And then the one in front.

He looked at Thea's feet for a long time.

She was carrying a tower shield on her back that was nearly as tall as she was, and a hammer at her hip. Her face was very pale and very calm. He'd seen that face on stream, a few times. Gigi had shown him, once, without saying why. But it was her feet he watched.

They weren't braced. That was the first thing. Most people coming up to something they were frightened of braced themselves, without knowing, the weight going down into the heels. Thea's weight was on the balls of her feet. Loose. Ready to give, a little, and turn.

Drew thought: *oh*. Somebody taught her that.

And then: *she's not here to fight anyone*.

***

They came to the bottom of the steps and stopped.

Wendy didn't move. Wendy never moved. She stood in the gate with her hands in her pockets and looked down at them. Over the months she'd become something the round town told stories about, a woman who stood at the top of the steps and asked one question. Kite was at her left, Rafi at her right. Bram and Joy and Tobi below. And the whole of the steps behind them, full of people pretending not to watch.

"Hi," said Wendy.

"Hi," said Thea.

"I'm Wendy," said Wendy. "I'm the gate. I'm going to ask you all one question. It's not a test. There's no right answer. Whatever you say, you can come in. It's just—" She paused, the way she always paused here. "It's just a thing we like to know."

"We know," said Thea. "We heard."

"Everybody's heard," said Wendy. "It's gone very badly." She took her hands out of her pockets. "Who'd stand next to you?"

There was a small silence on the steps.

And then, before any of the six could answer, Patience walked forward.

She walked out from behind Slep's legs, across the bottom step, and stopped right in front of Wendy. A ewe, black-faced, a bit muddy, looking up at the gate with an expression of enormous seriousness.

Wendy looked down at her.

Drew saw Wendy's feet do something they hadn't done in eight months on that step. They shifted. Just a fraction. The way your feet shift when you're trying very hard not to laugh.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy, to the sheep.

Patience looked at her for a long moment. Then she turned round, walked back across the step, and stood with her side pressed against Slep's leg.

"Fair," said Wendy.

Somebody on the steps behind Drew made a noise like a kettle. It might have been Niko. The three muddy kids at the side of the steps had their hands over their mouths. And Drew saw Xola's mouth twitch, despite everything. Just once.

***

Wendy went along them.

"Slep," she said. She knew all their names. She'd watched, the way everyone in Constellation had watched, all spring. "Who'd stand next to you?"

"Xola," said Slep. Just that. He said it the way you'd give your own name.

Wendy nodded. "Xola?"

"*Thea*."

Xola said it hard. She said it like a word you throw. And as she said it her eyes went up the steps, past Wendy, past Kite and Rafi, to a step at the side in the sun. To the woman standing on it with her hands behind her back.

Gigi didn't look away. Drew watched her feet. They didn't move.

"Okay," said Wendy, mildly. "Xyro?"

"Tif," said Xyro, at once.

"Xyro," said Tif, at the same moment. "And Kotori. Is two allowed? She's not here. She's underground. But she'd stand next to me. She did, in May. She's the reason I can do any of it."

"Two's allowed," said Wendy. "We had a man say *everybody* once."

"Calvin," said Kite.

"Calvin," agreed Wendy. "PenguChi?"

PenguChi considered the question for so long that a pigeon landed on the step beside her and walked off again.

"Nobody," she said, eventually. "I'm at the back. I don't stand next to anyone. I stand behind everybody."

Wendy thought about that.

"That's next to everybody," she said. "From a different side."

PenguChi thought about *that*. It took her nearly as long.

"Okay," she said. "Yes. I'll have that one."

And then there was only Thea.

***

Drew had been waiting for it. Everybody on the steps had been waiting for it, he thought. The whole round town, probably. Hobb the carter and his betting book at the Hart. The three muddy kids from the moor, who'd told everyone that AltheaVT didn't know who'd stand next to her. Higu, up on the roof, where she'd chosen to be.

Wendy looked at Thea.

"Thea," she said. "Who'd stand next to you?"

Thea didn't answer straight away.

She stood at the bottom of the steps with the great shield on her back and her hands at her sides. And she looked up. Not at Wendy. Past Wendy, past the gate, to a step at the side in the sun.

To Gigi.

The steps went very quiet. You could hear the fountain in the market behind them. You could hear the pigeons up in the eye of the dome.

Drew watched Thea's feet.

They didn't go back. That was what he was looking for, and they didn't. Her weight stayed forward, on the balls of her feet, loose. She was looking at the person she'd spent a year being frightened of, a person she'd walked eight days toward. Her feet didn't do the thing feet did when they wanted to leave.

They didn't go forward either. They stayed exactly where they were.

"I don't know yet," said Thea.

She said it to Wendy, but she was still looking at Gigi when she said it.

"That's the truth," said Thea. "I've been asked it three times on the road. I didn't know any of the times. I thought I'd know when I got here. I don't." She took a breath. "I know who's standing next to me *now*. I know who's walked eight days next to me. That's easy. But that's not what you asked. You asked who *would*. And I don't know who would. Not yet." She looked back at Wendy. "I came to find out."

Nobody on the steps said anything.

Wendy turned her head and looked at Drew.

She did that sometimes. Not often. Once or twice in eight months, when somebody gave an answer she didn't know what to do with. She'd look across the gate at Drew on the edge, and he'd look at their feet, and decide.

Drew didn't need to look at Thea's feet again. He'd already seen them.

"That's a real answer," said Drew.

Wendy nodded slowly.

"Gate's open," said Drew. "Not my rule."

"Gate's open," said Wendy. And she stepped aside.

***

Thea came up the steps.

She came up them slowly, at the same unhurried pace she'd walked eight days at. Xola was at her right shoulder and Slep on Xola's other side, and the other three behind, and the sheep. She came up past Tobi and past Bram and Joy, up past the gate, past Wendy, past Kite and Rafi.

Gigi's step was at the side, a little higher.

Thea's feet stopped two steps below it.

Drew watched. He couldn't have looked anywhere else if he'd tried. Gigi's feet on her step, side by side, very still. He'd known Gigi for eight months, and he knew her feet better than he knew her face. He knew what they did when she was about to say *it'll fall down*. They went back half an inch, both at once, as if she were stepping away from whatever she'd just built before it could land on her. He'd seen them do it a hundred times.

They didn't do it.

Gigi stood on her step with her hands behind her back and her feet flat. She didn't step back. She didn't step forward either. She just stayed where Thea could see her and walk past her, and let Thea decide.

"Hi," said Gigi.

Her voice was very quiet. Drew didn't think anybody further away than him could hear it.

"Hi," said Thea.

Silence. The fountain. The pigeons.

"There's a chair," said Gigi.

"I heard," said Thea.

Another silence. Drew watched Xola's knuckles on the trident go white, and then, as Slep's hand came up and rested on her shoulder, go pink again.

"It's by the window," said Gigi.

Thea looked at her for a long time.

"I'd like to see it," said Thea.

And Gigi stepped aside.

Not much. Half a step, to the left, off the line of the stairs. Not leading Thea in. Not following. Just making the way clear, the way you move out of a doorway so somebody can come through it on their own.

Thea came up the two steps. She went past Gigi, close enough to touch, without touching. Drew watched her feet go past Gigi's feet. And on the step above, with her back to Gigi, she stopped.

"Not now," said Thea, without turning round. "I can't now. But later. Can we talk? Later?"

Gigi's feet stayed where they were.

"Whenever you want," said Gigi. "Or never. Never's allowed."

Drew heard something in that. He didn't think Gigi heard it herself. It was Higu's voice, nearly word for word. *No hurry. Nothing's allowed. Never's allowed.* Gigi had sat on a roof with Higu for months, and some of Higu had come down the stairs with her.

"Later," said Thea.

And she went on up the steps and through the south doors into the Hall.

***

Xola came past next.

She came past Gigi's step without slowing down. And then, exactly level with it, she stopped, the way Drew had known she would from the moment he'd seen her feet in the market.

She turned her head and looked at Gigi.

Gigi looked back.

Drew had seen a lot of looks at that gate. He'd never seen one like this. There was a whole year in it, on both sides, and it wasn't the same year. Xola's year had been on the other end of a phone, every night. Gigi's had been the one the phone was about. And neither of them said any of it. They just stood there on the steps of the Hall of Roads, two feet apart, and looked.

"Xola," said Gigi.

"Gigi," said Xola.

That was all. Xola's feet didn't move toward her and didn't move away. They just stood, very firm, very planted. The feet of somebody who'd decided exactly where the line was and was standing on it.

Then Xola went on, after Thea, into the Hall.

And Slep, behind her, as he came level with Gigi's step, stopped too.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Gigi for a moment with his calm, waiting face. Then he nodded at her, once. A small, plain nod, the kind you give a stranger on a road. Then he went on after Xola, with the sheep at his heel.

Gigi watched him go with an expression Drew couldn't read at all.

PenguChi came last, slowest, at the back. She didn't stop at Gigi's step. She just looked at her as she went past, the way she looked at everything, taking it in. On the top step she said, to nobody in particular, "Okay." Then she went inside.

***

When they'd all gone in, and the steps had started talking again, Drew went and sat on the step beside Gigi's. Not on it. Beside it.

She was still standing. Her hands were still behind her back. He could see now that they were shaking, badly.

"Your feet didn't move," said Drew.

"I wanted them to." Gigi's voice was very flat. "The whole time. I wanted to go back up two hundred and twelve steps and never come down. When she looked at me from the bottom. When she said *I don't know yet*. When Xola—" She stopped. "My feet wanted to go the whole time."

"I know," said Drew. "That's what made it count."

Gigi sat down, suddenly, on her step, as if her knees had decided it for her. She put her face in her hands.

"*I don't know yet*," she said through her fingers. "She said *I don't know yet*, Drew. To Wendy. Looking at me."

"Yeah."

"That's—" Gigi didn't seem to know what that was.

"That's honest," said Drew. "That's the first honest thing anybody's said at that gate about who they'd stand next to, in eight months. Everybody else has an answer ready. She didn't. She said she came to find out." He looked out at the market, at the fountain, at the place where the south road came round the curve. "It works both ways, Gigi. I said that to you in June. She came here to find out who'd stand next to her. That means somebody gets to be the answer."

Gigi didn't say anything for a long time.

"It'll fall down," she said, into her hands.

"Probably," said Drew.

"I'm going to build it anyway."

"I know," said Drew. "That's why you were on the step."

***

From inside the Hall, through the south doors, he heard Ama Corrie's voice. It carried, the way it always did, across the whole floor.

"Is that a *sheep*?"

And a pause. And then Slep, low and steady.

"She might be."

"Wipe her feet," said Ama Corrie.

Drew got up and went to the doorway, because he wanted to see this.

The six were at the counter. Ama Corrie was behind it with her pen in her hand, looking at the sheep over her spectacles with the face of a woman who'd seen a great deal in forty years and was adding this to the list. And Thea was holding something out to her across the counter. Something long, wrapped in sacking, tied with string.

"This is from Sedge," said Thea. "Of Fernwick. She said to give it to you."

Ama Corrie put down her pen.

She untied the string with her old careful fingers and folded back the sacking. Inside there was a broom head. Big and pale and bound tight, made of something fine and dry and silver-gold that caught the light from the eye.

Ama Corrie looked at it for a long time.

"Fen reed," she said.

"She said to tell you she heard it's kept," said Thea. "And that fen reed's better for under the chairs. Birch catches. And you're to use it on a Sunday and think of a fen."

Ama Corrie put her hand flat on the broom head, the way Drew had seen her put her hand flat on the old Roll when Salty laid it on the counter in the spring. She left it there.

"She's right," said Ama Corrie, after a while. "Birch does catch. I've been saying it for forty years." She looked up at Thea. "Did she say anything else?"

"She started to," said Thea. "And then she said, *nothing. She'll know.*"

Ama Corrie was quiet.

Then she nodded, slowly, once, the way you nod when someone a long way away has said the thing you'd have said.

"I know," said Ama Corrie.

She wrapped the broom head back up in its sacking, carefully, and put it under the counter. Then she picked up her pen and opened the Roll to the page where the next name would go.

"Not today," said Thea, quickly. "I'm sorry. Not today. We'd like to—we want to see the chairs. First."

Ama Corrie looked at her over her spectacles.

"Nobody signs on the day they come in," she said. "Not in my Hall. You'll want to sleep first, and look at the ceiling, and eat something that isn't off a road. The book'll be here." She put the pen down again. "The chairs are through there. By the window. Mind the sheep on the floor; it's waxed."

Drew watched the six of them go through into the banner room. Thea first, Xola at her right, Slep, and the others, and Patience walking very carefully on the waxed floor. He watched them stop in the doorway, all at once. They were looking at six chairs by a window at the far end of a long table, swept and polished and waiting.

He didn't follow them in. It wasn't his.

He went back out to the gate.
