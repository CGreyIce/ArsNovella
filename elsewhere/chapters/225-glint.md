John had used the mirror for a year and a half to see things, and never once to be seen.

He'd been given it in the first week, at a mountain tower at the end of a pass, by an old woman who'd taught him to catch the sun in it and throw it. *Glint*, the game called it. You held up the mirror and said the word, and a little bright point of light went where you sent it, as far as you could throw it, and you saw what it saw. He'd thrown it across valleys and down wells and into caves. And once, on the longest night, out past the last lamp, into a dark that was moving. He was a scout. It was what scouts did.

He'd never read the mirror's own window properly until June. It had been there the whole time, under the skill, small, the way the game put things it didn't think you'd need.

[ *A signal mirror* · *It was made to be seen from a long way off. By somebody who was looking.* ]

*To be seen.* Not to see. He'd stood on the shingle at the Bell Above all night, throwing glints at a tower he couldn't see into, and only somewhere around three in the morning thought about that line. And then, for no reason he could explain, he'd stopped saying the word and just tilted the mirror at the lamplight and flashed it. Plain light. At nothing. In case.

Nobody had flashed back.

On Thursday night, at the end of the north mole of Masmark's north-west harbour, somebody did.

***

The boats were out on the lake.

Forty of them, the fish man said. Small ones, with a lamp at the bow and nets in the stern, and men in them who'd fished Great Lake Masmark the way their fathers had and their fathers' fathers. For three days they'd come in at the first dawn to the north-west harbour and found the red ship across the gap between the moles. And turned round, and gone away round the rim to the east harbours, where there were other fish men and other carts.

On Thursday, at the second dusk, the fish man had gone out to them.

John had watched him go. In his own boat, the one he'd lent Constellation in March and said *bring it back*. On his own, rowing, out past the end of the north mole into the open lake, in his apron. He hadn't said where he was going. He'd just untied the boat and got in and gone.

He'd come back an hour later and walked up the north-west road past his empty cart. He'd stopped at the netloft door, where Wendy was, and looked past her into the netloft at John.

"The one with the mirror," said the fish man.

John stood up.

"They'll come at the third dawn," said the fish man. "All of them. They don't know where the mouth is. Nobody's used it in two hundred years." He looked at the mirror on John's belt. "Show them."

***

So John stood at the end of the north mole at the third dawn, with the mirror in his hand. The red ship was fifty paces off, across the gap, with lamps on its rail and men in dark red leaning on it, watching him.

He wasn't on his own. Hyvred was beside him.

She'd asked. At the netloft, at the second dusk, when the fish man said *show them*. She'd stood up from the bench under the wavy window and said *I'll go with him* before anybody else had said anything. And Dec had looked at her, and nodded, once.

"I can see them all," she'd said, to John, on the walk out along the mole. "At once. In the dark. All forty. I can tell you which one's where." She'd paused. "You flash. I'll tell you where to flash."

So she stood beside him at the end of the north mole in the dark, with the lake going away from them black to the edge of the world. And the city lit all the way round it. And out on the black, very small, forty lamps.

"*There*," said Hyvred. "The nearest. Left of the ship. Two hundred paces. Flash it."

John put the mirror to his eye, the way he'd learned on the mountain. Through the iron sight. The lamp behind him, the one Salty had set on the end of the mole, threw its light past his shoulder into the mirror. And he tilted it, very slightly, until the little bright spot it made went out across the water and found the boat.

He flashed it. Once, twice. Long, short. The way the fish man had told him. *The old way. They'll know it. Their grandfathers will have taught them.*

Out on the black, the nearest lamp stopped.

Then it moved. Toward them. Toward the base of the north mole, where it went down into the water. Where, under the water, under the stone, very old, there was a mouth.

"*Oh*," said Hyvred.

John didn't say anything. He was holding the mirror very still. His hand was shaking.

Somebody had flashed back.

***

He'd think about it for a long time afterward. A year and a half of throwing light at things to see what was there. And one night on a shingle, flashing it at nothing, plain, in case. Because the window said *by somebody who was looking*, and he'd thought, *maybe one day*.

And now forty boats were coming across a black lake toward a mouth in the rock they couldn't see. Because he was showing them where.

"*Next*," said Hyvred. "Right. Behind the first. Three hundred. He's turning the wrong way, flash him, *flash him*—"

He flashed him.

The boat turned.

***

Under the mole, the Undercut was lit.

Dec had done it on Wednesday, after the clearing. John had gone down with him to see. A line of lamps along the ledge, every twenty paces, all the way from the arch under the moles to the cellar grate, four hundred and something steps. And between the lamps, strung along the wall of the channel at the height of a man sitting in a boat, a rope.

Straw-coloured. Hand-twisted. Thick.

From the rope-walk at the top of the lane, under its red cloth. Dec had gone in on the Thursday before and bought a coil, the first since February, and brought it back to the netloft and put it on the floor under the hooks. Wendy had asked what it was for. *I don't know yet*, Dec had said.

He knew now. He'd strung it along the channel wall, all four hundred and something steps of it, so a man in a boat in the dark could put his hand on it and pull himself along. Even if the lamps went out. Even if he'd never been in the Undercut before and his grandfather's grandfather was the last one who had.

John had watched him do it. Dec had tied every knot himself. He hadn't said anything the whole time.

***

The first boat came through the mouth at a quarter past midnight.

John didn't see it. He was on the end of the mole, flashing. But he heard about it afterward, from Rafi, who'd been on the ledge at the arch, holding a lamp. A small boat with a lamp at the bow and an old man in it, ducking under the arch into the channel. With his eyes very wide. Putting his hand out, in the dark, and finding a rope.

"He said a word," said Rafi. "I don't know what it was. The game didn't translate it." He was grinning. "But I think it was *grandfather*."

The boat went along the channel, hand over hand on the rope, under the north-west quarter, under the lane and the netlofts and the harbour. Past the lamps, past Azac's sulking thing in its hollow. To the end, where the channel narrowed. Where the old iron grate had been, until Salty and Azac and Jun Liang had lifted it out on Wednesday night and leaned it against the cellar wall, with a noise like a bell falling down a stair.

Into the netloft's cellar.

Where the whole of the lane was waiting.

***

John came in off the mole at four, when the last boat had gone through the mouth, and went down the ladder.

He'd never seen the cellar like it. He'd never seen anything like it.

It was full. Boats, six of them at a time, nose to stern in the round stone room with the water round their keels, lamps at their bows. Old men in them handing up baskets. And up the ladder, through the trapdoor, out of the corner of the netloft by the wavy window, a line. A chain of people, one above the next, passing baskets of fish hand to hand. Up the ladder, through the netloft, out of the door, down the lane.

Constellation. All twenty-two of them. Kei on the ladder, with his map case on his back, passing baskets. Gigi and Thea side by side on the netloft floor, passing them between them without looking at each other, the way they did everything now. Salty at the door, because he could lift two at once.

And not just Constellation.

The lane.

The two old netmender sisters, from the last houses on the long row, sitting on the netloft step passing baskets with their net-needles stuck in their hair. The baker. Abel the boatbuilder, with sawdust in his eyebrows. The old man from the rope-walk and his grandson, from under the red cloth. The chandler, from under his. The woman from the smokehouse, who'd sold her lease on the Tuesday for four hundred gold and was now standing in the lane at four in the morning passing fish toward her own door.

Everybody. The whole quarter. The ones who'd sold and the ones who hadn't. In a line down the lane in the dark, passing fish.

And at the bottom of the north-west road, beside his cart, under its striped awning, the fish man. Taking every basket as it came. Laying the fish out on the ice one by one, the way he'd laid them out for three hundred years.

His cart was full.

John stood at the bottom of the ladder in the cellar with the mirror in his hand and looked up through the trapdoor at the line of people going away into the lamplight.

Nobody had told them to come. Nobody had asked. Dec hadn't said one word to any of them all week about the Open Hand, or selling, or the ship. He'd bought some rope. He'd said *good evening*.

They'd come anyway.

***

The red ship didn't move on Friday. It sat across the harbour mouth all day with lamps on its rail, the way it had all week. But on the rail, at the first dawn, John saw a big man in dark red with a big face, red from the wind. Looking down at the bottom of the north-west road, at a fish cart under a striped awning, full.

He stood there a long time.

Then he went below.

"He's angry," said Hyvred, on the netloft step, looking at the ship. All of it, edge to edge.

"Yes," said Kei.

"He's going to find the mouth."

"Yes," said Kei. "Tonight, probably. He's got eleven thousand people. One of them will have seen a boat go under the mole." He was quiet. "And then he'll put boats across that too."

"Can he?" said Wendy.

"It's the lake," said Kei. "Anybody can put a boat anywhere on the lake." He looked at his map. The new sheet, Masmark, with the north-west harbour on it and two moles and a red mark across the gap. And now, under the north mole, in pencil, a thin line: *the Undercut*. "It's not against the rules. It's just in the way."

Nobody said anything.

Then Kei took a letter out of his case. A blank one. And wrote on it, at the long table, while they all watched. Not long.

John read it over his shoulder. He didn't mean to. It was just there.

***

The Open Hand's boats came at the second dusk on Friday, round the end of the north mole, six of them. Low, fast, with red lamps. Making for the place under the stone where the mouth was.

John saw them from the end of the mole, where he was standing with the mirror and Hyvred, waiting for the fishing boats.

"*Six*," said Hyvred. "Red lamps. Coming round the end of the mole. They're going to sit on the mouth. They're going to *anchor on it*—"

They were nearly there. A hundred paces. Fifty.

And then from the west, along the rim, out of the dark, came three enormous blue barges, with ten rowers each.

John had seen them at the Crown Below and the Bell Above, going out to the island in the dusk. Deep blue, with one gold line from bow to stern. Enormous, slow, the biggest boats on the lake after the red ship. Rowed in perfect time, thirty oars going in and out of the water together, the way the twelve Ringers had pulled their ropes.

They came round the end of the north mole in a line and stopped.

Between the Open Hand's six red lamps and the mouth of the Undercut.

They dropped anchor. All three. Fore and aft. Across the water in front of the mouth, side by side, with lamps on their rails, blue and gold. So close to the mole you could have stepped from the stone onto the nearest one.

And on the nearest one, at the rail, very small, in plain blue with no gold on her anywhere: Aurelie.

***

The six red boats stopped.

They sat in the water fifty paces off with their red lamps, looking at three blue barges they couldn't get past. Nobody moved.

Then a voice came across the water. Big. Bigger than the boats.

"*MERIDIAN*."

The red ship. Marrok, on its rail, across the harbour mouth, shouting.

"*This isn't your quarter*," he shouted. "*This isn't your harbour. This has nothing to do with you.*"

Aurelie didn't shout back. She didn't need to. It was very quiet on the water at the second dusk, and her voice was small and clear, and it carried.

"No," she said. "It's theirs."

Then she turned her back on him. On the red ship and the six red boats. She stood at the rail of her barge facing the mole, and the mouth, and the end of the mole, where John was standing with a mirror in his hand.

She looked at him.

"You're the one with the mirror," she said.

"Yes," said John.

"Show them," said Aurelie. "We'll stay till they're in."

***

They stayed till they were in. All forty boats, through the mouth, under the mole, along the rope, into the cellar. All night, with three blue barges anchored across the water in front of them like a wall with lamps on it. And the lane passing fish till dawn.

The Open Hand's six red boats went away round the end of the mole at about two. Nobody saw them go. They just weren't there any more.

At the first dawn, the red ship weighed anchor.

John watched it from the end of the mole. The great red sail coming down off its yard, the black open hand painted on its hull turning slowly away from the harbour mouth. Out into the deep water, out past the end of the mole, out toward the island. Where it had sat since March, too big for any harbour.

The gap between the moles was open.

And through it, at the first dawn, the way they always had, the fishing boats came in.

***

Marrok came to the netloft door on Saturday at the second dusk. On his own.

John was on the bench under the wavy window. He saw him come down the lane. Not in step, not with forty. One big man in dark red with a big face and a black open hand on his chest, walking down a lane built for net-makers and fish, past twelve doors with red cloths on their latches.

Wendy was in the door. Hands in her pockets.

Marrok stopped in front of her.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He looked past her into the netloft. At the long table, and the bench, and the banner on its two iron hooks with the star in the middle. At Dec, on the bench beside John, with a cup in his hand and Hyvred beside him. At the trapdoor in the corner, open, with a smell of fish coming up out of it.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy.

She asked it the way she asked everybody. Not to win anything. Just because it was what you asked, at a door.

Marrok looked at her.

John watched him. He was good at watching. He'd watched the dark on the longest night past a lamp and seen a whole tide of it moving south, and told nobody. He watched Marrok now, in the netloft door, with the question in front of him. And saw him go through them again, the eleven thousand two hundred, one by one, the way he had on Wednesday. Looking for one face.

"Nobody," said Marrok.

***

The lane went very quiet.

He'd said it plainly. Not loud, for once. Not for the lane. Just to Wendy, in the door.

"Nobody," said Marrok again. "I've got eleven thousand two hundred people and eleven Halls and a ship. And a Hall with a fountain inside it." He looked at the red cloths up the lane. "And nobody. Not one. I tried on Wednesday and I couldn't find one. I've been trying since." His big red face did something. "I thought it was a stupid question."

"It's not a stupid question," said Wendy.

"I know," said Marrok. "That's why I hate it."

Wendy looked at him for a long moment.

"Fair," she said.

And then she did the thing. The bit people forget. She sat down on the netloft step, with her back against the doorframe, and moved over a bit. Leaving room.

Marrok stared at her.

Then, very slowly, the leader of the biggest guild in the world sat down on the step of the smallest Hall in Masmark. Beside a girl with two different eyes. In a lane that smelled of fish, at the second dusk. And didn't say anything.

Nobody in the netloft said anything either.

They sat there for about four minutes. Then Marrok stood up.

"I'll sell them back," he said. To Kei, in the doorway behind Wendy. "The twelve. To the people who live in them. For what I paid." He looked up the lane at the red cloths. "I can't use them. Twelve isn't sixteen."

"That's fair," said Kei.

"It's not fair," said Marrok. "It's business."

"It's both," said Wendy, from the step.

Marrok looked down at her for a long time. Then he almost laughed. Not the big one. A small one, surprised, like Lucan's on a bridge.

He went back up the lane on his own.

***

The fish man was at the bottom of the north-west road when they went down at the third dawn. John and Dec and Hyvred, to buy fish. His cart was full. He'd watched Marrok go past him up the road toward the rim, on his own, in dark red.

"Who was that?" said the fish man.

Dec looked up the road, at the big man going away.

He didn't say anything for a long moment. John watched him think about it. About a switchback in the dark in March, and the Open Hand passing the second banner on the road and saying *who're they*. And somebody saying *nobody*.

"Somebody," said Dec.

The fish man looked at him with his boot face.

"Hm," he said. And gave them three fish for two silver. And a fourth, for nothing, which he put in John's hand without looking at him.

"For the mirror," said the fish man.

***

Dec ordered a boat from Abel on the Sunday.

John was there. He'd gone up the lane with Dec at the first dawn because Dec had asked him to, and he didn't know why. They went into the big shed at the top of the lane, under the room with the lamp in the window. Abel was there with sawdust in his eyebrows, sitting on an upturned hull with nothing to do. The way he'd sat, Hyvred said, every day since February.

"I'd like a boat," said Dec.

Abel looked at him.

"A small one," said Dec. "A fishing boat. With a lamp at the bow." He paused. "For the fish man. He lent us his in March. He said *bring it back*. We did. But it's old." He looked round the shed. At the tools on the walls, and the timber, and the half-built hull nobody had ordered the rest of. "I'd like him to have a new one. Whenever you've got time."

Abel didn't say anything for a long time.

"Nobody's ordered a boat," he said at last, "since February."

"I know," said Dec. "I'm sorry I didn't sooner."

John watched the old man's face. He'd been watching it all week, across the lane, without meaning to. The face of somebody deciding whether to sell a thing he'd had all his life because nobody needed it any more.

It wasn't that face now.

"*Hm*," said Abel. And got up off the upturned hull and went to the wall and took down a plane.

***

John flashed the mirror that night, at the end of the north mole, at nothing. Plain light, the way he had on the shingle. Out across the black water, at the island and its tower and the bell hanging green and silent at the top of it. And at the city lit all the way round, and the edge of the world.

He didn't expect an answer.

Out on the lake, very far off, a fishing boat with a lamp at its bow stopped. And then, very slowly, its lamp went out and on again. Twice. Long, short.

John stood at the end of the mole with the mirror in his hand for a long time.

*By somebody who was looking.*
