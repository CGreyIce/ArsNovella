The old middle pole was a bench now, and Jared needed a new one, and he wasn't going to buy it.

He could have. The timber yard on the east road had poles. It had given him the seven outer ones last year, pale ash, straight, seasoned. They'd gone back to it a week after the roof came down and they were still there, stacked in the long shed, and he could have them again for April. The timber man had said so. *Middle one too, if you want. I've got a good one. Thirty years in the shed.*

Jared had thanked him. Then he'd gone out past the timber yard, into the wood beyond it, on his own, with his axe.

He didn't know how to explain it. He was a practical man. He didn't go in for reasons much. But the old middle pole had been a stranger's tree, out of a stranger's shed. It had only become theirs at the second dusk, with six hundred and twelve lanterns pulling on it in the wind. With his beam under it and Azac's back against his, and nine people, and Isa's hand. It had become theirs by nearly going.

He didn't want the new one to have to nearly go.

He wanted to know where it came from.

***

He found it on the fourth day.

An ash. Not the tallest in the wood, but the straightest, on a slope above a stream, where the trees thinned and the light came in. It went up and up without a branch for longer than three men lying end to end. Then it opened out at the top into a crown that caught the wind off the moor, and moved, and came back.

He stood at the bottom of it for most of an evening, looking up.

That was the thing. That was what he'd come for and what frightened him about it. The old pole had come out of a shed with thirty years of drying in it. It had been dead and hard and still, and it had held because it couldn't do anything else. This one was alive. Green. Sixty years of growing on a slope in the wind, bending and coming back. It had never held a roof. It had never held anything but its own crown.

"It'll bend," he said to Azac, on the call that night, out there. "Green wood bends. You put six hundred lanterns on a green pole in a wind and it'll go like a fishing rod."

"Then don't use it," said Azac.

"I want to use it."

Azac was quiet on the call for a while.

"Then teach it," said Azac.

***

He cut it with Azac on the Saturday.

Azac came out to the wood with his enormous shield on his back and an axe of his own, which Jared had made him in the first summer and he'd never used. They took it down together. Jared's axe on the low side, Azac's on the high, on the beat, the way you were supposed to. Chips the size of hands flying out white into the leaves. Then the long creak and the lean. Then the whole sixty years of it came down along the slope, exactly where Jared had said it would, with a sound like a door shutting in a very big house.

They stood in the quiet afterwards and looked at it lying there.

"Sorry," said Jared. To the tree. He didn't know why.

"It's going somewhere," said Azac. "Not just down."

They trimmed it where it lay. They took the crown off, and the bark, in long pale strips. Underneath it was white and wet and it smelled of rain. Jared ran his hand down it from end to end and felt every place it had bent in sixty years of wind, and come back.

There was no scar. Not one. It had never nearly gone.

They carried it out of the wood on the Monday.

Not on their own. Six miles of it, through the wood and down the east road past the timber yard into the round town, the length of three men and green-heavy. You couldn't carry it on two shoulders. Jared had asked at the long table on Sunday, *can anybody*. And five people had said yes before he'd finished the sentence.

Ice took the bottom end. Of course he did. He'd taken the bottom end of the old one, up two hundred and twelve steps, in August. Salty behind him. Then Milli, who wasn't strong but came anyway. Then Azac. Then Jared at the top end, where the crown had been, walking backwards. Hyv came too, not carrying, walking alongside, holding on to Ice's arm that wasn't holding the pole. Every half mile she said *rest*, before anybody knew they needed one.

Out of the wood, down the east road. Past the timber yard, where the timber man came out of his shed and looked at them going past with a green ash on five shoulders, and laughed, and shook his head, and went back in. Into the round town by the east gate in the evening, down Spoke Street, past the dyer's and the cookshop, into the round market. They put it down on Maigo's long trestles by the fountain with a sound that went right round the market and back.

People came out of the Hart to look at it.

Alice came out of the Hart to look at it.

She stood at the end of the trestles with her hands in her pockets and looked along it, end to end, white and wet and smelling of rain.

"It's new," said Alice.

"It's green," said Jared. "It hasn't held anything yet."

"Neither had the last one," said Alice. "Till it did."

***

He worked it for a week.

At the long trestles in the round market, by the fountain, where everybody could see. The plane, the drawknife, the spokeshave. Long white curls coming off it and piling up on the cobbles. Leaka, between lion practices, picking them up and wearing them as hair. He shaped it, top to bottom. He cut the seven iron rings into it at the top for the seven spoke-ropes, the way the old one had them. Ina forged him new rings at Joss's anvil and wouldn't take anything for them. He didn't plane out the places where it had bent. He couldn't have if he'd tried. They went all the way through.

On the fourth night Ice came and sat on the fountain rim and watched him.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He did that. He'd come and sit near a thing you were making and watch you make it. And you'd forget he was there, and then you'd remember, and it'd be fine. Better than fine. Like working with somebody holding a lamp.

"It's going to bend," said Jared, eventually. Not looking up. "Green wood. Six hundred lanterns. Wind off the moor at second dusk. It'll go like a fishing rod."

"Yes," said Ice.

"I should've taken the timber man's. Thirty years in a shed. It'd stand there like iron."

"Why didn't you?"

Jared planed. A long white curl came off and fell on the cobbles.

"It's for Alice," he said. "In April. I want it to be *hers*. Not a stranger's out of a shed." He stopped. "I want it to know what it's for. Before it has to do it."

"Then raise it now," said Ice.

Jared looked up.

"At New Year," said Ice. "Once. With the lanterns. Before April. So it knows." He was looking at the pole. "Isa said her mothers put the lamps up every year, knowing it'd fall down. And one year there'd be enough people to hold it." He looked at Jared. "You don't put it up the first time on the day. You put it up once before. So it learns."

That was what Azac had said. *Teach it.* Jared hadn't known what he meant.

"It'll bend," said Jared.

"It's supposed to bend," said Ice. "It's green. That's how it holds."

***

They raised it at the second dusk on the first day of the year.

The whole round town came out. It had been coming out all day. For the lion, which hadn't gone in the fountain, and then had, on purpose. For the red squares on the doors that Curse had written, forty-one of them, one upside down. Now they came out for the pole. Sixty-two and a sheep and half the round market besides. Chernn's candles in every window. Ama Corrie in the south door. Isa in her chair at the top of the south steps, where Suzu had carried it out again after the lion, under the grey blanket.

The pole lay along the cobbles from the fountain to the south road, its foot in the hole by the fountain that Jared had dug out on Monday. Seven ropes from its top, out along the seven spokes, to the seven outer poles at the seven road ends, already up. Twenty people on the ropes. Kei with his map. Higu with the guy-ropes. Rae counting.

Last year they'd raised it by hand. Jared had called it. *Heave*. And twenty people had heaved, but not at the same time. The pole had gone up in lurches, swaying, with everybody shouting, until it was up. And then the wind.

This year Ice stood at the foot of it with his shield.

Jared hadn't asked him. He hadn't needed to. He'd heard about the hill from Xola, who'd told everybody, twice. Nine people round a fallen stone in the still water and a man who'd struck his own shield with the Weighted Cut so they'd all pull on the same beat. *BOOM*. Twelve of twelve.

"On the boom," said Ice. To all twenty. Not loudly. "Not before. Not after. On it."

Twenty people took hold.

He brought the sword back, low and long. Jared saw his feet set on the cobbles. He saw the whole market hold its breath.

*BOOM*.

Not the Weighted Cut. Not quite. Something smaller, into the flat of his own shield. But it went round the round market like a bell. Off the dome, off the Hart, off every door with a red square on it. Twenty people heard it at the same moment and pulled at the same moment.

The pole came up.

Not in lurches. In one. Up off the cobbles, off the trestles, up and up, the whole sixty years of it. Its foot went down into the hole by the fountain with a thump Jared felt through his boots. It stood there, swaying a little at the top, against a gold sky.

Straight up.

The market made a noise like a wave coming in.

"Not yet," said Jared, under his breath. "Give it a minute."

***

They hung the lanterns.

Alice's six hundred, out of their crates, and Chernn's twelve, the candles from Mother Dipp. Red paper and gold, the same ones as last year, folded flat for a twelvemonth in Ama Corrie's cupboard and opened out again. On the seven spoke-ropes from the middle pole to the seven road ends. On the three rings of rope round. Every spoke and every ring. The whole ceiling of the Hall of Roads, the round town in the middle and the roads going out and the rings, in lanterns. Over the round market.

Kei had said it last year. *That's the ceiling.* Isa had said it too.

They lit them at the last of the dusk. One by one, from the middle out, the way Alice had wanted. Six hundred and twelve red lights over the round market, in rings, against a dark gold sky.

And then the wind came off the moor.

Jared felt it before he saw it. Same as last year. The sky going dark at the edges, and the wind coming up out of the north-west across the heather. It came over the roofs and into six hundred and twelve lanterns on seven ropes all at once. Every lantern pulled south, and the ropes pulled the top of the pole, and the pole—

The pole bent.

Like a fishing rod. Like he'd said. The whole top of it went over south, a long slow curve, green ash, sixty years of it, bending. The market went silent all at once. Jared was under it with his beam before he'd thought about it. His shoulder up against the beam and the beam up against the pole, low, where the bend would start.

Azac put his back against his.

He didn't say anything. He just did it. The way he had last year. A wall behind him.

"*It's going*," said somebody, in the crowd. Gabe, probably.

"It's *not* going," said Jared, through his teeth. "It's bending. It's supposed to bend."

The wind came harder. The pole bent further. Jared felt it in the beam, in his shoulder, in his back, through Azac's back. Sixty years of an ash on a slope in the wind, doing what it had always done. Bending.

And then somebody was beside him under the beam.

Ice. With his shield on his back and his shoulder under the beam next to Jared's. Not saying anything either. Just there.

Then Salty. Then Milli, who wasn't strong. Then Hyv, who put her hands on the beam beside Ice's and held it. Then Drew, then Gabe, crying, then Rae beside Gabe at no paces, then Hako, then Tiger. Then Alice, between Jared and Milli, the way she had last year.

Then Leaka, wet, from the fountain, with a lion's head under her arm. Then Curse, with *arrived* on Leaka's wrist and ink on his own hands. Then Kotori. Suzu. Thea and Gigi, close. Xola and Slep. Chernn and Deston, Deston's ears red. Dennis, saying *darling, move up*. Ina. Wendy. Maigo, from his trestle tables. Kite and Ash, beside Drew. Bee. Higu. Kei, with his map under his arm, last, because he'd been counting.

Not nine people under one beam. Everybody. More hands than the beam had room for. People holding the shoulders of the people holding the beam. People holding the people holding them. The whole of the round town under the roof of lanterns, holding one beam, under one green pole, in a wind off the moor.

The pole bent.

And came back.

Jared felt it. Through the beam, through his shoulder. The bend stopping and turning, the way the ash had turned on its slope above the stream every time the wind dropped for sixty years. Coming back. Up, slowly, against six hundred and twelve lanterns and the whole of the north-west wind. Up and up. Until it was straight again, with the lanterns pulling at it, and the wind still blowing. And it was holding them.

Not because it couldn't do anything else.

Because it could bend.

Jared stood under the beam with sixty people's hands on it and found he was crying. He was a practical man. He didn't go in for that much.

***

At the top of the south steps, in her chair, Isa had not got up.

Jared looked for her afterwards, when the wind had gone round to the west and dropped. When people had started to let go of the beam one by one and stand under the lanterns looking up. She hadn't got up. She hadn't come down the steps on her stick and put her hand on the beam. *There. Ten.*

She hadn't needed to.

She was sitting in her chair under the grey blanket with Suzu beside her on the step. She was looking down at the round market, at sixty people and a green pole and six hundred and twelve lanterns, all holding.

"*Hm*," said Isa Corrie. Jared heard it from the bottom of the steps. "Enough people."

***

He saw it from under the beam. He wasn't looking for it. He just saw it, the way you'd see anything under a roof of lanterns.

Hyv let go of the beam. She turned round to Ice beside her, under the middle pole, under six hundred and twelve red lights. She put both her hands flat on his chest, and pushed him, gently, back against the green pole. Up against it. And kissed him.

In front of everybody. Under the roof. Not on the cheek.

Gabe screamed. Leaka screamed. About forty people screamed. Ama Corrie, in the south door, said *hm* so loudly that Jared heard it across the whole market.

And Ice stood with his back against the middle pole under the lanterns, pink right up to the ears in the red light, with his hands at his sides. Then he put one of them, very carefully, on the back of her head. And kissed her back.

The market went up like a roof coming off.

Jared looked away. It wasn't his. He looked at Alice instead.

She was standing beside him under the beam, with Milli's hand in hers, looking up at the lanterns. Her face was doing the thing it had done last year, under the first roof. And on the roof by the eye, at the bench made of a pole that held, when she'd sat down on *Somewhere* and cried.

"April," said Milli, beside her. Quietly.

"April," said Alice.

She looked at Jared.

"*Y'all*," said Alice. Which was his word.

***

They stood under it until the third dawn. Everybody did. They ate the rest of Maigo's noodles standing up, the way they had last year. Leaka dried out by the trestles. Ama Corrie stood in the south door with her broom and did not sweep.

Near the end, Ice came over to the foot of the pole, where Jared was standing with his hand flat on it, feeling it hold.

"It's a good roof," said Ice.

Jared looked at him.

"You said that last year," he said. "In a letter. From Masmark. *Tell jared it's a good roof*."

"I wasn't here," said Ice. "I'm saying it here." He looked up the pole, all the way up, at the seven iron rings and the seven ropes and the six hundred and twelve lights. "It bent."

"It bent," said Jared.

"And came back."

"And came back."

Ice put his hand on the pole beside Jared's. Flat. Pale green ash, smelling of rain, holding.

"That's better than not bending," said Ice. "That's how it holds."

Jared didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Hyv came and took hold of Ice's other arm with both hands and said *come here*. And Ice went. Of course he did. Jared stood at the foot of the middle pole on his own, with his hand on it, and the lanterns over him, and felt it hold.

They'd take the lanterns down in a week. Fold them flat. Put them back in Ama Corrie's cupboard.

The pole would stay up. He'd decided that, standing there. It would stay up in the round market all spring, through the rain, through March, bare. Seven ropes from its top to nothing. So it would remember. So that on the seventh of April, when Alice and Milli came home from out there, from wherever it was and whatever it had been, it would already know.

What it was for. Who it was for.

*Somewhere*.
