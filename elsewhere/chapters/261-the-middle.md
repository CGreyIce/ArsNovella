Gabe had been in Malacca two years on Christmas Eve, and he said so at breakfast four times.

"*Two years*," he said, at the kitchen counter, at eight in the morning, with two cups of kopi, too sweet. "Two years ago today." He handed Ice a cup. "And Suzu was in the guest room. And you showed me the spare room and said *it's yours*." His eyes were wet. "*It's yours*. That was all you said. For a week."

"I said other things."

"You said *good*," said Gabe. "And *okay*. And *mind the step*." He wiped his face. "Two years."

Suzu, at the table, with toast, looked up.

"Good," said Suzu.

Gabe made the kettle noise and put both arms round Suzu from behind, toast and all. Suzu let him, the way he'd let him for two years. And Ice stood at the counter of the kitchen of the flat in Malacca on Christmas Eve, with a cup of kopi too sweet, and watched them. And didn't say anything. And didn't need to.

Through the wall, on their side, somebody was moving about. A presence. He liked it there.

***

He'd come out of the egg at eight on Friday morning, the dawn after the longest night, and lain in it for a long time.

He hadn't been able to get up. His body had decided, out here, that it should be tired. The way his hips had decided in July, after four nights in a cellar, that they should ache. He lay in the pod in the back room at Melaka Raya with the fan going round. Thinking about nine people shoulder to shoulder round a flat stone, facing out. In the lee of everybody.

*Nobody was at the front*, Hyv had said.

He'd been at the front of everything for two years. Of everything his whole life, nearly. A phone game with a stupid name, and twenty people all at the front. A fen and a stone. A bridge, a bell, a lake. He'd stood at the front and things had come at him and he'd held. He'd thought that was what he was for.

On the hill, at eleven, with the breath coming through all twelve gaps at once, he'd turned round. And said *everybody, in*. And there hadn't been a front any more.

He'd never felt anything like it.

He'd lain in the pod and thought about it until Gabe knocked on the lid with two cups.

The parcel came on Christmas Eve, at the second dusk, on the hill.

He didn't know parcels could come past the edge. Letters could; they'd come back the night the middle came out of the jar. A parcel was different. Mother Dipp's came to the round town in brown paper with string, carried in from the south gate by Kite and Bram. Nobody could carry a parcel across the still water and three hundred stones.

It came anyway.

Kotori found it. On the flat stone, beside the jar, at twenty past eight, when they came out of the dark. A small parcel in brown paper, with string, sitting on the edge of the flat stone where the tenth cup of tea had been, three weeks ago. As if it had always been there.

She looked at it for a long time. She didn't touch it.

"It's got my name on," she said.

It had. In a hand Ice knew from the Roll. Small, careful, very old.

*Kotori. On the hill.*

***

She opened it at the fire. All nine round it.

Leaves. Dark ones, curled small and tight, a double handful. With a smell that went right up the inside of your head. The leaf that grew on the backs of the waystones on the north road, where the lamps were. Nowhere else.

And a note. In the same hand.

Kotori read it twice. Then she put her face in her hands and cried. Not the frightened kind. The other kind. And didn't say sorry once. Suzu, beside her, put his hand flat on her back between her shoulders. And left it there, the way Rae had put hers on Gabe's at the north door.

"*Tiger*," said Kotori, through her hands. "*The tall one.* Tiger picked them. And Higu." She laughed. "All ninety-four. On the morning after. They must have walked all day."

"They walked it so there'd be tea," said Gigi.

"On a hill they'll never see," said Thea.

***

Kotori made tea. On Slep's fire, in her pot, with water from the still water that tasted of nothing. She boiled it very slowly, the way Isa said. And put in the leaves from the waystones, picked on the morning after the longest night by a tall one and a girl with a lantern. From every one of the ninety-four stones.

She poured nine cups. And a tenth, which she put on the edge of the flat stone, beside the jar, where the parcel had been.

"For her mothers," said Kotori.

"*My mother would have hated it*," said Suzu, and Kotori laughed through her crying.

They drank it round the fire, on the hill, in the middle of the still water. On Christmas Eve, with the middle standing straight up over a jar on a flat stone. It was horrible. It was the horrible one. It went down him like something lit.

It remembered being near ninety-four lights.

***

"Somebody has to stay," said Suzu.

It was about midnight. Christmas. The game's third dawn; daylight anywhere else, and here the dark, with edges, and the middle standing over the jar. They were round the flat stone. All nine. The way they'd stood on the longest night, except sitting, with their backs to it. Facing out.

Nobody said anything.

"Isa's mothers stayed," said Suzu. "Three hundred years. Mother to daughter. Every night." He had his hand on the edge of the flat stone, on the notches. On the cross, and the notch he'd cut beside it, and the thorn, and the flower. "Somebody always stayed. With it."

"They didn't have a road," said Ice.

Suzu looked at him.

"They stayed because there wasn't a road," said Ice. "The road was dark. Three hundred years. Every one of them walked to the edge once and looked south and it was dark. *Not yet.*" He looked at the flame. "So they stayed. Because if they left, there was nobody. And nobody could find their way back."

"There's a road now," said Hyv. Very quietly.

"There's a road now," said Ice. "Ninety-four. Standing. And the twenty-two. And three hundred stones, and a thorn in the gap that flowered." He looked at Suzu. "And a ring. Twelve of twelve. *Nothing comes inside.*" He was quiet. "And the jar. *It does not go out by itself. Something has to put it out.*"

"Nothing will," said Kotori. "Not with the ring."

"So nobody has to stay," said Ice. "Not every night. Not mother to daughter." He looked at the flame, standing straight up. "*Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep.* It watches itself now."

Suzu didn't say anything for a long time.

"In the spring," he said.

They looked at him.

"In the spring," said Suzu. "I'll come back. With a stone." He looked at the notches. "Three hundred and one. Every spring. Whoever keeps it walks out with a stone and sets it in the water a step further than the last. And cuts a notch." He put his finger on the rim, at the end of the second line, past Isa's crooked one. Where there was a space. "I'll come back every spring. And set one. And cut the notch."

Nobody said anything.

"So the way stays there," said Suzu. "In case somebody needs it."

"I'll come," said Ice.

"I know."

"Somebody'll need tea," said Kotori.

Suzu's face did the thing. Very small. Like the light used to do through the clay.

"Good," said Suzu.

He read it at about one in the morning, on Christmas Day, sitting against the flat stone. He'd hit sixty-one at the dawn after the longest night, in the last minute before the egg took him. He hadn't noticed till Friday afternoon, in the kitchen, when Gabe showed him on his phone and cried.

Sixty-two. For sitting against a stone.

He'd been one, once. In a fen, on the first day, with twenty-one people watching. Kopi typing *ok* at lunch.

He didn't look at the window for long.

***

At about two, Hyv went to the north stone. The twelfth. The one they'd raised. She stood at it the way he'd stood at it for twenty nights. Facing out, north, at the dark. Which was just dark now, with depth, and edges, because the middle was there.

He went and stood beside her.

"You're not at the front," she said.

"No."

"You've been at the front for twenty nights. At this stone." She was looking north. "And on the longest night you turned round." She swallowed. "And now you're standing beside me. At the stone. Not in front."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He thought about it.

"Because there's nothing coming," he said. "And you're here." He looked at the dark. "In the war there was always something coming. And nobody beside. So I was always at the front." He was quiet. "Here there's nothing coming. And everybody's beside." He looked at her. "So there isn't a front. There's just next to."

She didn't say anything for a long time. She looked at him. Edge to edge. All of him at once, in the gold light from behind them, at the north stone on a hill in the middle of the dark's own water. On Christmas Day.

Then she put both hands flat on his chest. And pushed.

Not hard. He went back a step. Against the twelfth stone. Surprised, the way he'd been on a bench in a park in Singapore, under a tree.

"*I go first*," said Hyvred.

And she kissed him. On the hill. In the light. Where everybody could see.

Somewhere behind them, by the fire, Hako said very quietly, in the voice, *the scientific community has seen enough*. And Xola hit him. And Thea laughed. And Gigi, close beside her, said *Hako* at the same moment Thea did.

When she let go, he didn't say anything. He stood against the twelfth stone with her hands still flat on his chest.

"You pushed me," he said.

"You were *leaning*," said Hyvred.

"I wasn't."

"You were going to," said Hyvred. "I could see it. Edge to edge." She was very red. In the light, where everybody could see. She didn't seem to mind. "I'm always going to see it. And I'm always going to go first."

"Okay," said Ice.

She hit him. On the arm, not hard.

"*Mrp*," she said.

"I know," said Ice. "It's allowed."

***

He went round the ring at four. He'd started doing it the night before the longest night. He did it again now.

Thea and Gigi at the third stone, close. Thea's head on Gigi's shoulder, both asleep, or pretending. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

Xola and Slep at the fifth. Slep with his glaive across his knees, still bent. He'd stopped trying to straighten it. He said it had held a stone up, bent. He was going to leave it like that.

"Ice," said Slep, when he came past. Waiting until nobody else had said it.

"Slep."

"Merry Christmas," said Slep.

Ice looked at him. Nobody had said it. Not one person, all night. Slep had waited until nobody else had, and then said the thing that needed saying.

"Merry Christmas," said Ice.

Hako at the eighth. Not narrating. Looking at the still water, flat and black, with the ring upside down in it.

"I'm going to stop doing the voice," said Hako. "When we get home."

"Why?"

"I don't need it," said Hako. "I needed it to look at things. Frightening things." He looked at the water. "I looked at the nothing for three weeks. In the voice. And then the light came out of the jar and I could see everything." He was quiet. "I don't think anything's going to frighten me that much again."

"Do it anyway," said Ice. "Sometimes. For Tiger."

Hako's face did something.

"*The narrator*," he said, in the voice, very softly, "*will consider it.*"

Kotori and Suzu at the flat stone. On the south side, where the mothers sat. Kotori asleep against Suzu's shoulder with the pot in her lap. Suzu awake, with his hand on the notches.

He looked up.

"Spring," said Suzu.

"Spring," said Ice.

He went on round. Past the first stone, and the second. To the north side. The twelfth. And didn't stand at it.

He went back to the middle.

***

He sat down against the flat stone. On the north side. Facing in, for once. Facing the flame, standing straight up over the jar on the worn crown. With his back to the north and the dark and the twelfth stone, and his shield flat on the moss beside him.

He'd faced out for two years. At everything. Like the heron on its stone in the fen, never once turning round to look at what was behind it.

He'd turned round on a broom in April and seen sixty-one chairs. He'd turned round on the bottom step of the Hall of Roads in August and given Kite three answers. He'd turned round on a hill at eleven at night on the longest night and said *everybody, in*.

He didn't need to turn round now. He was already facing it.

The middle.

It was small. A flame, gold, without a wick or oil, standing straight up in the air over the mouth of a plain clay jar. Tamsin's, from Larkhollow, that remembered keeping. On a flat stone with a crown cut into it. With notches round the rim, three hundred of them and a cross and a thorn and a flower. And a space at the end of the second line, for a stone in the spring.

Everything leaned toward it. The twelve stones. The nine people. The hill. The dark outside, which wasn't looking any more, because it knew where it was.

And past the dark, south, past the still water and three hundred stones and the edge and the road, ninety-four lamps standing straight. And a round town, with a candle in the window of the north door that Chernn changed every morning. And sixty-two names in a Roll. And twenty of Meridian, next to. And an old woman asleep in a chair by the north door. Who'd seen this from the roof, on the longest night, for the first time in three hundred years.

He'd thought, two years ago, that he was the front. That things came at him and he held, and that was what he was for.

He looked at the flame.

He wasn't the front. He'd never been. He'd been next to it. All of them had. Everybody, next to everybody, round something small that didn't lean. So the dark would know where it was. So the rest could sleep.

*So it knows where the middle is.*

He knew where it was.

***

At a quarter to eight, Christmas morning, they stood round the flat stone. All nine. Close. Facing in.

Suzu said it. He always said it now.

"*Set one to watch*."

"*So the rest may sleep*," said eight people.

And Ice said it with them this time. Not a breath late. Not looking. Just saying it, with everybody, at the same moment, the way nine people had pulled a stone upright on the *boom*.

And very far away, south. Too far to hear. At a long table in a round town on Christmas morning, sixty-two people said it. And twenty of Meridian. And an old woman in a chair by the north door, awake. Who said it first, because she always had.

He couldn't hear them.

But just before the egg took him, he looked south. Past the twelfth stone and the still water and the three hundred stones. Past the edge.

And at the bottom of the sky, very small, very far, gold, there was a light. On the roof of the round town, Kei would write, at the eye, on a bench with *Somewhere* on it. Where an old woman's grandmother's grandmother had sat before she went north. And where Kei had set a lamp on the night after the longest night. So the hill could see the round town too.

*Set one to watch*.

It was looking back.
