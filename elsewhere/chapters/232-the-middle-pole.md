Jared had kept the middle pole, and nobody had asked him why, which was good, because he didn't know.

It had been in the yard behind the Hart since February. Lying on two trestles under an oilcloth, the length of three men, pale ash, with seven iron rings round the top where the seven spoke-ropes had been tied. And a scar halfway up where it had nearly gone, at the second dusk, on Chinese New Year. With six hundred and twelve lanterns pulling on it south in a wind off the moor. And Jared's beam under it, and Azac's back against his, and nine people under one roof holding it up.

And Isa's hand. Flat on the wood, at the bottom, out of her chair for the first time. *There. Ten.*

And the wind had dropped.

They'd taken the roof down a week later. Rope by rope, lantern by lantern, all six hundred and twelve folded flat into boxes for next year. The seven outer poles had gone back to the timber yard on the east road, where they'd come from. Jared had carried the middle one round to the yard behind the Hart on his own, on his shoulder, and put it on the trestles and covered it up.

Kotori had looked at it through the kitchen window and not said anything. Chernn, dipping candles in the corner, had looked at it and not said anything. Ama Corrie had come out into the yard once, in March, and looked at it over her spectacles for a long time. Then said *hm*, and gone back in.

He'd thought about it all summer. What it was for. You didn't keep a pole the length of three men in a yard for six months for nothing.

He found out on a Tuesday in August, at the top of the two hundred and twelve, with Ice carrying the other end.

***

It started on the longest night. He just hadn't known.

He'd been in the triangle by the new well with Alice and Milli, all night, the second longest night. Alice holding up the star she'd found under Quarrymouth, lit for the first time. Milli with his knife at the joints. And Jared with his beam, held up over the two of them like a roof. Eleven hours. And somewhere in the middle of it, at midnight, when it was quiet, Alice had said something to Milli. Not to Jared. He'd just been there, holding the beam.

*One day. Somewhere with a view.*

And Milli had looked round at the round market in the dark, at the window candles and the well and the three of them in a triangle. And said, *This is somewhere.*

Jared had heard it. He'd thought about it on and off ever since. Through Chinese New Year and the roof, and the summer with half the guild away. Through a letter from Masmark in June that said the GM had been to a corridor in Malacca and somebody had knocked on his door. And through another in July that said Deston had asked on a green at dusk with a candle with two wicks.

*Somewhere with a view.*

Alice and Milli were going to get married. Everybody knew. They'd been going to get married for as long as Jared had known about Milli, which was a long time. *One day.* Out there, where Alice lived in Singapore and Milli lived on the other side of the world. In a place with a view. Some day. They'd said *one day* for so long it had stopped sounding like a day at all. It sounded like *weather*.

He couldn't do anything about out there. He built roofs. He couldn't build a day.

But in here he could build a view.

***

He went up the two hundred and twelve on Monday afternoon, on his own, the day the twelve came home from Masmark.

He'd been up before. In January, with string and chalk, measuring the market for the roof, with Higu laughing at him. The stair from the back of the Hall, behind Ama Corrie's counter, round and round the inside of the dome, up and up. Two hundred and twelve steps. The third one loose; Ama Corrie told everybody. A hundred and six, then a landing. Forty, then a landing. Sixty-six, the longest, with the wind coming down through the eye.

And at the top, the roof.

The eye was a round hole in the very top of the dome, open to the sky, the width of a cart. Round it, a flat ring of stone you could walk on, with a low parapet. Kei and Higu and John had stood up here on the longest night and called the whole round town. The three who could see furthest. Kei's brass sighting arm was still fixed to the parapet on the north side, pointing up the north road.

And the view.

Jared stood at the parapet and looked.

He'd seen it in January. He'd been looking at the market, though, then. Measuring. Down. He hadn't looked *out*.

He looked out now.

The seven roads, going away from the round town like spokes from a hub. The north road over the moor, with ninety-four lamps on the backs of grey stones going away up it into the evening, all lit, facing the wrong way. The east road toward the timber yards and the sunrise. The south road, the way the twelve had just come, round the curve, with a woman sitting at the milestone at the bottom of it. The south-west, toward the Millrace and the White Hills, so far off they were a white line on the edge of the world. Somewhere past them, Tallow Green. The north-west, and the north-east, and the west.

And the sky. All of it. All the way round.

He stood there till the second dusk, till the lamps came on all over the round town below. The baker's window, the blue door, the cookshop on Spoke Street. And the ninety-four going away north, and the whole moor going dark round them.

*Somewhere with a view.*

"Y'all," said Jared, very quietly, to nobody.

***

He asked Kei on Monday night, at the long table. Kei had been home four hours. He was sitting at the head of the table with his new Masmark sheet unrolled in front of him, and his tea going cold, the way it always did.

"The eye," said Jared. "The roof. Round it. Can I put something up there?"

Kei looked at him.

"What?" he said.

"A bench," said Jared.

Kei looked at him for a long time.

"For who?"

Jared didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He looked down the long table at Hana's five by the window. At Alice and Milli, side by side, Milli's arm along the back of her chair, both of them laughing at something Leaka had said. Kei looked where he was looking.

"Oh," said Kei.

"Don't tell them."

"I won't." Kei rolled up his sheet. "It's the eye. It's for seeing from." He paused. "Sit there."

***

He needed somebody to carry the other end.

The pole was the length of three men and ash, and heavy, and the stair was two hundred and twelve steps round the inside of a dome with a loose third step and a wind at the top. He couldn't carry it on his own. Not up there. Not and get it round the turns.

He'd thought about asking Azac, who'd held it with him on Chinese New Year. But Azac was in Masmark, at the netloft, with Riverbank. He'd thought about Salty, who could carry anything. But Salty had gone down the fourth tunnel on Tuesday morning with Wendy and a lamp, the two of them, and hadn't come back up, and Jared wasn't going to be the one to go and get them.

He was standing in the yard behind the Hart at the first dawn on Tuesday, with the oilcloth off the pole, looking at it. When somebody came round the corner of the kitchen with a cup of tea in his hand.

Ice.

He stopped when he saw Jared. He looked at the pole. He'd been on Chinese New Year; he'd been in Masmark. He'd read Jared's letter about it, and written back *tell jared it's a good roof*. He'd never seen the pole.

"That's the middle one," he said.

"Yeah."

Ice looked at it for a long time. At the seven iron rings round the top. At the scar halfway up, where it had nearly gone.

"You kept it," he said.

"I kept it."

"What's it for?"

Jared thought about it. He'd known Ice a long time. Since before any of this, on calls, in his chat, in a stream that was ten people and a swamp. He'd been one of the four who chatted on day one. He'd called her Alice before Ice did, or at the same time, he couldn't remember. The two of them, the only two who did.

"It's for Alice," said Jared.

Ice put his cup down on the edge of the trestle. Without looking where it went.

"Can I help?" he said.

***

They carried it up the two hundred and twelve together. Jared at the front, because he knew the turns. Ice at the back, at the bottom, where the weight was.

It took all morning. The stair was narrow and went round and round, and the pole was long and didn't. They had to stand it nearly upright at every turn and walk it round, the two of them, slow, one step at a time. At the third step, the loose one, Ice said *mind the third step* without being asked, in exactly Ama Corrie's voice. And Jared laughed so hard he nearly dropped his end.

Ama Corrie was at her counter when they started. She watched them go up past her, two men and a pole the length of three, round the first turn of the stair.

"What's that?" she said.

"A pole," said Jared.

"I can see it's a pole."

"It's going up," said Ice.

Ama Corrie looked at them over her spectacles. At Ice, at the bottom end of the pole, where the weight was.

"*Hm*," she said. And then: "Mind the third step."

"We minded it," said Ice.

"Mind it on the way down," said Ama Corrie, and went back to her ledger.

***

They talked on the stair. Not much. You didn't talk much carrying a pole up two hundred and twelve steps. But some.

At the first landing, a hundred and six up, they stood the pole against the wall and sat on the step and breathed. Ice had his eyes shut.

"You're different," said Jared.

Ice opened his eyes.

"Everybody says that," he said.

"Everybody's right," said Jared. "You carried a pole. For somebody else's thing. You asked if you could help." He looked at him. "You never used to ask. You used to just stand at the front and do it."

Ice thought about it.

"I turned round," he said.

"Yeah," said Jared. "I can tell."

They sat on the landing for a bit. Then Ice said, without looking at him:

"How long have you known her?"

Jared looked at him.

Ice had never asked him anything. Not in years. Not about Alice, not about anything. He'd read Jared's letters and said *good* and *tell jared it's a good roof*. He'd never once asked a question back.

"Longer than I've known how to build anything," said Jared.

Ice nodded slowly.

"She's always wanted a view," said Jared. "Since forever. On calls. She'd say *one day we'll have a house with a view*. Her and Milli. She'd say it like it was a joke." He looked at the pole leaning against the wall. "It's not a joke. It's just a long way off."

"It's not that long," said Ice.

"How do you know?"

"Deston had a sticker on a door for twenty-two months," said Ice. "And then he didn't." He stood up and went to his end of the pole. "Things are a long way off until they aren't."

***

They came out at the top at noon, into the wind and the light.

The roof. The ring of flat stone round the eye. The low parapet, Kei's brass arm on the north side, the sky. Jared walked the pole round to the south side of the ring, facing out over the round market and the south road and the White Hills on the edge of the world. Where the light would be at the first dawn, coming up on the left over the east road. Where you could see the most at once.

They put it down. And Jared went to work.

He'd brought his tools up on his back. Saw, adze, plane, chisels, a bag of pegs. He took the pole apart. Not all of it. He kept the seven iron rings, the top, the scar. He cut it in three, the long way, and split the middle third into planks. And Ice sat on the parapet and watched. Saying nothing, the way he watched people do things. Handing him whatever he asked for.

He built a bench.

Not a big one. Room for two, close. Three, if one of them was Leaka. The seat from the middle of the pole, the planks pegged across two legs made from the bottom third, where Isa's hand had been. And the back from the top third, with the seven iron rings still round it. Seven roads, seven rings. And in the middle of the back, the scar, where the pole had nearly gone on Chinese New Year and hadn't.

He didn't plane the scar out. He planed round it.

Jared read the window and sat down on the stone beside the bench and didn't say anything for a while.

***

Leaka and Curse found out, of course. Leaka found out about everything. She came up the two hundred and twelve at the second dawn shouting *JARRED, JARRED, WHAT ARE YOU DOING UP THERE, KOTORI SAYS YOU TOOK A POLE*. Curse came up behind her at a walk with a pen behind each ear and a pot of blue ink.

Leaka came out onto the roof and saw the bench and stopped.

She put both fists in her mouth.

"*No*," she said, round them.

"Don't tell her."

"*I'm not going to TELL her*," said Leaka, round her fists. "*I'm going to DIE*." She turned to Ice on the parapet. "*CGI*. Did you *carry* this? Up the *stair*?"

"The bottom end," said Ice.

"Icey *boi*," said Leaka, with enormous feeling, and burst into tears.

Curse didn't say anything. He walked round the bench twice, slowly, looking at it. At the seven rings and the scar. Then he knelt down at the back of it, on the stone, with his pot of blue ink. And looked up at Jared.

"Can I?" she said.

"What are you going to write?"

Curse thought about it. He'd labelled everything in the round town that needed it. Isa's empty cage on its hook by the north door. *Carried. And carried home.* The banner on the netloft's hooks. *Constellation (ours).* He knew what things were.

"One word," said Curse.

Jared nodded.

He wrote it on the back of the bench, under the scar, small, in blue. In his careful hand that made people want to read things.

*Somewhere.*

***

There was a space on the arm. Jared had left it on purpose. The left arm, the end of it, a flat place the size of a palm, planed smooth.

"What's that?" said Ice.

"Milli's," said Jared.

Ice looked at him.

"He puts a cat's-paw on things," said Jared. "On everything he makes. Salty's grip. The scabbards. He stamps it in the leather." He touched the flat place on the arm. "He'll want to put it on himself. It's not mine to put. It's his and hers."

Ice looked at the space for a long time.

"You left a gap," he said.

"Yeah."

"So somebody else could go first."

Jared looked at him.

"Somebody told me that," said Ice. "In a corridor." He got up off the parapet. "It's the best thing you can do."

***

They sat on it once, the two of them, at the second dusk, before they went down. Just to see.

Ice sat on the left and Jared on the right, close, the way it was built. And looked out over the parapet at the round town lighting up below. The seven roads going away. The ninety-four lamps on the north road, all lit. The south road, round the curve, and the milestone at the bottom. The White Hills, a white line on the edge of the world, and the sky going from gold to dark all the way round.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"It's a good view," said Ice.

"It's not for you," said Jared.

"I know," said Ice. "That's why it's good."

***

He told Alice that night, at the long table. Not what. Just when.

He went down the table to Hana's five by the window and stood behind her chair. She looked up at him. So did Milli.

"Go up to the eye," said Jared. "Tomorrow. At the first dawn. The two of you."

Alice looked at him.

"Why?"

"Don't ask," said Jared.

She looked at him for a long time. With her face doing the thing it did when she'd decided something, except she hadn't. He had. She looked past him, down the long table, at Ice in the first of the nine, with his feet on the rung and Hyvred beside him and his face doing nothing at all, very carefully.

Then at Leaka, who had both fists in her mouth again.

Then at Curse, who was looking very hard at his pen.

Then back at Jared.

"*Y'all*," said Alice.

It was his word. She'd stolen it. She'd never once said it in all the years he'd known her. She said it now, at the long table, looking up at him, with her face going pink.

"That's not a no," said Jared.

"It's not a no," said Alice. "It's a *y'all*."

Milli, beside her, looked from her to Jared and back. He didn't say anything. But Jared saw his hand, on the back of Alice's chair, go still.
