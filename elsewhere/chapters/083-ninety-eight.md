Ama Corrie had a list.

She'd had it for forty years. She kept it in a drawer under the counter, under the Roll, on a sheet of paper so old it had gone soft as cloth. When Kei asked her, on the afternoon of the last day, whether anything in the Hall needed mending, she looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. Then she opened the drawer and took it out and laid it on the counter, and smoothed it flat with both hands.

*The north door sticks.*
*The lamp chains on the east side jam.*
*The roof leaks over the west door, the south door, and the Roll.*
*The east steps are going.*
*The weathercock doesn't turn.*

Under those, in a different hand, older, faded nearly to nothing:

*The eye.*

"My grandmother's," said Ama Corrie, touching the last line. "She wrote it the year she shuttered it. She never said what was wrong with it." She looked at Chris, standing at the counter beside Kei, with his sheet open in the air. "You're the one with Engineering."

"Yes," said Chris.

"You're the one who's two short."

"Two per cent," said Chris. "Yes."

Ama Corrie looked at him for a long time. Then she turned the list round on the counter so that it faced him.

"Start with the door," she said. "It's been sticking since before you were born. It'll want to keep sticking. Don't let it."

***

He started with the door.

Kei watched from the steps. He couldn't help it. Everybody was watching. The fifteen members who weren't on the dome or the roads were standing round the north door of the Hall of Roads. So were Moonlight Harvest and Riverbank, who weren't members, and six thousand people in the round market. All of them watching a tired man with a plain longsword on his back take a door off its hinges.

He did it properly. That was the only way Chris knew how to do anything.

He took the north door down with Gabe holding the weight. Gabe had come straight over from the steps when he heard, because Gabe had Blacksmithing and a hammer, and because it was a door. Chris looked at the hinges for a long time, the great iron ones, black with age, each as long as his forearm. He turned one over in his hands. He measured it against the other. He took out a small notebook, a real one, and drew it.

"The pin's bent," he said. "Not much. A fraction. Since before anyone was born, Ama Corrie said. Every time the door swings it drags." He looked at Gabe. "Can you straighten it?"

"Can I straighten it," said Gabe, delighted, and went to find an anvil.

There was an anvil at the farrier's on the south side of the market. Gabe straightened the pin on it in front of four hundred people. They cheered every blow, because Gabe made every blow look like the most important thing that had ever happened. Chris set the pin back in the hinge, and greased it with something Gigi made, which smelled terrible. Then they hung the north door back on its hinges, and Chris pushed it with one finger.

It swung.

All the way open, silently, and all the way shut again, and didn't stick. For the first time in longer than anybody in Carrow Cross could remember.

Four people in the round market cheered. Then four hundred. Then, slowly, because people were coming out of the houses to see what the cheering was, more.

"Four tenths of a per cent," said Chris, looking at his window. "For a door."

"For a door that's stuck for a hundred years," said Ama Corrie, from the counter. She didn't smile. But she reached out and pushed the north door with one finger herself, and watched it swing, and said "Hm," and Kei had never heard the word sound so happy.

***

The lamp chains took an hour.

They ran up the wall on the east side of the Hall, iron chains on pulleys, so that you could lower the lamps to light them and raise them again. They'd jammed thirty years ago, Ama Corrie said, and she'd lit the east lamps with a pole and a taper ever since. Chris went up a ladder and looked at the pulleys for a long time. Then he came down and drew them, and went up again, and took one of them apart with a small knife, very slowly, the way you'd take apart a watch.

Gigi held the ladder. She said it would fall down. It didn't.

The weathercock on the top of the dome didn't turn because a bird had built a nest in it, and the nest had turned to something like stone. Chris went up the two hundred and twelve steps with Hyvred, who knew the way. He chipped it out with the end of his sword, sitting on the ledge round the open eye, with nothing between him and the round market a long way down but air. Then he oiled the spindle. The weathercock turned in the evening wind for the first time in a decade, and creaked, and pointed north.

"Ninety-nine," said Chris, on the call, from the top of the dome.

"Ninety-nine," said Kei, on the steps.

Nobody said *one more*. Everybody thought it.

***

CL_Steward_03 came to the steps at half past four, in the afternoon light.

He came alone, the way he'd come on Sunday. A neat young man with a ledger, in a sash so clean it looked as if it had never been out of doors. He stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at Kei. He was polite. He was always polite.

"Good evening," he said. "Seven and a half hours."

"Good evening," said Kei.

"I understand you have nineteen."

"We have nineteen."

"And the twentieth is two per cent short of fifteen. And fixing doors." CL_Steward_03 smiled, very slightly. Not unkindly. Kei thought he genuinely admired it, the way you'd admire somebody doing something very well that wasn't going to work. "There are seven roads out of this town. Every one of them has friends of ours on it tonight. I'm told they're very bored. If your twentieth goes out to find something to fight, I imagine they'll cheer up."

"He's not going out," said Kei.

"Then I hope the Hall has a great many doors," said CL_Steward_03. He looked up at the dome, at the open eye, at the little dark figure on the ledge round it that was Hyvred. "We'll be at the fountain. At one minute past midnight, if it's all the same to you, we'll begin." He paused. "I mean no offence by any of this. It's a very good Hall. We'd like it."

"So would we," said Kei.

"Yes," said CL_Steward_03. He seemed to consider something. "For what it's worth, we've got seventeen others. We bought them with gold this month. We wanted this one most, because of the roads." He tucked the ledger under his arm. "Good luck."

He went back across the market to the fountain.

Ice was on the step behind him. He'd been there the whole time, not saying anything.

"Vivian was right," said Ice quietly. "Eighteen writs. Seventeen rooms and this one."

"I know."

"Kei."

"I know," said Kei. "I know. He's not going out."

***

The roof didn't help. It was tiles and lead, not a mechanism. Chris looked at it for a long time from the ledge and said it wasn't Engineering, it was roofing, and roofing was somebody else's skill, and he didn't have it. The east steps weren't Engineering either. They were stone, and mason's work.

Which left the eye.

He went up again at seven, with a lantern for the stairs. Gabe went with him, and Kei, and Hyvred, who was already there. The four of them sat on the narrow ledge round the open eye at the top of the dome with the evening wind coming in, and looked at the shutter.

It was where Gigi and Kei had left it a month ago. Pushed back on its rusted track, open, jammed. A great round iron lid in two halves, each half on a toothed track. A wheel and a chain in the wall to turn them, and the chain was rusted solid. Gigi had broken a nail forcing it. Kei had sworn, quietly, which he never did. It had opened with a shriek and a crash and stuck there, and not moved since.

*The eye.* Ama Corrie's grandmother's writing, faded nearly to nothing, at the bottom of a list forty years old.

Chris looked at it for a long time with the lantern.

"It's not broken," he said slowly. "It's not *broken*. It's just rusted. The chain and the track. Somebody shuttered it and nobody ever opened it again, so it rusted where it stood." He put his hand on the wheel in the wall. "If I clean the track and free the chain and grease all of it, it'll open and shut. Properly. Like it's supposed to." He looked at Kei. "That's Engineering."

"How long?"

"Three hours," said Chris. "Maybe four."

It was seven o'clock. The writ ended at midnight.

"Then do it," said Kei.

***

He did it.

Kei sat on the ledge and held the lantern for four hours, and watched him do it. He'd measured Steadying Draughts to the drop in a stillroom on a hill. He'd stood on a step for a month. He'd never watched anybody be as careful as Chris was with a rusted chain at the top of a dome at night.

Link by link. With a small knife, and a rag, and Gigi's terrible-smelling grease, and Gabe's hammer when a link wouldn't come. Not fast. Not slow. On the beat. Newish was down on the floor of the Hall, two hundred feet below, with the drum between her knees, playing so that he could hear it up the stairwell. *Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.* And Chris worked on the beat, and didn't make a list, and didn't hurry.

At ten, the track was clean.

At ten forty, the chain was free.

At eleven fifteen, he put his hand on the wheel in the wall and turned it. The great iron shutter of the eye of the Hall of Roads, that had been jammed open for a month and rusted for sixty years, rolled closed on its track with a long, smooth, iron sigh. It met in the middle with a soft clang. Then he turned the wheel back the other way, and it rolled open again, silently, onto the night.

Four tenths.

Chris looked at the window for a long time. He didn't say anything. Neither did anybody else. Down in the Hall, Newish stopped playing.

"Ninety-nine point six," said Chris, finally, very quietly. "Forty-five minutes."

"There's the roof," said Gabe. "We could—"

"It's not Engineering."

"The steps—"

"Not Engineering." Chris sat back against the wall of the dome, beside the wheel, with the rag in his hands. "That's all of it. That's everything on the list that's mine. I did all of it." He looked at the open eye, and the night through it, and the moon coming up somewhere behind the clouds. "Point four. I'm point four short. Of a level. Of a name."

Nobody said anything.

Kei looked at the moon. At the clouds over Carrow Cross, moving fast in the wind off the moor. At the weathercock above them that Chris had freed, turning slowly, pointing north. He thought about the twenty red sashes at the fountain, and the seven roads with Nightjar on every one of them, and the five hundred gold in Ama Corrie's iron box. And he thought about his map, the sheet of paper on his desk in Australia, with every dot a person and every line something that had already happened.

He'd rather lose the room.

He'd said that to Ice, on the steps, that afternoon. He'd meant it. He meant it now. He'd rather lose the room and the gold and the writ and the whole thing than send a man with point four of a level out onto a road in the dark to die.

He opened his mouth to say so.

"Kei," said Hyvred.

She was on the ledge on the far side of the eye, where she'd stood every evening for a month. She was looking up. Not at the roads. At the sky.

"There's something on the dome," she said.

Her voice was very calm. It was her lake voice, her weather-report voice. But under it, Kei heard something he'd never heard in it before.

"It's big," said Hyvred. "It's on the top. Above the eye. It's been there a while, I think. In the dark. I didn't look up." She was quiet for a moment. "It's looking down at the lantern."

Kei looked up.

Above the open eye, on the very top of the dome, on the curve of the stone beside the weathercock, something sat against the clouds. Something black. The size of a cart. Folded. A head on it the size of a barrel, with a beak like a pickaxe, turned down toward the light of their lantern on the ledge.

And on the counter in the Hall below, two hundred feet down, a fat old book lay open under the lamps. Ama Corrie's grandmother had shuttered the eye because it let the rain in on the Roll.

Rain, she'd said. And other things.
