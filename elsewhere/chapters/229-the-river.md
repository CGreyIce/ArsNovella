Lucan had been early his whole life.

Not for appointments. He was late for those, mostly, the way everybody was. Early in the other way. The way that mattered. He got there before the other person had decided. That was all it was, all it had ever been, in every game he'd ever played. You moved, all the time, so nobody could say *that's where he is*. And when they decided where you'd be, you were already somewhere else. And when they decided what to do about it, you'd already done something.

*You're slow*, he said, to everybody. On a forum, on a bridge. It was the truest thing he knew.

He'd been early for everyone. For the four hundred people above him on the wind game's board when he started, one at a time, until there were three. For the first fifty levels of this game, before anybody else in the world. For Dawnmere's gates. For every person who'd ever stood in front of him with a sword and thought they knew where he'd be.

For everyone but one.

A tank, on a bridge over a gorge, in the wind game. A long time ago. Who stood in the same place every morning on the board, in the top twenty, not moving, like a rock in a river. Who Lucan had gone round, again and again. And never once moved.

*I don't remember how most of them ended*, he'd said, on this bridge, in March.

That was a lie. He remembered all of them.

***

"Now," said the tank.

And moved.

Lucan saw it. He'd been watching for it for a month, every night, with twenty of his own doing the cellar at him on these stones. He'd been ready for the tank to go first. Everybody said he always went first. Kestrel had said so, in the post she wrote after the oak floor. *A boss. It always goes first.* He'd planned for it. He'd planned to be early anyway. To be moving already when the tank moved, so that whatever the tank started, Lucan was already past it.

He was moving already. He always was.

The tank moved anyway. Before him.

Not much. A shift of weight. Left foot to right. The shield coming round a hand's width. The head turning very slightly toward the east end of the bridge and back. The sword hand loose, then not, then loose.

All of it at once. All the time.

Lucan's weight went left. The tank's had already gone. Lucan's hands moved. The tank's had already moved. Lucan turned his head. The tank's head was already turning, a breath ahead, the same way.

Lucan stopped dead in the middle of the bridge.

He'd never stopped in a fight in his life.

***

*He's doing me*, thought Lucan.

He stood there on the old stone with the water roaring white underneath and looked at the man ten paces away. Moving. Always. A little, all over. Like water over stones. Never in the same place twice.

The rock was a river.

The crowd made a sound. Thousands of people on the terrace and the banks and the roofs. Lucan heard it the way you hear rain on a window, far off. He didn't look at them. He couldn't. He couldn't take his eyes off the tank, because the tank was doing the thing Lucan did. The thing nobody else had ever been able to do. And doing it a breath ahead.

He'd watched every one of the tank's fights this year. Every VOD. Every clip. The seven in the ring in the market in September. The oak floor at Elmcross. The bottom of a lake. He'd watched the tank stand in the middle of things and not move. Wait on the back foot. A breath. And then go, once, and it was over.

He'd trained for that. For a month. And for the other thing, the thing from the cellar, the rain on a tin roof. Twenty of his own doing it at him every night on these stones.

Nobody had ever told him the tank could do *this*.

Nobody knew.

***

He went in.

He had to. You didn't stand still in front of a river. He went in from the left, low, the long curved blade coming up from his hip in the cut that had taken him to the top of three boards. Fast. Early.

The tank was already gone.

Not back. Not with the shield. *Round*. The way Lucan went round things. A step and a turn, his weight already somewhere else. Lucan's blade went through the air where he'd been. And as it went through, there was a touch on Lucan's right shoulder, light, from the rim of the shield. Not a blow. A tap. The way you'd tap somebody to tell them you were there.

He'd fought a lot of people who moved. He'd been the best at it for years. You learned things, being the best at something. You learned that everybody who moved had a beat. A rhythm underneath the moving. You learned to hear it, the way you'd hear a song through a wall. And once you heard it, you could be early for it.

Lucan listened.

He went in and out, round and round, on the old stone bridge, with the water roaring under them. Four minutes, five, six. The tank going round him, always a breath ahead. Lucan going round the tank. Two rivers on a bridge over a third. And the crowd, very far off, making the sound of a crowd that doesn't understand what it's seeing and can't stop looking.

At seven minutes, Lucan heard it.

Under the moving. Under the shift of weight and the turn of the head and the loose sword hand. Something the tank couldn't hide, because it wasn't a thing he did. It was a thing he was.

The back foot.

It was still there. Under the river. The way it had been under everything the tank had done for a year and a half, since a stone in a fen. Every few steps, very briefly, it waited. A breath. Not long enough to see. Long enough to hear, if you'd been listening for a month.

*There*, thought Lucan.

***

He went in on the back foot.

The way Drew had told him. On the shingle at the bottom of the steps in March, all night, talking about feet. *I went in the breath before. I watched his feet for fifteen months. And I went in the breath before the back foot came round.* Nobody had ever done that. Not in the wind game. Not Lucan. He'd sat on the shingle with Drew until dawn thinking about it.

He did it now.

He heard the back foot start to wait, under the river, and he went. In the breath before. From the left, low, the long curved blade coming up.

It went in.

Under the rim of the shield. Along the tank's side, above the hip. A real cut. The first one.

The back foot didn't wait.

***

It came forward.

Everything did. All at once. The river stopped being a river. The shift of weight and the turn of the head and the loose sword hand, gone. And instead: the tank, coming forward. Into the place where Lucan was going. And not stopping.

Sword. Shield rim. Sword. Step. Step. Rim. Sword. Step.

Every beat.

No rests. No back foot. No breath anywhere. Like rain on a tin roof. Like somebody who'd been reading a line slow and careful for ten minutes suddenly reading it so fast you couldn't breathe. And getting every word right.

The cellar.

He knew it. He'd known it was coming. He'd trained for it for a month. Twenty of his own on these stones every night, taking turns, doing it at him, so that when it came he'd be ready.

He was ready.

He found the gaps.

***

That was the thing he'd be proudest of, afterward. Not the end. The middle. The eight minutes in the middle of the bridge in front of half of Masmark, when the tank did the thing from the cellar at him. Fifty-one seconds, people said, about a cellar nobody had seen, though nobody would say how they knew. And Lucan stood in it for eight minutes.

The gaps weren't there. There were no gaps in it. It was every beat, every one. But every beat was a beat. And between every beat and the next there was the smallest space in the world. Not a gap. Not a rest. Just the fact that one thing came after another. And Lucan, who'd been early his whole life, found that if he was early enough, he could live in it.

He moved in the space between the beats. Round the sword. Under the rim. Back from the step, and in again, behind it. He'd done it every night for a month against people who weren't the tank. It wasn't the same. It was so much faster. It was so much *more*. It was like standing in a waterfall and finding, somehow, that you could still breathe.

At eighteen minutes, he had it.

The rain was a rhythm too. That was the thing. Every beat, every one, the same. It was the fastest thing he'd ever seen, but it was the same. Sword, rim, step. And after eight minutes of living in the space between, he knew it. He was early for it. He was in it the way he'd been in the river, a breath ahead of everything. Moving before the sword came. Turning before the rim. And for the first time since *now*, he was the one who—

The tank stopped.

***

Lucan didn't.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing. He'd been moving for eighteen minutes. Moving his whole life. And for the last eight minutes he'd been moving in time with the rain. Early for every beat, every one. His body had learned it. It knew where the next beat would be.

He went there.

Into the next beat. Round the sword that was going to come. Under the rim that was going to swing.

And it didn't come. Nothing came. The tank was standing in the middle of the bridge, planted. Not on the back foot. Not light. *Down*. His feet wide, wider than Lucan had ever seen him stand. His weight gone down, into his legs, into the old stone. His sword low. His shield out. And perfectly, completely still.

Lucan was in the air.

Mid-turn. Round a sword that wasn't there. Under a rim that hadn't moved. Committed, the whole of him, the way you are when you've been early for every beat for eight minutes and the next beat doesn't come. Like a man who steps for a stair in the dark that isn't there.

He saw the sword start to move.

***

It was slow.

That was what he'd remember. Out of everything. How slow it was.

It came from the tank's right, low, from the floor. Not from the arm. He could see that. He'd been watching people swing swords his whole life, and this wasn't a swing. It came from the stone. From the tank's feet, and his hips, and his back. The whole of him going round under it, turning, slow. A one-handed sword, an ordinary one, moving the way the big one moved. The black one the size of a man, that the big quiet one at the netloft door carried.

He could see the whole of it. The whole way round. He could see exactly where it was going to end.

On the place where he was going to land.

He couldn't do anything.

He was in the air. Mid-turn. The river with nothing under it. He'd been early for a beat that didn't come, and now he was coming down onto the stone exactly where the slow, heavy, obvious sword was going to be. And it didn't matter that he could see it. It didn't matter at all. By the time he'd seen it, it was already there.

*BOOM.*

It hit his blade, the flat of the long curve, where he'd got it across himself at the last moment. And it was like being hit by a door. By a bell. By the whole weight of a man coming round from the floor up.

He went across the bridge.

Backward, off his feet, across the old stone, into the parapet. The stone of it hard against his back. And the water roaring white below.

He got up.

Of course he did. He'd never not got up. He got up off the parapet with thirty-one per cent and his blade still in his hand, numb to the shoulder. And the crowd making a sound like the bell had made at twenty to four in the morning, six seconds before the other one. And he looked at the tank.

Who was standing in the middle of the bridge, planted. Still. Heavy. Down in the stone.

Waiting.

Not the back foot. Not the heron. Lucan knew the heron; he'd gone round it for years. This wasn't that. The heron waited light, a breath, ready to go either way. This waited *heavy*. All the way down. As if there were all the time in the world, and the tank had it.

Lucan went in.

He had to. He couldn't stand still. He'd never been able to. He went in fast, faster than he ever had, the river at its fastest, because he had to fill the silence. Round, and in, and round, early, early for everything. Moving so fast the tank couldn't possibly—

The sword started to come round. Slow. From the floor.

He could see it. All of it. The whole way.

It came round to where he was going to be.

*BOOM.*

He'd set the tempo. From the first step. And Lucan had answered. He'd been a river, so Lucan had been a river, and learned it, and got early for it. And the moment Lucan was early for it, it had been rain. And Lucan had learned the rain, and got early for that. And the moment he was early for the rain, it had been a bell.

***

He got up again. He didn't know why. Seventeen per cent. He got up because he'd never not got up, and because he wanted to see what came next. He wanted to see it so much he couldn't stand still.

The tank was waiting. Heavy. Down.

Lucan didn't go in.

For the first time in his life, in any fight, he didn't go. He stood at the east end of the bridge with his blade up and seventeen per cent and his weight on both feet. Still. The way he'd stood on Saturday to ask. The way he'd sat on the shingle with Drew all night, talking about feet. Not moving.

*Let him go first*, thought Lucan. *He always does. Let him. And this time be still for it.*

The tank looked at him across ten paces of bridge.

And then the tank did something Lucan had never seen him do, in three years, on any board, in any VOD.

He smiled.

Not the corner of the mouth. All of it. On a bridge in Masmark, in the middle of a fight, in front of half the city. He smiled at Lucan, the way you'd smile at somebody who'd finally understood a joke you'd been telling them for three years.

And came.

Thousands of people. The terrace and the banks and the roofs and the boats on the lake. Silent. Lucan could hear the water under the bridge, roaring, the way it always had. And his own breathing. And the tank's, above him, very hard. The tank was breathing very hard.

A line along his side and his shield arm and his thigh and his sword arm and his neck where Lucan had been, five times, inside the river and the rain. He was breathing like a man who'd run a long way.

He'd tried. Lucan could see it. He'd actually tried.

"Yield?" said the tank.

He'd moved him tonight. Five times. On the back foot and in the rain. *There you are*, the tank had said, at seven minutes. Lucan had heard it. Nobody else would have. Under the roar of the water. *There you are.*

***

Lucan laughed.

On his back, on the old stone, at six per cent, with a sword at his neck and his blade gone into the third stream. He laughed until he couldn't breathe. It went up off the bridge into the dark and over the water. And somewhere on the terrace, somebody laughed with him. And then somebody else. And then the whole of the north terrace and the banks and the roofs, thousands of people, laughing and screaming and making the sound a crowd makes when it has just seen something it will tell people about for the rest of its life.

"Yield," said Lucan, through it. "*Yield*. Yes. Yield."

The tank took the sword away.

He didn't step back. He stood there over Lucan for a moment. And then he did something nobody would believe afterward, even with the clip, even with Tif's clip on a loop. He put his sword away. And held out his hand, palm up, the way Lucan had heard he'd held it out by a fish cart in February.

Lucan looked at the hand.

He took it.

"Was that everything?" said Lucan.

"All three," said the tank. "The fen. Before. And one I borrowed." He was still breathing hard. "Nobody's seen all three. Not at once."

"Thank you," he said.

The tank looked at him.

"For asking properly," said the tank. "And for training. For a month." He let go of Lucan's hand. "I couldn't have used the third one if you hadn't been ready for the second."

***

He looked past the tank, then. To the west end of the bridge.

She was there. Where she'd been the whole fight. At the end of the bridge, on the old stone, with her bow on her back. The woman with the gold horns, who didn't like him, who'd said *I'll watch*. She was standing very straight. Both hands over her mouth. Her eyes on the tank, all of him, the way the tank said she saw everything.

She'd watched. All twenty-seven minutes. Lucan could tell.

"She watched," said Lucan.

The tank looked round.

"She looked," he said.

And walked back across the bridge to her. Not fast. He always went slowly. Past the place where Lucan had first gone in on the back foot, in the breath before. Past the place in the middle where the rain had been, and the place where it had stopped. To the end of the bridge.

She took her hands off her mouth.

Lucan didn't hear what she said. Nobody did; the crowd was too loud. But he saw her hit him on the arm, not hard. Twice. And then put her forehead against his shoulder. And the tank's hand come up, slowly, to the back of her head, between the gold horns.

He looked away. The way everybody in Constellation looked at the harbour when two people were being two people. He'd learned that from Drew too.
