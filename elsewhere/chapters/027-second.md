There was a link to the sheet. There was always a link to the sheet. He didn't open it. He opened the other link instead, the one Chris had sent at half past three with no comment. It went to the forum, to a post the moderators had pinned within the hour. It already had nine thousand replies.

It was four lines long.

> Second.
> Congratulations again to Moonlight Harvest. Properly, this time. You sang it. We hit it. Both work.
> See you at the next one.
> K

He read it twice. Then he put the phone face down on his chest and lay there looking at the ceiling fan going round.

Their own door. In a salt marsh on the other side of the world, with herons carved on it and five hollows for five hands, and their own green steps going down into their own drowned church. The same nave. The same pews in the clear cold water. The same Precentor with his same staff, and the same Choirmaster at the altar with the bone baton, keeping the same time. Four nights they'd gone down those steps, and four nights they'd come back up. The fifth time, at four in the morning, too late for any window, they'd done it anyway.

He found he was glad.

It surprised him. He'd expected something smaller and meaner. *Too late*, or *we beat you*. And it was there, a little, if he went looking for it. But mostly, lying in the dark with the fan going round, what he felt was that it had been real. There'd been somebody on the far side of the world going down the same steps on the same nights. Wiping where they'd wiped. Learning the downbeat the way they'd learned it, one cold minute at a time. It had been a race, and there'd been someone in the other lane, and she was good.

And he'd won it.

He lay there a moment longer with that. Then he smelled coffee, and remembered what day it was, and got up.

Suzu was in the kitchen with the kettle on and his backpack by the front door.

He'd made two cups of kopi-o in the two mugs that didn't match, and he was standing at the counter in a black T-shirt, looking out of the kitchen window at the block across the way. There was nothing out of the kitchen window but the block across the way and somebody's washing. There never had been.

"You're up," said Suzu.

"Vanta cleared it."

"I know." Suzu pushed one of the mugs along the counter toward him without looking round. "Chris messaged me too. I said *good*."

"Good?"

"They earned it." Suzu drank his coffee. "Four nights. I'd have hated them if they hadn't."

Ice laughed and picked up his mug.

The bus was at half past eight. Suzu hadn't gone home last weekend. He'd said he would, on the drive in from Melaka Sentral on the first day, *home on weekends, starting next week*. Then there had been a ford, and five people who needed to be five, and he'd stayed. He'd video-called his mum on Saturday night instead, sitting on the end of the bed in the guest room with the fan going round, and read her post to her through the phone. Ice had heard his voice through the wall, low and patient, going on for a long time.

He wasn't staying this weekend. The backpack had been by the door since last night.

They drove in with the windows down because the car's air conditioning took ten minutes to admit it existed. Melaka on a Saturday morning was already warm and already busy. Motorbikes, a lorry full of coconuts, an uncle on a bicycle with a long wooden ladder over his shoulder, perfectly balanced and not looking at anything.

"The stuff," said Suzu, at the first lights.

"The haul."

"Mine." Suzu was looking out of his window. "Sell it with yours. Whatever you're doing, do it with mine too."

"Okay."

"Not to the red ones."

"Not to the red ones."

"And I want the lights." He said it the same way, flatly, to the window. "All forty. Don't let Xav sell them. He'll want to sell them, because they're worth something. I don't care what they're worth."

Ice looked at him. "You want all forty."

"I want to know where they go." The lights changed. "One's not enough to find out."

Ice drove on. Past the river, past the old red buildings, past the sign for the bus terminal. He thought about the jar. *Reliquary Jar. What you keep in it does not fade.* He thought about Suzu standing on the altar step in the drowned church with the lights going up around him like snow falling the wrong way, and his blade not moving, and one light held back in his hand.

"Okay," he said. "The lights are yours."

"Good."

They pulled into the drop-off at twenty past. Suzu didn't get out straight away. He sat with his hand on the door handle and his backpack on his knees.

"I told her," he said. "Last night. After you went to bed."

"Your mum?"

"About the church." He was looking at the backpack. "I put the video on and I told her what was happening. All of it. Four hours." A pause. "She can't see it. So I said it. The water, and the pews, and Deston in the shower that morning." Another pause. "The lights."

Ice didn't say anything. He didn't trust what would come out.

"She said I sounded happy," said Suzu.

Then he opened the door and got out and swung the backpack onto his shoulder. He bent down and looked back in through the open window.

"Eat properly," he said.

"That's your mum's line."

"She told me to say it again." The corner of his mouth went up about a millimetre. "Sunday night. Nine o'clock bus. Don't be late."

He went in through the glass doors with the crowd, a tall thin figure in black, and didn't look back. Ice sat in the drop-off until a man in a fluorescent vest knocked on the bonnet.

The shoplot was quiet without him.

Ice let himself in through the glass front door at ten to nine. He walked through the front office, which had a desk and a chair and a pot plant Vivian had sent and nothing else, because nobody had ever worked in it. Then the back room. Two white eggs side by side with the fan going round above them. The folding table. The kettle. Suzu's pillow, in its dark blue pillowcase, on the floor where he'd slept on it last night, because he'd said the floor was cooler.

Ice moved it onto the chair. Then he sat down at the folding table with his laptop and opened the Exchange.

He clicked through them one by one. *CL_Procurement_02. CL_Procurement_07. CL_Procurement_11. CL_Procurement_19. CL_Procurement_23.* Thirty-one accounts, every one with a red sash on its little portrait, every one asking for Choir Silver at one hundred and forty dollars, any quantity. Every few minutes, somebody somewhere who'd scraped one bit of silver out of somewhere put it up for sale, and it was gone before the page refreshed.

He sat back.

He'd seen this before. Not here. In a different game, years ago, in a different universe made of spreadsheets. He'd watched a corporation buy every single unit of one particular mineral across a whole region of space for three weeks. Quietly, through a hundred accounts, at a little over the going price, so nobody noticed. At the end of the three weeks there was nobody left in the region who had any of it except them. And then the price was whatever they said it was. For a month, everyone who wanted to build anything that needed it had bought it from one corporation, at one price, and said thank you.

He'd been nineteen. He'd thought it was the cleverest thing he'd ever seen.

*Everyone sells to us eventually*, CL_Procurement_07 had said, on the boardwalk, three weeks ago.

*Name your price*, she'd said last night.

They didn't want the haul because they wanted the haul. They wanted it because there was hardly any choir silver in the whole of Elsewhere. Twenty-four bits in his party's bags. Twenty-four in VANTA's. A handful of odd ones that singers had dropped in the first rooms on nights when people ran, like the one Kotori kept in a sock. VANTA didn't sell. VANTA wore. If Crimson Ledger owned the rest, then until someone else got down a set of green steps and back up again, there was no Choir Silver in the world that didn't belong to them.

Then the price would be whatever they said it was.

He closed the laptop and went to lie down in the egg.

He didn't go live.

He logged in where he'd logged out, inside the heron-stones, the way he always did. The warm clear water was round his ankles and the nine grey stones stood in their ring with their long necks bent. The moonbells were shut tight in the sun like little green fists. It was mid-morning in the fen. A heron was standing on one leg in the shallows outside the ring, pretending to be a stone, and doing a better job of it than the stones.

*It's not going dark*, he told himself, *if you never went light*. He could hear exactly what Tif would say to that. He decided he'd hear it later.

Deston was already there. He was sitting on the flat stone at the north side of the ring with the book open on his knees and the hymnal pages spread out beside him on the rock, all five, weighed down with pebbles. He was frowning at them.

"I can't read them," he said, before Ice had asked.

"At all?"

"The words are under water." Deston lifted one carefully by the corner and turned it to the light. It was old brown vellum, soft as cloth, and the writing on it was there and not there, the way writing on the bottom of a stream is there. "It says so. Look."

So we don't sell them ours. We put it in nine different guilds across the whole world, in people who'll make it into swords and use them. And then there isn't a corner. There's just silver."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The heron outside the ring gave up being a stone and walked off through the shallows on its long legs.

"Three each," said Xav. He wrote it down. "Named contracts. Red sash means off the list." He looked up, and his mouth was doing the thing it did. "You know we're leaving about three thousand dollars on the table."

"I know."

"Fine." He folded the paper. "Then I'm selling all of mine today. Everything. Into the contracts, at your prices, straight away."

Ice looked at him. "You don't want to wait? It's only going to—"

"I want cash." Xav grinned, quickly, and looked away at the stones. "I've got plans."

He didn't say what plans. Ice didn't ask. He noticed, the way he noticed a man's back foot, and put it away for later.

She did it on her fingers first, with her lips moving, and then in the Exchange window, and then on her fingers again, to check. Twenty-four bits of silver at a hundred and ten. "No," said three people at once.

It was the draughts that did it, in the end.

He'd forgotten about the draughts. He'd been brewing them every evening for a month, in the little clay pot over Sedge's hearth and then at the inn fire in Brackenford, out of fenwort that Rafi picked and silverleaf from the beds. Fair ones and Poor ones for the crate by Marta's door at forty copper and twenty, the same as always. Good ones never, for anybody, because the Good ones had moonbell in them, and nobody needed to know that. He had forty-one Good ones in his bag right now, the same forty-one he'd had since before the door, because when they went down they'd hardly needed them.

He'd never put draughts on the Exchange. The crate had always been enough. But it was the middle of a Saturday afternoon, and he had sixty Fair ones in his bag and a market window already open. So he put up the sixty at forty copper each, the same as the crate. Four cents, US.

They were gone in nine seconds.

He blinked. He refreshed. They were gone. All sixty. Sixty different buyers, sixty times forty copper, and the money was already in his wallet.

"Hm," she said. She went back inside. A minute later she came out again with a stool, and set it down beside the crate, and sat on it. She folded her arms and looked down the boardwalk toward the causeway, like a woman who'd been told the tide was coming in.

He followed her look.

It was the fen's night by then, the second of the day, with lanterns lit along the boardwalks. Up the causeway from the north-east, where the fen turned to fields, the morning walk from Brackenford, there were people coming. Not a few. A line of them, with lanterns. A long, loose, wandering line of travellers in new gear and old gear and beginner tunics. They were spread out along the raised road for as far as he could see, all walking toward the marsh town at the end of it, all looking around them. Looking at the reeds and the water and the round houses on their stilts, the way you look at a place you've seen in a video.

The first of them were already at the bridge.
