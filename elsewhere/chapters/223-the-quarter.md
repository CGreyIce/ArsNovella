The day after the bell, four hundred people came down the north-west road to the netloft, and Wendy kept the door.

It wasn't a real gate. It was the netloft's one door, at the end of a lane off the north-west harbour, the one that stuck. And her, standing in it. Bow on her back, hands in her pockets, the way she'd stood at the bottom of the south steps in the round town for a year and a half. Salty on her left with his black sword planted point-down in the cobbles of the lane like a post. And four hundred people, all the way back up the lane and round the corner onto the harbour, wanting in.

They'd heard the bell. Everybody had. Two bells, six seconds apart, at twenty to four in the morning, and then a third. And then the board, in the corner of every window in Masmark: *Constellation — rung · first.* A name that had meant nothing in this city in March. *Nobody*, on a switchback in the dark. Sixty is not a guild, young man, sixty is a *wedding*.

Now four hundred people wanted to sign it.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy.

The first one, a tall girl with a spear, looked at her blankly.

"What?"

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy again, patiently. "If you were in. Who'd you stand next to?"

The girl thought about it.

"*Him*," she said, and pointed past Wendy into the netloft. At the old bench under the wavy window, where a man was sitting with a cup of tea and a woman with gold horns asleep on his shoulder. "The tank. Obviously."

"Fair," said Wendy. "But that's not how it works. He doesn't stand next to anybody. He stands in front." She smiled. "And the Roll's in Carrow Cross. The round town. Seven roads. That's where you sign. If they say yes."

"That's *thirty days* away—"

"Fourteen," said Wendy. "Each way." She looked at the girl. "If you'd walk fourteen days to stand next to somebody, you'll find out who on the road."

***

She sent all four hundred away like that, over two days. Kindly. Most of them went. Some of them argued. A few of them, she could tell from their faces, would actually walk.

She liked those ones.

"You're good at this," said Salty, on the second evening, when the lane was empty at last and she'd sat down on the netloft step with her back against the doorframe.

"I've had practice."

"You were good at it the first day," said Salty. "In the round town. When it was just ten people and a swamp." He pulled his sword out of the cobbles, looked at the point, put it back. "You've always been good at it."

Wendy looked at him.

He'd walked eleven days to meet her, once, in the first winter. They'd held hands across eighteen inches every night for two months before that. And when they met at a stone cross on the moor, she'd said *eighteen inches* and put her hand flat on his chest. He'd forged a sword the size of a man in the dark and quenched it in a stream with the lamp out, and she'd held the lamp. He'd stepped out one pace on the longest night and the whole dark had leaned back, and she'd had his belt.

He didn't say much. When he did, he meant it.

"Thank you," said Wendy.

Salty went slightly pink and looked at the harbour.

***

The red cloths started on the Tuesday.

She saw the first one from the door at the first dawn. The rope-walk, at the top of the lane, the long low shed where an old man and his grandson twisted rope by hand. There was a strip of dark red cloth tied to the latch. A clean one. New.

By the second dawn there was one on the chandler's, two doors down. By the third, on the smokehouse.

"What are they?" said Hyvred. She was on the step beside Wendy, with her knees up, looking at the lane. All of it, edge to edge. Three red cloths, very bright against the old grey wood.

Wendy didn't know. She found out on Wednesday.

***

The Open Hand came down the lane at the second dusk on Wednesday, forty of them, in dark red, in step.

Wendy heard them before she saw them. Forty pairs of boots on the cobbles of a lane built for net-makers and fish. The sound came round the corner off the harbour like a drum. She stood up in the door. Salty stood up beside her and pulled his sword out of the cobbles and didn't put it anywhere, just held it.

At their front, the big man. The one with the bigger voice, who'd stood in front of the fish man's cart in February and said *name your price* to a tank with a fish in his hand. Wendy had never heard his name said out loud. She heard it now, because one of the forty said it to him, quietly, as they stopped.

"Marrok," said the man behind him. "This is it."

Marrok looked at the netloft.

He looked at it for a long time. The old grey boards. The wavy window. The one door that stuck. The two iron hooks inside, just visible past Wendy's shoulder, with a banner on them. Deep blue, with a gold star in the middle that hadn't been painted by anybody.

"*This*," said Marrok, "is the smallest Hall in Masmark."

"Yes," said Wendy.

"It's a *netloft*."

"Yes."

"You rang the Bell Above," said Marrok, "out of a *netloft*." He shook his head slowly. He had a big face, red from the wind, and a black open hand on his chest. "I've got eleven Halls in this city. One of them has a fountain *inside it*. And I've got six people left out of twenty from the bell tower, because nobody told us the stair moved." He looked at her. "And you've got a netloft that smells of fish."

"It's a good netloft," said Wendy.

***

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy.

She didn't plan it. It came out of her the way it always came out, at the door, to whoever was standing in front of it. Marrok looked at her as if she'd spoken in a language the game didn't bother to translate.

"What?"

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Wendy. "It's what I ask. At the door. Everybody."

Marrok laughed. Big and loud, the way he did everything, for the lane.

"*Eleven thousand two hundred*," he said.

"That's not a person," said Wendy.

The laugh stopped.

"It's eleven thousand two hundred persons," said Marrok.

"Then pick one," said Wendy.

He looked at her.

It went on for a long time. The forty behind him in dark red, not moving. Salty beside her, very still, with his black sword in his hands. The lane. The red cloths on three doors. Somewhere behind her in the netloft, the small sound of a cup being put down on a bench.

Marrok didn't pick one.

He didn't say anything. He just looked at her. And Wendy, who'd stood at a gate for a year and a half and asked a thousand people that question, could see him trying. Going through them, the eleven thousand two hundred. Looking for one face.

And not finding it.

"I'm here to buy your lease," said Marrok, instead.

***

Kei came to the door then. He'd been at the long table with his maps. He stood in the doorway beside Wendy with a pencil behind his ear.

"No," said Kei.

"I haven't said a number."

"No," said Kei again.

Marrok looked at him. Then he looked past both of them into the netloft. At the bench under the wavy window, where the tank was sitting with his cup and Hyvred awake now beside him, very straight. Looking back at Marrok with eyes that saw the whole lane at once and didn't like any of it.

"A thousand gold," said Marrok. Loud, for the lane. "For the lease. For a *netloft*. Ten thousand dollars, real money. For a room that smells of fish."

"No," said Kei.

"Two thousand."

"It's not for sale," said Kei.

"*Everything* is for sale," said Marrok. "That's the game. That's the whole game. That's what it says on the box." He pointed at the three red cloths up the lane. "The rope-walk sold. The chandler sold. The smokehouse sold. An old man and his grandson twisting rope by hand for three hundred years, and he sold for four hundred gold, because nobody's bought rope from him since February. Since the cities opened and everybody started buying it from the stalls on the east rim." He spread his hands. The black open hand on his chest. "Everything's for sale. Everybody has a price."

Behind Wendy, in the netloft, the tank put his cup down on the bench.

"Not everything," said Ice.

***

He didn't get up. He didn't come to the door. He just said it, from the bench, under the wavy window, not loud.

Marrok looked at him.

"That's what you said on the harbour," said Marrok. "In February. *Sixty chairs.*"

"Sixty-one," said Ice. "Now."

Marrok laughed again. It wasn't as big this time.

"I'm going to buy the quarter," he said. Not to Ice. To Kei, and Wendy, and the lane. "There's a charter. In the Rim Hall. Old as the city. A guild that holds the leases of more than half the doors in a quarter can ask the harbour reeve to seal it. As theirs. And build what they like." He looked up the lane. "Thirty-one doors in the north-west quarter. I need sixteen. I've got three." He smiled. "By Sunday I'll have twelve. By the end of the month I'll have sixteen. And then I'll pull down every one of them and build a Hall with a fountain in it, facing the island, so everybody in Masmark can see it when the next bell rings." He looked at the netloft. "And you'll be a room that smells of fish in the middle of my Hall."

"Okay," said Ice.

Marrok stared at him.

Then he turned round, and the forty in dark red turned round with him, in step. They went back up the lane, past the three red cloths, round the corner onto the harbour. The sound of them went away like a drum.

***

Wendy sat down on the step.

"*Okay*?" she said, to the netloft behind her. "*Okay?* He's going to pull down the *quarter*—"

"It's his to buy," said Ice. "If they sell."

"*Ice*—"

"It's theirs to sell," said Ice. He was looking out of the wavy window at the lane. At the rope-walk at the top, with its red cloth on the latch. "The old man sold because nobody's bought his rope since February. That's true. I didn't buy any." He was quiet. "It's his rope-walk. It's his to say."

Wendy looked at him for a long time.

She thought about the round town. About the gate, and the question, and *fair*. Whatever anybody said, you said *fair*. And then you sat on the steps with them after.

"You're not going to tell them not to sell," she said.

"No."

"Even the boatbuilder? Your *room*?"

"It's his," said Ice. "I rent it." He picked up his cup again. "If he sells, I'll find another room."

Hyvred, beside him, said nothing. But Wendy saw her hand go very tight on his.

***

He didn't tell them not to sell. Wendy watched him not.

She watched him all week. He went down to the bottom of the north-west road every evening at the second dusk and bought a fish from the fish man and ate it on the harbour wall with Hyvred. He went up the lane past the red cloths and said *good evening* to everybody, the way he always had, and they said *good evening* back. He sat on the bench in the netloft and drank tea.

He didn't say one word to any of them about the Open Hand.

But on Thursday, Wendy saw him go into the rope-walk.

She didn't follow him. She didn't need to. She stood in the netloft door and watched him go up the lane and in through the rope-walk's low door, under the red cloth. He came out half an hour later with a coil of rope over his shoulder. Thick, hand-twisted, the colour of straw. He came down the lane and in past her and put it on the floor of the netloft under the hooks.

"What's that for?" said Wendy.

"I don't know yet," said Ice. "I hadn't bought any since February."

The red cloth was still on the rope-walk's latch. He hadn't asked the old man to take it off. He'd just bought some rope.

***

By Sunday there were twelve red cloths in the lane.

The rope-walk. The chandler. The smokehouse. The cooper's. The sail-loft. Four of the six netmenders' houses on the long row by the water, where old women sat on the steps in the evenings mending nets in the last of the light. A tavern. The tackle shop. A house nobody lived in.

Wendy counted them from the door every morning. Twelve. Then she counted the ones without. Nineteen.

The fish man's pitch, at the bottom of the north-west road, where his cart had stood for three hundred years. The boatbuilder's, with Ice's room over it. The two last netmenders, the oldest, sisters, who'd mended nets on the same step since before the city had a Rim Hall. The baker. The old harbour-master's house, empty, with the reeve's seal on the door. And the netloft.

Marrok's clerks had been to all of them. Twice. Wendy had watched them go in and out with their purses and their papers. The fish man had sent them away so loudly that people on the harbour wall turned round. The two old sisters had sent them away with a net-needle each, held point-out, and said nothing at all. The boatbuilder, an old man called Abel with sawdust in his eyebrows, had listened to them for a long time and said he'd think about it.

"He's going to sell," said Hyvred, on the step, on Sunday night. "Abel. I saw his face."

"Maybe," said Wendy.

"He's old. He's got no boats to build. Nobody's ordered one since the cities opened. Everybody buys them on the east rim now, ready-made." Hyvred was looking up the lane at the boatbuilder's, the big shed at the top with the room over it and a lamp in the window. Ice's lamp. "He's going to sell, and the room's going to go, and Ice won't say anything. Because it's his to say."

Wendy looked at her.

"Does that bother you?" she said.

Hyvred thought about it, with her knees up and her tail round her feet.

"No," she said at last. "That's the bit I like." She was quiet. "He never tells anybody what to do. Not ever. He just stands where he is and lets them decide." She swallowed. "He did it to me. At your gate."

Wendy put her arm round her.

***

On Monday, the red ship moved.

Wendy saw it at the first dawn, from the door. She'd come out to count the cloths. She didn't count them. She stood in the doorway of the netloft and looked past the end of the lane at the harbour.

The Open Hand's great red ship had been anchored out in the deep water off the island since March, the way it had to be, too big for any harbour. It wasn't there.

It was here.

Across the mouth of the north-west harbour. Where the two old stone moles came round from either side and nearly met, with a gap between them just wide enough for fishing boats. Anchored fore and aft, across the gap, side-on. With its great red sail furled and the black open hand painted on its hull, enormous, facing the harbour.

The gap was closed.

Nothing could get in. Nothing could get out.

"*Oh*," said Salty, behind her.

***

The fishing boats came at the first dawn the way they always did. Wendy watched them from the harbour wall with Salty and Kei and half of the twenty-two. Forty small boats, from out on the lake, where they'd been all night. Coming in to the north-west harbour with their catch for the fish man and the smokehouse and the stalls on the quay.

They came to the gap and stopped.

The red ship sat across it, side-on. Men in dark red on its rail, looking down. Not doing anything. Not saying anything. Just there.

The fishing boats sat in the water outside the gap for a long time. Then, one by one, they turned round and went away. Round the rim. To the other harbours, Wendy supposed, the east ones, where there were other fish men and other carts.

The fish man stood at the bottom of the north-west road beside his cart, under its striped awning, and watched them go.

His cart was empty.

Wendy had never seen it empty. Not once, in four months. Not since the day a tank had put a fish down on its edge to go and hold somebody's hand.

"It's not against the rules," said Kei. Very quietly, beside her, looking at the red ship. "I checked. It's their ship. It's anchored. It's not attacking anybody." He was quiet. "It's just in the way."

"It's to make them sell," said Wendy.

"Yes."

"If nobody can land, nobody can buy fish. If nobody can buy fish, the smokehouse doesn't need the fish man. If the smokehouse doesn't need him—"

"Yes," said Kei.

Wendy looked at the fish man. Three hundred years at the bottom of the same road. Boot face. Standing beside an empty cart, with his hands in his apron.

He saw her looking. He didn't say anything. He just looked back, the way he'd looked at the tank in February. As if he'd seen every kind of person at the bottom of this road, and every kind of hurry, and this was just another one.

***

That night, on the netloft step, with the lane dark and twelve red cloths on twelve doors and the red ship across the harbour mouth with lamps on its rail, Salty sat down beside her.

"What do we do?" he said.

Wendy thought about it.

"We keep the gate," she said.

"There's nobody coming through it."

"Then we keep it anyway," said Wendy. "That's the job. You don't stop keeping it because nobody's coming." She looked at the red ship. "That's when you keep it most."

Salty didn't say anything for a while. Then he put his hand out, palm down, flat, on the step between them. Not on her. Just beside her. Eighteen inches from where her hand was.

She put hers on top of it.

Neither of them said anything. They sat on the netloft step in the dark like that for a long time.

***

Ice came back up the lane at the third dawn, from the bottom of the road, wet to the waist.

Wendy saw him coming. She stood up. Salty stood up. He came up the lane past the twelve red cloths with his shield on his back and the coil of straw-coloured rope over his shoulder, wet. And Rafi beside him, with the big black sword that hummed, wet to the chest. Rafi was grinning.

They stopped in front of the netloft door.

"Where've you *been*?" said Wendy. "You're soaked. Both of you. Did you fall in the *lake*—"

"The Undercliffs," said Ice.

Wendy looked at him.

He'd levelled in the Undercliffs alone in February, while Aurelie watched him. And with Hyvred every night since. The drowned roads, under the new ones, all the way round the lake inside the crater wall. Dark and wet and quiet, the way he liked it.

"I went further," said Ice. "Past where I usually go. On the north side. Down." He looked at Rafi. "Rafi came."

"Boss asked," said Rafi. "Properly."

"There's an old road," said Ice. "Under the north-west quarter. Under the harbour. It goes down, and it keeps going down, and it comes out at the water." He was dripping on the cobbles. "Under the moles. A long way under. Below where the ship's anchored. A channel, in the rock. Old. Big enough for a boat. A small one." He paused. "It comes up in the netloft's cellar."

Nobody said anything.

"The grate," said Salty slowly. "In the cellar. Where the harbour comes in."

"It's not the harbour," said Ice. "It's the lake. The grate's the end of the channel. The water in the cellar comes in from *under* the moles. From outside." He looked past Wendy into the netloft. At the trapdoor in the corner by the wavy window, under the nets, where the ladder went down. "That's why it breathes."

Wendy stared at him.

"There's another way in," said Ice.
