It was Azac's first longest night, and he'd only realised it on Saturday.

He'd been at the long table with Kei's map, looking at the north door, working out where the wall should go. Somebody had said something about *last time*. About Fernwick, and the ring of nine, and Salty in his shaft and Gigi at her gate. And Azac had opened his mouth to say *last time I*—

And stopped. Because there hadn't been a last time. Not for him.

Riverbank had started on the first of January. All eight of them, in the old cinema in Singapore, in a row of booths on the steps. Xav on a call from Japan, already nine days ahead. The first Long Night had been on the twenty-first of December. Eleven days before. They'd watched it, most of them, on Dec's stream and Kei's and Higu's, in their rooms, in the dark.

They hadn't been in it. None of them. Not RX, not Malcolm, not John, not Ryan or Val or Jun Liang. Not him.

"It's our first," said Azac, at the long table, out loud. "Isn't it. The seven of us. It's our first one."

Xav looked up from his paper sketchbook.

"Yes," he said.

"Huh," said Azac.

He sat with that for a while. Then he went back to the map, because a wall didn't care whether it was your first one or your fiftieth. A wall just had to be where it had to be.

***

The north door was the problem.

Kei's map had the round town in the middle, and the seven roads going out, and the line of ninety-four dots going north up one of them to the edge. And John's report, in very small letters, at the top: *the whole black, moving south, like a tide on the turn.* And Kei's arrow, pointing down the road of lights to the round town. To the Hall of Roads. To the north door.

It wasn't a big door. That was the thing. Azac had gone and stood in it on Sunday, in the grey morning, and measured it with his own shield, the biggest one in the guild. Two shields wide. A bit more. The Hall of Roads had seven doors, one for every road, and the north one was the smallest and the oldest. It stuck. Gabe had fixed it in July and it still stuck. Ama Corrie said it had always stuck. Behind it, inside, the Hall, and the floor of the Hall with Bee's gold dots and silver lines on it. And at the north end of the banner room, by itself, facing the door, the chair.

"Everything comes here," Ama Corrie had said to Maigo, in November, at one in the morning. *And if it ever comes south all at once, it comes here. So keep the lamps.*

Everything. Down ninety-four lamps. All at once. To a door two shields wide.

"So we make the door the light," said Azac.

He said it on Monday night, at the long table, with everybody there. Kei had asked him to. Kei had said *you're the wall, you tell them where the wall goes*. So he stood up at Riverbank's end of the second table with the map in front of him and his bread in one hand, and told them.

"They go to lights," said Azac. "Dec said. Hako saw. They lean to the nearest one and go to it. And the road brings them here, one lamp after another, all the way down, to the last one." He put his finger on the first waystone, a mile out of the north door, where Tiger's three would be. "That's the last lamp on the road. After that, the next nearest light is the town." He moved his finger. "The north door. If it's lit."

"So we light it," said Tiger.

"So we light it *brighter than anything else*," said Azac. "Brighter than the forge. Brighter than the windows. Brighter than the eye. We make the north door the biggest light in the round town. So they come *there*. Not to the windows, not to the market, not to the houses. Like Emberly's forge." He looked at Ina. "You fed it till it roared and they walked past the farms to you."

"I did," said Ina.

"So we do that. At the north door. And we stand in it." He put the bread down, because this was the important bit. "Three threes. Left, middle, right. Thea's three on the left. Salty's in the middle. Mine on the right. A wall. Two shields wide." He looked round the table. "And everything comes to the wall. And nothing gets past it. And the rest of the town *sleeps*."

The banner room was quiet.

"What about the Seven Below?" said Gigi, from chair twenty-one.

Azac looked down the room. At the first of the nine, where Dec was sitting with his feet on the rung, listening. At Hyvred beside Bee, not looking at anybody.

"That's not mine," said Azac. "That's theirs."

Dec looked back at him down the long table. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

***

He went and found Dec on the Tuesday, in the north tunnel, because two walls should talk.

He'd done it before. On the plain road in the spring, walking east, Gabe and he had talked shields for four hours. The weight, the rim, the angle, the way you held the strap. Dec had walked behind them the whole way and not said a word. Afterwards Gabe had said *he was listening, though*. Gabe always knew when Dec was listening.

Dec was at the cold place at the end of the tunnel. His back to the wall where the old stone met the new, the way he always was. Hyvred and Bee were a little way back up the tunnel, sitting on the floor against the side, eating something. Bee waved. Hyvred didn't look up.

Azac went and sat down at the cold place beside Dec, with his enormous shield against the wall behind him.

It *was* cold. He hadn't believed it, from the stream. He'd thought it was the sort of thing Dec said. It was colder than the moor in December. Colder than anything in the game. A cold that came through the old stone into your back and sat there.

"Huh," said Azac.

"Yes," said Dec.

They sat there for a while.

"It's going to come up through here," said Azac. "Isn't it."

"Some of it."

"How much?"

Dec thought about it. "Don't know. Some. The Delvers left. Things move away from where something's coming." He looked at the black water in the groove in the floor, the little stream. "It's still water down past the end. Under the old stone. Hyvred can see it. There's a lot of it."

"They come up out of still water."

"Yes."

Azac thought about that. He thought about the road of lights, ninety-four lamps, all facing north. About the north door, two shields wide. And about this, underneath, where nobody would see. Still water under old stone, and a tunnel leading straight up into the Seven Below. And the Seven Below had a hatch, and the hatch came up under Ama Corrie's stair. In the middle of the Hall.

"If it comes up through here," he said slowly, "and gets past you—"

"It won't."

"If it does, it comes up the hatch. Into the Hall. Behind us." He looked at Dec. "Behind the wall."

"I know," said Dec. "That's why I'm here."

Azac didn't say anything for a while.

He'd known Dec for years. Through Xav, through the others, on a call one night on Discord. Dec had been the tank, in every game they'd ever played together. Always. Azac had never minded. There was nobody in the world he'd rather stand next to than a man who never flinched.

"You don't get the door," said Azac. "On the longest night. You don't get to stand in the door where everyone can see. You're under it. In the dark. With two people."

"Yes."

"Do you mind?"

Dec looked at him. And then he did a thing Azac hadn't seen him do much, in all the years. He smiled. Not big. Just at the corner. The way you smile at a friend who's asked a question he already knows the answer to.

"You're the door," said Dec. "I'm the floor."

Azac laughed. It came out of him like a cough, surprised, too big for the tunnel, and went away down the dark to the north and came back.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I'll hold the door. You hold the floor."

"Deal," said Dec.

Up the tunnel, Bee laughed too, and Hyvred hit her in the arm.

***

The sword was finished on Wednesday.

Azac was at the long table when Salty brought it in. Everybody was. Salty came in from Joss's smithy at the second dusk with it on his back. Not wrapped in sacking now. Bare. Black. The length of a man. And the whole banner room went quiet, the way it had gone quiet for the blue banner coming round the west road.

He didn't make a speech. Salty never made speeches. He came down the long table to the empty space in the middle, where Kei unrolled the map on Sundays. He lifted the sword off his back with both hands and laid it down on the table, very gently, the whole length of it.

It was beautiful.

Azac didn't know much about swords. He knew shields. But he knew it was beautiful the way you know a mountain is. The blade was black, all the way, and dull, and so dark that the lamplight seemed to sink into it and not come out. The guard was black too, a plain cross, heavy and square. Ina said afterward it was made from nineteen broken test pieces melted down together, nineteen failures, every one. *Nothing wasted*, Salty had said. The grip was wrapped in leather. Dark, plain, very neatly done, so tight you couldn't see where the wrap began. A little stamp on the end of it in the shape of a cat's paw.

"Milli did the grip," said Salty. "Gabe did the rivets. Ina did the heat. Drew taught me to hit it with my hips." He looked down the table. "Wendy told me why it cracked."

At the far end, by the window, Milli had gone faintly pink and was looking at his hands. Hana, beside him, was beaming.

A window had opened over the sword. Everyone could see it. Salty must have let it show.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"*Right Size*," said Gabe, finally. In a voice of enormous emotion. "Salty. You called it *Right Size*."

"Ice said it," said Salty. "In the tunnel. When it came out of the water." He shrugged. "I couldn't think of anything better."

Azac looked down the long table. At the first of the nine.

Dec was sitting very still. His face wasn't doing anything, the way it never did. But he'd put his cup down on the table. And he was looking at the sword the way he'd looked at the black bar on Joss's anvil, Ina said: the way he looked at a door nobody had opened.

"Harlan said don't call it anything with *murder* in," said Salty. "So I didn't."

The whole banner room laughed. Even Dec. Azac heard him: short, under his breath, at the end of the table.

***

Deston came home on Thursday at the second dusk, and he brought Chernn, and two hundred candles.

They came in by the south road, the way everybody came. Azac was on the south steps when they came round the curve into the round market. He'd been on the steps a lot that week, looking at the north door from the outside. Deston was enormous and walking very slowly. Beside him, at the same pace, was a small person in plain grey starter clothes with a stick, walking in time.

Wendy was at the bottom of the steps.

"Who'd stand next to you?" she said.

Deston opened his mouth. Then he stopped, and closed it, and looked down at the person beside him. And she looked up the steps at Wendy, the way you look at a gate.

"Him," said Chernn.

"Fair," said Wendy.

"I'm not signing the Roll," said Chernn. "Not yet. I'm level nine. I'd like to come in, though. For the longest night." She held up the stick. "I'm in his three."

"I know," said Wendy. "Kei told me. Two n's." She stepped aside. "Gate's open."

Chernn went up the steps. Deston went up behind her, with the sack. Azac saw it close up as he passed. It was the biggest sack he'd ever seen anybody carry, bigger than Salty's black iron, bulging at every seam. It smelled of warm wax. It smelled, Azac thought, like the inside of a church at Christmas.

"What's in it?" he said.

"Candles," said Deston. "Two hundred. From Tallow Green." He hoisted it higher on his shoulder. "Their line. From the old book. *On the longest night, a candle in every window. And one more for the road.*"

Azac looked at the sack.

"A candle in every window," he said slowly.

"Every window in the round town," said Chernn, from the top of the steps. She'd turned round. "Mother Dipp said. If anybody from Tallow Green's going to the round town on the longest night, they're not going without a sack." She held up one last candle in her other hand, separate from the sack. A tall white one, plain and straight. "And this one's for the road."

"Which road?"

"Whichever one needs it," said Chernn. "That's what she said. That's always been the way."

Azac thought about the north door. About ninety-four lamps up a road, all facing north, and the first waystone a mile out with Tiger's three at it. About his own plan, at the long table on Monday. *Make the north door the biggest light in the round town. So they come there. Not to the windows.*

"Not the windows," he said. "If you light every window, they'll go to the windows."

Chernn looked at him.

"No," she said. "Mother Dipp says that's not what they're for. The windows. They're not to keep anything *out*." She looked at the candle in her hand. "They're so the people inside aren't in the dark. While they wait. That's all. So nobody's sitting in the dark on the longest night. Not anybody. Not even in a house."

Azac stood on the south steps and didn't say anything.

"They're not lures," said Chernn. "They're just *company*."

***

They put the candles in the windows on Monday, the last day, all of them together.

Every window in the round town that faced into the market or onto one of the seven roads. There were a hundred and ninety-one, Kei said, when they'd counted. Ama Corrie's Hall had thirty of its own. The Hart had eleven. The houses had the rest, and the people in the houses came to their doors to watch the travellers come round with candles. Some of them cried. Old Joss, in the window of his smithy, held his candle in both shaking hands while Salty lit it, and wouldn't let go.

"*Tallow Green*," said Joss. "I knew a woman from there. Fifty years ago. She dipped candles." He looked at Chernn. "You've got her hands."

"I've got Mother Dipp's hands," said Chernn. "She says I hold them like a fishing line."

"Aye," said Joss. "That's the one." And he put his candle on his windowsill, next to nineteen black pieces of broken iron in a row that nobody had moved.

Azac carried the sack for the last part, because Deston's shoulder hurt and Azac was the biggest after Salty. He went round the north side of the market with it, and the north road, and the houses near the Hall. At the end of the north road, at the edge of the town, before the moor began, there was one last window. A little cottage, the last house in the round town before the road of lights. An old woman lived there on her own. She'd come to her door and stood watching them with her arms folded, the way Ama Corrie stood.

Chernn went up to her door with the last candle from the sack.

"For your window," she said.

The old woman looked at her for a long time.

"Nobody's put a light in this window on the longest night," she said, "since my mother. She said it was so the ones on the road could see the town was still here." She took the candle. "Even if the road was dark."

"The road's not dark," said Chernn. "Not this year. There's ninety-four."

The old woman looked up the north road at the dusk coming down over the moor. At the first waystone a mile out, where, if you went round to the north side, you'd see a little flame in a black cage, burning.

"No," she said. "So there are."

She put the candle in the window and lit it herself.

Azac stood in the road with the empty sack and watched her do it. He wasn't a crier. He had stories for everything; he always had a story. He couldn't think of one for this.

***

That night, the last night, the banner room was full.

Sixty chairs and a stool for Chernn. Everybody. Kotori and Tif going round with pots. Ama Corrie sweeping, though it was Monday. The chair by the north door, by itself, facing north, swept too. And outside the windows of the banner room, all round the round market, a hundred and ninety-one candles burning in a hundred and ninety-one windows. So nobody would be sitting in the dark.

Somebody asked Azac for a story. It was RX. RX always asked for a story, the night before something. It was a thing he'd done since poly.

Azac thought about it. He had lots of stories. He had stories about every game he'd ever played, IdleOn and Lost Ark and all the MMOs he and Dec and the others had gone through together over the years. He could have told any of them.

He stood up at Riverbank's end of the second table instead. He told them about a guild.

An old one, from before. So old the rain had taken its name. They'd kept a Roll in a round town, and swept the banner room on Sundays, and read the ceiling by the moon. And one day they'd gone north to carry the lights. All of them. They'd set them out behind them, every mile, facing the wrong way, so that only the dark could see. So that on the longest night, when it came south all at once, it would come down the road to the round town. To a door two shields wide. And not to anybody's house.

And then they'd gone further north than anyone, carrying one last lamp. And they'd never come home.

"But somebody kept it," said Azac. "That lamp. For three hundred years. Somebody out in the black, carrying it. Waiting." He looked down the long table. At sixty people, and a stool. "Waiting for somebody to light the road again. So they could come home."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the story," said Azac. "I don't know how it ends." He sat down. "I think we find out tomorrow."

He picked up his bread.

At the first of the nine, Dec was sitting very still. Beside Bee, Hyvred had stopped not looking at him and was just looking at him. Openly, for once. As if she'd forgotten to stop. And Dec, at the end of the table, was looking at the chair by the north door.

Kei stood up at the head.

"Not a minute before eight," he said. "Tomorrow. Everybody in at eight. The dark falls at twenty past. It doesn't lift till eight on Wednesday morning. Twelve hours." He looked round the room. "The egg throws you out at twelve. So you go in at eight, and you're out at dawn. Exactly." He paused. "That's what Xav said, last year. *Not a minute before eight.*"

At Riverbank's end of the table, Xav, who'd said it, nodded.

"Threes," said Kei. "Nobody alone." He looked at the list in his hand, all twenty lines. He'd made a copy for the banner room. "And if your three's in trouble, you tell the eye. And the eye tells everybody. And somebody comes." He rolled the list up. "That's all."

Azac looked down the table at his own two. RX, already pretending he wasn't nervous. Ryan, beside him, eating, saying nothing.

"Ryan," said Azac. "You ready?"

Ryan looked up.

"Yeah," said Ryan.

And somehow, from Ryan, that was the whole of everything.
