Isa Corrie couldn't see the windows, and never minded.

The others could. The travellers. They went still, all at once, the whole long table, and looked at the air in front of them. And their faces did things. She'd watched it since Christmas from her chair by the north door. Sixty people looking at nothing, and smiling at it, or going white, or putting their hands over their mouths. Ama Corrie said they'd done it for two years and she'd never once seen what they were looking at, and stopped wondering in the first month.

Isa hadn't stopped wondering. But she'd stopped needing to know.

You didn't need the window. You had the face. The face told you everything the window did, if you'd learned to read it. And she'd had three hundred years of her mothers' learning, on a hill in the dark, with nothing to look at but one lamp and each other's faces in the light of it.

She was very good at faces.

***

On the hill there had only ever been two faces at a time.

A mother's and a daughter's. In the middle of the still water, inside a ring of twelve standing stones, round a flat stone with a crown cut into it, worn nearly smooth. And on the flat stone, the lamp. That was all there was. The ring, the stone, the lamp, the wind going round and round very faint, and two faces in the light.

Isa's mother had had a long face, and quiet hands. She'd kept the lamp forty years. She'd taught Isa to trim the wick and fill the oil and say the dusk-words. To set the spring stone, and walk to the edge once and look, and come back and say *not yet*. And then one winter her face had gone very still in the lamplight, and not come back. And there'd only been one face on the hill after that.

Isa's. For a long time.

You got good at faces, on the hill. There were so few of them. You learned every one you were given, all the way through, because you never knew how long you'd have it.

She'd come to a room with sixty in it. She was still learning them.

***

The boy with the jar carried her out at the first dawn on Thursday. The way he did every morning. He picked up her chair with her in it, and she felt his arms take the weight, and the floor go away. And the north door, which stuck, and somebody had fixed, and you had to mind anyway. And the morning on her face, cool, off the moor.

He set her down at the top of the north steps, facing up the road. He put the jar in her lap. She put her hands round it.

He sat on the step below.

She looked at his face. She looked at it every morning, before she looked at the road. He didn't know she did. He had a still face, the boy with the jar. People thought it didn't do anything. They were wrong. It did a great deal. It just did it very small, the way the light in the jar did, through the clay, so you had to be close.

This morning it was doing the thing it had done since Tuesday. Since he'd stood at the head of the long table and asked.

*Decided*.

"Is it leaning?" she said.

He looked up the road.

"I can't see," he said. "But yes."

***

She'd known it would. She'd known since Christmas.

She hadn't said. Not to the long table, not to the boy, not to Ama Corrie, who was the nearest thing she had to a sister and was really a cousin, three hundred years removed. She'd said *the dark won't notice for a while*. That was true. *It's very slow*. That was true too.

But it noticed in the end. Everything did.

Her grandmother had told her about the winter the lamp nearly went out. Before Isa's mother. Before her mother's mother. A winter when the woman who kept it had been ill, and the oil had run low, and for nine nights the flame on the flat stone had been so small you could cover it with a thumb. And the dark had noticed. It had leaned. All of it, the whole black of the still water, leaning in toward the hill, toward the small flame, to see if that was it. And then, when the flame came back, it had settled.

But that year, on the longest night, it had come south all at once. Looking. Because for nine nights it had forgotten where the middle was. And the round town had nearly not kept.

Nine nights, that was.

It had been eight months now.

***

"I want to go up," said Isa.

The boy looked at her.

"The roof," said Isa. "The bench. The one the carpenter made from my pole." She looked down at her hands round the jar. "The one with *Somewhere* on it. The girl with the star told me. And her one with the knife. On Sunday. When they swept under my chair."

The boy didn't say anything for a long time.

"It's two hundred and twelve steps," he said.

"I know how many steps it is," said Isa. "My grandmother's grandmother counted them. Before she went north." She looked at him. "Carry me."

He looked at her face. She saw him read it. He was learning; nobody had taught him, but he was learning. He read it and saw that she meant it, and that it wasn't a thing she was going to ask twice.

"I'll need somebody for the other end," he said.

"The one under the floor," said Isa. "He carries bottom ends. The girl with the star said."

***

They carried her up together. The boy with the jar at the top, because he knew how she liked to be held. And the one under the floor at the bottom, where the weight was.

Ama Corrie was at her counter. She looked up when they came past with the chair between them, and Isa in it, and the jar in her lap.

"Where are you going?" said Ama Corrie.

"Up," said Isa.

Ama Corrie looked at her over her spectacles for a long time. Isa looked back over the jar. They'd looked at each other like that every day since Christmas, across the banner room. Two Corries. One who'd stayed and one who'd gone. Three hundred years of two lines that had never once met. Until the north door opened on a morning in December, and somebody came in with a lamp.

"Mind the third step," said Ama Corrie.

"I minded it before you were born," said Isa.

"You weren't born before me."

"My grandmother's grandmother was," said Isa. "She minded it. I'm minding it for her."

Ama Corrie made a noise that Isa had only heard her make once before. On Christmas, when the one under the floor said *six*. A small noise, at the back of her throat, that wasn't a word.

"*Hm*," she said, and looked down at her ledger very hard.

***

It took a long time. They went slowly. At every turn of the stair the chair tipped, and the boy with the jar said *sorry*. He'd learned that from the girl who made the tea, Isa thought; he'd never said it before. And the one under the floor said nothing at all, and held the bottom end so steady the jar in Isa's lap didn't move.

At the first landing, a hundred and six up, they put her down and sat on the step and breathed.

Isa looked at the one under the floor.

She'd been looking at him since Christmas, too. She'd seen his face on the first night, in the banner room, at the dawn after the longest night, when he came up out of the round room under the floor too late to see her come in. She'd seen it on Christmas, at her chair, with the jar open. She'd seen it go still, at the south gate in January, when the girl with the horns said *me*. And then not.

It was different now. She'd told him so, when he came home. *You're different.* And he'd said *I turned round*.

She looked at it now, on the landing, a hundred and six steps up. He wasn't looking at anything. He was sitting with his back to the wall of the stair and his eyes shut, breathing.

"You're going with him," said Isa.

He opened his eyes.

"North," said Isa. "This winter. The boy asked you. In a kitchen, he said. Out there." She looked at him. "You said yes."

"Yes."

"You'll be at the front."

"Yes."

"It's dark out there," said Isa. "Past the edge. You don't know dark. Nobody here does. You think the longest night was dark." She folded her hands round the jar. "The longest night had lamps in it. Four hundred and fourteen. And a road. And a town." She was quiet. "Out there there's nothing. Not dark. *Nothing*. There's nothing to see by but the light you're carrying, and you can't use that. You mustn't." She looked at him. "You'll be at the front of nothing."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"I've been at the front of nothing before," he said.

Isa looked at him.

"In a war," he said. "A long time ago. Twenty people, all at the front, and nobody beside." He looked down the stair, round and round, to where it went out of sight. "It was like that. Nothing behind. Nothing beside." He paused. "I didn't know it was dark. I thought that's what the front was."

"And now?"

"Now there's somebody behind me," he said. "And beside. And I've turned round, so I know." He looked at the jar in her lap. "So it won't be nothing."

Isa looked at him for a long time.

"*Hm*," she said.

***

The last sixty-six had the wind in them.

It came down through the eye at the top, off the moor, cold. She felt it on her face, and in what was left of her hair, and in her root hands round the jar. She hadn't felt wind like that since the hill. Up there, at the top of the hill in the middle of the still water, there'd always been wind. Very faint. Going round and round the ring of twelve stones all night, every night. Her mother had said it was the dark breathing.

And then light.

They brought her out onto the roof, into the morning. And set her chair down beside the bench, on the south side of the eye. And she looked.

***

She'd heard about it from her grandmother. Her grandmother had heard it from hers, and so on, back and back, to the one who'd sat up here before she went north. *You can see every road from it. It's where people go, in the round town, when they want to say something they mean. Because you can see everything from there. So you know it's true.*

She'd never believed it. Not really. It had been a story, on the hill, in the dark. The roof of the round town. Like the round town itself. A thing you knew was there, the way you knew the sun was there, without ever seeing it.

She saw it now.

The seven roads, going away from the round town like spokes from a hub, in the morning. All the way to the edge of everything. The south road, round the curve, with somebody sitting at the milestone at the bottom of it. The south-west, to a white line on the edge of the world. The east, with the sun coming up over it.

And the north.

The north road, going away over the moor. Ninety-four grey stones with ninety-four little lights on the backs of them. She'd lit every one, with her own hands, on the longest night, walking south behind the dark. Going away up the moor, smaller and smaller. To where the road went over the top and out of sight. Toward the old hall, and the twenty-two past it, and the edge.

And past the edge. Where she couldn't see. Where nobody could.

"Turn me north," said Isa.

***

They turned her chair to face north, the two of them, very carefully. The jar in her lap. And then the boy sat down on the bench beside her chair, on the end of it, by the arm with the cat's-paw. And the one under the floor sat at the other end, by the arm without one.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"There are stones," said Isa.

They both looked at her.

"In the water," said Isa. "Past the edge. When you get to the still water." She was looking north, up the road, at the far lamps leaning. "You won't see the water. It's black. It doesn't move. You'll think it's ground. It isn't." She folded her root hands tighter round the jar. "There are stones in it. Standing. A step apart. Going out from the edge across the still water to the hill. One after another."

"How many?" said the boy.

"Three hundred," said Isa.

The boy went very still.

"My mothers set them," said Isa. "One every spring. Every year. Whoever kept the lamp that year walked out from the hill in the spring and set one stone in the water, a step further than the last. So the way would be there. So it would always be there, in case somebody came." She was quiet. "Three hundred years. Three hundred springs. Three hundred stones."

"From the hill to the edge," said the one under the floor.

"From the edge to the hill," said Isa. "That's the way you'll be walking." She looked at the jar. "The first one's very old. It's sunk a bit. You'll have to feel for it. The last one's mine." She almost smiled; she could feel it at the corner of her mouth, the small dry one. "I set it the spring before I came home. It's crooked. I was old."

***

The boy didn't say anything. He was looking north. His face was doing the thing, very small. She watched it.

"Count them," said Isa.

He looked at her.

"Out loud," said Isa. "When you walk them. Every stone. Say the number." She looked at him. "You know that. You did it with the wicks. In December. A hundred and sixteen of them. *Put the wick in. And wait. And say the number.* The girl who makes tea told me."

"Kotori."

"Kotori," said Isa. "Say the number. So the stones know you. They were set by somebody who said the number every spring when she set them. Every one of my mothers. Out loud, on the water, in the dark." She folded her hands. "If you say the number, they'll know you're one of ours."

"I'm not one of yours," said the boy. Very quietly.

Isa looked at him.

"You're carrying our light," she said. "Back to our hill. To our ring. To set it on our stone." She looked at the jar in her lap, glowing faintly through the clay. "Who else's would you be?"

The boy didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he put his hand flat on the lid of the jar, in her lap. Beside where hers was. And left it there.

***

She sat on the roof all day. They let her.

The one under the floor went down at the second dawn and came back up with the girl with the horns, and the girl who made the tea, with a pot. They sat on the bench, the four of them, round Isa's chair, and drank tea, and looked north.

The girl with the horns looked at the far lamps for a long time. Isa watched her face. It saw everything; you could tell, the way it held still in the middle and moved at the edges.

"They're leaning more," said the girl with the horns. "Than Tuesday."

"Yes," said Isa.

"How long?"

Isa thought about it.

"Before the longest night," she said. "Not after. If it gets to the longest night still looking, it'll come here." She looked at the jar. "It'll come here anyway. It always comes here. But if it's still looking, it'll come here *for* this." She put her hand flat on the lid. "And there won't be enough lamps in the world."

Nobody said anything.

"So this winter," said the girl with the horns.

"This winter," said the boy.

The girl with the horns looked at the one under the floor. She didn't say anything. Isa watched her face do a great deal, very fast, and then stop. And then she reached over and took his hand, on the bench. And didn't look at him. And looked north.

"Go with him," said Isa.

The girl with the horns turned her head.

"Out there," said Isa, "you'll see nothing. There's nothing to see. You see everything, in here, edge to edge, he says. Out there you'll see nothing at all." She looked at her. "Go anyway. Somebody who sees everything should see nothing once. It teaches you to listen."

The girl with the horns looked at her for a long time.

"I was going anyway," she said.

"I know," said Isa. "I saw your face. I'm telling you so you'll know I said it."

***

At the second dusk, the light went off the moor, and the lamps on the north road came up gold in the dark, all ninety-four. Leaning.

Isa sat in her chair on the roof of the round town with the jar in her lap and looked at them.

She thought about her mother. On the hill, at the flat stone, with her hands round the lamp. Every night, at dusk, saying something to the dark. Isa had asked her once, as a little girl, what she said. And her mother had told her. It wasn't anything. It wasn't a spell, or a prayer, or a word from the old book. It was just something you said, to the dark, at dusk, so it would know you were there.

She'd said it every dusk for her whole life. Until Christmas. Since Christmas she'd said it to the jar, in her chair, very quietly, so nobody would hear.

She said it now. Out loud. To the north. To the black past the last lamp, where the still water was, and the three hundred stones, and the hill with nobody on it.

"Not yet," said Isa Corrie. "He's coming. This winter."

The boy beside her, on the end of the bench, made a very small sound.

The wind came down off the moor, round the eye, cold. And then, very slightly, for a moment, it dropped. The way it had on Chinese New Year, when she put her hand on the pole.

The far lamps, ninety-four of them, all the way up the north road, went on leaning.

But less.
