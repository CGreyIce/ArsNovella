They walked home the long way, because that was the only way there was.

Eight days from Carrow Cross to the fen, by Kei's sums, if you took the plain road east and then the river road south. Moonlight Harvest took it slowly. They didn't need to be anywhere. For the first time since November there was nothing ahead of them on the road with a clock on it. No door with *none below fifteen* cut over it. No long night. No writ. Just the road, and the six of them, and Fernwick at the end of it.

Ice didn't stream much of it. He put it on in the mornings for an hour and then turned it off and just walked. Chat didn't mind. Chat had watched him walk to a crossroads on a moor and let Ash go, and walk a rope across a gorge, and stand in a door while somebody else's guild got its name. Tif said they could all do with a week of looking at hills.

Gabe told them on the third day.

They were sitting on a river wall at the game's first dusk, the six of them, eating fen bread that Sedge had sent with them to Carrow Cross and that was now very old. Gabe ate his last bit, looked at the river for a while, and then said it the way he said everything: straight out.

"I'm going to go and get Dest's lot."

Deston looked up.

"Rachael's egg comes Monday," said Gabe. "Real Monday. Calvin's comes on Wednesday. You lent him the money. You told me. You said *don't tell anyone*, so I'm telling everyone." He grinned. "And Dennis has booked the café in town. They start next week. Three of them. In three random towns, at level one."

"I know," said Deston slowly.

"So I'm going to go and get them," said Gabe. "Like you lot got me. In the fen. Eleven to seventeen in six days, standing in front of things." He looked round at the five of them. "I'll walk to wherever they land, and I'll stand two paces from them, and I'll hit things with a hammer until they're fifteen. Then I'll bring them home."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"You'd be gone weeks," said Kotori. She said it carefully, the way she said things now, without a sorry in front of it.

"Couple of weeks. Maybe more. Depends where they land." Gabe shrugged. "Dungeons are five. We're six. I'm the extra. I've been the extra since January, I've just been too big for anyone to say so." He grinned at Ice. "The sub can go places."

"You're not the sub," said Ice.

"I'm *technically* the sub," said Gabe. "It's fine, Dec. I like being the sub. The sub gets to go and stand in front of Rachael."

Deston was looking at the river. He hadn't said anything. Ice watched him. Deston had been on calls with Rachael and Calvin and Dennis for years, the same way Ice had been on calls with everyone. They were his, the way the back row was Ice's. Ice knew the look on his face. He'd worn it himself on a bridge in Fernwick when Gabe came down the causeway.

"Thank you," said Deston quietly.

"Don't thank me," said Gabe. "Feed them. When they get here. That's your job." He stood up and stretched, enormous against the dusk. "*Everybody stays*. That's my year. Doesn't say everybody stays in the *same place*."

***

They came into Fernwick on the eighth day, at dawn, down the causeway, with the mist on the channels.

It looked exactly the same. That was the thing Ice hadn't expected. He'd been gone nearly three weeks, the longest since November. He'd half thought it would have changed: the town, or him, or both. It hadn't. The round houses on their stilts. The boardwalks bowing over the black water. The eel man at the end of his jetty, lighting his iron basket. Marta on her porch with a broom. The Landing. The narrows, with Gabe's four iron bands on the split post, black and solid.

And Rafi, on Sedge's steps, at the end of the north walk, with a black sword on his back. It wasn't too big for him any more. It hadn't been for a while.

He saw them coming and stood up so fast he nearly went in the channel.

"*Boss!*"

"Rafi."

"You're back! You walked to the round town and you let the Nightjar go and you stood in a door and you came *back*!" Rafi was grinning so hard it looked painful. "Bram didn't come back. Joy didn't come back. They're in the guild now. With the dots." He looked at Ice, suddenly anxious. "Are you *staying*?"

"We're staying," said Ice.

"*Good*," said a voice from the top of the steps.

Sedge was standing in her doorway with her pipe in her mouth and her pale sharp eyes on the six of them. She looked at each of them, one by one, the way she always did, as if counting. Then she took the pipe out of her mouth.

"You took your time," she said.

"It was a long way."

"Everything worth walking is." She looked at Suzu, at the back, with his scythe and the pack on his shoulder that glowed on one side through the canvas. Her eyes stopped there and stayed there for a long moment.

"Bring that in," she said. "The jar. I want to look at it."

***

She looked at it for a long time.

Suzu put it on the table in her round house among the drying herbs and the bottles and the iron pot on its hook. It was a plain glass jar with a stopper, the kind you'd keep sweets in. Inside it, eighty-one small blue lights were pressed together against one side of the glass, in a bright crescent, with the rest of the jar dark.

Sedge didn't touch it. She bent over it, with her hands behind her back, and looked.

Then she straightened up, and looked at the side of the jar the lights were pressing against. Then out of her round window, the same way. North. Across the old fen, past the deep water and the tussocks, toward the ring of grey stones, where she couldn't see them from here.

"They're going home," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"Where's home?" said Suzu, very quietly.

Sedge didn't answer at once. She went to the shelf by the window and took down a cup and filled it from the pot and didn't drink it. She held it in both hands, looking out at the fen.

"My mother went into the church once," she said. "Under the stones. When she was young. With four others. You know that." She turned the cup in her hands. "She came out, and she never said what was in there. Only that they sang. But she said one other thing, when she was very old. I thought she was wandering. She wandered a lot, at the end." She looked at the jar. "She said the lights that don't finish go north. Where the herons look. *All the way to where the herons look*, she said. *That's where they go to finish.*"

Suzu was very still.

"I thought she meant the church," said Sedge. "The singing. The ones that never got to the end of their song." She looked at Ice. "You let them finish. I heard it from here. The whole town heard it."

"The church is done," said Ice.

"The church is done," agreed Sedge. "These aren't from the church." She nodded at the jar. "These are other ones. From the fen, from the roads. Things that went out and didn't finish." She looked at Suzu. "Your scythe gathers them. You've been keeping them. Eighty-one." She was quiet for a moment. "My mother would've said you were carrying them home."

***

They went to the ring that night, at moonrise. Dark.

Six of them, out along the north walk, past the narrows, round the deep water, across the tussocks where the dark grass had roots. Between two of the nine grey stones, into the warm clear water, boots in hand. The moonbells opened. The bells rang, slightly out of time.

Suzu put the jar on the stone beside the ninth heron, the one that faced the wrong way. The lights pressed against the north side of the glass, toward where its long grey neck was bent. Ice stood up in the warm water and walked round behind the ninth heron. He stood with his back against the cold stone of its neck and looked where it was looking.

North.

Out over the stones, across the black water of the old fen. Past the place where the door to the church was, the sunken arch with the herons cut round it, done now, quiet. On, into the dark, where the fen went on further than he'd ever gone, into reeds and mist, and then nothing. He'd never been past the arch. Nobody had. There'd never been a reason.

"Dec," said Deston, from his flat stone.

"Yeah."

"Are we going?"

Ice looked at the dark where the heron was looking. He thought about a ceiling in a round hall, four hundred years old, with nine small stars in a ring in one corner and a silver line running out of it, north, across the whole sky, to a star that wasn't on any map. He thought about Kei, in a round hall, saying *the first thing*. About Suzu on a step, saying *after*. *You first. Then us.*

"Constellation's going north from the round town," he said. "Kei's plan. Along the lines. In a couple of weeks."

"So we wait?" said Kotori.

"No," said Ice. "We're not going to the star. That's theirs. They found it." He looked at the jar on the stone, glowing on one side. "We're going where *this* goes. Where the ninth heron looks. It's not the same line. It's ours."

"How far?" said Sheng Yang.

"I don't know."

"Good," said Gabe, from the middle of the ring, with his feet in the water.

Ice laughed.

"Gabe. You're leaving in four days."

"Then you'd better start walking north before Monday," said Gabe comfortably. "So I can see how far it goes before I go and stand in front of Rachael."
