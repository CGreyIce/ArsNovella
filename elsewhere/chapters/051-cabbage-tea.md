The tea was the worst thing Kotori had ever made, and it paid for the pod.

She hadn't meant it to. She'd taken Brewing on the first day because the room had been quiet and the rain was nice, and making tea had seemed like a gentle thing to do. She'd taken Foraging because you had to take something. For five weeks she'd brewed tea out of whatever she found growing in the hedges round Brackenford, and later round Fernwick. It always came out the same. Pale green and faintly steaming in a little clay cup, and tasting, everyone agreed, very slightly of cabbage.

It tastes a bit of cabbage.

She'd given it to Xav for luck, on the Landing, the day he told them he was leaving. He'd drunk all of it in front of eleven thousand people and said *that's terrible*. Somebody had clipped it. It wasn't Dee. It was a girl in Manila who'd been watching Ice's stream that day. She called it *cabbage tea for luck*. It had four hundred thousand views by the next morning.

By the next afternoon, people were asking to buy it.

***

"Sorry," said Kotori, the first time somebody asked. Then she heard herself, and thought of Ice in the ring the night before the church. *Mostly it means thank you.* She started again. "It's not very good. It tastes like—"

"Cabbage," said the girl from Manila, who'd walked all the way to Fernwick to ask in person. "I know. That's why I want it."

Kotori looked at her for a long time. Then she gave her a cup. The girl tried to pay, and Kotori said no. The girl put the money on the step of Marta's porch anyway and ran.

By Friday there were people on the Landing every evening, waiting for the bell girl to come back from wherever Moonlight Harvest had been. Holding out coins. *Can I have a tea. Can I have the cabbage one. For luck.* She brewed it at Sedge's hearth, where Ice brewed his draughts, because Sedge let her. She brewed it in batches of twenty, and they were all exactly as bad as each other.

"You have to charge for it," said Ice, on Saturday, when he found her on Sedge's steps with an empty pot and a crowd and a face like she was about to cry. "Otherwise somebody else will."

"But it's *bad*."

"Nobody's buying it because it's good." He crouched beside her on the step. "They're buying it because it's yours. Because you stood on a post in the dark on the longest night and kept me alive for twelve hours. They want a bit of that in their bag. Same as the draughts." He paused. "The draughts aren't good either. They're Fair. Nobody's buying them because they're good."

Kotori looked at him.

"How much?" she said, eventually, very small.

"What's the market saying?"

She opened the Exchange. Somebody had already bought one of her cups off the girl from Manila, and listed it again. Three dollars.

"Three dollars," she said, horrified. "For cabbage."

"Then sell it for three dollars," said Ice, "and sell a lot of it."

***

She did the sum that night, in her bedroom in Singapore, on the back of a lecture handout. She did it three times, and then once more on her phone to check.

She'd had one thousand five hundred and fifty-seven dollars on the twelfth of December, after the haul. She'd had nine hundred and forty-two dollars to go. Three dollars a cup, less the Exchange's ten per cent. Two dollars seventy. Nine hundred and forty-two divided by two dollars seventy.

Three hundred and fifty cups. Three hundred and forty-nine, and a bit.

She looked at the number for a long time. Then she wrote it on a sticky note, *350*, and stuck it to the end of the egg. The egg was in the corner of her room, white, humming quietly, where it had been since her dad had it re-registered to her thumb in November.

***

She brewed three hundred and fifty cups of cabbage tea in twelve days.

She brewed them in batches of twenty at Sedge's hearth, in the evenings, after Moonlight Harvest had finished whatever they'd been doing. Before the Long Night, it was levelling. After it, it was Ice's new class, which made the whole party stop and stare every time he used it. Then it was the trials, and Sheng Yang, and Xav going away up the steps of the Hall without looking back. Then it was five of them again. She brewed through all of it.

Sedge sat at her table and ground roots and didn't speak to her, which Kotori had learned was Sedge's way of being friendly. On the third evening, Sedge reached over without a word and moved the pot two inches further from the fire. The next batch came out Fair instead of Poor. It still tasted of cabbage, but a slightly better cabbage. Kotori said *thank you* about nine times, and Sedge said nothing.

People bought them. That was the strangest part. They queued on the Landing for them. Then they stopped queueing and bought them off the Exchange instead, from all over the world. People in Seoul and São Paulo and Ohio and Lagos bought a little clay cup of terrible tea that said *Brewed by Kotori* at the bottom. They kept it in their bags. Some of them drank it, on stream, on purpose, and made faces, and posted the faces. *Cabbage tea for luck*, they wrote. *The bell girl's tea. Drank it before my first dungeon. Didn't die.*

She didn't stream. She never had. But she read every one of them, at night, with her phone held close to her face in the dark, and she didn't know what to feel. So she felt all of it.

***

Sheng Yang taught her to stand.

She hadn't asked him to. That was the thing about Sheng Yang, she was learning. He watched you for a while, the way Xav used to, and then he just did something, as if it were obvious.

On the day after the trials, the five of them were in Hollis's yard. Ice was trying his new class on the straw dummy, very carefully, while Hollis watched with his arms folded. Sheng Yang came and stood beside Kotori at the edge of the yard.

"Can I hit Declan?" he said.

"Sorry?"

"Lightly. To practise. People, not things." He nodded at the dummy. "Monsters lean at the light. People don't. People look for the healer." He looked down at her from a long way up. "On the north walk, a woman with two blades looked for you the whole fight. You were on a post in the water. She couldn't reach you. So she stopped looking, and lost." He paused. "That was good. You did it without knowing. I'd like you to do it knowing."

Kotori held her staff very tight.

"I don't—sorry. I don't like it. Fighting people."

"I know." He said it plainly, kindly, in his clear calm voice. "Neither do I, much. I'd rather fight things that don't mind." He shrugged, a long easy shrug. "But people will come. They always do. So. Slowly."

*Slowly*. Deston had said that. On the river wall at Brackenford, on the first day, when she'd asked if they fought people. *We'll teach you the rest. Slowly. When you want to.*

She looked at the yard. At Ice, who'd stopped hitting the dummy and was watching them, very still, the way he watched feet. He didn't say anything. He just raised his eyebrows a millimetre. *Your call.*

"Slowly," she said. "Okay."

Sheng Yang hit Ice lightly with the flat of the long sword, over and over, all afternoon. Ice turned it, and turned it. And Kotori stood somewhere in Hollis's yard and kept Ice up, while Sheng Yang looked for her. Every time he found her, he stopped and said *there. I can reach you there.* She moved. He looked again.

By the evening he'd stopped finding her.

"Good," he said. "Again tomorrow."

She went to Sedge's and brewed twenty cups of cabbage tea, and her hands shook a bit the whole time. But it was a good shake. The kind you get after you've done something you didn't think you could.

***

On Christmas Eve, at four in the afternoon, the three hundred and fiftieth cup sold.

She was in her room. Not in the egg, at the desk, with her phone. She watched it go on the Exchange window: *Hedge Tea (Fair) · Sold · 3.00*. A buyer in Lisbon. She sat there and looked at the number in her wallet.

*US$2,502.40.*

She'd gone over. By three dollars and forty cents. She'd miscounted somewhere, probably on day four, when she'd had a cold and done the sum wrong twice.

She drew it out. All of it, through the Exchange, into her bank account, the real one. She watched the little bar fill up and the ten per cent come off, then the numbers turn into Singapore dollars, then the bank's app make the small happy noise it made. Then she sat very still for a long time.

Then she went to the shop downstairs and bought a card.

***

Christmas morning in Singapore was hot and bright and nothing like Christmas in Torrance had been, and they'd stopped minding about four years ago.

Her mom made pancakes, because she always made pancakes, in a flat on the fourteenth floor with the air conditioning on and a small plastic tree on the side table. Her grandmother rang from California on video at nine, which was Christmas Eve evening there. She held the phone very close to her face and said *Mia-chan* and told Kotori to eat more, in Japanese, the way she'd been saying it since Kotori was three. Kotori said *hai, hai*, and her grandmother told her she was too thin, and Kotori said she was exactly the same as last time, and her grandmother said that was what she meant.

Her dad sat at the end of the table in a T-shirt with a cup of coffee, reading something on his tablet, the way he did every morning, every Christmas, every day. An engineer, with reading glasses pushed up on his head and a pen behind his ear for no reason.

She waited until her grandmother had gone and her mom was in the kitchen. Then she put the card on the table in front of him.

He looked at it. Then at her, over his reading glasses.

"What's this?"

"Open it. Sorry. Just—open it."

He opened it.

Inside, in her handwriting, the very careful handwriting she'd used for every birthday card since she was six:

*Dad,*
*For the pod.*
*Thank you for buying it and getting sick in it, so I could have it.*
*US$2,499.00 (sent to your account this morning).*
*Merry Christmas.*
*Mia*
*(It's from tea. I'll explain.)*

He read it twice. She watched him read it twice. Then he put it down on the table, very carefully, square to the edge, and looked at her for a long time without saying anything.

"Mia," he said.

"I know."

"I didn't buy it so you'd pay me back."

"I know."

"I bought it because I saw an ad at Changi and thought it'd be fun, and then I lay down in it twice and threw up both times." He took his reading glasses off. "And you were sad that week, after your exams, and you liked the rain in the trailer. So I had it put on your thumb. That's all. It was a present."

"I know," said Kotori. Her eyes were doing something annoying. "I know it was. It was the best present I've ever had. That's why."

Her dad didn't say anything.

"I've been in a party," she said, "for five weeks. With people who are really good. Like, really, really good, Dad, the best in the world at some of it. And they let me stand where I stand. And I kept them up. All night, on Monday. Twelve hours. In the dark." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "I did that. Me. With the thing you bought me. I wanted to give you something back that I did. And it's tea." A laugh came out, a beat late. "It's terrible tea, Dad. It tastes like cabbage. People bought three hundred and fifty cups of it."

Her dad looked at her for a long, long time.

Then he picked up the card again and read it a third time. When he put it down, he didn't put it on the table. He got up, and went to the fridge, and put it on the front, under the magnet shaped like a pineapple that had come with them from California. He stood looking at it there.

"I watched it," he said, to the fridge. "The video. The church. The man with the shield's stream. Three times." He turned round. "You were on a rock."

"I was on a rock."

"The whole time. Everything was going on, and you were on a rock, and nothing could reach you, and you could see all of them." He rubbed his face. "I thought, *that's my daughter*. I didn't know you could do that."

"Neither did I."

"I'm keeping the card," he said. "I'm not keeping the money."

"Dad—"

"I'm putting it in an account," said her dad, "for the next thing. Whatever it is. When you know what it is." He looked at her over the top of his reading glasses, which he'd put back on without noticing. "And you're going to tell me about the tea."

So she told him about the tea.

She told him about all of it, at the kitchen table, on Christmas morning, while the pancakes went cold. Fernwick and Sedge and the moonbells, which she wasn't supposed to tell anyone about and told him anyway, because he was her dad. The drowned church, and the rock. The longest night, and the post in the water. Ice, who never made a sound. Suzu, who said *good*. Xav, who drew where she stood before she stood there. Deston, who counted. Sheng Yang, who'd taught her to stand where a person couldn't reach her, slowly. And three hundred and fifty cups of tea that tasted of cabbage.

Her dad listened to all of it. Every word. Halfway through, her mom came and sat down too, with the coffee pot, and listened.

At the end, her dad was quiet for a while.

"Can I try it?" he said.

"Try what?"

"The tea."

Kotori stared at him.

"You can't," she said. "It's in the game. You have to—you'd have to lie down in an egg, and you get sick—"

"There's a café," said her dad. "Downstairs from my office. They opened it last week. Cradle Commons. You can book an hour." He picked up his coffee. "I'll put the pain on zero. And I'll keep my eyes shut, if I have to. I want to try your tea."

Kotori looked at her father, an engineer in a T-shirt on Christmas morning, with reading glasses on his head and a pen behind his ear for no reason.

"It's really bad," she said.

"I know," said her dad. "That's why I want it."
