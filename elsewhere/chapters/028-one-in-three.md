On his first morning in Fernwick there had been sixty-four travellers in the whole town, and half of them had been trying to leave.

By Saturday night there were nineteen hundred.

[ Fernwick · the Wending Fen
Travellers: 1,912 ]

He watched the number go up all evening from the end of the north walk, with nine thousand people watching him watch it. It went up the way water comes up a step: not all at once, just every time you looked. The long loose line on the causeway never quite ended. As the fen's sun went down behind the reeds, the line was still coming, lit now with little lanterns, a slow wandering string of lights from the fields to the bridge.

Fernwick wasn't built for it. That was the first thing he noticed, and it kept being the thing he noticed.

The boardwalks were built for sixty people and some ducks. They bowed under nineteen hundred. You could feel them going down a finger's width under your boots and coming back up as the crowd passed, like walking on a long, slow breath. The round wattle houses on their stilts had people sitting on their steps. Not the people who lived in them. Travellers, in new boots, eating bread they'd brought from Brackenford because the bakery had run out at three. The eel man had sold every eel he had by four o'clock. He was sitting on his upturned bucket with a bag of money in his lap and a look of profound, quiet confusion, like a man who'd been struck by lightning and found it was money.

"*Eels*," he said, as Ice went past. That was all. He held up the bag a little, to show it. "*Eels*."

Hollis's yard was full.

It was a small square of beaten earth behind the militia house, with one straw dummy and a rack of wooden swords. On Ice's first morning it had held Hollis and Ice and a dragonfly. Now there were sixty people in it, in rows, each holding a wooden sword over their head and bringing it down on nothing. Hollis walked up and down between the rows with his three-fingered hand behind his back and his face like a slammed door.

"Back foot," he was saying. "*Back* foot. That's your front foot. That's—no. Look at the man next to you. No, he's wrong too."

"Hollis," said Ice, from the gate.

Hollis didn't look round. "Five copper. Join a row."

"It's me."

"I know who it is." He still didn't look round. "Five copper. They all want to learn the cut off the far-glass. They've got the far-glass on their own heads. They watched you do it four hundred times and they still can't put their weight back." He stopped beside a large young man with a red beard who was doing it very enthusiastically and completely wrong. He looked at him for a long moment. "Where are you from, son?"

"Ohio, sir."

"Is it flat?"

"Uh. Yes sir."

"Thought so." Hollis walked on. Over his shoulder, to Ice: "Thirty at a time. Five copper each. I've taught more people the Falling Cut this afternoon than in twenty years, and not one of them has asked what it's for." He stopped at the end of the row and turned and looked at Ice, finally, and his face was very tired. "Go away. Come back when they've gone."

Out in the old fen, beyond the north walk, the door-hunters were looking for the door.

They were doing it the way you'd look for a dropped earring in a field: everywhere at once and nowhere properly. Hundreds of them. In ones and twos and fives, wading through the dark water between the tussocks with sticks, prodding the reeds. Calling to each other. Holding up their own little floating eyes to look about, because half of them were streaming it. *LOOKING FOR THE DROWNED CHOIR DAY 1*. Somebody had a map. Somebody always had a map. It was being sold on the boardwalk by a man in a big hat, *THE DOOR · 5 silver*, with a cross on it in a place where Ice knew for a fact there was nothing but a dead tree and a very large frog.

The rafts were gone in the morning.

He knew before he got to the end of the north walk, because Rafi was sitting on Sedge's steps with his hatchet across his knees and his sack beside him, and he wasn't picking anything.

Sedge's raft gardens had floated behind her house for as long as there'd been a house. Six of them, flat woven platforms of reed and willow, anchored on long lines in the still black water and thick with herbs. Silverleaf. Fen-heart. Water-mint and bog-myrtle and three things Ice didn't know the names of and Sedge had never told him. On his first day she'd taken him out to the nearest one in her little flat boat. She'd shown him how to cut a stem so it grew back twice as thick, and said, *one in three*. Take one in three. Leave two to seed. *The fen's not a shop.*

The rafts were still there. They were floating on their lines in the morning mist, the same as always.

There was nothing on them.

Not one in three. Not two in three. Everything. Every stem, every leaf, cut down to the reed. Some of them pulled up by the root, which was worse, because it meant they wouldn't come back. Somebody had waded out in the night. Lots of somebodies, by the look of the trampled channels. They'd seen a video where a man in a swamp picked herbs and got rich, and they'd taken every single thing that grew. Here, behind the house of a woman who'd been tending them since before they were born.

"I told them," said Rafi. His voice wasn't steady. "Boss. I was here till midnight. I told them. I said *one in three*. I said she's got a rule. I said you can't just—" He stopped. He was nineteen and from Surabaya, and he was very angry, and he was trying hard not to cry in front of ten thousand people. "They laughed. A big group, eight or nine. They said *it's a game, bro*. They said *it respawns*." He looked at the bare rafts. "It doesn't respawn. Does it. Not like that."

"No," said Ice. "Not like that."

Sedge was on her porch.

She was standing at the top of her steps with one hand on the post, the way she'd stood on Friday when the fen went quiet. She was looking out at the rafts. She didn't look at Ice. She had a coil of new line over her other arm, and he realised she'd already been out in the boat at dawn, tying the rafts in tighter, because some of the lines had been cut.

"Was this you?" she said, to the water.

It would have been easy to say no. It hadn't been him. He'd never set foot on those rafts except with her. He'd taken one in three every time.

"Yes," he said.

Sedge turned her head and looked at him then. It was the look. Not the one she'd given him on the first day, when he'd been a stranger asking what she grew. Something older and tireder than that.

"Hm," she said.

She looked at Rafi on her steps, with his hatchet and his red eyes. Then back at Ice.

"Then you'll mind it," she said.

She went inside. She didn't close the door.

He minded it.

It took him most of Sunday morning, and some of it was easy and some of it wasn't. The easy part was Rafi. Rafi wanted the job before Ice had finished explaining it: stand on Sedge's steps, all day, and all night when he could, and stop people going out to the rafts. Twenty silver a day. Two dollars, which Rafi said was more than he'd ever been paid to stand still. And he knew two others who'd do it with him. A small serious girl from Cebu called Joy, who'd been picking cress at the edge of town since the first week. And a large silent Dutch boy called Bram, who had a spear and almost no words in any language and, as far as Ice could tell, no fear at all.

"Twenty silver each," said Ice. "Shifts. However you want. Nobody touches the rafts. Nobody goes past the end of her steps without her saying so. And if a group of nine turns up and laughs at you—"

Rafi looked at him.

"You'll come?"

"I'm a tank," said Ice. "It's the whole job. Somebody shouts, I come."

Rafi grinned for the first time that morning, with his eyes still red. Then he remembered something, and the grin went a bit lopsided.

"You never brought me a big sword," he said.

The draughts went on selling.

He brewed every evening at Sedge's hearth, with the door open and Joy on the steps and Rafi asleep on the bench under the drying herbs. Fenwort from the north walk, which Rafi's friends picked now as well as Rafi. Silverleaf from the beds by the old fen, where he took one in three and stood over each plant afterwards like a man guarding a grave. Fair draughts, and Poor ones. Never Good ones.

He put them on the Exchange at what the market said. Twenty dollars on Saturday. Sixteen on Sunday morning, when he'd brewed a hundred and twenty and so had the scalpers' memory of how many there were. Eleven by Sunday night. Nine on Monday. The price came down as fast as he could make them, which was what prices did. He didn't mind. He'd never thought anybody would pay twenty dollars for a draught. He hadn't thought anybody would pay eleven. People kept buying them anyway. In Manila and Seoul and Lisbon and Ohio, with *Brewed by Ice* at the bottom, to keep in a bag and never drink. Somewhere in the second hundred, on Monday afternoon, a tenth of all that brewing tipped him over into level eighteen, standing at a hearth, stirring a pot.

By Sunday noon the town had said six thousand two hundred and fourteen. It didn't go higher. There wasn't room.

And every day at Marta's door, on its two bricks, the crate sat half full at forty copper and twenty, with his note pinned to the front. *ONE EACH. IF YOU WALKED HERE, YOU CAN HAVE ONE.* Marta sat on her stool beside it with her arms folded.

He watched her from across the boardwalk on Sunday afternoon. A man in very expensive gear walked up, looked at the crate, looked at Marta, and put down forty copper for a draught. Then he put down another forty copper and reached for a second. Marta didn't say anything. She just looked at him.

He took his hand back.

He took his forty copper back too, after a moment, a little shamefaced. He put the one draught in his bag and went away up the boardwalk. Marta watched him go. Then she looked across the boardwalk at Ice, standing in the doorway of the eel man's shed with ten thousand people in his eyes, and gave him a short nod. One soldier to another.

Hollis's yard was empty on Tuesday night.

The crowds were thinning at last. Not gone; Fernwick would never be sixty-four people again. But the door was found, and the door was cleared, three times, and the door-hunters were going back up the causeway toward Brackenford in their long lantern-lit lines. They were looking for the next thing. By midnight the town said four thousand. By the time the fen's moon was up there was nobody in the militia yard but Hollis, on his bench by the rack, mending a strap.

Ice sat down on the other end of the bench without being asked. Hollis didn't look up.

"Two hundred and twelve," said Hollis.

"Sorry?"

"I taught the cut to two hundred and twelve people this week. Five copper each." He pulled the needle through. "Not one of them asked me what it was for. They all wanted to be the captain." He pulled it through again. "Not one of them asked what his name was."

Ice waited. Out in the dark, beyond the edge of town, the fen made its night sounds: frogs, and the slow drip of the reeds, and somewhere a long way off, a heron. No singing. There hadn't been any since Friday. He kept listening for it anyway.

"What was his name?" he said.

Hollis's hands stopped.

He sat for a long moment with the strap in his lap and the needle held up in his three fingers, looking at nothing. At the straw dummy, maybe. At something a long way behind it.

"You saw the cut," he said finally. "You saw the Anchor. You've had a bit of it." He put the needle down. "He held that ford a day and a half. Nineteen men and a broken arm, and he never made a sound, the whole of it, not once. And at dawn, on the second day, when they'd thrown everything they had at him, he broke them."

"How?"

"That's the bit nobody asks." Hollis looked at him then, at last. In the lantern light his face was all lines, and very tired, and something underneath the tired that Ice hadn't seen before. "Everybody wants the day and a half. Nobody wants to know what you do with it after."

"I'm asking."

Hollis looked at him for a long time.

"Not tonight," he said. He picked the needle up again. "It's a long story. You want the right night for it." He bent back over the strap. "Ask me on a long night."
