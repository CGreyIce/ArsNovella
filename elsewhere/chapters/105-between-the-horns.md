She'd planned it for five days.

Since Monday, since *go for the surgery dumbass*, since she'd sat on the stair to the eye of the dome and read it four times. Then she'd typed *fine*, and then *still hitting you*, and meant both. She'd planned what she'd say. She'd planned where she'd stand. She'd planned which arm.

Then she'd come through a grey door into a white city with no name, and stopped at the bottom of a flight of steps with her head tipped all the way back. And she'd forgotten all of it.

It was so *big*. Towers going up into gold light, and bridges strung between them like threads, and a dome that made the sky look small. In here she could see all of it at once, edge to edge, the whole city, every window. Her left eye kept swinging from tower to tower on its own, locking on, letting go, locking on, the crosshair going round and round like a child in a sweet shop. She'd stood there for a full minute with her mouth open, and Bee had said *Hyv* twice, and she hadn't heard either time.

Then a door opened at the edge of the square, and he came through it.

And she remembered all of it at once.

***

She went down the steps. She didn't run. Running was for people who weren't sure. She walked straight across the white square toward him, with her hands in fists at her sides. Somewhere behind her, she heard Drew say *oh, no* in a voice that meant *oh, yes*.

Ice watched her come. He didn't move. He didn't look surprised. He looked exactly the way he'd looked in every message all week, she thought. As if he'd known this would happen and had decided it was fine.

She stopped in front of him.

"Hi, Hyv," said Ice.

"*Don't* hi me."

"Hi," said Ice.

"I rang the clinic," said Hyvred.

He went still. Just slightly. Just for a second.

"And?"

"I'm not *telling* you," said Hyvred. "It's none of your *business*. You don't get to know. You don't get to know *anything*. You don't answer your messages, so you don't get to know."

"Okay," said Ice. "Good."

"*Don't* say good—"

She hit him.

In the arm, the left one, the shield arm, with her fist closed, as hard as she could. Not a slap. A proper hit, the kind Bee had taught her once on a call, *thumb outside, not inside, you'll break it*. It landed with a thump she felt all the way up to her shoulder.

"Ow," said Ice.

He was on fifty. She knew he was on fifty; everybody knew he was on fifty. The window could say what it liked.

"That's for the *dollar*," said Hyvred.

She hit him again. Same arm. Same place.

"*Ow.*"

"That's for the *heron*."

"The heron was watching me—"

"*THE HERONS ARE STONE.*"

From the steps behind her somebody shouted *HIT HIM AGAIN*, in capitals. Leaka.

She stood there in front of him in the white square, breathing hard, with her fist still up. She'd planned three. She'd planned *that's for the dollar* and *that's for the heron* and *that's for not answering*. She found she couldn't do the third one. Her hand wouldn't do it, the same way her thumb wouldn't write *0* in the note.

Ice rubbed his arm. He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he reached out and put his hand on her head.

Right between the horns, on top of her hair. He patted it, twice. Lightly. The way you'd pat somebody who'd done something good, and done it well, and you were proud of them and weren't going to say so out loud.

Hyvred stopped breathing.

She felt her face go hot. All of it, all at once, from her chin to the roots of her horns, so hot she was sure it showed. It must have shown. Everybody in the square must have been able to see it. Tiger could see it. Gigi could see it. *Kestrel* could see it. She opened her mouth to say something. What came out was a small noise she'd never made in her life, a sort of strangled *mrp*.

Then she turned round and walked away very fast. Back across the square, to where Bee had come down the steps after her with her book under her arm. And she went and stood behind her.

Directly behind her. Behind her wings, which were large and translucent and veined with gold, and almost completely see-through, and no use at all for hiding behind.

"She's hiding," said Bee, to Ice, flatly.

"*I'M NOT HIDING.*"

"She's behind my wings," said Bee. "That's hiding."

Ice had come a little way after her across the square, rubbing his arm. He stopped a polite distance from Bee. He looked at Bee, and then at the shape behind her wings, which was bright red and had its hands over its face.

"Hi, Bee," said Ice.

Bee looked at him. Up and down, the way she looked at everything she was about to draw.

"I still don't know who you are," said Bee.

"That's fair."

"You gave her three thousand eight hundred and twenty-one dollars."

"Did I?"

Bee looked at him a while longer. Then something happened to her face. It might, in a better light, have been the start of a smile.

"Okay," said Bee. "I know who you are now."

***

Kestrel had seen all of it. Of course she had. She'd come down from the far end of the terrace while it was happening, and she was standing at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded. She was looking at Ice with the face of somebody who'd decided not to waste anything, and who'd just been given something she didn't want to waste.

"Huh," said Kestrel.

Ice looked at her. They'd never met. Not in the game, not anywhere. Hyvred knew that, because she'd watched every minute of both of them since December. *Who the hell is Moonlight Harvest*, Kestrel had said, on stream, the day of the world first. *See you at the next one.*

"Kestrel," said Ice.

"Ice." Kestrel nodded at Hyvred, behind the wings. "She hit you."

"Twice."

"I've wanted to do that since December."

"You can," said Ice.

Kestrel considered it. Hyvred, peering through Bee's wing, saw her actually consider it, the way Kestrel considered everything: properly, all the way through. Then she shook her head.

"Next door," said Kestrel.

Ice looked at her for a moment.

"Next door," he said.

Kestrel nodded, once, as if something had been signed. Then she looked past him, at the wings, and the face behind them.

"The seat's still there," she said to Hyvred.

"I know," said Hyvred, through Bee's wing.

"Not asking. Saying."

"I *know*."

"Good," said Kestrel. And she went back up the steps to the terrace, with her short bow on her back, and didn't look round.

***

Suzu found her afterwards, at the balustrade. She'd come out from behind Bee eventually. Her face had mostly gone back to its own colour. She was standing at the edge of the terrace, looking out over the whole white city, letting her left eye do what it wanted.

He came and stood beside her. Not close. Just there.

"Mum's is Monday," said Suzu.

Hyvred turned and grabbed his arm. She hadn't meant to. She did it anyway.

"*Monday?*"

"Monday."

"This Monday? *Two days*?"

"Yes."

"Will you tell me? After? Will you—"

"Yes," said Suzu.

She held onto his arm for a moment longer. Then she let go.

"Did you tell him?" she said, quietly. "About me. At the clinic."

"I told him you were there."

"That's *all*?"

"That's all."

She looked at him. His long careful face, his half-closed pale eyes, looking out at the city. Suzu didn't lie. She'd known that for a long time without knowing how she knew it. He just didn't say things. And when he did say something, it was the one true thing, and nothing else.

"Thank you," she said.

Suzu thought about that.

"Okay," he said.

***

The man in the grey coat talked for a while at the top of the steps. Hyvred heard about half of it. Something about badges. Something about not being allowed to say anything about anything. She signed the page when it came up in front of her without reading it, and Bee told her off.

Then it was over. People broke up into little groups along the terrace, and Ice came over.

She saw him coming. Of course she did; she could see everything. She put her chin up and folded her arms and got ready.

"Can I borrow that?" said Ice.

He was looking at her bow.

"...What?"

"Your bow. For a minute." He held out his hand. The left one. The one she'd hit.

She looked at the hand. She looked at him. She couldn't think of a single reason to say no that didn't sound stupid. So she unslung the bow and gave it to him, with one arrow, and said, "If you break it I'm hitting you a third time."

"Fair."

He weighed it in his hands. Not the way people did who'd never held one, turning it over, looking for the trick. He held it the way she held a fork. He looked along the string. He ran his thumb down the arrow once and sighted along it at the light. Then he looked across the square, and up, to the top of the nearest tower. Two hundred paces off, more. A gold ball the size of a fist sat on a spike above the roof, catching the sunset that never set.

"That one," he said.

"You can't hit that."

"Probably not."

He stood side on to it. He nocked the arrow without looking. He drew, not fast, all the way back, to a place at the corner of his jaw, as if there had always been a place there waiting for it. Her left eye locked on him without being asked. She saw his shoulders go still. She saw him not hold on. He let go the way you'd put a cup down on a table.

The arrow went up across the square in a long curve, gold in the gold light. It hit the gold ball dead in the middle with a sound like a small bell.

Hyvred's mouth fell open. All the way open. Bee told her afterwards you could have fitted Leaka's apple in it.

"*Ninety-five*," said Hyvred. "*NINETY-FIVE.* You don't— you've never— you're a *shield*. You stand at the *front*. You hit things with a *sword*—"

"I used to do archery," said Ice. "In real life."

"You *WHAT*."

"A long time ago." He handed the bow back to her, carefully, grip first. "But I lost my passion for it."

"*Why?*"

He looked up at the tower for a moment, at the arrow sticking out of the gold ball at the top.

"It happens," he said. "A long time ago."

She stood there holding her bow. She'd shot it every day since November. On a cliff over the sea, off a mill roof, from the eye of a dome at seven roads at once. Ness had taught her to stop holding on, and she'd stopped holding on, and she'd never once hit ninety. Not once in five months.

"Show me," she said. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway. "Show me what you did."

So he showed her.

He didn't touch her. He stood beside her, a little behind, where she could see him at the edge of her left eye, and talked. Quietly, not much, the way she'd heard him talk to Suzu on stream when Suzu was learning something.

"You let go with your fingers," he said, after her first one. "Not with your back. Your back's still holding on after the arrow's gone. Let the back go too."

"*Ice*," she said, turning round, "*look*, I did—"

He wasn't there.

She stood there with the bow in her hand. She looked along the terrace with her left eye, the crosshair swinging. He was at the far end, at the balustrade, talking to Hana. Hana was crying, obviously. He'd been there a while. Hyvred had no idea how long. She hadn't seen him go. *She* hadn't seen him go. She could see seven roads at once, and she hadn't seen one man walk away from her across a terrace.

Then it hit her.

She owed him three thousand eight hundred and nineteen dollars. And now she owed him *this*.

She stamped her foot. Then the other one. On the white stone, one after the other, *bang, bang*, like a child who'd been told it was bedtime.

On the steps behind her, Bee laughed.

Bee had been sitting there the whole time with her book on her knees, drawing. She laughed her short flat laugh, like a door shutting. Then she laughed again, properly, a real one, which Bee almost never did. She put her pencil down to do it.

"That's not *fair*," said Hyvred. "He did that on *purpose*."

"Yes," said Bee.

"He *knew* I'd—"

"Yes."

"*BEE.*"

Bee held up two fingers.

"Two," said Bee. "You owe him two things now."

Hyvred stamped her foot again.

***

Later, while Tiger was trying to get back in the fountain, she got her phone out. In the game, the small window in the corner of her eye. She opened the note.

At the top, above everything, in her own capitals:

*ICE — 3,821.*

She looked at it for a while. At the white city going away gold in every direction, all of it, edge to edge. At the gold ball on top of the tower, bristling with arrows.

Then she took two off it. One for each hit.

*ICE — 3,819.*

And underneath, smaller, because she couldn't work out what it was worth and she hated that:

*+ one lesson.*

She looked at it and laughed, out loud, like a door banging. On the far side of the terrace, Ice looked round. She didn't look back. Her face was going hot again, and she was absolutely not going to let him see it.
