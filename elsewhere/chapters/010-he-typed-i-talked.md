The certificate had come on Friday.

SSM had been in a good mood, it turned out, even though Friday wasn't a Wednesday. Vivian Ong had emailed him at nine minutes past four in the afternoon. The email was three lines long, and he'd read it about twenty times since.

*Dear Mr Declan,*

*Moonlight Harvest Workshop Sdn. Bhd. was incorporated today. The certificate and the company's details are attached. Your bank appointment is confirmed for Tuesday at 10.30 a.m.; please bring the documents on the enclosed list, and do not, if possible, describe the business to the bank as "gardening".*

*Regards, V. Ong*

He didn't know how she knew about the gardening. He suspected the auntie.

The certificate was a PDF with a crest on it and a company number. He'd opened it on his laptop on Friday night and looked at it for a long time, and then, for reasons he couldn't explain to himself, printed it out on the dusty printer in the hall that he hadn't used in a year, and stuck it to the fridge with a magnet shaped like a durian.

It was still there on Monday morning, when he went down for coffee. He touched it on the way past.

That was how it started. Suzu typed, and Ice talked.

It was a strange way to have a conversation, and also the most natural thing in the world. Suzu had been in his ear for years: in Discord calls at three in the morning, in games where Ice did the shooting and Suzu did the thinking. Ice had won a tournament once with Suzu carrying him on his back. He knew Suzu's voice better than he knew his own. Low and slow and very calm, a voice that made you believe it had already thought about whatever you were about to say.

Now Suzu didn't have a voice. He had lowercase letters, a minute behind, and Ice answered him out loud, inside a game, with a sword in his hand.

At four, he took an hour off and drove to Melaka Raya with the stream off and the radio on.

The shoplot was in the middle of a row of identical shoplots, two storeys, pale yellow, with a roller shutter on the ground floor that the agent had to heave up with both arms. Inside was a room with white tiles and nothing in it, and a back room with a high window, and a narrow staircase going up to two more rooms and a small bathroom with a window that looked onto another row of identical shoplots. It smelled of new paint and dust. He'd signed for it on Saturday. The agent was bringing the keys tomorrow, with the bank; he'd only come today to measure.

He stood in the back room with a tape measure and measured the floor three times.

Two pods. Side by side, along the long wall, with a gap between them for a person to walk. It'd fit. It'd fit with room for a little table and a kettle, and maybe a chair.

He'd ordered them on Saturday night, from the same shop that had sold him his own, two of the newest model. The delivery date said Wednesday. That was two thousand, four hundred and ninety-nine dollars, twice. He'd done the sum once this time, not four times, and pressed the button.

He stood in the empty back room with the tape measure and thought about the room upstairs at his house. It had been a guest room for two years and never had a guest. He'd cleared it out yesterday: the boxes from the spare room that he'd moved into the hall, and then into the guest room, had gone into the garage. He'd bought sheets. He'd stood in the doorway of the empty room and thought about the ceiling fan, and about doors closing that weren't his.

"Soon," he said, out loud, to the white tiles, and his voice came back off them too loud.

And then he was heavy, lying in gel in the dim blue egg, with the fan going round, and his legs had forgotten how to be legs.

He sat in the kitchen for a long time, after, with a glass of water he didn't drink. He looked at his phone.

He'd rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it all week. He'd tried a dozen versions in the shower and the car and in bed. Every one of them sounded like a man trying to do somebody a favour without it looking like one. That was the thing he couldn't get past. He didn't want it to be a favour. In all the years Ice had known him, he'd never once heard Suzu ask anybody for anything, and he wasn't going to make him start now.

Eventually he gave up on the versions, and just typed.
