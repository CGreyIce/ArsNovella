The stag came out of the willows at the first dawn with its head down. Thea had about a second and a half to decide what to do with it.

It was a big one, a Sallow Buck. They were the reason anybody came to the Sallows: a drowned willow wood south of Brackenford where the river had given up on having banks. They stood in the shallows with their antlers grown through with living willow, green and dripping, and they ate the bark off the trees and minded their own business. Right up until somebody walked between them and their does. Then they put their heads down and came at you like a cart with no driver.

A second and a half was a long time now. In April it hadn't been. In April a crayfish the size of a footstool had knocked her flat on her back in a mill-race and Slep had stood over her and said *walls fall over* in the voice he used for weather. She'd lain there in the cold water thinking: I am so tired of being the one behind.

So she'd picked up a tower shield. Then she'd learned what it was for.

She didn't plant her feet. That was the thing everybody did wrong, Jessamy had said, standing in the roar of a hundred and twelve waterwheels with her knuckly hands on her hips. Everybody thinks a shield's a wall. Walls fall over. Be a wheel.

Thea put the shield's lower rim to the water and her shoulder to the boss. She let her left foot slide back, a little, the way you'd let a door open to someone you didn't trust. The antlers hit the face of the shield and the whole weight of the buck came into her, a ton of wet animal moving at the speed of a bad decision. She took it. She took it into the shield and into her shoulder and down into her hips. And at the bottom of it, where the push wanted to knock her over, she turned.

The buck went left. It didn't mean to. It meant to go through her. But she'd given it somewhere else to go, and it went there, past her hip, stumbling. It went straight into the space on her left where Slep was already standing, because Slep was always already standing there.

His glaive came round low and took the buck across both forelegs. It went down on its knees in the water.

"Chi," said Thea.

"Mm," said PenguChi, who'd been standing perfectly still on a hummock of roots behind and to the right of everyone for forty seconds, doing nothing at all. That was how PenguChi did everything.

The long spear went in once, at the base of the neck, where the willow-antler met the skull. It went in exactly as far as it needed to and no further. You could see the weight of forty seconds go into it.

The buck didn't get up.

From somewhere behind them came a sound like a very small, very offended trumpet. That was Patience, the sheep. She had not been consulted about the Sallows, and she was standing on a dry tussock making her views known.

"She's fine," said Slep, without turning round. "She's just saying."

There were two more bucks, and a doe who'd decided to be a buck about it. Thea Turned the first one right this time, into the shallows where Xola was. Xola hadn't been in the shallows a second before. She'd been under them. She came up out of the brown water with her pink gills flared wide and her trident already moving. The buck ran onto it the way a fish runs onto a line, because Xola had been watching the water and not the buck.

"*Don't watch the fish*," shouted Xola, delighted, soaked, holding the trident with the buck on it like a cocktail stick. "*Watch the water!*"

"We know," said Xyro. "You say it every time."

"Because it's *true* every time."

Xyro's arrows went into the second buck's flank, three of them, and bounced, the way arrows bounced off willow-bark hide. And then, the way only Xyro's arrows did, they came back. They curved round in the air like swallows and went into the buck's other side, where there was no bark at all.

Thirty. Ice had been thirty in March. She remembered, because she'd watched it. She'd been on the floor of her room on the other side of the world with the lights off, watching a man in a swamp hit thirty on a stream she had never once typed in.

"Again?" said Xola.

She'd started streaming the Sallows a fortnight ago. Before that it had been the fen edge, and the reed beds, and a stretch of the Brackenford road where things came out of the ditches at dusk. She'd streamed all of it. Every night, and some mornings. She had a schedule now, written on a scrap of paper on the wall by her desk, at home, in the real place. *Stream. Food. Sleep.* It was the first schedule she'd kept in a year.

She had known that question would come. It came most nights, in one shape or another. She'd got good at answering it without answering it.

"We're levelling," she said. "We're thirty. The halls are thirty-five. Ask me in a month."

That was true, as far as it went. It didn't go very far.

She had watched the Seven Below. Not live. You couldn't watch anything live from inside the egg, and she'd been in the Sallows that night. She'd watched it the next morning, on her phone, in bed, with a cup of tea going cold beside her. Ten people going down a stair under a round town. Ice at the front, the way he was always at the front. A girl with gold horns calling *South-east, forty, low*. And at one point the camera turned and the stair was behind them. At the top of the stair there was a woman with a hawthorn staff, watching them go down. She was only on screen for a second. Thea had watched that second four times.

She hadn't told anybody that either.

***

They came back into Brackenford at noon, wet to the hips, with three bucks' worth of hide and willow-antler for Chi and Slep to do things with, and Patience walking in front like she'd organised the whole outing.

Brackenford was quieter than it had been. It had been the busiest stone in the world once, after Ice's world first. Thousands of travellers had stood round the Tally in the square and watched the needle move. Then VANTA had come for the Long Night and stayed, and the stone had been theirs for months. *Kestrel* at the top of it, always, in plain black letters.

VANTA had gone a fortnight ago. They'd gone off to a Hall somewhere and written a writ by hand with twenty names on it, and they were a guild now. Kestrel's name had dropped off the stone the morning after she left. Thea had walked past it that day and seen the empty top line, and thought, *huh*. There was a lot of that going round. People leaving towns because they had somewhere to be.

MH had gone three weeks ago. Ice had stood on the east bridge with his pack on and Chi had threatened to kick him. Thea had put her hand on his shoulder and said *go on, then*, and he'd gone. Eight days east, to a round town she'd never been to, to sign a book in a Hall. To sit in a chair an old woman had swept under every Sunday for four months.

Thea hadn't gone. She'd said *not yet*.

She'd meant it.

She went back to the lodging-house on Tanner's Row and sat on the end of her bed and ate an apple she'd bought off a cart. Then she took her boots off and lay down and looked at the ceiling. The others were downstairs arguing about whether Patience could come into the common room. (She could. She always could. They argued about it every day anyway; it was a thing to do.)

*Ask me in a month.*

She'd been saying that for ten weeks.

Higu rang at the second dusk.

Twenty past eight in the game's clock, which was twenty past ten at night where Thea's body was. She was lying in the same place on the same bed, half asleep, and the little window came up in the corner of her eye.

She knew before she answered. Higu didn't ring for nothing. Higu rang three times in a year, and twice it had been about Gigi.

She answered anyway.

"Hi," said Higu. Her voice was the way it always was, warm and low, with wind behind it. She was on the roof again. The roof of the dome, Thea had worked out, the top of the round town where you could see seven roads at once. Higu went up there to think. "Are you somewhere you can sit down?"

"I'm lying down."

"That's better," said Higu. "Stay there."

Thea stayed there. She looked at the ceiling of the lodging-house, which had a water stain in one corner shaped like a pear. She waited.

"I've got a message," said Higu. "For you. From her. She asked me to bring it, and I said I would, and I'm going to read it exactly the way she wrote it. I'm not going to add anything or take anything off. Okay?"

"Okay."

"Okay," said Higu. Thea heard her breathe in. And then, very carefully, in a flat voice, the way you'd read out something that wasn't yours: "*Gu. If you talk to her. Tell her there's a chair. That's all. Just that there's a chair.*"

Thea didn't say anything.

She'd thought she would. She'd thought, since the first message in June, that one day there'd be another one. She'd thought she'd be ready for it, because she'd had six weeks to get ready. But it turned out six weeks wasn't anything. It turned out you could be lying on a bed in a lodging-house in a game, looking at a stain like a pear, and five short sentences could come through a window in the corner of your eye and take all the air out of the room.

The others were still arguing downstairs. She could hear Xyro say *she's a member of this party* and Tif say *she's a SHEEP*. Slep said nothing. That meant he was on Patience's side.

"A chair," said Thea, eventually.

"Yeah," said Higu.

And then Thea didn't say anything for a long time. Higu let her. Higu was very good at that. She'd been very good at that for as long as Thea had known her, which was a long time. Higu could sit on the other end of a silence for an hour and never once make you feel you owed her the end of it.

The window in the corner of Thea's eye said the call had been going for four minutes and eleven seconds. Then twelve. Then thirteen.

"Who set it out?" said Thea.

She heard Higu smile. She could actually hear it, the way you can on the phone with people you've known for years.

"There's a woman who keeps the Hall," said Higu. "Ama Corrie. She's old. She's been keeping it since before any of us came. She keeps the book everybody signs, the Roll, and she keeps the banner room, and she sweeps." Higu paused. "In March she set out seventeen chairs. Nine for Moonlight Harvest and eight for Xav's lot. Before either of them had said yes to anything. And she swept under them every Sunday for four months. Chairs nobody sat in. Because, she said, that's what keeping is."

Thea closed her eyes.

"And then last week they came," said Higu. "MH and Riverbank. And they signed. And they sat in the chairs. And on Sunday night, after everyone had gone to bed, she went to the store under the stair and got six more chairs. She put them at the end of the long table, by the window. Not at the table. Near it." Higu's voice went softer. "Nobody told her six. She counted. Kei's got a map with everyone on it, and she reads it over his shoulder. You're on it. Six dots. Dotted lines. She counted them."

"So she set them out."

"She set them out," said Higu. "And on Monday Gigi came into the banner room with two cups of tea, and saw them."

Thea opened her eyes.

"She stood in the doorway for a long time," said Higu. "With a cup in each hand. And then she went out again and came up two hundred and twelve steps to where I sit. She sat in my place and got her phone out. And that's what she wrote."

"She didn't set them out."

"No," said Higu.

"She just looked at them."

"She said that," said Higu. "I said, *Ama Corrie set it out*. And she said, *I looked at it. For me, that's the same.*"

Thea lay very still.

She knew what that meant. Higu knew she knew; that was why Higu had told her. In all the time Thea had known Gigi, Gigi had never once looked at a thing that might turn out to be her fault. She'd looked at Thea instead. Every time. Whatever Thea had done, whatever Thea hadn't done, the wrong had always turned out to be Thea's. It had been in the thing she'd said, or the thing she'd not said, or the way she'd done the thing she'd been asked to do. There'd never been a right answer. Thea had spent a long time looking for one anyway. It had taken her far too long to understand that there wasn't one to find.

And now Gigi had stood in a doorway with two cups of tea and looked at six empty chairs. She'd looked at what they meant, and who they were for, and whose fault it was that they were empty. And she hadn't looked away.

*For me, that's the same.*

"Is she okay?" said Thea. She'd asked that in May too. She didn't know why it was the first thing that came out of her every time. It was a stupid question. It was the only one she had.

"She's trying," said Higu. "Same as before. She's trying to be better than she was. Some days she's better at it than others." A pause. "She's been on the roof with me most nights since. She doesn't say anything. She brings tea."

"That's all?"

"That's all," said Higu. "That's what she said. *That's all.* She didn't tell me to say anything else, so I'm not."

"What do I do with it?"

"Whatever you want," said Higu. "Including nothing. Nothing's allowed." The wind behind her voice got louder for a moment, then dropped. "I'm not in the middle, Thea. I told you that. I'm the road. I just carry what people put on me. She put a chair on me. I've carried it. It's yours now."

"That's a lot to put on a road."

"Roads are for that," said Higu. "That's the whole point of them."

Thea laughed. It wasn't much of a laugh. It came out of her sideways, wet, and she put her arm over her eyes.

"Thank you," she said. "For being the road."

"Always," said Higu. And then, more quietly: "Ring me if you want. Or don't. No hurry."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true," said Higu. "Goodnight, Thea."

The window closed.

***

She couldn't stay on the bed after that. She put her boots back on and went downstairs, past the common room, where Patience was standing in front of the fire as if she'd paid for it. Xyro was asleep in a chair with his mouth open, and Tif was asleep against him with her ears twitching. PenguChi was sewing something by the window. She looked up as Thea went past and didn't say anything, which was the PenguChi way of saying a great deal.

Thea went out into the dark and down to the east bridge.

It was a good bridge. Old stone, five arches, with the river going under it black and fast. Torches on poles every ten paces. She'd stood on it three weeks ago and said *go on, then* to Ice. She'd watched him go east with nine people behind him, and she'd felt, watching him go, exactly two things at once. One was *good*. The other was something she hadn't let herself name.

She leaned on the parapet and looked east.

You couldn't see the round town from here. It was eight days away. But she knew which way it was. She'd checked the map for it on the first morning, in the little wooden room, before she'd even looked at her own face properly. Before she'd looked to see if her ears had moved. She'd found it on the map, a little round dot far east of where they'd woken. And she'd felt relieved, because it was so far away. Then ashamed of the relief, because of what it meant.

She'd never told anyone that. Not even Xola.

*There's a chair.*

What she felt, standing on the bridge, wasn't forgiveness. She was fairly sure of that. Forgiveness was a big, warm, finished thing. This wasn't finished. It wasn't anything yet. It was more like what you feel when you've been holding your breath for a very long time without noticing. Somebody says your name, and you find out.

She was frightened. That was in there too. She'd spent a long time being frightened of Gigi, in a way she'd never have said out loud. A low, tired frightened, that came from not knowing which version of anything she did would be the wrong one today. You didn't stop being that just because somebody looked at a chair.

But she'd also, for a second, on the bed, felt something that wasn't fright at all. She couldn't have named it. It was small. It was the size of a chair.

She thought about Ice in the Sallows, in her head, for no reason. Ice on the jetty at Fernwick, listening to her with his whole back, the way he used to. Saying almost nothing. And then, when she'd finally stopped talking: *no hurry*. Like it was the simplest thing in the world. Like nobody had ever told her she had all the time she needed.

She'd needed a year to hear it.

***

It was a while before she thought of Xola.

Which was strange, when she looked back at it later. She'd thought of Higu, and Gigi, and Ice, and Ama Corrie, whom she'd never met, sweeping under six chairs in the dark. She'd thought of six dots on a map. And it was only when she started counting the dots that she got to Xola. One of the six was Xola. And Xola didn't know.

Xola didn't know about May. About Higu ringing from the roof and saying *she knows you're in*, and *she asked if you were all right*. Xola didn't know about June, about *glad she's at the front*. She didn't know about the answer Thea had sent back through Higu: *tell her I saw her hold the counter. Tell her it was brave.* She didn't know about any of it. Thea had told Higu not to tell her. *Don't tell Xo. Not yet. She was on the phone for all of it. Let her have a rest.*

She'd meant it kindly. She'd meant: Xola had carried enough. Xola had been on the phone every single night, for all of it, every night until Thea ended it and the morning after too. Xola had held Thea up with both hands until her arms must have ached. Xola deserved to be in a game with gills that moved, laughing in the shallows, not carrying anything of Gigi's ever again.

Standing on the bridge, Thea understood, quite suddenly, that that wasn't her decision to make.

It had felt like a kindness. It had been a kindness, a bit. But it had also been her deciding, on Xola's behalf, what Xola could carry. And there was one person in the world who'd never let Thea decide that for her. One person who'd have been furious, absolutely spitting, at being kept off a thing like this to spare her feelings. And it was Xola.

*Let her have a rest.*

Xola hadn't been resting. Thea had seen her. Every day for ten weeks, Xola had kept half an eye on Thea, the way you keep half an eye on a pan. She did it when they walked. She did it when they fought. She did it on the stream, when Thea went quiet and Xola started talking louder to fill the gap, so nobody would notice. She'd been doing it for so long she probably didn't know she was doing it. You didn't rest from a thing like that because somebody kept a secret from you. You just did it in the dark.

Thea got her phone out. The game's phone, the little window, the one you could message people with.

She put the window away.

She waited on the bridge, under the torches, with the river going under her. She thought about how to start. She didn't come up with anything. Everything she tried sounded like an apology, or an excuse, or both. And it wasn't either. It was just a thing she should have said six weeks ago and hadn't.

She heard Xola before she saw her. Running feet on the cobbles of Tanner's Row, the slap of boots that had been in the river all morning and never quite dried. Then Xola came round the corner at the end of the bridge at a full sprint. She'd brought the trident, for some reason. Her pink gills were flared wide at the sides of her neck, the way they went when she was frightened or angry. She'd stopped pretending she could do anything about that.

She skidded to a stop in front of Thea and looked at her face, very hard, all over, the way you check a child who's fallen off something.

"You're not hurt," said Xola.

"I'm not hurt."

"You've been crying."

"A bit."

Xola's grip changed on the trident. Her eyes went past Thea, up and down the bridge, into the shadows under the torches, as if whatever had done it might still be there.

"Okay," said Xola. "Who do I hit?"

And Thea, who had been holding her breath for six weeks, let it go all at once. She laughed and cried at the same time, in front of her best friend on a bridge in the dark. And then she told her.
