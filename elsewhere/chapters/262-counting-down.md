Slep waited until nobody else had said it. That was the rule he'd made for himself, a long time ago, before the game. It still worked.

Most of the time, if you waited, somebody said the thing that needed saying, and you didn't have to. Xola usually. Xola said things the moment she thought them, loudly, with her whole face, and he loved that about her more than he'd ever been able to tell her. And when Xola didn't, Thea did, or Hyv, or Hako in the voice. And he'd stand on whichever side of Xola she wasn't on, with his glaive, and say nothing. And be fine.

Sometimes nobody said it. Then he did.

On the morning after Christmas, on the hill, inside the ring of twelve stones, with the middle standing over the jar on the flat stone, nobody was saying it. So he waited a long time. And then he said it.

"We should go home," said Slep.

***

Eight people looked at him.

They were round the fire. Slep's fire, the little one he'd made of nothing much on the first night and kept going for four weeks. Kotori had the pot on it. There was a little of the Christmas tea left, the waystone leaf Tiger and Higu had picked on the morning after the longest night. It had come in a brown-paper parcel nobody could explain.

"Today?" said Xola.

"Tomorrow," said Slep. "At dusk. After the words." He looked at the flame over the jar. "It's kept. Ice said. It keeps itself now. The ring and the jar." He looked at Suzu, at the flat stone. "And you're coming back in the spring. With a stone."

"In the spring," said Suzu.

"So we can go home," said Slep. "And come back."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"He's right," said Hyv. Very quietly. She was sitting next to Ice, the way she always sat now, close, with her shoulder against his arm. "I didn't want to say it."

"Nobody did," said Hako. "That's why he did."

Slep looked at the fire. He didn't say anything else. He'd said it. That was enough.

***

Xola found him at the fifth stone that night. His stone, the one he'd stood at on the longest night, on whichever side she wasn't. He was sitting against it with the glaive across his knees.

It was still bent. Badly. A long curve in the haft, two-thirds of the way up, where it had gone under the fallen stone as a lever and held, and then bent. He'd tried to straighten it on a rock on the second night and made it worse.

She sat down next to him. Close. She always sat close.

"You're going to fix it," she said. "When we're home. Salty will. Or Ina."

"No."

She looked at him.

"It held a stone up," said Slep. "Bent." He ran his thumb along the curve. "If they straighten it, it'll just be a glaive again."

Xola didn't say anything for a long time. Then she put her head on his shoulder.

"You're so weird," she said.

"I know."

"I love it," said Xola.

He didn't say anything back. He didn't need to. He put his hand on the back of her head, the way he'd seen Ice do with Hyv, between the horns. Except Xola didn't have horns. Just hair, and her head, warm, on his shoulder.

He'd never said it in here. Out there, yes. Not in the game, where there was a stream, and a long table, and Hako narrating everything. He'd thought about that a lot, on the hill. In the nothing, on the rope. He'd always waited.

He waited now. On the hill, at the fifth stone. Nobody else was saying anything.

"I love you," said Slep.

Xola went completely still.

Then she sat up and looked at him, at the fifth stone, in the gold light from the middle. With her whole face doing everything at once.

"*In here*," she said. "You said it *in here*."

"Nobody else was saying it," said Slep.

***

Ice came to the fire at about two. Slep was on his own there, for once, with the glaive across his knees, keeping it going. Xola was asleep against the fifth stone.

Ice sat down across the fire from him. He didn't say anything for a long time. He was like that. Slep didn't mind. He was like that too.

"Thank you," said Ice, at last.

Slep looked at him.

"For saying it," said Ice. "*We should go home.*" He looked at the flame over the jar, behind Slep, on the flat stone. "I didn't want to. Nobody did."

"I know," said Slep. "That's why."

Ice looked at him across the fire for a long moment.

"You wait until nobody else has said it," he said. "Xyro told me. He's been practising it. He said he learned it off you, on a road, at the back of the line, with blisters."

"He told me," said Slep. "He's bad at it."

Something happened at the corner of Ice's mouth.

"He's getting better," he said. "He waited for the second one. At the bottom of a lake. With a crown." He looked at the fire. "Wendy said. And you. *You always wait for the second one.*"

Slep didn't say anything. He hadn't known that had got all the way to the bottom of a lake.

"I borrowed it," said Ice. "On the hill. On the longest night. When the breath came through all twelve at once." He was quiet. "I waited. Until there was nothing else I could do. And then I said *everybody in*." He looked at Slep. "That's yours. Waiting till nobody else can. And then saying the thing."

"It's not mine," said Slep. "It's just what I do."

"That's what makes it yours," said Ice, and got up, and went back to the north stone.

Slep sat by the fire with the bent glaive across his knees and didn't say anything for a long time. Then he put another stick on.

***

At dusk the next day they stood round the flat stone for the last time. All nine.

Suzu said it. He always said it now.

"*Here's the middle*," said Suzu. To the dark outside the ring, which wasn't leaning. To the still water. To three hundred years. "*Not yet.*"

He didn't say *set one to watch*. It wasn't morning. He put his hand on the edge of the flat stone instead. On the notches. On the cross, and his notch beside it with a thorn under it, and a flower beside the thorn. And then, past Isa's crooked last one, at the end of the second line, on the space.

"Spring," said Suzu.

Nobody said anything.

They left the bowl and the blanket and the empty black jar and the stick where they were, at the foot of the flat stone on the south side. Kotori left the last of the tea in a cup beside them. *For her mothers.* Nobody argued.

And they went down the hill.

***

The still water was different going home.

Slep saw it the moment they came down off the hill, onto the heather at the edge. Going north, three weeks ago, it had been nothing. Black, flat, invisible, that you'd have stepped onto thinking it was ground. You'd found the first stone with your hand, a hand deep, by feel.

Now it was black and flat and you could see it.

Not well. It was still dark; the middle was behind them, on the hill, and its light only went so far. But the dark had edges now. It was just dark. The kind you could see into a bit, if you waited. And the still water lay in it like a black mirror, with the hill upside down in it, and the ring, and a small gold point at the top. And going away south across it, in a line, curving, left and then right, the stones.

You could see the first ones. Grey islands in the black, catching the light from behind.

"Three hundred," said Suzu.

Slep looked at him.

He was standing at the edge of the still water with the black iron case on his back. Empty now. Ina's case, with the hood and the star. Nothing in it. He'd carried it all the way north with three hundred years inside it. He was carrying it home with nothing.

"We count down," said Suzu. "Going home. Isa said. *So the stones know you're going home.*"

He stepped onto the first stone. The last one. Isa's, crooked, new, tilting left.

"*Three hundred*," said Suzu.

***

They counted down.

All nine, the way they'd counted up. Each saying their own number as they stepped on, one behind another. *Three hundred, three hundred.* Slep at the back with Hako, the way the rope had been coming north. But there was no rope now. You didn't need one. You could see the stones.

*Two hundred and ninety. Two hundred and eighty.*

They went faster than they had coming. Of course they did. Coming north they'd found every stone by feel in the nothing, by the light of one candle held low. Now the stones were there, in the dark with edges. You could see the next one before you got to it, and the one after that.

Slep didn't look at the stones much. He looked at Suzu.

At the front, with the empty case. Stepping, counting. And every few stones, turning his head. Not all the way. Just a bit. Looking back over his shoulder, north, at the hill. At the small gold point at the top of it, standing straight up, getting smaller.

*Two hundred and fifty.* He looked back.

*Two hundred and forty.* He looked back.

Nobody said anything about it.

***

At two hundred and seventeen, they stopped.

Gigi stopped first. Of course. It was hers. The knot of hawthorn, black and wet and twisted, as thick as a man's body. Its top a step on from two hundred and sixteen, where a stone should have been, where a spring hadn't been.

It had leaves.

Not many. A few. Small, green, very new, along the twigs at the top of the knot, where the flower had been in December. The flower was gone. In its place, a tiny hard green thing, the size of a fingernail.

"*Haw*," said Gigi. Very quietly. "It's a haw. A berry." She knelt on two hundred and eighteen and looked at it. "It set fruit. In the dark. In January."

Thea knelt beside her. Close.

"It's not supposed to," said Gigi.

"Nothing here's supposed to," said Thea.

Gigi touched the knot. Very gently. Just the wood, not the leaves.

"*Two hundred and seventeen*," she said, and stepped onto it, and it held.

***

At a hundred and eighty, Kotori stopped.

Slep nearly walked into Hako. The whole line stopped, one after another, the way it did when the front stopped. Kotori was kneeling on the hundred and eightieth stone with a cup in her hand. One of her little tin cups. She was pouring tea out of it, very slowly, into the still water beside the stone.

It made no sound. The black water took it the way it took everything. It didn't ripple.

"Kotori," said Suzu, from the front. Not a question.

"It went down here," said Kotori. She didn't look up. "In December. The shape. It came up at a hundred and fifty and went down here, at a hundred and eighty, when we said your number." She poured the last of it. "It was looking for us. For the light. It didn't know what it was looking for."

"It's not here now," said Hyv, from the middle of the line. "There's nothing under there. I'd see."

"I know," said Kotori. She shook the last drop out of the cup. "It's for if it comes back. Some spring. When it's looking again." She stood up and put the cup in her coat. "So it knows somebody was here who was kind to it. Once."

Nobody said anything.

Slep, at the back, looked at the black water by the hundred and eightieth stone for a long time after Kotori had walked on. He thought about the shape. Tall and black and smooth, leaning in toward the case on Suzu's back, in the dark, in December. Frightened, Kotori said. Looking for something it didn't know the name of.

He'd been frightened in December too.

"*A hundred and eighty*," he said, when he got to it. Out loud. To the water.

Xola, in front of him, looked back at him over her shoulder, and didn't say anything. She took his hand on the next stone, and kept it.

***

At a hundred and fifty, nothing rose.

Slep had been waiting for it. Since two hundred. He'd been watching the black water at the edge of the light to the left, where the shape had come up on the way north. Tall, black, smooth, leaning in toward the case on Suzu's back. And Kotori saying *keep counting* in her small level voice. And nine people saying Suzu's number.

He watched the water at a hundred and fifty.

It was still. Flat. Black. A mirror, with the hill upside down in it, very small now, and the point of gold.

"*A hundred and fifty*," said Suzu. And looked back at the hill.

"It's not looking," said Hyv, from the middle of the line. "It doesn't need to. There's nothing on his back." She was looking at the water, edge to edge. "It knows where the middle is."

"*A hundred and forty-nine*," said Suzu.

***

He said the thing at about eighty.

He'd waited. He'd waited the whole way, from three hundred. Watching Suzu look back over his shoulder every few stones. Waiting for somebody to say it. Kotori, who was right behind Suzu and knew him best. Or Ice, who'd known him longest. Or Hyv, who saw everything.

Nobody said it.

So at about eighty, Slep went forward. Along the stones, past Hako, past Xola, who looked at him and didn't ask. Past Thea and Gigi, close. Past Hyv and Ice. Past Kotori. Until he was on the stone behind Suzu.

Suzu looked round. He'd never once in two years had Slep come up behind him. They didn't know each other well. Slep stood beside Xola; Suzu stood beside the light.

"You can look back," said Slep.

Suzu didn't say anything.

"You keep looking back," said Slep. "Every few stones. Over your shoulder. Not all the way." He looked past Suzu at the hill, very small now, and the gold point. "You don't have to do it like that. Like you're not allowed."

Suzu stood on the eightieth stone with the empty case on his back and looked at him.

"You can stop," said Slep. "And turn round. All the way. And look at it." He paused. "It's yours. You carried it. You can look at it as long as you want."

***

Suzu didn't move for a long time.

Then he turned round. All the way. On the eightieth stone in the still water, with eight people on the stones behind him, stopped. He turned and faced north, and looked back across eighty stones and two hundred and twenty more to the hill. At the ring, very small. At the gold point at the top of it, standing straight up, not leaning toward anything.

He looked at it for a long time.

Nobody said anything. They stood on the stones and let him.

Slep stood behind him and didn't say anything else. He'd said it. That was enough.

"Thank you," said Suzu, after a long time. Without turning round.

"You're welcome," said Slep.

Kotori, two stones back, made a small sound. Slep didn't look. He knew what it was. He'd heard it from her a hundred times on the rope. The sound of somebody not saying sorry.

Then Suzu turned back round. South. And stepped onto the seventy-ninth stone.

"*Seventy-nine*," he said.

He didn't look back again. Slep watched him not. He didn't need to.

***

They slept on the stones that dawn. Not on the stones; on two of them, the forty-first and forty-second, which were wide, close together. Nine people sitting on two flat stones in the still water at a quarter to eight. With candles in a ring at their feet, on the stone, and one in the middle.

Not Malcolm's kept wick. They had five left, of the twelve. Kotori didn't take one out. She lit one of Chernn's instead, an ordinary one, and set it in the middle of the ring on the forty-first stone.

"It'll go out," said Gigi. "Before we're back. They burn eight hours."

"I know," said Kotori. "That's all right." She looked north, back at the hill, very small. "It doesn't have to watch any more. There's something watching already."

Suzu said the words. Nine said the answer. And at eight, the egg took them, on two stones, in the still water.

When Slep came back at eight in the evening, the candle in the middle was a cold stub. Out. As Gigi had said.

And it didn't matter at all. He looked north, and the gold point was still there, at the top of the hill, standing straight. Watching.

***

*Three. Two.*

"*One*," said Suzu.

The first stone. The oldest. Sunk a hand deep, worn smooth by feet before it sank. Suzu stood on it in the still water with the cold round his ankles. And then stepped off it, onto the heather, onto the edge. Where the still water stopped and the dark country began.

One by one they came off behind him. Each saying *one* on the first stone and stepping onto the heather.

Slep last. He stood on the first stone, the oldest, with the cold going up through his boot.

"*One*," he said.

He stepped off.

He turned round, once, on the heather, the way Suzu had at eighty. And looked back. Three hundred stones going away north across the black water, curving. A knot of hawthorn in the gap, too far to see. A hill. A ring. A point of gold.

Then he turned south, where Xola was waiting for him on the heather with her hand out.

He took it.

"You said it in here," said Xola. She still couldn't get over it. "Last night. At the stone."

"Nobody else was saying it."

"*Nobody else was going to say THAT*," said Xola. "*That was MINE to say. Or yours.*" She was laughing. She was crying a bit. Both. "Two years in this game and you said it on a *hill*. In the *middle of nothing*."

"It wasn't nothing," said Slep. "It was the middle."

Xola stared at him.

"You're so weird," she said.

"I know," said Slep.

He took whichever side of her she wasn't on. And walked.
