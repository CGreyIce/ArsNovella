The north walk was quieter than the south. There was nothing to fight on it.

It ran out of town along the edge of a wide, slow channel where the water was clearer, past a few last houses and a drying rack hung with nets, and ended at a platform that stood a little apart from everything, like somebody who didn't much want company. A house sat on it, round and low like the others, but older: the thatch had gone dark and furred with moss, and the wattle walls had been patched so many times they looked knitted. Bundles of drying plants hung from every beam of the porch, upside down, dozens of them, rustling. The smell reached him twenty planks before the house did. Green, and bitter, and somewhere under it something sweet, like hay.

Floating on the water around the platform, tied to it with ropes, were rafts. Each was a square of lashed reeds with a bed of black mud on top, and every bed was growing something different.

"Oh, she's got a *garden*," said Ice.

The traffic was drifting off now that he wasn't hitting anything. He'd expected that. A launch-day viewer wanted to see a monster. His regulars stayed, though; they always did. Teh Peng, Frog, Mangoes, and the handful of names that had been with him for years, and his friends. He didn't need to look at the list to know who was there.

The door of the house was open. Inside it was dim and warm and very full, and a small old woman was standing at a table, grinding something in a stone bowl with a stone club as if it had personally insulted her family.

"If you want a draught," she said, without looking up, "there are none. If you want to know when there will be, it'll be when I've got four more hands. If you want to tell me your ankle hurts, I know. Everyone's ankle hurts. Out."

"I don't want a draught," said Ice.

The grinding stopped.

She looked up. Her face was brown and creased like the bark of an old tree, and her eyes were very pale and very sharp. Her grey hair was pulled back so tight it looked painful. She had flecks of green on her chin.

"Then what do you want?"

"I want to know how to make them."

She looked at him for a long moment. He had seen that look three times today now, on Hollis and Marta and the eel man, and he thought he was beginning to understand it. These people had been talked at all morning. A thousand strangers had come out of a door that should only have fitted one, and every one of them had wanted something from them. *Where's the button. How do I leave. Give me a draught.* He thought the look meant: *is this one going to be the same?*

"You're the one who asked Hollis if he could come in," the old woman said.

He blinked. "How do you know that?"

"Fernwick's not that big." She put the stone club down. "Sedge. Widow Sedge. Come in, then, and shut your mouth. You'll get something in it."

***

She tested him first.

She took a handful of plants from a basket by the door and put them on the table in a heap: leaves and stems and roots, wet and muddled together. "Which of those," she said, "is fenwort."

He'd looked at the ground for three hours. He picked out the low dark leaves with the red vein, five of them, and the little white flowers still on two.

"And that?" She pointed at something with a blue stalk.

"I don't know. The game, sorry, it won't tell me what it's called."

"It won't tell you because you don't know it yet." She picked up the blue stalk. "Bluestem. Good for nothing, but it grows next to things that are good for something. If you see bluestem, look about." She put it down. "You've been looking at the ground."

"Everybody was standing on it."

"Your lot have been standing on my fenwort since the sun came up." She sniffed. "All right. Hands."

He held out his hands. She took them in hers, which were dry and very strong for their size, and turned them over and looked at them, the palms and the fingers and the nails, as if she were reading them.

"Soft," she said. "Never mind. That goes."

And then she taught him.

***

Picking came first.

You didn't pull. If you pulled, the root broke, and a plant with a broken root was a plant you'd killed, and a plant you'd killed was worth less than one you'd *taken*. She made him crouch on the edge of one of her rafts and do it over and over with fenwort: fingers down into the cold mud around the base, find where the stem became root, a little twist, a lift. If you did it right, it came up whole and dripping with its whole white beard of root, and the mud closed over the hole with a small wet sound.

"And never take a whole clump," Sedge said, standing over him with her arms folded. "Never. Take one in three, and leave the rest. You take a whole clump, and next year there's no clump. You take one in three, and next year it's back, and it's angry, and it's twice the size."

"One in three," he repeated.

"I'll know if you don't." She said it as though she meant it literally, and he found he believed her.

He picked fenwort until his fingers were numb with mud. Somewhere around the twentieth plant, a window came up that wasn't a quality grade.

[ You have learned the gatherer's way of Herbalism.
Each traveller may hold one gathering craft and one making craft, for good.
Take Herbalism as your gathering craft? ]

He looked at it for a while.

*For good.* He'd known that one too, from the dev diaries, but it was the kind of thing you read and nodded at and didn't feel until a window asked you. Mining, woodcutting, fishing, skinning: all the others, closed to him forever if he said yes. Forever was a long time in an MMO. People played these games for ten years.

But he'd been looking at the ground all morning, and he knew what he'd seen.

"Yes," he said, and pressed it.

Making came after.

The window for the second craft came while he was standing at her table with her old stone mortar in front of him and the club in his hand. A list. Smithing, cooking, tailoring, all the others. He didn't read it. He knew which one he wanted.

[ Take Apothecary as your making craft? ]

"Yes."

[ Apothecary · Lv 1 ]

She didn't give him the mortar. She sold him one, a small rough one from a shelf of others, for forty copper, which was very nearly half of everything he had in the world. Then she sold him ten little glass bottles with cork stoppers for two copper each. He handed it over without arguing. You didn't haggle with your teacher.

"Fenwort," she said, putting a handful of his own picked leaves in front of him. "Leaves only. The root you keep; the root's for something else. Tear them, don't cut them. Now grind."

He ground. She stopped him almost at once.

"Up and down? You're beating it. You're not trying to kill it. It's dead, you killed it when you picked it. You're trying to get it to give you what it's got. Round." She put her hand over his on the club, and moved it in a slow circle, pressing down and around against the side of the bowl, and he felt the leaves give under it, felt the wet green squeak of it. "Round, and even, and don't stop. If you stop, it goes bitter."

He went round. It was harder than the sword. The sword was one movement, done whole. This was a long one that you had to keep doing the same way for a minute and more without letting your mind wander off, and his mind kept wandering off. Every time it did, the stone squeaked differently, and he knew he'd lost it.

When the leaves were a dark green paste, she made him tip it into a little clay pot of water she'd set over the hearth, and stir.

"Not boiled," she said. "Never boiled. When it smells of cut grass, it's right. When it smells of cabbage, you've killed it twice."

He stood by the fire and stirred and sniffed. He'd never in his life tried to tell cut grass from cabbage with any kind of seriousness. He was standing inside a game sniffing a pot, and he thought, very clearly, *Deston is going to laugh at me for a week.*

It smelled of grass, and then of something greener, and then, very faintly, of cabbage.

"Off," said Sedge. "Off, off, *now.*"

He pulled it off. She showed him how to strain it through a square of cloth into a bottle, and how to push the cork in with his thumb and turn it, so it bit.

He held it up to the light from the door. It was a cloudy, muddy green, like pond water with ambitions.

[ Minor Healing Draught · Quality: Poor
Restores 12 health over 6 seconds.
+8 Apothecary XP · +1 XP to Ice ]

"Poor," he said.

"I could have told you that," said Sedge. "It smells of cabbage. Drink it yourself. Make another."

He made another. It smelled of grass when he took it off. It was clearer, a little.

[ Minor Healing Draught · Quality: Fair
Restores 25 health over 6 seconds.
+12 Apothecary XP · +1 XP to Ice ]

He stood looking at that last line for a moment. *+1 XP to Ice.* Small. A tenth of what the craft got, or about that. But it was XP he'd earned standing next to a fire, not being bitten. He hadn't known about that one.

"Chat," he said. "Making things gives you a little bit of your level, too."

He made six more. He got two Poor, three Fair, and one that was very nearly Good and then went cabbagey at the last second because he'd looked round when a frog went past the door. By the end, his arm hurt from the circles in a completely different way from how it hurt after the sword, and he'd learned more about the smell of cut grass than he had ever wanted to.

He had eight bottles. He looked at them in a row on the table.

Sixty-four people, more every hour, with bitten ankles. Twelve draughts in the whole town, gone by second breakfast. Marta would buy them, or any of the people in the queue would. He could walk back to the square right now and stand by the door and sell these eight for whatever he asked, and on day one with no competition he could ask a lot.

*Don't sell anything on day one,* Xav had said. *Prices on day one are always stupid.*

He knew what Xav meant. Prices on day one were stupid *low*, sometimes, because nobody knew what anything was worth yet. And they were stupid high, too, because people were desperate, and that never lasted. Either way, if he sold now, he sold for a price he'd regret tomorrow, and he'd have spent his herbs on money instead of on levels.

He put the eight bottles in his pack. All of them.

"You're not selling them," said Sedge, who'd been watching him think.

"Not yet."

She made a sound that might have been approval. Then she leaned on the table on both hands and looked at him, properly, with those pale sharp eyes.

"There's better than fenwort, you know. Out past the heron-stones."

***

The heron-stones were in the old fen.

Sedge told it the way you'd tell the way to a neighbour's house. Out past the end of the north walk, where the boards stopped, you went on along the tussocks: the hummocks of grass that stood up out of the water like stepping stones, if you knew which ones would hold you. You went toward the tall stones you could see from the end of the walk on a clear day. Grey, standing up out of the reeds in a ring, older than the town and older than her mother's mother, with herons cut into them. And inside the ring the water was shallow and clear and warm, the way it never was anywhere else in the fen, and things grew there that grew nowhere else.

"Silverleaf," she said. "Fen-heart. Marshlight, some years." She paused. "And moonbells, in the middle. Right in the middle, in the warm water. My mother used to pick them. They open to the moon only. In the day they're shut up like a fist, and if you pick them shut, you've just got a stalk."

"What are they for?"

"I'll tell you when you bring me three." She looked out of the door at the fen, where the light was already going gold and long. "Haven't been out there since my knees went. Things in the deep water are bigger than rats. And it's dark soon, and at night the fen wakes up." She looked back at him. "But nothing comes inside the stones. Nothing ever did. That's what the herons are for."

A window opened in front of him, edged in a soft brown.

The tussocks were worse than she'd made them sound.

Some held. Some were grass on top and nothing underneath, and his boot went through them into cold black water that came up to his knee and filled the boot and stayed there. After the third time, he started testing each one with the toe of his boot before he trusted it, and that was slow, and the light was going.

He had to fight twice on the way out.

The first was a leech, which turned out to be a word for a thing as long as his arm and as thick as his wrist, slick and black and in a terrible hurry. It came up out of the water at the side of a tussock, and his first Falling Cut slid off its wet back without biting.

[ Fen Leech (Lv 3) · Above your level ]

Above his level. Three, against his two. He didn't run. He set his feet on the tussock, which was the hardest thing he'd done all day, with the grass sinking slowly under him and black water all around. He waited, shield low and sloped, and let it come. It struck at his shin, and he turned it, and it went past, and he cut, sitting back, the whole of him arriving at once.

It took a long time. It got him twice, and the leech bite wasn't a staple. It was a slow, cold, sucking ache that didn't stop when the teeth came out, and his health kept dropping after it, a point at a time, a little red number floating up off his leg.

[ Bleeding · −1 health per second ]

At forty per cent, with the leech still a third full, he pulled one of the Fair bottles out of his pack with his sword hand and thumbed the cork out and drank it. He'd never in his life thought about what a healing potion tasted like. This one tasted of grass, bitter and green, with an aftertaste of earth. Warmth went down his throat and spread into his chest, and the red numbers floating off his leg slowed, and stopped, and his bar went up.

He'd made that. Two hours ago, by a fire, sniffing a pot.

He killed the leech.

The second fight was another leech, and it went better.

And then, as the sun went down into the reeds in a long red line, the tussocks ran out, and there was a step down into warm water, and the stones were in front of him.

***

There were nine of them, in a ring maybe thirty paces across. Each was taller than he was, grey and pitted and furred at the base with green, leaning a little, the way old stones did. Into each one, so worn it was nearly gone, somebody had cut a heron. A long neck, a long beak, a long leg, one foot lifted, in the patient stance herons always took on the edge of the water. When he walked past the first stone into the ring, he felt something change. He couldn't say what. Like stepping from wind into a doorway.

[ The Heron-Stones
A ring the fen keeps. ]

Inside the ring, the water was clear. It came up to his ankles, and it was *warm*, warm as a bath, and the bottom was fine pale sand instead of black mud. And everywhere in it, growing up out of the sand and along the base of the stones, there were herbs he'd never seen.

Silver-grey leaves that caught the last light like fish scales. *Silverleaf.* A squat plant with a dark red heart that pulsed, very slightly, when he crouched near it. *Fen-heart.* Little pale fronds trailing in the water that he couldn't name yet, and something with yellow star-shaped flowers, and a dozen more, more than he could look at.

He picked one in three. He picked slowly, and carefully, twist and lift, all the way round the ring. He kept his mind on his fingers. Most came up Fair. Two came up Good, and when they did, he felt the difference before the window told him: a give in the mud, as though the root had let go of its own accord.

[ Silverleaf · Rare · Quality: Good ]
[ Herbalism · Lv 1 → 72% ]

In the middle of the ring, in the deepest part of the warm water, where it came almost to his knees, grew a cluster of tall dark stems. Each one ended in a closed white bud the size of a thumb, folded tight. There were maybe thirty. They stood very still in the water, and didn't move in the evening breeze, and when he looked at one, the window said only:

[ Moonbell · Rare
It is not open. ]

He sat down on a flat stone at the edge of the ring, with his boots in the warm water, and waited for the moon.

"I'm sitting in a *warm* pond," said Ice. "Different thing."

Out past the stones, the fen was getting dark. He could see it happen: the gold leaking out of the water, the reeds going from brown to grey to black. And he could hear it. Things he hadn't heard all day: a deep, slow splash somewhere off in the reeds, and then another, closer. Long and heavy, like something turning over. A noise like a bubble, the size of a barrel, coming up and breaking.

The fen was waking up.

He looked out across the water, at the place where the second splash had been. For a second, in the last of the light, he thought he saw two things on the surface. Round, close together, about a hand apart. Not moving.

Then they sank, slowly, without a ripple.

He didn't get up. He sat very still on his stone inside the ring, with his sword across his knees, and watched the place where they'd been, and told himself that nothing came inside the stones. Nothing ever did.

And then the moon came up.

It came up fast, faster than any moon he knew, a white rim and then a white half and then a whole white face clearing the reeds, and its light came down across the fen and over the stones, and touched the water in the middle of the ring.

The first bud opened.

He heard it before he saw it: a tiny sound, clear and high, like a fingernail on the rim of a wine glass. Then the white petals came apart, slow, one and then the next, and there was a light inside. Not bright. Silver, soft, like the moon had left a little of itself in there. Then the next bud opened, with its own sound, a note higher. Then three at once. Then all of them, one after another, all thirty, until the middle of the ring was full of small silver lights standing up out of the warm water and a sound like somebody very far away playing a glass harmonica in a dream.
