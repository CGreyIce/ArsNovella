The ring had been in the market the whole time, and nobody had noticed it.

Ama Corrie showed them on Friday afternoon. She came out of the Hall of Roads with her broom, which she never took outside, and walked across the round market to a place just east of the fountain. Suzu and Ice and Kei followed her, not sure why. She stopped. She pointed with the end of the broom at the ground.

"There," said Ama Corrie.

Suzu looked. It was paving. The whole round market was paving, grey flagstones worn smooth by three hundred years of carts and boots and dropped apples. He'd walked over this bit a hundred times. He'd never looked at it.

Then he saw it. Nine of the flagstones were darker than the rest. They were set in a circle, a long way apart, about thirty paces across, with ordinary grey stones between them. You'd never have noticed unless somebody pointed. And then you couldn't stop seeing it, the way you couldn't stop seeing a face in a cloud.

"The settling stones," said Ama Corrie. "My grandmother's grandmother settled a boundary there. Two farmers on the east road, over a hedge. It took all afternoon. The one who yielded gave the other a pig." She sniffed. "Nobody's settled anything on it since they dug the new well. People go to the magistrate now. Or they shout."

"Does it still work?" said Kei.

"It's a ring," said Ama Corrie. "Rings don't stop working. People stop using them." She looked at Ice over her spectacles. "You can have it. For your seven. One a night. And you sweep it after."

"Thank you," said Ice.

"Under, not round," said Ama Corrie, and went back to the Hall.

The first one was on Saturday.

By the second dusk the round market was fuller than Suzu had ever seen it. Fuller than on the night the five signed, fuller than for the duel at Elmcross. It was a market, not a Hall, and there was room. People sat on the fountain and on the steps of the Hall and on the roofs of the stalls, and the stallholders charged them for it. The man who sold roast chestnuts by the east road had put up a sign that said CHESTNUTS — RINGSIDE in chalk. Hobb the carter had come all the way up the west road with his betting book, and was taking bets on the far side of the fountain. He was getting very few on Ice, because nobody would give odds.

"Everybody's betting on how long," said Dennis, who'd gone to look. He came back and sat down on the Hall steps beside Suzu with the expression of a man who'd seen something he disapproved of. "Not who. How *long*. Minutes. He's giving three to one on under two."

"Did you bet?"

"I don't bet," said Dennis. "It's not a bet if you know."

The nine of them sat together on the steps, near the bottom. Moonlight Harvest, all of them. Deston at the end, with his hands in his lap. Gabe in the middle, unable to keep still. Rachael with her arms folded. Calvin beside her, watching the crowd instead of the ring, out of habit. Sheng Yang a little apart, silent, the way he always was before anything. Dennis. Suzu. Kotori wasn't there. Kotori was in the Hart's kitchen, making tea.

And Xav was on the very edge of the ring, cross-legged on one of the ordinary grey stones between two dark ones, with a sketchbook on his knee and a pencil behind his ear.

Ice came out of the Hall at twenty past eight.

He walked down the steps, past them, and touched Deston on the shoulder as he went by. Just a touch. Deston put his hand up and touched his back, and that was all. Then Ice walked out across the market. The crowd moved for him without being asked, the way it had at Elmcross. He stepped over one of the dark stones, into the ring.

On the other side, a woman was already waiting.

She was small and dark, in grey leather, with two short knives at her hips. She looked nervous. She looked as if she'd been nervous for a week and had decided to stop, and hadn't quite managed it.

"That's her," said Suzu, under his breath. "The one from Lyon."

"Which one from Lyon?" said Gabe.

"She wrote a page. About the held draw." Suzu watched her. "She said she didn't want to win. She just wanted to know."

Gabe looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, very softly, for Gabe: "Oh. Poor thing."

The third was on Monday, and it was the first time Suzu thought he might be about to see Ice hit by something.

She was from Poland. Long Table, another of Xav's hungry guilds from a long time ago. Her name was Gosia and she was forty-one and she made fire. Proper fire. Suzu had seen Ina make fire, and Val, and Ren in VANTA. This was different. She'd done something to the fire classes on the forum, a long post with diagrams. She'd shown how fast you could get the word out if you put the gesture *first*, before the breath, instead of after. Her fire was the fastest on the staff tab by a quarter of a second.

"He can't walk at fire," said Rachael, on the steps. She had her arms folded and her healer's face on, the one that meant *someone's going to be an idiot and I'll have to fix it*. "Fire doesn't care where you're standing. It goes where she points."

"He knows," said Calvin.

"Then what's he going to *do*?"

Nobody answered. Nobody knew.

Suzu found him afterwards, at the third dawn, at midnight, with the first grey light coming over the roofs. He was sweeping the ring.

The market was empty again. The chestnut man had gone home and the stalls were shuttered. Ice was out on the paving with Ama Corrie's fen-reed broom, the one with *KEPT* on the tag. He went slowly round the ring from dark stone to dark stone. Under, not round, if you could sweep under a flagstone. Chestnut shells. A dropped ribbon. The scorch from Monday's fire, which wouldn't come up.

Suzu went and stood at the edge.

"Four," he said.

"Four," said Ice.

"None of them hit you. Except the fire. Which you let."

"Haneul did. The butt. Three times on the rim." Ice swept. "It counts."

"It doesn't count."

"It counts to him," said Ice. "He'll want it to have counted."

Suzu watched him sweep. He'd fought beside Ice for ten months. He'd stood behind him in the drowned church and the Long Watch and the Elm Below. He'd never once, in ten months, watched him from the front, from where the other person stood. Now he'd done it four nights running. He understood something he hadn't understood before. Something Kestrel had understood on the oak floor at Elmcross with her arm shaking.

It wasn't that Ice was fast. He wasn't, especially. Sheng Yang was faster. Xav was much faster. It wasn't that he was strong, or that he had the best shield, or the highest level. He'd been lower than all four of them.

It was that he was never once, in any of it, *second*.

"Does it feel like anything?" said Suzu. "Going first. Every time."

Ice stopped sweeping. He leaned on the broom and thought about it for a long time.

"It feels like being early for something," he said at last. "Like when you get to the station before the bus. And you stand there, and you know it's coming, and you know where it'll stop." He looked at the ring, at the nine dark stones in the grey light. "And then it does."

Suzu thought about a bus stop in Singapore. And a woman who read the number of a bus from the stop, before it got there.

"That's a good feeling," he said.

"Yes," said Ice. "I'd forgotten it was." And he went back to sweeping, slowly, round the ring.

Three more.
