In the Netherlands it was five to one in the afternoon, grey, and Milli ate a sandwich standing up in the kitchen because he knew he wouldn't eat again for twelve hours.

He did the sum while he ate. He always did the sum. Twelve hours in the egg, from one to one. In the game, the dark would fall at twenty past. At midnight in Malaysia, seven in the evening here, the dawn wouldn't come. At eight in the morning in Malaysia, one in the morning here, it would, and the egg would throw him out at the same moment. Not a minute before.

He'd watched the last one. A year ago. On Hana's phone, in a flat in Singapore, two feet away from her, in December, in the two months. She'd been on hiatus. She'd had her egg under a sheet. They'd watched Ice stand on a plank all night in a fen, and at dawn, give it back. And Hana had cried, and Milli had held her, and thought: *I'm glad that's not us*.

He put his plate in the sink. He went into the spare room, where the egg sat in the corner with its lid up. He'd done the sum for it on a balcony in December; it had come in March, slowly, the way things came to the Netherlands. He lay down in it.

*Same place. Same town. Same room.* He'd said that in December, on the balcony, at two in the morning. It had been true for nine months. He'd stood in the same room as her every night across eleven thousand kilometres.

It was going to be true tonight too.

The lid came down.

***

He came out of the dark into the banner room at Carrow Cross, in his chair by the window, next to Hana's. She was already there. She'd come in at the same minute, from Singapore, where it was five to eight in the evening. She was looking at him.

"Hi," said Hana.

"Hi."

"It's tonight."

"I know." He looked at her. She had her pick across her knees and her hand in her pocket, round the star. She always had her hand round the star now, the gold one from the last little guy in the wall. "Are you all right?"

"No," said Hana. "Yes. I don't know." She laughed, a bit wobbly. "Jared's doing the lid."

He was. Milli could see him through the window: in the round market, in the dusk, at the well. The new well, the one they'd dug the year Ama Corrie's grandmother stopped using the settling ring. A round stone well with a wooden roof over it on four posts, by the east side of the fountain. Jared was standing over it with a hammer and a mouthful of nails and a great flat lid of oak, three planks wide. He'd made it on Sunday at Joss's bench, measured to a hair. He was nailing it down.

"He says it's still water," said Hana. "In the well. Not like the fountain. The fountain runs. The well just sits." She swallowed. "They come up out of still water."

"I know."

"Jared says if they want to come up out of it, they'll have to get through his lid first." She laughed again. "He says nothing gets through a lid he's made. He says ask anyone. He says ask the *roof* in Quarrymouth."

Milli looked at her. At her hand in her pocket, round the star.

"Come on," he said. "Let's go and stand on his lid."

***

The round market at dusk on the longest night was the most beautiful thing Milli had ever seen, and he didn't say so, because there wasn't time.

There were candles in every window. A hundred and ninety-one, Kei had said. Every window that faced into the market or onto one of the seven roads, all round the round town, the whole ring of it. A little yellow flame behind every pane, steady, in the blue of the evening. They weren't bright. They weren't meant to be bright. They were Tallow Green's candles, from Mother Dipp's sack. Behind each one, in the windows, if you looked, there were faces. The people of the round town, in their houses, looking out. Old Joss in his smithy window with his shaking hands round his. A family above the baker's, three children's faces in a row. A woman alone in the last cottage on the north road, who'd put her own candle there with her own hands.

*They're not lures*, Chernn had said. *They're just company.*

And at the north end of the market, at the north door of the Hall of Roads, the lure.

The smiths had built it on Sunday. A great iron basket on a tripod, taller than Salty. It stood in the north door, half in and half out, with its back to the Hall and its face to the north road. Ina had filled it with Joss's coal and the black iron offcuts that burned hotter than anything. And at a quarter to eight, with everyone watching, he'd put his mask on and said his word.

*Kindle.*

It roared.

It roared like Emberly's great forge on the hill, Ina said, and he'd know. A great column of gold and white fire in the north door of the Hall, so bright you couldn't look at it. So hot you could feel it from the fountain. It lit the north road for a quarter of a mile, and the north side of the market, and the underside of the dome. Gabe's three were on the north steps to feed it all night: Gabe, Rachael, Calvin, with sacks of coal. In front of it, in the door, two shields wide, the wall. Thea's three on the left. Salty's in the middle with a black sword the length of a man. Azac's on the right with the biggest shield in the guild.

Milli looked at it from the well, for a moment, with the heat on his face.

Then he looked at the windows again. The little ones. The company.

"Okay," said Hana, beside him, very quietly. "Okay."

Jared stood on his lid with his beam over his shoulder, the one he'd brought all the way from Quarrymouth, and grinned at both of them.

"Y'all ready?" said Jared.

***

At twenty past eight the dark came down.

It came the way dusk came every night, except it didn't stop. The blue went out of the sky and the stars came out, a few of them, and then they went in again, one by one, as if somebody were turning them off. The air went cold. Not the cold of night; a different cold. The cold Milli had felt once on a ledge in Quarrymouth when a door in the rock opened onto a tunnel nobody had been in for three hundred years.

And all over the round town, every traveller stopped at the same moment, the way they did, and looked at nothing.

"Four hundred and fourteen," said Milli. He did the sum without meaning to. A hundred and ninety-one windows. Thirty in the Hall. Eleven in the Hart. The forge. The fire in the north door. The eye. And the rest: lanterns on posts, Ama Corrie's lamp, a few dozen he couldn't place. "That's all of them."

"Keep them," said Hana.

"That's the idea."

And from high above them, two hundred and twelve steps up, at the top of the dome where the eye was, a voice came down. Not Hyvred's flat carrying one, the way it had come down on every big night since July. A different one. John's. Confident, quick, the way he'd tell you anything.

"*The edge*," called John. "*The twenty-two. They're going out.*"

***

He couldn't see the road of lights. Nobody could, from here. It faced north. But from the eye, Higu could send her Lantern up it and John could send his Glint, and they could see. And they told the whole round town, out loud, from the top of the dome, every few minutes, all night.

"*Twenty-two's out. Twenty-one. Twenty. They're coming down the line.*"

Milli counted with them. He couldn't help it. He stood on Jared's lid by the well with his skinning knife in his hand and counted the lamps going out a hundred miles away. Down a line of stones he'd never seen, every fifty paces, toward a ruined hall with a birch growing through the floor.

"*The twenty-two are out. All of them.*"

A pause.

"*The light's still there*," called Higu. Softer, but it carried. "*Past the last stone. The carried one. It's still there. It's not gone out.*"

Hana made a small sound beside him.

"*The old hall*," called John. "*They're at the old hall. They're— oh.*" A breath. "*They're not stopping. They're going round it. The road. They've found the road.*"

"*Ninety-four's out*," called Higu.

Milli stopped counting the twenty-two and started counting the ninety-four.

***

The first one came up out of the well at nine minutes to ten.

He felt it before he saw it. A pressure under his boots, through Jared's oak lid. Like something very big breathing in, under water. Jared felt it too. He stopped humming. He'd been humming since half past eight, something country, under his breath.

"Oh," said Jared. "Hey, now."

The lid moved.

Not much. A quarter of an inch, up, all of it at once, as if something underneath had put its shoulder to it. Then down again. The nails held. Jared stood on it with his feet wide and his beam in both hands.

"Nothing gets through my lid," said Jared, to the lid. Very firmly. "*Nothing.*"

It came again. Harder. The lid lifted half an inch, and a thin line of black came out round the edge, like smoke, like ink in water. It was cold. It was so cold Milli felt it in his teeth. And it didn't go up. It went sideways, along the ground, toward the nearest light.

Toward the baker's window, ten paces off, with three children's faces behind it.

"No," said Hana.

She took the star out of her pocket.

She'd found it in the tunnel under Quarrymouth, at the end of the wall, in the last little guy's banner. *The Bearer's Star. It was set into the last of them.* That was all the window had said. She hadn't known what it was for. She'd kept it in her pocket ever since, her hand round it, and never once held it up.

She held it up now.

It lit.

Gold. He hadn't known it could. Brighter than a candle, brighter than a lantern. Not as bright as the fire in the north door, nothing was. But here, by the well, at ten paces from the baker's window, it was the brightest thing there was. And the thin black line coming out from under Jared's lid stopped going toward the window.

It leaned.

Toward Hana.

"Oh," said Hana. "Oh. Oh, that's what it's—"

"*For*," said Milli. "Yes."

*The last little guy had a banner, not a light,* she'd told the long table, in August. *And the star was in the banner.* Three hundred and forty-two little stone figures in a wall, walking north, every one holding up a light. And the last one holding up a banner with a star on it. Carrying the lights north. Setting them out behind.

*They're carrying them*, she'd said.

She was carrying one. She was standing by a well in a market, holding it up. So the dark would come to her and not to a window with three children behind it.

Milli felt something in his chest go very cold and very calm at the same time.

"Okay," he said. "Jared. Let it up."

Jared looked at him.

"It's going to come up anyway," said Milli. "It's going to come up round the edge, a bit at a time, like that. And go along the ground. Better it comes up all at once, here, where we are. And goes to her. And we're standing round her." He looked at Hana, with the star held up in her hand and the gold light on her face. "Than a bit at a time round the edge, where we're not looking."

Jared looked at the lid. At his nails.

"I'm *real* proud of that lid," he said.

"I know."

"Okay." Jared stepped off it. He took his beam in both hands. "Okay, okay. Alice. Hold it up high."

Hana held it up high.

And the lid came off the well like a cork.

***

He'd tell it afterwards, to Hana, on a call, at one in the morning in the Netherlands. He'd tell it the way he told sums: in order, carefully, so nothing got missed. He'd never tell it to anybody else. It was theirs.

They came up out of the well all night.

Not all at once. In threes and fours and tens. Tall thin ones, like the ones Hako had seen on the moor. Flat low ones, like ink otters, the kind Hako said had come up out of the river at Otterbank. Things with no shape at all, just a coldness moving along the ground. They all came up out of the black water in the round stone well. And they all leaned the same way.

To Hana.

She stood with her back to the fountain, which ran, so nothing came up out of it, and held the star up high. Jared stood on her left with his beam. Milli stood on her right with his knife. That was the shape of it, all night: the three of them in a little triangle with Hana at the point, and the well in front of them, and the baker's window behind.

The ones that came, came to her. And to get to her, they had to come past Jared's beam or Milli's knife.

Jared didn't fight like anybody Milli had ever seen. He didn't swing the beam. He *held* it, the way he held up roofs. Across, flat, at the height of a man's chest. When something came at it, he just stood there and let it hit the beam, and the beam didn't move, and the thing fell back. *Nothing gets through a roof I'm holding.* He'd said it in Quarrymouth in the tunnels, a hundred times. It turned out to be true of more than roofs.

Milli used the knife the way he used it on a rabbit. One cut, in the right place, no wasted movement. The skinning knife was sharp enough for Hana's fine work on the wall, sharper than anything she could make. The Unlit had places, it turned out, like everything did. Places where they were thinner, where they bent. He found them the way he'd find the joint on a leg. Calmly. Doing the sum. And when he found one, it came apart, and went back down into the dark like smoke into a chimney.

It didn't move. All night, that number didn't move, at the fountain end of the market.

***

At half past eleven, a window went out.

Not theirs. Not the baker's. Across the market, on the north-west side, by the road where Joy and Tobi and Newish were. A house with a blue door. Milli saw it go out from the fountain. One moment a little yellow flame in a window, a face behind it. The next, a dark square.

[ Carrow Cross · Lights: 413 ]

Something had come up somewhere they hadn't thought of. A rain barrel, probably, a water butt by a house wall. Still water. Something had come up out of it and gone to the window and folded over the candle like a hand.

Milli looked at it. Every part of him wanted to go. It was forty paces. He could run it in five seconds. He could relight it himself; he had a flint in his pack. There was a face behind that window, in the dark now, on the longest night. The one thing the candles were for was so nobody would be sitting in the dark, and somebody was.

He didn't go.

*Nobody alone.* If he went, there'd be two at the well, not three. And whatever came up next would come up into a triangle with one side missing.

He looked up instead. At the eye, two hundred and twelve steps up.

"*Eye*," called Milli. He didn't have a carrying voice. He tried anyway. "*North-west side. Blue door. A window's out.*"

There was a pause. Then John's voice came down, quick, the way scouts were.

"*Got it. Xav. Blue door, north-west. Your side.*"

And across the market, under the eye, on the north side, Milli saw three people move. Xav first, with his two blades, fast, the way he was fast. Malcolm behind him with his stick. Val behind both of them with her wand up. The three of them went across the north side of the market together, as one, and round the corner to the blue door.

A flicker, there. A little gold light from Val's wand, a word. And the window lit again.

[ Carrow Cross · Lights: 414 ]

"*Lit*," called John, from the eye. "*Blue door's lit. Thank you, fountain.*"

Milli let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

"That's how it works," said Hana, beside him, without lowering the star. "Isn't it. You don't go. You tell the eye. And the eye tells somebody. And somebody comes."

"That's how it works," said Milli.

***

At midnight the dawn didn't come.

He'd known it wouldn't. He'd done the sum at the kitchen sink with a sandwich. At midnight in Malaysia, seven in the evening in the Netherlands, the third dawn should come up over the east road, the way it did every night. Grey, then gold. It didn't. The sky over the round market stayed black. The stars stayed out, the few that were left. The cold went on.

[ The dark has not lifted. ]

All over the round town, every traveller looked at nothing for a moment. And then went back to what they'd been doing, a little more tightly.

Hana was still holding up the star. She'd been holding it up for two hours and nine minutes. Her arm was shaking. It had been shaking for an hour. She hadn't lowered it.

"Swap," said Milli.

She looked at him.

"Your arm," said Milli. "Swap. I'll hold it."

"It's mine," said Hana. "It came to me. In the wall."

"I know. I'll hold it for a bit. Then you have it back." He held out his hand, the one without the knife. "That's how it works. Isn't it."

She looked at his hand for a long moment. Then at the star, gold in her fist, with the cold black well in front of her and the baker's window behind.

She gave it to him.

It was warm. He hadn't expected that. It was warm the way a hand is warm, and it lit, in his fingers, gold, the same as in hers. And the next thing that came up out of the well, a tall thin one swaying like a reed, leaned toward him.

He held it up high.

Hana took the knife out of his other hand, gently, and stood where he'd been standing. On the right, with the knife, at the side of the triangle. She held it the way he'd taught her, on the wall in Quarrymouth, for the fine work.

"*One day*," said Hana, very quietly, not looking at him. Watching the well. "*Somewhere with a view.*"

Milli looked up. Past the well and the fountain and the market. At the ring of a hundred and ninety-one candles in a hundred and ninety-one windows, all round the round town, in the dark. At the faces behind them. At the great roaring gold of the fire in the north door, and the wall in front of it, two shields wide. At the eye at the top of the dome, where three people were looking north up a road nobody else could see, telling them all how far it had come.

It was the longest night. It was the coldest night. It was dark, and there were things coming up out of the well. There was a tide coming down the north road a hundred miles long. And it was, he thought, with great calm, quite clearly the best view he'd ever had in his life.

"This is somewhere," said Milli.

Hana laughed. Out loud, by the well, with his knife in her hand, at seven minutes past midnight on the longest night. It was the first time she'd laughed since twenty past eight. Behind them, in the baker's window, three faces in a row laughed too, because she had, without knowing why.

"*Seventy*," called John, from the eye. "*Seventy lamps left on the road. They're coming fast.*"

Jared hefted his beam.

"Y'all," he said, "this is the best night of my *life*."
