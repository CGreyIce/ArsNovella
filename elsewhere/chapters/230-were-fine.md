Vane didn't say things. He'd stopped a long time ago.

It wasn't a decision. Nobody decides that. It was more that he'd found, somewhere in his first year in a phone game with a stupid name, that most things didn't need saying. If somebody was going to do a thing, they'd do it. If something was going to be fine, it would be, and talking about it wouldn't help. And if it wasn't going to be fine, you didn't talk. You made it fine. Quietly, while everybody else was talking.

So he didn't say things. He made sure they were.

*We're fine*, he wrote, in letters, when people asked. It was the most he ever said. It meant *I've done it already. You don't have to worry. I've already made it so.*

He'd written it to the GM once, in April, a long letter to somebody who never wrote long letters. *We're fine.* And the GM had read it, Vane heard later, several times.

***

He'd stood on the steps off the rim for twenty-seven minutes and watched the GM fight Lucan on a bridge.

He wasn't going to tell anybody about it. Not the fight. Everybody had seen the fight. Three hundred thousand on the stream and half of Masmark on the terrace and the banks and the roofs. Tif's clip by the morning, on a loop, a million times. They'd all tell it, all of them, for years. They didn't need Vane to.

He'd tell one thing. Only to himself. One moment, out of twenty-seven minutes.

The rain.

At ten minutes, when the river stopped and the GM came forward into the place where Lucan was going and didn't stop. Every beat. Sword, rim, step. No rests. Like rain on a tin roof. The crowd screaming, and Harlan beside him with both hands over his mouth saying *oh God, oh God, oh God*.

And Vane, on the steps, very still, thinking: *I know that*.

He'd known it for two years, in a war, a long time ago. Before the GM learned to wait.

***

It hadn't looked like that, in the phone game. Nothing looked like anything in the phone game. You tapped. You tapped to walk and you tapped to fight and the fights were numbers going up and down on a screen. And a lot of people thought that meant there was nothing to it.

There was. The tempo.

That was the GM. That was the whole of him, in the war. Not the numbers. When. He attacked people before they'd logged in. He attacked them while they were deciding whether to attack him. He attacked them at three in the morning and at noon and at six in the evening and every hour between. Not because he was angry. Because he never stopped. He found whatever a guild was waiting for, and went first. Every time. Every hour. Until they stopped waiting, and ran, or joined.

Vane had been his vice.

He didn't talk about that part either. Twenty people who liked fighting, in a guild with a stupid name, and the GM at the front of all of them, going first. And Vane, the one who didn't say things. Making sure they were fine.

***

He'd seen the rain every day for two years.

He'd never once seen the river.

He stood on the steps off the rim at the start of the fight and watched the GM move like Lucan. A breath ahead of Lucan, round and round, on the old stone bridge. And thought: *I don't know that*. He'd never seen it. Not in the war, not on any VOD since.

And he'd never seen the third one.

The slow one. At eighteen minutes, when the rain stopped and the GM went down into the stone and the sword came round from the floor. Heavy, obvious, the whole way, and Lucan in the air couldn't do anything. *BOOM*, across the bridge, into the parapet.

Vane had never seen that in his life.

"*Where did that come from*," Harlan had said, beside him. Not a question. A noise.

Vane knew. He'd been in the netloft on Sunday morning, at the door, talking to the girl with the two different eyes. And he'd seen the GM come out of the netloft's trapdoor with the big quiet one with the black sword. The big one's face doing something, and the GM's doing nothing at all. And on Tuesday, walking past the netloft at the third dawn, he'd heard it. Through the boards. From under the floor.

*Splash.* Silence. *BOOM.*

He hadn't known what it was. He knew now.

"He borrowed it," said Vane.

Harlan stared at him.

"From the big one," said Vane. "At the netloft door. With the black sword." He looked at the bridge. "He went down into the cellar and asked him."

"*Asked* him."

"Yes."

Harlan looked back at the bridge, where the GM was standing in the middle with his sword at Lucan's neck. Thirty-four per cent. Breathing hard.

"He never asked anybody anything," said Harlan. "In the war. Not once. Not *anybody*."

"No," said Vane.

***

Aurelie didn't say anything on the walk back along the west rim. Vane walked beside her. He always walked beside her. She was very small and the rim was very crowded tonight, everybody going home talking. And she walked through the crowd the way she walked through everything, as if it would move for her, which it did.

At the castle gate she stopped.

"Six years," she said.

Vane waited.

"You've been telling me about him for six years," said Aurelie. She was looking at the gate, not at him. "Since the old game. *There was a GM. He went first. Every time. He never stopped. He was the best I ever saw.*" She paused. "I thought half of it was you being fond of him."

"You said."

"I said it in February, on the harbour wall, after I'd watched him for a week. And I was wrong then too. It wasn't half." She looked up at him. "You never told me about the river. Or the slow one."

"I never saw them," said Vane.

Aurelie looked at him for a long time.

"Six seconds," she said. "At the bell. He stood in front of a bell that rang every eight seconds, and you said *he'll take the tempo off it*. You knew he would." She folded her arms. "And tonight he did a thing you'd never seen. Two things."

"Yes."

"How does that feel?"

Vane thought about it. He didn't usually. He made things fine; he didn't think about how they felt. But she'd asked. She didn't ask much either. That was one of the reasons he'd stayed six years.

"Good," he said.

Aurelie's face did something.

"*Good*," she said. "You sound like him."

"I've known him longer than you have."

"Yes," said Aurelie, and almost laughed. "You have." She looked back along the west rim, at the north terrace going away in the dark, where the crowd was still coming off it. "Go and see him."

Vane looked at her.

"You haven't said anything to him," said Aurelie. "Since February. *You came. You asked. I don't ask things.* And a letter. Two words." She unfolded her arms. "Go and say something."

***

He found the GM on the harbour wall at the third dawn.

Of course he did. He'd found him there in February. The harbour wall at the bottom of the north-west road, by the fish man's cart. The fish man was back, with his awning up, though it was the middle of the night, and he was sitting on his stool doing nothing. The GM was sitting on the wall with his feet hanging over the water. Not with a fish this time. With a cup.

He was on his own.

Vane stopped a few paces off.

The GM looked round. He'd heard him coming, the way he always heard everybody coming. He didn't say anything. He looked at Vane for a long time, from the wall, in the lamplight. With his face doing nothing at all.

"Hyv's out," he said. "She logged out after. She was crying." He looked at the cup. "The good kind. Bee says."

Vane sat down on the wall beside him. Not too close. The way he'd sat in February.

They didn't say anything for a long time.

***

"The river," said Vane, at last.

The GM looked at him.

"I never saw it," said Vane. "In the war. Two years. Every day. I saw the rain." He looked at the water. "Not the river. Not the slow one."

"I didn't have them," said the GM. "In the war."

"No."

"The river's his," said the GM. "Lucan's. I watched him on the bridge. Every night in July, on the way back from the Undercliffs. Twenty people doing the cellar at him, and him going round it." He turned the cup in his hands. "I learned it off him. While he was learning the rain off his own." He paused. "He doesn't know that."

"He'll work it out."

"Yes," said the GM. "He's very good."

"And the slow one's the big one's."

"Salty's," said the GM. "Yes."

Vane looked at him.

"You asked him," said Vane.

"I asked him."

"In the war," said Vane, "you never asked anybody anything. Harlan said. Tonight. On the steps. *Not once. Not anybody.*"

The GM didn't say anything for a long time.

"I know," he said.

***

The fish man, on his stool by the cart, got up and went round the back of the cart and did something with the ice. Not because it needed doing. Vane could see that. Because the fish man had been at the bottom of that road three hundred years and knew when two people on the harbour wall needed him to be round the back of his cart.

Vane looked at the water.

He'd been going to say something. Aurelie had told him to. He'd walked down the west rim and round the north-west road and onto the harbour wall at the third dawn to say it. And now he was here, and he didn't know what it was.

He'd stopped saying things a long time ago. He'd forgotten how.

"Was it lonely?" said Vane.

It came out of him the way things came out of the GM. Plain, short, before he'd decided.

The GM went very still.

"The war," said Vane. "Two years. Every hour. Everybody at the front." He didn't look at him. "Was it lonely?"

He'd never asked. In two years. Not once. He'd been the vice; he'd made sure they were fine. He'd kept the twenty together while the GM went at everybody. He'd never once asked the GM how he was.

He hadn't thought he needed to. The GM was the GM. He went first.

"Yes," said the GM.

***

Vane sat on the harbour wall and looked at the water and didn't say anything.

"I told Hyv," said the GM. Quietly. "In May. On this wall. She asked what *before* meant. I told her about the war. I said I stopped because I was tired, and because it was lonely." He turned the cup. "Every one of them was at the front. That's what the war was. Everybody at the front, going at everybody. There wasn't a next to." He paused. "Nobody ever asked who'd stand next to me."

"I was next to you," said Vane.

The GM turned his head.

Vane didn't look at him. He looked at the water. He didn't know what his face was doing. It never did anything, usually. He could feel it doing something now.

"The whole war," said Vane. "Two years. I wasn't at the front. I was never at the front. I was the one behind you making sure the twenty were fine while you went at everybody." He swallowed. "I was next to you. Every day. The whole time."

The harbour wall was very quiet. The fish man, round the back of his cart, had stopped doing anything with the ice.

"I never said," said Vane.

***

The GM didn't say anything for a very long time.

Vane didn't look at him. He couldn't. He'd said it now. Two years in a war and four years after and a harbour wall in February and a letter in April. *We're fine.* And now he'd said the thing under all of it, the thing he'd made fine for six years so that he'd never have to say it.

"I didn't see it," said the GM, at last.

"No."

"You were behind me."

"Yes."

"I never turned round," said the GM. His voice was very odd. Vane had never heard it like that. Not in the war, not on the harbour wall in February, not on the bridge tonight with a sword at Lucan's neck. "I was always facing the front. Like the heron. I never once turned round to look at what was behind me." He was quiet. "I turned round in April. In the round town. With a broom. I looked at everybody. All sixty-one." He stopped. "I never turned round in the war."

"No," said Vane.

"You were there."

"I was there."

The GM put his cup down on the harbour wall. Without looking where it went. Vane had never seen him do that either.

"Thank you," said the GM.

Vane looked at him, then. He couldn't help it.

The GM was looking at him. Properly. On the harbour wall at the third dawn, with the lamp from the fish man's cart on his face. Not doing nothing. Doing something. The thing Harlan said it did now, since the gate, since the woman with the gold horns. Since he'd started letting people see it.

"I'm sorry I didn't turn round," said the GM.

"I didn't need you to," said Vane. "That's what a vice is for. You go first. I make sure we're fine." He looked back at the water. His eyes were doing something. He let them. "But I'm glad you asked."

***

They sat there until the first dawn.

Not saying anything. The way they'd sat in February. But it wasn't the same. Vane could feel it wasn't. In February he'd sat on the wall beside somebody he'd known in a war, a long time ago, who'd gone somewhere else. Tonight he was sitting beside somebody who'd turned round.

The fish man came back round the front of his cart at some point, and sat down on his stool, and didn't look at them.

At the first dawn, when the light came up gold off the lake and the fishing boats started coming in through the gap between the moles, the GM picked up his cup again. Empty. He looked at it.

"Are you fine?" he said.

Vane looked at him.

It was the first time anybody had ever asked him that. In the war, after it, in six years of Meridian. People asked *are we fine*, and he said *we're fine*, and made it so. Nobody had ever asked if *he* was.

He thought about it. He wanted to be honest. He'd just said the most honest thing he'd ever said, on a harbour wall, to a man who'd never once turned round. He didn't want to stop now.

"Yes," said Vane. "Now."

The GM nodded. Once.

"Good," he said.

***

Harlan found them at the second dawn, coming down the north-west road at a run with his great two-bladed axe on his back. Shouting before he could see them.

"*GM! GM, the clip's at two million, Tif called it* LOOK, *she called it* LOOK *in capitals, it's just you smiling, the bit where you smiled at him, on a loop, for ten seconds, over and over, and the comments are all just people saying* he SMILED *and*—"

He stopped.

He'd come round the bottom of the road onto the harbour and seen them on the wall. The GM and Vane, side by side. Not too close. With the light coming up off the lake. And Vane's face doing something Harlan had never seen it do, in six years.

"*Oh*," said Harlan.

"Harlan," said Vane.

"*Vane*. Are you— is your face—"

"We're fine," said Vane.

Harlan looked at him for a long time. Then at the GM. Then at the fish man, on his stool, who looked back at him with his boot face and said nothing at all.

"*Oh*," said Harlan again, much more quietly.

He sat down on the harbour wall on the GM's other side. Close. Much too close; Harlan was always too close. He put his great two-bladed axe down on the stones beside him. And for once in his life, in the war or after it, he didn't say anything at all.

The three of them sat on the harbour wall at the bottom of the north-west road, in the morning. Three of the twenty, from a phone game with a stupid name. Watching the fishing boats come in.

***

Aurelie was in the castle's great hall when he got back, at the end of the long table, too small for her chair. She looked up when he came in. She looked at his face.

"You said something," she said.

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

Vane thought about it.

"*I was next to you*," he said. "And he said *thank you*." He paused. "And then he asked if I was fine."

Aurelie looked at him for a long time across two thousand empty chairs.

"Are you?" she said.

"Yes," said Vane. "I said *now*."

She didn't say anything. She put her hand flat on the table in front of her, the way she did when she'd decided something.

"I said I'd ask their Kei something," said Aurelie. "Properly. In August, when they're home. In the round town." She looked at the window, where Masmark was going gold in the morning, all the way round the lake. "They're going home on Monday. All of them but the netloft's eight and the two at the door."

"Yes."

"Fourteen days," said Aurelie. "To the round town."

Vane looked at her.

"I'm going to walk it," said Aurelie. "In September. On my own feet. To their gate, with the girl who asks the question. Or the boy who used to be a bird." She looked at him. "And I'm going to stand at the bottom of their steps and answer it. And then ask him."

"Ask him what?"

"Whether he'd stand next to us," said Aurelie. "Not under. Not inside. *Next to*." She almost smiled. "He asked me that, at the mouth of the Undercut. In a letter. *Would you stand next to us?* I said yes in eleven minutes." She looked at Vane. "I'd like to ask him back. At his own door. The way you're supposed to."

Vane didn't say anything for a long time.

"You'll need somebody to walk with you," he said. "Kei says nobody alone."

"I know," said Aurelie. "Will you?"

It was the second time in one night that somebody had asked him something.

"Yes," said Vane.

He didn't say *we're fine*. He didn't need to. She could see it on his face.
