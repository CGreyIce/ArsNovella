Curse had only ever written what things were.

That was the job. Odette had taught him, in Merrowgate, on the footbridge, with a brush behind each ear. *A label makes a thing remember what it is*. You looked at a thing until you knew what it was, and then you wrote it, plainly, where it could see. And it remembered. A crate that said *NAILS* stayed nails. A door that said *HOME* stayed home. A banner that said *Constellation (ours)* in blue along the bottom edge answered when it was hung, because it knew whose it was.

He'd written hundreds. Thousands, maybe. On cages and crates and doors and the bottoms of cups. He'd written *Somewhere* under a scar on a bench made of a pole that held. He'd written *Next* on the inside of Leaka's wrist in a banner room a year ago, and it was still there, faint. He'd never once written something that wasn't true yet.

He was sitting by Maigo's trestle tables in the round market on the first morning of the year, wet to the skin, with a lion's back end in a heap at his feet, when Hana came and asked him to.

***

"Red paper," said Hana.

She had a roll of it under her arm. Deep red, the dyer's best, from Spoke Street. She put it on the trestle table in front of him, beside the remains of Maigo's noodles.

"For the doors," said Hana. "Out there, on the first day, you put words on the doors. On red paper. For the year." She looked at him. "Last year we didn't. Nobody could write them. We had the lanterns, and the lion went in the fountain, and nobody thought." She paused. "This year you're here. And you learned the lion's word."

"I learned one word," said Curse. "Three lines across and one down."

"So learn another one."

He looked at the red paper. Then at Hana. She had her hands in her pockets and the look she got when she'd thought about something for a long time and decided it.

"What word?" said Curse.

Hana told him.

He didn't know it. Of course he didn't. It had never been his. She drew it for him with her finger in the spilt tea on the table, and he looked at it, and it was nothing. Lines. More of them than the lion's word, crowded together, a whole small city of them.

"I can't read it," said Curse.

"I know."

"If I can't read it, I can't write it. It won't know what it is."

"Ask him," said Hana. And looked across the market, at the fountain. Ice was on the rim of it, still, with his shield across his knees. Hyv was on his knee, with her arm round his neck, as though the knee were hers and always had been. "He'll know what it is."

***

He knew what it was.

They sat at the long table in the banner room, because the market was too wet. Ice at one end, with a brush and a saucer of ink. Hyv beside him, or on him; Curse had stopped being able to tell. Nobody remarked on it any more, except Gabe, who still screamed about once an hour on principle. And Curse across from them with his inkwell and a stack of plain paper for practising, and the roll of red waiting at the end of the table.

Ice wrote it once, slowly, so Curse could watch.

"It's two halves," said Ice. "The left side's the small one. Dot, then this, then down, then dot." Four strokes. A thin upright thing, like a person standing very straight with something in their hand. "That side's for asking. For things you can't see."

"And the right?"

"The right's three things on top of each other," said Ice. He did them as he said them. A line across, at the top. Then a small box under it. Then a bigger box under that, with a cross in it. "One. A mouth. A field." He put the brush down. "One mouth, and a field to feed it."

Curse looked at it for a long time.

"Enough to eat," said Curse.

"Yes."

"Under a roof." He pointed at the line across the top. "That's the roof."

Ice looked at it. Then at Curse.

"Nobody ever told me that one," said Ice. "But yes. If you like."

"What does it mean?" said Curse. "All of it."

Ice thought about it. He did that. You asked him something and he thought about it properly before he answered, even when it was simple. Leaka said it was the most annoying thing about him. Curse thought it was the best.

"Luck," said Ice. "But not the kind you win. The kind where there's enough. Where everybody at the table's fed and nobody's out in the cold. And the field's still there next year." He looked at the word. "That kind."

***

Curse wrote it two hundred and twelve times.

He didn't mean to count. He just did, the way you counted steps. On the plain paper, over and over, at the long table, all through the first morning. Ice sat across from him and watched, and said *the dot's too far* or *the field's too big* or, once, *that's it*, very quietly. And then, the next time, *no, that was it, that one isn't*. Hyv watched too. She could see the difference between two of them at a glance, from across the table, upside down. She'd say *left one* before Ice had looked.

"She's better at this than you," said Curse.

"She's better at everything than me," said Ice.

"*Yes*," said Hyv, and kissed him on the ear, and went back to watching.

Gabe, at the far end of the long table, made a noise like a kettle.

At the two hundred and twelfth, Curse put the brush down and looked at it.

It wasn't perfect. It was his. The left side standing up straight with something in its hand. The roof. The mouth. The field. Enough, under a roof, for the year.

But it didn't remember anything. No window came. No *it remembers*. Just ink.

"It's not working," said Curse.

Ice looked at it.

"It's right," said Ice. "It's a good one."

"It's right, and it's not *working*." Curse turned the paper round, so Ice could see it the right way up. "When I write *NAILS* on a crate, the crate remembers it's nails. Because it *is* nails. I look at it until I know what it is and then I write it, and it's true." He pointed at the word. "This isn't true. This door isn't lucky. That field isn't full. Nobody's promised anybody enough." He sat back. "You can't label a thing as something it isn't. It doesn't take."

Ice was quiet for a long time.

"It's not a label," he said.

Curse looked up.

"It's a wish," said Ice. "A label says what a thing is. A wish says what you want it to be." He turned the paper back round, towards Curse. "You've only ever written labels. That's why it won't take. You're trying to make the door remember something it's never been."

"Then how do you write a wish?"

Ice thought about it.

"You mean it," he said. "And then you wait."

***

He wrote them on the red paper in the afternoon. Forty-one doors.

Every door the guild had, and some it didn't. The south door of the Hall of Roads. The banner room door. The north door. The door at the bottom of the two hundred and twelve. The Hart, front and back. Joss's smithy, where Ina bent stars. The cookshop on Spoke Street where Xola worked the fires. The dyer's, who'd sold them the paper and then wanted one. The chestnut man's shed. A small house at the end of Spoke Street with a blue door, where an old woman lived alone who'd once given Leaka an apple. Leaka had insisted.

He wrote each one at the long table, on a square of red, in black ink. He didn't hurry. He didn't think about what the door was. For the first time in his life, he thought about what he wanted for it.

The Hart: that the fire wouldn't go out, and there'd always be a table.

The smithy: that the iron would hold.

The cookshop: that Xola's fish would never run out, and nobody would leave hungry.

The south door: that whoever came up the steps, there'd be room.

He meant every one. And the windows didn't come, and he didn't wait for them. He just put the squares of red in a pile, one by one. Leaka, who'd come in from the market still damp, and was eating the last of Maigo's noodles, carried them out the door by the armful. She stuck them up with Milli's glue, singing.

He looked up.

It had come at the twentieth one. Not on the door. In front of him, at the long table, small and plain, the way the windows did.

[ *It is not what it is. It is what you want.* ]

He sat very still.

"There," said Ice, across the table.

"It came," said Curse.

"You meant it," said Ice. "And then you waited."

***

He took the south door's square out to the Hall himself.

Ama Corrie was at her counter with the Roll open. She watched him stick the red square on the inside of the south door, where people coming up the steps would see it as the door opened. She didn't say anything until he'd finished.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie. "What's it say?"

"That whoever comes up the steps, there'll be room."

"That's not true," said Ama Corrie. "The banner room's got sixty-one chairs and a stool."

"It's a wish," said Curse. "Not a label. He says." He looked at the Roll, open on the counter between her hands. Three hundred years of names. "Is there anything in the Roll that's a wish?"

Ama Corrie looked at him for a long time over the top of it.

"I don't write wishes," she said. "I write what happened. Who came. Who signed. Who kept what." She turned back a great many pages, slowly, to the first winter. She put her finger on a name. *Kite*. And under it, a line. It had been empty for two years. Now there was writing on it, in a different hand from hers. "I left that empty."

"I know," said Curse. "You said it kept itself."

"It did." She took her finger off it. "But I left it. Two years. A line with nothing on it, under a man nobody wanted." She closed the Roll. "That's not a wish. I don't do wishes."

"What is it, then?"

Ama Corrie picked up her broom.

"Room," she said.

She went off across the Hall to sweep something that didn't need sweeping. Curse stood by the south door for a while with the brush in his pocket. He thought that she'd just told him the same thing Ice had, in fewer words, and gone away before he could say so.

***

The north door was the last one.

He'd been saving it. He didn't know why, at first. He'd done forty. The pile was gone. Leaka had stuck the last of them up on the chestnut man's shed and come back in, and was sitting on the table beside his elbow, swinging her feet. He had one square of red left, and the north door hadn't got one.

"What does the north door want?" said Leaka.

Curse thought about it.

The north door had been shut for most of three hundred years, until Kei put his shoulder to it. It stuck. It always stuck. On the longest night Gabe had stood in it, and Rae had come the last pace and stood at his back. For seventy-one mornings there'd been a candle in the little window at the top of it, changed every day, *till they're home*. And three weeks ago nine people had come through it at midnight out of the dark country, and Gabe had caught Suzu as he fell in.

"It doesn't want anything," said Curse slowly. "It's got it."

Ice looked up.

"They're home," said Curse. "Everybody it was waiting for. They came through it." He looked at the square of red. "If I wish it luck, it's a lie. It's already got its luck. It's had it since the thirteenth of January."

"Then write it upside down," said Ice.

Curse stared at him.

"You want me to write it *wrong*?"

"Not wrong," said Ice. "Upside down. Out there, people do it on purpose. On some doors. Upside down, in Chinese, is *dao*. And *dao* sounds like *arrived*." He picked up the brush, turned it in his fingers, put it down again. "So if luck's upside down, it means luck's arrived. It's already here. It came."

Curse sat at the long table for a long time.

A wish, upside down, was a wish that had come true.

And a wish that had come true was just what a thing was.

"It's a label," said Curse. "Upside down, it's a label."

"If you like," said Ice.

***

He wrote it at the long table, the right way up, the way he'd learned it. The left side, standing straight. The roof. The mouth. The field. He meant it all the way down. Not *I want*. *It's here*. Nine people through a door at midnight, and Gabe catching Suzu, and Chernn not changing the candle.

Then he carried it out of the banner room, through the Hall of Roads, past Ama Corrie at her counter. Ama Corrie looked at the square of red in his hands and said nothing. He went past Isa's chair, where Isa was asleep under the grey blanket. And up the narrow stone stair inside the north door, to the little window at the top, where Chernn's last candle had burned all the way down and nobody had moved the dish.

He stuck it on the door under the window. Upside down.

[ *It remembers.* ]

[ *It always did.* ]

He stood on the stair for a while, looking at it. A square of red under a little window, upside down. On a door that stuck.

When he came down, Isa was awake.

She was looking up at the stair. Not at him. At the red square at the top of the door. She couldn't see it, he knew. Not the word. Not from there. But she was looking at it anyway, the way she looked north from the roof at dusk. At a point of light she couldn't see either.

"What does it say?" said Isa.

"It says *it came*," said Curse.

Isa looked at it for a long time.

"*Hm*," she said. "Yes. It did."

***

Leaka found him on the south steps in the evening, before the second dusk, before the pole.

The whole round market was getting ready for it below them. Jared and Azac at the long trestles, where the new middle pole lay along the cobbles, finished. Six hundred lanterns in their crates round the fountain. Kei with his map, and Higu with the ropes, and Rae counting people. Ice down by the pole with Jared, with Hyv on his arm, talking about something with his hands.

Every door in the round market had a square of red on it. Forty-one. He could see most of them from the steps. The Hart's. The smithy's. The blue door at the end of Spoke Street, where the old woman had come out and looked at it for a long time, and gone back in, and come out again with a second apple.

"They look *good*," said Leaka, sitting down next to him.

"They're wishes," said Curse.

"I *know* they're wishes. You said. About forty times."

"I've never written a wish."

"You wrote *Next*," said Leaka. She held up her wrist. It was still there, on the inside, faint, a year old. *Next*. "On me. In the banner room. That was a wish."

Curse looked at it.

He thought about it properly, the way Ice did.

"No," he said. "That was a label."

Leaka looked at him.

"You'd said it," said Curse. "*We'll go. Next.* You'd already decided. I just wrote down what you were." He was quiet. "It was always true. That's why it stayed."

Leaka didn't say anything. Which, for Leaka, was very loud.

He took the brush out of his pocket. The fine one, in its tin. There was still some black ink on it from the north door. He took her wrist, the one that said *Next*, and turned it over, and wrote on the inside of it, under the old word, very small.

The left side, standing straight. The roof. The mouth. The field.

Upside down.

[ Inscription · Label · *arrived* ]

Leaka looked at it.

"What does it *say*," she said. Very quietly. Which was the quietest thing he'd ever heard her say.

"Luck," said Curse.

"Why's it upside down?"

"Because it's arrived," said Curse. He let go of her wrist. "It came. A while ago. In Merrowgate, on the first night, with an axe taller than you were." He was quiet. "Louder than I thought."

Leaka stared at him.

Then she screamed. Of course she did. At the top of the south steps, on the first day of the year, so loudly that the whole round market looked up from the pole. Jared said *y'all* from the bottom. Gabe screamed back without knowing why. And Ama Corrie, in the south door behind them, said *hm* in a tone that meant *not on my steps*.

Leaka put her arms round his neck and held on.

"You wrote a *label*," she said, into his shoulder. Damp, still, from the fountain. "On me. *Twice*."

"They were both true," said Curse.

Below them, in the market, Jared looked up at the sky. It was going gold at the edges. He said something to Azac. And Azac put down his plane, and went and stood with his back to the bottom end of the pole.

"Second dusk," said Leaka, into his shoulder.

"Yes."

"Come on," said Leaka, letting go, getting up, holding out her hand with the two words on the wrist. "We're holding a beam."
