Kotori thought she was being fired.

Ice could see it from the first second the call connected. She was at the kitchen table in the flat on the fourteenth floor, in Singapore, in a pale blue T-shirt, with her hands flat on the table in front of her and her glasses on. Her face was the colour of paper. Behind her, slightly out of focus, her mother was standing at the kitchen counter pretending to wipe it. Her father was sitting beside her with his reading glasses on and a pen behind his ear, looking at the camera like a man looking at a machine he intended to understand.

"Hi," said Kotori. "Sorry. Hi. Good morning. Sorry."

"Good morning, Miss Hayashi," said Vivian. "Mr Hayashi. Mrs Hayashi."

Kotori's mother waved with the cloth.

"Is it—" said Kotori. "Sorry. Your email said it was about my contract. I just wanted to say, if I did something wrong, I'm really sorry, and I can—"

"You haven't done anything wrong," said Vivian.

"Oh," said Kotori. "Okay. Sorry."

"It isn't about your contract," said Vivian. "I said that so you'd come." She folded her hands on the teak desk. "It's about the company. Mr Declan would like to give you five per cent of it."

Silence.

Ice was sitting beside Vivian, in the chair that wasn't borrowed, where he could be seen on the screen. He'd come on his own. Gabe and Suzu had driven down with him and were downstairs in the kopitiam with the auntie, who had found out it was Kotori's day and was making something. He'd wanted to be the one in the room. He couldn't have said why. He watched Kotori on the laptop screen not understanding, and then understanding, and then not being able to believe that she'd understood.

"But you said," she said, very small. "You said I don't get shares. You said it to my face. You said I get paid."

"I did say that," said Vivian. "I was wrong. Mr Declan has corrected me." She paused. "It happens about once a year."

Kotori looked at Ice.

"I didn't ask," she said. "I wasn't going to ask. I said I was just happy to eat."

"I know," said Ice. "That's why."

***

She cried.

She didn't make a sound about it. She sat at the kitchen table with her hands flat in front of her and her face crumpled up, and tears came out from under her glasses, and she said "Sorry" about nine times. Her mother came round from the counter with the cloth still in her hand and put her arms round her from behind. Ice looked at the orchid on the windowsill until it was over. So did Vivian.

Her father didn't move. He'd taken the pen from behind his ear and was holding it, and he was looking at Ice through the screen. It was a level, careful look, the look of an engineer who'd been handed a drawing and been told it would stand up.

"Mr Declan," he said. "May I ask some questions."

"Please."

"Ms Ong, you'll send the documents?"

"They're already in your email, Mr Hayashi," said Vivian. "Eleven pages. The transfer form, the register entry, a summary of the company's accounts to the end of December, the constitution, and a letter from me in plain English explaining what all of it means."

"Thank you." He put his reading glasses straight. "I'd like to read them now. While we're on the call. If that's all right."

"That's why I sent them," said Vivian.

He read them.

He read every page. It took twenty-two minutes. He read with the laptop turned slightly away from the camera and the pen moving down the margin of each page, not writing, just following. Kotori stopped crying and sat beside him with her mother's arms still round her, and watched him read. Vivian sat at the teak desk with her hands folded and didn't say a word the whole time. Ice understood that she approved of this more than of anything that had happened in her office all month.

At the end, Mr Hayashi took his glasses off.

"What happens if the company loses money?" he said.

"It's a Sendirian Berhad," said Vivian. "A private company limited by shares. If it lost everything tomorrow, your daughter would lose five per cent of nothing. She'd never owe a single ringgit more."

"She hasn't paid anything for them."

"No."

"Then she can't lose anything she paid for," he said, slowly, working it out.

"That's right."

He nodded. He put the glasses back on and looked at page seven again, then at the screen.

"Why five?" he said to Ice.

Ice had thought about how to answer that. In the car on Wednesday, and since. He'd thought about saying something about the twelve hours on the post, or the church, or the tea. In the end he just told the truth.

"Because it fits," he said. "Under a line Vivian has to keep us under. Ten for Suzu, ten for Gabe, five for her." He paused. "And because she didn't ask."

Mr Hayashi looked at him for a long moment.

"And the rest?" he said. "What will you do with the rest?"

"Keep it," said Ice. "Vivian says I have to."

"Mr Declan holds seventy-five per cent," said Vivian. "He'll keep it."

Kotori's father nodded again, once, the way he'd nod at a drawing that would stand up. He turned to his daughter.

"Mia," he said. "It's your decision. You're twenty." He put his hand on her shoulder. "But I think that's a yes, then."

Kotori laughed, wetly, into her mother's arm. "That's what you said last time."

"It was a yes last time."

"Are they still nice?" said her mother, from behind her. It was the first thing she'd said.

Kotori looked at the screen. At Ice in the chair beside the orchid, and Vivian with her glasses on, and the photograph of Vivian's father on the wall behind them, in the slatted light.

"Yes," she said. "Sorry. Yes. They're still nice."

***

The form would go by courier that afternoon. She'd sign it on Monday and send it back.

"In black," said Vivian.

"In black," said Kotori.

"She has twelve black pens," said her mother. "She bought them for her exams. She's never used one."

"*Mom.*"

"I'm just saying, Vivian. She's ready."

Vivian looked at Kotori's mother through the screen for a moment. Ice had the distinct impression that two people who would never otherwise have met had just recognised something in each other.

"Thank you, Mrs Hayashi," said Vivian gravely. "That's very reassuring."

***

The auntie had made kuih.

She'd made a whole plate of it, green and white, in layers, and she wouldn't let them leave the kopitiam until all three of them had eaten some. She stood over the table with her arms folded while they ate. "For the small girl," she said. "Tell her. *From auntie.* She can't eat it, she's in Singapore, so you eat it for her." Gabe ate five. Suzu ate two, very slowly. Ice ate one and took a photograph of the plate, and sent it to Kotori with the words *from auntie*.

She sent back a row of crying faces, and then: *sorry*. Then: *I mean thank you*. Then, a minute later, by itself: *i didn't ask!!!*

*I know*, he typed. *That's why.*

***

They went back into the eggs at the game's second dawn.

It had been a good week, and a strange one. Gabe was seventeen. He'd been eleven on Sunday. Nobody Ice had ever heard of had climbed like it, not even Ice in the first week. Chris had a column for it now, and the column was called *stood in*. The black pike still came up out of the deep water now and then under whatever Kotori was standing on. Every time it did, Gabe was two paces away, and every time he took it, and every time the hammer went white. On Thursday he'd taken one at nine per cent and laughed about it all the way back to Fernwick.

The rest of them had climbed more slowly. Ice's bar had moved about as much in six days as it used to move in one. He hadn't minded. He'd spent the week in the shallows, holding the front, keeping a man with a row of holes in his shield alive while he did the most dangerous thing in the fen on purpose. It was the best week he'd had in the game since the ford.

Sheng Yang had climbed faster.

On Wednesday, when Gabe was done for the day and the light was going, Sheng Yang had stretched, said "I'm going to go and see how far," and walked off on his own toward the deep pools past the bull's water. That was where the pike lived, and the things that ate the pike. He'd done the same on Thursday, and Friday. He came back each night in the dark with his long legs muddy to the knee and his bar a little fuller, and said nothing about it at all.

So on Saturday night, sitting on the plank at the narrows with the others, with the stream on and the moonbells ringing faintly across the tussocks, Ice opened the Tally. He already knew.

Seventh.

He'd been fifth on Sunday. He'd fallen two places in a week, for the first time since the river. He looked at it, the number seven beside his name and the number five beside Sheng Yang's, and waited to feel something about it. The old feeling, the one he used to get in the first weeks, when he'd stood in front of the black needle in Brackenford and felt every name above his like a hand on his chest.

It didn't come.

Further along the plank, Sheng Yang was looking at the same window. He felt Ice looking. He lifted his eyes, slow and easy, and smiled in the moonlight, completely confident. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.

"For now," said Ice.

Sheng Yang's smile got very slightly wider.

"For now," he agreed.

Gabe leaned forward from the other end of the plank to look at the window, with his chin on his hammer.

"Seventeen," he said, finding himself, a long way down, not on it at all. "Where's seventeen?"

"Not on it," said Deston. "It's the top hundred. You need about twenty-one."

"I'll get twenty-one."

"By Wednesday," said Sheng Yang. "The way you're going."

"By Tuesday," said Gabe.

"Don't," said Suzu, with his eyes shut, leaning against the post with the iron bands on it.

Kotori wasn't looking at the Tally. She was sitting at the very end of the plank, on the far side of Gabe, with her feet over the black water and her staff across her knees. She'd been quiet all evening. Not sad-quiet. The other kind. She'd been humming under her breath with the moonbells for an hour, a beat behind, and she hadn't said sorry once since dusk. Ice had been counting.

She caught him looking. She went pink.

"I signed it in my head," she said. "Already. Sorry. I know it's Monday. I just signed it in my head."

"In black?" said Ice.

"In black," said Kotori. "Obviously."

He didn't tell them.

He sat on the plank in the cold fen with five people, a week into six. One of them had passed him on a list. One of them had climbed six levels by being hit in the face by a fish. One of them owned five per cent of something and had signed it in her head. He found he didn't want to be anywhere else at all.

Seventh, he thought. For now.

He closed the window.
