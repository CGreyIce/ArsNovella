On the sixth day out of the round town, Kei walked off the edge of his own map, and stopped, and got out a new sheet.

He'd known it was coming. He'd known for a month, since Ice put his finger on the blank paper past the Millrace road and said *I'll tell you what it's like*. But it was different, standing on it. The road went on. The hedges went on, and the fields, and the long slow rise that Ice had written about in his letters. And Kei's map, unrolled on his knee on a milestone at the side of the road, stopped. A neat line in his own ink, and then white.

He looked at the white for a long time.

"You've got a face," said Higu, beside him.

"I've never been off it," said Kei. "Not once. In fifteen months. Everywhere I've been, I'd already drawn. Or I drew it as I went." He touched the edge. "I don't know what's here."

"Nobody does," said Higu. "That's why you're drawing it."

He looked at her. She was sitting on the next milestone with her lamp in her lap, in the sun, in the wind, with her map sheets in their oilskin under her coat. She'd been off the edge of things a lot. The north, past the twenty-two, past the last stone. She'd sent her Lantern into the black and seen a lamp being carried.

He got out a new sheet. He pinned it to the edge of the old one with two little brass pins Hana had given him. He took his pen, and drew the road going on.

***

There were ten of them, and he drew all ten on the road every night, because that was what you did on a map. Little dots, where they'd slept.

Higu, who walked beside him and checked her sheets against his. Gigi, a pace off from Thea, always, on her left. Thea, with her tower shield and Xola on her other side. Drew at the edge of things, watching feet. Salty with Right Size on his back, the length of a man, black, which people on the road stopped and stared at. Wendy beside Salty, with her hands in her pockets because there was no gate to keep. Bee at the back with the second banner strapped across her shoulders, its end sticking up behind her head, with the faint gold star along its edge.

And Hyvred.

She walked at the front. Nobody had told her to. On the first morning out of the round town she'd just gone to the front and stayed there, all day, every day. Not fast. Not ahead. Just at the front. Calling things on the road before anybody else saw them, the way she'd called the north tunnel. *Left. A goose. Don't fight the goose.*

They walked round the goose. It was still there, on the second morning, a mile past the first milestone, exactly where Ice's letters had said it would be. Hyvred stopped in front of it and looked at it for a long time, and the goose hissed.

"It's *him*," said Hyvred. "The goose. That's the one he walked round."

"It's a goose," said Xola.

"It's *his* goose," said Hyvred, and walked round it, very carefully, through the wet grass. The other nine followed her, single file, one at a time. The goose turned on the spot and hissed at every one of them.

Kei drew the goose on the map. A tiny one, at the side of the road, a mile past the first milestone. He wrote beside it, very small: *don't*.

***

Ice's letters came every night, to Hyvred. She didn't read them out. Nobody asked her to. But she'd read them by the fire with her tail very still and her face doing something. And afterwards, sometimes, she'd say one line out loud, as if it had come out by itself.

"*There's a room*," she said, on the fourth night.

"What room?"

"He's got a room." She was looking at the letter. "In Masmark. For us. A hall for ten." She read it again. "*it's small. it's the smallest one. it smells of fish. i swept it*." She looked up. "He *swept* it."

Gigi, across the fire, put down her cup.

"Under," she said. "Or round."

"He doesn't say."

"It'll be under," said Gigi. "He's been swept under for a year. He knows the difference."

Kei wrote it on the map that night. Not the room; he didn't know where it was yet. He wrote it at the edge of the new sheet, where the road ran off toward the south and east, toward a city he hadn't drawn. *A room. Swept.*

***

They came to the ferry on the ninth day. The old man pulled them across on his rope in two loads. Kei asked him, on the second crossing, if he'd taken a man with a shield across in January.

"Lots of men with shields," said the old man.

"On his own. With an arrow in the strap of his pack. One white feather."

The old man stopped pulling for a moment. The ferry drifted.

"*That* one," he said. "Yes. A man said a thing to him on the landing. Two fingers to the eyebrow. Some sort of salute. And he said it back." He started pulling again. "I thought, there's a man people know. Somewhere."

"Somewhere else," said Kei.

The old man looked at him oddly, and didn't answer, and pulled them across.

***

On the eleventh night, on the long rise, Kei had the watch, and Hyvred didn't sleep.

They'd camped in the lee of a stone wall at the top of a field, with the land they'd come from spread out below them in the dark. The White Hills a pale line. Somewhere beyond them, too far to see, the round town. The other eight were asleep in a row along the wall. Salty was snoring. Bee had a wing over Wendy, who'd crawled under it at some point in the night and was sleeping with her face in the feathers.

Hyvred was sitting at the end of the row with her bow across her knees, looking south and east, up the road.

Kei went and sat a few paces off. Not close. Near.

"You can't see it from here," he said. "Masmark. It's three days."

"I know." She didn't look round. "I can see everything, in here. Edge to edge. And I can't see that." She laughed, a bit. "It's the first thing in the game I can't see."

Kei didn't say anything for a while.

"I gave him the arrow," said Hyvred. "At the gate. The one that leans." She touched her quiver, where there was a gap. "So he'd know which way. And now I haven't got anything that leans." She was quiet. "I didn't think about that. I didn't think about which way *I'd* know."

Kei looked at her for a long moment.

Then he unrolled the map on his knee, under the lamp, and turned it so she could see.

The old sheet and the new one, pinned together with Hana's brass pins. The round town at the top, with its seven roads and the line of ninety-four dots going north off the edge. The road south and east, drawn as they'd walked it. The goose, a mile past the first milestone: *don't*. The White Hills, the Millrace fork, the long rise. Ten tiny dots in a row against a stone wall, where they were sleeping.

And at the very bottom of the new sheet, past the end of the road, on the white, where Kei hadn't drawn anything else yet, one more dot. On its own. In the middle of the blank.

"What's that?" said Hyvred.

"Him," said Kei. "I put him on when his letter came from the rim. On day fourteen. I didn't know where Masmark was yet. So I worked it out from the days and the miles he wrote. And I put a dot where he'd be." He shrugged. "That's what a map's for. Where your people are."

Hyvred looked at the dot for a long time.

Then she leaned over and put her finger on the ten in a row by the wall. And moved it down the road, very slowly, along the line Kei had drawn, past the ferry, up the long rise, to the end of the ink. And off it, onto the white, to the dot on its own.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days," said Kei.

She took her finger away. She sat back against the wall with her bow across her knees. And for the first time in eleven nights, Kei saw her shoulders come down.

"It leans," she said. "Your map. It leans too."

"All maps lean," said Kei. "Toward whoever drew them. That's the problem with maps." He rolled it up. "Go to sleep, Hyv. I've got the watch."

She went to sleep. Kei sat up till the third dawn with the lamp. Every so often he unrolled the map a little and looked at the dot on its own in the white, and then rolled it up again.

***

They came over the rim on the fourteenth day, at the first dusk, exactly as Ice had.

Kei had read the letter. *it's very big. there's a lake the size of a country. harlan was right.* He'd thought Ice was being Ice: saying a small true thing about something large. He'd thought he'd come over the rim and see a city and think *yes, that's a big city*.

He came through the notch at the top of the road, and the world fell away, and he stopped.

He stood in the notch with his map on his arm and his pen in his hand and looked down at Great Lake Masmark. A great round lake inside a ring of black mountains, so wide its far side was blue. An island in the middle with a tower on it. And round the lake, all the way round, on every terrace and every slope, the city. Thousands of lights coming on in the dusk. Banners on every tower. Roads coming down from the rim on every side like the spokes of a wheel.

He'd drawn the round town a hundred times. The hub and the seven spokes. He'd drawn Jared's lantern roof on the back of Hana's list, and the ceiling of the Hall under the dome. He'd thought he knew what a crossroads looked like.

This was a crossroads the size of a country.

"Kei," said Higu, beside him. Softly.

He didn't answer. He couldn't. He was looking at the roads. Dozens of them. Coming down from every notch in the rim, from every direction, from every region of the world. North, south, east, west, and all the ones between. He'd never seen most of the places they came from. He'd never drawn them. All of them coming down to the same water.

He realised he was crying, a bit. He didn't stop.

"It's not on any map," he said. "Not mine. Not anybody's. Nobody's drawn it. It's too big." He looked down at the sheet on his arm, the new one, pinned to the old with Hana's brass pins. Fourteen days of road, drawn as he went, from the round town to here. And at the bottom of it, white. "I'll never get it all down."

"No," said Higu. "You won't."

"Then what's the—"

"You'll get *ours* down," said Higu. She was looking at the lake. "Where we sleep. Where we stand. The room he swept." She put her hand on his arm, on the map. "That's what a map's for, Kei. You told me that. In March. On a roof. It's not for everything. It's for where your people are."

He looked at her. Then at the lake. Then at the map.

He took his pen. At the bottom of the sheet, at the very edge, where the road came over the rim, he drew one line. Short, curved. The edge of the crater. And beyond it, nothing yet. Just white.

And he wrote, very small: *Masmark. Where many roads meet.*

***

They went down in the dark. All night, the switchbacks, the way Ice had. Past other parties going up and coming down, in every colour, with banners. Nobody looked at them twice. Ten people in plain gear with a blue banner on a winged girl's back. Nobody in Masmark had ever heard of Constellation.

Kei noticed that. He'd known it would happen. Ice had written *nobody here knows the name*. But it was different, walking through it.

In the round town, Constellation was the Hall. The sixty chairs and the long table and the Roll. Ama Corrie and the gate and the roof of lanterns. Everybody knew. Hobb the carter knew. The chestnut man knew. The pigeon on the dark stone in the ring probably knew.

Here, they were ten people on a road.

At the third switchback, a party of twenty in dark red came up past them in step. A black open hand on their banner, fingers spread. They didn't move aside. Kei's ten moved aside for them. Salty had to stand against the rock with Right Size flat against his back to let them through. One of the twenty, at the end, looked at the blue banner on Bee's shoulders as he passed, and the faint star on its edge. And said, not quite under his breath, "*Who're they?*"

And another, ahead of him, said, "*Nobody*."

Wendy took her hands out of her pockets.

She didn't say anything. She stood on the switchback in the dark with her bow on her back and looked after the twenty in dark red until they'd gone up round the next bend. Then she put her hands back in her pockets.

"Nobody," she said, to Salty.

"They didn't ask," said Salty.

"No," said Wendy. "Nobody asked them who'd stand next to them. I could tell." She started walking again. "You can always tell."

***

The fish man's cart was at the bottom of the north-west road, where Ice's letters said it would be. Kei smelled it from half a mile up. Salt and coals and something crackling. Every one of the ten slowed down when it hit them, like walking into a warm wall.

And standing beside the cart, at the first dawn, with a fish on a stick in his hand and his shield on his back, was Ice.

Kei saw him first. Of course he did; he was watching for him. He'd been watching for him since the rim. He was at the front, beside Hyvred, because Hyvred had stopped being at the front about a mile up. She'd slowed down, and slowed down, and now she was walking beside Kei with her eyes on the bottom of the road and her tail perfectly still.

Ice looked up the road at them.

He didn't wave. He never waved. He stood there with his fish and watched them come down the last of the switchbacks into the light. Ten of them. Dusty, slow, fourteen days of road on their boots, with a banner on a winged girl's back.

They came to the bottom and stopped.

Kei had thought about what Ice would say. He'd had fourteen days. He'd thought Ice might say *hi*. Or *you made it*. Or nothing, the way he usually said nothing, and just nod.

"You're slow," said Ice.

Kei looked at him.

He'd heard that line before. He'd heard Lucan of Sable Court post it to the whole world from a city on the far side of the map. He'd heard Deston tell the long table he'd said it on a green in a candle village, to a woman with a stick. Kei thought he knew what it meant. It was a thing you said to somebody you were very glad to see, when you couldn't say the thing itself.

"We walked fourteen days," said Kei.

"I know," said Ice. "I counted."

And something happened to his face. Not the small thing, the corner of the mouth. Something bigger. Kei had known him fifteen months in the game and a long time before that through Suzu. He'd seen it maybe twice.

Ice was glad. That was all it was. He was standing at the bottom of a road in the biggest city in the world, where nobody had heard of them, with a fish on a stick. And he was so glad to see them that it showed.

"There's a room," said Ice. "It's small."

"We heard," said Kei. "You swept it."

"Under," said Ice.

At the back of the ten, Gigi made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

***

Kei didn't see the rest. Not properly. He'd tell it afterward, at the long table, and he'd say that, honestly: *I didn't see the rest. It wasn't mine.*

He saw Hyvred stop beside him at the bottom of the road. He saw her look at Ice, standing by the fish cart with the fish. He saw Ice look at her.

And then Kei did the thing he'd been doing all his life, at the edges of other people's maps. He took a step back. And turned, and looked at the lake. At the boats going out in the first light, and the island with its tower, and the far side of the crater blue with distance. He unrolled his map on his arm and took out his pen and very carefully drew the harbour. The fish man's cart. The bottom of the north-west road.

He didn't look round. Higu didn't. Gigi and Thea and Xola and Drew and Salty and Wendy didn't. They all, at exactly the same moment, found something very interesting to look at in the harbour.

All except Bee. Bee was at the back, with the banner on her shoulders. Bee was the one who drew things.

The fish man, behind his brazier, looked at the ten of them all looking at the harbour. Then at the two who weren't.

"Hm," said the fish man, and turned his sticks.
