"Dylan," said Ice.

Salty was in the netloft door, with Wendy, keeping it. It was the first dawn on Sunday, the day after the bridge, and the lane was empty. Nobody came to the netloft on a Sunday morning. Even the fish man didn't open till the second dawn on Sundays.

He turned round.

Ice was standing in the middle of the netloft floor, with his shield on his back and no cup in his hand. Which meant something. He always had a cup in his hand in the netloft in the mornings. And he'd said *Dylan*.

He'd said it once in October, at Joss's anvil in the round town, with a bar of black iron the length of a man glowing on it. *Dylan. You've always swung it like it was too small.* And before that, Salty couldn't remember. Some time last year. Once a year, about. Never on stream, never in front of a lot of people. Just when he meant it.

Nine months. He was early.

"Yes," said Salty.

"Show me how you swing it," said Ice.

***

Salty didn't say anything for a long time.

He'd known Ice longer than most of them. Since before the first game they'd played together, a long time ago, before any of this. He'd been in his chat on day one, with Niko and Leaka, saying *he's a menace*. He'd watched him do everything. He'd watched him walk into a fen with a sword and a shield on the first day of a game nobody had heard of and come out the other side the most famous tank in the world.

Ice had never once asked him to show him anything.

It wasn't that kind of thing. Ice showed people. Ice was the one at the front, the one who knew where to stand, the one who said *Rafi, the ledge*. When Salty had been trying to forge a sword in the dark with nineteen cracked test pieces on the windowsill, it was Ice who'd come and stood at the anvil and said the thing about it being too small. And then *it's the right size*, when it was done.

He'd never once said *show me*.

"Swing what?" said Salty. Though he knew.

"That," said Ice, and nodded at the black sword planted point-down in the cobbles of the lane beside him. "The turn. The one you did at the north door. And at the bell." He paused. "The slow one."

Behind Salty, in the door, Wendy had gone very still.

***

They went down to the cellar. It was low tide. The floor was just wet, the water gone out through the place where the grate had been, back down the Undercut to the lake. The old iron grate was still leaning against the wall where Salty and Azac and Jun Liang had put it. The ladder, the hooks, the round stone room.

Wendy came down with the lamp. She didn't ask. She always held the lamp.

Salty stood in the middle of the cellar with Right Size in both hands and didn't know where to start.

"I don't know how to show it," he said.

"Just do it," said Ice. "I'll watch."

So he did it.

He didn't swing it. That was the first thing. He'd never been able to explain that to anybody, not to Drew at the anvil saying *get in your hips*, not to Wendy. You didn't swing Right Size. It was too heavy to swing. It was the length of a man and made of iron that had been in the dark for three thousand years. If you tried to swing it the way you swung an ordinary sword, fast, from the arms, it would just pull you over.

You *started* it.

He set his feet. Wide, the way the floor wanted. He let the sword's weight go down into his hands, into his arms, into his back, into his hips, into his feet, into the wet stone. Until it wasn't in his hands at all; it was in the floor. And then he moved his hips, very slightly. Just enough to tell it which way.

And it went.

Slowly. That was the second thing. It went slowly, because it was heavy. It came round, low, from his right, along the floor of the cellar, the black blade drinking the lamplight. And once it had started, it was going. You couldn't stop it. You couldn't hurry it either. You couldn't do anything to it at all except go with it, wherever it was going. Round. All the way round. Turning your whole body under it, the way you'd turn under a door swinging shut.

It came all the way round, the full turn, and ended where it had started. Low, on his right, the point an inch off the wet stone.

It ended where he ended.

He stood there breathing. The water on the floor was moving, a long slow ripple going out to the walls and coming back.

"Again," he said.

Salty did it again.

"Again."

And again. Seven times. Ice watched every one, standing by the ladder with his arms folded, perfectly still. Not watching the sword. Salty noticed that on the third one. He wasn't watching the blade, the way people did when they watched you swing something. He was watching Salty's feet. His hips. The place where he started it. And then, while it went round, nothing. He didn't watch it go round. He watched the *end*. Where it would come.

"It's slow," said Ice, after the seventh.

"It's heavy."

"Because it's heavy," said Ice slowly, "it's slow. And because it's slow, you know where it's going." He was looking at the place on the floor where the point always stopped, an inch off the stone. "But you can't stop it once it's started. And neither can anybody else."

"No."

"So it doesn't matter that they know," said Ice. "They can see it coming the whole way round. And there's nothing they can do." He looked at Salty. "Because by the time they've seen it, it's already there."

Salty didn't say anything. He'd never thought about it like that. He'd just done it.

"That's what the dark leaned back from," said Wendy. Very quietly, from behind the lamp. "On the longest night. At the north door. You stepped out one pace, and it came round, and the whole dark could see it coming." She swallowed. "And it couldn't do anything. So it leaned back."

***

"Can I hold it?" said Ice.

Salty looked at him.

Harlan had asked that, at the anvil, in October. *Can I touch it?* Salty had said *no*, and Harlan had said *fair*. Nobody had held it but him. Not Drew, who'd taught him to get in his hips. Not Gabe, whose rivets held the guard, or Milli, whose cat's-paw was on the grip. Not Wendy. He'd never even thought about letting anybody.

He held it out.

Ice took it.

It nearly took him over. Salty saw it happen. Ice's hands closed on the grip and the weight went into his arms, all of it at once, and he went forward half a step before he caught it. He was strong; the game had made him strong, fifty-six levels of a shield and a sword. But he'd never held anything like it. Nobody had. Three thousand years of the dark, in a bar the length of a man, quenched in a stream with the lamp out.

He didn't try to swing it.

He stood there in the middle of the cellar with Right Size in both hands and let it go down. Into his arms, his back, his hips. Salty watched him do it. Watched him find where it wanted to be, slowly, the way you'd feel for a wall in the dark.

Into the floor.

Then he moved his hips. Very slightly.

It went.

Not all the way round. Not the full turn; he couldn't, he'd never done it, he wasn't built for it. Half. A slow heavy arc from his right, low, a foot off the wet stone. And Ice went with it, turning under it, his face doing nothing at all. And at the half, where his hips ran out, it stopped. Where he ended.

The ripple went out across the cellar floor to the walls.

Ice stood there with the point an inch off the water, breathing.

"*Thirty-four*," said Wendy, behind the lamp, in a voice of enormous satisfaction.

"It's heavy," said Ice.

"Yes."

"I can't do it."

"No," agreed Salty. "You've got the wrong arms."

Ice looked at him. And then he laughed. Out loud, in the cellar, with Salty's sword in his hands, the way he'd laughed on the netloft step at *I pick fenwort*. It went round the low ceiling and came back off the walls.

"Thank you," he said, and held it out.

Salty took it back. It went into his hands, and into the floor, the way it always did. The right size.

***

He didn't understand what Ice wanted it for. Not then.

He thought he did. He thought Ice wanted to know how to fight something heavy. Something slow. Something that came round and couldn't be stopped. Salty thought he was learning how to stand in front of it, the way he stood in front of everything.

He found out on Tuesday he was wrong.

***

It was the third dawn. He was in the netloft with Wendy, on the bench under the wavy window, not sleeping. The netloft was dark and quiet. Riverbank asleep in the gallery. Kei asleep at the long table on his maps. Hyvred somewhere, or out of the game.

And through the floor, from the cellar, a sound.

It was the cellar's tide. High tide, at the third dawn. Water up to your ankles, breathing in and out through the place where the grate had been. And on the water, somebody moving.

Salty knew it was Ice before he heard anything else. Nobody else went down there at the third dawn on their own. He listened.

*Splash.*

A long silence. A very long one. Long enough that Salty thought that was it.

*Splash. BOOM.*

He sat up.

It wasn't a sword on a wall. It was a sword on a shield. His own, Ice's, the round one with the dent the shape of a hammer over the dent the shape of a crown, held on his arm. Hit with his own sword, from the other side, as hard as a man could hit his own shield. Salty knew the sound. He'd heard a hammer on that shield every eight seconds for twenty minutes at the top of a bell tower.

And then silence again. Long. Longer than the first.

*Splash. BOOM.*

"What's he doing?" said Wendy. She was sitting up too.

Salty listened.

*Splash.* Silence. Silence. *BOOM.*

It wasn't the cellar. It wasn't the thing Val had written down with her pencil going through the paper. It wasn't fifty-one seconds of rain on a tin roof. It was the other way. Slow. Slower than slow. A step, and a long silence, and then everything, all at once, the whole weight of somebody going into one blow. And then nothing again, for so long you'd think it was over. And then *BOOM*.

Like a bell.

Like the Hour, at the top of the tower, lifting its hammer all the way back over its shoulder, slowly. Taking forty seconds. And then bringing it down.

Like Right Size, coming round, so slow you could see it the whole way. And there was nothing you could do.

"He's not learning to stand in front of it," said Salty.

Wendy looked at him in the dark.

"He's learning to *be* it," said Salty.

***

He went down at the first dawn, when it was light, and Ice was still there.

He was standing in the middle of the cellar, ankle-deep, with his sword and shield. Breathing hard. He looked up when Salty came down the ladder, and didn't say anything.

"You've been down here all night," said Salty.

"Since the second dusk."

"Doing that."

"Yes."

Salty stepped off the ladder into the water. He looked at Ice for a long time. At his feet, wide, the way the floor wanted. Wider than he usually stood. Ice always stood narrow, ready to go either way, the back foot waiting. Not now.

"Show me," said Salty.

Ice looked at him. Then he did it.

He stood. Not on the back foot. Planted. The sword low. The shield on his arm, held out, the way you'd hold it for somebody to hit. And he waited. Not the way he waited at the heron-stone, light, ready, a breath. Heavy. Down into the floor, the way Salty went down into it with Right Size. Letting the weight go down. Waiting so long Salty thought he'd stopped.

Then his hips moved. Very slightly.

And the sword came round. Not fast. Slow. A one-handed sword, an ordinary one, his own. But he wasn't swinging it from his arm. He'd started it from the floor. And it came round the way Right Size came round, the whole of him under it, turning, so slow Salty could see it the whole way. He could see exactly where it was going to end.

It ended on the shield, held out. Where Ice ended.

*BOOM.*

The water went out to the walls in a long slow ripple and came back.

Salty read the window twice.

"That's mine," he said.

Ice looked at him.

"That's my turn," said Salty. "That's what I do. With Right Size. You've put it in a sword." He didn't know what his voice was doing. "In a *one-handed* sword."

"I borrowed it," said Ice.

***

Salty sat down on the bottom rung of the ladder with his feet in the water.

He thought about the round town, and Joss's anvil, and nineteen cracked test pieces on a windowsill. About *if he's on fifty and I'm on twenty, I'm not standing where he's standing, I'm standing somewhere softer*. He'd said that to Harlan, in October, and meant it. He'd meant it his whole life. Ice was the one at the front, the one people watched, the one who knew where to stand. And Salty was the big one with the sword, at the side, saying *he's a menace* in his chat.

And Ice had come down a ladder into a wet cellar and said *show me*. And stood here all night, doing Salty's thing, badly, then less badly. Until the game gave it a window.

"Why?" said Salty.

Ice thought about it. He was still breathing hard, standing in the water.

"Lucan's been training against the cellar," he said. "All July. Twenty people doing it at him, every night, on the bridge. By Saturday he'll be ready for it. He'll find the gaps." He looked at his shield, at the dent. "And he's been ready for the other one since the wind game. The waiting. He knows that one. He's been going round it for years."

"So you need a third," said Salty.

"I need a third," said Ice. "And I didn't have one." He looked at Salty. "So I borrowed yours."

Salty didn't say anything for a long time.

"It's not finished," said Ice. "Seventy-one. It needs to be more." He looked at the water. "I've got four nights."

"I'll come down," said Salty.

Ice looked at him.

"Every night," said Salty. "Till Saturday. I'll do mine, and you do yours, and I'll tell you when your hips are wrong." He stood up off the ladder. "They're wrong now. You're starting it from your shoulders. I can see it from here."

Something happened at the corner of Ice's mouth.

"Okay," he said.

***

He came down every night. Four nights. The second dusk to the first dawn, in the cellar, with the tide coming in and going out. Wendy on the bottom rung of the ladder with the lamp, because somebody had to hold the lamp.

He did his turn, with Right Size, slow, the whole way round. Ice did his, with a one-handed sword, into his own shield, half the way round. And Salty told him when his hips were wrong. They were wrong a lot.

*Splash.* Silence. *BOOM.*

By Wednesday, seventy-eight.

By Thursday, eighty-four.

On Friday night, the last one, at about two in the morning, Ice stood in the middle of the cellar in the high tide and went down into the floor. So far down and so still that the water round his feet stopped moving. And then his hips went, very slightly. And the sword came round, slow, heavy, the whole of him under it. Salty, on the ladder beside Wendy, could see it the entire way. He could see exactly where it would end, a whole second before it got there. And there was nothing he'd have been able to do about it at all.

*BOOM.*

Ice stood there with the ripple going out to the walls.

"Ninety-six," said Wendy. Very quietly.

"Your hips were right," said Salty.

"They were," said Ice. He looked up at Salty, on the ladder, in the lamplight. "You taught me."

"You borrowed it."

"I borrowed it," agreed Ice. "From somebody who taught me." He paused. "That's the good kind."

***

He went up the ladder at the first dawn. Salty stayed down with Wendy, sitting on the bottom rung, with the tide going out.

"He asked me," said Salty, after a while.

"I know," said Wendy. "I was holding the lamp."

"He's never asked me anything."

"He's never needed to," said Wendy. "Until now." She was looking at the water going out through the place where the grate had been. "He needed a third. And he came to you." She looked at him. "Not Xav. Not Kestrel. You."

Salty didn't say anything. He looked at Right Size across his knees. Black, dull, drinking the light.

*It ends where you end.*

"Wendy," he said.

"Mm."

"When we go home," said Salty. "To the round town. In August." He didn't look at her. "Will you come down the fourth tunnel with me? Past where the Delvers sleep. Where I found it." He touched the black blade. "Where the stream is. Where you held the lamp. I want to show you where it came from. Properly. With the lamp on."

Wendy looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," she said.

He put his hand out, palm down, flat, on the rung of the ladder between them. Eighteen inches from hers.

She put hers on top of it.

Above them, through the floor, the netloft was waking up. Somebody putting a kettle on. RX counting cups. And through the trapdoor, faint, Ice's voice. Saying something to Hyvred that Salty couldn't hear. And then Hyvred's, louder, *you were down there ALL NIGHT*, and the sound of somebody being hit on the arm, not hard.
