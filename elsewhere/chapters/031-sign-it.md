"It's Xav," said Suzu, in the car.

They were at the lights by the bridge on Saturday morning, on the way to the half-past-eight bus, with the windows down and the air conditioning still thinking about it. Suzu had his backpack on his knees and he was looking out of the window, the way he always did, as if the conversation was happening somewhere outside.

Ice looked at him. "What's Xav?"

"Whatever you're not saying." The lights changed. "You've been looking at the back of his head for a week."

Ice drove on. Past the river. Past an uncle on a bicycle with a long wooden ladder over his shoulder, perfectly balanced, the same uncle or a different one, not looking at anything.

"He wants to talk," he said. "Tonight."

"Hm," said Suzu.

He didn't ask anything else. At the drop-off he got out, and swung the backpack onto his shoulder, and bent down to the open window.

"Tell me tomorrow," he said. "In the car." He paused. "And eat properly."

***

He streamed the afternoon, because he'd said he would and because it was easier than not.

He did it from Fernwick, where everybody was getting ready for something nobody could describe. The eel man's boat was upside down on its trestles with fresh black tar on its seams. Marta's barrels of lamp oil were inside now, and there was a row of new lanterns along the Landing, hung on hooks that hadn't been there on Thursday. Hollis's yard was empty of travellers for the first time in a week. In it, Hollis and four men Ice had never seen before were working along the rack of old spears with rags and grease, not talking. Militia, Ice realised, after a while. Fernwick's own. Fishermen and reed-cutters with spears they hadn't held in years.

The moon came up over the old fen a little after nine.

He was in the heron-stones when it did. He sat on the flat stone at the north side of the ring, Deston's stone, with his boots off and his feet in the warm clear water, and watched it happen the way it had happened on the second night of the game, when there'd been three of them in this ring and no name yet.

First the stones caught it. Nine long grey necks, bent toward the middle, going silver along their tops. Then the water. Then, one by one, the moonbells. Each tight green fist in the shallows loosened. The petals opened in the moonlight with that sound he'd never been able to describe on stream: a high clean ringing, like somebody running a wet finger round the rim of a wine glass, very far away. One, and then three, and then the whole ring, until the warm water was full of small pale bells, all ringing very softly, all slightly out of time.

C, E and G, Deston had said, on the second night. They're tuned.

Xav came in across the tussocks a little after half past.

He came the way they always came, the long way round the deep water, stepping where the dark grass had roots. He was carrying his boots. He came in between two of the stones, into the warm water, and stood for a moment looking at the bells. Then he sat down on the stone next to Ice's and put his boots beside him and his feet in the water. They sat there, the two of them, looking at the ring.

"Five weeks," said Xav.

"Five weeks."

"It feels longer."

"It's been about fifteen, in here."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

They sat a while longer. A heron called somewhere out in the old fen, the long harsh croak they made at night, and the moonbells rang.

Then Xav took out his notebook.

He didn't open it. He just held it, on his knee, closed, with his thumb along the edge of the pages. He'd always done that, Ice remembered. In poly. In the back of a lecture hall with the notes he hadn't taken, nine years ago, holding a pen he wasn't using. He'd hold the thing like that when he'd already decided what he was going to say and was just waiting for the right place to put it.

"There's a group chat," said Xav.

"I know," said Ice.

Xav looked at him.

"I'm in it," said Ice. "I've always been in it. I read it." He looked at the moonbells. "I just don't say anything."

"Since when?"

"Since I moved." He shrugged. "I read all of it. Every night since Friday. I read Rui Xiang asking you what you'd do." He paused. "I read you not answering."

Xav didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he laughed. It wasn't much of a laugh. He put his head back and looked at the moon and laughed at it, quietly, the way you laugh when you've been carrying something carefully across a room for a week and someone tells you they could see what it was the whole time.

"Of course you read it," he said.

"Of course I read it."

***

He told it anyway. Xav always told it anyway. He told it the way he'd told the ford and the door, as a plan, in order, calmly, with every piece in its place, as if it were happening to somebody else.

The eight of them, the poly lot, on the first of January, in a pod café in Singapore. Eight eggs in the same room, booked by Rui Xiang the minute he could, and eight random beginner towns, possibly hundreds of kilometres apart. Crimson Ledger already in all their DMs, at fifteen dollars an hour, because somebody had found an old class photo. Malcolm saying *they'll pick us off one at a time*, and being right, which was the worst thing about Malcolm. Jun Liang, who'd make friends with anyone, and Val, who'd make friends with no one. Rui Xiang, who'd hold the rest of them together on his own if he had to, until he couldn't.

The Hall in Brackenford. Penhallow, with a pen behind each ear. A charter of company, a hundred and fifty gold, for up to twelve travellers under one name and one banner and one purse. A hundred and sixty-two gold, four silver and eleven copper in Xav's bag, and every single thing he'd brought up out of the drowned church gone to pay for it.

"The fourth line," said Xav. "*The founder may not belong to any named party.*"

"I know," said Ice. "You're in one."

"I'm in one." Xav turned the notebook over on his knee. "So I'm not going to buy it."

Ice didn't say anything.

"I'm going to tell them what I'd do," said Xav. "All of it. I've written it down. First week, everybody levels alone and nobody answers CL. Get to ten. Pick a town on a river, central. Meet there. Charter the day the last one walks in. I'll tell Rui Xiang to buy it. He'll be good at it. Better than he thinks." He stopped. "And I'll stay here, and plan where Kotori stands."

The moonbells rang. One of them, close to Ice's foot, was slightly flat.

"Unless you tell me to," said Xav.

Ice looked at him.

"Tell me Moonlight Harvest needs me," said Xav, to the water, "and I'll tell them no. Tonight. And I won't think about it again."

***

It would have been easy.

That was the thing. Ice sat on Deston's stone with his feet in the warm water and knew exactly how easy it would be. Four words. *We need you. Stay.* And it would be true. That was what made it so easy. It wasn't even a lie.

*Not with four. Not without Suzu. Not at night.* Xav had said that on the bridge in Brackenford, in the rain, about the ford, and he'd been right, and Ice had gone anyway, on his own, and fallen in the river. He'd lost a day for not listening. *Every raid I've ever planned has started with Dec doing something stupid on his own the week before.* Xav drawing the ford on a page so neatly you could have built it from the drawing. Xav drawing the door. Xav drawing a circle round the top of a mooring post in a channel off the north walk before Kotori had ever stood on it. *Sketch drew the whole thing and we just stood in it.*

He'd said that on stream. He'd meant to say it to Xav. He'd never quite found the moment.

Moonlight Harvest needed him. Of course it did. Moonlight Harvest was five people, and it needed all five.

"No," said Ice.

Xav went very still.

"No, I'm not going to tell you that," said Ice. "Buy it."

"Dec—"

"Buy it. On Wednesday, or whenever. Buy it and sign it and put it in a frame." He looked at Xav properly then, the way he'd look at somebody's back foot. "You know what you'd do. I can see you know. You've known all week. You've had a face on all week like a man carrying a plan round in his pocket and not letting himself take it out."

"That's not—"

"Every plan you've made in here," said Ice, "since day two, starts with me standing somewhere."

Xav stopped.

"*Dec stands here. Then.*" Ice said it quietly. "The ford. The door. The north walk. You plan around me. I know you do. I can feel it when I'm standing where you drew me. It's the best thing that's ever happened to me in any game, in fifteen years, and I've played a lot of games." He looked out at the bells. "But you've never once planned around you. Not in five weeks. You've never drawn a single thing where the first line was *Xav stands here*."

Xav didn't say anything. He was holding the notebook very tightly.

"Rui Xiang asked you what you'd do," said Ice. "That's what he was asking. Not *will you help us*. *What would you do.* He wants to see it. So do I." He paused. "I want to see what you'd build. I want to see it so badly. I want to walk into some town in March and see eight of my friends standing exactly where you drew them, doing something I've never seen anybody do, and know you drew it."

"And Moonlight Harvest?"

"Moonlight Harvest will be fine. We'll hold trials. We did it for a healer. We'll do it for a sword." He shrugged. "Kind people only."

"You'll be terrible without me."

"I was terrible with you."

Xav laughed. Properly, this time, out loud, and it went out over the old fen and a heron somewhere stopped croaking to listen to it.

"And it's one world," said Ice. "It's one server. You're not going anywhere. You'll be in Brackenford, or some town on a river, a few days' walk away. We'll see you around." He looked at the moonbells. "We'll see you around all the time. Your lot will beat us to something, probably. You'll beat us to something and you'll stream it in that voice where you sound like you knew it all along."

"I'll have known it all along."

"I know. That's why you have to go."

***

They sat there for a long time after that, not saying anything.

The moon went up over the ring and the bells went on ringing, slightly out of time. Somewhere out past the stones the lurker moved in the deep water, a long slow swell that came in between two of the herons and lapped at their ankles and went out again. Nothing came inside the stones. Nothing ever had.

"I'll stay for Monday," said Xav eventually.

"You don't have to."

"I drew it." Xav opened the notebook at last, and turned it toward him in the moonlight. Fernwick, in small neat lines. The north walk and the narrows. The Landing. The bridge to the causeway. Little marks for the lanterns, and a circle round the top of a mooring post. "I'm not leaving you a drawing and walking off. I'll stand in it with you. Monday night. However long it is." He closed the book. "And I'll run the trials."

"Xav—"

"Somebody has to pick who stands where I stand." He said it in exactly the voice he'd used on the bridge in the rain, *not at night*, the voice that meant it wasn't a discussion. "Kind is easy. You'll get a hundred kind people. You need someone who can stand next to you without getting in your way. That's harder than kind. You won't know how to look for it. I will."

Ice opened his mouth, and closed it again.

"Wednesday," said Xav. "Trials at noon. I pick the sword. Then I walk up to Brackenford and I give Penhallow a hundred and fifty gold and I sign it." He looked at the moonbells. "And then I go and wait for eight idiots on a riverbank."

"Seven idiots."

"Eight. Rui Xiang counts twice."

***

At midnight, the fen's midnight, with the moon high, they picked the bells.

It was what they'd done on their first night together in here, the three of them: picked moonbells in this ring by moonlight, before there was a party, before there was a name. Now they did it again, the two of them, with their boots off and the warm water round their ankles. They did it the way Sedge had taught them. One in three. Never a whole clump. Twist and lift, root whole. Leave two to seed.

Xav worked his way round the west side of the ring and Ice worked his way round the east, and they met in the middle, the way they'd met on the second night.

"We don't sell the flower," said Xav.

"We sell what it does."

"I'm keeping that one. In mine."

"It's yours. You said it first."

Xav stood up with a moonbell in his hand, ringing very faintly, and looked at it.

"You'll tell Suzu?"

"In the car. Tomorrow night. He's on the nine o'clock bus."

"He'll say *good*."

"He'll say *good*."

"Kotori will cry."

"Kotori will say sorry and then cry."

"And chat." Xav put the bell in his bag. "I'll tell chat myself. Tomorrow. On yours. I'm not having you explain it badly to ten thousand people with your hands."

"They'll be awful."

"I know." Xav almost smiled. "Tif will make a counter."

He logged out at ten to one.

He came up out of the gel in the spare room of the flat with the fan going round and the city quiet outside. He sat there for a minute in the egg with the lid up. Down the corridor, Suzu's room was empty, the door open, the three new pillows in a row and the dark blue one gone home in a backpack. On the other side of the opening in the wall, his parents' side was dark.

He picked his phone up off the floor where he'd left it and read Tif's messages, and answered them. Then he sat looking at the phone for a long time.

Then he opened the poly group chat.

He'd been in it for nine years. He'd said *happy birthday* in it, on the right days, always first, always at midnight. Apart from that he hadn't typed anything in it since the week he moved to Malacca. He'd read every word. He'd read every word since Friday.
