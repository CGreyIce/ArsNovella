The small cactus had grown.

Ice noticed it on Saturday morning, in the corridor, at a quarter to nine, coming back from the kopitiam with two cups. The one with the bent spine and the brown mark near the bottom and the chip in the pot. The one Chernn had chosen in June, crouching in the corridor, *it's honest*. It sat to the left of the big one now, by Deston's door, where she'd moved it, nearer, where you'd see it first.

It had grown about the width of a fingernail. And it had stopped leaning toward the window at the end of the corridor.

It was leaning toward the door.

He stood there with the two cups and looked at it for a long time.

The grille was open. The door was open. Deston was inside, on one of the three chairs now, with a phone to his ear, talking to somebody in Singapore in the voice he only used for one person. Ice could hear it from the corridor. Not the words. The voice.

He didn't go in. He put one of the cups down on the floor by the door, beside the cacti. And went on down the corridor to his own door, and in.

When he came out an hour later, the cup was gone. And on the floor where it had been, a piece of paper, folded, with *DEC* on it in Deston's handwriting.

*thank you. too sweet. she says hi. she says the small one's hers.*

***

He went in at eight that evening.

He came out of the dark into the first of the nine, in the banner room at the round town. Hyvred was in the chair beside it, a minute before him, the way she always was. She looked at him come out of the dark.

"Left side first," she said.

"Yes."

"Tiger says the far ones are worse."

He looked at her.

"The lamps," said Hyvred. "On the north road. He was up at the ninetieth with Maigo and Hako at the second dawn. He says it's not just leaning now." She was very pale under the horns. "He says something came up out of the heather past the ninety-fourth. Early. Months early. And looked at the lamp. And went back down."

"Okay," said Ice.

He got up.

"Where are you going?"

"To look," said Ice.

She was already on her feet.

***

They went up the north road in five. Him and Hyvred, and Tiger and Maigo and Hako, because Tiger said *I'm coming* before anybody asked, and Maigo said *then I'm coming*, and Hako said *a rare opportunity* in the voice. Nobody alone. Threes at least.

Out of the north door, which stuck. Past Kite's milestone, where the north road left the round town. Up onto the moor in the dark, with the heather going away on both sides black, and the stones going ahead of them up the road, one every mile. Ninety-four grey stones. A little black cage on the back of every one, facing the wrong way. A little flame in every cage.

He watched them as they went.

The first ones were straight. Steady, upright, the way they'd been since September a year ago, when the guild had relit the whole road stone by stone. The tenth was straight. The twentieth. At the thirtieth he thought, *maybe*. At the fortieth he knew.

Leaning. Not much. North. Like a flame near an open door.

"Fortieth," said Hyvred, beside him. "Like I said."

They walked all night. He'd walked this road before, with the whole guild, the night they lit the last of the ninety-four. He'd never walked it watching the flames. At the sixtieth they were leaning as if somebody were breathing on them. At the eightieth, like a hand had gone past. At the ninetieth, the cages themselves were ticking very faintly. The iron moving, very slightly, on the stones, the way iron ticks when it's cooling. Except it wasn't cooling. Something was pulling at it.

They stopped at the ninety-fourth at the third dawn.

***

The last stone.

Past it, the road went on. North. Over the top of the moor and down the other side, toward the old hall, a day off, and the twenty-two lamps past the old hall that ran to the edge. Those were lit too; Tiger had checked in March. But from the ninety-fourth you couldn't see them. The road just went on into the dark. And the dark, past the last lamp, wasn't like the dark on the moor behind them.

It was *thicker*.

He'd seen it on the longest night, from the north door. The dark itself, edge to edge, coming south like a tide. He'd thought then that it was the darkest thing he'd ever seen.

This was the same dark. Further off. Still. But leaning. He could feel it. Not see it; feel it. On his face, the way you feel somebody looking at you across a room. The whole black north, past the edge, leaning toward the little flame in the cage on the back of the ninety-fourth stone. To see if that was it.

The flame in the cage was nearly flat. Lying sideways in its little iron box, pointing north, like a compass needle.

"*Oh*," said Hako, very quietly. Not in the voice. In his own.

Tiger was standing in the middle of the road with his hands at his sides. He'd stood here before, on the longest night, in this exact place, saying *not yet* while the dark came down the road. Twenty minutes. On his own, until somebody called him in.

"It's looking," said Tiger.

"Yes," said Ice.

"For the jar."

"For the middle," said Ice. "It doesn't know it's a jar. It just knows it's gone." He looked at the flat flame in the cage. "So it's looking at every light it can find."

***

Something came up out of the heather at the side of the road, forty paces past the stone.

He felt it before he saw it. They all did. Maigo had her staff up before anybody said anything. A shape, coming up out of the black heather, tall, thin, swaying. One of the Unlit. The kind that came on the longest night in their hundreds, up out of still water and moor pools, and leaned toward lights.

Months early. On its own.

It stood in the heather forty paces off and looked at the lamp on the ninety-fourth stone.

Not at them. At the lamp. Leaning toward it. And then, very slowly, turning its head. And looking past the lamp. Down the road. South. Toward the round town.

"*Left*," said Hyvred.

He went.

He didn't think about which one to use. That was the thing he'd noticed since the bridge. He'd thought, before, that he'd have to choose. The waiting, or the rain, or the weight. He didn't. It just came. The way the right arrow came for Kestrel. The way the day had come for Alice on the roof, without deciding.

The weight came.

He went down into the road, into the old stone, wide. And the Unlit came at him out of the heather, fast, the way they came. And he let it, and waited, heavy, so still the dust on the road round his feet stopped moving. And then his hips went.

*BOOM.*

It went back into the heather. Not destroyed. Not like the longest night, where they'd sunk back into the dark like a tide going out. It just went *back*. Down into the black past the stone. As if it had been told something.

He stood in the road at the ninety-fourth stone and looked at the window.

Fifty-eight. He'd been fifty-four in May, in a cellar. Forty-eight at Christmas, on the floor of the Seven Below with a lake standing up. He'd been one on the first day of a game nobody had heard of, in a fen, with twenty-one people watching.

"Fifty-eight," said Hyvred, behind him.

"Fifty-eight."

"It looked at the round town," said Hyvred. Her voice was very small. "Before you went. It looked past the lamp. Down the road. At *us*."

"I know," said Ice.

He looked at the dark past the last stone. Thick. Still. Leaning.

"Not yet," said Tiger, behind him, to it. The way he'd said it on the longest night. "Not yet."

***

They walked back down the road at the first dawn. All ninety-four, every one leaning a little less than the one before, until at the tenth they were straight again, and at the first, Kite's milestone, the round town.

Isa was on the north steps in her chair, where Suzu put her every morning. With the jar in her lap and her root hands round it. Suzu on the step below.

She looked at his face as he came up the road.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She read it. He watched her read it, the way she read everybody's. All the way through.

"It looked," said Isa.

"Yes."

"At the town."

"Yes."

Isa Corrie looked down at the jar in her lap for a long time.

"This winter," she said.

"This winter," said Suzu, on the step below her.

***

Suzu stood up at the long table on Sunday at the second dawn.

The way Milli had, the Sunday before. Ice watched him do it. He'd known Suzu longer than most of them. Longer than the wind game. He'd watched him at the front of a lot of things with a scythe, and at the back of a lot of rooms saying *good*. He'd never once watched him stand up at a table and ask a room for anything.

The banner room went quiet. In a wave, from the north door outward, the way it had for Milli. Because Suzu was standing beside Isa's chair, and Isa was awake, and the jar was on the table in front of her.

Suzu looked at all of them.

He didn't say very much. He never did. He said the lights were leaning. He said the dark had noticed. He said that something had come up out of the heather past the ninety-fourth stone last night and looked at the round town. And that if it got to the longest night still looking, it would come here *for* the jar, and there wouldn't be enough lamps in the world.

And then he said:

"This winter. I'm going to carry it back. To the hill. Past the edge, across the still water, three hundred stones. And light the ring." He swallowed. "Before the longest night. So it knows where the middle is again." He looked at the long table. All sixty-one chairs, most of them sat in. "Who'll come?"

***

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Kotori, at the kitchen door with a pot, said: "I'm coming. I said."

Then Ice, in the first of the nine, put his feet down off the rung of the next chair. He didn't stand up. He didn't need to. He looked at Suzu across the banner room.

"Yes," he said.

Then Hyvred, beside him, without standing up either: "Me."

And then the pencil.

Kei had a sheet out at the head of the table. He'd drawn on it already; Ice could see from here. *THIS WINTER · the hill · Nobody alone · Threes at least.* And underneath, four names already, in four hands. Suzu's, small and neat. Kotori's, careful. Ice's own, which he'd written at the first dawn without anybody seeing. And under it, very small, a name in gold-brown ink, and a little drawing of a horn. Hyvred's. Written last night, Ice realised, while he was on the north road. Before she'd known he'd say yes.

Kei held out the pencil.

To the long table. Not to anybody. Just out, the way Ama Corrie held out the broom.

And it went round.

Ice didn't watch who took it. He didn't need to. He'd find out on the road. It wasn't his to say. He sat in the first of the nine and drank his tea, and listened to the pencil going round the long table. Hand to hand, chair to chair, with the scratch of it on the paper every so often, and sometimes a long pause and no scratch, and sometimes a laugh.

It went round all sixty-one. It took most of the morning.

At the end Kei rolled the sheet up without reading it out, and put it in his map case, and said: "September. We'll plan it in September. When Aurelie's here, and Vane, and Xav. With everybody." He looked at Suzu. "Thank you for asking."

"Good," said Suzu.

***

He read the last one twice. Not because of what it said. Because it was from somebody walking, on a road, fourteen days, toward a round town. Somebody who'd never asked anybody anything until a harbour wall at the third dawn.

He put the letters away.

***

He went up the two hundred and twelve at the second dusk with Hyvred.

He'd been up four times this month. Once with Jared and a pole. Once with Isa in her chair and Suzu at the other end. Twice with nobody, at the third dawn, to sit on the bench and look north. He'd never been up with her.

She went first. He went behind. Minding the third step.

At the top, the roof. The ring of flat stone round the eye. Kei's brass arm on the north side. And the bench, on the south side, pale ash, with seven iron rings round the back and a scar in the middle. *Somewhere* under it in blue. And a cat's-paw on the left arm.

They sat on it.

Close, the way it was built. And looked out. The round town lighting up below them. The seven roads going away to the edge of everything. The north road, ninety-four lamps, leaning. Less, Isa said, than Thursday; she'd said *not yet* to it. But leaning. And the south road, round the curve, with somebody at the milestone at the bottom of it who'd be there, she said, on the longest night.

And the sky, going from gold to dark all the way round.

Hyvred didn't say anything for a long time. She held his hand. She looked at everything, edge to edge, the way she did.

"Isa says it's where people come," she said, at last, "to say something they mean. Because you can see everything. So you know it's true."

"I know," said Ice. "She told me. On the stair."

Hyvred looked at him.

"Have you got something?" she said. "To say?"

***

He thought about it.

He thought about a door at the end of a long corridor, and a knock instead of a bell. A cup put down without looking. Somebody standing in a doorway saying *I wanted to see you the way I see you in there*. And him saying *okay, look*, and standing still so she could.

He thought about *next time*, and a girl who hadn't got any kueh. About the corridor being very long.

He thought about Alice and Milli on this bench, a week ago, picking a day in four minutes after years of *one day*. About Suzu at the head of the table, *this winter*, not *some year*. About Deston on a green with a candle. About Milli standing up at the long table and saying *we've picked a day*, and how it had stopped being theirs and become everybody's.

*It's catching*, Milli had said.

He'd never once in his life gone anywhere for somebody. He'd stayed. In Malacca, in a flat with a guest room and a spare room and a long corridor, and for years nobody his own age down his end of it who'd still be up. He'd let people come to him. Suzu at the bus station, Gabe on Christmas Eve, Deston on the six o'clock bus. Her, on a Saturday in June, with a bag on one shoulder, looking at both ends of the corridor at once.

He'd never once gone to them.

"Can I come and see you?" said Ice. "Out there."

***

Hyvred went completely still.

Not a little. All of her. On the bench, on the roof of the round town, with her hand in his. Her tail stopped. Her face stopped. The way his did when something hit him.

"In Singapore," said Ice. "Some time. If you—"

She hit him.

On the arm. Harder than usual. Not hard.

"*Not* some time," said Hyvred.

He looked at her.

"Not *some time*," said Hyvred. Her voice was doing something. She let it. "Milli stood up at the long table last Sunday and said *one day isn't a day*. Suzu stood up this morning and said *this winter*. Not *some year*." She was crying, a bit. She was laughing, a bit. Both. "Everybody in this town has spent the whole summer turning *some time* into a day. And you're sitting on the *Somewhere* bench, where people come to say things they *mean*, and you said *some time*—"

"Okay," said Ice.

"*Don't* say okay."

"A day," said Ice.

She stopped.

"Say a day," said Hyvred. Very quietly. "Properly."

***

So he said one.

A Saturday. In September. Before the north. Before Aurelie came to the gate and Xav came downstairs and the pencil's list turned into people on a road. A Saturday he'd take the six o'clock bus, the way Deston always had, the other way. Four hours. With the bumps after the toll and at the turn-off, which Deston had told him about so many times he knew where they were.

He said the date. On the bench. Out loud. Where you could see everything.

*It was theirs.* It didn't need writing down.

Hyvred looked at him for a long time.

"That's a day," she said.

"It's a day."

"You'll get the bus."

"Deston says there's a bump after the toll."

"There is," said Hyvred. "I felt it. In June. I could see both sides of the road when it happened." She wiped her face with the hand that wasn't holding his. "You'll see both sides of the road too. You always do."

"I'll look at the trees," said Ice. "At the edges. Nobody told you there were that many."

She made a sound. Very small, at the back of her throat. Like Ama Corrie's when he'd said *six*. Then she put her forehead against his shoulder, on the bench, on the roof, and stayed there.

"*Mrp*," she said, into his shoulder.

"I know," said Ice. "It's allowed."

***

They sat there till the stars came out.

Neither of them said anything else for a long time. There wasn't anything else that needed saying. The round town below them, lit. The fountain going; you could hear it from up here, very faint. Seven roads going away. Ninety-four lamps on the north road, leaning a little less than Thursday, a little more than the first dawn. And past them, past the last stone, past the edge, past the still water and three hundred stones, a hill in the dark with a ring of stones on it and nobody in it.

This winter.

He thought about a phone game with a stupid name. Twenty people at the front and nobody beside, and never once turning round. He thought about a fen, and a stone in the water, and learning to wait. About a cellar, and a bridge, and *show me how you swing it*. About standing in a doorway at the end of a corridor and letting somebody look.

He thought about *properly*.

Lucan had asked properly, on a bridge, in front of half a city. Deston had asked properly on a green at dusk with forty-three candles. Kei had asked Aurelie properly, at the mouth of the Undercut, *would you stand next to us*. Suzu had stood up at the head of a table and asked a room for the first time in his life. Kite had asked a woman at a milestone who'd stand next to her, and sat with her ten nights until she said *you*.

And he'd asked. On a bench. A day.

It wasn't hard. That was the thing he'd tell Gabe, out there, in the kitchen, at nine, with the kettle on. Gabe would cry, and then tell everybody within the hour. It wasn't hard at all. He'd thought it would be, his whole life. That asking was a thing other people did, people who weren't at the front. That if you were at the front, you didn't ask. You stood there, and things came at you, and you held.

It wasn't hard. You just said it, and then you waited. On the back foot. A breath.

And somebody answered.

***

When they came down the two hundred and twelve at the third dawn, minding the third step, the banner room was nearly empty.

Isa was asleep in her chair by the north door, with the blanket round her and the jar on the table in front of her, glowing through the clay. A row of Chernn's candles beside it, burnt low. Suzu on the stool beside her, awake, not saying anything. With the tall white candle from Tallow Green on his knee. Waiting to be lit, on a road, this winter.

Kotori in the kitchen door, with a tin of tea.

And Ama Corrie at the far end of the long table with her broom. Sunday. Doing the chairs, though she'd done them once already. Under, not round.

She stopped when she saw them come in. She looked at them over her spectacles. At the two of them, coming down off the roof at the third dawn, holding hands. Him with his face doing something, and not stopping it.

"You were up there a long time," said Ama Corrie.

"Yes."

"Saying something."

"Asking something," said Ice.

Ama Corrie looked at him for a long time.

"Properly?" she said.

"Properly," said Ice.

She didn't say anything else. She looked at him a moment longer. Then at Hyvred, with her horns gold in the light of Chernn's candles and her face wet. Then at the first of the nine, and the chair beside it that had been Bee's, both empty.

She went to them, with her broom. And swept under both chairs. Very carefully. Under, not round. With nobody in them.

When she'd done it, she stood beside the first of the nine for a moment, with the fen-reed broom in her hands, looking down the long table. The whole of it. Sixty-one chairs, and the chair by the north door with somebody asleep in it, and a jar.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie.

It sounded like *kept*.
