Chris had forty-one spreadsheets about a game he had never played.

He counted them on the ferry. He sat in the window seat on the seven o'clock boat from Batam to Singapore, on Monday the fourth of January, with his laptop open on his knees. The sea outside was flat and grey and full of container ships, and he scrolled through the folder he'd called *ELSEWHERE (not playing)*.

*Ice — forms and grades, day 1 to 50.*
*Ice — level times vs. other streamers.*
*Above-level bonus — observed multipliers.*
*Outnumbered bonus — ×1.5 confirmed (Ice, day 1).*
*Tally — top 100, daily snapshot.*
*VANTA — wipes (Drowned Choir).*
*Crimson Ledger — known accounts (412).*
*Nightjar — known members, kills, locations.*
*The Long Night — Unlit levels by town and hour.*
*Grades over 100% — all known cases (3).*

And so on. Forty-one of them. Seven weeks. Every evening after work, every weekend, every free minute. He'd sat in Ice's chat typing *i've made a sheet*, and *need more data*, and *it's on the public death log*. A hundred thousand people had started saying *Chris is making a sheet* as a joke, and it wasn't a joke. He was. He always was.

He'd never once put his thumb on the glass.

He closed the laptop. The ferry was coming into the harbour, past the cable cars and the cranes. He put the laptop in his bag and took out his phone and opened the forty-second spreadsheet. It was empty. He'd made it last night, at one in the morning, and given it a name, and not been able to write anything in it.

*ELSEWHERE (playing).*

***

The café was the one in the old cinema, on the fourth floor of the mall. Maigo had streamed from it on opening day, and Salty and Wendy played there every night. Seven of the poly lot had been in its projection booth since New Year's Day, eight in the morning.

Chris knew all of this. He had a sheet.

It was still different, walking in. The low blue light along the floor. The forty white eggs on their steps, each in its little curtained booth. The quiet, which was the quiet of forty people being somewhere else. A girl behind the counter in a grey polo shirt looked at his booking and said, "First time?"

"Yes," said Chris. And then, because he was Chris: "I've watched about four hundred hours of other people's first times. I have notes. I'm still nervous. Is that normal?"

The girl smiled at him, a bit helplessly. "Very normal."

"Good," said Chris. "I'll write that down."

He did, actually, on his phone, in the forty-second sheet. The first line.

*Day 1. Nervous. Normal (per staff).*

***

There were four of them coming in today. Chris had booked it. Of course he had.

Four eggs, in four cities, at the same minute, nine o'clock Singapore time. Him here, in the old cinema. Niko in Malaysia, two hours up the highway, in a café he'd said was *fine, quiet, smells like carpet*. Newish in Bosnia, where it was two in the morning. She'd said she didn't care, she'd have stayed up anyway, and also she was going to ragebait all of them the entire time to stay awake. And Winter in America, where it was eight o'clock the night before. She'd booked a café on the other side of her city because the nearer one was full, and she'd taken two buses, and she was annoyed about it in a way that made Chris very happy.

They'd played Apex together for over a year, the four of them, in various combinations, with Ice. Chris and Winter and Ice. Chris and Newish and Ice. Niko and everybody. Hundreds of nights. Thousands of rounds. Ice in the middle of all of it, doing something stupid on purpose to make them laugh, and then something brilliant on purpose to win.

And then Ice had gone into a swamp with a shield, and the four of them had watched, and watched, and watched.

[ Call · dumbo, Nikorasu, somebodynewish, Winter ]

"Is everyone lying down?" said Chris.

"I'm lying down," said Niko, in his chill voice, from Malaysia. "It smells like carpet."

"I'm lying down and it's two in the morning and I'm going to be so mean to all of you," said Newish. "Chris. Chris, your voice sounds weird in here. Have you always sounded like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like a podcast about trains."

"I'm lying down," said Winter, flatly. "I took two buses. Can we go."

Chris put his thumb on the glass.

***

It was raining. He'd known it would be. He wrote it down anyway, the moment he could, in his head, because he couldn't write in here. *Rain: confirmed. Smell: confirmed (wet wood).* Then he stood in the little wooden room for a minute and a half and did nothing at all, because it was real. He'd known it would be real. Everybody had said. He had a sheet. But knowing it and standing in it were two completely different rows.

He built himself as himself, because he'd never been anything else on a screen. He took the name he'd used for everything for years, *dumbo*. It went green, and he laughed out loud in the rain, because nobody else in the world had wanted it.

He took the longsword.

It was a plain one. Steel, straight, a simple crossguard, a leather grip. Nothing special about it at all. He'd decided which one he'd take three weeks ago and written it in a cell. *Longsword. Plain. Same as the Fernwick trainer's dummy sword, so the Falling Cut grade data transfers.* He'd been doing the Falling Cut in his head for seven weeks, every time Ice did it on stream. Back foot. Look through. Breathe out on the cut. He'd watched it four hundred times. He knew exactly what it was supposed to look like.

He opened the door.

[ Ferrowmere · the Iron Hills
Travellers: 122 ]

It was a mining town. Of course it was. He'd taken Mining and Engineering in the creator because the sheet said they paired well for anyone who wanted to build things. And the game had put him in a valley full of chimneys and slag heaps and rail tracks. Little carts of ore went up and down the hillsides on cables, creaking. There was a great water-wheel by the river, turning something inside a long brick shed that went *thump, thump, thump*. Everything smelled of hot iron and coal smoke and rain.

He stood in the doorway and looked at the cable-carts going up and down the hill. He felt something he hadn't felt in a long time. He didn't have a column for it.

"Oh," he said, out loud, to nobody. "Oh, I want to know how that works."

***

He found a training post in the yard behind the town's barracks. He did a Falling Cut on it.

[ Falling Cut · Beginner 23% ]

Twenty-three. He stood in the rain and looked at the number.

Ice had got thirty-one on his first try, on day one, in Hollis's yard. Suzu had got forty-one on his fourth, by doing it the way Ice did it. Chris had watched both of them. He knew exactly what they'd done. He'd charted it. And he'd just got twenty-three.

He did it again.

[ Falling Cut · Beginner 19% ]

Worse. He did it again. Twenty-six. Again. Twenty-one. He stood in the rain in the barracks yard with the plain sword and his numbers going nowhere. He felt the thing he always felt when code didn't work. Not anger. Interest. The sharp, specific, almost happy interest of something being wrong in a way he didn't understand yet.

*Okay*, he thought. *Debug it.*

He did it again, slowly, and watched himself. Not the sword. Himself. His feet, his hips, his shoulders, the way he'd watched Ice's for seven weeks. And he saw it.

He was doing the back foot early.

Ice did it inside the movement. *Inside the movement, not before it.* Ice had said it on stream a hundred times, on the walkway in the swamp, to forty people. Chris had written it down a hundred times. And every single time he did the cut, he was doing the back foot *first*. Then the cut. Two steps, in order, like lines of code. Because that was how he'd written it down. He'd written it as a list.

It wasn't a list. It was one thing.

He stood still for a moment in the rain. He thought about it, and then made himself stop thinking about it, which was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Then he did the cut. The back foot and the cut and the breath all at once, inside each other, the way you'd say a word you'd known all your life. Not two steps. One.

[ Falling Cut · Beginner 58% ]

He stood there. Then he sat down on the wet ground in the barracks yard, with the sword across his knees, and laughed until his eyes stung.

"*Chris?*" said Newish, on the call. "*Are you okay? You've been quiet for nine minutes. You're never quiet for nine minutes.*"

"I found a bug," said Chris.

"*In the game?*"

"In me," said Chris. "I'd written it down as a list."

There was a short silence on the call.

"*That's the most Chris sentence I've ever heard*," said Winter.

***

The others came and went on the call all morning.

Niko was in a river town in a gorge. Old stone bridges, very high, over very fast water. He'd taken a sword and a book, both. "Everybody's going to say that's a waste," he said, in his chill voice. "Train both and get good at neither." A pause. "I'm going to do it anyway. I want to see what happens in the middle." Chris made a note. He made a lot of notes about Niko. Niko was one of the very few people who'd known Ice longer than Chris had. Since a community server, when they were both teenagers, years before any of the rest of them. And he'd always done exactly this: picked the thing everyone said was a waste, and quietly made it work.

Newish was in a town by a lake, at two in the morning her time, ragebaiting all of them to stay awake. "Chris, your town sounds ugly. Iron Hills. Who wants iron hills. Niko, a *gorge*? That's just a hole with water in it." She was in a little brewing town full of orchards and beehives. She'd taken Foraging and Brewing, and a small hand drum off the rack, of all things. "It's for *tempo*," she said, with great dignity, when Winter asked. "The woman at the inn says you can play people faster. I want to play people faster." At four in the morning, her time, the innkeeper offered her a pork pie. She said, very politely, *no thank you, I don't eat pork*. The innkeeper said *ah* and brought her a cheese one instead, without any fuss, and remembered. Newish told them that, quietly, in a different voice from her ragebaiting voice. "She remembered," she said. "She'll remember tomorrow. They remember things here."

And Winter was somewhere cold.

She was the furthest north of any of them, Chris worked out later: a fishing village on a grey sea, under snow, with long black boats pulled up on the shingle and nets drying on frames. A girl called Winter in a town in the winter. Ice would have made the joke. Ice *did* make the joke, on the poly chat, ten minutes after she told them. Winter told him to shut up, flatly, and then laughed when she thought nobody could hear.

She'd built herself as her model. Two short black horns curving back from her head. Silver-white hair so long it went past her waist. One eye pale blue and one eye pink, calm and half-lidded. She'd taken a harpoon off the rack, of all things. She said she wanted to throw something heavy at something far away. Then she'd taken Fishing and Fletching, because the sea was there, and because she wanted to make her own.

"Drew made me watch it," she said, at one point, out of nowhere. "The church. On his stream. I said I wasn't interested. He said *just watch the first ten minutes*. I watched four hours and fifty-one." A pause. "I'm not saying he was right."

"He was right," said Chris.

"I'm not *saying* it."

***

He sat in the rain for a long time after that.

He'd had a sheet about it, for seven weeks. *Fernwick — anomalies*. Nine stones, eight facing in, one facing out. A ring of warm water in a cold fen. A man who walked in there every single morning and went dark before he got close. A hundred thousand people watching that man for seven weeks, and only one of them had seen the heron, and he'd never typed a word about it. Not in chat. Not on the forum. Not in a single cell of a single sheet that anybody else would ever read.

Some data, it turned out, wasn't for anything. You just kept it, because it was somebody else's.

***

At a quarter to three, his booking ended. He lay in the gel in the old cinema with the lid lifting and the blue light along the floor and opened the forty-second spreadsheet on his phone.

He'd thought he'd fill it with numbers. Grades and times and levels and rates, the way he'd filled the other forty-one. He'd planned the columns. He'd made a template.

He looked at it for a while, with the lid up. Then he deleted the template and wrote, in the first column, very simply, the things that had happened.

*Day 1. Nervous. Normal (per staff).*
*Rain. Wet wood. Real.*
*Name: dumbo. Nobody else wanted it.*
*Ferrowmere. Cable carts. Want to know how they work.*
*Falling Cut: 23, 19, 26, 21. Bug: it's not a list. 58.*
*Niko: sword + book. Going to make it work. (He will.)*
*Newish: the innkeeper remembered the pie.*
*Winter: Drew was right. (Not saying it.)*
*Ice: knows I know.*

He looked at it. It wasn't data. Not really. It was the most useful sheet he'd ever made.

On the call, Newish was saying goodnight, at six in the morning, with great drama. Winter was saying *finally*. Niko said nothing, and then, just before he went: "Kei messaged me. There's a call. Every night. Him and Maigo and Salty and some others." A pause. "He asked if we wanted to be on it. All four of us."

Chris looked at the forty-second spreadsheet on his phone.

"Yes," he said. "Obviously yes."

*Kei: a call. Every night. Yes.*

He saved it, and sat up, and went to catch the ferry home.
