Harlan found the smithy on the Tuesday, because Harlan found everything eventually, the way water finds the bottom of a hill.

Salty was at the grindstone when he came in. He'd been at it since the first dawn. The black bar, the length of a man, four hundred and eighty folds, lay along Joss's long bench on two trestles. He was taking the edge down it, a hand's width at a time, with the big wheel that Joss's grandfather had cut from a millstone. It was slow. The black iron ate grindstones. He'd gone through the fine side of Joss's wheel by Monday night and was on the rough side now. It sounded like somebody sharpening a mountain.

"Whoa," said Harlan, in the doorway.

Salty didn't stop. He'd learned that from Ice. If you stopped every time somebody said *whoa*, you'd never get anything done.

Harlan came in anyway. He had to duck under the lintel; he was even bigger close up. His great two-bladed axe was on his back and his blue was very clean for somebody who'd walked from the far side of the map. He stood by the bench and looked down the length of the bar, all of it, slowly, the way you'd look down a road.

"That's a sword," said Harlan.

"It will be."

"That's a *big* sword." He bent and looked along the edge where Salty had taken it down. It was black, and it didn't shine, even sharpened. "What is it? Nobody's selling anything like that on our side."

"Black iron," said Salty. "From under the round town."

"Can I touch it?"

"No."

"Fair," said Harlan, sounding exactly like Wendy. He pulled Joss's spare stool over with his foot and sat on it by the door, out of the way, with his elbows on his knees. And he watched.

Salty ground for an hour. Harlan didn't say anything for the whole hour. That surprised Salty, a bit. He'd thought the big man couldn't keep quiet for a minute; he'd seen him on the steps. But he sat on the stool by the door and watched the wheel and the sparks and Salty's hands. Old Joss, at the back in his chair, watched Harlan, and nodded slowly, as if he approved.

At the end of the hour Salty stopped to dress the stone.

"You're him," said Harlan. "Aren't you. The one on fifty."

Salty looked at him.

"I watched everything. After the duel. Every VOD, the whole guild, and everybody else's." Harlan shrugged. "There's a bit in the winter, on somebody's stream. Somebody says there's a big guy on the Grey Coast who turned his pain up to fifty because he heard Ice was on fifty. And he never made a sound when something with legs bit him." He looked at Salty's arms. "That's you."

"Yes," said Salty.

"Why fifty?"

Salty thought about it. He thought about how to say it. He wasn't good at saying things; he said them how they were, and people either liked it or didn't.

"Because he's at the front," he said. "If he's on fifty and I'm on twenty, I'm not standing where he's standing. I'm standing somewhere softer." He picked up the dressing iron. "I didn't want to stand somewhere softer."

Harlan didn't say anything for a long moment.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah. That's the GM. That's what he did to people." He laughed, quieter than on the steps. "Twenty of us. Every one of us had turned something up because of him. Not pain. That game didn't have pain. But something. Hours. Nerve. How stupid we'd be." He shook his head. "He never asked anybody to. Never once. He just stood at the front and you looked at where he was standing and thought, *I don't want to stand somewhere softer*."

Salty dressed the stone.

"He doesn't talk about it," he said. "Before. The other games. He doesn't say."

"No," said Harlan. "He never did. Even then. We'd win something enormous and he'd log off and go to bed." He looked at the black bar on the trestles. "There's people all over the place who know him, you know. Not just me. Half of Meridian's officers have either fought him or fought next to him, in something. There's a woman in our third Hall who still won't say his name. Out of respect, she says. I think it's fear." He grinned. "And the lead at Sable Court, out east. He was above GM on the board in that wind game. Top five. GM was top twenty. And the highest tank on the whole board. And Sable's lead *still* talks about him. Says the only fights he's ever lost on purpose were the ones where he could see GM was coming."

"What's Sable Court?"

"You'll hear," said Harlan. "Everyone's going to hear. They're nearly at fifty. First in the world, probably." He stood up. "Okay. I've got to go and lose an argument to your guild master. Thanks for letting me watch."

"You didn't ask."

"No," said Harlan. He stopped in the doorway, ducked under the lintel, and looked back. "Can I watch tomorrow?"

Salty looked at him for a moment.

"Ask Wendy," he said.

Harlan laughed all the way down the east road.

***

The argument was on the Wednesday, in the banner room, and Salty went because everybody went.

It wasn't really an argument. Salty had thought it would be, the way things were in films. A big guild with ten thousand people, a small guild with sixty, and a long table between them. But Harlan didn't sit across the long table. He came into the banner room at the second dusk, ducked under the door, and looked at the sixty chairs. Then he asked Ama Corrie, quite politely, if there was a chair he could have. She looked at him over her spectacles for a long time and brought him a stool from the kitchen. He sat on it at the end of Riverbank's table, next to Azac, who offered him bread.

Kei stood up at the head.

He didn't make a speech. Kei never made speeches. He held his map rolled in one hand and looked down the table at Harlan.

"We talked," said Kei. "Last night. All of us. Here."

"I know," said Harlan. "Your gate told me I wasn't allowed in."

"She was right," said Kei. "It was ours." He paused. "The first thing. The Carrow Cross chapter. Under Meridian. Keep our name, our Hall, everything, and you'd back us." He shook his head. "No. Thank you. But no."

Harlan nodded. He didn't look surprised.

"We're not anybody's chapter," said Kei. "We're not big. We don't want to be big. We're sixty. Every one of them said somebody's name at a gate." He looked down the table. "If we were under you, people would come to the gate because of you. Not because of somebody they'd stand next to. And it'd stop being what it is."

"Yeah," said Harlan. "Vane said you'd say that."

"And the second thing," said Kei. "You didn't say what the second thing was."

"No," said Harlan. "I saw GM and forgot." He grinned. "It was an alliance. For the advanced cities. You'd be on our side, in the zoo, when it opens. We'd be on yours."

Kei looked at him for a long time.

"No," he said again.

The banner room went quiet. Harlan's twelve, standing along the wall by the door in their blue, shifted.

"Not an alliance," said Kei. "We're not going to sign a paper that says we're on your side, before we've even seen what the sides are." He unrolled his map, just a little, at the corner, and looked at it, the way he did when he was choosing words. "But we'd like to be friends. That's different. If you need somebody to stand next to you in the advanced city, ask. And we'll come, if we can. And if we need somebody, we'll ask you." He rolled the map back up. "That's all. Friends. Ask me again when we're there, and I'll tell you if it's still true."

Harlan sat on his kitchen stool at the end of Riverbank's table and looked at Kei for a long time.

Then he laughed. Not the big one. A smaller one, surprised, and somehow pleased.

"That," said Harlan, "is the best *no* I've ever had."

"Is it a no?" said Kei.

"It's a yes to something I didn't ask for," said Harlan. "Which is better." He stood up, ducking a beam that wasn't there. He came down the long table and held out his hand to Kei at the head. "Friends."

"Friends," said Kei, and shook it.

And from somewhere down the table, very quietly, Ama Corrie said, "Hm," in a way that sounded like *kept*.

***

Harlan stayed four more days. He didn't need to. He said he was waiting for a letter. Salty thought he just liked it there.

He came to the smithy every morning. He asked Wendy first, every morning, at the gate. *Can I watch Salty?* She said yes, every morning, in a voice that made it clear she was letting him off. He sat on Joss's stool and watched the wheel. He told stories, when Salty stopped to dress the stone. Murderers of the Universe. A war with five thousand guilds. The GM who logged off and went to bed after every win. He never told the same one twice, and he never told one that made Ice look anything but what he was, which Salty noticed and liked him for.

He told them to Joss as well. Joss listened to all of them from the back of the forge, in his chair, nodding. Salty wasn't sure the old man understood any of it. He didn't think it mattered.

On the Friday the letter came.

Harlan read it on the stool by the door, in the forge-light, and laughed out loud. Then he read it again, and didn't laugh. Then he got up and went out without saying anything, and Salty heard him walk across the round market toward the Hall.

He came back an hour later. Ice was with him.

"Show him," said Harlan.

Ice looked at him.

"The letter. Show Salty. He's been here all week. He's earned it." Harlan held it out, folded. "Vane told Aurelie. Last night. Aurelie wrote back. This is for you."

Ice took the letter. He read it. His face did nothing. Then he held it out to Salty, without a word, the way you'd hand somebody something you didn't know what to do with.

Salty read it.

Tell him I'd like to see for myself.

Salty read it twice and handed it back.

"Okay," said Ice, to the letter.

"*Okay*," said Harlan. "He says *okay*. The guild master of the biggest guild in the game says *come and see me*, and he says *okay*." He put his hands on his head. "You haven't changed at all, GM."

"I'm not the GM."

"You'll always be the GM," said Harlan. "Even when you're not. *Especially* when you're not."

Ice didn't argue. He folded the letter very small and put it in his pocket, next to whatever else was in there. Then he looked down the long bench at the black bar on its trestles. The edge was all the way down it now. Black, dull, the length of a man, sharpened. It looked like nothing on earth.

"When?" he said.

"Tonight," said Salty. "Third dawn."

"Can I come?"

Salty looked at him. Ice had never once asked to come to anything. He went, or he didn't, or he stood at the side near the top and watched.

"Yes," said Salty.

***

There were seven of them in the fourth tunnel at midnight. Salty hadn't asked anybody else, but people had come anyway, the way they did.

Wendy, with the lamp. Ice. Drew and Ina and Gabe, carrying the bar between them on two poles, because it was too heavy for one person to carry any distance. Even Salty, even at his strength, would have taken all night. And Harlan, who had asked Wendy at the gate, *can I come to the tunnel*, with his hands together like a boy asking for sweets. She'd said, *fine. Don't talk.* And he hadn't. Not once. Not past the sleeping Delvers, not in the cold, not on the long way down into the dark.

They laid the bar down along the stream.

It was just long enough. The little stream ran along its groove in the floor of the tunnel, a hand deep and a few hands wide. Black water in a black tunnel where nothing had ever been lit. It had cut its way along in a straight line for a long way here, as if it knew where it was going. The bar lay beside it, on the stone. Salty knelt.

He'd brought the heat with him. That had been the hard part, and Ina had solved it, the way Ina solved things, with words. A long iron trough on legs, full of the forge's own coals, carried down on two more poles by Gabe and Drew. Ina walked beside it with his wand, Kindling it every two hundred paces so it didn't die, saying the one word under his breath, over and over. It was white-hot by the time they got to the bottom. The air round it shook.

They lifted the bar into it with the poles. All four of them. The black iron went into the white coals and lay there, and slowly, the way it always did, it began to glow. Not bright. That sullen deep red, like something angry under a lid.

They waited.

"Now," said Ina.

They lifted it out on the poles. Glowing, enormous, the length of a man. The heat came off it like a wall, and the cold of the tunnel came round it like water round a stone. They carried it the three paces to the stream.

"Lamp," said Salty.

Wendy put it out.

It was dark. Properly dark. Darker than anywhere. The only thing in the world was the red glow of the bar on the poles, a long smear of it in the black, and seven people breathing.

"Down," said Salty.

They lowered it into the water.

***

It didn't hiss. Not the way the test piece had. The test piece had sighed. This *sang*.

A long low sound came up out of the stream as the red went into it. It started on one note and went down, and down, and down. It filled the whole tunnel, all the way up to the Seven Below and all the way down to wherever the stream went. It was like a great bell being struck under water, very far away. It went on for a long time. Longer than any quench should. Salty knelt in the dark with his hands on the poles and felt it come up through the iron into his arms, into his teeth, into his chest. He didn't let go.

The red went out. Slowly, from the ends inward. Until there was only a little line of it in the middle of the bar, under the water, like an ember. And then that went too.

Black. The sound of the stream. Seven people not breathing.

He waited. He'd heard the test pieces crack. Nineteen of them, on Joss's windowsill. A small clean sound, like a fingernail on glass. He waited for it in the dark with his whole body.

It didn't come.

"Wendy," said Salty. His voice was very odd. "Light it."

She lit it.

The bar lay in the stream. Whole. The length of a man, the water running over it and round it, black on black. No line on it. No crack. Nothing. And over it, in the cold air of the deep tunnel, two windows.

[ Blacksmithing · Quench (in the dark) · Grade 83% ]

[ Black iron · Hardened · *It was never meant to see the light. It does not mind, now, if you carry it there.* ]

Nobody said anything.

Then Gabe said, "Oh my *God*," quite loudly, and the Delvers woke up, a long way back up the tunnel, and snuffled.

***

He lifted it out himself.

He hadn't been able to, before. Not on his own. Not the bar. But he put his hands under it in the water and lifted. It came up out of the stream, dripping, heavy, so heavy. And it was the right weight. It was exactly the right weight. It was as if the quench had done something to it, or to him, he couldn't tell which. He stood up in the fourth tunnel with a sword the length of a man in his two hands, black, dripping, not finished. No hilt, no grip, no name. Just the blade, the shape of the thing he'd wanted since the first day in Grimmet on a black cliff over a grey sea.

He held it out in front of him, level, the way you'd hold out something to show somebody.

It ended where he ended.

He didn't know how else to say it. He'd never be able to say it right, afterwards, to Wendy or anyone. He'd held swords for ten months that ended a little before he did. Like shoes a size too small. This one ended exactly where his arms and his back and his legs ended. Where the swing would end, if he swung it. As if it had been measured off him. As if it had been down here in the dark for three thousand years waiting for somebody the right size.

Nobody spoke. Ina had his mask in his hand. Drew was watching Salty's feet. Gabe had both hands over his mouth. Harlan, at the back, by the cold wall, had gone completely silent for the first time all week, which nobody would ever have believed.

Ice stepped forward.

He didn't say anything. He looked at the blade. Up and down the length of it, black, dripping, in Wendy's lamplight. He looked at Salty holding it. At the way he was standing. At where the blade ended.

"It's the right size," said Ice.

"Yes," said Salty.

That was all. It was everything. Ice nodded, once, the way he nodded when something was right. Then he stepped back to where he'd been, by the stream, out of the way.

Wendy put the lamp down on the stone and came and put her hand flat on Salty's chest, the way she had on the road the first time they'd met in the game. Eighteen inches. Eleven days. A step.

"Don't drop it on your foot," she said.

***

Harlan left on the Sunday, by the west road, under his great blue banner with the gold line down it.

He stopped at the gate on the way out. Wendy was there. She was always there.

"Thank you," he said. "For asking. All week."

"It's my job."

"Yeah." Harlan looked up the steps of the Hall, at the side near the top, where Ice was standing out of the way. "Look after him."

"He looks after us," said Wendy.

"Yeah," said Harlan. "He always did. That's why somebody has to." He lifted a hand to Ice. Ice lifted one back. Then Harlan turned round and looked at Salty, who was standing by the fountain with the black blade wrapped in sacking on his back. Not finished. A long way from finished. Already the right size.

"Name it something good," said Harlan.

"I haven't decided."

"Don't call it anything with *murder* in," said Harlan. "One of those is enough for one guild." And he laughed, the big one, and went off down the west road with his twelve, toward the far side of the map. The banner went round the curve of the road and was gone.

The round market was quiet.

Salty stood by the fountain for a while. He felt the weight on his back. He thought about the fourth tunnel, and the stream, and the long low sound like a bell under water. And about nineteen black pieces in a row on Joss's windowsill, which he wasn't going to throw away.

Ice came down the steps and stood beside him. Not close. Near.

"You never said," said Salty. "About the guild. The murder one. Or the war. Or any of it."

"No."

"Why not?"

Ice thought about it. He looked at the west road, where the banner had gone.

"Because it was somewhere else," he said. "And this is here."

Salty nodded slowly. He understood that. He didn't know why he understood it, exactly, but he did. Grimmet was somewhere else. The Grey Coast and the shaft mouths and the first Long Night with something with legs biting him at fifty per cent. Somewhere else. And this was here. The round town and the fourth tunnel and Wendy at the gate and a blade the right size on his back.

"I'm going to finish it," he said. "Before the longest night."

"I know," said Ice.

"I want to stand somewhere at the front with it."

Ice looked at him for a long moment.

"You always do," he said. "You just never had the right size."
