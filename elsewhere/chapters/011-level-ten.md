The bank had given him the look on Tuesday.

Vivian Ong had warned him it would, and it did. A very polite woman in a very cold room with a plastic orchid on her desk had asked what the company did, and Ice had started to explain. Before he'd finished the second sentence, Vivian had slid a single typed page across the desk: *Nature of business: trading in digital goods and in-game items through a licensed platform; provision of services related thereto.* The very polite woman had read it, and looked at him, and looked at Vivian, and given him the look anyway. Then she'd opened the account.

The paid-up capital went in that afternoon. Two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit, out of his own account and into the company's, by a transfer that took eleven seconds. Vivian had filed something she called a *return of allotment of shares*. She'd explained that the law gave them fourteen days to tell SSM about the new shares and she'd do it on Thursday, "because I don't need fourteen days, Mr Declan, and neither does SSM, whatever it thinks."

And then, at the end of the afternoon, sitting in his car outside the bank with the engine running for the air-con, he'd opened the ELSEWHERE app, and the Exchange wallet, and pressed the button he hadn't pressed for a week.

[ Withdraw to bank: US$12.60 (RM 52.92)
To: MOONLIGHT HARVEST WORKSHOP SDN. BHD. ]

He'd watched it go. Then he'd sent Vivian a screenshot.

*Noted,* she'd replied. *First line of the books. I've written "silverleaf" in the description. I hope that's correct.*

It was correct.

The pods came on Wednesday at nine.

The same two men who'd carried his own up the stairs at home ten days ago carried these through the roller shutter in Melaka Raya. They recognised him. One of them said, "Again, boss?" and Ice said, "Again," and the man laughed and said that they'd delivered forty this week in Malacca alone, and that half of the people they'd delivered to were "your age, boss, with the hair".

They put them in the back room along the long wall, side by side, with a gap between for a person. He'd measured it right. There was room for a little table, and he'd bought a kettle on the way. It sat on the floor in its box, because he hadn't bought the table yet.

When the men had gone he stood in the back room for a while and looked at them.

Two white eggs, side by side, with their smoked-glass lids up on their long arms, humming very faintly. Neither was registered. Each would wait for its first fingerprint, and keep it. One was for a man arriving on a bus tomorrow afternoon with his own pillow. The other was for somebody he didn't know yet. Somebody who'd need it someday.

He didn't get in either of them. They weren't his.

He locked the shutter, drove home, and climbed into his own egg in the spare room at a quarter to eight. On the way past the fridge he touched the certificate under the durian magnet.

"I said *probably*," said Ice. "Morning, everyone. Probably."

It wasn't probably. He knew exactly how far he had to go. Somewhere around the middle of the afternoon, if nothing went wrong, if they fought the way they'd been fighting all week. He'd known since last night. He'd lain in bed doing the sum, the way he'd done the sum about the pod.

Ten was the gate. Everybody knew that by now. At ten, the roads out of the beginner towns opened. You could walk out of your swamp, or your forest, or your field, toward the intermediate towns, which nobody had seen yet except on a few big streams. At ten, according to those streams, the game looked at you for the first time and told you what you were.

He'd watched four people get their classes that week, on four different streams with ten thousand viewers each. A woman with a spear in a mountain town had become a *Vanguard*. A man with a bow in a forest had become a *Skirmisher*. Somebody with a staff, in a city on a cliff, had become a *Hedge Mage* and been very upset about it for an hour. Chris had kept a list.

He hadn't watched those streams for the classes, though. He'd watched them for the faces. Every one of them, when the window came up, had gone very still. Like someone being told something about themselves they hadn't known.

"I'm only here till two," he said. "Then I'm doing a full past paper. Timed. No pod. Xav's going to text me every hour to make sure."

The thing about the last stretch to ten was that it was exactly as hard as everything before it, only more.

The rats on the south walk gave him nothing now. He was five levels above them, and the game didn't care how many he killed. The leeches gave him almost nothing. The things that paid were further out, in the deep water past the ninth heron's line. Fen stalkers, long and low, which came in threes and bit like the leeches but didn't let go. Bog lurkers, which they left alone when they could. And the thing that had made all of it possible all week: the narrow places, and the outnumbered line.

They'd been doing it for four days now, and they were good at it.

Ice found a place in the reeds where the water was only knee-deep and the clumps grew thick on either side, so that anything coming at him had to come through a gap two metres wide. He set his feet. He sloped the shield. He banged the flat of the sword on the rim.

Six things came. Three fen stalkers. Two leeches. And, from the deep water behind them, very slowly, a seventh: a Fen Stalker that was bigger than the others, nearly the size of the lurker, with a ragged fin down its back.

[ Fen Stalker Matriarch (Lv 11) · Above your level ]

"Oh," said Deston, very quietly.

"It's fine," said Ice. "It's fine. Seven. Dest, sing."

Deston sang.

It went out of him the way it always did now, low and sure, *Let the MANY move as ONE*, and Ice felt the count settle on him, felt Xav already moving at his right. And then Deston's voice dropped into the second line, the one that went down at the end like a bass part settling, *What one BEARS, two can CARRY.* And he said "Ice", and Ice felt something on his shoulders get lighter.

The stalkers hit the gap all at once.

He didn't think much after that. That was the strange thing about a good fight: there wasn't room to think in it, only to do. The slope came up and the first stalker went past it, off-line, and Xav was there. Then the next, and the next. A leech came at his shins and he turned it into the reeds. The matriarch came through the middle of the gap with its ragged fin up, and he didn't turn that one. He couldn't. It was too big, and it hit the slope straight on and drove him back a full step through the water.

He felt it land in his chest, a great flat bruising weight at fifty per cent. Behind him, in the middle of the line, he heard Deston's voice catch, just for a fraction of a beat, on the third of it Deston was carrying.

He didn't make a sound. He set his feet again.

"*Two*," said Deston, out loud, in the middle of the song, and Ice understood: Xav's second. Xav went round. Ice held the matriarch's attention for one whole second with the slope and the edge of the sword, and Xav came out of the reeds behind it, where it couldn't see.

[ Twin Cut · Grade 71% · Unseen ×1.5 · 24 damage ]

It took four minutes. It felt like ten. At the end, Ice was at thirty-one per cent and had drunk two Good draughts, and Deston was white and holding his own shoulder and still singing. Xav was standing in the water with both blades down and his chest going up and down, looking, for once, like a man who had just done something hard.

The matriarch went last. It came at him one final time through the gap, slow and furious, turning left, and he brought the Falling Cut down across the back of its head and breathed out on the cut, the way Suzu had told him.

It broke into grey dust.

[ Ding.
Ice has reached Level 10.
The roads are open. ]

It was gold-edged, like the others. He'd seen nine of them now. He read it and felt his chest go tight.

And then, before he could say anything, before he could turn round to the other two, the window closed by itself, slowly, and a different one opened in its place.

It was bigger. It was plain, with no gold on it at all, just white text on something like dark water.

[ Elsewhere has been watching how you carry yourself. ]

He waited.

[ You took up a sword and a shield.
You learned to turn a blow, not stop it.
You stood at the front, and you did not flinch.
You held the narrow places, and others came through them because you were there. ]

[ Class: Holdfast
One who holds. Where a Holdfast stands, a line exists. ]

He read it. He read it again.

He didn't know what he'd expected. Something like *Vanguard*, maybe. Something with a sword in it. Something that sounded like a tank in any other game. He hadn't expected to feel *seen*, as if the game had been sitting on a stone at the edge of the fen all week, watching him set his feet in a gap in the reeds, and had finally decided what to call it.

*You did not flinch.* It had noticed.

He stood there in the knee-deep water among the grey dust, very still. He felt the way the people on the big streams had looked.

Behind him, Deston said, carefully, "Dec?"

"Holdfast," said Ice.

He turned round. Deston was standing with the book against his chest. Xav was beside him, with his daggers sheathed and his face doing the thing it almost never did.

"I'm a Holdfast," said Ice. "It says. *Where a Holdfast stands, a line exists.*"

Deston's face went through several things at once, and settled on the biggest grin Ice had ever seen on it.

"*Obviously*," said Deston.

"That's you," said Xav. "That's literally you. That's every game."

"It said I didn't flinch."

"You don't," said Deston. "You never do." He paused. "I've been *feeling* it, Dec. All week."

Hollis was on his bucket in the militia yard, mending the same dummy he'd been mending for ten days. Ice was fairly sure he unpicked it every night so he'd have something to do in the morning.

He looked up as Ice came through the gap in the fence, and looked at him for a long time without saying anything. Then he looked up, at the air above Ice's head, where presumably there were now two words where there had been one.

"Ten," said Hollis.

"Ten."

"Holdfast." He said it slowly, as if tasting it. "My old captain was one. In the war in the hills, before your lot were born. He held a ford for a day and a half with nineteen men and a broken arm." He put the dummy down. "He was a stubborn old ass. He never made a sound when he was hurt. Used to drive the surgeon mad."

Ice didn't say anything.

"Roads are open," said Hollis. "You're going?"

"Not yet."

Hollis's eyebrows went up a very small amount.

"Not without my friends," said Ice. "They're nearly there. A few days. And there's a friend coming tomorrow who's starting from nothing." He shrugged. "We go together, or I'm just a man walking down a road."

Hollis looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and picked the dummy back up.

"Good," he said. "A holdfast who walks off on his own isn't one. He's just a man with a shield." He found his needle. "Go on, then. And show me that Anchor some time, before you go. I want to see if you do it like the captain did."

He did something else, before he logged out that night, that he hadn't told chat about. He took a single coin out of his pouch and held it up to the lantern on Sedge's porch.

He'd crossed it that afternoon, somewhere between selling Fair draughts on his crate and paying Rafi for his sack: a hundred silver. The game had quietly taken his hundred silver coins and handed him one gold one instead, and he hadn't even noticed until he went to count. It was small and thick and heavy for its size, stamped on one side with a heron and on the other with a crown, and it caught the lantern light and held it.

One gold. About ten US dollars, at today's price on the Exchange.

Ten days. Fifty draughts a day, and a boy with a sack, and a hundred and something people watching. One coin.

"That's slow," he said to Sedge, who was sitting in her doorway behind him with a pipe she never lit.

"Money's always slow," said Sedge. "The first coin. The second's quicker." She looked at it in his fingers. "You'll want more than one, for where you're going."

"I know."

"Five of you, my mother used to say about the stones. Five hands for the door under the fen." She said it without looking at him, as if it were the weather. "And none below fifteen."

He went very still.

"You knew," he said.

"I'm old, boy, I'm not blind. You've come back from the north with your far-glass off four times, looking like you'd seen a ghost. There's only one thing north of the ninth heron." She took the pipe out of her mouth and looked at it. "My mother went in once. When she was young. With four others. She never said what was in there. She only said they sang."

He didn't say anything.

"Go to bed," said Sedge. "You've got a friend coming."

[ Rest well, Ice. ]

He lay in the dark of the spare room for a long time after, in the egg, with the lid up and the fan turning. He didn't get out.

*Where a Holdfast stands, a line exists.*

Tomorrow at four, a bus from Singapore would pull into Melaka Sentral with Suzu on it. Suzu with a passport, and a pillow, and a contract he hadn't signed yet. Up the road, in the guest room, the bed was made with the corners folded in and the fan was going, so the room would be used to it.

He got up eventually and went downstairs and drank a glass of water at the kitchen counter, standing, in the dark. He touched the certificate under the durian magnet on his way past, and went to bed.
