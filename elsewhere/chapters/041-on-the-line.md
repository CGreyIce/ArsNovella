Kei had bought the passes four minutes after the window went up on Wednesday.

He'd been in Ysolde's stillroom when it came, measuring something. He'd read it once: *the dark will not lift when it should*. He'd put down the vial and opened the Cradle Commons site on his phone, in the other room of his head. And there it was already, a new banner at the top, the studio moving faster than anybody: *LONG NIGHT PASSES · 12 hours overnight · Monday 21 December · Limited*. He'd bought six. Australia, Singapore, Singapore, Singapore, Vietnam, England. He'd paid for all of them, and told nobody what it cost. By the time he'd closed the site, the banner said *SOLD OUT*.

Ina had found out anyway, on Thursday, and sent him a single message.

*You absolute saint.*

***

At eleven o'clock on Monday night, his time, he lay down in an egg in a pod café in Australia with the call already open.

[ Call · KeiKatamori, Maigo, Salty, itswendy, Inaxho, HakoP ]

"Roll call," said Kei.

"Orchard End!" said Maigo, immediately. "Here! In a tree!"

"Grimmet," said Salty. "Here."

"Ashcombe," said Wendy. "Here. Hester says hello. She didn't say that. She grunted."

"Otterbank," said Hako, from Vietnam, sounding like it was Christmas morning. "Here. There's an otter on the jetty and it's looking at me. I think it knows."

"Emberly," said Ina, from a booth in England at midday, in the voice of a man who'd had a very good lunch. "Present. Sitting by the fire. The boy who feeds it has gone home to his mother because he's nine. So I'm the boy who feeds it."

"Hallowell," said Kei. "Here."

Six. He lay in the gel and listened to them being there. Six towns, a long way apart. Two of them were eighteen inches apart in a cinema in Singapore, and eleven days apart in the game.

"Okay," he said. "Same as Sunday. Everybody keeps their own. Nobody hangs up. If it goes wrong anywhere, say so, and we'll all know." He put his thumb on the glass. "Lights on."

***

Hallowell at dusk smelled of hot stone and iron and lamp oil.

Every lamp in the town was lit. Ysolde had them set round the spring basin at the top of the hill, a ring of them on the old stone rim, forty or fifty, flickering in the steam. More along the channels that ran down through the streets, and in every window of every bathhouse on every terrace. The steam came up off the spring in the cold evening air, thick and white and slow, and the lamplight got caught in it. The whole hilltop glowed like a lantern itself.

Ysolde was on the rim of the basin in her stained green apron, with a lamp in one hand and a long iron poker in the other. Thirty people from the town stood round the rim beside her, each with a lamp. Bathhouse keepers. The man who sold towels. Two old women who sat in the water every morning for their knees. And a handful of travellers, nine or ten, who'd come to Hallowell for the springs and stayed for this.

"Where do they come from?" said Kei, standing beside her. "Here."

Ysolde looked down into the spring. The green steaming water, very deep in the middle, where it came up out of the hill.

"From the bottom," she said. "Where it's hottest." She tightened her grip on the poker. "They don't like the heat. But they like the light more."

The sun went down.

[ The Long Night has begun.
Keep your lights. Keep your towns. ]

***

They came up out of the spring.

He'd half known they would. He'd still never seen anything like it. The green water in the middle of the basin bulged, slow and smooth, and the steam over it darkened. Then something came up through the steam that the steam didn't move round: long, thin and wet, the colour of no colour, with its head hanging down. Then another. Then six. They came up out of the hottest water in Hallowell and waded toward the ring of lamps on the rim. The water hissed where they touched it, like a cold knife going into a pan.

[ The Unlit · Lv 9 ]

Ysolde put her poker straight through the first one's neck before it reached the rim. It came apart into black water and fell back into the spring with a hiss, and she didn't even look at it.

"Forty years I've kept this spring," she said, to nobody, and hit the next one.

Kei had his knife. He had three flasks on his belt, too, which he'd made that afternoon and hadn't told Ysolde about. Steadying Draughts, measured to the drop, but thinned a little further, so that a mouthful would do instead of a vial. The Unlit hit cold. Everybody said so, from every town, on every stream. Cold that went into your arm and stayed. A Steadying Draught steadied a heart, and a steadied heart warmed you from the inside.

He went round the rim of the basin all evening with the flasks. A mouthful each for anyone who'd been hit and gone pale and shaky. He put his knife into anything that got past Ysolde, which wasn't much.

And he listened.

***

That was the real job. He'd known it was the real job on Sunday, when they'd all waited to hear what he thought. He kept Hallowell, because Hallowell was his. But mostly he lay in an egg in Australia and listened to six towns at once, the way he'd listened to them all week. Six towns being kept, all at the same moment, all over a world nobody had a map of.

Maigo was in a tree.

He'd said he would be, and he was: up in the Old Pippin at the top of the orchard, in the half that was still alive. Mother Quince had hung a lantern on every branch, the living and the dead. And the Unlit had come up out of the mill pond at the bottom of the hill and walked up through the terraces of apple trees, straight past the living trees with their lanterns. Straight to the Old Pippin. Straight to the dead branches.

"They want the dead ones," said Maigo on the call. He sounded out of breath and completely happy. "They go for the lanterns on the dead branches first. Every time. Like they know they're easier." A pause, a crack of wood, a grunt. "They're not having them. Mother Quince hung them. *Hold*."

Kei heard the word go out. Not a spell this time. Just Maigo, saying it to the tree.

Hako was in a river.

He was up to his waist in the shallows below Otterbank's jetty with his spear, the way he'd stood every morning for a week to spear fish. Things came up out of the deep water in the middle of the river toward the lanterns along the jetty, and he narrated.

"And here," he said, very softly, "we see the young spearman. Waiting. In the cold. The Unlit approaches. It does not see him. It sees only the light." A splash. A grunt. "The Unlit has been returned to the dark. The young spearman is very pleased with himself." Another splash. "There is another one. The young spearman is slightly less pleased."

Salty was in a mine.

He didn't say much. Salty never did. But they could hear it. Grimmet had lit its mine all the way down, every shaft, every gallery, lamps on hooks all along the rock walls going down into the mountain. And the Unlit didn't come from the sea, or the cliff. They came up the shafts, out of the deep places in the rock where the water collected. Salty stood at the top of the main shaft, where it opened onto the cliff, with his greatsword. He'd put the pain up to fifty, because Ice was on fifty. They heard the things come up, and the scrape of his feet on the rock, and the great heavy *whumph* of the sword. They never once heard Salty.

"Salty," said Wendy, every twenty minutes or so. "Okay?"

"Okay," said Salty.

And Ina was by a fire.

***

"They're coming from everywhere," said Ina, at one in the morning, Kei's time.

He said it calmly. He'd been quiet for a while, feeding the great forge. Kei could hear it all the time under everything else on the call: a deep steady roar, like a waterfall, like wind in a chimney. Three hundred years, it had been burning. Brannagh had said to feed it till it roared, so it could be seen from the plains. Ina had been feeding it since sunset.

"What do you mean, everywhere?" said Kei.

"I mean I'm standing on the top of the hill, by the forge. I can see the whole plain from here. All of it, for miles, in the dark. And there are farms down there, and villages, little ones, with their own lights. And the Unlit are coming up out of the rivers and the ponds and the wells all over the plain." A pause. "And they're not going to the farms."

"Where are they going?"

"Here." The roar of the forge. "To the brightest light. It's the brightest light for fifty miles. They're walking straight past the farms, past the villages. Hundreds of them. Across the whole plain, in lines. Coming up the hill. To me."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Ina," said Kei carefully. "Do you need—"

"No. Listen." His voice had gone very clear. The one he used for the good words. "Kei. Don't you see what it's doing? It's not just the forge. It's *why* they feed it. Brannagh said, *so it can be seen from the plains*. I thought she meant so people could see it. So they'd know Emberly was still here." The forge roared. "She didn't. She meant so *they* could. So the dark would see it. So everything that comes up out of the ground on the longest night would walk past the farms and the villages and the little lights in the windows, and come here instead. To the forge. Where there's a wall, and a hill, and a lot of people with hammers."

Kei lay in the egg in Australia and understood it.

"It's a lure," he said.

"It's a lure. The whole town is a lure. That's what Emberly's *for*." Ina laughed, very quietly. "Three hundred years. Every longest night, they feed it till it roars, and they stand on the hill, and they take it all. So nobody down there has to."

"How many of you are there?"

"Forty-ish. Brannagh. The smiths. Some travellers." A pause. "Me."

"Ina—"

"I'm fine." The forge roared. And then Kei heard something he hadn't heard all week. A small sound, close to Ina's breath. Wood on cord, the click of something being put on. "I'm putting the mask on."

Nobody said anything.

They all knew the mask. The grey oni mask with the red-rimmed eyes and the wide fanged grin and the red cords hanging from the mouth. Ina had worn it on his model for years and had taken it as his keepsake on the first morning. He'd hung it at his shoulder all week where he could reach it, and never once worn it on his face.

"It doesn't do anything," said Ina. "It's a keepsake. It doesn't make the fire hotter or anything stupid." His voice came out a little different now, behind the mask. Lower. "But I'm on the top of a hill in front of a fire that's been burning three hundred years. Everything in the dark for fifty miles is walking up the hill toward me. And I'd like it, when it gets here, to see something with teeth."

"*Kindle*," he said.

They heard it go. A sound like a door to a furnace opening.

***

At half past one, the clamps started going out in Ashcombe.

Kei heard it in Wendy's voice before she said anything. She'd been talking all evening in short practical sentences, the way she did when she was concentrating. *One on the left. Got it. Hester's on the far clamp. Another one.* Every twenty minutes, *Salty, okay?* And then, at half past ten, she stopped.

"Wendy?"

"The far clamp's gone."

Her voice was very level. Too level.

"Hester's burner went down. Something came out of the deep of the wood and he went down and the clamp went out. That's three out." A breath. "There's four left. Hester's on one. There's two burners on the others. I'm on the last. And there's something—" She stopped.

"What is it?"

"It's big." She said it very quietly. "It's between the trees. Over there. It's taller than the first branches. The others were tall but this is—it's *walking*. Through the Greatwood. Toward the clamps. And it's not in a hurry."

On the call, everything had gone quiet. Maigo had stopped humming. Hako had stopped narrating. Even the forge seemed quieter.

"Wen," said Salty.

It was the first word he'd said in twenty minutes that wasn't *okay*. He was still at the top of his shaft. They could hear it. The scrape of something coming up the rock below him, and his breathing, short, and the sword. He couldn't go. Grimmet was eleven days from Ashcombe. If he left the shaft, the mine went dark.

And in a cinema on the fourth floor of a mall in Singapore, in booth sixteen and booth seventeen, two eggs sat side by side, eighteen inches apart, humming. He couldn't reach her. He was right there and he couldn't reach her.

"I'm here," said Wendy.

"What've you got?"

"Bow. Nineteen arrows. They're ugly." A pause. "I've been shooting it. They go in and it doesn't care. It's still walking. It's going for Hester's clamp. Hester's got a stave and a lamp and she's sixty-something and she's just *standing* there." Wendy's voice cracked very slightly. "I can't— I keep aiming. I keep trying to get it right. I keep holding it till it's—"

"Wen," said Salty. "Stop."

She stopped.

"You're holding it," said Salty.

He said it the way he said everything. Plainly, bluntly, as if it were the weather. Somewhere on a black cliff on the other side of the world something was coming up a mine shaft at him, and he said it anyway.

"You're waiting for it to be right. It's not going to be right. It's a big thing in the dark in a forest. It's never going to be right." A grunt, and the *whumph* of the sword, and something coming apart. "You told me. On Saturday. On the log. You decide, and you let go. You don't wait." A breath. "So don't wait. Let go."

Nobody on the call breathed.

They heard her draw. They'd all learned the sound of it, this week, on her stream: the creak of the bow, the little intake of breath. They heard her not hold it.

They heard the arrow go.

There was a long, terrible second of nothing. Then a sound none of them had heard before, from somewhere deep in a forest on the far side of the world: low, huge, like a tree coming down a long way off. And then Hester's voice, close to Wendy's, an old woman's voice, hoarse and fierce.

"*Again, girl!*"

She let go again. And again. Decide, draw, go. They heard every one. On the fourth, the low huge sound came again, closer, and then there was a splash. An enormous slow splash, as if a whole tree had fallen into a river. And then nothing at all but the crackle of a fire.

"Wendy?" said Kei.

"It's down." She sounded very far away. "It went into the stream. It came apart. It's water. Hester's clamp's still lit." A breath that shook. "Mine's still lit."

"Good," said Salty. And then, quieter, the way he never said anything: "Good. Good."

"I let go," said Wendy.

"I know," said Salty. "I heard."

On the call, Maigo started crying, loudly and happily and without any embarrassment at all, in a tree.

***

At two Kei went round the rim again with the last of the flasks, and counted lamps, and listened.

Six towns. All lit. Not all of every light; nobody had that. Ashcombe had lost three clamps and a burner. Orchard End had lost the lanterns on the lowest dead branch of the Old Pippin, the one that was furthest gone; Maigo was very upset about it. Grimmet had lost the bottom gallery of the mine. Otterbank had lost a jetty. Hallowell had lost six of Ysolde's lamps on the far rim, and Ysolde had taken it personally. And Emberly—

"Emberly?" said Kei.

"Still here," said Ina, behind the mask. The forge roared. "Can't talk. Very busy. Forge is fine. The hill's fine. I'm having the best night of my life. Talk to someone else."

And somewhere far off, on other calls, other people Kei had drawn on a sheet of paper were keeping their own. Ice on a plank in a swamp, on his stream with twenty-five thousand people. Hyvred on the roof of a mill in Brackenford, with Bee drawing ladders. Tiger on a staircase of locks, running lamps. Drew at a crossroads with the gate left open.

He'd drawn them all on a sheet of paper at two in the morning on Monday. Dots, and the lines between them. He thought about it now, standing on the rim of a hot spring in the steam with a knife in his hand and a ring of lamps round him. Every one of the dots had a light in it tonight. Every one of the lines was still there.

***

At half past two in the morning, his time, the steam turned grey.

He noticed it because the lamplight in it changed. All evening the steam over the spring had glowed yellow-white from the ring of lamps. Now, very slowly, along one side of it, the eastern side, it started to go a different colour. Cooler. Grey-blue, and then lighter. Dawn coming up behind it. The game's second dawn, the one that came at midnight in Malaysia and three in the morning in Australia, on time.

In the spring, the Unlit stopped.

All of them, at once, the way they'd stopped everywhere. Kei didn't know that yet. He'd find out later, on every stream, from every town. Their long heads came up out of the hot water and turned to the east, toward the grey in the steam, and they stood there, waiting.

"Kei," said Hako, on the call, in his ordinary voice for the first time all night. "They've stopped. In the river. They're all looking east."

"Here too," said Maigo. "They're all looking at the sky."

"Same," said Wendy.

"Same," said Salty.

"Same," said Ina. "Hundreds of them. On the plain. All looking the same way. It's the eeriest thing I've ever seen in my life."

Kei stood on the rim of the spring and watched the steam go grey, and lighter, and lighter. There was a little gold at the very bottom of it, so faint he wasn't sure if he was imagining it. Ysolde stood beside him with her poker. She'd stopped too. She was looking at the steam, and there was something in her measuring face he hadn't seen there before.

"Ysolde," he said. "Is it coming?"

She didn't answer.

At three o'clock, the steam went black.

All of it, at once, from grey to black, as if somebody had closed a hand over the sky. And every long head in the spring went down together, and the water hissed, and they came on toward the lamps again. More of them. Faster.

[ The dark has not lifted. ]

On the call, nobody said anything for a long moment.

Kei looked at the black steam. At Ysolde beside him, gripping her poker. At the ring of lamps round the spring, still lit, most of them. He thought about a sheet of paper with a lot of lines on it.

"Roll call," he said.

There was a pause.

"Orchard End," said Maigo, a bit wobbly. "Here. Still in a tree."

"Grimmet," said Salty. "Here."

"Ashcombe," said Wendy. "Here."

"Otterbank," said Hako. "Here. The otter's still looking at me."

"Emberly," said Ina, behind the mask, over the roar of the forge. "Here. Obviously. Where else would I be."

"Hallowell," said Kei. "Here."

Six. In six towns, in the dark that hadn't lifted, a long way apart, with a whole day of night still to go. He took a breath of the hot iron air and gripped his knife.

"Okay," he said. "Same as before. Nobody hangs up."
