Curse had waited to see if they'd go. That was the whole of it, really. He'd told himself it was sense.

In November, in Singapore, at a hawker centre, Alice had sat across a table from him with Milli. She'd said she wanted the egg so badly for streaming and wouldn't spend Milli's two months in it. Milli had said *an egg is every night*. And Curse had said *that's not a game, that's a door*. He'd believed it. And then he'd gone home to Australia and not bought one.

He'd told them he'd wait to see if they did. It was the sensible thing. Two and a half thousand dollars was two and a half thousand dollars. You didn't spend that on a door until you'd seen somebody walk through it.

They'd walked through it on the twenty-seventh of March, in a quarry, with a jump and a fist in the air. He'd watched it on his phone at eleven at night in his kitchen in Australia. Then he'd opened the pod page and bought one without reading anything.

So he'd been wrong about the waiting. He'd known it was coming. He'd just wanted to be sure of the door first.

***

Merrowgate was the loudest place he had ever been.

The fair never stopped. The carousel went round from first dawn to last dusk, wheezing its organ, the same seven tunes in the same order. Curse had written them down by Sunday afternoon. The great wheel turned. Jugglers juggled. The hot-apple woman shouted *apples* every forty seconds; he'd timed her. The bear, whose name was Gilly, it turned out, and who was very old and very gentle and slept most of the day, snored. And Leaka, in the middle of all of it, with an axe taller than she was, was louder than any of it, and happier than he'd ever heard her.

He liked it. He hadn't expected to. He'd expected to find it tiring. He found it tiring and liked it anyway, which was new.

He'd taken Foraging and Inscription.

Foraging because he liked knowing what things were. Which mushroom, which leaf, which root would heal you and which would stop your heart. Inscription because of the pen. He hadn't told anyone that. He'd given Alice a fountain pen at Lau Pa Sat because she wrote everything in pencil. Pencil was a crime, because you could rub it out, and a thing you'd decided should be written so it stayed. He'd taken Inscription on the creation page for the same reason, without quite admitting it to himself.

The mirror had offered him a keepsake, and he'd taken an inkwell. His own, the one on his desk at home, small and glass and full of the same blue ink he'd given Alice.

Merrowgate had a letterer.

She painted the fair's signs. Every stall, every awning, every sign on every painted house along the river, she'd painted. *APPLES. TICKETS. THE GREAT WHEEL. MIND THE BEAR.* She was an old woman called Odette, with paint on every finger and a brush behind each ear. She sat in a little shop under the great wheel surrounded by pots of colour, and she looked at Curse's inkwell on Sunday morning for a long time without saying anything.

"Blue," she said eventually. "Good blue."

"Thank you."

"You want to learn letters?"

"I want to learn what letters *do*," said Curse. "Here. In this place. Things seem to do what they're told, if you tell them properly."

Odette looked at him for even longer.

Then she picked up a small brown paper tag on a string, the kind you'd tie to a parcel, and a brush. She dipped the brush in his ink without asking. On the tag, in very plain capitals, she wrote one word.

*ROPE.*

She tied the tag to the coil of rope hanging on her wall, and handed him the rope.

"Pull," said Odette.

He pulled. It was rope. Ordinary rope. He pulled harder, and harder, and it didn't stretch, didn't fray, didn't give at all. He put his whole weight on it, and it was like pulling on a stone.

"It's more rope," said Odette, "now that I've told it what it is." She took the brush out of his ink and wiped it. "Most things forget what they are. They get old, or tired, or frightened, and they forget. A label reminds them." She tapped the tag. "That's all a sign is. A reminder. *This is apples. This is the wheel. That is a bear.*"

Curse looked at the rope for a long time.

"Does it last?" he said.

"As long as the ink does," said Odette. "Write it in pen."

He very nearly smiled. He didn't quite. It was close.

The mudlarks came up out of the river on Monday night.

Everybody in Merrowgate knew about them, the way everybody in every town knew about something. Small grey things, half fish and half something else, that lived in the mud under the stilts. On dark nights they came up the stilts and chewed them, so that every few years a painted house leaned a little further over the river. Then one day it went in.

On Monday night, the one that was chewing belonged to the hot-apple woman.

"*CURSE*," said Leaka, from somewhere below him in the dark. "CURSE. THE APPLE LADY'S HOUSE IS GOING IN THE RIVER."

He went down the steps to the bank. Leaka was standing in the shallows under the hot-apple woman's house with her axe, in the dark, with the river running brown round her knees. Above her the painted house was leaning. Not much. Enough. Three of its stilts had been chewed nearly through, and the whole thing was creaking. And round the stilts, in the dark water, small grey shapes were moving.

Leaka hit one with the axe. It went across the river like a skimmed stone. She was laughing. Of course she was. Another one came up the stilt behind her and she turned and hit that too, and the axe went into the stilt as well, and the whole house groaned.

"DON'T HIT THE HOUSE," said Leaka, to herself.

"Leaka," said Curse.

"I KNOW. I KNOW. I'M HITTING THE HOUSE."

He looked at the three chewed stilts, the house leaning over him in the dark, and the hot-apple woman at her window above with her hand over her mouth.

He took three paper tags out of his pocket. He'd been carrying a stack since Sunday, because Odette had given them to him, and because he liked having the right thing in his pocket. He unstoppered the inkwell. He didn't have a brush. He had a pen, his own. He dipped it, and wrote, in the dark, in plain capitals, by the light of the lantern on the hot-apple woman's porch.

*STILT.*

*STILT.*

*STILT.*

He tied one to each chewed post, quickly, with the string, standing in the river up to his knees while Leaka hit mudlarks round him.

The house stopped groaning.

It didn't straighten. It didn't mend. The chewed places were still chewed. But it stopped leaning, and stood there over the river on its three labelled stilts. As if it had remembered, quite suddenly, what it was for.

Leaka hit the last mudlark across the river and turned round, breathing hard, and looked at the house. Then at the three tags on the stilts, fluttering. Then at Curse, in the river, with his pen in his hand and the inkwell held against his chest.

"WHAT DID YOU *DO*?"

"I told it what it was," said Curse.

Leaka stared at him.

Then she waded across the shallows and hugged him, axe and all, so hard that he nearly dropped the inkwell. He held it up out of the way with one hand, above her head, very carefully, the way you'd hold a bird.

"YOU LABELLED A HOUSE," said Leaka, into his shoulder.

"I labelled three stilts."

"YOU *LABELLED* A *HOUSE*, CURSE."
