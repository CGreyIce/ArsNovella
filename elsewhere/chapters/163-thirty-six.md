The Elm Below had been kept on a Saturday, on the fifth try. Kei had been at the top of the stair when it happened, with his map under his arm. He thought he'd remember it for the rest of his life.

Not the fight. He hadn't seen the fight; he never did. He was the one at the top of the stair, in an old man's Hall, listening. He'd listened to five of them. Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, each one longer. Each one ending with ten people coming up a stair of light with less in them than they'd gone down with. Gabe at thirty per cent. Rachael furious. Kotori out of everything. Hyvred very quiet. And on Friday, Ice, at fourteen per cent, the lowest Kei had ever seen him. He'd come up the last step and sat down on Rook's oak floor and said *it's got a second heart*, and nothing else, for a long time.

On Saturday they went down at the second dawn and didn't come up at the second dusk.

Kei stood at the top of the stair and waited. Rook beside him with his lantern. VANTA on the benches along the wall. They'd stayed, all five days, all twenty of them, sitting there with their black banner still dark on its hook. Kestrel at the front, with her bow across her knees and her face perfectly unreadable. She'd watched every one of the ten go down five times and come up five times. She'd never said a word.

The second dusk came and went. The lamps came on. Nobody came up.

At a quarter to nine the window came.

[ WORLD FIRST · The first hall of the second rank has been kept.
The Elm Below · Elmcross · Constellation ]

Kei didn't see it, at first. He was looking down the stair. He only knew it had come because every traveller in Rook's Hall went still at once. Twenty of VANTA on the benches, and a few dozen others who'd stayed to watch. Rook looked round at them all and said *hm*.

Then, from the benches by the wall, there was a sound.

Kestrel had stood up. She was standing at the front of VANTA, with her bow in one hand. And she was clapping. Slowly, once, twice, three times. A small, precise, unwasteful sound in a quiet Hall. Then the rest of VANTA stood up behind her, one by one, and did the same.

And up the stair of light, one at a time, came the ten. Ice first, at nineteen per cent, with his shield in pieces on his arm. He came up the last step and saw twenty people in black standing by the wall, clapping. He stopped.

Kei watched his face. He'd been watching Ice's face for eight months, on the map, across rooms. He'd never seen it do what it did then.

Kestrel stopped clapping. She looked at Ice across the Hall for a long moment.

Then she lifted her hand. An inch.

He lifted his back.

Gabe told what had happened down there about forty times on the way home, adding a heart each time. But the bones of it were always the same. There'd been a thing at the far end of a long low hall, under elm roots, and it had two hearts. Every time they broke one, the other woke. Five days of breaking the wrong one first. And on the fifth day Hyvred had stood at the back of the ten, where she could see all of it, edge to edge. She'd watched both hearts at once for forty minutes without blinking. Then she'd said, in a voice Gabe swore went up the stair and into Rook's Hall, *LEFT. THE LEFT ONE'S AWAKE. NOT THAT ONE. THE LEFT.*

And Ice had gone left without looking. The way he'd moved on her calls in the Seven Below, before she ever called his name.

"She called it," Gabe said, every time. "Hyv called it. And he went. Didn't even turn his head."

Hyvred, every time, said *don't*, and went red, and walked faster.

Kestrel's post went up an hour after they came up. Kei read it on the road home, on the first night, by the fire, three times.

*First. Congratulations, Constellation. Properly, this time. You went down five times and he came up at fourteen and went down again. I'd have rested. That's the annoying part. Next door. K.*

***

That was a week ago. It felt like a year.

Now it was Saturday again, and Kei was on the roof of the dome of the Hall of Roads with his map, and there were sixty names in the book.

*Sixty.*

He'd started on the back of a café menu in December with one. Himself, small, in the middle, drawn small on purpose. Then twenty, at four in the morning on a Thursday in March, with a beak stuck in Ama Corrie's counter. Then thirty-two, in the spring. Forty-nine, in July, when a man in a fen said yes before he'd finished. Fifty-five, three weeks ago, when a woman with a tower shield said *can we join* before he'd started. And sixty, yesterday. A girl with a gold star in her hand, and a man who signed in blue.

He unrolled the map on the stone ledge in the evening light and looked at it.

Sixty dots. All of them solid. Hundreds of lines between them, all solid. He'd gone over the last five on Friday night with his pen, slowly, one at a time. Not one dotted line left anywhere on the whole map.

Except at the very top. Above everything, off every road, the gold dot. With nothing running to it.

And beside the gold dot, in his own careful hand, a line he'd written yesterday morning in the banner room. He'd stood at the long table with Ama Corrie and Hana and a man with blue ink on his fingers and watched it come back out of the water, black and clear, for about the length of a breath.

*We go north to carry the lights. Set them out behind us, so the rest may sleep.*

He looked at it for a long time.

***

Higu came up at the second dusk with two cups of tea. Kei moved over on the ledge so she could have her place.

"You're going to say it," said Higu.

"Say what?"

"*Not yet*." She sat down and gave him a cup. "You've got your *not yet* face. You've had it since Friday."

Kei looked at the line on the map.

"*Set them out behind us*," he said. "Higu. The rings."

Higu went still.

She knew what he meant. She'd been there, in March, on the north moor, the first time they'd gone. The road had run out past Kite's milestone and become waystones. A five-line star and a groove pointing north, every few miles, every one exactly on the dome's silver line. And three hundred paces up the line, the first night, an old ring. Twelve iron posts in a circle, each with an empty lantern cage on top, and a flat stone in the middle with a star cut into it. Tiger had lit them all, and the game had said *A kept place. Nothing comes inside.* And then there'd been another the next day. And the next. A ring every day's walk, all the way north to the old hall with the birch tree through its floor.

"The lantern rings," said Higu, slowly.

"They set them out," said Kei. "*Behind them.* On the way north. A ring every day's walk, with lanterns in, so whoever came after would have somewhere to sleep where nothing could get in. *So the rest may sleep.*" He put his finger on the dotted line he'd drawn north from the old hall, the one that went off the top of the map into nothing. "And past the old hall, the stones. Every fifty paces, into the black."

"I put my light out there," said Higu, very quietly. "In April. It went into the black and something closed round it. Like a moth in a hand."

"I know."

"It was so *big*, Kei."

"I know." He looked at the stones on his map. Tiny marks, every fifty paces, going north off the edge of the old hall into nothing. "Higu. What if the stones were rings too? Or lamps. What if every stone was a light, once? Every fifty paces, all the way in. Set out behind them as they went." He looked at her. "What if that's what the little figures on Hana's wall are doing? Not walking into the dark. *Lighting* it. One light at a time. Every fifty paces."

Higu didn't say anything for a long time. The moon was coming up over the rim of the eye. Below them, on the inside of the dome, the silver lines were starting to come out. Roads and towns and rivers, every one of them running north, to the great gold star above the north door.

"You want to go," said Higu.

"I want to see the edge," said Kei. "Not the north. Not in. Just the edge. The last stone. Just to look at it, now we know what they were for." He turned the cup round in his hands. "It's not the north, Higu. It's the edge of the north. That's different."

Higu laughed. It came out of her very softly, and went up through the eye.

"You sound like him," she said.

"I *know*," said Kei. "I hate it."

***

He found Ice on Sunday at the second dawn, at the counter, with Ama Corrie. Ice was holding the fen-reed broom.

Kei stopped in the doorway of the Hall and looked at that for a while. Ice was sweeping. Under the counter, under the stools by it, slowly, under not round. Ama Corrie was standing beside him with her arms folded, watching, with an expression Kei had never seen on her face.

"Twice now," Ama Corrie was saying, to nobody. "Forty years nobody asks. Then twice in a month."

"Thea asked first," said Ice.

"I know she did. I was there. I'm saying it's twice." She sniffed. "Under, not round."

"I know. She told me."

Kei came over to the counter. Ice looked up from the floor.

"Can I talk to you?" said Kei. "On the stair."

Ice handed Ama Corrie the broom. She took it and looked at the tag on the handle, *KEPT* in blue ink, and said *hm*, and went on sweeping herself.

***

They sat on the stair to the eye, on the first landing. A hundred and six steps up, where the little window looked out over the east road. Ice sat with his back against the wall and his arms on his knees. Kei sat a step below him with the map.

"I'm going north," said Kei. "Tuesday. Just to the edge. The last stone, past the old hall. I want to see it, now we know about the lights."

"Okay," said Ice.

"I want you to come."

Ice didn't answer straight away. He looked out of the little window at the east road for a while.

"No," he said.

Kei looked at him.

He hadn't expected that. In eight months he'd never asked Ice for anything and been told no. Not once. He'd asked him to join, in January, and Ice had said *make it big, then ask me again*, and that wasn't no. He'd asked him again, in June, and Ice had said yes before he finished. He'd asked him to go down a stair under the round town and to walk to Elmcross. He'd asked him to stand in a ring in an old man's Hall. Ice had always said yes. Or *not yet*, which was a kind of yes.

"Why not?" said Kei.

"Take Thea," said Ice. "And Gigi. Both of them. And Hana, she's got the star. And Curse, he's got the ink. And Higu, it's her map. And Tiger, for the rings." He looked at Kei. "And Drew. And whoever else you want. But not me."

"*Why?*"

Ice looked out of the window again.

"Because it's not always me," he said. "I said that. In July, at the long table. *There's enough of us now to take turns.* I meant it." He was quiet for a moment. "Somebody has to face out. But it doesn't have to be the same somebody every time. If it's always me, then it's mine. And it isn't mine. It's everybody's. It was the old guild's, and now it's ours. All of ours." He turned his head and looked at Kei. "You go and look at the edge. I'll stay here and keep the room."

Kei sat on the stair with the map in his hands.

"*Set one to watch*," he said, slowly.

"*So the rest may sleep*," said Ice. "You're going. I'm staying. It's the same line from the other end." Something happened at the corner of his mouth. "I'm the rest this time. Somebody told me that once. In a ring. *You're the rest. Tonight.* I didn't listen. I'm listening now."

Kei didn't know what to say. So he said, "Hm."

Ice laughed. Once, out loud, through his nose.

"That's a good word," said Ice. "You should keep that one too."

***

They sat on the stair for a while after that, without saying anything. The sun came up over the east road outside the little window, and the Hall filled up below them with Sunday. Ama Corrie's broom, *hush, hush*. Gabe telling somebody about the second heart. Leaka and Xola, somewhere, shouting *ICEY BOI* at each other, which had apparently become a sort of greeting.

"Kei," said Ice, after a long time. "Can I tell you something?"

"Yes."

"I counted," said Ice. "On Friday. In the banner room. After the five signed."

Kei waited.

"Everybody I've known for years," said Ice. He was looking at the window, not at Kei. "Not everybody in the guild. The guild's more than that now. Rafi and Bram and Joy and Kite and Tobi and Riverbank's three and Thea's three and Kotori. I didn't know any of them a year ago. They're mine now too. I'm not leaving them out." He paused. "But before. Before the game. The people I'd known for years. Some of them since school. Some from streams. Some I'd only ever been in a chat with, at three in the morning." He turned his hands over on his knees. "I counted them. Round the table. On Friday."

"How many?"

"Thirty-six," said Ice. "Counting me."

Kei looked at him.

"They've never all been in one place," said Ice. "Not once. Not ever. Not in a call. Not in a game. Not in a city. They live in about a dozen countries. Some of them have never met each other. Some of them have been in my life since I was thirteen and some for two years, and some of them don't even know the others' names." He looked down the stairwell, at the floor of the Hall, at the banner room door. "And on Friday they were all at one table. In one room. All thirty-six. I counted twice."

Kei didn't say anything for a long time.

"Did you know?" he said finally. "When you said *bring everyone*? When Higu said it? Did you know it would be—everyone?"

"No," said Ice. "I never thought it would be everyone. I thought it'd be some. I thought I'd be lucky if it was ten." He laughed, a little. "I was in everyone's chat for years, Kei. Everyone's. And none of them were ever in the same chat at the same time. Not once."

"And now they're in yours."

"Now they're in *yours*," said Ice. "It's Kei's. Leaka can say what she likes."

Kei unrolled the map on his knees, there on the stair. He looked at the sixty dots. He didn't know which thirty-six. He could guess at most of them, but he didn't know, and he understood suddenly that he didn't need to. That it wasn't his to know. That it was a thing Ice had counted, once, quietly, on a Friday, round a table. And that he'd told Kei because he had to tell somebody, and Kei was the one who drew the map.

"Do you want me to mark them?" said Kei. "On the map? I could. A little ring. Nobody'd know what it was."

Ice looked at the map for a long time.

"No," he said. "They're dots. Same as everybody's. That's the whole point." He touched the map, lightly, with one finger, near the middle, near his own small dot among the nine. "They're dots like everybody's. On the same map. With the same lines. That's what I counted. Not that they're special. That they're *here*."

Kei rolled the map up.

He didn't draw anything. He'd learned that, this year, from Higu, and from Bee and Deston at the long table in July, and from Ice. Some things you didn't draw. Some things you just knew, and kept, and swept under on Sundays.

***

He made the list on Monday morning and put it up on the board by the hatch.

*Tuesday · second dawn · north, to the edge*

*KeiKatamori · higuVT · gigichiii_ · Drew · Tiger · AltheaVT · xolalotl · Hanarisu · Cursiffe · Rafi*

He stood in front of it for a long time after he'd pinned it up. He'd thought about it all night. Ice had said *take Thea, and Gigi, both of them*. And Kei had thought, *are you sure*, and then thought, *it isn't mine to be sure about*.

Thea came to look at it at noon. She stood where Kei had stood, in front of the board, for about as long. He watched her from the counter. He didn't know what she'd do. She might take her name off. Nobody would think anything.

She took the pencil off its string. Next to her name she drew a small tick.

Xola, behind her, reached past her shoulder and ticked her own, hard, so the pencil nearly went through the paper. Then she looked at the name two above Thea's on the list, and her gills flared. But she didn't say anything. She just stood there for a second, and then followed Thea out.

Gigi came at the second dusk. She stood in front of the list for a long time. Then she ticked her name, with a little hook at the end of the tick. And on her way past the counter, without looking at Kei, she said, "You put us on the same list."

"Ice said to."

Gigi stopped.

"*He* did?"

"He said *take Thea and Gigi. Both of them.*"

Gigi stood very still by the counter for a while. Then she said, very quietly, "He's very strange."

"Yes," said Kei.

"It'll fall down."

"Probably," said Kei.

"Good thorn," said Gigi, to nobody, under her breath, and went up the stairs to the eye with two cups of tea.

Kei didn't know what that meant. He wrote it down anyway, in the margin of his map, in very small letters, near two dots on the north side that had a line between them now. It was the only thing he wrote that week that wasn't a road.
