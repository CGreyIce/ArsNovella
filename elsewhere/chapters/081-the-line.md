Winter didn't say much. She'd never seen the point. Most things people said were the same thing three times, and if you waited, someone else would say it for you.

So on Wednesday, the third of March, when the four of them came round the shoulder of a hill on the Ferrowmere road and saw the gorge, and the bridge over it, and the bridge on fire, she didn't say anything.

Newish said it for her.

"*Oh, come ON*," said Newish.

***

It had been a good bridge. Winter could tell that much, even burning. It was the only way across the gorge for two days in either direction. A long timber span on stone piers, high over a narrow black river that went white in the rocks far below. It had been there for a long time. Chris had it in a sheet. *South road: one bridge, the Gullet. Old. Fine.*

Now the middle third of it was gone. Just gone, fallen into the river, with smoke coming up out of the gorge. The two ends still stood, smouldering, reaching out toward each other from the two cliffs with forty yards of air between them.

On the far side, at the top of the cliff, somebody had left something on a post. A small piece of dark cloth, pinned up where you couldn't miss it.

Winter had the better eyes of the four of them in here. One pale blue, one pink. She'd never understood why her model had them, until the game, when she found the pink one saw further in the dark. She looked across the gorge at the post. A little dark bird, stitched on a scrap of collar.

"Nightjar," she said.

That was one word. She thought it was enough.

***

Chris had stopped walking.

He stood at the edge of the cliff, at the burnt end of the bridge, looking at the forty yards of nothing. Winter watched him do the sum. You could see Chris do sums; he did them with his whole face. She'd watched him do them across a year of Apex, at three in the morning, with Ice in the middle doing something stupid. He'd always had the number before anyone else had worked out there was a question.

"Two days round," he said. His voice was very calm, which meant it wasn't. "North to the ford at Kell, then back down the far side. Two days. We'd get to Carrow Cross on Friday."

Nobody said anything. Friday was too late. Everybody knew. The writ ended at midnight on Thursday. If they weren't in the Hall by midnight, the names were struck. The five hundred gold was kept. And twenty people in red sashes, who'd been sitting round a fountain in the round market since Sunday, would stand up at one minute past and begin a writ of their own. Kei had told them on the call. Twelve signed. Four at the gate. Four on the road. Them.

"And I'm fourteen," said Chris. "Ninety-one per cent." He looked at the gap. "Even if we got there, I'm fourteen."

"You'll be fifteen," said Niko.

"You don't know that."

"Yeah, maybe," said Niko, in his chill voice, and sat down on a rock at the edge of the cliff as if he'd decided to wait for a bus.

Newish was walking up and down, saying everything she thought of the people who'd burnt the bridge, in great detail, in two languages. Winter let her. Somebody had to say it, and it was better Newish than her.

Winter looked at the gorge.

Forty yards. The two burnt ends of the bridge, reaching out. The far cliff, the post, the scrap of black cloth. The river below, a long way down, white in the rocks. The wind, coming up the gorge from the south, steady, cold.

She had the harpoon on her back. The one she'd taken off the rack on the first day, in a fishing village on a grey sea, because she wanted to throw something heavy at something far away. Coiled on it, the rope. Fifty yards of it. She'd fished with it for two months, from the long black boats. She'd harpooned a seal with it, and an owl, from a cart on a cable a hundred feet up.

Fifty yards of rope. Forty yards of gap.

"I can throw it across," she said.

That was six words. Newish stopped walking.

***

"You can throw it across," said Newish. "Into *what*? There's nothing to throw it *into*. It's a cliff. With a post on it. With a *Nightjar* on it."

"The bridge end," said Winter. "The beams. The stone pier under them."

"And then what? We go across on a *rope*? Over *that*?" Newish pointed down at the river. "Chris is *fourteen*. If he falls, he dies, and if he dies he's thirteen, and then—"

"I know," said Chris quietly.

"—and then it's over, and the red ones get the room, and Kei's been sitting on a *step* for a *month*—"

"I know, Newish."

"Somebody has to hold the other end," said Niko, from his rock. "A rope's no good if nobody's holding the other end."

They all looked across the gorge at the far side, forty yards away. The burnt end of the bridge. The post. Nobody.

And then somebody.

***

They came out of the trees on the far side not long after. Quite a lot of them.

She saw them first, with the pink eye. Figures coming down through the pines to the top of the far cliff. Not dark coats. All sorts. A tall one at the front with a shield on his back, and a big one with a hammer, and a small one in blue with a staff and a bell. A very tall one with silver hair and a scythe. A tall one with a long two-handed sword. A tall one with a book.

Behind them, another lot. Eight. One in a plain coat with a notebook under his arm. One with two knives, counting the others out loud. One with a shield even bigger than the first one's.

And at the back, on his own, a narrow-faced young man with a bow. Strung.

"*Oh*," said Newish.

Moonlight Harvest, and Riverbank, and Kite. On the far side of a burnt bridge, forty yards away, waving.

The one with the notebook stepped to the edge of the far cliff and cupped his hands.

"*Hi*," shouted Xav, across forty yards of air. "*I drew it.*"

***

He had drawn it. Of course he had. He'd drawn it on the walk from Carrow Cross, the way he drew everything: *Dec stands here. Then.*

Ice stood on the far end of the burnt bridge, on the stone pier, at the very edge, with nothing in front of him but air. Winter threw.

She didn't think about it. That was the thing about throwing. If you thought about it, you missed. She set her feet on the burnt beam at her end, and drew the harpoon back over her shoulder. She felt the wind coming up the gorge, cold and steady from the south, and let it tell her where to aim, a little to the left, a little high. And she threw.

The harpoon went out across the gorge with the rope paying out behind it, a long grey line against the black water far below.

It went into Azac's shield.

Azac had planted it on the far pier like a door, and the harpoon went into the wood with a sound like a hammer on a barrel, and stuck. Azac grunted, and leaned, and didn't move.

"*Got it*," said Azac, across the gorge, in his big flat voice. "*I'm a wall.*"

And Ice put his hand on the rope beside the shield, and set his feet on the stone pier the way he'd set them on a plank in a swamp. Winter saw the stone under his boots go still, as if it had grown roots.

"*Tie it off*," shouted Xav.

Winter tied it off, round the base of the burnt beam at her end, the way you tied off a boat. Fifty yards of rope, forty yards of gap, ten yards spare. A line across a gorge, tight as a bowstring, humming in the wind.

***

Niko went first. He had to. The others wouldn't have.

He went hand over hand along the rope, with his legs hooked over it and the sword on his back, slowly, as if he were crossing a bridge with barrows on it. Halfway across, the wind gusted up the gorge. The rope swung. He said a word.

"*Hold.*"

He said it on the rope. On his hand, where it gripped. And the rope went still in his hand, just there, just for a heartbeat. Long enough. Spoken Edge. He'd been trying *together* on the cut every day since Tallbridge. He'd never once tried *hold* on a rope.

He got to the far side. Gabe pulled him up onto the pier by the collar.

"Easy," said Niko, in his chill voice, white as paper.

Newish went second.

She didn't want to. She said so, at length, at the edge of the cliff. Then she put the drum on her back and went out onto the rope, and talked the whole way across, every inch. She told the rope it was ugly, and the gorge it was ugly, and the river that it could forget about it, she wasn't coming down. Kotori, on the far side, laughed so hard she had to sit down.

When Newish got across, she didn't stop. She took the drum off her back, sat down on the stone pier beside Ice's boots, put the drum between her knees, and started to play.

*Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.*

"For Chris," she said. "Go on, then."

Winter went third. She didn't say anything. She went across hand over hand, steady, with her horns down against the wind, and didn't look at the river once. It was the same as hauling a net. When she got to the far side, Ice looked at her across the rope.

"Fine," said Winter, before he could say anything.

"Fine," agreed Ice.

***

Which left Chris.

He stood on the burnt beam on the far side of the gorge, on his own, at fourteen and ninety-one per cent, looking at the rope. Forty yards. The river a long way down. The wind.

Winter watched him from the pier. She could see him making the list. *Hands. Legs. Weight. Breathe.* One, two, three, four. Every step of it in order, before he'd taken any of them. The way he'd done every Falling Cut for five weeks until a drum on the floor of a cart taught him not to.

Newish played louder.

*Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.*

"*Chris*," shouted Newish, across the gorge. "*Stop listing.*"

He looked at her. Forty yards away, across the gap, at the four of them on the pier. Niko with his book. Winter with her horns. Newish on the stone with the drum between her knees. Ice beside them, with his hand on the rope and his feet set and his face completely calm. Behind them, Moonlight Harvest and Riverbank, fourteen people in a crowd on a burnt pier, all looking at him.

And at the back, on his own, Kite. He'd moved. He'd gone up to the post at the top of the cliff, the one with the scrap of black cloth pinned to it. He'd taken the cloth down and put it in his pocket without a word. Then he'd turned, with his bow strung, and looked out along the gorge to the south, where the wind was coming from. Watching for the people who'd pinned it there.

"*Listen*," said Newish.

Chris went.

He didn't go the way Niko had, or Winter. He went the way Newish played. *Doum.* A hand. *Tek.* The legs. *Tek.* The other hand. *Doum.* The weight going forward. Not a list. A beat. And he went across the gorge on it, hand over hand, on the drum. The rope swung in the wind and he swung with it. When it gusted, Niko said "*hold*" from the pier, on the rope, and it held. He didn't look down. He didn't stop. He came across forty yards of air in the time it took Newish to play the reed-cutting song from Fernwick twice. She'd learned it off Ice's stream for exactly this.

Gabe pulled him up onto the pier by the collar, the way he'd pulled Niko.

Chris lay on his back on the stone with the sky going over, breathing.

Nobody said anything.

Then something whistled down the gorge from the south. Fast and dark, from the pines on the ridge above the river. An arrow, black-fletched, at the rope, at the knot round Azac's shield.

It didn't get there.

Another arrow met it in the air. A grey-fletched one, from the top of the cliff, from the post. Kite's. The two of them went down into the gorge together, spinning, a long way, into the white water.

Up on the ridge to the south, in the pines, something dark moved, and went away, and didn't come back.

Kite lowered his bow. He didn't turn round. He just stood at the post on the top of the cliff, watching the ridge, the way he'd watched the left side of a wall.

***

They cut the rope.

Winter did it. She'd thrown it, she'd tied it, she'd cut it. That was how you did it with a line. You didn't leave it hanging for somebody else to use. She pulled the harpoon out of Azac's shield and coiled the rope. Fifty yards, wet from the gorge, heavy. She slung it back over her shoulder.

Chris was sitting up on the pier with his back against Azac's shield, very quiet, looking at his window.

"Fourteen," he said. "Ninety-eight."

"From crossing a *rope*?" said Newish.

"From crossing a rope," said Chris. "Forty yards. In the wind. Over that." He looked at the gorge. "The game counted it. It counted the rope."

"Ninety-eight," said Niko. "Two per cent."

"Two per cent," said Suzu, from the back of the crowd, with an expression Winter didn't understand. Ice looked at him and laughed, quietly, for no reason Winter could see.

***

They walked through the night. All of them. Niko's four, and Moonlight Harvest, and Riverbank, and Kite at the back with his bow strung, watching the ridges. Nineteen people on a road in the dark, going south, toward a round hall where seven roads met.

Newish talked the whole way. Nobody minded.

At about two in the morning, her time, Winter dropped back to walk beside Ice at the front. She didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did he. That was fine. That was the best thing about Ice. He'd never once needed her to talk.

"Drew was right," said Winter eventually.

Ice looked at her.

"In December," said Winter. "He made me watch your church. On his stream. He said, *just watch the first ten minutes*. I watched four hours and fifty-one." She looked at the road ahead, going on into the dark. "I said I wasn't saying he was right."

"I know."

"He was right," said Winter.

Three words. She'd said more tonight than all week. She thought, for once, it had been just the right amount.
