Deston had lent him the money and said *don't tell anyone*, and Gabe had told everyone, so Calvin told everyone too, louder, to save time.

"Dest paid for my egg," he said, on Thursday, to Rachael and Dennis, on the edge of the boiling lake, at two in the morning, with a dead mud-thing at his feet that he was trying to skin. "I'm paying him back. He said no rush. I said I'd rush anyway. He said *Cal*. You know how he says *Cal*."

"We know how he says Cal," said Dennis.

"Anyway. Now nobody has to pretend they don't know." He pulled. The skin came off the mud-thing all in one piece, which it hadn't done the first eleven times. "*Ha.* Look at that."

"You're getting mud on my boots," said Rachael.

"They're not your boots. They're the game's boots."

"They're on my *feet*, Cal."

He grinned at her. He was always grinning at her. She was the only one of them who'd never played anything like this, and she was better at it than he was. He'd read a hundred stories about people waking up in other worlds. He'd been sure that when it finally happened to him, he'd know exactly what to do.

What he did, mostly, was run.

He couldn't help it. He'd been like it in real life and he was like it in here, only more. There was always a next terrace, a next ridge, a next thing round the next corner. And in here there actually *was* a next thing round every corner, which was the most wonderful thing that had ever happened to him. By Sunday he'd been in the game four days and he'd already been up every hill round Mistbath, and down every gully, and into the boiling lake up to his ankles, once, by accident. That had cost him half his health and taught him a lot about boiling.

Gabe walked into Mistbath at dusk on Sunday.

Calvin saw him first, because Calvin was on a roof. He'd climbed onto the green roof of the cure-house to see the valley at sunset. He saw a big shape come over the top of the pass in the last of the light, with a shield on its back and a hammer on its belt, walking the way Gabe walked. Steady. Like he'd been walking for a week and could walk for another.

"*GABE*," Calvin shouted, from the roof. "GABE'S HERE."

Gabe stopped at the top of the steps and looked up at him. Then at Rachael, coming out of the cure-house with her sleeves rolled up. Then at Dennis, coming up from the market with a bag of something he'd bought cheap and was going to sell dear.

"Hi," said Gabe.

*Twenty-one.* Calvin stared at the window over Gabe's head. He'd known, in theory. He hadn't known.

"You walked a *week*," said Rachael.

"I slept some of it."

"I told you not to."

"Yeah," said Gabe. "I heard you." He looked round at the steam, and the pools, and the old people in their blankets on the benches. Then back at her. "Dest said it's a spa."

"It's a *hospital*."

"Dest said you'd say that." He put his shield down against the cure-house wall, and sat on the step, and stretched his legs out, and sighed the way he only sighed when he'd got somewhere. "Right. Who's what level?"

***

Gabe had a plan. Gabe always had a plan. It was usually one sentence long.

"Ten first," he said, on the step, that night. "Then we walk. Then we keep going."

"Walk where?"

"Brackenford. Declan's town. Well. Near it." He picked at the strap of his shield. "Dest wants a charter. MH is six. We'll be nine. A party only holds six. A company holds twelve."

"And we're in it," said Calvin.

"If you want."

"Obviously I *want*," said Calvin, so fast that Dennis laughed.

"Good," said Gabe. "Then ten first."

So that was what they did.

He took them up the hill past the boiling lake, to the Scalds. A long bare ridge where the ground was hot to stand on and the mud bubbled in pools, and things lived in the mud that were six and seven and eight levels above them. Scaldbacks, like armoured pigs. Kiln hares that came up out of steam vents with their fur smoking. They were much too high for the three of them. That was the point.

"Above your level, you get more," said Gabe. "A lot more. And if you die above your level, you lose a lot more. Twenty-five per cent of a level, plus ten for every level it's got on you. Past a hundred, you go down a level. And you can't come in for a day."

"So don't die," said Dennis.

"So don't die," said Gabe. "That's my job."

He put Rachael in the middle. Then he stood two paces from her. Exactly two. Calvin watched him measure it with his feet.

"That's very close," said Rachael.

"It's two paces," said Gabe. "Two paces isn't close. Two paces is two paces."

"Why two?"

Gabe held up his hand, and a window came up off it, and he turned it round so they could read it.

"Inside two paces, nothing touches you," he said. "Outside two paces, I can't get there." He looked at Rachael. "So it's two paces."

Rachael looked at the window for a long time. Then at him. Then she moved, very slightly, so that it was exactly two paces and not one and three-quarters. That was how Calvin knew she'd said yes.

"Everybody inside two paces," said Gabe. "Everybody. Calvin."

"Yeah, yeah."

"*Calvin.*"

"I heard you," said Calvin. "Two paces. Got it."

He didn't have it.

***

He lasted two days.

It wasn't on purpose. It was never on purpose. It was just that there was a ridge, and the ridge had a top, and he couldn't see over the top. And there might have been anything over the top. And everybody else was standing still, killing scaldbacks one at a time with Gabe in front taking all of it, which was fine. It was *fine*. It was just that it was slow. And the top of the ridge was *right there*.

He went.

On Tuesday, in the morning that started at midnight. Up the last of the ridge while Gabe had a scaldback on his shield and Rachael was watching Gabe's health and Dennis was waving his wand at things. Twenty paces. Thirty. Forty. Over the top.

On the other side of the ridge was a lake. Not the boiling one. A smaller one, round and still and brown, steaming gently, in a hollow at the top of the hill. In the middle of it was a rock.

The rock opened its eyes.

It was a toad. It was the biggest toad he'd ever seen, or ever imagined, or ever read about in a hundred stories. It was the size of a cart, and it was the colour of the mud, and it had been sitting in the hot lake for so long that the steam came off its back in a thin white plume, the way it came off a kettle. And it had been asleep, and now it wasn't.

Calvin said, "Oh."

Then the Kettle opened its mouth, and its tongue came across the lake like a rope.

It hit him in the chest. He went backwards off the lip of the hollow and down the ridge, and hit the hot ground, and rolled, and stopped. And his health went from all of it to a third of it in one go. He hadn't known that could happen. Then the tongue came again, over the lip of the hollow, looking for him. It hit him again on the ground, and his health went to almost nothing.

He heard Gabe shout his name. A long way off. Forty paces. Much more than two.

He heard somebody running.

It wasn't Gabe. Gabe was big and Gabe had a shield and Gabe was coming, but Gabe was slow on a slope. It was Rachael. Rachael, who'd never played anything like this, who'd been careful for twenty-six years. She was coming up the ridge flat out with her staff in one hand, faster than he'd ever seen her move, faster than he'd known she *could*. She didn't stop to think. She never stopped to think. She came over the hot ground and saw him lying there and pointed her staff at him from ten paces away, and said, furious, "*Don't you dare.*"

Something warm went into him. Not much. Enough. His health came up off the bottom: nine, then twelve, then twenty.

Then the tongue came again, over the lip of the hollow, at Rachael this time. And Gabe was there.

Calvin didn't see him get there. Nobody ever saw Gabe get there. He was just there, between Rachael and the hollow, two paces from her, with his shield up. The tongue hit the shield like a falling tree and Gabe went back half a step, and grunted, and didn't go back any further.

"Two paces," said Gabe, through his teeth, to nobody.

Then he went up over the lip of the hollow and into the hot shallows, after the Kettle, with his hammer. Dennis came puffing up the ridge behind them with his wand out, and pointed it, and shouted the one word he'd learned. A light went off in the Kettle's face like a camera flash, and it blinked its huge eyes. And Gabe's hammer, hot from every blow it had taken, came round and hit it on the side of the head. The Kettle staggered, all of it, the whole cart-sized weight of it, sideways into the steam.

It took them four minutes.

Calvin watched all of it from the ground with Rachael standing over him, still pointing her staff at him, still furious. She didn't take her eyes off his health once.

Afterwards they sat on the lip of the hollow with their feet in the warm brown water, and Gabe looked at Calvin. Calvin knew what was coming.

"Two paces," said Gabe.

"I know."

"You were forty."

"I *know*."

"If she hadn't run—" Gabe stopped. He didn't say it. He didn't have to. Twenty-five per cent, plus ten for every level. Calvin could do that sum. Eighty-five. A whole day locked out of the egg, and most of a level gone, on his sixth day.

"I can't do two paces," said Calvin. It came out more honest than he'd meant. "I've never done two paces in my life. There's always a next thing. I *have* to look."

Gabe didn't say anything. He was good at that. He let you keep going.

So Calvin kept going. Because that was the other thing about him, the thing everyone knew, the thing Deston said he'd been doing since the day they met. He asked the question. Whatever it was. Even the one you weren't supposed to.

"Who stands two paces from you?" said Calvin.

Gabe looked at him.

"You stand two paces from Rae," said Calvin. "You stand two paces from Kotori, Dest says. You stand two paces from everybody. Who stands two paces from *you*?"

It was very quiet at the top of the hill. The Kettle's lake steamed. Somewhere below, a kiln hare came up out of a vent with its fur smoking and went down again.

"Nobody," said Gabe. "That's the job."

"That's a stupid job," said Calvin.

Rachael made a small sound that might have been a laugh. Dennis looked at the sky.

Gabe didn't say anything for a long time. He was looking at the water.

"Okay," said Calvin. "Here's what I'll do. I'll go ahead. I'm good at ahead. I'm the best at ahead out of anybody you know." He took a breath. "But I'll come back. Every time. I'll go and look over the top, and then I'll come back to two paces, and tell you what's there. *Before* it opens its eyes."

Gabe looked at him.

"Every time," said Gabe.

"Every time."

"Even when it's boring."

"*Especially* when it's boring."

Gabe looked at him for a moment longer. Then he held his fist out, sideways, over the water, and Calvin bumped it.

"Every time," said Gabe.

***

He did it. He was surprised how much he liked it.

He went ahead over every ridge on the Scalds for a week, and looked, and came back. *Three scaldbacks in a dip. One hare. Nothing.* *A mud pool that's too hot, go left.* *Something big asleep under the overhang. Don't.* And every time he came back, the others were standing exactly where he'd left them, waiting for him. Gabe, and Dennis, and Rachael two paces from Gabe. It turned out that was the best part. Not the going. The coming back.

On Wednesday the seventh of April, at a quarter to one in the morning, on a hot ridge under a sky going gold, he went up over the top of the last rise on the Scalds. He looked, and came back down at a run, and stopped exactly two paces away, and opened his mouth to say *nothing over there*. And the window came up in front of him instead.

"*And comes back*," said Calvin.

"Ha," said Rachael.

Dennis had one too. He looked at it for a long time with an expression Calvin couldn't read. Then he shut it without showing anyone and said, "I'll tell you when I've worked out what it's worth."

They walked back down to Mistbath in the gold. Calvin went ahead, the way he did now, and came back, the way he did now. On the way down he looked back up the hill at the three of them coming down behind him. Gabe in front, big and steady, with his shield on his back. Dennis on the left, already counting something on his fingers.

And Rachael. Who didn't hug, and didn't like to be touched, and had never once in the three years Calvin had known her let anybody stand closer than she'd chosen.

She was walking two paces from Gabe. Exactly two. Not one and three-quarters. Not three.

Nobody had asked her to. Gabe hadn't noticed. Calvin did.

He didn't say anything about it. It was the only question he'd ever decided not to ask.
