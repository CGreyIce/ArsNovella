At four in the morning, Bram fell.

Ice didn't see it. He heard it in the window that came up at the edge of his vision, the way all the falls came, one line of grey text. He was three hundred paces out on the narrows with a level-seventeen Unlit leaning on his shield, and he couldn't turn round.

[ Traveller Bram_V has fallen. They will return in 24 hours. ]

He knew what it meant. Sedge's steps, at the end of the north walk. Three of them on it since sunset: Rafi, Joy and Bram, the big silent Dutch boy with the spear. He'd stood there like a post somebody had driven into the boardwalk. Something had come up out of the water at the steps, three at a time maybe, too many. Bram had stood in front of them and not moved, the way he hadn't moved all week, and now he was gone for a day.

"Rafi," said Ice, on the town's open channel, the one Xav had set up for the north walk. "Rafi, you there?"

A long pause. Then a voice, very young, very out of breath.

"Here, boss."

"Steps?"

"Steps." A clang. A ringing iron note, in time with Deston's count. "Joy's here. The eel man came. He's got a hook. Sedge's lamp's lit." Another clang. "I've got the sword."

"I know you have."

"I'm *level eleven*," said Rafi, as if this was the most important thing in the world. "I can hold it properly now."

Ice laughed on the narrows, at thirty-four per cent, with something leaning on his shield.

***

The night had a shape now. He could feel it. Eight hours in. Four to go.

The Unlit came in waves, with lulls between, and every wave was a little bigger than the last. Every lull a little shorter. At midnight they'd been level sixteen. At two, seventeen. At four, eighteen and nineteen, taller than the eaves, with heads the size of a man's chest. When they came apart, the black water came down over the narrows like a bucket emptied on a roof.

[ Fernwick · Lights: 241 ]

The town was getting darker. He could feel that too. Every lantern that went out on the outer boardwalks, the dark came in a step. Two thousand travellers at sunset. Now, the window said, a little over eleven hundred. Some had died. Some had been forced out by the egg. They'd logged in too early, before eight, not knowing, and the egg had thrown them out at their twelve hours, mid-fight, into their bedrooms. And some had just stopped and gone. You couldn't blame them. It was four in the morning in Malacca, and later or earlier everywhere else.

But eleven hundred hadn't. Eleven hundred were still in the swamp, keeping their lanterns.

He drank a Good draught at ten past four. Moonbell, from the ring. He'd given half of them away by now. Ten to Kotori. Five to Deston, whose voice was starting to go. A handful along the north walk to strangers with their bars in the red, who looked at the little *Brewed by Ice* at the bottom of the bottle and then at him like he'd handed them a medal. He had eleven left.

And at a quarter past four, in a lull, looking out over the old fen because there was nothing else to look at, he saw the stones.

***

He couldn't see the stones, of course. Nobody could, from the narrows. They were four hundred paces out in the old fen and the night was black. Nine grey stones in a ring with their long necks bent, in their pool of warm water, a ring he'd walked through every single morning of the game.

But he could see the Unlit. In the lull they were standing everywhere in the old fen, heads down, swaying, waiting, black shapes against the slightly less black water, all the way out to the edge of sight. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

And out there, four hundred paces off, there was a place where they weren't.

A round place. A gap in them, about the size of the ring. The Unlit stood thick all round it in the reeds and the water and the tussocks. Not one of them stood inside it. When the wave came, they moved, and he watched them go round it. Not over it, not through it. Round. The way a river goes round a rock.

*Nothing comes inside the stones*, Sedge had said, on the second day of the game. *Nothing ever has.*

He looked at it for a long moment, the dark round gap in the middle of all the dark shapes. Then he looked away, back at the narrows, and didn't say anything at all. Not on stream. Not to anyone.

At half past four, the fen went quiet.

Not a lull. Something else. The Unlit that were already up stopped moving. All of them, the way they'd stopped at midnight to look east. Except they didn't look east. They lowered their heads, all together, slowly, until their long faces were almost in the water. And they backed away. Not toward the town. Away from something. They backed away from a place out past the heron-stones, a hundred paces on, where the water was deepest. Where the lurker lived.

Ice knew that water. He'd killed the thing that lived there at dawn on his first day.

The water there didn't bulge. It *sank*. The whole surface of the deep pool went down, slowly, like water going down a drain, a long slow whirl in the black. It sank and sank, and the fen around it tilted toward it. And then it came up.

It came up for a long time.

It was a heron. That was the first thing his mind gave him, and it was right, and it was the most wrong thing he'd ever seen. A heron made of the deep water itself, of everything that had ever lain at the bottom of the old fen. Black and wet and slow. Its legs were as tall as the militia house, jointed backwards like reeds. Its body was the size of the eel man's whole jetty. Its neck went up into the dark further than he could follow, and its head hung down from the top of it, long, lowered, nodding, looking for something. No eyes. When it lifted one long leg out of the deep pool and put it down again, the whole fen rippled, and the water under the narrows slapped against the four tall posts.

The Deep stood in the old fen with its long head swaying, and looked for the thing it wanted.

He knew what it wanted before it moved. It didn't want the lanterns on the north walk. It didn't want the windows of the round houses, or Marta's string of lights along the Landing, or the eel man's fire. It swung its long head slowly across all of them, the whole of Fernwick, and passed over every one.

And stopped on a very small, very old light, at the end of the north walk, on a porch.

An old clay lamp, held in an old woman's lap. Lit tonight for the first time in eleven years.

*My mother's*, Sedge had said.

The oldest light in Fernwick.

***

It came along the deep channel.

That was the only way it could come. It was too big for the tussocks and the reeds and the shallows. A thing that size needed deep water to walk in. And there was a deep channel through the old fen, running from the lurker's pool past the heron-stones, through the reeds, under the narrows and on to Sedge's steps.

Right under the four tall posts. Right under the pale new plank.

Ice put his back foot on it.

"*Dec*," said Xav, on the channel, very fast and very calm. "It's coming down the channel. It's coming *through* you. We can't—"

"I know."

"You can't hold that. It's level twenty-four. It's the size of a *house*—"

"I know, Xav."

"Then what are you—"

"Where a Holdfast stands," said Ice, "a line exists."

There was a short silence on the channel.

"Okay," said Xav. And then, with a completely different voice, the plan voice: "Okay. Kotori, eel man's post, don't move, don't come closer. Deston, louder, everything you've got, it's going to want the count. Suzu, the small ones that come in behind it. I'll be behind it." A tiny pause. "Dec stands here."

"Then?"

"Then I don't know," said Xav. "I'll tell you when I do."

The Deep came down the channel.

***

It didn't attack him. Not at first. It didn't know he was there. It came down the black channel toward the narrows with its long legs lifting and falling, and its head swaying high up in the dark, fixed on the little lamp on Sedge's porch, a long way behind him. When it reached the four posts it simply leaned forward, the way they all did, toward the light. Over the narrows. Over him.

And met the shield.

It was like being under a building as it fell down.

The weight came down on Steadfast, and on the old iron boss in the middle of it, Osric Fell's boss, and through them into his arm. It went through his arm into his shoulder, through his shoulder into his spine, down his spine into his back foot. Through his back foot into the plank, and through the plank into the four tall posts. The posts *groaned*. The whole narrows dipped a hand's width toward the water and came back up. And the cold. The cold of all the deep water in the old fen at once, the bottom of every pool, everything that had lain there in the dark since before Fernwick had a name.

He didn't make a sound.

He turned it. Not much. A finger's width. Just enough to slide the very edge of the weight off the slope of the shield and down past him into the water. The Deep's long head went past his shoulder, and down, and the channel exploded upward around him in a black fountain. He was at forty-one per cent. Then Kotori's warmth came up through him from behind and to the right, and he was at fifty-six.

The Deep lifted its head and swayed. It looked down, for the first time, at the small thing on the narrows between it and the light.

It leaned again.

***

Nothing they did made it smaller.

That was the next thing he learned, over the next hour. Xav came up behind it in the dark, *were you busy*, and put both daggers into the back of one long leg. The leg opened, and black water poured out of it. Then the water of the channel came up into the wound like a tide filling a footprint, and closed it. Suzu came round in the shallows with Aldo's scythe and took the end off one of its trailing feathers of dark, and it fell and splashed. And the Deep stood up a little straighter out of the deep water, and it was the same size again.

"It refills," said Xav, on the channel, flat. "Dec. It just refills. From the channel. It's standing in the deepest water in the fen and it's made of the water. We can't kill it. We can cut it all night and it just fills back in."

"Then don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't kill it." Ice was at forty-four. It leaned again. He took it on the shield, the old iron boss. Turned it, a finger. The narrows groaned. "Keep the small ones off me. Keep the steps. Keep Kotori's post. I'll hold this."

"For how long?"

Ice looked at the little clock in the corner of his vision. *Four hours and forty-seven minutes.* Until the dawn. Until the egg.

"Till it's time," he said.

Xav didn't say anything for a while.

"*At dawn, give it back*," he said, eventually, very quietly. "That's what the window said."

"That's what it said."

"You don't know what that means yet."

"No."

"Neither do I." A splash, behind the Deep's leg, and something small came apart. "Okay. Hold it. I'll work it out."

***

He held it.

He would never be able to remember most of that hour, or the next. Afterwards, watching the VOD, he wouldn't recognise most of it as something he'd done. Just the shield filling the whole frame. The old iron boss in the middle of it, with its long bright scar. And the dark leaning on it, over and over, and the frame sliding a finger's width to one side each time, and the black water coming up white around it.

Kotori, on her post, kept him alive. He never knew how. She told him afterwards, very apologetically, that she'd stopped healing what the window said and started healing where the Deep was *going* to lean. She'd been watching its head, the way it swayed before it came down. Half a second before every blow, she'd already sent the warmth. He'd be at twenty per cent and then fifty, inside one breath, and not know which had come first.

"Sorry," she said, every few minutes, on the channel. Her voice had gone small and hoarse. "That one was—"

"It wasn't."

"Sorry."

He drank the Good draughts. Eleven. Ten. Seven. He gave the last three to Xav for Kotori, because she was running out of everything, and he could hear it in her voice.

And Deston counted. That was the thing that held all of it together, the narrows and the steps and the Landing and the bridge. Deston, in the middle of the Landing, where everyone could hear him, counting. Nine and a half hours. His voice was going. Ice could hear it fraying at the edges, the *one* coming out cracked, the *and* sometimes not coming at all. A third of the cold was going back along a thread into him, too, the whole time. *Share the Weight*. Deston was on twenty per cent pain and he'd never once in his life been hit by anything bigger than a drowned chorister. Now he was taking a third of the oldest dark in the fen through a thread from three hundred paces away, and counting through it.

At half past five, his voice broke.

It just went, in the middle of a *two*. A crack, and then air. And for one second the count stopped.

Everything on the north walk went out of time. Ice felt it go: the beat dropped out from under his feet like a missing stair. The Deep leaned, and he was half a beat late on the turn, and the weight came down on him straight instead of on the slope.

He was on one knee on the pale plank. Nineteen per cent. The narrows groaned under him like a ship.

He didn't make a sound.

***

Then somebody started singing.

Not Deston. Somewhere behind Ice, at the end of the north walk. On a porch. An old woman's voice, thin and dry and perfectly in tune, singing something he'd never heard. A slow, plain, rocking song with no tune to speak of, in a rhythm like a blade going through reeds, one, and two, and one.

Sedge.

*Not for courage*, Hollis had said, at one in the morning, sitting on the boards behind him. *For counting. If I can hear you, you're there.*

Then Rafi, beside her on the steps, joined in. He didn't know the words. He hummed it, badly, in a voice that cracked. Then the eel man, hoarse. Then Marta, on the Landing, loud and flat and completely sure of herself. Then the four old militiamen on the bridge, who'd been singing it as boys, cutting reeds, fifty years ago. Then, from the bridge, the loudest of all, an old man with three fingers, singing the song he'd sung on a riverbank at seventeen so that a stubborn man on a stone would know he was still there.

And Deston, on the Landing, got his breath back. He heard the song, and found the beat in it, the way he always found the beat. He came in under it, very low, rough, the *one* and the *two*. Not singing the words, because he didn't know them. Counting them.

He got up.

Off his knee, back onto his back foot, on the pale plank, with the old iron boss in front of him. The oldest dark in the fen leaned down on him out of the night, and all of Fernwick sang a reed-cutting song behind him. He turned it. A finger's width. In time.

***

At a quarter to seven, he was still there.

Most of the travellers weren't. The window said four hundred and twelve. The rest had died, or been thrown out, or gone. Fernwick had a hundred and thirty lights left. The outer boardwalks were dark, all of them, and the dark had come in to the edge of the town proper. But the north walk was lit. The Landing was lit. The bridge was lit. Every round house in the middle of the town had a lamp in the window. And at the end of the north walk, on a porch, in the lap of an old woman who was still singing, one very old clay lamp was burning.

The Deep was still leaning on him.

He'd stopped feeling the cold as cold. It was just the way things were now. The weight, coming down. The turn, a finger's width. The weight, sliding off. The beat. Again. He'd been doing it for two hours and fourteen minutes. His real body, lying in a white egg in a shoplot in Melaka Raya, was eleven hours into the longest single sitting of its life. In the corner of his vision, the little clock said *10:45 of 12*.

An hour and fifteen minutes to the egg. To the dawn.

"Xav," he said.

"Here." Xav was behind the Deep's left leg, in the water. He'd been there for an hour, cutting the small ones off it as they came up the channel behind it. "Here."

"Have you worked it out?"

There was a pause.

"Not yet," said Xav. "Nearly."

"Nearly's good."

"Nearly's *not* good. Nearly's the worst thing I've ever said to you." A splash. "But I think—Dec. At midnight. When the sky went grey. You remember what they did?"

"They stopped. They looked east."

"All of them. Every single one. In every town. Hyvred saw it on every bridge in Brackenford at once. They *stopped*, and they lifted their heads, and they looked at it." A breath. "They know what the dawn is. They know it's coming. At midnight it didn't come. This time it will."

"So?"

"So what do they do," said Xav, very quietly, "when they know the dawn's really coming? When they've got twenty minutes left in the whole night and they haven't got what they came for?"

Ice looked up at the Deep. Its long head, swaying in the dark, high above him. Still fixed, past him, on a very small lamp on a porch.

*At dawn, on the second day, they all came at once*, Hollis had said. *Every man they had left. One last push. All their weight together, at one man on one stone.*

"They push," said Ice.

"They push," said Xav. "Everything. All at once. At you." A pause. "And you've got two hours of everything it's ever put on you. Held."

Ice stood on the plank in the dark, with the oldest thing in the fen leaning on him, and the town singing, and an hour and fifteen minutes on the clock.

"Dec stands here," said Xav, very softly. "Then—"

"Then what?"

"Then that's yours," said Xav. "I can't draw that bit. Nobody can. That's the bit that's yours."
