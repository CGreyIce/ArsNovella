He'd read that one standing up in the kitchen. And then he'd read it again. And then he'd felt his face do something it didn't usually do, and Gabe had walked in with the kopi and stopped in the doorway and looked at him. And then turned round and walked straight out again, very carefully, as if he'd seen something he wasn't supposed to.

*It's allowed.*

He didn't know why that was the one. He'd thought about it for three days. He'd known for months. Since the step outside the banner room door, since *No*, since the tail. He hadn't done anything. He'd held the lamp the same as always and the cup the same as always, and he'd thought that was the right thing. That it was hers. That if she wanted to say something, she'd say it, and if she didn't, she wouldn't. And either way it wasn't his to make happen.

And somewhere under that, without ever looking at it straight, he'd thought something else too. That it wasn't *allowed*. Not for him. That he was the one at the far end of the room, at the front of the line, in the dark under the floor, and that was where he was useful. People who stood at the front of the line didn't get to have someone's tail on their boot. That wasn't what the front of the line was for.

He hadn't known he thought that until somebody wrote *it's allowed*.

***

Hyvred was at the hatch.

She'd been at the hatch every morning for six weeks. Since *Kei says nobody alone, he didn't say for when*. She'd come down the north tunnel with him every day and called every Delver in it, and every Scree-Runner on the high fells. And every morning at the cold place where the old stone met the new, she'd sat four paces off with her bow across her knees. Then, since the longest night, two.

He'd never said anything about the two.

"Hi, Hyv," he said.

"Hi," said Hyvred.

She looked at him. She looked at him the way she looked at everything now, openly, since the banner room on Christmas night. She hadn't looked away since. It was as if, having looked once, she couldn't find the place where she used to stop.

He looked back.

He'd always looked back. He just hadn't known, until somebody wrote *it's allowed*, that he'd been holding something while he did it. Like a man holding a door half shut with his shoulder without noticing, for so long that his arm had forgotten it was doing it.

He let the door go a bit. He didn't know what it looked like from outside. Something, probably. Hyvred's tail went straight up behind her, stiff as a broom handle, and she turned round very fast and went down the hatch stair in front of him without minding the third step. She nearly fell.

"*Third step*," called Ama Corrie, from the counter.

"*I KNOW*," said Hyvred, from the bottom.

He went down after her. He held the lamp out to the side, the way he always did.

***

He was forty-nine and three-quarters on Thursday.

It had been slow, the last bit. Everybody said it would be. The game had made the last levels before fifty long on purpose, a wall at the top of the climb, like the last flight of the two hundred and twelve. Sixty-six steps, the hardest ones, with the wind. He'd been going up and down the north tunnel and out onto the high fells for three weeks to get through it, with Hyvred calling and the Undertow filling and emptying and filling again.

On Thursday, at the second dusk, on the high fells above the north road, with the wind coming off the moor, it went.

He was in the middle of something. A Scree-Runner, the big kind, the ones that came down the slopes in a rattle of stone. Hyvred had called it from the ridge, *left, coming down, now*, and he'd gone left and it was there. He turned it on the shield's slope and the Falling Cut came down, eighty-eight per cent, and the thing went over in the heather and stopped.

The window came up.

He read the last four lines several times.

*Vane's here. He asked if you were coming. He doesn't ask things.*

He didn't. Vane never had. He'd been the quietest of the twenty, in a little phone game years ago, in a guild with a name nobody should have let a teenager choose. He'd been one of the first to join. Ice had run the war. Vane had been his vice, and made sure there was still a guild in the morning.

He'd never asked for anything. Not once in two years. He'd just done what needed doing, quietly, and been right.

*He asked if you were coming.*

Ice sat in the first of the nine with the letter in his hand and looked at it for a long time.

He told them at the long table on Friday.

He didn't make a speech. He never made speeches. He waited until the banner room was full at the second dusk, sixty chairs and a stool and the old woman asleep in the chair by the north door. Then he said it, from the first of the nine, to Moonlight Harvest first, because they were his.

"I'm going to Masmark," he said. "On Monday. Ahead."

Moonlight Harvest looked at him.

"Ahead of who?" said Dennis.

"Everyone," said Ice. "I'm the only one at fifty. It's fourteen days. Kei says the first ten won't be ready till the end of February." He looked down the nine. "Somebody I know is there. From before. I want to see him."

"From the murder guild?" said Rachael.

"From the murder guild," said Ice.

Rachael opened her mouth, and then shut it, and then said, "Okay," in a voice that meant she'd be saying a lot more later.

"What about us?" said Calvin. "The halls. We've got the Thornway on Monday night. The ten-hall. You're the front."

"Gabe can tank," said Ice.

Everybody looked at Gabe.

Gabe was sitting in his chair halfway down the nine with a pie he'd brought from the Hart. He'd been eating it. He stopped eating it. He looked at Ice for a long moment with the fork halfway to his mouth.

"I can tank," said Gabe slowly.

"You can tank," said Ice. "You're an Anvil. *One who is struck so that others are not.* You've been standing behind me for a year doing it on the side." He shrugged. "Do it at the front."

Gabe put the fork down.

He didn't say anything for a while. That was so unlike Gabe that the whole of Moonlight Harvest stopped and looked at him. Kotori with her pot. Sheng Yang, who never looked at anyone. Deston, who'd come up early for the weekend on the six o'clock bus, again, with the key to the unit opposite in his pocket, again.

"I'm not as good as you," said Gabe.

"No," said Ice. "You're different. You talk the whole time. Everything that hits you, you tell it about SimpleMMO." He looked at Gabe. "It works. I've watched it."

Gabe went red. All the way up.

"*Okay*," he said. "Okay. Okay okay okay. I can tank. I can *tank*." He picked the fork up again and pointed it at Ice. "You owe me a pie."

"I'll owe you a pie."

"A *Masmark* pie. From the fish-on-a-stick man."

"He sells fish," said Ice. "On a stick."

"Then a *fish*," said Gabe, with enormous dignity, and went back to his pie.

***

He told Kei last, at the head of the table, because Kei had to know where everybody was.

Kei listened. He unrolled the map and found the round town, and the road south and east, fourteen days of it. Past the White Hills and the Millrace road, and on, off the edge of everything Kei had drawn so far, into blank paper. He put his finger on the blank.

"Masmark's here somewhere," said Kei. "Off the map. I've never drawn it."

"I'll tell you what it's like."

"You said that about the north." Kei looked at him. "I'll hold you to it." Then, after a moment: "Nobody alone, Ice."

"It's not the longest night."

"I know. I'm saying it anyway." Kei rolled up the map. "It's on the board. It's not coming off the board."

"I'll be on the road two weeks," said Ice. "Then I'll be in a city with four hundred guilds in it." He thought about it. "I won't be alone. There'll be about ten thousand people."

Kei looked at him for a long time.

"That's not a person," said Kei.

Ice laughed. He hadn't meant to. It was Wendy's line, from the gate, to Harlan, in October. *Ten thousand people.* *That's not a person.* Kei had been there. Kei had been standing right beside her.

"No," said Ice. "It isn't."

And down the long table, halfway along, beside Bee, there was a sound. A chair going back. Very fast.

He looked.

Hyvred was standing up. She was looking at him, from her place beside Bee, with her face white. Not red. White, the way it never went. Her tail was perfectly still. Bee was looking at her with her pencil stopped on the page and her wing half out.

Hyvred didn't say anything. She turned round and walked out of the banner room. Not fast. Very straight, in a line, past Thea's six and Hana's five and the window. Out of the door and across the Hall, and he heard her feet on the stair to the eye. Going up. All two hundred and twelve.

The banner room was very quiet.

Bee closed her sketchbook.

She looked down the table at him. She didn't say anything either. She just looked at him, for a long moment, the way she looked at a thing she was about to draw. Then she got up and went after Hyvred. Out of the door, across the Hall, up the stair.

Nobody said anything.

Across the room, at the window, Thea was looking at him. Xola beside her had both hands over her mouth.

His phone wasn't in the game. It was on the shelf by his pod in the back room at Melaka Raya, face down. He couldn't see it. But he knew, quite certainly, sitting in the first of the nine with the whole banner room looking at him, that it was buzzing.

Gabe laughed. Then he put his arm round Ice's shoulders, the way he did, without asking, the big heavy arm of a man who'd been his friend since a school choir and had never once asked permission to do that. And Ice let him.
