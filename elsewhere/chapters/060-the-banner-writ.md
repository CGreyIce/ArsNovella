By the time Kei came into the game on Thursday evening, the whole of Carrow Cross was standing in the Hall of Roads, looking at a piece of paper.

He'd heard about it on the bus. Everybody had. It had gone up at the first dawn, late in the morning his time, while he was nowhere near an egg. One notice, the same notice, in every Hall in Elsewhere at once, the way the Long Night notice had gone up a month ago. By lunchtime, Maigo had screamed about it on stream for forty minutes. Chris had a sheet. Ice's chat had turned it into a meme. Salty had sent Kei a single message, which said *kei* and then nothing else. Kei had understood it perfectly.

So he'd known. But it was different, standing under it.

[ In every Hall in Elsewhere, from today:
THE BANNER WRIT
A guild is twenty who answer to one name.
Twenty founding names, each of level fifteen or more, signed by hand in one Hall, within thirty days of the first.
Five hundred gold, paid with the first name. Kept, if the thirtieth day ends short.
The first name is the founder's. The founder may belong to no named party or company.
A guild has no limit. A guild has a banner, a room in its Hall, and a place on the Tally. ]

He read it three times, the way he read everything. Then he looked up.

***

The Hall of Roads was round.

That was the first thing he'd noticed about it on Monday, when he'd walked into Carrow Cross after six days on the road from Hallowell. Most Halls were long, like barns: a door at one end and a counter at the other and a board on the wall. This one was round, and old, and built of a pale grey stone gone gold with lamp smoke. It had seven doors, one in every direction. Seven roads came into Carrow Cross across seven kinds of country, from the hills and the moors and the forests and the plain, and each one ended at its own door of the Hall. You could walk in from the Steaming Hills and walk straight out again toward the sea without changing direction.

And it had a ceiling.

It was a dome, high up, out of reach of the lamps. Somebody, a very long time ago, had painted it the deep blue of a summer night, and then painted stars on it. Hundreds of them, in gold leaf, most of it worn away now to brown smudges. Between some of the stars, very faint, so faint you had to stand in exactly the right place under exactly the right lamp to see them, there were lines. Thin silver lines, joining one star to another. Not all of them. Just some. As if somebody had stood on a ladder up there with a brush and decided which stars belonged together.

Kei had stood under it for a long time on Monday, with his head back, until his neck hurt.

He was doing it again now. With the notice in front of him and two hundred people round him all talking at once, and somewhere at the back a man in a red sash writing very fast in a little book.

*Lines between them.*

He touched the green tie in his hair without noticing. Then he looked back down, at the paper, and did the sums.

***

Twenty names. Level fifteen.

He was twelve.

He'd known he would be. He'd known it since the end of December, when the pod cafés went from twelve hours on opening day to six hours a night, and his levels started coming once a week instead of once a day. Six hours was a lot. It was a whole evening, every evening, in a little wooden room in a mall in Australia with rain on the window. But it wasn't twelve. Ice was twelve hours a day, every day, and Ice was twenty-three. Kei was six hours a night, and he was twelve. Everyone he knew was somewhere between eight and thirteen, except the ones who'd started early.

Twenty names, all fifteen or over, all here, in this round room, within thirty days of each other.

He did the sums again, standing still, while the crowd moved round him.

On the call there were twelve. Maigo, Salty, Wendy, Ina, Hako. Himself. Higu and Gigi, since Christmas. Niko, Newish, Chris and Winter, since the week after New Year. Twelve, scattered over most of the map, in twelve towns, every one of them six hours a night or less. Most of them nowhere near fifteen. Niko's four had started three weeks after everyone else and were barely past ten.

Then there were the others. The ones who weren't on the call but were on the map. He could see the map in his head, the sheet of paper on his desk at home, covered in dots and lines. Drew on his long walk, still most of a week short of Tiger. Tiger in his canal city. Hyvred and Bee in Brackenford. And the cluster of eight small dots around Xav, joined to each other and to Ice with their own lines.

And Moonlight Harvest.

That was the sum everyone else in this room was doing, he thought. Everyone who knew who he was, anyway, and a few people in the crowd did. He'd seen their eyes go to him and the green tie when he came in. Everyone would be thinking the same thing. *That's the swamp guy's friend. Moonlight Harvest are six. Their healer's from Singapore, their swordsman's from Singapore, half his chat's in this game. If the swamp guy puts his name on a writ, he'll have twenty by Friday.*

He would, too. Ice could fill a writ in a day. He could fill ten. He had thirty thousand people watching him every morning, and every one of them would walk to Carrow Cross barefoot to see their name on the same banner as his.

And it would be Ice's guild. Whatever it was called, whatever it was for. From the first day to the last, it would be the swamp guy's guild.

Kei stood under the painted sky with his hands in his pockets and thought about that for a long time.

***

"You're the one with the map," said a voice beside him.

He looked down. A small, sharp old woman was standing at his elbow, with spectacles on a chain round her neck and a heavy ring of keys at her belt. She was looking up at him with an expression of mild professional interest, the way Ysolde looked at a vial.

"Sorry?"

"The map," she said. "You were here on Monday, standing under my ceiling with your head back for half an hour, and then you sat on my steps and drew something in a little book. A long time. Lines." She put her spectacles on. "I keep this Hall. Ama Corrie. I notice people who look at my ceiling. Nobody looks at my ceiling."

"I'm sorry," said Kei. "I didn't mean—"

"Don't be sorry. Somebody painted it. Somebody ought to look at it." She tilted her head back and looked up at the dome herself, as if checking it was still there. "Four hundred years old, that ceiling. They say the first Rollkeeper painted it, so the guilds would have something to look at while they waited for their twenty."

Kei looked at her.

"There were guilds before?"

"There were guilds before." She took her spectacles off again and let them fall on their chain. "The room's through there." She nodded at a door he hadn't noticed, a small one, set in the wall between two of the seven big ones, with an iron lock. "The banner room. One banner, one guild, one Hall. The last banner in that room came down before my grandmother was born. It's been empty my whole life. I sweep it on Sundays." She looked at the notice on the board, and at the two hundred people talking, and at the man in the red sash at the back, writing in his little book. "And this morning, that went up. I've been asked about my room eleven times today. Four of them by people in red."

"What did you tell them?"

"That it's not for sale," said Ama Corrie. "That it's for a writ." She looked at him with her sharp old eyes. "And that the writ says twenty, and it says *by hand*, and it says *in this Hall*. I don't care what colour you are. If you can bring me twenty names, you can have my room."

She turned to go. Then she turned back.

"Your map," she said. "Is it the roads? Because I've got better maps than that of the roads."

"No," said Kei. "It's not the roads. It's—" He thought about how to say it. "It's people. Who already know each other. Lines between them."

Ama Corrie looked at him for a long moment, over the top of the spectacles she wasn't wearing.

"Hm," she said. Then she looked up at her ceiling, and back at him. "Well," she said. "Bring it in sometime. I'd like to see it."

***

He didn't stream that night.

He'd meant to. He'd set it up. But when he sat down on the steps of the Hall at the edge of the round market, with the lamps lit and the seven roads going away into the dark, he found he didn't want anyone watching while he thought. So he put the stream on a still picture of Carrow Cross at night, with the words *thinking, back soon*, and he thought.

Two hundred and fourteen people were watching a still picture of a market. Six weeks ago it had been twenty-five people watching him do anything at all. He thought about that too.

He thought about the sheet of paper on his desk at home. Every dot was someone. Every line was something that had already happened: a call at three in the morning, a raid, a game, a year. He hadn't invented any of them. He'd just drawn what was already there. And on the night he'd drawn it, a month ago, before the longest night, he'd thought: *something big enough to hold all the little groups at once, without making any of them stop being what they were*. *One day. Not now.*

And now the game had nailed a piece of paper to a wall that said *a guild is twenty who answer to one name*.

He thought about Ice.

He knew things about Ice that most of the thirty thousand didn't. He'd known him for years, through Suzu, through a call at three in the morning that had turned into a hundred calls. He knew about the CS skins, years ago, and what they'd turned into. He knew that the man who stood on a plank in a swamp for twelve hours and never made a sound could have paid the five hundred gold on the writ out of his pocket that night, and never noticed. He knew that if he asked, Ice would. Instantly. Without thinking about it for a second.

So he wasn't going to ask him for that.

He was going to ask him for the other thing. Because you had to. Because you didn't make a thing like this and not ask Ice first, and Xav, and the people who'd been there before anybody. You asked. That was how it worked. That was what the lines were.

And he was fairly sure, sitting on the steps of the Hall of Roads with the seven roads going away into the dark, what Ice was going to say.

He took out his notebook and opened it to the page from Monday. The lines, copied small from the sheet at home. He looked at it for a while. Then, under it, in his careful handwriting, he wrote three names.

*Maigo.*

*Niko.*

*Drew.*

Not the twelve. Not yet. Three. The three who'd make it real if it was going to be real, and tell him honestly if it wasn't. Maigo knew everybody. He'd brought Tiger and Drew to Ice in the first place, and he'd been at the café door at nine minutes to seven on opening day, and he'd say yes before Kei finished asking. Niko had known Ice longer than any of them, and he always picked the thing everyone said was a waste and quietly made it work. And Drew was on a long road in the middle of the map. He hosted things. He'd run tournaments for years, and he knew exactly what it cost to keep a door open. He'd stood in one all night on the longest night, with a bird on the road.

He sent the same message to all three.

*Can you talk tomorrow? Just us four. It's about the paper.*

Maigo answered in eleven seconds. *YES. I KNEW IT. I KNEW IT WAS GOING TO BE YOU.*

Niko answered in four minutes. *ok*

Drew answered in an hour, from the road, from somewhere south of the Waymeet in the dark, most of a week short of the canal city. *I'm walking. I can walk and talk. What time.*

Kei looked at the three answers for a long time, sitting on the steps of a round hall in a town he'd walked six days to reach. Then he looked up, past the lamps, at the dark where the dome was. He couldn't see it from out here. He knew it was there.
