Hana had said *one day* so many times it had stopped sounding like a day.

She knew it had. She'd heard herself. On calls, at three in the morning her time and nine at night his. On the long table, in the game, with her head on Milli's shoulder. In Singapore, once, the week he came. *One day.* *Somewhere with a view.* She'd said it the way you'd say *when it stops raining*. Something that would happen, obviously. Some time. And that you didn't have to do anything about, because it would happen anyway.

It hadn't happened.

It wasn't anybody's fault. That was the thing. Nobody was stopping it. She lived in Singapore and Milli lived on the other side of the world, six hours behind in summer, seven in winter. And everything about *out there* was papers and flights and families and money and where. And every time they got close to talking about it properly, one of them said *one day*. And the other one said *yes*. And they both felt better, and nothing changed.

She'd said it on the longest night, by the well, at midnight. With the Bearer's Star lit in her hand for the first time and the dark leaning in toward her. *One day. Somewhere with a view.*

And Milli had looked round at the round market in the dark and said *this is somewhere*.

She'd thought about that a lot.

***

Jared said *go up to the eye at the first dawn, don't ask*, so she went up to the eye at the first dawn and didn't ask.

She was very good at not asking. She'd been not asking all of Tuesday, when Leaka came down the two hundred and twelve at the second dawn with her face wet and her fists in her mouth. And Curse came down behind her looking very hard at his pen. And Ice came into the banner room at the second dusk with sawdust on his shoulders, which he'd never once had in his life, and sat in the first of the nine with his face doing nothing at all, very carefully.

She hadn't asked anybody. Not even Ice. She'd looked at the sawdust on his shoulders and looked away.

Milli met her at the bottom of the stair. He'd come in early. It was two in the morning for him, out there, in the Netherlands, and he'd set an alarm for it. He was standing behind Ama Corrie's counter at the bottom of the stair with his hands in his pockets, looking up the stair.

"He said don't ask," said Milli.

"He said don't ask."

"So we don't ask," said Milli.

"We don't ask," said Hana. And took his hand.

Ama Corrie looked at them over her spectacles from behind her ledger.

"Mind the third step," she said.

***

Two hundred and twelve steps.

She'd never been up. That surprised her, when she thought about it on the way. More than a year in the round town, and she'd never once gone up the stair to the eye. Kei went up, and Higu, and John. They were the ones who saw furthest. And Jared, in January, measuring for her roof. She'd stayed down. In the banner room, in the market, by the well. She'd never thought about going up.

A hundred and six, and a landing. She stopped there and looked back down the stair, round and round the inside of the dome, into the dark.

"You okay?" said Milli.

"It's a long way."

"It's a long way," agreed Milli. He didn't let go of her hand. "Forty more. Then the long bit."

"How do you know?"

"Kei's map," said Milli. "It's on the wall in the banner room. The stair. A hundred and six, forty, sixty-six." He looked up. "I looked last night. After Jared said."

"You *looked*?"

"I didn't ask," said Milli. "I looked."

She laughed. On the landing, a hundred and six steps up the inside of a dome, at the first dawn. And they went on.

***

The last sixty-six had the wind in them. Coming down through the eye at the top, cold and clean, off the moor. She could feel it on her face, then in her hair, then pushing at her. Milli went in front for the last of it, so the wind hit him first. She didn't ask him to. He just did.

And then the stair stopped, and there was light.

She came out onto the roof of the Hall of Roads into the first dawn.

***

She didn't see the bench at first.

She saw the sky.

All of it. All the way round. Gold in the east, over the east road, where the sun was coming up. Going pale blue above, and still dark in the west, with a few stars. She'd never seen so much sky in her life. Not in here, not out there. Not in Singapore, where there were always buildings. Not at the well, where the round market's roofs made a circle of it. She stood at the top of the stair with Milli's hand in hers and looked up, and turned round, slowly. And it went all the way round, the whole ring of it.

Then she looked down.

The round town, below, like the middle of a wheel. The fountain and the market and the ring of nine dark stones and the well with Jared's oak lid. And the seven roads going out from it, like spokes. North over the moor, with ninety-four little lights on the backs of grey stones going away into the morning. East toward the sunrise. South, round the curve. South-west, all the way to a white line on the edge of the world that she knew was the White Hills. And the moor, and the fields, and the woods, and the edge of everything.

"Oh," said Hana.

Milli didn't say anything. He was looking at her, not at the view. She could see him out of the corner of her eye.

Then he said, very quietly, "Hana. Look."

And she looked where he was looking. On the south side of the ring of flat stone round the eye, facing out over the round market and the south road and the White Hills.

A bench.

***

It was pale. Ash. Old wood, but new; she could see the marks of the plane on it. Room for two, close. Legs, and a seat, and a back. And round the top of the back, seven iron rings, rusty, the kind you'd tie a rope to. And in the middle of the back, a scar. A long split in the grain, healed, where something had nearly broken and hadn't.

She knew that scar.

She'd watched it happen. On Chinese New Year, at the second dusk, with the wind off the moor and six hundred and twelve lanterns pulling south. The middle pole starting to go. And Jared, at the bottom of it, with his beam on his shoulder, and Azac's back against his. Nine people under one roof. And Isa out of her chair, with her hand on the wood. *There. Ten.*

She'd watched it nearly go. And then not.

"It's the pole," she said. Her voice was doing something. "It's the middle pole. From my roof."

"Yes," said Milli.

"He *kept* it."

"He kept it," said Milli. "Since February. In the yard behind the Hart. Kotori told me last night. She said she wasn't going to say anything, and then she said everything." He was almost laughing. "She said *sorry* twice."

Hana walked round to the front of the bench. Slowly. She didn't sit down. She looked at it.

And on the back, under the scar, small, in blue, in a hand she'd have known anywhere, the one word.

*Somewhere.*

***

She sat down on the step at the top of the stair and cried.

She didn't mean to. She cried at everything; everybody knew. She'd typed *i'm crying* in chat a hundred times, and mostly she had been, a bit. This was different. This was the kind she couldn't stop.

Milli sat down beside her on the step and didn't say anything. He put his arm round her. The way he had on the longest night, at the well, when the blue door window went out and he didn't go, because nobody alone.

"It's a view," she said, into his shoulder.

"It's a view."

"He built us a *view*."

"He built us a view," said Milli. "And Curse wrote what it was."

"*Somewhere*," said Hana, and cried harder.

***

There was a space on the arm.

Milli found it. She'd stopped crying, mostly. They'd sat down on the bench at last, the two of them, close, the way it was built, and looked out. And Milli's hand had gone to the end of the left arm the way your hand goes to the arm of a bench. And stopped.

A flat place. The size of a palm. Planed smooth. Nothing on it.

Milli looked at it for a long time.

"He left a gap," he said.

Hana looked at it.

Everything Milli made had a cat's-paw on it. Every scabbard, every grip, every strap. Salty's sword had one on the grip, stamped into the black leather. He'd been doing it since the first week in Quarrymouth, with a stamp he'd cut himself out of a bit of bone. He said it was so you'd know. He never said know what.

"He left it for you," said Hana.

"For us," said Milli.

He took the stamp out of his pocket. He always carried it. A little bone thing on a cord, the cat's-paw cut into the end, worn smooth. And a scrap of leather from his other pocket, because he always carried that too, a square of good brown hide no bigger than his palm.

He laid the leather on his knee. He set the stamp on it. He looked at her.

"You do it," said Milli.

"It's yours."

"It's ours," said Milli. "He left it for both of us. Put your hand on mine."

She put her hand on his, on the stamp. And they pressed it down together, into the leather, on his knee, on a bench on the roof of the Hall of Roads at the first dawn.

Then he pegged the leather into the flat place on the arm with four little wooden pegs Jared had left on the seat in a row, without saying anything, exactly the right size.

A cat's-paw. On a bench. With seven rings and a scar and *Somewhere* on the back.

***

They sat there a long time.

The sun came up over the east road. The round town woke up below them. Moonlight Harvest going out onto the high fells, very small, Gabe at the front; she could hear him from up here, faintly, talking. Tiger and Maigo on the north road. Kite in the south gate, with his hands at his sides. The woman at the milestone at the bottom of the south road, asleep against the stone.

And on the north steps, at the far side of the Hall, where the north door opened onto the moor: a chair.

Hana leaned over the parapet to see. It was Isa's. The one from beside the north door, with the grey blanket. Somebody had carried it out onto the top of the north steps and set it facing up the north road. And Isa was in it, very small, with the blanket round her shoulders and the jar in her lap, both root hands round it. Looking up the road. At the ninety-four lamps on the backs of the grey stones, going away north over the moor into the morning.

Suzu was sitting on the step below her chair. Not saying anything. Just there.

"She's looking at the road," said Hana.

"Every morning now," said Milli. "Since the twelve went south. Suzu carries her out at the first dawn and carries her back in at the second." He was quiet. "Kotori says she sits there the whole time. Looking north. With the jar."

"At what?"

"At where it goes," said Milli. "I think."

Hana watched them for a long time. The old woman in the chair with the light in her lap, and the boy on the step below her who was going to carry it back one day. Up that road, past the last lamp, into the dark. Some year.

She thought about Deston, on a green at dusk, with a candle with two wicks. About Hyv on a bus with both sides of the road at once, knocking on a door at the end of a corridor. About a woman at a milestone who'd been sitting there fourteen weeks to find out what it was.

"Everybody's picking days," she said. "This year."

"It's catching," said Milli.

She looked at him.

"Ama Corrie said that," said Milli. "About the broom. *Forty years nobody asks. Then three times in a month.*" He looked down at the round town, at the long table somewhere under the dome below their feet. "*It's catching.*"

Hana took the star out of her pocket.

The Bearer's Star. From the tunnel under Quarrymouth, at the end of the wall, set into the last little guy's banner. She'd kept it in her pocket since Quarrymouth. It had lit once, on the longest night, when the lid came off the well. And not since. It wasn't lit now. It was just a small star of old metal, five-pointed, warm from her pocket.

She held it out over the parapet, toward the view. She didn't know why.

"One day," she said.

Milli was quiet for a long time.

"Hana," he said.

She looked at him.

"We keep saying *one day*," said Milli. "We've been saying it for years. On calls. In here. On the longest night." He was looking at the view, not at her. His ears were going red. "*One day. Somewhere with a view.* And I said *this is somewhere*, and I meant it. I still mean it." He looked down at the cat's-paw on the arm of the bench. "But it's not a day. *One day* isn't a day. It's just a thing we say so we don't have to say a day."

She didn't say anything. She couldn't.

"So let's say a day," said Milli. "Here. Now. Out there's where it'll be. Out there's papers and flights and everybody's families and where." He swallowed. "But we can pick the day in here. On this bench. With the view." He looked at her. "Then it's not *one day* any more. It's a day."

***

She looked at him for a long time.

At Milli, on a bench on the roof of the round town at the first dawn, with his ears red and his bone stamp still on its cord in his hand. Six hours behind her, out there. In a room in the Netherlands at three in the morning, with an alarm he'd set so he could be here when Jared said.

She'd known him a long time. She'd known him longer than she'd known how to say what she wanted. She'd learned it from him, mostly.

"Okay," said Hana.

Milli stared at her.

"Okay," said Hana again. Louder. "A day. Let's pick a day."

"*Now*?"

"You said now," said Hana. "You said *here*. On this bench." She wiped her face with the back of the hand that wasn't holding the star. "So pick one."

He laughed. Out loud, on the roof, in the wind. It went out over the parapet and down over the round market.

And they picked one.

***

It took about four minutes. It should have taken longer, Hana thought afterward. It should have taken months of calls and lists and arguments. It didn't. They'd been talking about it for years, really, without saying it. They both already knew.

Milli said a month. She'd known he'd say that one; it was the one he'd always said, on calls, when they were being silly. *Imagine if it was in—* And she said a day in it. Not a particular one. Just the first one that came, the way the right arrow came for Kestrel, without deciding.

And then they said the year at the same time.

It was the same year.

She looked at him. He looked at her. On a bench on the roof of the Hall of Roads, with the whole round town below and seven roads going out to the edge of the world.

The star in her hand was warm.

Not lit. She looked down at it. It wasn't lit. But it was warm, in her palm. The way it had been warm in Milli's hand at midnight on the longest night, by the well, when she'd said *swap* and given it to him to hold. Warm, the way something's warm when somebody's been holding it for a long time.

"It's warm," she said.

Milli looked at it.

"It knows," he said.

"It doesn't *know*. It's a star. It's a bit of metal."

"Everything here knows," said Milli. "Ice says."

She laughed, and cried, and did both, the way she did. And put her head on Milli's shoulder on the bench, and looked at the view. And the star stayed warm in her hand for a long time.

***

They went down at the second dawn.

All the way down the sixty-six with the wind at their backs now, and the forty, and the hundred and six. At the bottom, on the last landing before the Hall, two men were sitting on the step. Not doing anything. Very obviously not waiting for anybody.

Jared. And Ice, with sawdust still on his shoulders.

They both stood up when they saw them come round the turn.

Hana stopped on the stair.

Jared didn't say anything. He looked at her face. He'd known her face a long time; he'd held a lot of roofs up for her. He looked at it and he knew. She could see him know.

"*Y'all*," said Jared, very softly.

She came down the last few steps and hugged him. Hard. She'd done it for years, on calls, across eleven thousand miles, with the camera going sideways. She did it now on the stair of the Hall of Roads. And Jared stood there being hugged with his arms round her, and said *y'all* again into her hair, and his voice went.

When she let go, Ice was looking at her.

"Alice," he said.

He was the only other one who called her that. Him and Jared. For years. She'd never once asked him to. He'd just started, one day, and never stopped.

"Ice," said Hana.

He looked at the star in her hand. At Milli beside her, with his ears still red. At her face.

"Good," said Ice.

That was all. The way he said it. As if it settled something. She'd heard him say it to Suzu and to Gabe and to Kestrel at the top of the steps at the bottom of a lake. She'd never heard him say it to her.

"We're going to tell the long table," said Hana. "On Sunday. When everybody's there."

"I'll be there," said Ice.

"I know," said Hana. "You'll be in the first of the nine with your feet on the rung."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"Yes," he said.
