Jared had known Alice for a long time. He'd called her Alice for all of it, and in all that time he'd never once seen her as happy as she was at the bottom of a hole.

"*Jared*," she said, from somewhere below him in the dark. "Jared. *Jared.* Come and look at this."

"I'm holding the roof up, Alice."

"Hold it up from over *here*."

"That's not how roofs work."

He was standing in a tunnel under Quarrymouth, on Monday morning, his time. In the game it was the middle of a long afternoon three ledges down from where they'd woken up. He had his shoulder under a timber beam he'd cut and shaped himself that morning, out of a drift log off the beach, because he'd taken Carpentry. He'd taken Carpentry because he liked the idea of making a thing that held. He had not, when he took it, imagined holding a quarry up with his actual shoulder while his best friend dug a stone face out of a wall with a pick.

"Milli," he said. "Tell her that's not how roofs work."

"That's not how roofs work," said Milli, calmly, from somewhere between them, coiling rope.

"*Thank* you."

"But she's found something," said Milli. "So hold it up from here anyway."

***

There was a city under Quarrymouth.

Everybody in the town knew. They'd known for three hundred years. You couldn't cut stone out of a cliff for that long without cutting into the corners of something. The old quarrymen had just cut round it, and built round it, and left the bits sticking out of the walls. Columns, doorways, carved faces with half a smile. The townspeople hung washing off them. A stone hand stuck out of the wall of the harbourmaster's house, and he used it as a hook for his hat.

Nobody had ever gone *in*.

Alice had gone in on Sunday morning. She'd woken up, and gone straight to the quarry wall behind their three little rooms, and put her hand on the half-smiling face beside her door. Then she'd taken out her pick and started, very gently, to take the rock away from round it.

By Monday she'd found the door behind it.

Not a real door. The shape of one, in the stone, carved, very old, with letters across the top in no language any of them knew. She'd dug round it until it was clear, and then dug through the rubble in front of it. And she'd found that the rubble went back a long way, and that behind it was a tunnel, and that the tunnel went down.

"Jared," she'd said. "I need a roof."

So Jared was a roof.

"Look," said Alice, when he finally came along the tunnel with the beam wedged behind him and his shoulder aching.

She was crouched at the end of it, by the light of a lamp on a hook, in front of a wall. Not rubble. A real wall, of smooth fitted stone, with no tool marks on it at all. Carved into it, so finely that you had to put the lamp right up close to see them, were dozens of small figures. People. Walking. All in a line, all the same way, along the wall into the dark. Each of them carried something in front of them, held up in both hands, small and round. A light.

"They're carrying lamps," said Alice. "All of them. Look. Every one."

"Where are they going?" said Milli.

Alice followed the line along the wall with the lamp, a piece at a time. The walking figures went on along the tunnel for as far as the light reached, and further. She came back.

"I don't know," she said. "It goes on." She looked at the pick in her hand, and then at the wall. "I'm not going to dig it. Not yet. I don't want to break it. I want to *learn* how first." She put her hand flat on the stone beside the nearest little walking figure, very gently, the way you'd touch something asleep. "Ten thousand years. Maybe more."

Jared looked at her face in the lamplight. He'd known her a long time.

"You're doing the bookshop face," he said.

"I'm not."

"You're doing the bookshop face. The one from the old books section. Milli, she's doing the bookshop face."

"She's doing the bookshop face," agreed Milli, who had been to the bookshop in December, and had seen it.

Alice laughed, and the lamp swung, and something in the dark at the end of the tunnel moved.

[ Tunnel Grub · Lv 4 ]

It came out of a gap at the bottom of the wall. Pale and soft and the size of a big dog, with a mouth at the front that was mostly teeth, and a shell on its back like old pottery. It looked at the lamp. Then it looked at Alice, crouched in front of the wall, closest.

Jared didn't think. He'd never been good at thinking in a fight. In every game he'd ever played, with Ice and Alice and everybody, over years of nights, he'd been the one who just went. He went. He put himself between the grub and Alice, and the woodcutter's axe came off his back and round in a long flat swing. It hit the pottery shell with a sound like a plate breaking.

The grub reared back. Milli was already at its side, low, with the skinning knife, the way he'd taken apart a whole rabbit for the stew on Sunday without a single wasted cut. He went for the soft place under the shell. And Alice came up from her crouch with the pick in her hand, and she did the thing she'd been doing all weekend, to rock. Point first, very precise, into the crack Jared's axe had made in the shell.

The shell came apart like a pot.

They stood in the tunnel in the lamplight, the three of them, breathing.

"That," said Jared, "was a *bug*. With *teeth*. In a *hole*."

"That was a grub," said Milli.

"I know what it was, Milli. I'm describing my *experience*."

Alice was looking at the wall, at the little walking figures with their lights. She hadn't looked away from them, not even during the fight, not really.

"They're all walking the same way," she said quietly. "Somewhere."

They told Leaka on Wednesday.

Not about the egg. She knew about the egg. The egg had been in her flat in England for two and a half weeks, in its box, and she hadn't opened it. They'd told her about the egg in February, on a call. Jared had said *we bought you an egg, Leaka*, and Alice had said *it's a loan, it's not a present, you can pay us back*. And Leaka had said no.

She'd said no eleven times. Alice had counted. Jared had said they'd ask twelve.

They'd asked twelve. She'd said *fine*. In capitals. And then she'd said *I'm paying you back*, and Jared had said *argue about it later*, and she'd said *JARRED*. And the egg had arrived in her flat on the eleventh of March, and she still hadn't opened the box.

"Why haven't you opened the box," said Jared, on Wednesday night his time, which was Thursday morning in England. The three of them were sitting on the edge of the third ledge with their legs over the quarry and the sea going pink below them, with Leaka on the call.

"BECAUSE I HAVEN'T PAID YOU THE FIRST BIT YET," said Leaka.

"We said argue about it later."

"IT IS LATER, JARRED. IT'S BEEN LATER FOR TWO AND A HALF WEEKS."

Leaka talked in caps lock, on calls as much as in chat. She had for as long as Jared had known her. It was one of the best things about her. You always knew exactly where you stood with Leaka, because she was shouting it.

"Leaka," said Alice, gently.

"I'M NOT OPENING IT TILL I'VE PAID YOU THE FIRST HUNDRED," said Leaka. "THAT'S THE RULE. I MADE IT. I STREAMED FOR IT. I'M NEARLY THERE." A pause. "I'M SEVENTEEN POUNDS SHORT."

There was a silence on the call.

Jared looked at Alice. Alice looked at Milli. Milli, who did sums, was already doing this one.

"Seventeen pounds," said Milli, in his careful English. "That's about twenty-two US dollars."

"I KNOW WHAT IT IS, MILLI."

"I'm just saying it's not very much."

"IT'S *MY* SEVENTEEN POUNDS," said Leaka. "THAT'S THE POINT."

Jared lay back on the ledge with his hands behind his head and looked up at the sky going pink over a quarry town on a cliff. He laughed until his ribs hurt.

"*What*," said Leaka. "WHAT, JARRED."

"Nothing," said Jared. "Nothing. You're just the best person I know, that's all."

There was a very long silence on the call.

"SHUT UP," said Leaka, eventually. Much quieter. In caps, but quieter.

She found the seventeen pounds on Thursday.

She found it the way Leaka found everything. She streamed for nine hours straight about nothing in particular, loudly, and said so in her title. *17 POUNDS OR I DON'T OPEN THE BOX.* Her chat found it for her in about forty minutes, and she streamed for another eight hours and twenty minutes anyway, on principle.

At the end of it she sent them a hundred pounds. Exactly. With a message.
