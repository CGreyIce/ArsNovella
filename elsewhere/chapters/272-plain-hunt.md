Brann didn't say things. People had to ask him. Most people didn't, so he didn't, and that suited him.

He was sitting by VANTA's fire on the terrace below Saltreach's market on Friday night with his tower shield across his knees. He'd been there since the fourth tide. He'd watched the sea come back over the black pool in the middle of the bay, flat and grey. And he'd watched seven of the Ninefold come up the cliff stair with their leader's face like a shut door. He'd thought about it. He hadn't said anything, because nobody had asked.

Fenn came along the terrace in the dark and stood at the edge of the firelight.

Brann looked up.

The boy was nineteen. A bow, and not as good as the others, and his eyes always up. Kestrel had picked him for the Crown Below over people who were better. She'd said, at the long table, *I want one person who looks up*. Brann had said his name out loud so she'd hear it, and that was all. He'd watched Fenn on the terrace in Masmark every first dawn since. Missing gulls. Then not.

"Kestrel said ask you," said Fenn.

Brann waited.

"About the sea," said Fenn. "The way it comes back. Out in the middle. It comes back down the streets, one at a time. In an order." He swallowed. "She said your gran had a church. With bells. She said you'd know about orders."

Brann didn't say anything for a while.

He hadn't told anybody about his gran's church. Not in six years in two games. He'd told Kestrel once, in Masmark, on a terrace, when she asked him what the small bells in the tower were doing. *Changes. My gran's church. A method.* Four words and a name. She'd remembered.

"Say it again," said Brann. "The order. What you saw."

***

Fenn told him.

He'd watched all four tides from the cliff edge with his eyes up. On the second and third and fourth, when the sea came back, he'd watched the middle of the bay instead of the edge. He'd seen where the water rushed, and when.

There were five channels running into the black pool. You could see them at low water, five dark lines across the sand, meeting in the middle like spokes. Under them, Fenn thought, five streets. When the sea came back, it came back down the channels. Not all at once. One, then another, then another. A surge and then quiet. A surge and then quiet.

"I numbered them," said Fenn. "From the north. One to five, going round." He crouched by the fire and drew them in the ash with a stick. Five lines meeting in the middle. "The second tide, it came one, two, three, four, five. In order. Round. I thought that was all it was." He looked up. "Then it went round again. And it wasn't in order. It went two, one, four, three, five."

Brann sat very still.

"Then?" he said.

"I lost it," said Fenn. "The tide was nearly in. It was all coming at once by then. But the third tide I started at the beginning and it went one, two, three, four, five. And then two, one, four, three, five. And then—" He looked at the ash. "Two, four, one, five, three. I think. I'm not sure about the last one."

"You're sure," said Brann.

Fenn looked at him.

"Two, four, one, five, three," said Brann. "Then four, two, five, one, three."

Fenn's mouth opened.

"How do you—"

"Then four, five, two, three, one," said Brann. He took the stick out of Fenn's hand. He wrote them in the ash, one under the other, the way his gran had written them on the back of a hymn sheet for him when he was eight. Small numbers in rows. "Then five, four, three, two, one. Backwards. Then it comes back round." He put the stick down. "Ten rows and you're home. Rounds again."

Fenn stared at the ash.

"It's a method," said Brann. "Plain hunt. On five." He looked at the rows. "Every bell moves one place each row. Swaps with the one next to it. Up to the back, then down to the front. Like hunting. You don't ring the same bell in the same place twice." He was quiet. "My gran taught me it on her fingers. Before I could reach the rope."

"The *sea's* doing it."

"Something's doing it," said Brann. "Somebody built that city. They knew how the sea came back. Maybe they made it come back like that on purpose. So you'd know." He looked out at the bay, which was in, flat and grey under the half moon. "If you know the method, you know which street floods next. And which one floods last."

"The last one's the one that's dry longest," said Fenn slowly.

"Yes."

"So you walk down the last one."

"Yes."

Fenn sat back on his heels by the fire with ash on his hands. He looked at Brann as if Brann had grown a second head.

"Why didn't you *say*?" he said.

"Nobody asked," said Brann.

***

Kestrel listened to all of it at the fire at the third dawn, with her bow across her knees, without saying anything. Then she looked at the rows in the ash for a long time.

"Tomorrow," she said. "First low water."

"Tomorrow," said Brann.

"We go down," said Kestrel. "Twenty. And when it turns, we come out." She looked at him across the fire. "Down the last street."

"If I've got it right."

"You've got it right," said Kestrel. "You don't say things you haven't got right. That's why you don't say things."

Brann didn't answer that. It was true, and she knew it, so there was nothing to say.

***

They went down on Saturday at the first low water after dawn.

The Quiet Oar rowed them out. Wren herself, in the stern, with her hawser arms on the steering oar. Eight oars on each side going all together across the channels, fast, the water running out under them. VANTA sat in the bottom of the boat in two rows, black, twenty, very quiet. Ottie was holding on to the side with both hands. Ottie didn't like boats.

"You sat on the cliff," said Wren, from the stern. "Two days. Four tides."

"We rested," said Kestrel.

"You watched," said Wren. "I saw you. Every tide. Watching the middle, not the edge." She looked at Fenn, in the bow, with his eyes on the bay. "Him, mostly."

"Him mostly," agreed Kestrel.

Wren didn't say anything else. But when they got to the black pool she brought the boat round alongside it so gently that it didn't knock. Then she said, to nobody in particular, "Nobody's ever drowned in one of our boats." And then, after a moment: "Don't be the first out of one."

***

The black pool went down in a stair.

Round and round, wet stone, slippery with weed, the walls running with water, into the dark under the bay. Brann went first, with the tower shield up, because that was his place. Then Kestrel. Then the rest. Fenn near the back, looking up at the walls as he went down them, at the weed and the water and the little crabs. He looked at everything.

At the bottom, the city.

Brann had never seen anything like it. He stood at the foot of the stair with the shield on his arm and looked at it for a full five seconds, which in a great door was a long time.

It was under the sea, and the sea wasn't in it. Not now. Over their heads, a long way up, there was a ceiling of rock, dark, dripping. And under it a whole city, in green stone. Streets and houses and steps and bridges over dry channels, all hung with weed like washing. Pools in the street, left by the tide, with fish in them. Doorways with nothing behind them but dark. Lamps on iron brackets that had been out for a long time. And in the middle, far off down a long street, a glow. A square, with something in it.

"*Order*," said Fenn, behind him. Very quietly.

Brann didn't answer. He'd seen it too.

***

The Drowned Watch came up out of the pools at the first corner.

Six of them. Tall, in armour that had been bronze once and was green now, crusted with shell, with long hooked pikes. They came up out of the tide pools in the street the way the Unlit had come up out of the moor in the stories from the west, all at once, streaming water.

[ Drowned Watch · Lv 59 ] [ *They kept the streets. They still do.* ]

Brann planted the shield.

He didn't think about it. He'd been doing it for six years. He put the foot of the tower shield on the green stone and leaned into it, and the first pike hit it like a cart hitting a wall. The shock went up his arm into his shoulder and down his back into his heel. He gave nothing. The second pike came over the top, hooked, to drag the shield down. He took it on the rim and turned it, and it went past his ear and into the stone of the house beside him with a sound like a bell. The third came low, for his legs.

He'd looked up once. At the Saltwell, two years ago, at a window, for one second. And he'd lost his legs, and Kestrel had said *read it later*.

He didn't look up. He dropped the shield's foot an inch and the pike went into it instead of him.

"*Left two*," said Kestrel.

Mags went in on his left with her spear, low, under the first Watch's arm, where the bronze didn't meet. Ren's fire went past his right shoulder in a sheet and hit the two at the back, and the shell on their armour cracked like ice. Ottie, behind him, *ting*. Not on time. Never on time. But there.

The Watch at the front pulled its pike out of his shield and came again. Brann met it. Not with the shield. With the shield's edge, the way a man in a round town had shown him once on a market floor, walking round him for two minutes choosing which way he faced. Sideways, fast, into the Watch's hands on the pike shaft. It let go.

Kestrel's arrow went into the gap at its throat.

Six of them. Ninety seconds. Brann at seventy-one per cent.

"Good," said Kestrel. Which she said about once a week.

***

They went round the city's edge and down the long street toward the glow, past houses hung with weed, over bridges across dry channels. The third street. The east one. The Ninefold's scratches were all on the first and second, so Kestrel had taken them the long way. Every hundred paces, something came up out of a pool. Watch, or worse. A thing like a crab the size of a cart, with a shell VANTA had to break with Ren's fire and Mags's spear together. A swarm of small grey eels that went for Ottie, who screamed, and then healed herself, on time for once.

They got further than anybody had.

Brann knew it. He could see it in the street. The Ninefold's marks were on the walls: nine scratches, then seven, cut with a spear point at every corner they'd reached. VANTA passed the last seven-scratch at the fifth corner of the third street. Past it, the stone was clean. Nobody had been there.

Ahead, close now, the glow. The square. Brann could see the edge of it: a gate across the end of the street, iron, open. And beyond it, wide paving, and light.

"*Turn*," said Fenn.

Everybody stopped.

"The tide," said Fenn. "It's turned. I can hear it." He was looking up, at the rock ceiling, a long way above. Water was running down it. More than before. "It's coming back."

Brann heard it then. Far off, behind them, the way they'd come. A low sound, like a breath being drawn in very slowly by something enormous.

Then the first surge.

It came down a street to the north of them. Not theirs. He heard it go. A roar, sudden, and then the sound of water filling a street from end to end in about four seconds. Then quiet.

"*One*," said Fenn.

Then the second. Further round. Roar, rush, quiet.

"Two," said Brann.

Third. Theirs.

He didn't wait for it. He was already moving. "*Out of the street*," he said. Not loud. He wasn't loud. But everybody in VANTA had heard him say about nine things in six years and every one of them had been right. They went. Off the third street, through a doorway, into a house with weed hanging from the ceiling. Up a stair inside it to a landing, and stood there in the dark, twenty, packed in.

The third street filled under them. Four seconds. A roar and then grey water a man's height deep, rushing, from wall to wall, past the doorway below. Then quiet. The water sat there, still, in the street.

"Four," said Fenn, as the next one went, further round. "Five."

Then quiet.

Then it started again.

"Two," said Fenn. "One." His voice had gone very small. "*Four*. Three. Five."

The second row. Exactly as he'd seen it from the cliff.

And Brann knew, standing on a landing in a drowned house with nineteen people breathing round him in the dark. He knew the way he'd known when he was eight, with his gran's hand on the rope above his.

"*Two, four, one, five, three*," said Brann. Before it came.

It came. Two. Four. One. Five. Three.

Kestrel turned her head in the dark and looked at him.

***

It went round. Row after row. And every row, Brann said it before it came, and it came.

*Four, two, five, one, three.*

*Four, five, two, three, one.*

Between surges the water went out of the streets again. Down through iron gratings in the paving, toward the square, with a long sucking sound, as if the city were drinking it. But each row it drank a little less. The streets were filling. A man's height of water rushed past the doorway every row, and every row a little more of it stayed. The landing they were on was going to be under it in two rows.

"We have to go," said Kestrel. "Now. Which way."

Brann thought about it. Properly.

The stair out was at the top of the first street, the north one. They'd come down it at low water and gone round the city's edge to the third, because the Ninefold's scratches were all on the first and second. To get back to the stair they had to cross the second street. Then go up the first, three hundred paces, to the foot of the stair. Without being in either of them when it came.

Plain hunt. Every bell moved one place each row.

The next row was backwards. *Five, four, three, two, one*. The first street went last.

And the row after that was *five, three, four, one, two*. The first street went fourth.

"First street," said Brann. "It goes last next row. After it goes, it's quiet for three. Five, three, four. Then it comes again." He looked at the doorway below, at the grey water draining out of the third street. "We cross the second now. Get into a door on the corner of the first. Let the first go past us. Then up it in the three."

"Across the second," said Kestrel. "When does the second go?"

"Fourth," said Brann. "Next row. Just before the first." He picked up the tower shield. "We'll be in it when it does."

"How long can you hold it?"

"Six seconds," said Brann. "Maybe seven."

Kestrel looked at him in the dark for about one breath.

"Go," she said.

***

They went down the stair inside the house into the water.

It was draining, but it still came up to Brann's chest. Cold. Black. Nineteen people behind him, wading, holding on to each other. Ottie on Mags's shoulders, because Ottie was short. Out through the doorway into the third street. Along it to the corner. And round, into the second, where the water was the same. Chest-deep, from wall to wall, pulling gently toward the gratings.

They had to cross it. The corner of the first street was on the far side. Forty paces. A house on the corner with a doorway and a step up.

They were fifteen paces in when Fenn said "*Five*."

Fifth street. Surging, off to the south. The row had started.

"*Four*."

"*Three*."

They were not going to make it. Brann had known they wouldn't. He stopped in the middle of the second street, chest-deep in black water, and turned round to face up it. The way the sea would come. And planted the tower shield.

"Go past me," he said. "All of you. Don't stop. Into the door."

"*Two*," said Fenn.

It came.

He saw it. Down the second street from the north, out of the dark, a wall of grey water the height of the houses, white along the top. Coming at him, filling the street from side to side, so fast it didn't look like water at all. It looked like a wall falling over.

Nineteen people went past him through the water toward the doorway on the corner. He didn't watch them. He watched the sea.

It hit him.

He'd been hit by a lot of things. A pike, twenty minutes ago. A man from a round town, three edges square, who'd taken him down to six per cent in six minutes fifty-one and then held out his hand. *A tank can catch a tank*, the man had said. *If he holds it.*

This was the sea.

It hit the tower shield and the tower shield hit Brann. He felt every bone in his left arm. He felt his feet go on the drowned stones under the water and find them again. He felt the whole weight of the bay come down a street in a drowned city onto a slab of iron and one man behind it, and try to move them. And not.

"*IN*," said Kestrel, from somewhere behind him. Loud. She was never loud. "*Brann. We're in. MOVE*."

He let the sea have the shield.

It took it. Ripped it off his arm and away down the second street like a leaf, and took him off his feet. And for one moment he was in the water, under it, in the dark, going. Then a hand had his collar. Then two. Mags. And Fenn, who wasn't strong, flat on the step of the corner house with his bow over his back, holding on to Brann's coat with both hands. Pulling.

They pulled him up the step and into the doorway.

"*One*," said Fenn, on his back on the floor, with Brann on top of him.

The first street went past the doorway a pace from Brann's boots. A wall of grey water, the height of the house, roaring by from left to right. The whole doorway went dark with it. Then light. Then quiet. Then the long sucking sound of the gratings, drinking.

"*Now*," said Brann.

They ran.

Up the first street, ankle-deep, then dry, toward the stair. Three hundred paces. Twenty people running in a drowned city with the sea coming back down the other streets round them. *Five*, said Fenn, running. Off to the south, the roar. *Three*. *Four*. Brann ran with no shield and his arm hanging and Ottie's *ting* coming after him, late.

They were on the stair when Fenn said *one*.

The first street filled below them. Brann felt the spray of it on the back of his neck, going up the steps, round and round, wet stone, toward the light.

***

They came up out of the black pool onto the sand with the sea coming in across the flats round them. Twenty. All of them. Brann without his shield.

The Quiet Oar's boat was waiting. Of course it was. Wren in the stern, steering oar in her hawser arms, the water already round the hull.

Nobody had come out of a turn with twenty before. Nobody had come out from past the Ninefold's last scratch.

Wren looked at them climbing into the boat, wet, quiet, all twenty, and at Brann last, with no shield.

"Where's your shield?" said Wren.

"In the second street," said Brann.

Wren looked at him for a long time. Then she said, to the bay, "The sea doesn't usually give them back." And then, rowing, after a while: "It might give that one back."

***

Kestrel found him on the terrace at the third dawn, by the fire, with his left arm in a sling Ottie had made, which he didn't need.

She sat down across from him. She didn't say anything for a while.

"You didn't tell me about the bells," she said at last.

"You didn't ask."

"I'm asking."

Brann looked at the fire.

He thought about how much to say. He didn't say things, mostly. But she'd asked. And he'd held the sea in a street at nine per cent, and it had made him feel something come loose, a little.

"My gran rang the tenor," said Brann. "The big one, at the back. For fifty years. At a church in a village where I grew up. She taught me the methods on her fingers before I could reach a rope." He moved his hand on his knee. The way she'd done it. "She said, you don't ring your bell. You ring the change. Nobody cares about your bell. The change is the thing. Your bell's just where it happens to be this row."

Kestrel didn't say anything.

"I think about it in fights," said Brann. "I'm not the fight. I'm where the fight happens to be this row." He looked at her. "You decide everything. You used to. Then you stopped. It's like that."

Kestrel was quiet for a long time.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The square."

"The square," said Brann. "I'll need a shield."

"Ina's not here." She thought. "Pell Calder will sell you one."

"For three times what it's worth."

"Yes," said Kestrel. "Pay him." She got up. Then she stopped, and looked down at him by the fire with his arm in a sling he didn't need. "Brann."

He looked up.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Kestrel.

She said it badly. Worse than Marrok. Very fast, looking at the fire. As if she'd been practising it since Wednesday and lost it on the last step.

Brann thought about it properly.

"You," he said. "And the boy who looks up."

"That's two answers."

"I've got two," said Brann.
