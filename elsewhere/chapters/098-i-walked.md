Wendy had asked to be at the gate because she'd thought she'd be good at it. She'd been right. That was the annoying part.

She sat on the top step of the Hall of Roads every evening, on the south side, where the south road came in. She had a cup of whatever Gigi had brewed that day, which was always horrible and always good for you, and her bow across her knees. Kite sat on the other side of the steps, on the stone lion with no nose, with his bow strung. He'd strung it for good the night they founded, and hadn't unstrung it since.

And people came up the steps.

They'd been coming for seventeen days. Hundreds of them. Ever since the night the banner went up, with forty thousand people watching on Kei's stream and six thousand in the square. Some came in groups and some came alone. Some came in armour that cost more than Wendy's whole account, and some came in whatever the game had given them on the first morning. All of them stopped at the top of the steps, because Wendy was there, and said some version of the same thing.

*Can I join?*

And Wendy said, every time, "Who'd stand next to you?"

Most of them didn't understand. She'd watched it happen three hundred times. They thought it was a riddle. Or a test, or a rude way of saying no. They told her their level. They told her their gear, their class, how many hours they played, who they'd killed, what they could pay.

"That's nice," said Wendy. "Who'd stand next to you?"

"I don't know anyone in there."

"Then not yet," said Wendy. "Get to know somebody. Then come back."

Most of them went away annoyed. She'd known they would. She'd *said* it would go like this, at the meetings, the way she said everything would go badly at every meeting. She'd been right about that too.

But some of them came back.

That was the thing she hadn't expected. A girl with a crossbow had gone off down the hill in a temper on the second day. She'd come back on the ninth with Tobi, the two of them arguing about bolts, because she'd spent a week on the east road fixing Tobi's crossbow for him. A man with a lute had come back with Maigo. Of course he had. Everybody came back with Maigo. Nine people had come up the steps a second time, with somebody standing next to them. Nine names under the twenty in Ama Corrie's black book.

Wendy hadn't said yes to any of them. Not really. She'd just asked the question, and then got out of the way.

She had Ice's stream open on her phone under the café booth's curtain, the way she always did. She'd lurked in his chat since the first night, and never said a word in it. He was somewhere north of the fen with the others, a long way up a road of stone herons, and he'd gone dark again. She didn't mind. She liked it when he went dark. It meant he'd found something he didn't want to tell anybody about yet, and in Wendy's experience that meant it was good.

In the booth next to hers, eighteen inches away through a curtain, Salty was in his own egg. In the game he was inside the Hall, asleep on a bench under the dome, because he'd been up all the night before helping Chris with the roof. She could hear him breathing in both places.

In the game, it was nearly the second dusk. The sun was going down behind the west door of the Hall and laying long gold bars across the square. And a boy came up the south road with a black sword on his back.

Wendy knew him before he got to the bottom of the steps.

She'd watched him for four months. On a set of wooden steps at the edge of a fen, picking fenwort for two copper a plant. Grinning at the swamp guy. Holding a sword that was much too big for him. Then, on the longest night, holding a set of steps after the man beside him fell, all the way through to the dawn. Thirty thousand people had watched him do it. Wendy had been one of them, in this booth, under this curtain, with her hand over her mouth.

The sword wasn't too big for him any more.

He came up the steps slowly, one at a time, the way you walked when you'd been walking for eight days. At the top he stopped, because Wendy was there.

On the other side of the steps, on the lion with no nose, Kite had gone very still.

The boy looked at Wendy. Then he looked across at Kite. And his face changed.

"You were in the channel," he said. "In Fernwick. With the bird on your collar."

"Yes," said Kite.

"The one with the knives killed you."

"Yes."

The boy looked at him for a long time.

"Good," he said.

"Yes," said Kite.

Wendy, who had not expected any of that, drank some of Gigi's tea to have something to do with her face.

***

"Who sent you?" she said.

She asked everyone that. It was the second question. Not because the answer mattered, but because of what people did with it. People said the names of big streamers. People said the names of people in the guild they'd never met. Once, memorably, somebody had said *God*.

"The sw—" said the boy.

He stopped. Wendy watched him stop. He shut his mouth on the rest of it, and stood there for a second, and she saw him remember something somebody had told him. On a set of steps, probably. In the dark, probably, with the sword across his knees.

"Nobody," said the boy. "I walked."

Wendy looked at him.

She knew exactly who'd sent him. Everybody in Carrow Cross would have known. Thirty thousand people would have known. He could have said it, and nobody would have argued with it, and he'd have been through the gate in a minute.

He'd walked eight days, and he hadn't said it.

"From where?" she said, though she knew.

"Fernwick." He pulled the strap of the sword a bit straighter on his shoulder. "Eight days. Plain road east, then the river road south." A pause. "I'm Rafi."

"I know who you are," said Wendy. "Who'd stand next to you?"

He didn't hesitate at all.

"Bram," said Rafi. "And Joy. We held the steps."

***

She didn't have to send for them. Somebody in the square had already run.

Joy came first, out of the Hall's west door at a run, small and fast. She stopped dead in the doorway when she saw who it was, and said something in a language Wendy didn't know, very loudly, and then in English, "*RAFI.*"

And behind her, slower, came Bram. A tall silent Dutchman with a spear, who'd fallen at four in the morning on the longest night, holding Sedge's steps, and had spent the end of it locked out of the game, waiting. He'd never said a word in Wendy's hearing. Not one, in seventeen days.

He came down the Hall steps past Wendy, and stood next to Rafi.

That was all he did. He just stood there, at the top of the steps, shoulder to shoulder with a boy with a black sword, facing the gate. And Joy came and stood on Rafi's other side, and put her hand on his arm, and talked.

"He held the steps," said Joy. "After Bram fell. Me and him and the eel man, with a boat hook. All night. He held them till the dawn. He didn't go back once. Not one step." She looked at Wendy. "He was five levels under them. He didn't go *back*."

Wendy looked at Bram.

Bram looked back at her. And then, for the first time in seventeen days, he said something.

"Ja," said Bram.

Joy burst out laughing. Rafi's grin came back, all of it, so wide it looked painful.

Wendy turned and looked across the steps at Kite, on the stone lion. That was the rule. The gate was two people, not one.

Kite looked at Rafi for a long moment. At the sword. At the eight days of road on his boots.

"He didn't say who sent him," said Kite.

"No," said Wendy.

"He could have."

"Yes."

Kite nodded, once. "Then yes."

"Yes," said Wendy.

***

Ama Corrie had the black book open on the counter before they got through the door. She'd heard. Ama Corrie heard everything in the Hall. She said it was the dome; it carried.

"Another one," she said. "Mind the north door. It sticks."

"It doesn't stick any more," said Kite. "Gabe fixed it."

"Mind it anyway," said Ama Corrie. "Forty years. It's a habit." She turned the book round and held out a pen.

Rafi took it. He looked at the page for a while. Twenty names at the top, in twenty different hands. KeiKatamori, small and upright. Gigi's like a hedge. Chris's "dumbo", the last of the twenty, very carefully. Then nine more underneath, newer. Then a space.

He wrote his name in it, slowly, the way you'd write on a form at a bank.

[ Constellation · the Hall of Roads, Carrow Cross
Names: 30 · new: Rafi ]

Then he put the pen down and looked up at the dome. At the faded stars, and the eye at the top, open to the evening. And he said, quite quietly, to nobody, "*Oh.*"

***

They sat on the steps afterwards, in the last of the light. Wendy, and Rafi, and Kite on his lion, and Bram, standing, because Bram always stood. Joy had gone to get food. Rafi had the black sword across his knees, the way he'd sat with it on Sedge's steps every night for four months. The blade hummed a little under his hand. A choir a long way off.

"Boss said he'd see me here one day," said Rafi.

He said it to the square, not to her. But she was the one who answered.

"He says *not yet*," said Wendy. "He's been saying *not yet* to Kei since January."

"Yeah."

"He might not come for a long time. He might not come at all." She was always the one who said that kind of thing. Somebody had to.

"He said *one day*," said Rafi. "He didn't say *soon*." He turned the sword over on his knees. "He doesn't say things he doesn't mean. Ever. You can watch every minute of him and he never once does it."

Wendy didn't say anything. She'd watched every minute of him too, under a curtain in a café. And he was right, and she hated it slightly when people were right about things she'd been planning to be gloomy about.

"I have to write to Sedge," said Rafi. "She said."

"Can she read?"

"No," said Rafi, cheerfully. "The swamp guy reads them out. That's what she said. *The swamp guy'll read it out.*"

"He's at the top of the world," said Wendy. "On a road made of herons. He won't be in Fernwick for weeks."

Rafi thought about that.

"Then it'll wait," he said. "Sedge waits good. She waited eleven years for somebody to sit on her steps."

He wrote it there, on the Hall steps, on a sheet of paper Ama Corrie gave him, with the pen from the black book. Wendy didn't read it. She didn't have to. He read it out loud as he wrote, the way people did when they didn't write much.

*Sedge. I walked. They said yes. The gate is a girl with two different eyes and a man who used to be a bird. Bram said a word. I'm in the book. Tell the swamp guy I didn't say. Rafi.*

He folded it twice and looked at it.

"*Tell the swamp guy I didn't say*," said Wendy.

"He'll know what it means."

"Will Sedge?"

"No," said Rafi. "That's why he has to read it."

***

Kei came out onto the steps at dusk, with his map rolled under his arm and his green hair tie coming loose. He'd been inside under the eye all evening with Higu and Gigi and four sheets of paper, working something out. Wendy knew the look.

"Tuesday," he said. "We go north. Tuesday at the second dawn."

"Who's going?"

"Me. Higu. Gigi. Drew. Tiger. Maigo. Salty. Hako." He looked at the square. "Not everyone. Somebody has to keep the room."

"I'm keeping the gate," said Wendy.

"I know," said Kei. "I wasn't going to ask."

Kite didn't say anything from his lion. He'd been north on that road before, the long way, in a different life with a bird on his collar. He'd keep the gate too.

Kei looked at Rafi, with the black sword across his knees. Rafi looked back at him, very hopeful, and didn't say anything, which Wendy thought was the most impressive thing she'd seen all week.

"Who'd stand next to him?" said Kei.

It was supposed to be a question for the gate. Wendy was the gate.

"Take him," said Wendy. "He walked eight days. He can walk four more."

Kei looked at her. Then at Rafi. Then he nodded once, and went back inside, under the dome, to his map.

Rafi turned round on the step and stared at her.

"Don't," said Wendy. "It's going to be horrible. It's going to rain. Something's going to try to eat you. You'll hate it."

"Thank you," said Rafi.

"Don't," said Wendy again, and drank the last of Gigi's tea. It was cold now, and horrible, and she finished all of it.
