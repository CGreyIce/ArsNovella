Deston counted things. It was the thing he did. Steps, beats, breaths, arrows left in a quiver, days until something. He counted the way other people hummed, without deciding to, and when he stopped counting it usually meant something was wrong.

On Monday he counted the herons from the gorge back to the headless one, twice. Twenty-five to twenty-four. One mile. He counted the moorhens on the white river at the bottom of the gorge. Eleven. He counted the minutes until Suzu would be out of surgery in Singapore. He wasn't Suzu's mother and he wasn't in the hospital, and counting it did nothing at all. But he couldn't stop.

They didn't cross. Nobody said they wouldn't. Nobody needed to.

"We wait," Declan had said on Sunday night, when they got back to the lip of the gorge. That was all. Then he'd sat down on the stone pier of the broken bridge with his legs over the drop. He'd stayed there most of Monday, looking north at the mist, not saying much.

Leveti had looked at him for a long moment on Sunday night and not argued. Deston had liked him a lot for that.

***

Kestrel posted on Monday afternoon.

Deston saw it because everybody saw it. It went round every chat and every forum and every group call in the game in about four minutes. It was one line, the way Kestrel's posts always were. And a picture: a tall door of black stone, standing at the top of a flight of green steps in a salt marsh, with five people in front of it. One of them was small, in plain black, with a short bow.

*VANTA is in. Next door.*

"*Next door*," said Kotori, reading it over his shoulder. She'd been reading over his shoulder since Christmas. Deston had stopped noticing. "That's what she said to Ice. At the city. He told us."

"Yes."

"So she's—"

"She's in," said Deston.

Kotori looked across at Declan, on the pier, with his legs over the drop. He'd seen it. He must have. It was in the corner of everybody's eye.

He didn't move.

"He's waiting for Suzu," said Kotori, quietly, as if she were explaining something to herself.

"He's waiting for Suzu," Deston agreed.

At four o'clock in Singapore, Suzu sent one message to the five of them.

[ Messages · MH
Suzuyunn: out. she's ok. they switch it on friday
]

Deston read it. He stopped counting the moorhens.

On the pier, Declan read it too. Deston watched him put his hand flat on the stone beside him, the way you'd put your hand on a table to steady it. He kept it there for a long time.

Then he typed one word back.

[ Messages · MH
Ice: good
]

Deston thought it was probably the most anyone had ever meant by *good*.

***

Suzu came back into the game on Tuesday at the second dawn.

He came up the heron road from the twenty-fourth cairn where he'd logged out on Saturday. He was walking the way he always walked, scythe on his back, pack on his shoulder. The jar inside the pack was glowing so brightly now that the canvas looked lit from inside, like a lampshade. He didn't say anything about Singapore. He came and stood on the lip of the gorge beside the twenty-fifth heron and looked north across the gap. Then he said, "Okay."

That was all. Nobody asked him anything. Deston would have liked to. He didn't.

Declan stood up off the pier.

"Okay," he said.

***

The problem was the bridge.

There wasn't one. There were two stone piers, one on each lip of the gorge, forty yards apart. Between them there was nothing but air, and mist, and the white river a long way down, loud. They'd spent a month at the edge of it. They'd killed what came up out of it, every evening, a level at a time. Not one of them had found a way across.

"The Eye," said Suzu.

He took it out of his pocket. The heron's eye, from the twenty-fourth cairn. A grey-green stone the size of a hen's egg. *It looks where the herons look.* He'd been carrying it for a month. Deston had seen him take it out a hundred times and look at it, and put it away again.

Suzu walked out onto the near pier, to the very edge, with his toes over the drop. He held the Eye up in front of his face, between finger and thumb. He looked through it north across the gorge, the way the herons looked.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Suzu?" said Kotori.

"There's a bridge," said Suzu.

They all looked at the gap. Air. Mist. The river.

"It's there," said Suzu. "Through this. It's whole. Stone. Narrow. No rail." He lowered the Eye and looked across with his own eyes, and Deston saw his face change. "It isn't there, without it."

"Is it there," said Declan carefully, "or does it just look like it's there?"

Suzu thought about it.

"It's there," he said. "It's just that nobody looks where the herons look."

Deston thought about that for a moment. Then he understood. It went cold all the way down his back.

"We can't see it," he said. "Only you can. Through that. And there's one of it."

"Yes."

"So you'd have to tell us," said Deston. "Every step. Where to put our feet. On a bridge we can't see, forty yards, over that." He looked down at the river. "Out loud."

Suzu looked at him. Then at the four of them. Then down at the Eye in his hand.

"I've done that," said Suzu. "Before."

He said it very quietly. Deston knew what he meant. Everybody did. Suzu had told Sheng Yang on the heron road, a month ago, and Sheng Yang had told nobody, and somehow they'd all known anyway. *I've been her eyes since I was small. Reading things. Telling her what's there.*

His mother was in a hospital in Singapore with a small thing behind her ear that they'd switch on on Friday. Her son was standing on the edge of a gorge at the top of the world, holding up an eye.

"Okay," said Declan. "Then you're the eyes. Dest counts. We walk on the count. One at a time. Me first."

"*Me* first," said Suzu.

Declan looked at him.

"I can't tell you where to put your feet," said Suzu, "if I'm behind you. I have to see it before you do." He put the Eye up again. "I go first. You come behind me. I say *step*, and where. Dest counts. You put your foot where I said, on the count." He looked back at them over his shoulder. "Don't look down. Look at me."

Nobody said anything.

Then Declan said, "Okay," and stood behind him.

***

Deston opened the book.

The skill wasn't really in the book any more. He'd learned it from Brother Amon in a chapel in December, and it lived in him now. But he liked having the book open. It reminded him what it was for.

[ Accord · Let the many move as one. ]

He counted.

"*One.*"

Suzu stepped off the pier into nothing.

Kotori made a sound. Deston didn't stop counting. Suzu's foot came down on air, forty yards above a white river, and stopped there. Not on anything Deston could see. Just stopped, as if the air had decided to hold him. He stood on it. He shifted his weight. He didn't fall.

"*Two.*"

"Step," said Suzu, without turning round. "Straight. Two feet. Middle of the stone."

Declan stepped off the pier onto the place where Suzu had been.

He didn't hesitate. Deston would remember that for the rest of his life. A man stepping off a cliff onto nothing, on a count, because somebody he trusted had said *step*, and not hesitating. Declan's foot came down on air. Stopped. Held.

"*Three.*"

"Step. Straight. Left of middle, it narrows."

It was the longest forty yards of Deston's life.

He went fourth, after Kotori, before Sheng Yang. He counted the whole way, out loud, steady, and kept his eyes on the back of Suzu's head, way out in front over the gorge. He didn't look down. *Look at me.* He felt his boots come down on stone he couldn't see. Rough, narrow, very old, a little damp. He felt the wind come up out of the gorge and push at him. He heard the river roaring a long way below. And through all of it Suzu's voice, quiet, steady, never louder than it needed to be. Reading the bridge out loud for four people who couldn't see it, two feet at a time.

"Step. Straight."

"Step. There's a crack. Go over it. Long step."

"Step. Middle."

"*Thirty-one*," counted Deston. "*Thirty-two.*"

Behind him, Sheng Yang said, very calmly, "I'm not looking down."

"Good," said Suzu, from the far end, without turning round.

"*Forty.*"

Somewhere around fifty Deston realised he'd stopped being afraid. He couldn't have said when it happened. It was the voice. *Step. Straight. Middle.* The same flat quiet voice, over and over, telling you exactly what was there. As if the bridge was the most ordinary thing in the world and he'd been reading it out loud all his life.

He supposed he had.

"*Sixty-two*," said Deston, and his foot came down on the far pier.

***

They stood on the north side of the gorge, all five of them, by the twenty-sixth heron. Nobody said anything for a while. Kotori was shaking slightly. She sat down on the stone pier and put her head between her knees and said "I'm fine" four times.

Sheng Yang was standing very still at the edge, looking back across at the empty air they'd just walked over. Then at Suzu.

"You never once raised your voice," said Sheng Yang.

"You don't have to," said Suzu. "If you say it right the first time."

Declan didn't say anything. He just went and stood next to Suzu at the foot of the twenty-sixth heron, close enough that their shoulders were nearly touching. They both looked north, the way the heron looked. Suzu still had the Eye in his hand. His hand was shaking now, a little. It hadn't been, on the bridge.

***

Past the twenty-sixth heron there were no more herons.

The heron road ended. The land went up into the hills, grey grass and grey stone, into the mist. There was nothing to follow. No cairns, no stones, nothing pointing.

Suzu held up the Eye and looked north through it.

"There's one more," he said.

He walked. They followed. Up out of the gorge, a quarter of a mile, into the mist, to the foot of a grey hill. In the side of the hill, set into it, there was a door.

Deston saw it with his own eyes. It didn't need the Eye. A tall narrow door of grey stone, in a frame of grey stone, set into the hillside so that it faced back south. Back down the heron road, toward the gorge and the fen and everything they'd walked through to get here. The herons had all been looking north at it. It had been looking back at them.

Round the frame, carved deep and worn soft with age, there were herons. A ring of them. Deston counted them without deciding to.

Nine.

Eight of them faced inward, toward the door. One, at the very top, was turned round. Facing out.

Declan stood in front of it and didn't say anything at all. Deston had been there in the ring, in the old fen, in March, the night before they came north. He'd watched Declan stand behind the ninth stone, the one that faced outward, and look where it looked. He knew exactly what Declan was seeing now.

In Suzu's pack, the jar rang. Out loud, like a bell, so that all five of them heard it. Eighty-one small blue lights pressing against the glass so hard it sang.

[ The Long Watch
A door for five. ]

"*The Long Watch*," said Kotori, from behind them, very quietly.

Deston thought of Kei, two weeks ago, sending Declan one line from a ruined hall at the top of the world. Declan had told them about it that night. Just the one line, and nothing else. *Set one to watch.*

"Tomorrow," said Declan.

Nobody argued. They'd crossed a bridge that wasn't there. They'd stood on air. Suzu had read sixty-two steps of nothing out loud without raising his voice once, the day after his mother's surgery. Tomorrow was right.

They logged out at the foot of the twenty-sixth heron, all five of them, in the mist. The door stood in the hill above them, facing south, keeping its watch. Deston counted the herons on its frame one more time before he went. Nine. One facing out.

He didn't know why he needed to. He just did.
