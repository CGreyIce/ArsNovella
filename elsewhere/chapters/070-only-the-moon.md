The north door stuck, the way Gigi had said it would.

Kei put his shoulder to it and it gave all at once with a groan that went round the whole Hall, and the three of them stood in the doorway in the rain, dripping, listening to it echo. Nobody came. Higu had half expected the old woman to be standing in the dark with her keys, the way Ennis used to be standing in the dark of the Old Glass when she came up the stairs at night. But the Hall was empty. Just the lamps turned low on their brackets round the wall, seven doors, and the round floor worn smooth in the middle.

And up above, out of reach of the lamps, the dark.

"One step left," said Kei, very quietly, as if it were a church. "From the north door. Half a step back. Under that lamp."

Higu went and stood where he said.

She'd wanted to see it so much, for nine days, that she was almost afraid to look up. She stood there with her crook in her hand and the lantern on the end of it still hissing from the rain. She shut the lantern. In the near-dark, with just the low wall lamps, she put her head back.

Stars.

Hundreds of them. Small, worn, brown-gold, most of the gold gone. Somebody had painted a whole night sky on the inside of a dome, a long time ago, and then four hundred years of lamp smoke had tried to paint it out again. In the middle of the dome, where the lamps didn't reach at all, there was just dark.

And between some of the stars, from exactly here and nowhere else, faint as a hair, silver.

She saw them the way she'd seen the lines in the Old Glass the first time, at Selwick, before she knew what they were. A thin pale thread from one star to another. Then another. Not many. Six, maybe seven, scattered across the lower part of the dome where the lamplight caught them at an angle. They didn't go anywhere. They started and stopped. Fragments.

She stood there for a long time with her head back.

"Gu?" said Gigi eventually, behind her.

"They're not finished," said Higu.

"What?"

"The lines. They stop." She pointed with the crook, though nobody could see where she was pointing. "That one comes out of the dark and goes to that star and stops. That one starts at that star and goes into the dark. They're not *finished*. They're just the bits the lamp can reach." She lowered the crook. "There's more. Up there. In the middle. Where it's dark."

Kei came and stood beside her and looked up.

"How do you know?"

"Because at Selwick they only came out by moonlight," said Higu. "In the Old Glass. Ennis had been mapping that ruin for forty years by lamplight, and he'd never seen them. Not once. You had to put the lamps out and wait for the moon." She was talking faster than she usually did. She could hear it. "Silver lines. Silver paint. It's the same. I'd bet anything it's the same. The lamp only catches the edge of them, from one place, at an angle. You need the moon. From *above*."

They all three looked up at the dome. At the dark in the middle of it.

"There's no moon in here," said Gigi. "There's a roof."

***

There was a hole in the roof.

Ama Corrie told them so, at nine o'clock the next evening, standing in the round Hall with her keys and her spectacles, looking at the three of them with an expression that was very nearly a smile and very carefully wasn't.

"The eye," she said. "At the top. Every dome's got one. It's how they built them." She looked up into the dark. "My grandmother had it shuttered. It let the rain in on the Roll. That's been sixty years." She looked back down at Higu. "You want me to open the eye of my Hall, after sixty years, so you can look at my ceiling by moonlight."

"Yes," said Higu. "Please. Sorry. Yes."

"Why?"

Higu thought about how to explain it. The Old Glass. Ennis, going blind, with forty years of maps of a ruin he'd never really seen. The silver line that ran through three places in a lake where the dark came up on the longest night. Gigi's hedge, with its ditches dug the same year, and the dark coming up out of the ditches and nowhere else. *Somebody knew.*

She didn't say any of that. She just said:

"I think it's a map."

Ama Corrie looked at her for a very long moment.

"My grandmother said that," she said. "About the ceiling. When I was a girl. She said it was a map, and that only the moon could read it." She took her spectacles off and let them fall on their chain. "Nobody believed her. I didn't." She looked up into the dark of the dome. "She was a very difficult old woman."

"I like her already," said Gigi.

"You would," said Ama Corrie. Then she sighed, a long sigh with forty years in it, and took a key off the ring. A big old iron one, black, that looked as if nobody had used it in a lifetime.

"The stair's in the wall behind the east door," she said. "Two hundred and twelve steps. The shutter's rusted. You'll need someone strong."

"I'm strong," said Gigi at once. "I lay hedges."

***

It took Gigi and Kei forty minutes to get the shutter open.

Higu heard them from the floor of the Hall. A long way up in the dark, inside the wall of the dome, with a lantern, swearing. Gigi was swearing. Kei didn't swear; he just said "okay" in a voice that meant he wanted to. Something iron groaned, and stopped, and groaned again. Gigi said something about the person who'd shuttered it that would have made Granny Sloe proud. And then, all at once, there was a great shriek of rust, and a crash, and a draught of cold night air came down from the top of the dome and touched Higu's face.

"*It's open*," shouted Gigi, from very far up. "It's open, and I've broken a nail, and if this doesn't work I'm laying a hedge across your whole face."

"It'll work," said Higu.

"It'll fall down."

"It won't fall down," said Higu, and was surprised to find she meant it.

She went round the Hall with Ama Corrie and put out the lamps. One by one. Seven doors, seven lamps over them, and the low ones round the wall. Ama Corrie did it with a snuffer on a long pole, very precisely, the way she did everything. When the last one went out, the Hall was completely dark. Higu couldn't see her own hand.

She turned her stream off.

She'd thought about it all day, and in the end it wasn't even a decision. She knew who'd done this before her. Everybody did, even people who never watched him. A man who walked out to a ring of stones in a fen at moonrise and went dark before he got there, every time, so nobody would follow him to it. Fifty-odd times now. His head mod had a counter for it. Higu had never once asked him what was out there. She'd understood without asking that some things you looked at first, before you showed anybody.

Kei and Gigi came down the two hundred and twelve steps and felt their way across the floor in the dark. The four of them stood together in the middle of the round Hall. The old woman, the physician with the green tie, the hedge-layer with the broken nail, and Higu with her crook. They waited.

The moon came up at twenty to midnight.

***

It came through the eye.

A circle of white light, very small at first, on the curve of the dome high up near the top, where the eye was. Then, as the moon climbed, the circle widened, and slid, and fell down the inside of the dome like water poured slowly into a bowl. Silver-white light, cold and clear, lying across the painted sky.

And everywhere it touched, the lines came out.

Not six or seven. Not fragments. *All* of them.

Higu stood in the middle of the Hall with her head back and her crook forgotten in her hand and watched them come. Silver threads, hundreds of them, lighting up one after another as the moonlight slid across the dome. Joining star to star. Running out of the dark into the light and on across the whole curve of the ceiling, unbroken, finished, exactly the way somebody had painted them four hundred years ago. It was a web. It was a map. It was the most beautiful thing she'd seen in her life, in either world.

Nobody said anything. Beside her, Gigi had stopped breathing.

Higu read it.

She couldn't help it. It was what she did. She'd mapped nine sheets of a ruin by moonlight for a going-blind old man. She'd drawn a lake with three dots on it in a line. Her hands went to the satchel on her hip without her telling them to. She got out Ennis's maps, the copies she'd made, and held them up to the moonlight and looked from the paper to the ceiling and back.

And there it was.

Low on the dome, to the west, a long pale smudge of painted water. Three small stars in it, in a line. And a silver thread running through all three, exactly the way the silver line ran across the floor of the Old Glass and out over the lake.

"Selwick," she said. Her voice didn't sound like hers. "That's Selwick. That's the lake. That's the line."

Gigi made a sound.

Higu followed her eyes. Further round the dome, to the south-west, a small ring of stars. Close together, in a perfect circle, like a nest. And outside the ring, scratched into the paint beside it, short silver marks. Not stars. Little straight strokes, one after another, all the way round the outside of the circle.

"The ditches," said Gigi. Her voice was very small. "That's the hedge. And those are the ditches. Gu, those are the *ditches*."

Higu kept looking. She couldn't stop.

A long line, running from the north down to the east, with seven small stars on it one under the other, like a staircase. A crossroads with a tiny square drawn at it, where four lines met. A hill with a ring of stars round the top, like a fire. And, far round to the south-east, low down, a cluster of nine small stars in a ring, eight of them close, the ninth set a little apart and turned. Higu didn't know what that one was. She wrote it down anyway.

And every line, every single silver thread on the whole painted sky, went the same way.

North. Across the dome, past the top, toward the curve above the north door. Some of them took a long time to get there. Some of them wandered. But every one of them went north, and every one of them ended in the same place, a little way above the north door, where somebody had painted one star much bigger than all the others. Bigger, and brighter, and gold that hadn't worn away. With every silver line in the sky running into it like rivers into the sea.

Kei had his notebook out. His hand was shaking. He was looking from the big star to his own map, the copy he carried, with its dots and lines. Then back.

"That's not on my map," he said.

"It's not on anybody's map," said Higu. She knew. She'd seen every map Ennis had. "It's north of everything. It's off the edge."

They stood there, the four of them, in the moonlight.

"Chi," said Higu. She didn't care, now, who heard. "Somebody knew."

"Somebody knew," said Gigi. "And they painted it on a ceiling." She laughed, a strange cracked laugh, with her head back. "On a *ceiling*. Where the guilds would sit and look at it while they waited for their twenty. For four hundred years. And nobody looked."

"My grandmother looked," said Ama Corrie quietly.

They all turned. The old woman was standing a little apart, with her keys and her snuffer, looking up at her ceiling in the moonlight. Her face had gone completely still. Higu understood that she was seeing it too, for the first time in her life, the thing her grandmother had told her about when she was a girl.

"She was right," said Ama Corrie, very softly. "The difficult old woman. She was right the whole time."

***

They stayed until the moon slid off the far side of the dome and the lines went out one by one, the way they'd come. It took nearly an hour. Higu drew all of it. Every star, every line, on four sheets of paper on her knee on the floor of the Hall, in the moonlight, in her careful, upright hand. Kei held the lantern for her when the moon got low. Gigi sat beside her on the cold floor with her shoulder against Higu's, and didn't say it would fall down, not once.

When it was dark again, Kei said it.

"When we're twenty," he said. "When there's a banner. That's the first thing."

"What is?" said Gigi.

"That." He pointed up into the dark, toward the north door, where the big star was. "We go and find out what that is. Where all the lines go."

"It'll be horrible," said Gigi.

"Probably," said Kei.

Gigi looked up at the dark for a long time.

"I'm coming," she said.

Higu turned her stream back on at twenty to one, on the floor of the round Hall, with four sheets of paper in her lap. She didn't show them. She just turned the camera on her own face, with the white bow still damp from the rain and her eyes very bright, and said:

"Hi. Sorry. I went dark." A pause. "I found something. I'm going to tell you when it's time."
