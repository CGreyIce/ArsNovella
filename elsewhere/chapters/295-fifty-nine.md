Dennis counted everything twice, and he'd never once counted himself.

He didn't know that yet. On the first morning of the walk, at the milestone at the bottom of the south road, he only knew that there were fifty-eight of them and a sheep. He'd counted them coming through the gate past Tobi, one at a time, in his ledger. Then again at the milestone, by heads, because a gate count and a milestone count should always agree, and if they didn't you'd lost somebody.

They agreed. Fifty-eight. Fifty-five of the guild, he'd have said, if you'd asked, and Riverbank's four going home. He'd have said it, and not noticed that fifty-five and four was not fifty-eight. And a sheep.

"Fifty-eight, darling," said Dennis, to the ledger. "And a sheep."

"Fifty-nine," said Calvin, beside him.

Dennis looked up.

"Fifty-eight," he said. "I counted twice."

"I know you counted twice," said Calvin. "You always count twice." He looked at Dennis with the expression he'd had since Deston lent him the money for an egg, a long time ago. The face of a man who'd known you for years and was still slightly surprised by you. "Count again."

Dennis counted again. Fifty-eight.

"Fifty-eight," he said.

Calvin sighed, and walked on.

***

The goose was at the bend by the old stone barn on the second day.

Dennis had heard about the goose. Everybody had. It was the most famous goose in the game. It stood in the middle of the west road at the bend, grey and white and large, with an expression of permanent enormous offence. It didn't move for anybody. You walked round it. Dec had walked round it the first time he went to Masmark, alone, in January a year and a half ago. That had been one of the things Hyvred hit him for, in Masmark, when she got there.

And then, over the last year, it had started sitting down.

For two, the first time. *It's never seen two*, Dec had said, walking home from Masmark with the sword the lake gave back. For Aurelie and Vane, two Meridian, walking to the round town. For Harlan's twenty in December. For Aurelie and Vane again, in January, going home. Ama Corrie had written it in the Roll once. Dennis had seen it, reading over her shoulder, which she allowed from him because he read everything anyway. *The goose sat down.* She didn't write anything she couldn't see. She'd taken it on report.

Dennis had his ledger open when they came round the bend.

The goose was there. In the middle of the road. Looking at them.

At fifty-eight people coming round a bend in a road, two and three abreast, with a sheep in front. Patience walked straight up to it, in the middle of the road, and stopped. The two of them looked at each other, sheep and goose, from about a pace apart. Nobody said anything. Fifty-eight people stopped on the west road behind them and waited.

"Darling," said Dennis, very quietly, "if it doesn't sit down, I'm going to cry."

"It'll sit down," said Hyvred, beside him. She was holding Dec's arm and looking at the goose edge to edge. "It's thinking."

"It's a *goose*."

"Mother Dipp says geese consider," said Chernn, behind him.

The goose looked at Patience. Then past Patience, at the fifty-eight. Slowly, from one end to the other, as though it were counting.

Dennis knew a count when he saw one.

It got to the end. It looked back at Patience. And then it folded its legs, all at once, with enormous dignity, and sat down in the middle of the west road.

Patience, after a moment, sat down too. Facing it.

Fifty-eight people made a noise like a roof coming off.

Dennis wrote it down. *Day 2. The goose sat down. Fifty-eight and a sheep.* He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote, under it, because it was true: *The sheep sat down as well.*

***

They walked round both of them. It took a long time. Nobody minded.

"It counted us," said Dennis, after, on the road. To nobody. To Calvin, who was beside him. "Did you see? It looked at us one at a time. From one end to the other." He was still slightly shaky. "That's a count, darling. I know a count."

"What did it get?" said Calvin.

Dennis looked at him.

"Fifty-eight," he said. "Obviously."

Calvin looked at him for a long time, with the face.

"Obviously," he said. And walked on.

***

He did the count every night. That was his job. Nobody had given it to him; he'd taken it, the first night. Out of the gate in the morning and into a field in the White Hills at dusk. Fifty-eight people scattering across it to make fires and put up the things people put up. And Dennis standing at the gate of the field with his ledger, counting them in.

Kei had a map. Kotori had tea. Hyvred saw everything. PenguChi was at the back. Dennis counted. If you were going to walk fourteen days with fifty-eight people across half the world, somebody had to know, every night, that nobody had been left at a bend in a road. He'd done it on the longest night, in the round town. Every lit window, every one, from the south steps in the dawn. *Four hundred and thirty-one, darling. I counted twice.* He'd done it at Tallow Green in his head, every candle. He did it at the long table every night without anybody knowing, looking down the chairs. *Sixty-one and a stool.*

He liked it. He liked knowing. He liked the way a count came out the same twice, and the world was the size you'd thought.

On the third night it came out fifty-seven.

***

He counted again. Fifty-seven.

He stood at the gate of a field in the White Hills, with fifty-seven people round fires in it, and the dusk going, and his ledger open. He felt something go cold in his chest that he hadn't felt since the longest night. The first one, two and a half years ago, in a swamp, when there'd been ten of them and he hadn't been one yet.

"Somebody's missing," said Dennis.

He didn't say it loudly. He said it the way you'd say it if you were frightened of it being true. Kei heard. Kei was at the nearest fire with his map. He looked up at once.

"Who?"

"I don't know." Dennis was going down the ledger. Every name. Fifty-five and four. He'd written them all on the first morning. He'd ticked them all at the gate every night. Tonight he'd ticked fifty-seven. "I don't *know*, darling, I've got fifty-seven ticks and fifty-eight names, I don't know which—"

"Count by name," said Kei. Calmly. He'd got up. "Out loud. We'll answer."

So Dennis counted by name, standing at the gate of the field in the White Hills in the dusk. Out loud. And everybody, from their fires, answered.

"Kei."

"Here."

"Higu."

"Here."

"Gabe."

"*HERE*."

"Rae."

"Here. Next to him."

Down the ledger. Every one. Fifty-five and four. The answers coming back out of the dusk from every fire in the field. *Here. Here. Here.* Hyvred. Dec. Suzu, who said *here* from flat on his back under a hedge without opening his eyes. Xav, RX, Malcolm (*here, unfortunately*). Val. Kite and Drew and Ash, three, *here, here, here*. PenguChi from the back of the field. The sheep, from beside PenguChi, said something.

He got to the end. Fifty-eight names. Fifty-eight answers.

He'd ticked fifty-seven.

He stood at the gate of the field and looked at the ledger. He didn't understand it. He'd counted everybody twice by head and once by name and there were fifty-eight names and fifty-eight answers and fifty-seven ticks.

"I don't understand," said Dennis.

Calvin came across the field to the gate. He stood beside Dennis and looked down at the ledger over his shoulder for a long time.

"Darling," said Calvin.

Dennis looked at him. Calvin never said *darling*. That was Dennis's.

Calvin put his finger on the list. Not on a name. On the empty space at the bottom of it, under the last name, where there was nothing.

"You never count you," said Calvin.

***

Dennis looked at the empty space at the bottom of the list for a long time.

He thought about it. He went back through every count he could remember. The windows on the longest night, from the south steps: four hundred and thirty-one. Every lit window in the round town. He'd been standing on the south steps, outside. He hadn't been in any of them.

The long table, every night. *Sixty-one and a stool.* He sat at the long table. In a chair. He'd been counting the chairs from inside one of them for a year and a half and never once counted the one he was in.

The Tallow Green candles. Every one, from the crates. Four hundred and thirty-one, and one more for the road. Not one of them for him. Of course not. He'd been the one counting.

The gate, on the first morning. Fifty-eight past Tobi. And Tobi had asked him, as he went through, *who'd stand next to you*. And Dennis had said, without thinking, *Calvin and Rae and Deston and Gabe*. The four people he'd come into the game with, more or less, a long time ago. Tobi had written them down. And Dennis had gone through the gate and turned round and started counting everybody coming after him, and never once counted the one who'd just answered.

"Fifty-nine," said Dennis. Very quietly.

"Fifty-nine," said Calvin. "I told you at the milestone."

"You *knew*?"

"I've known since the first day in here," said Calvin. "When you counted the three of us at the start, and got two, and didn't notice." He almost smiled. "You count everybody so nobody gets left at a bend in a road." He was quiet. "And you leave you."

***

Dennis didn't say anything for a long time.

The field had gone quiet. It did that, Dennis was finding out, on this walk, with fifty-eight people. With fifty-nine. When something happened at one fire, the others went quiet to listen, the way the long table did. Everybody in the field had heard him count by name. Everybody had heard Calvin say *you never count you*.

Nobody said anything. They were waiting, he realised. To see what he'd do.

He looked at the ledger. At the empty space at the bottom of the list.

He'd never once written his own name in it. He'd never once written his own name in anything in the game, except the Roll, when he signed, a long time ago. He wrote everybody else's. He was the one who knew how many.

He took out his pen.

At the bottom of the list, under the last name, in the space where there was nothing, he wrote: *Dennis*.

He looked at it.

Then he ticked it.

*Fifty-nine.*

He'd expected it to feel like nothing. A tick was a tick. It didn't feel like nothing. It felt like the morning on the south steps after the longest night. Standing outside four hundred and thirty-one lit windows and counting every one of them, and thinking, for a moment, very quickly, before he put it away: *I wonder what it looks like from inside one.*

"Fifty-nine," said Dennis, out loud. To the field.

From every fire, at once, the field answered. Not *here*. Something else. A sort of cheer, the way you'd cheer somebody coming home. Gabe stood up and roared. Leaka screamed. Rae, beside Gabe, said *finally*, which was Xola's, and Xola said *that's MINE*. And Suzu, under his hedge, without opening his eyes, said *good*.

Dennis stood at the gate of the field in the White Hills with his ledger and found he was crying. He didn't know why. It was only a tick.

"Darling," said Calvin, beside him, gently. "It's all right."

"It's *not* all right," said Dennis. "I've been wrong by one for two and a half years."

"You've been right about everybody else," said Calvin. "That's two and a half years of being right." He put his arm round Dennis's shoulders. "One's allowed."

***

He went and sat at Dec's fire, after. He didn't know why that one. It was nearest.

Dec was there with Hyvred, who wasn't on his knee for once. She was sitting beside him with her chin on his shoulder, looking at Dennis across the fire edge to edge. Suzu was asleep under the hedge behind them. Kotori had her kettles going.

"You count everybody," said Dec.

"Yes."

"Every night."

"Every night," said Dennis. "At the long table too. Nobody knows."

"I know," said Dec. "I see you do it. Looking down the chairs, at a quarter to eight, before Kei stands up." He was quiet for a moment. "I do it too. At the long table. Who's there. Who isn't."

Dennis looked at him.

"I never counted me either," said Dec. "For a long time. I was the one counting." He looked at the fire. "It took me a long time to notice."

"Who told you?"

Dec looked at Hyvred, beside him, with her chin on his shoulder.

"Somebody who saw everything," he said. "And said *me*. At a gate."

Hyvred went red to the horns. She didn't say anything. She took Dec's arm with both hands, very tightly, and put her face in his shoulder. Dennis looked at the two of them across the fire, and thought about counting.

He didn't count them. He just looked.

***

Deston found him the next morning, at the gate of the field, before the count.

He came and stood beside Dennis, enormous, with his book under his arm. He didn't open it. He hadn't opened it since the milestone in April. He looked at the field, at fifty-nine people packing up.

"I counted you," said Deston.

Dennis looked up at him.

"On the longest night," said Deston. "Chernn and me. In the window over the Hart's kitchen. We had the windows; Kei put us there." He was quiet. "At the dawn after, you went out onto the south steps with your ledger. To count the windows. I saw you go." He looked down at Dennis. "I counted you. From inside. One man on the south steps, counting." He paused. "Chernn said *he's counting us*. And I said *I know. I'm counting him*."

Dennis didn't say anything.

"Four hundred and thirty-one windows," said Deston. "And one on the steps. That's four hundred and thirty-two." His ears had gone red. "I never told you. I'm slow."

"Darling," said Dennis. His voice had gone. "You're not slow."

"I am," said Deston. "Ask Chernn. Slow is how a candle burns all the way down."

***

They had a fire together on the fourth night, the five of them. Deston and Gabe and Rae and Calvin and Dennis. And Chernn, because Chernn was Deston's now. The old five, more or less, who'd come into the game at different times by different roads and ended up at the same long table.

Gabe talked. Of course he did. About the first weeks, when he'd split off from the others for a while to drag Rae and Calvin and Dennis up through the levels. All at once, roaring, at the front of everything, because that was the only way Gabe knew how to help anybody. *I had you at twenty in a FORTNIGHT*.

"You had me at twenty in a fortnight by standing in front of me shouting," said Rae.

"It *worked*."

"It worked," agreed Rae. She was beside him at no paces, with her staff across her knees. "I mended where you were going to be the whole time. You never noticed."

Gabe stopped talking.

"The *whole* time?"

"The whole time," said Rae. "From the first week. You were always going to be wherever the thing was. I just got there first."

Gabe looked at her for a long time across the fire. Then he went very pink, and very quiet, and kept it. Dennis watched him keep it. He'd known Gabe longer than most people. He'd never once seen him keep anything.

He didn't count the five of them. He didn't need to. He just sat at the fire with his ledger shut on his knee and looked at them, from inside.

***

On the fourth day they came down out of the White Hills onto the long road east, flat and white in the sun. On the fifth they saw the river, wide and brown, and the ferryman's hut on the near bank with its door covered in little cut marks.

Dennis counted them at the gate of every field, every night. Fifty-nine.

He always got to the bottom of the list now and ticked his own name last. Every time it felt the same as the first time. It felt like standing inside a lit window, for once, instead of outside counting them.

On the fifth night, in a field by the river, the count came out sixty.

He stood at the gate and looked at it.

He counted again. Sixty.

Calvin was beside him, looking down at the ledger.

"Darling," said Calvin. "That's not you this time."

Dennis looked round the field. Fifty-nine people at fires. And at the edge of the field, by the gate, where nobody had noticed it come in, a goose.

Grey and white and large, with an expression of permanent enormous offence. It had walked, apparently, three days from the bend by the old stone barn. It was standing in the field at the edge of the firelight, beside Patience, who was lying down. As if it had come to see where everybody was going.

"*Darling*," said Dennis.

"It followed us," said Calvin.

"It *counted* us," said Dennis. "At the bend. It counted fifty-eight. And then it came to check." He looked at the goose. The goose looked at him. "It got it wrong. It didn't count me either."

He looked down at the ledger, at sixty. He thought about it for a long time.

Then, under *Dennis*, at the very bottom of the list, he wrote: *the goose*.

And ticked it.
