The last of Xav's idiots walked into Brackenford on Saturday morning, the thirteenth of February, and it was Jun Liang, and he was singing.

Ice heard him before he saw him. They all did. The six of them were standing on the riverbank below the town, where the three rivers came together. It was the stretch of shingle and old stone below the market island, where Xav had said in January, on a call from a cold small room in Japan, *I'll be on the bank*. And from somewhere up the west road, getting louder, came a voice singing a song about being the leader. Loudly, and with total commitment.

"Oh no," said Xav.

He was standing at the edge of the water with a rolled paper under his arm, in the same plain coat he'd worn since the first day. Behind him on the shingle, in a loose untidy row, stood six of the seven people he'd been waiting for. Rui Xiang, with two knives and a face that couldn't keep still. Malcolm, leaning on a stick, looking at the sky as if it was going to rain. John, very tall and thin, with a little round mirror on a cord round his neck that caught the light. Azac, square and solid, with the biggest shield Ice had ever seen anybody carry, bigger than his own, planted in the shingle like a door. Ryan, in a dark coat, hands in his pockets, saying nothing. And Val, small, with a wand through her belt and her dark red hair tied back, standing a little apart, watching Ice with an expression he'd known for nine years.

"Is that him?" said Gabe. "The singing?"

"That's Jun Liang," said Ice.

"He sounds happy."

"He always sounds happy."

"*WE NEED A LEADER*," sang Jun Liang, coming over the brow of the west road with a hammer on his back and his arms in the air. "*AND WE'VE GOT ONE—*"

Xav put his face in his hand.

Moonlight Harvest had walked up from Fernwick at dawn to be there. Nobody had asked them to. Xav would never have asked. But it was a morning's walk. And Ice had known since the last day of December, when he'd read *rui xiang has messaged me 41 times* on a balcony in Malacca, that he was going to be on this bank when it happened. Whatever it was. Whenever.

So they stood a little way back on the shingle, the six of them. Not in the row. That was Xav's row. Just there.

Jun Liang came down the bank at a run, still singing, and nearly fell in the river. Azac caught him by the collar with one hand without looking, the way you'd catch a falling cup. Then Jun Liang was on the shingle with the others, panting, grinning, with his hammer, and there were seven of them in the row, and Xav in front.

Rui Xiang counted them. Out loud, pointing.

"One, two, three, four, five, six, seven," he said. "And Xav. Eight." He stopped, and counted again, and got eight again, and looked as if he might cry. "Eight. We're all here. We said we'd all be here and we're all here."

"You counted twice," said Malcolm.

"I always count twice," said Rui Xiang. "Somebody has to."

Xav didn't say anything.

He stood at the edge of the water with his back to the river, looking at the seven of them in their row. Ice watched him do it. He'd watched Xav look at things for nine years. At plans, at maps, at a fight in CS with a round on the line, at a stone in a market square with names on it. He'd never seen him look at anything like this. Xav's face had gone completely still, the way it did when he was trying very hard not to look as pleased as he was. It wasn't working.

"Okay," said Xav, eventually. His voice was the plan voice, calm and level, and it had something underneath it. "First week, everybody levels alone. Nobody answers CL. Get to ten. Then walk to me." He looked at them. "You did it."

"Jun Liang answered CL," said Val.

"I said *no thank you*," said Jun Liang. "With a *smiley*."

"He said no thank you with a smiley," agreed Xav. "That counts."

Penhallow came down from the Adventurers' Hall with the charter.

He was an old man with ink on his cuffs and a pen behind each ear, the Hall's clerk. He'd been keeping the paper in the top-left pigeonhole since December, *till Wednesday*, and then till the next Wednesday, and then until the founder's people arrived. He came down the steps to the bank slowly, holding it in both hands like a tray of glasses.

[ The Charter of Company · Brackenford
Up to twelve travellers, under one name, one banner and one purse.
Founder: Sketch
Name: ________ ]

"The name," said Penhallow. "If you please."

Everybody looked at Xav.

Xav looked at the charter.

"We should vote," he said.

"*THE LEADERS*," said Jun Liang at once.

"No."

"*THE LEADERS AND XAV*—"

"It'll still be March," said Malcolm, to the sky. "Whatever we call it. It's going to be March soon."

"We're not calling it March, Malcolm."

"I didn't say call it March. I said it'll be March." Malcolm looked at him sideways. "That's not a name. That's a prediction."

"I've got one," said Val.

She hadn't said anything since Jun Liang arrived. She said it in her calm, low, careful voice, the one that made people trust her. She was looking at the river behind Xav, the three rivers coming together, and the shingle, and the eight of them standing on it.

"On New Year's Day," she said, "on the call, from Japan. You said *get to ten, then walk to me*." She looked at Xav. "And then you said *I'll be on the bank*."

Xav went very still.

"You were," said Val. "Every single one of us. You were on the bank." She shrugged, slightly. "That's all. That's the name."

Nobody said anything.

Then Rui Xiang said "*Riverbank*" in a small voice, as if he were tasting it. And Azac said "Riverbank," in his big flat voice, and nodded once. John said it, and Ryan said "yeah," which was all Ryan ever said. Malcolm looked at the sky and said, "It's still going to be March, but yeah." And Jun Liang said it at the top of his voice, so that a heron on the far bank took off in a fright.

Xav stood at the edge of the water with the charter in his hands, and his face did something Ice had never seen it do in nine years.

"Riverbank," he said.

"I knew it," said Ice.

Everybody turned and looked at him.

"How?" said Xav. "I never—"

"You told Kei," said Ice. "On a call. By accident. Then you pretended you hadn't."

Xav stared at him. "*Kei* told you?"

"Kei said not to tell you that he'd told me."

"Then why are you *telling* me?"

"Because I said I'd say it in front of you one day," said Ice. "By accident."

Xav looked at him for a long moment across the shingle. Then he laughed, out loud, on a riverbank, in front of everybody. Ice had heard Xav laugh like that maybe five times since poly.

"You're terrible," said Xav.

"I was terrible with you," said Ice.

"You're *still* terrible without me."

"I know."

Penhallow cleared his throat. He held out a pen.

Xav wrote it in. Ice watched him. His careful, quick hand, the hand that had drawn every plan Moonlight Harvest had ever stood in.

*RIVERBANK.*

Afterwards, when Penhallow had gone back up the steps with the charter, and the eight of them were standing on the shingle being a company, Val came over.

She came across the stones to where Ice was standing, small, with her wand through her belt. She looked up at him. She'd been looking at him like that since poly. Ice had never quite worked out what it meant.

"Declan," she said.

"Val."

"You came."

"It's a morning's walk."

"You walked a morning to stand on a bank and watch Xav write a word." She looked at him for a while. "You're still weird."

"I know."

"You're a bit less weird," said Val. Then, after a moment, very quietly, so the others wouldn't hear: "Thank you for telling him to go."

She went back to the row before he could say anything.

On Wednesday night the jar moved.

They were in the ring, at moonrise, dark. The six of them in the warm water among the nine grey stones, with the moonbells opening and the bells ringing slightly out of time. Suzu had his back against the ninth heron, the one that faced the wrong way, the way he always did. The jar was on the stone beside him.

It had been there every night for two months. A plain glass jar with a stopper, the kind you'd keep sweets in, that Suzu had got from somewhere on the eleventh day of the game. The game had made it unique in the drowned church. Inside it, eighty-one small blue lights moved slowly, round and round, like fish in a bowl. Old Light. The lights of things that had been forgotten, that clung to his scythe when he cut, that he'd kept instead of letting go. Nobody knew what they were for. Suzu didn't either. He just kept them.

Tonight they'd stopped going round.

Ice saw it because Suzu went still. Suzu was always still, but this was a different still. He'd opened his eyes and was looking down at the jar on the stone. Ice looked too.

The lights weren't drifting. All eighty-one of them had gone to one side of the jar and were pressing against the glass there, crowded together. Like fish in a bowl when somebody holds food against the side. A bright blue crescent on one side of the jar, and dark everywhere else.

"Suzu," said Ice.

"I see it."

"Which way?"

Suzu didn't answer at once. He picked up the jar, carefully, in both hands, and turned it round. A quarter turn. The lights moved, all at once, streaming round the inside of the glass, to the same side as before. Not the same side of the jar. The same side of the *world*. He turned it again. They moved again, and pressed against the glass in the same direction.

He lowered it, and looked over his shoulder at the stone he was leaning against. The ninth heron. The one that faced the wrong way. Its long grey neck bent outward, away from the ring, toward the dark beyond the stones.

The lights were pressing the same way the heron was looking.

"They want to go somewhere," said Suzu.

"Where?"

"Where the ninth one's looking," said Suzu.

Nobody said anything. The moonbells rang.

Ice thought about a message on his phone. He'd read it on Tuesday night, three times, from a man on a ferry. *A painted ceiling. 400 years old. Your ring's on it. Nine. One turned. In gold. With a line going north.*

North.

He'd been going to keep it. The way Chris had kept the heron, for seven weeks, in a sheet nobody would read. *It's yours. You'll tell him when it's time.* But this wasn't Kei. This was the ring. These five people sat in it with him every night. Five people who'd been shown it, one by one, since the second night of the game. It was theirs as much as his.

So he told them.

About the ceiling in the round hall at Carrow Cross. About the silver lines that came out only by moonlight. About Higu's four sheets, and Selwick, and the ditches, and the staircase of locks, and the forge. And about nine small stars in a ring in the south-east corner, one set apart and turned, with a silver line running out of it north, across the whole of the painted sky, to a star that wasn't on any map.

When he'd finished, nobody said anything for a long time.

"So it's not just us," said Deston eventually. Quietly. "The ring. Somebody knew about it. Four hundred years ago."

"Somebody knew about all of it," said Ice.

"How far north?" said Sheng Yang.

"Off every map."

Kotori had both hands round her staff. The bell on it wasn't ringing. "Is it bad?" she said. "Sorry. I mean—is it a bad place?"

"I don't know," said Ice.

Gabe was sitting in the middle of the ring with his hammer across his knees and his feet in the warm water. He looked at the jar in Suzu's hands, the eighty-one lights pressing against the glass. He looked at the ninth heron. Then at Ice.

"Good," said Gabe.

Ice laughed, very quietly, in the dark.

"Not yet," said Suzu.

They all looked at him. He'd put the jar back on the stone beside him. The lights stayed where they were, pressed against the north side of the glass, a crescent of blue. He looked at them with his half-shut eyes.

"Kei's got fifteen days," said Suzu. "And thirteen names to get. And Nightjar on the roads." The corner of his mouth went up. "We go after. When there's a banner. When Kei says it's time."

"After," said Ice.

"After," said Suzu, and closed his eyes, and leaned back against the ninth heron. The jar sat on the stone beside him, glowing on one side, all night, pointing north.
