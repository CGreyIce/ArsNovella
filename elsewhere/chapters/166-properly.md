By Tuesday morning there were one thousand four hundred and twelve of them, and Suzu had read every one.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to read his messages, the way he did every morning on the edge of his pod before he went in. But the forum had a page now. Somebody had made it on the Monday after the duel and called it *FIGHT ME (ICE)*, in capitals, like that. Every morning there were more on it. He'd opened it on the first morning to see what it was. Then he'd kept opening it, the way you keep pressing a bruise.

"Fourteen hundred and twelve," said Suzu.

Ice was at the desk by the window with his kopi, reading something on his own phone. He didn't look up.

"Fourteen hundred and twelve what?"

"People who want to fight you."

"Oh," said Ice. "That's a lot."

"Two hundred and six of them are from Brazil."

"Why Brazil?"

"I don't know," said Suzu. "They're very excited. One of them has written it in a poem."

Ice did look up, at that. Suzu turned the phone round and showed him. The poem was eleven lines long and rhymed *Ice* with *sacrifice* twice. Ice read it all the way through without any expression at all. Then he nodded, as if it had been a perfectly reasonable thing to send a man you'd never met.

"It's not bad," he said.

"It's very bad."

"It's not bad for a *poem*," said Ice, and went back to his phone.

The back room was hot already. The fan went round. Across the road somebody was hosing down the front of the kopitiam, and the water ran past their shutter in a thin brown stream and into the drain. Gabe had gone in at six. His pod was shut, with the little light on the lid that meant somebody was home. Suzu could hear the hum of it, under the fan.

He scrolled.

Most of them were the same. *Fight me. I'll fight you. Lv 41 spear, I'll go first, you won't get six frames on me.* Some of them were rude. Some of them were very polite, in a way that was somehow worse. There was one from a girl in Lyon who had written him a whole page about the held draw. How Kestrel's held draw was the best in the game and he'd walked into it anyway. How she'd stayed up until four in the morning watching it. How she'd like to fight him. Not to win, she said. Just so she would know.

That one he read twice.

"Kei says the gate's bad again," said Ice, from the desk.

Suzu looked over. Ice was reading his messages too.

"How bad?"

"He says three hundred people on the south steps last night." Ice scrolled. "He says Wendy asked every one of them *who'd stand next to you*. And two hundred and eighty of them said *nobody, I just want to fight Ice*." He paused. "He says Wendy's going to start charging."

"She should."

"She says the ones who say nobody aren't getting in." Ice put the phone down. "That's fair."

"That's very fair."

"They keep coming, though." Ice looked out of the window at the wet road and the man with the hose. His face did nothing in particular, the way it never did. But Suzu had known him for a long time. He knew what it looked like when Ice was thinking about something he didn't like. It looked exactly like this. "Every night. More. Kei can't get people in to sign the Roll. Ama Corrie had a man asleep under the stair."

"Did she sweep under him?"

"Kei says she swept round him." Ice drank his kopi. "He says it's the first time anyone's ever seen her sweep *round*."

***

Vivian came at ten.

She didn't usually come in person. She sent messages at eight, on the dot, and you replied, and that was how it worked. But at ten past nine she'd messaged to say *I am coming with two folders. Please be dressed.* And at ten exactly the shutter rattled. There she was on the step, in her grey office suit, out of a Grab, with two folders under her arm and an umbrella in case.

"Mr Declan," said Vivian. "Mr Suzu."

"Morning," said Ice.

She came into the back room and looked at the pods. She always looked at the pods. She'd called them *the units* since the first day, and touched one once, on the shell but not the glass. She said it was because they were capital expenditure. Suzu thought it was because she found them slightly frightening and didn't want to say.

She put the two folders down on the desk, side by side, very straight.

"The two that make potions," said Vivian. "As I mentioned on Sunday. I declined the other nine. They wished to give you money to say their names, and their names were not suitable."

"What were they?"

"One was a betting company." She said *betting company* the way the auntie at the kopitiam said *Johor drivers*. "Two were betting companies wearing other companies' clothes. One was cryptocurrency. I won't say the others. You don't need to know." She tapped the first folder. "This one is large."

Ice opened it.

Suzu came and looked over his shoulder. He wasn't sure he was supposed to, but nobody told him not to, and he was technically an employee. He was the Game Operations Executive. It said so on his contract, all four pages of it.

The first folder was glossy. It had a logo on the front, a lightning bolt in a bottle, very bright, and the word VOLT. Inside there were eleven pages, and a lot of the pages had pictures of young men in headsets shouting. There was a new flavour, apparently. A *potion* flavour. It came in a bottle shaped like a bottle in a game. Red, blue and green.

They would pay a lot. Suzu saw the number on page six and blinked.

They wanted him to say *VOLT* every thirty minutes on stream. They wanted the bottle on his desk, on Earth, whenever he was out of the egg. And they wanted something else, on page nine, which Ice read twice.

"They want to make it in the game," said Ice.

"Yes," said Vivian.

"A potion with their name on it. That I'd drink. In the game." He read it again. "They've asked the studio. The studio said yes, if I do."

"Yes," said Vivian.

Ice closed the glossy folder. He did it gently, the way he'd close a book that belonged to somebody else, and put it to one side.

"No," he said.

"I thought you might say that," said Vivian. She didn't sound surprised. She didn't sound anything. But she took a pen out of her jacket pocket and made one small neat mark on her pad. Suzu thought she looked very slightly pleased. "And this one is small."

The second folder wasn't glossy. It was a brown cardboard folder, the kind you'd buy at a stationery shop for forty sen, and somebody had written on the front of it in blue ballpoint. *Lim Brothers Herbal Tea. Est. 1938. Jonker Street.*

Inside there was one letter. It was handwritten too, on lined paper, two pages, in careful English with the commas in slightly the wrong places.

Ice read it. Suzu read it over his shoulder.

It was from Mr Lim. The third Mr Lim, he said, the grandson. The shop had been on Jonker Street since his grandfather. They boiled the tea in the back in the same three pots, every morning, and sold it in bottles, and to the tourists in little cups. It was bitter. He wanted to say so at the start. It was very bitter. It was good for heat and for the throat and for staying up late. People in the old days had drunk it to study.

His daughter watched Ice's stream. She'd watched it since the second week. She'd told him there was a man in Malacca who made medicine in the game. The medicine was very good, and he never said anything bad about anybody. And she'd said, *Pa, you should ask him*.

So he was asking. He didn't have much money. He knew this was not how these things were done. He knew there were bigger companies. But if Ice would drink a bottle sometimes, on stream, when he was out of the game, only if he liked it, they'd send a crate every month. And a little money. And if he didn't like it, he should say so. They'd understand. It was bitter.

There was a number at the bottom. It was very small. It was smaller than the number on page six of the other folder by about a hundred times.

Under the letter, in the folder, was a single glass bottle of something very dark, with a paper label.

Ice looked at it for a long time.

Then he picked up the bottle and twisted off the cap and drank some.

Suzu watched his face. It was the most bitter thing Suzu had ever smelt, from a foot away. It smelt like the inside of an old medicine drawer. It smelt like something you'd use to punish a child. And Ice drank a long mouthful of it, and swallowed, and his face did absolutely nothing. Not a flicker. As if somebody had poured cold water into him.

He put the cap back on.

"Yes," said Ice.

Vivian looked at him over her pad.

"They are offering very little, Mr Declan."

"They asked properly," said Ice.

Vivian didn't say anything for a moment. Then she wrote it down. Suzu saw her do it. She wrote, in her small neat hand, under the mark for VOLT: *Lim Bros — yes. "Asked properly."* She underlined *properly*. Then she put the pen back in her pocket, and picked up the glossy folder, and put it into her bag in a way that made it clear she wouldn't be needing it again.

"I will write to Mr Lim this afternoon," said Vivian. "He will be very pleased. And his daughter." She paused. "I will also write to VOLT. They will be less pleased."

"Tell them thank you."

"I will tell them no," said Vivian. "Politely. That is what I'm for." She stood up. She picked up her umbrella. At the door she stopped and looked back at the bottle of herbal tea on the desk. "Is it very bitter?"

"Yes," said Ice.

"My grandmother used to make me drink that," said Vivian. "For exams." And she went out into the sun, and put the umbrella up even though it wasn't raining, and was gone.

***

Ice drank the rest of the bottle while Suzu watched him.

"You hate it," said Suzu.

"No," said Ice. "It's good." He looked at the empty bottle. "It's like what I make. In there. The Good draughts. They're bitter too. Everyone complains." He turned it round to look at the label. *Lim Bros. Est. 1938.* "His daughter's watched since the second week."

"Do you know her?"

"No," said Ice. "But she was there. In the second week. When it was forty people and the fen." He put the bottle down very carefully on the windowsill, by itself, where you'd put something you meant to keep. "That's properly. He wrote it himself. He told me it was bitter. He said I could say no."

Suzu didn't say anything.

He thought about the fourteen hundred and twelve. About the girl in Lyon. About the man who'd written the poem, and the two hundred and eighty on the south steps who'd said *nobody*.

"That's what you should tell them," he said.

Ice looked at him.

"The ones who want to fight you," said Suzu. "Tell them that. Ask properly."

Ice looked out of the window for a while.

"I said that to Kestrel," he said. "After. *If somebody asks. Properly.*"

"I know. I was there."

"Nobody's asked properly," said Ice. "They've just *asked*." He picked up his phone and looked at it and put it down again. "Fourteen hundred times."

"Fourteen hundred and twelve."

"Fourteen hundred and twelve." Ice sighed. It was a very small sigh. You'd only have known it was a sigh if you'd known him for a long time. "If I keep saying no, they'll keep coming. Every night. Wendy can't keep a gate with three hundred people on it who don't want to come in."

"So say yes."

"If I say yes to one, I have to say yes to fourteen hundred."

"So say yes to some," said Suzu. "Properly. And then say that's all."

Ice didn't answer that. He sat at the desk with his hands round his empty cup and looked at the wet road for a long time. Then he got up and went to his pod, and lay down in it. Suzu thought he'd gone in without answering, the way he sometimes did.

The lid was halfway down when Ice said, to the ceiling:

"How many classes are there? On the board."

Suzu looked at him.

"Thousands. They're all different. Everybody's is different."

"No. The board. The studio's board. The marks."

Suzu thought about it. The studio's class board, the big one on the official site that everybody argued about. It listed every class the game had ever named, thousands of them, every one different. But it put them under eight little marks, along the top, so you could find things. A shield. A long blade. Two blades crossed. A knife. A spear. A bow. A staff. A hand with light in it.

"Eight," said Suzu.

"Eight," said Ice. And the lid came down, and the little light went on.

***

He said it on the south steps, on Wednesday, at the second dusk, with Suzu beside him.

He didn't make a speech. Ice never made speeches. He went out of the Hall of Roads onto the steps, where the crowd was. It was more than three hundred tonight. It filled the round market from the steps to the fountain and halfway down the south road. People sat on the fountain's rim and people stood on each other's shoulders and some of them had banners. One of the banners had his face on it, painted, not very well. Another said FIGHT ME ICE in Portuguese and English. Wendy was standing in the gate, at the bottom, with her bow on her back and her arms folded. She looked like a woman who'd been standing in a gate for three weeks and was about to do something about it.

When Ice came out, the crowd noticed. It took a moment, and then all of it noticed at once. The noise went up, and up, like a wave coming in.

Ice stood at the top of the steps and waited for it to stop.

It took a long time. He waited the whole time. He didn't wave or shout or hold his hands up. He just stood there with his shield on his back, the way he stood at the front of anything. And slowly, from the steps outward, people noticed that he was waiting. They went quiet, and the people behind them noticed them going quiet, and went quiet too. Until the whole round market was so quiet that Suzu could hear the fountain.

He didn't say it loudly. He didn't need to.

"One from every class on the board," said Ice. "The eight marks. The best one of each who walks here and asks at the gate. Properly." He nodded down the steps at Wendy. "She asks you a question. You answer it. If you say *nobody*, you don't get in, and you don't get to ask me."

Silence.

"The bow's done," said Ice. "Kestrel was the bow." He paused. "So seven. One a night, in the ring in the market. Until one of us yields." He looked out over all of them, every face, the way he looked over a room. "And then that's all. No more. Not this year."

For a moment nobody said anything at all.

Then the round market went up like a firework.

Wendy, at the bottom of the steps, turned round and looked up at Ice for a long time.

Then she held up one finger. Not that one. The first one. *One*.

Ice nodded.

Wendy turned back to the three hundred, and the round market, and the banners, and unfolded her arms.

"Right," she said, very loudly, in the voice Suzu had heard her use on Hana once. "Line."

***

It took Kei and Tif two days to sort them.

Kei did the gate end. He sat on the steps beside Wendy with his map turned over to the blank side and wrote down every one who said a name. Tif did the other end, from outside the egg in Singapore, where she could see everything at once. She had a spreadsheet. Of course she had a spreadsheet. Suzu had seen it on Thursday night on Ice's second screen in the back room. It had eight tabs along the bottom with the eight marks on them, a little shield and a little spear and so on. On each tab was a list of everybody who'd walked to Carrow Cross and asked properly. Their level. Their class, the real one, the long name the game had given them. Their guild. Who they'd said, when Wendy asked.

"Fifty-one walked here," said Tif, on the call. "On foot. Some of them from very far. One from the other side of the sea. And said a name."

"Fifty-one," said Ice.

"Out of fourteen hundred and twelve," said Tif. "And a poem."

"What did the poem one say? At the gate."

"He didn't come," said Tif. "Poets don't walk."

On Friday morning Kei put up the list.

He put it on the board outside the Hall, by the south door, where the Hall put notices. People had been waiting on the steps since the first dawn to see it. Suzu was there, because Ice had asked him to be there, though he hadn't said why. Ice wasn't. Ice was at the far end of the north tunnel under the town, killing Delvers, alone, the way he did most mornings. He'd said he didn't need to see it.

Kei pinned it up with four nails. Then he stepped back and let people read it, and came and stood by Suzu at the side.

It was very short.

*Shield.* *Long blade.* *Two blades.* *Knife.* *Spear.* *Bow — Kestrel (done).* *Staff.* *Hand.*

And beside each one, a name.

The crowd read it in silence. Then someone at the front said a name out loud, as if he couldn't believe it, and the crowd took it up. It went back through the round market and down the south road like a rumour.

Suzu read the names. Most of them he didn't know. A Frenchwoman with knives. A spearman from a university club in Seoul. A woman from Poland who made fire. A man from Crimson Ledger with a big sword. That one made the crowd boo, cheerfully, the way you'd boo a villain at a pantomime. A girl from Osaka with two blades, whose name Suzu had heard on the forums. A woman from Manila with a little light in her hand who'd written, under *who'd stand next to you*, *Everyone I've ever healed*.

And by the shield, the last name.

Suzu read it twice.

"Is that—" he said.

"Yes," said Kei.

"Does Kestrel know?"

"He asked her first," said Kei. "That's what he said, at the gate. Wendy asked him who'd stand next to him." Kei looked at the board. "He said *Kes. Always*. And then he said, *She said I could. I asked her. Properly*."

*Shield — Brann (VANTA).*

***

He found Kotori in the kitchen of the Hart that evening, behind the bar, making tea. She was always making tea. Suzu thought sometimes that the Hart's kitchen was the only place in the game she could stand still.

"You're not coming to watch," said Suzu.

It wasn't a question. Kotori didn't pretend it was.

"No," she said. She poured hot water over the leaves and watched them open. "I'll make tea. For after. He'll want something after." She put the lid on the pot. "He always wants something after."

"You don't like it."

Kotori didn't answer for a while. She was very careful with the pot.

"I don't like watching people hit each other," she said. "For fun. I know it's a game. I know nobody's hurt." She put the pot on the tray. "I watched the Kestrel one on his stream from here. On my own. I couldn't watch it at the ring. I kept thinking, *what if he's not all right after*."

"He wasn't," said Suzu.

Kotori looked at him.

"His hand," said Suzu. "After. When he sat down. It shook."

She'd seen it. He could tell she'd seen it. She'd been the one who'd asked him, on the bench at the mamak at three in the morning, *did you like it*.

"He said he used to," said Kotori.

"I know."

"Used to," said Kotori. She put two cups on the tray, then a third, then looked at it and took the third off again. "I'll make tea," she said. "That's what I can do. He can do his thing. I'll do mine."

***

Ice came in from the tunnel at the third dawn, at midnight, with mud to the knee and three Delver claws in his pack. He'd gone up most of a level. It was 39 and three-quarters now, and he looked tired in the way he only ever looked when he'd done something right for a long time.

Suzu was waiting on the steps of the Hall, with the list.

He'd copied it out on a bit of paper, because he'd thought Ice might want to see it without the crowd. Ice sat down beside him on the cold step and read it under the lamp. He read every name, slowly, all seven.

When he got to the last one, he stopped.

"Brann," he said.

"He asked Kestrel first."

"Of course he did." Ice looked at the name for a long time. "He said a tank can't catch a bow. On the floor at Elmcross. Before."

"I know. Everybody heard."

"He was wrong," said Ice. "And he knows he was wrong. And now he wants to find out how." He folded the paper up very small and put it in his pocket. He didn't sound unhappy about it. He sounded, if anything, interested. The way he sounded about a door nobody had opened. "That's properly, too."

They sat on the step for a while.

The round market was empty for once. Wendy had shut the gate at the second dusk and gone to bed. The fountain went on running in the first grey of the dawn. Somewhere up in the eye, two hundred and twelve steps up, Higu's lamp was on.

"Suzu," said Ice.

"Mm."

"You said I wanted to."

"On Tuesday."

"In the back room." Ice was looking at the fountain. "I didn't answer."

"No."

Ice thought about it for a long time. Suzu let him. You let Ice think. If you hurried him, he'd say something true but small, to make you stop. If you let him think, he'd say the whole thing.

"A bit," said Ice. "I want to a bit."

He said it the way you'd admit to something you'd been told not to eat.

Suzu nodded.

"Then that's properly too," he said.

Ice laughed. Very quietly, at the fountain. Then he stood up and stretched and went into the Hall, and through to the banner room, and sat in his chair, the first of the nine, and was asleep before his twelve hours were up. Kotori came in from the Hart with a tray and two cups and put one down in front of him anyway, steaming, for when he woke up. Then she sat and drank the other one in Thea's six, by the window, by herself, where she could see the door.

Suzu stayed on the steps a long time. He read the forum one more time on the window, with the egg's little clock in the corner.

*FIGHT ME (ICE)* had stopped. Somebody had locked it. At the bottom was one last post, from a moderator. It said, in capitals: *HE SAID SEVEN. GO HOME.*

Under it, someone had written a poem.
