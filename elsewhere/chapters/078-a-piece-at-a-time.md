Hyvred read the news on Friday morning, on the edge of her bed, before she'd even put her feet on the floor. It took her eleven minutes, because it was long, and because she read the way she read everything: a piece at a time.

The phone close to her face. The small clear circle in the middle of everything, sliding along the words. Headline first. Then the first line. Then the picture, which she had to move across slowly, a corner at a time, to understand: a woman sitting in a chair in a white room, wearing a pair of plain dark glasses with something small and black on the bridge of them, and crying.

*Cradle technology restores sight in first patients.*

She read the headline four times.

Then she read the rest of it, a piece at a time, with her thumb very still on the glass.

***

It was the same thing. That was what she understood first, sitting on the edge of her bed. It was the thing she'd understood on the first day in the game, in a little wooden room with rain on the window, when she'd looked up and seen the ceiling. All four corners of it, at once, and her own hand at the edge. She'd read a thread about it afterwards, from a doctor, careful and kind. *The Cradle doesn't use your eyes. It sends you the seeing.*

Somebody had taken that out of the egg.

A hospital in Singapore had done it, with the company that made the Cradles. The article had long words in it and she read every one. It was a small thing behind the ear, and a camera on a pair of glasses. The camera did the looking. The thing behind the ear did what the Cradle did every time she lay down in it: it went past the eyes altogether, and sent the seeing straight in. Eleven patients so far, in a trial. Retinas that had stopped working, nerves that had stopped working, tunnels. Eleven people who'd sat in a white room with the glasses on and seen a whole room for the first time in years. Or ever.

The woman in the picture had seen her daughter's face. All of it, the article said. At once.

Hyvred lowered the phone and sat very still on the edge of the bed.

She didn't let herself think the next thing yet. She made herself read to the end first. That was how you did it. You read to the end before you let yourself feel anything, because the end was where they put the parts that mattered.

The end said: *The procedure will be offered more widely later this year. Initial costs are expected to start at around US$10,000 per patient, not including assessment. It is not yet covered by insurance. A waiting list has opened.*

Ten thousand.

She read it again. She read the word *possibly*, two lines up, which she'd skipped the first time. *Patients with certain conditions may possibly benefit.* She read *certain conditions* three times, and *may*, and *possibly*.

Then she put the phone face down on the bed beside her, and looked at her room. The room she knew by heart, the way you'd know a room in the dark. She'd learned where everything was so that she didn't have to look for it, because looking took so long.

She looked at it now. A piece at a time. The wardrobe. The desk. The egg in the corner, white and quiet, with the lid down.

She thought: *ten thousand dollars*.

And then she let herself think the other thing, the one she'd been holding off for eleven minutes. Very carefully, the way you'd pick up something that might break.

*Possibly.*

***

She rang Bee at nine, which was noon in Australia.

She didn't say anything for a while when Bee picked up. Bee didn't either. That was the thing about Bee. She knew when to wait.

"Hyv," said Bee eventually.

"Did you see it?"

"I saw it." A pause. Bee's voice went very careful. "Somebody sent it to me at six this morning. And then four more people. And then I read it eleven times."

"I read it once," said Hyvred. "It took eleven minutes."

Bee didn't laugh. Hyvred heard her not laughing.

"How much," said Bee.

"Ten thousand. At least. Not counting the tests." Hyvred looked at the egg in the corner. "Not on insurance. Not yet. There's a waiting list."

"Are you on it?"

"I put my name on it at half past eight," said Hyvred. "Before I rang you. I didn't even finish reading the form properly. I just put my name on it."

There was a long silence on the phone.

"Okay," said Bee. Practical. Bee was always practical when she didn't know what to say. "Okay. Then we save."

"*I* save."

"Hyv—"

"I save," said Hyvred. "Bee. Don't. I know what you're going to say. Don't say it."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say you'd put some in."

Bee didn't say anything, which meant yes.

"I don't want to owe anybody," said Hyvred. She said it quietly, to the egg in the corner. "Not you. Not anybody. I don't like owing people things. It sits on me." She stopped. "If I'm going to see, I want it to be because I did it. A piece at a time. The way I read."

Bee was quiet for a long time.

"Okay," she said at last. "Then I'll just be really annoying about it every day. Like Newish."

Hyvred laughed, despite herself. It came out wet.

"Okay," she said.

***

She did the sum before she went into the game. She had to. It was the kind of thing she had to do on paper, or it would sit in her head all day and get bigger.

She had her stream now. Nineteen thousand people followed her, since the longest night; three or four thousand came most evenings, which was more than she'd ever imagined. Some of them subscribed. She had what she earned in the game: she sold arrows. She'd taken Woodcutting and Fletching on the first day, because she wanted to look at things a long way away, and you needed arrows for that. Good arrows, straight ones, that flew true in a crosswind. People bought them on the Exchange because they were the archer from the mill roof's arrows. She had a little in the bank from before.

She added it up. She did it twice, sliding the circle down the column on her phone, the way she'd done every sum of her life.

It wasn't ten thousand. It wasn't anywhere near.

She looked at the gap for a while.

Then she opened a new note on her phone, and called it nothing, and wrote at the top of it, very simply:

*10,000.*

And under it, the first line.

***

In the game, she climbed the dome.

Ama Corrie had given her the key to the stair. Not the big iron one for the eye, that stayed on the ring. A smaller one, for the door behind the east door. *You've got the eyes for it*, the old woman had said, *and the knees*. Two hundred and twelve steps up inside the wall of the Hall of Roads, round and round in the dark, to the narrow ledge that ran round the open eye at the top of the dome.

From up there she could see all seven roads.

Not one at a time. Not a piece at a time. All of them, at once, without turning her head. That was the thing. The round town in its rings, and the round market, and the seven roads going away from it across seven kinds of country. Into the hills and the moors and the forests and the plain, all the way to the edge of the world, all at the same time.

It was the seventeenth day of the thirty, in the month Nightjar had promised would be the best of their lives.

Kei had said it the first night he asked her, on a call at two in the morning. *Somebody should be able to see all seven at once.* She'd laughed and said *you want my eyes*, and he'd said *I want you*. So when she got to Carrow Cross, she'd climbed the stairs. Every evening now, for the whole of her twelve hours if she could, she stood on the ledge round the eye of the dome. She watched the seven roads for dark coats with little birds on the collar, and called them in.

"North-east road," she said, calmly, into the call. "Two miles out. Three of them, behind the wall by the ruined barn. Waiting." A pause. "There's nobody on that road today. They'll get bored."

"Thanks, Hyv," said Kei.

"South road's clear. West road's clear. Moor road, a fox. It's looking at me." Another pause. "Tobi's at the milestone on the south road, practising. He's waving at me."

"He can't see you," said Gigi.

"He's waving at the dome. He knows I'm on it." Hyvred waved back, though nobody could see it. "He waves every evening."

She stood on the ledge and watched the world. All of it, edge to edge.

And under the watching, quiet, all day, the other thing. A woman in a white room, in dark glasses, seeing her daughter's face. All of it. At once.

*Possibly.*

***

She sold her first batch of arrows at nine that night, from the ledge, through the Exchange window.

Forty arrows. Straight ones, good in a crosswind, fletched with grey goose feather she'd bought from a woman in Brackenford. Every one of them made by hand on the dyers' bridge in the evenings, while Bee drew. She put them up at a fair price, not a high one. She didn't want anyone to buy them because they felt sorry for her. Nobody knew. Nobody was going to know.

They sold in four minutes.

Thirty-eight dollars and fifty cents, after the Exchange took its ten per cent.

She looked at it for a long time, standing on the ledge round the eye of the dome, with all seven roads going away into the dark below her, and her friends walking on four of them.

Then she took out her phone in the air, and opened the note, and wrote the first line under the number.

*10,000*
*− 38.50*

It looked very small.
