Rachael didn't do close. Everybody who'd ever known her knew that.

She'd spent twenty-six years keeping things out. Crowds, coughs, other people's colds, other people's hands. She'd learned the list before she'd learned to read. And somewhere along the way, keeping things out had stopped being only about germs. It had become about everything. People stood where she put them, at the distance she chose, and the ones who loved her learned the distance and kept to it without being asked. Gabe was the best at it of anyone she'd ever met. Two paces. Never one and three-quarters.

So when Declan stepped off the end of the north walk into the reeds in the dark, and said *stay on the tussocks, follow me*, she followed at a distance. And when the reeds opened up and she saw where they were going, she stopped at the edge of it and didn't go in.

***

It was a ring of stones.

Nine of them, grey, waist-high, standing in a circle in the middle of the old fen with the reeds all round. Inside the circle there was a pool. Clear water, still, with steam coming off it very faintly in the cold night air. Round the feet of the stones, in the wet grass, there were flowers. Small, closed, pale.

Eight of the stones had heads carved on top, birds' heads, herons, worn soft by however many years. They faced inward. Toward the pool.

The ninth faced out.

The five of them walked straight in, the way you'd walk into your own kitchen. Kotori sat down on the stone lip of the pool and took her boots off and put her feet in the water. Deston lay down on his back on the grass by the third stone with the book under his head. Sheng Yang leaned on the fifth. Suzu sat by the ninth, the one facing out, with his empty jar beside him.

Gabe went in. Dennis went in, looking round at everything with the face he made at things he was pricing. Calvin went in at a walk. Rachael had never seen Calvin go anywhere at a walk.

Declan stood just inside the ring, by the first stone, and looked back at her.

[ The Heron-Stones
Nothing comes inside. ]

She read it twice.

*Nothing comes inside.*

"Not the Unlit," said Declan. He said it quietly. He didn't come any closer. He just stood where he was, at the distance she'd stopped at. "Not the cold. Not the dark. Not anything that comes up out of the fen at night. I found it the first night, by accident. I've slept here most nights since November." He looked round the ring. "Nothing's ever come in. Not once."

Rachael stood at the edge of the reeds.

She'd been careful for twenty-six years. She'd kept everything out herself, every day, with lists and distances and saying no. It had been her job, the whole of her life.

She'd never once been anywhere that kept things out *for* her.

She went in.

She stood just inside the ring, by the first stone, a little way from Declan, and felt it. Nothing changed. The air was the same and the night was the same and the fen was the same. But something had shut. Some door, somewhere, that she'd been holding shut with her whole body all her life, was being held shut by something else.

She sat down on the grass where she was, quite suddenly, because her legs had decided to.

Nobody said anything. Nobody came over. Gabe, on the far side of the pool, looked at her across the water, once, and then looked away again. He didn't move. Two paces would have been too close, right then. He knew. Gabe always knew.

***

Then the flowers opened.

All at once, round the feet of the stones, in the wet grass. Small pale bells, one after another, opening in the dark like lamps being lit, pale blue, glowing very faintly. All the way round the ring until the pool was circled with them.

"Moonbells," said Declan.

Rachael looked at them. Then at him.

"Brackenford," she said. "The draught. I asked you what was in it."

"You did."

"You told me everything. Every herb. How much, how long, what goes wrong if you rush it." She looked at the flowers. "You didn't tell me where."

"No," said Declan.

He crouched down by the nearest stone, not close to her, and touched one of the pale bells with one finger. It swung a little.

"One drop," he said. "Of what these make, if you do it right. Sedge's way. One drop in a draught and it goes up a whole grade. Fair to Good. Good to Fine." He looked at the flower. "That's the whole of it. That's why mine are better. Not me. These."

Rachael looked at the moonbells. Then at Declan, crouched by a stone in the dark, telling her the one thing in the game nobody else knew. The thing that made him money, that kept his draughts at the top of every market. The thing people had been asking for in his chat for six months.

"You could sell this," she said. "The *where*. You could sell it for—"

"I sell the draughts," said Declan. "Not this."

"Then why are you telling *me*?"

He looked at her. He didn't seem to find it a hard question.

"You asked," he said.

Rachael sat on the grass inside a ring of stones and didn't say anything for a long time.

She'd asked him something once, years ago. Something she'd never asked anyone else, because she'd been too embarrassed to. She didn't think about it much now, but she'd never forgotten it. And he'd answered. Properly, all the way through, as if it had been a good question, as if she hadn't been stupid to ask. He'd been the only person in her life she'd ever been able to ask anything at all.

She'd asked him about a draught in a square in Brackenford, a month and a half ago. He'd told her all of it then except the one thing he didn't tell anyone. Now he was telling her that too, in the dark, because she'd asked, and because she was here.

[ Herbalism · Moonbell (Rare) · "One drop." ]

"You're an idiot," said Rachael. Her voice wasn't working very well.

"I know," said Declan.

"You're giving away the one thing—"

"I'm not giving it away," he said. "It's yours too now. You're in the company." He stood up. "Brew it angry. It works better angry. I think."

Rachael laughed. It surprised her. It came out of her like something breaking, the way Suzu had laughed in a hospital, and she put her hand over her mouth, and didn't stop.

***

Dennis was standing at the edge of the pool with his notebook open.

He'd been standing there for a while. Rachael had watched him, out of the corner of her eye, the way she watched everything. He'd been reading the first page. He'd been reading it every night since April. Then he reached into his coat and took out a small leather bag, heavy, the kind that clinked.

He walked round the pool to where Declan was, and put the bag down on the stone lip of the pool in front of him.

"One hundred and fifty gold," said Dennis.

Declan looked at the bag.

"It isn't owed," he said.

"It isn't *now*," said Dennis.

He took out his pen. A cheap one, from the stall in Mistbath, nothing like Curse's. He drew a line through the first line on the first page of the notebook, very neatly, from one side to the other. *Owed to Declan: 150 gold.* Then, underneath the small line that said *He says it isn't*, he wrote a new one, in the same neat hand.

*Paid. He still says it isn't.*

He turned the notebook round and showed it to Declan.

Declan read it. Rachael watched his face do something she'd seen it do perhaps three times in all the years she'd known him.

"I said I would," said Dennis. "In April. On the steps." He closed the notebook. "I don't say things I'm not going to do, darling. It's the only thing about me that isn't for sale."

Declan looked at the bag on the stone for a long moment. Then he picked it up and held it out. Not to Dennis. To the middle of the ring, to all of them.

"Into the purse," said Declan. "The company's. You keep it."

Dennis stared at him.

"That's a *donation*," said Dennis. "You can't— that's a *donation*, Declan, I just *paid* you—"

"You paid me," said Declan. "It's mine. Now I'm putting it in the purse. It's a purse."

Dennis opened his mouth, and shut it. He looked at the bag in Declan's hand. At the notebook in his own. Rachael watched him do the sum, and get the answer, and not like the answer, and decide to like it anyway.

He took the bag.

"Fine," said Dennis. "*Fine*. But it goes in the book as *yours*." He opened the notebook to a new page and wrote at the top of it, very neatly: *THE PURSE. From Declan: 150 gold. (He says it isn't.)*

"You're writing *he says it isn't* again."

"I'm going to write it every time," said Dennis, "until you stop saying it."

***

Calvin sat on the edge of the pool with his feet in the water, very still. Rachael had never seen him sit still. She thought he might not have known he could.

"This is it," said Calvin. "This is the thing you didn't tell anyone."

"Yes," said Declan.

"Six months," said Calvin. "Thirty thousand people a night. A napkin on the café wall." He looked round the ring at the stones and the moonbells and the pool, and the ninth stone facing out into the dark. "And it's a pond."

"It's a pond," Declan agreed.

Calvin was quiet for a while.

"Thanks for telling me," he said.

"You asked."

Then Declan told them the rest. About the ninth stone, the one facing out, and a line in an old guild's book with half of it washed away. And an old woman in a fen who'd known the other half by heart her whole life, because her mother had said it every night at bedtime, and blown out the lamp.

"*Set one to watch*," said Declan, "*so the rest may sleep*."

***

They slept there. All nine of them, round the pool, in the warm, with the moonbells open.

Rachael lay down on the grass by the first stone. It wasn't close to anyone. Then, after a while, she got up and moved. Not far. Just round the pool a little way, to where Gabe was lying on his back by the fourth stone, with his hammer beside him and his shield under his head.

She lay down two paces from him.

In a ring of stones where nothing came inside, where there was nothing to stand in front of and nothing to stand in for, two paces didn't mean anything. There was nothing for his Stand In to reach her from. She knew that. Gabe knew that.

She lay two paces from him anyway.

Gabe didn't say anything. He didn't move. He turned his head very slightly toward her in the dark and then turned it back, and she saw him smile at the stars over the old fen, just at the corners.

Across the pool, by the ninth stone, Declan lay with his back to the heron that faced out, and his shield beside him, not on his arm.

Rachael closed her eyes.

For the first time in twenty-six years, she didn't have to keep anything out. Something else was doing it. She let it.
