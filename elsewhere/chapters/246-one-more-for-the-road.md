Mother Dipp had said *one more for the road*, and Chernn had made two hundred and eleven.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to make nine. One for each of them, the nine who were going north. Tall and white and plain, the way Mother Dipp had made the one Suzu carried, the best one she'd ever made. *So you can see your feet without using the lamp.*

Then she'd done the sum.

Three weeks in the nothing, Isa said. Maybe more. You walked by a candle and a rope. A candle burned eight hours, nine if it was a good one, and hers were good ones now. Eighty-one per cent and straight, as of February. Nine people. Three weeks. Twelve hours a night in the egg, every night.

She'd sat in the corner of the Hart's kitchen with a slate and done it three times, and got two hundred and something every time. And put the slate down. And started dipping.

***

She'd written to Mother Dipp in September, after Suzu stood up. A letter-parcel, with a candle in it, so Mother Dipp would know she was still straight.

*Nine of them are going north. Into the dark past the edge. Three weeks. They need candles to see their feet. I've done the sum. It's two hundred. I can't do two hundred on my own by November.*

The parcel came back in a week. Heavier than any parcel Mother Dipp had ever sent. It took two people to carry it in from the south gate; Kite and Rafi's old friend Bram, who didn't say anything, and put it down on the Hart's kitchen table, and went away.

Inside: wicks. Hundreds. The fine ones, bound in bundles of forty with thread. And a block of Tallow Green's wax, the thin sweet kind, wrapped in cloth. And a note.

Chernn read it at the kitchen table and laughed so much that Kotori had to put the kettle down.

***

So she dipped. Every morning, before the first dawn, in the corner of the Hart's kitchen, with a frame of forty wicks Joss had made her and a pot on the back of the range beside Kotori's kettles. Down, and up. Down, and up. Let it cool.

Like breathing.

The hundred from Tallow Green came on the twentieth of October, in four crates. Kite carried them in. Every candle was wrapped in a twist of paper. And on every twist, in a different hand, a name. *Bramble. Old Tess. Wick's girl. The tallow man. Pip.* The whole green. Every window. Somebody in every cottage round the pond had dipped one candle for nine people they'd never met, walking north into the dark.

Chernn unwrapped them one by one on the kitchen table and read every name.

Kotori, at the kettles, read them over her shoulder.

"They don't know them," said Kotori.

"No."

"They've never met any of them."

"No," said Chernn. "They know there's somebody on a road, in the dark. That's enough. In Tallow Green." She held up a candle, the one marked *Pip*, a bit lumpy, honest. "*And one more for the road.* That's the whole line. They've been saying it for hundreds of years. It doesn't say *for somebody you know*."

***

Deston came to the kitchen on the twenty-second, at the first dawn. He did that sometimes now, before the long table. He'd stand in the doorway with his book and watch her dip, and not say anything. Which for Deston was a lot.

This time he came in and sat down on Kotori's stool.

Chernn knew. She'd known for a week. She could read his face; she'd always been able to. It had been doing the thing it did when he was about to say something he'd practised. Since the Sunday Milli stood up at the long table and said *we've picked a day*.

She went on dipping. Down, and up.

"Hana and Milli picked a day," said Deston.

"I know. I was there."

"On the bench. On the roof. In four minutes."

"I know," said Chernn.

Deston was quiet for a long time. She dipped. Down, and up. Forty little white fingers on the frame.

"It's catching," said Deston. "Milli said. Ama Corrie said it first. About the broom."

Chernn put the frame down.

She turned round. He was sitting on Kotori's stool with his book on his knee and his face red to the ears. Six foot whatever of him, in the game and out of it, on a stool meant for somebody small. The man who'd walked five days to a candle village to tell her she was slow. And dipped forty-three candles in this kitchen at night with big hands until one stood up. And knelt on a green by a pond with every window watching.

"Do you want to?" said Chernn.

He looked up.

"Pick a day," said Chernn. "Do you want to?"

"*Yes*," said Deston. Much too fast. And then, because he'd practised something, and it was coming out anyway: "I was going to ask you on the bench. On the roof. At the eye. Isa says it's where people go to say something they mean—"

"Then let's go up," said Chernn.

***

They went up the two hundred and twelve at the first dawn, the two of them. Ama Corrie said *mind the third step* and Chernn said *we know*. And Ama Corrie looked at her over her spectacles and said *everybody knows, nobody minds it*.

The bench was on the south side of the eye, in the wind. Pale ash, seven rings, a scar, *Somewhere* in blue on the back. A cat's-paw on the left arm. Somebody had been up since Hana and Milli and left a candle on the parapet in front of it, burnt down to a stub. One of hers. She'd know her own anywhere.

They sat on it.

She looked out. She'd never been up. The whole round town below, and the seven roads, and the White Hills on the edge of the world. Somewhere past them, Tallow Green. The north road, with its ninety-four lamps leaning.

"Out there," said Deston. "Not in here. In here's where we met."

"In here's where you asked."

"In here's where I asked," he agreed. "Out there's where— out there's the day." He was looking at the view, very hard, red to the ears. "I don't want to pick it on my own. I want to pick it with you. Here. Where you can see everything."

"So you know it's true," said Chernn.

He looked at her.

"Isa says," said Chernn. "That's the rest of the line. *Because you can see everything from there. So you know it's true.*"

They picked a day.

It took less than four minutes. Chernn would tell Hana afterward, on the stair, and Hana would scream. They'd both known for a while. A month, and a day, and a year. Out there. Not in here. And when they'd said it, both of them, on the bench, at the same time, Deston put his face in his hands and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

She didn't write it down. It wasn't for writing down. It was theirs.

She took his hand.

***

She wrote to Mother Dipp that night. One line, and a candle.

***

Isa taught Suzu the dusk-words on the last day of October.

Chernn was there. She hadn't meant to be. She'd gone out onto the north steps at the second dusk with a box of forty candles for Isa. Isa liked them lit along the table in front of her chair at night, and she'd taken to having them on the north steps too, in the evenings, in a row on the stone. And Suzu was there, on the step below Isa's chair, with the black iron case on his back in Milli's frame, because he wore it now, all the time, to get used to it.

Isa was talking. Very quietly. Chernn stopped at the north door with the box.

"You say it at dusk," said Isa. "Every dusk. On the hill. When the light goes. Before it's dark." She was looking up the road. "My mother said it. Her mother. All of them. Three hundred years. Every dusk."

"What is it?" said Suzu.

"It's not anything," said Isa. "It's not a spell. It's not in the old book. It's just what you say. So it knows you're there."

"Tell me."

Isa looked down at him for a long time. Then up the road again. The ninety-four lamps, going away north over the moor in the dusk. Leaning.

"*Here's the middle*," said Isa Corrie. "*Not yet.*"

The north steps were very quiet.

"That's all?" said Suzu.

"That's all," said Isa. "*Here's the middle.* So it knows where it is. *Not yet.* So it knows it's not time." She folded her root hands. "Every dusk. And then you sleep. And somebody watches." She paused. "*Set one to watch, so the rest may sleep.* The old guild left that in every town going north. That's the other half."

Suzu didn't say anything for a long time.

"Say it," said Isa.

He looked up the road. At the far lamps, the ones he couldn't see leaning, and knew were.

"*Here's the middle*," said Suzu. Very quietly. To the north. "*Not yet.*"

Up the north road, a long way off, the far lamps went on leaning.

But Chernn, at the north door with forty candles in a box, thought for a moment that the nearest ones stood up a little.

***

She lit Isa's candles along the step. Forty, in a row. She didn't say anything. Isa watched her do it, the way she watched everything, and said *hm* when the last one caught.

"Honest," said Isa.

"And straight."

"Still," said Isa. She looked at the box. "How many for the road?"

"Two hundred and eleven," said Chernn. "A hundred from the green. A hundred and eleven from me."

Isa looked at her for a long time.

"My mother carried one," she said. "For the road. When she walked to the edge to look. So she could see her feet without the lamp." She was quiet. "One. For four days. She walked very slowly."

"They'll walk slowly too."

"Two hundred and eleven," said Isa, and something happened to her face. "Two hundred and eleven candles, going north. That's never happened either." She looked at the box. "The dark won't know what to make of it."

"It won't see them," said Chernn. "They're small. They'll be on a rope. In the nothing. Isa says—" She stopped. "*You* say. Nothing out there can see a candle unless it's looking right at it. And it'll be looking for the jar. And the jar's in a box."

"So it'll see two hundred and eleven little lights going north," said Isa, "and no middle." She almost smiled. "It'll think it's a road."

***

The rope came on the first of November. From Masmark.

A letter-parcel, enormous, carried in from the south gate by Kite and Bram and Joy together. Wrapped in sailcloth, the old kind, with *NETLOFT* stencilled on it. And a letter on top, in a hand Chernn had seen on the long table in a hundred letters to Ice.

[ Letter · from Rafi (Masmark) → Ice
Boss.
The rope man says you'll need rope.
He says you bought some in July and strung it in the Undercut and it's still there.
He says the boats use it every morning.
He says you'll need more. For the dark.
He made it. A hundred fathoms. By hand. His grandson helped.
He says don't pay him. He says you already did.
I'm a door. Three more came this week. One said "the fish man".
— Rafi ]

Ice read it at the long table, out loud, twice. Then he unwrapped the sailcloth.

Rope. Straw-coloured, hand-twisted, thick. Coiled. A great deal of it, a hundred fathoms, enough to tie nine people together in a line in the dark and have some over. The same rope he'd bought in July from an old man who hadn't sold any since February. And strung along a channel under a harbour so fishing boats could find their way in the dark.

He looked at it for a long time. He didn't say anything. Chernn watched his face do the thing.

Then Hyvred, beside him, put her hand on the coil.

"*You already did*," she said.

"I bought some rope."

"You bought some rope," agreed Hyvred. "When nobody had. From a man who was going to sell." She looked at him. "That's paying."

***

She gave them out at the long table on the last evening. The nine.

She hadn't planned to make anything of it. She'd meant to put the crates by the north door and let them take what they needed. But Kotori said *no*, very firmly, at the kettles, which Kotori never said. *Give them one each. In their hands. With a name on.* So Chernn stood at the long table at the second dusk with the four crates from Tallow Green open on the floor beside her. And held out a candle to each of them, one at a time. And read the name on the twist of paper out loud.

Hako first, because he was nearest. *Old Tess.* He took it and held it up to the window and said, in the voice, *a fine specimen, from the green's most senior dipper*. Then, in his own voice, *thank you*, very quietly, and put it inside his coat.

Xola. *The tallow man.* She laughed. Slep, beside her, took *Wick's girl*, and looked at it for a long time, and said *I'll bring it back*. Chernn said *it'll be a stub*. And he said *I'll bring the stub back*.

Gigi. *Bramble.* Gigi went very still, holding it. Bramble was a hedge word. Chernn hadn't known; she hadn't chosen it. It had just been the next one in the crate. And two chairs down Thea took hers, *Hollin*, and looked at Gigi holding *Bramble*. And neither of them said anything, and both of them put the candles in the same pocket, on the left.

Kotori. Chernn had kept one back for her. *Mother Dipp*, in Mother Dipp's own hand, the letters big and floury. Kotori held it and her face went pink and she said *thank you* three times very fast and nothing else.

Suzu didn't take one. He had the best one already, in his pack, the tall white one from the green that Chernn had given him in April at the Hart. He just looked at the crates and said *two hundred and eleven* very quietly, the way you'd say a number you meant to remember.

Hyvred. Chernn held out the next one without looking, and then looked, and stopped.

*Pip.*

The lumpy one. Honest. The one she'd held up in the kitchen in October and said *it doesn't say for somebody you know*.

Hyvred took it. She turned it over in her fingers. She looked at the lump near the top, where somebody small had lifted it too fast.

"Who's Pip?" she said.

"I don't know," said Chernn. "Somebody in a cottage on the green. Somebody small, I think. It's a first try." She looked at it. "Forty-one per cent, if I had to guess. I made one just like it. My first day."

Hyvred looked at it for a long time.

"I'll light this one first," she said. "On the first night past the edge. When there's nothing." She put it in her pocket. "Somebody small should go first."

And Ice, last. In the first of the nine, with his feet on the rung.

Chernn held out the next candle. *Sedge*, said the twist of paper, in a hand she didn't know.

Ice went completely still.

"There's a Sedge in Tallow Green?" he said.

"I don't know," said Chernn. "It's just the name on it." She looked at it. "It's a common name. In the wet places. Mother Dipp says." She held it out. "It's a reed."

He took it.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He held a candle with a fen-woman's name on it in the first of the nine, at the long table. And Chernn, who could read Deston's face and was learning everybody else's, watched his do a great deal very quietly.

"I'll bring it back," he said.

"It'll be a stub."

"I'll bring the stub back," said Ice. "To Fernwick. One day." He put it inside his coat, flat, over his chest. "Somebody there should have it."

***

That night, the last night before they went, Chernn put a candle in the north door.

Not on the step. In the door. In the little window at the top of it, the one that let the light through from the Hall onto the north steps, that nobody ever looked at. It had a sill, very narrow. She'd measured it with her thumb on Sunday.

She put a candle on it. One of Mother Dipp's. Not the best one; Suzu had that, in his pack, for the hill. The second best. Tall, white, plain, straight. She lit it with a spill from Kotori's range and stood back.

The little window in the north door, lit. For the first time. Looking up the north road.

Deston came and stood beside her. He didn't say anything. He looked at the candle in the window for a long time.

"*A candle in every window*," he said at last.

"*And one more for the road*," said Chernn.

She looked at it. A small yellow flame in a small window in a door that stuck, at the north end of a round town. Facing up a road of ninety-four lamps, leaning, toward a dark nine people were walking into in the morning.

"It stays lit," she said. "Till they're home."

"That's weeks."

"I'll change it," said Chernn. "Every morning. Before the first dawn. In the corner of the kitchen, I'll dip a new one, and bring it here, and change it." She looked at him. "Down, and up. Like breathing."

Behind them, in her chair by the north door, Isa Corrie was awake. With the jar gone from the table in front of her for the first time in ten months. It was on Suzu's back now, in black iron, under a black star. And forty of Chernn's candles burning in a row along the table where it had been.

She looked at the candle in the window of the north door for a long time.

"*Hm*," said Isa. "Leave it lit."
