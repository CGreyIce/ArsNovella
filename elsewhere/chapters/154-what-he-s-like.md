The swamp guy had been gone two days, and Hyvred was furious that she knew it was two days.

She shouldn't know. Nobody else seemed to. The Hall of Roads went on exactly the same without him. Ama Corrie at the counter, writing. People going down the hatch at the first dawn and coming back up at the second dusk, muddy. Jun Liang being loud. The new six learning where everything was. Tif and Kotori had taken over a whole corner of the cellar for tea. Xyro had fallen in the fountain twice more. Nobody stood at the counter at the first dawn and said *he's gone west* in a sad voice. Nobody looked at the first of the nine chairs at the end of the long table, the one with nobody in it, and noticed.

Hyvred noticed. She'd been noticing since Monday. And she was so angry about it she could hardly see straight. Which, for her, was saying something.

She sat on the second landing of the stair at the first dawn with her bow across her knees and her glasses pushed up in her hair. She watched the west road. She didn't need to watch it. Nobody was coming down it. He'd said a week, or ten days, and it had been two. She watched it anyway, because it was a road, and she watched roads now. Edge to edge. All of it at once. Because she could.

She took the note out of her pocket.

*ICE — 3,700.50 + one lesson*

She'd written it on the back of a page torn out of Bee's sketchbook, in the spring, after the swamp guy had paid for her surgery and told nobody. After she'd found out. After she'd walked across a square in a straight line with her fists at her sides and hit him twice. She'd crossed the number out and written it again three times since. Once for each piece she'd sent. *Piece one.* *Piece two.* *Piece three.* Thirty-nine fifty each, every time, exactly.

She looked at *+ one lesson* for a long time.

That was the one she couldn't send. You couldn't pay back a lesson in pieces. He'd borrowed her bow, in a city with no name, between the horns of something huge. He'd hit ninety-five per cent on his first ever shot in the game, with no form learned, just like that. And then he'd said, quite quietly, *I used to do archery. In real life. But I lost my passion for it.* And then he'd taught her. For about an hour. And she'd been so caught up in it that she hadn't even noticed when he'd stopped standing behind her and gone off to talk to Hana.

She'd stamped. Bee had laughed. It had been very humiliating.

She put the note back in her pocket.

*I lost my passion for it.*

She'd been thinking about that sentence for three months. She thought about it far more than was reasonable for a sentence somebody had said in passing about a hobby. She thought about it at night. She'd thought about it during the worst possible moments. In a hospital, going under, when a nurse told her to think about something nice, and her brain had very helpfully supplied a hand, on top of her head, between her horns.

*No*, she'd said, out loud, to the nurse. *Your heart rate's gone up*, the nurse had said.

That had been the worst moment of her life. She'd had some bad moments. That had been the worst.

***

The thing was, she didn't know anything about him.

That was what made her so angry. Not the hand. Well. Also the hand. But mostly that she didn't *know* anything. She'd been in the same guild as him for a month and in the same game for eight. She'd watched hundreds of hours of his stream, back when she could only watch it through a tube, a piece at a time. She'd been the eyes for him in the Seven Below, and called his name, which she'd never done before. She'd been hit on by the stupid feeling, the head thing, Bee called it, at every possible moment for three months. And she didn't know the first thing about him.

She didn't know why he didn't flinch. Everybody flinched. Even Gabe flinched, a bit, and Gabe was an Anvil. The swamp guy didn't. Something hit him at fifty per cent pain and he just stood there, and then told you where it had hit.

She didn't know why he kept his pain at fifty. Nobody kept it at fifty. *Pain tells you where you got hit*, he'd said in the Seven Below. Fine. But you could keep it at forty and it would still tell you. Why fifty? Why the most?

She didn't know why he said *okay*. Every time. She'd say *don't say good*, and he'd say *okay*. She'd say *I'm still hitting you*, and he'd say *okay*. She'd sent him piece three with *don't say thanks, don't say anything, I'm still hitting you*. On his stream he'd read it and sat in his chair at the end of the long table and said, very quietly, to nobody, *okay*.

She didn't know why he'd paid for her eyes and told nobody. She didn't know why he'd lost his passion for archery. She didn't know why he went to the far end of rooms. She didn't know anything about where he'd come from, or who he'd been before he was a man in a fen with a sack of silverleaf. Before he was in everybody's chat.

She sat on the stair with her bow and was furious, and didn't know who at.

***

She couldn't ask Bee.

She'd tried. On Monday night, in their room over the Hart. She'd said, as casually as she could, *what do you think he's like*. And Bee had looked up from her drawing and looked at her for a long, long moment. Then she'd put her hand over her mouth and started laughing, silently, with her whole body, until her wings shook.

"*What*," Hyvred had said.

"Nothing," Bee had said, through her hand. "Nothing. Nothing."

"*What*, Bee."

"I'm not saying anything," Bee had said. "I said I wouldn't. Somebody else could. Some day. Somebody louder." And she'd gone back to her drawing, still shaking. Hyvred had thrown a pillow at her and gone to sleep furious.

She couldn't ask Deston. She'd decided that in July, at the bottom of the Seven Below. He'd held all ten of them together with that link thing, Accord, so a hit was split ten ways. And at the very end, as it let go, she'd felt him feel something. Just for a second. Something of hers. And she'd seen his face change. And she'd gone pink to the horns and left, and she'd been avoiding him ever since. If Deston knew a thing, and she was fairly sure Deston knew a thing, she didn't want to hear him not say it.

She couldn't ask Suzu. Suzu didn't talk. Suzu would just look at her and say *okay*, and she'd find out where the swamp guy had got it from, and that would be worse.

She couldn't ask Gabe, because Gabe would tell him. Immediately. Loudly. In the Hall. *DEC. HYV WANTS TO KNOW WHAT YOU'RE LIKE.* She could hear it.

She couldn't ask Kei, because Kei would say *hm*, and then go away and think about it for a week, and then draw something on his map.

She couldn't ask Xav. Xav had known him nine years, but Xav was the kind of person who'd answer a question with a better question, and she didn't want a better question. She wanted an answer.

And she couldn't ask him. Obviously. He was in the Fell Country. And even if he weren't, she'd sooner swallow her own bow.

So on Wednesday, at the noon dusk, she did the only thing left. She went and asked the new ones.

***

She knew where they'd be. Everybody did. The new six had found a place on Spoke Street, a little cookshop with three tables outside under an awning. Xola had made friends with the woman who ran it on the first day, by asking to use the kitchen. Now Xola cooked there most noons, fish from the river, and Thea sat at the outside table and ate whatever Xola made and said it was good. It usually was.

They were there today. Just the two of them, at the outside table. Slep was at the gate with Wendy. PenguChi was in the Fell Country with the swamp guy. Hyvred tried very hard not to think about that. Tif and Xyro were in the cellar. Thea and Xola were alone, eating fish pies out of their hands.

Hyvred stood on the other side of Spoke Street for about five minutes. She watched them the way she watched roads. Edge to edge. Thea, calm, tired-looking, with a scratch-shadow still on her cheek from the south-west road. Xola, talking with her mouth full, waving the pie, gills going.

They'd known him before.

That was the thing. That was why she'd come to them and not anyone else. She'd heard them call him things. *Icey.* Xola had hit him at full speed in the Hall on the night he came up from the hatch and been knocked into Gabe. And Thea had said *hi*, across the floor, in a voice Hyvred had never heard anyone use to him. Not careful, the way people were careful with him. Not cheeky, the way Gabe was. Just *hi*. As if they'd said it to each other ten thousand times.

And PenguChi had said something on the stair on Monday, to Tif, that Hyvred had heard because she heard everything now. *Thea used to say it on stream. Years ago. "This is Ice. He's my best friend."*

Best friend.

Hyvred had sat on the stair after that for an hour, very still.

She crossed Spoke Street.

***

"Hi," said Hyvred.

Xola looked up with her mouth full. Thea looked up more slowly.

"Hi," said Thea. "Hyvred. Right?"

"Hyv." Hyvred stood at the end of their table with her bow on her back and her hands in fists at her sides. She'd had something prepared. She'd lost it crossing the street. "Can I ask you something?"

Thea looked at her for a moment. Then she moved her chair a bit, and pushed the third one out from under the table with her foot.

"Sit down," said Thea. "Xola, give her a pie."

"They're *my* pies," said Xola, and gave her a pie.

Hyvred sat. She held the pie. She didn't eat it. Her tail, which she couldn't do anything about, had wrapped itself round the leg of the chair.

"What's he like?" she said.

Xola swallowed.

"Who?" said Xola.

Hyvred looked at the pie.

"Him," she said.

There was a small pause.

"Icey?" said Xola.

"*Don't* call him that in front of me."

It came out much louder than she'd meant. Two people at the next stall looked round. Hyvred felt her face go hot right up to the roots of her horns. Xola's eyebrows went all the way up, and Thea put her hand on Xola's arm, very lightly, under the table, where Hyvred wasn't supposed to see it. Hyvred saw it. She saw everything.

"Sorry," said Hyvred. "Sorry. I just—sorry."

"It's fine," said Thea. "What do you want to know?"

"Everything," said Hyvred, before she could stop herself. Then: "No. Not everything. Just. What he's like." She turned the pie round in her hands. "You knew him. Before. Before the game. Before the swamp. You called him your best friend. On stream. PenguChi said." She looked up. "Nobody here knew him before. Not like that. Xav, a bit. But Xav just asks you questions back."

Thea and Xola looked at each other. It was a very short look. Hyvred caught all of it. It had a whole conversation in it, the way looks between people who'd known each other for years did.

"Before the game," said Thea slowly. "He was in everyone's chat."

***

They told her.

Not all at once, and not in order. Thea tried to tell it in order and Xola kept interrupting with bits from the middle. But between them they told it, over two fish pies and most of the noon dusk.

He'd been in everyone's chat. That was the first thing, and the biggest. He'd had his own channel, tiny, ten people. He'd streamed five or six times a month, games nobody watched. But that wasn't really where he'd been. He'd been in everyone else's. Every small streamer Thea and Xola knew, and some they didn't. He'd turn up. He'd raid people with eleven viewers, as if eleven was a lot, which for some of them it had been. He'd gift subs and not say anything about it. He'd say *hi* at three in the morning in a chat with four people in it, and stay. He'd be at every event and every charity stream and every silly community thing anyone ran. Nobody ever noticed him there until he'd gone.

"You'd only see him afterwards," said Xola. "In the list. You'd scroll back through your chat after a stream and go, oh, Ice was here. The whole time. He never said anything. He was just *here*."

"He raided Chi once," said Thea. "With eleven. She lasted forty minutes of his stream after and decided he was boring." She almost smiled. "She's in the Fell Country with him now."

"I *know*," said Hyvred, much too fast. Then: "Sorry."

Thea looked at her. Hyvred looked at her pie.

"Why doesn't he flinch?" said Hyvred.

It came out of her all at once, the thing she'd been carrying since the Seven Below. "In fights. Everybody flinches. Even Gabe. He doesn't. Something hits him at *fifty per cent pain* and he just *stands* there. Why fifty? Nobody keeps it at fifty. And why does he say *okay* to everything? And why does he—" She stopped. Her tail had wrapped round the chair leg so tightly it hurt. "Why does he always go to the far end of rooms?"

Thea didn't answer straight away.

"I don't know about the flinching," she said. "He never did. Not in anything. Not in games, before. Not in—" She stopped. "He just never did. As long as I've known him."

"He flinched once," said Xola.

Thea turned and looked at her.

"He did," said Xola. She'd put her pie down. Her gills had gone still. "You know he did."

And Thea's face did something Hyvred couldn't read at all. It wasn't sad, exactly. It was older than sad.

"Xo," said Thea.

"She asked," said Xola. "She's asking what he's like. That's what he's like." She looked at Hyvred. Something had changed in her voice. It wasn't teasing any more. "Do you want to know? Properly? It's not a nice story. It's not a bad one either. It's just a story."

Hyvred's hands had gone very tight on the pie.

"Yes," she said.

***

Xola told it. Thea let her, and sat beside her with her hand on Xola's arm, and put in a word now and then.

"The three of us were best friends," said Xola. "Me and Thea and Ice. For ages. Before any of this. We were in each other's chats every night. Calls. Games. All of it." She picked at the edge of the pie. "And he liked me first."

Hyvred stopped breathing.

Xola shrugged, a small tight shrug. "Nothing happened. Nothing ever formed. We just stayed friends. He never made it a thing. He never made *me* feel like it was a thing. He just—stayed my friend. Like it was easy."

"It wasn't easy," said Thea quietly.

"No," said Xola. "I know that now. I'm not sure I knew it then."

There was a silence at the little table on Spoke Street. Somewhere down the street a barrow went past, rattling.

"And then Thea," said Xola.

Thea looked down at the table.

"He liked me," said Thea. Very simply. "After. Later. And I didn't—I was—" She stopped. "I was in a thing. With someone. It was a bad thing. A long, dark thing. I didn't see much of anything, that year. I didn't see him."

Hyvred knew who. Everybody in the round town knew who by now, more or less. You couldn't live under the same dome as two people who'd held one gap in a hole full of burrows, and said *good thorn, good turn*, and not know. She didn't say it.

"And he stepped back," said Xola. "From both of us. To protect his own head. He never said that either. He just went quieter in our chats. And then he was mostly with his other friends. Suzu and that lot. And we didn't see him so much." She looked at Thea. "We thought he'd just drifted. People drift."

"I worked it out," said Thea. "Later. That he'd stepped back because he had to. Because he couldn't stand there and—" She stopped. "And he didn't even know. About the bad thing. He didn't know about any of that. Not till much later. He stepped back before he knew. He just knew he couldn't stay where he was. So he went somewhere else." She turned her cup round on the table. "And he stayed in every chat anyway. Mine. Xola's. All of them. Every night. Raids, gifts, *hi* at three in the morning. He never stopped turning up. He just turned up from further away."

Hyvred sat there with the pie in her hands.

"That's the flinch," said Xola. "The once. That's the only time I ever saw him flinch. He took a step back. One. And then he never took another one. He just stood where he'd stepped to. For years."

***

Hyvred didn't know what she felt.

She sat at the little table on Spoke Street and tried to look at it, the way she'd learned to look at everything now, edge to edge. And it didn't have edges. It was all middle. It was hot and miserable and tight, and it went right through her, and she didn't know its name.

He'd liked Xola.

He'd liked Thea.

He'd liked them, both of them, a long time ago, and stepped back from it, and stayed anyway. He'd stood at the far end of their lives for years like he stood at the far end of rooms. Turning up. Saying *hi*. Never saying anything else.

And it was none of her business. None. She'd asked what he was like and they'd told her, and it was a story about three people who'd been best friends. It had nothing to do with her. It had all happened years ago, before the game, before the swamp, before her eyes. And she sat there with her tail wrapped round a chair leg so hard it ached, and felt as if somebody had opened a door in her chest and let the cold in.

"Does he still—" she said.

She stopped.

She hadn't meant to say it. It had come out of the cold place, on its own, before she could catch it. Three words, and the start of a fourth. She stopped them dead and closed her mouth on the rest.

But it was too late. She'd said it out loud, at a table, in front of two people who'd known him for years. And one of them was Xola.

Xola had gone very still.

Hyvred watched her go still. Watched her eyes, which had been soft through the whole story, go narrow. Watched her look at Hyvred's face. And then, slowly, down. At the tail, wrapped round the chair leg so tight it had gone white at the tip. Then back up at her face, which Hyvred could feel was the colour of a brick.

Beside her, Thea had gone still too. Thea and Xola didn't look at each other this time. They didn't need to.

"Hyv," said Xola.

Very slowly. Very calmly. In a voice Hyvred had never heard her use. It was the voice of somebody who'd just found the last piece of a puzzle on the floor, under the table, where it had been the whole time.

"Why," said Xola, "do you want to know?"
