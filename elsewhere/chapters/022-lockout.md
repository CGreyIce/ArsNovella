"I saw," said Suzu.

Ice lay in the gel and looked at the ceiling fan going round and didn't say anything.

Suzu didn't say anything either. He stood beside the pod for a while with his hands in his pockets. Then he went and got his pillow off the folding chair, and put it on the floor between the two pods, and sat down on it, cross-legged, with his back against the side of his own egg. He looked at the wall. He was very good at waiting. He'd always been better at it than anyone Ice knew.

After a long time, Ice sat up.

His arm still ached. It wasn't real, and his arm didn't know that. His face was still wet. He wiped it with the heel of his hand and climbed out of the pod, carefully, on legs that had forgotten, and sat down on the cold white tiles across the gap from Suzu with his back against his own egg. There they were, the two of them, on the floor of a back room in a shoplot in Melaka Raya at a quarter to eight on a Wednesday night, between two humming white eggs, facing each other.

"Twenty-four hours," said Ice eventually.

"I know."

"I'll be twelfth by the time I get back in. Thirteenth. Something."

"I know."

"Xav said not the ford."

"I know," said Suzu, for the third time, very gently. "I was watching. I watch you when I'm mowing. I've got you in the corner." He tilted his head at nothing, at the edge of his own vision, where Ice's stream must have been sitting for a week. "He said not the ford, and you said not the ford, and then you went to the ford."

Ice laughed, not very steadily, and put his head back against the egg.

"Yeah," he said. "I did."

***

They went to the mamak on the corner at nine, because Suzu said he was hungry, and because Ice couldn't sit in the back room with the pods any more.

It was the kind of place Malaysia did better than anywhere: a corner shop with its whole front open to the street, plastic tables spilling out under the five-foot way and onto the pavement, bright white strip lights and a big television showing football nobody was watching. A man at a steel counter at the front was slapping roti dough out thin as paper and folding it, over and over, faster than you could follow. It smelled of ghee and curry and the sweet burnt smell of teh tarik being pulled from a height, one cup to the other, in a long brown rope.

They got a table at the edge, under the awning. Roti canai with dhal, two plates. Teh tarik, two. Suzu tore his roti carefully into neat squares before he ate any of it, the way he did everything.

"Okay," said Suzu. "Tell me."

"Tell you what?"

"Why you went." He dipped a square in the dhal. "Not what you said on stream. *I'm only looking.* Why you went."

Ice looked at his teh tarik for a while. The froth on top was slowly sinking.

He hadn't meant to tell him everything. He'd meant to say something easy, about the bonus and the level, and laugh it off, and eat his roti. But it was Suzu. He'd been talking to Suzu at three in the morning for years, in calls and in games and in a hundred places where you could say things because it was dark and nobody could see your face. And this was the same thing, just with a table and a television between them.

So he told him.

He told him about the stone. How it had felt on Sunday, seeing his own name in silver, *6 · Ice*, and what the number meant, really. A hundred and sixty people came to his stream now because he was the POV guy, the back-foot guy, the one on the stone. If he wasn't on the stone, he was just a man in a swamp again, with ten viewers. That had been fine for four years. He didn't know if it would be fine now.

He told him about the workshop. About Vivian Ong and the best-filed phase in Malacca. About profit day in two weeks, when she'd add up the first month and look at him over her glasses. He'd promised her it was the next big thing. He'd said *trust*, out loud, to a woman who visibly didn't, and he'd put two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit into a company that had made fifty-two ringgit from a leaf.

He told him about Kestrel. Level thirteen on Sunday, fourteen on Tuesday night. A team of pros and ten thousand viewers, one level from the door. He didn't know if she'd ever heard of the Wending Fen. He'd lain awake for two nights doing sums, and all of them had come out the same: *faster*.

And then he told him about the door.

Properly, for the first time. Not *it's a door, you'll like it* in party chat. All of it. The ninth heron that pointed the wrong way. The deep black water past it, and the lurker. The mound, and the sunken arch with the herons cut wing to wing all round it. The slab, and the five hollows. The five words. And under all of it, through the stone, the singing.

"It's a choir," he said. "Under the water. Singing really slowly. Like they've been at it so long they've forgotten they started. Dest hummed along with the bass line." He turned his glass round on the table. "Sedge's mother went in, when she was young. With four others. She never told anyone what was in there. She only said they sang."

Suzu had stopped eating. He was looking at Ice across the table with his half-closed eyes, very still.

"Five voices," said Suzu.

"Five voices. None below fifteen."

"And you're the one who found it."

"I'm the one who found it." Ice looked at his roti. "On the first morning. Before anybody. It said *you are the first to find this place*. And I've been sitting on it for two and a half weeks, while everybody gets closer to fifteen, waiting for five people. And tonight I lay in bed doing sums about a woman I've never met. And I thought, if I can just get one more level tonight, just one, then I'm—" He stopped.

"Then you're what?"

"Then I'm not slow," said Ice, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

***

Suzu didn't say anything for a long time.

The man at the front of the shop slapped another roti out thin on the steel counter. Someone on the television scored a goal, and three men at a table by the door shouted. A cat came out from under somebody's motorbike and looked at the two of them and went back under.

"You know what the thirty per cent was," said Suzu eventually.

"What?"

"Plan seventy. Then trust." He picked up a square of roti and looked at it. "That's what I said. On your stream. The thirty per cent is the part you can't plan. The part you trust." He ate it. "But you could plan the night. Xav told you about the night. You saw it on the screen. *The river is awake.*" He looked at Ice. "So it wasn't trust, tonight. It was stubborn."

Ice laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out of him like air out of a tyre.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah. It was stubborn."

"You're not slow," said Suzu.

"I'm twelfth."

"You're twelfth in Brackenford," said Suzu, "out of nearly three thousand people, after seventeen days, in a game you found the first door in, on the first morning, by yourself. With a sword you learned to hold from an old man with three fingers." He shrugged. "And you hired your friend so he could come and play it too." He looked at his teh tarik. "You don't have to be first. You already were."

Ice didn't say anything.

"Also," said Suzu, "if you'd won tonight, you'd have gone back tomorrow night. And the night after. And one night you'd have been at the ford when Kotori was on her rock, and the small one would have gone for the rock." He drank. "So. Good. You lost."

***

They walked back to the car through the warm dark. Somewhere a dog was barking at a motorbike, the way one always was.

"Tomorrow you don't play," said Suzu, at the car door.

"I *can't* play."

"So tomorrow you do the boring things. The secretary things. The pass." He looked at Ice over the roof of the car. "And I play. All day. And by tomorrow night I'm ten." A pause. "And then I walk. And then there's five."

"Before the door," said Ice.

"Before the door," said Suzu, and got in.

***

Vivian Ong looked at him over her glasses at nine forty on Thursday morning and said, before he'd sat down:

"You died."

He stopped in the doorway.

"How do you know that?"

"The auntie downstairs watches your stream." Vivian turned a page of something. "She told me when I came in. She said, *Vivi, the gardening boy died last night in the river, very sad*." She looked up. "She's been watching since Saturday. She says the old man with three fingers is her favourite."

Ice sat down slowly in the chair across the teak desk.

"She said *gardening boy*," he said.

"I did ask you not to describe the business as gardening." Vivian took her glasses off and put them on the desk beside the orchid. "Are you all right?"

He hadn't expected that. It came out of her plainly, without any change of tone, as if it were one of the items on the list.

"I'm fine," he said. Then, because Suzu had made him say it properly last night, and it had helped: "I was stupid. I'm fine."

"Good," said Vivian. "Both of those." She put her glasses back on. "Then let's do the boring things."

***

The boring things took two hours, and he understood about half of them, and Vivian made him understand the other half.

The Expatriate Services Division had approved the company's registration on Tuesday. That was the big one, Vivian said: the gate before the gate. It meant the company had shown, on paper, everything it needed to show to be allowed to hire someone from outside Malaysia. The SSM profile. The paid-up capital, there in the bank statement with its two hundred and fifty thousand ringgit and fifty-two ringgit and ninety-two sen. The tenancy agreement for the shoplot. The licence from the city council, which had come through on Monday with a stamp on it so faint you had to tilt it to the light.

"So now," said Vivian, "we apply for the pass."

She did it on her laptop while he watched, on the division's portal, a website that looked as though it had been designed by somebody who didn't like websites. *Employment Pass. Category III.* The position: *Game Operations Executive.* The basic monthly salary: five thousand ringgit, which met the threshold since June. Suzu's passport, scanned. His CV, which Ice hadn't known existed, and which was one page long and very neat, and which Vivian said was the best-formatted CV she'd seen this year. A description of the job. She'd written it herself, and it said things like *operation of interactive digital equipment for the production and sale of virtual goods* and *market analysis of in-platform trade*. It was all completely true, and it made Suzu sound like a man who worked in a bank.

"You're very good at this," said Ice.

"I know," said Vivian. She pressed *submit*. "Some weeks. Not days. I'll tell him the moment I hear." She turned the laptop toward him so he could see the reference number. "And I've registered the company with LHDN as an employer, so the tax can come out of his salary properly when there's a salary. And he'll be covered by PERKESO from the day the pass starts, for injuries at work."

"Injuries at work."

"Should he fall off something," said Vivian. "In the office. Not in the river." She looked at him. "There's no form for the river."

***

At the end of it she squared her papers and he sat in the old chair across the teak desk, in the cool long room, with the slats of light across everything and the photograph of her father on the wall. He thought about what Suzu had said.

*You hired your friend so he could come and play it too.*

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You can ask."

"Have you looked at it? The game. At all."

Vivian considered this, the way she considered everything.

"I've read your Exchange statements," she said. "And the company's. And the auntie's told me about the old man with three fingers." She paused. "I have not lain down in an egg, if that's what you mean."

"You should try it."

"Mr Declan," said Vivian, quite kindly, "I have a practice to run."

"I know. I'm not saying—just once. There's a spare one. Upstairs at the office." He leaned forward. "It's going to be enormous. It's three million people already, after two and a half weeks. There's an ad at Changi the size of a bus. There are people in the news who've never played a game in their lives buying them for their kids, and then using them themselves. It's the next big thing. It is. Trust me."

Vivian Ong looked at him over her glasses.

"That word," she said, "does not work on me."

"It works on everyone."

"It works on your chat, Mr Declan. The auntie told me what happens on your stream when you say it." She almost smiled. "*It's over.*"

He laughed, despite himself.

"I'll tell you what does work on me," said Vivian. She opened a drawer and took out a single sheet of paper and laid it on the desk between them. It was a page from a calendar, the month of December, with one date circled in neat blue pen. *16*. "The company's first month closes on the fifteenth. On the sixteenth, I'll have the management accounts: every ringgit in, every ringgit out, every unit, every draught, every leaf." She tapped the circle. "Show me the numbers, Mr Declan. If the numbers are good, I'll look."

"Look at what?"

"At the egg," said Vivian, and put the calendar page back in the drawer, and shut it.

***

He went home at lunchtime and sat at the folding table in the back room in Melaka Raya, with Suzu in the pod beside him and his laptop open on Suzu's stream. He watched it all afternoon.

It was strange, being on this side again. Two weeks ago he'd sat at this table and raided Suzu's first stream and typed *hi*. Now he sat here locked out, with his own stream dark, and watched Suzu on the hill. The long silver hair and Aldo's scythe. The long low mowing stroke going out through three barrow wisps at once in the grey afternoon, and the blue lights clinging to the blade for a second before he caught them in the jar.

Suzu's chat had grown. Not like Hyvred's, which had gone round the world and come back with four thousand people in it. But Ice's raid had brought a hundred and twelve, and a lot of them had stayed, and some of them had brought friends. There were fifty or sixty people in there most days now. They typed *SHHHK* every time the scythe went round, and *catch it catch it* when the lights came off the blade.

Ice typed in it sometimes. *nice.* *back foot.* *that one cut high.* Suzu answered out loud, without looking, in his slow low voice, the way Ice had answered him a week ago.

At four minutes past four, on the ridge above Larkhollow in the rain, a mowing stroke went through four wisps at once. The window came up gold.

[ Ding.
Suzuyunn has reached Level 10.
The roads are open. ]

And then the other one, the plain one, white on dark water. Ice leaned in to read it on the laptop.

[ Elsewhere has been watching how you carry yourself.
You took up a scythe, and you cut a wide row.
You kept time with the others in the line.
You gathered what fell, and kept it from fading. ]

[ Class: Harvester
One who cuts a wide row, and gathers what falls. ]

Ice read it twice. *Harvester.* In a party called Moonlight Harvest.

On the stream, on the ridge, in the rain, Suzu stood very still with his scythe in his hands. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then the corner of his mouth went up, about a millimetre.

"*Harvester*," he said. "Ice."

Ice typed.

His lockout ended at half past six.

He knew the minute. He'd been watching it all afternoon in the corner of the companion app, a little counter going down. At six twenty-nine he was lying in his egg with his finger over the glass. At six thirty the glass let him in.

He came back on the riverbank in Brackenford where he'd been standing when he went down to the ford, in the dusk, with the water going pewter. For a moment he just stood there, looking at the ford in the last of the light. The Warden was a grey hump in the deep water below the stones, very still. So was the smaller one, upstream, which he could see now that he knew to look.

He turned his back on them and walked up the bank to the town.

They were waiting for him at the end of the first bridge, the three of them. Deston was sitting on the parapet with his legs swinging. Xav stood with his hands in his pockets. Kotori had her staff held in both hands like the strap on a bus, and the little bell was ringing faintly because she couldn't keep still.

"Dec," said Deston.

Xav didn't say anything for a while. He just looked at him.

"Not the ford," said Xav eventually.

"Not the ford," said Ice. "Not without five. Not at night."

"Good," said Xav. And that was all. That was the whole thing, from Xav, and it was more than enough.

Kotori stepped forward. She had something in her hands, held out in front of her, wrapped in a bit of cloth. She looked worried.

"Sorry," she said. "I hope it's okay. I kept these."

He unwrapped them. Six silverleaf. Grey-green and shining like fish scales, a little battered, still damp. The ones he'd dropped where he fell.

"They washed up on my rock," said Kotori. "This morning. We went to look at the ford, in the daytime, the three of us, from the bank, just to look, and they were all on my rock, the one Xav said was mine. Six of them." She looked anxious. "Was that okay? Sorry. I didn't know if you were allowed to keep things somebody dropped. I asked the clerk and he said it was a filing error."

Ice looked at the six silverleaf in his hands for a long time.

Then he laughed. He couldn't help it. It was a big helpless laugh, the kind he hadn't done since Sunday, and it went out across the bridge and made three strangers turn round. A second later, Kotori laughed too, a beat behind, the way she always did, and the bell on her staff rang.
