Higu had been told, by four different people, that she should have bought a pod in November. She had decided, in November, that she would wait and see.

She had waited. She had seen. She had watched a man in a swamp make fifty-two ringgit from a leaf, and found the first five-player dungeon in the world, and stand on a plank in the dark for twelve hours. She'd watched Hyvred see the whole sky for the first time and cry about it on a cliff. She'd watched Bee draw a ladder. She had watched all of it carefully, with her chin on her hand, the way she watched everything before she decided anything.

And then on the seventeenth of December, a Thursday, at eleven in the morning, Australian summer, she lay down in an egg in a pod café across town. It was in what had been a carpet shop until October. She put her thumb on the glass.

"You're late," said Gigi, on the call. Gigi was in a different café, somewhere else entirely, and had been in her egg for four minutes. "I'm already in the room. It's raining. It's very nice. Hurry up."

"I'm here."

"You're always last."

"I'm always *on time*," said Higu. "You're always early."

"Same thing," said Gigi, and Higu heard her smiling.

The mirror was the first thing.

She'd built herself on her phone, over a week, a feature at a time, very carefully. She'd done it the way she'd built the model in the first place, years ago, when she'd first started streaming. Very long hair, wavy, so dark it was nearly black, with a violet sheen where the light caught it. Blunt bangs. Pale lilac-grey eyes, heavy-lashed, half-lidded, calm. The face she'd streamed with for years. The face people said looked like it was always slightly listening to something far away.

She looked at it in the mirror in a small wooden room with rain on the window for a long time, without saying anything.

Selwick was a town at the edge of a lake, under a hill, and on top of the hill there was a ruin.

That was the first thing she saw. She saw it before the town, before the lake. She came out of the wooden room into a cobbled lane between leaning stone houses, and the lane ran straight down to the water. And over the roofs, up on the green hill behind the town, against a pale summer sky, there was a ruin. A tall broken tower of white stone, round, with the top fallen in. Around it, half-buried in the grass, there were long low walls, and stone rings, and the curved ribs of something huge, like the skeleton of a dome.

"I've got a ruin," she said.

"I've got a hedge," said Gigi. "A big one. A really, really big one. The whole village is inside it." A pause. "I don't like it."

The lake was wide and still and very clear, the colour of pale glass, with the hill reflected upside down in it. There were boats pulled up on the shingle. There was a jetty with an old man fishing off the end of it. There was a smell of water and cut grass and something she couldn't name, cool and clean, like the air in a room where somebody's just washed the windows.

She went to look at the ruin. Of course she did.

***

The ruin was called the Old Glass, and it had been an observatory.

She found that out from the man who was digging in it. He was very old and very thin, in a long grey coat with the pockets full of string and pencils, and he was kneeling in a trench at the foot of the broken tower. He was brushing earth off something with a paintbrush. He had spectacles so thick his eyes looked enormous, and he was holding the brush about an inch from his nose.

"Careful," he said, without looking up, as she came to the edge of the trench. "There's a step."

There was a step. She hadn't seen it. She went down it carefully.

His name was Master Ennis, and he was the cartographer of Selwick, and he was going blind.

He told her that straight away, as if it were a fact about the weather. He'd been mapping the Old Glass for forty years, ever since he was a young man and had first dug up a corner of it by accident, planting a pear tree. The map was in his house by the lake. Forty years of it, on a long table, sheet after sheet. And he'd nearly finished. There were only the last rings left, the ones at the very top of the hill, round the base of the tower.

And his eyes had gone. Slowly, then all at once, this last year. He could see light and shapes. He could see the step if he knew it was there. He couldn't see the lines.

"What lines?" said Higu.

Master Ennis put his paintbrush down. He felt along the edge of the stone he'd been cleaning with his fingertips, the way you'd read something written in raised letters.

"Silver," he said. "In the stone. Very fine. Finer than a hair. You can't see them in the day." He turned his enormous eyes up toward her. "You can only see them by the moon."

She took the lantern staff because Master Ennis had one.

It had been on the rack in the wooden room and she'd walked past it. She'd taken nothing, in the end, because nothing had felt right. But Master Ennis had a long thin pole of pale wood, as tall as he was, with a crook at the top like a shepherd's. From the crook hung a small brass lantern with glass sides. When he walked across the dig at dusk, the lantern swung in front of him and lit the ground a step ahead of his feet.

"Every cartographer in Selwick has had one," he said. "Since the Old Glass was whole. You hold the light out in front of you. Not by your face. Out there. Where you're going." He held it out over the trench. "And you look at what the light shows you. Not at the light."

The shop by the jetty sold them. She bought one with the last of her copper and walked back up the hill with it as the sun went down over the lake.

And Master Ennis taught her the other thing. The thing the lantern did, if you knew how to ask.

She did it again. She did it forty-one times on the top of the hill in the dusk, with Gigi on the call grumbling about her hedge. On the forty-second, the little light went out of the lantern and drifted across the dig, over the trench, slowly, steadily, to the foot of the broken tower. And she saw the tower from where the light was. Close. As if she were standing there. The broken white stone and the moss and a beetle walking up it, all at once, from fifty feet away, while she stood at the edge of the trench with her eyes half shut.

She sat down in the grass very suddenly.

"Gu?" said Gigi, on the call. "What happened?"

"I can see from somewhere else," said Higu. "I sent a light and I can see from where it went."

Gigi didn't say anything for a moment.

"That's so you," she said. "That's so *annoyingly* you."

On the third night, there was a moon.

She'd been waiting for it. The first two nights in the game had been clouded over, soft grey cloud over the lake, and the ruins had stayed dark. Master Ennis said *wait*, the way he said everything, and she'd waited. She'd mapped by lantern-light instead. She sent the little light down the trenches and along the walls and across the stone rings, and drew what it showed her on sheets of Master Ennis's paper with his pencils. Excavation and Cartography: she'd taken them both on the first afternoon, without hesitating, the only quick decision she'd made all week.

On Saturday night, the cloud went away.

The moon came up over the far side of the lake, very large and very white, and its reflection lay across the water like a road. The light came up the hill. It came across the grass and the trenches and the low walls and the stone rings. It came up the broken white tower. And as it touched the stone, the lines came out.

Silver. Finer than a hair. In every stone, every wall, every ring, every rib of the fallen dome. Running across the whole hilltop in long curves and circles and straight lines, crossing and meeting and going on. It was like a map of the sky had been drawn on the ground in the moon's own ink. And it had only been waiting for the moon to come up so it could be read.

She stood at the top of the hill with her lantern and her pencil and her paper and didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she sent the little light out.

She sent it along the lines. One after another, out across the hilltop, following the silver where it went, while she drew. She drew all night. When the moon went down behind the hill, she had nine sheets. They were the last nine. The rings round the base of the tower that Master Ennis hadn't been able to see.

She took them down the hill to his house by the lake at dawn. He laid them on the end of his long table, beside forty years of the others, and felt along the lines with his fingertips, one sheet at a time, very slowly.

"Oh," he said. Just that. "*Oh.*"

On the Monday, the dark came.

She had the window on Wednesday, the same as everyone. *Keep your lights. Keep your towns.* She'd read it twice, carefully, and then she'd gone down to the jetty and asked the old man fishing off the end of it what Selwick did on the longest night. He'd looked at her for a long time, the way the people here did when you asked them something that mattered. Then he'd said, *we light the water*.

They lit the water.

At sunset on Monday, every boat in Selwick went out onto the lake, every one, with a lantern in the bow. The whole town. The fishermen and the shopkeepers and the children and Master Ennis, who couldn't see where he was going and went anyway, rowed by a girl from the bakery. They rowed out in a great slow ring and stopped there, all over the dark water, a hundred small lights floating on the lake. Their reflections hung under them like a second ring of lights going down into the deep.

*Things come up out of still water*, the old man had said. *So you don't leave the water dark.*

She stood on the jetty with her lantern staff and watched them come.

They came up out of the lake, of course. Long thin wet shapes that the lantern light didn't land on, rising out of the deep water between the boats and wading toward the lights. And she sent the little light out, and saw them. From above, from the side, from wherever she sent it. All of them, all over the lake, before they reached the boats.

"*North boat*," she called, across the water, calmly. "*Two coming up on your left. Baker's girl, one behind you, close.*"

She did it all night. She stood at the end of the jetty and sent her little light out over the dark water, over and over. She called where the dark was coming up, before it got there, so the boats could see it in time. A man in a fishing boat on the far side of the lake, whose name she never learned, heard her voice coming across the water all night, every few minutes, calm, steady, telling him where to look. He said afterwards he'd have lost his lantern four times without it.

At three in the morning, her time, the dawn didn't come. She'd known it wouldn't. She'd watched four different streams of four different friends since Wednesday and read every theory on the forum, and Gigi had read her the worst ones. She didn't stop. She sent the light out again.

And at four in the morning, sitting on the end of the jetty with her legs over the water and her lantern held out over the lake, she noticed something.

They came up in the same places.

Not everywhere. Not at random. She'd been sending the little light out all night, and she'd been drawing as she went, because she couldn't not. She'd been marking on a sheet of Master Ennis's paper every place she saw one come up out of the lake. And they weren't scattered. They came up in three places. Over and over, all night, three patches of deep water out in the lake, in a line.

She looked at the sheet.

Then she took the map out. Master Ennis's map, the last nine sheets, the rings round the base of the broken tower, that she'd been carrying in her bag since Sunday because she couldn't bear to leave it. She unrolled it on the boards of the jetty in the light of her lantern, and laid the sheet of the lake beside it.

There was a long straight silver line on the map. It came out from the base of the tower, from the innermost ring, and ran down the hill toward the lake. It went off the edge of the last sheet, pointing at the water.

She lined up the edge of the map with the edge of the lake.

The silver line ran straight out across the water. Through all three places.

"Chi," she said, very quietly, on the call. Not loud. She only ever called her that when it was the two of them.

"*What?*" said Gigi, from her hedge, on the other side of the world, sounding exhausted. "*What is it, Gu?*"

"The ruin," said Higu. "It's pointing at them. The old lines. They knew where the dark comes up." She looked at the silver line on the map, and the dark lake, and the ring of a hundred small lights floating on it. "Somebody drew it. A long time ago. Somebody knew."

On the call, Gigi didn't say anything for a long moment.

"*That's so you*," she said, finally. And then, much more quietly: "*Gu. Write it down. Write all of it down.*"

Higu wrote it down.

She wrote it down all night, while the boats held the lake and the dark came up in the three places and she called them, one after another. When the gold finally came across the lake at eleven in the morning, her time, she was still sitting on the end of the jetty. A map in her lap, a sheet of notes beside her, and a hundred lanterns going pale on the water. Under it all, very small, in her careful, upright hand, she'd written one more line.

*Ask Kei.*
