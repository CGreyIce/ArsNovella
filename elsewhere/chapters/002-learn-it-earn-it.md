The seventh cut was worse than the first.

[ Sword Form I: Falling Cut
Grade: 9%. You were fast. Speed is not weight. ]

"Huh," said Ice, and lowered the sword.

He'd been climbing: twelve, then seventeen, then fifteen, then twenty, then twenty-two. On the seventh he'd thought *faster* and whipped it, all wrist and shoulder, and the blade had hit the dummy with a lovely crisp sound and the game had looked at it and said *nine*.

Hollis was back on his bucket, sewing up the other dummy. "That one was pretty," he said, without looking. "Fast as anything. Wouldn't have cut butter."

"It sounded good."

"Things that sound good mostly aren't." Hollis bit off a thread. "Your feet stopped. You threw your arm at it and left the rest of you behind. Back to the start."

Ice went back to the start.

The chat panel was a ghost at the edge of his eye, scrolling a minute behind him. It had been scrolling the same thing for twenty minutes.

"Chris is making a sheet," Ice said to the dummy, fondly.

He knew he should be bored. Twenty minutes on one cut at one straw man, the same step in, the same diagonal. In any other game he'd have been bored silly. But every time he did it, it was different, because *he* was different: a fraction more weight one time, the back heel a fraction late the next. And the game noticed. It noticed everything. It was like being watched by a teacher who never blinked.

So he did what Chris was doing in his spreadsheet. He stopped trying to get it *right* and started changing one thing at a time.

He did a cut where the only thing he thought about was the back foot, letting it come round behind him like a door swinging shut. Nineteen. *Your feet were behind you. Your arm was early.*

He did one thinking only about his arm arriving with his feet, not before. Twenty-five.

He did one where he stopped looking at the sword and looked through the dummy instead, at the fence post on the other side of it, as if the cut had somewhere to be. Thirty-one.

[ Sword Form I: Falling Cut
Grade: 31%. The whole of you arrived at once. ]

"Oh," he said.

Hollis had stopped sewing. "That one," he said. "That one was something."

"Thirty-one."

"Don't know what that means." Hollis put the needle down. "But that's what it's meant to feel like. Did you feel it?"

He had. It was a strange thing to try to describe. The blade hadn't hit harder, exactly. It had hit *heavier*, as if the dummy had been standing in the way of something much bigger than one arm. The jolt had come back up through his wrist and elbow and gone into his shoulder and through his hips and down into the boards, all at once, and nowhere had it been stopped by a part of him that wasn't ready.

"I felt it," he said.

"Good. Do it fifty more times." Hollis went back to his sewing. "And don't do it when you're tired. You learn tired, you fight tired."

***

He found out what tired meant at about cut thirty.

There was a bar in the bottom of his vision, a thin green line under the red one for health. He hadn't paid it much attention. It went down a little with every cut and crept back up when he stood still. After a while he noticed it was creeping back slower than it was going down, and around cut thirty it was mostly empty, and the sword weighed twice what it had.

It wasn't fake heaviness. His arm actually ached, a dull fizzing burn in the long muscle of his forearm that got worse when he lifted the blade. He did a cut anyway, to see. Fourteen. *You were tired.*

"Stamina," he told chat, and sat down on the packed earth to let it fill, with his back against the fence. "It's not just a number. Your arm actually gets tired. I can feel it in the—" he flexed his wrist—"here. It's like the end of a gym set."

It was a strange kind of rest. The sun was coming out over the fen now, burning off the mist in slow rags. He could hear the town around him: somebody chopping wood, somebody's child shouting, the long low knock of a boat pole against a jetty. He could smell bread from somewhere. His forearm ached, and his back was cold through his shirt where it touched the fence, and he was sitting on a game's ground in a game's sunshine, and he couldn't tell, honestly couldn't tell, what part of any of it wasn't real.

He'd thought he'd be impressed. He hadn't thought he'd be *unsettled*. He kept waiting for the edge, the place where the painting stopped and the frame began. He'd looked for it all morning out of habit: the bit of the wall that didn't have a texture, the tree that was really a flat picture of a tree. He hadn't found one.

"Okay," he said, and got up when the green bar was full. "Again."

By the time the sun was full up, the Falling Cut sat in the high thirties and stayed there, and Hollis said he'd seen enough of it for one morning.

"Shield," he said.

He didn't teach it like the cut. He picked up a length of wood about the size and weight of a club, told Ice to put the shield on, stood in front of him, and hit him with it.

Ice got the shield up. Hollis's stick hit it square in the middle with a bang that shook his whole arm, and drove the rim back into his shoulder, and the fifty per cent turned up exactly where the rim landed, a hot bright sting that made his eyes water.

[ Guard I: Turned Block

"Ow," said Ice, mildly.

"You held it flat," said Hollis. "Like a door you're trying to keep shut. Big man comes along with a big club and kicks the door in, and you're behind it. Don't keep it shut. Angle it." He reached out and tilted Ice's shield with two fingers, so it leaned away from the stick, the top edge turned out. "Now the blow comes, and it meets the slope, and it slides. It goes past you. "I'm fine," he said, a minute ahead of them, still holding his ear. It *stung*, properly stung, like being flicked hard by somebody who meant it. He was beginning to see why the company had set it to twenty. "I'm fine, Hana. Hollis, again."

Hollis hit him again. And again. Each time a little differently: high, then low, then straight at the middle, then a feint that wasn't one. Ice got the slope wrong fifteen ways. Too flat. Too steep. Too late, so the blow caught the rim. Too early, so he'd committed the angle before he knew where it was coming from. Each time the window told him, and each time it stung somewhere new. His shoulder, his ear, the knuckles of his shield hand once, which made him say something his mother wouldn't have liked.

But by the thirtieth or fortieth time, the stick started doing what Hollis had said it would. It met the shield and went *past*. Not with a bang. With a scrape and a sort of shrug, the club going off down and to the side as if it had changed its mind, Hollis's weight carrying him a half step past, open.

"There," said Hollis, catching his balance. "See where I am?"

"That's the point," said Hollis. "You don't hold the line by being a wall. You hold it by making them miss, and being there when they do." He lowered the club. "Right. That's the morning. Go and get bitten."

***

The rats lived on the south walk, where the boardwalk ran out past the last of the houses into the open fen, and broke up into narrower and narrower planks over the black water until it was barely a path at all.

There were a lot of people on the south walk.

Most of them were fighting. Swinging was a better word. He watched a man in a linen shirt bring a hatchet down on a rat's back from straight above his head, with both hands, his feet planted wide and flat as a table. The hatchet bounced. The rat bit his ankle. The man yelled and swung again, the same way, and the hatchet bounced again.

Another player, a woman with a spear, was jabbing at a rat from a long way off, with her arms only, her body leaning so far forward she was almost falling in. Every jab, a little window popped over the rat. She wasn't reading them.

Ice stood at the edge of it all for a moment and watched, and his chest did something complicated.

*Everyone's going to be like this,* he thought. *For days. Most of them for weeks.* All over the world, in a thousand swamps and forests and fields, a million people who'd waited a year and a half for this were standing with their feet flat, swinging with their arms, and wondering why everything was so slow.

The rats were the size of small dogs, with wet dark fur and long bald tails and yellow teeth that showed when they moved. They were fast and low, and they didn't stand still.

His first one came out of the reeds at the edge of the walk and went straight for his ankles. He did a Falling Cut at it out of pure habit, and the cut came down from his shoulder to the place where a man's hip would have been and found air a foot above the rat's back. The rat bit him on the calf.

It felt like a stapler. A hot, sharp, closing pinch, at fifty per cent, through the trouser leg and into the muscle, and a quick ache after it. His health bar dipped. He made a small noise.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. It's short."

He'd been taught to cut a man. The rat wasn't a man. Hollis's cut went across where a body was, and the rat's body was at his ankles. So he did the only other thing he had: he put the shield down in front of his shins like a low wall, with the slope, and the next time it lunged, its teeth met the angle and it skidded off sideways and went past him, the way Hollis's club had, and for a moment it was off balance on the wet planks with its side to him.

He stepped in and cut down at it, sitting low. The blade landed.

[ Falling Cut · Grade 34% · 5 damage ]

He found the bonus about twenty minutes later, by accident, when a second rat came out of the reeds while he was still fighting the first.

He killed them both. It was messy, and he took four bites he didn't need to take, and the health bar went down to just under half. But when the second one broke into motes, the window was different.

[ Marsh Rat (Lv 1) defeated
+4 XP · +Outnumbered ×1.5 → 6 XP ]

He stood and looked at it for a long time.

The dev diaries had hinted at it. *The world rewards those who take on more than they should*, one of them had said, in that infuriating way the company had of saying things. A forum had decided that meant fighting monsters above your level. Nobody had said anything about numbers.

"Chat," he said slowly, "I think if there's more than one of them, it pays more."

He looked up and down the south walk. Out past where the boards got thin, three rats were nosing about together on a little island of mud and reed. Nobody else was near them. Everybody else was fighting one rat at a time, as you would.

He knew what he was going to do before he'd finished thinking it, and he also knew exactly how stupid it was. If he died, the game would lock him out for twenty-four hours. On launch day. He'd come back tomorrow to find a thousand people a level ahead of him in his own town, and he'd have spent ten thousand ringgit to watch them do it.

He didn't go out to the island. He went the other way, back along the walk, until he found what he wanted: a place where the boardwalk narrowed to two planks, one person wide, with reeds thick on both sides and black water underneath. Anything that wanted to reach him there would have to come along the planks. One at a time.

Then he went back toward the island, close enough, and banged the flat of his sword on his shield.

All three rats looked up at once.

He turned and ran.

He reached the narrow place two strides ahead of them and turned with the shield down low and the slope on it, and the first rat hit it and slid off sideways into the reeds with a splash. The second came straight behind it and he cut it as it reached the slope, sitting low, the whole of him arriving at once. It was not clean. It was not even close to clean. A rat came out of the reeds at his left ankle while he was busy with the one in front, and bit him there, and the hot staple pinch went up his leg. It wasn't the sword and it wasn't the shield; it was the thing you learned in ten thousand hours of other games, where you count what's in front of you and keep your back to the wall and you don't panic. *That one's at a third. That one's turning. That one is in the water and has to climb out, and that takes a second.*

At eighteen per cent he killed the second rat. At eleven he killed the one from the reeds. At nine he put the shield's slope into the last one's lunge, felt it go past him off balance, stepped in and brought the Falling Cut down across its back, and it was the best one he'd done all day.

The general store was a long low house at the centre of town, with a counter made of a single split log, and behind it a woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. When Ice came in, sore and pleased with himself, there was a queue.

He waited behind four people in linen shirts, all with bitten ankles.

The first one asked for a healing potion. The woman said they were gone. The second asked for a healing potion. The woman said they were gone, a little more slowly. The third asked if there was anything at all that healed, and the woman pointed at a shelf of bread and said bread helped, and the man bought three loaves out of sheer despair.

When it got to Ice, she looked at him with the expression of somebody who had said the same sentence a hundred times since dawn.

"We're out," she said. "Before you ask. Draughts. We had twelve. Your lot bought them before the bell for second breakfast."

"Who makes them?"

That stopped her. She blinked. "The Widow Sedge," she said. "At the end of the north walk. She brings them on market days. She's three days behind already, she told me, and that was before." She sighed. "You'd think the world had grown a hundred extra ankles overnight."

"It has," said Ice. "Thank you." He sold her his four poor rat pelts for a copper each, which she took with an air of great generosity, and turned to go. Then he turned back. "Sorry. What's your name?"

She looked at him the way Hollis had. The way the eel man had looked at the streamer, but going the other way.

"Marta," she said.

"Thanks, Marta."

He went out. In his head, without his asking it to, a part of him that had spent fifteen thousand hours watching a market in a game about spaceships had already started adding things up. Sixty-four people in Fernwick this morning. More arriving every hour. All of them getting bitten. Twelve potions in the whole town, gone by second breakfast. One old woman at the end of the north walk who made them, three days behind. And a herb on every walkway that everybody stood on.
