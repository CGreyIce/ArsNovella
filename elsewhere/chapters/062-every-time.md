"Roll call," said Kei.

It was Monday night. He said it the way he'd said it every night since the longest one, from the steps of the Hall of Roads with the lamps lit. And one after another, from towns all over a world nobody had a map of, they answered.

"Orchard End," said Maigo. "Here."

"Grimmet. Here."

"Ashcombe," said Wendy. "Here."

"Emberly," said Ina, in the voice of a man having a lovely time. "Present. Here. Both."

"Otterbank," said Hako, "where the young spearman has just been bitten by an otter, and is pretending it didn't hurt. Here."

"Selwick," said Higu softly. "Here."

"Briarwick. Here, unfortunately."

"Here," said Niko, from his bridge.

"Here!" said Newish. "Here, here, here, I'm the most here, nobody's more here than me—"

"Here," said Winter flatly, over the top of her.

"Ferrowmere," said Chris. "Here. I've got a sheet."

There was one more name in the call window tonight. It had come up five minutes before, and nobody had said anything about it yet, the way you didn't say anything when someone you liked walked into a room and sat down quietly at the back.

[ Call · KeiKatamori, Maigo, Salty, itswendy, Inaxho, HakoP, higuVT, gigichiii_, Nikorasu, somebodynewish, dumbo, Winter, Drew ]

"On the road," said Drew. Crunch, crunch. "Two days out of Sevenlocks. Here."

"Carrow Cross," said Kei. "Here." He looked at the notebook on his knee. "Thirteen."

"*Thirteen,*" said Hako, in his documentary voice. "And here we see the young physician, attempting to found a civilisation."

***

He told them all of it.

Plainly, in order, with the sums. The fourth time now. He'd thought it would get easier, and it had, a bit. But it was different with thirteen. With the core four it had been a conversation. With Xav it had been a plan. With Ice it had been something he didn't have a word for. With thirteen, all listening, it was like standing at the front of a room.

He told them about the writ, and the twenty, and the fifteen, and the thirty days. He told them about the core four, and the gate, and *who's it not for*. He told them about Xav, and Ice. He didn't need to explain Ice. Every one of them had watched the plank on Sunday. *It's theirs. I'm not in it.* Salty had texted him four times about it.

Then he stopped, and waited.

"I'm in," said Salty, immediately.

"You don't know what it is yet," said Wendy.

"I'm in anyway."

"Is it going to be work?" said Wendy. She said it carefully, the way she said most things now, from her forest of red clamps. Kei knew what she meant by *work*. He'd known her a long time. "Like, meetings? Is it going to be a thing I have to turn up to, and be good at?"

"No meetings," said Kei.

"There'll be meetings," said Salty.

"Then I want to be at the meetings," said Wendy, after a moment. "If there are meetings, I want to be at them. I don't want there to be meetings I'm not at." A pause. "I'm not saying yes. I'm saying I'll wait and see if it's right."

"That's you," said Salty softly. "You wait for it to be right."

"Shut up," said Wendy. But she said it the way she said it to him.

"It'll fall down," said Gigi.

She said it from Briarwick, from inside her ring of three-hundred-year-old hedge, in the voice of somebody stating a fact about the weather. Nobody argued. Everybody on the call knew her by now.

"It'll fall down," she said again. "Guilds always fall down. Somebody gets weird, somebody leaves, everybody takes sides, it falls down." A pause. "So I'm in. Every hedge I ever laid that's still standing, I laid expecting it to fall down. You build it like it's going to fall down. That's how it stays up."

"That's the most Gigi yes I've ever heard," said Maigo, delighted.

"It's not a yes. It's a prediction."

"Present," said Ina. "Yes. Obviously yes. I'd like it on the record that I said yes before Gigi's prediction, and that I'm having a lovely time."

"It's on the record," said Chris.

"Is there an actual record?"

"There's always an actual record," said Chris.

***

"A guild," said Newish, "called whatever Maigo says, is going to be the worst thing that's ever happened to me."

"I haven't *said*—"

"You've got a pun. I can hear it. I can hear it in your breathing."

"I'm not *breathing* a pun—"

"Fine," said Winter, over the top of both of them. "I'm in. Fine." A pause. "Drew's in?"

"I'm in," said Drew, from the road.

"Then fine," said Winter, and didn't say anything else.

"I've got a drum," said Newish, suddenly serious. "For tempo. The woman at the inn says you can play people faster. If there's a guild, and there's a fight, and there's twenty of us, I want to play twenty people faster at once. I want to see what that sounds like." A pause. "So I'm in. But Maigo doesn't get to name it."

Niko didn't say anything. He'd said it on Friday. He didn't say things twice.

Hako narrated something about the otter.

Higu was quiet.

Kei had noticed. He'd been noticing since the roll call. She'd said *here* softly, and then nothing. Higu was quiet a lot, the way people who watch things are quiet. But this was a different quiet. He waited until the others had finished, until Maigo and Newish had stopped arguing about the pun. Then he said, gently, "Higu?"

"Sorry," she said. "I was thinking. You said—on the steps. You said the Hall's round. And there's a ceiling."

"There's a ceiling."

"With stars on it."

"With stars on it," said Kei. "Painted. Four hundred years old, Ama Corrie says. Most of the gold's gone." He hesitated. "There are lines. Between some of them. Silver. You can only see them from one place, under one lamp."

Higu didn't say anything for a long time. On the call, very faintly, Gigi said, "Gu," and then stopped, as if she hadn't meant to say it out loud.

"I'd like to see it," said Higu. "In person. If that's okay."

"It's okay."

"Then I'm in," said Higu. "Sorry. Yes. I'm in."

***

"Okay," said Chris. "Can I do the sheet?"

"Do the sheet," said Kei.

Chris did the sheet.

He did it the way he did everything: thoroughly, in a calm, slightly apologetic voice, as if he knew it was too much and couldn't help it. He'd made it on Thursday, the day the writ went up, on the ferry and at his desk at work and on his phone in the queue for lunch. It had a tab for every one of them.

"Levels," he said. "Today. Kei twelve. Maigo thirteen. Salty thirteen. Wendy twelve. Ina twelve. Hako thirteen. Higu thirteen. Gigi twelve. Niko ten. Newish nine. Winter ten. Me—" a small pause "—eight. Drew fifteen. Off the call: Tiger seventeen, Hyvred sixteen, Bee fifteen. Those last ones are from their streams, so they might be wrong."

"They're not wrong," said Drew.

"So that's four of us at fifteen," said Chris. "Sixteen people. We need twenty." He went on, before anyone could say anything. "At six hours a night, by my sheet, the average of us gets to fifteen around the middle of February. Me at the end of March."

There was a silence on the call. Kei felt it settle.

"But," said Chris. Kei could hear him smiling. "Cradle Commons put a notice up this afternoon. From the first of February, they go to twelve hours. Because the eggs are back in the shops in February, and they want to keep us." A pause. "At twelve hours, the average of us gets there in the first week of February. Kei about the third. Me—" another small pause "—about the end of February. If I do twelve hours. Which I can't, every day, because of work. So call it the end of February. Give or take."

"The end of February," said Kei slowly.

"The first name starts the clock," said Chris. "And the first name has to be fifteen, and it has to be yours. So you can't start it till you're fifteen. And you shouldn't start it till the last of us is less than thirty days out." He paused. "By my sheet, those are about the same day. Early February. Then thirty days. Which gets you to the beginning of March."

"That's a long time," said Maigo.

"It's six weeks," said Chris. "It's not long. It's exactly long enough, if nobody dies in the wrong place." He paused. "I've got a tab for that too."

"Of course you have," said Winter.

"And the money," said Kei.

"The money," said Chris, and his voice changed slightly, the way it did when the numbers were less fun. "Five hundred gold. Paid with the first name. Kept if we're short." He took a breath. "Between all thirteen of us, tonight, in our bags, we've got about sixty-one gold. If everybody puts in what they can spare every week, and everybody sells what they make, we get there. Just. By early February. If Ice really does sell us things at cost."

"He will," said Kei.

"He said he would, on stream," said Salty. "To twenty-seven thousand people. He has to now."

"He'd have done it anyway," said Kei.

"I know," said Salty. "I'm just saying. He has to now."

***

"So who's it not for," said Drew.

He'd been quiet on the road for a while, crunching along. He said it into a lull, the way he'd said it to the core four on Friday. Calm, steady, not a challenge. A question somebody had to ask out loud so it would be asked.

Nobody answered at once.

"Not for the red ones," said Maigo eventually.

"Not for anybody who'd sell us," said Salty.

"Not for anybody who'd make it weird," said Gigi.

"Not for anybody who'd make Wendy not want to come to the meetings," said Salty, and Wendy said "*Shut up*," and laughed.

"That's a list," said Drew. "Lists are fine. But they're not the gate." Crunch. "There's an inn back on this road. The woman who keeps it kept the gate open all night on the longest night. She let in people she didn't know. One of them had a bird on his collar." A pause. "She said, *if the gate's shut, you have to decide. Every time.*"

"Every time," said Kei.

"Every time," said Drew. "Not a list. Somebody at the gate. Whoever wants in, the people who'd have to stand next to them decide. Every time. Even if it's slow. Especially then."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That's a lot of standing," said Wendy quietly.

"Yeah," said Drew. "That's the job."

"Okay," said Wendy. She was quiet for a moment longer. "Okay. Then I want to be at the gate. Sometimes. If that's a job. I'd be good at that." She stopped. Then, very softly, the way she'd said it on the longest night after she let go of four arrows at something huge in the dark: "Here."

Salty didn't say anything. Kei thought he was probably holding her hand, eighteen inches away, in two eggs in an old cinema in Singapore.

"Then that's thirteen," said Kei. "Fourteen with Tiger."

"Fourteen with Tiger," said Drew. "I'll be there Wednesday night. I'll tell him."

"Hyvred and Bee," said Maigo.

"I'll ask Hyvred myself," said Kei. "She's my friend. I'll ask her properly."

"Sixteen," said Chris. "Four to find."

"We'll find four," said Maigo.

"We'll find the right four," said Drew.

***

On Wednesday, the first banner went up.

Kei was in the Hall of Roads when it happened, at the counter, buying a map he didn't need from Ama Corrie, because he liked her. The window came up in front of him, and in front of everyone in the Hall at once. A murmur went round the round room like a wind.

[ The first banner in Elsewhere has been raised.
CRIMSON LEDGER · the Hall at Saltmarket · 20 founding names · Day 66 ]

He looked at it for a long time.

Somewhere far away, on the needle in Saltmarket's market square, there would be a new column now, one that had never been there before. *Guilds.* With one name in it.

Twenty names in one day. They'd started it that morning, and they'd finished it the same afternoon. Twenty accounts at fifteen or over, all in one Hall, all signed by hand. Every one of them had a red sash and a number instead of a name. The first guild in the world. A company that had hired people to play all day, and paid them, and never once asked any of them who they were.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie, behind the counter. She'd put her spectacles on to read it. She took them off again. "Twenty in a day."

"They've got four hundred people," said Kei. "They could do it twenty times."

"They asked about my room again," said Ama Corrie. "Yesterday. A man in red, with a purse. Not *now*, he said. For later. He said they'd like a room in every Hall where seven roads meet." She folded her spectacles. "I told him what I told the others. It's not for sale. It's for a writ." She looked up at Kei over the counter, small and sharp. "Same as anybody."

"Same as anybody," said Kei.

"So you'd best not take forever with yours."

"Early February," said Kei. "When I'm fifteen. Chris says."

"Who's Chris?"

"He's got a sheet."

Ama Corrie considered this.

"Bring me your map," she said. "Now. While it's quiet."

***

He showed her.

Not the sheet from his desk at home. That was in Australia, on paper. But he'd copied it into his notebook small, on the steps on Monday, and copied it again since. Every dot, every line. He opened it on her counter, under the lamp, and turned it round so she could read it.

She put her spectacles on. She looked at it for a long time, the way she'd looked at the notice. Following the lines with one finger, not touching the page. Fernwick, in the fen, with its thick line to a dot called Suzu. The six, in their six towns. Two dots on a lake and in a hedge. Four dots in a cluster. A long dotted line across most of the map, from a snowy valley to a canal city, with a little cross where an inn was. Eight small dots round a riverbank. And the lines between all of them, crossing and recrossing.

"Every dot's a person," said Kei. "Every line's something that already happened. I didn't make any of them up. I just drew what was there."

Ama Corrie didn't say anything.

Then she took her spectacles off, and looked up, past the lamp, at her ceiling. At the worn gold stars, and the dark between them, and the place where, if you stood exactly right, you could see the silver lines.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Hm," she said.

That was all. She gave him back his notebook and went to lock the side doors for the night, jangling her keys. At the last door she stopped, and looked back at him, standing alone at her counter in the empty round room.

"Stay as long as you like," she said. "Just pull the big door when you go."

***

He stayed.

He stood in the middle of the Hall of Roads, on his own, with the lamps turned low, and found the place. It took him a while: one step to the left, half a step back, under the lamp by the north door. And there they were. Thin silver lines in the dark of the painted dome, joining one worn star to another. Not all of them. Just some. As if somebody had stood on a ladder up there four hundred years ago with a brush and decided which ones belonged together.

He didn't say the word. Not yet. It was still too early. And it wasn't his, not yet, not on his own. It was going to be twenty people's, or it wasn't going to be anything.

He took out his phone in Australia, in the little wooden room, with the rain on the window. In Carrow Cross he stood under the ceiling and sent three messages.

To Drew: *Tell Tiger properly.*

To Higu and Gigi, both at once: *When you can, come to Carrow Cross. There's a ceiling I want you to see. Both of you. In person.*

And to Hyvred, on her own, because she was his friend, and because you asked properly: *Can I call you this weekend? It's about the paper. No pressure. I'd just like to ask you something.*

Drew answered first, from the road. *Walking in now. I can see the top lock. I'll tell him properly.*

Gigi answered second. *it'll fall down. we're coming.*

Hyvred didn't answer for a long time. When she did, it was short.

*yes. call me. bee says hi.*

He put the phone away. He stood under the ceiling a while longer. Then he pulled the big door shut behind him, and sat down on the steps of the Hall of Roads, at the place where seven roads met, and looked down each one of them in turn into the dark.
