Tiger fell into the canal for the fourteenth time on his sixth day in Sevenlocks, and this time a girl laughed at him.

Not a traveller. Travellers laughed at him all the time; he didn't mind; he laughed too. This was an NPC. A girl of about sixteen, thin and brown and quick, with her hair tied back in a scarf and a satchel across her chest. She was sitting on the top beam of a lock gate, eight feet over the water, swinging her legs. He came up spluttering out of the green canal by the Fourth Lock, with his ears flat to his head and his hair in his eyes. She looked down at him and laughed, out loud, delightedly, the way you'd laugh at a dog chasing its tail.

"That's fourteen," she said.

He stopped treading water. "You've been *counting*?"

"Everyone's been counting." She pointed along the canal. On the far bank, at the door of a chandler's, a man in an apron held up four fingers and then, after a pause, ten. "Old Gaspard's got a bet on with the lock-keeper. Twenty by Sunday."

Sevenlocks was built on a hill, and the hill was built on water.

That was the only way he'd ever found to describe it. Seven great stone locks climbed the hillside one above another, like a staircase for boats. Each was a long deep chamber with heavy wooden gates at both ends, and a lock-keeper's cottage beside each with a lamp over the door. Barges went up the hill and down it all day. They floated into the bottom of a lock, the gates closed behind them, the water came roaring in from above, and they rose, slowly, all the way to the top, with the bargemen leaning on the rails eating their lunch. Then the top gates swung open and they slid out into the next. Seven times, from the river at the bottom to the high basin at the top.

And all round the locks, the city. Tall narrow houses of yellow stone, leaning on each other. Little canals running off the big one in every direction, too narrow for anything but a punt, with stone bridges so humped you had to climb them like stairs. Towpaths a foot wide along the water's edge, worn smooth and slick and shining by three hundred years of feet. Steps going down into the water everywhere, green with weed.

It was the most beautiful place he'd ever been. He'd thought so the second he came out of his little wooden room on the first morning, two weeks ago. He'd been so excited he'd run straight out of the door, down the steps, along the towpath and into the canal, in that order, without stopping. Hyv had been watching. Hyv had said, on the call that night, very kindly, *you did that in about four seconds*.

He liked his face in here.

He'd built it the way he built everything, fast, on his phone, in bed, the night before. Messy chestnut hair with the one bit at the top that never lay down. Brown tiger ears that moved on their own, which he hadn't expected and loved. Blue-grey eyes, and the little *XX* mark under the left one, and the grin. The mirror had offered him his earring as a keepsake and he'd said yes before it finished asking. It was the same face he'd streamed in for years, and it worked. It ran. It jumped. He'd taken two short swords off the rack in the wooden room, because two was more than one. He'd taken Skinning and Leatherworking, because the first thing he'd killed in Sevenlocks was a towpath rat the size of a terrier, and the man at the tannery by the Second Lock had offered him a copper for it. She was. She came down the face of the lock gate the way you'd come down a ladder, except that there was no ladder: just the heavy oak planks of the gate, slimy with weed, and her hands and feet finding places on them he couldn't see. She dropped the last four feet onto the towpath beside him and didn't slip. She landed and was already walking.

"You stop," she said.

He was sitting on the steps, wringing out his tunic. "What?"

"That's why you fall in. You stop." She crouched down beside him on the step. She was looking at his feet the way, he'd realise later, Ice looked at people's feet. "You run like this." She made two fists and thumped them down on the step, one, two, flat and heavy. "*Bang. Bang.* Every step, you land. You put your whole weight down on the stone and you stop on it, just for a second, before you go again. And the stone's wet, and it's three hundred years smooth, and you've stopped on it. So." She opened one fist and made it slide off the edge of the step. "In you go."

Tiger looked at her hand.

"How do you not stop?"

"You don't land." She stood up. "You leave."

Her name was Pell, and she was a runner.

The runners were the fastest thing in Sevenlocks. That was what everyone said, and he found out it was true. They carried messages. Letters, packets, bills of lading, the little brass tokens that told a lock-keeper up the hill that a barge had paid at the bottom. Sevenlocks ran on paper going up and down the hill all day. The barges took an hour to climb the seven locks. A runner could take a letter from the river to the high basin in four minutes. They didn't use the bridges. Bridges were slow. They went over the lock gates, along the top beams, across the backs of moored barges, along the narrow stone lips of the locks themselves, eight feet above the water. They didn't fall in.

"Never?"

"Not since I was eleven," said Pell. "Once. In front of everybody. Never again."

She taught him for a copper an hour, which he'd have paid ten times over. She taught him on the towpath by the Fourth Lock, while Old Gaspard watched from the chandler's door and the lock-keeper watched from his window, both of them with money on it.

She taught him to run with his weight forward, over his toes, so that he was always slightly falling, always catching himself with the next step. She taught him to touch the stone and leave it, not land on it. *Kiss it*, she said, *don't marry it*. She taught him to look where he was going, not where he was. Three steps ahead, always three, so his feet knew where to go before he got there. She taught him to *never, ever stop*. If you had to stop, you stopped on something dry. If there wasn't anything dry, you didn't stop.

He came up out of the green water for the twentieth time and lay on his back on the steps with the canal running off him. He looked up at the blue sky between the leaning yellow houses and laughed until his ears went flat. Pell stood over him with her hands on her hips.

"You're not bad," she said, sounding surprised.

"I've fallen in twenty times!"

"Six today. You fell in six today. And you got further every time." She held out a hand. "You were running past the Fourth Lock gate on the last one. Nobody gets past the Fourth on the first day. You're just bad at the end bit."

"Which bit's the end bit?"

"The stopping." Pell pulled him up. "Again."

On Thursday he ran the Top Lock.

He hadn't planned to. Nobody planned to. That was what Pell said afterwards: nobody ever planned the first time. You just found yourself at the bottom of the hill with a letter in your hand and the whole staircase of locks going up in front of you. And you went.

It was a real letter. Pell had given it to him. *The lock-keeper at the Top wants this before the four o'clock barge, and I've got three more to run, and you're not bad.* It was ten to four. The four o'clock barge was already in the Sixth Lock, rising. The Top Lock was a long way up the hill.

He went.

He didn't think about it. That was the thing. If he'd thought about it, he'd have gone up the bridges, the long way, and been late, and been dry. Instead he did what Pell had taught him. Weight forward. Look three steps ahead. Kiss it, don't marry it. He went up the stone lip of the First Lock at a dead run, eight feet over the water, and across the top beam of the gate. Down onto the back of a moored barge and across it in three strides, the bargeman ducking. Up the steps beside the Second Lock, three at a time. Along the towpath by the Third, where it was a foot wide and green, touching it, leaving it, touching it.

The Fourth Lock gate. Where he'd fallen in fourteen times. He went over it without slowing, along the top beam, and Old Gaspard in the chandler's door shouted something that was either a cheer or a curse. The Fifth. The Sixth, with the four o'clock barge in it, rising, the water roaring in at the top. The bargemen looked up and saw him coming along the lip of the lock above their heads, and one of them threw up his arms.

And the Top Lock.

The Top Lock was the oldest and the highest. Its gates were the biggest on the hill, black oak twice as thick as the others, and they were opening. Slowly, groaning, the two great leaves swinging apart over the deep green water. The gap between them was getting wider. The top beams parted in the middle, an inch, a foot, two feet.

He was on the left-hand beam. The lock-keeper's cottage was on the right.

He didn't stop. You didn't stop.

He went along the left-hand beam at a dead run, eight feet over the deep water, and at the end of it, where the gap was, he jumped.

It wasn't far. Three feet, maybe four. But it was over the deepest water on the hill, with the gates moving under him and the whole of Sevenlocks spread out below. He was in the air for what felt like a long time. He looked three steps ahead, the way she'd taught him. He came down on the right-hand beam with the ball of one foot, touched it, left it, and was off it and onto the stone of the lock-keeper's step before his weight had even arrived.

He stopped on something dry.

The lock-keeper was standing in his doorway, an old man with a white moustache, holding a cup of tea. He looked at Tiger, soaking wet from the spray, ears straight up, grinning like a lunatic, holding out a damp letter.

"Four o'clock," said Tiger, out of breath. "Delivery."

The lock-keeper looked at the letter. Then at the clock on his wall, which said a minute to. Then at the gates, still swinging open behind Tiger over the deep water.

"Well," he said. "Gaspard owes me money."

He hit level ten on Saturday, in the Top Lock, in a fight with a pike.

It was a big pike. It lived in the deep water above the Top gates and it took ducks, and once, the lock-keeper said, a small dog. It had taken a runner's satchel off her shoulder in the summer and Pell had never forgiven it. Tiger fought it from the gates, the way the runners moved. Along the top beam, two short swords, never stopping. It came up out of the green and he cut it and was gone along the beam before it landed. It came up again and he was somewhere else.
