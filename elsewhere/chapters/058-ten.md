Vivian rang at twenty to eight on Wednesday morning, while the three of them were in the lift coming up from the gym.

She'd never rung that early before. Ice knew why the moment he saw the screen. She knew they went into the eggs at eight. She'd known for weeks; the auntie downstairs told her everything, and the auntie watched the stream. She'd rung at twenty to eight so that it would be before.

But she hadn't rung Ice. She'd rung Gabe.

Gabe looked at his phone, sweating, in a T-shirt soaked through from the weights. He looked at Ice. Then he answered it on speaker, because Gabe did everything on speaker, and held it out in the middle of the lift between the three of them like a microphone.

"Mr Gabe," said Vivian's voice. "Good morning. Your Employment Pass has been approved. Category three, two years. Your contract begins today." A pause. "I wanted you to hear it first. From me. Not from Mr Declan, in a car, being excited about it."

Gabe didn't say anything.

That was the strangest part. Ice had known him since they were thirteen, and he'd never once known Gabe not to say anything. He stood in the lift between the sixth floor and the seventh with the phone held out, and his mouth open, and nothing coming out of it. Suzu looked at the floor numbers going up. The corner of his mouth was going up with them.

"Mr Gabe?"

"Yes," said Gabe. "Sorry. Yes. Thank you." He swallowed. "Thank you, Vivian."

"Come and see me at one," said Vivian. "All three of you. Mr Declan knows why." Another pause. "Agree the number first. *In the car.*"

She rang off.

The lift doors opened on their floor. None of them moved. They stood there in a row, the three of them, sweating, until the doors started to close again and Suzu put his arm out and stopped them.

"Two years," said Gabe, to nobody.

"Two years," said Ice.

"I live here," said Gabe. "I *live* here now. Officially. I'm a *resident*." He looked at Ice. Then he grabbed him round the neck and hugged him in the doorway of the lift, sweat and all. "Dec. *Dec.*"

"I know."

"I'm going to cry in the lift."

"Cry in the flat," said Suzu. "The lift has a camera."

***

They went into the eggs at eight anyway, because the reedbacks didn't care about passes and Gabe was fifteen and wanted to be sixteen. They came out at the game's first dusk, at twenty past twelve, when the light went orange over the old fen and the cows lay down in the shallows. Ice drove.

Gabe sat in the front. Suzu sat in the back, behind Ice, the way he always did, with his long legs folded sideways and his head against the window. It was hot, the flat white heat of one o'clock, with the car still an oven from sitting outside the shoplot all morning. Gabe waited until they'd turned onto the main road. Then he clapped his hands once and turned round in his seat.

"Right," he said. "The car. Number."

"Same as Suzu's," said Ice.

"Which is?"

"Not mine to say."

Gabe looked at him. Then he looked into the back seat, at Suzu, who was looking out of the window at a row of shophouses going past, with an expression of perfect peace.

"Suzu," said Gabe.

Suzu went on looking at the shophouses.

"Fine," said Gabe. "Fine. I'll do it myself." He settled himself sideways in the seat, with one arm over the back of it, facing Suzu, the way he'd face a bar before he lifted it. "One."

Nothing.

"Two."

Nothing.

"Three. Four. *Five.*"

Nothing at all. Suzu could have been a photograph of a man looking out of a car window.

"Six," said Gabe. "Seven." He narrowed his eyes. "*Eight.*"

There. Ice saw it in the mirror. It wasn't much. The corner of Suzu's mouth, about a millimetre.

"Nine," said Gabe, slowly.

Another millimetre.

"Ten."

"Don't," said Suzu.

"*TEN,*" said Gabe.

"Don't."

"TEN PER CENT," said Gabe, at the top of his voice, in a car on the road into Malacca at twenty-five to one in the afternoon. "SUZU. You own a TENTH. A TENTH of a COMPANY. You've owned a tenth of a company for TWO WEEKS and you didn't—"

"Don't," said Suzu, to the window. But he was laughing. He was laughing with his whole face, which Ice had seen him do perhaps four times in his life, with his forehead against the glass.

Ice had to pull over.

He did it outside a tyre shop, with the hazard lights going, because he couldn't see the road. The three of them sat there in the car in the heat, with the air conditioning roaring. A man in a vest came out of the tyre shop to see whether they needed a tyre, looked at them, and went back in again.

When they'd stopped, Gabe sat back in his seat and looked out of the windscreen for a while.

"Ten's a lot, Dec," he said. Quietly, for Gabe.

"It's the same as Suzu's."

"Yeah." Gabe was quiet for a bit longer. "Okay," he said. Then: "Yes. Obviously." But not in the voice he'd used in Vivian's office. In a different one.

Ice pulled back out into the traffic.

They'd gone about half a mile before he said the other thing. He'd been carrying it around since Saturday, since the bus station, since a small girl with a backpack had stood in Vivian's office and said *I wasn't going to ask*. He hadn't said it to anyone. He'd wanted to say it here, in the car, where the numbers got agreed.

"And Kotori," he said.

Nobody said anything. In the mirror, Suzu turned his head from the window.

"Vivian said she doesn't get shares," said Gabe slowly. "She said it to her face. *You don't get shares. You get paid.*"

"Vivian said it so she wouldn't feel she had to ask," said Ice. "She didn't ask. She said she was just happy to eat."

"How many," said Suzu.

"Five."

Suzu was quiet for a moment, doing it. Ice could see him doing it in the mirror, the way he'd done it on profit day in the chair by the window, before anyone else in the room had even started.

"Ten, ten and five," said Suzu. "Twenty-five."

"Under thirty," said Ice.

"You did the sum."

"*You* did the sum. On profit day. I've just been carrying it round since."

"And you?" said Gabe.

"Seventy-five."

"Three quarters," said Gabe.

"It's still mine," said Ice. "Most of it. Vivian says it should be. She says somebody has to be the one who decides." He signalled and turned onto the road along the river, past the old godown with the mural on it. "She's right."

"Good," said Suzu, in the back.

They both looked at him in the mirror.

"Somebody has to decide," said Suzu. He'd put his head back against the window and shut his eyes. "It should be the one who does the stupid thing first." A pause. "So that the rest of us know where to stand."

***

Vivian had the orchid on the windowsill again. That was how Ice knew it was going to be a long meeting: she only ever moved the orchid out of the way of paper.

She looked up at the three of them in the doorway. Then she looked at Gabe, who'd been crying a bit in the car and hadn't bothered to hide it, and her face did something very slight, very quickly, and went back.

"Congratulations, Mr Gabe," she said. "Sit down. All of you." There were three chairs. She'd kept the borrowed ones. "Well?"

"Ten," said Gabe.

"Mr Gabe."

"Sorry. Ten. *Ten.*" He sat down. "Same as Suzu."

Vivian looked at Suzu over her glasses. Suzu looked at the orchid. "Yes," said Vivian dryly. "I imagine the whole of Melaka Raya now knows that it's the same as Mr Suzu." She wrote something. "And?"

Ice looked at her. She looked back at him, steadily, over her glasses, the way she'd looked at his bank statements on the day they met. And he understood that she already knew. She'd known since Saturday, probably. She'd known since before he did.

"Five," he said. "For Kotori."

Vivian put her pen down.

"Miss Hayashi is a contractor," she said.

"She can be a contractor and own five per cent."

"She can." Vivian took her glasses off and put them on the desk. "I told her she doesn't get shares, Mr Declan. I told her to her face, in that chair. I told her so that she wouldn't feel she ought to ask."

"She didn't ask."

"No," said Vivian. "She didn't."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, down in the street, a motorbike went past, and the auntie's voice came up the narrow stairs from the kopitiam, shouting an order to somebody in the kitchen.

"Ten, ten and five," said Vivian. "Twenty-five per cent between three foreign nationals. Under the line." She looked at Suzu. "You did the sum."

"On profit day," said Suzu.

"I know. I watched you do it." She picked her glasses up again and put them on, and when she looked at Ice through them she was Vivian again, entirely. "Then Mr Declan holds seventy-five. Three quarters of the company, and every decision that matters in it. You'll want to keep it that way."

"I know."

"Most people give a company away one favour at a time. They don't notice until it's gone." She pulled a form toward her. "You've given away a quarter, to three people. Two of whom would never in their lives have asked you for it."

"Gabe asked," said Ice.

"Mr Gabe asked," agreed Vivian. "In a voice like that." And she smiled, small and brief, the way she had at Kotori. It went away again quickly, like something she wasn't used to keeping out. "Keep the rest, Mr Declan."

"I will."

***

It took an hour.

There was a transfer form for Gabe, from Ice, ten per cent. There was the stamp duty, which Ice said he'd pay, and Vivian said Mr Gabe could perfectly well pay his own. Ice said he'd pay it, and Vivian said it was a matter of principle. Ice said he'd pay it, and Vivian said, "That's three times, Mr Declan. You said it three times last time, too," and wrote it down. There was the register. There was a letter to the Expatriate Services Division, because Vivian liked to tell them things before they found out.

And there was a folder for Kotori. It was empty, because Kotori wasn't there. Vivian clipped a fresh card to the front of it in her small neat capitals.

*SHARES (MISS HAYASHI) — 5%. FROM MR DECLAN. SHE DID NOT ASK.*

"She's twenty," said Vivian. "It's her decision in law. But I'd like her father to read it before she signs it. All of it. I'd like her to have somebody in the room who isn't you." She looked at the calendar on the wall beside her father's photograph. "Saturday. On a video call. Eleven o'clock."

"You said you'd never do another Saturday this month," said Gabe.

"I did say that," said Vivian.

She didn't say anything else. She wrote *SATURDAY 11:00* on the card, under the other line.

Then she slid Gabe's form across the desk and held out a pen. Black.

Gabe signed it. He signed it the way he did everything: enormously, with his whole arm, so that his name ran right across the line and off the end of it and into the margin. Vivian looked at it for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is a very large signature."

"It's a very large day," said Gabe.

***

The auntie caught them at the bottom of the stairs.

She was waiting by the kopitiam counter with a paper bag, the way she'd waited for Suzu two weeks before. She looked at the three of them coming down the narrow stairs, Ice and Suzu and the big one at the back with his red eyes. Her face lit up.

"*The hammer boy,*" she said.

"Hello, auntie," said Gabe.

"You took the fish for the small girl. On Monday." She pushed the paper bag into his hands. Kaya puffs, still warm. "I saw. I was so scared. I shouted at my phone. My husband said, *why are you shouting at a fish*." She patted his arm. "Good boy. You're big. Eat more."

"I'll eat more," said Gabe, holding the bag.

"He'll eat more," said Suzu, with great certainty.

They ate the kaya puffs in the car with the air conditioning on, outside the kopitiam, before Ice started the engine. Nobody said anything. Gabe had three. Suzu had one, slowly. Ice had one and gave the last one to Gabe.

"Ten," said Gabe eventually, with his mouth full, looking out of the windscreen at the river.

"Don't," said Suzu.

"I'm not saying it to *you*. I'm just saying it."

"You're saying it at me."

"I'm saying it near you," said Gabe. "It's different." He finished the kaya puff. Then he looked at Ice, and his face did something it didn't do often, something quiet. "Thanks, Dec."

"You asked," said Ice.

"Yeah," said Gabe. "I did, didn't I." He grinned. "I'll win in the car. I *said* I'd win in the car."

"Nobody wins in the car," said Suzu, with his eyes shut.

They went back in at the game's second dawn, at four o'clock, and Gabe killed two reedback cows on his own before the sun was properly up. Nobody in chat knew why he kept laughing.
