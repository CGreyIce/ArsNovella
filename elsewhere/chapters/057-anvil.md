The trouble with six was that one of them was level eleven.

Ice hadn't thought about it on the bridge. He'd thought about it at four in the morning, in bed, with the fan going round and Gabe snoring through two walls. By the time the sun came up over Fernwick on Monday, the fifty-seventh day, he'd thought about it so much that he'd written it down. He read it out on the Landing to the other five, with the stream on and the lanterns going out one by one along the boardwalks.

"Everybody else is twenty-one to twenty-three," he said. "Gabe's eleven. If we go where we went last week, the first thing that looks at him kills him."

"It wouldn't kill me," said Gabe. "I'd hit it."

"You'd hit it once," said Deston. "Then you'd be in a church in Tinner's Cove for a day, and you'd have lost a level and a bit." He had the book open on his knee, but he wasn't reading it. He was counting on his fingers. "Twenty-five per cent for dying. Ten per cent for every level it's got on you. Something at twenty, you're down a hundred and five."

Gabe thought about that.

"That's a lot of per cent," he said.

"It's all of them," said Deston. "And then five more."

Before they went, Gabe showed them the window.

He'd had it since New Year's Day. He'd hit level ten on the first morning of the year, on a cliff path above Tinner's Cove, against a grey seal the size of a boat that had come up onto the rocks and refused to leave. On the call it had just been Gabe shouting *I'm an ANVIL* and Deston laughing so hard he dropped his phone.

The reedbacks lived in the shallows on the far side of the deep water, where the old fen opened out into a wide grey flat of sedge and standing pools. They were bog-oxen, more or less. Long-horned and shaggy, with mud dried grey on their flanks and reeds growing out of the mud. They grazed with their heads down and their backs up like islands, and they didn't care about anybody until somebody cared about them.

The cows were sixteen. Gabe killed his first one at a quarter to nine.

It took him eleven minutes and he nearly died twice, and he enjoyed every second of it. Ice watched from the front with his shield up, holding the cow's horns off while Gabe worked round the side. Gabe swung the hammer like a man splitting logs. Every time the cow caught him a glancing blow, the hammer head went a shade darker, then a dull red, as if it had been in a forge. And every time it was red and he swung it, the cow's legs went out from under it for a second, and it staggered and bellowed, and Sheng Yang put the long sword into the place where its neck met its shoulders.

They did it six more times before noon. They had a rhythm by the third. Ice in front. Suzu wide on the left, in the water up to his knees, cutting a row through anything that came at them from the side. Sheng Yang behind whatever was looking at Ice, the way Xav used to stand, but not like Xav. Xav had been a knife in the dark. Sheng Yang was a door slamming. Deston on the nearest dry tussock with the book on his knee, counting them in. Kotori on whatever was highest: a hummock, a dead stump, once the bleached ribcage of something much older than a reedback. And always, two paces to the side of her and one in front, Gabe.

By noon he was thirteen.

Ice's own bar had moved by three per cent.

He looked at it once, at a quarter past twelve, while they ate fen bread on a tussock and Gabe told the stream about seals. He looked at the three per cent. Then he closed it, and didn't look again.

The bull came at the second dawn.

They sat out the short night on the tussock, the way you did in the fen, with a lantern and the reedbacks lowing somewhere out in the dark. At four o'clock the sun came up over the flat again, and the bull was where it had been all morning. They'd seen it from the start, a long way out across the flat, standing alone in the biggest pool with the water up to its chest. It was twice the size of the cows. For a while nobody said anything.

The pool settled. The reeds stopped shaking. Out in the wide grey flat, the cows lifted their heads one by one, looked at the six of them standing over the bull, and went back to grazing as if nothing had happened.

Gabe sat down in the shallows. He sat right down in the water, with his hammer across his knees, steaming as the white went out of it. He looked at his shield arm and at the shield, which had a row of holes along the rim like a bread knife. Then he started laughing. He laughed until he had to lie back in the water and laugh at the sky.

Kotori came down off the stump.

She came slowly. She stood in the shallows beside him, with her staff and her bell, looking down at him, and her round face was white.

"That was mine," she said. "That was going to be me. You took it."

"Yeah," said Gabe, from the water, still laughing a bit.

"You were at six per cent. You'd have lost a whole level. More. You'd have—"

"That's what it said I'm for," said Gabe.

He said it simply, lying in the water, the way he'd said *the hammer* at the bus station. As if it were the obvious thing, the thing anybody would do. Kotori looked at him for a long moment. Then her face did the thing Ice had seen it do at the mamak, over a roti canai, and she sat down in the water beside him, robe and all.

"Sor—" She stopped herself, the way she mostly could now. "Thank you."

"Don't be sorry," said Gabe anyway. "Be on your post." He sat up and shook the water out of his hair like a dog. "And next time, see the fish."

"I'll see the fish," said Kotori.

"Nobody could have seen the fish," said Sheng Yang, cleaning the long sword on a handful of sedge. He looked at Gabe for a moment, an easy, measuring look. Ice had seen it before, at the old ford, across the water, on Xav. "Two paces," Sheng Yang said. "You made it in one."

"I've got long legs," said Gabe.

"You haven't," said Deston. "You've got average legs. You've got a very long *step*." He closed the book. His own bar was on its way back up from where Share the Weight had taken it, and he was grinning. "The back row," he said. "All three of us. In the same fight. In a game. Twenty years later."

"Thirteen years," said Ice.

"*Twenty* years," said Deston. "It feels like twenty. Let me have it."
