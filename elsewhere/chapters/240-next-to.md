Kei didn't know how to draw it.

He'd been trying all week. At the head of the long table, with the new sheet unrolled in front of him and his tea going cold, the way it always did. With a pencil, and a rubber, and Higu across the table saying nothing and watching him rub things out.

He knew how to draw Constellation. He'd drawn it a hundred times. A dot for every person, and a line between every two who knew each other. And when you drew enough lines, a shape came out of them that nobody had put there. A star. It was why he'd called it that, the first winter, when it was him and Maigo and Niko and Drew and some dots on a page. *Every star is someone.*

Sixty-one dots now. And Isa, who wasn't on the Roll, and was a dot anyway, because Kei said so. And Ash, at the milestone, who was a dot in pencil, very small, with a dotted line to Kite.

He didn't know how to draw Meridian.

Ten thousand people. He couldn't draw ten thousand dots. He'd tried, on Tuesday, a corner of it, and given up after four hundred. And even if he could, where would they go? Inside the star, and it wasn't Constellation any more. It was Meridian with a star in it. Under it, and it was the same thing the other way up.

*Next to*, she'd said. On the road, to Xav. *Not under. Not inside. Next to.*

He didn't know how to draw *next to*.

***

"Draw her," said Higu, on Saturday night.

Kei looked up.

"You're trying to draw Meridian," said Higu. She had her chin on her hand. She'd been watching him rub things out for six days. "You can't draw Meridian. Nobody could. It's ten thousand people in sixty-one Halls on half the world." She looked at the sheet. "So don't. Draw her."

"She's one dot."

"Yes."

"Meridian's not one dot."

"Neither was Constellation," said Higu. "When you started. It was you and some people who knew each other." She reached across the table and tapped the place on the sheet where the first dot had been, the first winter, before there was a star. His own. "You didn't draw a guild. You drew you. And then a line to Maigo. And then the guild came out of the lines."

Kei looked at her for a long time.

"Hm," he said.

***

The long table was full on Sunday. It always was, on a Sunday. More than full: Riverbank's three home from Masmark in their own chairs for the first time since April. And at the near end, by Moonlight Harvest's nine, a small old chair with a mended seat, with a small woman in plain blue sitting in it, her feet flat on the floor.

And behind her, standing, because there wasn't a chair for him yet, Vane.

Ama Corrie came down the long table with her broom at the second dawn, the way she always did on a Sunday. Under, not round. She swept under the small chair with Aurelie in it, very carefully, and Aurelie lifted her feet without being asked, the way Ice had for Milli and Hana. Ama Corrie looked at her feet in the air. Then at her face.

"*Hm*," said Ama Corrie, and went on.

When she'd done all sixty-one and the chair by the north door, she sat down on the window seat with the broom across her knees. And looked at Kei, at the head of the table. The way she did when something was about to happen in her room and she wanted to see it.

Kei put his pencil down.

Aurelie stood up.

***

She'd been going to stand at the head of the table. Kei had seen her think about it on Wednesday. She'd stood in the banner room door and looked at the head of the long table, where he sat, the way a person who'd sat at the head of every table for six years would look at it. And then at the small chair at the near end. And she'd gone and sat in the small chair, and stayed there all week.

She stood up from it now. At the near end, by the window, beside the first of the nine.

The banner room went quiet.

"I'm not good at this," said Aurelie.

Nobody said anything. That was the round town. It let you start.

"I've run a guild for six years," said Aurelie. "Two games. Ten thousand people. I'm good at that. I'm good at Halls and barges and plans and choosing twenty out of two hundred." She looked down the long table. At all of them. "I'm not good at asking for things. I've never had to. People ask me."

She stopped. She looked at her hands. Then she took them out of the pockets of her plain blue coat and let them hang at her sides, where everybody could see them. The way Kite stood in the gate. She'd practised it all week, Kite said. Very badly.

"In February," she said, "Harlan came to your gate and asked if Meridian could have you. Your round town. A chapter. Under us." She looked at Kei. "You said no."

"I said ask me again," said Kei.

"You said *ask me again when we're there*," said Aurelie. "In Masmark. And then in Masmark you went up a bell tower and rang it six seconds before me. And then you put three of your people in a channel under a harbour. And when a ship was in the way you wrote to me. *Would you stand next to us?*" She swallowed. "And I said yes in eleven minutes. And brought three barges round a mole and sat them in front of a cave mouth all night. And I've never been so happy in a guild in my life."

The banner room didn't make a sound.

"So I walked here," said Aurelie. "Fourteen days. To ask you back."

***

She looked at Kei.

He'd known she would. He'd known since Saturday; he'd told the door. He sat at the head of the table with his hands flat on the sheet and looked back at her. And waited. Not deciding. The way Ice waited on the back foot. A breath.

"Would you stand next to us?" said Aurelie.

She said it very plainly. Not loud. The way Ice said things.

"Not under. Not inside. We don't want your round town. We don't want your banner, or your Roll, or your sixty-one. They're yours." She glanced, very briefly, at the old woman by the north door, asleep in her chair with the jar in front of her. "We want to stand next to you. When you need somebody, you write, and we come. When we need somebody, we write, and you come. If you want to." She looked at her own hands at her sides. "That's all. That's what I walked here for."

Kei didn't say anything.

He looked at her for a long time. Then he looked down the long table. At all of them. Sixty-one, and Isa, and Ama Corrie on the window seat with her broom.

"Hm," said Kei.

And then he did what he always did. What he'd done for Kite, the first winter, round the fire. For Rafi, at the gate, and Bram and Joy, and Hana's five, and Thea's six. What Constellation did, when somebody asked to stand somewhere.

He asked everybody.

***

"Maigo," said Kei.

He started with Maigo because Maigo had said no to Kite, the first winter. Not unkindly. Maigo didn't say anything unkindly. But she'd said no, round the fire, sharply, when everybody else was saying yes, because somebody had to. *He took fourteen people's days. I'd like to know why we're sure.* And the long table had argued for an hour, and been better for it.

Maigo looked at Aurelie down the long table.

"In February," she said, "Harlan said *have*. Not *stand next to*. He said could Meridian *have* us."

"He did," said Aurelie.

"You wanted to absorb us."

"I wanted to absorb everybody," said Aurelie. "It's what you do with ten thousand people. You get more." She didn't look away. "I'd never sat in a chair that fit, in February."

Maigo looked at the small chair with the mended seat. At Ama Corrie, on the window seat, who looked back at her over her spectacles and said nothing.

"What changed?" said Maigo.

Aurelie thought about it. Kei watched her think. She was honest; he'd noticed that all week. She didn't say the thing that would work. She said the thing that was true, and then waited to see if it worked.

"I was second," said Aurelie. "By six seconds. To him." She nodded at the first of the nine, where Ice was sitting with his feet off the rung for once, very still. "And he didn't say *good for them*. He wanted to. I watched him want to, at the top of the steps, in front of half of Masmark. And he didn't. Because I'd asked him not to." She paused. "He said *you were first up the stair*."

Maigo didn't say anything.

"Nobody had ever been on my side," said Aurelie, "and beaten me at the same time." She looked at Maigo. "I didn't know you could do both. I'd like to be next to people who can."

Maigo looked at her for a long time.

"Yes," said Maigo.

***

It went down the long table. It took most of the morning. Kei asked every one of them, the way he'd asked about Kite, about Rafi, about everybody. Most of them said yes. Some of them said other things first.

Dennis, at Moonlight Harvest's end, with his pencil ledger open: "What does it cost?"

"Nothing," said Aurelie.

"Then it's worth exactly what each of us puts in," said Dennis. "That's the best kind of deal there is, darling. I'm in."

Rachael: "If they need us on the longest night and we need us on the longest night, who goes?"

"We do," said Aurelie. "This year. You've got the longest night here, and a road of lights, and the jar." She glanced at the north door again. "We'll send twenty. To hold one of your roads. Harlan will bring them. I'll stay." She looked at Rachael. "Next year, ask me again."

"Fine," said Rachael. "Finally."

Gabe, much too loudly, before Kei had even said his name: "*YES*." And then, when everybody looked at him: "Sorry. Yes. *Yes*. Dec's old vice is standing *right there*, Kei, of course yes."

Tiger, after a long time: "Do they know about the first mile?"

"Tell me about it," said Aurelie.

"It's the bit of the north road past Kite's milestone," said Tiger. "On the longest night. When the road runs out of lamps. Somebody has to stand in it." He looked at his hands. "I stood in it last year. Saying *not yet*. Twenty minutes, on my own, till somebody called me in." He looked at her. "If you're next to us, you'll be next to that."

Aurelie looked at him for a very long time.

"Then I'd like to be," she said.

Isa, awake in her chair by the north door, who wasn't on the Roll and wasn't asked, said "*Hm*" anyway, and everybody heard it.

***

Ice, last.

Kei had left him to last on purpose. Not because it mattered more. Because he knew what Ice would do if he asked him first: say *okay*, and then the whole long table would look at him and say *okay* too. It wasn't fair to anybody. It wasn't fair to him.

"Ice," said Kei.

Ice looked at him from the first of the nine.

He didn't say *okay*. Kei watched him not. He sat with his feet on the floor, not on the rung, and his cup on the table, and Hyvred beside him with her hand on his arm. And thought about it, properly. Kei could see him do it.

"I was in a guild once," said Ice. "A long time ago. In a war."

The banner room was very quiet.

"Everybody was at the front," he said. "Twenty of us. Going at everybody. All the time." He looked down the table at Aurelie, standing by her small chair, and past her at Vane behind it. "Nobody stood next to anybody. There wasn't a next to. There was just the front. And I thought that was what a guild was."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Then I came here," he said. "And Kei put me at the front, and people stood behind me. And beside me. And I didn't turn round for a year and a half, so I didn't see." He looked at Vane. "And somebody told me on a harbour wall in July that he'd been next to me the whole war. And I hadn't seen that either."

Vane, behind Aurelie's chair, very still, looked at the floor.

"I'd like to be in a guild that's next to somebody," said Ice. "On purpose. Where everybody can see it." He looked at Kei. "That's a yes."

Kei looked at him for a long time.

"Thank you," said Kei.

***

He stood up then. He didn't usually. He did things from his chair, at the head of the table, with his maps. He stood up now and looked down the long table at Aurelie, at the near end, standing beside the first of the nine with her hands at her sides.

"Yes," said Kei. "We'll stand next to you."

A window came. Not for him; for everybody. Kei had never seen one like it. It came up in the corner of every eye in the banner room at once, plain, small:

[ Constellation · Meridian ]

[ *An accord. Neither under. Neither inside.* ]

[ *Next to.* ]

The banner room let out its breath.

Not like the gate in January. Not like Milli standing up. Quieter. Like a room full of people who'd just watched something get put down very carefully on a table, and were looking at it.

Aurelie stood there with the window in front of her face. Kei saw her read it. Twice. Then she sat down in her small chair, very suddenly, as if her legs had decided for her. Her feet flat on the floor.

Vane, behind her, put his hand on the back of the chair. Not on her. On the chair.

"We're fine," said Vane.

And the whole long table, who had heard about Vane and his two words for a year, laughed. All at once. Even Ama Corrie, on the window seat, made a sound that might have been one.

***

He went up the two hundred and twelve at the third dawn with Higu, to the eye, the way they did.

The bench was there, on the south side, pale ash, with *Somewhere* on it. He didn't sit on it. It wasn't for him. He sat on the parapet on the north side, by his brass arm, with the sheet on his knee and Higu beside him and a lamp. And the north road going away up the moor, ninety-four lamps, leaning.

More, Tiger said, every week.

He unrolled the sheet. Constellation. Sixty-one dots and the lines between them and the star that came out of the lines. Isa in pencil. Ash in pencil, with her dotted line to Kite, and now a second dotted line, to Drew.

He took the pencil and drew a dot. Outside the star. Beside it. Not touching.

"Her," said Higu.

"Her," said Kei.

He looked at it. One dot. Next to sixty-one. It looked very small.

"Now a line," said Higu.

"Between who?"

"Between her and you," said Higu. "You asked. She asked back. That's a line."

Kei put the pencil on his own dot, at the top of the star, where it had started in the first winter. And drew a line out of the star, to the dot beside it.

And stopped.

***

Because there were already lines.

He hadn't seen them. He'd been trying to draw Meridian all week, ten thousand dots, and hadn't looked at what was already on the page. He looked now. In the lamplight, at the eye, with Higu beside him.

From Ice's dot, in the first of the nine, there was already a line going out of the star. He'd drawn it in October, a year ago, when Harlan came to the gate and pointed and said *GM?* A line to Harlan, outside the star, in pencil. He'd drawn it because it was true.

And from Harlan's dot, a line to Vane's. He'd drawn that in March, when Vane came to the netloft door and said *the GM, and Harlan, don't tell Harlan*.

And from Vane's dot, a line to Ice's. Back into the star. Drawn on a harbour wall in July. *I was next to you.*

And from Xav's dot, a line out to Kestrel. From Drew's, a line out to Lucan, on a shingle, all night, talking about feet. From Rafi's, a line to a fish man. From Kite's, to Ash.

Lines. Going out of the star, everywhere. To people next to it. He'd drawn every one of them, because they were true, and never once looked at what they made.

He looked now.

Outside the star, beside it, the lines had made something. Not a star. Not yet. Just the start of one. Some dots, and lines between them, coming out of Constellation the way Constellation had come out of him and Maigo, the first winter. Harlan and Vane and Aurelie. Lucan and Kestrel and Fenn and Brann. Marrok, on a netloft step, in pencil, with a dotted line to Wendy.

It had already been drawn. He just hadn't looked.

"*Oh*," said Kei.

Higu leaned over and looked.

She didn't say anything for a long time. She looked at the lines going out of the star to the new dots beside it. At the shape they were starting to make, out there, next to.

"It's catching," said Higu.

Kei laughed. Out loud, at the eye, at the third dawn, which he never did. And drew the last line, from his own dot, to the small dot beside the star that was a woman in a mended chair. And wrote under it, very small, in the place where Curse would have put a label, one word.
