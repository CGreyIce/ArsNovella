Xav was in the ring at first dawn, alone, practising a back foot.

Ice saw him from the south steps. He'd come out of the Hall on his way to the north tunnel, the way he did every morning. And there was Xav, in the middle of the round market, inside the nine dark stones, in the grey light. Nobody else. The fountain. The pigeon asleep on its stone. And Xav, with his two blades in his hands, stepping.

Not fighting. Stepping. One step, forward, and the back foot staying. A breath. Then coming round. Then again. Forward, the back foot staying, a breath, round. Again. Again. He'd drawn a little chalk mark on the paving where the back foot went, and he was putting it there every time, exactly, the way you'd practise a chord.

Ice stood at the top of the steps and watched him for a while.

He'd stood on a hundred mornings at the side of somewhere and watched Xav practise. In a dozen games, on a dozen servers that weren't there any more. He knew what it looked like when Xav was doing it for fun, and when he was doing it because Riverbank needed it, and when he was doing it because he'd been told to. This wasn't any of those.

This was the face from the bottom of the Seven Below. *Again. A month. I want to be ready.*

He went down the steps. He went the long way round the market, by the east side, so he wouldn't walk past the ring. At the corner by the chestnut stall, where the shutter still said KESTREL WAS HERE in chalk, Xav said, without looking up and without stopping:

"Don't."

"I wasn't."

"You were going to say something."

"I was going to go to the tunnel," said Ice.

"Good," said Xav. Step. The back foot staying. A breath. "Go to the tunnel."

Ice went to the tunnel.

He didn't say anything. But he went a little faster than usual, all the way to the north door, and he knew Xav had seen.

***

Hyvred was waiting at the hatch.

She was sitting on the top step of Ama Corrie's stair with her bow across her knees, the way she'd sat outside the banner room on the night the ten came home. She looked up when he came across the Hall. Her gold horns caught the lamplight from the counter.

"Kei says nobody alone," she said, before he could say hello.

"For the longest night."

"He says *nobody alone*," said Hyvred. "He didn't say for when. He just said it." She stood up. "So I'm coming. To the tunnel. I'll call it."

Ice looked at her.

He'd been going down the north tunnel alone every morning for months. That was how he levelled. That was how he'd always levelled, in every game, since he was a boy. On his own, in the dark, in the quiet, without anybody watching. Moonlight Harvest knew that. Constellation knew it. Nobody had ever offered to come.

"It's cold at the end," he said.

"I know. You said. On stream." She was looking at the hatch, not at him. "The bit where the old stone meets the new. You sit there." She paused. "Something's going to come up out of there one of these nights. All the Delvers have moved. Did you notice? They used to sleep in the side-holes by the end. They've all gone back up. Past the stream." She looked at him at last. "Things move away from where something's coming. I can see it on the map. I can see everything."

He thought about it.

She was right. He'd noticed the Delvers moving. He hadn't thought about why.

"Okay," said Ice.

Hyvred's tail did a thing.

He saw it. He couldn't not see it. It was a long tail, white at the tip, the kind the VTubers' models had, and she'd had it since the first day. For months it had been doing things when he was in the room that he hadn't understood. Going stiff. Going round the leg of a chair. Once, in the banner room, going straight up and bottle-brushing like a startled cat, while Xola laughed so hard she cried and Hyv shouted that she was going to hit him.

It did one of those things now. It went round her own ankle, twice, tight, as if it was holding on to something.

He looked at it. Then at her face, which had gone red to the horns.

And he knew.

He'd known for a while, if he was honest. He wasn't stupid. He was slow about some things, everyone said so, but he wasn't stupid. He'd known since the step outside the banner room door, the night the ten came home. Since *No*. Since Xola's face across the room, looking at him with the most enormous delight. Since Bee moving one wing, without looking up, so there was room behind it if anybody needed it. Since the tail. You couldn't not know, eventually. Not with the tail.

He'd known, and he hadn't said anything. He didn't say anything now.

"Come on, then," said Ice.

He went down the stair the same as always, with the lamp, minding the third step. She came after him. He held the lamp the same way he always held it, a little out to the side, so the light went where the person behind him would put their feet.

He'd always done that. He didn't do it any differently. He wasn't going to.

***

She was very good. He'd known she would be. She'd called the left heart at the Elm Below, at Elmcross, on the fifth try, and he'd gone without turning his head.

She called the north tunnel the same way. From thirty paces back, from the shadows, with her bow up and her eyes that could see everything, edge to edge, all of it at once.

"*Two. Right side-hole. Low.*"

He went right. Two Delvers came out of the side-hole, low, exactly where she'd said, and he was already there.

"*Behind you. Ceiling.*"

He didn't look. He put the shield up behind his head without turning, and something fell on it out of the dark ceiling and slid off, and he finished it on the floor.

"*Left. The big one. It's waiting.*"

He went left. It was waiting. It stopped waiting.

They did the whole north tunnel in half the time it took him alone. He'd known they would. It wasn't that he couldn't see things coming; he could. It was that she saw them *first*, before they'd moved, the way he saw people before they'd decided. And when somebody saw it first and told you, you didn't have to spend anything on seeing it. You could spend all of it on being there.

At the end, at the cold place, where the old stone met the new, they stopped.

It was colder than it had ever been. He could feel it through his boots. The stream ran past here too, a branch of it, in its groove in the floor, black water. Past it, the tunnel went on into the old stone, smooth and pale, and then stopped. A blank wall, three hundred years old, nothing on the other side of it but the north.

He sat down with his back to the wall, the way he always did. After a moment she sat down too. Not close. Four paces off, against the other side, with her bow across her knees.

"You sit here," she said. "Every morning."

"Most mornings."

"Why?"

He thought about it.

"It's like the stone in the fen," he said. "At Fernwick. The heron-stone. I used to sit with my back to it. You can feel what's coming." He shrugged. "You wait on the back foot."

Hyvred was quiet for a long time.

"I watched you do that," she said. "In the winter. Before I was in the game. On your stream. Every night, the fen, the stone." She was looking at the black water in the groove. "I used to fall asleep with it on. You sitting there. Not saying anything. Just sitting with your back to a stone in the dark, waiting." She paused. "Nobody else's stream was ever that quiet."

He didn't say anything. He didn't know what to say. He knew that she'd watched since the winter, since before she was in the game. He knew that she'd turned notifications on for him, the first person she ever had. Tif had told him, a long time ago, and he'd thought it was nice. He'd thought it was the sort of thing people did.

He knew more than that now.

He sat with his back to the cold wall and looked at the black water and didn't say anything, the same as always. Four paces off, Hyvred sat with her bow across her knees, and her tail was very still.

"Thank you for calling it," said Ice.

"*No problem*," said Hyvred, much too loudly, and then put her face in her hands.

***

It came on Wednesday, at the second dusk, while they were in the tunnel again.

He knew it was coming the way everybody knew things were coming in the game: the world went still. All at once. Every traveller everywhere stopped at the same moment and looked at nothing. And in the corner of his eye, in the dark under the round town, a window opened.

[ The first traveller in Elsewhere has reached the fiftieth level.
Lucan · Sable Court ]

And under it:

[ Dawnmere has opened its gates.
The first of the great cities is awake. ]

[ This message has been shown to every traveller in Elsewhere. ]

He read it. Then he read it again.

Behind him, thirty paces back, Hyvred had stopped calling.

"*Lucan*," she said. "Is that—"

"Sable Court," said Ice. "Out east. Harlan said they were nearly there."

He didn't know there was a clip. He didn't want to know. He'd been younger, then, and he'd minded about things like the board, and about who was above him and who was below. He'd stayed up for it. He'd fought Lucan a few times, on a bridge over a gorge with the wind going through it. He didn't remember how most of them had ended, and didn't want to.

He didn't mind now. That was the difference. Somewhere between then and the fen, he'd stopped minding.

"Good for them," he said again, to nobody. And then he got up, and went back to the tunnel, and killed Delvers until the window told him to rest.

Lucan posted on the World board that night.

It was short. Everything Sable Court did was short. They were an elite guild, like VANTA; they didn't do speeches. Somebody put the link in chat, and Tif didn't delete it, so Ice read it on the walk back up the tunnel.

*Fifty. Dawnmere's open. Come when you're ready; it's very big.*
*— L.*
*(To the tank in the round town: you're slow.)*

He stopped in the tunnel.

Hyvred, behind him, stopped too.

"*What*," she said. "*He said*— Did he just— He called you *slow*." She sounded outraged on his behalf. "In front of *everyone*. He's never even *met* you here."

"He's met me," said Ice. "Somewhere else." He read it again. "People say it. When they're glad to see you." He shrugged. "Or when they're ahead."

"Which is it?"

Ice thought about it, in the dark, with the lamp.

"Both," he said. And he laughed, out loud, properly, in the north tunnel. The sound went away down the dark toward the cold place and the wall and the north beyond it. He hadn't meant to. Behind him, Hyvred made a small sound, and he didn't turn round to see what her face was doing. He didn't need to.

He read the letter again that night, in the banner room. The one from Aurelie, the one he kept folded small in his pocket with everything else.

*At the first city, when it opens. He can come to me.*

The first city was open. It was called Dawnmere and it was on the far side of the map, past everything. Sable Court's, not Meridian's; Meridian was west. She'd said *the first city*. She hadn't said whose. He thought she'd done that on purpose. He thought she was probably a person who did most things on purpose.

He thought about Dawnmere. About Lucan on a bridge over a gorge, never stopping moving. About Harlan on the top step, saying *you're not running it?* and the woman in Meridian's third Hall who wouldn't say his name. About all the people, all over the world, on the far side of everything, who'd known him somewhere else. Somewhere before the fen.

None of them were here.

He looked down the long table. Sixty chairs, most of them sat in, the noise of the evening. Gabe telling somebody about SimpleMMO again. Leaka telling Jared she was going to Dawnmere to fight a man called Lucan. Riverbank at the long end of the second table, Malcolm cleaning his stick. Xav beside him with a paper sketchbook, a real one, which nobody in the game had ever seen before. He must have bought it somewhere. He was drawing feet.

Thea's six by the window. Hana's five. Kei at the head with his map, drawing threes.

Hyvred, beside Bee, in her chair. Not looking at him. Very carefully not looking at him.

He didn't look at her either. He looked at the chair at the north end of the room, by the north door, set apart, facing north. For nobody yet.

*Somewhere else*, he'd told Salty, by the fountain, with Meridian's banner going away down the west road. *And this is here.*

He wasn't going to Dawnmere. Not this year. He was going to sit in the first of the nine until the longest night, and go down the north tunnel every morning, and come back up, and sweep under the chairs on Sunday. And then on the longest night he was going to stand in the round town with fifty-nine other people in threes, and keep the lamps.

He'd been first at a lot of things. He'd been first at things in games nobody here had ever heard of, and in games everybody had, and on this one's wall at the very top. He didn't need to be first at this. He'd stopped needing to be first at things a long time ago, somewhere around a fen.

He needed to be *here*.

"Good for them," said Ice, very quietly, to the room. Nobody heard. That was fine.

***

Kei came down the table at about eleven with his map rolled under his arm and stood by the first of the nine.

"Threes," said Kei.

"Yes."

"I'm putting the list up on Monday." Kei looked at the map, and then at him. "I've done fifty-seven of the sixty. The others are hard."

"Which others?"

"Yours," said Kei.

Ice looked at him.

"Everybody wants to be in yours," said Kei. "And nobody wants to ask. And I don't want to put anyone in it without them asking." He shrugged. "So I've left it blank. Yours and two spaces. I'm putting it up blank on Monday, and whoever writes themselves in, writes themselves in." He paused. "Is that all right?"

Ice thought about it. About a list on the board by the south door. And his name, with two spaces after it, blank.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," said Kei. And then, very quietly, the way Kei said the things he'd thought about for a long time first: "I think somebody will."

He went back up the table to the head.

Ice sat in the first of the nine with his feet on the rung of the next chair. Down the room, beside Bee, Hyvred had gone completely still. Not looking at him. Bee, without looking up from her drawing, moved one wing.

He saw it. He didn't say anything.

He held the cup the same as always.
