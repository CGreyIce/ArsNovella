Out there, for most of her life, Hyvred had seen the world down a tube.

One thing at a time. A small round window in the middle of everything dark, and whatever was in it, and nothing at the sides. She'd learned to turn her head a lot. She'd learned to listen. She'd learned where things were in her room by heart, because she couldn't see them unless she was looking straight at them. She'd learned to be very quick and very careful, both at once, all the time.

Then the game. Edge to edge. All of it, at once, the first time she opened her eyes in a beginner town. She'd sat down in the road and cried.

Then the surgery, a year and a half ago, and the edges out there too. Wobbly at first, then less. A wall behind Bee on a video call. The sky round a window. Both sides of the road at once, on a bus, in June.

She'd gone from the tube to everything. In two years.

And now, past the edge, on a rope, in the nothing, she had nothing at all.

***

It wasn't like the tube. She'd thought it would be, the first night. She'd stepped past the twenty-second stone with *Pip* in her hand, lumpy and honest, and thought: *I know this. I've done this my whole life. I can do this.*

She couldn't. It wasn't the same.

The tube had always had something in it. One thing. Whatever you turned your head to. You could choose. You turned, and there it was, small and round and bright in the middle of the dark. A face, a door, a cup. You saw it.

Out here there was nothing to turn to.

She turned her head and there was nothing. She turned it the other way and there was nothing. Up, down. Her own hand. Nothing. The candle in it, a small yellow flame on the end of nothing, and that was all. It didn't light anything. There was nothing for it to light.

She'd never in her life had nothing to look at.

She hadn't known that was the thing she was frightened of. She'd thought she was frightened of the dark. She'd been frightened of the dark her whole life, out there, at the edges of the tube, where it was. But the dark had always been round something. Round the tube, round the window, round the face.

This wasn't round anything.

***

She listened. Kotori had taught her. In the Seven Below, in October, with the lamp out. *I don't need to see you. I can hear where the beat lands.*

It hadn't worked, the first night. The beat went out and didn't come back. There was nothing for it to come back off.

Then Hako had started talking, and it had.

She listened to Hako every night now. All night. The voice, smooth and British and unhurried, going on and on, about all of them. And she listened to the answers. Xola laughing. Slep saying *thank you* with dignity. Thea and Gigi, who'd stopped being near and started being close at the twenty-second stone, saying *Hako* at the same time.

And the swamp guy, at the front, saying very little.

She listened to him more than anybody.

***

He walked differently, out here.

She'd watched him walk for two years. In the game, on stream, before that. On a goose road and a crater wall and the stair of a bell tower. She knew how he walked the way she knew her own room. Steady, never fast. The back foot coming round a breath late. Stopping at the top of things to look. Slow down steps.

Out here she couldn't see him. She could only hear. And he walked differently.

Slower. Much slower. And with stops in it that weren't for looking, because there was nothing to look at. He'd stop, every forty or fifty steps, for a few seconds. And then go on. She'd listened for nights before she understood what the stops were.

He was feeling the ground.

Not with his hands. With his feet. He'd put his front foot down and leave it there and not put his weight on it. And wait. A breath. On the back foot. The way he'd waited on a stone in a fen for a year and a half, for things to come up out of the water. And then, when the ground had told him whatever it told him, he'd go. Or he'd say *left*. And the whole rope would go left, very slightly, and he'd go on.

He was doing the fen. Out here. With his feet.

"How do you know?" she said, on the third night, when the rope had gone left for the fourth time.

He didn't answer straight away. He never did. She heard him stop, ahead, two places on the rope. Then go on.

"It's softer on the right," he said. "A bit. Like the fen. Where the black water was under the reeds."

"You can feel that?"

"I spent a year and a half feeling it," said the swamp guy. "In the fen. On the first day I went in up to my waist. After that I learned."

She thought about that for a long time.

***

On the fourth night after Year Two, Kotori gave her tea.

It was the coldest night. Hyvred didn't know how she knew. There was no wind, no frost, nothing. Just cold, getting into her through her boots and her gloves and her horns. In the game she'd never been cold like that. Not on the high fells. Not under the lake.

They stopped for a few minutes at about three in the morning. The swamp guy said *stop*, and they did, and stood on the rope in the nothing, and Kotori came back down the line.

Hyvred heard her come. The beat, *ting*, getting closer. On Suzu, then on nothing, then on her. And then a hand, finding hers in the nothing, and putting something in it.

A cup. Warm.

"Drink," said Kotori.

Hyvred drank.

It was horrible. It was the horrible one, the dark one, from the leaf that grew on the backs of the waystones. The one Isa had said her mother would have hated. It went down her like something lit.

[ *It stays warm a long time. Longer than you'd think. It remembers being near a light.* ]

"It's warm," said Hyvred.

"It's been in the tin for four hours," said Kotori. "It's still warm." She was very close; Hyvred could hear her breathing. "It remembers the lamps. On the north road. Where the leaf grew."

Hyvred held the cup in both hands in the nothing and drank the rest of it, slowly. She couldn't see the cup. She couldn't see Kotori. She could feel the warm going down into her. And she thought about a road of ninety-four lamps a very long way behind them, leaning. Every one of them lit.

"Thank you," said Hyvred.

"You're welcome," said Kotori, and didn't say sorry. And went on down the line with the tin, *ting*, *ting*, to Thea and Gigi.

***

She'd been together with the swamp guy for two months.

Out loud, she meant. Since a bench in a park in Singapore, under a tree, with the light coming down through the leaves in pieces. Since *are we together*. Since *say it properly*. Since she'd put both hands flat on his chest and pushed.

She thought about that a lot. Out here, in the nothing, with nothing else to think about. She'd been thinking about it for two months.

She'd gone red every single time. Two months of it.

And out here, for the first time in her life, nobody could see.

She'd always gone red. Out there, in the tube. In here, edge to edge, in front of everybody. At the gate. At the bottom of the lake. On the bench, after. *I go first.* Her face had always done it, and somebody had always been looking. Bee, or Thea, or Xola, or chat. Or him.

Out here she went red in the nothing, on a rope, thinking about a bench. And nobody could see it at all.

She found she didn't mind. She found she liked it. She walked along the rope in the nothing, thinking about it, red to the horns, and nobody knew.

"You've gone quiet," said Hako, behind her somewhere, in the voice. "*The one with the horns has not spoken in some time. Observers suspect she is thinking about a bench.*"

"*HAKO*."

The whole rope laughed. She heard every one of them. Exactly where.

***

The ring, at a quarter to eight. Every morning.

She'd stopped dreading it. She'd dreaded it the first few mornings. Nine candles on the ground and the watch in the middle, and the egg taking them, and the nothing staying. She'd lain in her bed out there, in Singapore, all day, thinking about the ring with nobody in it.

Then the first evening she'd come back and the watch was still lit, standing straight up, exactly as tall. And she'd stopped dreading it.

She sat beside him in the ring now. Every morning. Not on the rope, where she was two places behind him. In the ring, the nine of them sitting close, facing in, she sat beside him. Their shoulders touching. And the candles lit their faces from underneath, very faint, and she could see him.

Not all of him. Not edge to edge. His face, a bit, from below, in the light of nine candles. The line of his jaw. His eyes, when he turned them to look at her. Like the tube. One thing, small and round and bright, in the middle of everything dark.

She'd hated the tube her whole life.

She didn't hate this.

"I can see you," she said. On the seventh morning after Year Two. Very quietly. "A bit."

He turned his head.

"Like the tube," said Hyvred. "Out there. Before. Just your face. Nothing else." She swallowed. "I used to hate it. Only seeing one thing."

"And now?"

She looked at him. His face, from below, lit by nine candles and a watch.

"Now it's you," she said.

He didn't say anything for a long time. Then he put his hand out, in the ring, on the ground between them, palm up. The way he'd held it out by a fish cart in February.

She put hers in it.

"*Set one to watch*," said Suzu, across the ring, lighting the watch.

"*So the rest may sleep*," said nine people. Two of them holding hands.

***

"Are you frightened?" she said, on the ninth night. On the rope. In the nothing.

She'd asked him before. In the game, at the gate, on the bridge. He'd always said *no*, and she'd always said *liar*, and he'd always said *a bit*.

"No," said the swamp guy.

"Liar."

"No," he said again. And something in his voice made her stop walking. The rope went tight between them, through Kotori and Suzu. He stopped too.

"I'm not," he said. "I thought I would be. Isa said *you'll be at the front of nothing*. On the stair. I thought it would be like the war. Everybody at the front, nobody beside." He was quiet. "It's not."

"What's it like?"

He thought about it. She heard him think. She'd learned what his thinking sounded like, out here, these last two weeks. A breath held, very slightly. Like the back foot.

"Restful," he said.

"*Restful*?"

"Hako said it, the first night," said the swamp guy. "*At the front of nothing, which he appears to find rather restful.* He was right." He started walking again. Slowly. Feeling the ground. "There's nothing coming at me. Out here. For the first time. In any game. In my life, nearly." He was quiet. "There's always been something coming. At the front. That's what the front is. Things come at it, and you hold." He put his foot down, and waited, and went on. "Out here there's nothing. Nothing coming. Nothing to hold. Just the ground, and the rope." He paused. "And you, two places back, listening."

Hyvred didn't say anything.

She walked along the rope in the nothing, two places behind him. Listening to him walk. Slowly, with his feet, the way he'd learned in a fen.

*Restful*, she thought. *He finds this restful.*

It was the most frightening place she'd ever been. And he'd never been so at peace.

She thought maybe that was what it meant, being two people. That the same nothing could be both.

***

On the tenth night, the silence changed.

She heard it at about midnight. Not a sound. The other thing. The silence itself, the nothing, the not-there. It had sounded the same for nineteen nights. She'd got used to it. It was like a room you'd lived in your whole life; you stopped hearing the room.

At midnight on the tenth night after Year Two, the room changed.

She didn't know how to say it. She tried, afterward, to Bee, on a call, and couldn't. It was as if the nothing ahead had got *deeper*. Not further. Deeper. As if the not-there in front of them had started going down as well as on.

And it had gone very, very still.

Not still like the nothing. The nothing had never been still or moving; it hadn't been anything. This was *still*. On purpose. The way water is still. Like the lake under the round town on the longest night, before it stood up.

"*Stop*," said Hyvred.

***

The rope stopped.

All of it. Nine people, at once, in the nothing. She heard everybody stop. Kotori ahead of her. Thea behind. Hako, mid-sentence, *and here the tank*— Silence.

"What?" said the swamp guy. From the front. Very quietly.

"Don't step," said Hyvred. "Don't— whatever you were about to put your foot on. Don't."

She heard him not.

She heard him stand there, at the front of the rope, with his front foot out. Not down. Out. Over whatever was there. Not putting his weight on it. Waiting. On the back foot. A breath.

"What is it?" he said.

"I don't know," said Hyvred. Her voice was shaking. "The silence changed. Ahead. It's deeper. And it's still. Not nothing still. *Still* still." She swallowed. "Like the lake. Under the round town. Before."

Nobody said anything.

Then the swamp guy lowered his front foot. Very slowly. Not putting his weight on it. Just touching. The way he'd touched the ground every forty steps for nineteen nights.

She heard it.

Not a splash. Nothing so loud. A sound so small it was nearly not there. Like the end of a breath. Like a finger touching the surface of something that had been perfectly flat for a very long time.

"*Oh*," said the swamp guy.

***

He took his foot back.

He knelt down. She heard him do it, at the front of the rope, in the nothing. Heard his knee go down on the ground he could feel. And then his hand, in front of him, very slowly, reaching.

The same small sound. A finger on a flat surface.

"It's water," he said.

Nobody moved.

"It's water," he said again. "It's black. It doesn't move. I put my foot on it and it was cold, and it didn't go anywhere." He was very quiet. "I'd have stepped on it. I'd have thought it was ground. Isa said. *You'll think it's ground. It isn't.*"

"The still water," said Suzu. Right behind him on the rope. His voice had gone very odd.

"The still water," said the swamp guy.

He stayed kneeling for a long time. She could hear him. Not moving. With his hand on the water, flat, the way you'd put your hand on the lid of something.

"Hyv," he said.

"Yes."

"You heard it."

"The silence changed," said Hyvred. "I just— I heard the silence change."

He was quiet.

"I didn't feel it," he said. "With my feet. I've felt the ground for nineteen nights. I'd have stepped on it." He stood up. She heard him stand. "You heard it first."

Hyvred stood on the rope in the nothing with *Pip* in her hand, nearly burned down, and found she was crying.

"I go first," she said.

"You go first," said the swamp guy.

***

They made the ring there. At the edge. It was half past twelve, not a quarter to eight. But the swamp guy said *here*, and nobody argued.

Nine candles on the ground. Close, facing in. And in the middle, the watch, lit by Suzu from the best candle, Mother Dipp's. And for once nobody said the words. Because it wasn't morning. Because the egg wasn't going to take them for seven hours. Because they'd found the still water, and they were going to sit at the edge of it for a while and look at it. Even though there was nothing to see.

Hyvred sat beside him. Shoulder to shoulder.

On the far side of the ring, past the candles, past the edge of the light, was the still water. She couldn't see it. Nobody could. But she could hear it now that she knew. The particular stillness of it. Deep and flat and black, going away north into the nothing, perfectly still.

And somewhere out on it, a long way, three hundred stones.

And past them, a hill.

"Isa's mothers set them," said Suzu. Very quietly. Across the ring. "One every spring. Three hundred springs. So the way would be there."

"The first one's sunk," said Gigi. "She said."

"And the last one's crooked," said Thea.

Suzu didn't say anything for a long time. He was sitting at the north side of the ring, the side nearest the water, with the black iron case on his back in Milli's frame. His hand was on the strap. On the cat's-paw. Where your hand fell when you reached up to check it was there.

"Tomorrow," said Suzu. "We'll count them."

***

She sat in the ring at the edge of the still water till the egg came at eight. Seven hours, nearly. Nobody slept. Nobody said much. Hako narrated for a while, very softly, and then stopped, and nobody asked him to go on.

She listened to the water.

She thought about her mothers, Isa's mothers, every one of them, walking out across it in the spring with a stone. Setting it in the still water a step further than the last. Saying the number out loud. Walking back to the hill in the dark. Three hundred springs. So the way would be there, in case.

In case somebody came.

She thought about the tube. One thing at a time, small and round and bright. And the edges, and the game, and the nothing. And a face in the light of nine candles, from below, that was all she could see.

*Now it's you.*

At a quarter to eight, Suzu said *set one to watch*. And nine people said *so the rest may sleep*. Quietly, at the edge of the still water, in the nothing.

And somewhere very far behind them, back down a road of lights, past the edge, past the old hall, past ninety-four lamps leaning, at a long table in a round town, sixty people said it too. At the same moment.

She didn't know that. Nobody had told them.

But she heard something. Just before the egg took her. Very faint, from the south, from a long way off, through the nothing.

She'd have said it was the wind, except there wasn't any.
