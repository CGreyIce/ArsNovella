Ash liked crossroads at dusk. She always had.

Not for any reason she'd ever told anybody. Kite had told Kei, once, the first winter, on a call from a hedge. *Ash likes crossroads. At dusk.* As if it were a thing about her, like her two blades or the bird on her collar. It was a thing about her. It just wasn't the thing he'd thought.

He'd thought she liked them because you could see four ways at once. Because people came to them from every direction, at dusk, tired, not looking. Because a crossroads at dusk was the best place in the world to take somebody's day.

That was true. That was why Nightjar had used them.

It wasn't why she liked them.

She liked them because at a crossroads, at dusk, you had to decide. Which way. Every road went somewhere and you could only go down one. And the light was going. And you couldn't stand there forever.

She'd never once decided. Not in Nightjar. Nightjar decided for you: where the money was, which road, whose day. She'd stood at a hundred crossroads at dusk and never once chosen a road. She'd just waited for the money to tell her.

Then the money went. And twelve became four, and four became one. And she'd stood at a crossroads at dusk in March on her own. And there'd been nobody to tell her.

So she'd walked to a milestone. And sat there. For seven months.

***

Kei had put them at the corner.

The north-west road came out of the round town between two houses and went off over the moor toward somewhere Ash had never been. And a mile out, it crossed an old drove road. A track, worn deep into the heather, going east to west, that cattle had walked once, before anybody remembered. A crossroads. Four ways. A standing stone at the corner with nothing cut into it.

*The dark goes round*, Kei had said, at the long table, on Sunday. With his chalk board. *It comes down the north road at the north door. But it comes round, too, on the longest night. Down the drove road, both ways. Into the north-west road and the north-east. To get in at the sides.* He'd tapped the chalk. *Meridian's on the north-east. Somebody has to hold the corner on the north-west.*

And he'd looked at her. At the far end of the long table, where she'd been sitting for two weeks. On a stool. Not on the Roll. On the sheet.

*Kite. Drew. Ash.*

She'd looked at the chalk for a long time.

"Crossroads," she'd said.

"Crossroads," Kei had agreed. "At dusk. Kite says you like them."

***

She'd made the tea herself.

Kotori had left a tin. Ash had found it on the end of the counter in the Hart's kitchen the morning the nine went north, with a label on it in Kotori's careful hand. *For the milestone.* And under it, smaller: *Boil the water for a long time. Two spoons. It's supposed to be horrible.*

She'd made it every night since. For two. At the milestone, for her and Kite, in the chipped cup and the new one. It wasn't as good as Kotori's. Kite said so. Kite said it was more horrible.

She made it now, at the crossroads, at the second dusk, on a little fire Drew had made in the lee of the standing stone. Three cups. The chipped one, the new one, and one Drew had brought from the forge, black, with soot on it.

She handed them out.

Drew looked into his for a long time.

"It's horrible," he said.

"It's supposed to be."

"It's *more* horrible," said Drew. "Than Kotori's."

"Kite says that."

"Kite's right," said Drew, and drank it all.

***

Nothing came for six hours.

She'd known it wouldn't. Kei had said. *It goes to the middle first.* So they sat at the crossroads in the lee of the standing stone with the fire going. And watched four roads go away into the dark. And the round town behind them, a mile back, every window lit. And far off, north, past the moor, nothing.

She thought about the nine.

She'd never met most of them. Not properly. The swamp guy, she'd met. In the fen, the first time, on a boardwalk with Nightjar round him. *We'll have your day another day.* At the stone cross on the moor, the February before last, on the ground with his shield at her throat. *Not today, then.* At the milestone, in August, coming home. *Not today.* She'd said it back. She'd laughed.

She'd watched him for a year. Every night. On a stream, from a hedge, at first because somebody was paying. Then because nobody was, and she couldn't stop.

*I wanted to know what it was*, she'd told Kite.

She'd sat at a milestone for seven months, and on a stool at the end of a long table for two weeks. And she still didn't know. Not really. She'd seen it. Every night, on the stream. People standing next to each other on purpose. She'd seen the swamp guy turn round on the bottom step of the Hall of Roads in August and say *behind me, and beside me, and in front*. She'd seen it.

She'd never done it.

Tonight, Kei had said. *Ask me again on the longest night. Here. If you still want to, after that, you'll know what it is.*

She was at a crossroads. At dusk. And for the first time in her life, somebody else hadn't decided which road.

She had. She'd stayed.

***

At four minutes past midnight, Kei's call came. In the corner of her eye. She'd had Kei's calls since Sunday; Kite had shown her how. They were the first windows she'd ever had from Constellation.

*She says they held.*

Kite, beside her, let out a breath.

"The hill," he said.

"I know."

"They held." Kite looked north. "Nine people. On a rope. Three weeks. And they held."

Ash looked north too. At nothing. The dark, past the moor, very far.

"How do you know it's good?" she said.

Kite looked at her.

"*She says they held*," said Ash. "Isa. On the roof. How does she know?"

"She can see it," said Drew, from the fire. Without looking up. "Kei says. From the bench. She's been looking north since dusk." He put a stick on the fire. "She says there's a light."

***

It came round at half past two.

Down the drove road. Both ways at once. The way Kei had said. Ash heard it before she saw it: the sound of the Unlit, tall and thin and swaying, coming through the heather. A sound like reeds in a wind that wasn't there. From the east, where the drove road went off toward the north road. From the west, where it went off toward nowhere she'd ever been. Toward the crossroads. Toward the north-west road and the round town behind it.

"*Up*," said Kite.

They stood up.

She'd stood at crossroads a hundred times with two blades in her hands, waiting for something to come down a road. She'd never stood at one with somebody on either side of her. Kite on her left with his bow strung. Drew on her right with his shield, standing very still, the way Drew stood. *If I'm still, it's still.*

She'd always been the one in the middle, in Nightjar. Watching the gaps. The one who said *now*.

She was in the middle now.

"Gaps," said Kite. Very quietly. "You fight the gaps. Not the line. You always did." He drew. "Fight the gaps."

***

They came out of the heather on both sides at once.

She'd seen Unlit on streams. Hundreds. On the longest night, two years running, coming up out of moor pools and leaning toward lights. She'd never been in front of one.

They were tall. Taller than she'd thought. Thin, black, swaying. With nothing where their faces should be. Leaning toward the fire in the lee of the stone, toward the round town behind, toward the light. Not toward her. She wasn't a light.

She went into the gaps.

The way she always had. The way the swamp guy said she fought, on the moor, at the stone cross: *she fights the gaps, not the line*. Between the Unlit. Into the places where they weren't, where they were about to be. Two blades, short and curved, going in and out, low. She'd never fought anything that wasn't a person. It turned out it was the same. Everything had gaps.

Kite's arrows over her shoulder. One, and another, and another, into the tall black swaying things coming down the drove road from the east. Drew's shield on her right, still, so still the Unlit leaned into it and stopped. *If I'm still, it's still.*

And her in the middle. In the gaps.

She'd never once done it next to anybody.

It was different. She couldn't have said how. Faster, and slower. She went into a gap and knew Drew's shield was on her right. So she didn't have to look. She went into another and heard Kite's arrow go past her ear into the thing beyond. So she didn't have to finish it.

She didn't have to do all of it.

She'd always done all of it. In Nightjar. Even with twelve. Every one of them doing all of it, on their own, at the same time. That was what Nightjar was.

This was something else.

***

At half past four, Kite ran out of arrows.

She heard it. The empty sound of his hand going back to the quiver and finding nothing. She'd heard it once before, a long time ago, the first winter. On a road. When he'd come at a run out of arrows with three Unlit behind him, toward the round town's light. And she'd been somewhere else, with Nightjar, on another road. And somebody she'd never met had stepped out into the road and held it for him.

Drew.

She looked at Drew. On her right. Still.

He was looking at her.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He'd stood in a road once for somebody running toward the light. He'd stood on a keystone in a lake. He was the one who stays.

He was waiting to see what she'd do.

The Unlit came down the drove road from the east, five of them, tall and thin and swaying. Toward Kite. Toward the one who'd run out of arrows, standing at the corner of the crossroads with an empty bow and his skinning knife in his other hand. The knife he'd cut the bird off his collar with, in a ditch, the winter before last.

Ash stepped in front of him.

***

She didn't decide it.

That was what she'd think about, afterward. For weeks. At the long table, on a stool, and then on a chair. She didn't decide it. She'd never decided anything at a crossroads in her life. She'd always waited for something to tell her which way.

Nothing told her. She just went.

Into the road. In front of Kite. Between him and five Unlit coming down the drove road from the east. With two short curved blades and eighty-one per cent and no idea at all what she was doing.

"*Ash*—" said Kite.

"Not today," said Ash.

***

She fought the gaps.

There weren't many. Five Unlit, tall, close, swaying, coming at the place where she was standing in the road in front of him. She went into the gaps between them anyway. Low, fast, in and out. The way she'd gone into the gaps in a line of people on the moor at a stone cross. The way she'd cut the swamp guy twice before he put her on the ground.

She couldn't get all of them.

She knew that about three seconds in. There were too many and they were too close and she was on her own in front of him. The way she'd always been on her own, even with twelve. She went into a gap and the one beside it came round, and she couldn't—

Drew's shield.

On her right. Where it had been all night. Not moving. He'd come forward the one pace she'd gone, and stood there. Still. And the Unlit that had come round leaned into his shield and stopped.

*If I'm still, it's still.*

"*I'm the one who stays*," said Drew. Very calmly. "*You go in. I'll stay.*"

She went in.

***

It took a long time. She didn't know how long. Five, and then more behind them, coming down the drove road from the east and the west, round the town, in the dark. She went into the gaps and Drew stayed. And behind her, at the corner, Kite. Not with his bow. With his knife, and his hands at his sides. Where she could see them.

She looked back once. She couldn't help it.

He was standing at the corner of the crossroads with his skinning knife in one hand and the other open. The way he stood in the gate. So she'd know he wasn't holding anything back.

He was watching her. With his narrow face doing something she'd seen it do once. In August, when the swamp guy came up the road and said *not today*.

She went back into the gaps.

At five, the dark came down the drove road after the Unlit. The thing itself. Edge to edge. The way it came down the north road, Kite said, last year, at the north door. It came round the corner of the crossroads, black, all of it, leaning. On the fire in the lee of the standing stone. On the three of them. On the north-west road and the round town behind it.

Not looking, Kite said, afterward. Tiger had said it too. *It's not looking. It's just coming. Like weather.*

It leaned on them.

Drew didn't move.

Ash stood beside him, at twenty-two per cent, with two blades. And didn't move either. She didn't know why. She'd never stood still in front of anything in her life. She'd always gone into the gaps.

There weren't any gaps in the dark.

So she stood. Next to the one who stays.

And Kite came the pace from the corner and stood on her other side. With his knife and his open hand. So the three of them were in a line across the north-west road, at the crossroads, at five in the morning, on the longest night. And the dark leaning on them.

Not on her. On them.

She'd wanted to know what it was.

***

Kite sent a call, at about five past five. She saw him do it, his eyes going to the corner of his window. He'd never sent one before, Kei said after. Kite didn't send calls.

[ Kite · the north-west road · 05:06 ]
*North-west. We're fine.*

She looked at it.

"That's Vane's," she said.

"I borrowed it," said Kite.

"From who?"

"The swamp guy says you borrow things," said Kite. "From people who teach you." He didn't look at her. He was looking at the dark, leaning on the three of them. "Vane taught everybody that one. In July. On a harbour wall. He said it to the swamp guy and the swamp guy told Kei and Kei told everybody." He was quiet. "It means *I've already made it so*."

"Have you?"

Kite looked at her, then. At twenty-two per cent, standing next to him and Drew, in front of the dark.

"Yes," he said.

***

At half past six, the lamps came back on.

She saw it from the crossroads. Away to the north-east, past the moor, where the north road went up toward the edge. A point of light, a long way off. Then another, below it. Then another. Coming back on one after another, south toward the round town, the way Higu said, in a call, in the corner of everybody's eye. *From the far end.*

And the dark on the drove road, leaning on them, went back.

Not fast. The way a tide goes out. Down into the heather, into the moor, into the pools it came out of. Sinking, all along the drove road both ways, until there was just the road, and the heather. And the standing stone at the corner with nothing cut into it. And the fire Drew had made in its lee, nearly out. And the grey of the morning coming up over the east.

She sat down on the road.

***

Drew sat down beside her. Then Kite.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Three people sitting in the middle of the north-west road where it crossed the drove road, in the morning. With the round town a mile behind them, every light still lit, and the road of lights all the way up the moor, lit.

"Seventy-eight," said Drew, at last.

Ash looked at him.

"You went into the gaps seventy-eight times," said Drew. "I counted. Off your feet." He was looking at her feet. "Your weight's on the left. Always. But this morning it was even." He was quiet. "You weren't waiting for anything. You were just there."

She didn't know what to say.

"Kite," she said.

He looked at her.

She'd sat at a milestone with him for seven months. He'd brought her cups. He'd asked her, every night for ten nights in May, who'd stand next to her. And she'd said *nobody*. And then *you*. And he'd said *fair*. And sat a bit closer.

She'd never asked him back.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Ash.

Kite looked at her for a long time. In the morning. On the road. With his bow empty across his knees and his skinning knife put away.

"You," said Kite. "And him." He nodded at Drew. "That's two."

"That's two," said Ash.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Kite.

She looked at the crossroads. At the four roads going away into the morning. North-west, toward somewhere she'd never been. East and west along the drove road. And back. South-east. A mile, to the round town. Every light lit.

She'd never once chosen a road.

"You," said Ash. "And him." She swallowed. "And whoever's at the end of that one." She looked back down the north-west road, toward the round town. "I'm going to ask."

"Fair," said Kite.

***

They walked back at the first dawn. Three of them, down the north-west road, side by side, not quite in step. Drew on the right, the way he'd been all night. Kite on the left. Her in the middle.

She'd always been the one in the middle. In Nightjar. Watching the gaps, saying *now*.

It wasn't the same middle.

At the end of the road, where it came into the round town between two houses, she could see the south steps of the Hall of Roads, round the curve of the market. The whole round town on them. The way it always was, at the dawn after the longest night. Everybody who'd held something, sitting on the steps.

And at the top, by the gate, with a map case under his arm, standing very still: Kei.

Waiting.

She stopped at the end of the road. She didn't mean to. Her feet just stopped.

Kite stopped beside her. Drew stopped.

"Go on," said Kite.

She looked at him.

"I'll be at the gate," said Kite. "Asking. You know what I'll ask." He almost smiled. She'd seen it four times now. "You know what you'll say."

She did.

She'd wanted to know what it was. For a year. On a stream, from a hedge, at a milestone, on a stool. Watching people stand next to each other on purpose, and not knowing what it was.

She knew now.

It was seventy-eight gaps with somebody's shield on your right. It was stepping in front of somebody who'd run out of arrows. Without deciding, because you didn't need to. It was standing still in front of the dark, because there weren't any gaps, and you weren't on your own. It was *we're fine*, in a call, from somebody who'd borrowed it.

It was a crossroads. And choosing a road.

She went down it. Toward the steps.
