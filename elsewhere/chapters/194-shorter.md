Winter fished in the dark because it was quiet, and because nobody ever came.

Not the moor pools. Things came up out of the moor pools. Still water. She'd seen eleven of them stand up out of one in November, on Hako's stream, tall and thin and swaying like reeds. She didn't fish in still water any more.

She fished in the stream.

The little one in the north tunnel under the round town, in its groove in the floor. A hand deep, a few hands wide. Black water, running. It came down from under the old stone at the cold place and went away south under everything. Nothing came up out of running water. That was the rule. Ice had put a whole lake into it on the longest night, Bee said, and it had taken it down like a drain.

She sat on the edge of the groove a little way up from the Seven Below, past where the Delvers used to sleep, with her line in the water and a lamp beside her. There were fish in it. Small, blind, white. Nobody had known. She'd found them in the autumn. You couldn't eat them; they tasted of nothing. She let them go.

It wasn't about the fish. It was the quiet.

At ten o'clock on Sunday night, somebody came.

***

She heard her before she saw her. Light feet on the old stone, and the knock of a bow against a wall, and breathing. The kind of breathing you do when you've been crying and stopped and are trying not to start again.

Hyvred came round the curve of the tunnel with no lamp, because she didn't need one. She saw in the dark the way she saw everything, edge to edge. She stopped when she saw Winter.

"Oh," said Hyvred. "Sorry. I didn't— I was going to the end."

"The cold place," said Winter.

"Yes."

"Where you sit."

Hyvred went pink. Winter could see it even by lamplight.

"Where we sit," said Hyvred. "In the mornings. When I call it." She looked up the tunnel toward the cold place, a long way off in the dark. "He's not there. He's in the banner room. I just wanted to—" She stopped.

"Sit there," said Winter.

"Yes."

Winter looked at her line in the water.

"Fine," she said. "Sit here first."

***

Hyvred sat down on the other side of the groove, with her bow across her knees, and looked at the water.

Winter didn't say anything. She was good at not saying anything. People thought she was unfriendly, sometimes, because she didn't say much. She wasn't unfriendly. She just didn't see the point of saying things that were already true. Everybody knew they were true. Why say them?

The stream ran. A blind white fish came up under her lamp, looked at nothing, and went away.

"Can I ask you something," said Hyvred, after a long time.

"Yes."

"You're blunt."

"That's not a question."

"You're *blunt*," said Hyvred. "Everybody says. You just say things. You said *something always goes wrong* to Chris on day eighty-one and he's put it in a column. You said *fine* when they asked you to walk seven days to the round town." She was looking at the water. "How do you just *say* things?"

Winter thought about it.

"I say them," she said.

Hyvred laughed. It went a bit wrong in the middle.

"That's not *helpful*, Winter."

"It's true," said Winter. "You asked how. That's how." She reeled in a little, let the line out again. "What do you want to say?"

Hyvred didn't answer.

"To him," said Winter. "Tomorrow. At the gate."

Hyvred went very still.

"Niko told you," she said.

"Nobody told me. I was on the ridge." Winter looked at her. "You asked Niko what if you don't know the word. I heard. Everyone heard. Chris stopped typing." She shrugged. "Chris never stops typing."

Hyvred put her face in her hands.

"Everyone *knows*," she said, through them. "Everyone. Xola knows. Thea knows. Bee knows. Deston knows. Slep knows, and Slep doesn't even *talk*. Chi knows. Gabe *probably* knows. Niko's done the *sums*. The whole guild knows." She took her hands away. "And I've been trying to work out what to say for three days and I *can't*."

"Say it to me," said Winter.

Hyvred stared at her.

"Practise," said Winter. "Say it to me. I'm not him. I don't care. I'll tell you if it's wrong."

Hyvred looked at her for a long, long moment. At Winter, sitting on the edge of a stream in the dark under the round town, with a fishing line in the water and a lamp, looking back at her with no expression at all.

"Fine," said Hyvred.

***

The first one was very long.

Winter let her say all of it. It took about four minutes. It started in the winter, a year ago, before Hyvred was in the game, with a stream that had ten people in it and a man sitting with his back to a stone in a fen. It went through the first time she turned notifications on for anybody, and the dollar, and the heron. The punch, the first one, and every one after. The left heart at the Elm Below, and the tail, and Xola in the banner room, and the stepped-on cat, and *MRP*. The step outside the banner room door, and *no*. The two hundred and twelve, and the list on the board, and her name in pen. The tunnel and the lake. Sitting beside him at the stream. *Somebody did.* And the banner room on Friday, and *Monday, ahead*, and the roof.

She stopped. She was out of breath.

Winter looked at her line.

"Shorter," she said.

***

The second one was two minutes. It left out the dollar and the heron and most of the punches, and some of the tunnel.

"Shorter," said Winter.

The third one was a minute. It started at the fen and went straight to the tunnel and then straight to *I like you* at the end, very fast, as if it were trying to get there before anyone noticed.

Winter thought about that one for a while.

"Better," she said. "Still too long."

"It's a *minute*."

"He's going to be standing at a gate," said Winter. "With a pack on. With everybody watching. With Wendy asking him who'd stand next to him." She reeled in a little. "He's not going to listen to a minute. He's going to be thinking about the road."

Hyvred opened her mouth to argue. Then she shut it again.

"He doesn't listen to long things," said Winter. "You know he doesn't. You've called for him for six weeks. You don't say *there's a Scree-Runner coming down the slope on your left, it's the big kind, I think it's going to try to go round the boulder*. You say *left*." She looked at Hyvred. "And he goes."

Hyvred didn't say anything for a long time.

The stream ran. A blind white fish came up, looked at nothing, went away.

"*Left*," said Hyvred, very quietly.

"Yes."

"You want me to say *left*."

"I want you to say the *left* of it," said Winter. "The one word. The thing that's true. He'll go."

***

Thea and Xola came at eleven, because of course they did. They came round the curve of the tunnel with a lamp each, very quietly. They'd clearly been looking for Hyvred everywhere else in the round town first, and they stopped when they saw the two of them sitting either side of the stream.

"Oh," said Thea.

"Oh, *Winter*," said Xola, in a voice of enormous relief. "Winter's got her. Okay. Okay. Winter's got her."

"I haven't got her," said Winter. "She's practising."

Xola sat down on the floor of the tunnel with a thump. Her gills were flared all the way.

"We've been practising *all week*," said Xola. "All *week*. I wrote her a speech. A good one. It had a *beginning*. It had a *middle*. It had a bit about the dollar that made Thea cry."

"It did make me cry," admitted Thea.

"And she won't *say* it," said Xola.

"It's too long," said Winter.

Xola looked at her.

"It's *perfect*."

"It's perfect and it's too long," said Winter. "He's not going to listen to a speech at a gate. He's going to be thinking about fourteen days of road." She looked at the water. "She's got to say *left*."

There was a silence.

Then Thea laughed. Softly, and then not softly, sitting on the floor of the north tunnel with her lamp, laughing until she had to wipe her eyes.

"She's right," said Thea. "Xola. She's right. He doesn't listen to speeches. He's never listened to a speech in his life. He listens to—" She stopped. "He listens to one thing. The true one. Then he goes."

Xola looked at Thea for a long time. Then at Winter. Then at Hyvred, sitting on the edge of the stream with her bow across her knees and her face doing about five things at once.

"Fine," said Xola. "Fine. *Fine*." She threw her hands up. "Throw out my speech. My beautiful speech. With the dollar." She pointed at Hyvred. "But you *say something*. You hear me? You stand at that gate and you say *something*. Even if it's *left*."

"Even if it's *left*," said Hyvred.

***

They practised until midnight. The four of them, on the floor of the north tunnel, by the stream, with three lamps and Winter's line in the water.

Hyvred said it to Winter. *Shorter.* She said it to Thea. Thea cried, and said *shorter* anyway. She said it to Xola. Xola said *that's still too long and also I love you, shorter*. She said it to the stream. She said it to a blind white fish that came up under the lamp and looked at nothing. She said it to the wall.

It got shorter.

By midnight it was one breath. Not one word. Winter had stopped asking for one word at about half past eleven. Some things weren't one word, Niko had said on the ridge, and he was right. But it was one breath. One true thing, and then the next true thing, and then stop. You could say it at a gate with a pack on and everybody watching. You could say it before he had time to start thinking about the road.

Hyvred said it one last time, to the stream, very quietly.

Winter listened to it. She didn't say *shorter*.

"Fine," she said.

Hyvred looked at her.

"That's the one," said Winter. "Don't change it. Don't add anything. Don't take anything out." She reeled in her line, all the way, and looked at the empty hook. "Say that."

Hyvred sat there for a long time with her bow across her knees.

Then she reached into her quiver and took something out. An arrow. Grey goose fletching, very neat, Winter's own work. But one of the three feathers was gone, torn off. And the shaft had a scratch on it from a rock, a finger's length from the point. As if it had been shot into stone.

"Niko gave me this," said Hyvred. "Last night. On the ridge. He enchanted it." She held it out across the stream. "It remembers the cold place. Where we sit. If you hold it up a long way off, it leans. Toward here." She looked at the torn fletching. "I'm going to give it to him. Tomorrow. So he knows which way."

"It's missing a feather," said Winter.

"It went into a rock."

"It'll fly crooked."

"He's not going to *shoot* it," said Hyvred.

Winter held out her hand.

Hyvred gave her the arrow. Winter turned it over by the lamp. Then she opened her fletching roll, the little leather one she kept in her pack, and looked through the feathers in it. Grey goose. Grey goose. Grey goose. And one, at the back, that she'd found on the moor in December and kept because it was pretty, and she'd never used it on anything.

White. Pure white, from root to tip. Like the tip of a tail.

She took it out. She trimmed it, and set it, and bound it in where the torn feather had been. It took about four minutes. Nobody said anything the whole time. When she'd finished she held the arrow up to the lamp and looked down it, the way you'd look down a straight line.

Two grey feathers and one white one.

"So he can find it," said Winter. "In the quiver. In the dark." She handed it back. "Fine?"

Hyvred took it. She looked at the white feather for a long time.

"Fine," she said. Her voice did something.

Xola was crying. Thea was crying. Winter wasn't, because she didn't, but she looked at the stream for a while, quite hard.

***

Thea and Xola went up at one, because Xola had a lecture in the morning, real ones, and Thea had said *bed* in the voice that meant it. They went round the curve of the tunnel with their lamps. Xola looked back three times.

Hyvred didn't go up. She went the other way. Up the tunnel, north, toward the cold place.

Winter reeled in her line and went after her.

She didn't hurry. She didn't say anything. She walked a long way behind, with her lamp, the way you'd walk behind somebody on a moor at night so they wouldn't be alone and wouldn't have to talk. At the cold place, where the old stone met the new and the stream came out from under the wall, Hyvred sat down with her back against the east side. In her place. Two paces from where he always sat.

Winter sat down four paces off, on the other side, and put her lamp between them.

It was cold. Colder than she'd thought. She'd heard about it on Ice's stream, the cold at the end of the north tunnel. She'd thought it was the sort of thing he said.

"You didn't have to come," said Hyvred.

"Nobody alone," said Winter. "It's on the board."

Hyvred laughed, a bit.

They sat. The stream came out from under the old stone, black, running, and went past them down the tunnel. The lamp burned.

"I harpooned a seal," said Winter, after a long time.

Hyvred looked at her.

"In Ferrowmere," said Winter. "The first month. Day eighty-one. I was thirteen." She meant the level. "There's a bay there with ice on it. The seals come up through holes. The old man who taught me said you sit by the hole. With the harpoon. And you wait." She looked at the stream. "So I sat by the hole. Six hours. In the cold. Nothing came. Chris kept messaging. *Are you dead.* Niko said come back. I didn't."

"And?"

"And it came up," said Winter. "At six hours and nine minutes. Right in front of me. Its whole head, out of the hole. Looking at me." She paused. "And I didn't think. I didn't wait any more. I'd waited six hours. When it came, I just went."

Hyvred didn't say anything.

"That's the thing about waiting," said Winter. "You wait a long time. And then you don't." She shrugged. "Sixty per cent. It was a good seal."

Hyvred looked at the stream for a long time.

"I've been waiting a year," she said.

"I know."

"Since before I was in the game."

"I know," said Winter. "Xola said. Everyone said." She looked at Hyvred across the lamp. "So tomorrow you don't."

Hyvred sat with the arrow across her knees, two grey feathers and one white. She looked at the place beside her, two paces off, where he always sat with his back to the wall. Empty now. Cold.

"So tomorrow I don't," she said.

"Fine," said Winter.

They sat there until two. Then Winter took her lamp and went up, and a few minutes later Hyvred followed her. Not fast. Not slow. A long way behind, the way you'd walk behind somebody on a moor at night. So neither of them was alone, and neither of them had to talk.

***

She was at the gate at first dawn on Monday, because everybody was.

She hadn't meant to be. She'd meant to stay in the north tunnel and fish. But at a quarter to eight she'd lain down in her egg, a long way from the round town, and lain there for a minute in the dark of the lid, and then gone in. And when she came out into the banner room, it was empty. Every chair. Sixty chairs, and the stool, and nobody in any of them. Only the old woman asleep in hers by the north door with her blanket, and the empty cage on its hook above her.

Ama Corrie, at the counter in the Hall, didn't look up.

"South steps," she said.

Winter went to the south steps.

The whole of Constellation was there. All of it. On the steps and round the fountain and along the edge of the round market. Not making a noise. Moonlight Harvest by the fountain, Gabe at the front of them with his shield on his back, looking enormous and nervous. Riverbank along the steps. Hana's five. Kei on the top step with his map. Higu. Tiger. Salty and Wendy at the bottom, at the gate, where Wendy always was.

And Thea's six, at the side. Thea and Xola together, holding hands so hard their knuckles were white.

And at the bottom of the steps, a little apart, by herself, with her bow on her back and an arrow in her hand, Hyvred.

Two grey feathers and one white.

Winter went and stood at the side, near Niko and Newish and Chris. Out of the way. Chris had his spreadsheet open, but he wasn't typing. Newish had her drum on her back and her hands at her sides. Niko was looking at Hyvred with his patient face, the one he'd worn on the ridge.

"Is she going to," said Newish, very quietly.

"She's going to," said Winter.

"How do you know?"

Winter looked at Hyvred. At the arrow in her hand. At her tail, which was perfectly still, white at the tip, like a feather.

"Because it's short enough," said Winter.

And the Hall's south door opened, and Ice came out with a pack on his back.
