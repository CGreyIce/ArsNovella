Kestrel had been second four times, and she'd decided, somewhere on the long rise three days out of Masmark, that she was going to stop counting.

Not stop minding. She'd never stop minding. But stop *counting*. Counting was a thing you did at the top of a held draw, holding a decision so big and so obvious that everybody froze and waited for it. And a man with two blades and a sketchbook had stood in a ring in a market at first dawn for two weeks and taught her that holding a decision was the worst thing you could do. *He'll just wait. He's very good at waiting.*

So she'd stopped holding it. She'd stopped deciding early. She shot where things *were*. And somewhere in the stopping, she'd stopped counting the seconds too.

Four times. The drowned church, the Saltwell, the first-rank hall under the round town's stair, the Elm Below. She knew the number. She didn't say it any more.

She came over the rim of Great Lake Masmark on the seventh of March with twenty of VANTA behind her. She looked down at the biggest city in the world, and the island in the middle of the lake with its tower. And she thought, quite calmly, with no decision in it at all: *that one*.

***

VANTA had a Hall on the east rim, on a terrace above the water, rented in January by an advance party of five. It was plain. Black stone, white door, nothing on the walls. Kestrel liked it. She'd built VANTA to be plain. No speeches, no gold, no shouting. Twenty people who were very good at what they did, and didn't look up when a window came.

Except Brann had looked up, once. In the Saltwell, in April. For one second. And lost his legs.

He didn't look up any more. He'd lain on his face in a market in September and laughed at the sky. *A tank can catch a tank. If he holds it.* Since then he'd been different. Quieter, if that was possible. More *still*. He'd sat behind the chestnut stall for three mornings watching her lose to a pigeon and never said a word about it.

"Island," said Brann, at the window of the Hall, on the first evening.

"Island," said Kestrel.

"Ten nights."

"Seven now," said Kestrel. "The notice went up on the fourth. We've lost three on the road."

"Everyone's lost something on the road," said Brann. "Sable Court lost four days coming down from Dawnmere. Meridian's been here a month and they've got two hundred people to choose twenty from. The Open Hand's got eleven thousand to choose from and they still can't decide." He looked at the island. "Constellation's got eleven here. And nine on the road."

"Constellation's got *him*," said Kestrel.

Brann didn't say anything.

"I'm not going to watch for him," said Kestrel. She said it to the window. "I watched for him for nine months. Every VOD. I knew where he stood in a room. I'm not going to do that this time. I'm going to look at the door." She paused. "Just the door."

"Good," said Brann.

***

She saw him anyway, on the third day. You couldn't not see him in Masmark. Everybody was talking about him.

She'd been on the bridge over the third stream on the north terrace, with Brann and Ottie, coming back from the Rim Hall. The streams came down off the crater wall into the lake all the way round Masmark, fast and loud. There were bridges over all of them. This one was old, stone, with a parapet you could lean on, and the water going under it so fast it was white.

He was on the other side of the bridge. Coming the other way. On his own, with his shield on his back.

No. Not on his own. Kestrel looked again. There was a woman beside him with gold horns and a bow, walking very close. Close enough that her shoulder was touching his arm. Close enough that Kestrel, who'd watched nine months of his VODs and knew every way he stood in a room, stopped dead on the bridge.

*Oh*, she thought.

She'd seen the clip. Everybody had. The gate, the first dawn, *he went dark and it didn't matter*. Ottie had watched it eleven times and cried every time. Kestrel had watched it once and turned it off halfway. Not because she didn't like it. Because it wasn't hers. It had felt like reading somebody's letter.

She'd thought it would look different, in person. It didn't. It looked exactly like what it was.

He saw her. Of course he did.

He nodded. Once.

She nodded back.

And then somebody else came onto the bridge, from the east end, behind Kestrel. She felt him before she saw him. The way you feel weather coming. She turned round.

***

She'd never seen Lucan in person. Nobody in VANTA had. Sable Court were from the other side of the world, the far east. Nobody she knew had ever been there. Lucan had been the name at the top of their board since February, and the first man in the world to fifty, and the one who'd opened Dawnmere. And the one who'd posted to the whole game, the night he did it, *(To the tank in the round town: you're slow.)*

He was walking onto the bridge from the east end with four of Sable Court behind him. All in black, with a silver thread at the cuff. Plain, like VANTA. And he wasn't walking, exactly.

Kestrel would try to explain it afterward to Ottie and fail. He was *moving*. All the time. Not fast. Not restless. But every part of him was always going somewhere. His weight shifting, his hands, the way he turned his head. Like water going over stones. Like the stream under the bridge, white and loud, never in the same place twice. You couldn't look at him and say *that's where he is*. By the time you'd thought it, he was somewhere else.

*He fights like a river*, the tank in the round town had said, on his stream, in December. *He never stops moving. You can't find the place where he's going to be, because he's always already left it.*

Lucan stopped in the middle of the bridge.

He'd seen who was on the other end. Kestrel watched him see. The man with the shield and the woman with the gold horns, coming the other way, stopping too. Twenty paces apart on an old stone bridge over fast water.

The whole bridge went quiet. People coming and going stopped. Somebody on the far bank said *oh my God* in a language the game didn't bother to translate.

Lucan looked at him for a long time. He didn't stop moving while he did it. His weight went from one foot to the other and back. His hand went to his belt and away.

"You're slow," said Lucan.

Kestrel held her breath.

The tank in the round town looked at him across twenty paces of bridge. His face didn't do anything. Then something happened at the corner of his mouth.

"You're early," he said.

***

Lucan laughed.

It was a short laugh, surprised, out loud, over the noise of the water. The four of Sable Court behind him looked at each other. Kestrel didn't think they'd heard him do that very often.

"I'm always early," said Lucan. "That's the point."

"I know," said Ice. "I remember."

"Do you." Lucan's weight shifted. "The bridge. In the wind game. Over the gorge. You remember that?"

"Yes."

"A few times," said Lucan. He was looking at Ice very closely. "I don't remember how most of them ended. I've tried."

"Nor can I," said Ice.

"Liar," said Lucan.

"Yes," said Ice.

Lucan looked at him for a long, long moment, with the water going white under the bridge. Then he laughed again, louder.

"You were the only tank on the first page," he said. "The *only* one. The next tank was in the thousands. I used to look at the board every morning and there was you. Top twenty. Every morning. Not moving. Like a rock in a river." He shook his head. "I went round you. Every time. But I couldn't *move* you."

"You went round very well," said Ice.

"I'm going to go round you again," said Lucan. "At the island. Seven nights."

"Kei says we're racing."

"*Kei* says?" Lucan looked at him. "Not you?"

"Not me."

Lucan stopped moving.

Kestrel saw it happen. For the first time since he'd walked onto the bridge, every part of him went still. His weight, his hands, his head. He stood in the middle of the old stone bridge and looked at the man on the other end, perfectly still, the way the water never was.

"You're not running it," said Lucan.

"No."

"Harlan said. On the forum. I didn't believe him." Lucan was quiet. "In the wind game, everybody on the tank side knew your name."

"It was somewhere else," said Ice. "This is here."

Lucan looked at him for a long time. Then at the woman with the gold horns beside him, standing very close, looking back at Lucan with eyes that saw everything edge to edge, and not liking what they saw much. Then at Kestrel, on the other side, who'd been standing there the whole time with Brann and Ottie, not breathing.

"Kestrel," said Lucan. "VANTA."

"Lucan," said Kestrel. "Sable Court."

"You lost to him on an oak floor."

"I did."

"Eleven minutes."

"Eleven minutes," said Kestrel. She didn't look away. "I'm going to be first through the island."

Lucan looked at her. His weight started moving again. Left foot, right foot. The river coming back.

"Everybody's going to be first through the island," he said.

"No," said Kestrel. "Only one."

Something happened to his face. Kestrel didn't know what it was. She didn't know him. But it looked, very briefly, like respect.

"Then I'll see you at the door," said Lucan. He looked back at Ice. "Both of you." He went past them, across the bridge, west. His four behind him. Moving, all the time, like water.

At the far end, he stopped and looked back.

"Tank," he called. "When it's done. When the island's done." He paused. "Fight me. Properly. On a bridge."

Ice looked at him.

"Ask me properly," he said. "After."

"I will," said Lucan. And went.

***

"*Fight me properly on a bridge*," said Ottie, on the walk back to VANTA's Hall. She said it in a voice of awe. "He said it in front of half the north terrace."

"He did," said Kestrel.

"And the tank said *ask me properly*."

"He always says that."

"And *you* said *only one*," said Ottie. "To *Lucan*. Kes. You said *only one* to Lucan of Sable Court on a bridge, and he looked at you like—"

"I know what he looked at me like."

"Like *respect*," said Ottie.

Kestrel didn't say anything. She walked along the north terrace in the evening, with Masmark lighting up round the lake, and the island in the middle with its tower.

She'd meant it. *Only one.* She'd said it the way she'd learned to shoot in the ring in the market, without deciding. It had just come out, true, the way an arrow goes where something *is*.

She thought about the bridge. About the woman with the gold horns, standing so close to him. About how he'd stood, on the bridge, with her there. It wasn't the way he stood at the front of a room. It wasn't the way he'd stood on the oak floor at Elmcross with his sword pointing at the floor. Something else. Something she'd never seen in nine months of VODs, because he'd never done it on stream.

He'd stood like somebody who was *with* somebody.

"Brann," she said.

"Mm."

"He's happy."

Brann thought about it for a while, walking. Brann always thought about things.

"Yes," he said.

"I didn't know he could be."

"Nor did he, I think," said Brann. He looked at the island. "Does it change anything?"

Kestrel thought about it. She thought about the oak floor, and eleven minutes, and *she'd been answering him all along*. About the half-second at the top of the draw that he'd walked into. About a pigeon on a dark stone in a ring, and sixty-one misses on the first morning, and a man with two blades saying *decide later*.

"No," she said. "He's still going to be at the front. Kei's going to put him there. He'll tank it." She looked at the island. "And I'm still going to be first."

"Good," said Brann.

***

She scouted the island on the fifth day.

Everybody did. You couldn't get into the tower yet; the door at the bottom of it was shut, and it would be shut until the tenth dusk. But you could go out to the island in a boat and walk round it. You could look at the tower, and the water round the bottom of it, and the steps going down into the lake. Old, wide, stone steps, going down under the water to a door you couldn't see.

There were boats everywhere. Meridian had barges, three of them, enormous, blue, rowed by ten each. Sable Court had black boats, narrow, fast, that went round the island so quickly they left a white line on the water. The Open Hand had a great red ship with an open black hand on its sail that couldn't get close to the island because the water was too shallow, and had to anchor out and send boats. VANTA had two plain grey boats with no flag.

And Constellation had borrowed a fishing boat from the fish man, who'd said *bring it back*. Ten of them crammed into it. The big one with the black sword was rowing, and the boat was very low in the water.

Kestrel watched them from her grey boat for a while. Then she stopped watching them, because she'd said she would, and looked at the steps.

They went down under the water. Wide and old and worn in the middle by feet. You could see them going down through the clear water near the island, step after step, into the green, into the dark. And at the bottom, too deep to see, the door.

She put her hand in the water. It was very cold.

*Don't decide*, she thought. *Just look. See what's there.*

She looked. She held the whole island in her head, the tower and the steps and the water and the boats going round. She didn't pick anything. She let it come.

And she saw that the steps went down in the same pattern as the stairs in VANTA's Hall on the east rim. As the stairs in every old Hall in Masmark. As the two hundred and twelve in the round town, though she'd only climbed those once. A long flight, then a landing, then a shorter flight, then a landing, then the longest.

A hundred and six. Forty. Sixty-six.

She took her hand out of the water.

"Brann," she said.

"Mm."

"The steps under the water. Count them. When we go down."

"Why?"

"Because the last flight's the longest," said Kestrel. "And the hardest. With the wind. Everybody says so, about the round town." She looked at the green water. "I think it's the same. I think the hall's the same shape as the stair. Three parts. And the last is the longest. And everybody's going to go into it thinking they're nearly there, because they've done two thirds of the steps." She paused. "And they're not. They've done half."

Brann looked at her for a long time.

"How do you know?"

"I don't know," said Kestrel. "I didn't decide it. I just saw it."

Brann smiled. It wasn't something he did much.

"Xav," he said.

"Xav," agreed Kestrel.

***

She picked the twenty on the eighth night, at the long table in VANTA's plain black Hall, with the island in the window.

She'd always picked them herself. She paid them; she chose them. Six years of teaching people to be good, and the ones who got good enough went through the door with her. She'd thought she'd pick the same twenty she'd taken to the Elm Below. Brann, Ottie, Mags, Ren, and the fifteen from the writ, all of them very good, none of them people who looked up when a window came.

She picked nineteen of them.

The twentieth was a boy called Fenn, from the fifteen who hadn't made the Elm Below. Nineteen years old, a bow, and not as good as the others. He looked up when windows came. He'd looked up at the Elm Below, waiting on the bench, every time a window came for somebody else, and she'd seen him do it.

"Fenn," said Brann, at the long table, when she read it out. Not a question. Brann didn't ask questions. He just said the name so she'd hear it out loud.

"Fenn," said Kestrel.

"He looks up."

"I know," said Kestrel. "I've stopped minding." She looked at the boy, down the table, who'd gone white and then red. "In the deep, everybody's going to be looking at the door. At the end. At being nearly there. I want one person who looks up. Who sees what's behind." She paused. "Somebody taught me to see the whole room. I've been bad at it for six years. Fenn's never been bad at it. He just didn't know it was a thing you were allowed to be good at."

The table was quiet.

"And in the deep," said Kestrel, "we rest at the first kept place. Before anything. Before we go down. Whatever the board says. Whoever's ahead." She looked at Brann. "We'd have rested before the Elm Below. I said that, in my post. *I'd have rested. That's the annoying part.* This time we rest *first*." She looked at the island. "The last part's the longest. You don't run the longest part tired."

Brann looked at her for a long time.

"Xav," he said.

"Xav," agreed Kestrel. "And a pigeon."

***

On the ninth night, the night before, she sent a letter. She didn't send many letters. She'd sent one in September, a long time ago, to a man in a round town, and he'd answered *ask me then*.

She read it twice. Then she laughed. Out loud, at the window of VANTA's plain black Hall, with the island in the middle of the lake and its tower, and the door at the bottom of the steps under the water.

Brann, behind her, didn't ask what was funny. He never did.
