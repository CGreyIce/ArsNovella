Rachael had been careful for twenty-six years.

She was good at it. It was the thing she was best at, if she was honest, and she was always honest, because she didn't have time not to be. Crowds were a risk. Coughs were a risk. Other people's colds, other people's kids, other people's hands. She'd learned the list before she'd learned to read, and she'd kept it ever since, and it had kept her.

So when the egg came on Monday, she read the safety leaflet twice, and then she put it in the drawer, and then she took it out and read it a third time. Then she rang Deston.

"I'm not reading the rest of anything," she said. "Tell me what to press."

"There's nothing to press," said Deston, from inside his own egg across Singapore, and from somewhere north of a fen at the top of a game. His voice came out of her phone relaxed and patient, the way it had come out of every phone she'd owned since she was seventeen. "You lie down. It does the rest."

"That's what they said about the last three things that went wrong with me."

"Rae."

"I'm lying down," said Rachael. "I'm lying down, Dest. Don't *Rae* me."

*Herbalism. Apothecary.*

You learned a lot about medicine, being the patient. More than most people learned. You learned what everything was called and what it was for and what it did to you, because you'd had all of it. She'd sat in enough waiting rooms reading the backs of enough boxes to write half of them herself.

[ Mistbath · the Warm Hills
Travellers: 64 ]

It was night.

She came out of a little wooden door onto a wooden walkway, and the first thing that hit her was the warm. Warm air, wet air, smelling of stone and something green. The second thing was the steam. It came up out of the ground everywhere she looked, in white columns, out of cracks in the rock and out of gratings in the street and out of the roofs. It drifted across the lamps in the dark so that every lamp in the town had a halo round it.

A town in a valley, on terraces cut into a hillside. Every terrace had a pool. Square stone pools full of water that steamed and glowed faintly in the dark, green-blue, as if there were lights at the bottom. Bathhouses with wide low roofs. Covered walkways between them. Lanterns on poles. And people.

There were people on every bench.

Old people, mostly. Wrapped in blankets on the benches beside the pools, with cups of something hot in their hands. A man with a bandaged leg propped on a stool. A woman with her eyes shut and her feet in the water. Somebody's grandmother in a wheeled chair being pushed along the terrace by a girl, very slowly. Somebody somewhere coughing, the long tearing kind.

Rachael stopped.

She knew what this was. She'd have known it anywhere. She'd been to places like this all her life. Different buildings, same benches. Same blankets. Same cough.

"Dest," she said. "What is it."

"What's what?"

"The town. What is it. What do they *do* here."

There was a pause while he looked something up.

"Mistbath," he said. "It's a spa. People come from all over the Warm Hills to take the waters. It says here it's—" He stopped. "Oh."

"Yes," said Rachael. "*Oh.*"

"Rae—"

"They put me in a *hospital*," said Rachael, to the steam and the lamps and the whole warm wet valley. "Twenty-six years. I finally get out. I go somewhere *else*. And they put me in a *hospital*."

On the bench nearest her, the old man coughed again, the long tearing kind. And Rachael did what she'd done her whole life without thinking, the way you'd take your hand off a stove. She took a step back.

Then she stood there on the wooden walkway, one step back, and looked at the space she'd made.

"Rae," said Deston, very gently. "You can't catch anything in here."

She didn't say anything.

"I checked," said Deston. "Months ago. When you said you might. Nothing in here catches. Not off people. Not off anything. You can get hit. You can bleed. You can't *catch*." A pause. "It's in the rules. I read all of them. I wanted to be sure before I told you."

Rachael stood on the walkway for a long time.

Then she walked forward, one step, back to where she'd been. And then another. To the bench where the old man was sitting in his blanket, coughing. And she sat down on it, right next to him, close enough that their shoulders were nearly touching. She put her staff across her knees.

The old man looked at her. He had a cup of something hot. He held it out to her, without saying anything, the way old men on benches did.

She took it. It was some kind of tea. Bitter, green, too hot. She drank it anyway.

"Rae?" said Deston. "You okay?"

"I'm sitting on a bench," said Rachael.

"Okay."

"Next to a man with a cough."

Deston didn't say anything. She could hear him not saying anything. He'd always been good at that.

She sat there for twenty minutes, in the steam, in the dark, with the old man coughing beside her and a cup of bad tea in her hands. She didn't move away. Not once. She didn't have to.

Dennis answered in forty seconds. He'd been lying in an egg at the café in town since five to nine, waiting, because he didn't have one of his own and the café was where you went. The little wooden door beside hers opened. Dennis came out onto the walkway in the steam, and stopped, and looked round at all of it. The pools and the lamps and the old people in their blankets.

"Rachael," he said. "*Darling.* It's a *spa*."

"It's a hospital."

"It's a *spa*," said Dennis, with enormous satisfaction, and looked at the nearest bathhouse the way some people looked at a cake. "Do you know what people pay for this? On Earth? There are *queues*." He had a wand stuck through his belt like a pen, thin and a little crooked. "I took the wand," he said, seeing her look. "It was the smallest. It fits in a pocket. Small things are always worth more."

"That isn't true."

"It's true *enough*," said Dennis. Then he saw what she was sitting on, and who she was sitting next to, and what was in her hands.

He didn't say anything about it. Dennis knew her. He just sat down on her other side, so that she was in the middle of the bench, and stretched out his legs, and said, "Well. I'm staying."

Mistbath had a physician.

They found her on Tuesday, after the sun came up at midnight. Rachael would never get over that, a sunrise at midnight, the steam going gold all down the valley. The physician kept the cure-house at the top of the terraces, the big bathhouse with the green roof, and her name was Hettie. She was old and square and had her sleeves rolled up past the elbow, permanently. She was in the middle of telling a farmer with a burnt arm that he was a fool when Rachael walked in looking for someone to sell her herbs to.

Rachael looked at the farmer's arm. Then at the dressing Hettie was putting on it.

"That's too tight," she said.

Hettie looked up.

"It's swelling," said Rachael. "Look at his fingers. It's going to be too tight in an hour."

Hettie looked at the fingers. She looked at Rachael. She loosened the dressing, a little, without saying anything. Then she said, "You've done this before."

"I've had it done," said Rachael. "Lots."

Hettie looked at her for a long time. Then she put a roll of bandage in Rachael's hands and pointed at a woman on a bench by the door with a cut forehead.

"You're not going to fix her," said Hettie. "Nobody fixes anybody. People mend themselves. All you do is make sure they've got nothing else to do while they're at it." She went back to the farmer. "Go on. Make sure."

Rachael looked at the bandage. She looked at the woman with the cut forehead. She went and knelt down in front of her, and asked if she could look, and looked.

"Huh," said Rachael, quietly, to the window.

She stayed in the cure-house until two in the morning. Dennis came and found her there at one and stood in the doorway with his arms folded and watched her wrap a child's wrist.

"You're working," he said. "In a *game*. On your first *night*. For *free*."

"Hettie pays in herbs."

"Hettie pays in *herbs*," said Dennis to the ceiling, like a man in great pain. Then he went and sold the herbs for her, for more than they were worth, and came back pleased with himself.

He went straight past the bench and up the steps to the next terrace, and the next, two at a time, a spear in one hand. He was gone up the hill into the steam before either of them had stood up.

He didn't come back.

"*CALVIN*."

A long way up the hill, out of the steam, his voice came down to them.

"THERE'S A LAKE UP HERE," Calvin shouted, "AND IT'S *BOILING*."

Dennis put his face in his hands. Rachael sat on the bench and looked up the hill at the steam where Calvin had been.

She was smiling. She didn't notice for a while. When she did, she didn't stop.
