Hyvred knew her room by heart.

She didn't need to see it. Four steps from the bed to the desk. The chair was always pushed in, because if it wasn't she'd walk into it, and she'd walked into it enough times in her life to have stopped finding it funny. The shelf on the left was at the height of her eyebrow. The doorframe she touched with her right hand every time she went through it, without thinking, the way other people blinked.

She saw the world through a tube. That was how she'd explained it once, on stream, years ago, in a clip she'd made to show people. You hold a cardboard tube up to one eye and close the other, and that's it, that's the window. What's in the middle is sharp. Everything else is just dark. Not black. Not *nothing*. Just gone. Edges, she didn't have. Corners, she didn't have. You moved your head and the window moved with it, and you learned to move your head a lot.

She'd made the clip because people kept asking why she turned her whole face to look at chat instead of just her eyes. Somebody in chat, a friend of Maigo's she'd only just met, had watched it and said *oh, it's like the Flashlight mod*, laughing. Then, about ten minutes later, when it had sunk in that it wasn't a mod, it was her eyes, he'd apologised so many times and so badly that she'd laughed until she cried. She'd told him it was fine. It was fine. It was a good description. She'd been living in the Flashlight mod her whole life.

That had been Ice.

She thought about it now, standing beside the pod at the end of her bed, with her glasses in her hand.

***

The pod had come on Wednesday. She'd sat on the bed and looked at it for two days. Not because she was scared. Hyvred was rarely scared of anything; she was the one who jumped first and asked how deep it was on the way down. But she'd read something on Monday night, in the long technical thread about how the pods worked, and she hadn't been able to stop thinking about it.

*The Cradle doesn't use your eyes,* somebody had written. *That's the whole point of full dive. It talks to your brain directly. It doesn't send you a picture through the lens. It sends you the seeing.*

Somebody else had replied: *so what happens if your eyes don't work right?*

And somebody else, a long way down, a doctor or claiming to be one, had written: *in theory, nothing. The visual cortex gets the whole scene regardless. In theory, the pod doesn't care what your eyes are like.*

*In theory.*

She'd read that line about forty times.

She put her glasses on the desk, folded, in the place where she always put them. She climbed into the pod and lay down in the warm gel, and felt it settle under her shoulders and the back of her head like a hand.

Her phone was propped on the desk where she could hear it, on a call.

"Are you in?" said Bee, from Australia, three hours ahead and very loud. "Are you *in*? Hyv. Hyv. Tell me when you're in."

"I'm lying in it."

"Then go *in*."

"I'm going."

"You've been going for two days."

"I'm *going*, Bee." She put her finger on the glass. *This Cradle registers to one person, permanently.* The thin line of light went across, once.

"Are you scared?" said Bee, in a different voice.

Hyvred thought about it, honestly.

"No," she said. "I'm—I don't know what I am."

"Okay," said Bee. "Well. I'm going in mine in ten minutes, so if you see something cool, you have to tell me before I see it, or it doesn't count."

"Deal," said Hyvred, and closed her eyes, out of habit.

***

She opened them in a room of pale wood, with rain on the window, and she saw the ceiling.

Not a part of the ceiling, and not the bit in the middle of the tube. The whole ceiling. All of it, at once, from where it met the wall on the left to where it met the wall on the right. The beams, and the knots in the beams, and the corners. The *corners*. All four. She was looking straight ahead at the window, and at the same time, without moving her eyes or her head or anything at all, she could see the ceiling and both walls and the floor, and the mirror at the edge of everything on the left, and the rack of weapons at the edge of everything on the right.

Edges.

She had edges.

She didn't make a sound for a long time. She stood very still in the middle of the room, because she was afraid that if she moved, it would stop. Then she moved her hand, very slowly, out to the side, away from her body, where hands always went into the dark. And she *saw it go*. Her own hand, at the edge of the world, moving, not in the window but out at the side, where it had never in her life been.

She started to cry.

It wasn't the kind of crying she'd done before. It came up from somewhere deep and wouldn't stop, and she didn't try to stop it. She stood in the little wooden room and cried with her hand held out at her side, watching it, moving her fingers, watching them move at the edge of everything.

The stream had been running since she'd lain down. She'd set it up last night, in Follow, so people could see her model in the room. She'd wanted the first thing they saw to be her standing there with her horns and her tail and her red ribbon, and her saying *hi*. She'd planned a whole bit.

She hadn't planned this.

Her chat was a ghost panel at the edge of her vision. At the *edge*. She could see it there without turning her head. That set her off again, and she laughed, loud, right in the middle of crying, because she couldn't help it.

"Sorry," she said to it, to all of them. "Sorry, sorry, give me a minute. I can see the ceiling."

Her chat didn't know yet. They were a minute behind, and some of them had never seen the old clip. She watched them catch up: watching her stand there, watching her cry, typing *are you ok?? HYV??*, and then someone who'd been around a long time typing *she can see the whole room. she has tunnel vision IRL*. And then the chat went quiet in a way she'd never seen it go quiet. Then it filled up with something else entirely.

"It works," she said. "It's—it actually works. You guys. I can see the corners. I can see my *hand*." She held it out again and moved it and laughed. "Look. Look where my hand is. I can see it *there*."

On the desk at the end of her real bed, in her real room, her phone was still on the call. She'd forgotten. Bee had been listening the whole time.

"Hyv," said Bee's voice, very quietly, close at her ear. The Cradle had paired with her phone when she set it up, and calls came through like that, small and near, as if someone were standing just behind her. "Hyv. Are you okay?"

"I can see the ceiling, Bee."

There was a long pause, in Australia.

"Okay," said Bee, and her voice had gone strange. "Okay. That counts. That counts as something cool. You win."

***

She did the rest of it in a haze.

The mirror: her model, there in the glass, horns and tail and the long dark wavy hair with the crimson through it. The red ribbon. The gold hairpin. Her eyes, gold, the left one with the little red crosshair in the iris. She'd always thought of it as a scope. A narrow sight, a little circle with a cross, lined up on one thing at a time. She looked at it now in the mirror, in a body that could see the whole room, and laughed again.

*A keepsake of your likeness*, the mirror offered, and showed her glasses. Round, gold-rimmed, her real ones, scanned from the desk in the forty seconds before she'd gone under.

She took them. She didn't need them. She pushed them up into her hair, on top of her head between the horns, the way she sometimes did at home when she was looking at something close. They sat there, gold in the dark hair.

"I'm keeping these," she told chat. "As a souvenir. Of the old window."

The pain slider she left at twenty, because she wasn't stupid, whatever certain people in swamps did. Her name was free; nobody had stolen *Hyvred*, which she thought was very respectful of them. At the rack she stood for a long time, and then took the bow.

She'd known since Monday night, really. Since the line in the thread. *In theory, the pod doesn't care.* If it didn't care, she wanted the weapon that was all about looking. The one where you stood very still and saw everything, and picked one thing out of everything, and sent something there.

"Archer," she told chat. "Obviously. I want to look at things a long way away. I've never looked at anything a long way away in my *life*."

***

The wind hit her first, and then the light, and then the edges.

She was standing on a wide stone platform at the top of a cliff. Behind her a little white town climbed the slope in steps: whitewashed walls and blue doors and red roofs, washing lines between the houses, gulls on every chimney. In front of her the cliff went down, a long way down, white and streaked with green, to a bay. And beyond the bay was the sea.

All of it.

She'd seen the sea before. She'd been to beaches. She'd stood on East Coast with Bee on the phone and turned her head slowly from left to right, a piece at a time, building it in her head like a jigsaw: the sea there, the sky there, the edge where they met there. She'd always known roughly what it was supposed to look like. She'd seen photographs. She'd never once seen it the way it was, all at once, from one side to the other, in one look.

Now she did.

The sea went from the edge of the world on her left to the edge of the world on her right, flat and enormous and grey-blue and moving, a hundred thousand small moving lights. The sky went up from it, pale at the bottom and deeper at the top, and she could see the *top*. She could see the place where the sky went over her head, without tipping her head back, at the very top edge of everything. And at the bottom edge, at the same time, the white stones of the platform under her boots, and the tips of her own boots.

And gulls. Gulls everywhere. Not in the window. *Everywhere*. On the left, wheeling. On the right, landing on a roof. At the top edge, very high, hanging on the wind. At the bottom edge, one walking past her feet, looking for crumbs.

She kept flinching.

That was the thing she hadn't expected. Every time a gull moved at the edge of her sight, her whole body jumped, the way it would have jumped at home if somebody had touched her out of the dark. Her brain had never had anything at the edges before, not in twenty-five years. Now it had *everything* at the edges, all of it moving, and it didn't know what to do. It kept saying *something's there, something's there, look, look*, and she kept turning her head, and then remembering she didn't have to.

After about a minute of it, she sat down on the stone with her legs crossed and her bow across her knees and just looked.

"Sorry," she said to chat. "Sorry. I need to sit down. There's too much. There's too much at the sides."

She sat on the clifftop at Saltwhistle, in the wind, with her horns and her red ribbon and her glasses pushed up in her hair. For a long time she did nothing at all except look at the sea, from one side to the other, without moving her head.

Players went past her, coming out of the door, stopping, staring, going on. A few looked at her, a cow girl crying on the ground, and looked away again, politely. One, a boy in a linen shirt, stopped and asked if she was all right. She said yes. She said *I'm better than all right*, and he said *oh, okay, cool*, and went off to look for rats.

***

When she could stand up without flinching at every gull, she went looking for someone to teach her the bow.

She found Ness, down on the harbour wall, mending a net with her feet dangling over the water. Ness was about sixty, tanned like leather, with a short bow lying beside her on the stones and a quiver of arrows with grey gull feathers. Every few minutes, without looking up from the net, she'd pick up the bow, draw, and loose. A gull that had been going for the fish on the drying racks along the harbour would go tumbling into the sea with a squawk, unhurt, very offended, and Ness would go back to her net.

"Blunt heads," she said, when Hyvred asked. "Don't hurt 'em. Just teaches 'em." She looked up at Hyvred properly, for the first time: the horns, the ribbon, the bow. "You've been crying."

"I can see the sea," said Hyvred. "All of it. I've never seen all of it before."

Ness looked at her for a long, careful moment. It was the look Ice's NPCs had given him on stream, Hyvred thought. The one that went in and checked what you were made of.

"Well, then," said Ness, and put down her net. "You'll want to learn to hit something in it."

***

The first form was called the Long Draw.

You stood side-on to the target, feet apart. You held the bow out at arm's length in your left hand and the string with three fingers of your right, and you pulled. Not with your arm. With your back, Ness said, putting a hard brown hand flat between Hyvred's shoulder blades. *Here. Squeeze it here. Like you're trying to hold a coin between them.* You brought the string back to the corner of your mouth. You looked. And then you didn't let go. You *stopped holding on*, which was different, Ness said, and the arrow went on its own.

"Let go," said Ness. "Told you. Again."

She did it again. Nineteen. Again. Twenty-six. Her back ached, a new ache, a deep pull between her shoulders. The target was a straw bale on the harbour wall with a painted circle on it, thirty paces off, and her arrows went everywhere except into the circle.

Then, on the ninth or tenth draw, something happened that she didn't understand until much later.

She'd been doing what everybody did: looking at the circle. Narrowing down on it, the way she'd narrowed down on everything all her life, pulling the whole world in to that one small sharp place in the middle of the tube. That was what she knew how to do. She'd had twenty-five years of practice.

And this time, without meaning to, she let the edges come back.

She looked at the circle, and she *also* saw everything else. The wall, the water, the wind moving on the water, a gull at the top edge leaning on the air. The way the tops of the waves were all moving left, and the pennant on the fishing boat at the right edge of everything, standing out stiff, pointing left. The whole of the wind, all at once, without looking for it.

She moved the bow, a hair, to the right.

She stopped holding on.

The arrow went out in a long shallow curve, and the wind took it, a hair to the left, exactly as far as she'd known it would. It went into the circle, not quite in the middle, but in the circle, with a solid *thock* she felt in her chest.

Ness had stopped mending her net.

"Do that again," she said.

Hyvred did it again. Fifty-one. Again. Forty-eight, a little low. Again. Fifty-five, and the arrow went in nearly in the middle.

"Most people," said Ness, slowly, "take a week to see the wind. Some never do. They look at the target." She looked at Hyvred, at her face, and her eyes went for a moment to the left one, the gold one with the little red cross in it. "You're looking at all of it."

"I've never been able to look at all of it before," said Hyvred. "I keep wanting to."

She stayed out until it was properly dark and the stars came out. She'd never seen the stars all at once either, the whole sky of them from edge to edge. She lay on her back on the clifftop on the warm stone and looked at them for an hour, not playing, not streaming much, not saying anything. Her chat kept her company and didn't say much either.

When she logged out it was nearly midnight, and she lay in the gel in the dark with the lid lifting slowly over her face. Her real room came back: the tube, the dark at the sides, the window in the middle with the ceiling fan in it, turning. She lay there for a long time and looked at the ceiling, the bit she could see.

Then she reached out to the right, without looking, to where the desk was, four steps away from the bed. She found her glasses in their place, folded, exactly where she'd left them, and held them in her hand for a while.

She didn't put them on. She just held them.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. She knew who it was. Bee always messaged when she logged out. *You up? Was it cool? Tell me everything.*

It wasn't Bee.
