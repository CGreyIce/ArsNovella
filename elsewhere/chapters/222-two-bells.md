*Here, at the top of the tallest bell tower in the world,* said Hako, in his head, in the voice, *we find a group of twenty travellers in a state of considerable distress.*

He didn't say it out loud. He'd been told not to, at the second quarter, with some force. But the voice went on in his head anyway, the way it always did. Smooth, British, unhurried. The voice of a man in a hide with a long lens, watching something enormous come down to a river to drink.

He'd done the voice since Otterbank. Since the first day, waist-deep in the shallows below the jetty with his spear, narrating an otter. It had started as a joke for nobody. Then it had stopped being a joke. He'd found out, somewhere in the first winter, that if he narrated a thing he could look at it. Even if it was enormous and frightening and coming at him. The voice made it something you watched instead of something that happened to you.

*The third quarter,* said the voice, *is notable chiefly for its wind.*

***

It was. It came down the hollow from the top of the tower every time the great bell rang. Not often. Once every forty seconds or so, very slow, very deep. *Boom.* Like a door shutting somewhere at the top of the world. And a moment after the sound, the wind. A great push of air straight down the hollow. Hard enough that if you weren't holding the wall when it came, it would take you off the stair and down into the dark past four hundred feet of ropes.

"*Brace*," said Hyvred.

They braced. Twenty people with their backs flat to the stone on the inside of the stair, hands on the wall. The wind came down past them, roaring, and the ropes in the hollow swung out and back like the strings of a harp somebody had dragged a hand across.

"Go."

They went. On the change, with Val calling the small bells, because the stair still moved. Six steps, eight. Then *brace*. And the boom, and the wind.

The great bell was ringing on its own now. Under the method, under the changes. Slow. Val had heard it on the rocks with her ear against the stone. *Like a heartbeat. Getting faster.*

*It was not getting faster yet,* said the voice in Hako's head. *But one had the distinct impression that it intended to.*

***

The board came up when they were nearly at the top. Everybody looked. You couldn't not.

"Fourteen fell on the first quarter," said Kei. "They didn't know about the bells. They fought the Ringers and the stair came down." He was quiet for a moment. "Six left. They went back down."

*The Open Hand,* said the voice in Hako's head, *a large and confident species, had failed to notice that the tower was a musical instrument.* He felt sorry for them, a bit. Not much. They'd called the second banner *nobody* on a switchback in the dark.

"VANTA's *resting*," said Gigi. "Again."

"She said she would," said Ice.

He was at the front, as always, with his back to the wall and his shield on his arm. He hadn't said anything since the second night. Not since Val had told them what was at the top. *Something very slow. Like a heartbeat. Getting faster. Like it was running out of time.*

He'd said *okay*. And nothing else, for a whole day.

Hako had watched him not say anything. He'd watched a lot of things not say anything, in Otterbank, from the shallows. Herons, mostly. They stood very still in the water for a very long time, and then they didn't.

"*Brace*," said Hyvred.

***

The belfry was the biggest room Hako had ever been in that wasn't outside.

It was open on all four sides. Great arches of grey stone, taller than the round town's dome, and through every arch the night. The stars, enormous and close. And far below, so far below it didn't seem real, Great Lake Masmark, black. With the whole ring of the city lit all the way round it like a necklace somebody had dropped in the dark.

The wind came through the arches. Not the hollow's wind, the push. Just wind, high and cold and clean, the wind at the top of the world.

And in the middle of the belfry, hanging from a beam the size of a ship, the bell.

*The great bell of Masmark,* said the voice, very quietly. *Seen at close range for the first time by anyone living.*

It was green. Bronze, gone green with a thousand years. And huge. Bigger than the netloft. Bigger than the fish man's cart and the boatbuilder's and the lane between them put together. Its mouth hung open over a hole in the floor that went straight down the hollow of the tower, four hundred feet, to the stair. It wasn't moving.

Beside it stood the thing that rang it.

A Ringer. Like the twelve in the first quarter, but not like them. Taller than the bell. Bronze, green, in a long bronze robe that went down into the floor as if it had grown there. It had no rope. It had a hammer. A bronze hammer as long as Salty's sword was long, held in both hands, resting head-down on the stone beside the bell.

Its face was turned to the stars. It wasn't looking at them. It was looking out through the north arch at nothing, the way the Ringers in the first quarter had looked at each other to keep time. As if it were listening for something a very long way off.

Then it lifted the hammer, slowly, all the way back over its shoulder.

And swung.

*BOOM.*

Hako had heard it for two nights from below, through stone. He'd thought he knew what it was. He didn't. It went through him like a wave through a jetty. Through his feet and his ribs and his teeth, and out the other side. And with it, from the mouth of the bell, a ring of force, rolling out across the belfry floor toward the arches. Toward the open sky and the four hundred feet down to the lake.

"*BRACE*—"

Bee's Walls came up, six of them, grey, along the arches. The ring of force hit them and they held, just. Everybody behind them went down on one knee, or flat, or into somebody else. Salty grabbed Wendy. Azac grabbed Jun Liang. Hako grabbed the nearest thing, which turned out to be Malcolm's stick, with Malcolm still holding it.

The ring went past.

The Hour lowered its hammer. Slowly. And turned its bronze face back to the north arch. Listening.

"Forty seconds," said Val, from the floor. She had her book open. She'd been counting. "Forty-one. Between that one and the last one in the hollow."

"And the window," said Kei. "Between strikes."

"Thirty-something," said Val. "It takes about eight seconds to lift."

***

They fought it the way you'd fight it.

The way, Hako was quite sure, Meridian were fighting it at that moment in their own belfry in their own tower. Twenty minutes ahead, maybe more, by the book. Carefully. Nobody falling.

Brace for the strike. Bee's Walls along the arches. Then, in the window, go. Everybody. Thirty seconds of everything they had. Salty's black sword and Xav's two blades and Jun Liang's hammer. Xola's trident. Gigi's thorns coming up through the floor round its bronze robe. Thea's lamp. Malcolm and Val and every bow. Ice at the front taking whatever the Hour did with its free hand between strikes, which was mostly push. And then, when the hammer started to go back over its shoulder, *brace*. Everybody back behind the Walls. The boom. The ring. And again.

*A sound strategy,* said the voice in Hako's head. *Tried, tested, and, one suspects, rather slow.*

He was standing in front of the bell.

Not behind the Walls with everybody else. Out on the open floor of the belfry, between the Hour and its bell, with his shield up. Close. Closer than anybody had been all fight. Close enough to touch the green bronze lip of the bell's mouth if he'd put his hand out.

The bell didn't ring.

No ring of force. No wave across the floor toward the arches. Nothing. The Hour stood there with its hammer on Ice's shield and its bronze face looking down at him. And the belfry was completely silent except for the wind.

Hako would try to narrate it afterward, in the voice, at the long table in the netloft, and fail.

It wasn't like anything. It wasn't like a fight. It was like a drum. The Hour lifting the hammer, and Ice going in under it, and *crack*. And the Hour lifting it again, faster, because it was getting faster, and Ice going in under it again. Faster. Not waiting for it. *Going* for it. Moving the moment the hammer started back, as if he knew exactly when it would come down. Knew it better than the Hour did.

Hako watched him and thought about a cellar he hadn't been in. Fifty-one seconds, Val had said, at the long table, holding her book. No rests. Every beat.

It was Salty.

Of course it was. Hako would think about that for weeks. Salty, who'd swung a sword his whole life as if it were too small. Who'd forged one in the dark that was the right size, and quenched it in a stream with the lamp out, and stepped out one pace on the longest night and made the whole dark lean back.
