Whitecairn sat in a high valley under three mountains, and it had been snowing since he got there.

It wasn't heavy snow. It was the kind that came down slow and steady and forever, big soft flakes drifting past the windows. It lay a foot deep on every roof in the village and had done, he gathered, since about October. The houses were low and thick-walled and built of grey stone, with turf on the roofs under the snow and woodsmoke coming out of every chimney. There was a mine in the side of the nearest mountain, a black mouth with a wooden frame round it and a cart track leading down. There was a smithy at the bottom of the track, where the smith worked iron out of the mine. There was a long low barn at the edge of the village where, on winter evenings, the miners boxed.

That was why he'd stayed. That was why he'd taken nothing off the rack in the wooden room on the first morning, and walked out into the snow, and asked the first person he met where he could learn to punch.

He'd always been a fighter-game guy. Not just fighters: anything where you read the other person. Where you watched what they did, and waited, and when they showed you the thing they always did, you were already there. He'd hosted tournaments for years, in other games. He'd run brackets, read rooms, kept fifty people calm on a call while a final went to the last round. He liked the moment before anybody moved, when you could see what was going to happen in the way somebody stood.

So: fists. He'd thought it would be the hardest thing in the game. It was.

***

The man who ran the barn was called Coll Rudd, and he'd been a prizefighter.

You could see it in his face. His nose had been broken so many times it went two ways at once, and both his ears were thick and cauliflowered. He had about half his teeth and the slowest, most careful walk Drew had ever seen. He was sixty, maybe. He sat on an upturned crate at the edge of the sawdust ring in the barn with a mug of something hot. He watched the miners box in the evenings with his eyes half shut, and he hadn't said a word to Drew for the first two days.

On the third, he said, "You keep your eyes open."

Drew had been sparring with a young miner called Fenn, who was bigger than him and faster than him and hit like a falling door. Drew had been losing, steadily, all evening. He looked over at the crate.

"Sorry?"

"When he swings." Coll Rudd nodded at Fenn. "Everyone shuts their eyes. Just a bit. Just when it comes. Can't help it. You don't." He drank from his mug. "You watch it come."

"I like to see what's happening."

"Hm." Coll Rudd put the mug down. He got up off the crate, slowly, the way he did everything, and walked into the ring. He stood in front of Drew in the sawdust, a short old man with a broken face and his hands down by his sides. "Hit me."

Drew looked at him.

"Go on."

Drew hit him. Not hard. A straight right, the way the window had taught him on the first day, at the old man's broken nose.

It wasn't there.

Coll Rudd hadn't moved. That was what it looked like. He hadn't stepped back or ducked or put up his hands. His head had just gone a little to one side, two inches, the way you'd tilt your head to hear something. The fist had gone past his ear into nothing. And while Drew's arm was still out there, empty, with all his weight on the front foot, Coll Rudd's right hand came up from his side, very slowly, and tapped him under the chin.

It didn't hurt. It barely touched. But Drew felt exactly how hard it could have been.

"That," said Coll Rudd, "is all there is."

It was the hardest thing he'd ever learned in any game, and he loved every minute of it.

Slip was the first half: moving your head off the line of the punch. Not away. Not back, not ducking. Just *off*, two inches, at the last moment, so the fist went past. You couldn't do it by guessing. If you moved early, the other man saw you move and hit you where you'd gone. You had to wait. You had to watch it come, all the way, with your eyes open. And then, at the last possible instant, you had to move just enough and no more.

Answer was the second half. While their arm was still out there and their weight was still forward, you were already hitting them. Not after. *While*. The window graded it like everything else. The first night his Slip was nineteen per cent and his Answer was eleven, and Fenn hit him in the face about forty times.

He kept the pain on twenty. He wasn't Ice.

"You're watching his shoulder," said Coll Rudd, on the fourth night.

"Is that wrong?"

"It's late." The old man was on his crate again. "Shoulder's the last bit to move. Watch his feet. Everything starts in the feet. By the time it's in the shoulder, it's already in your face."

Drew watched Fenn's feet.

He felt it then, for the first time. Fenn's back foot turned, very slightly, a half-second before the punch came. A tiny shift, like a man getting ready to stand up. It was there every time. Fenn did it every single time and had no idea he did it.

He took Mining and Blacksmithing, because the mine was there and the smith was there and his fists needed something on them.

The smith was a big quiet woman called Hild, the mother of the girl with soot on her nose. She showed him how to work the iron that came out of the mountain into knuckle-guards. Four rings joined by a bar, shaped to the hand, with a pad of leather inside. It took him three days to make a pair that didn't hurt to wear. They were ugly and lumpy and he was prouder of them than of anything he'd made in years.

He hit level ten on the afternoon of the eleventh of December, his time. A Friday. He did it on the mountain, above the mine, in the snow, against something the miners called the Old Grey.

It was a bear. Or something like a bear: bigger, greyer, with a pale frost-coloured ruff, and claws that left five black grooves in the rock where it missed. It came down out of the high snow in winter when the hunting was bad and took goats, and once, Hild said, a miner. Every year the village went up and drove it back. This year it had come early, and the village was short of men, and there were five travellers in Whitecairn who were nearly level ten.

Drew went up with them, and with Fenn and Coll Rudd. The old man walked all the way up the mountain in the snow, slowly, and sat on a rock to watch.

He didn't hit it first. He let the others do that. A spear, two swords, a girl from Brazil with a bow. He stood in the snow a little way off and watched the Old Grey's feet.

It took him a while. Bears weren't miners. But everything starts in the feet. On the fourth time it reared up to bring both paws down on the spear, he saw it. Before it rose, before the ruff came up, its back feet dug in. A long scrape in the snow, like a bull pawing. It always did that.

On the fifth time, he was standing in front of it when it did it.

He watched it come. All the way, with his eyes open, the whole huge grey weight of it rising up over him and the paws coming down. At the very last instant he moved his head two inches to the left.

The paw went past his ear and into the snow beside him. And while it was still there, while the bear's whole weight was forward on that one paw, he answered. Iron knuckles, right hand, everything from his back foot up through his hip and his shoulder, into the soft place under its jaw.

Then he looked at the map.

He'd been looking at the map for days, if he was honest. Every night after the call with Tiger he'd opened it and looked at the pale nothing between Whitecairn and the place Tiger described. A city of locks on a hill, with canals, and a girl called Pell on a gate. It was a long way. It was most of the world away. The game's map only showed what you'd seen. But Kei had been asking everybody where they were, and Kei had done some sums, and Kei's sums said twenty-four days' walk, if nothing went wrong, once the roads opened at ten.

The roads were open.

Twenty-four days. Across mountains, and a big river, and a lot of country nobody he knew had walked yet. At level ten, on his own.

He thought about Tiger falling into a canal on his first morning, about four seconds after he came out of the door. He thought about Tiger trying to walk twenty-four days the other way. Tiger would be in a river by day two and a different river by day five, and he'd love every second of it. He'd arrive in Whitecairn in March, soaking wet, with forty new friends and no idea how he'd got there.

Nine years. As long as Ice and Xav, give or take. The same kind of thing: easy, deep, nothing to explain. And for nine years it had been Drew who called, Drew who remembered, Drew who stayed. Not because Tiger wouldn't. Tiger just forgot, and then turned up anyway, with total joy, three hours late. Drew had never minded. He was the one who stayed with Tiger. That was just what it was.

He packed. It didn't take long. His knuckles. His pick. Hild's daughter gave him a twist of paper with three oatcakes in it, and said, "Is it eighty-eight days?", and he said, "Twenty-four," and she looked disappointed.

He walked for four more days.

Through the pine forest, and out of it into rolling country with sheep on it and no people. Across a river on a ferry, a flat raft on a rope with an old ferryman who charged him two copper and didn't speak. Past an intermediate town on a crossroads where he stopped for one night, sold some ore, bought bread, and left before dawn. Up into low hills and down out of them again. He slept at waystations: a fire, a lean-to, a stone with the miles carved on it. He fought what came out at him on the road, which wasn't much in daylight. A wolf on the second evening, which he watched, and waited for, and answered. Two men on the third, who looked at him from the trees beside the road for a long time and decided not to.
