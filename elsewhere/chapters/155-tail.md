Xola had known for about twelve minutes.

She'd known since *don't call him that in front of me*. That had been the first thing. A bit loud, a bit fast, for somebody asking a casual question about a guildmate. She'd put it down to Hyvred being Hyvred. Everybody in the round town knew Hyvred went off like a firework if you looked at her wrong. It was sort of her thing.

Then *I know*, much too fast, when Thea mentioned Chi was in the Fell Country with him. That had been the second thing.

Then the tail.

Xola had gills. She knew about having a part of you that told on you whether you liked it or not. Hers flared when she was angry and fluttered when she was nervous. When she was happy they did a sort of ripple, which Slep said looked like a curtain in a breeze. She'd given up trying to make them behave in about the first week. So she knew exactly what she was looking at when she glanced down, about ten minutes into the story, and saw Hyvred's tail.

It was wrapped round the leg of Hyvred's chair so tight the tip had gone white.

It had got tighter at *he liked me first*. Xola had watched it happen. It had got tighter again at *he liked me, after*. And at *he just turned up from further away*, it had gone so tight that Xola had nearly stopped talking to say *are you okay*.

She hadn't stopped. She'd kept telling it. Because Hyvred had asked, and it was a true story. And because by then Xola knew, absolutely knew, with the whole of her chest, exactly why Hyvred had asked.

Thea had known too. Thea had put her hand on Xola's arm under the table at *don't call him that*. That was their old signal, from years of calls. *Careful. Something's here.* Thea's eyes had gone to the tail about a minute after Xola's had.

And then Hyvred had said *does he still—* and stopped. She'd gone the colour of a brick, with her tail strangling the chair. And Xola thought, with a feeling like the whole world had just tipped very slightly sideways: *oh. Oh, Hyv.*

"Hyv," said Xola. "Why do you want to know?"

***

Hyvred stared at her.

"Because," said Hyvred.

"Because what?"

"Because I *owe* him."

Xola waited.

"*Money*," said Hyvred. Her voice had gone up. It went up a lot. Xola had heard it on Ice's stream, in the chat, in capitals. It was even more impressive in person. "I owe him three thousand seven hundred dollars and fifty cents. And a *lesson*. It's a *debt*. It's *financial*. I need to know who I'm *paying*. That's *all*. You'd want to know who you were paying. Anyone would. It's *normal*."

"It's normal," agreed Xola.

"*Thank* you."

"It's very normal," said Xola, "to ask the two people who've known somebody longest in the whole guild what he's like. And why he doesn't flinch. And why he keeps his pain at fifty. And why he goes to the far end of rooms. And whether he *still*—" She stopped, on purpose, exactly where Hyvred had stopped. "For *financial* reasons."

Hyvred's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"Xo," said Thea, beside her, in her careful voice.

Xola ignored her. She couldn't help it. It had been the worst, longest, most tangled week she could remember, in or out of the game. She'd watched eleven hours of Gigi's streams, and sat on a step for an hour and a half. She'd said *I heard* to someone she'd sworn she'd never say anything to. And now, here, at a little table on Spoke Street, the funniest thing that had happened to anyone in months was sitting across from her holding a fish pie. And it was *Hyvred*. And it was *Ice*. Of all the people in the world.

She leaned forward, put her chin on her hands, and smiled the biggest, slowest, most delighted smile she had.

"Hyv," she said. "You *like* him."

***

What happened next, Xola would describe to Slep later as the single best thing she had ever seen in her life. She'd seen a sheep knock over a Moor Hound, so that was saying something.

Hyvred's tail went straight up.

It let go of the chair leg all at once and shot straight up behind her like a flagpole. And every single hair on it stood on end, so that it went from a long thin tail to a sort of bottle-brush in about a quarter of a second. It was exactly like a cat whose tail had been stepped on. Xola had seen a cat do exactly this once, on somebody's stream, when a dog walked past it. The noise had been the same too.

"*MRP*," said Hyvred.

She stood up so fast her chair went over backwards. Her knee hit the table, and the table jumped. The fish pie flew out of her hands and landed upside down in Thea's lap. Thea said *oh* in a very small voice.

"I DO *NOT*," said Hyvred.

Half of Spoke Street turned round.

"You do," said Xola, delighted.

"I DO *NOT*. I *OWE* HIM. That's *DIFFERENT*. That's a *COMPLETELY DIFFERENT THING*. You can owe somebody without—that's *NOT*—I'm going to *HIT* him. When he comes back. I'm going to hit him *SO* hard. That's what I *DO*. I *HIT* him. That's the *OPPOSITE*—"

"You hit him because he patted you on the head," said Xola.

Hyvred made a sound that wasn't a word. It was higher than *mrp*. It was a sort of *eep* with a kettle behind it.

"*Everyone* knows about the head," said Xola. "There's a *clip*. It's got about a million views. *Somebody got patted on the head.* Hana said it on her stream. And then you went and *hid behind Bee*."

"I DIDN'T *HIDE*, I *STOOD* there—"

"Behind Bee's *wings*."

"*THEY'RE VERY BIG WINGS.*"

Thea had put her face in her hands. Her shoulders were shaking. Xola couldn't tell if she was laughing or crying, and then Thea made a small helpless snorting noise through her fingers and Xola knew. Thea was laughing. Properly. The way she'd laughed on the bridge in Brackenford, loud and surprised, with fish pie all over her lap.

Hyvred looked at Thea laughing. She looked at Xola, grinning. She looked at half of Spoke Street, staring. Her tail was straight up, like a bottle-brush, and her face was the colour of a postbox.

And she ran.

***

Xola went after her. Of course she did. It wasn't even a decision.

"*HYV*," she shouted, running down Spoke Street with her trident bouncing on her back. "*HYV, COME BACK, I'M NOT GOING TO*—"

"*GO AWAY*."

"I'M NOT GOING TO TELL ANYONE—"

"*THERE'S NOTHING TO TELL*."

"THEN I'M NOT GOING TO TELL ANYONE *NOTHING*!"

Hyvred went round the corner into the round market at a speed Xola hadn't known she had. She went past the fountain, where Xyro was sitting on the rim drying out from his third fall of the week. He looked up and said *hi Hyv* and got no answer. She took the south steps of the Hall of Roads three at a time. She shot past Wendy at the gate. Wendy opened her mouth to ask the question and closed it again, because Hyvred was already through.

Xola went past the gate too.

"Who'd stand next to *you*?" Wendy said, to her back.

"*HYV*," shouted Xola, "*BUT SHE WON'T LET ME*."

She heard Slep laugh, behind her, at the gate. Slep never laughed.

Hyvred went across the floor of the Hall of Roads like an arrow, past the counter. Ama Corrie looked up, said *no running*, and was ignored by both of them. Up the stair. Up a hundred and six steps to the first landing. Up forty more to the second.

Where Bee was sitting with her sketchbook, drawing.

Hyvred went round behind Bee without stopping. She went down on her knees on the step above her, right behind Bee's back, and put her face in between Bee's wings. Bee's wings were very big, Xola had to admit. Pale and soft and much too large for the stair, like a swan's. Hyvred more or less disappeared into them. All that was left was the very top of a pair of gold horns, and the tail, still straight up, still bottle-brushed, sticking out over Bee's shoulder like a feather duster.

Xola arrived on the second landing out of breath.

Bee looked up at her from her sketchbook. She didn't look surprised. She looked like a woman who'd been waiting for this exact moment for months and was now very satisfied that it had arrived.

"Hi, Xola," said Bee.

"Hi," said Xola, panting. "Is she—"

"Yes," said Bee.

"Since when?"

Bee thought about it.

"The terrace," she said. "In the city with no name. When he put his hand on her head. I saw her face." She went back to her drawing. "I said I wouldn't say anything. I said somebody else could. Some day. Somebody braver. Or louder." She glanced up at Xola, and her eyes were full of laughter. "You're very loud."

"*Bee*," said a muffled voice from between the wings.

"You're very loud too, Hyv," said Bee kindly.

"*mrp*."

***

Xola sat down on the step below them. She was still out of breath, and her gills were going like a fan. She didn't care.

She looked at the tail sticking out over Bee's shoulder. After a while she stopped grinning so much, and something else came up underneath. Something softer.

"Hyv," she said.

"Go away."

"I'm not going to tell him."

"There's *nothing* to tell."

"Okay," said Xola. "Then I'm not going to tell him nothing. I'm not going to tell anybody nothing. Thea won't either. She's sitting on Spoke Street with fish pie in her lap, laughing. She hasn't laughed like that in a year." She leaned her back against the stair wall. "I just. I wanted to say. It's okay."

The tail moved. Not much. It came down about an inch.

"It's *not* okay," said the voice between the wings. "It's the *worst* thing that's ever happened to me. And I've had some *bad things* happen to me."

"I know," said Xola. And she did. Everybody knew about Hyvred's eyes now. About the tube she'd seen the world through, and the surgery in the spring, and the four corners of a ceiling. "I know you have."

There was a long silence on the stair.

"He won't notice," said Xola. "You know that. He won't notice in a million years. He notices everything about everybody. He'll notice if you've slept badly, or your bow's strung wrong, or you're about to walk into a door. He notices everything except anything about himself." She laughed a bit, quietly. "He'll be the last person in the whole game to work it out. You could stand in front of him with a sign."

"I'm not *going* to stand in front of him with a *sign*."

"I know. I'm just saying. You're safe. He's the most oblivious man alive about this one thing."

The tail came down another inch.

"Also," said Xola, "if you hit him too hard, I'm putting a trident through your foot."

A gold horn came out from between the wings. Then an eye, behind a pair of glasses, glaring.

"You say that to *everyone*," said Hyvred.

"I say it to people I like," said Xola.

The eye looked at her for a long time.

"Does he," said Hyvred. Very small. From behind the glasses. "Still. You didn't answer. At the table."

Xola stopped smiling.

She hadn't answered. She'd been too busy being delighted. She looked at the one eye between the wings, and the glasses, and the gold horn, and thought about how long it must have taken Hyvred to get those three words out, the first time, at the table.

"No," said Xola.

She said it carefully, the way Thea said things she'd thought about for a long time. Because it mattered, and she wanted it to be right.

"Not like that. Not for years. Not for a long, long time." She looked at her hands. "He's my friend. He's Thea's friend. He's the one who turns up. That's what he is to us now. That's all it is. It's a lot. But it's that." She looked up. "I'd know, Hyv. I've known him a long time. I'd know."

The eye didn't move.

"Okay," said Hyvred, after a long time. Very quietly. It was the first time Xola had ever heard her say it. It sounded exactly like him.

Then the eye went back between the wings. But the tail came down all the way, slowly. It lay along the stair. It wasn't a bottle-brush any more, just a long thin tail. And the tip of it, very slightly, curled.

Bee, drawing, smiled at her page.

***

Thea came up the stair about ten minutes later, more slowly, with the fish pie wiped off her trousers, mostly. She stopped on the first landing and looked up at the three of them on the second. Bee drawing, Xola on the step, and a tail.

"Is she all right?" said Thea.

"No," said Hyvred, from the wings.

"She's fine," said Xola.

Thea came up the last forty steps and sat down on the step beside Xola. She didn't say anything to Hyvred. She didn't say anything to anyone for a while. And then she said, very gently, to the wings:

"It's a good thing. To like someone. Even if it's the worst thing. It's still a good thing."

The wings didn't say anything.

"I'm sorry about the pie," said Thea. "It was a very good pie. Xola made it."

"*Mrp*," said the wings, but quieter.

Thea stood up after a minute. She held her hand down to Xola, and pulled her up.

"Come on," said Thea. "Leave her with Bee."

***

They went back out into the afternoon. Not to Spoke Street; neither of them felt like facing Spoke Street. Instead they went up onto the wall of the round town, by the west gate, where you could sit on the warm stone and look down the west road toward the Fell Country.

They sat there for a long time without saying anything.

"I've never said it out loud," said Xola eventually. "Before today. The bit about him. Liking me first."

"No," said Thea. "Nor me. About after."

"It was weird."

"It was."

Xola picked at the moss between two stones.

"I don't feel anything about it," she said. "Not like that. I've got Slep. I wouldn't change Slep for anything. I'd carry Slep over a footbridge." She smiled a bit. "It's just. Saying it. Out loud. To someone who—" She stopped.

"Who cares," said Thea.

"Yeah."

"It made it real again," said Thea. "For a minute. That it happened. That he did that. Stepped back, and stayed, for years. And we didn't even see it." She was looking at the west road. "I think about that sometimes. How much he must have stood there. At the far end."

"He's still doing it," said Xola. "He went to the Fell Country so he wouldn't be in the middle of you and her."

"I know."

"He said *it's not mine*."

"I know. Gigi told Higu. Higu told me." Thea pulled her knees up. "He's always been like that. He'll stand at the edge of anything, so it isn't about him."

They sat.

"He's going to be so oblivious," said Xola. "About Hyv. Thea. He's going to be *so* oblivious."

"He's always oblivious about himself," said Thea.

"Should we—"

"No," said Thea. Gently, but at once. "It's not ours. It's hers." She paused. "And his. When it's time. If it's ever time."

Xola thought about that. She thought about Ice, standing in the Hall at the bottom of a stair of light, saying *it's not mine, it's hers, and yours*, and going underground for a day.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay. Not ours."

"Not ours."

"Did you know he did archery?" said Xola, after a while. "In real life? Before?"

Thea lifted her head.

"No," she said.

"That's the lesson. The one she owes him. It's in a clip from the city with no name, I watched it on the road. He borrowed her bow and hit ninety-five on the first go. And said he used to do it. In real life. And lost his passion for it." Xola pulled at the moss. "I didn't know that. I was his best friend for years and I didn't know that."

Thea was quiet for a long time.

"Nor did I," she said.

"What else don't we know?"

"I don't know," said Thea. "That's sort of the point, isn't it."

"I want somebody to look at him like that, though," said Xola, after a long time. Very quietly. She was looking at the west road too. "Like Hyv does. Did you see her? When she talked about him? She looks at him like she looks at roads. All of it. Every edge. Like she's trying to see the whole of him at once." Her gills fluttered. "Nobody's ever looked at him like that. Not even us. We just—he was just *there*, for us. In the chat. At the edge. We never looked at the whole of him. We never even thought to."

Thea didn't say anything. But after a moment she leaned sideways, and put her head on Xola's shoulder, and left it there.

***

She told herself it was just to ask how the levelling was going. She knew that wasn't why.

She lay there looking at it. *Yes mum.* *You started it.* She thought about a tail going straight up like a flagpole on Spoke Street, and a girl hiding in a pair of wings.

He had absolutely no idea.

She laughed into her pillow until Slep woke up.

"What," said Slep, sleepily.

"Hyv likes Icey," said Xola.

Slep lay there in the dark for a moment.

"I know," he said.

Xola sat bolt upright. Patience made a noise of deep complaint.

"*How?*"

"Her tail," said Slep. "At the Hall. When he came up from the hatch and got kicked. It went into a hook." He turned over. "I was watching the other side."

"*Since MONDAY?* You knew since *Monday* and you didn't *say*?"

"Somebody else was going to," said Slep, into his pillow. "Somebody louder."

And he went back to sleep. Xola sat in the dark with her mouth open, and the sheep glaring at her, and her gills going like a fan. Then she lay down again and laughed until she cried.
