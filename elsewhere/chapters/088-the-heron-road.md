Sheng Yang liked walking behind Declan, because Declan never once looked back to see if you were there.

He'd noticed it in poly, years ago. Nine years. Every time the class split into groups and went somewhere, Declan walked at the front and didn't look back. Not because he didn't care. Because it had never occurred to him that you wouldn't follow.

He walked the same way in the fen. With a shield on his back and his hands in his pockets, at the front, out past the ring of nine grey stones on Friday morning, into country none of them had ever seen. He didn't look back once.

Sheng Yang followed, with the long sword on his back, and didn't mind at all.

***

North of the ring the fen went on.

He hadn't expected it to. He'd thought the old fen ended at the sunken arch, the door to the drowned church, a long morning's walk past the ninth heron. Everybody did. There'd never been a reason to go further, so nobody had. The arch was where the stories stopped.

It didn't stop. It went on past the arch in reeds taller than Gabe and pools of black water with no bottom you could see, and mist that lay on the water in the mornings and didn't lift. And it went on further than that, into a flatness that went all the way to the sky. Not a sound in it but the wind in the reeds, and every so often, a long way off, a heron calling.

Sheng Yang looked at the number for a while. He'd played a lot of games. He knew what zero meant. It meant nobody had ever stood here before. Not one person, in four months, out of three million.

"Huh," he said.

"Yeah," said Declan, at the front, without turning round.

Suzu walked beside Declan, with his scythe and his pack, and the pack glowed through the canvas on one side, brighter than it had in the ring. Every few hundred yards he took the jar out and looked at it. Eighty-one small blue lights, pressed against the north side of the glass, so hard now that the glass seemed to bulge with them.

"Pulling," said Suzu, each time, and put it away.

***

They found the first heron at noon.

It was standing in the reeds on a little island of firmer ground, alone, half sunk in the peat up to its knees. A grey stone heron, the same as the nine in the ring: long neck, long beak, worn smooth by a long time and a lot of rain. It was facing north. Not bent. Straight. Looking out over the reeds the way the ninth heron in the ring looked, as if it could see something none of them could.

Declan stopped in front of it and didn't say anything for a long time.

"There's another one," said Kotori.

She'd climbed onto a dead stump to look, because she always climbed onto something to look. She was pointing north. A mile on, maybe more, on another island of firm ground in the reeds, small and grey, another heron. Facing north.

Sheng Yang looked past it. He had good eyes for distance, not Hyvred's, but good. A mile past that one, on the edge of seeing, a third.

"It's a road," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"Not a road," he said. "A line. Somebody put them there. One every mile. All facing the same way." He looked at the heron in front of them, at its long grey beak, pointing north. "So you'd know which way to go."

"So you'd know which way the lights go," said Suzu quietly.

***

They walked the heron road for two days.

Twelve hours on Friday, twelve on Saturday, the six of them, from heron to heron across the old fen. The reeds gave way to bog, and the bog to peat, and the peat to a long low rising moor where the herons stood on little cairns of stone. Each one had been put there by somebody, a long time ago, so it would stand above the heather and you could see the next one from it. They counted them. Rui Xiang would have been proud. Twenty-three herons. Every one facing north.

And every one they passed, the jar pulled harder.

Sheng Yang watched Suzu. He'd known Suzu two years. They'd met in a winter in a game with wind in its name, in a guild Declan had dragged them both into and then mostly forgotten about. He knew Suzu's stillnesses. He knew the ordinary one, half-shut eyes and his back against something. He knew the other one, the one that meant something had gone into him very deep and he wasn't going to say.

He saw it now, every time Suzu took the jar out and looked at it.

"Suzu," said Sheng Yang, on Saturday night, when they'd stopped by the twenty-third heron to sit out the short dark.

Suzu looked at him.

"Your mother," said Sheng Yang. He said it carefully, every word clear. He'd heard about the clinic letter from Declan, in the car, because Declan told the people who needed telling and nobody else. "The fifth of April."

"The fifth of April," said Suzu.

"And these." Sheng Yang nodded at the jar.

Suzu was quiet for a long time.

"Sedge's mother said they go north to finish," he said. "Where the herons look." He turned the jar in his hands. The lights moved with it, staying north. "I've been her eyes since I was small. My mum's. Reading things. Telling her what's there." He looked at the lights. "And these have been in a jar since December because I couldn't let them go. Eighty-one. I don't know what they are. I just know they wanted to go somewhere and I kept them."

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. Sheng Yang understood. A man walking north to let some things go, while his mother was waiting to see.

"Good," said Sheng Yang. It was Suzu's word. He'd never said it before.

The corner of Suzu's mouth went up, about a millimetre.

***

The heron woke on Sunday morning.

The twenty-fourth. It stood on its cairn at the top of a long rise, bigger than the others, nearly as tall as Gabe. It was facing north like all of them. As they came up the rise toward it, in a line, Declan in front, it turned its head.

Stone grinding on stone. A sound like a mill.

It looked at them. Then it stepped down off the cairn.

"*Positions*," said Declan.

They didn't need telling. They'd done it a hundred times. Declan in front. Gabe two paces from Kotori, and Kotori on the cairn the heron had just left, the highest thing on the rise. Deston behind, with the book. Suzu wide, on the left, in the heather. And Sheng Yang behind whatever was looking at Declan. It was always Declan. Everything always looked at Declan.

The heron struck.

It didn't swing. It stabbed, with its long stone beak, straight down, like a heron spearing a fish, at Declan's head. It was so fast that Sheng Yang barely saw it. Declan's shield was already there, sloped. The beak hit the boss and slid down it and into the heather beside his boot, and the peat came up in a spray.

It stabbed again. And again. Each time faster. Each time the shield was there, and the beak slid, and went down into the ground. Each time Declan's feet stayed exactly where they were. Sheng Yang had watched him do this for two months. He still didn't understand how. Nobody stood like that. Nobody.

"It's going for Kotori," said Deston, suddenly. "Gabe—"

The heron's head had turned. Not at Declan. Past him, at the small blue figure on the cairn with the staff. It had worked out what every big thing worked out eventually: that if you couldn't get through the one in front, you went for the one keeping him up.

It stabbed at Kotori.

Gabe took it. Of course he did. He'd been two paces away for two months. The stone beak hit his shield so hard that he went down on one knee on the cairn. He laughed, the way he always laughed, and the hammer came up white.

"*Last one*," said Gabe, to nobody, getting up. "Last one before I go. Make it a good one."

"*Mend*," said Kotori, and the light went into him.

"*Now*, Leveti," said Declan.

Sheng Yang was already there.

He'd been behind the heron since the first stab, waiting. That was what he did. He waited for the moment when everything was looking at Declan and nothing was looking at him. Then he ended things. *Breaker. One who ends things quickly.* The game had looked at him on the first day and known exactly what he was.

The heron's long neck was stretched forward, toward the cairn, toward Gabe on one knee. Sheng Yang stepped in under it, close, and brought the long two-handed sword up and round. All of his weight, and all of the heron's, the way you'd fell a tree. Into the place where the stone neck met the stone body.

It came apart.

Not like a monster. Like a statue. It cracked along the neck and the head came off and fell into the heather, and the body stood there for a moment, headless. Then it went down on its knees, slowly, with a sound like a wall settling. And it went still. Facing north.

In the heather where the head had fallen, something was lying. Small, round, grey-green, the size of a hen's egg. The heron's eye.

Suzu picked it up. He looked at it for a long time, in the palm of his hand.

In his pack, the jar blazed. Not a crescent any more. The whole jar, lit, every one of the eighty-one lights pressing so hard against the north side of the glass that it rang, very faintly, like a bell.

***

They saw the edge at noon.

The heron road went over one more rise, and then stopped. Not because the herons stopped. Because the ground did.

A gorge. Long and deep, running east and west across the moor as far as they could see, with a river at the bottom of it, white. On the near side, on the lip, the twenty-fifth heron, facing north across the gap. On the far side, a long way off, the twenty-sixth, facing north on its cairn. And between them, the stumps of a bridge. Two stone piers, one on each lip of the gorge, with nothing between them but air.

Beyond the far side, the land went up into mist. Hills, grey, and then something higher behind the hills that might have been mountains, or cloud.

Sheng Yang looked at the number for a long time.

Thirty. He was twenty-seven, as of an hour ago, with a stone head lying in the heather behind him. Declan was twenty-six. Suzu was twenty-five. Kotori and Deston were twenty-three. Gabe was twenty-one. None of them were thirty. Not close.

"For now," said Sheng Yang.

Declan laughed. He was standing on the lip of the gorge beside the twenty-fifth heron, with his hand on its stone neck, looking north across the gap at the mist.

"For now," he agreed.

***

Gabe left at the second dusk.

They walked back down the heron road to the twenty-fourth cairn, where the headless heron knelt facing north, and stopped there. Rachael's egg came tomorrow, real Monday. Gabe had to be somewhere else by then. Somewhere south, wherever she landed, at level one, in a world she'd never played.

He hugged all of them. He hugged Kotori, who didn't say sorry. He hugged Deston for a long time. He picked Suzu up off his feet, which nobody had ever done, and Suzu let him. He hugged Sheng Yang, briefly, heavily, and said "Two paces. Look after her," and Sheng Yang said "I will."

Then he stood in front of Declan.

"Don't do the door without me," said Gabe.

"We won't," said Declan.

"You will," said Gabe. "You'll be thirty in a few weeks and you'll find a door and you'll do it with five. Because dungeons are five." He grinned. "That's fine. I'm the sub. Do it with five. Just tell me after. Tell me everything."

"Everything," said Declan.

"*Everybody stays*," said Gabe. "Doesn't mean everybody stays in the same place." He put the hammer on his shoulder. "Go on, then. Thirty. I'll bring you three more."

He walked south, down the heron road, in the dusk, alone, with a hammer and a shield, the way he'd walked into Fernwick in January. He didn't look back. Like Declan. Like all of them.

Sheng Yang watched him go until he was a shape against the heather, and then not even that.

Then he looked at the five of them left on the rise, by a headless stone heron facing north. Declan, with his hand on its neck. Suzu, with the Heron's Eye in his palm and the jar blazing in his pack. Deston with the book. Kotori on the cairn, watching Gabe's road.

Five. Dungeons were five.

He thought about the Tally, on the needle in Brackenford. His name had been fifth on it for a month, and Declan's sixth, or the other way round. *For now.* He'd never cared much about it. He'd played on his own for two months before the trials and told nobody, because he'd wanted to see how far he could get. He'd thought that was the point. How far he could get.

He looked north, at the mist beyond the gorge, where the herons were looking.

He thought, for the first time, that maybe the point was who you got there with.
