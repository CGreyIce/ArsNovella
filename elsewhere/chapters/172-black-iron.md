Salty had wanted a bigger sword since the day he got the first one, and he'd never said so to anybody, because it sounded stupid.

It didn't sound stupid in his head. In his head it sounded completely reasonable. He was a big man. He was a big man in real life, and in the game he'd come out of the egg in Grimmet on the first morning looking exactly like himself, arms and all. He'd stood on a black cliff over a grey sea and thought *right*. The first thing anyone had put in his hands was a greatsword. It was a good one. Grimmet's smith had made it for the miners' sons, back when miners' sons went down the shafts with swords. He'd carried it for ten months. He'd killed everything on the Grey Coast with it. He'd stood in a shaft mouth with it all of the first Long Night, at fifty per cent pain, and never made a sound.

It was a good sword. It just wasn't big enough.

He couldn't have explained it. It was like wearing shoes a size too small. Everything was fine, you could walk, nobody else could tell. But you knew.

So on the Friday after Ice and Xav went downstairs, he went down the north-east tunnel under the round town with a pick. He came back up at the third dawn with his pack so full of black iron that Ama Corrie made him leave it on the step.

***

"What is it?" said Wendy.

She was sitting on the step beside the pack with her chin on her knees, the way she sat when she'd been keeping a gate all day and didn't want to talk to anybody else but him. In the game they'd been eleven days' walk apart, once. Now they were a step apart, and he still noticed it every time.

"Iron," said Salty.

"It's black."

"Black iron." He picked up a lump and turned it over in the lamplight. It was heavy, much heavier than it looked, and so dark it seemed to eat the light. It didn't shine. Ordinary iron shone a bit when you broke it, a grey glint. This didn't. It was black all the way through. "There's a seam of it. In the fourth tunnel, past where the Delvers sleep. I found it in July and I didn't say anything."

"Why not?"

"Didn't know what it was for." He put it down. "Now I do."

Wendy looked at the lump, and then at the pack, and then at him. She'd known him a long time. She could read him the way Kei read a map.

"You're going to make a sword," she said.

"Yes."

"A big one."

"Yes."

"Bigger than that one." She nodded at the greatsword on his back.

"Yes," said Salty.

Wendy didn't laugh. That was why he'd told her first, before anybody. She didn't laugh at things that mattered to people, even when they sounded stupid. She just put her head on one side and looked at him for a long time.

"Okay," she said. "Good." She stood up and dusted off her knees. "Don't drop it on your foot."

***

The smithy was behind the Hart, on the east road, and it belonged to an old man called Joss who didn't use it much any more.

He'd been the round town's smith for fifty years. He'd shod every horse that came through all seven roads and mended every gate. Now his hands shook, and he sat in a chair at the back of his own forge in a leather apron and watched other people use it. He didn't seem to mind. He said the fire was happier with somebody at it.

There were six of them in Constellation who could use it, and they took it in turns, mostly. Drew, who'd learned in Whitecairn under a man with no fingers on one hand. Ina, from the great forge at Emberly that had never gone out. Gabe, whose Anvil made the things he forged *hold*, which nobody could explain, including Gabe. Slep, who made Xola's hooks and his own glaive and didn't talk while he worked. Jun Liang from Riverbank, who sang at the bellows. And Salty.

He was the worst of them. He knew that. He'd mined more than any of them, his whole life in the game had been shafts and seams, but at the anvil he was the slowest. His grades were all right. They were never good. He hit things very hard, and the game didn't always think hard was the same as right.

He told them all on the Saturday morning. He didn't make a thing of it. He stood in the doorway of the smithy with the black iron on the floor beside him in a sack and said, "I'm going to make a sword. A big one. Out of this."

Drew looked at the sack. Ina looked at the sack. Gabe, who'd been halfway through a horseshoe for no particular reason, put it down and came over and looked in.

"What *is* that?" said Gabe.

"Black iron. From the fourth tunnel."

Gabe picked up a lump and immediately put it down again, because it was much heavier than he'd expected.

"Oh, that's *heavy*," said Gabe. "That's *really* heavy. Salty. What is it?"

"I just said."

"No, I mean—" Gabe crouched and looked at it closer. "It doesn't shine. Why doesn't it shine?"

Nobody knew.

Old Joss had come forward in his chair. He was looking at the sack with an expression Salty hadn't seen on him before. He didn't say anything for a long time. Then he held out one shaking hand, and Salty put a lump into it. Joss turned it over and over. He held it up to the forge-light, and the forge-light went into it and didn't come out.

"Where'd you get that, lad?" said Joss.

"The fourth tunnel. Under the north-east road. Past the Delvers."

Joss was quiet.

"My father's father talked about that," he said at last. "Deep iron. Black. Came up from under the round town in his day, a bit at a time, when they cut the cellars. He said it was the dark's own iron, from where the dark lives." He gave the lump back. His hand was shaking more than before. "He said nobody could work it. He said it cracked in the quench, every time. It doesn't like the light."

"How do you know it doesn't like the light?"

"Because it doesn't shine," said Joss, as if that was obvious.

***

He started on the Sunday.

He did it properly, the way Drew had taught him in March, the way the game wanted it done. Smelt, bloom, fold. You made the forms with your hands, the same as you made a sword-cut. You struck at the right place and the right weight and the right time, and the game graded you, and the grade was what the metal became. A seventy per cent sword was a seventy per cent sword. Nobody could fake it.

The black iron smelted badly. It wanted more heat than anything he'd ever put in the forge. Ina had to come and Kindle it twice, standing at the side with his short black wand, saying the one word under his breath. The fire went white. The bloom that came out of it was dull and dark and enormous, and it didn't glow the way bloom should. It glowed a deep sullen red, like something angry under a lid.

He folded it. He hit it very hard. He hit it the way he hit everything.

He hit it again.

[ Blacksmithing · Fold · Grade 48% ]

"You're hitting it like it's a Scree-Runner," said Drew, on the Monday, from the doorway.

Salty stopped.

"It is a Scree-Runner," he said. "It's being very annoying."

"It's iron," said Drew. "It doesn't want to be hit hard. It wants to be hit *right*." He came in and stood beside the anvil and looked at the bloom on it. Drew always watched feet. He'd said once that he'd learned to in lobbies, hosting tournaments, before the game, watching who was going to stay and who was going to leave. He was watching Salty's feet now. "You're all in your shoulders. Get in your hips. Like with the sword."

Salty looked at him.

"With the sword," said Drew, patiently, "you don't hit with your arms. You hit with all of you. You turn. You know you do; I've seen you on the moor. You've never once swung that greatsword with just your arms. So why are you hitting the anvil with them?"

Salty didn't have an answer.

He set his feet. He thought about the moor, about the greatsword going round, the way the weight came up from the ground through his legs and his back. Then he hit the iron.

[ Blacksmithing · Fold · Grade 64% ]

"There," said Drew. "Now do it four hundred times."

***

He did it four hundred times.

It took all week. He went down the tunnel in the mornings for more iron and spent the afternoons and the nights at the anvil. Wendy came at the second dusk and sat on Joss's woodpile and didn't talk, and he didn't talk either. That was how it was with them. In Singapore they'd sat in two eggs eighteen inches apart for months without saying anything. It was the best part of the day.

Ice came on the Wednesday.

Salty didn't hear him come in. He looked up from the anvil and Ice was in the doorway, with mud to the knee from the north tunnel and his shield on his back. He was looking at the black bar on the anvil the way he looked at a door nobody had opened.

"Hi," said Ice.

"Hi."

"Kei said you were making something."

"A sword," said Salty. He didn't stop hammering. "A big one."

Ice came in and stood by the forge and watched. He didn't say anything. He watched Salty's hands and the iron and the window that came up after each blow. He watched the way the black iron took the hammer and didn't glow. Salty kept working. It was easier with Ice watching, somehow, not harder. It was like playing with him in the old days. Ice behind him on a site, saying nothing, and you just knew that if anything came round the corner it would already be dead.

"It doesn't shine," said Ice, eventually.

"Joss says it doesn't like the light."

"Hm." Ice looked at it for a long time. "Where's it from?"

"The fourth tunnel. Past the Delvers. Deep."

"Where the cold is," said Ice. "Where the old stone meets the new."

Salty stopped hammering.

"How do you know about the cold?"

"I sit there sometimes," said Ice. "At the end of the north tunnel. It's cold there. It's cold at the end of all of them, I think. You can feel the north." He was still looking at the iron. "That's where it comes from. The dark. From under there."

Salty didn't say anything for a moment.

"Joss said that," he said. "His grandfather said it was the dark's own iron."

"Then it probably is," said Ice. He said it the way he said most things, quite seriously, as if he was telling you the weather. "Things in this game mostly are what the old people say."

He watched for a while longer. Then he went to go. At the door he stopped.

"Dylan."

Salty looked up. Ice only called him that about once a year.

"You've always swung it like it was too small," said Ice. "The greatsword. On the moor, in the shafts. Like it ended before you did." He shrugged. "I didn't know if you knew."

He went out.

Salty stood at the anvil with the hammer in his hand for a long time. Wendy, on the woodpile, didn't say anything at all. But when he looked at her she was smiling at her knees.

***

By Thursday the bar was the length of a man.

It lay on the anvil in the evening forge-light, black, dull, enormous. Longer than he was tall, wider than his hand, so heavy that Gabe couldn't lift one end of it without going red. It wasn't a sword yet. It was a bar, folded four hundred and eighty times, with no edge and no shape. But it was the right size. Salty could see that. He stood beside it and looked at it and it was the right size. For the first time in ten months, something was.

[ Blacksmithing · Fold · Grade 71% (average) ]

He'd never made anything at seventy-one in his life.

"Now the test," said Ina.

He'd come to watch. They'd all come, all six of them, and Joss in his chair at the back. And Wendy on the woodpile, and a few others who'd heard. Kei, with his map. Tiger, who never missed anything that might go wrong. Ina stood by the forge with his oni mask hanging at his shoulder and his short black wand in his hand. He looked like a man who'd been around a lot of forges and knew what came next.

"The test?"

"You don't quench the whole thing first," said Ina. He had a way of saying things, Ina did. Smooth and certain, the way the British said things on the radio. It made you want to do whatever he said next. "Not with something that size. Not with iron nobody's ever worked. You cut a bit off the end. A finger's worth. You shape it, you harden it, you quench it. And you see what it does." He shrugged. "Brannagh made me do it every time. Four words a day, that man, and two of them were *test piece*."

Salty cut a test piece.

He shaped it on the horn of the anvil into a little blade, the length of his hand. Black, like the bar. He put it back in the fire and brought it up to the heat Ina told him. It glowed that sullen angry red again, like a coal under ash. He took it out with the tongs.

The quench trough was by the door. Old Joss's, a long stone trough of water that had been there for fifty years, with a scum of scale on the bottom.

He put the little blade in.

It hissed. Steam went up, white, into the lamplight. He held it there for the count Ina gave him, and then he lifted it out and turned it over in the light.

It was fine.

For one second, it was fine. Black and hard and the right shape, the size of his hand, and the window came up—

[ Blacksmithing · Quench · Grade 68% ]

—and then there was a small, clean sound, like a fingernail on glass.

The test piece cracked.

Right across. A thin bright line, the only bright thing on it. It went from one edge to the other in a heartbeat. Then a second one, and a third. And the little blade came apart in the tongs into four black pieces, and they fell into Joss's trough one after another with four small plops.

Nobody said anything.

[ Blacksmithing · Quench · Failed ]

Salty looked at the trough.

He thought about the bar on the anvil. The length of a man. Four hundred and eighty folds, a week of mornings in the fourth tunnel, a week of nights at this forge. Seventy-one per cent, the best thing he'd ever made. If he'd quenched the whole thing, if he hadn't cut the test piece, that would have been four pieces in Joss's trough. Every one of them too big to lift.

"It doesn't like the light," said Joss, from the back, very quietly. "My father's father said."

***

He tried again on the Friday. A thinner test piece. Slower. He did it in oil, because Drew said sometimes oil was gentler. It cracked.

He tried in brine on the Saturday, because Grimmet's smith had used the sea. It cracked.

He tried with Ina Kindling the oil warm first, the way they did it at Emberly for blades that mattered. It cracked. It took longer to crack, a whole two seconds, which made it worse.

He tried sand. He tried ash. He tried holding it over the trough without putting it in, and letting it cool in the air, which you weren't supposed to do because it never hardened. It didn't harden. It didn't crack either, but it bent in his hands like lead, which was no better.

By Sunday night there were nineteen black pieces in a row along the windowsill of Joss's smithy. Salty had laid them out himself, in order, the way Kei laid things out. Nineteen failures. He'd counted. He'd started counting on Friday, and then he couldn't stop.

He sat on the woodpile beside Wendy and looked at them.

"It's not the iron," he said.

She looked at him.

"It's not the iron," said Salty again. "The iron's fine. The folds are fine. It's seventy-one. It's the best thing I've ever made." He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. "It's the *quench*. Every time. Water, oil, brine, ash. Doesn't matter. The second it cools, it cracks. Like it's—" He stopped.

"Like it's what?"

"Like it's scared," said Salty. He heard it as he said it, how stupid it sounded. He said it anyway, because it was what it was like. "Like you take it out of the fire into the light and the lamp and everybody looking at it, and it can't stand it. And it just goes." He held his hands apart. "Bang."

Wendy didn't laugh.

She looked at the nineteen pieces on the windowsill for a long time. At the lamp hanging over the trough. At the forge-glow and the door open on the east road, and the lamps of the Hart next door, and the market lamps beyond.

"Joss said it doesn't like the light," she said.

"Joss says a lot of things."

"Ice said things in this game mostly are what the old people say." She was quiet for a moment. "You told me. On the step."

Salty looked at her.

"Where does it come from?" said Wendy.

"The fourth tunnel."

"Is it light there?"

"No," said Salty. "It's black. You can't see your hand. You go by the walls."

"Then it's never been in the light before," said Wendy. "Has it? Not ever. Not since the world was made. It's been down there in the dark for three hundred years, or three thousand, or however long. Then you dug it up and put it in a fire and took it out under a lamp in front of nine people." She put her chin on her knees. "I'd crack too."

He sat there.

He sat there on Joss's woodpile and looked at her for so long that she went a bit pink and said "*What*," in the voice she used when he looked at her too long. He didn't answer. He was thinking about the fourth tunnel, past the Delvers, where it was black and you went by the walls. Somewhere down there, he'd heard it every time he mined, there was water. A little underground stream that ran along the floor of the deep part, so cold it made your teeth hurt, in the dark where nothing had ever been lit.

"Wendy," said Salty.

"*What*."

"You're a genius."

"I know," said Wendy. "I keep telling you."

He got up off the woodpile. He went to the anvil and cut a twentieth test piece, the size of his hand. He shaped it and heated it until it glowed its sullen red, and put it in the tongs. And then, while Wendy watched and Joss watched from his chair, he picked up a lamp in his other hand and went out of the smithy door into the night.
