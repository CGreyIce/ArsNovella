Hyvred woke up on Saturday morning to a phone she couldn't use.

It wasn't broken. It was simply full. The screen lit up when she touched it and then stayed lit, buzzing and buzzing, notifications piling on top of each other faster than she could read them. Even holding it close to her face, in the narrow bright window in the middle of her sight, they went past in a blur. Messages. Mentions. Follows. Emails. Somebody had tagged her in something forty times.

She sat up in bed and turned it face down on the duvet and looked at the bit of ceiling she could see for a while.

Then she rang Bee.

"Have you seen?" said Bee, picking up at once, as though she'd been holding the phone waiting.

"I can't see anything. My phone's broken. It's just—it won't stop."

"It's not broken," said Bee. "You're famous."

***

Somebody had clipped her first minute.

Not the whole stream. Forty-three seconds. Her, standing in the little wooden room with her horns and her red ribbon, very still, crying. Her hand going slowly out to the side, and the fingers moving. Her voice, wet and laughing: *Sorry, sorry, give me a minute. I can see the ceiling.* And then, cut in at the end, her saying *Look. Look where my hand is. I can see it there.*

Under it, whoever had cut it had written: *This VTuber has tunnel vision. This is her first minute in a full-dive pod.*

That was all. It was enough.

It had gone round the world overnight. Bee read her the numbers over the phone, and Hyvred made her stop after the second one, because they didn't mean anything. They were the kind of numbers that happened to other people. A news site in Korea had it. A news site in Brazil had it. A doctor in Canada had made a thread explaining why it worked: the pod didn't use your eyes, it sent the seeing straight to the part of your brain that does the seeing, and so in theory it didn't matter what your eyes were like. Somebody had replied to the doctor with a long, careful, angry post about people being promised miracles. The doctor had replied, carefully and kindly, that it wasn't a miracle, it was a game, and that the woman in the clip had probably still had to take her glasses off before she lay down.

And at seven o'clock that morning, the official ELSEWHERE account had posted it. No comment, no words. Just the clip, and under it, the line from their ads.

*Where are you?*

"They *reposted* it?" said Hyvred.

"They reposted it," said Bee. "Fourteen minutes ago. You've got—" she stopped. "Doesn't matter what you've got. A lot."

Hyvred sat on her bed with the phone against her ear and the duvet round her knees.

"I didn't do anything," she said. "I just saw the ceiling."

"I know," said Bee. "That's why."

***

She didn't know what to do with it, for most of the morning.

She was proud of it. She was, she realised, slowly, enormously proud of the forty-three seconds. Not because she'd done anything, but because it had been *true*. It had happened. It had been real, and she hadn't hidden it, and if she'd known it was going to be seen by half the world she wasn't sure she'd have been brave enough to stand there and cry like that. She was glad she hadn't known.

And it also made her feel strange, in a way she couldn't put her finger on.

It took her until lunchtime. She was sitting at her desk with the phone held close, scrolling slowly through the messages, a few at a time, in the window in the middle. A lot of them were lovely. A lot of them were from people who'd cried. A few were horrible, the way a few always are. And some of them, more than she'd expected, were from people who couldn't see either. People with tunnel vision, like hers. People who saw only light and dark. People whose eyes were going slowly, and who knew it. One was from a mother whose daughter had been born without much sight at all.

*Is it real? Does it really work? Can she see the sky?*

That was what the strange feeling was. A great many people were suddenly looking at her eyes.

She'd been looked at all her life for her eyes. She'd turned her whole face to read chat, and people had asked why. She'd walked into a chair in front of somebody and watched their face do the thing. She'd made a clip to explain, so she wouldn't have to keep explaining, and a funny guy in Maigo's server had called it a Flashlight mod, and she'd laughed, because at least he'd been looking at the clip and not at her.

She didn't want to be the face of anything. She wanted to be Hyv, who was loud, and who jumped first.

She thought about it for a long time. Then she answered the mother. Only her, out of all of them. She held the phone very close and typed slowly with one thumb.

*It's real. Out here nothing changes for me. But in there I can see the sky, all of it, edge to edge. I don't know if it works the same for everyone. But it worked for me, and I didn't think it would. Save up.*

Then she put the phone face down and went and lay in the egg, and went to look at the sea.

***

She didn't talk about her eyes on stream that day, or the day after. She'd decided. If people asked, she'd tell them, honestly, because it was true and she wasn't ashamed of any of it. But she wasn't going to stand on a cliff and make a speech. She was going to stand on a cliff and shoot things.

So she did.

Her chat was enormous. That was the first thing. For three years she'd streamed to about twenty-five people, thirty on a good night, people she knew by name. On Saturday afternoon the number in the corner said two thousand eight hundred and sixty, and it went past at the edge of her sight much too fast to read, in more languages than she knew. A lot of the new people had come for the clip and stayed for about four minutes, and then left when it turned out to be a cow girl shooting crabs on a beach. That was fine. A lot of them didn't leave. A lot of them, it turned out, liked a cow girl shooting crabs on a beach.

And she was *good* at it.

She hadn't expected that either. Not really. She'd hoped. But in the two days since she'd stood on the harbour wall with Ness's hand flat between her shoulder blades, she'd found that the bow made sense to her in a way almost nothing ever had. Everybody else, Ness said, looked at the target. They narrowed down, and lined up, and shot at the one bright small thing in the middle, and the wind took their arrows and they never knew why.

Hyvred had spent twenty-five years narrowing down. She knew how to do that. She could do it with her eyes shut.

What she'd never been able to do was the other thing. And now she could. Now she could look at the target and at the same time see everything else: the whole wind, the whole sky, the gull leaning on the air at the top of the world, the flag on the boat at the right edge, the grass on the clifftop all pressed one way. And the bow, it turned out, was the one thing in the world that wanted both.

The clip that did it, the second clip, was on Sunday afternoon.

She'd gone up to the top of the cliffs above Saltwhistle, past the last of the white houses, to where the turf ran right to the edge and dropped three hundred feet into the sea. The sea-hawks lived there. They were big, grey and white, with wings as long as she was tall, and they hung in the wind off the cliff edge, barely moving, looking down at the water for fish. Ness had told her not to bother. *Nobody hits a sea-hawk from the cliff,* she'd said. *The wind comes up the face and does what it likes. I've seen men empty a quiver.*

Hyvred had gone anyway. Of course she had.

She stood at the edge of the cliff with her toes over the drop and the wind coming up the face into her face, and she looked.

There was one about a hundred and twenty paces out, hanging, very still. Out past the edge of the cliff, over the sea, in nothing but air. The wind came up the cliff face in a great invisible wall and curled over the top and went back out to sea, and under that, lower down, there was a different wind going the other way, along the coast. She could see both. She could see the grass at her feet pressed flat one way, and the foam on the water three hundred feet below moving another. She could see a gull at the left edge of the world being pushed up, and a gull at the right edge being pushed sideways. She could see the whole shape of the air.

She drew.

Her left eye, the gold one with the little red cross in it, settled on the hawk. She felt it settle. It was the strangest thing: the same narrowing she'd done her whole life, the scope coming down onto one small bright thing. Except that this time, all the way round it, out to every edge, she could still see everything else.

She moved the bow. Up, and a long way left, a little more than felt right.

She stopped holding on.

The arrow went out over the sea. Up first, on the cliff wind, higher than the hawk. Then it hung at the top of its curve for a moment, and the lower wind took it, and brought it round, and down, in a long, slow, impossible bend.

The hawk didn't see it coming. Nothing ever does, from there.

That one went round the world too. Not as far as the first. But it went to different people. It went to people who played games, who'd watched archers in a dozen other games for years, and who knew exactly how impossible that shot was. People who'd never cared about the first clip at all.

Somebody put the two clips side by side, the first minute and the hawk, and wrote under them: *she can see now. and apparently she can see better than all of us.*

***

Bee's clip went round on Sunday too, though Bee pretended not to care.

It wasn't the pig. Well, it was partly the pig. *A girl hitting a pig with a book* had done very well on Saturday, mostly among people who found pigs being hit with books funny, which was a lot of people. But the one that went round on Sunday was the shield.

Somebody had cut together her first two shields, side by side. The first one, the good picture, hanging in the air for three seconds with the window underneath: *34%. You drew the grain before the shape.* And the second, drawn shape first in one long sure line, brighter and longer: *58%. The line was sure.* And under it somebody had written: *the game is a better art teacher than mine was.*

Every artist on the internet found it by lunchtime. Bee's chat had been thirty-odd people for as long as she'd been streaming, a few more than Hyvred's on a good night, which she never let Hyvred forget. On Sunday night it had nine hundred people in it, and every one of them wanted to know what the book thought of hands.

"They're all doing it," said Bee on the call, Sunday night, sounding scandalised. "Every one of them. I've had forty messages from people who draw. They all want pods so they can find out what the game thinks of their *line*."

"That's amazing, Bee."

"It's *embarrassing*. They keep asking me what the book thinks of hands. I don't *know* what the book thinks of hands. I haven't drawn a hand yet." A pause. "I'm going to draw a hand tomorrow."

"Of course you are."

"I'm going to draw a *good* hand," said Bee, with great dignity.

She'd been up at Coombe's skeps all Saturday, it turned out. She'd been stung four times. She'd laughed about it, which she never did. She'd come back with a jar of honey and Farming at level one. Coombe had shown her how to brew a little honey and water and yeast into something he called *mead* and she called *disgusting*, and that was Brewing at level one too. She'd also found out, entirely by herself, that she could draw ahead.

"You draw it on the page before," she explained. "Shields, walls, stings, whatever. Then you just keep the page. It waits. You only have to say it." A pause. "For about an hour. Then the lines go soft and it won't do it any more. I tested it. I drew six walls and timed them."

"You *timed* them?"

"I'm not *stupid*, Hyv. I'm just new."

***

On Monday, Tiger and Drew went in.

Hyvred watched them on her phone, on the MRT, held close to her face. Tiger's stream and Drew's stream, side by side, both in Follow, in two completely different towns on opposite sides of the world map. Tiger came out of his door into a city of canals and stone bridges and immediately fell into one. Drew came out of his into a mountain village under snow, looked at it for a long moment, and said, very calmly, "Huh. Cold." Then he went to find somebody to teach him how to punch.

There were so many people now. That was the thing she kept noticing, every time she opened the game, every time she opened her phone. On Monday morning there'd been a headline on the news site she read on the train: *ELSEWHERE passes three million players in its first fortnight; pods sold out until February*. There was an ad for the game on the side of a bus. There were two people on her carriage on the MRT talking about it, in a language she didn't understand, except the word *ELSEWHERE*, which came up every few sentences. There was a café near her flat with a sign in the window: *ELSEWHERE STREAMS SHOWN HERE*.

Two weeks ago it had been a game for people who'd waited a year and a half and had two and a half thousand dollars. Now it was a thing people talked about on trains.

She watched Ice's stream on Tuesday night, for the first time since the clip.

She'd been meaning to. She'd watched bits of it before, Hyv-style, at random, and he'd messaged her on Friday night, *no flashlight*, which she'd read twenty times. She hadn't gone back properly since. On Tuesday night she lay on her bed with her phone held close and opened it.

He had a hundred and forty-three people watching.

She looked at that number for a long time. Five days ago it would have been five times what she had.

He was standing in the shallows at the edge of his swamp, in a gap in the reeds, with his shield sloped and his sword back by his ear, explaining to a hundred and forty-three people how to stand. He said *back foot*. Chat said *BACK FOOT* back to him like a choir. Somebody with a mod badge called *titanium_ii_fluoride* told him he'd said back foot eleven times that morning, and he said "And I'll say it twelve," and did.

A hundred and forty-three people.

She thought about her own chat, too fast to read. About the official account reposting her clip, and the news sites in Korea and Brazil, and the side of the bus. And about how, in the comments under the hawk, the people who knew archery best had all said the same thing in a dozen different ways: *she's doing the ice thing. she's not looking at the target. she's letting the whole of it in.* Half of them had linked to a forty-second clip of a man in a swamp with a circle drawn round the tilt of his POV camera, and *HERE* in red.

He'd taught half the internet how to stand. A hundred and forty-three people were watching him do it.

She didn't type anything. She just watched him for a long time, in the small bright window in the middle of her sight. Then she went to his channel page and turned on notifications, which she'd never done for anyone. When she'd done that, she lay back and laughed at the ceiling, at the small part of the ceiling she could see.

***

She was level five on Tuesday night. Bee was level four, and drew hands now, and the book said her hands were *Adequate*, which had made her so angry she'd drawn forty more. They were sitting in two different towns on opposite sides of a map the size of a world, on a call, each of them on a stone at the edge of the night.

"Where are you, on the map?" said Hyvred.

"How do I tell?"

"Open it. Find your town. Tell me what's near it."

There was a pause in Heatherbrook, and some muttering.

"There's a lot of purple," said Bee. "And a road going off the edge. It says *to the Weald*."

Hyvred found Heatherbrook on her own map. It took her a while, holding the map close, even in here. She found it, a little dot on a high moor, and her own little dot on the white coast, and she traced the roads between them with her finger.

"Eleven days," she said. "If we walk. After ten."

"Eleven days to where?"

"Somewhere in the middle. There's an intermediate town between us. On a river. Brackenford." She looked at the dot. "If we both get to ten, we walk, we meet there. Halfway."

"Brackenford," said Bee. She said it the way she said everything, as if it were already decided. "Fine. I'll draw you a door when I get there."

"A door to what?"

"I don't know yet," said Bee. "I'll draw it first and find out."

Hyvred laughed, sitting on the clifftop at Saltwhistle in the dark, with the whole sea in front of her from edge to edge, and the stars over it, all of them, all at once.

Far off along the coast, a light came on in a lighthouse she'd never noticed, at the very edge of the world on her left. She saw it without turning her head.
