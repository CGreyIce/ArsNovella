The walk back across the ring was the longest thirty paces Ice had walked in years.

It wasn't far. It was an old man's Hall, oak floor, a lantern on a hook. Kestrel was behind him with her ten round her, Ottie's heal ringing on her for the third time. The iron ring in the floor was open under his banner, a stair of light going down. Six hundred people were making a noise that went up into the slate roof and came back down. And his own ten were at the far edge of the ring, waiting for him, and every one of them was looking at him in a way they never had.

He walked toward them at a walk. He always walked.

His hands had stopped being still. That was the first thing he noticed. Halfway across the ring, they started to shake. Not much. A fine tremble in the right one, the sword hand, that he could feel all the way up to the elbow. He knew what it was. He hadn't felt it in a long time, but he knew it. It was what came after. In another game, years ago, in the dark, it had come after every fight that mattered. You'd been still for so long that your body had to give the stillness back somewhere.

He put the sword away so nobody would see.

Somebody saw. Of course they did.

"Your hand," said Hyvred.

She was at the front of his ten, at the very edge of the ring, with her bow in her fist and her glasses pushed up into her hair. She'd been the eyes for the ten on the march. She was looking at his right hand, where he'd just put the sword away. Then up at his face.

"It's fine," said Ice.

"It's *shaking*."

"It does that. After." He stopped in front of her. "It's fine, Hyv."

She looked at him for a long moment, with her eyes that could see everything now, edge to edge, all at once. And then she said, very quietly, so that only he could hear it:

"You let her hit you."

Ice was quiet.

"The one on the cheek," said Hyvred. "You saw it. You turned your head less than you could have. I *saw* you." Her voice was shaking too, he realised. "Why?"

Nobody else had seen it. He'd have bet on that. Six hundred people in the Hall and two hundred thousand on the stream, and he'd have bet nobody had seen him turn his head an inch less than he could have. He hadn't counted on her.

"To see what she'd do," said Ice. "If she thought she'd got one in."

Hyvred stared at him.

"And what did she do?"

"Nothing," said Ice. "She stood there. She didn't press and she didn't panic. She just stood there and understood." He looked across the ring at Kestrel, who was sitting on a bench by the wall now with her head down and Brann's hand on her shoulder. "That's very rare. That's why I let her go."

Hyvred didn't say anything. She looked at him as if she was seeing a road she'd walked a hundred times and never noticed went somewhere else. Then her face went red, all at once, from the collar up. She turned round and walked away into the crowd in a straight line, and didn't hit him.

He didn't know what that meant either.

***

Then everybody else was there.

Gabe first, because Gabe was always first. He came through the edge of the ring like a wall falling over, with Ice's helm still in one hand. He grabbed Ice by both shoulders and shook him.

"*DEC*," said Gabe. "*DEC. YOU DIDN'T TELL ME.*"

"You were there," said Ice. "In ESO. You saw."

"I saw *once*." Gabe shook him again. "I played Elder Scrolls with you for about a *month*, Dec. And you spent the whole month picking flowers. Flowers and crafting. Every night. And then one night we were in Cyrodiil and there were twenty of them on a bridge. And you went in on your own and stunned them. In a *chain*. One after the other, like you were *reading them off a list*. And they all just fell over." He let go of one shoulder to point at the ring. "And I thought it was a *fluke*. I thought the game had *bugged*. Deston said it wasn't. I told Deston he was being dramatic."

"He wasn't," said Deston, beside him.

"*HE WASN'T*," said Gabe. "And SimpleMMO. I played SimpleMMO, on and off, for *years*. After you'd stopped fighting in it. You'd gone off and become a *market* man by then. You just bought things and sold things and never hit anybody. And every guild I was ever in, the high rankers, the old ones, they used to tell this *story*. About somebody who made a guild on his own and declared war on everybody. Every guild in the game. On his own. And killed so many people that the people who liked fighting came and joined him." Gabe's voice had gone up and up. "They used to go *quiet* when they told it, Dec. Like it was a ghost story. Like he might be *listening*. And I thought it was a *ghost story*. I thought they were *making it up*. I never once put it together."

"It wasn't a ghost story," said Ice.

"*IT WASN'T A GHOST STORY*," said Gabe, to the whole Hall. And then, because he couldn't help it: "*ELEVEN MINUTES*."

"Eleven minutes and forty seconds," said Deston, beside him. He had his notebook out, the one he'd started carrying because Dennis had one. He hadn't written anything in it. He was holding it like he'd forgotten what it was for. "I counted. Eleven minutes and forty seconds. She hit you once." He looked at Ice. "You've never been hit once in eleven minutes in your life. Not in this game. Not in any game I've watched you play."

"Not this kind," said Ice.

Deston looked at him for a long time. Then he did a thing Ice didn't expect. He put his hand on the top of Ice's head, briefly, the way you'd check a child for a fever. Then he took it away.

"Okay," said Deston.

Sheng Yang was standing a little behind him, with his blade sheathed, looking at the ring. He hadn't said anything. He didn't, mostly. Then:

"Where Winds Meet," said Sheng Yang.

Gabe turned round. "*What?*"

"Before this. Before the game. He played it for three hundred hours." Sheng Yang said it the way he said everything, flatly, as if he were reading it off a board. "Three hundred hours is nothing. That's a holiday. People put three thousand into that game." He looked at Ice. "Top twenty on the PvP ranking. On a tank. The highest-ranked tank on the whole board."

Gabe's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it, for once.

"The next tank down was somewhere in the top thousand," said Sheng Yang. "I looked. I couldn't find the next one for ages. I thought the board was broken."

"How do you *know* that?" said Gabe.

"I saw the board," said Sheng Yang. "Nobody else noticed. He didn't say anything about it either." He looked at Ice for a moment longer. "I liked monsters better than people. I always thought you did too."

"I do," said Ice. "Mostly."

"Mm," said Sheng Yang. Which, from Sheng Yang, was agreeing with all of it.

Kotori was behind them. She'd come through with her white staff held in both hands against her chest, the way she held it when she was frightened. Her face was wet. She'd been crying, quietly, the whole fight, he thought.

"I don't like it," said Kotori. "When it's people."

"No," said Ice.

"I know we said. I know you said, in the fen, *we don't start it*. And you didn't. It was the old man's custom." She held the staff tighter. "I still don't like it. I don't like watching people do that to people. Even if it's you. Even if it's the right thing. Even if you stopped." Her voice wobbled. "*Especially* if you stopped. Because that means you could have not stopped."

Ice looked at her.

"Did you like it?" said Kotori. Very small. "When you were doing it?"

He thought about it. He made himself think about it properly, because she'd asked properly.

"I used to," he said.

It came out of him the way it had come out in a city with no name, about a bow. *I used to do archery. In real life. But I lost my passion for it.* He heard it, and so did Kotori, and so did everybody standing near enough.

"I used to," he said again. "A lot. A long time ago." He looked at his right hand, which had stopped shaking. "Then I didn't. I stopped. I went and grew things instead. Traded. Made potions." He almost smiled. "It turns out you can be very good at a thing and stop wanting to do it."

"And tonight?" said Kotori.

Ice was quiet for a long moment.

"A bit," he said. "Tonight I liked it a bit. Because she was good."

Kotori looked at him. Then she nodded, slowly, as if that were an answer she could put somewhere and keep.

"Okay," she said. "Sorr—" She stopped. "*Thank you*. For telling me."

***

Suzu was the last of Moonlight Harvest to come over. He didn't say anything for a while. He stood a little way off, with the scythe on his back, and looked at Ice with the same face he always had. The face that was most expression of anyone, because it had none.

"Okay," said Suzu.

That was all. From Suzu, it was the biggest word there was.

***

Then the guild.

He hadn't expected that. He'd expected Moonlight Harvest. They'd known there was something. Gabe had seen it once, years ago, on a bridge in another game, and decided it was a fluke. But he hadn't thought about the rest. Fifty-odd people had walked three days to Elmcross to watch a race, and found themselves watching something else. They came through the edge of the ring in ones and twos, and every one of them had the same face. He'd seen it on the faces of the people on the far side of a fight, years ago, in another game. He'd never once seen it on the faces of people on his own side.

Tiger got to him first. Tiger, who skimmed faster than anybody in the game.

"Mate," said Tiger. And then, louder, "*Mate.*" He pointed at the middle of the ring. "You weren't even *fast*. I've been fast my whole life. You walked. You *walked* at her and she couldn't—" He stopped. "I've never seen anybody move like that. And you weren't even *moving*."

Drew came and stood in front of him, and looked at his feet. He looked for a long time.

"I watched your feet for ten days," said Drew. "In the Fell Country. Every day."

"I know."

"I never saw that."

"It wasn't there," said Ice. "It's only there when it's people."

Drew looked at his feet a moment longer. Then up at his face.

"Huh," said Drew, the way Kestrel had.

Kei had picked his map up off the oak. He was holding it rolled in both hands, against his chest, the way Kotori held her staff when she was frightened.

"I didn't know," said Kei.

"Nobody did."

"I've had you on my map since December." Kei looked at the rolled map. "I drew you as a man holding a door."

"I am," said Ice.

Kei looked at him for a long moment.

"I'll draw the door bigger," said Kei.

Rafi was behind him with his big sword, and his mouth open.

"Boss," said Rafi. "I carried your herbs. In the fen. Two copper a plant. For *months*." He shut his mouth and opened it again. "I've been carrying a bag for *that*."

"You were a very good bag-carrier," said Ice.

"*Boss*."

The rest came in a sort of wave after that, and he couldn't keep track of all of it. Wendy at the edge of the crowd, saying *well, that went well, I think, I'm not sure, I've never had anything go well before*. Bee sitting on the floor with her sketchbook, trying to draw it, with her hands shaking. *It doesn't stay still. You don't stay still. I can't.* Azac, Riverbank's wall, looking at the ring with deep professional disapproval. *Walls don't do that.* Rui Xiang beside him, for once saying more than one word: *…yes. Walls don't.* Malcolm, very calmly, as if consulting a calendar: *Well. That changes things.* A pause. *It's still going to be August.* And Jun Liang, Riverbank's loudest, who'd shouted the whole three days, standing at the very edge with his hands over his mouth. He was saying nothing at all. Then, finally, very quietly, almost to himself:

"I'm going to make a command for this. *!eve.*" He took his hands away. "I'm not even going to be loud about it. That's how you know it's serious."

***

Xav came last. Of course he did. He'd been sitting cross-legged on the oak at the edge of the ring through the whole fight, drawing. He got up slowly, closed his sketchbook, put it under his arm, and walked over. The two blades were still crossed on his back. He'd never drawn either of them.

Gabe saw him coming.

"*XAV*," said Gabe. "*DID YOU KNOW?*"

"Nine years, Gabe," said Xav.

"That's not a *yes*."

"It's the same thing," said Xav.

Calvin had come up behind Gabe at some point. He looked from Xav to Ice and back.

"Could *you* beat him?" said Calvin. "Xav. Could you?"

Everybody went quiet. Ice felt them go quiet, all round him, the whole edge of the ring.

Xav thought about it. He actually thought about it. He looked at Ice for a while, the way he looked at a bridge he was about to draw.

"No," said Xav.

Somebody let their breath out.

"But I'd make him work," said Xav. "It wouldn't be eleven minutes."

"It wouldn't," said Ice.

"*HOW LONG*," said Gabe.

"Long enough that you'd get bored," said Xav, "and go and get food."

He opened the sketchbook and held it out. It was the ring, from the edge, in quick sure pencil. Kestrel on one knee at full draw. And Ice a quarter-step off the line of the arrow, already gone, with the sword coming down flat.

"You held back," said Xav.

"A bit."

"A lot."

"She was good," said Ice.

"She was," said Xav. He closed the sketchbook. "You'd never hold back against me."

"No," said Ice.

"Good," said Xav. He looked at Ice for a moment longer, and something went across his face that Ice had seen there maybe three times in nine years. "Don't make it a thing, Dec."

And he walked off first, back toward Riverbank, with the sketchbook under his arm. He didn't look back. He'd been wanting to do that, Ice suspected, since about the middle of the fight.

And then, from the crowd at the edge of the ring, there was a noise like a kettle, and Ice turned round. Xola was coming through the crowd with her trident over her shoulder and her gills going like fans, shouting *ICEY*. She was being held back, slightly, by Slep's hand on the back of her collar. Thea was behind her, and PenguChi behind Thea, walking slowly at the back as always.

"*ICEY WHAT WAS THAT*," shouted Xola.

"EVE," said Ice.

"*THAT'S NOT AN ANSWER*."

"Kestrel said that."

"*KESTREL'S RIGHT*."

Slep, holding her collar, said nothing. But he looked at Ice over Xola's head, and nodded once, the way he'd nodded at Gigi on the steps.

Thea didn't shout. She came and stood in front of him and looked at his face. At the thin line of red under his eye. She'd known him a long time. She'd watched him in a hundred games, from a hundred chats, years ago, at the far end of everything. She'd never seen this.

"I didn't know," said Thea, quietly.

"Nobody did," said Ice. "It was before."

"Before us?"

"Before most things."

Thea nodded slowly. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled. A small, tired, real smile.

"Go on, then," she said. "You've got a hall."

And PenguChi came up last, slowest, at the back. She stood beside Thea with her long spear upright and looked at him. She'd been looking at him for ten days, at the back of every line, in every stream and ravine. She'd asked him by a fire in the Fell Country when he wasn't slow.

"When it's people," said PenguChi.

"Yes," said Ice.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she kicked him in the shin, very gently.

"That one's for not telling me it was *that*," she said.

***

They didn't go down that night.

Kei decided it, at the top of the stair of light, with the map under his arm and his face still pale. "Not tonight," he said. "Tomorrow. Second dawn. Everybody's been walking three days, and somebody's been—" He looked at Ice and didn't finish. "Tomorrow."

Nobody argued.

Rook came and stood by the iron ring in the floor, looking down the stair of light into his own Hall's cellar, which was apparently now a great deal bigger than it had been on Wednesday. He looked at it the way Ama Corrie looked at things. Then at the night-blue banner on its hook.

"Hm," said Rook. "Down you go, then. Tomorrow. Mind the stair."

"Thank you," said Kei. "For not selling it."

Rook looked at him over his beard.

"They asked what it was worth," he said. "Nobody asked what it was for." He looked at the black banner still hanging dark on the other hook, VANTA's, with its single white line. "She can leave that up. If she wants. It's a good banner. It doesn't waste anything."

Ice looked across the Hall. Kestrel was still on the bench by the wall, with Brann beside her. She'd heard. She looked at her banner on the hook for a long moment. Then at Rook. Then she nodded.

The black banner stayed up.

***

He came out of the egg at twenty to one in the morning, and the shoplot was dark, and he lay there for a while.

The back room at Melaka Raya, with its white tiles and the two pods side by side, his and Suzu's. The lids humming open. The ceiling fan going round slowly in the dark. Out in the street, a motorbike went past. Somewhere upstairs, in the front room by the window, the third egg was opening too, louder, because Gabe had never opened anything quietly in his life.

Ice lay in his pod with the lid up and looked at the fan.

His hand had stopped shaking in the game. It started again now, out here, in the real one. Just the right hand, faintly. He held it up in the dark and watched it.

"You're shaking," said Suzu, from the next pod.

He hadn't heard Suzu's lid. Suzu's lid was always quiet.

"It does that," said Ice. "After."

"I know," said Suzu. "It did it after the drowned church. In December. You put it in your pocket so I wouldn't see."

Ice turned his head.

"You never said."

"You never said," said Suzu. And sat up in his pod, and put his feet on the tiles. "Mamak?"

***

Gabe came down the stairs like an avalanche, still shouting, and didn't stop shouting all the way to the mamak. It was the one on the corner that never closed, with its plastic chairs and its fluorescent lights and its television in the corner playing a football match nobody was watching. It was ten to one. There were three other people in it, all asleep at their tables.

They sat down. The uncle came and took their order without asking, because he knew it. Roti canai, three. Teh tarik, three.

"*Eleven minutes*," said Gabe, for the fourteenth time.

"Forty seconds," said Ice.

"*DON'T*."

Ice got his phone out.

He shouldn't have. He knew he shouldn't have. He'd turned it face down in the pod when he came out, because he'd felt it buzzing against his leg through the whole walk across the ring. He turned it over now.

There were four thousand one hundred and eighteen notifications. The number was the same as the night of the world first, in December. He looked at it twice to be sure. Then he scrolled to the top and looked at the stream summary.

*Peak: 204,880.*

He looked at it for a long time.

"What," said Gabe, and leaned over. "*What.* Dec. Dec, that's—that's *two hundred thousand*. That's *five times* what you get. That's more than *Kestrel* had. That's—Suzu, look at this. Suzu, look."

Suzu looked. Suzu looked for a while.

"Okay," said Suzu.

"*OKAY?* It's *TWO HUNDRED THOUSAND*—"

"It's a number," said Suzu. "It's a big one. He was the same person at forty thousand." He picked up his teh tarik, which had just arrived. "He'll be the same person tomorrow."

Ice put the phone down on the plastic table, face down again, and found that he'd stopped shaking.

He put the phone in his pocket. He didn't look at it again.

Across the plastic table, Gabe had finally stopped shouting. He was eating his roti with his eyes half shut, the way he ate when he was very tired and very happy. Suzu was drinking his teh tarik and watching the football match nobody was watching. The uncle had turned the fluorescent light over their table off, because they were the last ones, and left them in the light from the street.

"Dec," said Gabe, with his mouth full.

"Mm."

"Are you going to do it again?"

Ice thought about Kestrel on one knee at nine per cent, drawing to full and holding. About the half-second at the top. About walking straight down the line of her arrow because it was the one thing he'd been waiting the whole fight to see, and she'd shown it to him.

"If somebody asks," said Ice. "Properly. Like she did."

"And if somebody doesn't ask properly?"

Ice drank his tea.

"Then we don't start it," he said. "And we don't run."

Gabe grinned. Suzu didn't look away from the football. But Ice saw his mouth move, very slightly, at the corner.

***

They went down into the Elm Below at the second dawn on Tuesday. Ten of them, with the black banner still hanging dark on its hook beside theirs. Six hundred people had gone home, and Rook was standing at the top of the stair with his lantern. Kestrel's VANTA were sitting on the benches by the wall, watching. Kestrel herself was at the front of them, with her bow across her knees and her face perfectly unreadable.

They didn't clear it. Not that day.

It was a long low hall under an old man's floor, with elm roots for a ceiling. At the far end there was something that had been waiting three hundred years for a banner, and wasn't going to give its hall to anybody in a day. They came back up the stair at the second dusk with Gabe at thirty per cent and Rachael furious and Kotori out of everything. Rook said *hm* at the top. Kestrel didn't say anything at all. But as Ice came up the last step of light she caught his eye, across the Hall, and lifted her hand.

Just an inch. The way she had on the Long Night, when an archer on a roof shot three things off her back.

He lifted his back.

"Next week," said Kei, at the top of the stair, looking at his map. "Or the one after."

"Not yet," said Ice.

And half his ten laughed, because it was Kei's line now, and Kei went red. And Ice stood at the top of the stair in an old man's Hall with his hands at his sides.

They were still. The ordinary kind. The kind that came from holding a door.
