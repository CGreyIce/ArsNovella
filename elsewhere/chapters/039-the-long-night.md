"Not a minute before eight," said Xav.

He'd said it on Sunday, on the Landing, with the notebook open. He'd said it again on Monday morning in the party chat, in capitals. He said it a third time at a quarter to eight on Monday night, on the call, when Ice was sitting on the edge of his egg in the back room at Melaka Raya with his shoes off. Suzu was sitting on the edge of his, and the clock on the wall said 19:46.

"I know," said Ice.

"The egg gives you twelve hours. Then it throws you out, whatever's happening. If the dark doesn't lift when it should, and it's going to go till the next real dawn, that's eight tomorrow morning." Xav's voice was very calm, the plan voice. "Log in at eight tonight and the egg throws you out at eight tomorrow. At dawn. Not before. Log in at seven-thirty and you're out at seven-thirty, with half an hour still to go, standing in the dark, and you can't do anything about it."

"I know, Xav."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so it's a rule."

Suzu, on the other egg, made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

There were two boxes of fried rice on the folding table between them, half eaten, and two cups of teh tarik going cold. Suzu had looked at the clock at six and said "you'll want to eat," in the tone that meant there wasn't a discussion, and gone out to the mamak on the corner and come back with them. They'd eaten sitting on the floor between the eggs, the way they'd sat on the floor the night Ice died in the river.

At 19:58, Ice lay back in the gel.

At 19:59, his phone buzzed on the folding table, where he couldn't see it. He'd read it afterwards.

Fernwick at sunset was full of lights.

He'd never seen it like this. He came out of the heron-stones and along the north walk, and every lantern on every post was lit. Every window in every round house on its stilts had a lamp in it. Marta had hung her new lanterns all along the Landing, a string of them, swinging a little in the evening air off the fen. Out on his jetty, the eel man had a fire going in an iron basket on a pole, crackling and spitting sparks over the black water. Even the old militia house had a lantern over the door, and another in the yard, on the straw dummy's head, like a hat.

And people. Not four thousand any more. Two thousand, the town said, give or take. And they weren't door-hunters now. They'd come for tonight.

Everybody was where Xav had drawn them.

He walked the drawing in the last of the light, on his way out to the narrows, and every single person was standing in it. Deston on the Landing, in the very middle, where he'd hummed one low note on Sunday and listened to it go out across the water. He had the book open and a mug of something in his other hand. Kotori was already out on the eel man's mooring post in the channel off the north walk, standing with her feet together and her staff held out, looking at everything. She waved at him with her free hand, and the bell rang.

Hollis's four men and their twelve spears on the long bridge to the causeway. Hollis himself among them, in an old leather coat that must have been the militia's once, with a spear in his three-fingered hand. Ice stopped beside him at the foot of the bridge. Out past the far end, on the causeway, the fields were going blue in the dusk.

"Long night," said Ice.

"Not yet it isn't." Hollis didn't look at him. He was looking out at the fields. "It's not a long night till the dawn doesn't come. Then it's a long night."

"You said to ask you on a long night."

"So I did." Hollis spat over the parapet into the water. "Ask me when it's one."

Rafi on Sedge's steps, at the end of the north walk, with the black Chorister's Blade on his back. Joy beside him with her little knife. Bram behind them both, with his spear. Huge and silent, like a post somebody had driven into the boardwalk. Sedge herself on the porch above them, sitting in her chair in the doorway with a lamp in her lap. Not a lantern. An old clay lamp, the kind you filled with oil, with a little flame on a wick. She was holding it in both hands, as carefully as Kotori had carried the tea.

"Is that—" said Ice.

"My mother's," said Sedge. She didn't look at him either. Everybody in Fernwick was looking at the fen tonight. "She lit it on the long nights. I haven't lit it in eleven years." She turned it a little in her hands. "Go and stand where you stand."

He went and stood where he stood.

The narrows were three hundred paces out along the north walk. The boardwalk crossed a deep channel there on four tall posts, with black water on both sides, too deep to wade. The plank that had cracked under the maul a week ago had been replaced with a new one, pale against the old grey wood. He stood on it and turned round and planted his back foot.

Behind him: the north walk, and its lanterns, and the lit round houses of Fernwick. In front of him: the old fen, and the dark coming down over it.

Xav was somewhere off to his right in the reeds. Ice couldn't see him. He never could. Suzu was somewhere off to his left, probably in the water. Kotori on her post, twenty paces back. Deston's voice, very faint, from the Landing, humming. Not counting yet. Just humming. Finding the note.

The sun went down behind the reeds.

They came up out of the deep water.

That was the first thing he understood about them, and it was the most important. They didn't come from anywhere. They came *up*. Out of the black channels between the tussocks, out of the pools where nothing grew, out of the deep water a hundred paces past the heron-stones where the lurker lived. The water bulged, slow and smooth, the way it did before the lurker came, and then it wasn't water any more. It was something standing up out of it.

They were tall. Taller than him. Long thin legs like reeds, jointed backwards, stepping slowly through the water without a ripple. A long thin body like a heron's, made of something wet and black that the lantern light didn't land on. A long neck. A head that hung down, low, nodding, the way a heron's head hangs when it's looking for fish. No eyes. He looked for eyes and there weren't any. Just the long dark shapes, wading out of the old fen toward the north walk, toward the lanterns. Dozens of them. More coming up behind.

The world fell into time. It always did. Ice felt it go into him like a hand on his back. Then he was on the beat, and the beat was in his feet. The Unlit at the second post had its long neck curving up toward the lantern when he got there. He stepped in under its neck, between it and the light, and put Steadfast up.

It noticed him then.

It didn't turn its head. It didn't have to. The whole long dark body just *leaned*, toward him, toward the lantern behind him, and the head came down. It wasn't a peck. Fifty per cent. It was like putting his whole arm into a river in winter and holding it there.

The first two hours were the shape of the whole night, if he'd known it.

They came, and they came, and they kept coming. Not fast. The Unlit never did anything fast. They waded up out of the old fen in long slow lines, heads down, toward the lights, and Moonlight Harvest stood between the lights and them.

He held the narrows. That was the job. Xav had drawn it, and he stood in it. Everything that came at the north walk from the old fen had to come through the four tall posts, or else wade round through the deep water. The deep water was where Suzu was.

Suzu was always where they came. Ice would hear the *shhhhk* off to his left in the dark, low, at knee height, and see two or three long shapes go down at once in a burst of black water. Then Suzu would be standing chest-deep in the channel with the scythe across his shoulders and his long silver hair floating round him, already looking for the next. The scythe cut high. The Unlit were tall. It was, Suzu said afterwards, the first time Aldo's scythe had ever cut exactly the right height.

And Xav was behind whatever was looking at Ice. Ice never saw him. He'd just feel the weight on his shield go suddenly light, and the thing in front of him come apart. And Xav would be there in the water behind it with both daggers out and his face perfectly calm, saying, "Sorry. Were you busy?"

Kotori on her post. Kotori, who never moved, and who was somehow always healing him before the cold had finished arriving. Ten times. Fifty times. He lost count. He'd feel the chill go into his arm and the warmth come up through him to meet it before it got to his chest, every single time, from somewhere behind and to the right. And her voice, very small, every so often: "Sorry. That one was late. Sorry."

None of them were late.

Deston, on the Landing, counting. Ice couldn't see him. He could hear him. Everybody on the north walk could hear him, and everybody on the bridge, and everybody on the Landing. He was standing exactly where Xav had drawn him. *One. And two.* Two thousand strangers in a swamp moved on Deston's beat without knowing why.

Lanterns went out on the outer boardwalks, one at a time, *closed like a hand*. The dark came in a step each time. Strangers died on the north walk, travellers who'd come to stand with the swamp guy and stood somewhere he couldn't reach.

At ten o'clock, the bridge called for help.

He heard it on the party channel. Xav's voice, quick and level: "*Bridge. They're coming off the fields. Lots. Hollis can't hold the far end.*"

"Go," said Ice. "Take Suzu. I've got the narrows."

"You'll be alone out here."

"I've got Kotori."

A tiny pause. Then: "Five minutes."

They went. He felt them go, Xav out of the reeds on his right and Suzu out of the water on his left. They went back along the north walk toward town at a run, and he stood at the narrows on his own with Kotori on her post behind him and the old fen in front of him, full of long dark shapes coming up out of the water.

It was the longest five minutes of the night.

They came at him three at a time, because there was nobody to come at but him. He took them on the shield, one, two, three. The cold went into his arm like a river and Kotori's warmth came up to meet it. He turned them, and cut on the beat, and they came apart into black water. Three more came, and he did it again. The boardwalk shook under him. The new pale plank under his back foot was the only thing in the world he was sure of.

The blue got lighter. Lighter. Grey, now, along the reeds, with the faintest edge of something that might have been gold.

He looked at the little clock in the corner of his vision. The real one, the one the egg kept. *Four hours, zero minutes.* Eight to go. Eight real hours, twenty-four in here. A day. A whole day more of this, in the dark, until the egg threw him out at eight in the morning at whatever came next.

*Not a minute before eight.* Xav had known. Xav had drawn this too.

Behind him, a long way back, across the water and the boardwalks and the lanterns of Fernwick, a voice called his name. Hoarse, and old, and loud enough to carry over Deston's count. From the long bridge.

"*Holdfast!*"

He didn't turn round. He couldn't. But he called back over his shoulder, as loud as he could, across the whole of the town.

"*Is it a long night yet?*"

There was a pause. In it, he heard the Unlit coming up out of the water again, all over the old fen, more of them than before. Bigger.

"*Now it is!*" Hollis shouted. "*Come and ask me!*"
