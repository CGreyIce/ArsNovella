"Poison and medicine," said Ysolde Marr, "are the same thing."

She said it on Saturday afternoon in her stillroom at the top of Hallowell, with the windows open on the steam. She held a small glass vial up to the light between two fingers. Inside it, a few drops of something clear as water.

"In different doses," said Kei.

Ysolde looked at him over the vial. She was a tall, thin, very upright woman somewhere past sixty, in a dark green apron with stains on it of every colour. She was the town's physician and the town's poisoner, and as far as Kei could tell she saw no difference between the two jobs. She had a way of looking at you as if she were measuring you for something. She'd been looking at him like that since Thursday.

"Who told you that?" she said.

"Nobody. It's just—" He thought about it. "It's the only way it can work. If a thing can stop a heart, it can slow one. If it can slow one, it can steady one. It's the same thing. It depends how much."

"Hm," said Ysolde. She put the vial down on the bench between them. "Then measure me one that steadies."

Hallowell was built round a hot spring, and it had been built round it for a long time.

The spring came up out of the hill in the middle of the town in a wide stone basin, green and steaming. It ran down through the streets in stone channels to the bathhouses on the lower terraces, and everything in Hallowell smelled faintly of it. Warm stone, and iron, and something mineral underneath. People came from all over the hills to sit in the water for their joints and their chests and their sorrows. Ysolde's stillroom was at the top of the town, where the spring was hottest, and her physic garden ran down the slope below it in narrow beds. Feverfew and foxglove. Comfrey and hemlock. Valerian and wolfsbane, side by side, in the same soil, watered from the same channel.

He'd arrived on Thursday at noon, his time. He'd come out of a small wooden room with rain on the window into a steam-filled street with a channel of hot water running down the middle. He'd stood in it, in the warm fog, with his hair coming loose from its green tie, and felt his shoulders drop for the first time in about a year.

He'd built himself carefully. He built everything carefully. Black hair fading to dark teal-green at the ends, side-swept, half up in a ponytail. Golden eyes. The face he'd streamed in for years. The mirror had offered him the green hair tie as a keepsake, and he'd said yes, because it was the thing he touched most, without noticing, when he was thinking. He was touching it now, standing at Ysolde's bench, looking at the vial.

*Steadies.*

He'd taken a knife from the rack. A long plain knife, single-edged, the kind you'd use for cutting roots as much as anything else. And he'd taken Herbalism and Alchemy, because he'd watched Ice brew draughts in a swamp for a month and thought *that's the bit I'd want*. Not the fighting. The making. The part where you understood how a thing worked well enough to change it.

He measured.

The vial was adder venom. Ysolde had milked it that morning from an adder in the physic garden, holding the thing behind the head with two fingers while it struck at the lip of a glass. Kei had watched her do it without stepping back. She'd noticed that too. You thinned it with spring water, drop by drop, and you added a pinch of ground valerian and a pinch of something that made the window call it *heartsease*, and you watched the colour.

Too little, and it did nothing. Too much, and it was what it had been: something that stopped hearts.

He did it slowly. He thinned and watched, thinned and watched, the way you'd tune a string.

The call had been open since Thursday.

Not all the time. People came and went, the way they would in a house. But it was open, the channel, all six names on it, and whenever any of them was in, they'd be on it. Sometimes talking. Mostly not. Kei liked it best when nobody was talking, and he could hear them all being in their towns at once.

Maigo in his orchard, humming. Maigo hummed when he worked and didn't know he did it. Every so often he'd stop and say "*hold*" to something, in a voice like he was talking to a friend. He'd found the living wood on the Old Pippin on Friday morning: one branch, high up on the north side, still green under the bark. He'd been grafting from it ever since, onto young stock, one twig at a time. Mother Quince said nobody had thought to try it in forty years.

Hako in his river town, Otterbank, full of trappers and otters and very cold water. He'd hit Lv 4 on Friday night, the first of them. He narrated everything he did in the voice of a wildlife documentary, quietly, for nobody. "And here we see the young spearman," he'd say, standing in the shallows with his spear raised, "waiting. Waiting. He has been waiting for some time." Then a splash. "He has missed."

Salty on his cliff. Grimmet, a mining town on black rock over a grey sea, all wind and gulls and the sound of picks. He'd taken a greatsword nearly as long as he was tall and put the pain up to fifty, because Ice was on fifty. On Friday something with a lot of legs had come out of a mine shaft and bitten him on the leg. On the call, they'd heard the bite. They'd heard the thing scream when he cut it in half. They hadn't heard Salty make a sound, at all, the whole time.

"Salty," Wendy had said, afterwards, very quietly. "Are you okay?"

"Yes," Salty had said. "It's heavy. Good."

And Wendy, in her forest. Ashcombe, under the tallest trees any of them had ever seen. She'd decided not to like it, and she was clearly in love with it, and she still wouldn't admit it. On Saturday night she'd streamed for the first time in four months, twenty-six minutes, no title. Kei had watched every second from his bed with the lights off, and hadn't said a word on the call about it afterwards, because she'd have stopped.

And Ina.

***

Ina was in Emberly, a town on a hill with a forge at the top of it.

It was a great stone forge, very old, with a chimney you could see from the plains and a fire in it that had never gone out in the town's memory. Emberly had been built round it the way Hallowell had been built round the spring. Ina had taken Mining and Blacksmithing, the way Salty had, and a short black wand from the rack, and magic. He'd built his face exactly as it had always been: the single curved purple horn, the white hair going lavender at the ends, the teal eyes, the grin with the fangs in it. The mirror had offered him the mask. The grey oni mask with the red-rimmed eyes and the cords. He wore it hung at his shoulder on its cord, where he could reach it.

He'd learned his first spell on Friday, from the forge-master, a huge silent woman called Brannagh who spoke about four words a day and made each one count.

"*Kindle*," Ina had said on the call, on Friday night. He'd said it very carefully, rolling it round his mouth like a good wine. "That's the word. The gesture's like this," and they'd all heard him move. "And the focus is the thing you want to burn. You hold all three at once, and the fire comes. And it comes *out of the word*. You can feel it. It's the word that does it. You say it right and the fire comes, and you say it wrong and you get smoke and a headache." A pause. "I love it. It's a spell made of a word. Of course I love it."

He was good at it. He was good at all of it. He'd always been good with words, and in here words were the thing.

But on Saturday night, late, Kei's time, when the others had all logged out and Kei was lying in bed with the call still open, Ina was still on.

Kei could hear him. Just breathing, and the roar of the forge somewhere close, and the occasional crack of something in the fire. Not doing anything. Not talking.

"Ina," said Kei, quietly.

"Mm."

"You okay?"

There was a long silence. The forge roared.

"I'm sitting by the fire," said Ina. "At the top of the hill. It's the great forge. It's been burning for three hundred years, apparently. They feed it all night. There's a boy whose whole job is to feed it all night, and he's asleep, so I'm feeding it." A pause. "I'm sitting by a fire on a hill, feeding it, in a game. At two on a Saturday afternoon, in a booth in a café in England, in the dark."

Kei waited. That was the thing he'd always known how to do. Wait, and let it come.

"It's the first thing in a long time," said Ina, eventually, in a very level voice, "that I haven't had to make myself do."

Kei didn't say anything.

"You know what it was like," said Ina. "Before. The stopping. You saw. Everybody saw, a bit. I'd sit down to stream and I'd just sit there. Not because anything was wrong. Because there wasn't anything. There wasn't anything there to make it out of." The fire cracked. "And I'd make myself. And make myself. And then I couldn't, and I stopped. And everything was very grey and very far away for a long time."

"And now?"

"Now I'm feeding a fire on a hill in the middle of a Saturday." Ina laughed, very quietly. "And I want to. That's all. I'm doing it and I want to be doing it. I didn't have to make myself log in tonight. I didn't have to make myself say the word. I just wanted to." He stopped. "I'd forgotten what that was like. Wanting to do a thing. Just wanting to."

Kei lay in bed in the dark, in Australia, with the window open on a summer night and the call in his ear. He thought about Ysolde's vial. *Too little, and it does nothing. Too much, and it's what it was.*

"Then do that," he said. "Just that. Feed the fire."

"For how long?"

"As long as you want to." Kei closed his eyes. "And then stop. And then come back when you want to again. Nobody's counting, Ina."

There was a long silence on the call. The forge roared.

"Thank you, Kei," said Ina, very softly, in his best voice. The one he used for the good words.

***

On Sunday the six of them talked about Monday night.

They'd all seen the window on Wednesday, or heard about it. *The year turns in five days. On the longest night, the dark will not lift when it should. Keep your lights. Keep your towns.* None of them knew what it meant. Chris had made a sheet. Milli had theories. Every forum on the internet had a thread.

"It's an invasion," said Hako, cheerfully, from his river. "Obviously. Things come out of the dark at the towns. It's a tower defence. I love tower defence."

"It's going to be long," said Wendy. "The night. Hester says *it depends on the year*. That's not a thing people say about a night that's going to be short."

"The orchard's putting lanterns in the trees," said Maigo. "All of them. Every tree on the hill. Mother Quince says the Old Pippin gets a lantern in every branch, even the dead ones. *Especially* the dead ones. I've been up a ladder since nine."

"Grimmet's lighting the mine," said Salty. "Every shaft. All the way down."

"The great forge doesn't go out," said Ina. "Ever. That's the whole point of Emberly. Brannagh says on the longest night they don't just keep it lit, they feed it till it roars. So that it can be seen from the plains." A pause. "I've volunteered to feed it."

"And the spring doesn't go cold," said Kei. "Ysolde says. Every lamp in Hallowell goes on round the basin, and somebody sits up with every one."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Six towns, a long way apart, all doing the same thing in their own way, the way the fen had been doing it in Ice's stream all week. Lamp oil and lanterns and fires and old men greasing spears. Nobody had told any of the people in any of the towns about the window. They'd just known.

*Keep your lights. Keep your towns.*

"So," said Hako. "What do we do?"

They were all waiting for him to answer, Kei realised. Not because he'd said he'd decide. Nobody had decided that. They just were. Maigo, and Salty, and Wendy, and Ina, and Hako, in six towns, on a call, waiting to hear what he thought.

He touched the green tie in his hair.

"We keep six towns," he said.

"Separately?"

"Separately. Each of us in our own. However long it goes." He looked out of Ysolde's window at the steam coming off the spring, and the lamps she'd already set out round the basin, unlit. "We can't walk to each other. Not yet. So each of us keeps our own, and we stay on the call all night. All of us. The whole time. And if anyone's town starts going wrong, everybody else knows straight away, and we talk them through it." He paused. "That's all. We keep our own lights. And we keep each other on the line."

There was a short silence.

"That's a good plan," said Maigo, warmly.

"It's not really a plan."

"It's a good one," said Salty.

"I'll feed the fire," said Ina. "And I'll talk. All night. You'll all hate it."

"We won't," said Wendy.

"You will," said Ina. "But you'll be very gracious about it."

Kei laughed. He stood at Ysolde's bench with the steam coming in through the window and his knife on his hip and six names on the call. He thought about a sheet of paper on a desk in the dark in Australia, covered in dots, and the lines between them that had already been there before he drew them.

"Tomorrow," he said.

***

On Sunday night he couldn't sleep.

He got up at two in the morning, two in the morning on the day the year turned, and sat at his desk with the lamp on. He took a sheet of paper out of the printer, and a pencil, and drew a map.

It wasn't a real map. Nobody had a real map. The game gave you your own: the places you'd walked, the places you'd seen from hills, a big pale nothing everywhere else. But he'd asked them all, over three days, quietly, one at a time. *Where are you? What's north of you? Which way does the sun come up?* And he'd asked about the intermediate towns at Lv 10, and the roads that opened there, and how many days' walk it was from each of them. He'd put it all together in his head, the way you'd work out where a sound was coming from with your eyes shut.

He drew six dots.

Orchard End, in the middle of a lot of green, to the west. Otterbank, on a river, south. Grimmet on its cliff, far north, almost at the edge of the paper. Ashcombe in the forest, east of Grimmet but not close. Eleven days, Salty said, once the roads opened, and he'd walk it. Emberly on its hill, out on its own. Hallowell, himself, in the hills in the middle.

Six dots, scattered all over a sheet of A4, nowhere near each other. Two feet apart in a cinema in Singapore, some of them. Hundreds of kilometres apart in here.

He looked at it for a while. Then he drew lines.

Not roads. He didn't know the roads. Lines between people. Maigo to Salty, because they'd played together for years. Salty to Wendy, a short thick line he went over twice. Wendy to Maigo. All three of them to Suzu, who wasn't one of the six but had brought every one of them in. Through Suzu, years ago, in a Discord call at three in the morning, *you should meet my friends*. Ina to Suzu. Hako to Suzu. Himself to Suzu.

So he drew Suzu.

He had to put Suzu somewhere, so he put him where he was: south and east, in the fen, at a place called Fernwick, beside a dot called Ice. He drew a line between them, a strong one. Then Ice to Maigo, and Ice to Salty, because Ice was closest to those two of all of them, and everyone knew it. Ice to Kei. Ice to Hako, who Ice had taught CS, patiently, for months. Ice to Wendy. Ice to Ina, who Ice had always thought was cool, and had said so, to his face, often.

Then he couldn't stop.

He drew Hyvred, on a white cliff by the sea, and Bee on her moor, and the line between them, short and strong. Hyvred to him, because they'd been friends a long time. Bee to him. He drew Tiger in his canal city, falling in, and Drew in his snowy mountain village, and the lines between them, and from both of them to him. Then Drew to Ice, a thinner line, and Tiger to Ice. Then Higu and Gigichi somewhere he wasn't sure of, a question mark. He drew Deston in Brackenford and Xav beside him. Then the line from Xav to the eight friends who were coming in on the first of January, whom Xav had announced on Ice's stream that afternoon he was leaving to lead. He drew them as a cluster of eight small dots with no town yet, joined to Xav and to each other with eight short lines, and one long thin one going back to Ice.

He drew Niko, who wasn't in yet, beside Maigo and Salty, and Ice, with a dotted line. Newish. Chris. Winter. Hana and Milli, in Singapore, with Cursiffe and Aleaka and Jared. Rachael and Calvin and Dennis. Dotted lines, all of them. People who weren't in yet but would be. People who were going to come in, one wave at a time, in their own small groups, into random towns scattered all over a world nobody had a map of.

He sat back.

The sheet of paper was covered. Dots everywhere, and lines between them, crossing and recrossing. Short thick ones and long thin ones. Solid ones and dotted ones. Little clusters of four and five and eight, joined to each other by single threads, and those threads joined to other threads. Every dot was someone. Every line was something that had already happened: a call at three in the morning, a raid, a game, a year. He hadn't invented any of them. He'd just drawn what was already there.

And almost every line went, in the end, by one route or another, through the same few dots. Through Suzu. Through Ice. Through him.

He looked at it for a long time, with the lamp on and the summer night coming in through the window.

Everybody was going to be in little groups. That was what the game was going to do to them. Random towns, and parties of five and six, and companies of eight, and long walks. Moonlight Harvest. Xav's eight. Hyvred and Bee. The six. Tiger and Drew. Little clusters, scattered, each one keeping its own town. That was fine. That was how it should be. A party of five was a good size, and a company of eight was a good size. You needed to be small to move.

But you'd want something to hold them, too. One day. Not now. Something big enough to hold all the little groups at once, without making any of them stop being what they were. Something you could draw like this. Dots, and the lines that were already there.

It looked like something. He knew it did. He'd seen it before, on clear nights away from the city lights. Points of light that were nowhere near each other, joined by lines somebody had drawn between them a long time ago so they'd mean something.

He didn't write the word. Not tonight. It was too early, and too big, and he was the only one who could see it. He put the pencil down and turned the lamp off and sat in the dark for a while. The sheet of paper was a pale square on the desk, covered in lines.

Later today the sun would go down in a world nobody had a map of, on the longest night of the year. It would go down on every dot on that page at once: on an orchard full of lanterns, a mine lit all the way down, a forest of red clamps, a forge fed till it roared, a spring ringed with lamps, a river, a fen. And it wouldn't come up when it should.

Then he went back to bed.
