Ama Corrie had kept the Roll in the Hall of Roads for forty years, and her mother before her, and her mother's mother before that. Nobody had ever told her what it was for.

It was a book. That was all. A great black book on the counter of a round hall where seven roads met, with names in it. Old names at the front, faded so far you could hardly read them. Then blank pages, hundreds of them, for longer than anyone remembered. Then, in January, a name. *KeiKatamori.* Small and upright, the way a careful young man wrote when he was frightened of getting it wrong.

She'd kept it before there were names in it. She'd kept it when there weren't. That was what keeping was. You didn't keep a thing because it was useful, or because somebody had told you why. You kept it because it had been given to you to keep, and because if you didn't, nobody would.

She'd said that to a girl who made hedges, in February. The girl had looked at her as if she'd said something astonishing. Ama Corrie hadn't thought it was astonishing. She'd thought it was obvious. But she'd liked the girl for being astonished. You didn't meet many people, at her age, who could still be astonished by the obvious.

***

On Tuesday morning she swept under the chairs.

It wasn't Sunday. She swept the banner room on Sundays. Her mother had, and her mother's mother, and now Ama Corrie knew why. A girl with a lantern had read it out to the whole world from the top of the dome a month ago. *Carrow Cross. Keep the Roll. Sweep the banner room on Sundays. Read the ceiling by the moon.* Three hundred years of Sundays, and nobody had known.

She swept on Tuesday anyway, because they were coming.

She'd known since Thursday night. The dome carried. Everything said in the Hall of Roads came up into the dome and round and down again, and Ama Corrie heard all of it, whether she wanted to or not. She'd heard the young man with the map tell the others he was going to walk and ask. She'd heard the girl with horns, every morning since, go up the stair to the eye at seven and sit there, not saying anything, looking at the roads. Just after midday she'd heard the girl with horns shout *THEY'RE COMING* so loudly that a pigeon fell off the weathercock.

So she swept.

The banner room was small and arched, off the west side of the Hall. It had iron hooks in the wall where the banner hung now, the new one. Night-blue, with gold dots and silver-white lines, painted on a bridge by a girl with wings and an eyelash brush. Under it, a long table. Round the table, chairs.

There'd been twenty chairs in March, on the night of the founding, because there'd been twenty names. Ama Corrie had set them out herself, from the store under the stair, where they'd been stacked since before her grandmother was born. Then, as names came, she'd set out more. Thirty-two chairs, now, for thirty-two names.

And seventeen more, at the far end of the table, by the wall. She'd set them out in March, the night after the founding. Nine for the swamp one and his. Eight for the bank lot. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had sat in them. The young man with the map had seen them, once, in April, and stood in the doorway looking at them for a long time without saying anything.

She'd swept under them every Sunday for four months. Even the ones nobody sat in. That was what keeping was.

She swept under them now, on a Tuesday, one at a time. Seventeen chairs. Then she put the broom back under the stair and went to her counter, and opened the book, and took out her pen, and waited.

***

They came in by the south door.

She'd thought they would. The young man with the map would have made sure of it. *Everybody comes through the gate.* She heard them on the steps first, the girl with odd eyes saying *who'd stand next to you* in her flat voice, the way she'd said it to three thousand people. She heard the answers. She heard a big voice shout *WE CAME IN THE SOUTH ONE*. She said, without looking up, "Mind the north door."

"*WE CAME IN THE SOUTH ONE*," said the big voice again, closer, at the counter.

"Mind it anyway," said Ama Corrie. "Forty years. It's a habit."

She looked up.

Eight of them, in front of her counter. A young man with a pencil behind his ear and a face that had decided a long time ago to give nothing away, and gave away everything. A quiet one with two knives. A big one with a hammer who was trying not to shout and failing. A tall one looking at her ceiling. A woman with a wand and a calm low voice. A big flat wall of a man. One who said only *yeah*. And a young man who kept making small lights float off his fingers and catching them again.

"The bank lot," said Ama Corrie.

"Riverbank," said the one with the pencil.

"I know what you're called. I wrote it in a charter in Brackenford's records, in February, when the old clerk there sent me the copy." She looked at him over her spectacles. "You'll be first, I suppose. You said so on my steps, in March, the night of the founding. *First through the door.*"

The one with the pencil went very still. Ama Corrie had seen people do that before, when they found out the dome carried.

"I hear everything," she said. "It's the dome. Sign."

She turned the book round and held out the pen.

He signed. *Sketch.* Quick and sure, the way a man drew something he'd drawn a hundred times in his head. Then the seven. *theidiot*, quietly. *Serenity*, looking at the ceiling. *Mango*. *Pingu*. *Resent*. *Sakami*, in a very neat hand. And *Historia*, so large it took up two lines, at which Ama Corrie said nothing at all, very pointedly.

Eight names. Forty.

***

Then the swamp one came to her counter.

She'd met him once before. In February, a few days before the founding, when he'd walked into her Hall with his friends to sign nothing and stand in the north door. *Not a member. A friend.* She'd said *you're the swamp one* and he'd said *hm*. She'd liked him. She didn't like many people. He'd stood in the north door all through the founding, and then, when the great bird came through the eye for her book, he'd been in front of her counter before she'd seen him move. With his shield, between her and the beak. She'd had a bucket. He'd had a shield. She'd thought, afterwards, that the shield had been more use, and been very annoyed about it.

He stood in front of her counter now, with the eight behind him. The big one with the hammer. The tall one with the book. The girl with the bell. The silver-haired boy with the jar. The quiet one with the long sword. A short woman who looked as if she'd tell you off for needing her, a young man who couldn't stand still, and a man with a notebook who looked at Ama Corrie's counter the way some people looked at a cake.

"Swamp one," said Ama Corrie.

"Ama Corrie."

"Second," said Ama Corrie.

His face did something small.

"He said you'd be annoyed about it," said Ama Corrie. "The pencil one. On my steps. In March. *Dec'll be second. He'll be annoyed about it.*" She looked at him over her spectacles. "Are you?"

"A bit," said the swamp one.

Behind him, the one with the pencil laughed out loud.

Ama Corrie turned the book round and held out the pen.

He looked at the page for a long moment. Thirty-two names, and eight more. *KeiKatamori* at the top, small and upright. The rest under it in all their different hands. The girl who made hedges, like a hedge. The girl with the lantern, careful. The pen one, *dumbo*, very neatly, the twentieth, at eleven fifty-two on a Thursday night in March, with Ama Corrie's counter cracked in front of him where the beak had hit it.

He signed.

*Ice.*

Small and plain. Three letters. Not quick, not slow. The way a man wrote something he'd decided a long time ago.

Ama Corrie looked at it.

"Hm," she said.

Then the eight. *Kai*, with a careful hand that looked as if it had been taught to write in choirs. *Suzuyunn*, with a little flourish at the end that Ama Corrie hadn't expected. *Kotori*, very small and very neat, and then she said *sorr—* to the page and stopped, and went pink, and said *thank you* instead. *Leveti*, once, without fuss. *Baer*, heavily, pressing hard enough to dent the page underneath. *minksminko*, quick and plain, as if she didn't have time to waste on it, though she looked at it afterwards for a long time. *cal.calvin*, nearly through the paper. And *tomatosoup30*, in a hand Ama Corrie recognised at once. The hand of somebody who'd kept accounts for a long time and was proud of every one of them.

"You keep a book," said Ama Corrie.

"I keep the purse, darling," said the man with the notebook.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie, and found that she liked him.

Seventeen names. Forty-nine.

Ama Corrie didn't see the window. She never saw them. She only knew it was there because forty-nine travellers in her Hall, and the three thousand on the steps outside, all went still at once and looked at nothing for a moment. Then the noise came. The way it had in March. All at once, from the market and the steps and the Hall and the stair to the eye, so loud the dome rang with it.

She waited for it to stop. It took a while.

***

The girl with wings had taken the banner down off its hooks while Ama Corrie wasn't looking.

She'd laid it out on the floor of the Hall, under the eye, the way she had in March. Night-blue, edge to edge across the flagstones, with the gold dots and the silver-white lines. And she was kneeling at the edge of it with a pot of gold and a pot of silver-white and a brush so fine it was barely there. An eyelash brush, she'd called it, in March, when Ama Corrie asked.

"What are you doing?" said Ama Corrie, from the counter.

"Fixing it," said the girl with wings.

Ama Corrie came round the counter to look. She didn't usually come round the counter. She did now.

The girl had painted the banner in January, on a bridge, over two nights. A dot for every person, a line between every two who'd stand next to each other. The young man with the map had drawn it first, on paper, and she'd painted it true. And on the right of the banner, in a cluster, there were nine dots. Their lines to the middle had been painted dotted. Little gaps in the silver-white, all the way across, the way the young man had drawn them on his map. *Not yet.*

And a line from one dot on the edge, the pencil one, going back toward the middle. Dotted too.

The girl with wings dipped her eyelash brush in the silver-white and began to fill in the gaps.

One at a time. Slowly. Each little gap in each dotted line, so that the line ran solid. She didn't hurry. She painted the way Ama Corrie's grandmother had said the first Rollkeeper painted the ceiling, four hundred years ago. *Slowly. All the way through. As if there was only one ceiling and you'd only get to do it once.*

Nobody said anything. Forty-nine people stood round the edge of a banner on the floor of the Hall of Roads and watched a girl with wings fill in the gaps in some lines. The young man with the map was standing next to Ama Corrie, very still. The swamp one stood at the edge of the banner, looking down at the nine dots that were his. Ama Corrie watched his face. It was doing something she hadn't seen it do before. She thought it might be the thing faces did when they found out they'd been somewhere the whole time.

It took the girl an hour. Nobody left.

When she'd finished she sat back on her heels and looked at it. Then she looked up at the swamp one.

"I painted you dotted in January," she said. "I said *I don't know who you are*."

"I remember."

"I know who you are now," said the girl with wings. "So." She nodded at the banner. "Solid."

***

Afterwards, when the Hall had emptied a bit and the noise had gone out into the market, Ama Corrie took the swamp one to the banner room.

She didn't say why. She just said *come here*, the way she said it to people who'd tracked mud on her floor, and he came. She took him into the small arched room with the banner back on its hooks, solid now, and the long table, and the chairs.

He looked at the chairs. Forty-nine. All the way down the table. The seventeen at the far end, by the wall, that she'd set out in March.

"I set those out the night after the founding," said Ama Corrie. "Nine for you and yours. Eight for the bank lot." She looked at them. "Nobody asked me to."

He didn't say anything.

"I swept under them every Sunday," said Ama Corrie. "For four months. Even though nobody sat in them." She sniffed. "I told the young man with the map, when he left. *Even the ones nobody sits in.* He looked at me as if I'd said something astonishing." She looked at the swamp one. "It's not astonishing. It's what keeping is. You keep the chair until somebody sits in it. Even if it's never." A pause. "Especially if it's never."

The swamp one looked at the chair at the end of the table, by the wall. The first of the nine.

He pulled it out and sat down in it.

Ama Corrie watched him. He sat in the chair at the end of a long table in a small arched room, under a banner with his own dot on it painted solid. He put his hands flat on the table. He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Thank you," he said, eventually.

"For what? Sweeping?"

"For keeping it," said the swamp one.

Ama Corrie looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment.

"Hm," she said. Then she went out of the banner room very quickly, because something had got into her eye, and it was nobody's business what.

***

She found him again at the end of the afternoon, at her counter.

He was looking at the other book. The old one. The black book the big silent man from Grimmet had carried home in his arms in April, swollen and green, from a ruined hall at the top of the world. She'd been drying it ever since, a page at a time, between sheets of blotting paper, under a weight. A hundred and forty pages now. The names, mostly rain. The maps of the dark. The list.

It was open on the counter at the list.

*What we left.*

He was looking at one line, near the bottom of the page.

*Fernwick. Cut the reeds in song, to keep the count. Set one to watch—*

And then the grey cloud where the rain had been.

Ama Corrie came and stood beside him.

"The girl with the lantern read the rest of that one out," she said. "On the roof. A friend in a fen, she said. An old woman."

"Sedge," said the swamp one. "Her mother used to say it. At bedtime."

Ama Corrie looked at the grey cloud on the page.

She'd been keeping the old book for two months. Drying it. Not touching it with ink. You didn't write in somebody else's Roll; she'd been told that by her mother, and her mother by hers. But this Roll had nobody left to keep it. Its keepers had gone north three hundred years ago and not come back. It had come to her counter. It was hers, now, the way the chairs were hers.

And a line in it was missing. And she knew what it said.

She took out her pen.

The swamp one watched her. He didn't say anything. She uncapped the pen and leaned over the old book, and in the margin beside the grey cloud, very carefully, in her own small hand, she wrote the rest.

*—so the rest may sleep.*

She looked at it. Then, under it, smaller, because a Rollkeeper wrote down where a thing came from:

*(Given back by Sedge of Fernwick, whose mother said it every night. Written in by A. Corrie, Rollkeeper, Carrow Cross.)*

She capped the pen.

The swamp one was looking at the line. The whole line, now, in two hands, three hundred years apart.

"She'll want to know," he said. "Sedge. That it's written in."

"Then tell her," said Ama Corrie. "Tell her it's kept."

"I will."

"And tell her," said Ama Corrie, "that her mother was right to say it. Every night. Even if nobody knew why." She closed the old book gently, the way you'd close a door on somebody asleep. "Tell her that's what keeping is."

***

That night, after the Hall was empty and the lamps were out, Ama Corrie swept under the chairs again.

It wasn't Sunday. She didn't care. She went down the long table in the banner room with her broom. All forty-nine, in the moonlight from the eye, which was open, because a girl with a lantern had said the ceiling should be read by the moon and Ama Corrie had decided she was right.

Under the first thirty-two chairs she swept the way she always did.

Under the last seventeen, by the wall, she found something she'd never once found under them in four months. Mud. Real mud, from boots, from a long road. Fen mud, under the first chair of the nine, green-black, smelling of old reeds.

She stood there for a long time with the broom, looking at it.

Then she swept it up. Carefully. All of it.

She didn't throw it away. She put it in a small clay pot from the shelf behind her counter and put the lid on it, and put it on the shelf beside the old black book.

Nobody would ever know why. She didn't need them to.

That was what keeping was.
