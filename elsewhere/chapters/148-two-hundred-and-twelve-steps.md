Gigi knew how many steps there were from the floor of the Hall of Roads to the eye of the dome. She'd counted them so many times that she'd stopped needing to and kept counting anyway. Two hundred and twelve. A hundred and six to the first landing, where the stair turned and the little window looked out over the east road. Forty to the second, where you started to feel the wind. Sixty-six to the top. And then the ledge round the inside of the eye, and the sky, and Higu.

On Sunday night she went up and down them three times.

The first time was at the second dusk. She was carrying two cups of tea, the way she always did, and she sat down in her place on the ledge, which was Higu's place really. Higu didn't seem to mind. Higu was sitting on the other side of the lamp they'd brought up that week and never taken down, looking west.

"Hyv's on the stair," said Higu.

Gigi knew. Hyvred had been on the stair at the second landing since the noon dusk with her glasses pushed up in her hair. She was watching the north-west road the way she watched everything now, edge to edge, all of it at once. She'd been doing that every evening for a week. Ever since a carter called Hobb had come in through the south gate on Thursday with a load of turnips and the news that there was a betting book at the Hart.

Gigi drank her tea and didn't look at the north-west road. She looked at the moon, which wasn't up yet.

At a quarter past nine Hyvred's voice came up the stair, flat and carrying, the way it carried when she'd seen something.

"*Last hill. North-west road. Six.*"

Gigi put her cup down on the ledge very carefully.

"*And a sheep*," called Hyvred. A pause. "*They're holding hands.*"

Gigi stood up. She didn't decide to. She just found she was on her feet. Then she went down two hundred and twelve steps to the floor of the Hall, past Hyvred on the second landing, who looked at her and didn't say anything. She stood at the bottom of the stair for a minute in the lamplight with her hands at her sides. Ama Corrie was at the counter, writing, and didn't look up.

Then she went back up again.

"Better?" said Higu, when she sat down.

"No," said Gigi.

"Okay," said Higu.

The second time she went down was an hour later, and she got as far as the banner room door. She stood in it the way she'd stood in it a fortnight ago with two cups of tea. Six chairs by the window, at the far end of the long table, near it, not at it. Somebody had polished them. She was fairly sure it had been Ama Corrie. There was a smell of beeswax in the room that hadn't been there in the morning. Then she went back up.

The third time she didn't go anywhere. She just stood up on the ledge and sat down again. Higu, beside her, very gently, took the cup out of her hand before it went over the edge into the Hall.

"I'm going to be sick," said Gigi.

"You can't be sick in the game."

"I'm going to be sick in my *head*."

"That's allowed," said Higu. "That's allowed up here."

***

The thing about the old year was that Gigi remembered it differently now than she had at the time.

At the time it had been a long, dark, tangled thing in which she was the one who'd been wronged. She'd been sure of that. She'd been sure of it the way you're sure of the floor under your feet. Thea had done things, or not done them, or done them the wrong way. And Gigi had been the one who had to put up with it, and point it out, and be disappointed. Every time. She'd told it to herself so many times that it had become the shape of things.

It hadn't been the shape of things.

She'd worked that out slowly, over the last year, mostly in the game. On the north steps at midnight with Drew. On the roof with Higu and a cup of tea. On the night of the founding, holding a hedge of thorn across a counter with her knuckles white on the staff. Nobody had told her to *find the wrong in it* that night. There'd been a thing coming over the counter with too many teeth, and a dozen people behind her who'd be dead if she let go. She'd held, and held, and not once in all that holding had she thought about whose fault it was. There hadn't been time. There'd just been the counter and the hedge and the people behind it.

And afterwards, sitting on the floor with her arms shaking, she'd thought, very clearly: *that's what it's like*. To hold something and not look for someone to blame for how heavy it is.

She'd never done that with Thea. Not once.

Whatever Thea had done, she'd found the wrong in it. There'd never been a right. Thea would try one way and Gigi would show her why it was wrong, and Thea would try the other way and Gigi would show her why that was wrong too. And on the other end of a phone, every night, somewhere, Xola had been holding Thea up. Gigi had known that, at the time, in the back of her mind. She'd been angry about it. *She tells Xola everything*. She'd thought it was disloyal.

It hadn't been disloyal. It had been the only thing keeping Thea standing.

Then Thea had ended it. And Gigi had told herself, for a long time, that she'd been wronged. That it would have worked if Thea had only tried harder. *It'll fall down*, she'd said about it, afterwards, to anyone who asked, as if it had been a building with bad foundations that collapsed on its own.

It hadn't been going to fall down. She'd pushed it.

That was the thing she'd worked out on the north steps with Drew in June. *I know what I was*, she'd said to him, and he'd said, *then you know what you're deciding*.

She'd been deciding ever since. Every day. She said *my fault* out loud now, about small things. A potion she'd got wrong, a thorn laid late, a cup knocked off the ledge. She said it like someone learning a new language, badly. She wasn't good at it. But she said it, and didn't hand the wrong to anyone else, and every time it got a tiny bit less like lifting something heavy.

And now Thea was on the last hill, a day away, holding hands with people who'd stood next to her through all of it.

And what frightened Gigi most, sitting on the ledge with her tea going cold, was this. She'd see Thea. And the old thing would come out of her mouth before she could stop it, out of pure habit, the way your hand goes to a light switch in the dark in a house you've moved out of. Some small sharp thing about the way Thea was standing, or what she'd said, or how long she'd taken. The wrong, found, and handed over.

She'd practised so hard not to. But she'd practised the other thing for so much longer.

***

"What do I say?" she said to Higu, at about one in the morning.

Higu was lying on her back on the ledge with her hands behind her head, looking straight up through the eye at the stars.

"Not mine," said Higu.

"I know it's not yours."

"Then why are you asking me?"

"Because you're *here*."

Higu laughed softly.

"I'm the road," she said. "I'm not going to tell you what to say, Chi. I'm not going to tell her either. I'm not going to stand in the middle and pass things across. I did that for six weeks and it was right for six weeks. It's not right now." She turned her head on the stone and looked at Gigi in the lamplight. "She's going to be here. In the town. You can say things to her yourself. With your face. That's what she's walking eight days for. So you can."

Gigi looked at her hands.

"What if I say the wrong thing?"

"Then you'll have said the wrong thing," said Higu. "And you'll know, because you know now. And you can say *that was wrong*. That's the new bit. That's the bit you didn't use to have."

"I hate that you're right."

"I know," said Higu. "I'm proud of you anyway."

"*Don't*."

"I'm going to," said Higu, and closed her eyes.

***

Drew came up at the third dawn with bread.

He did that sometimes. Not often. Once a week or so he'd appear at the top of the two hundred and twelve steps, with his hair still the strange platinum on one side and red streak on the other and a bag of something from the baker on Spoke Street. He'd sit down on the ledge with them without saying anything and hand things round. He wasn't a roof person, really. He was a gate person. But he came up when he thought he was needed. He was usually right about when.

He handed Gigi a roll. She held it and didn't eat it.

"They'll be at the gate by noon," said Drew. "Hyv says. They're walking slowly. She thinks on purpose."

"They're always slow," said Higu, with her eyes shut. "PenguChi's at the back."

Drew looked at Gigi.

"You going to be there?" he said.

Gigi didn't answer.

"You don't have to be," said Drew. "Nobody'd think anything. You can stay up here. You can be in the banner room, or the cellar, or out in the market. Wendy's on the gate. Wendy'll ask them. They'll answer. They'll come in. It doesn't need you."

"I know it doesn't need me."

"So it's not about whether it needs you," said Drew. "It's about whether you decide to be there."

Gigi turned the bread roll over and over in her hands.

"*If the gate's shut*," she said slowly. "That's Nell's. *You have to decide. Every time.*"

"Even if you don't know what they are," said Drew.

"Especially then," said Gigi.

Drew didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He'd said it all to her in June, on the north steps, in the dark. *It works both ways. It's about people you used to know, too. Especially then.*

"I'll be on the steps," said Gigi.

Drew raised an eyebrow.

"Not at the front," said Gigi. "Not in the gate. Not in her way. Behind Wendy, up on the steps, where she can see me." She looked at the roll. "Where she can see me, and walk past me if she wants. So she gets to choose. Whether to stop. I'm not going to make her stop. I'm just going to be somewhere she can."

Drew looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at people at the gate when he'd decided.

"That's a good place," he said.

"It'll fall down."

"Probably," said Drew, and handed her another roll, because she'd crushed the first one.

***

She went down at the first dawn to wash her face, which you didn't need to do in the game. She did it anyway, at the pump in the courtyard behind the Hall, because it was a thing to do with her hands.

When she came back in, Moonlight Harvest was at the counter.

All nine of them, with their packs on, the way they were most mornings now, about to go down the hatch. Kei had made a list. Every morning a different few went down into the seven roads under the roads, to level and learn the tunnels and learn each other. Today the list said MH. They were standing at the counter while Ama Corrie wrote them down in her small hand. *Down at first dawn*, she wrote, *the south-east road*, and their names, so somebody would know where they were. Ama Corrie wrote everyone down. She said a Hall that didn't know where its people had gone wasn't a Hall, it was a building.

Ice was at the front, with his shield on his back. He turned round when Gigi came in.

"Morning, Gigi," he said.

"Morning."

He looked at her face. Whatever he saw in it, he didn't say anything about it. He'd always been like that, Gigi remembered. In all the years they'd been in each other's chats, before the game, he'd been the one who noticed and didn't say.

"They're coming," said Gigi. She didn't know why she said it. Everybody knew. Hyvred had told the whole Hall, at a volume that made the dome ring.

"I know," said Ice.

"Are you going to be there? At the gate?"

"No," said Ice. "We're going under. South-east road. Kei's list."

Gigi looked at him. At the pack, and the shield, and the nine people behind him, all looking somewhere else very carefully, the way people did when they were pretending not to listen.

"On purpose," said Gigi.

Ice was quiet for a moment.

"It's not mine," he said. Simply, the way he said most things. "It's hers. And yours." He hitched the shield higher on his back. "I'll see them tonight. When we come up."

"PenguChi's going to kick you."

"Eventually," said Ice. And something in his face nearly smiled. Then he turned round and went to the hatch under the stair, with its seven spoke-lines round a ring. Bee was waiting there with the banner to open it. The nine went down one by one into the light under the floor, and the hatch closed behind them.

Gigi stood in the middle of the Hall and looked at the place where they'd been.

*It's not mine. It's hers. And yours.*

She hadn't expected that. She didn't know what she'd expected. That he'd be there, maybe, in the gate, at the front, the way he was always at the front, between them. Making it easier, or making it about him without meaning to. Instead he'd looked at it, and decided it wasn't his, and gone underground for the day so it couldn't be.

She thought, not for the first time, that she'd never really known him at all. She'd known a name in a chat. Somebody who raided and gifted and said *hi* at three in the morning. She hadn't known this.

"Sit or stand, girl," said Ama Corrie from behind the counter, without looking up from the book. "Don't hover. You're making the floor nervous."

Gigi stood.

She stood for about a minute. Then, because standing in the middle of a floor was apparently worse than hovering, she went over to the counter.

Ama Corrie was writing in the Roll. Not names; there were no new names today, not yet. She was writing in the back pages, where she kept the things that weren't names. The weather, and who'd gone down the hatch, and that the east lamp chain had stuck again on Thursday. She wrote everything.

"The tall one," said Ama Corrie, without looking up. "With the shield. On the hill last night. That's her."

It wasn't a question. Gigi answered it anyway.

"Yes."

"Hm." Ama Corrie dipped her pen. "You've been up and down my stair all night. I heard you. Three times."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry," said Ama Corrie. "Be useful or be still. One or the other." She wrote a line. "You polished those chairs."

Gigi hadn't known anyone had noticed.

"At about two," she said. "When Higu fell asleep."

"I know. I'd done them on Saturday. You did them again." Ama Corrie didn't sound displeased. "Beeswax. Good. Birch is no good for under them, it catches. I've been saying it for forty years and nobody listens."

Gigi looked at her hands on the counter.

"What if they don't sit in them?" she said. She didn't know why she was asking an old woman in a game, at a counter, at eight in the morning. She asked anyway.

Ama Corrie put down her pen. She looked at Gigi over her spectacles for a long time, the way she looked at everyone who came to her counter. As if she were reading the line they'd write, before they wrote it.

"You can't make anybody sit in a chair, girl," she said. "Nobody can. Not me, not Kei, not you. All you can do is keep it fit to be sat in. Clean. Swept. Out where they can see it. And then it's theirs. Whether they sit or not." She picked the pen up again. "If they don't, you've kept a chair. There's worse things to have done with a morning."

She went back to her writing. Gigi stood at the counter for a while longer. Then she went and got a cloth from the cupboard under the stair and wiped down the long table in the banner room, all of it, end to end, though it didn't need it. It was something to do with her hands. And it was useful, a bit. Ama Corrie had said one or the other.

***

At eleven Hyvred's voice came down the stair, the whole height of the dome, so loud that the pigeons in the eye went up all at once.

"*TWO MILES. NORTH-WEST ROAD. THEY'VE COME ROUND TO THE SOUTH.*"

Everybody in the Hall stopped what they were doing.

Of course they'd come round to the south. Everybody came through the gate. Ice had walked nineteen people round to the south road in July so they'd come in the right way, because Rafi was on the gate. *Everybody comes through the gate. Every time.* It was the only rule Kei had ever made that nobody argued with.

Gigi went out through the south doors into the sunlight.

The south steps of the Hall of Roads were wide and shallow and old, worn into dips in the middle by three hundred years of feet. At the top of them, by the doors, there was the gate. It wasn't a gate at all, really, just a place. Wendy stood in it with her hands in her pockets. Kite stood at her left with his bow. Rafi stood at her right with the big two-handed sword on his back that still kept time a little. Bram and Joy were a step down, Bram with his spear, Joy with her round shield. Tobi was on the bottom step with his crossbow across his knees. And Drew, a little apart, on the edge, the way he always stood, where he could see everybody's feet.

And there were other people on the steps. A lot of them. More than usual for a Monday noon. Gigi saw Maigo and Salty and Hako. She saw Niko and Newish and Winter sitting in a row like people at a cricket match. She saw three young players she didn't know, very muddy, with a bad sword and a worse bow, sitting right at the side. They'd come in the day before and told everyone in the Hart they'd been rescued on the moor by AltheaVT and a sheep. Kei was there, near the doors, with his map rolled under his arm. Bee was halfway up the steps, drawing. Hyvred had come all the way down from the eye and was standing in the shade of the south door with her tail very still.

Higu wasn't there.

Gigi looked for her, and she wasn't there, and Gigi understood. Higu was up on the roof. On purpose. Not in the middle.

Gigi went down the steps, past Kei, past Bee, to a place a little way behind Wendy and a little way up. Not in the gate. Not in the way. On a step, at the side, in the sun. A place you could see from the road. A place you could walk past.

She stood there.

Her hands were shaking. She put them behind her back.

"Breathe," said Drew, from the edge, very quietly, not looking at her.

She breathed.

Down at the bottom of the steps, the south road came into the round market in a long curve between the stalls and the fountain. And round the curve, slowly, the way everything about them was slow, came six people and a sheep.
