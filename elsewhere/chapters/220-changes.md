There were no pigeons in Masmark. There were gulls.

Gulls were worse.

Kestrel stood on the terrace outside VANTA's plain black Hall on the east rim at the first dawn, with her bow, and a gull on the parapet four paces off looking at her with one yellow eye. Fenn stood beside her with his bow, and the gull looked at him too. It had been looking at both of them for twenty minutes. It had not moved once.

"Again," said Kestrel.

Fenn drew. Held. Loosed.

The arrow went past the gull's left wing by a hand's width and out over the lake. The gull didn't even turn its head.

"You decided," said Kestrel.

"I didn't—"

"You decided it would go left. Before you loosed." She didn't take her eyes off the gull. "You decided at the top of the draw, while you were holding it. I saw your shoulder. It knew where it was going before the arrow did." She drew her own bow, slowly, all the way, and didn't hold it at all. She just let it go, at the top, the moment it got there. "Don't decide. Look. See where it *is*."

Her arrow went into the parapet a finger's width from the gull's foot.

The gull looked at the arrow. Then at her. Then it turned round with enormous dignity and walked off along the parapet, in no hurry at all.

Fenn stared at her.

"How long did that take you?" he said.

"Two weeks," said Kestrel. "And a pigeon."

***

She'd become a teacher. She hadn't meant to.

She'd taught people for six years. That was what VANTA was: twenty people she'd made good. But she'd taught them the way she'd been taught, a long time ago. Do it again. Do it better. Do it until you don't miss. She'd been good at it. Everybody in VANTA was very good.

Then a man with two blades and a sketchbook had stood in a ring in a market at first dawn and taught her to stop deciding. And now she couldn't teach any other way.

She'd started with Fenn in April, after the Crown Below. Every first dawn on the terrace with the gulls. Then Ottie had asked, which surprised her. Then Ren. Then, last week, Brann, who didn't ask questions. He'd just come out onto the terrace at first dawn with his tower shield and stood at the end of the row and waited. She hadn't said anything. She'd just started.

Six of them, now. Every morning. On a terrace, with the gulls.

*Decide later.*

***

She'd been thinking about the cellar for six weeks.

She hadn't told anybody what she'd seen. She'd written it down: three pages, very small, the timings to the half-second. And she'd given the pages to Xav at the netloft door the next morning without a word, because it was his fight, and his homework. And then gone back to the east rim and stood on the terrace for an hour, looking at the lake, not seeing it.

*He said BEFORE.*

Nine months. She'd watched him for nine months. Every VOD, every clip, every second of footage anybody had ever posted. She'd known where he stood in a room. She'd known how long his back foot waited. She'd known the angle he held his shield at against a caster and the angle against a blade. She'd known him better than she knew anybody in VANTA.

She'd known half of him.

Less than half. She'd known the half he'd learned in a fen. The waiting half. And there was another one, from *before*. One that didn't wait at all, that came forward into every gap and kept coming, and had never once been on a stream. Not in nine months. Not in eighteen.

She'd lost to the waiting half on an oak floor in eleven minutes.

She'd never even seen the other one.

Sometimes, on the terrace, with the gulls, she laughed out loud about it. Brann had stopped asking why.

***

The notice went up on the Tuesday, the twentieth of June, on the wall of the Rim Hall. The way the last one had.

[ The Bell Above · Masmark · the island's tower · a hall for twenty · Lv 56 · Opens at the tenth dusk ]

Half of Masmark was in the Rim Hall by the second dawn. Kestrel came with Brann and stood at the back. She didn't need to see it. Everybody had known for a month. You could see the bell from every terrace in the city, green against the sky at the top of the tower. *Up, this time. Not down.*

The Rim Hall was loud. Forty counters, four hundred and nineteen guilds, and all of them shouting. Meridian in deep blue by the west wall. The Open Hand in dark red under their banner by the doors, two hundred of them, their leader talking to the room in his big voice about how they'd be first this time. *First this time.* He'd said it about the Crown Below too, and fourteen of his twenty had fallen in the Long Gallery.

No Sable Court. She looked for them. Black, with silver at the cuffs. Not one.

And at the side of the hall, by the oldest counter, out of the way: Constellation. Twenty of them. No banner. The big one with the black sword. The woman with the gold horns. And him, beside her, not saying anything.

He saw her across the Rim Hall. Of course he did.

She went over. She didn't decide to. She just went.

"Next door," said Ice.

"Next door," said Kestrel. She looked up at him. "You'll be slow."

"Good," said Ice.

She stared at him.

"That's *mine*," said Kestrel. "I said that. In a letter. *You'll be slow. Good.* That's my line."

"I borrow things," said Ice.

Beside him, the woman with the gold horns made a noise like a kettle and hit him on the arm.

***

"Sable Court aren't racing," said Brann, on the walk back along the east rim.

Kestrel looked at him.

"Lucan posted," said Brann. "On the forum. An hour ago." He held up his window so she could read it. Two words.

[ Lucan · Sable Court
We're training. ]

Kestrel read it twice.

She knew what for. Everybody did. A man who'd stood on a bridge over the third stream in March and said *fight me properly*. Then in May, on the same bridge, *not yet*. *I heard about a cellar.* And now a whole guild, twenty of the best in the world, the first to fifty, the ones who'd opened Dawnmere, not going up the tower. Training.

For one fight. On a bridge.

"He's frightened," said Kestrel.

Brann thought about it.

"No," he said. "He's *ready* to be frightened. That's different." He put his window away. "That's what you looked like. In September. Before the oak floor."

Kestrel didn't say anything for a long time.

"I wasn't ready," she said.

"No," said Brann. "But you looked it."

***

She went out to the island on the Wednesday, in VANTA's grey boat, to look at the tower.

Everybody did. The island was crowded with people walking round the bottom of the tower, looking up. Meridian had a barge moored on the west side and twenty people on the rocks with paper, drawing. The Open Hand's great red ship was anchored out in the deep water, the way it had to be, sending boats.

The tower went up from the middle of the island. Grey stone, very old, very plain. No windows, except right at the top, where the belfry was, open on all four sides to the wind. And in the belfry, the bell. Green with age. Huge, even from down here. It had rung once, people said, when the first road came over the rim. Before there was a city. And never since.

The door was at the bottom, above the waterline this time. On the north side. A plain door, iron-banded, shut. It would stay shut until the tenth dusk.

She didn't look at the door.

She walked round the tower slowly, the way she'd walked round the island in March, looking. Not deciding. Holding the whole thing in her head. The stone and the wind and the water and the people. The bell at the top, green against the sky.

And on the third time round, on the north side, beside the door, she stopped.

Because she could hear something.

***

She put her ear against the stone.

Inside the tower, very faint, through walls a yard thick: bells.

Not the great one. Small ones. A lot of them. Ringing, one after another, high and low, in a long falling line. And then again, the same line. And again. And then, on the fourth time round, something changed. Two of the bells swapped places. The line was the same except for those two, and then the next time round two different ones swapped. And the next.

She stood with her ear against the tower for an hour.

Brann stood behind her and didn't ask.

She didn't decide anything. She just listened. And after an hour, she'd heard it go all the way round, every bell swapping with every other bell, one pair at a time. Never the same line twice. Until, at the very end, it came back to where it had started. The long falling line, high to low.

And began again.

She took her ear off the stone.

"Brann," she said.

"Mm."

"Listen."

Brann put his ear to the tower. He listened for a long time. His face didn't do anything. Brann's face never did anything. Then he took his ear away and looked at her.

"Changes," said Brann.

"What?"

"Changes," said Brann. "Bell-ringing. My gran's church, when I was small. They had eight bells and eight people on ropes. And they'd ring them round, high to low. Then every time round, two bells swap places. All the way through, until every order's been rung, and it comes back to the start." He looked at the tower. "It's called a method. There's a name for every one. My gran knew them all. She'd stand outside the church on a Sunday and tell you which one they were ringing from the first two changes."

Kestrel stared at him.

"You never told me you had a gran," she said.

"You never asked," said Brann.

***

It was a method. It was a tempo.

She sat on the rocks on the north side of the island with her back against the tower and the bells going inside it, very faint. She wrote it down, the way she'd written the cellar. The order of the bells, every change, to the half-second. Brann sat beside her and said which bell was which. *That's the treble. That's the tenor. That one's the fourth.*

She didn't know what it was for. Not yet. But she knew, the way she'd known about the steps in March, that it was *for* something. That a tower with a bell at the top that hadn't rung in a thousand years didn't have small bells ringing changes inside it for no reason. That when the door opened at the tenth dusk, whatever was inside would move on the bells.

*It's like the cellar*, she thought. *It's a tempo. And if you know it, you know what's coming.*

She looked across the island at the west side, where Meridian's barge was moored. Twenty people on the rocks with paper, drawing the tower. Drawing the stone, the door, the belfry, the wind. Not listening.

And at their front, very small, in plain blue with no gold on her anywhere, a woman sitting on a rock with a board on her knees. Looking up at the bell.

***

Kestrel thought about it for a long time.

She thought about being second four times. About the Elm Below, and the Saltwell, and the drowned church, and the first-rank hall under the round town's stair. About a post she'd made in the night, once, after the Elm Below: *I'd have rested. That's the annoying part.* About the Crown Below, and being first, and a man at the bottom of a lake reading the window and saying *good for them* and meaning it.

She thought about a woman who'd been in a guild for six years, two games, ten thousand people. Never first. Not once. Not at anything.

She got up.

"Where are you going?" said Brann.

"I don't know," said Kestrel. "I haven't decided."

She walked round the island to the west side. Past the door, past the people walking round the tower looking up. To the rocks where Meridian's twenty sat drawing. Where the woman in plain blue sat on her own with a board on her knees, looking at the bell.

Aurelie looked up when Kestrel's shadow fell across the board.

"VANTA," she said.

"Meridian."

They'd never spoken. Kestrel had seen her at the Crown Below, at the front of Meridian's twenty, nodding across the shingle at him. She'd seen her at the Rim Hall. She'd heard about her for a year. The woman who ran ten thousand people and was too small for every chair in her own castle.

"I was second four times," said Kestrel.

"I know," said Aurelie. "I watched."

"You've never been first."

Aurelie's face did something. Not much. A tightening round the eyes, the way Kestrel's own face used to do when somebody said *second*.

"No," said Aurelie.

Kestrel looked at the tower. The plain grey stone, going up. The bell at the top.

"Put your ear on the wall," she said. "On the north side. By the door. For an hour. Don't decide anything. Just listen." She paused. "Take somebody who knows about churches."

Aurelie looked at her for a long time.

"Why are you telling me?"

Kestrel thought about it. She didn't decide. She let it come.

"Because somebody told me to decide later," she said. "And somebody else told me I was first because I was there. Not because I was trying." She looked at Aurelie. "You've been trying for six years. I'd like to see what you look like when you're there."

Aurelie didn't say anything.

Kestrel turned round and walked back round the island to Brann.

***

"You told her," said Brann, in the boat, going back.

"Yes."

"Meridian."

"Yes."

"Not him," said Brann. "Not Constellation."

Kestrel looked across the water at the north-west harbour, where an old netloft sat at the end of a lane. With a girl with two different eyes in its door, and a banner with a star on its hooks.

"He doesn't need it," she said. "He'll hear it himself. Or one of his will. They always do." She thought about the cellar, and Fenn, and the gulls. "Somebody in that netloft has ears. I don't know who. But somebody heard the cellar better than I did. I could tell from Xav's face the next morning. He had a page in his hand that wasn't his, and he was holding it like a jar."

Brann rowed for a while.

"And us?" he said.

Kestrel looked at the tower.

"We rest first," she said. "At the first kept place. Whatever the board says. Whoever's ahead." She looked at him. "I said that in March. And we were first. I'm not going to stop doing it the time it makes us third."

"Third?"

"I don't know," said Kestrel. "I haven't decided." She smiled. It wasn't something she did much either. "But I'd rather be third and rested than first and *not there*."

Brann looked at her for a long time over the oars.

"Xav," he said.

"Xav," agreed Kestrel. "And a pigeon. And a gull."

***

She taught them the changes on the terrace for nine days. With handbells.

She bought them from a shop in the Old Market that sold everything, twelve small brass bells with leather handles. She stood VANTA on the terrace at first dawn in a row of twelve with a bell each. Brann said which was the treble and which was the tenor, and they rang. Rounds first, high to low. Then the changes, the ones she'd written down from the wall, one pair swapping at a time. Badly, at first. Ottie couldn't keep time at all. Ren kept ringing early.

Fenn could. Fenn rang the fourth bell, and never once rang it early or late. He did it with his eyes on the lake, not on the paper, the way he looked at everything. As if he'd always been able to hear where things were going to be.

"How are you doing that?" said Ottie, on the third day, furious.

Fenn looked at her, surprised.

"I'm not deciding," he said. "I'm just listening to the one before."

Kestrel didn't say anything. But she thought about it for the rest of the day.

On the ninth day they rang the whole method from the wall, start to finish, with only three mistakes. Brann said his gran would have said *hm*. The gull on the parapet watched the whole thing with one yellow eye and, at the end, very slowly, walked away.

***

The tenth dusk came on Friday, the thirtieth of June.

Every boat on the lake, again. She'd thought it would feel different, the second time. It didn't. It was the same. Hundreds of boats coming off the harbours all the way round the city as the light went, every colour there was. Meridian's three blue barges with ten rowers each. The Open Hand's great red ship anchored out, sending boats. VANTA's two grey ones. A fishing boat from the fish man, very low in the water, with a big one with a black sword rowing.

No black boats. No Sable Court. She looked for them, on the water, out of habit, and then found them, by accident, on the shore. On the north terrace. On the bridge over the third stream, where the water went under it white. Twenty of them in black, standing on the bridge, not moving. Watching the island.

And at their front, one who was moving. All the time. Like water over stones.

The island was full. Thousands of people on the rocks round the bottom of the tower. The door on the north side, iron-banded, shut. And above it, at the top of the tower, the bell, green, against a sky going from gold to dark.

She stood at the front of VANTA, twenty paces from the door. Meridian to her left, in deep blue. Aurelie at their front, very small, not looking at anybody. Looking at the door. The Open Hand to her right, two hundred in dark red, their leader in front, still talking. And somewhere behind her, by the water, twenty with no banner. A big one with a black sword. A woman with gold horns. A woman with a wand, small, with dark red hair, who'd sat on the rocks on the north side yesterday with her ear against the tower for three hours and not said a word to anybody.

Kestrel had seen her. She hadn't said anything either.

The last of the light went off the bell.

[ The Bell Above · Masmark · the island's tower · a hall for twenty · Lv 56 · **Open** ]

The door opened.

***

Inside, a stair. Going up. Round the inside of the tower wall, very steep, very worn, into the dark. And in the middle of the tower, a hollow, going up and up, as far as she could see. Hanging down through the hollow, from somewhere a long way above, ropes. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Swaying very slightly in an air she couldn't feel.

And the bells. Louder now. Inside the walls, everywhere, above her and below. Ringing changes.

Meridian went in first. Of course they did; they'd been closest. Twenty in deep blue, up the stair into the dark, and the door closed behind them, and opened again. Every party into its own way.

Then VANTA.

Kestrel stood on the bottom step with her bow on her back and VANTA behind her. Brann. Ottie. Mags. Ren. Fenn, with his eyes up. She stood there for a moment, and didn't decide anything. She just listened. To the bells. To the change coming. The treble and the fourth, swapping, about to.

"Rest first," she said, without turning round. "At the first kept place. Whatever the board says."

"Whatever the board says," said Brann.

The change came. She heard it. The step under her foot moved, very slightly, on the beat. As if the tower were breathing.

*Don't decide*, thought Kestrel. *Listen to the one before.*

She went up.
