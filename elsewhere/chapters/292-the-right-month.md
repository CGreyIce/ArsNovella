Gigi had grown a hedge out of the still water in November to keep a gap closed, and in May she went back to see what it had done with itself.

She'd said so at the long table on the last night of April. In the middle of everything else. Ice had just stood up and said *she's very loud and I love it*, and the banner room had gone up twice. Then, when it had come down, Gigi had said, to nobody in particular, *I'm going north tomorrow*.

Nobody had heard her. Except Thea.

"The thorn," said Thea, beside her. Not a question.

"It'll flower in May," said Gigi. "Kotori said. In the right month. For the first time." She'd looked at her hands on the table. "I want to see it."

"I'll come," said Thea.

Gigi had looked at her. Close. She'd been practising close since the twenty-second stone in November. She was still bad at it, sometimes. Not at this.

"It's two weeks," said Gigi. "There and two back."

"I know."

"You'll miss the fight. Xav and Suzu."

"Xola will write," said Thea. "Every day. In capitals." She almost smiled. "I'd rather see the thorn."

***

They went out through the north door on the first of May at the third dawn, the two of them.

Kei had said *threes at least* in the winter. But it was spring, and the middle was lit, and the dark knew where it was. And Kei had looked at the two of them standing in front of the board in the banner room for a long time. Then he'd crossed out *threes at least* on the north road's corner of his map and written, in pencil, *twos, if it's them*.

Ama Corrie had stood in the north door with her broom as they went out.

"You're slow," she'd said, to Gigi. She'd never said it to Gigi before.

"We're walking," said Gigi.

"*Hm*," said Ama Corrie. Then, as they went down the north steps: "Bring me a flower."

Gigi had stopped on the bottom step and looked back up at her.

Ama Corrie had already gone in.

***

The north road was easy. Ninety-four flames standing, a mile apart, the moor green all round them and starting to flower. Purple, low, in the heather. Gigi knew all their names. She'd started the game in a hedge town called Briarwick, where they taught you the name of everything that grew before they let you touch anything that cut.

She told Thea the names as they walked. She didn't mean to. They just came out.

*Ling. Bell heather. Bog asphodel, that yellow one, don't eat it. Tormentil.* Thea listened. She didn't say anything, mostly. Every so often she'd repeat one, softly, as if she were putting it somewhere. *Tormentil.*

"You never talk this much," said Thea, on the third day.

"I'm not talking. I'm naming."

"It's the same."

Gigi thought about it.

"In Briarwick," she said, "they said you can't keep a hedge you can't name. If you don't know what's in it, you don't know what it's for." She looked at the heather. "So I learned everything. In the first week. In here."

"And now?"

"Now I'm not keeping anything out," said Gigi. "So I'm just naming it." She was quiet. "It's nicer."

***

They slept at the old hall on the fourth night, on the bench. The same one. In the middle of it, together, with their backs against the wall, under the ghost of the star.

It was the first time they'd been alone together in the game for longer than an evening.

Gigi realised it on the bench, in the dark, with Thea's shoulder against hers. Two years and a half in here. A year and a half of near, a pace to the left, at gates and gaps. Then close, since November. But never alone. Always in a ring of nine, or a hall of twenty, or at a long table of sixty-two. Always somebody else to look at, if looking at each other got too much.

There was nobody else here. Just the old hall, and the bench, and the star that wasn't there.

"It's very quiet," said Thea.

"Yes."

"Is it all right?"

Gigi thought about it. Properly. She'd started doing that. Everybody had.

"Yes," she said. "I thought it'd be frightening."

"Is it?"

"No," said Gigi. "It's just quiet."

Thea put her head on Gigi's shoulder, the way she'd done on this bench in January. Gigi sat very still and let her, and didn't decide anything, and after a while they were both asleep.

***

The twenty-two. Thea's lantern at the end, standing straight. Thea touched the stone below it, not the glass, and didn't say anything.

Then the edge, and the dark country.

It was green.

Gigi had heard. PenguChi had told the long table in April, in her slow calm way, about the shoots in the heather past the edge. And Patience eating the first one, and the whole dark country coming up green from the fourth day. Gigi had believed her. PenguChi didn't say things that weren't true; it was effort.

She hadn't imagined it like this.

Not a few shoots. Everything. The heather that had never once flowered in three hundred years, the old woman said, was flowering. Low and dark and purple, under a dark sky with edges, all the way to the horizon. In the light of the candles, as they walked, it came up out of the dark in front of them like something waking.

Gigi stopped on the heather and couldn't go on for a while.

Thea stopped with her.

"Name it," said Thea.

Gigi looked at it. She knew it. Of course she knew it; she'd known it since the first week, in Briarwick. But she'd never seen it here. In the dark. Past the edge. Where nothing had grown for three hundred years until a girl from a hedge town said *no* to a gap in the stones.

"Ling," said Gigi. Her voice went. "It's just ling. It's the commonest thing in the world."

"Not here," said Thea.

***

Nine days north. Then the still water, a black mirror with the hill's light laid across it like a road. Then the stones.

They counted them. Of course they did. Out loud, the two of them, one after another, the way the nine had and the five had. *One.* The first, sunk a hand deep, very old. *Two.* So the stones would know whose they were.

At a hundred and eighty, there was a cup.

On the stone. Small, tin, one of Kotori's. Gigi stopped and looked at it. It was empty, and dry, and set very carefully on the edge of the hundred and eightieth stone. Beside it, the water was perfectly still.

"Kotori," said Thea.

"In January," said Gigi. "On the way home. Slep told me. She poured a cup into the water where the shape went down. *So it knows somebody was kind to it.*" She looked at the cup. "She left the cup."

They didn't touch it. They said *a hundred and eighty* to the water, both of them, quietly, and went on.

***

At two hundred and sixteen, Gigi stopped. She couldn't go on.

The hawthorn was white.

All of it. Every black stem, every twig, every one of the small bright leaves from March. Covered. Thick white blossom from the water to the top, so much of it that the knot of hawthorn didn't look like a step any more. It looked like a cloud that had come down onto the still water and decided to stay. In the gold light off the hill it shone. You could smell it from the two hundred and tenth stone, sweet and heavy, the way a hedge smells in May anywhere in the world.

In the right month.

For the first time.

Gigi stood on the two hundred and sixteenth stone and looked at it.

She'd grown it in November with her hands, kneeling on this stone in the nothing. Suzu had said *no* to Ice when Ice wanted to jump the gap, and Gigi had put her hands in the black water and asked the thorn to come. It had come. Black and twisted and thick as a man's body, a step on from the stone that should have been there, from the spring that wasn't. She'd made it to keep a gap closed. That was what she'd always made thorn for. To keep something out.

In December it had flowered. One white blossom, alone, in the dark, in the wrong month. *It's a spring*, she'd said, kneeling by it. *Just late. Three hundred years late.*

In January it had set fruit. A haw, small and hard and green.

Now this.

"I didn't know it could do this," said Gigi. Very quietly.

"You grew it," said Thea.

"I grew it to close a gap." Gigi was crying. She didn't wipe it. "I've only ever grown things to close gaps. Since the first week. A hedge is a wall that's alive, they said in Briarwick. That's what it's for. To keep things out." She looked at the white, all of it, shining. "I didn't know you could grow a wall and have it *flower*."

Thea didn't say anything.

She took Gigi's hand. On the two hundred and sixteenth stone, in the still water, with the hawthorn in front of them white from top to bottom. She held it.

"You can," said Thea. "Look."

***

They crossed it.

Gigi first. It held. It had held nine, and five and a stone, and it held her. The blossom came up round her ankles and her knees as she stepped onto it, soft. White petals came loose and fell onto the black water and floated there, perfectly still, not sinking. A ring of white round the hawthorn on the still water, growing as they crossed.

"Two hundred and seventeen," said Gigi. Her voice was steady, for once.

Thea came after her. "Two hundred and seventeen."

On the far side, on the two hundred and eighteenth, Gigi turned round and knelt down. She put her hand on the black stems at the edge, under the blossom. Not asking it anything, this time. Just touching it. The way Isa touched the jar.

[ *a step, grown* · *in flower* ]

[ *It holds what it's given. It was given more than a gap.* ]

She looked at the window for a long time.

Then, very carefully, she broke off one small sprig of blossom. White, three flowers and a leaf. She put it inside her coat, against her chest, where it wouldn't crush.

"For Ama Corrie," said Gigi.

"She asked?"

"She said *bring me a flower*," said Gigi. "On the north steps. And then went in before I could answer." She stood up. "She's never once asked me for anything."

***

The hill. Three hundred and one stones, the last one flat and grey and new in the stride of water before the shore, a hand above it. *Right size*, Salty had said. Gigi stepped on it and it didn't move at all.

The ring of twelve. The flat stone with the crown. The jar, and the flame over it, standing straight up, small and gold. Isa's bowl. Her blanket, folded. Her stick. Kotori's cup from December, for her mothers, still full. And a second cup, newer, beside the rim, from the spring stone. Gigi knew it from Kotori's telling at the long table: *a tea of the first green*. *For whoever comes next.*

They stood at the flat stone and looked at it.

"That's us," said Thea.

Gigi looked at her.

"Whoever comes next," said Thea. "That's us. We're next."

Gigi hadn't thought of it. She looked at the cup for a long time. Small and tin, with a tea in it made from the first green shoots in the dark country, that PenguChi had seen first and Patience had eaten most of.

They drank it. Half each. Sitting against the third stone of the ring, the one they'd sat against in November, with their shoulders together.

It was very good. It tasted of heather, and of the backs of stones, and of something Gigi couldn't name. She thought she'd been naming things her whole life and had just found one that didn't have a word yet.

Thea put the empty cup back on the rim, beside the three hundred and one notches.

"For whoever comes after us," she said.

***

Gigi walked round the ring at the second dusk, the way she had every night in December. Slowly, touching every stone. Twelve. The twelfth a little newer at the base, where nine people had raised it on a boom.

Between the ninth and the tenth, on the inside of the ring, she stopped.

There was something on the ground.

Small. Pink. A tight little cushion of it, no bigger than her palm, growing out of a crack in the grey stone of the hill, just inside the ring. A tuft of thin green leaves like grass, and above them, on short stalks, round pink heads of flower.

She knelt down and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"What is it?" said Thea, from the fire.

"Thrift," said Gigi.

She knew it. Of course she did. It grew on cliffs, on rocks, on the walls of Briarwick where the hedge met the stone. It grew in places nothing else would. It was the commonest thing in the world, on rocks by the sea.

It had never grown on this hill. In three hundred years. Isa had said. *Stone and black water and heather that never flowered.* Nothing green inside the ring. Ever.

*Nothing comes inside*, the window had said, when nine people raised the twelfth stone in November. And nothing had. Not the dark, not the still water standing up as high as the Hall of Roads on the longest night. The ring kept the dark out.

It hadn't kept this out.

Because this hadn't come from outside, Gigi thought. It had come from inside. From the hill itself, from a crack in the stone, in the light. Three hundred years of nothing, and then a middle that stayed lit, and then, in May, the hill had made something on its own.

She didn't grow anything. She didn't ask it anything. She just knelt beside it in the ring, a girl who'd grown walls her whole time in here, and looked at a thing that had grown without anybody asking.

"Thrift," she said again, to Thea, when she came back to the fire. "Inside the ring. On its own."

Thea looked at her face for a long time.

"Are you going to pick it?" she said.

"No," said Gigi. "It's not mine." She sat down against the third stone. "It's the hill's."

***

Thea sang that night.

Not like in December, the first time since the jetty, against the third stone, with the whole of the nine round the fire. Then, Ice's shoulders had gone down for the first time in a month and Hyv had shut her eyes. This was different. Just the two of them, on a hill in the middle of the dark's own water, in May, with a hawthorn flowering white at two hundred and seventeen and the flame standing over the jar.

She sang low. Gigi didn't know the song. It didn't matter.

She sat against the third stone with Thea's shoulder against hers and listened. She didn't name anything. She didn't decide anything. She just didn't move.

When it was over, Thea was quiet for a long time.

"I sang on the jetty," she said. "A long time ago. In here." She was looking at the flame. "And then I didn't. For a long time." She didn't say why. Gigi didn't ask. It wasn't hers. "And then on this hill in December I did. Once. And I didn't know if I'd do it again."

"You did it again."

"I did," said Thea. She turned her head and looked at Gigi. Close. In the light. "I sang because you were here. Not because of the hill. Because you were here, and it was quiet, and it was all right."

Gigi looked at her.

She'd been practising close for six months. She thought she was getting better at it. She thought this was the closest she'd ever been to anybody in her life.

"Sing it again," said Gigi.

Thea did.

***

They walked home in fifteen days. Down the three hundred and one, counting backwards. Past the hawthorn, white, and the ring of petals on the still water, wider every day. Past Kotori's dry cup on the hundred and eightieth, which they didn't touch. Into the dark country, flowering.

A letter found them at the old hall on the way back. Xola's. In capitals.

*EVEN*

*ONE EACH AND THE THIRD ONE LEVEL*

*SUZU SAT DOWN. IN THE FIGHT. TWICE. AND LAY DOWN ONCE. AND LAUGHED. OUT LOUD. HE LAUGHED, THEA. NOBODY HAS EVER HEARD HIM LAUGH*

*XAV SAT DOWN TOO*

*MALCOLM DIDN'T SAY MARCH*

*SUZU BET ON EVEN AT 40 TO 1 AND GAVE THE MONEY TO RX FOR HIS EARS. I DON'T UNDERSTAND THAT PART EITHER*

*I MISS YOU BOTH. COME HOME. BRING ME A FLOWER. AMA CORRIE SAYS SHE ASKED FIRST*

Thea read it on the bench at the old hall and laughed so much she had to lie down on it.

***

They came in through the north door on the last day of May.

It stuck. It always did. Gigi put her shoulder to it, and it gave. Ama Corrie was at her counter, with the Roll open, the way she always was. She looked up as they came across the Hall. She didn't say *you're slow*.

Gigi went to the counter.

She took the sprig out of her coat. It had been against her chest for fifteen days. It should have been brown. It wasn't. It was still white. Three flowers and a leaf, as fresh as when she'd broken it off on the two hundred and eighteenth stone, smelling of May.

She put it on the counter, beside the Roll.

Ama Corrie looked at it for a long time.

"From two hundred and seventeen," said Gigi. "It flowered. In May. In the right month. All of it."

Ama Corrie didn't say anything.

She picked up the sprig. Very carefully, in her old hands, the way Gigi had picked it. She looked at it for a long time. Then she opened the Roll to a page Gigi had never seen, a long way back. Very old, the ink gone brown. A page from before the old guild went north.

She laid the sprig on it, flat, between the pages. Pressed.

"My mother's grandmother's grandmother," said Ama Corrie, "kept a hedge." She shut the Roll on the sprig, gently. "In a town called Briarwick. Before the war."

Gigi stared at her.

Ama Corrie picked up her broom.

"*Hm*," she said. And went off across the Hall of Roads to sweep something that didn't need sweeping. And didn't look back, which was how Gigi knew she'd been crying.
