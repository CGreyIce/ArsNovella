Chris had forty-two spreadsheets, and on Sunday night Kei asked him for all of them.

Not in those words. Kei didn't ask for things in those words. He asked at the end of the big call, after roll call, after everybody had said *here* from wherever they were. There were sixteen of them on it now, all over a world nobody had a map of. Kei waited until the call had gone quiet the way it did at the end, people drifting, and then said:

"Chris. Can I ask you something?"

"Yes," said Chris, who had a tab for things Kei might ask him, and was already looking at it.

"Your sheet. The one with the purse in it, and everybody's levels, and the roads." A pause. Chris could hear him choosing it. "Can it be ours?"

Chris didn't say anything.

"Not instead of yours," said Kei. "You keep it. You're the only one who could. But everybody could see it. The names on the writ, and who's walking, and from where, and how far, and how long. The purse. All of it." Another pause. "Ama Corrie keeps the Roll. Somebody has to keep everything else. I can't. I'm on a step."

"I'm the person with the sheet," said Chris slowly.

"You've always been the person with the sheet," said Kei. "I'm just asking if it can be the guild's sheet."

Chris sat in the egg in the old cinema in Singapore, with the blue light along the floor and the quiet of forty people being somewhere else. In Ferrowmere he was sitting at the bottom of the main cable tower with Niko and Newish and Winter. And he thought about the first day. The ferry, and the forty-one spreadsheets marked *(not playing)*. And the forty-second, which he'd made at one in the morning and not been able to write anything in. *ELSEWHERE (playing).*

"Yes," he said. "Obviously yes."

That night, after the call, he made the forty-third.

He sat on the cable-tower steps with the sheet open in the air in front of him. He looked at the title bar for a long time. The guild didn't have a name. Kei wouldn't say it. Kei had a word in his head, everyone on the call knew he had a word, and he wouldn't say it until there were twenty. So Chris couldn't call it that.

So he called it the only thing it was.

*ELSEWHERE (ours).*

***

He did it properly. That was the only way he knew how to do anything.

A tab for the writ, with the names in the order they'd signed, and the day, and who'd been on the call when they did. A tab for the roads: every one of them who wasn't in Carrow Cross yet, where they were, how far they had to walk, how fast. A tab for levels. A tab for the purse, now empty, five hundred gold gone into Ama Corrie's iron box. A new column started for what came after. And a tab he didn't name, for the things that had happened. Because he'd learned on the first day that those were the most useful columns he'd ever made.

He filled it in from the call, and from Kei's notebook, which Kei read out to him line by line on Monday night from the steps of the Hall.

*Day 80. Kei, Higu, Gigi sign (1, 2, 3). The room is held. Gigi brought Kite a cup.*

*Day 86. Hyvred, Bee sign (4, 5). Bee brought the banner. Rolled. In Ama Corrie's cupboard. Nobody's seen it unrolled except Hyv, Bee, Wynn and Kei.*

*Day 86. Tobi at the gate. Sits with Kite at the milestone. They don't talk much. Kite taught him to restring a crossbow one-handed.*

*Day 88. Drew, Tiger sign (6, 7). Tiger fell in the fountain in the round market. Ama Corrie: "That's new."*

*Seven of twenty.*

He looked at the roads tab for a long time.

Maigo in Orchard End, eleven days out. Salty in Grimmet, fourteen. Wendy in Ashcombe, twelve. Ina in Emberly, ten, and very cheerful about it. Hako in Otterbank, thirteen. All five walking now, from five towns, on five roads, alone, because the cafés were twelve hours and they couldn't wait any longer. And four in Ferrowmere, eleven days out, waiting for one of them to be fifteen.

Nine on the roads. Two at the gate. Seven signed. Two still to find.

Twenty.

He'd written it out so many times that it had stopped looking like a number.

***

Higu showed them the ceiling on Tuesday.

Not on her stream. She'd said *when it's time*, and it wasn't time. Just on the call, the sixteen of them, late, after roll call. She'd taken photographs of her four sheets, the ones she'd drawn on the floor of the Hall by moonlight, and she held them up one at a time in the air. Higu didn't do speeches. She just read them out, in her calm, low, weather-report voice, while everybody looked.

*Selwick. Three stars in a lake, in a line.* Everybody knew about that one.

*Briarwick. A ring, with the ditches round it.* Gigi said "*Ugh*" very quietly, and everybody knew what that meant.

*Seven stars like a staircase.* Tiger said "That's ME. That's my LOCKS," and Drew said "That's your locks," and Tiger said "*why are my locks on a CEILING.*"

*A crossroads with a square.* Drew didn't say anything at all.

*A hill with a ring of stars round the top, like a fire.* Ina said, "Oh, that's my forge. Hello, forge," in a voice of perfect delight.

And then Higu held up the fourth sheet.

"There's one I don't know," she said. "Down in the south-east. Low. Nine small stars in a ring. Eight of them close together. The ninth set a bit apart, and turned. Facing out." She paused. "I don't know what it is. I don't know anywhere with nine of anything."

Chris looked at it.

He didn't need to look at it twice. He'd looked at it every day for seven weeks, on a stream, and once in a sheet that nobody else would ever read. *Fernwick — anomalies. Nine stones, eight facing in, one facing out.* A ring of warm water in a cold fen. A man who walked out there every morning at moonrise and went dark before he got close.

On the ceiling of a round hall four hundred years old, somebody had painted it in gold. With a silver line running out of it, north, toward a star that wasn't on any map.

"Anyone?" said Higu.

Nobody said anything.

Chris didn't either.

He sat in the egg in Singapore with the blue light along the floor and his heart going quite fast, and didn't say anything at all. It wasn't his. It had never been his. He'd known that on the first day, sitting in the barracks yard in the rain in Ferrowmere with his messages open. *Some data, it turned out, wasn't for anything. You just kept it, because it was somebody else's.*

"Okay," said Higu, after a while. "I'll keep it on the list."

"Put it at the bottom," said Kei. "We'll find out. One day."

"One day," said Higu, and put the sheet away.

He didn't go to sleep. He sat by the window with the dark sea going past and finished the sheet. Then he added one line to the tab with no name, the one for the things that had happened.

*Day 93. Higu showed us the ceiling. The ninth star's on it. I didn't say. Ice said thank you.*

Then he closed it and looked at the water until Batam.

***

They pushed him all week.

That was the only word for it. The three of them stood round him in the Iron Hills like the walls of a well. They fought things four and five and six levels above him, in mines, on cables, on ridges in the rain. Every weeknight, the four hours he had after work and the ferry. Every hour of the twelve he had on Saturday.

Niko in front with the sword and the book, saying *hold* and *still* and *wait* on the cut, so that the thing in front of them froze where the blade went in. Winter behind, with the harpoon, pulling things down out of the air or off the cliffs. Newish on the floor of whatever cart or ledge or mine-tunnel they were in, with the drum between her knees, playing. *Doum. Tek. Tek. Doum.*

And Chris in the middle, listening.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Not the fighting. The listening. He'd spent his whole life thinking in lists. *Back foot. Shoulder. Breathe out. Bring it down.* One, two, three, four, in order, every time, the way you did a sum. And every time Newish played, he had to put the list down and just hear the drum and go. *Doum.* And his body went, on the beat, without him telling it.

Seventy-nine. Eighty-four. Eighty-one. Eighty-eight.

He wrote them all down. He couldn't help that. But he wrote them down *afterwards* now.

[ Level 14 ]

It came on Saturday, the twentieth of February, at four in the afternoon, at the bottom of a flooded mine. A thing with too many eyes had come up out of the black water. Winter's harpoon was in it, Niko's *hold* was in it, and Chris's sword came down on the *doum* at eighty-eight per cent.

Fourteen.

He stood in the black water up to his knees, in the dark, with his sword in his hand and three people round him. He looked at the window for a long time.

"Fourteen," said Newish. "Fourteen, Chris. You were *ten*. Two weeks ago you were *ten*, and I walked to your ugly town—"

"I know."

"—through a *forest*—"

"I know, Newish."

"—with a *bear*—"

"There was a bear," Chris agreed. "It's in the sheet."

***

They set out on Sunday morning, at the game's first dawn, from the bottom of the main cable tower in Ferrowmere. Four of them, south, on the long road toward a round hall where seven roads met.

Eleven days, Kei's sums said. The thirtieth day ended at midnight on Thursday the fourth of March. Eleven days from Sunday the twenty-first was Thursday the fourth.

Chris had done the sum. He'd done it forty times. It came out the same every time. If nothing went wrong, they'd walk into Carrow Cross on the last day. And he'd be fifteen somewhere on the road, or he wouldn't.

"Something'll go wrong," said Winter, beside him.

"I've got a tab for it," said Chris.

"I know you have."

Niko was walking in front, with the sword on his hip and the book under his arm, not hurrying. Newish was behind, with the drum on her back, already talking. She had a great deal to say about the road, which she'd decided was ugly.

And somewhere south of them, on the same road, though none of them knew it yet, a crew in dark coats with little birds stitched on their collars had been watching the roads to the Halls for three weeks. They'd been counting the people who walked them alone.

Chris looked at the roads tab, in the air, as he walked. Nine names on nine roads, all moving the same way, slowly, toward one dot. At the top of the sheet, the title.

*ELSEWHERE (ours).*

He didn't close it. He walked with it open in front of him all morning, like a lantern.
