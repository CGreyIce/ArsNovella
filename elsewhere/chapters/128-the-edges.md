Bee drew things. That was what she was for. She'd decided it a long time ago, and she'd never once changed her mind.

She drew the way other people talked. She drew what she saw, and she drew it true, and she didn't draw things she didn't see. Ambry the sign-painter in Heatherbrook had said it best, in November, with a brush behind her ear. *Draw it true, draw it whole, say what it's for.*

On Thursday night she sat at her desk in Australia until three in the morning drawing something she'd never seen. She drew it anyway, because it was for Hyv.

***

At eleven o'clock on Friday morning, Bee's time, nine o'clock Hyv's, the phone rang.

Bee answered it on the first ring. She'd been holding it for twenty minutes.

Hyv's face came up on the screen. Close. Too close, the way it always was, because Hyv held the phone close to see it. Her real face, the one Bee had known for years, not the one with the horns. Pale, a bit. A small dressing behind her right ear. And a pair of glasses Bee had never seen before, thin and black, with a tiny round camera in the bridge between the lenses, like a third eye.

"Hi," said Hyv.

"Hi."

"They're about to do it."

"I know. You said nine."

"You're the first thing," said Hyv. "I said you'd be the first thing. So I've got to hold you up." Her voice was doing something. "I don't know where to hold you. They said hold it at arm's length. I've never held a phone at arm's length in my life, Bee. What's the *point*, I can't see it at arm's length—"

"Hold it at arm's length," said Bee.

Hyv held the phone out. Bee watched her own view swing away, and steady. Now she could see more of Hyv: her face, her shoulders, the edge of a hospital chair. Somebody's arm in a white sleeve, at the edge of the screen, holding something. A tablet.

A voice said, "Ready?"

"Yes," said Hyv. Then, to the phone, to Bee, quietly: "Are you there?"

"I'm here," said Bee.

***

She'd known Hyv for a long time. Long enough to know what her face did when she was lying and when she wasn't. When she was scared and pretending not to be. When she was so happy she couldn't stand it and was trying to stand it anyway. Bee had drawn all of those faces. She had a book of them. Hyv didn't know.

She'd never seen this one.

It started small. Nothing happened, and nothing happened, and Hyv's face stayed exactly the same, pointed at the phone, at Bee, the way it always was. Bee thought, for a second, *it hasn't worked*, and her stomach went somewhere she didn't like.

Then Hyv's eyes moved.

Not toward Bee. Away. To the side. Out of the middle of her face toward the edge of it, slowly, and then the other way. To the other edge. Then up. Then down. As if somebody had put something at the corners of the world and she'd only just noticed it was there.

"Oh," said Hyv.

Bee didn't say anything. She didn't trust herself to.

"Bee," said Hyv. Her eyes kept moving. Edge to edge. "Bee, there's a— the *phone*. I can see the *phone*. I can see the edge of the *phone*. I can see your— there's a *wall* behind you. With *things* on it."

Bee looked round. There was a wall behind her. It had always been there.

"There's always been a wall behind me," said Bee.

"I never *saw* it." Hyv's voice cracked in the middle. "Years of calls and I never saw your *wall*. I saw your face. Just your face, in the middle. In a circle. And now there's a *wall*, and *things* on it, and a *corner*, and your *ceiling*—"

"Don't say anything about my ceiling."

"Your ceiling is *ridiculous*, Bee."

"I said don't."

"I can see *all of it*," said Hyv. And then her eyes went further, past the phone, to the side, and stopped.

Bee couldn't see what she was looking at. She didn't need to. At the very edge of the screen she could make out a sleeve, and a shoulder, and the back of a chair. Hyv's family, who'd been standing at the side of the room the whole time, out of the middle, where they'd always had to stand to be seen.

"You're all there," said Hyv. Very quietly. Not to Bee. "I can see you all. At once. Without turning round."

Then she stopped trying to stand it, and cried.

***

It wasn't perfect. Bee could tell. The doctor said so, at the edge of the screen, kindly. *It'll be blurry. The edges especially. It takes the brain weeks. Months.* And Hyv's eyes kept moving out to the edges and squinting there, as if the edges were very far away and very faint, the way stars were when you looked at them sideways.

But they were there. Hyv was looking at them. For the first time in her life, outside a game, there was something at the edges to look at.

"The window," said Hyv suddenly. "Suzu's mum said. Look at a window." She turned her head, slowly, away from the phone. Bee watched her face go toward a window Bee couldn't see, somewhere in the hospital room. "Bee. There's a *window*. It's huge. It's— I can see the *whole* window. All four corners." Her voice went very small. "I can see the sky round the building across the road. Not just the building. Round it. There's sky *round* it."

Bee sat at her desk in Australia and didn't say anything. She'd put her hand over her mouth at some point. She wasn't sure when.

She didn't cry. She didn't, as a rule. She'd decided a long time ago that somebody in any room should be the one who didn't, and it was usually her.

She cried a bit, quietly, behind her hand, where the camera couldn't see.

***

"You drew me something," said Hyv, when she'd turned back to the phone. "You said. On Monday. You said I'd see on Friday."

"I did."

"Show me."

Bee picked it up off the desk.

She'd drawn it on Thursday night, till three in the morning. On a big sheet, the biggest she had, in ink and then in colour, slowly, with an eyelash brush for the small things, the way she'd painted the banner.

It was a drawing of nothing in the middle.

That was the point of it. The middle of the page was almost blank. Just a pale wash, soft, empty, the colour of the inside of a tube. All the drawing was at the edges. Every single thing Bee could think of that Hyv had ever missed, round the edge of the world, round the rim of the page. A plant. A lamp. A cat on a windowsill, the corner of a door. Hands, people's hands, at the sides of things, holding cups and pens and other hands. The edge of a table, with a phone on it. Birds, at the very top. Somebody's shoes at the very bottom. Feet. The corners of rooms. Sky round buildings.

All of it at the edges, crowded right up against the rim, as if the world had been pushed out to where Hyv had never been able to look. Now it was there, waiting, all at once.

Bee held it up to the camera.

Hyv looked at it. Bee watched her look at it. Not at the middle. At the edges. Her eyes went round the rim of the page, slowly, all the way round, the way you'd walk round the edge of a garden you'd only ever seen from the window.

"You drew me the edges," said Hyv.

"I drew you the edges," said Bee.

"There's a cat."

"There's always been cats, Hyv. You just never saw them till they were in the middle."

Hyv looked at it for a long time.

"I hate it," she said, crying. "I love it. I hate it."

"I said you would," said Bee.

***

Afterwards, when the doctor had gone and Hyv was sitting with the phone close again, talking about nothing, Bee watched her face.

It was different. Not much. Bee didn't think anybody else would have seen. But Hyv's eyes didn't stay in the middle any more. Every few seconds they went out to the edge of the phone, to the edge of whatever was round it, and came back. As if she were checking the world was still there at the sides. As if she couldn't quite believe it would stay.

And once, in the middle of telling Bee about the woman with the knitting, she stopped, and her eyes went somewhere else entirely. Not to an edge. Inward. And her face did a thing Bee had seen it do exactly once before. On a white terrace in a city with no name, in the hour before a sunset that never came, right after somebody put a hand on top of her head.

Then Hyv blinked, and went pink, and said, "Anyway. The *knitting*—"

Bee didn't say anything.

She knew. She'd known since the terrace. Since before the terrace, if she was honest, since a stream in April and a donation to the dollar and a name at the top of a note. *I'm going to hit you the first time I see you.* Bee had drawn every one of Hyv's faces for years, and she knew this one. She'd just never seen Hyv aim it at anyone before.

She wasn't going to say anything. It wasn't hers to say. And Hyv would hit her, and Bee would deserve it.

Somebody else could say it. Some day. Somebody braver, or louder, or less likely to get hit.

"The knitting," said Bee. "Go on."
