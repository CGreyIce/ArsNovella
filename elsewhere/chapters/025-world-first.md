The first half of the nave took forty minutes, and it was the cleanest forty minutes of fighting Ice had ever done.

They'd done it once. They knew it now. Deston on the bass line from the foot of the steps, low and steady and a little flat. Every group that came at them on the downbeat came apart into one and one and one. Ice turned them one at a time, Anchoring the ones he couldn't turn. Xav was a second behind each. Suzu's row went round at knee height, and the lights came off the blade and clung. Kotori went from pillar to pillar along the left side of the church without getting her feet wet, and the bell rang before the cold went in.

Nobody spoke. They didn't need to. Five people in a drowned church, moving like a hand.

Suzu caught every light. Ice watched him do it. He caught them and stoppered them and hung them on his belt with the others, until by the step up to the screen there were twenty jars on him and they were all singing. Faintly, one note each, a soft high chord that went with them down the aisle like a second, smaller choir.

At the step, the Precentor saw them, and shouted the downbeat, and brought its staff down on the stone, and the whole back half of the choir turned round.

"*Now*," said Xav.

Suzu turned his back on the Precentor completely. He walked back down the aisle, alone, toward the whole of the rest of the choir coming at them on the beat, and stopped in the middle of the nave with the water to his knees and the long dark scythe held low.

The silence was the strangest thing Ice had ever heard.

Three hundred years of the first verse, and now, all at once, nothing. Every chorister in the nave stood exactly where it was, in the water, between the pews, with its face lifted and its mouth open and no sound coming out. Suzu stood in the middle of them with his scythe still raised, and they didn't come at him. They didn't come at anything. They were waiting.

Through the arch in the stone screen, up three wide steps, on what must once have been the altar, something moved.

It had been standing there the whole time. Ice had seen the shape of it yesterday, through the arch, in the green gloom. He'd thought it was a statue. It was tall, twice as tall as the Precentor, thin and stooped, in a long dark cope that hung to the floor and spread out on the water like ink. It had no face that he could see. It raised the baton.

The fourth verse changed key.

Deston had said it would. He'd said it on the mound yesterday: *the fourth time it changes key at the end and resolves.* He'd sung it under his breath in the ring until Xav asked him to stop and then asked him to start again. But hearing it was different.

It went *up*. The whole choir together, in the drowned church, a whole step up. Everything got brighter, the voices and the light and the green water. It was the moment in a hymn when the organ opens all its stops, and the hair stands up on the back of your neck, and you don't know why. And the Choirmaster, on the steps, with nobody left keeping time with it, made the first sound it had made: a long, high, wordless howl, like wind in a chimney. The choir didn't stop singing.

They held the last chord. Every voice in the drowned church held it, long and low and bright, and as they held it, they began, one by one, to go.

The one in the front row went first. The first one to have kept time with Deston. It came apart into light, a single soft white flame, still singing its one note of the chord. Then it rose, slowly, through the green water, up past the tops of the pillars toward the dark roof, and through it.

Then the next. Then a whole row. Then all of them, rising, a whole church full of small white lights going up through the water like snow falling the wrong way, every one of them singing. The chord got quieter as they went, voice by voice, until there were only a few left, and then one, and then none.

Ice stood at the foot of the altar steps with his shield hanging from his arm and watched them go, and didn't say anything at all. He couldn't.

He looked across at Suzu.

Suzu was standing at the top of the steps with Aldo's scythe lowered, and twenty small glass jars on his belt, all singing. The lights were going up all around him, past him, a few feet from the long dark blade. All he'd have had to do was lift it. He'd caught every one he could reach all day. They were worth something, Ice knew, now. He was the only player in the world who could catch them, and they wanted to know where they went.

They came up the old green steps into the afternoon, out of the arch, onto the mound, into grey light and mist and a frog somewhere a long way off. They stood there in a row, blinking.

The fen was quiet.

It had always been quiet. But underneath it, for the whole of the twenty-six days he'd known it, there had been the singing. Faint and slow, through the stone, under everything, verse one, round and round, so constant he'd stopped hearing it. It wasn't there now. It had stopped. He heard the gap where it had been, the way you hear a fridge stop humming in the night.

They went back through the heron-stones, and across the tussocks, and down the north walk.

Sedge was on her porch.

She was standing at the top of her steps with one hand on the post, with her pale sharp eyes on the old fen. She didn't look at them as they came along the boardwalk. She was listening. Ice didn't know how long she'd been standing there. Since the window, maybe. Since every traveller in Elsewhere had seen it at once, and the woman at the end of the north walk who wasn't a traveller at all had heard the fen go quiet.

They stopped at the foot of her steps.

"They finished," said Ice.

Sedge didn't say anything for a long time. The bundles of drying herbs rustled over her head. Somewhere behind the house a heron called.

"My mother would have liked to hear that," she said eventually.

She went inside. She didn't start grinding. Through the open door, Ice could see her sit down at her table, in the dim green light of her house, with her hands flat on the wood, and just sit there.
