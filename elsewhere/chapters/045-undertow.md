Everybody was still there.

At twenty to eight, the sky began to grey.

He saw it first, because he'd been facing that way all night. East, out over the old fen, past the Deep's long legs and the black water and the reeds. Right at the bottom of the sky, along the line of the reeds, the black went to the darkest blue. The same as at midnight. On time, eight hours late.

All over the old fen, the Unlit stopped.

Every small one, in every channel and pool and tussock, at once. Their long heads came up off the water and turned east, the way they had at midnight, and they stood there, perfectly still, like herons in a dawn that hadn't come yet. Watching.

The Deep stopped too.

It lifted its long head, high up in the dark, and turned it, slowly, toward the east. Toward the line of blue along the reeds. He felt the weight on his shield ease for the first time in three hours, the way a wave eases at the very top before it comes down. He stood on the pale plank with Osric Fell's boss in front of him, and breathed, and watched it look at the sky.

The blue went grey. Lighter. Lighter than it had got at midnight. Along the very bottom of it, so faint he wasn't sure, a thread of something that might have been gold.

And this time it didn't go out.

The Deep looked at it for a long moment. Then it looked down.

Past him. At the little clay lamp on Sedge's porch, at the end of the north walk.

And then at him.

***

"*Dec*," said Xav, on the channel. "Here it comes."

He knew. He felt it before it happened, the way Coll Rudd would have said you feel a man's back foot turn. The whole long dark body of the thing *gathered*. Not leaning toward the light any more. Not wanting. Something worse than wanting. It was a thing that had come up out of the bottom of the fen on the longest night of the year, and it knew the night was ending, and it hadn't got what it came for.

*At dawn, on the second day, they all came at once.*

It came down on him with everything.

Not a lean. All of it. All the weight of all the deep water in the old fen, every pool and channel and drowned thing, every year of dark since before Fernwick had a name, came down out of the sky onto one shield on one plank on four posts. The posts didn't groan this time. One of them cracked. He heard it go, a long splintering sound under his feet. The narrows dropped a hand's width toward the water and stayed there.

He was at nineteen per cent.

He didn't kneel.

That was the one thing he knew. In the drowned church he'd gone down on one knee, and come up out of it, and that had been right, then. This was different. *Never went forward. Never fell back.* He didn't kneel. He stood on his back foot on the cracking plank and held all of it, every bit, on the slope of a shield made of drowned oak and choir silver, with an old iron boss in the middle that had held this much before. Once. At a ford. For a stubborn man with a broken arm, who never made a sound.

He didn't make a sound.

Kotori's warmth came up through him from behind and to the right. Twenty-six per cent. The weight came down harder. Fourteen.

"*Sorry*," said Kotori, very small. "*Sorry, I've got nearly nothing left, I'm sorry—*"

"*Keep singing*," said Ice. He didn't know who he was saying it to. All of them. "*Everybody keep singing.*"

Behind him, a long way off, at the end of the north walk, an old woman was still singing a reed-cutting song in a voice like dry grass. A boy with a sword too big for him was humming it, badly. Marta was singing it, flat and certain, on the Landing. Four old militiamen and an older man with three fingers were singing it on the bridge, the way they'd sung it fifty years ago, on a riverbank, so that a man on a stone would know they were still there.

And Deston, with almost no voice left at all, was counting it.

*One. And two. And—*

The gold came up along the reeds.

A thread, and then a line, and then a long low edge of light, along the whole bottom of the sky. It lit the tops of the reeds first, far out in the old fen. Then the heron-stones, nine long grey necks catching the first gold. Then the water.

Everywhere the light touched, the small Unlit came apart.

Not in a fight. Just went, standing where they were in the shallows with their heads lifted toward the dawn, into black water, one after another, as the gold reached them. Like frost going off a window. All over the old fen, a long rippling line of them, collapsing from the east.

The Deep felt it. He felt it feel it. The whole enormous weight on his shield *shuddered*.

"*Now?*" said Xav.

"*Not yet.*"

The light came across the old fen toward the narrows. Across the black channel. Up the long legs of the Deep, gold on wet dark, and where it touched, the dark steamed.

And the Deep did what Hollis had said they all did, the last of them, at the very end. It leaned. One last time. Everything it had left. Away from the light, away from the dawn, onto the one small thing standing between it and the oldest lamp in the fen. All of it, forward. Every last scrap of weight, onto his shield.

He'd never felt anything like it. He felt it all the way down to the bottom of his real spine, lying in an egg in Melaka Raya. Nine per cent. Seven. He stood on his back foot and held it.

"*Now*," he said.

***

Xav came up out of the water behind its neck.

Ice never saw him. He never did. He just felt it: the last inch. Behind whatever was looking at him. Xav came up out of the channel at the base of the Deep's long neck, at the place where all of it bent toward Ice. He put both daggers in, one high, one low, the way he'd put them into a thousand things over five weeks. Not to kill it. You couldn't kill it. To make it lean. One more inch. All of it, every bit, over Ice, onto the shield, forward, with nothing at all left behind it holding it up.

"*Dec stands here*," said Xav, very quietly, on the channel.

And at the edge of Ice's vision, two windows opened, one under the other, grey as water.

He didn't read them. Not then. He'd read them afterwards, a hundred times. He just felt it, the way you feel water move under a boat. Everything he'd held. Four thousand one hundred and twelve. All of it, the whole night, every cold and every weight and every lean, from sunset to now. It was all still there, under the surface of him. Waiting. Like a river under a ford.

He stepped back.

Half a step. Off the pale new plank, onto the old grey one behind it. And as he went, he didn't push. He *pulled*. Shield and boss and arm and back foot and everything, the way a current pulls, all at once, downward and back and toward him.

And everything the Deep had given him came back up out of him, and went into the pull.

The Deep came forward off its legs.

All of it. Every bit of the weight it had put into the lean, with nothing behind it, and a current pulling it from the front. It came over the narrows, over the cracked post, over Ice, the whole enormous dark of it, falling forward off its long jointed legs like a tower going over. And it went down into the deep channel beyond the narrows. Into the deepest water in the fen, where the current ran underneath.

The channel took it.

Down. Away. The black water closed over it in a great slow whirl, like water going down a drain. For one second its long head was still up, high, in the gold light, and then it wasn't. The water came apart, and smoothed, and went still. Clear, brown, ordinary fen water, with the dawn on it.

It came up gold, and huge, and slow, the way it had come up on his first morning in the game. Across the reeds and the tussocks and the heron-stones and the long black channels. The light came over the narrows and over him, standing on an old grey plank behind a cracked one with his shield still up. It lit the north walk behind him, and every lantern on it, still burning, pale now in the daylight. It lit the steps at the end of it, where a boy of nineteen was sitting down very suddenly with a black sword across his knees. It lit an old woman on a porch with a clay lamp in her lap, still lit.

She'd stopped singing. She was looking at the sun.

The lid lifted.

He was lying in the gel in the back room at Melaka Raya, with the ceiling fan going round. The high window over the eggs was full of real morning. White and bright and ordinary, the sun on the car park, a motorbike starting somewhere outside. His hands were shaking. Not a lot. A fine steady tremor that wouldn't stop, like the night of the world first.

Beside him, the other lid was lifting too.

Suzu sat up slowly in the gel. His hair was flat on one side. His face was very pale, and there were marks under his eyes, and he looked about a hundred years old. He looked at Ice across the gap between the eggs, for a long moment, in the morning light.

"Good," said Suzu.

Ice laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. He put his hand over his eyes and lay back in the gel and laughed until it wasn't only laughing, and Suzu didn't say anything else at all. After a while Suzu reached across the gap and put a hand flat on the edge of Ice's egg, the way you'd put your hand on a wall to know it was there, and left it there.

His phone was on the folding table. It had been buzzing for twelve hours.

He got to it eventually.

He sat on the floor between the eggs with his back against his own and the phone in his shaking hands, in the morning sun, and read them all twice. Then he read the windows again, the ones the egg had saved for him: the quest, and the class, and the line at the bottom.

*What strikes you, you hold. What you hold, pulls.*

He thought about an old man sitting on a boardwalk behind him in the dark at one in the morning, telling him the end of a story nobody had ever asked for. He thought about a captain on a stone in a ford, who'd never thanked a boy for two fingers. Who'd looked at the hand for a long time, and nodded, and that had been more than enough.

He'd go back in tomorrow. He'd walk down the north walk to the bridge, and find Hollis, and not thank him. He'd just nod.

It would be more than enough.
