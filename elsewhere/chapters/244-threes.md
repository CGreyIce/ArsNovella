Kotori had written her name on Kei's sheet in August without thinking, and she'd been thinking about it ever since.

She'd been at the kitchen door with a pot when Suzu stood up at the long table and asked who'd come. And she'd said *I'm coming, I said*, before anybody else, because she had said, in April, in the Hart's kitchen. *Somebody'll need tea.* And then the sheet had come round to her end of the table, the pencil hand to hand. And she'd put down the pot and written *Kotori*, small and careful. Under Suzu's, and under Ice's, and next to a little drawing of a horn.

She hadn't thought about it at all. It had just come out of her, the way *thank you* came out of her now, most of the time, instead of the other word.

She'd thought about it every night since.

***

She thought about the dark.

Not the longest night's dark. She knew that one. She'd held the banner room through two of them, with Suzu and Sheng Yang, healing on the beat. The first one alone, nearly, in the fen, frightened out of her mind. And the second in a three, in the round town, with four hundred and fourteen lights in the windows and the whole guild round her. She'd been frightened then too. But it had been the kind of frightened you could stand in.

Isa had told the long table about the other dark. Past the edge. *Not dark. Nothing.* *There's nothing to see by but the light you're carrying, and you can't use that.*

Kotori was a healer. She'd been a healer since the first week of the game, when she'd stood on a mooring post in a fen and kept five people alive by standing where nothing would hit her. And where she could see all five. That was the whole of it. That was the only thing she was good at, Ice had said once, and he'd meant it as the best thing anyone had ever said to her. *You always stand where nothing will hit you and where you can see everybody. Nobody else does that.*

*Where you can see everybody.*

Past the edge, she wouldn't be able to see anybody.

***

Kei read the sheet out on the first Sunday in October.

He'd said he would. *October. With everybody.* He stood at the head of the long table at the second dawn with the sheet in his hands. Everybody had written on it in August, all sixty-one, the pencil going round. And nobody had seen it since. He'd kept it in his map case for five weeks and not let anybody look.

The banner room was full. It always was, on a Sunday. Aurelie in her small chair at the near end, with her feet on the floor, and Vane standing behind her, because Ama Corrie still hadn't found him a chair and he said he didn't mind. Riverbank's three. Isa awake by the north door, with the jar.

Kei unrolled the sheet.

"Everybody wrote something," he said. "Sixty-one. Some of you wrote your names and *north*. Some of you wrote your names and *here*. Some of you wrote somebody else's name." He looked down the table. "Chris wrote a list of everybody who'd written before him, in order, with times."

"Somebody should know," said Chris.

"Somebody should," agreed Kei. "Thank you." He looked at the sheet. "Thirty-one of you wrote *north*."

The banner room went very still.

"Thirty-one," said Kei. "I can't take thirty-one into the dark. Isa says it's nothing out there. Not dark. Nothing. You can't see your feet. You walk by a candle and a rope. Thirty-one people on a rope in the nothing is a very long rope." He looked at Isa, in her chair. "And the round town needs everybody else. More than last year. Because last year Ice was under the floor and Suzu was in the banner room and Kotori was healing on the beat. And this year they won't be."

Nobody said anything.

"So," said Kei.

***

"Nine," said Kei. "Three threes. Nobody alone."

He read them out slowly, the way he read everything. In order. With the usernames, the way the game wrote them, because that was how they'd be on the board when they went out of the north door.

"*Suzuyunn. Kotori. Ice.*"

Kotori heard her name and didn't move.

"*Hyvred. AltheaVT. gigichiii_.*"

At the window end, Thea's face did something, and two chairs down, Gigi's went very still. They'd been *not close, near* for a year and a half now. Isa had said that about them, on the night she came home. *A girl with a shield who set the last one. With the thorn on her left. Not close. Near.* They'd been near ever since. And now Kei had put them in a three, together, with Hyv. For weeks, in the nothing, on a rope.

Kotori watched Thea look at Gigi. And Gigi, very slowly, look back.

"*HakoP. xolalotl. sleptzzy.*"

Hako put his hand on his heart and said *a rare honour* in the voice. Xola laughed. Slep, beside her, nodded once, the way he did when something was right.

"That's nine," said Kei. "Everybody else is here. On the board. I'll put you where you go this week." He rolled up the sheet. "Some of you wrote *north* and you're not going. I'm sorry. Bee."

Bee, at the window end, with her wings folded and a pencil behind each ear, looked at him.

"You wrote *with Hyv*," said Kei. "Underlined. Twice."

"I did," said Bee.

"I need your Walls at the north door," said Kei. "With Gabe and Salty. Six of them. All night." He was quiet. "I'm sorry."

Bee didn't say anything for a long time. Then she looked down the long table at Hyvred, in the chair beside the first of the nine. And Hyvred looked back at her, and her face was wet.

"It's fine," said Bee. "Somebody who sees everything should see nothing once." She smiled at the table. "Isa said. It teaches you to listen." She looked at Hyvred. "Ring me first. When you're back. Before anyone."

"I always do," said Hyvred.

***

Kotori went to the kitchen after. She always went to the kitchen when she didn't know what to do. The kettles, the big black range, Chernn's pot of wax in the corner. Eleven tins of tea on the shelf above, dented, from Joss.

She stood in front of the kettles and didn't put any of them on.

*Suzuyunn. Kotori. Ice.*

She'd known she'd be in Suzu's three. She'd said so in April. *I'll come. Somebody'll need tea.* And Suzu had said *okay* in a way that meant more than okay.

She hadn't thought about the third name.

Ice. The one at the front. The one who'd found her on a mooring post in a fen and said *nobody else does that*. Who'd said *you don't have to be sorry, not to us*, in a ring, the night before the first world first. Who'd said *mostly it means thank you, or I'm here*.

She'd be healing him. In the nothing. At the front of nothing, Isa said, on the stair, on the way up to the roof. *You'll be at the front of nothing.*

How did you heal somebody you couldn't see?

"Sorry," said Kotori. To the kettles. Out loud. It came out of her on its own, the old way.

She heard herself say it. She stood very still.

"Sor—" she said, and stopped.

***

"It's allowed," said somebody behind her.

She turned round.

Hyvred was in the kitchen doorway. With her bow on her back and her face still wet from the long table, looking at Kotori. All of her, edge to edge, the way she looked at everything in here.

"Sorry," said Kotori again, before she could stop it.

"It's fine," said Hyvred. "Ice says. In a bus station. *It's allowed.*" She came into the kitchen. "You're frightened."

"Yes."

"Of the dark."

"Of not seeing," said Kotori. It came out very small. "I stand where I can see everybody. That's all I do. That's the only thing I'm good at." She looked at the eleven tins on the shelf. "Out there I won't be able to see anybody. I won't know where to stand."

Hyvred didn't say anything for a long time.

"Me neither," she said.

Kotori looked at her.

"I see everything," said Hyvred. "In here. Edge to edge. That's the only thing *I'm* good at." She sat down on the stool by Chernn's corner. "Out there it's nothing. Isa says. I'm going to be the person who sees everything, in a place where there's nothing to see." She laughed, a bit. It wasn't a happy one. "Isa told me to go anyway. She said *it teaches you to listen*."

"Do you know how to listen?"

"No," said Hyvred. "Do you?"

Kotori thought about it.

"I heal on the beat," she said slowly. "In the Long Watch. In April a year and a half ago. With Suzu, lighting eighty-one lanterns, and me trying not to say sorry." She looked at the kettles. "I couldn't see what was coming. It was too dark. So I stopped waiting to see. I just healed on the beat. Every beat, whether they needed it or not." She looked at Hyvred. "I didn't say sorry once that night."

Hyvred looked at her for a long time.

"Teach me," she said.

***

They went down to the Seven Below at the third dawn, the two of them. With a lamp, and then without one.

Ama Corrie opened the hatch for them and said *mind the third step*. And then, when she saw that they were going down with the lamp and Hyvred put it out on the bottom step: "*Hm*." She didn't say anything else. She left the hatch open. A square of grey light at the top of the stair. That was all there was.

The Seven Below in the dark was very dark.

Not nothing. Kotori could tell that, even with her eyes open and nothing in front of them. The square of grey at the top of the stair, behind her. The stream in the north tunnel, a quarter of a mile off, running. The silver lines in the floor, which she couldn't see but could feel through her boots, very faintly, the way you feel a step you know is there.

It wasn't nothing. But it was the nearest thing they had.

"I can't see you," said Hyvred, somewhere to her left.

"I know."

"I can't see *anything*. I've never—" Her voice did something. "In here I've always been able to see. Even on the longest night. Even under the lake, with the crown. There was always light. Something. Edges." She was quiet. "There's no edges."

"Then don't look for them," said Kotori. "Listen for the beat."

"What beat?"

Kotori thought about it.

"Mine," she said. "I'll heal on the beat. Every beat. You'll hear it." She lifted her short white staff, which she couldn't see either, and said the word, small, the one she'd said ten thousand times. *Mend.* And somewhere to her left, she heard the soft sound it made. A little chime, like a spoon on a cup, very faint, where it touched Hyvred.

Then she did it again. On the beat. Then again.

*Ting.* Rest. *Ting.* Rest. *Ting.*

"I hear it," said Hyvred.

"Now tell me where you are."

"*What*?"

"I'm healing you on the beat," said Kotori. "Every beat. So every beat, there's a sound where you are. And I hear it." She did it again. *Ting.* "Left of me. Four paces. A bit forward." She swallowed. "I don't need to see you. I can hear where the beat lands."

There was a long silence in the dark.

"*Kotori*," said Hyvred, very quietly.

***

They did it every night for three weeks.

Then not just the two of them. Suzu came down, the second week, because Kotori asked him to. And then Ice, because Suzu asked. And then Thea and Gigi and Xola and Slep and Hako. All nine, in the Seven Below at the third dawn with the lamp out and the hatch open a crack. Learning to hear where everybody was.

Kotori healed on the beat. Every beat. Whether they needed it or not. *Ting.* Rest. *Ting.* And nine people learned to stand in the dark and listen to where the small chime landed on each of them. And know where everybody was.

Hyvred learned fastest. Of course she did. She'd never had to listen before, and it turned out she'd been listening the whole time, under the seeing. She just hadn't known. By the end of the second week she could tell where all nine were from the beat alone, and call it. *Thea left, two. Gigi behind her. Not close. Near.* And Thea laughed in the dark, and somewhere a pace behind her, Gigi did too.

Ice learned slowest. That surprised everybody but Kotori.

"I always know where everybody is," he said, on the fourth night, standing somewhere in the middle of the dark. "I don't know how. I just do. In a fight. I know where all nineteen are, at the bottom of a lake." He was quiet. "I can't do it here."

"Because you're looking," said Kotori.

"I'm not looking. I can't see anything."

"You're looking anyway," said Kotori. "In your head. You're trying to see where everybody is. You've always seen." She did the beat, *ting*, on him. "Stop seeing. Just hear."

He was quiet for a long time.

"You sound like Isa," he said.

"Isa sounds like me," said Kotori. "I was healing on the beat in the Long Watch before she came home."

She heard him laugh. In the dark. Out loud, the way he'd laughed on a netloft step, Rafi said, at *I pick fenwort*.

***

She asked Suzu on the last night of the third week, in the Hart's kitchen, with the eleven tins on the shelf.

"Are you frightened?"

Suzu was at the table with the jar. He always had the jar now, when he wasn't on the north steps with Isa. He looked at her across it.

He'd never once said he was frightened. Not in the Long Watch, with eighty-one lanterns. Not at the bottom of the lake. Not on the longest night, holding the banner room with Sheng Yang. Kotori had known him two years and he'd never said it.

"Yes," said Suzu.

She stopped with the kettle in her hand.

"Of the dark?"

"Of carrying it," said Suzu. He put his hand flat on the lid of the jar. "Three hundred years. Her mothers. Every one of them. Kept it lit. Never let it go out." He looked at the jar. "And I'm going to carry it through the nothing for three weeks, in a cage, on my back. Where nobody can see it. And if I fall, or the cage breaks, or I'm slow—" He stopped. "It's not mine. That's the thing. It's theirs. All of them. And I'm the one carrying it."

Kotori put the kettle down.

She thought about the Long Watch. Eighty-one lanterns, and him lighting them one by one with his hands shaking. And her behind him, healing on the beat. Trying not to say sorry. And him saying *better*, at the end, when she hadn't once.

"You won't be on your own," she said.

"I know. Nobody alone."

"No," said Kotori. "I mean you won't be carrying it on your own. You'll be carrying the light. I'll be carrying the tea. Ice'll be carrying the front. Hyv'll be carrying the listening." She sat down across from him. "Everybody's carrying something. That's what a three is. Kei says. It's not three people. It's three things carried."

Suzu looked at her for a long time.

"Kei says that?"

"I say that," said Kotori. "Just now. I made it up." She felt her face go red. "Sorry."

"Don't," said Suzu.

"Thank you," said Kotori.

Something happened to his still face. She'd known him two years. She knew what it was.

"Good," said Suzu.

***

She went to Isa the next morning. On the north steps, at the first dawn, where Suzu had carried her. With a cup of the tea from the first tin. The dark one, from the leaf that grew on the backs of the waystones, where the lamps were. The one that stayed warm a long time and remembered being near a light.

She held it out.

Isa looked at it. Then at her. Her bright black eyes.

"What did your mothers drink?" said Kotori. "On the hill?"

Isa thought about it for a long time.

"Water," she said. "From the still water. You boil it on the lamp. Very slowly. It tastes of nothing." She took the cup in both root hands. "There's nothing on the hill. Twelve stones and a flat one and the lamp. And a bowl, and a blanket, and the oil. That's all there's ever been."

"No tea?"

"No tea," said Isa.

She drank. Kotori watched her face. She'd learned faces from Isa, the way Isa had learned them from three hundred years of her mothers on a hill. She watched it now.

"*Hm*," said Isa.

"It stays warm," said Kotori. "A long time. In the cold. The window says it remembers being near a light."

"It's horrible," said Isa.

"Yes."

"Good for you."

"Yes," said Kotori.

Isa drank the rest of it, slowly, on the north steps, looking up the road at the ninety-four lamps leaning.

"Take it," she said, at last. Holding out the empty cup. "Up there. To the hill." She looked at Kotori. "There's never been tea on the hill. In three hundred years. Just water, and the lamp." Her face did something, very small. "Somebody should bring some."

"I am," said Kotori. "Eleven tins."

"Then there'll be tea on the hill," said Isa Corrie. "For the first time." She folded her root hands round the empty cup. "My mother would have hated it."

Kotori laughed. She couldn't help it. On the north steps, at the first dawn, with an old woman who'd come home.

She didn't say sorry once.
