He was at the shoplot by nine, with a folding table under one arm and a box of chairs he didn't need under the other.

It was ridiculous. He knew it was ridiculous. The bus wasn't until four. The contract was Vivian's job and would be perfect whether he was standing in an empty room or not. The pods had been plugged in since yesterday, humming to themselves in the back room with their lids up. There was nothing to do.

So he did all of it anyway. He put the table up in the gap between the two pods, and took it down because it was in the way, and put it up again against the side wall. He unboxed the kettle and found the one socket that wasn't already taken by a pod. He put four chairs round the table. Then he took two away, because four chairs round a table for two looked like a man expecting people who weren't coming, and put them upstairs. He swept the white tiles, which didn't need sweeping. He stood in the back room with his hands in his pockets and looked at the two white eggs with their lids up, waiting for fingers.

At ten, the delivery men came back.

"*Again*, boss?" said the older one, at the roller shutter, with real wonder.

"Again. Sorry."

This time it was his own pod. He'd rung them last night after he logged out, lying in bed with the fan going round. He'd asked whether they moved pods as well as delivered them. The man had said *for you, boss, anything*, in a voice that suggested he'd decided Ice was the most entertaining customer in Malacca. So now his own egg, the one from the spare room, the one with his fingerprint in it, came through the roller shutter on a trolley and went into the back room.

It didn't fit. He'd measured for two. The men looked at the long wall with the two new pods on it and the little table against the side wall, and then at him, and he said "Upstairs," before they could. So the spare went upstairs. The one that wasn't anybody's yet went into the front room on the first floor, by the window, alone. His own went into the back room beside Suzu's.

When the men had gone he stood in the back room again. Two pods side by side, one with his fingerprint and one waiting. A table. A kettle on the floor, still, because the table was full of chairs.

It looked like an office. Almost.

"The shutter was up," she said. "I knocked. You were in the egg."

"Sorry. It's—you can't hear anything, in there."

"So I gathered." She didn't come any further in. She stood in the doorway and looked at the two white eggs, and then at the third, the empty one, through the ceiling, as if she could see it. "So these are the units."

"These are the pods."

"The *units*," said Vivian, "are what I'll be calling them in the books. *Equipment, gaming, full-dive, three units.* Two thousand four hundred and ninety-nine US dollars each." She glanced at him. "You'll want to keep the receipts. They're capital expenditure. You may be able to claim capital allowances on them, if the tax office agrees they're for the business."

"They're for the business."

"I'm sure they are, Mr Declan." She came one step into the room, the first step, and looked down at Suzu's pod. Its lid was up. The gel inside lay smooth and grey. Then, after a moment, she put out one finger and touched the edge of the shell, very lightly, the way you'd touch a car somebody had told you was expensive.

"Don't put your finger on the glass," said Ice.

"I wasn't going to."

"It registers to whoever touches it first. Permanently. It's his."

Vivian took her finger off the shell and looked at him. For half a second her face did something he hadn't seen it do before. It wasn't quite curiosity, but it was close, the face of a woman who'd just found out that a thing she'd decided was simple wasn't.

"Permanently," she said.

"Yeah."

"Hm." She stepped back out into the doorway. "The contract."

***

They went over it at the little table, in the chairs he'd known he'd need.

It was four pages. He'd expected more. Vivian said the good ones were short, and he believed her. She'd written it the way she wrote everything, in plain sentences with the edges filed off. He read every line while she watched him read, and found that he understood all of it, which was not something he'd been able to say about any contract he'd signed in his life, including the one for the house.

*Position:* Game Operations Executive. He laughed out loud at that. Vivian didn't.

"It's accurate," she said. "And it is the kind of title the Immigration Department will read without having to call me."

*Basic monthly salary:* RM5,000.00. Paid on the last working day of each month into an account in Malaysia.

*Commencement:* on the date the Employment Pass is approved by the Immigration Department of Malaysia, and not before. Until that date, nothing in this agreement requires the Employee to perform any work, and nothing in it entitles him to any salary.

"Read that one twice," said Vivian. "And make him read it twice too."

"He'll read it twice. He reads everything twice."

*Hours:* not more than eight hours on any day, save by agreement. Not more than twelve hours on any day in any case, "the equipment not permitting more".

He looked up.

"It's true," said Vivian. "I read the manual. It throws you out at twelve. I'd rather the contract agreed with the equipment than argued with it."

*Leave.* *Medical.* *Termination, by either party, with one month's notice.* And at the end, a clause he hadn't asked for. He read it three times.

*Accommodation:* the Employer shall provide accommodation at the Employer's residence, without charge, for so long as the Employee wishes.

"You didn't have to put that in," he said.

"You told me there was a room," said Vivian. "If there's a room, there's a room. If it's in the contract, there's a room even on the days you're annoyed with each other. People are annoyed with each other, Mr Declan. Especially people who live together." She squared the four pages against the table. "Pens."

***

He was at Melaka Sentral at a quarter to four.

He'd been there twice in two years, both times to see somebody off, never to wait for anyone. It was big and loud and too bright and smelled of diesel and fried chicken and the cold breath of air-conditioning every time a door opened. There were long rows of bus bays and long rows of plastic seats and a lot of people waiting the way people wait at bus stations, with their bags between their feet and their eyes on the road. Announcements came over the speakers in Malay and English and occasionally Mandarin, all of them slightly too fast to catch.

He found the bay. He stood at it. He sat down. He stood up.

The bus from Singapore was eleven minutes late, which was nothing, and every one of those eleven minutes took about an hour.

He'd been meaning to have Suzu visit for two years. They'd said it to each other a dozen times on calls at three in the morning: *you should come up*, *yeah, I should come up*, *come for a weekend*, *I will, I will*. Neither of them had ever quite made it happen. There'd always been something, the way there always is. And now Suzu was on a bus with a passport and a pillow and four pages of contract waiting for him on a folding table, and it wasn't a weekend at all.

The bus came round the corner at four eleven, big and white and dusty, and pulled into the bay with a long sigh of brakes, and the door folded open.

People got off. An auntie with three shopping bags. Two students with suitcases. A man in a suit who looked like he'd regretted every minute of the journey. A family, all at once, with a sleeping toddler.

And then Suzu.

He had a backpack on, and a big duffel bag over one shoulder, and, under his other arm, exactly as promised, a pillow in a dark blue pillowcase. He stepped down off the bus and stopped on the kerb and looked round the bus bay, slowly, the way he did everything. Calm and unhurried, looking at each thing in turn as if he were filing it. Then his eyes got to Ice.

Ice had been worried, a little, on the drive. He'd been worried about the first minute. You spent years with someone's voice in your ear at three in the morning, and you thought you knew them, and then they stood in front of you in the actual air, and you had to work out what to do with your hands.

Suzu walked over to him, and stopped, and looked at him, and said, in his low slow voice, exactly the voice from the headset, but *here*, in the diesel air of Melaka Sentral:

"You look like you."

Ice laughed so hard a woman at the next bay turned round.

"I *am* me."

"I know," said Suzu. "I wasn't sure." And then he did a thing Ice hadn't expected at all. He held out the pillow. "Hold this."

Ice held the pillow. Suzu shrugged the duffel bag round onto his other shoulder, settled the backpack, took a breath, and hugged him. It was a short hug, one-armed, very firm, the kind you give somebody when you don't do hugs but you've decided this one counts. Then he stepped back and took his pillow back and tucked it under his arm again as though nothing had happened.

"I told you I was bringing my own pillow," he said.

"I bought pillows."

"I know. You said." Suzu looked round the bus station. "Is it always this loud?"

"It's a bus station."

"Hm." He looked at Ice again. "Is there a scythe?"

"Not yet."

"Okay," said Suzu. "Then let's go and find one."

***

He drove them back through Malacca with the windows down because the air-con in his car took ten minutes to think about it.

Suzu sat in the passenger seat with the pillow on his knees and looked out of the window at everything. The low houses. The palm trees along the road. The red-roofed shophouses of the old town in the distance, and a temple, and a mosque, and a petrol station with a giant fibreglass durian on the roof that Ice had always meant to ask somebody about. He didn't say much. He'd never said much. He'd always been like that: the quiet one in a Discord call of six people, who said four words an hour, and all four of them right.

"It's flat," Suzu said eventually.

"It's Malacca."

"I thought it'd be hillier."

"Why?"

"I don't know. In my head it was hilly." He looked at the road. "In my head you lived on a hill."

They drove for a while.

"I told my mum," Suzu said eventually, still to the window. "That I'd be home on weekends. Starting next week."

"Weekends," said Ice. "I'll drive you to the bus."

"She said to tell you to eat properly."

Ice laughed. "Tell her I'll try."

They drove a bit further.

"Thanks," said Suzu, to the window.

Ice didn't say anything. He didn't trust himself to say the right thing, so he didn't say anything at all, and drove, and after a while Suzu reached out without looking and turned the radio on. It was a song neither of them knew, in a language neither of them spoke, and they listened to the whole thing.

***

Vivian was waiting at the shoplot.

She'd stayed. He hadn't asked her to. She was sitting at the little table in the back room with the four pages squared in front of her and a pen laid parallel to them, drinking something from a paper cup that the kopitiam auntie must have sent over in a Grab. When they came in, she stood up.

"Mr Declan," she said. "And this is—"

"Suzu," said Ice. "This is Ms Ong. My secretary. I told you she's scary."

"He did tell me," said Suzu.

"I'm not scary," said Vivian. "I'm thorough. It's often mistaken for the same thing." She held out her hand, and Suzu shook it, and she looked at him. Ice watched her do the measuring look, the one Sedge and Hollis did, the one he'd started to think everybody worth anything did. It went across Suzu's face once, and settled. "Please, sit. Read it twice."

Suzu sat. He read it twice.

He read it the way Ice had known he would: slowly, every line, with one finger moving down the margin, not skipping. When he got to *Commencement* he stopped, and read it a third time, and looked up.

"What happens if they say no?" he said. "The pass."

Vivian looked at him, and something in her face approved.

"Then this agreement never starts," she said, "and you've been a guest in Malacca who plays video games. Which is what you'll be until they say yes, in any case. Nothing is lost. You go home, or you stay as a visitor for as long as a visitor may. We try again, if there's a reason they said no that can be fixed." She paused. "I don't expect them to say no. The company is in order. Your salary meets the threshold. And I'll be filing it."

Suzu looked at her for a moment more.

"Okay," he said. He went back to reading.

He got to *Accommodation*. He stopped again. He read it, and didn't say anything, and didn't look up. Ice saw his finger stay on the line for a long time.

Then he picked up the pen, and signed. Both copies. Initials on every page, where Vivian's small square pencil ticks told him to.

Vivian took one copy and put it in her folder and closed the folder.

"Welcome to Moonlight Harvest Workshop," she said. "I'll have the pass application lodged as soon as the company's registration with the Expatriate Services Division comes back. Weeks, not days. Please don't do any work in the meantime." She looked at the pods. "Whatever that means here."

"It means I play a game," said Suzu.

"Then please play it," said Vivian, "for fun." She picked up her bag and her paper cup. At the doorway she stopped and looked back, not at either of them, at the second pod with its lid up. "Permanently," she said again, to herself. Then she went.

***

There was only one thing left.

Suzu stood beside his pod in the back room. He'd put the pillow down on the folding table, carefully, as though it might mind. He looked at the pod for a long time: the smooth white shell, the smoked-glass lid on its long arm, the grey gel, the little glass square at the head end with a fingerprint printed on it in thin grey lines.

"It's bigger than on stream," he said.

"Everything is."

"It looks like an egg."

"*Thank* you."

Suzu climbed in. He did it the careful way, one leg and then the other, and lay down in the gel, and Ice watched his face when it warmed under him. A small surprise, quickly hidden. Then a soft voice came from somewhere in the shell.

*This Cradle registers to one person, permanently. Only that person's fingerprint can open it. Place your finger on the glass to continue.*

Suzu held his finger above the glass, and looked up at Ice, standing beside him.

"Permanently," he said.

"Yeah."

"Okay." He pressed it down.

A thin line of light went across the glass, once, the way a scanner reads a page.

*Registered. Welcome.*

***

He wasn't going to play tonight. That had been the plan. Long journey, new house, dinner, sleep, start fresh tomorrow at dawn, the way Ice had. That was what Ice had said in the car and Suzu had agreed with.

Suzu lay in the gel with the lid up and his finger still on the glass and looked at the ceiling.

"Can I go in now?" he said.

Ice laughed.

"It's seven o'clock."

"It's daytime in there," said Suzu. "I did the maths on the bus. Seven here is three in the afternoon there. Three in the afternoon is a good time to start." He turned his head and looked at Ice. "I've watched you for ten days. I want to see it."

"Go," said Ice.

He went live first, very quickly, for one minute, from the laptop, camera off, black screen. A hundred and twelve people came, the way they always did now, a little faster every day.
