Aurelie sat in Ama Corrie's mother's chair for the last time on Sunday morning, and her feet touched the floor.

It was a small chair. Plain, dark wood, worn pale on the arms, by the counter in the Hall of Roads. Ama Corrie's mother had made it because she'd swept this room for forty years and never had a chair that fit her either. Aurelie had sat in it every morning since September, whenever she was in the round town, for about a minute. She'd never told anybody why. She thought Ama Corrie knew.

"It'll be here," said Ama Corrie, from behind the counter, not looking up from the Roll.

"I know."

"Next year."

"I said that to Kei."

"I heard," said Ama Corrie. She turned a page. "You're slow."

Aurelie had been told she was a lot of things in six years. Small, mostly, and then sorry, once people found out who she was. Nobody had ever told her she was slow. She'd been in the round town four months, on and off, and Ama Corrie said it to her about once a week. She'd come to look forward to it.

"We're walking," said Aurelie.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie.

***

They went out through the south gate at the second dawn.

Kei came to see them off. Higu beside him. Gabe, who came to see everybody off, with Rae beside him at no paces at all. And the GM, because Vane was going, and he'd been Vane's GM for two years in a war, and he walked people to the gate.

He wasn't alone at the gate. Hyvred was holding on to his arm.

Aurelie had been watching it for a fortnight, with great interest, the way she'd watch a new kind of weather. Hyvred held his arm in the Hall. She held it on the stair. She'd held it at the long table on Wednesday, then let go of it to kiss him on the cheek in front of sixty people, then taken hold of it again. Gabe had screamed. Hyvred held it now, at the south gate, with both hands, and looked at Aurelie over it with her chin up. Like a girl who had decided to be very loud about something and was waiting to see if anybody would stop her.

Nobody was going to. Aurelie certainly wasn't.

She'd known the GM six years. Two games. A war. She'd never once seen anybody hold on to him. People had held on to his *orders*. Thousands of them. They'd held on to the sound of his voice in their ears at the front of a guild called MotU, in the rain, every day for two years. Nobody had ever held on to his arm.

He was pink. He'd been pink, as far as Aurelie could tell, for about a fortnight.

"Safe road," said the GM.

"We're walking," said Aurelie. "It's always safe walking. Things see you coming."

Kei held something out.

A sheet of paper, folded. She took it and opened it. It was a board. Kei's board, the one he drew for the longest night, with the round town in the middle and the roads going out. The north door. The first mile. The well, the windows, the banner room, the eye.

And the north-east road. With a name on it.

*Meridian*.

In pencil.

Aurelie looked at it for a long time.

"I said next year," she said. "Ask me again next year."

"It's in pencil," said Kei.

She looked up at him. Kei was looking back at her with his ordinary face. Calm and grey-eyed, a map under his arm, waiting for her to finish.

"You're asking early," said Aurelie.

"I'm not asking," said Kei. "It's pencil. Pencil isn't asking. Pencil's just where I'd put you." He paused. "You can rub it out."

Aurelie folded the board up very carefully along its creases. She put it inside her coat, against her chest, the way she'd seen Riverbank's swordsman keep a page.

"I'm not going to rub it out," she said.

"I know," said Kei. "That's why it's pencil."

***

Fourteen days on foot, the round town to Masmark. She'd done it in September, the other way, on feet that hadn't walked anywhere far in six years. Her feet were better now. She'd walked the round town for four months. The north-east road, on the longest night, eleven miles out and back. The market every morning, because Ama Corrie said a person who sat in a chair all day forgot she had legs.

Vane walked beside her. Not a pace ahead, the way he'd walked for six years, to see what was coming. Beside.

The milestone was at the bottom of the south road, five minutes from the gate.

A grey stone with a worn base, at the edge of the round market. Kite had sat beside it for months, the first winter, before he'd come any closer. Ash had sat on its base all autumn, with two blades across her knees, waiting. Aurelie had watched her do it every evening in September, from Kite's step in the gate, asking him things very badly.

They were both on the Roll now. Kite had a line under his name that had been empty for two years, and then wasn't.

Somebody else was on the base of the milestone.

A boy. Not a boy; a young man, very thin, in a coat that didn't fit, with a short sword across his knees and no guild on his collar. He was watching the road. He watched them come along it, a small woman and a tall still man, on foot, and he didn't get up. He didn't know who she was. Aurelie could see that he didn't. It was the nicest thing that had happened to her in a week.

She stopped.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Aurelie.

The young man stared at her.

"What?"

"It's a question," said Aurelie. "Somebody asked it at this milestone. For a year. Who'd stand next to you?"

He looked up the road. At his sword. At the empty place on his collar.

"Nobody," he said. "Yet."

"That's an answer," said Aurelie. "It's a bad one. But it's a start." She pointed back up the south road, the way they'd come. "At the top of this road there's a gate. In the gate there's a man with a bow, standing with his hands at his sides. Go and ask him the same thing. He'll know what you mean."

"Who are you?"

Aurelie thought about it.

"Somebody walking," she said.

They left him at the milestone. When she looked back, from the first rise, he'd stood up. He was looking up the south road, at the gate. He hadn't gone yet.

He would, though. She'd watched people sit at that milestone. People went.

"That was good," said Vane.

"It was *bad*. I said *that's an answer, it's a bad one*."

"It was good," said Vane, "and bad." He walked a bit further. "Like Kite."

***

The goose was on the road on the second day.

It stood where it always stood, in the middle of the road, at the bend by the old stone barn. Grey and white and large, with an expression of permanent enormous offence. In September it had sat down for two Meridian, which it had never done for anybody. They'd walked round it. And it had looked at them both and folded its legs and sat in the road.

It looked at them now. At Aurelie and at Vane.

It sat down.

"It remembers," said Aurelie.

"It's a goose," said Vane.

"It *remembers*."

Vane looked at the goose. The goose looked at Vane.

"It's a goose that remembers," said Vane. Which was one more sentence than he needed. Aurelie wrote it down in her head with the others.

***

The ferryman was on the near bank on the sixth day.

An old man with a pole and a hut, and on the hut's door, cut into the wood with a knife, rows and rows of little marks. *o7*. A salute, a small one, the kind people did with their hands. In September there'd been four hundred and twelve. Everybody who'd gone across to the round town and come back.

There were more now. She didn't count them. There were a lot more.

"Longest night," said the ferryman, seeing her look. "They went across in December. Hundreds. All the Halls from this side. To stand on somebody's road." He spat in the river. "Came back in January. Every one of them stopped and cut one." He looked at her. "You cut one in September."

"I did."

"You going to cut another?"

Aurelie looked at the door.

"Can I?" she said. "I've already got one."

"There's no rule," said the ferryman. "Some people have three." He held out the knife. "Him, with the shield. He's got five."

She took the knife. She cut one more, small, under the first. *o7*. Her hand wasn't steady. She'd signed treaties for ten thousand people with a hand steadier than that.

She held the knife out to Vane.

He looked at it. He'd never cut one. In September, he'd stood beside her while she did, and not.

He took it. He cut one, very neat, very small, next to hers. Not under. Next to.

***

On the ninth night, at the rim of the crater, he asked her.

They'd stopped at the top of the long rise above Masmark's lake. It was the third dawn, out there midnight, and they'd made a fire in the lee of a boulder. Below them, the whole lake lay under the dark, the size of a country, with the lights of Masmark along its far shore like a string of beads. Five days. Then home.

Vane was sitting on the other side of the fire with his hands on his knees.

"Are you fine?" said Vane.

Aurelie looked up.

He'd never asked her that. Not in six years. Not in two games. She'd asked *him* it, once, on a harbour wall, in Masmark. *Are you fine?* The first time anybody ever had. And he'd said *Yes. Now.* And she'd thought, then, that it was the best thing she'd ever done. Asking.

He'd never once asked it back.

"Why," said Aurelie.

"I watched a man at one per cent," said Vane, "not take the next beat. On Saturday." He was looking at the fire. "Two years in a war. I never saw him not take it. I asked him why. He said, *I didn't want to.*" He looked at her across the fire. "And I thought: I've known you six years. I've never once seen you not take it either."

Aurelie didn't say anything.

"So I'm asking," said Vane. "Are you fine?"

She thought about saying *yes*.

She'd said it every day for six years. To ten thousand people. To two hundred officers. To sixty-one Halls with sixty-one chairs in them that were all too big. To Harlan, who carried things. To Vane, who made sure everything was fine before she knew it needed to be. *Yes. Fine. Go on.* It was the only answer. If she wasn't fine, ten thousand people would stand there.

"No," said Aurelie.

It came out of her very quietly, at the rim of the crater, by a fire in the lee of a boulder. She hadn't known she was going to say it until it was out.

Vane didn't move.

"I've taken every beat," said Aurelie, "for six years. Every one. Because if I don't, they'll stand there. All of them. Ten thousand people waiting for me to go first." She looked at the lake. "And I watched him on Saturday. At one per cent. With the next beat in his hand. And he just *held* it. To see if somebody else would." She was quiet. "And nobody stood there. Somebody on the step said *it'll be March*. And it was over. And it was *fine*."

"Yes," said Vane.

"I've never once not taken it."

"No."

"I'm *tired*, Vane," said Aurelie.

The fire cracked. Down on the far shore, one of the lights of Masmark went out, and another came on further along.

***

Vane was quiet for a long time.

She thought he wasn't going to say anything. He usually didn't. He'd say one more sentence than he needed, about once a day, and he'd said his for today at breakfast, about the weather.

"They'd stand there," said Vane at last. "If you didn't go. For a bit." He looked at her. "And then they'd go anyway. They're people. People go." He paused. "The round town went. On the longest night. Sixty of them. Nobody at the front. He was on a hill. He wasn't even there."

Aurelie stared at him.

That was four sentences. Five.

"*We're fine*," said Vane. "You know what it means."

"It means you've already made it so."

"Yes." He looked at the fire. "Six years. I made it so. Before you knew it needed making. Everything." He was quiet. "I never asked what you wanted made."

"Vane."

"So I'm asking," said Vane. "What do you want made so?"

Aurelie looked at the lake, a long way down, in the dark.

She thought about it properly. Nobody had ever asked her that either. Not in six years, not in two games. *What do you want?* Ten thousand people had asked her what *they* wanted. What should they do, where should they go, who should they stand behind. Nobody had asked what she wanted, because she was the one who decided.

"I want somebody else to go first," she said. "Once. Somewhere. And me not be there. And it be fine."

Vane nodded slowly.

"Okay," he said.

It was the GM's word. She'd heard him say it a thousand times, in a war, in her ears. *Okay.* Meaning *I heard. I'll do it. It's done.*

"We're fine," said Vane.

And she believed him. She always had.

***

They came down to Masmark on the fourteenth day, in the afternoon, at the long bridge over the river where it went into the lake.

Harlan was on the bridge.

Of course he was. He'd have been on the bridge since the morning. He'd have had somebody on the crater rim with a glass since yesterday, watching the road. He was big and loud and he waved both arms when he saw them, from two hundred paces, the way he did. Like a man trying to flag down a barge.

"*Aurelie*," shouted Harlan. "*Vane*. *You WALKED*."

"We said we would," said Aurelie, when they reached him.

"*Fourteen days*. *Again*. Your *feet*—"

"My feet are fine." She looked at him. He had something in his hand. A letter, folded small, with a seal on it she didn't know. Blue-grey, a wave. "What's that?"

Harlan's whole face changed. He held it out.

"From the east," said Harlan. "Came Tuesday. Everybody's got one. Sable Court. VANTA. Us. Every guild over five hundred." He was nearly bouncing. "There's a *city*. On the east coast. The fiftieth level. It opens on the first of March. White cliffs and a black harbour and a tide that goes out a mile, twice a day. And under the bay, when the tide's out, there's a great door." He took a breath. "They're calling it the Low Tide."

Aurelie opened the letter.

A city of the fiftieth level. *Saltreach*. A great door under the bay, a drowned city, a hall for twenty. Lv 60. Open only between tides. First to the bottom takes the city's banner. Signed by somebody called Marisol, of something called the Tidebound. *Come and see the sea*, it said, at the bottom, in a different hand. *It's very big. You'll like it.*

"VANTA's going," said Harlan. "Kestrel wrote. Sable Court's going. Every big guild's sending a twenty." He looked at her. He'd already done it, she could see. Harlan always did things before she'd said. "I've picked twenty. The best we've got. They're on the board in the Hall. They leave on the fifteenth. Thirteen days on the coast road." He paused. "Who leads?"

Aurelie looked at the letter.

Then at Vane. Vane was looking at the lake.

"You," said Aurelie.

Harlan stopped bouncing.

"*Me*?"

"You," said Aurelie. "Go first. I'm not going." She folded the letter up and gave it back to him. "I'm going to sit in a chair."

Harlan looked at her for a long time. He wasn't loud now. She'd known Harlan six years too. He'd carried things, mostly. He'd carried a castle while she walked to a round town and back, twice, and not once said he minded.

"Aurelie," said Harlan. "Are you—"

"No," said Aurelie. "But I will be." She looked at Vane. "He's making it so."

Vane, looking at the lake, said nothing at all, which was how she knew he'd heard.

***

At the castle gate there was a boy.

Young, in Meridian's blue, very nervous, standing in the gate with his hands at his sides. Where people could see them. She'd asked Harlan in September to put somebody in Meridian's doors to ask. Harlan had. He'd put somebody in every door of every one of the sixty-one Halls.

The boy saw her coming and went white. He knew who she was. Everybody knew who she was.

He asked anyway. Very fast, very badly, the way she'd asked it herself, in a gate in a round town four months ago.

"Who'd stand next to you?"

Aurelie stopped in the gate.

"Vane," she said. "And Harlan, who's going east. And whoever will." She thought about it. "And a round town in the west. With a road on a board with my name on it. In pencil."

The boy stared at her.

"That's four answers," he said, before he could stop himself. And went whiter.

"I've been practising," said Aurelie.

Behind her, Vane said, "And me."

She turned round.

"You said me already," said Aurelie. "I said Vane. First."

"I know," said Vane. "I'm saying it again." He looked at the boy in the gate. "Write it down. Twice."

The boy wrote it down. Twice.

***

The great hall of the castle at Masmark had a long table and two hundred chairs. At the head of it, on a step, a chair that had been made for the head of Meridian six years ago. Carved, high-backed, blue. Enormous. When she sat in it her feet hung four inches off the floor, and had done for six years. She'd sat in it every day of them anyway, because it was the chair.

She stood in front of it now, in the evening, with her coat still on and her feet aching from fourteen days, and Kei's board inside her coat against her chest.

"Harlan," she said.

He was at her shoulder. Of course he was.

"Get the carpenter," said Aurelie. "Tonight. I want the legs cut down." She looked at it. "Four inches."

Harlan opened his mouth. Then shut it.

"Four inches," he said.

"So my feet touch."

The carpenter came at the third dawn and cut the legs of the head of Meridian's chair down four inches, in the great hall, with a saw. Two hundred officers stood round and watched in total silence. Vane held the chair still while he did it.

She sat in it at the first dawn. Before the egg.

Her feet touched the floor.

She sat there for a long time, at the head of the long table, in a chair that fit, with Kei's board in pencil inside her coat. Vane sat in the chair beside hers, the one that had always been a pace behind and to the left, which he'd moved, at some point in the night, to beside.

"We're fine," said Vane.

"I know," said Aurelie.
