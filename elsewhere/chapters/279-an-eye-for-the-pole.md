The roof stayed up for a week, and there was no wind the whole week, and Jared stood under the beam every night anyway.

Nobody asked him why. That was good, because he'd have had to say *I said I would*, and it would have sounded like less than it was. He was a practical man. He didn't go in for reasons. But he'd stood at the top of a ladder at three in the morning, a week ago, and told Alice *every year it bends, I'll be under it*. Then he'd stood under it on the night when it didn't bend at all, with Milli and Alice's hands beside his. And he'd found out that the not-bending was the part he'd meant.

So every night that week, at the second dusk, he went and stood under the beam for a bit, with his shoulder against it and his hand on the green pole. And Azac stood behind him. Not with his back against Jared's. Just there.

"It doesn't need you," said Azac, on the Tuesday.

"I know," said Jared.

"You're doing it anyway."

"I know," said Jared. "That's the job."

Azac thought about that for a long time. Then he said, "That's the bit people forget," which was Wendy's, from the gate. And didn't say anything else.

***

Leaka came back in on the Wednesday.

He heard her before he saw her. The whole round market heard her. She came out of the Hart's door at the second dusk, into the market under the roof, and stopped dead on the step. She looked up at six hundred and twelve lanterns over her head. And then she screamed.

Not a word. Just a scream. A long one, with a week of being out there in it.

"*JARRED*," screamed Leaka.

He was under the beam. He didn't move. She came across the market at a run and hit him at full speed, beam and all. She put her arms round him and her face in his chest and held on. He stood there with one shoulder still under the beam and the other arm round her and let her.

He'd known her for years. Longer than the game. She'd been loud in his chat, and Alice's, and everybody's. She'd told people to eat something green. She'd booked a plane on the end of her bed and told everybody about it. She was the best person he knew, and he'd told her so once, and she'd told him to shut up.

"You *stood under it*," said Leaka, into his chest. "Curse told me. There's no *wind*. There's been no wind for a *week*. And you've been standing under it every *night*."

"I said I would."

"*JARRED*." She hit him on the arm, not hard. "You're *ridiculous*. You're the most ridiculous man I've ever met."

"Probably."

She let go of him and looked up at the roof. At the lanterns. At the green pole, and the ring of twelve at the top of it, where Chernn's two-wicked candles had burned down to stubs over the week. Two flames in each, side by side, getting shorter, never going out.

"I missed it," said Leaka. Quieter. "The roof. I missed the night."

"You were out there."

"I *know* I was out there." She wiped her face. "I was *there*. I wouldn't have been anywhere else." She looked at him. "But I missed it. Both. I wanted *both*."

"You were on a lantern," said Jared. "Curse had you on a lantern."

"I *know*," said Leaka. "*LEAKA WAS HERE (OUT THERE)*. He sent me a picture." She was laughing and crying at once, the way she did. "It's the best thing anybody's ever written about me."

***

She didn't tell anybody about out there.

That was the thing that surprised the round town. Leaka told everybody everything. She'd told Alice about the plane for forty minutes, by message, from the plane. She'd told them about the napkin. Everybody at the long table that Wednesday night was waiting for her to stand up and tell them all of it. At volume. With her hands.

She didn't.

Gabe asked. Of course Gabe asked. He leaned across the long table with his whole face asking.

"Was it—"

"It was *theirs*," said Leaka.

Gabe stopped.

"I'm not telling," said Leaka. "Not you. Not anybody. Not even *Jarred*." She looked down the long table, at all of them. Sixty people who'd stood in the dark for an hour with tapers in their hands for a boom. "It was theirs. It was out there. I was there and it was the best thing I've ever seen in my life and I'm not telling *any* of you *one single thing* about it." She sat back. "So don't ask."

Nobody asked.

Down at the end of the long table, at Hana's five by the window, Alice had her face in Milli's shoulder. Jared couldn't see her face. He could see Milli's. Milli was looking at Leaka with an expression Jared had never seen on anybody, as if somebody had just handed him something very heavy and very good.

"Thank you," said Milli. Down the long table. Not loud.

"*Shut up*," said Leaka, and burst into tears, and Curse handed her a napkin, which was the third thing that worked.

***

On the Thursday, Milli came to the yard behind the Hart with two hinges.

Jared was there with the old trestles and the oilcloth and nothing on them. Milli stood in the gate of the yard with the hinges in his hand. Black iron, the length of a hand, a small five-line star stamped in the middle of each. He held them out the way you'd hold out something you'd been given and didn't know what to do with.

"Joss gave us these," said Milli. "Under the roof. For a door."

"I heard."

"We haven't got a door."

"Joss says everybody gets one eventually."

"I know," said Milli. He looked at the hinges. "I don't want to wait for eventually." He looked up at Jared. "Will you show me? How to make one."

Jared looked at him for a long time.

Milli made things. Leather, mostly. Bags and straps and harnesses, the lion's jaw, the frame for Ina's black case, the grip on Salty's greatsword. Every one with his cat's-paw stamped on it somewhere. He'd never once, as far as Jared knew, picked up a plane.

"It's wood," said Jared.

"I know."

"It's not leather. You can't stitch it."

"I know," said Milli. "That's why I'm asking."

***

They made it over three nights. In the yard behind the Hart, on the old trestles, out of oak from the timber man's long shed. The timber man laughed when Jared asked, and gave it to him for nothing. *You don't want my pole but you'll have my oak*, he said, and Jared said *yes*, and the timber man laughed again.

Milli was terrible at it. For the first night. His hands knew leather, which gives. Oak doesn't give. He planed against the grain and tore it. He measured twice and cut wrong anyway. Jared watched him do it and didn't fix it. He'd learned that from Ice, on the fountain rim, at New Year. You sat near the person making the thing and you let them make it.

On the second night Milli stopped fighting the wood. Jared saw it happen. He picked up the plane and put it on the oak and just stood there for a minute, feeling which way the grain went. Like a man feeling for a stone under black water with his front foot. Then he planed with it, not against it. A long pale curl came off and fell on the cobbles, whole.

"Oh," said Milli.

"Yeah," said Jared.

On the third night they hung it. Not in a house. They didn't have a house. Jared made a frame for it, two posts and a lintel, standing on its own in the middle of the yard behind the Hart, braced. A doorway with nothing on either side of it. And Milli hung the door in the frame on Old Joss's two hinges with his own hands. Then he stood back and pushed it.

It swung. All the way open, slow, without a sound. And all the way back.

"It doesn't go anywhere," said Milli.

"Not yet," said Jared.

Alice came out of the Hart's back door at that moment. She stopped on the step and looked at the door, standing on its own in the middle of the yard, swinging. At Milli beside it with sawdust in his hair, which he'd never once had in his life.

"What *is* that?" said Alice.

"A door," said Milli.

"To *where*?"

Milli thought about it. Properly. Jared watched him do it.

"Somewhere," said Milli.

Alice didn't say anything. She came down the step and walked across the yard, through the door, from one side of nothing to the other. She turned round on the far side and looked back at Milli through the doorway.

"It works," said Alice. And started crying, and laughing, both. Milli came through the door after her, and Jared went inside, because it wasn't his.

***

They took the roof down on the Saturday.

It took all day. Six hundred and twelve lanterns, one at a time, off seven spokes and three rings. Everybody. Ladders against the outer poles at the road ends, people on the ropes, people on the cobbles below with the crates. Each lantern lifted off its hook and passed down. Its candle out. Its red paper folded flat along the creases it had had for two years, and laid in a crate.

Jared did the middle. Nobody else touched the middle.

He went up the ladder against the green pole and took down the ring of twelve at the top himself. One at a time, the last of Chernn's candles in each, burned down to the dish. Both wicks, side by side, down to the bottom. Two flames. One candle. All the way.

Chernn was waiting at the bottom of the ladder for the dishes. She took them from him one at a time and wrapped each one in a twist of paper and didn't say anything. Deston stood beside her with the box.

"You're keeping them," said Jared, coming down. "The dishes."

"For the twenty-first," said Chernn.

Jared looked at her.

"Two weeks," said Chernn. She put the last dish in Deston's box. "I'll need dishes."

Beside her, Deston's ears went the colour of the banner. Jared didn't say anything. He'd known for months. Everybody had. The two of them had picked a day on the *Somewhere* bench in October, and it was two weeks after Alice's. *It's catching*, Alice had said. It had caught.

***

At the end of the day, with the last crate shut, the green pole stood in the round market with its seven ropes bare again. Seven iron rings round the top. No lanterns.

Jared stood at the foot of it with his hand on it.

He'd thought, at New Year, that he'd leave it up through the spring and take it down after. Bring it in. Put it on the trestles in the yard behind the Hart under an oilcloth, the way he'd done the old one. Until it turned into something. A bench. A beam. A door.

He found he didn't want to.

"It's in the way," said Ama Corrie.

She was standing at the top of the south steps with her broom. She'd been standing there most of the day, watching six hundred and twelve lanterns go into crates, not sweeping.

"I know," said Jared.

"People walk into it. At night. The chestnut man walked into it on Thursday."

"I know."

"You're going to leave it there," said Ama Corrie.

Jared looked up at the pole. Pale green ash, sixty years on a slope above a stream in the wind, bending and coming back. Raised twice. Held twice, once when it needed it and once when it didn't.

"Yes," said Jared.

Ama Corrie looked at it for a long time from the top of the steps.

"*Hm*," she said. Then, after a moment, as though it had cost her something: "It can stay."

***

Alice found him there at the third dawn.

She came across the empty market from the Hart with her hands in her pockets, the way she walked. The round town was quiet. The lanterns were in their crates. The roof was gone. Just the pole, bare, in the middle, and Jared at the foot of it.

She took her hand out of her pocket. In it was a square of red paper, folded small. One of Curse's, left over.

"For you," said Alice.

Jared took it. It was heavy. Much heavier than paper. He unfolded it on his palm.

A stone. Gold. Not yellow. Gold all the way through, the size of his fist, cut into a shape he'd never seen. Lots of small flat faces going all the way round, so that wherever he looked at it from, it caught the light from somewhere else. The light from the Hart's window. From the moon. From the lamp by the fountain. And at the back of it, deep in, a light of its own, like Kei's lamp.

[ *It will see from wherever it is put.* ]

Jared didn't say anything for a long time.

"I dug it," said Alice. "The week before. Down the north tunnel, at the cold place. Where I got the lion's eyes." She was looking at the pole, not at him. "I cut it every night at the long table. Curse saw. He said *it's not a stone yet*." She almost smiled. "It is now."

"Alice."

"You built me a roof twice," said Alice. "And a bench. Out of a pole that held. And then you went into a wood and found another one, because you didn't want a stranger's. And taught it to bend." She looked at him at last. "And then you stood under it every night for a week when it didn't need you."

"I said I would."

"I know." Her voice had gone. "So I made the pole an eye. For the top. So it can see." She swallowed. "Everything it's holding. And who."

***

He set it in the top of the pole that night.

Up the ladder, at the third dawn, with Azac holding the bottom and Alice at the foot of it with her hands in her pockets and her face wet. Above the seven iron rings, at the very top, where the crown of the ash had been on its slope. He cut a socket for it with his chisel, slowly, in the green wood. The stone sat in it as though it had grown there. The green ash closed round its base a little, the way green wood does, holding.

He climbed down.

They stood at the foot of the pole, the three of them, and looked up. At the very top of the bare green pole in the middle of the round market, a small gold light. Catching everything. The moon, the Hart's window, the lamp by the fountain, the first grey at the edge of the sky. And behind it all, its own.

"There," said Alice.

***

He went in through the south door at the end of the night to log out at the long table. He passed the counter.

Ama Corrie had the Roll open. She always did. She was writing, slowly, in her small hand, the way she wrote. She didn't look up.

Jared didn't mean to look. He just saw it, going past, the way you'd see anything on a counter. A name. His. *YoJareddo*, in his own hand, from a winter ago. And under it, a line. It had been empty. It wasn't now.

She'd written on it. In her hand. Not his.

*Held it when it didn't need holding.*

Jared stopped.

Ama Corrie didn't look up. She finished the last word and put down her pen and blotted it, carefully, the way she did everything. Then she shut the Roll.

"That happened in here," said Ama Corrie. To the Roll. "So I can write it."

Jared stood in the Hall of Roads at a quarter to eight in the morning. He was a practical man. He didn't go in for that kind of thing much.

He went in for it.

***

At the long table, at a quarter to eight, he sat at Alice's five by the window. He never sat there. Alice had pulled the chair out for him with her foot.

Kei stood up at the head of the table.

"Set one to watch," said Kei.

"*So the rest may sleep*," said sixty-two people and a sheep.

Alice leaned over, after, before the egg, and said in his ear, very quietly:

"How long have you known me?"

It was what Ice had asked him in August, at the top of the two hundred and twelve, with the old pole between them. *How long have you known her?*

Jared thought about it. Properly.

"Longer than I've known how to build anything," he said.

"You know how to build everything now."

"Yeah," said Jared. "Took a while." He looked down the long table. At Milli, beside her, with his arm along the back of her chair. At Leaka, at the far end, asleep on Curse's shoulder with her mouth open. At Ice, with Hyv on his arm, pink. At Chernn and Deston, two weeks off. At all of it. "Things are a long way off," he said, "until they aren't."

That was Ice's. He'd said it in August, on the first landing of the two hundred and twelve, with the old pole between them, about Alice. About *one day*. Jared had borrowed it.

"I know," said Alice. "He says that." She put her head on his shoulder for a moment, the way she put it on Milli's. "Y'all," she said. Which was his.

The egg took him at eight with her head on his shoulder. Out in the round market, in the first gold light of the morning, the eye at the top of the middle pole caught the sun and held it.
