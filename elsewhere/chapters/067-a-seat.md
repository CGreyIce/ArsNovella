Bee didn't move toward things. She waited for them to arrive.

She'd been like that on Earth for as long as she'd been anything, and the game hadn't changed it. At the dyers' bridge on Sunday evening, with the sun going down orange behind the mills, she sat where she always sat. On the parapet by the middle lantern, with her book open on her knees and her antennae up and her wings still. And she waited for Kestrel to arrive.

Hyv didn't wait. Hyv walked up and down.

She walked from the middle lantern to the south end of the bridge and back, eleven times. Bee counted, the way you'd count a heartbeat. She had her bow on her back and her glasses pushed up in her hair, the gold-rimmed real ones she'd brought in through the mirror and didn't need. Every time she got to the south end she looked across the water at the island. Every time she got back to the middle she looked at Bee. Bee didn't say anything. She drew the island, and the mills, and the dye-water running blue and red and yellow under the bridge in ribbons that didn't mix.

"Stop drawing," said Hyv, on the twelfth time.

"No," said Bee.

"You're drawing because you're nervous."

"I'm drawing because I've got a pencil," said Bee. "I'm a person with a pencil."

Hyv almost laughed. Then she stopped walking and stood at the parapet, looking across at the island, very still.

Old Wynn was in the doorway of the dye-works at the end of the bridge, with his blue hands folded on his stomach, watching. He'd known something was happening since the afternoon. He'd given Bee a pear without being asked, which meant he thought she needed one.

At a quarter past eight, Hyv's time, a woman in black came along the bank.

***

She came alone. That was the first thing Bee noticed, and she noticed it the way she noticed the grain in wood before she drew the shape. VANTA didn't go anywhere alone. Bee had watched them for a month, since the longest night. In thirties, and fives, and pairs, always in step, always quiet. But Kestrel came along the river path on her own, without hurrying, with her hands in the pockets of a plain black coat.

And she didn't have a camera orb.

Kestrel always had a camera orb. Kestrel streamed everything. Ten thousand people watched her walk to the market in the morning. And there was nothing over her shoulder, nothing at all. She'd turned it off, walking here.

Bee drew that. A woman with an empty space over her shoulder.

Kestrel stopped at the end of the bridge. She looked at Wynn in his doorway, and nodded. Wynn nodded back, slowly, with great dignity. Then she came along the bridge to the middle lantern and stopped, a little way off, the way you'd stop near something you didn't want to startle.

Up close, she was smaller than she looked on stream. Not small. Just not as big as her name. Short dark hair, plain black everything, nothing on her anywhere that wasn't for something. A short bow on her back, and a long knife at her hip, and a narrow, calm face that looked as if it had decided a long time ago not to waste anything. Including expressions.

"Hyvred," she said.

"Hi," said Hyv.

Kestrel looked at Bee.

"Bee," said Bee.

"The ladder," said Kestrel.

Bee looked at her.

"On the longest night," said Kestrel. "We could see it from the island. The ladder against the mill. It kept fading. Somebody kept drawing it again." She almost nodded. "Every hour."

"Every hour," said Bee.

"Hm," said Kestrel. And Bee understood she had just been paid a compliment, by someone who didn't usually pay them, and didn't know what to do with her face. So she went on drawing.

***

Kestrel sat on the parapet. Not close. Two lanterns away, with her boots on the cobbles and her hands still in her coat. She looked at the water for a while, the blue and the red and the yellow, before she said anything.

"Ask me things," she said. "Whatever you want."

Hyv didn't sit down. She stood with her back against the parapet beside Bee's knee, close enough that Bee could feel her shoulder, and looked at Kestrel.

"What would I do?" she said.

"Stand behind us," said Kestrel. "Somewhere high. See what we don't."

"What don't you see?"

"The back." Kestrel looked across at the island. "We're thirty people who are very good at what's in front of us. We practise it. We're the best at it there is. And on the longest night, three things came up our back piers in the dark, and none of us saw them." She paused. "You did. From the other side of a river, at three hundred paces, in two crosswinds."

"Good eyes," said Hyv.

"Good eyes," said Kestrel. It didn't sound like a compliment the way she said it. It sounded like a measurement.

"What's it like?" said Hyv. "VANTA. Being in it."

Kestrel thought about that properly. Bee watched her do it. She didn't answer fast.

"Quiet," she said. "We practise. We play. We win most things. We review what we didn't win, all of it, every time." She looked at her hands. "We don't do much else."

"Is it a job?"

"It's a job." Kestrel looked at her. "We pay. Every month. Properly. Not like the red ones, who pay you to stand on a bridge and shout numbers. We pay you to be good." She named a figure. Quietly, just the figure, the way you'd say the time.

Bee's pencil stopped.

She didn't mean it to. It just stopped. She looked down at the page so her face wouldn't do anything, and she thought about the figure. A salary. A real one, every month, from the best people in the world at this, for standing on a roof and seeing things. She thought about what it would mean for anybody. She thought about the train Hyv had read the message on, and the walkway home. Then she made herself start drawing again, because she was a person with a pencil, and that was what she was for.

Hyv hadn't moved.

"Why me?" she said. "Really. There must be other people who can see."

"There are other people who can see," said Kestrel. "There aren't other people who see *all* of it. At once. I watched your stream. The first one. The ceiling, all four corners." She was quiet for a moment. "You don't look at a fight. You look at everything, and the fight's in it. That's different. I can't teach that. I've tried to teach it for six years."

Hyv was very quiet.

Bee drew Kestrel. Not the way she was sitting now. The way she'd stood on the island on the longest night, turning round, looking across the water at a mill roof, with one hand lifted. *I see you.*

***

"My friend asked me something too," said Hyv eventually.

Kestrel turned her head.

"Last night," said Hyv. "On a call. He's making something. With people."

"The swamp guy's thing," said Kestrel.

"It isn't his." Hyv shook her head. "He said. On Sunday. *It's theirs. I'm not in it.* He said no to it."

Kestrel was quiet for a moment.

"Huh," she said. "Smart." She looked across at the island. "He's always smart. It's very annoying."

"It's Kei's," said Hyv. "You don't know him. Nobody knows him, really, except us. It hasn't got a name. It's twenty people who already know each other. Nobody's paid. Nobody's famous, except by accident." She took a breath. "And a friend of mine says it'll fall down."

"Will it?"

"Probably," said Hyv. "She says that's how you make it stay up."

Kestrel looked at her for a long time.

"And you want that," she said. Not a question. A measurement.

Hyv didn't answer at once. She turned round and leaned on the parapet beside Bee, and looked out at the whole of Brackenford in the dusk. Bee knew she was seeing it the way only Hyv saw it in here, edge to edge, all at once. The three rivers and the dozen bridges and the mill wheels and the island with its quiet lanterns. A cat on a wall. A boy at the south end of the bridge with a crossbow, who'd been there yesterday too.

"You want my eyes," said Hyv. "Kei wants me." She was quiet for a moment. "He said, *your eyes come with you*."

Kestrel didn't say anything.

"Nobody's ever told me my eyes were good," said Hyv. Quietly, to the river. Bee stopped drawing and didn't start again. "Not out there. Not till here. And the first person who ever did was you. On a roof. And I cried." She almost laughed. "I've still got the message."

"I know," said Kestrel. "I've got it too."

"And then Kei called, and he said he'd want me at the gate with my eyes *shut*." Hyv turned and looked at her. "So I think I want to be wanted like that. More than I want to be good." She paused. "Sorry. I don't know if that makes sense."

Kestrel sat on the parapet with her hands in her coat for a long time.

"It makes sense," she said.

***

She stood up.

She didn't argue. Bee had known she wouldn't. You could tell from the way she sat, from her face that didn't waste anything. A woman who'd asked a question properly and got an answer properly, and didn't need to ask it twice.

"Second time this month," said Kestrel.

"What?"

"Somebody in this town's said no to me. For their friends." She looked along the river, toward the inn by the east bridge where a man with a blank charter had a room on the first floor. "The last one said *it'll work out*." She almost smiled. "I told him I thought he'd say that. I thought you would too."

"Sorry," said Hyv.

"Don't be." Kestrel looked at her. "The seat stays. Not a favour. Not a trial. If your thing falls down, or if it doesn't, and one day you want to stand behind thirty people who don't look at the back, it's there." She paused. "No strings. I mean that."

"I know you do."

Kestrel turned to go. Then she stopped, and looked at Bee, and at the book on Bee's knees, and at the drawing on it.

Bee looked at the drawing too. It had come out well. Better than well. A woman on an island in the dark, turning round, one hand lifted, across a river.

She tore it out. Carefully, along the spine, the way Ambry had taught her. She held it out.

"It'll stay," she said. "It's not a making one. It's just a drawing."

Kestrel took it. She looked at it for a long time, in the lantern-light, on the dyers' bridge, with old Wynn in his doorway pretending not to watch. Her face did something. Not much. Something.

"Huh," she said.

She folded it once, very carefully, and put it inside her coat.

"Good hands," she said to Bee. And then she went back along the bank the way she'd come, alone, without a camera orb, without hurrying. Bee watched her go until she was a shape against the island's lanterns, and then not even that.

***

For a long time, neither of them said anything.

Then Bee turned back a page in her book. Past Kestrel's empty place, past the dye-water, to a page near the back. On it, drawn on Saturday, in great detail, was a door. A very ugly door. It was crooked and badly hung, with a knocker shaped like something rude. It had been drawn, with real care and some joy, to be the ugliest door in Brackenford.

She tore it out, too.

She leaned over the parapet and let it go. It dropped onto the dye-water and floated away under the bridge, slowly, turning, on a ribbon of blue.

"You drew that in advance," said Hyv.

"I draw everything in advance," said Bee. "Then I find out if I need it."

They watched it go round the bend toward the mill race, a small pale square on the blue, until Hyv couldn't see it and then Bee couldn't either.

"In?" said Bee.

"In," said Hyv.

"Both of us."

"Both of us."

Hyv sent the message. Bee watched her do it, standing at the parapet with one finger moving in the air, slowly, the way she'd type on a phone held close to her face on a train.

*talked to her. properly. we're in. both of us.*

Kei answered in a minute, from Australia, where it was nearly midnight on a Sunday.

*Sixteen.*

And then, a minute after that, by itself: *Thank you, Hyv.*

Hyv put her hand over her mouth, on the dyers' bridge in the lantern-light, and for a second Bee thought she was going to cry again, the way she had on the roof. She didn't. She laughed instead, into her hand, and leaned against Bee's shoulder. Bee put up with it.

Old Wynn, in his doorway, went inside and came back out with a second pear.
