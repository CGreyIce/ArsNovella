The woman with ten thousand people sat on his step every evening that week, and Kite didn't know what to do about it.

He kept the gate. He'd kept it since February, the way Wendy had told him. In it, hands at his sides, the question, *fair*, and the steps after. He'd kept it through the summer with half the guild in Masmark, and through ten nights at a milestone with somebody who'd said *you*. He'd kept it on the day the twelve came home, when the swamp guy turned all the way round on the bottom step and gave him three answers.

He'd never had to keep it with the leader of Meridian sitting on the top step every evening at the second dusk, with her feet flat on the stone, watching the road.

She didn't say much. That was the strange part. He'd thought somebody who ran ten thousand people would talk all the time. Harlan had, in October, a year ago; Harlan had talked from the moment he came up the steps to the moment he went down them. She didn't. She sat on the step with her chin on her knees and watched the round market go by. The fountain, the chestnut stall, the people going in and out of the Hart. And at the bottom of the south road, the milestone.

"She's there every night," said Aurelie, on Tuesday.

"Since May," said Kite.

"Waiting."

"Yes."

"For what?"

Kite thought about it.

"For Kei to say," he said. "On the longest night. He said ask me again then. Here." He looked at the milestone. "She said *I'll be here*."

Aurelie didn't say anything for a long time.

"And you sit with her," she said.

"Most nights."

"Why?"

"Because it's the bit people forget," said Kite.

***

He'd been a bird once. That was how Rafi's letter to Sedge had put it, the spring before last: *a man who used to be a bird*. A little dark bird stitched on his collar, with Nightjar, on the roads, in the first winter. He'd taken fourteen people's days. Chris had counted them, from the public death log, at the long table, and said the number out loud. *Fourteen.* And Wendy had said *that's three*, and Gigi had brought him a cup of something horrible, and the long table had argued round the fire on a Sunday about whether to trust him.

They'd said yes. He still didn't entirely know why.

He'd stopped asking himself. It didn't help. What helped was the gate. Standing in it every morning with his hands where people could see them, asking people who'd stand next to them, saying *fair*, and sitting on the steps after. Doing the thing that had been done for him, over and over, every day, until it stopped being a thing he did and started being a thing he was.

*Some people are doors*, Wendy had said.

He was a door. He'd got used to it.

He wasn't used to a giant on his step.

***

On Wednesday, Aurelie went down to the milestone.

Kite saw her go. He was in the gate. It was the second dusk; he'd been about to go down himself with two cups, the way he did. Kotori had them ready on the end of the counter in the Hart's kitchen. The chipped one for Ash, and the plain one for him.

Aurelie got up off the top step without saying anything. She went down the steps, across the market, past the fountain and the ring of nine dark stones, to the bottom of the south road. And stopped in front of the milestone.

Ash looked up at her.

Kite didn't go down. He stood in the gate with his hands at his sides and watched. It wasn't his to go down. Not yet. You didn't go down while somebody else was asking.

He couldn't hear them. The market was loud at the second dusk. But he could see them. The small woman in plain blue standing in front of the milestone. And the woman with two blades across her knees on the base of it, looking up, with her calm narrow face.

They talked for a long time.

Then Aurelie sat down.

Not on the base of the milestone. On the cobbles beside it, a sword's length off. The way Kite sat. The way Kei had sat, in August, with two cups.

Kite went into the Hart.

***

"Three cups," he said, to Kotori.

Kotori looked up from the kettles. She looked past him, out of the kitchen window, across the dark market to the bottom of the south road. At two people, now, by the milestone, one on the base and one on the cobbles.

She didn't ask. She reached for a third cup.

"Is that the one with the castle?" she said.

"Yes."

"She's very small," said Kotori.

"Ama Corrie gave her her mother's chair."

Kotori stopped with the pot in her hand.

"*Ama Corrie's mother's chair*," she said. "The one in the cupboard behind the counter. With the mended seat." She put the pot down. "Nobody's ever sat in that. I asked once, in the first winter, and Ama Corrie said *that's not for sitting in, that's for keeping*." She looked out of the window again. "She gave it to her?"

"On Sunday."

Kotori was quiet for a moment. Then she poured three cups, very carefully, of something horrible and good for you.

"Take her the plain one," she said. "Ash gets the chipped one. You get—" She looked at the third cup. "You get the new one. It doesn't have a chip yet."

***

He took them down.

He walked across the market with three cups in his hands and stopped in front of the milestone. They both looked up at him.

He gave Ash the chipped one. He gave Aurelie the plain one. He sat down on the cobbles on Ash's other side, a sword's length, with the new one.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"She asked me to come to Meridian," said Ash.

Kite looked at her.

"Just now," said Ash. She was looking at the cup in her hands, the chipped one. "She said she's got sixty-one Halls. She said I could walk into any of them and nobody would ask about the bird. She said there's a Hall on the east coast where nobody's ever heard of Nightjar. Where I could start." She drank, and made the face. "She said *come and be somebody*."

Aurelie, on the other side, didn't say anything. She was looking at the fountain.

Kite thought about the bread stall on the east rim of Masmark in July. Rafi, with his bread. *Come and be somebody.* And Rafi saying *I pick fenwort*.

"What did you say?" said Kite.

"I said I'm waiting," said Ash.

The fountain went. A lamp came on in the baker's window.

"I said I asked somebody a question," said Ash. "In August. At this milestone. And he said *ask me again on the longest night*. And it might be no." She turned the chipped cup in her hands. "I said I'm not going anywhere till he answers."

Aurelie turned her head and looked at her across Kite.

"Even if it's no," she said.

"Even if it's no," said Ash. "Then I'll know." She looked up the steps at the gate. "I've been waiting four months. I can wait four more." She almost smiled; Kite had seen her do it twice. "I've never waited for anything in my life. Nightjar didn't wait. We went where the money was, when it was there. I'd like to find out what it's like. Waiting for something that might be no."

Aurelie looked at her for a very long time.

"I waited six years," she said. "For something that was always no." She looked down at the plain cup in her hands. "I was second for the first time in my life in July. By six seconds. To him." She nodded up the steps, at the gate, at the Hall, at the banner room somewhere inside it with a man in the first of nine. "It was the best thing that ever happened to me. I still don't know why."

"Because you stopped waiting for it to be yes," said Ash.

Aurelie went very still.

Kite looked from one of them to the other. The woman with ten thousand people. And the woman who'd been left by twelve. Sitting either side of him at the bottom of the south road of the round town, at the second dusk, drinking something horrible from Kotori's kitchen.

He didn't say anything. It wasn't his to say.

***

They sat there till the third dawn. All three of them. Aurelie didn't go back up the steps. Vane came down at midnight, tall and still, and stood behind her for a while. Then sat down on the cobbles behind her without being asked, and stayed.

Four, then. At a milestone.

Kite had sat here a hundred nights. He'd never once sat here with four.

***

He asked Drew on Thursday.

He found him at the forge, the way you always found Drew. Soot on his hands, at the anvil beside Ina's, hammering something small. He looked up when Kite came in, and then at Kite's feet. Drew always looked at feet. He'd learned it, he said, watching people in lobbies, a long time ago, before the game. You could tell everything about somebody from where they put their weight.

"Your left's heavier," said Drew. "It always is. But it's more, today."

"I want to ask you something."

Drew put the hammer down.

Kite thought about it. He'd thought about it all night at the milestone, with four. He'd thought about the longest night last year, on the Waymeet road, coming at a run out of arrows with three Unlit behind him. And Drew stepping out into the road and holding it. *You were running toward the light.* He'd thought about Kei's board, the one that said *nobody alone, threes at least*.

"The longest night," said Kite. "Kei's going to put people in threes again. On the roads. At the doors." He looked at the anvil. "Ash is going to ask him again. That night. Here. He said."

"I know," said Drew.

"She can't hold a road on her own," said Kite. "Nobody alone. And she's not on the Roll. She's not on Kei's sheet." He stopped. "But if she's in a three, she's not alone. And she can be somewhere. Doing something. When she asks him." He looked at Drew. "So she's not just sitting at a milestone. So he can see."

Drew didn't say anything for a long time.

"You want to be her three," he said.

"Yes."

"And you need a third."

"Yes," said Kite.

He made himself say it. He'd never asked anybody for anything. Not in this game. He'd walked toward a gate once, and sat at a milestone, and let people decide. He'd never once walked up to somebody and said *I need you*.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Kite.

He hadn't meant to say it like that. It was the gate's question. It just came out, the way it came out every morning.

Drew looked at him. At his face, and then at his feet, where his weight was.

"You," said Drew.

***

Kite stood in the forge with soot in the air and didn't say anything.

"I held a road for you once," said Drew. "On the longest night. The first one. The Waymeet road. You were running toward the light with three behind you and no arrows." He picked the hammer up again and looked at it. "I didn't know you. You'd been in Nightjar. Ash had put a blade in Ice's leg in the fen. I didn't know anything about you except that you were running toward the light." He put the hammer down on the thing he'd been making. *Tink.* "So I stood in the road."

"I know."

"You never asked why."

"I asked," said Kite. "You said *you were running toward the light*."

"That was the why," said Drew. "That was all of it." He looked up. "She's been sitting at a milestone for four months. In front of the light. That's the same thing, just slower."

Kite couldn't say anything.

"I'm the one who stays," said Drew. "I stayed on a keystone. Tiger says I'm the one who stays." He went back to his hammering. *Tink.* "I'll stay on a road with you two. All night."

***

He told Ash at the second dusk, at the milestone, with two cups. Aurelie had gone back up to the banner room; Kei had asked her something about Meridian's Halls in the east, and she'd gone in to answer it.

"Drew," said Ash.

"Drew."

"The one who stays." She was looking at the chipped cup. "On the keystone. I watched it eleven times."

"He'll stay on a road with us," said Kite. "On the longest night. If Kei puts us on one." He looked up the road. "I haven't asked Kei. It's not mine to say where. But I've asked Drew, and he said *you*."

Ash looked at him.

"He said *you* to you," she said.

"Yes."

"And you said—"

"I haven't said anything," said Kite. "I came straight here."

Ash put the cup down on the base of the milestone. She looked at him for a long time with her calm narrow face. He'd known that face since the first winter, on the roads, at crossroads at dusk. He'd never seen it like this.

"You asked somebody," she said. "For me."

"For us," said Kite.

***

That night, at the third dawn, Aurelie came down the steps again. Without Vane, this time. She stood in the gate beside Kite, which nobody did. And looked down at the market and the fountain and the milestone, where Ash was asleep against the stone with her blades across her knees.

"I've got sixty-one Halls," said Aurelie.

"I know."

"Every one of them has a door," said Aurelie. "Big ones. Iron. Two of them have gates. The one in the far west has a gatehouse with a portcullis." She was quiet. "And nobody stands in any of them. They're just doors. People go in and out."

Kite didn't say anything.

"Nobody asks," said Aurelie. "Nobody asks anybody anything. You walk into a Meridian Hall and there's a clerk with a ledger and you write your name. Ten thousand names. And nobody's ever once asked one of them who'd stand next to them." She looked at him. "I didn't know you could. Before February."

"Wendy asked first," said Kite. "At the round town. On the first day, when it was ten people and a swamp. I just do what she did."

"Who taught her?"

Kite thought about it.

"Nobody," he said. "I asked her once. She said it was just the thing she'd want somebody to ask her. If she was walking up to a door on her own."

Aurelie stood in the gate beside him for a long time.

"I'm going to write to Harlan," she said. "In the morning."

"What for?"

"To put somebody in the door," said Aurelie. "Of the castle. On the west rim. Somebody who'll stand in it, with their hands at their sides. And ask." She almost laughed. "Harlan will want to do it himself. He'll ask everybody, very loudly, and then tell them his answer before they've said theirs."

"That's fine," said Kite. "That's still asking."

"Is it?"

"It's somebody standing in a door," said Kite. "On purpose. So nobody walks through it on their own." He looked at the milestone. "That's most of it. The rest is just sitting down after."

Aurelie looked at him for a long, long time.

Then she did a thing Kite would think about for weeks. She took her hands out of the pockets of her plain blue coat, and let them hang at her sides, where people could see them. And stood like that, in the south gate of the Hall of Roads, beside him. Not saying anything. The way he stood.

She was very bad at it. She fidgeted. She kept looking at him to see if she was doing it right.

"You're doing it fine," said Kite.

"It's harder than it looks."

"It's the hardest thing I've ever done," said Kite. "You get used to it."

***

On Saturday, Kei came out onto the steps at the second dusk with his map case and stood beside the gate. He didn't say anything for a while. He looked at the market, and the milestone, and Kite in the gate.

"Tomorrow," he said. "At the long table. She's going to ask."

"I know," said Kite.

"I'm going to say yes," said Kei.

Kite looked at him. Kei never said what he was going to say. He said it at the long table, with everybody, or at the gate. He didn't say it on the steps on a Saturday to the person keeping the door.

"Why are you telling me?" said Kite.

Kei looked at him for a long moment.

"Because you sat on the step with her," he said. "On Sunday. When she came up the road. Before anybody else had said anything to her." He looked at the gate. "And because she's been sitting at the milestone with Ash all week. And you've been taking three cups." He paused. "I wanted the door to know first."

Kite didn't say anything.

"Drew told me," said Kei. "About the road. The longest night. You and Ash and him." He opened his map case and didn't take anything out of it. "I haven't decided where. But I've written it down. The three of you." He closed the case. "It's on the sheet."

"She's not on the Roll."

"She's not on the Roll," agreed Kei. "She's on the sheet." He looked down at the milestone, where Ash was sitting with her back against the stone. "Those are different things. Tell her."

He went back inside.

Kite stood in the gate with his hands at his sides for a long time. Then he went down the steps, across the market, to the milestone. He didn't stop at the Hart for cups. He forgot. It was the first time since May.

Ash looked up at him.

"You're on the sheet," said Kite.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. He watched her face do the thing it had done in August, when the swamp guy came up the road and said *not today*. Something nobody in Nightjar had ever seen.

He sat down. A sword's length.

Then, after a while, a bit closer.
