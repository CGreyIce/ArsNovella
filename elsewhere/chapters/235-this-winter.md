Suzu carried her out every morning at the first dawn, and every morning she was lighter.

He didn't say so. He didn't say most things. He'd come into the banner room at a quarter to eight, out there, and the sun coming up over the east road, in here. And she'd be awake in her chair by the north door, with the blanket round her and the jar on the table in front of her. Looking at it. And she'd look up when he came in.

"Is it morning?" she'd say.

"It's morning."

"Then take me out," she'd say. "I want to see the road."

And he'd pick up the chair. With her in it. And carry it out through the north door, which stuck, and Gabe had fixed, and you had to mind anyway. And set it down on the top of the north steps facing up the north road, the way she liked it. And put the jar in her lap, and her root hands round it.

And sit on the step below.

He'd been doing it since April, since the twelve went south. He'd done it every morning while Ice was in Masmark. Before Ama Corrie was up, before Kotori had the kettles on. A hundred and twenty mornings, near enough. He hadn't missed one.

And every morning, the chair was a little lighter when he picked it up.

***

He'd known Isa eight months. Since the dawn after the longest night.

He'd known Ice a lot longer. Longer than the wind game, and the guild Ice had dragged him into one winter. Longer than most of the others. He'd lived in Ice's guest room in Malacca since the eleventh day of this game. *I need him*, Ice had told Vivian. He'd never said it to Suzu. He'd never needed to.

But he'd never known anybody like Isa.

She'd been in the dark three hundred years. Not her; her mothers. Her line. Mother to daughter, on a hill in still water past the edge of the world, keeping one lamp. And she was the last. No daughter. She'd walked to the edge once, the way they all did, and said *not yet*, and gone back. And then one year a road of lights had come on behind her, and she'd walked home. All the way, behind the dark, on the longest night, relighting ninety-four lamps as she came. And sat in a chair by a north door that had been waiting for her for three hundred years.

And she'd given him the light.

*It can be home for a bit*, he'd said. On Christmas Eve, with the jar open between them. And she'd said *go on*. And it had gone in.

Eight months, it had been home.

***

"It's leaning," said Isa.

It was the Tuesday, the twenty-second of August. The first dawn. He'd carried her out and set her down and put the jar in her lap and sat on the step below. And they'd sat there in the morning with the north road going away over the moor in front of them. Ninety-four grey stones with a little black cage on the back of every one, facing the wrong way, and a little flame in every cage.

He looked at her.

"What's leaning?"

"The lights," said Isa. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking up the road, a long way up, where the stones got small. "On the road. Look at the far ones."

He looked.

He couldn't see much. The near ones, the first few stones past Kite's milestone, the flames in their cages small and steady in the morning, the way they'd been since September a year ago. Further up, smaller. Further still, just points.

"I can't see," said Suzu.

"Then get somebody who can," said Isa.

***

He got Hyvred.

She was in the banner room, in the chair that had been Bee's, beside the first of the nine. A minute before Ice, the way she always was, so she could see him come out of the dark. He wasn't in yet. Suzu stopped beside her chair.

She looked up at him. She'd always looked at him a bit warily, Suzu thought. As if he were a book in a language she could nearly read.

"Isa wants you," said Suzu. "To look at the road."

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment. Then she got up without a word and went out through the north door with him, onto the steps.

Isa looked up at her.

"The girl with the horns," said Isa. "You see edge to edge. In here. He says."

"Yes," said Hyvred.

"Look at the far lights," said Isa. "Tell me what they're doing."

Hyvred looked up the north road.

Suzu watched her do it. He'd seen her look at things before, on the stream, at the bottom of a lake, at the top of a bell tower. She didn't turn her head. She just looked, and you could see her see all of it at once. The whole north road, ninety-four stones, all the way to where they went out of sight over the moor.

Her face changed.

"They're leaning," she said.

***

"The flames," said Hyvred. "In the cages. The near ones are straight up. But from about the fortieth, they're not." She was very still. "They're leaning north. Not much. A bit. Like there's a wind, except there isn't a wind, the grass isn't moving." She looked further. "And the further up, the more. The last ones, the ones at the top of the moor, the ninetieth, the ninety-fourth. They're leaning like there's somebody breathing on them from the north."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"It's noticed," said Isa.

Suzu looked at her.

"The dark," said Isa. "It's noticed the middle's gone." She was looking up the road, at the far lamps she couldn't see either. Her bright black eyes. "I said it was slow. It is slow. It's very slow. But it's been eight months. And the hill's been dark eight months, for the first time in three hundred years." She folded her root hands tighter round the jar. "And it's started looking."

"For the middle," said Suzu.

"For the middle," said Isa. "It doesn't know where it's gone. So it's leaning. Every light it can feel, it leans toward. To see if that's it." She looked down at the jar in her lap. "The road's lit. Ninety-four. So it leans toward the road."

"And the road comes here," said Hyvred, very quietly.

"The road comes here," said Isa.

***

Suzu sat on the step below her chair for a long time after Hyvred went back in.

He thought about the longest night, last year. The dark coming south all at once, a hundred miles long. Because it had forgotten where it was. The north door two shields wide, and the iron basket the smiths had made, and four hundred and fourteen lights, and the whole guild holding the round town till the dawn.

He thought about Isa, at the table, in April, telling him what the hill was for. *So it knows where the middle is.*

He thought about what he'd said. On the stool, beside her chair, with Kotori listening from the kitchen door. *I'll take it. When it's time. Not this year. Not this winter. But some year.*

*Some year.*

He'd meant it. He'd meant every word. He'd go. He'd carry it back. Kotori would come with tea. Ice would come. Chernn had given him a candle for the road, tall and white, so he could see his feet without using the lamp.

But he'd said *some year* the way Hana had said *one day*. He knew he had. He'd heard Milli say it on Sunday, at the long table, standing up. *One day isn't a day. It's just a thing we say so we don't have to say a day.*

He looked at the jar in Isa's lap. Glowing through the clay, faintly, the way it had for eight months.

*It can be home for a bit.*

It had been home for a bit.

***

He told Kei at the second dawn.

Kei was in the banner room at the head of the long table with his maps. He had the north sheet out, the one he'd drawn in March a year ago, the first time anybody had gone up the north road. And the old Roll's map beside it, the one from the black book. With the road going north from the round town through the old hall and the twenty-two and off the top of the page, to a place Kei had drawn very small, in pencil, from what Isa said. A hill in still water, with a ring round it.

Suzu stopped in front of him.

Kei looked up.

Suzu didn't say anything for a long time. He didn't know how to start. He never started things. He was the one who said *good* when somebody else had started them. He'd said maybe four hundred words to Deston in all the years he'd known him, and every one of them had been an answer.

"The lights are leaning," said Suzu.

Kei went still.

"On the north road," said Suzu. "From about the fortieth. Hyvred saw. Isa says the dark's noticed. That the middle's gone." He looked at the map, at the hill in pencil off the top of the page. "It's looking. Every light it can feel, it leans toward. The road's lit. So it leans toward the road."

Kei didn't say anything. He looked down at the north sheet. At the ninety-four stones, every one marked, every one with a little circle beside it for a lit lamp.

"It'll come here," said Kei.

"It always comes here," said Suzu. "Isa says. But this year it'll come here *looking*. Because the middle's here." He looked at the north door, at the far end of the banner room, open, with the old woman in her chair on the steps beyond it. "In a jar. On a table. In front of her chair."

***

"So," said Kei.

He said it the way he said it at the start of everything. Very calm. With his pencil in his hand, as if he were about to draw something.

Suzu took a breath.

He'd never asked for anything. Not really. He'd wanted things. He'd wanted shares, on profit day, a long time ago. And he'd sat at a table with Vivian and Ice and not said yes or no. Until Ice read the stillness and said *whatever he says*, and it had come out of him on its own. *Not zero.* He'd wanted to carry the light. And he'd said *I'll take it*, on a stool, without deciding, the way *go on* had come out of him in the Long Watch at the eighty-first lantern.

He'd never stood in front of somebody and asked.

"This winter," said Suzu.

Kei looked at him.

"Not some year," said Suzu. "This winter. Before the longest night. I'll carry it back. To the hill. And light the ring. So the dark knows where the middle is again." He swallowed. "So it doesn't come here looking."

The banner room was quiet. It was the second dawn, and most of the guild was out. Moonlight Harvest on the high fells, the road of lights in threes, Hana's five on the roof. The long table was half empty.

Kei put his pencil down.

"How far is it?" he said.

"I don't know," said Suzu. "Isa walked it. Behind the dark. One night. Thirty-five hours." He thought about it. "But she was walking south. With the dark ahead of her. Clearing the way." He looked at the hill on the map. "Going north, it'll be in front of us. All the way. Past the last stone. Where there's nothing to see by."

"Weeks," said Kei.

"Weeks."

"In the dark."

"In the dark," said Suzu. "With a light we can't use. And a candle to see our feet."

Kei looked at him for a very long time.

"You're asking," he said.

"I'm asking," said Suzu.

***

Kei picked up the pencil again.

He didn't say yes. Suzu hadn't thought he would; Kei didn't say yes to things at the head of the table on a Tuesday morning. He said yes at the long table on a Sunday, with everybody, or at the gate. He drew instead. On the north sheet, past the top of the page, where the road went off the edge into nothing. A line. Going on north. Dotted.

And beside it, very small, a word.

*Winter.*

"Tell them on Sunday," said Kei. "Everybody. At the long table. And ask who'll go." He looked up. "Nobody alone. Threes at least. It's on the board."

"I know."

"And Suzu."

Suzu looked at him.

"You've never asked me anything," said Kei. "In all this time. You've never once come to the head of the table and asked." He put the pencil down. "Thank you."

Suzu didn't know what to say. So he said what he always said.

"Good," said Suzu.

***

He told Kotori at the second dusk, in the Hart's kitchen, at the kettles.

He didn't get very far. He came in through the kitchen door and she looked round from the stove, and he said "Kotori," and she said "I know."

He stopped.

"I haven't said it," said Suzu.

"You don't have to," said Kotori. "Hyv told me about the lights. At the second dawn. And then you went to the head of the table and stood there for a long time." She turned back to the stove. "You never stand at the head of the table."

She reached up to the shelf above the kettles and took down a tin. A plain one, dented, the kind Joss made for anybody who asked. She put it on the table in front of him and took the lid off.

Tea. Dark leaves, curled small and tight, with a smell that went right up the inside of his head.

"I've been making it since April," said Kotori. "Since you said *some year* on the stool and I said *somebody'll need tea*." She was looking at the tin, not at him. "I didn't know when. So I started. I've been drying it in the Hart's attic. Foraged on the north road, in threes, with Tiger. A leaf that grows on the backs of the waystones, where the lamps are. Nowhere else."

Suzu read it twice.

"You started in April," he said.

"I started in April," said Kotori. "I thought it'd be years. I thought I'd have a cellar full of it by the time you asked." She put the lid back on. "There's eleven tins. That's enough for a winter. If nobody drinks it too fast."

"Gabe'll drink it too fast."

"Gabe's not coming," said Kotori. "Gabe's tanking Moonlight Harvest on the longest night. Rae says. She's already decided." She looked at him at last. "I'm coming. I said I would. And I won't say sorry."

"I know."

"Not once," said Kotori. "The whole way. Even in the dark." She pushed the tin across the table to him. "You carry the light. I carry the tea."

He looked at the tin for a long time.

"Thank you," said Suzu.

It was, Deston would tell her later, about the fourth time he'd ever heard Suzu say it.

***

He told Ice that night. Not in here. Out there.

In the kitchen of the flat in Malacca, at nine, with the fan going and the kettle on, the way they'd done a thousand times since the eleventh day. Gabe was in the spare room on a call with somebody, loud. Through the wall, on the other side, Ice's parents had gone to bed. A presence.

Ice was at the counter making tea. Suzu stood in the doorway.

"This winter," said Suzu.

Ice turned round with the kettle in his hand.

He didn't say anything. He looked at Suzu for a long time. The way he'd looked at him for years. Since a winter in a game with wind in its name, when he'd dragged Suzu into a guild and Suzu had never once asked why. He put the kettle down. Without looking where it went.

"Not some year," said Ice.

"No."

"The lights are leaning," said Ice. "Hyv told me. On a letter. At the second dawn." He was quiet. "I wondered if you'd say."

"I said," said Suzu. "To Kei. I asked." He looked at the floor. "I've never asked Kei anything."

"I know."

"He said thank you."

Ice's face did the thing at the corner of his mouth.

"He would," he said.

They stood in the kitchen, the two of them, with the kettle going quiet on the counter. Suzu thought about all the years. The wind game. The guest room. A bus station, Melaka Sentral, on the eleventh day, and a contract signed the same afternoon. The Long Watch, and eighty-one lanterns, and *go on*. A jar from Larkhollow, and *Tamsin's*, and a flame three hundred years old going in.

"Will you come?" said Suzu.

He'd never asked Ice anything either.

Ice looked at him for a long, long time.

"Yes," he said.

***

He carried her out again in the morning. Wednesday. The first dawn.

She was lighter. He felt it when he picked up the chair. Not much. A bit. The way she'd been a bit lighter every morning for a hundred and twenty mornings, so slowly he'd stopped being able to tell whether he was imagining it. Kotori said it was him; that he'd got stronger, carrying her. Ama Corrie, when he asked, looked at him over her spectacles and said nothing at all.

He set her down on the top of the north steps, facing up the road. He put the jar in her lap. Her root hands came round it.

He sat on the step below.

The far lamps were leaning. He couldn't see it. But he knew.

"Isa," said Suzu.

"Mm."

"This winter," said Suzu.

She didn't say anything for a long time. She sat in her chair on the top of the north steps of the Hall of Roads, at the first dawn, with three hundred years in her lap. Looking up a road of ninety-four lights she'd walked down once, behind the dark, relighting them one by one as she came.

"Not some year," she said.

"No."

"You asked."

"I asked Kei," said Suzu. "And Ice. And Kotori, last night, in the kitchen. She said *I know* before I'd finished." He looked up the road. "I'm going to ask the long table on Sunday. Who'll come."

Isa looked down at him from her chair, on the step below her. Her bright black eyes.

"You've never asked anybody anything," she said. "You told me. On the stool. In April. You said *I'll take it*, and then you said *I didn't decide, it just came out*." She folded her root hands round the jar. "This didn't just come out."

"No," said Suzu. "I decided."

Isa Corrie looked at him for a very long time.

Then she took one root hand off the jar, very slowly, and put it on his head. On the top of it, flat, the way you'd put your hand on the lid of something to keep it. And left it there.

"*Hm*," she said.

He knew what *hm* meant, from a Corrie. He'd known since Christmas.

*Kept.*
