Deston's alarm went off at six, and the pod was the first thing he saw.

It took up the whole end of his room, between the bed and the window, white and smooth in the grey light. It was looking at him. He knew it wasn't, but it was. He'd put a towel over it on Tuesday night so he could sleep, and that had been worse, because then it looked like a pod with a towel over it, which was somehow sadder.

He got up. He made coffee. He sat down at his desk with his back to the pod and opened his notes.

Twelve days until the last paper. He had a timetable. He'd made it on Sunday night, before any of this, in four colours, and stuck it to the wall above the desk with tape. Thursday: two past-year papers, one chapter, flashcards. It was a good timetable. It had been made by a man who didn't own a pod.

He did the first past-year paper from six until seven-forty. He did it properly, too, with a timer, no phone, the pod humming faintly behind him the whole time like somebody in the next room trying not to make noise.

At seven-forty-nine his phone lit up on the desk, face down, and buzzed once.

He didn't have to look. *Ice is live.*

He looked at the timetable. He looked at the past-year paper. He looked at the second past-year paper, still clipped together, unmarked, smug.

He put his pen down.

"Mornings," he said out loud to the timetable. "Mornings in there. Afternoons and evenings out here. One hour at night. That's the deal."

The timetable said nothing. He took that as agreement.

***

Fernwick was waiting at dawn, the way Dec had said it would be. Mist on the channel, frogs, and the three of them in a row on the end of Sedge's porch, sitting up out of nothing with their boots off.

Deston had worked out on Tuesday that it was the moment he loved most. Not the moonbells, not the door, not even the choir. It was this: opening his eyes in the morning and finding the other two already there beside him, close enough to touch, blinking at the same mist.

"Morning," said Xav.

"Morning," said Dec.

"I did a whole past-year paper," said Deston.

"Did you mark it?"

"I'm going to mark it this afternoon."

"You're not going to mark it this afternoon," said Xav, pulling on a wet boot.

***

The thing nobody had told him about the book was how much of it was *listening*.

He'd thought, on the first day, that Accord was a spell. You read the line, the spell went off, everybody got a bit better for a while. That was how it worked in every game he'd ever played: you pressed the button, the bar filled, the buff appeared.

It wasn't a spell. It was a *count-in*.

He found it out properly on Thursday, at the edge of the old fen, with four leeches on a narrow place in the reeds and Dec's shield out low and sloped and Xav already gone sideways into the water. He'd read the line the way he'd been reading it, carefully, in time, and it had taken. He felt it go out of him like a breath and settle on the other two. And he'd *felt* them. That was the part nobody had warned him about. For as long as it lasted, he could feel where they were in the beat. Dec's weight sitting back, waiting. Xav low and coiled, waiting. Both of them waiting for the same moment, and he could feel the moment coming, like the bar before the chorus, and he could feel when one of them was about to be early.

Xav was about to be early.

Deston didn't think about it. He sang the last word of the line half a beat late, on purpose. He held it, a long low note he'd held a thousand times in the back row, and Xav, who couldn't possibly have known why, *waited*. He came in on the end of the note, exactly together with Dec's cut, and the leech came apart between them like paper.

[ Accord · Grade 71%
The many moved as one. ]

"What did you do?" said Xav.

He'd turned round in the water, both daggers still up, and he was staring at Deston.

"I don't know," said Deston. "You were going to go early. I held the note."

"How did you know I was going to go early?"

"I could feel it." He heard how that sounded. "I could—when it's on, I can feel where you are. In the count. Both of you."

Xav looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at Dec, who had turned round too and was grinning the grin he'd had at thirteen when somebody did something cool.

"That's not a buff," said Xav. "That's a conductor."

***

He went to see Brother Amon that afternoon, before revision, because he couldn't not.

The chapel stood by the east jetty, small and round like the houses, with a reed roof and a single room inside, cool and dim and smelling of candle wax and old paper. There were benches for the children, low ones, and a lectern, and on the wall behind the lectern a carving of a heron, wings half open, just about to take off. Brother Amon was asleep on the bench at the front with his chin on his chest.

He woke up as Deston came in. He was very, very old, and had no teeth, and smiled anyway.

"The boy with the book," he said.

"I held a note," said Deston, and sat down on the low bench beside him with his knees up round his ears, and told him.

The old man listened all the way through without saying anything. When Deston had finished, he nodded slowly, as if Deston had told him the weather.

"Yes," he said. "That's what it does."

"But the window, the—it only says *keep time with you*."

"Hm." Brother Amon held out his hand, and Deston gave him the book, and he opened it on his knees to the first page with its one brown line. He touched it with one dry finger. "*Keep time with you.* Not *move faster*. Not *strike harder*. You keep time. That means you're the one keeping it." He closed the book and handed it back. "My teacher could hold a line of eleven men together all night with that. Not because he made them stronger. Because none of them was ever early, and none of them was ever late, and none of them was ever alone. Eleven men who aren't alone are a wall."

Deston sat with the book in his lap.

"Read it every day," said Brother Amon. "Out loud. And sing it, if you sing. You sing, don't you?"

"I used to. In a choir. At school."

"Good," said the old man. "The book likes singers. When it's ready, it will write you more."

"Write me more?"

"The pages aren't empty, boy. They're *waiting*." He shut his eyes again, and settled his chin back on his chest. "Come back when there's a second line. Bring me something sweet."

***

He did his afternoon. He marked the past-year paper. It was fine. Not great, but fine. He did the chapter and the flashcards, and then he did another past-year paper, and then it was dark outside. He went and ate dinner and was a person for an hour, mostly, though he caught himself smiling at nothing twice.

At ten he opened the VOD of Dec's stream from that morning on his phone and watched it in bed, with the volume low.

It was strange, seeing it from outside. He'd been *there*. Two metres to Dec's left, in the water, with the book open. But on the stream there was no Deston, mostly. There was only what Dec could see: the reeds, the leeches, the slope of the shield coming up into frame, Xav moving in and out at the edges like a thought. And Deston, when Dec glanced back at him. Each time he looked, Deston was in the middle of the screen for a second, book open, singing. Then Dec looked forward again.

He hadn't known Dec looked back at him that often. He looked back every time he heard the note.

The chat ran down the side of the screen. It was bigger than on Monday. Forty-something, fifty at one point. There were a lot of names he didn't know now, among the ones he did.

***

Xav, it turned out, had been working on something too.

He showed them on Friday morning, at the edge of the old fen, on a leech. He had the patient look on his face that meant he'd been thinking about it for a while and didn't want to say anything until it worked.

He waited for Dec to turn the first strike. Then, instead of cutting straight away, he went *round*. All the way round, behind the leech, to the place it couldn't see, while it was still busy with the slope of Dec's shield. He took one whole extra second to get there. Then he cut.

[ Twin Cut · Grade 66% · Unseen ×1.5 · 19 damage ]

"*Unseen*," said Xav.

"Where did *that* come from?"

"I've been trying it since Tuesday." He wiped his blades on his trousers, which did nothing. "Things that are looking at Dec, and get cut from where they can't see, take more. It's not in any thread. I just thought it might. In every other game it does." He looked at Deston. "It needs a second. A whole second, behind the thing, before the cut. If I'm early, it doesn't count. If I'm late, Dec's taken another hit."

"So you need a whole second," said Deston slowly. "Exactly."

"Exactly a second."

Deston looked at the book in his hands.

"I can count you a second," he said.

***

They were good by Friday.

Not *good* good. Deston knew what good looked like. He'd watched clips all week from the big streams, the ones with ten thousand people watching, the pro players and their camera orbs. Those people were better than the three of them at almost everything. Faster with their hands, more experienced, more ruthless. But they were doing it alone. Or they were doing it in groups of four or five who'd met on day one and didn't know each other, all four or five trying to be the best in the clip.

The three of them were doing it *together*. Dec at the front with his shield sloped, sitting back and waiting, never in a hurry. Xav round the side and behind, gone into the place things couldn't see. And Deston in the middle, singing them in, counting Xav his second, holding the note when somebody was going to be early.

He found he could do more than count. He could see. It was a strange sort of seeing, from the middle of the beat: Dec's health going down with every hit that got through. He saw Dec take a hit he couldn't turn, a leech straight onto his forearm, and *nothing happened on his face*. Nothing. Dec didn't flinch, didn't swear, didn't even look down. He just set his feet again.

He was at fifty. Deston kept forgetting. Dec had put it on fifty on the first morning and never said another word about it. Every hit Deston watched him take, Dec was feeling two and a half times what Deston would have felt.

He didn't say anything about it. Dec wouldn't have wanted him to.

***

The second line came on Saturday morning.

It came in the middle of a fight, which seemed rude of it. Four leeches at the narrow place, the same as every morning now. Dec was at the front, Xav round the side, and Deston in the middle singing *Let the MANY move as ONE*, hearing the shape of the fight under the words the way you hear the shape of a song under the melody. Dec took a hit he couldn't turn. A big leech, coming straight up out of the black water at his chest, too fast for the slope, and it landed square.

Deston *felt* it.

Not like before, not just *where* Dec was. He felt the hit. It was a hot, flat, bruising weight in the middle of his own chest, and it knocked the next word of the line right out of him. Just for a second. Faint, like an echo of somebody else's pain, through a wall. Twenty per cent of an echo of fifty per cent.

He stopped singing.

The book, open in his hands, was warm.

He looked down. On the first page, under *Let the many move as one*, in the same neat brown hand, as if it had always been there and he'd simply never noticed it:

*What one bears, two can carry.*

"I'm fine," said Deston, out loud, and found that he was. "I'm fine. Dec. Hold still a second."

Dec held still, in the water, with the shield up, the leech coming round for another go.

Deston put his hand on the spine of the book and read the new line, not carefully this time. He sang it, the way the first one wanted to be sung, a low line that went down at the end like a bass part settling under a chord. *What one BEARS. Two can CARRY.* And he said, "Ice," because that was the name over Dec's head.

The leech struck. Dec turned it, mostly. Some got through.

Deston felt a third of it land in his own shoulder. A dull, real, bruising thump at his own twenty per cent, which meant that for Dec, at fifty, there was a third less of it. He watched Dec's health bar dip less than it should have. He watched Dec stop for half a second, in the middle of the fight, and look back at him, and he saw on Dec's face that Dec had felt it too, the weight going somewhere else.

"*Dest*," said Dec.

"Keep going," said Deston, and his voice came out rough. "It's fine. Keep going. I've got it."

***

They didn't talk about it until the end of the fight. Then Dec waded back to him, through the reeds, with the shield hanging off his arm, and stood in front of him, and didn't say anything for a moment.

"You're at twenty," said Dec.

"I know."

"It hurts you. When you do that."

"A bit," said Deston. "A third. Of a twenty. It's like being poked. It's nothing." He looked at Dec. "You're at *fifty*."

"I know what I'm at."

"You don't make any noise," said Deston. "Every hit. All week. You don't make any noise at all. I've been watching. I've been *feeling* it, Dec, through the book. You took one on Thursday that went right through the slope into your arm, and I could feel where you were, and you didn't even *look*."

Dec didn't answer. He looked at the water.

"Front of the line," he said eventually. "If the front flinches, everybody flinches. Every game. I've always—it's just a habit."

"I know," said Deston. "I'm not asking you to stop." He held up the book. "I'm just telling you. Some of it comes to me now."

Xav had come back through the reeds too, and was standing a little way off with his daggers sheathed, pretending not to listen and listening very hard.

"Good," said Xav, after a moment, to nobody. "That's good."

***

He took Brother Amon a honey cake that afternoon, from the woman who sold them on the south walk. The old man ate the whole thing very slowly with his gums, and then read the second line with his finger, and nodded, and didn't say anything for a long time.

"That one," he said finally, "costs."

"I know. It hurts a bit."

"Not that cost." Brother Amon looked at him with his old wet eyes. "You'll feel them. More and more, as it grows. The ones you carry. You'll know when they're hurt before they do. You'll know when they're tired, and when they're lying about it." He patted the book. "My teacher said it was the heaviest book he ever owned. He also said he'd never have put it down."

He shut his eyes, and Deston thought he'd gone to sleep. Then he said, without opening them:

"Your friend at the front. The one who doesn't make any noise."

"Yes?"

"Carry him," said Brother Amon. "Somebody should."
