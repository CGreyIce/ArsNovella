They rested.

Ice had never been very good at it. In November he'd gone from the swamp to the door to the world first without stopping. In December, from the world first to the longest night. In the new year, from the longest night to the founding to the north. There'd always been a next thing. He'd always been glad there was.

There wasn't one now. Not this week. Gabe's three were sixteen, and seventeen, and somewhere south of them, and they'd be thirty when they were thirty. The Long Watch was kept. The lights were home. Kestrel would mind for a week. Thea's six had been in the game for four days, somewhere he didn't know yet. Thea wanted to surprise him, Tif said, so he wasn't allowed to ask.

So they rested. In Fernwick, in the rain, for three days.

Kotori brewed cabbage tea at Sedge's hearth for the people on the Landing who still came every evening to buy it for luck. Deston sat on the end of the eel man's jetty with the book closed and counted nothing at all. He said it felt very strange, and that he liked it. Sheng Yang went out with Hollis's militia in the mornings to help clear the reed channels, because he couldn't sit still and Hollis could always use a long sword. Suzu sat on Sedge's steps where Rafi used to sit, with the empty jar on the step beside him. He didn't do anything. Ice had never seen Suzu not do anything before.

And Ice brewed draughts, and streamed it, and let thirty thousand people watch him do almost nothing. They stayed anyway, which he still didn't understand.

Sedge looked up from her grinding.

"Far-glass again," she said.

"Messages," said Ice. "A friend. She's new. She's got ears that move."

Sedge thought about that, the way she thought about everything, slowly and all the way through.

"Rabbit?" she said.

"Cat."

"Hm," said Sedge, as if that were reasonable, and went back to grinding.

***

On Wednesday night, after the stream, he took them into the ring.

Not the stream. The ring was always dark. That was the one rule he'd kept since November, the only one that had never bent. He went to the end of the north walk in the dusk with the other four, and said, "Going dark," and turned it off.

He waited until they'd typed it. Then he stepped off the end of the north walk, into the reeds, east.

The heron-stones stood in a ring in the old fen, a quarter of a mile from the walk, where nobody went. Nine of them, grey, waist-high, set in a circle round a pool of clear warm water that never froze. Moonbells grew at their feet and opened at night, pale blue, like small lamps. Nothing came inside. Not the Unlit, not the cold, not the dark. The game said so. *Nothing comes inside the stones.* He'd found it on the first night, in November, by accident, and he'd kept it ever since, and never once shown it on stream.

Eight of the stones were carved at the top into the shape of a heron's head, worn soft, all facing in. Toward the pool. Toward the middle.

The ninth faced out. North.

They sat round the pool in the warm, the five of them, with the moonbells opening one at a time round their feet. Nobody said anything for a while. Suzu put the empty jar down on the stone lip of the pool beside him.

Then Ice told them. Sedge's mother. The bedtime line. The rest of the half-line that had been washed away in an old guild's book at the top of the world, that an old woman in a fen had known by heart all her life without knowing what it was.

"*Set one to watch*," said Ice, "*so the rest may sleep.*"

Nobody said anything.

Then Deston looked across the pool at the ninth stone. At the heron's head on top of it, worn soft by however many years, facing north. Toward the herons, and the gorge, and a door in a hill that faced back.

"That's what it's doing," said Deston slowly. "The one that faces out. It's watching. So the town can sleep."

"And the door watches back," said Kotori, very softly. "From the other end."

"And the Long Watch," said Sheng Yang. "Eighty-one of them. All facing north. Watching." He looked at the stone. "So something can sleep."

"So everyone can," said Suzu.

They sat with that for a while. The moonbells were all open now. The pool was warm. Somewhere out in the fen a bird called once and didn't call again.

Then Deston looked at Ice.

"That's you," said Deston.

Ice looked at him.

"Set one to watch," said Deston. He said it gently. He'd known Ice thirteen years. "That's you, Dec. That's what you do. You stand at the front and you don't flinch and you don't sleep. You stood on the narrows all night. You stood under that thing for forty minutes. You go dark in here, every night, on your own, and keep this place." He looked round the ring. "You're the one that faces out."

Nobody said anything.

Ice looked at the ninth stone for a long time.

He thought about the first night. A little wooden room with rain on the window, and twenty-one people, and *I'm going to stand at the front with a shield, and things are going to hit me*. He thought about the plank. The narrows. The Last Watcher's lantern coming down on the shield, again and again. *I know exactly where that was.*

He'd never thought of it as watching. He'd thought of it as standing there.

"Maybe," he said.

"Not tonight, though," said Suzu.

They all looked at him. Suzu was sitting on the stone lip of the pool with his feet in the warm water and his empty jar beside him. He was looking at the ninth stone, not at Ice.

"It's watching," said Suzu. "It's been watching longer than any of us have been alive. It's good at it." He nodded at the stone. "Let it."

Ice looked at him.

"*So the rest may sleep*," said Suzu. "You're the rest. Tonight."

***

He didn't log out in the middle of the ring, where he always did. Where he could see all nine stones and the way in through the reeds and the dark beyond, the way he'd slept every night in here since November, if you could call it sleeping.

He lay down on the warm stone at the edge of the pool instead, with his back to the ninth stone. With his back to the north, and everything up there, the gorge and the door and the Unlit Country Kei had stood on the edge of and the dark at the edge of the world. All of it behind him. And a stone heron with its head turned out, keeping it.

He put his shield down beside him. Not on his arm. Beside him.

Kotori, across the pool, saw him do it, and made a very small sound, and didn't say anything.

Deston lay down by the third stone with the book under his head. Sheng Yang sat with his back against the fifth and his long sword across his knees, and then, after a while, lay down too, which Ice had never seen him do. Kotori curled up by the moonbells with her bell in her hand. Suzu lay on the lip of the pool with one hand on the jar.

Ice lay on the warm stone with the ninth heron at his back, looking up at the stars over the old fen. He felt, for the first time since a little wooden room in November, that something else had the front.

He didn't remember logging out. He just remembered the moonbells, and the warm, and Suzu saying, very quietly, from the other side of the pool, to nobody, "Goodnight."

And then he slept.
