Night in the fen lasted about three hours of his life and most of a whole day of the fen's.

He spent it inside the ring.

There wasn't really a choice. He'd tried, once, an hour after moonrise, to see whether he could get back across the tussocks in the dark. He'd stepped out past the first stone, and the doorway feeling went away behind him like a coat taken off, and the night air outside the ring was cold and smelled of rot. Somewhere off to his left, something very large moved in the water. Not a splash. A push, the sound of a lot of water being shoved out of the way by something that didn't have to hurry. He'd stood there for about four seconds with his shield up, and then he'd stepped back inside and sat down again.

"Nope," he'd said. "We live here now."

So he made himself useful.

He picked, slowly, all the way round the ring under the moonlight. Silverleaf and fen-heart and the pale trailing fronds, and the yellow stars, which the game finally told him were called *marsh-candle*. One in three. Always one in three. He found he didn't even want to cheat. The ring was so obviously a place somebody had kept for a long time, and a part of him that he didn't examine too closely felt that Sedge really would know.

When his pack started to get heavy, he stopped picking and practised. He stood in the warm shallow water with his sword and did Falling Cuts at nothing until his stamina ran out, then sat on a stone until it came back, then stood up and did them again. He found out you could do a Falling Cut in ankle-deep water, but the water dragged at your back foot and the game didn't like that. *Your feet were slow.* He found out that if he set his back foot first, before the cut, as a small separate movement, it was better. Then he found out that was cheating, because the game noticed the pause and docked him for it. Then he found a way to start the foot a heartbeat earlier inside the same movement, so it was all one thing, and the grade went up to forty-four.

At about half past two in the afternoon, Malaysian time, he took a break.

He hadn't expected to need one so badly. He'd been lying in a pod for six and a half hours, and his real body, which he'd almost entirely forgotten about, sent him a polite and then a less polite message about it. He told chat he'd be twenty minutes, put the stream on its *be right back* card, and sat down on the flat stone in the middle of the ring.

[ Rest here?
You are inside a safe place. Your body will remain while you are away. ]

He said yes.

And then he was lying in the warm gel in the dim blue egg in the spare room, and the lid was lifting slowly on its long arm, and the ceiling fan was turning, and everything was wrong.

His body was so *heavy*. That was the first thing. He'd been light all morning without knowing it, and now every limb weighed what it really weighed, and he lay there for a second feeling it settle on him like a wet coat. His back was stiff. His mouth was dry. The air in the room was warm and still and smelled of nothing, no water, no reeds, no green. The light coming round the edge of the blind was a flat white Malacca afternoon.

He sat up slowly and climbed out. His legs felt strange for the first few steps, as if they'd been told about walking but hadn't tried it yet. He went to the bathroom. He drank two glasses of water in the kitchen, standing at the sink. He put a frozen nasi lemak in the microwave and stood there watching it turn, and the hum of the microwave sounded like the hum of the pod, and for a moment he forgot which one he was in.

He checked his phone while he ate. Forty-one messages in *the usual*. He didn't read them. If he read them, he'd answer them, and then it would be an hour. He put it face down and ate his nasi lemak and looked at the wall, and thought about a warm ring of stones in the middle of a fen at night, and about a pair of eyes a hand apart, and felt his heartbeat go up a little, here in his kitchen, about a thing that wasn't real.

Twenty-two minutes after he'd left, he climbed back into the egg.

***

The fen was still dark when he got back, but it was going grey at the edges. Dawn of the fen's second day. He'd spent its whole night in a pond.

He stood up and stretched, and something about the stones caught his eye.

He'd looked at them a hundred times since sunset. Nine stones, nine herons, cut in the same patient stance, all of them facing inwards, toward the warm water in the middle, as if they were all watching the moonbells. He'd taken that in at the start and not thought about it since.

But the ninth heron didn't face inward.

He walked over to it. It was the most worn of the nine, the heron almost gone, just a long neck and a beak you had to want to see. But you could see it, if you looked. The beak didn't point at the middle of the ring. It pointed out. North, past the stone, into the reeds, where the water went from clear to black.

"Huh," said Ice.

He looked out the way the beak was pointing. There was nothing to see but reeds and grey water and the beginnings of a mist. The tussocks didn't go that way. Nothing went that way.

He thought about the eyes.

He thought about the fact that it was still mostly dark, that he was level two, that he had three Fair draughts and four Poor ones and thirty-eight copper to his name, and that he'd promised a woman in England he wouldn't die while she was asleep.

Then he went anyway, carefully, testing every step.

It came up out of the water about a hundred paces past the stones, in water up to his waist.

He'd known it would. He'd been ready for it the whole way, with the shield low and sloped and his sword back by his ear like a cup he didn't want to spill. It didn't make a difference. When it came, it came so fast and so big that for a second all he could do was look at it.

It was mostly mouth. A head as wide as a door, flat and grey-green and slick, and two eyes on top, a hand apart, exactly where he'd last seen them. The mouth opened sideways, the whole front of the head splitting, full of little backward-pointing teeth like a fish's. Behind it, under the water, a body went on for longer than he wanted to think about.

Three levels above him.

It struck. He got the shield up and the slope on it, just about, and the head hit the slope and went past him and over his left shoulder in a rush of foul water and stink. He felt the edge of the jaw go along his upper arm like a dragged rasp. At fifty per cent it felt like being scraped down a brick wall. A third of his health went in one go.

He didn't make a sound. He was too busy.

He went sideways, to the right, away from where its head had gone, toward a clump of reeds thick enough to stand behind. The water dragged at his legs. It was turning in the water behind him, a long slow shove of a turn, and he heard it come round.

It hit the reeds. They broke. But they slowed it, and the head came through them at an angle, off-line, and he was ready, standing in the gap with his feet set and his back foot already moving inside the cut, the way he'd found in the ring two hours ago. He brought the Falling Cut down across the top of its head, between the eyes, and the whole of him arrived at once.

[ Falling Cut · Grade 46% · 11 damage ]

Eleven, against a bar that didn't seem to notice. It roared, if a thing with no lungs worth mentioning could roar, a wet bubbling bellow, and pulled back into the deep water to come again.

It was the longest fight of his life.

He couldn't stand still and let it come; it was too strong, and his slope too young. Every time he took a head-on hit, even sloped, it cost him a fifth of his health. So he never let it hit him head-on. He kept moving, sideways through the waist-deep water, from reed clump to reed clump, so that every time it came for him it came through something, and arrived a little slow and a little off-line, and he was ready, and cut. Eleven. Nine. Twelve. A bad one, six, when his foot slipped on the bottom. The leech bites had taught him to drink early, so he did: a Poor draught at sixty per cent, another at fifty. They were cabbagey and weak and tasted like regret, but they kept the bar from falling faster than he could fix it.

At one point, it got him properly. He misjudged a clump that was thinner than it looked, and the head came straight through it and hit his shield flat, no slope, and drove him backwards under the water.

For a second, he was underneath. Cold black water in his nose, in his ears, in his mouth, the taste of iron and rot. He couldn't see anything. He couldn't tell which way was up. His body, his *real* body in the egg in Malacca, did something he felt from a long way off: a jolt of pure animal panic, like falling in a dream.

He got his feet under him and came up coughing, and the bar said twenty-two per cent.

It was a doorway in the side of a mound.

The mound rose out of the deep water at the edge of the reeds, low and long and grassed over, like a barrow, and he'd taken it for an island in the mist. Its near end had sunk at some point, centuries ago maybe, and gone half under the black water. And set into that end, sunk to the middle of its height, was an arch.

He waded closer. The water came up to his chest. He held his sword and shield above it, which was silly, and he knew it was silly, and he did it anyway.

The arch was grey stone, the same grey as the heron-stones, and carved all around the edge with herons. Dozens of them, standing on one leg, wing to wing, the whole curve of the arch a line of them. And within the arch, filling it, there was a single slab of the same stone, flat and smooth, set into the doorway so closely he couldn't have slid a knife in at the edge.

At the height of his chest, just above the water, there were five shallow hollows cut into the slab in a row. Each was the shape of a hand: an open palm, the fingers a little spread.

Above them, cut into the stone in a plain old hand, were five words.

FIVE VOICES, AND NONE BELOW FIFTEEN.

A window opened, very quietly, the edges dark.

[ The Drowned Choir
A dungeon for a party of five. Each must be level 15 or higher.
You are the first to find this place. ]

He stood in the black water up to his chest and read it three times.

*The first to find this place.* Out of every person who'd stepped out of a door in a thousand towns this morning, the whole world, he was the first one to stand here. He felt it in his chest, and then in his face, a big stupid grin he couldn't do anything about, alone in a swamp.

And then he heard the singing.

It was very faint. It came from behind the slab, or under it, or through the stone itself. He couldn't tell. Voices. Many voices, high and low together, singing something slow, in a language he didn't know, so muffled by stone and water that it was more a shape than a sound. A choir. Somewhere under the mound, in the dark, a whole choir was singing, very quietly, the way people sing when they've been at it so long they've forgotten they started.

He put his hand flat in the first hollow without meaning to.

The stone was cold and smooth and fit his palm exactly. Nothing happened. The singing didn't stop. The slab didn't move. A small line of text came up, polite and final.

[ One voice. ]

He took his hand away.

Five voices. Fifteen, each. He was level three. It was the first morning of the world. It would be weeks before anyone was fifteen. It would be weeks before *he* was, and he'd started first, and he'd just spent a third of the night not dying to the thing that guarded the door.

He thought about who his four voices would be.

Two came straight away. He didn't even have to think about it. The other two he didn't know yet, and he found he didn't mind not knowing. It would be weeks. People would come.

And under all of it, under the singing, he thought: *Deston is going to lose his mind.* Thirteen years ago, in the back row of a school choir, two boys mouthing the high parts. And now a dungeon full of drowned singers, five voices, none below fifteen.

He opened his map. It was mostly blank: the game drew it as you walked, and he hadn't walked much. Fernwick, the walks, the dotted line of his path across the tussocks, the little circle of the heron-stones. He put a pin down in the blank water north of the ninth heron, and the game asked him what to call it and whether to share it.

*Share with: no one.*

He called it *later*.

He'd laid them on her table, still in the linen, folded back. In the daylight they'd closed again, three tight white fists on dark stems, and she looked at them the way you'd look at a letter from somebody you hadn't heard from in a long time.
