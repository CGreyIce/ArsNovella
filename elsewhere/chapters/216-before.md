She was always in a minute before him.

He'd come out of the dark into the room over the boatbuilder's, the small one he'd rented in February with a bed and a chair and a window onto the harbour. And she'd be there. On the windowsill, usually, with her knees up and her tail hanging down the wall. Looking at him, the way she'd said she liked to: coming out of the dark.

"Hi," said Hyvred.

"Hi."

"You came out left side first."

"Did I?"

"You always do," said Hyvred. "Your left hand, then your face. Like you're feeling for a wall." She considered him. "It's very boring. I love it."

He didn't know what to say to that. He'd been told a lot of things in the last four months that he didn't know what to say to. He'd started just saying *okay*, and she'd started hitting him for it, and somewhere in the middle of that they'd worked out that it was fine.

"Okay," he said.

She hit him. On the arm, not hard.

***

The day after the cellar, his left foot still remembered it.

Not in the game. The game had put him back to a hundred overnight, the way it did. In his head. He'd walk down the stairs from the room over the boatbuilder's and his left foot would go down a fraction carefully, as if Xav were still coming in on that side. Fourteen times, in twenty-eight minutes.

Fourteen. Nobody had ever come close to fourteen.

He went down to the netloft at the first dawn. Riverbank were at the long table, all eight of them, the way they always were in the mornings. Malcolm with his stick. RX counting the cups. Val by the window with a new book, not looking at anybody. And Xav, at the end, with his sketchbook shut and two pages in front of him on the table.

One was Val's. Dashes and dots in pencil, damp at the edges, with a hole near the bottom where the pencil had gone through.

The other was in ink, very small, very neat, three sides of it. Kestrel's. She'd come to the netloft door at the first dawn, Wendy said, and asked if she could come in, and Wendy had asked her who'd stand next to her. And Kestrel had said *Fenn. And Brann. And him*, and pointed at Xav through the door. And Wendy had said *fair*. And Kestrel had come in and put the page down in front of Xav without a word and gone away again.

Xav was reading it for the fourth time when Ice sat down.

"Don't tell me what it says," said Ice.

"I wasn't going to." Xav didn't look up. "It's about you. It's all about you. It's three pages of you." He turned it over. "She's very good. She's better than me at this bit. She's got the timings. To the half-second. From the top of a ladder." He put it down. "She's got the end as *no rests. Fifty-one seconds*. And then underneath, *he said BEFORE*. In capitals. Underlined twice."

Ice didn't say anything.

Xav looked at him. Properly, for the first time that morning.

"I'm going to need a long time," he said.

"Take it."

"September," said Xav. "Downstairs. Same cellar."

"September."

"And Dec." Xav's face did the thing it had done in the cellar, between being hit and being given a present. "Don't learn a fourth one before then."

"I won't," said Ice.

"You *will*," said Xav. "You'll learn one by accident. Off a goose." He picked up Val's page, very carefully. "Go away. I've got homework."

***

He owed the fish man for a fish.

Harlan had told him in April, by letter, in capitals. He'd put the first fish down on the edge of the cart in February to go and hold somebody's hand, and never come back for it. He'd meant to pay. He'd forgotten. He didn't forget things, usually, but February had been full.

He went down to the bottom of the north-west road with Hyvred at the second dusk on Friday, and the fish man was there. Of course he was. He'd been there three hundred years, he said. Boot face. Fish on ice in a cart with a striped awning, at the bottom of the road where it came down the crater wall into the harbour.

"Took you long enough," said the fish man.

"I'm sorry," said Ice. He put four silver down on the edge of the cart. Too much, for one fish. "For February."

The fish man looked at the four silver. Then at Hyvred, beside him, holding his hand. Then at Ice.

"I saw," said the fish man. "In February. You put it down and went." He picked up the silver, one coin at a time, and put it in his apron. "Three hundred years, I've seen everything at the bottom of this road. Every kind of person. Every kind of hurry." He looked at the place on the edge of the cart where the fish had been. "Never seen anybody put a fish down like that."

"Like what?"

"Like he'd never wanted anything in his life," said the fish man, "and then did."

Hyvred's hand went very tight in his.

The fish man gave them two fish, wrapped in paper, for the four silver. And then a third, for nothing, which he put in Hyvred's other hand without looking at her.

"For the one with the horns," he said. "For walking fourteen days."

***

They ate the fish on the harbour wall. Hyvred ate hers and half of his, and then the third one. He watched the lake.

Great Lake Masmark in the evening was the biggest thing he'd ever seen. Bigger than space, in a way; space had been black and you had to imagine the size of it. This you could see. The whole ring of the city going round it, lighting up, terrace by terrace, all the way to the far side, where it was just a line of sparks on the edge of the world. The island in the middle with its tower. Boats everywhere.

At the top of the tower, very small, a shape against the last of the sky. A bell, the boatbuilder had told him. Bronze, green with age, in an open belfry at the top. It had rung once, the boatbuilder said, when the first road came over the rim, before the city. And never since.

"Ice," said Hyvred.

"Mm."

"What did you mean," said Hyvred, "*before*."

He'd known she'd ask. She'd been not asking for two days. He'd watched her not ask in the netloft, and on the stair, and over the fish. She was very bad at not asking things. She went pink with it.

"Before I learned to wait," he said.

"I heard. I was *there*. I'm asking what it means."

He looked at the lake for a long time.

***

"There was a guild," he said.

Hyvred didn't say anything. She'd learned that from him, he thought. Or from Bee. When somebody started to say something, you didn't say anything back until they'd finished.

"A long time ago. In a phone game. It's not even— it's a game where you tap things. You don't *move*. You walk by tapping and you fight by tapping." He looked at his hands. "I made a guild. On my own. Just me. And I declared war on every other guild in the game."

"*Every*—"

"Every one. There were five thousand." He shrugged. "It was a stupid name. Harlan says don't say it out loud."

"Murderers of the Universe," said Hyvred.

He looked at her.

"Gabe," said Hyvred. "He's told the story to everybody. Seven times to me." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Go on."

"I fought everybody," said Ice. "All the time. Every hour I was awake. I didn't wait for anyone to come at me. I went at them. Before they'd decided anything. Before they'd even logged in, sometimes." He was quiet for a moment. "That's what it was. Not waiting. Going, and going, and going. At everybody. Until they ran, or joined."

"And they joined."

"Twenty," said Ice. "People who liked fighting. I liked them. Harlan. Vane." He looked at the lake. "We got into the top fifty. Out of five thousand."

"So why'd you stop?"

He thought about it.

***

He'd never told anybody. Not Suzu, who'd been in the wind game with him. Not Deston, not Xav, not Gabe, who'd told the ghost story seven times to Hyvred without knowing the end of it. He'd never been asked. Nobody asked him things. That was what he'd always thought, anyway, until a winter ago, when people had started asking.

"Because I was tired," he said. "The tiring kind. Not the other kind." He looked at his hands again. "And because it was lonely."

Hyvred went very still beside him.

"You had twenty people."

"I had twenty people," agreed Ice. "And every one of them was at the front. That's what the war was. Everybody at the front, going at everybody. Nobody behind anybody. Nobody beside." He paused. "Nobody ever asked who'd stand next to me. There wasn't a next to. There was just a front."

The lake went on lighting up, terrace by terrace.

"So I stopped," said Ice. "I went somewhere else. A game with spaceships. I flew on my own. And then a lot of games, on my own, farming things. And then this. A fen. With stones in the water, and things coming up out of it. And you couldn't go at them. They were under the water. You had to wait." He was quiet. "So I waited. And it was the first thing I'd ever done that let somebody else go first."

Hyvred didn't say anything for a long time.

"Like me," she said at last. Very quietly. "At the gate."

He looked at her.

"You waited," said Hyvred. "For a year. You knew. Since the step. You said. You knew, and you didn't go at it. You waited." She was looking at the lake, not at him. Her face was very pink. "And then I said *me*. First. Because you'd left a gap."

He hadn't thought of it like that. He thought about it now, on the harbour wall, with the fish paper on his knee.

"Yes," he said.

"Don't stop," said Hyvred.

"Waiting?"

"Waiting." She turned and looked at him. "Do the fast thing on Xav. Do it on Lucan. Do it on whoever. I don't care. But don't stop waiting." She swallowed. "Not with me."

"Not with you," said Ice.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her head on his shoulder and looked at the lake.

"Do you miss it?" she said. "The war."

"No."

"Liar."

"A bit," said Ice.

***

Aurelie asked them to the castle on the Tuesday.

She'd said *bring the sixty-one* in her letter, but sixty-one weren't in Masmark. Twenty-two were. Riverbank's eight, and Salty and Wendy, and the twelve who'd come down the south road on the first of May. So he brought Kei, because you asked Kei. And Hyvred, because she was holding his hand when the letter came and didn't let go.

Meridian's castle sat on the west rim above the water. Grey stone, deep blue banners with one gold line down the middle, top to bottom. Two thousand chairs in a hall the size of a valley, and hardly anybody in them at the third dawn. Aurelie at the end of it, very small, in plain blue with no gold on her anywhere. Vane beside her, still. Harlan behind them both, not still at all.

"*GM*," said Harlan, from forty paces away, in the voice that had been in Ice's ear in the war for two years. "*GM, you came, she said you'd come, Vane said you wouldn't*—"

"I said he'd come if you asked," said Vane.

"*I* didn't ask, *she* asked—"

"Harlan," said Aurelie.

Harlan stopped.

Aurelie looked at the three of them coming up the hall. At Kei with his map case. At Hyvred with her bow, holding Ice's hand, looking back at Aurelie with eyes that saw the whole hall at once and weren't sure about any of it. At Ice.

"The bell," said Aurelie.

"The bell," said Kei.

***

The notice wasn't up yet. It would go up in June, on the wall of the Rim Hall, the way the last one had: a door, a number, ten nights. But everybody knew. You could see it from every terrace in Masmark, at the top of the tower, green against the sky. The second great door. Not down this time. Up.

"Ten thousand people," said Aurelie. "Sixty-one Halls, across half the world. Six years in the guild before this one, under another name, in another game." She sat back in her chair. It was too big for her; all the chairs in the castle were too big for her. "And we have never once been first."

Nobody said anything.

"Not at anything," said Aurelie. "Not here. Not in the last game. Third at the Crown Below. Fourth at the first hall for twenty, in the west, in December. Second, once, in the old game, by a day." She looked at Kei. "We're very good. We're careful. We plan everything. We have two hundred people to choose twenty from, and we choose well. And somebody else is always first." She looked at Ice. "Usually somebody who didn't plan anything at all."

"Kei plans," said Ice.

"Kei plans," agreed Kei.

"I'm going to be first at the bell," said Aurelie.

She said it quite calmly, the way Kestrel had said *only one* on a bridge. Not a threat. Just a thing that was true, said out loud so she'd hear herself say it.

"Good," said Ice.

Aurelie's eyes narrowed.

"Don't," she said. "Don't you dare say *good for us*. Not before. Not after. I've watched Tif's clip four hundred times. I know exactly how you say it." She pointed at him, very small, in her big chair. "If we're first, you're not allowed to say it."

"I'll mean it," said Ice.

"I *know* you'll mean it," said Aurelie. "That's the *problem*. Kestrel told me. Everybody says it's the problem." She looked at Vane. "Vane, tell him."

Vane looked at Ice for a long moment, the way he'd looked at him on a harbour wall in February.

"We're fine," said Vane.

Harlan put his face in his hands.

***

On the way back along the west rim, Kei said nothing for a long time. Then, at the top of the north-west road, he said:

"She really wants it."

"Yes."

"Ten thousand people. Never first." Kei was quiet. "I'd want it too."

"Do you want it?" said Hyvred.

Kei thought about it, with his map case under his arm.

"I want us to go up the stairs," he said. "All twenty. And come down again. And I want to see what's at the top." He looked at the tower across the water. "If we're first, that's nice. If she's first, I'll be glad for her." He paused. "I think he's catching."

"He's very catching," said Hyvred darkly.

***

He saw Lucan on the Thursday.

On the bridge over the third stream, on the north terrace, where they'd met in March. The water going under it white and loud. He was coming back from the Undercliffs with Hyvred, and Lucan was coming the other way with two of Sable Court behind him, in black, with the silver at the cuffs. Moving, all the time, like water over stones.

They both stopped in the middle of the bridge. Twenty paces apart.

People on the bridge stopped too. They always did, now. Somebody on the far bank said *oh* in a language the game didn't bother to translate.

Lucan looked at him for a long time. His weight went from one foot to the other and back.

"Not yet?" said Lucan.

"Not yet," said Ice.

"Good," said Lucan. "I'm not ready either."

Ice looked at him.

"I heard about a cellar," said Lucan. His face didn't do anything. "Nobody saw it. You went dark. But Kestrel walked out of the netloft afterward as if somebody had set her on fire, and Kestrel doesn't walk like that." He shifted his weight. "Something happened in that cellar that isn't on any of your VODs."

Ice didn't say anything.

"So I'm not ready," said Lucan. "I thought I was. I've been ready since February." He looked at the water going under the bridge. "Now I think I'll wait a bit."

"You don't wait," said Ice.

"I don't," agreed Lucan. "It's very annoying." He looked at Hyvred, standing close beside Ice with her hand in his and her eyes narrowed. "She doesn't like me."

"No," said Hyvred.

"That's fair," said Lucan. And laughed, short, surprised, over the noise of the water, and went past them across the bridge. The two of Sable Court behind him. Moving.

At the far end, he didn't look back. Ice had thought he would. He didn't.

"He's going to ask in July," said Hyvred.

"How do you know?"

"Because he said he'll wait a bit," said Hyvred. "And people like him think a bit is about six weeks."

***

Out in Malacca, on the other side of the egg, Gabe was being strange.

Ice noticed on the Saturday. You couldn't not notice. Gabe was never strange; Gabe was always exactly as loud as he looked. But on Saturday afternoon, in the back room of the shoplot at Melaka Raya, with the fan going round and both of them out of the egg, Gabe's phone buzzed on the shelf. Gabe looked at it. And his whole enormous face went red from the neck up. And he turned the phone face down and put his hand on it.

Ice looked at the hand.

"Who's that?" he said.

"Nobody," said Gabe.

It was the worst lie Ice had ever heard. Gabe had never once in eleven years successfully not said something.

"Okay," said Ice.

Gabe looked at him with enormous relief and enormous guilt, both at once.

"Dec," he said. "Saturday the tenth. Next month. Are you— are you home? In the evening?"

"Deston's got the keys that weekend," said Ice. "I'll be in the corridor."

"Right," said Gabe. "Right. Good. Okay. No reason." His phone buzzed again under his hand. He didn't look at it. He was looking very hard at the ceiling fan. "No reason at all."

Ice looked at him for a long time.

He thought about asking. He'd learned to ask. It was one of the things he'd learned this year, that you could, and people would tell you. But he looked at Gabe's face, red to the ears, looking at a ceiling fan as if his life depended on it. And he thought about a gap. About leaving one, on purpose, so somebody else could go first.

"Okay," he said again.

And didn't ask.

***

He hit fifty-five in the Undercliffs on the Sunday night, with Hyvred calling.

The Undercliffs ran under the crater wall all the way round the lake, the old drowned roads beneath the new ones. He'd levelled there alone in February while Aurelie watched him. It was quiet. That was why he liked it. Dark wet stone, the sound of the lake through the rock, things that came up out of the cracks at you.

He didn't go alone any more. He hadn't since March. She came with him, every night, and stood two paces back on the old stone and called.

"Left," said Hyvred.

He went left. The thing that had been coming out of the crack on the right came out into empty air.

"Behind. Low. Don't turn round yet."

He didn't turn round. He took a step back instead, on the back foot, and waited. The thing behind came in low and found his shield already there.

"*Now*."

He went.

He stood in the dark of the Undercliffs and looked at it. Fifty-five. Halfway to a capital. He'd been forty-eight at Christmas, on the floor of the Seven Below, with a lake standing up. That felt like somebody else.

"Fifty-five," said Hyvred, behind him.

"Fifty-five."

"You did the slow thing," said Hyvred. "The whole night. You didn't do the fast thing once." She came up beside him. "You could have. They're only Lv 54. You could have just gone at them."

"I know."

"So why didn't you?"

He thought about it. About a fen, and stones, and things coming up out of the water. About a guild of twenty all at the front, and nobody beside. About a harbour wall.

"Because you were calling," he said. "If I went fast, you'd have nowhere to put the words."

Hyvred didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then she hit him. On the arm, not hard.

"That's for making me cry in the Undercliffs," she said. "Again."

"That's fair."

"*Stop saying that's fair*."

"Okay," said Ice.

She hit him again. And then held his hand, and they walked back up through the dark toward the light at the top of the stair, and the lake, and the city lit all the way round it. Across the water, at the top of the tower on the island, the bell hung green and silent in its belfry against the stars. It had rung once, when the first road came. And never since.
