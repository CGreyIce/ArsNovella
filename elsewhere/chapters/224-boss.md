Rafi had picked fenwort for two copper a plant, once, and he still thought it was a lot.

It had been. In the first week, in Fernwick, in the fen, up to his knees in black water with a sack. He'd been a boy from Surabaya with a new pod and no idea what to do in it, and a man with a sword and a shield had come past and asked if he wanted a job. *Two copper a plant. I'll show you which ones.* Rafi had said yes before the man finished. He'd picked four hundred plants the first day. He'd never been so happy.

He'd been calling the man *boss* ever since. The man had never once told him to stop.

He thought about it a lot, in Masmark. In the biggest city in the world, at Lv 53, with a big black sword on his back that hummed when he swung it. *Two copper a plant.* It was the first money he'd ever made anywhere. And the first time anybody had ever looked at him and decided he was worth something before he'd done anything at all.

***

The Open Hand found him on the east rim on Saturday, buying bread.

Two of them, in dark red, with the black hand on their chests. A man and a woman, older than him, smiling. They came and stood either side of him at the bread stall, the way people do when they want you to know they're there.

"You're Rafi," said the woman.

"Yes."

"The one with the big sword. From the island. You were on the shingle all night at the bell, with the one with the mirror." She looked at the sword on his back. "Constellation."

"Yes."

"How long have you been in Constellation?"

Rafi thought about it.

"Since I walked," he said. "Eight days, to the round town. And they said yes."

"And what do you *do* in Constellation?" said the man. Kindly, the way you'd ask a child. "You weren't in the twenty. At the bell. You weren't in the twenty at the crown either. We checked." He smiled. "You sat on the beach."

"Somebody has to," said Rafi.

The woman laughed. Not unkindly. She put her hand on his shoulder.

"You're nobody there," she said. "I don't mean it badly. It's just true. Sixty-one of them, and you're the one on the beach." She squeezed his shoulder. "Come to us. We've got eleven thousand. We've got *Halls*. We'd put you in a twenty. You'd go through a door, not sit outside it." She leaned in. "Come and be somebody."

Rafi looked at her.

He thought about it. He wanted to be fair. Boss was always fair. He thought about the twenty at the bell, and the beach, and sitting on it all night with John while John flashed his mirror at a tower for no reason. He thought about the round town, and the long table, and his chair at it, near the end, by Bram and Joy.

"I pick fenwort," said Rafi.

The woman blinked.

"What?"

"I pick fenwort," said Rafi. "For Boss. I'm his first hire. Two copper a plant." He paid for his bread. "It's a good job."

"*Two copper*—"

"It was a lot," said Rafi, and went back down the east rim with his bread, and didn't look round.

***

He told Boss that night, on the netloft step, and Boss laughed.

Out loud. Rafi had heard him laugh maybe five times in nearly two years. He sat on the step with his cup and laughed at the lane, at the dark, at the red cloths on twelve doors, and Hyvred looked at him sideways as if he'd started speaking another language.

"*I pick fenwort*," said Boss.

"It's true."

"I know it's true." He wiped his eyes. "You told the CL people that. In the first month, when they asked what you did. *I pick fenwort. Which is true.*" He shook his head. "You've been saying it for nearly two years."

"It's still true," said Rafi.

Boss looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

***

He went into the Undercliffs with Boss on the Monday, the day the red ship moved. Boss asked him. Properly. At the second dusk, at the netloft door, with his shield on his back.

"I'm going further," Boss said. "Past where I usually go. On the north side. I don't know what's there." He paused. "Will you come?"

Rafi had been waiting nearly two years for somebody to ask him that.

"Yes, Boss," he said.

They went down at the second dusk, the two of them. Hyvred wanted to come and Boss said *no*, very gently. Rafi had never heard him say no to her. And she'd looked at him for a long time and said *fine* and hit him on the arm. Then she'd gone up the lane to Abel the boatbuilder's to sit in the window of the room over it, where she could see the whole harbour at once.

The Undercliffs went down under the crater wall all the way round the lake. The drowned roads, Boss called them. The old ones, under the new. Dark wet stone, the lake coming through the rock in drips and trickles, the sound of it everywhere. Things that came up out of the cracks at you.

Boss went first. He always went first. Rafi went behind with the big black sword, and held it up with the floor, the way Boss had taught him in Fernwick a long time ago when it was too big. *Hold it up with the floor. Let the ground take the weight. You just steer.*

They went past where Boss usually stopped. Past a carved stone with a five-line star on it, very worn. Past a stair going down, and another. Down and down, on the north side, under the north-west quarter, under the lane and the netlofts and the harbour. Until the stone was running with water and the sound of the lake was so close it was like being inside a drum.

And then the road ended at water.

***

It wasn't a pool. It was a channel. Cut in the rock, old, wide enough for a small boat. Straight. Going away from them in both directions into the dark. Black water, very still, and very cold.

Boss crouched at the edge and put his hand in it.

"It's moving," he said.

Rafi crouched beside him. It was. Very slowly. Toward the left, and then, a moment later, back to the right. In and out.

"Like it's breathing," said Rafi.

Boss looked at him.

"Like the cellar," he said.

They followed it. Left first, because the water had gone left first. Along a ledge cut beside the channel, single file, very narrow, with the black water a foot below. It went a long way. Rafi counted his steps the way RX did, and lost count at four hundred. And then the channel went under a low arch, and through the arch there was light. Not lamplight. Grey, faint, coming up through the water from below.

Daylight. Through the lake.

"We're under the moles," said Boss. Very quietly. "Outside. That's the lake. That's daylight through the lake." He looked up. Above them, a long way up, through the rock and the water, something enormous and dark sat on the surface, blotting out the light. A shape. A hull. "And that's the ship."

Rafi looked up at the shadow of the Open Hand's great red ship, sitting across the harbour mouth, from underneath.

"It doesn't know we're here," he said.

"No," said Boss.

***

They went back the other way. Right, along the ledge, past the stair they'd come down. The channel went on, under the lane, under the netlofts. Narrower and narrower. Until it ended at a wall of old iron, rusted, bars as thick as Rafi's wrist, with water going in and out between them.

And through the bars, a round stone room. A low ceiling full of old hooks. A ladder.

"The *cellar*," said Rafi.

"The cellar," said Boss.

He put his hand on the grate. He looked through it for a long time, at the netloft's cellar, where he'd fought Xav in the dark in May and done a thing nobody had ever seen. Then he looked back down the channel the way they'd come, toward the arch and the grey light through the lake.

"It's a road," he said. "For boats. Under the moles. From the lake to here." He was quiet. "Somebody built it. A long time ago. Before the moles. So the boats could come in whatever was in the harbour mouth."

"The fish man would know," said Rafi.

Boss looked at him.

"The fish man knows everything," said Rafi. "He's been there three hundred years. He'd know about a road under his own harbour." He thought about it. "He just wouldn't tell anybody unless they asked."

Something happened at the corner of Boss's mouth.

"Then we'll ask," he said.

***

The fish man knew.

They asked him at the second dusk on Tuesday, at the bottom of the north-west road beside his empty cart. He listened to the whole thing with his boot face. Then he looked out past the end of the harbour at the red ship across the mouth with lamps on its rail.

"The Undercut," said the fish man.

"The what?" said Rafi.

"The Undercut. My grandfather's grandfather's day. Before the moles. When there was a chain across the harbour mouth at night, against the lake things." He looked at Boss. "The boats came in under. Through the rock, to the netlofts. Unloaded straight into the cellars. Up the ladders. Out the doors." He sniffed. "Nobody's used it since they built the moles. Two hundred years. It'll be full of things."

"It is," said Boss. "Some."

"You'd need to clear it."

"Yes."

"And light it. Boats can't see in the dark. Not in a channel. Not under the rock." The fish man looked at him. "And somebody'd have to tell the boats. Out on the lake. Where the mouth is. In the mole, under the water. They'd never find it."

"We've got a man with a mirror," said Boss.

The fish man looked at him for a very long time.

"*Hm*," he said. And then, very quietly, looking at the red ship: "Three hundred years. I've had a lot of people stand in my harbour mouth." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Never had anybody go *under* them."

***

They cleared it on Wednesday. Not all of them. Kei sent eight. Salty and Wendy, Azac and Jun Liang, Xola and Slep, Rafi, and Boss at the front.

It took all night. The fish man had been right; it was full of things. Silt-crawlers, mostly, long pale things with too many legs that lived in the mud at the bottom of the channel and came up when you walked past. And something bigger, near the arch, that lived in a hollow in the rock and came out at Azac like a door opening. Azac said a word the game didn't translate and put his shield in its face. It went back into its hollow and stayed there, sulking.

Rafi took the ledge. The narrow one, by the water, where only one person could stand. Because Boss said *Rafi, the ledge*, and he went. He stood on it with the big black sword held up with the floor, and when a silt-crawler came up out of the water at him, he steered.

*Hum.*

The sword went through it like a bell going through a note. It kept time a little. It always had. Rafi had never known what time it was keeping. He'd thought, once, that it was Deston's count, on the longest night, on Sedge's steps. Now he thought it was just his own heart.

"*Good*," said Boss, from the front.

Rafi went red in the dark.

***

The Open Hand found them at the end of the night.

Not by accident. Rafi worked that out afterward. They'd been watched. Somebody had seen eight of Constellation go down into the Undercliffs at the north-west stair at the second dusk and not come up. And somebody had told Marrok. And Marrok had sent six.

The Undercliffs were open ground. Anybody could fight anybody. Everybody knew that. It was why Boss levelled there; it was quiet, mostly, because most people didn't want to be in a place where anybody could fight anybody.

The Open Hand didn't mind.

Rafi was on his own when they came. Boss and the others had gone on along the ledge toward the arch, to see the mouth in the daylight. Rafi had stayed at the bottom of the stair, the narrow one, where the old road came down to the channel. Because somebody had to watch the stair. That was what you did. You didn't all go one way and leave the way back open. He'd learned that from Sedge, on her steps, a long time ago.

He heard them before he saw them. Six pairs of boots on wet stone, coming down. Fast. Not in step this time.

He stood up.

He was at the bottom of the stair. It was narrow. Very narrow. Old stone, worn in the middle, wet. One person wide. And at the bottom, a landing, a little flat place before the ledge, just wide enough for one person with a big sword.

The first of them came round the turn above him and stopped.

A man in dark red with an axe. Behind him, five more, coming down. Rafi could see them in the light of the lamp he'd left on the landing. Two axes, a spear, a mace. A woman with a bow at the back. The woman from the bread stall.

She saw him and smiled.

"Rafi," she said. "The one on the beach."

"Yes," said Rafi.

"Where's everybody else?"

"Down there," said Rafi. He didn't point. "Doing something."

"And you're watching the stair."

"Yes."

"On your own," said the woman. She was still smiling. "Nobody next to you." She looked at the big black sword. "Last chance. Come and be somebody."

Rafi thought about it. He wanted to be fair.

He thought about Sedge's steps, in Fernwick, in the first Long Night. Bram falling at four in the morning. Joy and the eel man with a boat hook. Him, at Lv 11, with a sword too big for him, saying *I can hold it properly now*. And holding it.

He thought about Boss, paying him and Bram and Joy twenty silver a day each to guard some rafts, in the second week. *Somebody shouts, I come.*

He opened his mouth and shouted.

"*BOSS*."

It went away along the ledge, along the channel, into the dark. Bouncing off the water.

Then he held the sword up with the floor, and let the ground take the weight, and steered.

***

They couldn't get past him.

That was the whole of it. That was the thing he'd think about afterward, for weeks. The stair was one person wide. They could only come at him one at a time. And he was at the bottom, on the landing, with a sword as long as he was tall. Every time one of them came down the last three steps, the sword was there. Not swinging wild. Steering. On the beat. *Hum.*

The axe man first. He came down fast and the sword met his axe and turned it into the wall. The axe man went back up two steps, surprised.

"*Again*," said the woman at the back.

The spear came next. Long, over the axe man's shoulder. The spear was the hard one. It could reach him from three steps up. Rafi didn't try to reach it back. He let it come, and stepped, and the spear went past his shoulder into the empty air over the channel. And on the way back up, he put the flat of the sword against the shaft and pushed. Not hard. Just enough. The spearwoman went over the edge of the stair into the black water with a splash that echoed for a long time.

"*Get her out*—"

They got her out. It took two of them. Rafi stood on his landing and let them, because Boss never hit anybody while they were helping somebody out of the water. He'd seen him not do it, in the first Long Night.

He got up.

"You can't hold it," said the woman with the bow. Not smiling now. "There's six of us. There's one of you."

"I know," said Rafi.

"So why are you still standing there?"

Rafi thought about it. He was breathing hard. His leg hurt; he kept his pain at thirty, the way Boss had told him, *enough to know where*.

"Because I shouted," he said. "And he said he'd come."

***

It was four minutes, he worked out afterward. From the shout to the first sound on the ledge behind him. Four minutes on a landing at the bottom of a stair, one person wide, with a sword that kept time.

The axe man was on the second step, coming down for the last time. Rafi could see it in his face. The woman behind him had an arrow on the string. The mace behind her. Six of them, and one of him, at nineteen per cent.

And behind him, on the ledge, from the dark: boots. Fast. More than one pair.

The axe man stopped.

Salty came round the corner of the ledge first. Black sword in both hands, at a run, his face like the face he'd had stepping out one pace on the longest night. Wendy behind him with an arrow already drawn. Azac with his enormous shield. Jun Liang with his hammer. Xola, Slep.

And at the back, last, walking, not running, with his shield on his back. Boss.

They stopped on the ledge behind Rafi. Nobody said anything.

The axe man looked at them. At Salty's sword. At Wendy's arrow. At Azac. Then at Rafi, on his landing, at nineteen per cent, with the big black sword still held up with the floor.

Then he went back up the stair.

The others went with him. Fast, round the turn, up into the dark. The woman with the bow went last. She stopped at the turn and looked back down at Rafi for a long moment.

She didn't say *nobody*. She didn't say anything at all. She just went.

***

Rafi sat down on the landing.

He hadn't meant to. His legs just stopped. He sat down on the wet stone at the bottom of the stair with the big black sword across his knees and nineteen per cent, and breathed.

Boss came along the ledge past the others and stopped in front of him.

"You shouted," said Boss.

"I shouted."

Boss looked at the stair. The wet stone, worn in the middle. The marks on the wall where an axe had gone. The black water below the landing, still moving where a spearwoman had been pulled out of it.

"Four minutes," said Boss.

"I didn't need you, Boss," said Rafi. It came out wrong. He'd meant it as a joke and it came out true. "I held it. They couldn't get past. I didn't need you to come."

"I know," said Boss.

He crouched down on the landing, in front of Rafi, so their faces were level. He didn't say anything else for a long time. Then he took a draught out of his belt and put it in Rafi's hand and closed Rafi's fingers round it.

"I came anyway," he said.

Rafi drank it. It was the good kind, the kind Boss made with the moonbells from the fen. It went down him like warm water. Nineteen to forty-one. He sat there on the landing with the empty bottle in his hand.

"That's what you said," said Rafi. "In the second week. *Somebody shouts, I come.*"

"Yes."

"You meant it."

"I don't say things I'm not going to do," said Boss. "I borrowed that from Dennis." He stood up. He looked down at Rafi for a moment, at the bottom of the stair. "That was a good hold."

Rafi didn't say anything. He couldn't.

Behind Boss, on the ledge, Wendy had both hands over her mouth. Salty was looking very hard at the channel. And Azac, leaning on his enormous shield, said, very loudly, to nobody: "*Four minutes*. Against *six*. On a *stair*. With a sword he got out of a *church*."

"It keeps time," said Rafi.

"It *does*," said Azac, with enormous feeling.

***

He wrote to Sedge that night. In the netloft, at the long table, with a candle, while the others talked about the Undercut and the fish man and the mirror. And how many boats, and what night.

He wrote to her every month. She couldn't read. She never had. When he'd first written to her, from the round town, the letters had waited in Fernwick for the swamp guy to come and read them out. Now somebody else read them. The eel man, mostly, slowly, on her steps in the evening.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he crossed out the last line, because the swamp guy was sitting four feet away on the bench under the wavy window with a cup of tea, and had read the whole thing over his shoulder.

He wrote it again underneath anyway.

*Tell the swamp guy I didn't say.*

Boss didn't say anything. But Rafi saw his face do the thing, at the corner of the mouth. And then, very quietly, to the cup in his hands:

"Two copper a plant," said Boss.

"It was a lot," said Rafi.
