Hana had stopped saying *one day*, and it felt very strange in her mouth.

She'd said it for years. On calls, at three in the morning her time and nine at night his. On the long table, with her head on Milli's shoulder. On the longest night, by the well, with the Bearer's Star lit in her hand. *One day. Somewhere with a view.* She'd said it so often it had stopped sounding like a day at all. It had sounded like weather.

Then, in August, on a bench made of a pole that held, they'd picked one. Four minutes. His month, her day, the same year, at the same time. And she'd stopped saying *one day*. Because it wasn't one day any more.

It was Saturday.

***

Milli wasn't in that week.

Out there, there were things to do. She didn't mind. She'd known since August that the last week would be like this. She'd be in the game on her own, at night, the way she'd been before Milli had an egg. Doing ordinary things, so that the ordinary things would still be there afterwards.

So on the Monday she went down the north tunnel on her own, to the cold place where the old stone met the new, and dug.

She didn't tell anybody why. She'd got good at not asking things; she was learning to be good at not telling them. She took her pick and her lamp and went down past the Delver workings into the deep rock. She found the seam she'd found for Leaka's lion in February, where the dull grey stones came out the size of eggs with a light at the back of them. She dug for four hours. She brought up one.

Not the size of an egg. The size of her fist. Grey and rough and heavy, like nothing.

She took it to the long table and sat with it every night that week. Cutting.

"What is it?" said Curse, on the Tuesday, from across the table.

"A stone," said Hana.

Curse looked at it for a long time. He'd learned to write a word that wasn't his, and then a wish, and then a wish upside down. He looked at things properly now before he said what they were.

"It's not a stone yet," said Curse.

"No," said Hana. "Not yet."

***

The round town was getting ready round her. She could feel it, the way you can feel a house getting ready for something while you sit in one room of it.

The middle pole stood by the fountain, green and bare, with its seven ropes going out to nothing. Jared was up a ladder against it every evening, checking the rings, checking the ropes, checking things that didn't need checking. Azac held the ladder. Six hundred and twelve lanterns sat in their crates round the fountain, folded flat. Every evening somebody came and opened a crate and looked in, as though they might have gone somewhere.

Chernn was dipping candles in the Hart's kitchen. Twelve. White. The ones she'd dipped last year for the roof had come from Mother Dipp. These she was dipping herself, one at a time, with Deston on the stool beside her holding the wick-rod and his ears going red whenever anybody came in. Hana didn't ask why his ears went red. She knew. They'd picked a day too. Two weeks after hers.

*It's catching*, she'd said, in August.

It had caught.

***

On the Monday night, late, she held Jared's ladder.

Azac had gone to bed. Jared hadn't. He was at the top of the ladder against the green pole at the third dawn, in the lamplight, with a rope in one hand, checking the fourth ring. He'd checked it on Saturday. And on Sunday. Hana came across the empty market from the Hart and put both hands on the ladder without saying anything, and he looked down and saw who it was.

"Alice," said Jared.

"Hi."

"You should be asleep."

"So should you," said Hana. "It's three in the morning. Out there. Your time it's—" She tried to work it out and gave up. "Whatever it is."

"Afternoon," said Jared. "I'm fine." He went back to the rope. "Just checking."

"You checked it on Sunday."

"I know."

She held the ladder. He checked the rope. Above them, the top of the green pole went up into the dark with its seven iron rings, and the seven ropes went out from it to the seven road ends with nothing on them. Bare. Waiting. She looked up at it for a long time.

"It bent," said Hana. "At New Year."

"It bent," agreed Jared. "And came back."

"I was under the beam."

"I know. Between me and Milli." He tied something off. "Where you were last year."

Hana didn't say anything for a while.

"You've built me a roof twice," she said. "And a bench."

Jared stopped working. He stood at the top of the ladder with the rope in his hand and didn't look down.

"I can't build out there," he said, at last. Quietly, to the pole. "I've never been able to do anything about out there. Papers and flights and where. All of it. I just—" He stopped. "I can do in here. So I do in here."

"I know."

"Tomorrow night," said Jared. "Saturday night. When you come in. It'll be up."

"I know."

"And I'll be under the beam," said Jared. "If it bends." He started down the ladder. "Every year. If you want. Every year it bends, I'll be under it."

Hana held the ladder until he was at the bottom. Then she let go of it and hugged him, at the foot of the green pole in the empty market at three in the morning. He stood very still for a moment, the way he did, and then hugged her back. Sawdust everywhere.

"*Y'all*," said Jared, into the top of her head, which wasn't the right word for one person and was exactly the right one.

***

On the Wednesday, Chernn showed her the candles.

Twelve of them, on the rack in the Hart's kitchen, white, cooling. Hana had thought they'd be ordinary. The ones from Mother Dipp last year had been ordinary, tall plain white ones, one in each of the twelve lanterns at the middle of the roof, round the top of the pole.

These had two wicks.

Every one. Two wicks, side by side, coming up out of the top of each candle a finger's width apart.

"Tallow Green," said Chernn. She was wiping her hands on her apron. Deston was on the stool behind her, very carefully looking at the ceiling. "When you ask somebody something there, you light a candle with two wicks. One for you and one for them. You light both, and then you ask. And they answer before it burns down." She looked at the rack. "I don't know what you do with one when the asking's already done."

"Neither do I," said Hana.

"I thought you could just light them," said Chernn. "Both wicks. And let them burn." She was quiet. "Two flames. One candle. Down to the bottom."

Hana looked at the twelve candles for a long time.

Behind Chernn, on the stool, Deston's ears had gone the colour of the banner.

"You did this," said Hana. "On the green. In July. With him."

"Yes," said Chernn.

"And it burned all the way down."

"I answered before it was half gone," said Chernn. "So we let it." She looked at Hana. "We let it burn all the way down anyway. On the green, by the pond. Past the third dawn. The whole village stood round in a ring." She almost smiled. "Mother Dipp put the stub on her windowsill. At the end of all the others."

Hana didn't say anything. She couldn't. She picked up one of the candles, still warm, and held it.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's catching," said Chernn. "You said."

***

On the Tuesday, she asked Ama Corrie.

She hadn't meant to. She'd gone to the counter for something else, a key to the cupboard where the lanterns lived. Ama Corrie had given it to her and then gone back to the Roll, open on the counter, the way it always was.

Hana looked at it.

Three hundred years of names. Everybody who'd come in through a door and signed. Everybody who'd kept something. *Isa Corrie. Came home through the north door. Kept the light.* *Ash*, three letters. Kite, with a line under him that had been empty for two years. *Hanarisu*, somewhere in the middle, in her own hand, from a winter ago. And beside it, *Millianna*, in his.

"Will you write it?" said Hana.

Ama Corrie didn't look up.

"It's not mine to write," she said. "It's out there."

"I know."

"I write what happens in here. Who comes in. Who keeps what." Ama Corrie turned a page. "Out there's yours."

"I know," said Hana. "I just wanted to ask."

Ama Corrie looked at her then, over the top of the Roll, for a long time. Hana couldn't read her face. Nobody could. Isa could, maybe. They had the same chin.

"*Hm*," said Ama Corrie.

And went back to the Roll.

Hana took the cupboard key and went away. She didn't mind. She'd known what the answer would be. She'd asked anyway, because asking was a thing you could do now, in the round town. A man in a gate had taught everybody that. And because it would have been worse not to have asked.

***

Leaka was out there.

That was the other strange thing. Leaka wasn't in the game that week either. She'd stopped coming in on the Monday, and the round town was so quiet without her that people kept looking round at the Hart's door. As if she might come through it at any moment screaming *JARRED*.

She was on a plane.

*AT THE AIRPORT*, Hana's phone said, out there, on the Wednesday.

*ON THE PLANE*

*CRYING ON THE PLANE. A MAN ASKED IF I WAS OKAY. I SAID I'M GOING TO A WEDDING. HE SAID WHOSE. I SAID MY BEST FRIEND'S. HE GAVE ME HIS NAPKIN*

*leaka*

*WHAT*

*you've been on the plane forty minutes*

*I KNOW. I'VE GOT ELEVEN MORE HOURS. I'VE GOT A LOT OF CRYING TO DO*

Hana sat on the end of her bed out there and laughed until she cried too, which she hadn't expected to, and which was fine.

On the Thursday:

*HERE*

That was all. One word. No capitals left over for anything else.

***

On the Wednesday, at the second dusk, she went up to the eye.

Isa was there. She was there every dusk now. Suzu carried her up the two hundred and twelve at the second dusk and down again after. And she sat on the *Somewhere* bench, Hana's bench, beside Kei's lamp, under Ama Corrie's grey blanket, looking north.

Hana had given her the bench in December. She hadn't been asked. She'd come up the stair one dusk and found Isa on the parapet. Small, old, on cold stone. And she'd said *sit here* and patted the bench, and Isa had looked at her for a long time and said *it's yours*. And Hana had said *it's for somebody who's looking for something*. And Isa had sat.

Hana sat down beside her now. There was room. It was a bench for two.

They didn't say anything for a while. Isa looked north, at the gold point at the bottom of the sky. Hana looked at Isa's hands in the blanket.

"You're getting married," said Isa.

Hana looked at her.

"Out there," said Isa. "On Saturday. Ama Corrie told me." She was still looking north. "She tells me things. She doesn't tell anybody else."

"Yes," said Hana.

"My grandmother's grandmother," said Isa, "sat on this roof. Before she went north. I told you that. In August." She moved her old hands under the blanket. "It was where people came. In the old guild. Up the two hundred and twelve, to the eye. To say something they meant. Out loud. So the roof would hear it."

"I remember."

"Have you said it?"

Hana thought about it.

"Out there," she said. "On Saturday. I'll say it there."

"That's out there," said Isa. "This is the roof." She turned her head at last and looked at Hana, close, so she could see her face. "Say it here too. Not to him. He's not here. To the roof."

Hana didn't say anything for a long time.

She looked out from the eye. At the round town under the dusk. The fountain, and the green pole beside it, bare. The seven roads going out. The market, and the Hart, and the blue door on Spoke Street, and forty-one squares of red on forty-one doors. And north, at the bottom of the sky, the gold point.

*Somewhere with a view.*

"I'm not going to say *one day* any more," said Hana.

She said it to the roof. Out loud, not very loud. It came out of her in her ordinary voice. As if she were telling somebody where she'd put the cupboard key.

"I said it for years. *One day*. Because it was easier than saying a day. Because a day could go wrong and *one day* couldn't." She was quiet. "And then he said *this is somewhere*. By the well. On the longest night. And I thought, it is. It's somewhere. We're *in* it." She looked at the bench under her hands. "So we picked a day. Here. On this. And I'm not going to say *one day* ever again. About anything. I'm going to say *Saturday*."

Isa looked at her for a long time.

"*Hm*," said Isa Corrie.

She put her hand out from the blanket and laid it flat on the bench between them. On the pale old ash, with *Somewhere* written under the scar, and the cat's-paw stamped into the left arm.

"That's something you meant," said Isa.

***

On the Thursday she finished the stone.

It took her until the third dawn. Sitting at the long table with the lamp, and the cutting wheel she'd made in Quarrymouth out of a grindstone and a treadle, and the stone. Grey and rough at the start of the week. Now it wasn't.

It was gold.

Not yellow. Gold, all the way through, like the lion's eyes but bigger. Cut in a shape she'd never cut before, with a lot of small flat faces all going round. So that wherever you looked at it from, it caught the light from somewhere else. And at the back of it, deep in, a light of its own, like the light in Kei's lamp.

She sat and looked at it for a long time at the long table, at the third dawn, on her own.

Then she wrapped it in a square of red paper that Curse had left over, and put it in her coat, and didn't tell anybody. Not yet. It was for afterwards.

***

On the Friday, the five came home from the hill in time. She was at the eye when they came up. She heard Suzu say *three hundred and one*, and Isa say *I know*. She stood at the parapet and cried, and Gabe cried, and Ama Corrie put her hand over her mouth. Then everybody went down the two hundred and twelve and left her there.

She stayed. It was the last night.

She sat on the bench on her own after Suzu carried Isa down, with Kei's lamp beside her. She looked at the round market. The green pole. The crates of lanterns. Jared, still, at the third dawn, up the ladder, checking a rope that didn't need checking.

Somebody came up the stair.

She knew who it was before he came out of the stair-head onto the ring. She'd known him for years. He walked like a person who'd been at the back of every room in the world and decided it was the best place to be.

"Alice," said Ice.

Only Ice and Jared called her that. Ice had called her it since the first night he turned up in her chat, years ago, before any of this. He'd never once called her Hana.

"Hi," said Hana.

He didn't sit on the bench. It was hers. He sat on the parapet across from her, with his shield on his back, and looked at her.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow."

"Are you frightened?"

Hana laughed.

"*Yes*," she said. "Obviously. Aren't you, about everything?"

"Yes," said Ice.

They sat there.

"In Singapore," said Hana. "In September. On the bench under the tree. With Hyv." She'd heard it from Gabe, about forty times, with hearts. "Were you frightened?"

"Yes."

"Then what?"

He thought about it. Properly. The way he did.

"Then I said it," said Ice. "And then I waited." He looked at the round market below them. "And somebody answered."

Hana didn't say anything for a while.

"That's all?" she said.

"That's all," said Ice. "It's not hard. You just say it. And then you wait." He looked at her across the eye. "You've been saying it for years. Not the words. The rest. Coming in at two in the morning his time. The well. The star. The bench." He was quiet. "Tomorrow's just the words."

"Just the words," said Hana.

"And then you wait," said Ice. "And he answers. He always has."

Hana looked at him for a long time across the eye. Her friend from a chat, years ago. Who'd been in everybody's chat. Who'd said *hi* at three in the morning in rooms with four people in them, and stayed. Who'd sat in the first of the nine chairs at the long table on the day Milli said *we've picked a day*, with his face doing nothing at all, very carefully.

"Thank you," said Hana.

"For what?"

"Coming up," said Hana. "On the last night. You didn't have to."

"I know," said Ice. "That's why I came."

***

At a quarter to eight, they stood at the eye. Not at the long table. Ice had stayed, and she'd stayed, and Kei came up the stair at twenty to eight with his map. He said nothing. He just stood at the parapet. Then Jared came up, with sawdust on him. And then, one at a time, without anybody asking, about fifteen more.

Hyv, who went straight to Ice and took his arm with both hands. Gabe, red-eyed. Kotori. Curse, on his own, without Leaka, holding his inkwell. Chernn and Deston. Suzu, who'd only just carried Isa down and came straight back up.

Nobody said anything about tomorrow. They didn't have to.

"Set one to watch," said Kei.

"*So the rest may sleep*," said Hana. With everybody. At the eye.

The egg took her at eight, on the bench.

***

Out there, it was Saturday.

She isn't going to write it down here. It's hers. And his. There was a room, and people in it, and the words, and then the wait. There was Leaka, who had flown a very long way. And there was Milli's hand, and then his answer, which he'd always given. That's all anybody in the round town will ever know.

It's enough.
