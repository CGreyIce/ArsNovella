At eight o'clock exactly, he lay down in the gel, pressed his finger to the glass, and closed his eyes, and smelled rain.

***

He came back where he'd left, on the end of Sedge's porch with his boots off, and for a few seconds he simply sat there.

It was dawn again. That still felt strange. He'd lived through a whole night and a whole day in here yesterday, and gone home, and slept, and woken, and eaten bread, and now it was dawn in the fen as if nothing had happened, mist on the channel, the frogs saying their one word. Eight of his hours made one day here. He'd done the maths in bed: every morning at eight, when he logged in, it would be sunrise in Fernwick. Every morning, the fen would be waiting for him in the same light. He liked that more than he could explain.

The bundles of herbs rustled overhead. Inside the house, someone was already grinding.

"You're back," said Sedge, without coming out.

"I'm back."

"Your boots are wet."

He looked at them, sitting by his feet. They were. He'd left them in the water. He put them on anyway.

"Friends of mine are coming this morning," he said. "Two of them. I called them."

The grinding stopped. "More of you."

"Two more. They're good," he said. "I promise."

"Hm," said Sedge, and the grinding started again.

***

There were more people on the Landing than yesterday.

He stood at the edge of the big square platform with the mist round his ankles and watched them come out of the door that was only big enough for one. They looked the way he had yesterday: the stop, the stare, the slow turn. There were a lot more of them, and they came faster. The second day was the first day for anybody who'd waited a day to see, and he guessed a lot of people had watched a lot of streams last night and gone to bed and woken up and bought a pod.

He also guessed most of them hadn't been hoping for a swamp.

"Oh, *come on*," said a woman in a linen shirt, to the sky.

He was looking at the door when Deston came through it.

He'd have known him from the other side of the fen. Deston was tall, the tallest of any of them, a whole head over most of the people around him, and he'd made himself look exactly like himself. Same short hair, same build, same easy way of standing with his weight on one leg as if he'd been there for a while and was happy to stay. He was wearing the linen shirt and a dark green jerkin, and he had a book under one arm. A real book, the blank one from the rack, with the strap round it.

He came out of the door and stopped, the way everyone did. He looked down at his hands. He breathed in. Ice watched his face do the exact thing his own had done yesterday morning, a sort of surprise so big it went past surprise into something quieter.

Then Deston looked up and saw him.

For a second he obviously didn't recognise him at all. He looked straight at Ice, then past him, then back. Ice watched him work it out: the hair, the streak, the eyes, the face that wasn't Ice's real face but had been Ice's face in every drawing and every emote and every stream for years.

"*Dec?*"

"Hi," said Ice.

Deston crossed the Landing in about four strides and hugged him.

He'd known Deston since they were thirteen. He'd hugged him a hundred times: at graduations, at birthdays, on the day he moved to Malacca. He hadn't hugged him in months, because Deston was in Singapore and he wasn't. And the thing about this hug was that it was *exactly* a Deston hug. Too tight, one hand thumping him twice on the back, and then a little shake, as though Deston was checking he was really there.

"You look like your *drawings*," said Deston into his shoulder.

"I *am* my drawings."

"You're so short."

"I'm the same height I've always been. You're just tall. You've always been tall. It's a problem you have."

Deston let go of him and held him at arm's length and looked at him and laughed, a big helpless laugh that went out across the Landing and made three strangers turn round.

"I can feel the *air*," said Deston.

"I know."

"Dec, I can feel the air. I can smell. It smells like— what is that—"

"Peat. And woodsmoke. And frogs, a bit."

"It smells like *frogs*." Deston sounded delighted. "We paid two and a half thousand dollars to smell frogs."

Xav came through a minute later.

He'd also come as himself, which was no surprise. Xav had never once in his life used a picture that wasn't his own face. Same height as Ice, near enough, the same sharp, steady look he'd had since poly, as if he were always quietly checking a sum. He came out of the door and stopped and breathed, and Ice saw his eyes go round the whole Landing in one slow sweep: the door, the mist, the people, the reeds, the houses on their stilts, the eel man on his crate, the sign for the militia yard. Taking it all in and putting it in order.

Then the eyes found Ice and Deston, and stopped.

"Huh," said Xav. "You look like your emotes."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment. It was an observation." Xav walked over, unhurried, and held out his fist, and Ice bumped it with his own. Then Xav looked at him for a second longer, and his face did something it almost never did. It smiled all the way, right up into the eyes.

"Hey, Dec," he said.

"Hey, Xav."

They'd played every game together for years, and these days, with Xav in Japan, they never got to be in the same room. And here they were. The three of them, on a wet wooden platform in a swamp, at dawn, standing close enough to touch.

"Okay," said Xav, after a moment. "Where is it?"

***

He didn't tell them. Not yet.

He showed them the town instead, quickly, walking them along the boardwalks while Deston kept stopping to touch things: a rope, a wattle wall, the cold surface of a water barrel. Ice pointed out the general store, the eel man, the herbs between the planks ("don't stand on them, I mean it"), and, at the top of the north walk where Xav could see it, the dark-thatched house of the woman who'd taught him everything.

He also told them, quietly, about the pain slider.

"Fifty?" said Xav.

"Fifty."

"I'm not putting mine on fifty," said Xav. "I'm not insane."

"I put mine on twenty and the eel man looked at me funny," said Deston. "Is that related?"

"No. The eel man looks at everyone funny."

He asked what they'd taken off the rack.

Deston held up the book.

"Nobody knows what it does," said Deston happily. "I read every forum. There's a whole thread on the book, three hundred replies, and the best theory anybody had was 'it's a joke'. Somebody said it was for people who wanted to read in game. Somebody else opened it and said all the pages were blank and threw it in a lake." He tapped the strap. "I want to know what it does."

That was Deston all over. Give him a room full of people he didn't know and he'd talk to all of them. Give him something nobody understood and he'd pick it up.

Xav had the daggers.

He drew them for Ice to see, one in each hand, short and plain and leaf-bladed. He turned them over, once, the way you'd test the weight of a pen. "Two blades," he said. "You're going to stand at the front and make things look at you. I'm going to stand behind them and cut them while they're looking." He put the daggers away. "It's what we did in every game. Why would it be different here?"

"It's very different here," said Ice. "You have to learn how to hold them."

Xav looked at him. Then his eyes went past Ice to a sign with an arrow that said MILITIA YARD.

"Of course we're going to the trainer first," said Xav.

***

Hollis looked up from his bucket as they came through the gap in the fence, and looked at Ice, and then at the other two, and then back at Ice.

"You brought more," he said.

"Two more. They asked if they could come in."

"Did they." Hollis put down the dummy he was mending. "That's three, then. Three out of the however-many-hundreds who've come through my fence since yesterday." He stood up, knees cracking, and looked at Xav's daggers. "I don't teach those. Close work. Old Pell taught knives, before he died. But I know enough to show you how to stand so you don't stab yourself."

He looked at Deston's book. He was quiet for a moment.

"And I definitely don't teach that," he said. "You want Brother Amon. Chapel by the east jetty. He teaches the children their letters." He paused. "He used to teach other things, when there was anybody who wanted them."

Deston looked at the book in his hands, and then at Hollis, and then at Ice, with an expression Ice knew very well. It was the expression of a man who'd been told there was a door, and wanted to go through it immediately.

"Go," said Ice. "We'll meet you here at second breakfast."

"What's second breakfast?"

"It's a time. Marta says it. I've no idea. Two hours."

Deston went.

***

Xav learned faster than Ice had.

That was no surprise either. Xav had always learned faster than anybody, because Xav did the thing that most people didn't, the thing Ice had only worked out yesterday after twenty minutes of being graded down: he didn't try to do it right. He asked what *right* was made of, broke it into pieces, and fixed the pieces one at a time.

Hollis showed him how to stand: light, weight forward, the opposite of Ice's sit-back. Close work was about being somewhere before the other man knew you'd moved. Xav did the cut he'd been shown, a short crossing slash with both blades, the right hand then the left, and the window gave him eleven.

He did it again. Fourteen. He asked Hollis where his weight should be at the end of the cut. Hollis showed him. He did it again and got twenty-two. He asked Ice to stand where a monster would stand and to swing at him slowly, and he cut the space just behind Ice's swing, where a man's ribs would be as his arm went past, and got thirty-one.

"Your arm was in the way," Xav said to Ice. "You open up when you swing. Everything does. That's where I go."

"That's where you always went," said Ice.

Deston came back at second breakfast, which turned out to be about half past nine, glowing.

"He's lovely," he said. "Brother Amon. He's about a hundred years old and he's got no teeth and he made me sit on a bench for the children while he looked at the book." He held it up. The strap was undone. "And then he opened it."

He opened it now, with great care. The pages were blank, as the forums had said: cream-coloured, thick, rough-edged. Except the first. On the first page, in a neat brown hand, there was a single line.

*Let the many move as one.*

"It's a skill," said Deston. "That's the skill. You read it out. You have to *read* it, out loud, and you touch the spine while you do, and then for a little while—" He stopped, and looked at Ice, and his whole face lit up. "Dec. You know when we were in choir. When the whole row came in on the same breath, and you could feel it?"

"Yeah."

"It's that," said Deston. "It's exactly that."

"What's it do?" asked Xav.

"I don't know yet," said Deston, perfectly happy. "Brother Amon said I'd find out. He said nobody's opened one of these in thirty years." He closed the book and strapped it. "He said the last person who could read it was his teacher, and his teacher used it to hold a bridge with eleven men for a whole night. He said it like that. Very casually. And then he asked me if I wanted tea."

"Did you?"

"I *had* the tea, Dec. It was *nice*."

***

He took them out to the south walk after that, and they fought rats together for the first time.

It was clumsy and noisy and wonderful. Ice did what he'd learned: found a narrow place, put the shield's slope out low in front of them like a wall with a lean on it, and banged his sword on the rim until three rats came to see what the fuss was. They hit the slope and slid. Xav was behind them before they'd finished sliding. Deston stood a step back with his book open and his hand on the spine and read the line, *Let the many move as one*, in a voice that wobbled because he was trying very hard not to laugh.

Nothing happened.

"It says *keep time*," Deston said, frowning. "Time with *what?*"

He read it again. Nothing again. Then, on the third try, he slowed down. He read it like he'd have sung it, years ago in the back row, in time, the stresses falling where they wanted to fall. *Let the MANY move as ONE.*

Ice felt it.

He couldn't have said what it was. It was like the half second before you jump: a sort of readiness, all through his arms and legs. When the next rat came off the slope, his cut came down at exactly the moment it should, and he felt Xav's blades go in on the other side in the same breath, as if they'd counted it in together.

***

He sold his first draughts that morning, standing on a crate beside Marta's door.

He hadn't meant to. But on the way back from the south walk they passed the store, and the queue was out of the door and along the boardwalk. Two dozen people in linen shirts with bitten ankles were waiting for a shelf that had been empty since yesterday morning, and Marta's voice from inside was saying "*No*" at regular intervals in a tone of great suffering.

He looked at the queue. He looked at the pack on his back, which held twenty-two draughts he'd brewed at Sedge's fire last night. A third were Poor, the rest Fair, and six were Good with a drop of moon in them.

"Xav," he said. "What would you charge?"

Xav looked at the queue for a long moment, doing the sum.

"Marta charged thirty copper," he said. "There's no supply. You could ask a silver and they'd pay it. But they've got almost nothing. Nobody's got anything. Day two. A silver's most of what some of them have." He looked at Ice. "What do you want? Money, or them?"

"Them," said Ice. "I want them back tomorrow. And the day after."

"Then forty," said Xav. "A bit more than Marta, so she doesn't hate you. Not so much that they do." He paused. "Don't sell the Good ones. Not yet. Nobody here knows what Good means yet. When they find out, they'll pay four times."

"You've thought about this."

"I watched you make them for four hours last night," said Xav. "Of course I've thought about this."

So Ice stood on a crate and said, quite loudly, "Healing draughts. Fair. Forty copper," and the queue turned round like a field of sunflowers.

He sold sixteen in about four minutes. Poor ones he gave away for twenty, with a warning that they tasted of cabbage, and nobody cared. A boy with a hatchet and two bitten ankles bought one and drank it standing in front of him. Ice watched the boy's face change, watched the little warmth go through him, and the boy said "*Oh,*" in exactly the voice Deston had used, and then "thank you," and then, a bit embarrassed, "Is there more tomorrow?"

"There'll be more tomorrow," said Ice.

Marta came out of her door halfway through and looked at him for a long moment, and he braced for it. But all she said was "Forty," with a sniff, and went back inside. Later he'd find out she'd put her own price up to forty the same afternoon, and she seemed to feel this made them even.

They ate lunch together. Not real lunch. A loaf of Marta's bread split three ways on the end of the north walk, with their legs over the water. It did nothing for their health bars and tasted of good bread, and they agreed it was the best thing they'd eaten all week.

Deston had his book open on his knee, running his finger along the one line as if more might appear. Xav was cleaning his daggers with a scrap of rat pelt, which didn't make them any cleaner. Ice had six silver in his pouch. It was the most money he'd ever been so pleased about.

"Okay," said Xav. "Now."

"Now what?"

"Now you tell us."

Ice looked at them both. Then he looked up at the sun. It was past its height, going over toward the west, and the light on the water had started to go long and gold.

"I'll show you," he said. "We have to go now, though. It's a long walk, and we need to be there before dark." He paused. "And I'm going dark. For a while. It's not mine to show. I promised somebody."
