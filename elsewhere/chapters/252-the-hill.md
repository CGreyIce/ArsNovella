The hill wasn't high. He'd thought it would be. In his head, for three weeks, it had been a mountain. It was a hill. Forty paces up, steep, over stone and grit and a moss that was cold and dry and crackled under his boots. He went up it behind Suzu. Second. With the best candle going ahead of him, low, and the black case on Suzu's back, and nothing coming out of it.

At the top, the ground flattened. And Suzu stopped.

Ice stopped behind him.

He couldn't see anything. The candle made its little circle on the ground, the size of a cart wheel. Grit, moss, the edge of something grey. That was all. But he could feel it. All round, in a ring, close. Standing things. Tall, still, old. The way you feel people standing round you in a dark room, before your eyes have got used to it.

"Twelve," said Hyv, behind him. Very quietly. She'd come up the hill last but two and stopped at the top, and he could hear her listening. "There's stones. In a ring. Twelve—" She stopped.

"What?" said Ice.

"Eleven," said Hyv. "I can hear eleven. Standing." She was quiet. "And one that isn't."

***

They lit everything.

Every candle. Nine of Chernn's, and the best one, and the watch in its middle. They set them down on the ground at the top of the hill in a line, out from where they'd stopped. Ten small flames in the nothing, making a little path of light across the grit. And then Hako took his and walked it round, slowly, in a circle, with the voice very quiet. *The narrator proceeds clockwise.* And as he went the stones came out of the dark one at a time, at the edge of his light.

Grey. Tall. Taller than Salty. Rough, unworked, the way they'd come out of the ground somewhere. Each one leaning very slightly in, toward the middle of the ring, the way the lamps on the north road had leaned north. As if for three hundred years they'd been looking at the same thing.

Hako counted them as he went. Out loud, in his own voice.

"One. Two. Three."

At eleven, he stopped.

Where the twelfth should have been, on the north side of the ring, there was nothing standing. Hako held his candle low. And there on the ground, long and grey, lay a stone. On its side. Its foot still in the hole it had come out of, a dark pit in the grit. Its top a long way off across the moss, toward the middle of the ring.

Fallen. In. Toward the middle.

Toward the flat stone.

***

The flat stone was in the middle of the ring. Exactly. Ice found it when Suzu did, walking in from the edge with the best candle, the two of them. A flat grey stone the height of a table, round, very old. And on its top, cut into it, a crown. A ring of points round a ring, the way you'd draw a crown if you were a child. Worn so smooth by three hundred years of hands that you could hardly see it in the candlelight. You could feel it, if you ran your fingers over it.

Nothing on it.

There'd been a lamp on it. For three hundred years. A lamp, and a woman, and her daughter, every night. And then one woman, on her own, for a long time. And then nobody.

Suzu stood beside it with the case on his back and didn't touch it.

Ice looked at the fallen stone. It had come down inward. Its top was lying across the moss three paces from the flat stone. As if it had leaned in, and leaned in, and kept leaning, for ten months, toward the place where the light had been. And then, one night, gone over.

"It fell toward it," he said.

"Looking," said Suzu. Very quietly. "Isa said. Everything leans toward the middle. Even the ring." He looked at the fallen stone. "When the light went, it leaned in to look for it. And there was nothing there. So it kept leaning."

***

There were things on the hill.

Not many. Kotori found them, at the foot of the flat stone, on the south side, out of the wind. A bowl. Clay, plain, chipped. A blanket, grey, folded, very old, gone stiff with the cold. A jar, the same size as the one in Suzu's case, empty, with its lid off and its inside black with three hundred years of oil.

And a stick. Short, worn smooth at the top where a hand had held it.

Kotori knelt and looked at them for a long time in the light of her candle. She didn't touch them either.

"She left them," said Kotori. "When she walked south. She took the lamp and left everything else."

"She was coming back," said Hyv. "She thought. Or she knew she wasn't." She knelt beside Kotori. She put out one finger and touched the edge of the blanket. Just the edge. "It's the same grey," she said. "As the one on her chair. Ama Corrie's."

Ice looked at the blanket.

Ama Corrie had put her own grey blanket round Isa in the chair by the north door, on the night she came home. The first night. And Isa had been asleep under it ever since.

The same grey. Two blankets, three hundred years apart, a road of lights between them. Kept by two sisters' lines who'd never once met until a morning in December.

"Leave them," said Ice. "They're hers."

***

He found the notches at about three in the morning.

He'd gone back to the flat stone on his own, after. Not alone; Hyv was on the south side of the ring, listening, and the others were round a little fire Slep had made of nothing much. But on his own at the stone. He'd put his hand on it, on the worn crown on the top. And then, because he couldn't help it, he'd run his fingers round the edge.

There were marks on the edge.

Small. Cut into the rim of the flat stone, all the way round. Little straight lines, close together, one after another, the way you'd count something. He felt them with his fingers in the dark and couldn't see them. Then he brought his candle down, *Sedge*, and held it low. And there they were.

Notches. Hundreds of them. Round the whole rim of the flat stone, in a line. Each one a little different, cut by a different hand. Some deep, some shallow. Some straight, some leaning. Going all the way round, once, and starting again on a second line, under the first.

He didn't count them. He didn't need to.

*One every spring.*

"Suzu," he said.

Suzu came across the ring with the best candle. He looked where Ice was looking. He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Three hundred," said Suzu.

"I think so."

"One for every stone. Every spring. When they set the stone in the water, they cut a notch." He put his finger on the rim, at the start, where the first one was, so old it was nearly gone. "So they'd know how many."

He moved his finger along. Slowly. Not counting out loud. Ice watched him not count, the way he'd watched him count three hundred stones in the still water with nine people counting behind him.

At about two hundred and something, Suzu's finger stopped.

It wasn't a notch. It was a cross. Two short lines, crossed, cut very shallow, as if by somebody whose hand wasn't steady.

"The spring that wasn't," said Suzu.

Ice looked at it.

She'd been ill, Isa had said. The woman who kept it. The winter the lamp nearly went out. Nine nights with the flame so small you could cover it with a thumb. And in the spring she hadn't walked out with a stone. She couldn't. But she'd cut a mark. Not a notch, because there wasn't a stone. A cross. So the count would still be right. So whoever came after would know a spring had been missed, and not think they'd lost one.

So they'd know where the gap was.

"Gigi grew one," said Ice.

"Gigi grew one," said Suzu. He took his finger off the cross. "Somebody should cut a notch."

***

He didn't. It wasn't his. He'd find out later that Suzu did it the next night, at the third dawn, with Kotori's little knife, very carefully, beside the cross. Not on top of it. Beside. A notch, small and straight, and under it, smaller, a little thorn.

***

They raised the fallen stone over two nights.

It was the only thing to do. Isa had told them on the north steps, in October: *A ring of stones round a hill. A kept place. Nothing comes inside.* Twelve stones. Not eleven. A ring with a gap in it wasn't a ring. It was a wall with a door in it. And the dark would come in through the door to the middle.

You couldn't light it in a ring with a gap. Suzu said that first, at the fire, the first night. And then nobody argued.

So they raised it.

It was the heaviest thing Ice had ever tried to move. Heavier than a crown at the bottom of a lake. Heavier than a bell's hammer every eight seconds. It lay on its side in the moss, longer than three of them, grey and rough and cold. With its foot still in its pit and its top three paces from the flat stone. And when the nine of them put their hands on it, all at once, and pushed, it didn't move at all.

"*Again*," said Ice.

It didn't move.

They tried levers. Slep's glaive, the long haft of it, under the top of the stone, with Slep and Xola both on the end. Xola's trident beside it. Thea's shield as a fulcrum, which made Hako say *the one with the lamp has, appropriately, provided the pivot*, and Thea hit him. It lifted a finger's width. Then the glaive bent and Slep said a word the game didn't translate and they let it down.

They tried the rope. The rope man's rope, from the rope-walk in Masmark. A hundred fathoms, hand-twisted, straw-coloured, that had tied nine people together across the nothing and three hundred stones. They looped it round the top of the fallen stone and ran it out across the ring and over the top of the stone opposite, the sixth, like a pulley. And all nine pulled.

It lifted. A hand's width. Two.

And stopped. And came down again in the moss with a sound like a door shutting.

***

"It's not the weight," said Ice. On the first night, at the fire, at four in the morning, with nine people sitting round it with their hands hurting. "It's when."

They looked at him.

"Everybody's pulling," said Ice. "Everybody's pulling hard. But not at the same time." He looked at his hands. "I pull, and then Slep pulls, and then Hako. A breath apart. Like the numbers on the stones. Everybody one behind." He was quiet. "That's nine small pulls. Not one big one."

"So we pull together," said Xola.

"On what?" said Thea. "We can't see each other. We can't see the rope. We can't see *anything*."

Nobody said anything.

"Deston would count it," said Kotori. Very quietly. "*Accord.* He'd sing, and everybody would be in time. Without knowing why." She looked at the fire. "He did it for Chernn on a chalk hill. He does it for twenty on a bridge."

"Deston's at home," said Gigi. "On the windows."

"I know," said Kotori.

Ice looked at the fire for a long time.

He thought about the cellar in Masmark, in May. Fifty-one seconds with no rests. And Val on the steps, writing it down. Dashes and dots. And then the page she'd given Xav, three kinds of mark, from the bridge.

He thought about tempo.

"I can't count it," he said. "I don't know how." He looked round the fire. At all of them. "But I can set it."

***

The second night, he stood at the fallen stone.

Not on the rope. At the stone. At its foot, where it was still in its pit. With his shield off his back, set down against the stone's side, and his sword in his hand, point down on the moss. The way he'd stood in a cellar at the end of a ladder, in the high tide, four nights, with Salty on the bottom rung saying *your hips are wrong*.

"Don't pull," he said. "Not yet. Listen."

He went down into the ground.

Wide. Heavy. All the way down, his weight into his legs, into the grit, into the hill. So still that the dust round his feet, which he couldn't see, he knew had stopped moving. The weight. Salty's. Borrowed, in a cellar, for a bridge.

And waited.

Not for anybody to go. For the moment.

He felt it come. Up through the hill. The way he'd felt the ground for nineteen nights in the nothing with his front foot. The way he'd felt the still water under the round town on the longest night, standing up. Something under the hill, very deep. Slow. A breath, the dark breathing round the ring, Isa's mother said. In, and out.

He waited for the in.

His hips moved.

The sword came round. Not at anything. Slow, heavy, from the floor, the whole of him under it, turning. The way it had come round on the bridge into Lucan's blade. Into the flat of his own shield, set against the fallen stone's side.

*BOOM.*

It went through the stone. Through the hill. He felt it go. Out across the ring and through every one of the eleven standing stones and back. Like the bell at the top of the tower, the last time, when Salty rang it with the flat of a black sword.

"*NOW*," said Ice.

***

They pulled.

All nine. At once. Not one behind another; at once. On the *boom*. Because you couldn't not hear it. Because it went through the hill and through the rope and through their hands and their feet. And everybody's body did the same thing at the same time, the way Deston's *Accord* made people move before they knew why.

The stone came up.

Not a finger. Not a hand. Up. Off the moss, its top rising, the rope going taut as iron over the sixth stone opposite. Slep's glaive under it, Xola's trident, Thea's shield. All of them, on one beat. The stone's top coming up out of the dark at the edge of the candles, higher, grey, rough, three hundred years old.

And stopping. Halfway. The rope singing.

"*Again*," said Ice.

He went down into the hill.

Waited for the in.

*BOOM.*

"*NOW*—"

It went over the top.

Up, and past the balance, and settled, slowly, into its pit, the way a tooth goes back in. Upright. Leaning very slightly in toward the middle, the way the others leaned. Toward the flat stone with the worn crown.

Twelve.

***

Nobody said anything.

Nine people standing round a ring of twelve stones at the top of a hill in the still water, in the nothing, past the edge. Breathing very hard, with their hands on fire. The rope gone slack, lying across the moss. Slep's glaive bent. Thea's shield with a new dent in it.

And then a window. Small, plain, in the corner of everybody's eye, the way the game put things it didn't think you'd need.

[ The Ring · a kept place · twelve of twelve ]

[ *Nothing comes inside.* ]

He'd seen that window before. In a fen, on the first night, a long time ago. *The Heron-Stones. Nothing comes inside.* Rachael had stopped at the edge of it, once, and sat down where she was. For the first time in her life, something was keeping things out *for* her.

He read it twice.

Then he felt it.

Not the window. The other thing. All round the ring, outside it, in the nothing, in the still water. The dark, leaning in. The way it had been leaning on the north road for ten months. Toward the middle. And for the first time since the twenty-second stone, it stopped. At the ring. At the twelve. It leaned, and the ring was there, and it couldn't come in.

He'd been at the front of nothing for three weeks.

Now there was a ring round him. And nothing came inside.

***

Hyv found him at the twelfth stone afterward. The one they'd raised. He was sitting at the foot of it, inside the ring, with his back against it, the way he'd sat against a bell at the top of a tower. His shield on his knees. Breathing.

She sat down beside him.

"You did the bridge," she said. "On a stone."

"I did Salty's."

"You did Salty's on a *stone*," said Hyv. "So nine people would pull at once." She was quiet. "You used to only do it at people."

He thought about it.

"It's not just for people," he said. "Tempo." He looked at the ring, at the eleven stones going round in the candlelight, and the twelfth at his back. "It's for anything that needs to happen at the same time."

She didn't say anything for a long time. She put her head on his shoulder.

"I couldn't see it," she said. "The stone. Coming up. I couldn't see anything." She was very quiet. "But I heard it. All nine of us. At once. On the *boom*." She swallowed. "I've never heard that. Nine people doing one thing at the same moment. You can't hear it in the light. There's too much else."

He didn't say anything. He put his hand on the back of her head, between her horns.

Across the ring, in the middle, Suzu was standing at the flat stone. With the black case on his back. Not opening it. Just standing there, with his hand on the cat's-paw on the strap, looking at the worn crown.

"Tomorrow," said Suzu, without turning round. "At dusk."

"At dusk," said Ice.

"*Here's the middle*," said Suzu. Very quietly. To the stone. To the case. To the dark, leaning on the ring outside. "*Not yet.*"

Outside the ring, in the nothing, the dark went on leaning.

But it didn't come in.
