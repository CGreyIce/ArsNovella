They went out through the north door at the second dawn on Tuesday. Ten of them, and the north door stuck, the way it always did.

"Mind the north door," said Ama Corrie, from behind the counter, without looking up.

"Gabe fixed it," said Kei.

"Mind it anyway," said Ama Corrie.

Kei put his shoulder to it. It stuck, and then gave, and swung open onto the north road and the grey morning. A cold wind came in off the moor with the smell of heather in it, and the ten went out one by one.

Ice was standing outside.

He wasn't going. He'd said so on the stair on Sunday, and he'd meant it. But he was standing at the bottom of the north steps in the grey light, with his shield on his back and his hands at his sides. As if he'd come to see them off and didn't quite know how you did that, because it was usually him being seen off.

"Go on, then," said Ice.

Kei looked at him.

"You stole that," said Kei. "From Thea."

"I borrow things," said Ice. "I give them back." He looked past Kei at the others. At Higu with her map sheets in oilskin under her coat. Tiger with a sack of lanterns over his shoulder. Hana with her pick and Curse with his inkwell. Rafi with the big sword that still kept time a little. Drew at the edge, watching feet. Xola, glaring at nothing. And Thea and Gigi, at opposite ends of the line, not looking at each other.

His eyes stayed on those two for a moment longer than on anybody else.

Then he looked back at Kei.

"Look at it properly," he said. "The edge. For me. And tell me what it's like."

"I will."

"Not yet," said Ice. "Don't go in."

"I know," said Kei. "I'm not going in. I'm going to the edge."

"I know," said Ice. And something happened at the corner of his mouth. "That's different."

***

The north road ran out past Kite's milestone the way it had in March, into heather and stone and sky.

Kei had walked it once before. He remembered every step. Higu at the front checking her map sheets every hour. Gigi with her thorn. Salty silent, Tiger skimming, Rafi stepping in front of Higu in the dark and swinging on the blade's hum. The first waystone, with a five-line star cut in it and a groove pointing north. The first lantern ring, three hundred paces up the line. Twelve iron posts in a circle with empty cages on top, and a flat stone in the middle with a star.

[ A kept place. Nothing comes inside. ]

He'd thought, in March, that it was beautiful. He hadn't known what it was.

He knew now. Standing in the middle of the ring at the first dusk, while Tiger went round it with his sack and lit the cages one by one, Kei knew. Somebody had set these out. A long time ago. Behind them, as they went north. Twelve lanterns in a ring every day's walk. A kept place, so that whoever came after would have somewhere to sleep where the dark couldn't get in.

*So the rest may sleep.*

"They did this for us," said Hana, beside him. She was looking at the lanterns coming on round the ring, one by one, in the dusk. "Didn't they? Not for themselves. They were going *forward*. They didn't need somewhere to sleep behind them." She had her hand in her pocket, round the star. "They set these out for whoever came after."

"For whoever came after," said Kei.

"For us," said Hana. "Three hundred years later."

Tiger lit the last cage and came back into the middle of the ring, and stood with them, and looked round.

"I always just thought they were lamps," said Tiger.

"They are lamps," said Kei.

"Yeah," said Tiger. "But I thought they were *just* lamps." He looked at the ring for a long time. "I've lit these every night we've been up here. Since March. I never knew they were for anybody."

***

The Unlit came on the second night, the way Kei had known they would.

They came up out of the moor pools at the third dusk, low and pale and wet. They didn't come up out of running water, the old Roll said. Only still. *Comes to light, and puts it out, and goes on.* There were more of them than in March, and bigger, the nearer you got to the edge. Kei counted forty before he stopped counting.

They were in the second ring. It held. Nothing came inside; that was what a kept place was for. But the Unlit came up to the iron posts and stood there in a pale crowd, reaching for the lanterns. And when one of them got a hand round a cage, the light inside it went out, the way a candle goes out in a cupped palm.

[ The second ring · Lights: 11 of 12 ]

"*Front*," said Thea.

She was already moving. Kei watched her go to the gap where the light had gone out, between two posts, with her tower shield up. She stood in it, with her weight on the balls of her feet. Loose. Ready to give a little and turn.

The first Unlit came through the gap at her and she Turned it. Right, into Drew, who was there.

The second came, and she Turned it left, and Xola was there, and the trident went in.

The third came low, and there were two behind it, and a fourth at the next post along, reaching for the next cage. Thea's shield was busy. And without turning round, in a perfectly level voice, the voice Kei had heard her use on her own stream when she called things for her six, Thea said:

"*Thorn, left.*"

And from the middle of the ring, at once, without a pause, Gigi said "*Lie*."

[ Lay · Grade 84% ]

The thorn came up out of the heather in a black hedge between the two left-hand posts. The Unlit at the next cage ran into it, and stopped, and came over it bent double, slow. Tiger skimmed in and out and it was gone.

"*Turn, right*," said Gigi.

Thea Turned right.

Kei stood in the middle of the ring with his knife and three drops of venom, and watched. He'd seen a lot of fighting in eight months. He'd been in a lot of it, badly. He knew what it looked like when two people fought well together, and this was that. It was what Ice and Gabe looked like, or Xav and his lot, or Hana and Jared and Milli in a tunnel. Two people calling for each other without looking, and each one there when called.

It was also, he knew, two people who weren't friends. Who'd said so, to each other's faces, on a window seat, with tea. *We're not going to be what we were.* *Guildmates.* *Two things and a rule.*

He watched them hold one ring together for twenty minutes, in the dark. *Thorn, left. Turn, right.* Never once looking at each other.

When it was over and the last Unlit had gone back into its pool, Thea stood in the gap with her shield down, breathing. Gigi stood in the middle of the ring with her staff, breathing.

"Good thorn," said Thea, without looking round.

"Good turn," said Gigi, without looking round.

That was all. Thea went to sit by Xola. Gigi went to sit by Higu. Neither of them said anything else all night.

But Xola, sitting by the fire with Thea's head on her shoulder, looked across the ring at Gigi for a long moment. Then, very slightly, she nodded. Once.

Gigi saw it. Kei saw her see it.

Neither of them said anything about it. Kei didn't write it down. He just kept it.

***

They came to the old hall on the third day at noon.

It was exactly as he remembered it. Round and grey on a hill, half its dome fallen in, three doors and no north door. The north half was open to the sky, and a birch tree grew up through the floor where a long table must once have stood. A curved counter under a fallen beam, like Ama Corrie's. And on the wall of what had been the banner room, the hooks. Below them, a paler rectangle in the stone where a banner had hung for a long time and then not hung any more.

Hana went to the pale rectangle without being asked. She took the star out of her pocket and held it up to the top edge, where there were marks in the stone. Old marks, like the ghost-marks behind the banner in the Hall of Roads. And among them, near the top, a small five-pointed shape, very faint, where something had once been fixed.

The star from the wall was the same shape. Exactly. The same five points, the same lean.

Hana didn't say anything. She stood there holding it up for a long time. Then she put it back in her pocket.

"They took it with them," said Higu, very quietly. "Their banner. With the star on it. North."

"And somebody carved it," said Hana. "On a wall, under a quarry, a long way south. The last of them. Holding it up." She looked at the empty north half of the hall, open to the sky. "Somebody who stayed behind, maybe. And remembered."

Nobody said anything.

Gigi, standing at the open north edge of the hall with her staff, looking out, said quietly, "It fell down."

"It did," said Higu.

"And they kept it anyway," said Gigi. "All of it. Every custom. Every town. For three hundred years." She looked at the north. "That's how it stays up."

Thea, across the hall, by the birch tree, didn't say anything. But Kei saw her look at Gigi's back for a long moment, the way you look at somebody who's said a thing you've been trying to say yourself.

***

North of the broken wall, the stones began.

They began exactly where Kei remembered. A line of standing stones, waist high, every fifty paces, going north across a long bare slope of heather. Straight as a ruled line. Down into a wide shallow valley, and up the far side, and on. And at the top of the far side, about a mile off, the light stopped.

Not the stones. The light.

It was the strangest thing Kei had ever seen, in the game or out of it. He'd seen it once before and it was no less strange the second time. It was early afternoon, a bright day, the sun high in the south behind them. And a mile north the day simply ended. Not in a line, exactly. More like a wall of weather that wasn't weather. A darkness standing up out of the land as high as you could see, flat black, with no sunset in it and no stars. The line of stones went up the far slope toward it, every fifty paces, and went into it, and didn't come out.

Higu stood beside him at the broken wall and looked at it. Her hand had gone to her coat, where the map sheets were.

"I put my light in there," she said. "In April. And something closed round it."

"I know."

"I'm not putting it in again."

"I know," said Kei. "We're not going in. We're going to look at the stones."

***

They looked at the first stone.

Kei had seen it in March and thought it was a stone. Waist-high, grey, weathered, with a five-line star cut in its north face and a groove running north across its top. Every waystone on the north moor had one.

He looked at it now, properly, the way Ice had said. *Look at it properly. For me.*

The groove wasn't a groove.

It had worn into one, over three hundred years. Rain and frost and wind. But at the southern end of it, on the very top of the stone, there was a hollow. Round, about the size of a cupped hand, worn smooth. And when Kei put his finger in it and drew it out again, his fingertip was black.

Soot.

"Curse," said Kei.

Curse was already there. He was looking at the hollow with his head on one side, the way he looked at things he was going to write on. He took a blank tag out of his coat, uncapped his inkwell, and wrote one word in blue.

*LAMP*

He tied it round the stone with a careful knot, just under the hollow.

[ Inscription · Label (Intermediate 61%) · it remembered ]

Nothing glowed. Nothing lit. Curse's labels didn't do that. They didn't make things into what they'd been. They reminded them. And the stone, reminded, looked—Kei didn't know how else to say it—it looked like a lamp. It stood a little straighter. The hollow on its top looked like what it was: a cup for a flame, worn by three hundred years of nobody filling it.

"They were all lamps," said Hana. She'd gone very pale. "Every stone. Every fifty paces. All the way in."

"*Set them out behind us*," said Kei.

He looked up the line of stones. Down the shallow valley and up the far side toward the black. Every fifty paces. He counted them, the way Ama Corrie counted things. Twenty-two stones between here and the edge of the dark.

Twenty-two lamps. Out for three hundred years.

***

Tiger had the lanterns. He'd brought a whole sack, because he always did. He looked at Kei. Kei looked at him.

"How many have you got?" said Kei.

Tiger looked in the sack.

"Twenty-three," said Tiger.

Nobody laughed. It was too much like a story for anybody to laugh at.

They did it slowly. One stone at a time, down the valley and up the far slope, fifty paces apart. Tiger took a lantern out of the sack at each stone and lit it, and set it in the hollow on the stone's top, where it fitted. Exactly. As if the hollows had been made for them. Then they walked on to the next. And behind them, as they went, a line of small lights came on across the heather in the bright afternoon. One every fifty paces, going north.

The light from the lanterns was hardly anything, in the daylight. You'd barely see it from the old hall. But at the third dusk it would be different. At the third dusk it would be a road.

Twenty stones. Twenty-one. The black was very close now, a few hundred paces off, standing up out of the heather like a cliff with no top. Kei could feel it on his face, a coldness that wasn't the wind.

Twenty-two.

The last stone stood about fifty paces from the edge of the dark. It was leaning a little, toward the black, as if it had been trying to see in. Its hollow was full of old soot. Curse had made it remember.

Tiger took the last lantern but one out of the sack. He looked at it in his hand. Then at the stone. Then at the black.

"You do it," said Tiger. And he held the lantern out. Not to Kei. To Thea, standing beside him at the front of the line, with her tower shield on her back.

Thea looked at it.

"Why me?"

"Dunno," said Tiger. "You're at the front."

Thea took the lantern. She lit it from Tiger's flint. Then she walked the last few paces to the last stone and set it in the hollow on top. It fitted. She stood there with her hand on the stone, at the very edge of the light, with the dark fifty paces in front of her.

And behind her, without anybody saying anything, Gigi came up and stood on her left. Not close. A pace off. And Xola came up and stood on her right, close enough to touch. And then the rest of them, one by one, in a line along the last stone. Ten people at the edge of the world, looking into the dark.

***

The light came about a minute later.

Kei saw it first. He'd always been the one who saw the small things, the dots on maps, the lines that weren't drawn yet. He was looking into the black, the flat endless dark. He was thinking about three hundred and forty-three little figures on a wall, and a page that wasn't blank, and twenty-two stones. And out in the black, a very long way in, so far he couldn't have said how far, there was a light.

One light. A single point. Small and steady and gold. Not moving.

Kei stopped breathing.

"Higu," he said.

"I see it," said Higu.

Everybody saw it. He felt them see it, one by one, the way you felt a window come up in a room. Hana's hand came out of her pocket with the star in it, and the star was shaking, because her hand was. Curse made a small sound. Rafi said *oh*, exactly the way he'd said it under the dome in March. Xola's gills went wide.

It didn't go out. That was the thing. Higu's light had gone into the black in April and something had closed round it. This one didn't go out. It just stood there, a long way in, small and gold and steady. As if it had been there all along and they'd only just set out enough light behind them to see it by.

[ … a light, north. ]

"That's a stone," said Higu. Her voice was very strange. "Isn't it. That's another stone. With a lamp on it. A long way in."

"Somebody lit it," said Hana.

"Or somebody kept it," said Gigi, very quietly, from Thea's left. "Lit. All this time. In there." She was looking at it with an expression Kei had never seen on her face. "*If we do not come back, keep them anyway.*"

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Someone's still carrying them," said Thea.

She said it so quietly that Kei almost didn't hear. She had her hand on the last stone and her eyes on the light. And beside her, a pace off, Gigi turned her head and looked at her. Just for a moment. And then away.

Kei looked at the light. Then at the black. Then at the line of twenty-two lanterns behind them, going back down the valley, one every fifty paces, toward the old hall. Toward the moor and the rings and the round town and the Hall of Roads. Toward a man standing at the bottom of the north steps, who'd said *look at it properly, for me*.

He'd looked.

He wanted to go in. He'd never wanted anything so much. He could feel the wanting in his feet, the way Drew said you could. He wanted to walk past the last stone into the black, toward a light nobody had seen for three hundred years.

"Not yet," said Kei.

And all ten of them laughed. All at once, at the edge of the world, softly, into the dark. Because it was his line now, and Ice's before that, and everybody's. Because there wasn't a better one.

"Not yet," said Higu, beside him, still laughing, with her eyes wet.

"Not yet," said Thea.

"Not yet," said Gigi.

They left the lanterns burning. All twenty-two. Set out behind them.

***

They walked back to the old hall in the third dusk. And Kei turned round at the broken wall and looked back. The line of lanterns ran away north across the dark valley and up the far slope, one every fifty paces, small and gold, all the way to the edge. And past the edge, a long way in, one more.

He got his map out, there at the broken wall, by the light of the nearest lantern. He unrolled it on the stone. Sixty dots, all solid. The gold dot at the top, off every road, that wasn't anybody yet. And the dotted line he'd drawn in April, running north from the old hall off the top of the map into nothing, with *Not yet* written beside it.

He took his pen. Very small, just past the edge, where the dotted line ran off into the black, he drew a single tiny gold mark.

Not a dot. Not anybody. Just a light.

And beside it, in letters so small that only he would ever be able to read them, he wrote:

*Somebody kept it.*

Then he rolled up the map and went to find the others. Thea and Xola were by the birch tree. Gigi and Higu were at the open north edge, where the wall had fallen. Hana was asleep against the curved counter with the star in her hand, and Curse sat beside her keeping watch with his inkwell. Rafi, Drew and Tiger were round a small fire. Ten people in a broken hall at the edge of the world. None of them the same as they'd been when they walked out of the north door that morning.

He sat down by the fire and messaged Ice.
