Hyvred saw Bee before Bee saw her, because Hyvred saw everything now.

She was on the west road into Brackenford, the last mile of eleven days of walking, with the sun going down behind her. The town was ahead: three rivers, a dozen bridges, the mill wheels turning, the black needle of the Tally in the middle of the market island catching the last of the light. And at the very edge of everything, right out at the side of her vision, where on Earth there had only ever been dark, something moved. A small figure on the parapet of a bridge at the edge of town, sitting with its legs dangling and a book on its knee. Behind it, a pair of wings buzzed now and then like an annoyed insect, for no reason, because they were for show.

Hyvred stopped in the road.

She didn't flinch any more, when things moved at the edges. That had taken her a week. The first days in Saltwhistle she'd flinched at everything: gulls, waves, a child running. Things kept coming into a part of the world that had never had anything in it. Now she just saw them. She saw the girl on the bridge, small and far off and perfectly clear, with her antennae up and her wings going, drawing something. And she knew who it was. She'd have known from a mile further. She'd have known from the other side of the world.

She started to run.

Bee heard her coming at about forty paces and looked up. She shut the book. She slid off the parapet onto the cobbles and stood there with her antennae straight up and her wings going like mad, and didn't move, because Bee never moved toward anything. She waited for it to arrive. Hyvred arrived, at a dead run, and nearly knocked her over the parapet into the river.

"Hyv," said Bee, into her shoulder.

"Bee."

"You're taller."

"I'm the same."

"You're taller in here." Bee pulled back and looked at her, holding her by both arms. At her face. At her eyes, which she'd been looking at on calls for years. At the glasses pushed up in her hair, gold-rimmed, the real ones. "You kept them."

"I kept them."

"You don't need them."

"I know." Hyvred touched them. "That's why."

Bee looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, as if that was the right answer, and opened the book, and turned it round. On the page, in charcoal, very carefully, was a door. A plain wooden door, in a plain stone wall, a little ajar.

"I said I'd draw you a door," said Bee. "When I got here. I drew it on Saturday. It's gone a bit faint." She looked at it critically. "It's not for anything. It's just a door."

Hyvred looked at the door for a long time, standing on a bridge at the edge of a town with the sun going down behind her. Then she laughed, and hugged her again, and Bee put up with it.

Brackenford was full of people, and none of them were on this bridge.

That was what Bee had noticed, waiting. She'd got in on Sunday night and spent the whole of Monday walking round, because she wanted to see it before the dark. Bee needed to see a place before she could draw it, and she needed to draw a place before she'd trust it. Six thousand travellers, the town said. And they'd all gone to the same three places.

"The big bridges," said Bee, pointing with her pencil. "There, there and there. The ones on the main road and the one to the island. The red ones are on two of them. The money people. There's about two hundred of them. They've got lanterns on poles every ten feet, and a man shouting numbers."

"Crimson Ledger."

"Them. And the island." The pencil went to the market island in the middle of the town, where the three rivers met, with the Tally on it. "That's the other lot. The black ones. The ones who came second at your swamp guy's church."

"VANTA."

"Them. There's about thirty. They're very quiet. They've got the whole island and they're very quiet about it." Bee turned the pencil round. "They're all there for the board. There's a board for tonight. Whoever puts out the most of the dark things gets their name on it. So everybody's gone where there'll be the most."

"And this one?"

They looked at it.

It was a small bridge. Old, humped, stone, over the narrowest of the three rivers, at the very edge of town. On the far side was an old mill with its wheel still turning in the race, and beside the mill a low house with one window lit. Three lanterns on the bridge, one at each end and one in the middle. That was all. A dyers' bridge, Bee said. The water underneath ran in slow streaks of colour from the dye-works upstream, blue and rust and yellow. Nobody was on it. Nobody had been on it all day.

"There's an old woman in the house," said Bee. "Her name's Wynn. She's been at the window all day watching me draw. She gave me a pear." She paused. "I don't think anyone's coming to this one."

Hyvred looked at the three lanterns. At the old woman's window. At the slow coloured water going under the bridge, and the dark coming down over the fields beyond.

"We'll keep this one," she said.

"Obviously," said Bee. "That's why I've been sitting on it all day."

The sun went down on Brackenford at twenty past eight, Singapore time. All over the town six thousand people stopped and read the same words.

[ The Long Night has begun.
Keep your lights. Keep your towns. ]

Hyvred had logged in at half past seven. She'd done the sum on the road, in the last hour, the way half the forum had been doing it since Wednesday. If the night went till the real dawn, and it was going to, the egg would throw her out half an hour early. Half an hour short, at half past seven tomorrow morning, in the dark, with Bee still on the bridge.

She'd done the sum, and logged in anyway. She'd said she'd meet Bee in Brackenford, and she'd meant before the dark.

*Half an hour*, she thought. *I'll sort it out when it's half an hour.*

Bee drew her a ladder.

It was Hyvred's idea, but Bee did it. Hyvred stood on the bridge in the last of the light and looked at the mill. It was tall, three storeys, with a flat lead roof and a stone parapet round it. From the top of it you'd see the whole of this edge of the town. The bridge and the race and the fields beyond, and the river going off to the next bridge, and the island, and everything.

"I need to be up there," she said.

"There's no stairs," said Bee. "I asked. The miller locked the top floor and went to the island with everyone else."

"Then I can't—"

Bee was already drawing.

She sat down on the parapet of the bridge with the book on her knee and drew. Hyvred had watched her draw ten thousand times, on calls, on stream, in the margins of things. She'd never seen her draw like this. Not fast. Bee was never fast. But completely still, except for the hand, with her antennae lowered and her face very close to the page. Long uprights. Rungs, one at a time, each one drawn and then gone over, *true*, Ambry had told her, *whole*. Twenty rungs. The pegs that held each rung. The grain of the wood.

At the bottom of the page, in very small letters, she wrote what it was for.

*To hold my friend up.*

Then she stood up, and walked to the foot of the mill wall, and tore the page out of the book, which Hyvred had never seen her do. She held it flat against the stone and said the word.

[ Sketch: Ladder

It was a ladder. It was there, leaning against the wall of the mill, solid, wooden, twenty rungs, going up into the dark to the roof.

Bee looked at it. She put her hand on a rung and shook it. It didn't move.

"Ambry said her grandmother made a ladder that held a man up the church," she said. "I've been practising."

Hyvred went up it.

***

From the roof of the mill she could see the whole of the night.

She didn't know how else to say it, then or later. She'd try, on stream, and fail, and in the end she'd just turn the camera slowly round, all the way, and let them see. But that wasn't it either. They saw what the camera saw: a slice of it, a window. She saw everything at once.

The whole of this edge of Brackenford, laid out under her in the dusk. The dyers' bridge with its three lanterns, and Bee standing on it with her book open, looking up. The mill race, black and fast, foaming at the wheel. The fields beyond, going grey and then blue and then dark. The river going off to the left toward the next bridge, and the next. And beyond them, the market island in the middle of the town, ringed with VANTA's lanterns, quiet. The two big bridges with the red sashes on them, ringed with CL's lanterns on poles. And the man shouting numbers.

All of it, edge to edge, from the far left of the world to the far right. No tube. No dark round the outside. No flashlight. Just everything, at once, the way other people had apparently been seeing it all their lives.

And at the edges of it, coming up out of the water, the dark.

[ The Unlit · Lv 14 ]

They came up out of the rivers. Of course they did. Brackenford was a town made of rivers. They came up under the bridges, out of the deep water where the three rivers met, out of the mill races and the weirs: long, thin, wet shapes, wading up out of the water toward the lights. Here they weren't like herons. They were lower. Longer. More like something swimming that had decided to stand up. They came up on the banks and out onto the bridges, bent over the lanterns, and the lanterns went out.

She saw all of them.

That was the thing. All of them, at once. Every one that came up out of the water anywhere along this edge of the town, she saw it, the second it broke the surface. Not because she was looking for them. Because she was looking at everything.

"*Bee*," she said, very calmly. "Two coming up under the bridge. Left side. Now. And one at the race, by the wheel. And one in the reeds on the far bank, behind the house."

Bee didn't ask how she knew. Bee never asked. Bee turned a page, and said a word, and a wall went up across the left side of the dyers' bridge, drawn and true and about four feet high. The first Unlit came up out of the coloured water and leaned over the parapet toward the middle lantern, and hit the wall, and stopped.

Hyvred drew.

She didn't hold it. Ness had taught her that, on the cliffs at Saltwhistle, a long time ago now. *Stop holding on.* She found the wind over the race, which was coming down the river off the fields, cold. She found the one by the wheel, a long dark shape leaning toward Wynn's lit window, and let the arrow go into the wind. It curved, the way the sea-hawk arrow had curved. Into the thing's long neck, just where it bent.

It came apart. Black water, pouring back into the race.

People came to the dyers' bridge after that.

Not many. A few. A man and a woman with spears, who'd been on CL's bridge and got tired of the man shouting numbers. A boy from Lagos with a crossbow who'd heard the girl on the mill roof calling things out before they happened. Three friends from Lyon with matching shields. A very old traveller, very slow, with a staff and a lantern of his own, who said he'd always liked small bridges. Nine of them by ten o'clock. Twelve by eleven.

Hyvred told them where things were coming from. She did it from the roof, calmly, all night, the way she'd once told Bee where the gulls were on the cliff. *Two under the left side. One at the race. One coming round behind the house, Wynn's back door. Three in the reeds, far bank, they're going for the far lantern.* And they went where she said, and the things were there.

And Bee drew.

Walls on the bridge, at the edges, where the Unlit came up over the parapets. Shields on the strangers, one each, drawn in thirty seconds on the parapet with her tongue between her teeth. A Sting, when one got past everything and onto the bridge. Six damage, which wasn't much. But it was enough to make the thing lean away from the light for a second, and in that second the boy from Lagos put a bolt through it. And every hour, without being asked, Bee went to the foot of the mill and drew a new ladder, and tore the page out, and put it against the wall over the old one. The old one was going faint by then, the rungs thinning like breath on a window.

*To hold my friend up.* Every hour. Every one the same.

The dyers' bridge wasn't on it. It never would be. Twelve people on a bridge at the edge of town, keeping three lanterns lit. But all three lanterns were still lit, and Wynn's window was still lit, and Hyvred could see all of them from the roof at once, and that was enough.

***

At a quarter past eleven, she saw something nobody else did.

She was looking at the island. She wasn't meaning to. She was looking at everything, and the island was part of everything. VANTA were holding it beautifully: thirty of them in black, quiet, a ring of lanterns round the market and the Tally. Every Unlit that came up out of the water on the near side of the island came apart before it reached a light. They didn't shout. They didn't call numbers. They were just very, very good.

But they were all facing in.

Toward the three rivers, toward the bridges, toward where the Unlit came up. And on the far side of the island, the side that faced away from the town, there was a long low wall of old stone where the island's piers went down into the deep water at the meeting of the rivers. It was dark there. There was no lantern. Nobody was looking at it.

And something was coming up the piers.

Three of them. Long and low and wet, climbing out of the deepest water in Brackenford, up the old stone, very slowly. Toward the back of the ring of VANTA's lanterns. Toward the Tally.

Nobody on the island had seen them. Nobody could. They were behind everyone.

Hyvred could see them. She could see them because she could see everything.

"Bee," she said. "Keep the bridge. I need a minute."

She drew.

It was a long way. Two hundred paces, three, across the mill race and the river and the end of the island, in the dark, with the wind coming down two rivers at once and crossing over the water in the middle. She'd have said no to it, a month ago. She'd have said *I can't see that far*. But she wasn't in a tube any more. She could see the whole of the air between her and the piers. She could see the wind in it, the way she'd seen it on the cliff the day she shot the sea-hawk: two streams of cold coming off two rivers, meeting in the middle, twisting.

She didn't hold it. She let it go into the wind.

It went out over the race, over the river, into the crossing winds over the meeting of the waters, and curved. Left, and then back right. It went into the first long dark shape on the pier just as its head came up over the top of the wall, behind VANTA's lanterns.

It came apart, and fell, and was black water.

She drew again. The second. Again. The third was nearly over the wall, its long neck bending toward the nearest lantern in VANTA's ring. She let go while it was still bending, and it came apart with its head a foot from the glass.

Then she lowered the bow and stood on the roof of the mill, breathing.

Across the water, on the island, a woman in black turned round.

She was the one at the front. The one who'd been quietly directing the others all night with small movements of her hand. She turned round, and looked at the back wall, and the three patches of wet on the stone where the three Unlit had been. Then she looked up and across the water, over the river and the race, at the roof of an old mill at the edge of town. At a girl standing on it with a bow and horns and a red ribbon, and glasses pushed up in her hair.

She looked at her for a long time. Then she lifted one hand, very slightly. Not a wave. Just an acknowledgement. *I see you.*

Then she put her hand over her mouth, on the roof of a mill in a town made of rivers in the middle of the longest night of the year, and cried. Not for long. Not much. Just enough. Down on the bridge, Bee looked up from her book, and saw, and didn't say anything, and drew a new ladder.

***

At half past eleven, she saw the sky begin to grey.

She saw it first, before anyone in Brackenford, because she saw all of the sky at once. Not a slice of it, a window. All of it, edge to edge, the whole round bowl of the dark over the three rivers. And along the bottom of it, in the east, behind the island and the Tally and the big bridges with the red sashes on them, a line of dark blue came up. Lighter than the black. The fen's second dawn, the town's, everyone's. On time.

All over Brackenford, the Unlit stopped.

She saw that too. All of them, everywhere, in every river, at every bridge, at once. Every long low wet shape in the town lifted its head out of the water at the same moment and turned it east. Toward the line of blue. Hundreds of them. She saw every single one.

"Bee," she said.

Bee was at the foot of the mill with a new ladder. She looked up.

"They've stopped," said Hyvred. "All of them. They're all looking at it."

"Is that good?"

Hyvred didn't answer. She watched the blue go grey. Watched it go lighter, along the whole edge of the sky, with something at the very bottom that might have been gold. Watched the whole town hold its breath, CL's man stop shouting numbers, VANTA turn on the island to look.

At midnight, she watched it go out.

All of it, all at once, along the whole edge of the sky from the far left to the far right. She saw every bit of it go, at the same moment, everywhere. Like a hand closing. The grey went black, and the black went deeper, and there were no stars. And every one of the hundreds of long wet heads in every river in Brackenford went down at once, together, back toward the lights.

[ The dark has not lifted. ]

She stood on the roof with the bow in her hand and looked at all of it. The whole of the night, edge to edge, and nothing in it but dark, and lanterns, and things coming up out of the water.

"Hyv?" said Bee, from the bottom of the ladder.

"It's not coming," said Hyvred.

Bee looked up at her for a moment. Then she looked at the book in her hands, and at the ladder against the wall, its rungs going faint. Then she sat down on the bottom rung and turned to a clean page.

"Then I'll draw another ladder," she said.
