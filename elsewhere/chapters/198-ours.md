Bee painted the second banner on the long table in the banner room, and Curse watched her do it for three days, and on the third day she asked him.

"Will you write on it?"

Curse looked up from his inkwell.

"Write what?"

"What it is," said Bee. She had paint on her hands and her wings and, somehow, the back of her neck. She'd been at it since Monday. "That's what you do. You write what a thing is, and it remembers." She looked down at the banner, spread the length of the table, the paint still wet in places. "I want this one to remember."

He put down his pen.

***

It was a copy. That was the problem, and everybody knew it, and nobody had said it out loud until Kei did on Sunday.

The real banner was on its two hooks under Ama Corrie's stair. Bee had painted it a year ago, with sixty dots on it now and silver lines between them. And along its top edge, a faint old gold star that nobody had painted. *It answered*, Ama Corrie had said, when the star came. When Bee hung it on the hooks, the iron hatch had read it and sunk into a stair, and the Seven Below had been theirs.

It couldn't leave. It was the Seven Below's. If it came off the hooks, the hatch wouldn't know them.

But Masmark had Halls. Hundreds of them, Ice said in his letters. Halls for ten and twenty and forty and more, in every street round the great lake, and every one of them answered only to a banner. If the ten were going to walk into Masmark and stand in a Hall and say *we're Constellation*, they'd need something to hang on the hooks.

So Bee was painting a second one.

"It won't be the same," said Kei, at the long table on Sunday. Not unkindly. Kei said true things gently. "It won't have the star. The star came to the first one. Nobody knows why."

"I know," said Bee. "I'm painting it anyway."

She'd painted it exactly. Curse had watched her. Every dot, every line, the same as the first, in the same order, with the same brush. Sixty gold dots on deep blue, and the silver lines between them, the shape of who'd stood next to who at the gate. And one more dot, very small, in the corner. Not gold. Not silver. Plain white.

"Who's that?" said Curse, on Tuesday, pointing.

"Chernn," said Bee. "She's not on the Roll yet. So she's not gold." She dabbed at it. "But she's here. So she's a dot."

Along the top edge, where the old star was on the first one, Bee had painted nothing. She'd left it blank, on purpose. You couldn't paint a star that had come by itself.

On Wednesday she finished. She stood back from the long table with her brush and looked at it for a long time, the way she looked at everything she drew.

It was beautiful. It was exactly the same as the first. Curse knew, the way you know a thing about ink, that it wasn't.

That was when she asked him.

***

He didn't do it straight away.

He sat at the long table with his inkwell and his fountain pen. He looked at the banner for most of the afternoon. People came and went round him. Gabe, back from a hall, enormous and happy. Leaka, who sat down beside him and said nothing for nearly twenty minutes, which was a record. Isa, in her chair by the north door, watching from across the room with her bright black eyes.

*Write what it is*, Bee had said.

He knew how labels worked. *Most things forget what they are. A label reminds them.* He'd said that to Ama Corrie once, over the old Roll. You wrote the true word on a thing, in ink, in pen, and as long as the ink lasted, the thing remembered. He'd written *LAMP* on a soot-filled hollow on the moor in September, and the stone had remembered it was a lamp. He'd brought back a line on the old Roll's blank page for a breath that the water had taken. He'd written *HOME* on a tag tied to the arm of his own chair, and *KEPT* on the handle of Ama Corrie's broom.

You couldn't write a lie. It didn't take. You had to write what it was.

So what was it?

*Banner*, he thought. True. Useless. *Constellation's banner.* Also true. But the first one was Constellation's banner too, and this one wasn't the first. Writing it would be like writing *the same* on a copy. The ink wouldn't believe it.

*A copy of Constellation's banner.* True. Worse than useless. You couldn't hang that on a hook in Masmark and expect a Hall to answer.

He sat there until the second dusk.

"You're doing the face," said Leaka, beside him, at last.

"What face?"

"The *thinking* face. The slow one. You did it for an hour before you wrote *LAMP*." She put her chin on her fist. "What's wrong with it?"

"Nothing's wrong with it," said Curse. "It's perfect. That's the problem. It's a perfect copy." He looked at the banner. "I don't know what it *is*."

Leaka looked at it too, for a long time. Leaka never looked at anything for a long time. She looked at this.

"It's ours," she said.

Curse turned his head.

"It's *ours*," said Leaka. "It's not the first one. Who cares? The first one's the Seven Below's. It's on the hooks. It's the *Hall's*." She waved at the banner on the table. "This one's ours. It's the one we take with us. It's the one that goes where *we* go." She shrugged. "That's what it is."

Curse looked at her for a long moment.

Then he unscrewed the cap of the fountain pen.

***

He wrote it small. On the bottom edge of the banner, at the left, where nobody would see it unless they looked. In his careful hand, in blue ink that Bee said afterward matched the silver lines exactly, though he hadn't meant it to.

*Constellation (ours).*

The ink went into the cloth the way ink went into paper, slowly, and dried. And for a moment nothing happened. He sat there with the pen in his hand and felt the whole banner room go quiet round him, without looking up.

Then the window came.

And along the top edge of the banner, where Bee had painted nothing, very faint, gold, old, a star.

***

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Bee had both hands over her mouth. Kei had stood up at the head of the table and was staring. Leaka, beside Curse, had grabbed his sleeve and was holding it so tightly he could feel her nails through the cloth.

And across the room, by the north door, in her chair, Isa Corrie had leaned forward.

She looked at the star for a long time with her bright black eyes. Then she sat back.

"It answered," she said.

Ama Corrie, in the doorway with her broom, looked at her.

"That's what it does," said Isa. "The star. My mother said. It doesn't come for the cloth. It doesn't come for the paint. It comes for who's carrying it." She looked at Bee. "You can paint a banner a hundred times. It answers when it's *yours*."

Bee took her hands away from her mouth.

"It came for the *label*," she said.

"It came for what the label said," said Isa. "The boy wrote the truth. *Ours.*" She nodded at Curse. "So it believed him."

Curse looked down at the five words in blue ink on the bottom edge of the banner. His own handwriting, small and careful. And he felt something he didn't have a word for. Which was strange, for a man whose whole skill was words.

"Hm," said Ama Corrie, from the doorway. And went back to sweeping.

***

Hyvred hit fifty on Thursday.

Curse wasn't there. He was in the round town, at the long table, labelling things for the road. Water skins, packs, a map case for Kei, a little tin of Tif's tea that she said was for *emergencies only*. But everybody heard it. It came over Kei's call at a quarter past two in the afternoon, from the high fells. From Niko's four and Bee, who'd taken her up there every day for three weeks.

"*FIFTY*."

It was Hyvred's voice. Flat and carrying, the calling voice, the one that came down from the eye of the dome. Except it wasn't flat. It cracked straight down the middle.

"*FIFTY. I'M FIFTY. I SAID FEBRUARY. I SAID IT. FEBRUARY THE SEVENTEENTH. I SAID FEBRUARY.*"

And under it, Niko, very calm: "She said February."

And Winter: "Fine."

And Chris: "I've logged it."

And Newish, laughing so hard she couldn't talk, and then the drum, *Tempo*, going faster and faster.

The whole banner room laughed. Curse laughed. Leaka screamed *HYV* at the ceiling, though Hyvred was four hours away on a fell and couldn't hear her.

And in the corner of everybody's window, because she'd let it show, a letter. Hyvred had sent it before she'd even come down off the fell.

And four seconds later, because he must have been looking, from a city on a great lake a long way south and east:

Curse didn't know why that made him want to cry. He was a calm man. He was slow and relaxed and nothing much bothered him. He sat at the long table with a tin of Tif's tea in one hand and his fountain pen in the other. He looked at those four words in the corner of his window for a long time.

*You said february.*

Not *congratulations*. Not *well done*. Just: *you said it, and it was true.* The way you'd write a label.

***

Isa called him over that night. Thursday, late, after the banner room had gone quiet and most of the sixty were asleep in the real world. She was awake in her chair by the north door with the grey blanket round her shoulders, the way she usually was at that hour. Above her, on its three-hundred-year-old hook, the empty black cage with the star on top.

She didn't say his name. She didn't know it, probably. She just lifted one root hand off the blanket and crooked a finger at him, the way you'd call a child across a room. And he went.

"You write on things," said Isa.

"Yes."

"And they remember."

"As long as the ink does," said Curse. "If it's true."

She looked up at the cage over her head. Empty. The little door shut. No flame in it any more; the flame was in a clay jar in Suzu's pack now, where it could be home for a bit.

"My mother carried that," said Isa. "And hers. All the way back to the first one, who carried it out of this room." She was quiet. "Nobody'll ever carry it again. It's done. It came home." She looked at him. "Ama Corrie hung it there so they'd know. Whoever comes in that way. But they won't know, will they. Not unless somebody tells them. A lamp on a hook's just a lamp on a hook."

Curse looked at the cage for a long time.

"What would you want it to say?" he said.

Isa thought about it. She thought about it for so long that he thought she might have gone to sleep, the way she did, between one word and the next. Then she said it, quite clearly, in her dry thin voice like a page turning.

"That it was carried," she said. "And that it was carried *home*. Both. People forget the second bit."

He got Ama Corrie's stool from the kitchen. He stood on it, carefully, under the hook, with his inkwell in one hand. On the iron of the cage's base, on the little rim under the oil dish, where nobody would see it unless they looked up, he wrote. Small. In blue.

*Carried. And carried home.*

He got down off the stool. Isa was looking up at it with her bright black eyes.

"Hm," she said. It sounded exactly like Ama Corrie. Then, after a moment, softer: "My mother would have liked that."

"What was her name?"

"It's in the book," said Isa. "Second from the bottom." She closed her eyes. "Go to bed, boy with the ink. Your friends are leaving in the morning."

He went. But he looked back from the banner room door, once. She was asleep already, with her hands folded on the blanket, under a lamp that knew what it was.

***

They left on Friday, at the first dawn. Ten of them.

Kei, with his map case. Higu with her lamp. Gigi with her staff and her thorn. Drew, watching everybody's feet. Thea with her tower shield, and Xola beside her. Salty with Right Size on his back, the length of a man, black. Bee with the second banner rolled and strapped across her back over her wings, so its end stuck up behind her head like a flag. And Hyvred.

And Wendy.

Curse hadn't thought about that until Thursday night, when Kei read out the ten at the long table. *Wendy.* And the banner room had gone quiet, and everybody had looked at the same thing at the same time. At the south door. At the gate, where Wendy had stood every day since the spring a year ago and asked everybody who came in the same question.

"Who keeps the gate?" Tiger had said, at last.

Wendy had looked down the long table. Not at Kei. At the far end, by the window, where Kite was sitting.

Kite. Who'd come up a road to Drew on the longest night a year ago, ex-Nightjar, and stood beside Wendy at the gate every day since. Who'd never once said much. Who had a bow, and good eyes, and when he'd been asked who'd stand next to him had said *her*, meaning Wendy. Meaning the gate.

"Him," said Wendy.

Kite had gone very still.

And on Friday, at the first dawn, at the bottom of the south steps, Wendy stood in the gate for the last time for a while. She took her hands out of her pockets. She looked at Kite, standing beside her, where he always stood.

"You ask everybody," she said. "Every time. Doesn't matter who."

"I know," said Kite.

"Even if you know them. Even if they live here. Even if it's Ice."

"I know."

"And if they say *nobody*," said Wendy, "they don't get in. And then you sit on the steps with them after. And talk." She looked at him. "That's the bit people forget."

Kite looked at her for a long time.

"I know," he said. "I watched you."

Wendy nodded, once. Then she stepped out of the gate, onto the road, beside Salty. And Kite stepped into it.

He stood there. Exactly where she'd stood. Hands at his sides; he didn't put them in his pockets. He looked at the ten, on the road, with their packs on, about to go. He took a breath.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Kite.

And Wendy, on the road, with her bow on her back and Salty beside her, started to cry. She didn't stop for about a mile.

***

They went one at a time. Out of the gate, past Kite, down the south road. Each of them said a name. Kei said *Higu*. Higu said *Kei*. Gigi said *Gu, and whoever calls the Turn*, and Thea, behind her, said *Xola, and the thorn on my left*, and Gigi didn't look round, and her hands went still on her staff. Drew said *the one who stays*, which made Tiger, on the steps, go red. Salty said *her*. Wendy said *him*, still crying. Xola said *Thea*. Bee said *Hyv*.

Hyvred came last.

She stopped in the gate in front of Kite with her bow on her back and an empty place in her quiver. There'd been an arrow there with two grey feathers and one white, and now there wasn't. She looked at Kite.

"Who'd stand next to you?" said Kite.

Hyvred looked south. Down the road. South and east, fourteen days of it, past the White Hills and the long rise and a ferry and a goose, to a city on a lake she'd never seen.

"He's already there," she said. "Waiting."

Kite looked at her.

"That's not a name," he said.

Hyvred laughed. Out loud, in the gate, at the first dawn. Curse, on the steps with Leaka, saw her tail go up behind her, all the way.

"*Ice*," said Hyvred. "The swamp guy. *Him*."

"Fair," said Kite.

And she went.

***

Curse stood on the south steps with Leaka and watched them go. Ten small figures on the south road, getting smaller. Bee at the back with the banner strapped across her shoulders, its end sticking up behind her head. Even from here, in the first light, he could see the faint gold of the star along its top edge.

*Constellation (ours).*

"You did that," said Leaka, beside him. Very quietly, for Leaka.

"You said it," said Curse. "I just wrote it down."

"That's what you always say."

"It's what I always do," said Curse.

Leaka didn't say anything for a while. She stood beside him on the steps, close, her shoulder against his arm. She was forty-eight. He was forty-nine and a bit. Neither of them was going to be in Masmark for a month.

"We'll go," said Leaka. "Next. Won't we. With the next lot."

"With the next lot."

"*Together*."

"Together," said Curse.

He took out his fountain pen. He didn't know why, at first. He looked at it in his hand on the south steps, in the first light, with the ten getting smaller on the road. Then he took Leaka's hand, the one against his arm, and turned it over. On the inside of her wrist, very small, where nobody would see it unless they looked, he wrote one word.

*Next.*

Leaka looked at her wrist for a long time.

She didn't scream. She didn't say anything at all. She just put her wrist against her chest, under her other hand, and held it there. And leaned her head on Curse's shoulder. And they stood on the south steps and watched the road until the ten were gone round the curve of it, and there was only the road.

At the gate, Kite stood with his hands at his sides.

"Who'd stand next to you?" he said, to the next person coming up the steps. A stranger, with a pack. And waited for the answer.
