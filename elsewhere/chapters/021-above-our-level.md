He was eighth on the Tally that morning. He'd been seventh on Sunday.

He tried not to look. He'd told himself he wouldn't. On Sunday he'd stood at the foot of the needle with Deston shaking him by the neck, and it had felt like the best thing that had ever happened to him in a game, and he'd decided then and there that he wasn't going to be the kind of player who checked it every hour.

He checked it every hour.

The thing about the Tally was that it moved. Everything on it moved. Every person on it was out in the world right now, as he was, fighting something, and getting a little bit further along their bar. It didn't wait for you. They fought all afternoon, the three of them, him and Xav and Kotori. Kotori on her post or her stone or her bit of wall, wherever the place was that nothing would go. Him in the middle of the bridge with the shield. Xav round the back.

It was good. It was very good. With Kotori behind them, the fights at the weir had gone from careful to something else. Ice could feel it. He could take a hit he'd have turned a week ago and not worry about it, because before he'd even finished feeling it, a bell would ring off to the side and the warmth would come into wherever it had landed. He could pull five instead of four. He did pull five, twice. They killed them all, and the *Outnumbered* line came up every time.

It was faster than he'd ever levelled. It still wasn't fast enough.

"Sorry," said Kotori, at one point, from a rock in the middle of the river that she'd somehow got onto without getting her feet wet. "Sorry, Ice, can I ask something?"

"Sure."

"Why are you looking at the town?"

He blinked. He'd been standing on the bridge between fights with his shield down, looking back up the river at the roofs of Brackenford, and the tip of the black needle sticking up out of the middle of them.

"I wasn't," he said.

"Oh," said Kotori. "Okay. Sorry." And a second later, very quietly, mostly to herself: "You were, though."

Deston came in at nine that night with snacks. Real snacks. He'd bought them on the way home from the exam hall, and he described every single one to Ice in party chat while eating them in his real room in Singapore, which was a crime against everybody who could read it.

He played until the window threw him out. He'd never played without a timetable before, he said. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He sang the whole time, both lines, *Let the MANY move as ONE*, and *What one BEARS, two can CARRY*, louder and louder, until Kotori started humming along on her rock in the river. She was always a beat behind, and it somehow made it better.

They got Deston to level eleven at about half past ten, and the whole party cheered, and Deston said "*Eleven*," with great tenderness. Then he went to look at the stone, came back, and said he was fourteenth.

Ice was eleventh.

***

He didn't sleep well.

He lay in bed in the house in Malacca with the fan going round and Suzu asleep down the hall, and thought about the needle. It wasn't really the needle. He knew that. It was what the needle stood for.

It stood for the stream. A hundred and sixty people came because he was the POV guy, the back-foot guy, the one on the stone. If he wasn't on the stone, he was just a man in a swamp again.

It stood for the workshop. In two weeks Vivian would add up the first month, and he'd promised her it was the next big thing. A man who was eleventh and falling didn't feel like the next big thing.

And it stood for the door. It stood for the door more than anything.

Kestrel was level thirteen on Sunday. On Tuesday night she was fourteen. Fourteen, and the door said *none below fifteen*. Somewhere out there was a woman with a team of pros and ten thousand viewers, a day away from fifteen. He didn't know where she was, or whether she'd ever heard of a fen called the Wending, or a ring of stones with a heron that pointed the wrong way. He was almost sure nobody knew where the door was.

Almost.

He lay there doing sums until two in the morning. All of them came out the same way: *faster*.

***

On Wednesday morning he was twelfth.

He looked at it once, on the way through the square at dawn. He didn't let Xav see. Twelfth, and level twelve, with about sixty per cent of the bar. If he could get to thirteen by tonight, he'd be back in the top ten by tomorrow. And if he could get to fifteen by—

"Where are we going?" said Kotori, beside him.

"The ford," said Ice.

They'd heard about the ford on Monday from the clerk with the small spectacles. Downstream of the weir, past the last of the mills, there was an older crossing than the bridges: a line of great flat stones laid across the river where it ran wide and shallow, so you could walk from one bank to the other with your boots barely wet. Nobody used it. It was faster than the bridges. Everyone in Brackenford took the bridges anyway, and when you asked them why, they said *the Warden*.

They'd gone to look on Monday afternoon, the four of them. A great grey shape, sitting in the deep water just below the stepping stones, three times the size of a man, with river stones grown into its hide like barnacles. It had two little black eyes, and it didn't move at all while they watched. Ice had looked at the *Lv 15* and the *Elite* and the stepping stones, one person wide, every one of them a narrow place. He'd felt the old itch.

Xav had said: "Not yet."

Now it was Wednesday, and they went down to the ford in the morning light, and it sat in the deep water and looked at them with its little black eyes.

"Not with four," said Xav, before Ice had opened his mouth. "Not without Suzu. Not at night."

"It will be, by the time we've got it to half." Xav looked at the thing. "Listen. Things here get stronger at night. The barrow lights on Suzu's hill only come out after dark. He nodded at a big flat rock in the middle of the river below the ford, where the water split round it. "That's her rock. Look at it."

Kotori was already looking at it. "That's a good rock," she said seriously.

"Fine," said Ice.

He meant it, when he said it.

They fought things their size all day, along the river below the ford, mudwolves and a thing like a heron made of sticks that Kotori was very sad about killing. They did well. Deston, with no exam, sang like a man let out of prison. Xav was always one second behind something's back.

Ice got to level twelve and eighty per cent. Then eighty-five. Then the light started to go gold on the river, and Kotori said, apologetically, that she had to go. Her mum was making curry, and her dad had already rung twice, and it was the first proper family dinner since her exams.

"Go," said Ice. "Go, go. Eat curry."

"Sorry," said Kotori. "Sorry. Thank you! Tomorrow!" The bell rang once, and she went still on her rock, the way people did when they logged out where they stood.

Deston went at seven. His friends from his course were taking him out to celebrate, and he'd promised, and he was very sorry, and he was going to eat so much. Ice stood on the bank by himself, in the last of the light, and looked at the ford.

He told himself he was only looking.

The Warden sat in the deep water below the stones, very still, a grey hump with the river breaking white round it. It was so close to dark that the water had gone the colour of old pewter. He could see the line of stepping stones going across, wide and flat and wet, each one a narrow place.

He did the sums.

He was level twelve, eighty-five per cent. The Warden was fifteen. Three levels above him: the above-level bonus would be enormous, nearly double. One kill would be nearly a whole level. One kill would put him back in the top ten tonight. One kill, and tomorrow he'd be thirteen, and he'd be two levels from the door instead of three.

He had Anchor. He had the narrow stones. He had six Good draughts in his pack. He'd fought a bog lurker three levels up at level two, alone, on day one. He'd fought it in waist-deep water in the dark and won, with nine per cent left, because it turned left slowly and he'd seen it.

*Plan seventy per cent*, Suzu had said on stream, ten days ago. *Then trust.*

He planned.

It sat in the water at the downstream side of the third stone. If he stood on the third stone, it could only come at him from one side. It was too big to get round. He'd turn the first strike, and Anchor the second, and cut when it was off-line, like the lurker. He'd watch for the thing it did slowly. Everything had a thing it did slowly. Draughts at sixty per cent, not forty; he'd learned that. And if it went wrong, he'd step back off the third stone onto the second, and the second onto the first, and up the bank, and it wouldn't follow him onto land. Nothing in the river ever had.

Seventy per cent.

He looked at the chat panel at the edge of his eye.

It didn't move until he was on the third.

He stood there with his feet set and his shield sloped and his sword back by his ear, like a cup he didn't want to spill. The river ran fast and loud round the stone, ankle-deep, cold through his boots. The last of the gold went off the water all at once, as if somebody had turned it off. Then it was dusk, and then, quickly, as it always was here, it was dark.

Two little black eyes opened in the water at his feet.

He'd expected it to come up the way the lurker had, a head and a mouth. It didn't. It *stood up*. All of it, all at once, out of the deep water below the stone, streaming. It was taller than him by a body and a half, grey and huge, river stones grown all over its shoulders and arms like armour. It was so close he could smell it, cold and green and old. Then it brought one arm down on him from above like a falling tree.

He got the slope on it. It went past, mostly, down into the river with a crash that soaked him to the waist. Some of it didn't go past. Some of it came through the shield into his arm, and at fifty per cent that was a white, bright, ringing pain from his wrist to his shoulder. His health went down a fifth in one go.

He didn't make a sound.

It was the longest fight of his life, longer than the lurker, and he lost it.

He did everything right for a long time. He turned the strikes he could turn and Anchored the ones he couldn't. Each Anchor sent the great stone arm back the way it came with a sound like a door slammed in a storm, and Hollis would have laughed. He cut when it was off-line. He found the thing it did slowly. It always brought its right arm down and then its left. When the left came, it was a half-second late, because the stones on its shoulder were heavier on that side. He cut into the half-second, again and again. He breathed out on every cut, the way Suzu had told him. It was the best he'd ever fought. He knew it while he was doing it.

He drank at sixty per cent, not forty, the way he'd learned. And again. And again.

And then, about halfway through its bar, with four draughts gone, something happened that he hadn't planned for, because he hadn't planned for the night.

A second pair of little black eyes opened in the water on the *other* side of the stone.

He'd been so sure. Nothing could get round. It was too big. He'd been right about that. This wasn't round. This was another one, smaller, younger, that had been sitting in the deep water upstream of the stones the whole time, under the night. It stood up behind him.

Two of them. One on each side. On a stone one person wide, in the dark, in fast water. No Xav round the back. No song. No bell from a rock in the river.

He had about a second to think it. He thought: *step back to the second stone*.

He stepped back. The second stone wasn't there.

It was. But the river was faster at night, and higher, and the second stone had water over it now, black and fast, and his boot came down on the slick wet top of it at the wrong angle. His back foot, the one he'd taught a hundred and sixty people to start inside the cut, went out from under him.

He went down on one knee on the stone. And from both sides, at once, the two of them came down on him.

He got the shield up. He Anchored. It held the first one. The second came down on his back.

He felt it everywhere. Fifty per cent, and it was everywhere, and there was nothing to do with it.

He drank his fifth draught, on his knees, under the arms. He was still doing the sums. Even then, part of him was still doing them. Fifth draught at thirty per cent. Not enough. Sixth at twenty. Not enough. He set his feet on the stone and came up off his knee with the Falling Cut. It was a good one, he'd think later, maybe the best he'd ever done, and it opened the big one's shoulder in a long bright line.

Then the small one took him off the stone.

He went into the river backwards, into the black, the cold closing over his face. He didn't know which way was up. In the egg in the back room in Melaka Raya, his real body did the thing it had done once before, in the fen: a jolt of pure animal panic, like falling in a dream.

And then the red bar ran out.

It didn't hurt, at the end. That was the strange thing. There was the cold, and the dark, and the noise of the river. And then there was none of it, and no body at all. Only a stillness so complete it wasn't even dark, the way it had been on the very first morning, a second before the rain.

Then words, in the stillness.

He opened his eyes in the egg.

The lid was already lifting on its long arm, slowly, the way it did. The back room was dim and blue, the ceiling fan turning, the high window black with night. The kettle stood on the folding table, and his phone, and Suzu's pillow, which Suzu had started bringing to the office for the chair.

He lay there. He didn't move.

His body was heavy, the way it always was, coming back. But it was heavier than usual. His heart was going hard in his chest, still in the river. His right arm ached from wrist to shoulder with an ache that wasn't real and wasn't not. His face was wet. He didn't know when that had happened.

Twenty-four hours.

He'd known. He'd known since the first day, when he'd stood on the south walk and watched a man called Vinnie_K blow away across the water like dust. He'd felt clever and sorry for him at the same time, and he'd said *nobody's dying today*. He'd known what it cost. He'd known it was a day, and a level, and that the names behind him would pass him on the stairs while he lay in a room in Malacca looking at a ceiling fan.

He'd still gone.

Beside him, in the other pod, the hum changed. It went down and down, and stopped. The lid of Suzu's egg began to lift.

Ice didn't turn his head. He couldn't, yet.

He heard Suzu sit up in the gel, slowly. He heard him not say anything for a long time. Then he heard Suzu climb out, one leg and then the other, careful on legs that had forgotten, the way they all did. He heard his footsteps come round the end of the pod, two of them, three. They stopped beside him.

"I saw," said Suzu.
