Hana's egg had come in February, and she hadn't got into it for six weeks, and she'd had to explain why to about four thousand people.

*Waiting for Milli*, she'd said, on her socials, every time somebody asked. And then, when they kept asking: *waiting for Milli's to come. It's coming. Shipping to the Netherlands is slow. We're going in together. That's the whole point.*

Her egg had sat in the corner of her room in Singapore with a sheet over it, like a piece of furniture in a house where somebody had died. Jared's had come the week after hers, in America, and he'd sent her a photograph of it in his living room with a hat on it. Milli's had come on the eighteenth of March. Three days late, two weeks after the date on the order, eleven weeks after the bookmark. He'd sent her a video of himself sitting on the box in his flat in the Netherlands with his arms folded, saying nothing, just looking at the camera. Then he'd said, in his flat, careful English, "*Now.*"

So: Saturday. The twenty-seventh of March. Eight in the evening in Singapore, one in the afternoon in the Netherlands, eight in the morning for Jared, who'd said he'd be awake for this if he had to staple his eyes open.

She'd planned it for a month. In her small square notebook, in blue ink, with the fountain pen Curse had given her at Lau Pa Sat because he said pencil was a crime.

*1. Stream on at 7:45. Say hi. Don't cry.*
*2. Lie down at 7:58.*
*3. Go in at 8:00. All three. Same minute.*
*4. CALL THEM.*
*5. Don't cry.*

She'd underlined *call them* three times.

She came back to her stream at a quarter to eight, after twenty-two weeks.

She'd been dreading it. You couldn't go away for twenty-two weeks and come back and expect anyone to be there. That was what everybody said. Your chat moved on. Your numbers went. You came back to forty people and an empty room.

She turned the camera on in her model, the one she'd streamed in three times a week for three years, sitting at her desk in front of the egg. She looked at the number.

She came out into a town cut into a cliff.

The whole town. It had been a quarry once, a long time ago. You could see it in the walls: the tool marks, the straight-cut faces of the stone, the ledges where they'd taken out blocks the size of houses. And then somebody had built a town in the hole they'd left. Houses carved straight into the quarry walls, one above the other, with wooden stairs between them and ropes and pulleys and baskets going up and down. At the bottom, a harbour, and the sea, grey-green. At the top, the sky.

And everywhere, in the walls, in the stone, half-buried, things. Old columns. Carved faces. The corner of a doorway, with letters on it in no language she knew. As if the quarry had cut down through something much older, and the people who'd built the town had just left it there, sticking out of the walls, and built round it.

She stood in the doorway of her little wooden room, three ledges up the quarry wall, with the pick in her hand, and looked at a stone face sticking out of the rock beside her door. Older than anything. Half a smile.

"Oh," she said, out loud, on stream, to three thousand people. "Oh, *Milli*."

And a window came up in front of her.

[ You are among the first travellers to wake in Quarrymouth today.
You may Call up to four friends to wake here with you.
The Call lasts two days. ]

***

She'd written it down. She'd underlined it three times. And when it came, she couldn't move her hand.

She stood in the doorway and looked at the window and thought about December. The balcony at two in the morning, and the kaya biscuits, and Milli doing the sum on his phone: *two months*. The Botanic Gardens, and the bookshop, and a car-park roof in Toa Payoh at pink sky. And on the last night, *talk about one day*. Then Changi again, the other way, in late January. She'd stood at the barrier and watched him go through the gate, and he'd turned round at the last moment and put his fist in the air, the way she'd put hers in the air on his back when he arrived.

Eight weeks since then. Calls every night. Seven hours apart. Him asleep when she woke, her asleep when he woke, and the hour in the middle where they both were.

*An egg is every night*, he'd said at Lau Pa Sat. *Same place. Same town. Same room.*

She put her hand on the window.

*Millianna.*

*YoJareddo.*

[ Calls sent. ]

She waited.

It was the longest minute of her life. She stood in the doorway on the third ledge of a quarry town with three thousand people watching. And on the call in her ear, Jared was saying *yo, I got something, I got a window, Alice, is this you, is this—*

[ Millianna has answered your Call. ]

[ YoJareddo has answered your Call. ]

Two doors opened on the third ledge.

One beside hers, to the left. One beyond it. Two little wooden rooms carved into the quarry wall, side by side, as if they'd always been there, waiting.

Jared came out of the far one first, because he would, in plain travelling clothes, with a woodcutter's axe on his back and his hands in the air like he'd scored something. He looked at the quarry and the harbour and the sea and the stone faces in the walls and said, very loudly, "*Y'ALL*."

And Milli came out of the near one.

He looked like Milli. Exactly like the face on the other end of the call every night for eight weeks, and on the balcony in December, and at the gate at Changi with his fist in the air. He had a skinning knife at his belt and a coil of rope on his shoulder, and he was looking at her.

Same town. Same ledge. Same doorway, practically. Two feet apart.

He didn't say anything. Neither did she.

Then she did the thing she'd done at the barrier at Changi in November, which wasn't in the notebook. She ran the two feet, which wasn't far enough to be running. She jumped onto his back with her fist in the air.

He staggered, and caught her, and laughed. A real laugh, surprised out of him, in a quarry town on a cliff above the sea. And on the call in her ear, from the other ledge, Jared said, "*Oh my God*, Alice, you did the photo, you did the *photo*—"

"He raided me," she said. "He raided me with *twenty-five thousand people*. From a *swamp*."

She looked at the number for a long time. Thirty-two thousand people, watching her sit on the edge of a quarry in a model she'd drawn and redrawn, with her two best friends, at sunset. She thought about forty people. She thought about Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays for three years. About the word *Partner*, which she'd written in the notebook a long time ago, in pencil, and rubbed out, because it felt stupid. And written again. And rubbed out.

"Say something to them," said Milli quietly.

"Hi," she said. "I'm Hana. This is Milli, and that's Jared. We're in a quarry." She laughed, wetly. "We waited for each other. That's all. That's the whole stream."
