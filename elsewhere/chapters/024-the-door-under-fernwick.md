Fernwick was smaller than he remembered.

It had only been eleven days. He came down off the end of the causeway at mid-morning with the other four behind him, and the reeds closed round them, and there were the boardwalks and the round wattle houses on their stilts and the smoke lying flat. It was exactly the same. And it was so small. He'd thought of it for two weeks as the place where everything was. Now he walked onto the square wooden Landing, where he'd come out of the door on the first morning to sixty-four people and the smell of rain, and he could see from one end of it to the other.

"Oh," said Kotori, behind him, softly. "It's so cosy."

"It's a swamp," said Xav.

"It's a *cosy* swamp," said Kotori, and the bell on her staff rang.

The eel man was on his crate at the edge of the Landing, gutting an eel into a bucket with great care. He looked up as they passed. He looked at Ice, and at the four people behind him, and at the five names over their heads, all with *Moonlight Harvest* under them. He went back to his eel without a word. But Ice was almost sure he nodded first.

***

Hollis was at the fence of the militia yard. He'd heard. Fernwick wasn't that big.

He didn't say anything as they came along the boardwalk. He just stood in the gap in the fence with his three-fingered hand on the post and counted them as they passed, one by one, under his breath. Ice. Kai. Sketch. The tall one with the silver hair and the scythe, at whom Hollis's eyebrows went up a very long way. And the small one in the blue robe with the bell, who said "Good morning! Sorry!" as she went by, and at whom his eyebrows came back down into something almost soft.

"Five," said Hollis.

"Five."

"That's right, then." He looked at Ice's shield. "You'll want the Anchor, where you're going. My captain said the old ones under the fen don't hit hard. They hit *long*."

"What does that mean?"

"Don't know," said Hollis. "He never told me. He just said it." He went back into his yard. "Go on. Don't die. It's a nuisance."

***

Sedge was on her porch, which she never was, and she was waiting.

She stood at the top of her steps with her arms folded and her pale sharp eyes on the boardwalk. When the five of them came along the north walk past the last of the houses, she watched them all the way. The bundles of drying herbs rustled overhead.

Kotori had the cress. She'd picked it at the mill in Brackenford at dawn, kneeling on the wet stones by the race in the half-dark: twist and lift, root whole, one in three. She'd wrapped it in a damp cloth and carried it the whole way in both hands as if it were a bird. Now she went up the steps and held it out, and said "Sorry—this is for you—Ice said you wanted it fresh—I picked it this morning—one in three—sorry," all in one breath.

Sedge took the cress and unwrapped it and looked at it. Then she looked at the roots. Then she looked at Kotori for a long time.

"Who taught you to pick?"

"Ice," said Kotori. "Sorry. I mean, he told me. Twist and lift."

Sedge looked at Ice. Then back at the small girl in front of her, who was holding her staff again like the strap on a bus and had gone pink.

"You've got good hands," said Sedge. "For soft ones."

Kotori opened her mouth, closed it, and looked at Ice in helpless astonishment. Ice grinned at her over Sedge's shoulder. He mouthed *it's not a compliment, it's a fact*, and Kotori laughed, a beat late.

Sedge's eyes went to Suzu.

She looked at him longer than she'd looked at any of them, at the long silver hair and the pointed ears and Aldo's dark scythe. And at the small glass jar on his belt, the one with the big Warden's light in it, pale blue and still humming after five days. Ice saw her see it. Something went across her face. It wasn't surprise. It was more like recognition, from a long way off.

"That's an old light," she said.

"From under a ford," said Suzu. "In Brackenford."

"Hm." She looked at it a moment longer. Then she looked at all five of them. "Five," she said. "Five hands. None below fifteen." It wasn't a question.

"None below fifteen," said Ice.

Sedge nodded slowly. She turned to go in. At her door she stopped, with her back to them, the way she always talked to him, and said something Ice would think about for a long time afterwards.

"My mother said they sang," she said. "She said they'd been singing since before her grandmother's time, and they'd never got to the end of it. She said it was the saddest thing she ever heard, a song that couldn't finish." She was quiet for a moment. "When you get down there, let them finish."

She went inside. The grinding started.

***

Rafi was at the end of the north walk with his sack, as he always was, and he grinned at Ice like a man who'd been saving something up.

"Boss," he said, low. "The red sashes came back. Last week. Three of them." He jerked his head toward the old fen. "Asking about the potion guy's herbs. Where he got them. Offered me *money*, boss. Real money. On the Exchange."

"What did you tell them?"

"I said I pick fenwort," said Rafi, with enormous satisfaction. "Which is true." He looked at the five of them, and their packs, and their weapons. "Where are you going?"

"Fishing," said Ice.

Rafi grinned even wider. "Bring me back a big sword, boss."

***

He went dark at the end of the north walk, where the boards ran out. That was the rule. Before he did, he looked at the chat panel at the edge of his eye for a while. Two hundred and twenty-seven people.

"Chat," he said. "I'm going dark. For a long time. Probably all day."

***

The tussocks were easier with Xav. The dark grass had roots.

The heron-stones came up out of the reeds at noon, grey and leaning, nine of them, and he watched Suzu and Kotori feel the doorway as they stepped inside. Kotori stopped dead in the warm water with her mouth open. Suzu went very still, the way he did, and turned his head slowly, all the way round the ring, looking at the herons.

"It's *warm*," said Kotori.

"Nothing comes inside the stones," said Deston.

"There's one looking out," said Suzu, and pointed at the ninth heron, the one that faced north. Nobody had told him.

They didn't pick. It was day, and the moonbells were shut in the middle of the ring in a cluster of tight white fists. Ice only went over and looked at them for a moment, and counted, and saw they'd come back. More than he'd left, he thought. One in three. Then he went to the ninth heron, and put his hand on the worn stone where the beak pointed out, and looked north past it, at the black water.

"This way," he said.

***

The lurker had come back. Of course it had.

It came up out of the deep water a hundred paces past the stones, in the same place, mostly mouth, eyes a hand apart, and stopped dead. Five people stood in front of it in the waist-deep water, in a line, with a small girl on a tussock to one side and a song going.

It was a level five. They were fifteen.

"It turns left slowly," said Ice, to nobody, for old times' sake.

It took about forty seconds. Ice didn't even need the Anchor. When it broke into grey dust, he stood in the water where he'd stood on the first morning, with nine per cent of his health left and no idea what he was doing, and felt something go through him that wasn't quite pride and wasn't quite grief. It was more the feeling of walking past the house you grew up in.

Then they went round the last clump of reeds, and the mist thinned, and the mound came up out of the black water. There was the sunken arch with the herons cut wing to wing all round it, and the slab, and the five hollows, and the five words.

FIVE VOICES, AND NONE BELOW FIFTEEN.

And the singing.

***

Suzu heard it first, of the two who hadn't. He stopped in the chest-deep water ten paces from the arch with his scythe held up out of the water, very still, and tilted his head.

Kotori heard it a moment later, and put her hand over her mouth.

It was the same as it had been. Very faint, very slow, coming through the stone or under it. Many voices, high and low together, in a language none of them knew. A choir in the dark, singing as if it had forgotten it had ever started.

"It's so sad," said Kotori, behind her hand. "Sorry. It sounds sad."

"It's stuck," said Deston.

They all looked at him. He was standing by the slab with his ear almost on the stone and his eyes shut, the way he had eleven days ago.

"Listen," he said. "Listen to the end of the phrase. There. It goes up, and it should resolve. It should come down onto the home note, the way every hymn does at the end of a verse. And it doesn't." He opened his eyes. "It goes back to the start. Every time. It gets to the end of the first verse and goes back to the beginning. Over and over." He swallowed. "Sedge's mum was right. They can't finish."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Suzu waded forward to the slab, and looked at the five hollows, and put his right hand into the fourth.

Deston put his into the second. Ice into the first. Xav into the third, without a word. And Kotori, last, reaching up, because the hollow was nearly at the height of her shoulder, put her small hand into the fifth.

[ Five voices. ]

The singing stopped.

***

It stopped all at once, the way a room goes quiet when a door opens. For a long moment there was nothing at all: the black water, the five of them with their hands in the stone, the mist, a frog somewhere a long way off.

Then the slab sank.

It went down into the water slowly, smoothly, without a sound, the way a stone goes down into a well. As it went, the arch opened above it onto darkness and steps. Old wide stone steps going down, wet and green, into the mound, and air coming up them. Cold air, smelling of stone and still water and something like old candle wax.

And from somewhere far down inside, very faint, the singing started again, from the beginning.

***

He'd told them it was a scout.

That was what he'd said on the walk, and in the ring, and at the slab, and they'd all agreed. They'd go in, they'd see what it was, they'd see what hit, they'd see how far they could go. And the first time something made Xav's face do the thing it did when he'd decided, they'd turn round and walk out. No wipe. A wipe was five people locked out for a day, and VANTA would be back in their own drowned church tonight.

He told himself that all the way down the steps.

At the bottom, the steps opened into a nave.

It had been a church once. You could see that at once, even under the water. A long high stone hall with pillars down both sides, going away into dim green light that came from nowhere he could see. Maybe from the water itself, which came up to their knees and was perfectly still and perfectly clear. Under the water, rows of wooden pews ran in lines toward the far end, their tops just breaking the surface like a field of low planks. Moss on the pillars. Bits of coloured glass on the floor under the water, where windows had been, high up, before the fen took the church.

And standing in the water between the pews, in rows, was the choir.

They were people. They had been. They stood in their places in the water in long pale robes, faces lifted, mouths open, singing. And they were made of something like the barrow wisps on Suzu's hill: mist and faint light, not quite there, so that you could see the pews through them, and the moss on the pillars. A dozen in the first rows. More behind, and more behind that, going away into the green dimness toward the far end of the nave. Ice couldn't count them.

They hadn't noticed the five people at the foot of the steps. They were singing.

Kotori was already moving. Ice watched her, the way he'd watched her at the weir. She stood at the foot of the steps for a long moment, looking at the whole nave, slowly. Then she went sideways, to the left, out of the line of the pews, and climbed onto the stone base of the first pillar, where it stood up out of the water. It was the size of a dinner plate. She stood on it with her feet together, like a heron, and held her staff out.

"Ready," she said quietly. "Sorry. Ready."

***

The first three came at them on the beat.

That was the first thing he learned, and he learned it fast. The choristers didn't attack like rats or leeches or Wardens, when it suited them. They attacked in *time*. All three on the same note of the hymn, at the same instant, three pale shapes coming through the water at him, arms out, on the downbeat. His shield turned the first, and the second hit the slope at the same instant and the third at the same instant as that. Three strikes landing as one, and the slope couldn't turn all three. Some of it got through.

It didn't hurt the way the Warden hurt. It was cold. It went into his arm like cold water into a crack, and it *stayed*. A long, slow, freezing ache that didn't stop when the strike was over.

*They don't hit hard*, Hollis's captain had said. *They hit long.*

"They're on the beat!" shouted Deston, from behind him, over the singing. "Dec, they're attacking *on the beat*. All of them, the downbeat, every bar—"

"I see it!"

The bell rang, from the pillar. Warmth went into his arm and fought the cold, and the cold gave a little and didn't go. The bell rang again.

And then Deston did something Ice didn't expect.

He stopped singing his own line.

For a second there was only the choir, the long slow first verse going round and round in the green dark, and the three pale shapes drawing back for the next downbeat. Then Deston opened the book against his chest, put his hand on the spine, and sang *their* line. The bass line. The one he'd hummed through the slab eleven days ago, a little flat on the low notes the way he'd always been, and he sang it the way you sing when you're coming in under a choir that's already going. Quietly at first. Then not quietly.

*Let the many move as one.*

It wasn't the words. The words were nothing like theirs. But the *time* was. He sang in their time, on their beat, under their melody, as if he were the bass section of a choir that had been missing its basses for three hundred years.

And the three choristers in front of Ice faltered.

Not all the way. For a second. Their downbeat came, and it came a fraction late, because somewhere under them a new voice had come in, and for one instant they didn't know whether to keep time with each other or with *him*. And in that instant they weren't together. One came at Ice a breath before the others. One came a breath after. And one shield could turn one strike at a time.

He turned the first. Turned the second. Anchored the third and sent it back into the water. Out of nowhere, a second after it, came Xav. And from the left, at knee height, came the long low sound he'd heard on a hill in the rain.

*Shhhhk.*

Three lights came up out of the mist where they'd been. Not blue, like the wisps and the Warden. White. Soft and white, and *singing*. Each of them still singing, very faintly, one note each, as they rose.

Suzu had the jar out before Ice had finished seeing them. He caught one on the blade, and it clung, white and bright along the dark edge of Aldo's scythe. He moved it into the jar and stoppered it, and held the jar up in the green light, and stood there very still.

It was still singing. Inside the glass. One note, faint and clear, on and on.

"Huh," said Suzu, very softly.

***

They went down the nave, slowly, a few pews at a time. Ice had never fought anything harder, and he'd never fought anything better.

Deston sang under them the whole time. Every time a group of choristers came at them on the downbeat, he came in on their bass line and broke their time, and they came apart into one and one and one. Ice turned them one at a time, and Xav was a second behind each, and Suzu's row went round at knee height. Kotori moved from pillar to pillar along the left side of the nave, always on a base or a broken pew-end or a fallen stone, always where nothing went, always able to see all four of them. The bell rang before the cold went in.

Suzu caught every light. He had nine jars by the second pillar from the end, and they were all singing, faintly, one note each, on his belt as he walked. Together, Ice realised slowly, they were making a chord.

Two-thirds of the way down the nave there was a step up, and a low stone screen across the church with an arch in it. Through the arch, a raised space where the altar must have been once. And standing in front of the arch, facing them, was something bigger than the rest. A chorister twice the height of the others, in a darker robe, with a long staff in one hand that it was beating on the stone floor. *Thud.* *Thud.* *Thud.* Every downbeat.

It saw them.

It didn't sing. It opened its mouth and *shouted* the downbeat, a great hollow wordless sound, and brought its staff down on the stone. Every chorister left in the nave behind them turned round at once, all of them, and came back up the aisles on the beat.

It was the worst minute of the day.

They were caught between the Precentor in front and the whole back half of the choir behind. Deston's bass line wasn't enough to break that many, not with the Precentor shouting the time over the top of him. Ice Anchored, and Anchored, and the cold went into him from three sides and stayed there. The bell rang and rang and rang. Kotori had stopped saying sorry, which was how he knew it was bad. Suzu's row went round behind them, again and again, holding the back half. Xav got behind the Precentor once, twice, three times, *Unseen*, and it barely noticed.

At thirty-one per cent, with the cold going into him at six a second, Ice made the call.

"*Out*," he said. "Everybody out. Back to the steps. *Now.* Dest, keep singing. Kotori first."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about the five of them that he'd remember. Nobody said *but we're nearly there* or *one more go*. Kotori went first, from pillar to pillar back along the left side, fast, without getting her feet wet. Suzu went after her with his scythe going round behind him, and Xav after Suzu. Ice came last, backwards, with his shield up and Deston beside him singing the bass line all the way back down the nave. The Precentor didn't follow past the step. The choir followed to the third pew from the steps, and then stopped, all at once, as if they'd come to the end of a line drawn in the water. They turned back to their places and lifted their faces and went on singing the first verse.

The five of them went up the steps into the cold air and the mist, out of the arch. They sat down on the wet grass of the mound in a row, and nobody said anything at all for a long time.

It was Deston who spoke first, in the end, with his head back against the stone of the arch and his eyes shut.

"It's four verses," he said.

"What?"

"The hymn. The music. You can hear it in the shape. It's built to go round four times, and the fourth time it changes key at the end and resolves." He opened his eyes. "They're stuck on the first one. They've been singing verse one for three hundred years. That thing at the front, the Precentor, it's keeping them there. Every time they get to the end of verse one, it shouts the downbeat, and they go back to the start."

"So we kill the Precentor," said Xav.

"We kill the Precentor," said Deston. "And then there's something else past the screen. I saw it. Up on the altar steps, at the very end. Bigger. Something with a baton." He looked at Ice. "That's the one that's really keeping them. And I think—" He stopped.

"What?"

"I think if somebody sang them *through* it," said Deston slowly. "All the way through. Verse two, verse three, verse four. Kept time with them, and didn't let the thing at the front pull them back." He looked at the book on his knee. "*For a short while, those you are bound to keep time with you.*" He looked up. "I think they'd finish."

*When you get down there*, Sedge had said, *let them finish*.

Nobody said anything.

Then Suzu, sitting at the end of the row with nine jars on his belt, lifted one of them and held it up to the grey light. The note inside was still going, faint and clear. With the other eight on his belt, they made a chord.

"They want to," said Suzu.

***

They went back to the heron-stones in the late afternoon and sat in the warm water in the ring and made a plan. Xav drew it on the back of a scrap of paper with the stub of pencil he'd been carrying since Brackenford. Kotori said sorry to her own healing bell for having to ring it so much. Deston sang the bass line over and over under his breath, all four verses' worth, working out the shape, until Xav asked him very politely to stop, and then asked him, five minutes later, to start again.

"They're going again tonight," he told the others.

"Then we go in the morning," said Xav. "At dawn. Fresh. All of us." He looked at each of them. "And we do it once."

Kotori, in the warm water, with her staff across her knees, was very quiet. Ice saw her looking at something in her own window, at the edge of her vision. When she saw him notice, she went pink and closed it.

"Sorry," she said. "I was—one of the singers dropped something. By the screen, just before we ran. A bit of silver. I picked it up." She held out her hand.
