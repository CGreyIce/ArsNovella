Before they left Fernwick, Hollis wanted to see the Anchor.

He'd asked on Wednesday, and Ice had said *before I go*, and Hollis had obviously been holding him to it. When the three of them came along the boardwalk at dawn on Sunday, with their packs on and the mist still on the channel, he was standing in the gap in the fence of the militia yard with his club in his hand, waiting. He didn't even pretend to be mending anything.

"Shield," he said.

Ice put his pack down. He took the shield off his back and settled the strap over his forearm, and stepped into the middle of the packed earth, and set his feet.

He'd done it a hundred times since Wednesday. He'd done it on rats and leeches and once, memorably, on a fen stalker that had bounced so hard off the slope that it went into the reeds and didn't come out for a minute. But he hadn't done it in front of Hollis, and he found, a little to his surprise, that he was nervous.

Set the feet. Sink the weight, all the way down, into the heels and the earth. The shield's lower edge to the ground, sloped, the top tilted out. Breathe in.

[ Anchor ]

Hollis hit him.

He didn't hold back. Ice had seen him swing at the dummy for ten days and had never once seen him swing like this. It was a full swing, from behind his shoulder, his whole old body in it, the club coming round like a door slamming in a storm.

It met the slope. And it *stopped*. It didn't slide, the way a Turned Block slid. It came off the shield as if it had hit a wall that had decided to push back, and it went back the way it had come, and took Hollis's arm with it. He staggered back two steps and nearly sat down in the dirt.

Ice hadn't moved at all. Not a hair.

For a second, Hollis just stood there with the club hanging from his hand, breathing.

Then he started to laugh. It was a big, hoarse, surprised laugh, and it came from somewhere a long way down. Ice had never heard it before.

"*That's* the captain," said Hollis. "That's him. That's exactly him, the stubborn old ass." He wiped his eyes with the back of his three-fingered hand. "Go on. Go. Get out of my yard."

Sedge didn't come out. She never did. She said, through her open door, with her back to them, grinding:

"You'll be back for the door."

"I'll be back for the door."

"Bring all five." The grinding stopped for a second. "And bring me something from the river. They grow a cress in Brackenford that my mother used to swear by. Fresh, mind. Not dried."

"Fresh," said Ice. "I promise."

"Hm," said Sedge, and the grinding started again.

Rafi was at the end of the north walk with his sack, already picking. Ice had sorted it out on Friday: Rafi would keep picking fenwort and bring it to Sedge's porch every day, and Sedge would pay him two copper a plant out of a little bag of Ice's silver that she was keeping for exactly that. She'd been very clear that she wasn't doing it as a favour. She was doing it because she'd get the herbs for nothing. Rafi was nineteen and from Surabaya, and he'd called it the best job anybody in Fernwick had, and shaken Ice's hand twice.

"Bring me back a big sword, boss," said Rafi now, grinning.

"I'll bring you back a big sword," said Ice.

***

The road to Brackenford went out of Fernwick on the old causeway, a long raised bank of earth and stone running north-east across the fen like a spine. Ice had been looking at it on his map for four days and never walked more than a hundred paces of it.

It took them the rest of the morning.

It was the first time he'd walked anywhere in Elsewhere that wasn't somewhere he'd already been, and he'd forgotten what that felt like. The fen fell away on both sides, flat and silver and endless, full of herons. Then, slowly, without anyone noticing it happen, it started to change. The reeds got shorter. Firmer ground came up through the water, then grass, then a hedge, then a field with sheep in it. The sheep stared at the three of them with deep suspicion. Deston said "*Sheep*," in a voice of great joy, and Xav said "They're sheep, Dest," and Deston said "They're the first sheep I've ever seen in a *game* that looked at me like that." Which was fair.

They talked the whole way. It was Sunday, and Deston's last paper was on Tuesday, and he'd said in the group chat at six that morning: *I'm coming to Brackenford. I'm revising tonight. Don't argue.* Nobody had argued.

Brackenford came up out of the fields at the end of the morning, all at once, when they came over a low rise in the causeway. It was so much bigger than Fernwick that Ice stopped walking.

It was a town built where water met. Three rivers came down out of three valleys, from the fen and the moors and the hills, and they ran together into one broad brown river right in the middle of the town. The town was built on all the banks and all the islands between them, and joined up with bridges. Stone bridges, old and humped, a dozen of them, wide enough for carts. Mills with their wheels turning in the current. Tall narrow houses of grey stone with slate roofs, leaning over the water. A market square on the biggest island, crowded. And in the middle of the square, black against all that grey, a tall stone needle three times the height of a man, and people standing round its base looking up.

[ Brackenford
Intermediate town · The Three Waters
Travellers here now: 2,847 ]

"Two thousand," said Xav.

"Eight hundred and forty-seven," said Deston.

There were more people in Brackenford than Ice had seen in one place in the whole twelve days. They were everywhere: on the bridges, in the market, sitting on the river walls with their legs over the water, the way he did on Sedge's porch. Not all of them wore linen shirts now. A lot of them had real armour on: leather and studded leather and, here and there, a glint of mail. A woman went past in a long blue robe with a staff taller than she was. Two men argued loudly over a hand-drawn map on a bridge parapet. Somebody's camera orb drifted overhead, then somebody else's, then four more. And the noise: a hundred conversations at once, in thirty languages coming to him in English a heartbeat late, all with their accents on, so the whole town sounded like the whole world.

They knew what the needle was before they reached it. Everybody did. It had been in every stream from every intermediate town all week, and in every forum thread, and in the group chat at six in the morning. Deston had sent *THE TALLY* in capitals, and then *I'm not looking until you're both there*, and then *Xav don't look either*, and Xav had sent *I'm not going to look*, and then, a minute later, *I'm a bit going to look*.

It stood in the middle of the market square on a plinth of the same black stone, smooth and dark and faintly warm when Ice put his hand on it, like a stone that had been in the sun. From the plinth up to about the height of his head, it was blank. Above that, cut into the black in letters that shone faintly silver, were names.

A hundred of them. One under the other, all the way up the needle until they got too small to read. Beside each name, a number.

[ The Tally
The hundred travellers of greatest standing, by level, across all of Elsewhere.
It changes as they do. ]

Ice found he didn't want to look up.

It was stupid. He'd known it was coming for twelve days. He'd done the sums. He'd watched other streams, and chat had told him a dozen times that he was *probably top twenty, maybe top ten, dude*. He'd known he was somewhere. It was a hundred names out of three million people, and if you'd started first and played twelve hours a day and pulled seven things at once into a gap in the reeds, you were *somewhere*.

He still didn't want to look up.

"Together," said Deston, beside him. "Okay? Together. Three, two—"

They looked up.

It was Deston who made the noise first. It wasn't a word. It was a sound like a kettle, high and rising. Then he had both arms round Ice's neck from behind and was shaking him, saying "*Seventh*, Dec, *seventh*, you're on the *needle*," and then, "I'm *thirteenth*," in a voice of total disbelief. "I'm revising for an exam on Tuesday and I'm *thirteenth in the world*."

Xav hadn't moved. He was standing beside them with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the needle with his face completely still. Ice had known him long enough to know what that meant. Xav was trying very hard not to look as pleased as he was.

"Eleven," said Xav eventually.

"Eleven," said Ice.

"Two places outside." Xav's mouth did something. "That's fine. That's fine. I'll take two places."

***

There was a lot of noise in the square after that, and some of it was about them.

People had seen the stream. More people than he'd realised. Somebody near the plinth said "*That's* him, that's the POV guy," in Portuguese and then in English, and pointed. Somebody else said "the swamp guy" and laughed. A girl with a bow came over and asked, very shyly, whether he really started his back foot inside the cut, and he showed her, there in the square, slowly. A little crowd stood round and watched him do a Falling Cut at nothing in the middle of the Brackenford market, and when he finished, three of them clapped.

He'd never been recognised in his life. He didn't know what to do with his hands.

Xav did. Xav waited until the crowd had thinned and then stood at the foot of the needle and read it again, the whole top fifteen, slowly, the way he read anything that mattered. Ice watched him file it.

"Five Crimson Ledger in the top fifteen," said Xav quietly. "Field one, two, five, eight. And I bet there's more lower down. They've hired people who are good at games and paid them to play all day." He moved down the list. "Two VANTA. That's the pro team. Kestrel's their best. I watched her stream on Thursday. She's very good." His eyes went on. "Tavinho's the Brazilian who was level three before you on day one. Sori's a Korean player with no stream who never stops. Halvard I don't know. Oduya I don't know. Juniper I don't know."

"And Ash," said Ice.

"And Ash." Xav looked at the tenth line for a while. *Ash · Nightjar.* "Nightjar's a crew. They're the ones who've been killing people in the open areas around the intermediate towns since day ten, when the first people got here. You lose a day if they get you. It's been all over the forum." He paused. "They wait at the edges of streams."

"Like the sashes."

"Worse than the sashes. The sashes wanted your potions. Nightjar want your day." He looked back up at the stone. "Look at the rest, though."

Ice looked.

"Everybody in the top fifteen is either alone or paid," said Xav. "Pros. Money guilds. Solos who don't sleep. People who play for a living, or play like it." He tapped the stone, very lightly, beside *Moonlight Harvest*. "And then there's us. Three people who started in a swamp, who aren't paid by anybody, and one of them's got an exam on Tuesday."

"I really do have an exam on Tuesday," said Deston.

"We know, Dest."

"I just want it on the record."

Ice stood with his hand flat on the black stone, warm as if it had been in the sun, and looked up at the silver names. At *Kestrel*, at the very top, level thirteen. Thirteen, and fifteen was the door.

He thought about a slab of grey stone half sunk in black water at the edge of a fen, a morning's walk behind them. Five hollows, and five words cut above them. And a choir under it, singing as if it had forgotten it had ever started.

Somewhere in the top fifteen, somebody was two levels from fifteen. Somebody with a team of pros, and a stream with ten thousand people watching, and nobody to tell them where to look.

Nobody knew where the door was. He was almost sure of that. Almost.

"We need five," he said, quietly, to the stone.

"We've got four," said Deston. "Suzu's level eight."

"We need a healer."

Xav looked at him. Then he turned round, slowly, and looked across the square, past the stalls and the crowds and the camera orbs, at a long low building on the far side with a sign over its door: a painted sword crossed with a painted staff, and under it, in old gold letters, *THE ADVENTURERS' HALL*. In front of it stood a wooden board as big as a barn door, covered in pinned paper.

"Then we go and find one," said Xav.
