Leaka had opened the box on the first of April, and then she'd stood and looked at the egg for nine days, because opening the box and getting into it were different things.

She'd told everyone this. Loudly. On stream, in every chat she was in, on the group call at least twice a day.

"OPENING IT WAS THE DEAL," she said, on Friday night, to Jarred and Alice and Milli on the call, from her flat in England. "I PAID THE FIRST HUNDRED. I OPENED THE BOX. THAT WAS THE DEAL. GETTING *IN* IT IS A DIFFERENT DEAL."

"What's the different deal?" said Alice, patiently, from a ledge in a quarry town on the other side of the world.

"CURSE," said Leaka.

"Curse's egg came on Wednesday."

"I KNOW IT CAME ON WEDNESDAY, ALICE. HE SENT ME A PHOTO. HE'D PUT IT IN THE CORNER OF HIS ROOM AND PUT A PLANT ON IT." She was walking up and down her flat, past the egg, which took up half the space between the sofa and the window and was very white. "WE'RE GOING IN TOGETHER. TOMORROW. TEN IN THE MORNING, MY TIME. SEVEN AT NIGHT, HIS. SAME MINUTE. LIKE YOU LOT DID."

"That's lovely," said Alice.

"IT'S NOT LOVELY, IT'S *LOGISTICS*," said Leaka, and went red, and was glad nobody could see.

***

Leaka was not, as a rule, a person who waited for things.

She'd been in CGI's chat on the first night. Twenty-one viewers, a man in a swamp, a shield. She'd been in it every night since. She'd typed in it, in capitals, about everything, for five months. She'd watched him go from ten to thirty thousand. She'd watched everyone she knew go into the game one wave at a time, the way you'd watch people board a train you didn't have a ticket for. Alice and Jarred and Milli, two weeks ago, in a quarry, with a jump and a fist in the air. And she'd said once, *haha well that's not happening this year*, and meant it, and been wrong.

She'd been wrong because two people had bought her an egg and called it a loan, and then asked her twelve times.

She'd said no eleven times. She'd meant every one. You didn't take two and a half thousand dollars off your friends, not ever, not for anything, no matter how much you wanted to go. And she'd wanted to go so much it was embarrassing.

Then on the twelfth time, Jarred had said, *Leaka, it's not a present. It's a door. You can pay for the door. Just walk through it.*

So she'd said fine. In capitals. And then she'd paid for the first hundred of the door, seventeen pounds at a time.

[ Welcome to ELSEWHERE.

*Aleaka.*

It was free. Of course it was free. Nobody else in the whole world was called Aleaka, and that was the point of being called Aleaka.

[ Your likeness. ]

She built herself the way she streamed. She didn't change much. She'd never seen the point of changing things.

[ Bring one thing with you. ]

The mirror offered her things, and she didn't look at most of them. She took the headset. The one she'd streamed in for years. It was hers. It had been on her head for every caps-lock word she'd ever said to anyone. It came into the white room hanging round her neck, and she left it there.

[ Pain. ]

Fifty.

She didn't think about it. She knew exactly why. *Because CGI's on fifty.* She'd heard Salty say it on the first night Salty went in, and she'd thought *that's stupid*, and she'd thought *that's exactly what I'd do*.

[ Take something from the rack. ]

There was a rack. She looked along it. Swords. Bows. A staff. A hammer.

At the end, leaning against the wall because it was too big to hang, an axe. A great big two-handed thing with a head like a half-moon and a haft as long as she was tall.

She took the axe.

"OBVIOUSLY," she said, to the empty room, and the rain on the window.

She came out into a fair.

Not a fair that had come to town. A fair that *was* the town. A river town of tall painted houses, red and yellow and green, leaning over a wide brown river on stilts. In the middle, on a big flat island where the river split, a fair that never left. Stalls under striped awnings. A carousel of carved wooden animals going round to a wheezing organ. A great wooden wheel, taller than the houses, turning slowly, with little swinging cabins on it. Jugglers. A woman selling hot apples. A man with a bear on a chain, which was concerning.

It was noisy. Everything was noisy. Music and shouting and the organ and the river and somebody somewhere banging a drum.

Leaka stood on the steps of her little wooden room with her axe, in the middle of all of it, and grinned so wide her face hurt.

"OH, IT'S *LOUD*," she said, delighted.

[ You are among the first travellers to wake in Merrowgate today.
You may Call up to four friends to wake here with you.
The Call lasts two days. ]

She didn't hesitate. She'd planned it with Curse on the phone at two in the morning his time, in very specific detail, because Curse did things in very specific detail.

*Cursiffe.*

[ Call sent. ]

She waited. On the call in her ear, from Australia, she could hear Curse breathing. Calm, slow, the way he always breathed. And then a pause, and a small sound that might have been a window coming up in front of him.

"*Leaka*," said Curse. "There's a window. It says *Aleaka is Calling you to Merrowgate*."

"YES. THAT'S ME. THAT'S THE PLAN."

"I know it's the plan. I made the plan." A pause. "It's a fair?"

"IT'S A WHOLE TOWN THAT'S A FAIR. THERE'S A BEAR."

There was a very long silence on the call.

"I'm coming anyway," said Curse.

***

The door of the little wooden room next to hers opened, and Curse came out.

She'd never seen him. Not in person. Alice and Milli had, in November, for four days in Singapore. Curse had flown up from Australia and said *you're shorter than I thought* and given Alice a fountain pen. But Leaka had only ever been a voice to him, and he to her, on calls and in chats, for years. A dry voice. Precise. The voice of a man who'd tell you exactly what was wrong with your plan and then help you do it anyway.

He came out of the door into the noise of the fair and stopped on the step and looked at her, very calmly.

He had a quarterstaff, a plain one, ash. And something else, held carefully in one hand, close to his chest, the way you'd hold a bird. A small glass inkwell, with a stopper, full of dark blue ink.

He looked at her. At the axe, which was taller than she was. At the headset round her neck.

"You're louder than I thought," said Curse.

"I'M *EXACTLY* AS LOUD AS YOU THOUGHT," said Leaka.

"Yes," said Curse. "That's what I mean. I thought you'd be very loud. You're louder."

She stared at him. Then she laughed. She laughed so hard she had to lean on the axe. The woman with the hot apples looked over and shook her head, and the man with the bear moved the bear slightly further away.

Curse didn't laugh. But his face did something at one corner, very small, that she'd only ever heard in his voice before.

"Hi, Leaka," he said.

"HI, CURSE."

"Is that a bear?"

"THAT'S A BEAR."

"Hm," said Curse, and looked at it for a long time, and moved slightly in front of her without saying anything about it. He held his quarterstaff in one hand and the inkwell very carefully in the other.

She messaged CGI at eleven, her time, from the top of the great wheel.

She'd made Curse go on it with her. He'd said it was a terrible idea. Then he'd got in the little swinging cabin and sat in it very still, with the inkwell held against his chest, while it went up and up over the painted roofs of Merrowgate and the brown river and the fair. At the top it stopped, the way they always did, and swung. And from there you could see the whole of the Riverlands going away green and flat in every direction to the edge of the world.

She typed with one finger, in the air. In capitals. Obviously.
