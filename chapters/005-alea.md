Alea waited a week, which she thought showed tremendous restraint.

She waited through seven mornings of forms on the jetty, and seven evenings of her father reading by the fire and saying nothing at all about five girls in strange clothes. She waited while the whole of Wrenhaven talked about nothing else: the axolotl girl who'd made the baker's wife drop a tray, the blue jersey, the little purple octopus that rode around on somebody's head. She waited until she was absolutely certain that her father had decided to do nothing, and that he would go on doing nothing for as long as she let him.

Then she put on her good boots and her copper token from the adventurers' guild, told him she was going into town to look at the notice board, and went to the old militia hall instead.

It wasn't exactly a lie. There was a notice board on the way.

They were in the yard when she got there, sitting cross-legged on the cold flagstones with their eyes shut, the way you did at Initiate when you were trying to feel for mana and mostly feeling for the ground. All except one. In the far corner, the girl with the yellow heart on her head was standing in a wide stance with her eyes closed and her hands open at her sides, breathing slowly, while the big scarred guard watched her.

Aura, then. The hard way. Alea liked her already.

The guide saw her first. Alea had expected that. The guide was the neat, serious woman who'd bowed so low on the market road, and she knew Alea's face; Alea watched her recognise it, and watched her decide, very quickly, what to do about it.

"Can I help you?" said Maren.

"I hope so." Alea held up her token. "I'm Alea. I'm a fledgling with the guild, copper rank, and I'm on the aura path, and there's nobody in Wrenhaven to spar with who isn't either a hundred years old or my—" She stopped, very slightly too late. "Or someone I already know. I heard you had someone training aura. I wondered if I could join in. I'll be useful. I'll fetch water."

Maren looked at the token. Then at Alea's face. Alea could almost see her thinking: about a paper folded in her sleeve, and an order that said nothing whatsoever about daughters.

"You may watch," said Maren at last.

"Oh, let her spar," called the guard from across the yard. "Gods know Penguin could use someone her own size."

"PenguChi," said PenguChi, without opening her eyes.

"That's what I said."

***

The thing about sparring with someone at Initiate, Alea discovered, was that you had to be very careful not to win.

She was only Drawn, the first tier of aura, the lowest rung that counted. In Wrenhaven that made her a promising girl. Against the real guards at the castle in Solmere it would have made her a joke. But Drawn meant her aura was *out*, moving, slightly quicker and slightly stronger than her body alone, and PenguChi's wasn't yet. The first time they crossed practice blades, Alea knocked hers clean out of her hands without meaning to.

"Sorry," said Alea. "Sorry. That was the aura, not me."

PenguChi picked up her blade and looked at it, then at Alea, completely unbothered. "How did you get it out?"

Nobody had ever asked Alea that before. Everyone else just wanted to see her do it.

"It's not in your arms," she said slowly. She was trying to remember exactly how her father had said it, three years ago, on the jetty, when she'd cried because nothing was happening. "Everyone thinks it's in your arms, because that's where you hold the sword. It's lower. You breathe from the very bottom of you, and you wait, and you stop trying to make it come, because it won't come if you chase it. It's like waiting for a fish."

PenguChi closed her eyes, and breathed, and waited.

"How long did it take you?" she asked, after a while.

"Three years."

PenguChi opened one eye. Alea braced for disappointment. But PenguChi only nodded, as if three years were a perfectly reasonable amount of time to wait for anything, and closed it again.

Alea liked her a great deal.

***

The axolotl girl found her at the water butt during the break.

"You're a *fox,*" said Xola, with enormous delight, and then immediately looked horrified. "Sorry. Is that rude? Is that a rude thing to say here? I don't know the rules. I'm an axolotl. I've been an axolotl for two weeks."

"It's not rude," said Alea, who was trying very hard not to stare at the small purple octopus on Xola's head. It was staring at her. "Is that Squiddles?"

Xola froze. "How do you know his name?"

Alea's heart stopped for one terrible second. *Because my father used to tell me about you at bedtime. Because he said you had a little purple friend who sat on your head and judged everyone.*

"Everyone in town knows his name," she said. "You shout it a lot."

"Oh." Xola relaxed. "Yeah. That's fair. He keeps trying to climb into the soup."

They talked for the whole of the break. About ears and tails, and gills, and whether Xola would grow back a finger if she lost one ("I don't *know*, I haven't lost anything yet, and Maren won't let me test it"). About what it was like to be stared at in the street ("It's not the staring, it's the *whispering*"). About the demihumans in Wrenhaven, who weren't treated badly but weren't treated quite like anyone else either. Alea had grown up as the only fox in a human town, and she had never in her life had anyone to complain about it to who understood. By the end of the break she had decided that Xola was going to be her friend whether either of them liked it or not.

***

They let her stay for the afternoon. Then for the next day, and the one after that.

On the fourth day she sat with them at the long table in the militia hall, eating bread and cheese, and listened to them do something they apparently did every day: compare their windows.

"Fifty-two," said Thea.

"Five," said Higu. "It was six last night."

"Three," said Gigichi, and when nobody looked at her, "the roots are definitely there, by the way. I can feel them under the whole yard. There's an old tree out by the gate and it's *grumpy.*"

"Four," said Xola, "and I made the washbasin go still on purpose this morning. Well. Mostly on purpose."

"Two," said PenguChi. "Alea's helping."

Alea had spent her whole life knowing what status windows were, because her father had one, and so did she. She could call hers up the way you could hum a song you'd learned as a baby. But she had never once heard people *talk* about them like this, openly, round a table, as if they were the weather.

"What's yours say?" asked Xola.

Alea opened her mouth and nearly told her.

Across the table, very quietly, Maren put down her cup.

"I don't really have one," said Alea. "Most people here don't. Only a few. I've just got a guild token." She held it up. "It says copper."

"Wait," said Thea, leaning in, interested. "But you can feel your aura. You know you're at the first tier. How do you know if you can't see it?"

*Because I can see it. Because my father taught me how, and he learned it from nobody, because he was the first.*

"Someone told me," said Alea. "Someone who could see it."

Thea studied her for a moment with an expression Alea didn't entirely like: thoughtful, and careful, and very, very bright. Then she shrugged and went back to her bread.

"I can see more than the window," Thea said. "That's the weird thing about me. There's structure underneath it. Like code. I can't change any of it yet, but I can read bits." She frowned at nothing. "Fifty-two percent. Everyone keeps saying that's fast. It doesn't *feel* fast."

"It's fast," said Alea, and meant it. "It took me three years to get to the first tier."

"You keep saying three years," said Gigichi, "like it's a normal amount of time."

"It is here."

The table went quiet in an unhappy sort of way. Alea realised, belatedly, that she'd said something that mattered to them. Three years for one tier. Ten tiers to Sun. She saw Higu do the arithmetic and stop.

"Different for you, though," she said quickly. "You can see your progress. That's what the Advisor told everyone, isn't it? You'll go faster than us."

"Faster than three years a tier," said Thea. "That's still..." She didn't finish.

Alea sat with her bread going dry in her hand and thought about her father, who'd had a window since the day he was born and had still taken twenty-seven years to reach the sixth tier. She didn't say anything about that either.

Over the four days, Thea and Gigichi didn't speak to each other once. Alea had heard the shouting on the market road; half of Wrenhaven had. She watched them carefully not look at each other across the yard. Thea did more than her share of everything, very loudly and without complaint. Gigichi did exactly her share and not one thing more, and looked, the whole time, like someone waiting to be told off.

On the fourth evening Alea found Gigichi alone by the gate, sitting with her back against the old grumpy tree.

"You were there," said Gigichi, without looking up. "On the road. With the fisherman."

"Everyone in Wrenhaven buys fish from him."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Alea agreed, and sat down next to her.

They were quiet for a while.

"She thinks I don't care," Gigichi said at last. "I do care. I just... I'm tired. I was tired for a long time. And she was always *there*, doing everything, and it made me feel like I wasn't doing anything, so I did less." She pulled at the grass. "That's stupid. I know that's stupid."

Alea thought about what her mother would have said. Her mother had been good at this; everyone said so. She'd been a healer, and not only of bodies.

"My dad says a road's only blocked if you stop on it," she said.

Gigichi looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and surprised and only a little bitter. "Your dad sounds like a fortune cookie."

Alea didn't know what a fortune cookie was. She decided it was probably a compliment.

***

On the fifth night there was a fire in the yard, because Bram said there should be, and Maren said nothing, which in Maren was a form of permission.

They sat around it wrapped in blankets, and the guard leaned forward with the firelight on his scarred face and told them about the Hero of the Redmarch.

"Eight feet tall," said Bram. "I swear it. I saw him once from a distance, after, and he was a head taller than the horses."

Alea bit the inside of her cheek.

"They say he went into the battle alone. Ten thousand demons, and one man. They say he killed their general with his bare hands, because he'd broken his sword on the general's armour, and then he picked up the general's sword and kept going." Bram lowered his voice. "They say he hasn't slept since. He lives in a fortress on the eastern border, alone, with his sword across his knees, waiting for them to come back."

Alea bit the inside of her cheek so hard she tasted blood.

"Bram," said Maren, from the dark.

"I'm just telling them what people say."

"You're telling them wrong."

"Is it wrong?" asked Higu at once, sitting up. "Which part?"

Maren didn't answer. She went back to her book. Bram winked at the fire.

Alea had to get up and fetch more wood, because otherwise she was going to laugh until she cried, and she didn't think she could explain either one.

When she came back with an armful of branches, the conversation had moved on. Xola had her chin on her knees and Squiddles in her lap, and she was looking into the fire with an expression Alea hadn't seen on her before.

"Ice would have *loved* this," Xola said.

"He'd already have a nickname for Bram," said Thea, and something in her voice was softer than Alea had heard it all week.

"He'd have stolen the story," said Higu. "He'd have told it better. And then he'd have said 'trust' at the end, and everyone would have known it was a lie."

Alea set the wood down very carefully.

"Who's Ice?" she said.

"A friend," said Xola. "He was in our streams, our calls. He was the funny one. Always."

"He modded my chat," said Higu, after a moment. "For two years. He banned a guy once for being rude to me and then wrote him a whole apology poem from the ban message. I've still got it." She smiled down at her hands. "Well. I had it."

"He was *so* good at word games," said PenguChi. "It was annoying."

"What happened to him?" Alea asked.

"He died," said Thea. "In his sleep. Nobody knows why. He just didn't wake up."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl said something to another owl.

"When?" said Alea.

"Three years ago," said Xola, and then, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand and laughing a little at herself, "sorry. Sorry. It still gets me. It's been three years. You'd think."

Alea didn't hear the rest of what anyone said.

She knew the arithmetic. She'd known it all her life, the way you know your own birthday. Her father had been born in this world thirty-four years ago. He'd told her once that when he'd first opened his eyes as a baby, he'd thought about home every single day, and then every week, and then every year, and then one day he'd noticed that he hadn't thought about it in a long time, and that had been the worst day of all.

Thirty-four years.

*Three.*

She did the sum again. It didn't change.

***

She walked home in the dark. She knew the lake road well enough to walk it with her eyes shut, and she half did, because she didn't want to see anything for a while.

The lamp was lit in the cabin window. Her father was by the fire with the net across his knees and a book open on the arm of the chair, and he looked up when she came in, the way he always did, as if he'd heard her a mile away. He probably had.

"How was the guild?" he asked.

She stood in the doorway with her boots in her hand.

Three years. His friends had lost him three years ago. Somewhere, in the world he never talked about, there were people who'd known him for a whole life and had only had to miss him for three years. His father. The friends he'd told her about. The ones from the school choir, and the ones from the long nights playing games, and the ones who called him by a name she'd only ever heard him say once.

It would take him one sentence to understand. She could see him understanding it. She could see what it would do to his face.

"Fine," said Alea.

He looked at her a moment longer than usual. Then he went back to his net.

She went to bed, and lay on her back, and looked at the ceiling of the cabin her father had built with his own hands, and decided she wasn't going to tell him.

Not yet. Not until she knew what it would do to him.

Her mother would have known what to do. Her mother would have known exactly. But all her mother had left her were a few words, carried home across a battlefield, and one of them was *listen to your father*, which was no help at all, because her father was the one person she couldn't tell.
