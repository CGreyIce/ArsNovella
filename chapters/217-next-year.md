Sorrel was standing on the hard below the harbour wall when the *Gannet* came in on the morning tide, barefoot, with a boat-hook in each hand, and the first thing she shouted across the water was "*You look terrible*."

Alea did look terrible. She knew. She'd been sick over the side of the *Gannet* for most of two days, in the swell off the headlands, while Brisa chewed her unlit pipe at the tiller and the three men on the sheets looked politely at the horizon and the cat slept on a coil of rope and didn't care. On the third morning she'd stopped being sick, all at once, the way a fever breaks, and stood at the bow with her weight forward and her knees giving and looked at the edge of the world until it came into focus as a grey line with a white edge to it, and then as a headland, and then as a long low harbour wall with a town behind it climbing up a hill in steps of slate roofs, and a girl on the hard below the wall jumping up and down.

"*I know*," Alea shouted back.

"*Good*," shouted Sorrel. "*It means you did it properly*."

***

Gullhaven was a town that faced the wrong way.

That was what Alea thought, standing on the quay with her legs still going up and down under her the way they would, Brisa said, for a day, and her pack on her back and Astra over it. Every town she'd ever known faced inward. Wrenhaven faced its square; Cinderhold faced its yard; Valcrest faced the great bowl of the Lists like a crowd facing a stage. Gullhaven faced *out*. Every house on the hill had its windows on the sea side, and every one of the long low sheds along the quay had its doors open to the water, and every face she saw, mending nets on the bollards or gutting fish at the troughs or rolling barrels down the slip, looked up at the harbour mouth every few breaths, the way her father looked at the water from the end of the jetty: not because there was anything there, but because there might be.

They smelled of fish. All of them. The whole town did. It was in the stone.

"You'll stop noticing," said Sorrel. "In about a week. And then you'll go home and your dad'll say *you smell of fish* and you'll be so cross."

"He smells of fish."

"Lake fish," said Sorrel, with tremendous scorn. "That's not fish. That's a pond with ideas."

She was taller than she'd been at the Lists. Not much. A finger's width. Salt-burned, brown as a nut, with her hair cropped short under a headband of old sailcloth and a fisherman's smock to her knees and a grin like a gap in a fence; and the hooks. Two of them. Long ash shafts, cut down when she was ten, with iron hooks at the end worn bright on the inside of the curve by twenty years of somebody else's hands and seven of hers. She carried them the way Alea carried Astra: without thinking about it, the way you carry your own arm.

"Come on," said Sorrel. "She's in the slip. I want you to see her before you see anything else."

***

NEXT YEAR was small.

Alea had known she would be. She'd built her in her head out of letters for a year, out of *she leaks like a basket* and *the harbourmaster says it's not a name, it's a threat* and *four of them on the hard by NEXT YEAR when I came down, with pitch*. She'd built a boat the size of the *Patient Widow*. NEXT YEAR was half that. Less. A broad-bottomed open boat with a short mast and a lug sail rolled on its yard, clinker-built, the planks overlapping like scales, tarred black below the waterline and painted a faded grey above it that had once, you could see under the grey, been blue. She sat in the slip at the bottom of the hard with her bow up on the shingle and her stern in the water, rocking very slightly, the way the deck of the *Widow* had rocked at the quay below the mill.

And across her stern, in big uneven white capitals, painted by somebody who'd been angry and crying and sixteen, who'd done it with a brush too wide for the job and gone over the letters twice where the paint was thin: NEXT YEAR.

"She doesn't leak," said Sorrel. "Not any more. Abner and the Hallams caulked her in a day, after your letter came. The one with his name in it." She put her hand on the gunwale. Flat. The way Alea put hers on Astra's flat at night. "I'd been mending her for a year and she still leaked. They did it in a day. Because of a name." She looked at the stern. "Abner says I've got to paint it out at midsummer. He says it's this year now and it's still on the stern and it's bad luck to sail a boat with a date on her that's gone."

"Are you going to?"

"No," said Sorrel. "It's a name, not a date. He knows that. He just likes saying it so I'll say that." She looked at Alea. "Your dad. Did he come?"

"No."

Sorrel looked at her for a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, the way she'd nodded at the end of the final on the sand at Valcrest with both of them sitting there crying, and didn't say anything else about it, then or ever.

***

Abner was at the bollard by the slip mending nets. He was very old and very brown and had three fingers on his left hand, and he looked at Alea over the net in his lap for a long time with eyes gone pale as the sea at noon.

"You're the lake girl," said Abner.

"Yes."

"Tobyn's girl talks about you," said Abner. "All the time. *The lake girl this. The lake girl that. The lake girl came at me like a pike*." He went back to the net. "I sailed with him twenty year. Her dad. Best hand on the bar in Gullhaven, and he went over on it, and that's the sea for you." He pulled a knot tight with his teeth. "Your dad says his name off a jetty. Every morning, in the snow. You wrote it in a letter. She read it out to me on this bollard and I stood up and said *Tobyn*. First time anybody on this quay had, since."

"I didn't know him," said Alea.

"No," said Abner. "That's why it worked." He didn't look up. "We knew him. That's why we couldn't."

***

Sorrel's mother was at the troughs.

The salt-sheds ran along the back of the quay, long and low and open on the sea side, with troughs of brine down the middle and women along both sides of them gutting herring so fast their hands were a blur, and the smell came out of the open doors like a wall. Sorrel's mother was at the end of the nearest trough. Alea knew her before Sorrel said anything. She had Sorrel's face, older and thinner and paler, with all the grin taken out of it and something else put in its place that Alea had seen before, on Rowan, at the top of the lake road, and on Halvard, on the end of a jetty in the cold with his hand over his eyes. She was gutting herring with a short knife, one, two, three, into the basket at her feet, without looking at her hands.

"Mam," said Sorrel. "This is her. The lake one."

Sorrel's mother looked up.

She looked at Alea for a long time. At the ears, and the sword, and the face. Then she wiped her hands on her apron and came round the end of the trough, and Alea saw that she was smaller than Sorrel, and that her hands were raw to the wrist from the brine.

"Ruth," she said. "You'll be hungry. You'll have been sick on Brisa's boat. Everyone is." She looked at Alea's face. "You're very like your mother."

Alea's ears went up.

"You knew her?"

"No," said Ruth. "Sorrel's told me. She says Lucan read it out. *Daughter of Diwa, a healer*. She says you've got her face, because your father said so to somebody who said so to somebody, in Valcrest, the way things go." She almost smiled. It didn't quite get there. "You've a healer's face. I can see it from here. My mother was one. Herb-wife, up the hill. You look at a person the way she used to. As if you're checking what's wrong."

Alea didn't know what to say to that. So she did what Odette had said, on the quay, a month and half a kingdom ago. She took off her pack, and opened it, and took out the jar wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the same red cord as the curtain in her loft, and held it out.

"From Odette," she said. "At home. She said you don't arrive at somebody's house with nothing."

Ruth took it. She untied the cord, slowly, with her raw fingers, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and looked at what was in the jar. Dark, almost black, with the light coming through it red at the edges.

"Bramble," said Alea. "From the hedges along the lake road. She does it every autumn. She says it's the only thing she makes that the mayor doesn't argue with."

Ruth looked at the jar for a long time.

"He used to bring brambles down," she said. "In the autumn. From up the hill. In his cap. For Sorrel, when she was small." She held the jar against her apron. "Since. I haven't—"

She stopped. Alea saw her stop. The way you'd see somebody come to the edge of a step in the dark and feel for it with their foot and not find it.

*Since*. Sorrel had written about that, in her letter, the first one, last summer: *Everyone at home crosses it out. They go round it. They say "since" and stop*.

Alea didn't say anything. She didn't know Ruth. She'd known her for five minutes. She wasn't going to say a dead man's name to his wife on a quay.

"Thank you," said Ruth. To the jar. "Tell Odette thank you." And went back round the end of the trough, and put the jar on the shelf over it where the women kept their bread, and picked up her knife, and went on gutting herring, one, two, three, without looking at her hands.

***

She cast off the end of the quay on the third morning.

Not from the boat. Sorrel had wanted to take her straight out, past the bar, at dawn on the first day, and Alea had looked at the sea beyond the harbour mouth with her legs still going up and down under her from the *Gannet* and said *tomorrow*, and on the second morning said it again. On the third morning she could stand on the quay without it moving, and she went down to the end of it at dawn with the hook Gabe had made her and a length of good line and a lump of herring off the trough for bait, and stood at the end of the harbour wall where the stone went down into deep green water, and baited the hook.

*Sto*. Inside the curve. She couldn't read it. She knew what it said.

The salt-sheds were open behind her. The women were at the troughs already, in the grey, with the lamps lit. She could hear the knives. One, two, three.

She did what her father did. She'd watched him do it every morning since the snow. She stood with her weight forward and the hook in her right hand and the coil of line in her left and looked at the water, and then, as she let it go, out over the edge, in a long low arc that went further than she'd meant it to and dropped into the green with hardly a splash, she said the name.

"*Tobyn*."

Not loud. The way he said it. To the water. The way you'd say a name to tell someone you were there.

Behind her, in the salt-shed, a basket hit the floor.

She turned round.

Ruth was standing at the end of the nearest trough with her knife in her hand and a basket of gutted herring all over the stones at her feet, silver, slithering, the gulls on the roof already screaming about it. All the women at the troughs had stopped. All of them. Twenty women with knives in their hands, standing very still in the lamplight, looking at Ruth, and not at Alea at all.

Ruth was looking at Alea.

Alea didn't move. She stood at the end of the quay with the line going out of her hand into the green water and her ears flat and thought, *I've done it wrong. I said it in front of her. I said a dead man's name to his wife on his own quay*.

Ruth bent down. Slowly. She put her knife on the edge of the trough, and knelt on the wet stones, and began to pick up the herring. One at a time. Back into the basket. The other women didn't help. Alea understood, watching, that they weren't supposed to; that this was a thing Ruth was doing, and you didn't help somebody do a thing like that, any more than you'd help somebody stand up off the sand at the end of a final.

When the basket was full again, Ruth stood up. She picked it up, and held it against her hip, and looked out past Alea, past the end of the quay, past the harbour wall, at the bar, where the white water broke on the sand in the grey a mile out, the way it had broken on a night in a squall, three winters back.

"Tobyn," said Ruth.

Quietly. To the sea.

Then she put the basket back on the end of the trough, and picked up her knife, and went on. One, two, three. And the other women went on, all of them, at once, as if nothing had happened, and the gulls came down off the roof for the herring that had got away under the trough, and Alea stood at the end of the quay with the line in her hand and her face wet and didn't know when it had got wet.

Sorrel was sitting on the bollard by the slip. She'd been there the whole time. She had her hooks across her knees and her face in her hands.

The line went tight.

***

It was an eel.

Alea had never seen one like it. There were eels in the lake, thin brown wriggling things you got on a night line by accident and threw back because they bit. This came up out of the green water at the end of the harbour wall like something out of one of her father's stories: as thick as her arm, and grey, and longer than she was, much longer, with a head like a dog's and a mouth full of small white teeth, and it did not want to come up. It came up anyway, because she didn't let go, and because she'd been standing on a moving deck for a week with her weight forward and her knees giving, and she gave with it, and let it run, and let it think it had won, and then went *with* it the way she'd gone with a pike once on a jetty at home, until it was on the stones at her feet and three of the women from the troughs were standing round it with gutting knives and one of them was laughing.

"Conger," said Abner, from his bollard. He hadn't moved. "Biggest I've seen off the wall in ten year." He looked at the hook in its mouth. "That hook should've opened. Hooks off the market do. That one didn't."

"Gabe made it," said Alea. She was out of breath. Her arms were shaking. "A friend. A smith. It says *stand* inside."

"Does it," said Abner. "Well. It did."

Sorrel had come off her bollard. She stood looking down at the conger, and then at Alea, and then at her mother, at the trough, gutting herring and not looking at any of them, and her grin came back. Slowly. Like a gap in a fence.

"You said his name," said Sorrel, "and you caught the biggest conger off the wall in ten years, in front of my mam, on your first cast, with a hook that says *stand*."

"I didn't mean to do any of it."

"I know," said Sorrel. "That's how you do everything. You did it at the Lists." She put her hooks over her shoulder. "Tomorrow. Past the bar. In NEXT YEAR. You're ready."

***

She wrote to her father that night, on the king's paper.

One sheet. At the table in Ruth's kitchen, in the house on the hill with its windows to the sea, by the light of a lamp that smelled of fish oil, with Sorrel asleep across the room on a pallet with her hooks beside her and Ruth at the hearth mending a smock and not watching, very carefully.

She'd thought about it all the way down the river. What you put on a king's paper. *For when you've got something to say*. He'd used one sheet in two years and he'd sent it to a king with one word on it. She'd thought she'd have a great deal to say, on a sheet that size, and when she sat down with the pen she found she didn't.

She wrote what had happened. Short. The way he would. The name of the town at the top, and then the sea, and the first cast, and Gabe's hook, and her heels, and Rachael's horizon: five lines, and not half the sheet.

She stopped there for a long time. The lamp ticked. Ruth's needle went in and out.

She'd said, on the jetty, in the spring, *I'm never going to ask*. She'd meant it. She meant it now. It wasn't hers to ask. And she'd stood on the quay below the mill and watched him lift his empty hand, with the rod left behind on the end of the jetty, and she'd known exactly what that was, because she'd been reading the rest of him all her life: he'd left it there so that if he came, it wouldn't be because he was carrying it. So it'd be his feet that decided. Not his hands.

She didn't know if he'd come. She wasn't going to ask. But she found, sitting there, that she couldn't put the pen down without saying that she wasn't. That it would be a lie to stop at *don't tell her*, as if that were all; that there was one more true thing, and it was that.

*I'm not asking.*

*A.*

She folded it in four. She tied it in oilcloth with the fisherman's knot, the one she'd learned at the table in the spring, tight, the way you'd tie it on a boat.

"That's short," said Ruth. From the hearth. Not looking.

"He doesn't like long ones."

"No," said Ruth. "They don't." She bit off a thread. "Tobyn wrote me one letter in twenty years. From Kingsferry, when he was selling the catch and got stuck a week in a storm. It said *weather bad, fish sold, home Thursday*." She was quiet. "I've still got it."

Alea didn't say anything.

"Thursday," said Ruth. "He always came home on a Thursday." She put the smock down. "He went over on a Thursday. Did she tell you that? Sorrel. In a letter."

"No."

"No," said Ruth. "She wouldn't. She doesn't know I know. I've never said." She looked at the lamp. "You said his name off my quay this morning and I dropped the herring. I've not said it since. You said it like you were saying good morning to him. Like he might answer." She picked the smock up again. "That's what your father does, Sorrel says. Every morning. Off a jetty, on a lake, half the kingdom off. For a man he never met."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Alea thought about it. She thought about her father on the end of the jetty at the first cast, every morning since the snow, saying a dead fisherman's name to a lake. *Somebody should*. She thought about the knoll. The stone. Her mother's name in the book of the dead, on a line on its own, *A healer. Diwa*, and under it, in Aldous's hand, *Sung*.

"Because somebody should," said Alea. "That's what he said. Somebody should say it. Every day. So it doesn't get crossed out."

Ruth didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said, "Tobyn," to the lamp. Quietly. As if she were trying it again. As if she wanted to see if it was still there.

It was.

***

They went out past the bar the next morning, and every morning after, for six weeks.

NEXT YEAR in the March swell. Alea had thought the *Gannet* was the sea. The *Gannet* had been a cart on a road. NEXT YEAR was a horse with nobody on it. She went up the long grey slopes of the swell past the bar as if she meant to climb all the way to the sky and then down the backs of them as if she meant to go to the bottom, and in between she went sideways, and round, and once, on the second morning, very nearly over, and Sorrel stood in the middle of her the whole time with a hook in each hand and her bare feet set wide on the boards, holding nothing.

That was the new thing. Sorrel had written *I've got a new thing; I'm not telling you; you'll see*, and Alea had seen it on the first morning out. Sorrel hauled the pots standing up. With both hands on the hooks. In the swell. With nothing to hold on to and no hand free to hold on with, so that the only thing keeping her on her feet in a boat going up and down and round like a cork in a mill-race was her feet, and her knees, and whatever it was Sorrel had in her that Alea had in her too, under the stillness, that came up from the boards through the soles of her feet when the boat moved and told her where to put her weight before she'd decided to.

"I don't hold the boat," said Sorrel. On the first morning, with a pot coming up out of the green on the end of her left hook and her right hook already reaching for the next line and NEXT YEAR going up the side of a swell like a goat up a wall. "The boat holds me. If I hold it, it throws me. If I let it hold me, I can do anything." She grinned. "That's the thing. That's all it is. It took me all winter."

"That's going *with*," said Alea.

"I know," said Sorrel. "I got it off you. On the sand. In the final. You let me pull you, and then you went *with* the pull, and I went over backwards. I thought about it all winter. If you can go *with* a pull, you can go with a boat." She hauled the pot in over the gunwale, one-handed, on the hook, and tipped it. Three crabs and a lobster, blue-black and furious. "And if you can go with a boat, you can go with anything. Even a girl with a sword the size of a door who goes sideways."

Alea looked at her.

"You're going to use it in the final."

"If I get there," said Sorrel. "Lucan says nobody can put anybody opposite anybody. So I've got to get there." She threw the lobster back, because it was a hen with eggs, and watched it go down into the green. "I'll get there."

***

They fought on the boat.

Not every day. Sorrel wouldn't let her every day; she said you could only learn a thing on a boat so fast before the sea taught you a worse one. But two mornings in three, out past the bar, when the pots were hauled and the swell was long and slow, they'd stand up in NEXT YEAR, one at each end, Alea with Astra and Sorrel with her hooks, and go at each other.

It was the stupidest thing Alea had ever done. She knew it the first time. You couldn't do forms on a boat; there wasn't room. You couldn't go sideways on a boat; sideways was the sea. You couldn't do anything you'd learned on a shingle or a sand or a gravel bar, because all of them held still under you and waited, and the boat didn't. The boat had its own ideas. It went up when you'd decided to go down. It went round when you'd set your feet. And Sorrel, at the other end, standing with her bare feet wide and holding nothing, just let it, and came at you up the slope of a swell with the boat's own weight behind her, and hooked Astra's broad back and pulled, and the boat pulled too, and you went over the gunwale up to your waist in the sea.

She went over four times the first week. Three the second. Sorrel hauled her back in by her belt every time with a hook, laughing so hard she nearly went over herself.

The third week she didn't go over.

She didn't know what she'd done differently. She thought about it all that night, in Ruth's kitchen, with her legs still going up and down under her. She hadn't done anything. That was it. She'd stopped doing anything. She'd stopped setting her feet, and stopped deciding where her weight should go, and stood in the stern of NEXT YEAR with Astra low and her knees loose and let the boat hold her, the way Sorrel did; and when Sorrel came up the swell with the hook, she hadn't gone *with* the hook. She'd gone with the boat. And the boat had gone sideways, on its own, the way boats do, and she'd gone with it, and Sorrel's hook had closed on the place where Astra had been, and Astra had been a hand's width further round, and resting, very lightly, flat, against Sorrel's ribs.

Sorrel had looked down at it for a long time. Then up at Alea.

"Oh, *no*," said Sorrel. Delighted. "Oh no. You've got it. You've got my thing. That's *mine*."

"It's the sea's," said Alea.

"It's *mine*," said Sorrel. "I'm going to have to get a new thing now. I've only got four weeks." And then she put both hooks down in the bottom of the boat, which she never did, and sat down on the thwart, and laughed until she cried, and Alea sat down opposite her with Astra across her knees and laughed too, and NEXT YEAR went up and down on the long swell past the Gullhaven bar with the two of them in it like a cork, and the gulls came round to see what was wrong with them.

Her bar moved that night. She looked at it in Ruth's kitchen, by the fish-oil lamp. Forty-four. It had been forty-one when she left the lake.

***

Sorrel talked to her dad every morning, off the bar.

She did it the way she'd said she did it, in the letter, in the winter. Out loud. In the boat. Once the pots were hauled and the swell was slow and the sun was just up, she'd sit down on the thwart with her back to the shore and her face to the open sea, past the line of white water where the bar was, and talk. Not loudly. Not to Alea. Just talking, the way you'd talk to somebody in the next room, about the pots and the weather and the lobster with eggs and the lake girl who'd stolen her thing.

On the fourth week, Alea did it too.

She hadn't meant to. She'd been sitting in the bow while Sorrel talked, looking at the water, and found she'd started. Quietly. To the water on the other side of the boat from where Sorrel was talking. The way she talked to the stone on the knoll, at the end of the summer, under a white cloth. *Mum*.

She told her about the sea. That it didn't care, and that Sorrel had been right, and that you could love it anyway, or you could start to. She told her about the eel, and the hook that said *stand*, and Ruth dropping the herring. She told her about the boat, and going over the side, and not going over. She told her that her weight went back on her heels when the ground stopped moving, and that she was fixing it, and that she thought she'd fixed it, here, on a thing that never stopped moving, by forgetting the ground was supposed to be still at all.

She didn't tell her about the jetty. About the rod, left on the last post. About a man on a quay with his empty hand up. She didn't need to. Her mother would know. Her mother had always known what he was going to do about a minute before he did it, he said; it had annoyed him for ten years.

When she'd finished, Sorrel had stopped talking too. She was looking at Alea from the stern with an expression Alea had never seen on her face.

"Who was that?" said Sorrel.

"My mum."

"I told her about you," said Sorrel. "In the winter. Off this bar. I said you were going to lose."

"I know. You wrote."

"Did you tell my dad about me?"

Alea thought about it. Then she turned round, on the thwart, to the open sea, past the bar, where the white water was, where a fisherman had gone over the side in a squall three winters back, on a Thursday, hauling.

"Tobyn," said Alea. "Your girl's going to lose. This year. To me. I thought you should know." She paused. "So you can tell her it's coming."

Behind her there was a silence. Then a sound like a gull being sat on.

"*That's my line*," said Sorrel.

"I know," said Alea. "I got it off you."

***

They walked to Valcrest along the coast road at the end of the sixth week, the two of them, with the hooks and the sword and a bag of Ruth's oatcakes and a smaller jar of Odette's bramble, which Ruth had decanted out of the big one into a little one that had been Tobyn's, for his salt, and given to Alea at the door without a word.

Ruth came down to the quay to see them go. She stood at the end of the nearest trough in her apron with her raw hands folded, the way she'd stood every morning, and when they got to the top of the hard by the slip, where NEXT YEAR sat with her bow on the shingle and her stern in the water and her name in big uneven white letters across the back, Sorrel stopped.

She looked at the boat for a long time.

Then she went down to it, barefoot, on the shingle, and stood at the stern, and put her hand flat on the letters. On the *N*. The one she'd gone over twice, at sixteen, angry and crying, because the paint was thin.

"Midsummer," said Sorrel. "Abner says I've got to paint it out at midsummer."

"Are you going to?"

"No," said Sorrel. "But I'll tell you what." She took her hand off the stern. "If I win. If I get there, and I win. I'll paint it out myself. And I'll paint *THIS YEAR* on her instead. In gold. Like Lucan's boxes." She grinned. "And if I lose, it stays."

"Then it stays," said Alea.

"We'll see," said Sorrel. And came back up the shingle, and picked up her hooks, and didn't look back, the way her father had probably never looked back either, going out past the bar at dawn on a Thursday with the tide.

Ruth did. Alea did. They looked at each other across the quay, the fisherman's widow at the trough and the healer's daughter on the hard, over a little boat with a name on her stern that wasn't a date.

"Tobyn," said Ruth. Not loud. To the boat. The way you'd say good morning.

Then she went back to the herring.

And Alea went up the hill after Sorrel, with Astra on her back and the conger hook in her pocket that said *stand*, and her weight forward on the cobbles, which didn't move; and found, at the top, where the coast road went over the headland and the town went out of sight, that she didn't sit back on her heels. Not even for a step. She'd forgotten how.

She didn't look north either. Not once. All the way along the coast to Valcrest, five days, with Sorrel talking and the sea on their left.

She didn't know that a few days north of her, on the road down from the lake, there was a man walking at the back of a cart with two buckets on a yoke.
