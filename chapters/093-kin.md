Yan had a reason for the tenth tier, and he'd never told anyone what it was.

Not Salty. Not Wendy. Not Maigo, who'd have told everyone in capitals, out of love. Suzu knew; but nobody had ever had to tell Suzu anything. He'd kept it the way he kept everything that mattered: on his own, quietly, turned over and over until it had a shape. He'd carried it for a year and a half. Through the snow on the Redmarch and the cold one crossing the red, and the sword, and the stone.

It was very simple. It was his mother.

He'd vanished in the night, from a house that was hard to live in, and left her in it. That was all. He didn't think about the rest of it, the house and the people in it. He thought about her, standing in a kitchen, waiting. For a year and a half he'd thought she'd been standing there for a year and a half.

And then, in the summer, Thea had posted the clock. *At home, it's been a month.*

He'd written *She's only been looking for a month* in the channel before he could stop himself, and then sat on the wall at Cinderhold in the dark with Suzu round his neck and cried for an hour, quietly, the way he did everything.

It was six weeks now. He checked every morning. Six weeks of a woman in a kitchen, waiting. Not a year and a half. Six weeks.

Only a Sun mage could send anyone home. In theory. *No one we can confirm.*

He didn't mind that nobody could confirm it. He'd been carrying things nobody could confirm all his life.

***

The snow came to the Redmarch and stayed.

It was strange, seeing it white. The first winter it had lasted a quarter of an hour, the first frost, before the red came back through. This winter it stayed. It came down on the night Wendy shot her arrow at a star and kept coming, on and off, for weeks, until the whole plain from the walls of Cinderhold to the mountains of Kharvos was white and flat and silent, with the markers standing up out of it in their long rows like the masts of sunken ships.

The red was still there. Underneath. Maigo said he could feel it through the snow, through his bare root-toed feet, every morning on the wall. Hurt, the way it had always been. But quieter.

No scouts came. Not since the anniversary. Not one, in three months. The horns hung over the east gate with snow on them and nothing on the white plain below to look up at them.

"It's the quietest I've ever felt it," said Maigo, one morning on the wall. "The ground. Since we came." He had his sunglasses pushed up on his antlers and his breath was going up white. "It's like it's asleep. Like the tree."

"It's winter," said Wendy.

"It's not just winter." Maigo put his bare foot flat on the stone of the parapet. "It's like somebody put a hand on it."

The snow made Suzu hungry. Cinderhold killed most of its beasts in the autumn and salted them down for the winter, and after the first month there was nothing left in the kitchen yard to die but the cook's three laying hens, which the cook had named, and which Yan would sooner have eaten himself. So Wendy went out with him in the mornings, across the white, past the first row of markers, with her bow. She put one arrow into a hare at sixty paces, every time, clean, and stood back with her hood up while he knelt in the snow beside it and Suzu took the little blue light before it thinned.

"Don't thank me," Wendy said, the first time, before he could. "I'm not doing it for you."

She wasn't. He could see her soul. It leaned, a little, every morning, toward the white snake round his neck.

***

Garrow stayed the winter.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to set the stone and turn round and go home, a month back west behind his oxen, and be in Wrenhaven by the first snow. The first snow had come the night they set the stone. The second had come three days later and closed the road over the ridge. By the end of the month, the passes between Cinderhold and the west were shut until the thaw, and Garrow the smith from the north was snowed in at the edge of the Redmarch with four militiamen, two oxen and the king's father's hammer.

He didn't seem to mind. He'd gone to the fort's forge the first morning, and stood in the door, and the fort's smith, a grey-bearded man called Oakes who'd been at Cinderhold since before the war, had looked at his apron and his hands and the hammer in his belt and said *you'll want the second anvil*. And that had been that.

He worked the forge all winter. Mending. Horseshoes for the patrols, hinges for the gates, the endless small iron of a fort. And in the afternoons, when the work was done, he taught Salty.

Yan hadn't seen that coming. Neither, he suspected, had Salty. But it made a kind of sense: the strongest man in Cinderhold and the only smith who'd ever worked the bellows for a king. Salty stood at the second anvil with his sleeves rolled up and his big square hands round a hammer, and Garrow stood beside him and said *slower*, and *wait for it*, and *the heat stops being angry*, the way somebody had said it to him once on a shingle shore.

"The king said that," Garrow told them, at supper, the first week. "And his father said it to him. And now I'm saying it to you." He looked at Salty across the table. "That's how it goes, with smiths. Somebody says it to you, and then you say it to somebody else."

"Like lessons," said Maigo. "Like *wait for the fish*."

"Like *wait for the fish*," said Garrow, and went red, because he hadn't meant to say that out loud, and they all pretended not to notice.

In the third week of the snow, Yan went down to the forge in the afternoon to fetch Salty for supper, and found him and Garrow bent together over a slate with chalk lines on it. They both stopped talking.

Salty wiped the slate with his sleeve. "Horseshoes," he said.

"Okay," said Yan.

He let him have it. But he could see Salty's soul, the way he could see everyone's now, and it was leaning hard, the way it did when Salty had picked something up and meant to carry it whether it could be carried or not. And it was leaning at him.

Wendy told him a fortnight later, on the wall, when Salty was asleep. It wasn't horseshoes. It was a long straight line, and a long curved one, and the place where they met.

"Garrow says it's a year's work," she said, "for a man who's been smithing three months. Salty says he's got a year." She looked at Yan sideways. "Act surprised. I mean it. He'll know if you don't."

Yan stood on the wall for a long time after she'd gone down, with his hands not quite steady on the cold stone and Suzu warm round his neck, and found he couldn't think of a single thing to say. Not even to himself.

***

Mashi got to five heartbeats in the second week of the snow.

He came down off the wall at dawn with the handkerchief in his hand and no blood on it, which was the first time in a year, and sat down at breakfast and didn't say anything until Maigo noticed.

"*Five*," said Maigo. "*Five?*"

"Five," said Mashi.

"No nosebleed?"

"No nosebleed." Mashi looked at his porridge. "And the third line stayed. The one with no name. For all five." He looked up. "It's still grey. It's still locked. But it's longer, every time. Like it's waiting for something."

"For what?" said Salty.

Mashi didn't answer for a moment. He'd been quiet since the summer, since the anniversary and the fight and the man who'd reeled in three hunters without looking round. Quieter than usual. Watching. The way he'd watched games on Earth, other people's, replays, until he could play them himself with his eyes shut.

"I don't know," he said. "Something I haven't seen yet."

***

Yan had been at ninety-six percent for five weeks.

Stuck. He knew what it was, by now. Everybody did. Thea had written it to all of them, the first winter: *you have to write. One small line, in its language, not yours.* And Chris had made a list, in the summer, of every line that had worked: *they're all asking something to do what it already wants to.* Maigo's *Stay*. Wendy's arrow, that was a line without words. Salty's table, that was a line made of breaking.

He didn't know what his line was. He'd tried a dozen. *See.* *Hold.* *Keep.* He kept threads of souls, a little, the way he'd kept the cold one for seven nights. He'd tried writing that. Nothing. He saw souls, their grain, the way they leaned. He'd tried writing that. Nothing.

Ninety-six. For five weeks.

He went out to the grave on the last day of the fifth week.

***

He didn't go alone. Wendy came. She always came now, when he went out on the red. She'd never said why. She just appeared at the postern gate with her bow and her silent headphones round her neck, and fell into step beside him, and didn't say anything, and walked.

It was an hour, across the snow. The markers going by on either side, white-capped. The old riverbed under the snow, and the gullies, smooth and soft now, and then the open plain, white and flat to the mountains. Nothing moving. No birds. No tracks but theirs.

The grave came up out of the white.

The low rise, and the dead trees on the far side of it, grey against the grey sky. The cairn under its snow. The young tree, bare and asleep, its slim branches holding snow along their tops like white fur. And at the head of the cairn, standing up out of the snow, black, the stone.

Snow on the rounded top of it. Snow in the letters. *TO DIWA, MY LOVE*, white on black, the way it had been the night they set it, so that anyone could have read it from across the rise. And at its foot, under the snow, a ring of small humps where the children's stones were, inside the ring of green that hadn't died.

Yan stood at the edge of the ring and looked at it for a long time.

Wendy stood beside him. She didn't say anything.

***

He knelt down in the snow, in the end. Inside the ring. By the stone.

He didn't know why. He wasn't going to try anything. He'd stopped trying a week ago; trying wasn't working. He just knelt there, the way he'd knelt by this cairn the first time, in the first month, when Aldous brought them out across the red and Yan had felt the cold under it and not told anyone. He put his gloved hand flat on the snow at the foot of the stone. Suzu unwound from his neck and lay along his arm.

He felt the cold. Still there, under her. Still asleep.

He felt the warm thing Maigo had told him about. The hand, laid on the ground a long time ago, holding on. And under it now, around it, all the small warm things Maigo said came with the children's stones. A town's worth of hands.

And he felt the grain.

Not here. Not in the ground. In himself. In Wendy, standing at the edge of the ring behind him. In Suzu, along his arm. The grain he'd spent a year learning, that he knew the way you know your own handwriting. The grain of a soul that had grown somewhere else first, under a different sun. *Earth.* The grain of home.

He'd always felt it in the five of them. In himself. In the man who'd walked across the red with a rod.

He'd never once reached for it.

***

He reached for it now.

He didn't decide to. It was the stone, maybe, with its letters in English. Or the date in the corner of his window that morning, six weeks, a woman in a kitchen. Or just that he was very tired of being stuck. He knelt in the snow with his hand on the ground and reached, past the cold, past the warm, past Wendy and Suzu and himself, for the grain. Not for a soul. For the *grain*. For the thing that all of them had in common. The thing that made them what they were here: people who'd come from somewhere else, and hadn't quite taken root.

He found the edge.

It was just there, at the bottom of the reaching, where Thea had said it would be. He didn't look for it. He felt it the way you feel a step in the dark with your foot.

He wrote one word. It was the only one he had. It was the one he'd been carrying for a year and a half, on his own, about a kitchen.

*Home.*

***

The world opened.

That was the only way he'd ever be able to say it. Like a door. Like a window, at night, in a dark room, and outside it, all at once, the stars.

He felt them.

Not the five of them, here. Not just. *All* of them. Across the whole kingdom. Thirty-five small warm lights, scattered across the dark the way you'd scatter seed, every one of them with the same grain. He couldn't see them. He didn't need to. He knew where every single one was, the way you know where your own hands are with your eyes shut.

Wendy, behind him. Salty and Maigo and Mashi, an hour away, inside the walls of Cinderhold, three lights close together. That was four.

And then the others. Out there. A long way. In every direction.

West, very far, five together, near something large and cold and dark that he didn't let himself reach toward. *Thornwall.* Hyvred, Bee, Tiger, Drew, Kei. He knew which was which. He didn't know how. He just did.

South-west, two clusters of five, a long way apart along a line that must have been the river. *Kingsferry. Duskhollow.* Gabe's, the biggest of those, steady as a wall; Deston's, beside it, reaching out in every direction like the roots of a tree.

South, five, together, in a place that felt like a crowd even when it was empty. *Valcrest.* One of them cold. One of them hot. One of them keeping a beat.

West, nearer, in the middle of everything, five more. *The capital.* One of them warm in a way that made you want to sit nearer.

North-west, five, close together. Very close. Closer than any of the others. As if they were sitting in one room.

That was thirty-five.

***

And there was one more.

North-west.

In the same direction as the five, so close to them that he couldn't tell, at first, that it wasn't one of them. And then he could. Because it wasn't a small warm light. It was the sea.

He'd felt it three times. Once on the night the cold one went out, too big to see. Once worn into the grip of a black sword in a chapel. Once on the red, coming in across the snow like a tide, pinning fifty demons where they stood.

He felt it now from three hundred miles away, through a word he'd written in the snow by a grave. The same grain as all of them. *Home.* Rooted all the way down. Older than its body. And so big that the thirty-five small lights scattered across the kingdom looked, beside it, like candles round a lake.

It was warm.

That was what made Yan put his other hand down in the snow, to hold himself up. He'd felt it three times, and every time it had been still, and deep, and grieving, worn smooth like a stone step. It was still all of those things.

It was warm now. The way a hearth is warm when somebody's sitting at it. When there are people in the room.

Five people, he thought. Very close. As if they were sitting in one room.

***

He opened his window, when he could.

The fourteenth.

*Kin.* He looked at it for a long time. He thought about the word. The world had looked at him kneeling in the snow, reaching for the thing they all had in common, and called it that. Not *soul*. Not *sight*. *Kin*.

He thought it was the best word anybody had ever given him.

"Yan," said Wendy.

He turned round. She was standing at the edge of the ring, in the snow, with her bow in her hand and her mismatched eyes very wide. She hadn't moved. She'd seen his face.

"Spark," he said. His deep voice wasn't quite steady. "*Kin*."

"What does it do?"

Yan looked at her. At the light in her that had the same grain as his. At the thirty-four others, out there in the dark, scattered across the kingdom. At the one more.

"I can feel all of us," he said. "All thirty-five. Wherever they are. Direction, not distance. Like candles." He swallowed. "And one more."

Wendy didn't ask who. She didn't need to.

"Where?" she said.

Yan didn't answer straight away.

"North-west," he said at last.

***

Wendy looked at him for a long moment, in the snow, by the stone.

She knew what was north-west. They all did. Every one of the thirty-five knew where every group was posted; they'd been writing letters to each other for a year and a half. Thornwall west. Valcrest south. Kingsferry and Duskhollow down the river. The capital in the middle.

And north-west of Cinderhold, north of the capital, at the edge of the lake country: Wrenhaven. Where Group 1 was.

"How close to them?" said Wendy quietly.

Yan looked at the five small lights in the north-west, close together, as if they were sitting in one room. And the sea beside them, warm.

"I can't tell distance," he said. "Only direction." He was quiet. "Same direction."

Wendy didn't say anything for a long time.

"Don't," she said at last.

He looked at her.

"Don't do that sum," said Wendy. "Not out loud. Not to the others. Not in the channel." She held his eyes. "He gave us *it's a long way, and it's cold, and the fish are good*. That's what he gave us. That's what's ours." She looked at the stone, with the snow in its letters. "If he wanted us to know the rest, he'd have told Garrow to tell us."

Yan looked at her. Wendy, who didn't believe in much. Who'd believed in Salty, and then in a man with a fishing rod, and nothing else.

"You're right," he said.

"I'm always right," said Wendy. "Nobody ever notices because I'm right about bad things."

***

He told the others that night. In the barracks, the five of them, with the door shut and Garrow snoring through the wall.

He told them about *Kin*. He told them about thirty-five lights. He told them *direction, not distance*. And he told them about one more.

He didn't say north-west. Wendy, sitting on the end of Salty's bunk, didn't either.

Maigo went very still. Then he put his sunglasses down over his eyes, and said, very quietly for Maigo, "Is it warm?"

Yan looked at him.

"His," said Maigo. "Is it warm? The sea. You said it was cold, before. On the red. Grieving." He swallowed. "Is it still?"

"It's warm," said Yan.

Maigo didn't say anything else. He just nodded, slowly, behind his sunglasses. And then got up and went out to stand on the wall for a while, barefoot, in the snow. And nobody went after him, because they all knew he needed to.

***

They wrote the letter the next week.

Garrow asked them to. He'd been thinking about it, he said, at the anvil. He'd be going home in the spring, when the passes opened. Back west over the ridge with his oxen and his four militiamen, a month on the road, to a town in the north that he wasn't supposed to name. And he'd be seeing the fisherman, because everybody in that town saw the fisherman, every day, at the market, buying eggs.

"If you wanted to send something," said Garrow, very red, at the supper table. "I'd carry it. Into one pair of hands. Nobody else's." He looked at his plate. "He'd want to hear from you. I think. He talked about you, at the forge. The five of you. Like he knew you."

Nobody said anything.

"I'll carry it," said Garrow again. "That's all."

***

They wrote it together. It took a whole evening, in the barracks, with one candle and Cursiffe's blue ink, which Yan had been hoarding since the summer. Not in English. Not in the channel. On paper, in a letter, the old way, the way the thirty-five had written to each other for a year before there was a channel.

They argued about every line. The way they'd argued about callouts on Earth.

In the end it was short. Yan wrote it. He always did. One line each.

*From Salty: Eleven. You know what I mean. When I see you, you owe me eleven.*

*From Maigo: The tree is asleep. It's fine. Trees do that. It'll wake up in the spring. So will the flowers. They stayed green all winter. That's new.*

*From Wendy: Thank you for the stone. I believe it now.*

*From Mashi: Five heartbeats. The third line's still grey. I think I'm waiting to see something. I think you know what.*

And at the bottom, Yan's. He'd thought about it for a week. In the end it was two lines.

*From Yan: The cold under her is still asleep. It hasn't woken since you sat there. Something's holding it.*

*And: thirty-six. I counted.*

***

He didn't sign it. None of them did. They didn't need to.

At the very bottom, after a long argument that Maigo won, by being louder, they added one more line, in Maigo's big loopy hand, because none of the rest of them could think how to say it.

*Group 3 will be in Valcrest at midsummer for the tournament. If you're anywhere near. No pressure. Trust.*

Wendy looked at it for a long time.

"You can't put *trust* in it," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because it's *his*."

"I know it's his," said Maigo. "That's why I put it."

Wendy opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked at the line, and then at Maigo, and then, very slightly, smiled.

"Fine," she said.

***

Garrow took it.

He folded it very carefully, along the lines, and sealed it with a drop of wax from the candle, and Mashi pressed one of Cursiffe's blue strips across the fold, the way the real letters were sealed now. Garrow put it in the inside pocket of his coat, against his chest, next to the king's father's hammer.

"Into one pair of hands," he said.

"Nobody else's," said Yan.

"Nobody else's."

***

Yan went up on the wall that night, alone, with Suzu round his neck. He stood at the parapet and looked north-west, over the white plain, at nothing. At the dark. At the snow.

He opened the edge. Just a little. *Home.*

Thirty-five small lights, scattered across the kingdom like seed. Thirty-four, out there, and Wendy's, close, inside the walls, on the end of Salty's bunk.

And north-west, the sea. Warm.

He didn't know where. He didn't let himself do the sum. He just stood there in the cold and felt it, the way you'd stand at a window on a winter night and feel the warmth of a house across the street with its lamps lit, and people moving in it, and know you weren't one of them yet, and that it didn't matter. That it was there.

Six weeks, he thought. A woman in a kitchen.

*Thirty-six.*

He'd been carrying things on his own all his life. Suzu tightened round his neck when he thought it, once, the way she always did, because it hadn't been true since the hall.

He thought, standing on the wall with the sea warm in the north-west and thirty-four candles round it, that he might not have to for very much longer.
