Milli had asked one question in the summoning hall, and nobody had ever answered it properly.

*You said we'd hear how to go home. So what's the catch?*

The Advisor had answered it, technically. A Sun-tier mage. The only way. Nobody alive had reached it; nobody, for certain, ever had. It would take years. It might take decades. Everyone else in the hall had heard that and done the thing people do when the news is bad and the door is shut: decided to hope anyway. Hana had decided to hope. Aleaka. Jared. Even Cursiffe, in his calm way. Milli had watched them all do it, one after another, and felt like the only person in the room who'd noticed that *nobody, for certain, ever had* was not an answer. It was the absence of one.

He wasn't a pessimist. Hana said he was, sometimes, when she was cross with him, but he wasn't. He just liked to know things. Not hope them. *Know* them. Back home he'd been the one who read the terms and conditions, who checked the reviews, who wanted to see the receipt. He wasn't going to walk down a road for ten years toward a door somebody had once heard a rumour about.

He wanted proof. That was all. Proof the door was there.

He'd been looking for it for a year. In the archive, with Oswin. In the court, with Hana. In Kestra's yard, every morning, *again, again, again*, until he'd learned to wait, because waiting was the only thing that seemed to make him stronger and he needed to be strong to be allowed in the same room as the king, and the king might know.

A year, and nothing. No proof. Not of the door. Not of anything.

And then the sword.

***

The winch party came on a grey morning at the start of summer.

Six soldiers in the king's colours, with a cart, and on the cart a great timber frame with ropes and pulleys and an iron hook as big as a man's arm. They'd brought the sword from the Redmarch to the capital with that winch a year ago. Now they were taking it back. East, to Cinderhold, by road, a month's slow journey; and then, in the last week of summer, on the anniversary of the battle, the summoned of Group 3 would carry it the rest of the way across the red by hand, to the grave it had been taken from.

Oswin had told them all of this at breakfast, with his eyes very bright. He'd been waiting a year for the sword to move. So had Cursiffe, though he hadn't said so.

"The king has given leave," said Kestra, from the door, without preamble. "Before they lift it. The summoned scholar may look." She glanced at Cursiffe. "Close."

The table went quiet.

"Close?" said Cursiffe.

"Close." Kestra's face didn't move. "As close as you like. For as long as it takes them to rig the winch." She paused. "Oswin asked. Last week. Through me. The king said yes." Another pause. "He said it's leaving anyway. And that he'd like to know what it says, if anyone in this kingdom can tell him."

Oswin had gone pink. Cursiffe had gone very still.

Milli looked from one to the other. He knew what this was about; they all did. The carving. The scratched letters near the hilt, in a script nobody in this world could read. Cursiffe had tried to look at it once, in the winter, from the edge of the circle, while trying and failing to draw the summoning for Thea, and had come back to the prince's wing pale and quiet, and said only: *It wants to be read. I could feel it. But I couldn't get close enough.*

Now he could.

"I'm coming," said Milli.

Hana looked at him across the table. She didn't say anything. She just reached over and put her hand on his, briefly, the way she did when she knew what he was thinking.

"So am I," she said.

In the end they all went.

***

The summoning hall was cold, the way it always was. Milli had only been back twice since the first day. He didn't like it. He didn't like the painted ceiling, with its gold suns, or the high windows with the light coming down in long bars, or the circle cut into the floor, grey and dead now, rings inside rings, spokes running out from the centre to the edges like the spokes of a wheel.

He didn't like it because he'd woken up on it. He remembered the cold of the stone under his cheek. He remembered opening his eyes and seeing Hana a few feet away, with cat ears in her lavender hair and a look on her face of absolute, perfect calm, the calm she used when she was more frightened than she'd ever been in her life.

He remembered reaching for her hand. And saying the only thing he could think of.

*Honey.*

And at the centre of the circle, where it had stood for a year, the sword.

It was enormous. He'd forgotten. A buster sword, Jared had called it, the first day, in a whisper: a thing out of a game, too big to be real. A slab of black iron taller than Milli and as broad as his chest, its edge notched in a dozen places, its grip wrapped in old leather gone dark with handling. It stood straight up out of the stone floor of the hall, sunk into it a hand's depth, as if it had been driven there by a giant. Nobody had ever been able to move it. Not without six men and a winch.

The soldiers were already setting up the frame round it, with a lot of shouting and creaking. Kestra went and stood by them with her arms folded. Oswin hovered at the edge of the circle, holding his notebook to his chest like a shield.

And Cursiffe walked into the circle.

Milli watched him do it. He watched Cursiffe stop, two steps in, and sway, very slightly, as if he'd walked into a strong wind. He watched him put out one hand, as if to steady himself on nothing, and then take it back, and keep walking. Slowly. Carefully. Across the dead grey rings and spokes, toward the sword at the centre.

"Is he all right?" said Aleaka, very quietly, beside Milli.

"I think it's the circle," said Hana. "Thea said. It pulls at you."

Cursiffe reached the sword. He stood in front of it for a long moment, looking up at it. Then he knelt down on the stone at its foot, where the blade went into the floor, and leaned in close to the place just above the guard where the iron was scratched.

He didn't move for a long time.

***

"Can he read it?" said Oswin, in an agonised whisper, from the edge of the circle. "Can he—is it—"

"Give him a minute," said Jared.

Milli was watching Cursiffe's face. A year of living in the same rooms, and he knew it by now. It was a calm face, usually. A face that tipped its head back a little and half-smiled, as if it knew something you didn't.

It wasn't calm now.

Cursiffe was kneeling at the foot of the sword with his glasses pushed up onto his forehead, so close to the iron his nose nearly touched it, and his lips were moving. Very slightly. Shaping something. And his face had gone white.

He sat back on his heels.

He didn't say anything. He just knelt there, at the foot of the sword, in the middle of the dead circle, with his hands on his knees, and stared at the iron.

Then he turned his head and looked across the hall at them. At Milli and Hana and Jared and Aleaka, standing in a row at the edge of the circle. And Milli saw his eyes, and felt something cold go down his back.

"Can you read it?" said Oswin, out loud this time, unable to bear it any longer. "Cursiffe. Please. Can you read it?"

Cursiffe looked at Oswin for a long moment.

"Give me time," he said quietly.

Oswin's face fell. Kestra, by the winch, turned her head and looked at Cursiffe. Then she took her little book out of her coat, and opened it, and wrote something down, without hurrying and without looking away.

Cursiffe got up. He walked back across the circle, slowly, swaying again at the edge as if the wind had caught him, and stepped out of it, and stopped in front of the four of them.

"Not here," he said, very low.

***

He told them in the prince's wing, in the sitting room, with the door shut and the fire lit, though it was summer. He sat in the big chair with his hands clasped in front of him, and they stood round him. Hana and Milli and Jared and Aleaka. Nobody sat down.

"It's not English," said Cursiffe.

Milli frowned. "Then what—"

"It's Latin."

There was a short silence.

"Latin," said Jared slowly.

"Latin. Four words." Cursiffe took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes. "I did Latin at school. For years. I was the only one in my year who actually liked it. I can still read most of it, if it's simple." He put his glasses back on. "This is simple. It's one of the most famous phrases there is. It's on crests and badges and the gates of schools. You've probably all seen it somewhere without knowing what it meant."

"What does it say?" said Hana.

Cursiffe looked at the fire.

"*Ad astra per aspera*," he said. "To the stars, through hardships."

Nobody said anything.

Milli found he'd sat down after all, on the arm of the sofa, without noticing. He was thinking very fast. He was thinking the way he always thought, in a line, one thing after another, step by step, because that was the only way he knew how to think when it mattered.

Latin. On the sword. On the Black Blade's sword, the sword of the Hero of the Redmarch, which the Hero had driven into the ground over the grave of a healer seven years ago and walked away from. Scratched into the iron, by hand, in letters nobody in this world could read.

Letters from *their* world.

"Someone from Earth," said Milli. "Carved that."

"Yes," said Cursiffe.

"Someone from Earth was here. Seven years ago. At least." Milli's voice sounded strange to him. Very calm. Very flat. "Standing over that grave."

"Yes."

"With that sword."

Cursiffe didn't answer that. He didn't need to.

***

Hana hadn't said anything. She was standing by the window, with her back to them, very straight, her purple tail perfectly still. Milli looked at her, and then at Jared, and saw Jared looking at Cursiffe. And Cursiffe looking back. And both of them looking away.

He'd seen that look before. All winter. In this room. He'd never asked about it. He'd known it was something, and he'd known Hana knew what it was, and he'd known that if Hana wasn't telling him, it was because she had a reason. He trusted her reasons. He always had.

He saw Aleaka see it too.

Aleaka had gone pale. She was standing by the fire with her fox ears flat against her head and her big orange tail curled round her legs, and she was looking at Hana's back.

"Hana," she said. Her voice was very small. "Is this it?"

Hana turned round.

"The thing," said Aleaka. "The thing you weren't sure of. In the winter. In the mews. You said you'd found something, and you didn't know what it meant, and when you did, I'd be first." She swallowed. "Is this the thing?"

Hana looked at her for a long moment. Milli watched her face. Calm. Pleasant. Still. And underneath it, something so tired and so torn that he wanted to go to her.

"Part of it," said Hana quietly.

Aleaka's face crumpled, very slightly, and then pulled itself back together.

"Part," she said.

"Part." Hana came away from the window. She crossed the room and stopped in front of Aleaka and took both her hands. "Leaka. I promised. I haven't forgotten. When I'm sure, you'll be first. Before anyone. I swear it." Her voice wobbled. "I'm still not sure. This makes me less sure, not more. I don't know what it means. I don't want to give you something to hold on to, and then have it turn out to be nothing, and watch you go back to how you were." She squeezed Aleaka's hands. "I watched that once. I'm not doing it again."

Aleaka didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she nodded. Just once. And took her hands back, gently, and went and sat in the window seat with her knees pulled up, and looked out at the city, and didn't say anything else at all.

***

Cursiffe put it in the channel that afternoon.

They'd talked about whether to. Not for long. Jared said they had to; Hana said they had to; even Milli said they had to, and Milli was the one who usually wanted to wait until things were certain. But this wasn't the kind of thing you kept from thirty people who'd come through the same floor. They'd all woken up in that circle. They all had that sword's line in their windows, whether they could see it or not. *Medium: one blade.* They had a right to know whose blade.

So Cursiffe wrote it, carefully, one line at a time, the way the channel made you.

[ Cursiffe: The sword in the summoning hall left for Cinderhold today. Before it went, I got to look at the carving near the hilt.
Cursiffe: It isn't the native script. It isn't English either.
Cursiffe: It's Latin.
Cursiffe: AD ASTRA PER ASPERA
Cursiffe: "To the stars, through hardships."
Cursiffe: Someone from our world carved that into the sword. On the Redmarch. Seven years ago, at least. ]

Milli watched it go up, sitting on the arm of the sofa with his own window open. Six lines. He watched them sit there at the bottom of the channel, with nothing under them, for one heartbeat, two, three.

Then it went mad.

[ Tiger: WHAT
Newish: LATIN??
Newish: the hero is a ROMAN???
Chris: it's a common motto. schools, regiments, space programs. lots of people would know it.
Maigo: WAIT WHO ON EARTH SPEAKS LATIN
Hyvred: priests? scholars? old people??
Malcolm: IF the hero's from Earth, THEN the hero was summoned before us. Or something like it.
Wendy: so the advisor DID know. or someone did.
Milli: We don't know that. We don't know anything except the sword. ]

He'd written that last one himself, without quite meaning to. His hands had moved on their own.

It went on. Questions, mostly. Theories. Somebody from Duskhollow asked if Latin was one of the Hero's words, like *status* and *inventory*, and nobody knew. Hyvred asked if anyone knew any Latin at all, apart from Cursiffe. A few people said they knew a word or two. *Et cetera.* *Carpe diem.* Newish said she knew *veni vidi vici* and nothing else and was prepared to use it in combat.

A priest. A scholar. Someone old. Someone from long ago. That was where it was going. Milli could feel it. The whole channel turning toward the same picture: some learned man from centuries back, summoned here somehow, the way they'd been, who'd become the Hero and carved a school motto into his sword. It made sense. It was neat. It explained everything without explaining too much.

And then, from Kingsferry, Gabe.

***

[ Gabe: Declan loved Latin. ]

That was all, at first. Just that. Four words, at the bottom of the channel, under all the theories.

Then:

[ Gabe: every discord status he ever had was some latin phrase.
Deston: Every Steam group he ever made had a Latin name. Every single one. We gave him so much grief for it.
Deston: He didn't even speak it. He just loved it. He'd find a phrase somewhere and use it for months.
Xavier: He'd say things in Latin in the middle of a match and refuse to translate.
John: He did it on stream all the time. Nobody ever knew what he was saying. He thought that was the best part. ]

The channel went completely silent.

Milli sat on the arm of the sofa and read it, and felt the room tilt very slightly, the way the summoning hall had tilted under his cheek a year ago.

He hadn't known that. He hadn't known Ice well enough to know that. He'd known Ice as the Minecraft bingo guy: a voice in Hana's chat, a name she laughed at, a funny man on the edge of his wife's life. He'd known him for less than a year. He'd never once seen his Discord status. He'd never been in one of his Steam groups. He hadn't known.

Four people had. The four who'd known him in rooms, in schools, in person. The four who'd known his real name.

Nobody wrote anything for a long time.

Then Gabe again. Shorter. As if he'd thought about it a long time before he sent it.

[ Gabe: i don't know if that one was his. there were so many. i never learned any of them.
Deston: I don't know either. I never asked what they meant. I thought there'd be time. ]

***

Milli closed his window.

He looked up. The others were all looking at him. Jared, by the fire. Cursiffe, in the big chair, with his hands still clasped. Aleaka in the window seat, with her face turned toward them now and her eyes very wide. And Hana, by the door, with her hand over her mouth.

"No," said Milli.

Nobody said anything.

"No," he said again. He was surprised by how hard it came out. He took a breath. He made himself slow down. Think in a line. One thing, then the next. That was the only way. "Listen. I know what everyone's thinking. I'm thinking it too. But listen."

He held up one finger.

"Someone from Earth carved that sword. Fine. That's true. Cursiffe read it. I believe him." Another finger. "Ice loved Latin. Fine. That's true too. Four people who knew him say so. I believe them." A third. "But the sword's been on that grave for seven years. The Hero's been the Hero for longer than that. Oswin's got accounts going back *twenty* years." He looked round at them. "Ice died three years ago. In his sleep. In Malacca. Three years. People in that chat went to his *funeral*."

Silence.

"It doesn't fit," said Milli. "It can't fit. It's a coincidence, or it's someone who liked the same things he liked. Lots of people love Latin. Lots of people put mottos on things. Chris said: it's on school gates. It's on badges." He spread his hands. "Someone from Earth was here. Before us. That's what this proves. That's *all* it proves. It doesn't mean it's him."

Nobody argued. Milli almost wished somebody would.

Hana was looking at him across the room, and he couldn't read her face at all.

***

He couldn't sleep that night.

He lay in the big bed in their room at the end of the corridor, on his back, with the window open on the warm city and the summer stars, and Hana breathing beside him. He knew she wasn't asleep either. She was lying on her side, facing the wall, very still. Her tail was twitching at the tip, the way it did when she was thinking too hard.

He'd said *it doesn't mean it's him*. He'd meant it. He still meant it.

But that wasn't what was keeping him awake.

What was keeping him awake was the other thing. The thing he hadn't said, in the sitting room, in front of everyone. The thing that had been sitting in his chest like a stone since Cursiffe said *someone from Earth*.

Someone from Earth had come here. Before them. Years before them.

And that someone had become the Hero. The strongest swordsman in the kingdom. Strong enough to hold the Redmarch on his own. Strong enough to break an army in one night, kill a demon general, cut its horns off. Strong enough that the demons were still afraid of him, seven years later; strong enough that fourteen of them had seen his trophies hanging over a gate and run.

Someone from Earth, with all that strength. More than any of the thirty-five would ever have. More than they could imagine having.

And he'd never gone home.

He'd stayed. For seven years at least, maybe twenty, maybe longer. He'd planted his sword on a grave and walked away and never been seen again, and he'd never gone home.

Maybe he hadn't wanted to. Maybe he'd found something here worth staying for. That happened. People did that.

Or maybe he couldn't.

Maybe the strongest man anybody in this world had ever seen, a man from their own world, had looked for the way home, the way Milli was looking, with everything he had. And not found it. Because it wasn't there.

*Nobody, for certain, ever had.*

Milli lay on his back in the dark and felt, for the first time since the summoning hall, genuinely afraid.

***

"Honey," said Hana, very quietly, into the dark.

He turned his head. She'd rolled over. She was looking at him. In the starlight from the window he could see the shine of her one visible violet eye, and her ears, flat.

"Honey," he said.

"You're thinking about the way home."

He didn't ask how she knew. She always knew. "Yeah."

"You're thinking he didn't go back."

"Yeah."

Hana was quiet for a moment.

"I've thought about it too," she said. "Since the winter. Since..." She stopped. Milli knew she'd stopped on something. He'd learned, over the years, where Hana stopped. He didn't push. "I've thought about it a lot," she said instead. "Whoever he is. Whatever he is. Why someone like that would stay."

"And?"

"And I don't know." She reached over and found his hand on the blanket between them, and held it. "I don't know, honey. I don't know any more than you do. Maybe he couldn't go back. Maybe he didn't want to." She squeezed his hand. "But I know one thing."

"What?"

"He carved *to the stars, through hardships* on his sword. Over a grave." Hana's voice was very soft. "That's not something a man carves when he's given up. That's something a man carves when he's still going."

Milli lay there and thought about that for a long time.

He didn't know if it was true. He didn't know if it was proof of anything. It probably wasn't. It was a guess, from a woman who loved him, at two in the morning, in a strange bed in a strange world.

But he found that it helped.

"I'm still going to find out," he said. "Whether the door's there. I'm not going to just hope."

"I know," said Hana. "I'd be disappointed in you if you did."

"You'll help?"

"I'm already helping." She shifted closer, until her head was on his shoulder and her ears were tickling his chin. "I've been helping all year. You just never asked what with."

He almost asked, then. What she'd found. What she and Jared and Cursiffe had been looking at across the sitting room all winter and not saying. He felt the question right there, on his tongue.

He didn't. He was learning to wait. Kestra had been teaching him for a year. *Again.* He'd finally learned it.

"When you're sure," he said instead.

Hana was still for a moment against his shoulder. Then she laughed, very softly, a little wetly.

"When I'm sure," she said. "You'll be second. After Leaka. I promised."

"Second's fine," said Milli. "Maigo says second's just first but later."

Hana laughed properly at that, muffled, into his shoulder. And after a while her breathing slowed, and her tail went still, and she slept.

Milli lay awake a little longer, with his wife asleep on his shoulder and the summer stars over the capital through the open window.

*Ad astra per aspera*, he thought. *To the stars, through hardships.*

He didn't believe in stars. He believed in proof.

But he looked at them anyway, for a long time, before he slept.

***

Far to the east, on the road out of the capital, by the light of those same stars, six soldiers and a cart and a great timber winch were making camp at the side of the road. On the cart, wrapped in canvas and lashed down with rope, lay a slab of black iron as long as a man, with four words scratched into it near the hilt, going home.

It would be a month on the road. And in the last week of summer, on the eighth anniversary of the day a man had driven it into the red earth over the grave of the woman he loved, five people who had loved him in another life, and didn't know he was alive in this one, would carry it the last stretch across the Redmarch by hand.

They wouldn't get there. Not quite. But that was months away.
