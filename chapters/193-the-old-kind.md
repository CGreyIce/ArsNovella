Salty knew weights.

He'd known them before he knew anything else about this world. Before tiers, before aura, before the red. You picked a thing up and it told you what it was. A sack of meal was a sack of meal. An anvil was as much as a horse. A black sword the length of a man was more than anything had a right to be, and then, seven steps later, it wasn't, because something in his chest had opened up and taken it. You couldn't lie to a weight. That was the whole point of them.

Everything had one. That was what he'd thought.

Then three tall grey people came into Oakes's forge on the ninth morning, out of the snow, and the boards didn't creak.

He heard them not creak. That was how he knew they were there. The forge floor was old oak planks over packed earth, laid by somebody's grandfather, and every one of them had a voice. Oakes's was a long low groan by the door. Wendy's, when she came to stand in the doorway with her cold hands under her arms, was a click, like a knuckle. His own was a thump. You could tell who'd come in without turning round, if you'd worked there a winter.

The boards didn't say anything at all.

He turned round with the tongs in his hand and a shoe on the end of them, glowing, and they were standing just inside the door in their lichen-coloured coats, looking at the fire. The old one, Tavrin, with her stave. The young man, Oen. And the girl with the pale hair, Liraun, who'd already gone past both of them, down the length of the forge, to the back wall, where the pots were.

"Morning," said Salty.

"Morning," said Tavrin. In the kingdom's tongue. He heard it as English. He heard everything as English; they all did. You only knew it was the other tongue because Oakes, at the bellows, looked up with his old face gone blank, and then looked at Salty, and then back at the fire, with the expression of a man who'd stopped being surprised by things in about the second year and saw no reason to start again now.

"They want to watch," said Salty. To Oakes.

"Let 'em," said Oakes. And pumped the bellows.

***

He was making horseshoes.

Real ones. That was the joke, if anyone asked, and somebody always asked. All last winter and into the spring, everything Salty had made in this forge had been *horseshoes*, out loud, to anybody who wanted to know, while he and Garrow chalked the curve of a scythe blade on the floor under a sack. Wendy had told him he was the worst liar she'd ever met. He'd agreed. It was why he didn't do it much.

Now it was horseshoes. Actual horseshoes. The spring relief was coming up the road from Ashby in six weeks with forty horses, and Oakes had looked at the stores in the winter and said a word Salty hadn't known Oakes knew, and here they were. Thirty-one done, on the rack by the quench tub. Nine to go.

He put the one on the tongs back in the fire, because it had gone dull while he was saying *morning*, and waited for it to come up again, and didn't look at the elves. He'd found that worked with most things. You didn't look at them, and they got used to you, and after a while they stopped being a thing that was happening and started being a thing that was there.

It worked with horses. It worked with Rhea, mostly. He didn't know if it worked with people out of a song.

The shoe came up. Cherry, going to orange. He took it out and put it on the horn and hit it.

He felt them watch him hit it.

Not the way people watched in the yard, when he sparred. Not the way the recruits had watched in the autumn, when word got round that the short one with the arms had carried the Black Blade's sword across the red. Not like he was something. Like the hammer was. Like each blow was a word, and they were listening for the sentence.

He hit it nine more times and put it back in the fire.

"Again," said Oen.

Salty looked at him.

The young man was standing by the anvil, a careful pace back from it, with his long hands folded in front of him and his long ears very slightly forward. He was taller than Rhea. He'd had to duck under the door. He looked about twenty and moved like somebody who'd been twenty for a long time and got very good at it. And he'd said *again* exactly the way Rhea said it every night in the courtyard, to him, across a barrel, after she lost. Which was every night, since the gate. Every time.

"He's been listening to Rhea," said Wendy, from the door.

"Everyone's been listening to Rhea," said Salty.

He did it again.

***

The old woman picked up a finished shoe off the rack.

He hadn't seen her move. That was another thing about them. One moment she was by the door and the next she was by the quench tub with a horseshoe in her long grey hand, turning it over, holding it up to the light from the fire. It had been out of the water an hour. It was cool. She ran one long finger round it, slowly, along the outside of the curve, the way you'd run your finger along a line of writing.

"This one," she said. "Here." She touched the heel. "You were angry."

Salty stopped with the hammer up.

"Here," said the old woman. Her finger moved on, round the toe. "Here, not. Here, you were thinking of somebody. Here—" a little further, three marks close together, "—you counted. And here you were tired, and did not stop."

Behind her, at the bellows, Oakes laughed.

It wasn't a sound Salty heard often. Oakes was old, and had been the smith at Cinderhold for longer than anybody could remember, and he'd buried most of the men he'd come east with, and he ate standing up. He laughed the way an old door opens. All at once, after a lot of creaking.

"She's got you, lad," said Oakes. He didn't know what she'd said. He didn't need to. He'd watched Salty's face.

Salty put the hammer down.

"That one," he said, and nodded at the shoe in her hand, at the heel. "I'd just lost to Rhea. Arm-wrestling. I don't lose to Rhea." He looked at the toe. "Thinking of somebody. That's Wendy."

In the doorway, Wendy made a small noise.

"Counted," said Salty. "Yeah. I count when I'm doing the toe. Three, three, three. Garrow taught me." He looked at the last bit. "Tired. Yeah." He was quiet. "You can read all that? Off a horseshoe?"

"Off anything that was made by hand," said the old woman. "A thing that is made remembers being made. Your marks are very loud."

"Is that bad?"

She looked at him with her winter eyes. For a moment he thought she wasn't going to answer.

"No," she said. "It is honest." She put the shoe back on the rack, exactly where it had been, in its place in the row. "We do not make things like this. We have a rule. We do not make anything that will last longer than we do."

Salty thought about that.

"That's a stupid rule," he said.

Oen went very still. Wendy, in the doorway, said "Salty—"

But the old woman laughed.

He'd never heard her laugh. None of them had; Yan had said so at breakfast, that she didn't, that her light didn't do the thing that laughing did. It was very short and very low and it came out of her as if it had surprised her too, and she put her hand up to her mouth afterwards, the way you'd put your hand over a cup you'd spilled.

"Yes," she said. "It is very old. Old rules are often stupid. That is how you know they are old." She looked at the rack of shoes. "These. They will last longer than you."

"They'll fall off in a field," said Salty. "Eventually. Horse throws a shoe. It goes in the mud. Then it's in the ground." He shrugged. "Everything goes in the ground. Eventually. It just takes iron a bit longer."

The old woman looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," she said again. Differently.

***

Liraun was talking to the peppers.

He'd known she would be. He'd seen her go straight to them, the moment she came in, the way Maigo went straight to anything green. She was crouched at the back wall of the forge with her long hands on her knees and her pale hair hanging down, in front of the row of clay pots along the warm stones behind the hearth, where the heat stayed all winter. Six pots. Six plants. Knee-high and dark green and glossy, and on four of them, hanging down under the leaves like little red lanterns, peppers.

She was saying something to them. Very low. In the singing tongue. He heard it as English: *you are very far from home*.

"They are," said Salty.

She looked up at him. She didn't speak the kingdom's tongue at all, Yan said. She didn't seem to need to. She just looked at him with her pale eyes, waiting, the way you'd wait for somebody to tell you a story you knew was there.

So he told her.

He hadn't told anybody. Not properly. Wendy knew, because Wendy knew everything; Oakes knew, because they were his stones. But nobody had asked where they came from, because nobody at Cinderhold knew what a pepper was supposed to look like. They thought it was a southern thing. It was.

"There was a man," said Salty. "Last summer. You know the one. With the rod." He crouched down beside her, by the pots. His knees cracked. Hers didn't. "He owed me eleven dinners. From before. From where we come from. He'd promised, and we never got to have them, and then we came here, and he—" He stopped. "He paid. All eleven. Here. In this forge, on that hearth, with Oakes's skillet. One a night."

He looked at the plants.

"He bought the stuff in Valcrest. In the south. On a street by the ships. Dried peppers, in a paper bag, from some islands nobody here's ever been to. Proper ones. The kind that hurt." He was quiet. "He cooked with them. And when he went home, there was a handful left in the bottom of the bag. Dried. Dead. Crumbled up. And I don't know why, I just— I picked the seeds out. With a knife-point. On the bench. And I put them in pots, back here, where it's warm. In the autumn." He shrugged. "Six came up."

Liraun looked at the plants. And at him.

"It's stupid," said Salty. "They're not supposed to grow in winter. Not this far north. They're not supposed to grow from dried ones, either, half the time. Oakes said they'd die." He looked at the old man at the bellows. "Oakes waters them."

"I do not," said Oakes, who couldn't understand a word, and didn't need to.

Liraun put out one long finger and touched a pepper. Very gently. On the end of it, where it came to a point.

Then she said something else. To him, this time. Still low.

*A thing that was dead, in a bag, from very far. And you put it by the fire, and it came up.*

"Yeah," said Salty.

She smiled. It was the first time he'd seen her do that, too.

***

They came to supper in the long hall that night.

They hadn't, before. They'd eaten in the chapel every night for nine days, with Aldous, because the hall was too loud. But Liraun had wanted to. Salty didn't know how he knew that; she hadn't said. She'd just stood at the chapel door at the supper bell and looked across the yard at the long hall with its windows lit up and its door banging and the noise coming out of it, the way a kid looks at a fair. And the old woman had looked at her, and sighed, the way old women sigh in every world, and said *very well*.

So the whole hall went quiet when they came in.

All of it. Two hundred people. The old ones at their end, and the recruits at theirs, and the garrison in the middle, and Group 3 at the end of the long table by the fire where they always sat. The noise stopped as if somebody had shut a door on it. The three tall grey people stood just inside, very still, with their staves, being looked at. And then the red-haired boy from Pellow's Cross, at the recruits' end, who'd sung *The Thirty-Five* in this hall every night for ten weeks, stood up very fast and knocked his bench over.

Nobody laughed. Then Rhea did, very loudly, from the end of the long table, and the noise came back all at once like water through a broken dam, and it was fine.

They sat with Group 3. Of course they did. Liraun next to Maigo, who'd gone pink under the bark and stayed pink. Oen across from Rhea, because Rhea had pointed at the bench and said *there* and Oen had sat there. The old woman at the end, next to Aldous, who'd come out of his chapel for the first time in a week and looked as if he hadn't slept, which he hadn't, and as if he didn't mind.

Cook sent down fish. They ate the fish.

And then Salty got up and went to the end of the serving hatch, where the jar was.

The whole hall watched him do it. They always did, now. It had started at midwinter, as a joke, and stopped being one by the first week of the new year. A big brown crock with a wooden lid that Cook kept by the hatch, and in it a thick dark-red paste. Peppers, pounded, with garlic and salt and oil and a spoon of the brown paste from Valcrest that the man had left behind and that Cook guarded like gold. Salty's own. Not the man's. He'd never got the man's to taste the same. But close.

The old ones had it on everything. On porridge. On bread. Teague put it on his eggs and swore at it every morning and put it on his eggs again. Ansel said it was the only thing in nine years that had made him feel his teeth.

Salty brought the crock to the table and put it down in front of the three of them and took the lid off.

"Try it," he said. "If you want. It's angry."

The young man looked at the crock. Then at Rhea, across the table, who was grinning.

"It's *very* angry," said Rhea, in the kingdom's tongue, slowly, so Oen would get it. "Too angry for elves. Probably."

Salty saw Oen hear that.

Oen picked up a spoon. He put it in the crock. He brought it out with a great deal more on it than anybody had ever brought out of that crock in one go, and he looked at Rhea, and he ate it.

Nothing happened for a moment.

Then his ears went red. The tips first. And then all the way down, both of them, the long swept-back length of them, red as the paste, while the rest of his face stayed perfectly still. And then they went flat. Back against his head. Like a cat's.

He didn't say anything. He picked up the spoon again. He put it back in the crock.

"Oh no," said Wendy.

He ate a second spoon.

Rhea made a noise Salty had never heard her make. It wasn't a laugh. It was bigger than a laugh. She put both her hands flat on the table and her head down between them and *shook*, and the bench shook, and the table shook, and the young man sat across from her with his ears flat and red and tears running down his perfectly still face, and reached for the spoon a third time.

The old woman put her hand on his wrist.

She said something to him. Low. In the singing tongue. Salty heard it as English, and so did the rest of Group 3, and he saw Niko, down the table, put his hand over his mouth.

*You have made your point, child. The human has also made hers. Both of you are very stupid. Drink your water.*

Oen drank his water.

Then the old woman took the spoon out of his hand, and put it in the crock, and took out a very small amount. About as much as you'd put on the end of a knife. And ate it.

The hall had gone quiet again. Not all the way. Most of the way.

She sat with it for a long time. Her face didn't change. Her ears didn't change. She looked at the fire, and then at the crock, and then at Salty.

"It is angry," she said. In the kingdom's tongue.

"It's supposed to be," said Salty.

"Yes," said the old woman. "Good." And she took another small amount on the end of the spoon, and put it on her fish.

***

Liraun didn't have any of the paste.

Salty saw her not have it. When the noise had come back and Rhea had stopped shaking and Oen's ears had gone from red to pink, she got up, very quietly, and went out of the hall, and came back a few minutes later with something in her closed hand. She sat down by Maigo again. She opened her hand.

A pepper. A fresh one, red, from the pots behind the hearth. She'd gone across the yard in the dark to the forge and picked one.

She ate it. Slowly. In small bites, the whole thing, seeds and all, with her eyes shut. Her ears didn't go red. Her face didn't change. When she'd finished, she opened her eyes and said something, very low, not to anyone at the table.

To the pepper. To where it had been.

Salty heard it: *thank you for coming so far*.

***

They sat on the step outside the forge afterwards.

It was the best seat in the fort. Everybody knew it. It had been since the summer, since the man cooked on Oakes's hearth for eleven nights and half the garrison had come and sat on the step to smell it, and nobody had stopped sitting there since. The forge wall was warm against your back even in winter. You could see the whole yard, and the west wall, and over it, on a clear night, the stars. Oakes didn't sit on it. Oakes stood in the door behind it with his arms folded, because he'd stood his whole life and his knees didn't know how to do anything else.

Salty sat on one end. Wendy beside him, with her cold hands in his coat pocket and her lavender headphones round her neck. Maigo on the bottom step, because he was short and liked to have his root foot on the cold ground so he could feel the fort. Liraun beside Maigo. Oen standing, a little way off, with his ears still pink. And the old woman at the other end of the step, sitting very upright with her stave across her knees, looking at the stars.

Nobody said anything for a while. It was that kind of step.

Then Maigo said, "Can I ask you something?"

He'd been dying to. Salty had watched him be dying to for nine days. He'd been dying to since the gate, since the old woman had looked at him in the snow with her winter eyes and said *not one of the old kind*, and he'd cried, and been glad, and hadn't known what he was glad about. Maigo didn't do not-knowing. He asked. He was always the first one to say anything, about everything. Nine days was the longest Salty had ever seen him not ask a question in his life.

"You may ask," said the old woman.

"The old kind," said Maigo. "You said I wasn't one. At the gate. You said I'm something new." He swallowed. "What's the old kind?"

The old woman didn't answer at once.

She looked at the stars a while longer. Then she said something to the young man, Oen, in the singing tongue, and he came and stood behind her, and she said something else, and he answered. Salty heard it all as English. It was about which song. The young man thought one song, and the old woman thought another, and in the end she said *the short one; he is cold*, meaning Maigo, and the young man smiled and stood back.

"In our forest," said the old woman. In the kingdom's tongue, for Wendy and Oakes. Slowly. Each word by itself. "Trees live a very long time. Longer than us. The oldest are older than your kingdom. Than the one before. They do not walk." She looked at Maigo's root foot on the cold ground. "Almost never."

Maigo didn't move.

"Sometimes," said the old woman, "a very old tree knows it is going to die. Not soon. Soon, for a tree. A hundred years. And it knows that where it stands, when it falls, nothing new will grow, because there is no room. The forest is too thick there. Too old. So it does a thing that trees do almost never." She was quiet. "It pulls up its roots. And it walks."

The stars were very bright over the west wall.

"It walks once," said the old woman. "Only once. Slowly. A step in a day. A step in a season. Out through the forest, to the edge, to where there is room. Where the light comes down. It does not stop for rivers; it walks through them. It does not stop for us; we move out of its way. It does not speak. It does not look back. It walks to the place it has chosen, and it stands there, and it lets itself fall." She looked at her hands on her stave. "And where it falls, the forest begins again. Its seeds. Its wood, going into the ground. The light, where it was. In a hundred years, there is a new wood there, where there was nothing."

"That's the old kind," said Maigo. Very quietly.

"That is the old kind. We have a song for every one. Who it was. Where it walked from, and where it fell. We sing it out, when it goes." She paused. "The last one walked when I was younger than Liraun. I sang it out. It took eleven years to reach the edge. I walked beside it. All the way."

Nobody said anything.

Salty looked at Maigo. Maigo was sitting on the bottom step with his root foot on the frozen ground and his antlers bare, with the bells on them muffled in Nell's scarf, and his face doing something Salty had seen it do once before. On the red, on the anniversary, two summers ago, when a man with a sword on his shoulder had walked out to a grave and Maigo had walked behind him and not asked a hundred and three questions.

"So when you heard," said Wendy. Slowly. Working it out. Wendy always worked things out. "In your forest. That there was a tree. Walking. Somewhere—"

"We thought something was ending," said the old woman.

She looked at Maigo.

"The song came to us," she said. "From the downs. From the roots. The way things come to us. *A tree that walks*. And we thought: somewhere, far away, out past our edge, an old one has decided. Has pulled up its roots. Is walking to where it will fall." She was quiet. "And nobody there would know the song. Nobody would walk beside it. Nobody would sing it out." She looked at her hands. "So we came."

Maigo had stopped breathing. Salty could see it.

"For the white," said the old woman. "Yes. Liraun dreamed of white for a year. That was true. But also for that. Because a tree should not walk to its falling alone." She looked up at Maigo. "And it was you."

"I'm not—" said Maigo. It came out of him very small. "I'm not old. I'm not going anywhere. I'm not—"

"No," said the old woman. "You are not."

And she smiled. The second time in a day.

"You walk every week," she said. "Through the snow, two hours, to a young tree on a grave. And you sit with it. And you *talk* to it." Her voice had changed. "For an hour. About the hens. About the cook. About a boy who knocked over a bench. And then—" She stopped. She seemed to have to stop. "And then you come back."

Maigo's face crumpled.

"The old kind walk once," said the old woman. "And they do not come back. They do not talk. They do not laugh. They are grave and slow and very beautiful, and when one walks, it means a thing is ending. We have sung them out for longer than your kingdom has had a name." She was quiet. "And then there is a tree that walked out of a song, in another world, to come and see his friends. And walks every week. And comes back. And is very loud." She looked at him for a long time. "Nothing is ending. Something is beginning. That is why we are glad."

Maigo put his face in his hands.

Liraun put her long arm round him. Without saying anything. The way you'd put a coat round somebody. And he leaned into her, very small, with his bare antlers against her shoulder and his bells going very softly in the scarf, and cried the way he'd cried at the gate, and in the hall at breakfast when Yan told him about the leaf, and on the red two summers ago, and every time anything was too big. Out loud. Not hiding it. Maigo didn't know how to hide things. It was one of the best things about him.

Salty looked at the stars.

Beside him, Wendy had taken her hand out of his pocket and was wiping her face with it.

***

The old woman gave it to him later. When the others had gone in.

He hadn't expected it. He'd stayed on the step because the forge needed banking and Oakes had gone to bed, and because he didn't feel like going in yet, and when he came out again with the ash bucket she was still sitting at the end of the step in the dark, very upright, as if she'd been waiting.

"Smith," she said.

He put the bucket down.

She held out her hand. Closed. He held out his under it, the way you do, and she opened hers.

A seed.

He looked at it in the light from the forge door. It was small. Smaller than the end of his little finger. Brown, and smooth, and shaped a bit like a teardrop, with a fine dark line round it like a seam. It sat in the middle of his palm and weighed almost nothing.

He felt it anyway.

That was the odd thing. He knew weights. He'd known them all his life, and here they'd got into his chest and become a skill, *Bear*, that took a thing's weight off his joints and put it on something else. He knew what a seed weighed. Nothing. Less than nothing; less than the air in your hand.

This one weighed something. Not much. But something. As if it had been carried a very long way, by a lot of people, and they'd each left a bit of the carrying in it.

"From the last one," said the old woman. "That walked. That I sang out. From the tree that grew where it fell." She looked at it in his palm. "It is a hundred and forty years old, the tree. That is young. This is its first seed that I kept."

Salty looked at her.

"Why me?" he said. Blunt. It was the only way he knew how to say things. "Why not Maigo? He's the tree."

"Trees should not carry seeds," said the old woman. "They drop them. That is what they are for." She looked at the pots behind the hearth, through the open door, red in the firelight. "You carry. And you put dead things from very far by the fire, and they come up."

He closed his hand round it.

"What do I do with it?"

"Plant it," said the old woman, "where you are going to stay."

Salty didn't say anything.

She got up. All at once, the long grey length of her unfolding off the step. She took her stave. She looked down at him, short and square on the step of a forge on the edge of a red plain, with a seed in his fist.

"You do not know where that is," she said. Not a question.

"No," said Salty.

"Good," said the old woman. "Then you will not plant it in the wrong place." And she went away across the yard to the chapel, without a sound, without a single board or stone or patch of frozen mud saying anything at all under her feet.

***

He told Wendy. In the barracks, in the dark, with the others asleep and Maigo still sniffing a bit in his bunk across the room.

She lay with her head on his shoulder and turned the seed over in her fingers, the way the old woman had turned the horseshoe. He could feel her thinking. Wendy thought the way other people breathed: all the time, without noticing.

"Where we're going to stay," she said.

"Yeah."

"We were going to go to Malacca," said Wendy. Very quietly. "Remember? We had the spreadsheet. Flights. The eleven. We were going to book it in the summer." She was quiet. "We never booked it."

"I know."

"And now it's—" She stopped. She didn't say *home*. None of them said it much, any more. Not since the summer, since a man had cooked them eleven dinners on a forge hearth and laughed and then, every night, gone very quiet afterwards, when he thought nobody was looking. "I don't know where we're going to stay."

"No," said Salty. "Me neither."

He held out his hand. She put the seed back in it.

"I'll know when I know," he said. "That's all. You can't lie to a weight." He closed his fist. "It'll tell me. When I'm standing in the right place, it'll be heavier. Or lighter. Or something." He shrugged, in the dark. "I'll know."

Wendy didn't say anything for a while.

"That's a stupid rule," she said. Into his shoulder.

"Yeah," said Salty. "It's very old."

She laughed. Very quietly, so as not to wake anyone. And he lay there in the dark with her laughing on his shoulder and a seed in his hand that weighed almost nothing, and felt it anyway, all night. A long way, and a lot of people, and the bit of carrying each of them had left in it.

He'd carry it. He was good at that.

In the morning he put it in the rivet tin Oakes had given him, with the paper Wendy had copied the eleven onto in the summer, *ELEVEN* at the top in capitals, and a line through every one. And went to the forge, and made nine horseshoes, and didn't count any of them out loud.
