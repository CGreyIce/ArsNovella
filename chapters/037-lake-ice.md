Thea told them about the fisherman on a Tuesday, in the last hard weeks of winter, and Alea heard about it on the Wednesday, and by Wednesday night she'd made up her mind to ask.

She didn't ask straight away. That wasn't her way, and anyway, she had to work up to it. She sat through supper, and through her father reading by the fire, and through her own clumsy mending of the same practice tunic she always seemed to be mending. She waited until the fire had burned down to embers and he'd put his book face-down on the arm of the chair and was just sitting, the way he did sometimes in the evenings, looking at nothing.

Then she said, very casually, "Did you meet Thea at the market?"

"Mm," said her father.

"What did she say?"

"She thanked me for the fish."

Alea waited. Nothing else came.

"Anything else?"

Her father considered it. "We talked about the ice. Whether the lake had frozen all the way."

"Has it?"

"Not yet. Another week." He picked up the poker and moved an ember. "She told me she'd reached the first tier. I said congratulations."

"Anything *else*?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"She said she'd pay me back," he said. "For the fish. One day."

Alea put down her mending.

She knew about *trust*. Of course she knew. She'd been hearing about it since she was old enough to hear bedtime stories: her father's old bit, from the world before, that he'd said after everything completely untrustworthy, so often that his friends could recognise it from a single word. *I would never betray you in this game. Trust.* She'd laughed at it when she was six. She'd tried it on Rowan once, when she was eight, and Rowan had looked at her as if she'd grown a second tail.

And Higu had mentioned it round the fire in the autumn: *he'd have said 'trust' at the end, and everyone would have known it was a lie.* Thea had been sitting right there.

"Dad," said Alea slowly. "Did she say *trust*?"

Her father looked at the fire.

"She did," he said.

Alea stared at him.

"And?" she said. "*And?* What did you say?"

"I said there's plenty of fish."

Alea opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then, quite without meaning to, she started to laugh. Helplessly, with her hands over her face, curled up on the rug by the hearth, laughing until her ribs hurt and her eyes were streaming, while her father sat in his chair and watched her with an expression that wasn't quite a smile.

"*There's plenty of fish,*" she gasped. "She said *trust* to you. To *you*. And you said *there's plenty of fish*."

"There is plenty of fish," said her father mildly.

That set her off again.

She laughed until she couldn't anymore, and then she lay on the rug looking up at the beams of the ceiling, still hiccupping a little. And underneath the laughing, very quietly, something else.

Thea had tried. Thea had stood in the market in the snow, in front of her father, and said the one word that would have made the Ice in his bedtime stories laugh out loud. And her father had stood there and said *there's plenty of fish*, and walked away, and not given her anything.

Alea didn't know if that was the funniest thing she'd ever heard or the saddest. She thought it might be both.

***

The lake froze a week later, the way he'd said it would. From the north shore, in a single cold night, so that in the morning the whole of Lake Ilse was a grey-white plain stretching away to the far trees, and the end of the jetty stood up out of it like a finger.

He took her out onto it at dawn.

Not far. Thirty paces out from the end of the jetty, where the ice was thickest. He'd tested it with the butt of the rod before he let her step down, tapping and listening, the way he tested everything. Then he stood her in the middle of a patch of clean grey ice with her practice sword, and stepped back, and said, "The next lesson."

Alea's ears went up. There hadn't been a *next lesson* in months. Not since the autumn, when he'd shown her the second form of the overhead cut and then gone back to just watching, the way he did. Correcting. *Your weight's on your heels.* She'd assumed there wasn't any more to teach. Not until she got to the second tier. Not until she was ready for the sword.

"Ice is good for this," said her father. "You can't cheat on ice. If your weight's wrong, you'll know."

"My weight's never wrong."

"Then this'll be easy."

It wasn't easy.

***

The first thing he taught her was about pushing.

"You push it," he said, watching her go through the first form on the slippery grey ice. "Your aura. When you want it to come up. You push it up from your belly into your arms, like you're shoving a door." He shook his head. "Don't. It doesn't like being pushed. Let it rise. Like water filling a jar. You don't push water into a jar. You just tip the jar and wait."

Alea frowned. "I don't push it."

"You do. Everyone does at first. Watch your shoulders."

She did the form again, watching her shoulders. And there it was: a tiny lift, a hunch, right at the moment she called the aura up. Like someone bracing to shove.

She stopped. She stood there on the ice and breathed, and called it up again. *Let* it come up, this time. Tipped the jar and waited.

It came up slower. Much slower. But it came up *smooth*, the way it never had before. Like water. And her shoulders stayed down.

"Better," said her father.

***

The second thing he taught her was about the back foot.

"Your aura runs out through your sword," he said. "Good. That's what it's for. But it also has to run *down*. Into the ground. Through your back foot." He tapped the ice with the rod. "If it can't go down, it has nowhere to stand. It rises, and then it has nothing under it, and it falls back. Like a wave with no beach."

Alea stared at him.

She'd heard that before. Not from him. From PenguChi. In the yard of the militia hall, in the autumn, after they'd sparred, sitting on the wall with their legs dangling, PenguChi had said in her slow, soft, puzzled voice: *it keeps coming up and then falling back down. Like a wave. I don't know why. It comes up, and there's nothing for it to stand on, and it just... goes away again.*

Alea hadn't known what to say. She'd never had that problem. Her aura had always gone down through her feet without her thinking about it, because her father had taught her to stand that way when she was thirteen, before she'd ever felt aura at all. She'd never had to learn it on purpose.

But PenguChi had.

And PenguChi had been stuck, all winter, at the place where it came up and fell back. Twenty percent. Twenty-one. Climbing so slowly, one percent a week, the way she did everything. Never quite *staying*.

Alea looked at her father.

He was looking at the ice, tapping it lightly with the butt of the rod, as if he were testing its thickness. He wasn't looking at her at all.

"Again," he said. "Back foot. Let it go down."

***

The third thing he taught her was about breathing.

"On the out-breath," he said. "Not the in-breath. Everyone calls it up on the in-breath, because breathing in feels like gathering. Like filling up." He shook his head. "But the in-breath is tight. Your chest is full. There's no room. Call it on the out-breath, when everything's loose and empty and still. That's when it can come up and *stay*."

Alea stood on the ice with her practice sword, and breathed out, long and slow, until her chest was empty and still. And called it up.

It came. Up from the bottom of her, slow and smooth, like water filling a jar, and down through her back foot into the ice, and *stayed*. Just *stayed*, warm and steady, filling her right to the edges, the way it had only ever done once or twice before when she was very tired and not trying at all.

She stood there and felt it. Then she looked up.

Her father was watching her. Just watching. The way he always did.

And Alea knew.

Not suspected. Knew. The way you know your own name.

Three lessons. Pushing; the back foot; the out-breath. She'd never had problems with any of them. Not really. Not the way PenguChi did. Not the way PenguChi had described to her, sitting on the wall in the autumn, and in the snow at midwinter, and in the yard last week: *it comes up and falls back down. Like there's nothing for it to stand on. Like it can't find room.*

Three lessons. Exactly the three things PenguChi needed. Exactly the three things Alea had never needed at all.

He knew.

He knew where she went every day. He'd known for months. Since the first week, probably; since the first two fish in the basket, which she'd always thought was just him being kind. He knew about PenguChi. He knew she was stuck. And he'd stood Alea on the ice and taught her three things she didn't need, so she'd carry them up the hill to someone who did.

She stood on the frozen lake with her aura warm inside her and her eyes stinging, and looked at him.

He looked back. Patient. Tired. His face giving away nothing at all, the way it had given Thea nothing in the market.

"Again," he said. "From the first form."

Alea didn't say anything.

She wanted to. She wanted to say *Dad, I know*. She wanted to say *you've known all along, haven't you*. She wanted to say *why didn't you just tell me*, and *thank you*, and a hundred other things, all at once, the way her mother always used to, at the worst possible moment.

But he hadn't said anything either. All those months. Two fish in the basket every morning, and then three, and then four, and never once a word. The coats that had come up the hill from the *mayor*. And now this. Three lessons, on the ice, with his eyes on the ice and not on her.

He was giving her the choice. Whether to say it out loud. Whether to make it a thing between them, or leave it where it was, quiet, in the background, where it could just keep happening.

She thought about the other thing she was carrying. The thing she hadn't told him since the autumn. *Three years.* She thought about how heavy it was, and how she still didn't know what it would do to him.

And she thought: *we both have things we're not saying. And we both know the other one knows.*

It was the strangest feeling. Like standing on ice and knowing exactly how thick it was.

"From the first form," said Alea, and lifted her sword.

***

She went up the hill that afternoon with four fish in the basket and three lessons in her head.

PenguChi was in the far corner of the yard, where she always was. Standing in her wide stance with her eyes closed and Waddles sitting on the wall beside her, the way he always did, like a small yellow coach. Bram was watching from his bucket by the door. When Alea came in, PenguChi opened her eyes and smiled her slow, soft smile.

"Hi," she said. "You're late."

"I had a lesson," said Alea. "On the ice." She put down the basket. "I want to show you something."

She showed her.

The first lesson: don't push it. Let it rise, like water in a jar. PenguChi frowned, and tried, and stopped hunching her shoulders, and it came up slower, and smoother.

The second lesson: the back foot. Let it go down into the ground, so it has somewhere to stand. PenguChi stood very still, listening, the way she listened to everything. And Alea saw her shift her weight, just slightly, onto her back foot, and her face changed.

The third lesson: the out-breath. Not the in-breath. Call it when everything's loose and empty and still.

PenguChi breathed out. Long, and slow, and steady. Her chest went still. And she called it.

Alea felt it happen. Standing right next to her, a pace away, she felt PenguChi's aura come up from the very bottom of her, slow and smooth, like water filling a jar. And then go *down*, through her back foot, into the cold flagstones of the yard. And *stay*.

It stayed.

PenguChi didn't move. She stood there in her wide stance, with her eyes closed and her hands open at her sides, and her aura stayed. Five seconds. Ten. Twenty. Warm and steady and full, filling her right to the edges, not falling back. Not going away.

On the wall, Waddles made a noise like a squeaky door being opened very slowly.

PenguChi opened her eyes.

"Oh," she said. Very softly. In that voice that steadied everyone, that had steadied Xola on the first day and Thea in the snow. "Oh. It *stayed*."

"It stayed," said Alea.

PenguChi looked at her. Her eyes were very bright. Then, slowly, she opened her window, and looked at it, and her mouth fell open.

"Twenty-eight," she said. "It was twenty-one this morning. *Twenty-eight.*"

From his bucket by the door, Bram stood up so fast he knocked it over. "Seven?" he said. "*Seven*, in one go? Penguin, that's—"

"PenguChi," said PenguChi automatically, and then she laughed, and then she grabbed Alea's hands and held them and didn't let go. "Where did you *learn* that? Who taught you that?"

Alea opened her mouth.

*Someone told me. Someone who could see it.* That was what she'd said in the autumn. *My dad always said.* That was what she'd said at the bonfire.

"My dad," she said.

She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. PenguChi just squeezed her hands and smiled her slow, soft, enormous smile.

"Tell him thank you," said PenguChi. "From me."

***

She walked home in the dusk, down the lake road, with the empty basket over her arm.

The lake was grey and frozen and very still. The end of the jetty stood up out of the ice like a finger. And at the end of it, as she came down the path, she could see a figure sitting in the cold, with something across his knees. A net, probably. He was always mending something.

She didn't go down to him. She went into the cabin, and put the basket by the door, and stood there for a moment in the warm dark, by the fire he'd banked for her before he went out.

*Tell him thank you. From me.*

She wouldn't. Not tonight. Maybe not for a long time. That wasn't how it worked between them, apparently. Not anymore.

But in the morning, when she came down the stairs yawning with her ears flat, there would be fish in the basket by the door. Four of them. And she would pick it up, and stand there for a moment, and look at him at the stove with his back to her.

And she would say, "Bye, Dad."

And he would say, "Bye."

And they would both know exactly what that meant.
