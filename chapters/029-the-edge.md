Ninety-nine percent was the worst number in the world.

Thea had been sitting on it for eleven days. Eleven days of opening her window every morning and every night and every hour in between, and seeing it there, unchanged, smug, one single percent from the edge.

[ Althea · Tier 0: Initiate · Progress to Spark: 99% ]

She'd tried everything. She'd sat in the yard in the snow until she couldn't feel her legs. She'd read the lattice under her window for so long that she saw it when she closed her eyes: the nested lines, the conditions, the strange looping function that called itself over and over at the bottom of everything. She'd written to Chris twice. She'd written to Cursiffe. She'd stopped sleeping, mostly, and then Maren had caught her and made her sleep, and she'd lain awake instead, staring at the ceiling, with the edge of the first tier right there in front of her like the last line of a program she couldn't make compile.

"It's like a sneeze," said Xola, at breakfast, on the eleventh day. "You can't make it come by trying. You have to look at a bright light."

"That's not how magic works."

"How do you know? Have you *done* it?"

Thea didn't have an answer to that.

***

The midwinter festival in Wrenhaven was on the twelfth day.

It wasn't like the market in Kingsferry that Deston had written about, with its thousands of people and its candle-boats on the river. Wrenhaven was small. The festival was small. It was a bonfire in the market square, as tall as a house, built over three days by every man and boy in town under the direction of the mayor, Rowan Hale, who stood in the middle of the square in his chain of office bellowing instructions like a sergeant. There were trestle tables round the edges, piled with food: roast pork and bread and honey cakes and a great steaming vat of something hot and spiced that Bram had already been warned off twice. There was a fiddler, and a man with a drum, and a woman with a pipe, playing tunes that everyone in the square seemed to know but them. And there were lanterns, hundreds of them, hung from every eave and every window round the square, so that the snow on the rooftops glowed gold.

They went together, all of them. Maren too, in a good grey cloak with her hair, for once, a little less tightly pinned. Bram went straight for the spiced vat and was warned off a third time.

And Alea met them at the edge of the square, in a red scarf, with her tail fluffed out against the cold and a look on her face like the whole festival had been arranged for her personal enjoyment.

"Isn't it *wonderful,*" she said, and grabbed Xola's hand, and dragged her off toward the honey cakes.

"Is your dad coming?" Higu asked, when they came back.

It was a perfectly ordinary question. Thea saw Alea hear it, and saw the tiny pause before she answered.

"He doesn't like crowds," said Alea. "He says he's too old for them." She shrugged and smiled. "He'll be at home. Mending something."

Higu didn't say anything. But Thea saw her glance, very briefly, out past the edge of the square, past the last of the lanterns, toward the dark where the lake road went down to the water.

***

They lit the bonfire at full dark.

Rowan Hale did it himself, with a torch, after a speech that went on slightly too long and that nobody minded. He touched the torch to the kindling at the base, and the flame caught and ran up through the dry branches with a sound like a great breath being drawn in. Then the whole thing went up at once, a column of fire as tall as the houses around it, roaring, and the whole square cheered.

And the sparks went up.

Thousands of them. Pouring up out of the top of the bonfire into the black sky, swirling, spiralling, bright gold against the dark, rising and rising until they faded out among the stars. The crowd went *ahhh*. Children shrieked and pointed. Somewhere the fiddler struck up a reel.

Thea stood very still, and watched the sparks go up.

And she saw the lattice.

Not under her window. Under the *fire*. She'd never seen it outside a window before; she hadn't known she could. But it was there. A structure in the flames, faint and shifting and impossibly complicated: lines of heat and light and mana nested inside each other, folding and unfolding, calling themselves over and over the way the function at the bottom of her window did. Every spark that went up carried a tiny piece of the pattern with it. A single line of code, burning, rising, going out.

She stared at it.

It was the same. That was the thing that made her breath stop. The structure in the fire and the structure under her window were written in the same language. The same shapes, the same nesting, the same looping heart. She'd been reading it for months and thinking of it as the window's language. It wasn't. It was the world's. The window was just the first place she'd seen it.

"Thea?" said Gigichi, beside her.

Thea didn't answer. She was looking at the sparks.

For eleven days she'd been trying to *read* her way over the edge. Reading more, reading deeper, reading harder, as if the last one percent were a line she hadn't found yet. But that wasn't it. She'd read everything. She knew the language. She'd known it for weeks.

You didn't learn a language by reading it. Not all the way. At some point you had to *write.*

She held up her hand.

She didn't know what she was doing. She did it anyway. She held up her right hand in front of her face, with the bonfire roaring and the sparks going up, and she looked at the lattice in the air around her fingertip: the faint, shifting structure of the cold and the dark and the mana in the air. And she found the place in it where a line was missing. A gap. A space where the next character should go.

And she wrote it.

Not with a pen. Not with anything. She just reached into the structure with the part of herself that had been reading it for six months and *put something there*: one small line, in the world's own language, in a gap that had been waiting for it.

*Here. Light.*

***

There was a flame on her fingertip.

Small. Smaller than a candle. A little bead of gold light sitting on the end of her index finger, flickering in the cold, not burning her, not doing anything at all except *being there*. Warm. Steady. Hers.

She stared at it.

Next to her, Gigichi made a sound like she'd been punched.

"Thea," she whispered. "*Thea.*"

The window opened on its own. Thea didn't ask it to.

[ Althea · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Parse ]

*Spark.* The first tier. The first rung of the ladder that counted. Six months, almost to the day, since she'd woken up on the floor of the summoning hall.

And under it, in the tab that had been empty since the first night: one word.

*Parse.*

She laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her shaky and startled and much too loud, and the flame on her finger wobbled and nearly went out, and she laughed harder, because of course. Of *course* that was her first skill. Of course it was a programming word. Of course the world had looked at her and decided that what she did was *read things and work out what they meant.*

"*Parse,*" she said, out loud, to nobody, to the bonfire, to the sky. "It's called *Parse.*"

***

And the sealed value shivered.

She felt it before she saw it. Deep down, at the bottom of the lattice, under everything: the line she'd found on the first night. *Medium: one blade.* And next to it, the second value. The one that was sealed like a door with no handle. The one that had rattled every time Alea came near, and once, on the road, the first week, when a fisherman walked past.

It shook. Hard. Like a door in a gale.

And for one heartbeat, as the first tier settled into her and the flame burned on her fingertip and the whole of the lattice seemed to shift one step upward, something surfaced on the other side of the door.

A shape.

A letter. It looked like a letter. It looked, for one heartbeat, almost exactly like a letter she knew, from her own alphabet, the one nobody in this world could read.

Then it sank, and the door was shut again, and the value was sealed.

She stood there with her heart going very fast.

*Look at the root,* Chris had written. *See if it changes.*

So she did. She reached down, past the sealed value, past everything, to the very bottom of her window: the place where every window she'd ever looked at began. The root. The line that pointed *away*, out of the window, toward the first window that everything else was copied from.

At Initiate, she hadn't been able to follow it. It had gone nowhere she could see.

At Spark, she could see one step further.

Not much. Not where it went. But she could see what it *was*, now. She could see the shape of the thing at the other end, the way you can see the shape of a house through fog, without seeing the door.

It was running.

That was the only word she had for it, and it was a programming word, and it was the right one. The first window, the original, the one every window in the world had been copied from: it wasn't finished. It wasn't a template sitting in a drawer somewhere. It was *running*. Warm. Live. Somewhere at the other end of that line, the very first window in the world was still open.

Which meant that whoever it belonged to was still alive.

***

"Thea!" Xola was shrieking. "Thea, Thea, *THEA!*"

And then everyone was there at once. Xola with her arms round Thea's neck and Squiddles squashed between them, crying and laughing. PenguChi with both hands over her mouth and her eyes enormous, Waddles squeaking. Higu standing very still, very close, looking at the flame on Thea's finger with an expression of pure fierce joy. And Gigichi, who'd been beside her the whole time, who'd been the first to see it, standing a pace away with her silver hair falling round her face and her eyes very bright.

"Of course you're first," said Gigichi.

She'd said it before. Months ago, at the breakfast table, in the first week, bitter and defensive: *of course you're ahead*. Thea had heard it as a knife.

This time she said it like a gift.

Thea let go of Xola and turned and hugged her, hard, flame and all. And Gigichi hugged her back, harder, and neither of them said anything at all, because there wasn't anything that needed saying.

Over Gigichi's shoulder, Thea saw Maren.

The guide was standing a little apart, in her good grey cloak, by the corner of a trestle table. She was looking at Thea. And for the first time in six months, for the first time since she'd checked their names off a list in a castle courtyard, Maren was smiling. Not a small polite smile. A real one, wide and unguarded, that made her look ten years younger.

Bram was beside her, with a cup of the spiced stuff he'd finally got his hands on. He raised it toward Thea across the square, and drank, and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, and pretended he hadn't.

***

And Alea.

Alea was standing at the edge of the firelight, in her red scarf, with her hands pressed together in front of her mouth. Her ears were straight up. Her tail had gone completely still. She was looking at the little flame on Thea's fingertip with an expression Thea had never seen on her face before.

She looked like someone watching something she'd been told about all her life, finally happening in front of her.

Thea went to her. She didn't know why. She just did.

"Alea," she said. "Look."

"I see it." Alea's voice was very small. "I see it. That's... that's the first tier. That's Spark." She swallowed. "You did it."

"I wrote it," said Thea. "That's how. I didn't read it. I *wrote* it."

Alea looked up at her. Her eyes were very sharp, and very kind, and very wet.

"My dad always said," she began, and then stopped.

Thea waited.

"He always said," said Alea, more carefully, "that the first tier is the hardest one. Not because it's difficult. Because it's the first time the world answers back." She smiled, shakily. "He said you never forget it. The first time the world answers back."

She threw her arms round Thea's neck and hugged her, hard, and Thea felt the sealed value at the bottom of her window rattle against its door like something trying to get out.

She didn't say anything about it. Not tonight.

But over Alea's shoulder, past the edge of the firelight and the last of the lanterns, she looked out at the dark where the lake road went down to the water. And she thought about a fisherman who didn't like crowds, mending something at home on the longest night of the year. She thought about a window somewhere in the world that was still running. She thought about the shape of a letter, surfacing for one heartbeat and then sinking again.

She held the little flame up in front of her, small and gold and steady in the cold, and watched the sparks from the bonfire go up past it into the dark, thousands of them, each one a single burning line of the world's own language, rising and rising until they were lost among the stars.

The first time the world answers back.

She thought she'd remember it for the rest of her life.
