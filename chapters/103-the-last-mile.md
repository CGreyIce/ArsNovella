The last thing Gabe ever said to him was *sleep, Declan. Seriously.*

He'd said it every night for eleven years. On calls, in chats, at the door of his flat when Declan stayed over too late. *Sleep.* Because Declan never did. He'd stay up till four playing one more game, one more round, one more match, and then drag himself through the next day like a man walking through mud, and Gabe would tell him off, and he'd say *trust*, and do it again.

That night, the last night, there'd been five of them on the call. Yan and Mashi and Newish and Gabe, and Declan blaming the server for a shot he'd missed. And at nearly two in the morning, Declan had said goodnight to everyone, one at a time, the way he did. And Gabe had said what he always said.

*Sleep, Declan. Seriously.*

And Declan had.

Gabe had stood at the back of a room full of people in black a week later, beside Deston, in a shirt that was too tight across the shoulders, and watched Declan's dad shake every single hand that was held out to him and not cry once. And later, outside, he'd seen him sitting on a low wall at the edge of the car park on his own, very straight, looking at nothing. And after a while a woman had come out and sat down beside him, and taken his hand, and Gabe had thought: *good. He's not on his own.*

And then he'd gone home and not slept either. For about a week.

He'd been thinking about that wall all morning.

***

The lake opened out at dawn.

Gabe had been in the stern, where he always was, keeping out of the way of the people who knew how to sail. He'd felt it before he saw it. The *Patient Widow* coming out of the last long reach of the river between low green hills, and the hills falling back on both sides, and the air changing. Getting bigger. And then the barge was out of the river and onto the lake, and Gabe stood up in the stern without meaning to, and looked.

It went on forever.

Grey and silver under a white spring sky, flat and still, going away north to a dark line of pines on the far shore so distant that they looked like a smudge of ink. The ice was going. There were still white floes out in the middle, drifting, and a rim of grey ice along the north shore under the trees. But near the south end, where the river came out, the water was open and dark and very still, with the sky in it, so that the barge seemed to be floating in the middle of a white bowl.

And at the foot of it, where the lake narrowed into the river, there was a town.

Timber and stone. Smoke from a hundred chimneys going straight up into the still air. A church with a square tower. A market square. A long timber hall on a hill above the town with a yard in front of it. Boats drawn up on a shingle beach below a mill. A jetty with fishing boats.

Wrenhaven.

Haskett had said the name last night. The tavern-keeper at the causeway had said it. Every one of the thirty-five had known the name for a year and a half. It was where Group 1 was posted. It was in the channel every night: *Thea, Wrenhaven*. *Higu, Wrenhaven*.

It looked like nowhere. It looked like a hundred other towns on a hundred other rivers.

Gabe stood in the stern and looked at it and couldn't breathe.

***

Deston was at the bow.

He'd been at the bow for fifteen days. Since Kingsferry. He hadn't left it, as far as Gabe could tell, except to sleep, and some nights not even then. With his hand out over the water, first, pointing north. Then, since the gorge, both hands on the rail, holding on, leaning forward over it like a dog on a lead.

He wasn't leaning now.

He was standing very straight at the bow of the *Patient Widow*, with his hands at his sides, looking at the town. Not at the town. *Past* it. Along the shore. To the east of the town, where a road ran out along the edge of the lake, between a long stone wall and the reeds, for a quarter of a mile, and stopped.

Gabe followed his eyes.

At the end of the road, by the water, there was a cabin. Timber gone silver. A stone chimney with smoke coming out of it. A shed. Something that might have been a hen-yard. A long low shape on the shingle beside it under a roof of sailcloth that might have been anything.

And a jetty. Running out from the shore on old grey posts, over the dark open water, with nets hung along it.

And on the end of the jetty, very small at this distance, something that might have been a person. Sitting. With something long and thin held out over the water.

Deston didn't say anything.

He didn't need to.

***

The barge came in to the landing below the mill.

Gabe didn't remember much of it. Haskett at the tiller, not roaring for once. The boys on the poles. The sail coming down. Calvin sliding down the mast. The landing, a long shingle beach with fishing boats drawn up on it and a few nets drying on poles and two old men mending one, who stopped mending and stood up and stared at a salt barge that had just come up the lake from the river under a sail full the wrong way.

People were coming down from the town. Of course they were. Gabe heard them before he saw them, a murmur, getting louder. A barge was unusual. A barge in spring, before the river traders, was strange. A barge with thirteen strangers on it, two of them Reaching swordswomen with their swords out across their knees, and a man in the stern the size of a door, coming up the lake under a wind that wasn't blowing, was something else.

Deston was off the barge before it stopped.

He didn't jump, this time. He just stepped off. Onto the shingle. And started walking. Not toward the town. Not toward the people coming down from it. Along the beach. East. Toward the place where the beach ended and a road began.

Gabe went after him.

He didn't decide to. He just went. Over the rail, onto the shingle, with his blade on his back, after Deston. And behind him, he heard the others come. Xav first, because Xav always came first when it mattered. Then the rest. John, and Malcolm, and Azac, and RX, wherever RX was. Rachael with Dennis on one side and Calvin on the other. Emrys. Sabine. Petra, last, with her sword in her hand and her eyes on everything.

Haskett stayed on the barge with his boys. He watched them go, from the tiller, with his pipe unlit in his teeth. He didn't say anything. He took his cap off.

***

The town was out.

They had to go through the edge of it to get to the road. Past the mill, and the boat sheds, and up a short lane between two rows of houses to the corner of the market square, where the road along the lake started. And the town was out. In doorways, at windows, along the lane, in the square. In silence.

Not hostile. Gabe had been stared at in a lot of towns, in a year and a half. He knew what hostile felt like. This wasn't it. This was something else. Two hundred people, standing in their doorways and at their windows in the spring morning, watching thirteen strangers come up from the landing behind a man who was walking as if he were being pulled on a rope. And not saying anything. And not following.

As if they'd seen this before.

A baker, at his door, with flour to his elbows. A big soot-faced man at a forge on the square, with a hammer in his hand. A thin woman in black with keys at her belt, standing very straight on the steps of a stone building, with her hand at her mouth.

Nobody asked who they were. Nobody asked where they were going. They just watched, and made room.

Gabe had never been anywhere like it.

***

Group 1 was at the corner of the square.

He knew them before he was close enough to see their faces. He'd seen them in the summoning hall, the first morning, in the chaos, among thirty-five frightened people. He'd seen them that night in the barracks, comparing windows. And he'd read every word they'd written in the channel for a year and a half, every number, every line, every *rule three*, every *good news, loudly*.

Five of them. Standing together at the corner where the lake road left the square. In heavy wool coats, mended, each one exactly the right length.

A girl with pink and violet streaks in her hair. Thea. A girl with a white bow, with a notebook, very still. Higu. A girl with silver hair and her arms folded and her jaw set, as if she were about to tell somebody off. Gigichi. A small girl with lavender hair melting into pink and a purple creature on her head, already crying. Xola. And a girl with a yellow heart in her hair and a penguin inside the front of her coat, standing very calmly, with her golden eyes on Gabe's face. PenguChi.

And with them, a little in front of them, the way someone stands in front of people they've decided to protect, a sixth.

A fox girl. Sixteen, perhaps. Fox ears, standing very straight up. A fox's tail, very still. A black sword on her back in a plain leather harness, with an old dark grip standing up over her shoulder.

She was looking at Gabe and Deston. Not at the others. At the two of them. Her face was doing something Gabe couldn't read at all.

***

Deston stopped.

He'd been walking since the landing, steadily, without looking at anything, like a man being pulled. He stopped at the corner of the square in front of Group 1, because they were standing in his way. Not in his way, exactly. Beside it. At the top of the road. Where it went down, along the lake, between the stone wall and the reeds, a quarter of a mile, to a cabin and a jetty.

Gabe stopped beside him.

Nobody said anything.

Then Thea said, "Gabe."

He looked at her. She'd never said his name. Not out loud. He'd never heard her voice except once, in the hall, a year and a half ago, saying something about code. She said it now as if she'd been saying it for years.

"Thea," said Gabe.

"He doesn't know you're coming," said Thea. Her voice was shaking. "None of you. He doesn't know. You kept it out. We saw that you kept it out." She swallowed. "He's been reading every line for a year and a half and he doesn't know."

"Good," said Gabe.

He hadn't known he was going to say it. It came out rough. Thea looked at him for a moment. And then her face did something, crumpling and lighting at the same time, and she laughed, a short wet startled laugh, and put her hand over her mouth.

***

"We know," said Higu.

Quietly. From beside Thea. With her notebook held shut against her chest. She was looking at Gabe and Deston and Xav, one after another, the way somebody looks at people they've been writing about in a book for a long time and are finally seeing.

"We know," said Higu again. "Go on."

She stepped aside.

They all did. All five of them. Out of the top of the road, onto the grass at the edge of the square, the way you'd step out of a doorway to let somebody through. Xola still crying, with Squiddles gone gold on her head. Gigichi with her arms folded, glaring at the sky, blinking very fast. PenguChi with her hand on Xola's arm. Thea with her hand over her mouth.

Not the fox girl.

***

The fox girl didn't step aside.

She stood at the top of the lake road, in front of it, with the black sword on her back, and looked at Gabe and Deston. At their faces. Very carefully. One and then the other. The way you'd look at a picture of somebody you'd heard about all your life, to see if it matched.

"You're Gabe," she said.

Gabe stared at her.

"And you're Deston," said the fox girl. She looked at Deston. "You sang the middle part. He sang the top. Too loud. He always says he sang it too loud." Her ears had gone very slightly flat, then up again. "And you," to Gabe, "sang the low part, and you always told him to go to bed."

Nobody said anything. Gabe couldn't. He was standing at the corner of a market square in a town he'd never been to, in another world, and a fox girl he'd never seen in his life was telling him what part he sang in a school choir in Singapore at fifteen.

"How do you—" said Deston. His voice didn't work properly.

"Bedtime stories," said the fox girl. "Every night. One winter. When I was thirteen. All thirty-five of you." She looked at Xav, behind them. "And you're Xav. You're the one he talked into Counter-Strike, and then you got better than him, and he never forgave you."

Xav made a sound Gabe had never heard him make.

***

The fox girl looked at them a moment longer. Then she lifted her chin, very slightly, the way a person does when they're about to say something they've been rehearsing.

"I'm Alea," she said.

***

Gabe sat down.

He didn't decide to. His knees just went. He sat down on the low stone wall at the corner of the square, the one that ran along the top of the lake road, very heavily, the way he'd sat down on the roof above the chandler's the night Deston finally told him about the thread. He sat there with his hands on his knees and looked at the fox girl, and couldn't see her very well.

*Alea.*

He knew that name. He'd known it since he was fifteen. Declan had called everything Alea. Every horse in every game. Every pet. Every companion, every party member, every NPC that followed you round. Every single one, for eleven years. *Alea.* Gabe had asked him once, at sixteen, why. And Declan had said, very seriously, the way he said things he wasn't joking about, *it's a good name. One day I'm going to give it to someone who deserves it.*

And then *trust*.

Only four of the thirty-five knew that. Aleaka, who'd teased him about it. And Gabe. And Deston. And Xav.

Three of the four were standing at the top of a lake road, looking at a fox girl with a black sword on her back.

*One day I'm going to give it to someone who deserves it.*

He had.

***

Deston was crying. Openly. Standing at the top of the road with his hands at his sides and his face wet, not wiping it.

Xav, behind them, had put his hand over his eyes and was laughing. Very quietly. Helplessly. The way you laugh at the end of a round when somebody's done something so completely, ridiculously *them* that there's nothing else you can do.

"Of course you are," said Xav, through his hand. "Of *course* you are. Of course he did."

Alea looked at the three of them. Gabe on the wall, and Deston crying, and Xav laughing into his hand. Her face did something. Gabe couldn't read it. He thought, later, that it was probably the face her father made when he was trying very hard not to cry in front of people, and that she'd learned it from him.

"He's on the jetty," said Alea. Her voice had gone very small. "He's been on the jetty since dawn. He's been there every morning. He doesn't know." She swallowed. "He thinks he'll have to wait till the summer. He told Thea, *he'll probably turn up*. He said you always found him first." Her ears went flat. "He doesn't know it's today."

Then she stepped aside.

***

Gabe stood up.

It took him two tries. He stood up off the wall at the corner of the square with his blade on his back, and looked down the lake road.

It ran away from him along the shore. A quarter of a mile. Packed earth, with grass down the middle, between a long stone wall on the left with green fields beyond it, the first shoots coming up through the brown, and on the right the reeds, pale green with the spring, and the lake beyond them, dark and still and open, with the sky in it.

At the end of it, the cabin. The smoke. The hen-yard.

The jetty.

And on the end of the jetty, small, a long way off, with his back to them, a man. Sitting. With his feet over the water. With a fishing rod held out over the lake, at an easy angle, the way you'd hold it on a still morning, waiting.

He didn't turn round.

***

There was a big man at the top of the road.

Gabe hadn't seen him. He must have been there the whole time, standing to one side, by the wall. A big grey bear of a man in his fifties, with a chain round his neck that sat on him like a collar, and a face like a cliff. He was standing at the very top of the lake road, at the edge of it, where it left the square. Not in it. Beside it. Looking at the thirteen of them. At Gabe. At Deston.

At their faces.

Gabe had never seen him before in his life. He didn't know who he was.

The big man looked at them for a long time. Gabe didn't know what he was seeing. He thought, later, that it was probably the same thing the man had seen on five other faces, one evening in the winter, at dusk, with lanterns. The thing faces do when somebody they've been waiting for a very long time is at the bottom of a road.

The big man didn't say anything.

He stepped back. One pace. Off the edge of the road, onto the grass, out of the way.

Then he turned round, and faced the town, and the two hundred people in the square behind them. And folded his arms. And stood there, between the lake road and everybody else, the way a wall stands.

***

Deston put his hand on Gabe's arm.

Gabe looked at him. Deston's face was wet. He wasn't wiping it. He was looking down the lake road, at the man on the end of the jetty, small, with his back to them, fishing.

"Gabe," said Deston.

"I know."

"It's so warm." Deston's voice cracked. "Gabe, it's like standing in a fire. I can't—" He stopped. "It's *him*."

"I know," said Gabe.

He looked down the lake road. A quarter of a mile. He'd come three hundred miles up a river for this. He'd come across a world. He'd stood at the back of a room full of people in black and watched a man shake hands and not cry, and gone home, and not slept for a week. He'd told him to sleep. Every night. For eleven years. And the one night he'd listened.

A quarter of a mile.

"Together," said Deston.

"Together," said Gabe.

***

They went down the lake road.

The two of them in front, side by side, the way they'd stood in the back row of a choir. Not fast. Not slow. Just walking, on the packed earth, between the stone wall and the reeds, with the lake on their right and the sky in it.

Behind them, a little way back, the others. Xav first, alone, a few paces behind, because Xav knew when to wait. Then Group 6, and Group 4, and Emrys, and Sabine, and Petra. And behind them, at the top of the road, Group 1, and a fox girl with a black sword, not following. Watching. And behind them, a big grey man with his arms folded, standing in front of a whole town.

Nobody followed them down.

The man on the end of the jetty didn't turn round. He sat there with his rod held out over the open water, small and still and a long way off, with his back to them, the way he must have sat there every morning for years.

He didn't know.

Gabe looked at his back. At the shape of his shoulders. He didn't know them. It wasn't Declan's back; it couldn't be. It was a stranger's, a man of thirty-five in a plain coat, in a body born in another world. He'd known Declan's back since he was thirteen, walking into a choir room, and this wasn't it.

But the way he was sitting.

Hunched, a little, forward, with his elbows on his knees. Patient. Not doing anything. Just waiting. The way he'd sat at his desk at three in the morning at the end of a lost round, holding an angle, waiting for somebody to make up their mind.

Gabe had seen him sit like that a thousand times.

***

Halfway down the road, Deston started to hum.

Very quietly. Under his breath. The middle part. The one he'd sung at fifteen, in a rehearsal room in Singapore with bad carpet and a piano out of tune, and every night on a roof above a chandler's in Kingsferry for a year and a half.

Gabe didn't decide to. He never did, with this.

He came in under him. The low part.

And the two of them walked down the lake road toward the jetty in the spring morning, side by side, humming *Seasons of Love* without the words, the two parts, the way they had a thousand times.

And when they got to the place where the third part came in, they left the gap.

They left it open. Walking. A quarter of a mile from the end of the road, and then less, and less. The way you'd hold a door.

On the end of the jetty, the man with the rod went very still.
