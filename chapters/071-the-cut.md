Severin had been born at an anvil, more or less.

His mother always said so. She'd been in the forge when her time came, in the river town of Brakewater, in the south, bringing his father his dinner, and there hadn't been time to get her home. So he'd come into the world on a pile of sacking in the corner of a smithy, with the fire going and his father swearing at it, and the first thing he'd ever heard, his mother said, was a hammer on hot iron.

He believed it. He'd never heard a sound he loved more.

He'd grown up in that forge. Plough blades and horseshoes, gate hinges and cartwheel rims, the odd knife for the odd farmer who wanted something better than he could afford. His father had been a good smith. Not a great one. A good one, honest and slow, who said *the iron tells you when it's ready, if you let it*, and *the heat stops being angry*, and *keep your thumb out of the way, you'll want it later*. Severin had worked the bellows from the age of six and the hammer from the age of ten, and by sixteen he'd been better than his father, and his father had known it, and been glad.

And at twenty the war had come through Brakewater, the first war, the long one, and taken him with it, the way it took everyone. And he'd found, in the war, that he could fight. That the thing that made his hands good at iron made them good at other things too. That aura came to him the way heat came to a forge, slowly and then all at once. He'd climbed. Tier after tier, year after year, faster than anyone in the army. Gleaming at twenty-four. Shaped at twenty-nine. Dominion at thirty-five, and everyone saying *the youngest ever*, and *he'll be Ascendant before he's fifty*.

He'd been Ascendant at thirty-nine.

The songs said it happened on a battlefield. In the last great charge of the long war, when he'd broken the line single-handed and the world had bent around him for a heartbeat and everybody had seen. He'd let them sing it. It was a better song.

It hadn't happened on a battlefield. It had happened in Brakewater, in the winter of his thirty-ninth year, on leave, the month his father died. He'd gone home for the burial and found an order on the forge wall in his father's crooked hand: twelve plough blades for the Ashdown farm, due by the thaw, half paid. And he'd stayed. He'd stoked his father's fire, and worked his father's bellows, and made twelve plough blades for the Ashdown farm, one a day, slowly, honestly, the way his father had taught him. Not thinking about the war. Not thinking about tiers. Just the iron, and the heat, and the hammer, and the long quiet winter evenings in the forge where he'd been born.

On the twelfth night, with the last blade on the anvil, the world had gone quiet. And then it had bent.

He'd never told anyone that. Not the Council. Not the court. Not Halvard, though Halvard had guessed. *You have to find your own way up*, the old masters said, *and it's never the way you expect*. His had been twelve plough blades for a farmer called Ashdown, in a dead man's forge, in the middle of winter. He'd never once in twenty-five years been sure anyone would believe him.

The crown had come later. That was a long story, and a dull one, and he'd never enjoyed telling it.

***

He'd felt it the moment he walked into the hen-yard.

He'd known Ars twelve years. He'd walked round him on a parade ground at Cinderhold when he was twenty-two, a sweating Honed swordsman with a slab of black iron on his back, and felt him: raw, furious, enormous, like a forge with the door kicked open. He'd fought beside him. He'd sat beside him on the red ground the morning after, and felt him then too. A forge with the fire gone out. Dominion, two tiers in one night, the whole weight of the eighth tier sitting in him like a great cold stone, with nothing burning under it at all.

He'd stood in the hen-yard and felt him again, after eight years. And he'd nearly dropped his hammers.

The stone was still there. The weight. But something was burning under it again. Not the way it had burned at twenty-two, kicked open and roaring. Slowly. Steadily. Like a forge that somebody had banked very carefully and was bringing back up a little at a time, the way you bring up a fire you mean to keep for a long winter.

He knew that fire. He'd felt it in himself, once, in Brakewater, over twelve plough blades.

He hadn't said anything. He'd picked up the hen instead.

***

They lit the long hearth properly at dawn on the second day.

Not the curing fire, now. The real one. Garrow's charcoal, a whole cartload of it, and the brewer's charcoal on top of that, and the two great bellows going one and then the other, Garrow at the near end with his face rapt and shining, Ars at the far end, going too fast. Severin laid the sword in the fire himself. He and Ars together, one at each end of it, lifting it off the stones where it had lain all night in its canvas and laying it down in the long trough of the hearth, in the heart of the charcoal, the way you'd lay a body on a bier.

Then they heated it.

It took all morning. He'd known it would. You couldn't hurry iron like this. He kept the heat on the middle of it, where the cut would go, a hand's width either side, and let the rest stay cooler, and watched the colour come up. Black, and then the faintest dull red you could only see if you shaded your eyes. Then cherry. Then, by midday, orange, along the middle, glowing through the charcoal like something alive.

The town was on the field above the shore. All of it, again. Hale had come down at dawn and planted himself at the top of the shingle with his arms folded, and the town had stopped behind him in a long quiet line along the field wall, and watched, and nobody had come any further. Kestra was on the jetty, sitting on the boards with her back against a post, with her book on her knee. The girl was on charcoal, carrying it from the cart in a basket, back and forth, without being asked. The hen was on the anvil stump.

Severin worked, and watched the iron, and didn't think about anything else. It was the happiest he'd been in years.

***

He marked the line at noon.

Not in chalk; chalk burned. In soapstone, a white stick of it from his roll, held in the tongs, because you couldn't put your hand near iron that hot. He'd been looking at the sword for two days, on the rug and in the fire, deciding where.

Not the middle. The middle would have been easy, and wrong. The girl's half needed the grip, and the old leather, and the letters by the grip, and enough blade beyond them to be a sword in its own right: shorter, narrower, still black, still heavy, still a slab of a thing, the way the second tier was meant for. And the other half needed to be a stone. A broad flat face to stand at the head of a grave. The point end, where the blade widened before it narrowed.

There was a place, two-fifths of the way from the point, where the grain of the iron changed. You couldn't see it. You could feel it, if you'd spent your life with iron, the way you can feel the grain of a plank through the plane. A place where the iron went from one kind of strong to another. He'd felt it on the rug, the first night, under his hand.

He drew the line there. A thin white stroke across the glowing orange.

"There," he said.

Ars looked at it from the far bellows. Then he looked at the king.

"Who forged this?" said Severin.

***

Ars didn't answer for a while.

He let go of the bellows handle and came round the end of the hearth to the anvil, slowly, and stood looking at the sword in the fire with the white line across it. The heat was coming off it in waves. His face was orange in the light of it, and very still.

"I said I'd tell you at the anvil," he said.

"You did."

"Nobody I know," said Ars.

Severin waited.

"I found it," said Ars.

He said it quietly. Garrow, at the near bellows, had stopped pumping, and the fire had gone down a little, and Severin didn't tell him to start again. On the jetty, Kestra's pencil had stopped.

"Found it where?"

"In Kharvos."

Severin looked at him.

"Past the border," said Ars. "A long way past. Further than anybody's maps go." He was looking at the iron, not at Severin. "I was young. Younger than the parade ground. A garrison boy with a borrowed sword, and too angry to know better. I went in. Once. On my own. Further than I should have. Further than anyone." He was quiet a moment. "I don't know why. I just kept walking. There wasn't anything, for a long time. Weeks of nothing. Black ground and black hills and things that didn't come close. And then there was this."

"Lying there?"

"Standing," said Ars. "In the ground. On a hill, on its own. With nothing round it for miles. No bones. No camp. No tracks. Nobody." He was very still. "Standing in the ground, like a marker. Like somebody had planted it there a very long time ago, over something, and gone away."

Severin didn't say anything. The fire ticked.

"I pulled it out," said Ars. "It took me a day. I couldn't lift it, then. I dragged it. Back west. It took me most of a summer to get it home." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "By the time I got it back to the garrison, I could carry it. By the time you walked round me on the parade ground, I could swing it." He looked at the white line on the glowing iron. "That's all I know. I never found out who made it. Or what it was standing over. I never went back."

***

Severin had been a smith for fifty years.

He'd looked at iron from every corner of the kingdom. From Valcrest and Kingsferry and the mines under the Greyspines. From the old armouries in Solmere that had been there since before the kingdom had a name. He'd looked at Kharvos iron, plenty of it, black and good and brutal, off the bodies of the dead after the long war and the short one. He'd always known, looking at a piece of iron, who'd made it, or what kind of smith, or where. It was what he was for. It was the one thing he'd always known, all his life, without having to think.

He looked at the sword in the fire.

"Not them," he said slowly. "Not Kharvos. I know their iron. That isn't theirs."

"No."

"It burned them. On the Redmarch. You said. When they took hold of it."

"It always did," said Ars. "Every time. All through the war. They could never stand to touch it." He looked at Severin across the anvil. "I don't know what that means. I've never known. I just knew it was the one thing they were afraid of, and it was mine."

Severin stood there with the soapstone in his tongs and looked at the iron he couldn't name.

He'd never once in his life not known.

He found that he was smiling. He didn't mean to. It was the same smile he'd felt on his face at eleven, the first time his father let him look into the fire of a really big forge, at a fair in the south: the smile of a boy finding out that the world was bigger than he'd thought.

"Well," he said. "Then let's find out what's inside you."

***

He tried the chisel first.

He'd known it wouldn't work. Some part of him had known since the hall, before the summoning, when he'd put his hand on this iron for the first time and felt it hold. But you had to try. That was what his father had always said. You tried the honest way first, and then you knew.

It was his best chisel. A hot-cut, broad and heavy, that he'd forged himself thirty years ago and never once had to sharpen. He set it on the white line, on the glowing iron, held in the tongs by Ars, and lifted the big hammer, and struck.

*Clank.*

It bounced.

He looked at the line. Not a mark. Not a scratch. The chisel had gone off the orange iron as if he'd hit a cliff face with a twig.

He struck again. Harder. With the ninth tier in it, this time, a little: aura down his arms and into the haft. *Clank.* Again. *Clank.* He felt the shock of it go up his arms into his shoulders. He felt the sweat start on his back. He looked at the line.

Nothing.

He struck for an hour.

He didn't stop. He put more in each time, and more, until he was red to the elbow with heat and aura and the anvil was ringing under him and the town on the field above had gone completely silent, and Garrow at the bellows was white to the lips. He put more into that chisel than he'd put into anything since the long war. And the iron on the anvil, glowing orange in the noon sun, took every blow and gave nothing back. Not a mark.

At the end of the hour he stopped, and lowered the hammer, and stood there breathing hard, and looked at it.

And laughed.

***

"It held," he said.

He couldn't stop laughing. It came up out of him in great helpless gusts, with the sweat running into his eyes, and the hammer hanging from his hand, and the hen on the anvil stump looking at him reproachfully.

"Fifty years," said Severin. "Fifty years, and I've never met a piece of iron I couldn't mark. Not one." He wiped his face on his forearm. "It *held*."

"I told you it would," said Ars.

"You did not tell me."

"I told you it was strange iron."

"*Strange*." Severin shook his head. "It's not strange. It's impossible. I'm the ninth tier, Ars. I just hit it with the ninth tier for an hour. It doesn't *care*."

"No," said Ars. "It never did."

Severin looked at him across the anvil.

"You could cut it," he said. "You said so, in the letter."

"I could."

"With the rod?"

"With the rod. It'd go through." Ars looked at the white line. "But it'd go through the way I go through things. Clean. All the way. Like it wasn't there." He was quiet a moment. "It wouldn't be a cut you could work. It'd go through the grain like it wasn't there either. You'd have two pieces of iron that didn't know how to be anything anymore. You'd heat them and they'd crack." He shook his head. "I'd ruin it. I'd have two dead halves."

Severin knew he was right. He'd known since the letter. You couldn't cut iron that you meant to work again the way you cut through a demon. You had to cut it the way a smith cuts: along the grain, with the iron's agreement, so that both halves remembered what they were.

He looked down at his hot-cut on the anvil. At the soapstone line. At his own hands.

"Then we'll do it together," he said.

***

He'd seen it done once, in the long war.

Not with a sword. With a gate. The great iron gate of a fortress on the eastern border, that the enemy had barred and chained, and a Dominion swordsman and a smith had cut the chain together. The swordsman holding the chisel, with his aura in it, so that the chisel could cut what it touched, the way anything could cut in an Unbound man's hands. The smith bringing the hammer down, so that the cut went where a smith wanted it, and stopped where a smith wanted it to stop. One to give the edge. One to give the blow.

It had taken them both, and it had worked, and Severin had never seen it done since.

He explained it. Ars listened, standing by the anvil, with his face orange in the light of the fire. He didn't argue. When Severin had finished, he just held out his hand for the chisel.

Severin gave it to him.

"It needs your aura in the steel," he said. "All of it, at the edge, and none of it anywhere else. Not *through* it. *In* it. You're not cutting the sword. You're making the chisel into something that can." He picked up the big hammer. "And when I bring this down, it'll have the ninth tier in it. All of it. You'll feel the world go."

"I know what it feels like."

"You've felt it from the outside. You've never held something while it happened." Severin looked at him. "Don't let go. Whatever you feel. Don't let go of the chisel until it's through."

"I won't."

They looked at each other across the anvil.

***

Ars set the chisel on the line.

He held it in his bare hands. Severin hadn't expected that. You held a hot-cut with tongs, or a rod, or a wrapped handle, never your hands; the iron under it was orange-hot, and the heat would come up through the steel. Ars held it in his bare hands, the haft gripped in both fists, the edge resting exactly on the white soapstone line across the glowing sword, and Severin opened his mouth to tell him and then didn't. Because he could feel it. The aura coming down Ars's arms, into his hands, into the haft, and not into the haft at all, not really. Past it. Into the very edge of the chisel, the last hair's breadth of steel where it touched the iron, and nowhere else.

It was the most precise thing he'd ever felt. Like a man threading a needle with the whole sea.

The heat didn't touch Ars's hands. It couldn't get to them. There wasn't room.

"Ready?" said Severin.

Ars didn't look up from the chisel. "No," he said.

"Good," said Severin. "Nobody ever is."

He lifted the hammer.

He let it come up in him. All of it. Not the way he'd struck for the last hour, adding a little and a little more. All of it, all at once, the whole of the ninth tier, the thing that had come to him in a dead man's forge in Brakewater over twelve plough blades for a farmer called Ashdown. He let it come up through his legs and his back and his shoulders and down his arms into the haft of the hammer, and he felt the world, the way he always felt it, at the very top of the ninth tier, begin to lean.

Then he brought the hammer down.

***

The world bent.

Only for a heartbeat. Only around the anvil. He felt it go, the way he'd felt it go twenty-five years ago in Brakewater: everything around him, the fire and the shingle and the lake and the sky, leaning in toward the place where the hammer met the chisel, the way water leans in toward a drain. Bending. Not breaking. Just *bending*, for one heartbeat, around one blow.

And at the bottom of the bend, where the hammer met the chisel and the chisel met the iron, the sea.

He'd never felt anything like it. The ninth tier coming down from above, and meeting, at the edge of the chisel, the eighth tier coming up from below, and neither of them giving way. Two of the strongest things in the kingdom, pressed together on a hair's breadth of steel. He felt Ars's hands not move. Not by a grain. He felt Ars hold it.

And the iron, which had held against everything for fifty years of his life and longer, felt the two of them, together, and agreed.

It parted.

***

The note went out over the lake.

High and clear and long, like a bell. Severin felt it more than heard it, through the hammer, through his arms, through his feet on the shingle. It came up out of the anvil and went out across the water in every direction at once, and rang off the hills on the far shore and came back, and went on, and on, and on, until it seemed to him to be ringing inside his own ribs.

Then it stopped.

The sword lay on the anvil in two pieces.

The cut was perfect. Severin knew it before he looked. You knew, with a cut like that. A straight clean line exactly along the soapstone, through the glowing iron, so fine you could barely see the gap. Two pieces. A long one with the grip and the old leather and the crooked letters, and a broader one with the point. Lying side by side on Garrow's anvil, orange in the noon light, not quite touching.

And the faces of the cut. He bent over them with the tongs. He couldn't help it.

Folds. Inside the iron, where nobody had ever seen, layer on layer of it, folded and folded and folded again, finer than paper, finer than any smith in Aurelan could have folded iron, finer than Severin could have folded it, thousands of layers, packed so close they shimmered in the light like the inside of a shell.

Somebody had made this. Somebody who was better than him. Long ago, a very long way east, past where anybody's maps went.

He stood over it with the tongs in his hand, and for a moment he couldn't see very well.

***

When he looked up, Ars had put the chisel down.

He'd laid it on the shingle beside the anvil, very carefully, the way you put down something you've been holding a long time. His hands were empty. He was looking at the two halves of the sword on the anvil, and his face was doing nothing at all.

Then he turned round and walked away.

Not fast. Down the shingle, past the hearth, past Garrow at the bellows with his mouth open, onto the jetty. Past Kestra, who drew her feet in. Out along the grey boards, all the way to the end, where the little boat was tied up. And he sat down there, on the end of the jetty, with his feet over the water and his back to all of them, and didn't move.

Garrow took a step after him.

"Leave him," said Severin.

Garrow stopped.

"Leave him be," said Severin, more gently. "Bank the fire. Low. We've got time."

He stood at the anvil with the hammer still in his hand and watched the man on the end of the jetty, and felt him. He couldn't help that either. He'd felt the stillness in him for two days, the banked fire under the great cold stone. He felt it now.

The stone had moved.

Not much. Not gone. Still there, still enormous, still the whole weight of the worst night of a man's life, carried for eight years. But it had shifted, just now, on the anvil, at the moment the iron parted. The way ice shifts on a lake in early spring: one long crack, from one shore nearly to the other, with a sound you feel in your teeth more than hear. Not breaking up. Not yet. But no longer one piece.

And under it, the fire came up. A little. Steady. The way it comes up when you've finally got the draw right.

Severin knew that feeling. He'd felt it in Brakewater, in the dark, over the twelfth plough blade, a moment before the world went quiet.

He didn't say anything. It wasn't his to say.

***

He waited an hour.

He banked the fire. He laid the two halves on the stones at the edge of the hearth to keep their heat. He sat down on the anvil stump beside the hen, with the hammer across his knees, and waited, the way he'd waited on the red ground eight years ago, beside a grave, while the sun came up.

The girl went out to him, after a while.

She'd stood by the charcoal cart the whole time, with the empty basket in her hands, watching her father on the end of the jetty. Severin saw her decide. She put the basket down and walked out along the boards, past Kestra, all the way to the end, and sat down beside him with her feet over the water, close. She didn't say anything. She leaned her head on his shoulder.

He put his arm round her.

On the field above the shore, two hundred people stood in silence along the wall and watched the two of them sitting on the end of the jetty, and nobody said a word, and nobody went home.

Kestra, on the jetty, had closed her book.

***

At dusk, Ars came back.

He walked back along the jetty with the girl beside him and came down the shingle to the forge. His face was the same as ever. Plain. Still. Most people couldn't hold on to it; their eyes slid off. Severin never had that trouble, and never had known why. He'd known him twelve years, and he saw that his eyes were red.

He didn't say anything about it. Neither did Severin.

Ars went to the far end of the hearth, and put his hands on the bellows handle.

"Bellows," said Severin.

"Slower?" said Ars.

"Slower."

Ars worked the bellows. Slowly. Evenly. Like breathing. Better than he'd ever done it at Cinderhold, twelve years ago, when the garrison smith used to hide the bellows when he saw him coming. The fire came up red under the sailcloth, and then orange, and Severin took the long half of the sword out of the edge of the hearth with the tongs, the half with the grip and the old leather and the crooked letters, and laid it in the heart of the fire, and watched the colour come.

When it was ready, he laid it on the anvil, and picked up the small hammer, and began.

*Ting. Ting. Ting.*

Steady as a heartbeat. Out over the lake in the dusk, and up the hill to the town, and past the militia hall where five people were sitting at an upper window. The iron moved under it like clay. As if it had only been waiting for someone to ask it properly.

Severin was humming. He didn't know he was doing it. An old river song from Brakewater, under his breath, in time with the hammer.

At the far bellows, after a while, very quietly, the fisherman began to hum it too.
