Declan was asleep.

Gabe checked twice. He woke on the floor of the cabin on the third morning with a fox blanket over his feet again, and the hearth gone to embers, and an empty hook beside it where the kettle used to hang. Calvin had carried the kettle up to the town last night. Deston said he'd slept with it. And the first thing he did, before he'd even sat up, was turn his head and look at the bed against the far wall, under the window.

There was somebody in it.

Face down. One arm under the pillow. The other hanging off the edge with the knuckles on the floorboards. Breathing slow and heavy, the way you only breathe when you've been asleep a long time and are going to be asleep a lot longer.

Gabe lay on the floor and looked at that arm under the pillow for a while.

*He lay down with one arm under the pillow the way he always did.* Gabe had known that about him for half his life. He'd thought about it too much, the first year after. He'd never wanted to picture it. He'd pictured it anyway.

He got up very quietly and stepped over Deston and went and stood by the bed and looked down at a stranger asleep, in a stranger's body, with his arm under the pillow, exactly the way.

"Sleep," said Gabe, under his breath. "Seriously."

Then he went out, and shut the door behind him, very carefully, and stood on the step in the cold morning with his hands over his face for a minute, until he was all right.

***

Alea was feeding the hens.

She was in the yard behind the fence in an old coat, scattering grain from a bowl, with the three hens round her feet and the black sword leaning against the fence post beside the gate. She looked up when he came round the side of the cabin.

"He's asleep," said Gabe.

"I know." She threw another handful. "He came in at dawn and lay down and was gone before his head touched the pillow. He hasn't done that since I was small." She looked at Gabe sideways. "He said you made him promise."

"I did."

"Nobody makes him do anything."

"I know. I've known him longer than you." Gabe leaned on the fence. "He used to stay up till all hours, every night, on calls, playing games. For years. I told him to sleep every single night. Every night, on the call, at the end. *Sleep, Declan. Seriously.* Every night." He looked at the cabin. "He never listened. Not once."

"He listened once," said Alea quietly.

Gabe didn't say anything.

"He told me," said Alea. "When I was twelve. How it happened. On Earth." She threw the last of the grain. "He said he went to bed early, for once. Because his friend kept telling him to. And he didn't wake up." She looked at Gabe. "He said it was the one time he ever did what you told him, and look where it got him."

Gabe laughed. It hurt. It came out of him anyway.

"That's what he said? *Look where it got him*?"

"He said it was a joke." Alea's ears went back. "He tells jokes about things that aren't funny so they stop hurting. It doesn't work. He does it anyway."

"Yeah," said Gabe. "He always did."

***

The forge was on the shingle below the cabin.

He'd seen it the first morning, from the end of the lake road. A long low shape under a sailcloth roof, with a stone hearth at one end and a chimney and an anvil. He hadn't looked at it properly. He'd had other things to look at. But he went down to it now, with Alea a step behind him and the black sword on her back, because he was a smith, and you don't walk past a forge.

He stopped under the sailcloth.

It was a good forge. It was a *very* good forge. Better than Harl's on the south wharf at Kingsferry, and Harl's was the best on the Wren. The hearth was dressed stone, laid by somebody who knew exactly what they were doing, with a bellows of oxhide and ash wood that must have cost more than Gabe earned in a season. The anvil was old. Very old. Black iron, worn into a shallow dip in the middle of the face by a lifetime of somebody else's hammer.

He put his hand on it.

He'd been able to feel metal since the first winter. Not much. Not like a skill. Just a sense, in his hands, of what the iron was doing. Whether it was ready. *The heat stops being angry*, he'd told Harl once, and Harl had looked at him as if he'd said something holy.

The anvil wasn't angry. It hadn't been angry for a long time. It felt like a very old dog lying in the sun.

"The king built it," said Alea, behind him. "In the autumn. To split the sword. That's his father's anvil. He brought it on a cart all the way from Valcrest." She came and stood beside him. "He came back at midwinter to see the hens. He says the anvil stays here. He says it's happier here."

Gabe looked at the anvil.

"It is," he said. "You can feel it."

Alea looked at him strangely. Then she nodded, as if he'd passed something.

On the bench at the back, there was a roll of soft leather, tied with a thong. Gabe untied it without asking. Inside, on a row of little pockets, there were tools. Fine ones. The finest he'd ever seen. Gravers and punches and one small chisel, very old, with a point like a needle and a handle worn smooth and dark by a hand much bigger than his.

"That's the king's," said Alea. "He gave it to Dad. For the letters on the stone."

Gabe put it back very carefully. He tied the thong again. He didn't say anything for a while.

***

"Can I see it?" said Gabe.

Alea knew what he meant. She unbuckled the black sword from her back and held it out to him across the anvil, flat, on both hands, the way you'd hand somebody something holy, or something heavy, or both.

Gabe took it.

It nearly took his arms off. He hadn't expected that. He was the strongest of the five of them. He'd held a tow rope in the Narrows with a loaded barge on the other end of it. And the sword came down out of Alea's hands into his and *pulled*, and he had to set his feet and let his shoulders take it, and even then the point dipped to the stones.

"Sorry," said Alea. "I should've said."

"You carry this?"

"All day. I'm Tempered. It's what Tempered's for." She shrugged. "Dad carried the whole thing. Twice as big. He was younger than me when he found it, and he carried it home on his own. The king says that's not possible."

Gabe got the blade up onto the anvil and laid it there, and stepped back, and looked at it.

It was black. That was the first thing. Not dark. *Black*, all the way through, the way coal is black, with no shine on it at all until the light caught the edge, and then a line of white running down it like frost on a wire. It was still huge. Still a slab. Wider than his hand was long. They'd made it narrower, he could see that, drawn it out, put a new point on it like the head of an axe. But it was still the black sword from the stories. Half of it.

He put his hand flat on the face of it.

And felt it.

Not angry. Not asleep, like the anvil. Something else. Something he'd never felt in metal before. He stood there with his hand on it for a long time, trying to find the word, while Alea watched him.

Folded. That was it. That was the first thing his hands told him. Folded more times than he could count, thousands of times, finer than any smith he'd ever heard of could fold anything, so that the iron inside was like the pages of a book shut so tight you couldn't get a knife between them.

And under that, very faint, a long way down: *waiting*.

"It's not from here," he said.

"No," said Alea. "The king said that too."

Gabe took his hand off it. He looked at the grip. The old leather, dark and worn, wrapped round and round. And just above it, on the flat of the blade, a line of letters scratched in with a knife point, crooked, one of them gone over twice.

*AD ASTRA PER ASPERA.*

He looked at it for a long time.

"He told us," said Gabe. "Last night. What it means."

"*To the stars, through hardships*," said Alea. "I named her Astra. After it. The stars bit." She touched the grip. "Dad let me choose."

"It's a good name."

"I know." She paused. "Gabe. Can I ask you something?"

"Yeah."

"The grip." She put her hand round it. Her fingers didn't go all the way. "The king says I'll curse it for a year. He says I'll grow into it. But my hands are tired every night. And it slips in the rain." She looked at him. "Dad says you can feel metal. Like the king. He says you were always good with your hands. He says you fixed his chair once with a coat hanger and it lasted for years."

Gabe looked at her hand on the grip. At the gap between her fingertips and the heel of her palm. At the old leather, wrapped for a hand much bigger than hers.

"It's wrapped for him," he said. "Not you."

"I know."

"I can fix that," said Gabe. "Not today. It wants doing properly. With the right leather. But I can fix that."

Alea's ears came forward.

"Really?"

"Really," said Gabe. "Seriously."

She laughed. It surprised her. He saw it surprise her. She put her hand over her mouth, and her ears went pink, and she laughed again behind it.

"He said you'd say that," she said. "He said you say *seriously* when you mean it."

***

Declan came down at noon.

Gabe heard him before he saw him. The door. Bare feet on the step. A long silence, which was Declan standing in the doorway in the sun looking at the lake, the way Gabe was beginning to understand he did every morning. Then the shingle.

He came under the sailcloth with his hair on end and a cup in his hand and his face still creased from the pillow. Gabe couldn't hold on to the face. Nobody could. But he could see the creases. He could see the hair.

"You slept," said Gabe.

"Five hours."

"*Seriously?*"

"Seriously." Declan drank. "Don't look so pleased with yourself."

"I'm going to look exactly as pleased with myself as I want." Gabe leaned on the anvil. "Ten years of telling you. More. And you finally go to sleep the day *after* I get here. So I can see it."

"I went to sleep because you hit me."

"I barely touched you."

"You hit me in the chest on the end of my own jetty in front of a whole town." Declan looked at Astra on the anvil, and at Alea beside it, and at Gabe, and something in his shoulders went soft. "She's shown you."

"She's shown me."

"What do you think?"

Gabe thought about it.

"I think it's the best thing I've ever touched," he said. "And I think it isn't from here. And I think the grip's wrong for her and I'm going to fix it."

Declan looked at him for a long moment.

"The king'll be offended," he said.

"The king can come and watch."

Alea made a sound like a hen being stepped on.

***

He'd been meaning to ask all morning. He'd been meaning to ask since the barge. Since before the barge. Since a forge on the south wharf at Kingsferry, a year ago, with a bronze crucible cracking down his arm.

He waited until Alea had gone back up to the cabin. Not because it was a secret. Just because.

Then he reached over his shoulder and drew his blade.

It came out the way it always did, heavy and short and ugly, with a sound like a door being shoved open. He laid it on the anvil next to Astra. It looked stupid there. Like a dog sitting next to a horse.

Declan looked at it.

"Harl says it looks like a butcher's cleaver married a gate," said Gabe.

Declan laughed. Out loud. Under the sailcloth. It startled a heron in the reeds.

"He's right," he said.

"I know he's right." Gabe looked at it. "It's my first one. I made it in Kingsferry. Last year. Harl let me use his forge at night. I got it wrong eleven times. The twelfth time the crucible cracked and I burned my arm from here to here." He touched his left forearm. There was nothing there now. Rachael had seen to that. "And then I finished it. One-handed. While Rachael was still yelling at me."

"I know," said Declan quietly. "I heard."

Gabe didn't ask how. He was getting used to that.

"I carried it all the way up the river," he said. "I drew it in the Narrows. When the rope went. I let go of the rope, and I drew it, and everyone felt me draw it. Deston says they all felt it through the threads, like the floor stopped moving." He looked at the blade. "It's never had a name."

Declan didn't say anything.

"I kept thinking," said Gabe. "Every time I picked it up. Every time I sharpened it. Every night. *Declan would have a name for this.* In Latin. Something stupid and old that nobody but him could read. Like his Steam groups. Like his statuses." He swallowed. "I thought about it every time I picked it up for a year and a half. *Declan would know what to call it.*"

He looked up.

"So," said Gabe. "Name it."

***

Declan put down his cup.

He came round the anvil and picked the blade up. One hand. Easily. Like it weighed nothing. Gabe had stopped being surprised by that. He held it out at arm's length and looked down the edge, and turned it over, and looked at the flat. He set the point on the stones and leaned it and watched how it fell. He put his thumb on the edge, very lightly.

"It's a good blade, Gabe," he said.

"It's ugly."

"It's honest. That's better." Declan turned it in the light. "It's heavier at the front than it should be. You did that on purpose."

"I like it heavy at the front."

"I know you do. You always did. You used to carry the shopping like that. All the heavy bags on one side, so you'd lean." He set the blade flat on the anvil again, gently. "It's you. That's why it's good. It doesn't look like anybody's but yours."

He stood there and looked at it.

Gabe waited. He'd waited a year and a half. He could wait a bit longer.

"*Murus*," said Declan, after a while. "That's *wall*."

"Okay."

"No. Too obvious. Everybody'd call you the wall. You'd hate it." He thought. "*Custos*. Guard. Guardian." He shook his head. "No. You're not a guard. Sabine's a guard. You don't guard things. You just..."

He stopped.

He stood there under the sailcloth with his hands flat on the anvil, either side of the ugly blade, and looked at it. And something happened in his shoulders. The same thing Gabe had seen on the end of the jetty. A settling.

"*Sto*," said Declan.

Gabe waited.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"One word?"

"It's a whole sentence," said Declan. "In Latin. The *I* is inside it. You don't need to say it." He looked up. "*Sto*. It means *I stand*."

***

Gabe didn't say anything.

"That's all you ever do," said Declan. "That's all you've ever done. In the back row, on the risers, at fifteen. Standing next to me so I didn't fall off when I was laughing too hard. At the back of a room full of people in black, Deston said, all day, and you never once sat down. On a rope, in a gorge, with a barge on the end of it. In front of Rachael, in the night market, with a man who wasn't a man trying to get past you." He breathed. "On the end of my jetty, two days ago. Hitting me in the chest."

"You stand there," said Declan. "And nothing gets past. That's not guarding. That's something else. That's just what you are. You don't even do it on purpose. You just *stand*."

He put his hand flat on the blade.

"*Sto*," he said again. "*I stand.* That's its name."

Gabe found he'd sat down.

He hadn't meant to. He was sitting on the edge of the stone hearth, with his elbows on his knees, the way he'd sat down on the wall at the top of the road when a fox girl said *I'm Alea*. His legs had just decided. He was getting tired of his legs doing that.

"Okay," said Gabe. His voice didn't work properly. "Okay. Yeah. That's... yeah."

"You can have a different one."

"*No*."

Declan smiled.

***

"Can I put something on it?" said Declan.

Gabe looked up.

"On the blade," said Declan. "Here. On the flat, by the spine." He touched the place. "Just a line."

"The words," said Gabe. "The ones you used to say."

"Xav told you."

"Xav told us. Last night, on the terrace, when you'd gone down to the jetty. He said it out loud and Deston started crying and Calvin asked him to say it again." Gabe swallowed. "*Fortune favours the bold.*"

"*Audentes fortuna juvat*," said Declan.

"Yeah," said Gabe. "That. Put that on it."

Declan looked at him for a long time.

Then he went to the bench at the back, and untied the soft leather roll, and took out the king's chisel.

***

It took him all afternoon.

Gabe watched the whole time. He didn't go anywhere. Deston came down at some point with a plate of bread and cheese, and stood under the sailcloth for a minute, and saw what was happening, and put the plate on the bench without saying anything and went away again. Xav came and sat on the shingle at the edge of the forge and didn't say anything either. Alea came and went. The hens came and looked and decided it was boring.

Declan sat on a stool in front of the anvil with the blade laid flat across it and the king's chisel in his right hand and a little hammer in his left. And cut.

Gabe had seen engravers work. There was a man on Rope Street in Kingsferry who did names on rings. He'd watched him once, through the window, for an hour. He'd been slow, careful, frowning, breathing through his nose, stopping every few strokes to blow the dust away and squint.

Declan wasn't like that.

He wasn't slow, exactly. He wasn't fast. He was just *steady*. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* The point of the chisel going along the steel a hair at a time, and a curl of bright metal coming up after it, finer than a hair, and falling, and the next one, and the next. He didn't frown. He didn't squint. His face, which Gabe couldn't hold on to, had gone completely still, the way it went still on the end of the jetty with the rod. As if he wasn't there at all. As if there was nothing in the world but the next stroke.

He was fishing, Gabe realised.

That was what he was doing. He was fishing through it.

*A*. *U*. *D*. Gabe watched the letters come up out of the steel one at a time, clean and square and even, in a straight line along the flat of his ugly blade. *E*. *N*. *T*. Not the crooked scratches on Astra's grip, the letters of a man in a hurry with a knife in his hand and something awful behind him. Not that. These were different. These were patient.

*AUDENTES.*

Declan stopped. Blew the dust off. Looked at it. Moved the chisel along.

*FORTUNA.*

The sun went round. The shadow of the sailcloth moved across the shingle. Out on the lake, a fish jumped and nobody looked.

*JUVAT.*

Declan put down the hammer.

***

Gabe got up and came and looked.

It was perfect.

Not perfect the way a machine does it. Perfect the way a hand does it, when the hand knows exactly what it's doing and isn't afraid. Three words, in a straight line, along the flat of the blade, below the spine, from just above the grip to a hand's width short of the point. Square, even capitals, each one as deep as the last. *AUDENTES FORTUNA JUVAT.*

He looked at them for a long time.

He looked for the one gone over twice. He couldn't help it. He'd seen the crooked letter on Astra that morning, gone over twice by a knife in somebody's shaking hand. He looked along all twenty letters on his blade for the one where the hand had shaken.

There wasn't one.

Not one. Every stroke clean. Every letter once.

He looked up at Declan.

Declan was looking at the letters too. He was very still. And Gabe saw him see it. Saw him look along the line, the way Gabe had, for the place where his hand had shaken. And not find it.

Something went across his face. Gabe couldn't hold on to it. He didn't need to.

"Huh," said Declan, very quietly.

That was all. Just *huh*. The way he'd said it in a game, at three in the morning, when a shot went in that shouldn't have.

***

"You do the name," said Declan.

Gabe looked at him.

"It's yours," said Declan. "I did the words. You do the name. Here." He touched the base of the blade, just above the grip, on the other side from the motto. "Three letters. Use a punch. Don't think about it. Just do it."

"I'll get it wrong."

"Good," said Declan. "Get it wrong. It's yours."

So Gabe did it.

He took the smallest punch out of the king's leather roll, the one with a blunt square end, and set the blade flat on the anvil, and stood over it, and put the punch where Declan had touched. And hit it. Once for each stroke of each letter. Too hard. Too fast. The way he did everything.

*S. T. O.*

They came out crooked. The S was too big. The T leaned. The O wasn't quite round. They went down into the steel at a slant, deeper at the bottom than at the top, so that the whole word looked like it was leaning into a strong wind.

Gabe looked at it.

"It's crooked," he said.

"It's yours," said Declan.

Gabe looked at it a moment longer. At the crooked word leaning into the wind on one side of his blade. At the straight clean line on the other. His. And his brother's.

He picked it up. One-handed. It came up off the anvil the way it always did, heavy at the front, so that he leaned.

It felt exactly the same. It felt completely different.

He couldn't have said how. It wasn't any lighter. It hadn't gone up a tier, or started glowing, or done anything at all. It was the same ugly lump of Kingsferry iron he'd been carrying for a year. But he held it now, under the sailcloth, in the late afternoon, and his hands told him the thing they'd told him about the anvil that morning, and about Astra, and about every good piece of metal he'd ever touched.

It wasn't angry.

It hadn't been angry before. Not really. But it had been *waiting*. For a year and a half. For something. He'd felt that in it, every time he drew it, and hadn't known what it was.

It had stopped waiting.

"*Sto*," said Gabe.

He said it the way you'd say somebody's name for the first time, when you've just been introduced. Testing it. Seeing if it fitted.

It fitted.

***

He sheathed it. Then he sat down on the hearth again, because his legs had decided again, and he let them.

Declan sat down next to him.

They didn't say anything for a while. Out on the lake the sun was going down towards the western hills, and the water had gone gold, and the heron in the reeds was standing on one leg.

"Gabe," said Declan, eventually. Very quietly.

Gabe knew. He'd been waiting for it all day. Longer.

"My dad," said Declan.

Gabe looked at the lake.

He had it all, in his head. He'd had it for three years. The funeral. The back of the room. The car park after, and a man on a low wall with his hands between his knees, not crying, shaking hands with everyone who came out, one after another, like a man doing a job. And a woman coming to sit beside him, and taking his hand, and holding it, and him letting her. Gabe had carried that for three years. He'd carried it up a river. He'd known, from the very first night on the roof in Kingsferry, when Deston said *it's him*, that one day he'd have to put it down in front of the one person who'd want it most and could bear it least.

"Tomorrow," said Gabe.

Declan looked at him.

"Me and Dest," said Gabe. "Together. Properly. Not like this, at the end of a day, when you're tired and I'm..." He stopped. "He's okay, Declan. I'll tell you that now. That's the most important bit, so I'll tell you that bit now. He's okay." He looked at Declan. "The rest, tomorrow. With Deston. He was there for all of it too. It's his to tell as much as mine."

Declan was quiet for a long moment.

Then he nodded.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Gabe heard him breathe out. A long breath. The kind you let go of when you've been holding it for three years.

"He's okay," said Declan. Testing it. The way Gabe had said *Sto*.

"He's okay," said Gabe.

***

Before they went up, Gabe showed him the knife.

He hadn't meant to. It just seemed like the right time. He took it out of his belt, the small plain knife he used for everything, and held it out, handle first.

"Rachael used this," he said. "In the Narrows. On the hat man's hand. When he came off the cliff for her."

Declan took it. He turned it over.

There was a mark on the blade, a finger's length from the point. A patch of grey. Not rust. Not dirt. Gabe had tried everything on it. It was like the steel had gone dead there. Like a coal that's burned out. Whatever Rachael had cut, whatever had been moving inside the hat man's hand like water, it had stopped where the knife went in. And the knife had gone grey where it touched it.

Declan looked at it for a long time.

He didn't say anything. He didn't touch the grey. He held the knife very still, the way he'd held the rod on the end of the jetty, and looked at it, and Gabe saw something move very far down in his stillness, something he couldn't read.

"Don't grind it out," said Declan at last.

"I wasn't going to."

"Don't. Keep it just like this." He handed it back, handle first. "And when we go to Valcrest, show it to the king."

Gabe took it. He looked at Declan.

"When we go to *Valcrest*?"

Declan didn't answer. He'd already stood up, and was looking at the lake, and the last of the sun was on the water, and somewhere up at the cabin Alea was shouting that supper was going to be burned if nobody came, and Deston was shouting back that it was already burned, and the hens were complaining about all of it.

"Come on," said Declan. "Bring Sto."

Gabe stood up. He put his hand on the hilt, over his shoulder. It was heavy at the front. He leaned.

He followed his brother up the shingle in the last of the light.
