"Again," said Kestra.

Milli picked himself up off the sand of the practice yard, retrieved his wooden sword, and came at her again. He was fast. He'd always been fast, Jared thought, watching from the fence; fast and smart and so impatient that he started every move a half-second before it was ready.

Kestra stepped aside. She didn't even raise her own blade. Milli's momentum carried him past her, and she put two fingers on his shoulder and pushed, and he went down in the sand again with his tail lashing.

"Again," said Kestra.

It was the third week. Jared had heard her say perhaps forty words to him in total, and at least thirty of them had been *again*, all of them to Milli. To Jared she'd said something different on the very first morning. She'd watched him take up a stance in the yard, feet planted, weight low, eyes on the space in front of him rather than on her, and she'd looked at him for a long moment and said, "Who taught you to stand there?"

"Nobody," Jared had said. "It's just where I'd stand."

"Mm," said Kestra, and that was all. But she'd been different with him after that. Not kinder, exactly. Kestra wasn't built for kind. More like she'd filed him in a different drawer.

Milli got up again. Jared could see the frustration all over him, in his ears and his tail and the set of his jaw. He could also see, now, after three weeks, the thing Kestra was waiting for. It was very small. Milli's shoulders dropped half an inch before he moved. That was the half-second. That was the tell.

"Wait for it," Jared called.

Milli shot him a look of pure betrayal. But on the next pass he waited, just slightly, just enough, and Kestra had to actually raise her sword to turn his aside.

She stepped back and lowered her blade.

"Better," she said.

Milli looked like he'd been handed a crown.

***

They lived in a wing of the castle that had once belonged to a minor prince, which Oswin had told them with tremendous enthusiasm and Kestra had told them was unimportant. There were tapestries on the walls, and draughts behind the tapestries, and a sitting room with a fire that the five of them had claimed as their own.

That was where they compared windows, most nights. It had started as something to do. It had become something more like a confession, or a prayer.

"Three," said Hanarisu.

"One," said Milli, flatly. "It was one last week. It's been one for a week."

"Kestra said better, though," said Jared. "That's got to be worth something."

"It's worth a word. It isn't worth a percent." But Milli's tail had stopped lashing.

"Two," said Jared.

"Four," said Aleaka, and then, with the slight stammer she got when she was excited, "and I— the stable, the grey horse, the one that bites everyone? It put its head on my shoulder today. It just... walked over and put its head on my shoulder. And the castle cats have started following me everywhere. Six of them. I counted." She touched one of her fox ears. "I don't think it's because I'm a fox. I think they can feel something."

"Taming," said Cursiffe. "Your path. It's coming early."

"Not *early.* Four percent. That's not early, that's—"

"It's a start," said Hanarisu gently, and Aleaka subsided, pink and pleased.

"Five," said Cursiffe. He held up his hand. Across the back of it, faded but still there after three weeks of washing, were the letters he'd written in ink on the first day: STATUS. "And this. It shouldn't still be here. Ink doesn't last three weeks on skin. But it does when I write in English. When I try to copy their script, nothing. It's just ink. When I write English, it feels..." He searched for the word. "Warm. Like it's holding on."

They were quiet a moment, thinking about that.

"Kestra writes in a little book," said Hanarisu, apropos of nothing. "Have you noticed? Every evening. She doesn't let anyone see it."

"Reports," said Milli. "For the king. She's the king's, not the Advisor's. Oswin told us."

"I know," said Hanarisu. "I just wondered what she writes."

***

Hanarisu found out four days later.

It was the archive. It was always the archive with Oswin; he'd been trying for a week to get them into the restricted shelves, where the oldest records of past summonings were kept, and for a week a narrow, dusty man called the Keeper had been saying no. He said no to Oswin with the tired relish of a man who had been saying no to Oswin for fifteen years. He said no to Cursiffe without even looking at him. He said no to Milli, who had not even asked anything yet.

Then Hanarisu stepped forward, and smiled, and said, "Please."

She'd meant it to be ordinary. She'd smiled at difficult people her whole life, on stream, in chat, in queues and offices and family dinners; it was a tool, like a pen or a key, and she was good with it. But this time something moved when she did it. She felt it, very clearly, for the first time: a warmth that started somewhere under her ribs and went *out*, toward the Keeper, like breath on a cold window.

The Keeper blinked.

"Well," he said. "I suppose, for a short while. If you're very careful with the bindings."

"Thank you," said Hanarisu, and her voice came out perfectly steady, which surprised her, because her heart was going very fast.

Oswin was already through the door, delighted. Milli followed him. Hanarisu went last, and as she passed the Keeper she saw that he was looking at her with a faintly puzzled expression, as if he couldn't quite remember why he'd agreed.

At the end of the corridor Kestra was leaning against the wall with her arms folded. She'd watched the whole thing. As Hanarisu passed, she took the little book out of her coat, opened it, and wrote a single line, without hurrying and without looking away.

Hanarisu kept walking. She kept her face pleasant. She was very good at that.

That night she lay awake next to Milli for a long time, with her tail curled around her own ankle and her hand pressed flat under her ribs, where the warmth had come from.

"What's wrong?" Milli murmured, half asleep.

"Nothing."

"Liar."

"I think I made someone say yes," she said, very quietly. "I don't know if I did it. I don't know if I meant to."

Milli was quiet for so long she thought he'd gone back to sleep. Then his hand found hers in the dark.

"Then we'll find out," he said. "And then you'll decide whether to do it again."

***

She found out something else two days after that, which she wasn't supposed to.

She was walking back from the practice yard alone, by the long way, through the older part of the castle where the Advisor kept his study. The door was half open. She didn't stop. She only slowed down, a little, the way anyone might.

"It isn't a small thing," Oswin was saying, low and urgent, without any of his usual cheer. "I've been through every record. Every summoning in four hundred years. One person, sometimes two. Never more than three. And never, ever people who *knew each other*. Thirty-five, and every one of them is connected to the others somehow. That isn't chance. Something chose them. If we understood what—"

"Leave it, Oswin."

The Advisor's voice was quiet and very tired.

"With respect, Advisor, I don't think—"

"I know you don't. Leave it."

There was a pause.

"Yes, Advisor," said Oswin.

Hanarisu was already past the door and around the corner by the time he came out. She didn't look back. She went to the sitting room and sat by the fire and opened her window, and looked at her three percent, and thought very hard about what kind of question a man would order buried, and why.

***

Oswin and Kestra fought about the Hero at dinner. They did it most nights. Jared had started to look forward to it.

"He held the Redmarch *alone,*" Oswin was saying, waving a chicken leg. "Ten thousand of them. There are eyewitness accounts, Kestra, I've read every one, there are *forty-one* of them—"

"Forty-one soldiers who were running the other way," said Kestra, not looking up from her plate.

"Some of them were running the other way. Some of them were *watching.*"

"From a hill. In smoke. At night." Kestra cut a potato in half with great precision. "I've stood a watch in smoke at night. You see what you're afraid of. Or what you want."

"So you think it didn't happen."

"I think something happened." She ate the potato. "I think the songs are better than the man."

"You've never met him," said Oswin, wounded.

"Neither have you."

"Have you ever met anyone who has?" Jared asked. Both of them turned to look at him, as if they'd forgotten anyone else was at the table.

Kestra considered it. "Soldiers," she said. "Old ones. They don't talk about him much. When they do, they say he was quiet." She shrugged. "Then they stop talking."

"*Quiet,*" said Oswin, with enormous satisfaction, as if this proved everything.

"That's not proof of anything."

"It's proof that he's not a song."

Hanarisu, at the end of the table, had been listening without a word. "Where is he now?" she asked.

There was a small pause. It was the kind of pause, Jared thought later, that happened when two people who disagreed about everything suddenly realised they agreed about one thing and didn't like it.

"Nobody knows," said Oswin. "He left after the battle. He just... went."

"The king knows," said Kestra. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but she looked at her plate when she did. "And the Advisor. And they don't say."

"He has given enough," said Hanarisu softly.

Kestra glanced at her, sharp. Then she took out her little book, and wrote something down.

***

The first letters came at the end of the third week, in a courier's satchel, sealed with the royal crest and addressed in handwriting that made Cursiffe make a small, involuntary sound of joy.

"English," he said, turning the first one over. "It's in *English.*"

It was from Thea. Cursiffe read it standing up, then sat down and read it again, and then, after a moment's thought, read parts of it aloud.

*Fifty-two percent, which apparently is fast and doesn't feel it. Wrenhaven is small and wet and everyone stares at Xola. We train every morning. Nobody's casting anything yet; Maren says that's normal and nobody can at Initiate, which Gigi says is a scam.*

*The code thing is getting clearer. I can read whole lines now, if I'm slow. Here's something I didn't tell anyone the first night. There's a line in everyone's window, deep down, that names the medium. The thing the summoning used to pull us through. Mine says:* Medium: one blade. *And then there's a second value next to it that I can't read at all. It's not in the window's language or the code's. It's just sealed. Like a door with no handle.*

*I keep thinking about the sword in the hall.*

*Write back. I'm sending Chris the bits I've worked out, too.*

Cursiffe lowered the letter.

"The sword," said Jared. "The big black one. Salty asked who could lift it."

"One blade," said Cursiffe. "And a sealed value. A name, maybe. An owner." He was quiet a moment. "Oswin said every summoning needs a medium. Something with a hook in it. Something that belongs to wherever you're reaching."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"So the sword belongs to Earth," said Milli eventually, slowly, as if he didn't like the sound of it. "That's what you're saying. Somehow. A sword that belongs to where we came from."

"Or to someone who does," said Hanarisu.

***

The book came the next day.

Oswin brought it to the sitting room himself, holding it in both hands like something holy. It was small and much handled, bound in plain brown leather that had gone soft at the corners, and there was no name on the spine.

"For you," he said to Cursiffe. "Since you like words. You'll find it in no archive. It's from the provinces, from up north, from somewhere in the lake country. A trader brings a new one down every so often and I buy every copy he has." He was beaming. "Fishing, mostly. And stories. And the most wicked sense of humour I've ever read. Whoever wrote it should be at court, and probably thanks the gods every day that they aren't."

The title page said only: *Written down by someone who should be fishing.*

Cursiffe started reading that night, by the fire, with the others drifting off to bed one by one. Jared stayed. He wasn't sure why. He was sitting in the big chair with his eyes half closed, half watching the fire and half watching Cursiffe, when Cursiffe stopped turning pages.

He didn't say anything. He just went very, very still.

"What?" said Jared.

Cursiffe didn't answer. He read something again. Then a third time. Then he got up, crossed the room, and held the book out to Jared, open, with his thumb beside a paragraph near the bottom of the page.

Jared took it.

*On the question of bait, opinion in my town is bitterly divided. Some men swear by worms. Some men swear by bread. One old man swears by a secret paste his grandmother made, which I have tasted, and which is mostly mud. I use none of these. I have a method of my own. I have never once, in all my life, lost a fish.*

*Trust.*

Jared read it twice.

Then he sat there with the book in his hands and the fire popping, and for a moment he wasn't in a draughty castle in another world at all. He was in a call, at three in the morning, half a world away from a friend in Malaysia who had just said something completely outrageous and finished it with that one word, that stupid little word, and Jared had laughed so hard he'd had to mute himself.

"That's—" he said.

"Don't," said Cursiffe quietly.

Jared looked up.

"Don't say it." Cursiffe's face was very careful. "I've read forty pages. It's not just that line. It's the rhythm. The way the jokes turn at the end. I knew him a little, from the calls, and I remember thinking nobody's brain worked that fast." He took a breath. "But he's dead. We both know he's dead. And a lot of people make jokes."

"Not like that."

"No," said Cursiffe. "Not like that."

They didn't say the name. Neither of them said it all night. It sat in the room with them anyway, by the fire, between the pages.

"Coincidence," said Jared at last.

"Probably," said Cursiffe.

Neither of them believed it.

***

In the morning, Jared found Oswin in the archive before anyone else was up, and asked him, as casually as he could, who wrote the books.

"Nobody knows," said Oswin cheerfully, not looking up from a scroll. "That's half the charm. They just come down from the north. Lake country. I've always pictured some old hermit in a hut, with a pipe."

Jared thanked him and went to the window at the end of the long gallery, and stood there for a while looking north, at the hills and the grey distance and the long road going away into it, as if, if he looked hard enough, he might see all the way to wherever it ended.
