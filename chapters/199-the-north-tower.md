You couldn't see heat.

Severin's father had told him that in the first week, at Brakewater, with the river going past the open door. You thought you could. You thought it was the glow. But the glow was only the iron telling you, in a language, and you had to learn the language before it meant anything. Black heat, that you could only feel. Then the dull red you saw in the dark. Cherry. Orange. Straw. The pale yellow that was nearly white. And going the other way, after, when the iron came out and cooled: the colours that ran across a polished edge like oil on water, straw and bronze and purple and blue, each one a different hardness, each one gone in a breath if you weren't looking.

*You don't see the heat*, his father had said. *You see the colours. And you trust them. You trust them the way you'd trust a man who'd never lied to you. Because if you stop to put your hand on it and check, lad, you'll lose your hand*.

He'd trusted the colours for fifty years.

He was standing at the top of the north tower at Thornwall at the grey, with his hands on the cold stone of the parapet and a hundred and twelve steps behind him in his knees, looking north at a brown valley with a smudge in it. And he couldn't see anything. Not the rings. Not the road. Not the thing at the bottom of the pass that wasn't there.

He was going to have to trust somebody else's colours.

***

He'd been up three times now.

The first morning, the morning after he came. He'd come up behind the cow girl, slowly, with Kestra behind him counting the steps under her breath in case he wanted to know, which he didn't. And Hyvred had pushed her glasses up into her hair at the top, and turned to him with her gold eye locked, the red mark in it like a drawn line on a target, and said *I'll tell you everything, Majesty. You stop me if it's too much*.

He hadn't stopped her.

She'd talked for an hour. He'd stood beside her with his hands on the parapet and his eyes shut, most of the time, because he'd found it was easier to see what she said with them shut. Thirteen rings, round a black circle the size of a fort. Hundreds. Standing in meltwater to the ankle, facing in. A green coat; a hat; a widow's shawl and a stick. A freckled face in the third ring, turned up. The road going up the pass between walls of snow, clear, wet, swept, as if somebody had come down it with a broom. And at the bottom of the pass, at the near end of the rings, between them and the road, a place the size of a hill where her sight went out and found nothing and came back. *Not dark*, she'd said. *Not a shape. A hole. In the seeing*. Meltwater going through it and coming out the other side unchanged. Snow falling through it all winter. *It doesn't even take anything, Majesty. That's the worst bit. It doesn't even take anything*.

He'd listened the way he'd have listened to his father calling the colours. Every word. And somewhere in the middle of it, he'd felt his own heat move. Deep down. The ninth tier, banked, held in, the way he'd held it in every day since the spring he ascended, because a man who walked about with his door open would have frightened every child in the kingdom into fits; a king more than most. It had moved in him the way a forge-fire moves when a door opens somewhere else in the building and the draught comes through.

He'd shut it again. Carefully. He'd been shutting it all his life.

And then, toward noon, the cow girl had stopped in the middle of a sentence about the third ring, and gone white, and screamed down a hundred and twelve steps, and he'd looked down from the parapet and watched a young man walk out of a postern gate on his own.

***

He'd seen a lot of men stand in roads.

He'd stood in a few. He'd been a smith's son who went to war at twenty, and he'd come up through it one tier at a time, the way you came up through anything: by standing where you were told and not running, and then by standing where nobody had told you, and then by being the one who told them. He'd seen boys of nineteen stand in front of things on the Redmarch with their spears shaking. He'd seen them run. He'd seen them not run and die anyway. He'd seen, once, a commoner's son with a black sword walk into the dark of the red on his own and come out the other side two tiers higher, carrying a dead woman.

He'd never seen anything like the road.

Twelve people walking up it, smiling. A baker's wife with flour on her arms. An old man in a leather apron. A girl of fourteen with a chalk dog trotting beside her, barking. And the young man with the platinum streak and the red one, the one who'd run Lucan's trials, Drew, slow, so slow, running past them up the road with his arms going, and turning round, and standing.

With his hands open.

Severin had watched them walk into him. He'd watched the young man's boots go back in the mud, a foot, another foot. He'd watched him start to turn. He'd seen it, from the top of the tower, a hundred and fifty paces off, the way you'd see a bar start to bend under a weight it couldn't carry: the shoulders, beginning to go round. Toward the valley. Toward whatever was calling. And beside him the cow girl had made a sound he'd hear for the rest of his life, and he'd put his hand flat on his chest without knowing he'd done it, the way old soldiers did when they couldn't do anything else.

And then the young man had stopped turning.

He hadn't seen what happened. He'd felt it. Something came out of the young man on the road, a hundred and fifty paces off, small, the first tier, the very first heat a body ever makes; and went north. Up the valley. Hard. Along exactly the line something else had come down. He'd felt it go past the tower like a thrown hammer goes past your ear.

And the twelve had stopped.

*He gave it back*, the cow girl had said, beside him. Crying. *Majesty. He gave it back. All of it. It went back up the road*.

*I know*, Severin had said. *I felt it*.

He'd gone down the hundred and twelve steps faster than a man of sixty-five had any business going down anything, and stood at the foot of them all afternoon, watching the young man come back from walking twelve people home and sit on a mounting block with his hands shaking, because he'd needed to look at him for a while before he could say anything.

The anvil. That was what he'd said, in the end. It was the only thing he'd had. His father's.

***

The second time had been at dusk, the next day, and he hadn't been invited.

He'd been at the farrier's. He'd spent the afternoon at Old Tull's anvil making shoes for the north patrol, because his hands needed something and Tull's forge was the nearest, with a boy of eleven in a red scarf on the bellows who breathed for the fire as if he'd been born to it. *Steady, lad. Breathe like you're asleep*. He'd burned his thumb, the boy, on a bit of scale, at the end, and Severin had wrapped it for him in a strip of rag and put his hand on the boy's head and sent him home for his tea.

Sent him home.

He'd been sitting on Tull's bench after, in the dusk, with a cup, when he heard the cow girl scream from the tower for the second time in two days. *TIGER. IT'S JOSS*.

He'd gone up the hundred and twelve steps that time too. Faster than he'd come down them. Kestra had been behind him, and for once she hadn't been counting. He'd got to the top with his heart going like a bellows and his ninth tier pressing at the door of him so hard he'd had to stand still for a breath with his hand on the post to keep it shut; and then he'd turned and looked north, at a valley going dark that his old eyes couldn't see a mile into, and let the cow girl be his eyes.

She'd called it for him. Every step. Not running, she'd said; *going*. The way a spark goes off an anvil when the hammer comes down: there, and then somewhere else, with nothing in between. A streak, out along the dark valley floor toward the rings, faster than a horse, faster than anything she'd ever seen move on two legs or four. And then, at the very end, at the edge of the outermost ring, he hadn't needed her. He'd felt it himself, ten miles off: the very first heat a body ever makes, coming up out of nothing, all at once. *There*. A hundred and fifty paces in no time at all. And back.

There and back.

*He's got him*, the cow girl had said. She'd been holding on to his sleeve. She'd been holding on to the king of Aurelan's sleeve with both hands, and neither of them had noticed. *Majesty. He's got him. He got there first*.

***

The third time was this morning, and he'd asked them all to come.

He'd been up all night. He hadn't been able to sleep. He'd gone down to Old Tull's at the second hour of the morning, in the dark, and woken him, and the two of them had made new bars for the millstream grating: six of them, square, as thick as a big man's thumb, with Tull on the bellows this time, grumbling, and the king of Aurelan at the anvil with his sleeves rolled. He'd set them himself at first light, standing up to his knees in the millstream under the north wall with the water so cold it hurt, and a soldier holding a lamp, and Old Tull on the bank telling him he was doing it wrong. He hadn't been doing it wrong. He'd been doing it the way his father did it. But he'd let Tull tell him, because Tull needed to tell somebody something.

So his hands smelled of the forge, and his knees were wet, and he'd climbed a hundred and twelve steps for the third time in three days, and he stood at the top of the north tower in the grey with all of them round him.

Corvin, the guide. Thirty-eight, the third tier, thin and earnest, with two years of maps under his arm and his good coat on wrong again. He'd been right about the west for two years before anyone would listen. Severin had read every one of his reports, in Halvard's drawer, the year Halvard finally opened it. He'd sent the young man a royal seal for his trouble and it hadn't been enough.

Hild. Sitting on the parapet with her back to the drop, the way nobody else would, with her one eye shut. Shaped; the sixth tier; fifty-five. He'd known her in the war. She'd been the only person on the east line who'd ever told him to his face that his plan was stupid, and she'd been right, and he'd changed it, and it had worked, and she'd never mentioned it again.

Fenwick, with a report. Brannoch, with his scars and his hand on his hilt. Kestra, two steps down the stair, with her book.

And the cow girl, with her glasses in her hair.

"Tell me again," said Severin. "From the beginning. All of it. Slowly. As if I'd never heard it."

Hyvred looked at him.

"Majesty," she said. "You've heard it twice."

"My father made me call the colours every morning for five years," said Severin. "He'd heard them a thousand times. He made me call them anyway." He shut his eyes. "Because the morning you stop calling them is the morning one of them's different. Go on."

***

She called them.

The valley, first. The snow nearly gone off it now, only the north faces of the hollows still white; the meltwater running everywhere in brown threads, all of it going one way, down the valley floor toward the pass and the place at the foot of it. The rings. Thirteen. Standing in water. Not one of them moved since yesterday. The green coat. The hat. The old woman with her stick. The boy in the cap.

"The edge of the thirteenth," said Hyvred. "On the south side. Where Tiger—" She stopped. "There's a mark. In the water. Where he went in and out. The mud's still churned. Nothing's filled it in." She was quiet. "The boy who was nearest. The green coat. He hasn't turned round. None of them turned round. Tiger was a pace off and none of them even *looked*."

"Go on," said Severin, with his eyes shut.

The road. The pass road, going up between its walls of snow in its long slow switchbacks to the cut at the top. Clear. Wet. Grey. Swept.

"It's further," said Hyvred.

He opened his eyes.

"The clear bit," said Hyvred. "It's further down this side than it was. Yesterday it started at the foot of the pass. Today—" She turned her head, very slightly, the red mark moving. "Today it starts a little way out. Past the bottom. Into the valley." She swallowed. "As if whoever's sweeping it is sweeping toward us."

Nobody said anything.

Brannoch's hand had gone white on his hilt.

"How far?" said Severin.

"A furlong," said Hyvred. "Maybe less. Since yesterday." She looked at Fenwick. "Fenwick, you've measured the road. Haven't you? The first winter. With the chains."

"Every stone of it," said Fenwick. Very quietly. He was writing. "To the foot of the pass. I'll have the figure by noon."

"And the place," said Severin. "At the bottom."

Hyvred turned her face to it.

He watched her do it. He'd watched her do it twice now, and it was the thing he'd remember about Thornwall, after, when he'd forgotten the rest. Not the rings; he'd never seen the rings. Her face, looking at the absence. A young woman with golden horns and a red mark in her eye and the whole of the valley open to her like a page, the first person in two worlds, maybe, who'd ever had the whole of the world open to her like a page; and the way her face went, every time, when the page came to the one place that was blank. Like a reader who'd come to a word she didn't know. And kept coming to it. Every morning. And kept not knowing.

"It's there," said Hyvred. "It's the same. Facing out. Facing the road." She was quiet. "Not looking. It's never looking. It's—"

And stopped.

Not because she'd seen something. Because somebody was coming up the stair.

***

He knew who it was before the head came round the last turn of the stair. He'd have known that tread in the dark. He'd heard it go through a postern at dusk and come back. Light, uneven, one step and then a pause and then another, the way a man climbs stairs when his legs have been somewhere else in the night and only just come home.

Tiger came round the top of the stair.

He was white. He had one hand on the wall and the other on Drew's shoulder, and Drew behind him, very calm, holding him up by the back of his teal coat without making a thing of it. His ears were flat. His legs were shaking; you could see it in the knees. He'd climbed a hundred and twelve steps on legs that had carried him a hundred and fifty paces in no time at all the night before, and you could see every one of the hundred and twelve on him.

He stopped at the top. And stood there, holding on to the post, breathing.

"Tiger," said Hyvred.

She'd gone very quiet. The way you'd say somebody's name who'd done something you'd stopped hoping they'd ever do.

"You've never come up," said Hyvred.

"No," said Tiger.

"Two years and a half."

"I know." He was looking past her. North. Out over the parapet at the valley, the brown floor of it, the smudge. The way Severin looked at it. A man who couldn't see. "Hyv. I need to ask you something."

She waited.

"In the third ring," said Tiger. "Ossie." His voice went, and he got it back. "Last night. When I came back out. I looked back. And he—" He stopped. "Is he still looking up?"

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment.

Then she turned. And Severin watched the red mark move in her eye, away from the blank, back across the valley to the rings; and in, ring by ring, the way you'd count rings in a cut log; to the third.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"No," said Hyvred.

Tiger shut his eyes.

"He's turned," said Hyvred. Very quietly. "A little. Hardly at all. I'd never have seen it. I check him every morning and I'd never have seen it if you hadn't—" She stopped. "Not up. He's not looking up at the pass any more. He's looking—" The red mark moved. Measuring. "South. Down the valley. Along the line of the gap. Toward the road. Toward the place you went in and out." She swallowed. "Toward us."

"After me," said Tiger.

"After you," said Hyvred.

Nobody on the tower moved.

Drew had his hand on Tiger's back. Hild had opened her eye. And Severin, standing at the parapet with his wet knees and his forge-smelling hands, felt something go through all of them on the top of that tower at once, the way you feel a bell go through a room: the thought nobody would say. That a face in the third ring had been turned up toward the pass for a year and a half, and had never once moved, and now it had. Toward a boy who used to race it to the gate.

He didn't know what it meant. Nobody did.

But he'd been a smith fifty years. He knew what it looked like when iron that had been cold a long time showed the first colour.

***

And then the cow girl said, "Oh."

Not loudly. Not a scream. A small sound, the smallest she'd made in three days. The sound of somebody who'd put their foot on a stair that wasn't there.

Severin turned.

She was still facing north. Her face had gone grey. The gold eye was locked, and the red mark in it had moved again: off the rings, off the third ring and the freckled face; back to the blank. To the place at the bottom of the pass where her sight went out and found nothing. And she was staring at it. Staring at nothing. And her hand had come up off the parapet and was in the air in front of her, open, the way you'd put your hand up against a light too bright to look at.

"It's turned round," said Hyvred.

"Hyv," said Drew.

"It's not facing the road any more. It's turned round. It's—" Her voice cracked. "It's not *facing*, Drew. It's looking. It's *looking*. For the first time. It's never once looked, not in the summer, not in the autumn, not once, it just *faces*, and now it's—"

She took a step back from the parapet. Then another.

"It's looking back," said Hyvred. "Up the valley. Up the road. At the tower." Her whole body had started to shake. "At me. It's looking at *me*. It's seen me looking and it's looking back and I can't see it, I can't see *anything*, it's a hole and it's looking at me through the hole—"

"Close it," said Drew. He was beside her. He'd let go of Tiger. "Hyv. Close it. Like the autumn. Close the—"

"No," said Severin.

They all looked at him.

***

He didn't decide it. He'd think afterwards that he should have. That a king of sixty-five, Ascendant for twenty-six years, ought to have stood at the top of a tower on the edge of his kingdom and weighed it, the cost and the gain, the way he weighed everything in council with the Treasurer whispering in one ear and Halvard not whispering in the other. He didn't weigh it. He'd been a smith's son a lot longer than he'd been a king.

There's a moment, at the anvil, when the iron's at the colour and you either hit it or you don't. His father had never once in his life let him stop and think in that moment. *Now*, his father would say. Not *are you ready*. *Now*.

He stepped round the cow girl.

Between her and the parapet. Between her and the north. Between her gold eye with the red mark in it and the hole in the valley that was looking back through it. A big grey man in a plain coat, with wet knees, standing at the top of a tower with his back to a young woman who could see everything and his face to a thing that couldn't be seen.

"Keep it open," he said to her, over his shoulder. Very gently. "Don't close it. I need you to tell me what it does."

"Majesty—"

"It's seen you looking," said Severin. "Good. Now let it see what's standing in front of you."

And he opened the door.

***

He hadn't opened it in nine years. Not all the way.

He'd had it open on the Redmarch, the last time, the day the line broke, for an afternoon. He'd had it open a little in Halvard's study the night Halvard told him about the drawer, and Halvard had gone white and he'd shut it again. He'd held it in through his whole reign, through every council and every crowd, through a lake and a jetty and a forge on a beach, so carefully that people forgot it was there. So that children at farm gates didn't cry when he rode past. So that a fisherman on a jetty could sit beside him for an hour and not feel watched.

He opened it now.

All of it.

The ninth tier came out of him the way heat comes out of a forge when you take the door off. Not a light. Not a sound. Weight. It came up out of the middle of him and went out in every direction at once, through the stone of the tower and the air round it and the valley below, and the whole top of the north tower at Thornwall went thick, like the air in a hall on the first day of the world, like being underwater. He heard Brannoch's breath go out of him. He heard Fenwick's pen drop and roll. Behind him, he heard Kestra on the stair, who'd stood beside that weight for thirteen years and had never once felt it all at once, say a word she'd never in her life have written down.

He didn't look round. He looked north.

He couldn't see it. He'd never be able to see it. He looked at the place where the cow girl said it was, at the bottom of the pass, past the smudge, where his old eyes saw a brown valley and grey mountains and the morning coming up pale behind the Greyspine. And he stood there with the door of himself wide open, all of it, Ascendant, the ninth tier of ten, the whole of what fifty years of war and iron and grief had made, and said, not out loud:

*Here I am*.

*Look at me*.

***

"It's moving," said Hyvred.

Behind him. Very small. Her voice coming through the thick air as if from a long way down.

"Its—the looking. It was on me. I could feel it on me. Like a hand." She was crying; he could hear it. "It's moving off me. It's—Majesty. It's on you."

"Good," said Severin.

"It's looking at you."

"Good. What colour?"

He didn't know why he asked it. It was the thing he'd have asked his father.

There was a silence.

"There isn't a colour," said Hyvred. "It's—there's nothing. There's never anything. It's a hole. But the hole's turned round, and it's pointed at you, and it's—" She breathed. "It's staying. On you. It's not going anywhere. It's like—" She stopped. "It's like somebody looking at a bar of iron to see if it's true."

Severin almost laughed.

He stood at the parapet with the door open and let it look. He didn't push. He didn't do anything. He stood there and was what he was, all of it, the way a mountain stands there being a mountain while the weather looks at it. He felt it on him, now, a little, at the very edge, the way you feel somebody watching you from across a dark room: a cold place in the air, very far off, that the heat went toward and didn't come back from. Like the place in a forge where the fire's gone out and the draught goes in.

He let it look.

He didn't count. He didn't need to. He measured it the way he measured everything, the way his father had taught him to measure on the anvil before he'd ever learned numbers: by the colours. It looked at him for as long as it takes a polished edge to run from straw to blue. Which is a long time, when you're watching. Which is no time at all.

And then the cow girl said, "It's turning."

He waited.

"It's turning back," said Hyvred. "Off you. Back round. To the road. To the pass." Her voice was shaking so hard he could barely make out the words. "It's facing again. Not looking. Facing. Like before."

"Like it was?"

A pause. The red mark, measuring.

"No," said Hyvred. "Not like it was." She was very quiet. "It's facing the pass. But it's facing it the way you'd stand with your back to a door you knew somebody was behind." She swallowed. "It knows you're here now."

"Good," said Severin.

And shut the door.

***

It took a long time. Shutting it always did. Twenty-seven years of practice and it still took a long time, like getting a big fire banked: you couldn't just slam it, or the whole forge went up the chimney. He stood at the parapet and drew it back in, slowly, all of it, the weight and the heat and the thick air, back into the middle of him, and shut it in there, and leaned on the door.

His knees were shaking. He noticed that. He was sixty-six. He hadn't had it all open in nine years.

He turned round.

They were all looking at him. Every one of them. Brannoch with his face slack. Fenwick on one knee, looking for his pen. Corvin very white. Drew with his arm round Tiger, both of them staring, Tiger's ears straight up. Hild on the parapet, the only one who hadn't moved, with her one eye open, looking at him with an expression he'd last seen on the east line nearly thirty years ago, the morning she'd told him his plan was stupid.

And the cow girl. With her glasses in her hair and her face wet and the red mark in her gold eye still locked, still pointed north, past him, at the place that had looked at her.

"Was that wise, Majesty?" said Corvin. Very quietly. The way you'd ask a man if he'd meant to put his hand in the fire.

Severin thought about it.

"No," he said. "My father'd have clipped my ear for it."

Hild laughed.

It was a short hard bark of a laugh, the first he'd heard from her in nearly thirty years, and it went off the top of the north tower and down the valley like a dog barking at a door. And then the cow girl laughed too, crying, with her hands over her face. And then Drew, because Drew always did. And Tiger, because Drew had.

Severin stood at the parapet with his wet knees and his shaking legs and let them laugh. He didn't. He was looking at Hyvred.

"It was looking at you," he said.

She stopped laughing.

"Not any more," said Severin. "Not at you. Whatever it looks at next, up that road, it's going to look at me first." He put his gloves on. "Now. Tell me about the road again. The furlong. And then we'll go down. I'm told it's a hundred and twelve steps."

***

They talked in the map room.

Corvin's map room, at the bottom of the tower, with two years of maps on the walls and the long table covered with them, every one with the same blank at the foot of the pass, drawn in his small careful hand. Severin sat at the head of the table on a stool. Not the chair. He'd looked at the chair and seen it was Corvin's and taken the stool.

Kestra stood behind him with her book. She'd written something on the stair, coming down. He'd seen her do it. She'd stopped on the ninetieth step and written one line and underlined it, and then stood looking at it for a long time before she closed the book.

He didn't ask.

"Captain," said Severin.

Brannoch had been waiting. You could see it on him. He'd been waiting three days, through the twelve in the road and the boy in the valley and the hole turning round, to say it, and he said it now, in the voice of a man who'd said it to himself every night on the wall.

"We go up," said Brannoch. "Majesty. Now. While it's still facing. With every man in the Watch and every man you brought and whatever we can get from Ashby in a week. We go up the valley and we break the rings." His jaw went tight on the ploughed side. "Burn the circle. Take the road back. Before it sweeps any further. Before it's at the wall." He looked at the map. At the blank. "It turned round this morning, Majesty. For the first time. It's done facing. Next time it won't turn back."

"Thank you, Captain," said Severin. "Corvin."

Corvin had been waiting too. Not like Brannoch. Like a man who'd been right for years and had learned that being right didn't mean anybody did anything.

"We go south," said Corvin. "The town. Not the Watch; the town. The farms, the children, the old ones. Everyone who can be called and can't fight. Down the valley road to Ashby, before the next one." He swallowed. "It took the boy without knocking. Hyvred had no warning. If it can do that, she can't keep them safe by looking. Nobody can. Get them out of the door."

"Thank you, Magister," said Severin. "Hild."

Hild was leaning in the doorway. She hadn't sat down. She never did.

"I've nothing to say," said Hild. "You know what I think."

"Say it anyway."

Hild looked at him with her one eye.

"I think there's one man in the kingdom who's walked into the dark on his own and come out of it," said Hild, "and he's at a lake in the north mending a jetty. And I think everybody in this room knows it, and nobody's said it, because he's yours." She folded her arms. "That's what I think. You asked."

The room went very quiet.

Severin didn't say anything for a while.

He looked at the map. At the blank at the foot of the pass in Corvin's small careful hand. At the road going up from it, that Corvin had drawn the first winter, every switchback, every cut, all the way to the top. At the place where tomorrow, maybe, Fenwick would draw a mark a furlong further down.

And he thought about a lake. A jetty with too many planks. A man sitting on the end of it in the morning with a rod, and a fox girl beside him with her feet pointing at the end of it, and the whole of the long hard life behind him finally, for once, behind him.

*Plough blades*, he'd said to the man, in the frost, the autumn just gone. When the man asked him what you spend the last of the good time doing. *Don't spend it saying goodbye. Plough blades*.

The man was doing his plough blades.

***

"Here's what we'll do," said Severin.

He put his hands flat on the table. On the maps. He could feel the ninth tier sitting under his ribs, shut, banked, the door a little warm still from being open.

"The watch doubles," he said. "Not the Watch; the watch. Two on every post where there's one now. Every gate and every grating and every gap in the walls of this town that a child could get through, I want found, and I want shut, by tomorrow night. Fenwick. You've measured everything else in this valley. Measure that."

Fenwick wrote it down.

"Every child in Thornwall inside four walls by dusk and counted," said Severin. "Counted, by name, at the door, by somebody who knows their name. Every night. And every one of the drawn dogs your bee girl can make, at every one of those gaps, barking." He looked at Hyvred. "And you. You don't watch the road at dusk any more."

"Majesty—"

"Somebody else watches the road," said Severin. "Fenwick can watch the road; he knows every stone of it. He'll tell you if it moves. You watch the people. That's what you're for. You've been doing two people's work with one pair of eyes, and last night it nearly cost us a boy." He said it gently. "That's not your fault. It's mine. I should have seen it the first morning."

Hyvred didn't say anything. Her tail had gone still.

"Second," said Severin. "Kestra."

Kestra's pen was ready.

"To Ashby and Solmere, by the young man's cord, today," said Severin. "And to Kingsferry, to the old sister at the infirmary there, by their guide's cord. Every apothecary who can be spared, and every healer who'll come. Up here. By the fastest roads there are."

"Healers," said Corvin. "Majesty, the doses—only Kei can—"

"I know who makes the doses." Severin looked down the table. At the far end of it, very quietly, trying not to be noticed, with a green snake round his neck and a face so grey it looked like ash, Kei. He'd slept. Severin had made sure of that. Not enough. "I sat on his stool. He told me. Each one takes most of a day and most of what he has. And most of the day," said Severin, "is boiling."

Kei looked up.

"Steeping," said Severin. "Grinding. Straining. Waiting for the pot. You told me so. Hours of it. And then, at the end, a word. In the air. Over the cup." He held up one big burned finger. "That's yours. The word. Nobody else can do that. But nobody ever made his own charcoal in my father's forge, lad. Nobody dug his own ore. You don't do the whole of a thing on your own because you're the only one who can do the end of it." He put his hand back on the table. "You'll have apothecaries. As many as I can find. They'll do the boiling. You'll do the word. And you'll sleep."

Kei opened his mouth. Shut it again. Round his neck, the green snake had lifted its head.

"Hisui says," said Kei, and his voice wasn't steady. "Majesty. Hisui says you're the first person who's ever told me to sleep and meant *and I'll do something about it*."

"Tell him he's welcome," said Severin.

***

"And the rest of it," said Brannoch. "Majesty. The rings. The road."

"Not yet," said Severin.

Brannoch's face went dark.

"Majesty—"

"Not yet," said Severin again. Not loudly. "Captain. Look at me."

Brannoch looked.

"There are hundreds of people standing in that valley," said Severin. "In rings. Thirty-nine of them walked out of Valcrest last summer, under my nose. One of them's a soldier from your own Watch who used to race a boy with tiger ears to the gate every morning, and this morning, for the first time in a year and a half, he's turned his head." He let that sit. "You want to go up there and burn them."

"I want to go up there before it comes down here."

"I know. And so do I. And if it comes, we'll go, and I'll be at the front, and you'll have to run to keep up." Severin leaned back on the stool. "But I've had my hand on a lot of iron, Captain. And I've never once known it go well to hit a thing before it's at the colour. You don't know what's in those rings. Nor do I. Nor does anybody. You don't know what that is at the bottom of the pass, or why it's sweeping a road, or what it wants with a boy of eleven and a baker's wife and a corporal from Ashby. You go up there with two hundred men and a torch, and you'll find out, and so will they." He looked at the map. "And so will the boy in the third ring."

Brannoch didn't say anything.

"Magister," said Severin, to Corvin. "Not yet."

"Majesty, the town—"

"Where would you send them?" said Severin. "Ashby? It called a corporal whose wife's in Ashby. It called him with her voice, I'd bet, if I asked him. It doesn't call from a place. It calls from a door, and every one of those people has a door in them, and you can walk them all the way to the sea and they'll take it with them." He was quiet. "If it gets to the wall, we'll go. All of us. I'll carry the old ones myself. But not yet. Not because it might. Here, at least, they've got a man who can stand in the road, and a man who can get there first, and a girl who can see whose window's open, and a young man with a snake who can shut it." He looked round the table. "And me. Now. Facing it. That's more than they'd have in Ashby."

Corvin looked at him for a long time.

Then he nodded. Slowly. The way a man nods who's been right for years and has just been told, for the first time, that somebody else is going to carry some of it.

"And the lake," said Hild. From the doorway. Not asking.

"Not yet," said Severin.

Hild looked at him.

"Not yet," said Severin, more quietly. "He's ploughing."

She didn't say anything. She'd known him nearly thirty years. She wouldn't know what he meant; nobody did but Halvard, who'd guessed. But she'd been on the east line the winter his father died, when he'd gone home to Brakewater in the middle of a war and come back after the thaw an Ascendant, and never once said how; and she'd know that he meant something, and that it was his.

"You'll know," said Hild.

"I'll know," said Severin. "And then I won't wait."

***

Kestra caught him on the stair, after.

Not the tower stair. The little one, from the map room up to the walk on the north wall, where he'd gone because he wanted to look at the millstream grating in the daylight. He'd set the bars at dawn by lamplight and he wanted to see them. He stood on the wall-walk above it and looked down at the six new bars, square and black and as thick as a big man's thumb, set in the old stone where the ice had bent the old ones, with the water going through them, brown, fast, out into the valley.

They were good bars. His father would've said so. His father would have said *adequate*.

"Majesty," said Kestra.

She was holding her book. Open. She held it out to him. She'd let three people read her pages in thirteen years: him, Oswin, and once, at Hethe, Halvard, on his way to a lake. He'd never asked. She'd never offered.

She was offering.

He took it.

It was the fourteenth book. Begun in Valcrest. Her small square hand, page after page, the way it had been every day for thirteen years, every word he'd said that she'd thought worth keeping and a great many she hadn't, and at the bottom of the newest page, written on the ninetieth step of the north tower at Thornwall, one line.

*He opened the door.*

Underlined. Twice.

He looked at it for a long time.

"I've never seen it," said Kestra. "Not all of it. Thirteen years." Her voice was perfectly flat, the way it always was. "I didn't know it was that big."

"Nor did I," said Severin. "It's been a while."

She took the book back. She closed it.

"It looked at you," said Kestra.

"It did."

"Was it frightened?"

He thought about it. He stood on the wall-walk above his six new bars with the water going through them, and thought about it honestly, the way he'd have thought about a colour on an edge.

"No," he said. "It doesn't get frightened. It isn't that kind of thing." He was quiet. "It was measuring. The way you'd measure a bar to see if it's true. And it'll come back and measure again, one day, when it's finished whatever it's doing at the bottom of that road." He looked north. "And when it does, I'll still be here."

"You'll be in Valcrest," said Kestra. "By midsummer. You've the Lists. You promised Lucan."

"I'll be here," said Severin, "tomorrow too."

Kestra looked at him. Then she opened the book again, and took out her pen, and wrote something under the underline, small, that he didn't read.

***

He wrote to Halvard that night.

He couldn't use a cord. The cat girl hadn't made him one; he'd never asked. He'd thought about asking, in the yard at Valcrest, and hadn't, because a king who could talk to the whole kingdom in a heartbeat ought to have to think about it first. So he sat on the edge of the bed in the room they'd given him, which was Corvin's, with Corvin's maps on the walls, and dictated it to Corvin, who held the strip and the blue pen and wrote it in his own small careful hand, and tied it on his green-and-grey cord, and shut his eyes, and sent it to the cat girl in Solmere, who would read it to Halvard in his study off a strip in her own hand, which would be Corvin's meaning, which would be Severin's words.

It had to be short. The cords didn't carry much.

He'd thought about it all evening, at the long table, with the soldiers and the summoned and Old Pruett, who'd come to supper and refused to sit anywhere but by the door. He'd thought about the twelve in the road and the boy in the valley and a face in the third ring, turned a hair. The furlong. The hole turning round. The way it had felt, at the edge of him, a cold place in the air where the heat went in and didn't come back.

He'd thought about what Halvard would want to know. Halvard would want to know everything. Halvard would want eleven pages, with a map. He'd get them; Kestra was writing them now, in the next room, by lamplight. They'd go down the valley road with the first rider in the morning.

But tonight. On the cord. In a dozen words.

"*Seen it*," said Severin. "*It looked at me. I looked back. Not yet*."

Corvin wrote it.

Severin sat on the edge of the bed. Then:

"And put," he said, "*How are the hens*."

Corvin looked up.

"He'll know," said Severin.

Corvin wrote it. He tied it on. He held the cord, with his eyes shut, the way they all did, and sent it.

The answer came while Severin was getting his boots off. Corvin read it to him by the lamp, very carefully, in his own hand, that was the cat girl's sending, that was, Severin knew, the way it always was, Halvard's.

"*They hate me*," Corvin read. And then, after a gap: "*Good. Come home when it's ready. Not before. H.*"

Severin laughed. Quietly. On the edge of Corvin's bed with one boot off.

"Thank you, Magister," he said. "Go to bed."

***

He didn't sleep.

He lay on Corvin's bed in the dark, a big grey man on a bed too short for him, with his feet over the end and his hands folded on his chest, and the ninth tier shut in under them, banked, warm. And listened to Thornwall. The soldiers on the wall, two to every post now, calling the hours. The dogs: not barking. A chalk dog somewhere, padding along the wall-walk above the millstream, on watch. The wind coming down off the Greyspine over the roofs, cold, tasting, the young man said his snake said, of nothing.

He thought about his father.

The last winter. The forge at Brakewater, with the river going past the door, frozen at the edges. The old man by the fire in his chair, too weak to lift the hammer, wanting to talk about the price of charcoal; and his son across the table from him all winter, saying goodbye. A bit at a time. Every day. So busy saying it that he never once had him. And then the end of the winter, and the burial, and the morning after, the empty forge, and an order on the wall in a crooked hand. *Twelve plough blades, Ashdown farm, by the thaw. Half paid*.

Twelve days. One a day. On the old man's anvil, with his hammer, at his fire. And on the twelfth, at straw, the whole of him going up, all at once, a door opening in him he hadn't known was there. Ascendant. At thirty-nine. On a plough blade, for a farmer called Ashdown, in a dead man's forge.

The songs said it was the last charge of the long war. He let them.

*You can't make it ready*, his father had said. *You can only wait till it is. And then you can't wait any longer, or it's gone*.

He thought about a man on a jetty doing his plough blades. A man who'd carried a kingdom for most of his life, and a dead woman off a hill, and a black sword out of the dark; and who'd sat down on the end of a jetty at last, with his daughter, and was doing the work in front of him instead of saying goodbye to it.

*Not yet*, thought Severin. *Let him finish*.

*I'll know when it's ready*.

*And then I won't wait*.

He lay in the dark at the edge of his kingdom, at the top of the valley, with the north tower standing over him and the six new bars in the millstream and the boy in the red scarf asleep in his mother's house in the lane, counted. And far off, eleven miles, at the bottom of a swept road, something that had looked at him for as long as it takes an edge to run from straw to blue, and turned away, and was standing now with its back to a door it knew he was behind.

He kept the door shut.

He didn't sleep. But he lay there till morning, keeping it.
