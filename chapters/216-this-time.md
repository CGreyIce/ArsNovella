The first thing he got wrong, after she went, was the porridge.

He made enough for two. He did it on the first morning without thinking, the way he'd done it every morning for nine years, two handfuls of oats and then two more, and stood at the stove stirring it with the hens round his feet before he looked into the pot and saw what he'd done. He ate it anyway. Both bowls. It was a long time since he'd let food go to waste, a long time since a winter on the south line when there hadn't been any, and he wasn't going to start because his daughter was on a river.

The second morning he made enough for two again.

The third morning he stood at the stove with the oats in his hand and looked at them for a long time, and put half back in the jar, and the red hen, at his feet, looked up at him with one eye as if to say she'd been wondering when he'd notice.

***

It was very quiet.

He'd known it would be. He'd lived alone before, in his own way, in a barracks full of men, and on the road, and for a night on the south line in a ditch with forty recruits. He'd lived nine years at a lake with a child who didn't talk for the first year after her mother died, and who then talked to the hens more than to him for the second, and then, one morning at the end of the jetty, started telling him about a heron and didn't stop. He knew quiet. He'd thought he'd know this one.

He didn't. This one was different. It wasn't that she wasn't there; she'd been away before, at the militia hall in the evenings, at Group 1's fire, at the market, on the Shiver every day for a week in the spring falling into the black water up to her waist. It was that she wasn't *coming back*. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not for a month. Two months. More, if she went straight on from Valcrest with Group 3 to the east the way she had last summer, and met him there for the tenth.

The ladder to the loft didn't creak at night. That was the thing he noticed most. It creaked on the fourth rung, the one he'd been going to fix for nine years, every night at ten when she went up, and every morning at five when she came down with her hair wet from the bucket, and he'd stopped hearing it years ago the way you stop hearing your own heart.

He heard it not creak.

He fixed it on the fourth night. He took the rung out and planed it and put it back and it didn't creak any more. Then he sat at the table and looked at it for a while and wished he hadn't.

***

He did the fish.

Five, every morning, off the end of the jetty, the way he always had. Two for the hall, which Group 1 still wouldn't take from his hand and still took off the step. Three for the town, because the town expected it now, and because Robin's mother had started leaving eggs on the end of the jetty in a basket in exchange and he couldn't stop her. He said *Tobyn* at the first cast. He'd said it every morning since the snow, since a letter had come down a lake road in Maren's voice with a dead fisherman's name in it, spelled with a *y*. Somebody should.

And at the second cast, the last few mornings, he'd found he said *Alea*.

He hadn't decided to. It came out. Quietly, to the water, at the second cast, the way the first one did. As if it were a thing you could say to a lake and it'd go down the Wren and across the bar and get there.

He didn't think it got there. He said it anyway.

***

He read the channel.

That hadn't changed. Every night, on the end of the jetty, with the lamp lit on the last plank and the hens in their row, all of it, from the top: two hundred lines, going up and away. He'd read it every night for two years and a half. He'd read Thea build it. He'd read thirty-five people find out what a window was, and write to each other about a first step none of them could find, and find it, one at a time, all the way to thirty-five of thirty-five on a spring morning with a bell. He'd never written in it. He never would.

It was a different thing now. It had been, since the winter before last. Since Group 1 had come down the lake road with lanterns and he'd said *it's me*, and told them at their own table that he read it. They knew he was there now; by now they all did. They wrote knowing it. Not to him; nobody ever wrote to him. But past him, the way you'd talk in a room with somebody asleep in the corner, a little more gently, in case.

He didn't know the Warden. He'd never met him. He knew, from two years of reading, more about the Warden of the Summoners' Hall than about most men he'd served with for ten years on a border. He knew the old man had stood on the third ring and felt thirty-five people come through it like hooks. He knew he'd asked for the leaves to be burned, and lost, and that a boy had tried to do it anyway, and that the boy had said good morning to him for a hundred and nine days. He'd read that four times, the night it happened. *It isn't a charm on you. You can put it down whenever you want*.

Every night. At the bottom. Whatever else Chris had logged.

He'd read that line every night since the Lists. *Still there*. He knew who it was for. Everybody knew who it was for. Nobody had ever said so, and he'd never answered it, and Chris had never once stopped writing it.

He closed the window. He sat on the end of the jetty in the lamplight with the hens behind him and looked at the water.

Thirty-five lines on a wall in the militia hall. In chalk. In a little ring like a child's drawing of the sun. Thea had come down the jetty at dusk with chalk to her elbows and sat behind him with the red hen and said *it wrote us down*.

Frame thirty-seven, the last one with marks. Frame thirty-eight, the first one without.

*Somebody stopped*.

He'd said *not yet* to her. He'd heard himself say it. He'd said it to Rowan once, on a hill, with a demon in pieces, about coming back, and he'd meant it then the way you mean a thing you're too tired to say no to. He hadn't known, then, that he'd ever mean it the other way.

He meant it the other way now. He didn't know what to do about that either.

The letter came on the *Patient Widow*, in the sixth week.

He was on the quay. He hadn't meant to be. He'd been in town with the three fish, and heard Haskett from the top of the mill road roaring at the lock-keeper, and walked down to the quay below the mill without deciding to, the way he'd walked down to it for the last barge in the autumn with six fish in a basket. He stood at the corner of the mill where he'd stood when she went, and watched the long black hull come round the bend with Haskett at the steering oar and his boys at the poles, and didn't let himself look for anything.

Haskett saw him. Haskett roared something at the elder boy and the elder boy jumped onto the quay before the barge was even tied and came straight down the quay to him through the crowd, a young man of about Alea's age with tar on his hands and his cap in them, and held out a square of oilcloth tied with a fisherman's knot.

"From the coast, sir," said the boy. "Come up from Kingsferry in the harbourmaster's sack. Master Dennis pulled it out and gave it Da with his own hand. He said it'd ring if we lost it."

He took it.

He knew the knot. She'd learned it this spring, at the table, on a length of spare line, the night she sent her first letter to the coast by water; she'd tied it wrong four times and then right, and looked up at him with her mother's face, astonished, as if the knot had done it on its own.

She'd tied this one right. Tight. Neat. The way you'd tie it on a boat.

He didn't open it on the quay. He put it in his coat, beside the stone and a half of gold heart, and walked back up the mill road and along the lake road and down to the cabin and out to the end of the jetty, and sat down, and the hens came and sat behind him, and then he untied it.

The king's paper. One sheet. Folded in four. The faint crown in it when he held it to the light.

*For when you've got something to say*.

He unfolded it.

Her hand. Her mother's, really; she wrote the way Diwa had written, small and quick and leaning forward, the letters running into each other because her mind was always a word ahead of her pen. Diwa had taught her her letters at this table the winter she was six, from a slate; and she'd learned them in a week and then complained for a year that they were too slow.

It wasn't long. It didn't use half the sheet.

*Gullhaven.*

*The sea doesn't care. Sorrel said it wouldn't. I was sick for two days and then I wasn't.*

*I said Tobyn at the first cast. Sorrel's mother dropped a basket of herring. Then she picked it up and said it too.*

*Gabe made me a hook. It says Sto inside. It caught a fish as long as me and didn't bend.*

*My weight goes back on my heels when the ground stops moving. I'm fixing it.*

*Rachael says look at the horizon. It works. Don't tell her.*

*I'm not asking.*

*A.*

He read it a great many times.

The sun went down while he read it. The water went gold and grey. One of the hens got up and walked down the jetty to the land end and came back, the way they did when they thought he'd forgotten about supper. He hadn't forgotten. He sat on the end of the jetty with the king's paper on his knee in the last of the light and read the last line over and over.

*I'm not asking*.

She'd said it on the last plank of this jetty in the spring, with the lamp lit, to his back. *I'm never going to ask*. And she hadn't. Not at the table with the pack. Not on the quay. She'd stood in front of him on the quay with six sheets of cream paper against her ribs and he'd watched her not ask, the whole of her not asking, and then put her arms round him in front of the town and gone.

And now she'd taken one of six sheets of a king's paper, for when you've got something to say, and carried it all the way to the edge of the sea, and filled half of it, and the last line of what she'd had to say was that she wasn't asking.

He knew what that was. He'd done it himself. He'd done it for two years, every night, on this jetty, with a window open on his knees and thirty-five people talking about him in it. You said the thing you weren't going to say by saying you weren't saying it. You did it so carefully that nobody could ever hold you to it. And you hoped, the whole time, that somebody would hear it anyway.

She'd learned that from him too.

He folded the letter. He put it in his coat, with the stone and the heart.

He lit the lamp.

***

He packed on the Thursday.

He did it the way he'd done it on the Thursday before the bell, in the spring: out of the list in his head, the one he'd made at nineteen, before the war, when he was a garrison boy with a sword he'd found on a hill in Kharvos and somewhere to be in the morning. A blanket. A flint. Two shirts. Socks, three pairs, because feet go first. A plain knife out of the drawer, the one he gutted fish with, because the good one was on a girl's left hip at the edge of the sea and his old one was at the bottom of the lake off the end of the jetty where her mother had thrown it the summer she was five. A tin cup. Salt. Hooks, and line.

He packed it on the table, in the middle, in the evening, the way she'd packed hers. Openly. Where anyone could see.

Nobody saw. There was nobody to see. Just the hens, who'd seen him do it once already this spring and looked at him now with the deep weariness of hens who expected him to take it all out again in the morning.

He didn't take it out in the morning.

He sat at the table with his porridge, one bowl, and looked at the pack. Then at the wall over the table. At the drawing, the blue crayon jetty with forty-one planks and three people on the end, holding hands. And beside it, pinned at the corner so the corners touched, the page from Higu's book.

*Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them*.

*Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)*

Four lines through it. *Answer them*. *A tree*. *Ask again*. *Fish*. And under it, at the bottom, in his own hand, the first word he'd ever written on that page, the morning of the bell: *Fish*. Again. Because you couldn't cross off fish.

*Twelve dinners. Gabe. East*. That was what was left. Summer things. The twelve dinners here, at the lake, with the right peppers. Gabe at the anvil a week before the tenth, with the king and whatever the king was going to make, which the king still wouldn't tell him. East, with three of them, and four more asking, and a sword-woman from a ditch who'd sent word down a river by his daughter: *tell him I said so*.

Nothing on it about the south.

He got up. He took the pencil off the shelf, the stub, the one he'd crossed off *Fish* with. He stood in front of the wall with it in his hand for a long time.

Then he wrote, under *Fish*, in his own hand, small:

*South*.

He looked at it. Two words on the page that were his now. *Fish*. *South*. One under the other. A man who'd been here thirty-six years and never once put anything of his own on a list.

He put the pencil back.

***

Rowan came down the lake road at noon. He'd have heard; Rowan always heard. Odette would have told him, because Odette knew everything that happened in Wrenhaven about an hour before it happened, and the egg-woman would have told Odette, because Robin's mother had come down to the end of the jetty that morning with the eggs and seen the pack on the table through the open door and gone back up the lake road at a pace she reserved for news.

Rowan didn't knock. He hadn't knocked since the autumn, since the night Ars had knocked on his door and they'd sat at his table all night with the chipped cup between them. He came round the side of the cabin to the jetty, big and grey in his chain, and stood at the land end of it by Gigichi's tree, and looked down the planks.

Ars was at the end. With the rod.

"South," said Rowan.

"South."

Rowan came down the planks. All thirty-four. Slowly, the way a big man walks on a jetty he isn't sure of. The hens moved over for him, all three, in a row, which they'd never once done for anybody but Alea, and he sat down on the last plank but one with his knees up round his ears and his chain clinking on the boards, and looked at the water.

"Does she know?" said Rowan.

"No."

Rowan was quiet for a while.

"Good," he said.

Ars looked at him.

"She'd only spend the whole of the Lists looking for you," said Rowan. "Up in the stands. Every round. Instead of at whoever's in front of her with a sword." He looked at the water. "I've seen her do it. Last summer, at the end of the first day. She found you on the fourth tier and you could see her whole back go easy. Like a horse when you take the saddle off." He was quiet. "She's going to have to stand on that sand without it, this time. With her back not easy. That's what she went south early for." He looked sideways, not at Ars's face, which he'd never once been able to hold, but at his hands on the rod. "If you're going, you go like a man going to the market. You don't go like a man going to her."

"I know."

"Good," said Rowan again. "I'll light your lamp."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to," said Rowan. "I stood in a door for a season. I can walk down a lake road with a taper." He got up, slowly, the planks creaking under him. "Odette'll take the hens. She's already told the hens. I don't know how you tell a hen anything but Odette's done it." He looked down at Ars on the end of the jetty with the rod across his knees. "Thirteen years I've known you. And this is the first time I've ever seen you go somewhere just to watch."

He went back up the planks. At the land end, by the tree, he stopped, and turned round, and said, the way you'd say a thing you'd been keeping for a long time:

"She'll know you came. Afterwards. Whatever you do. She'll know it was you that decided." And went up the lake road.

Group 1 knew by supper.

Of course they did. Higu had written it down before Rowan had got to the top of the lake road; she'd been on the militia hall step with a moth out over the lake, the way she was every evening, and seen the pack on the table through the cabin's open door from half a mile off, and opened her book.

They came down at dusk, all five, the way they'd come down the lake road the winter before last with lanterns, when nobody in Wrenhaven had known what was going to happen. They didn't have lanterns this time. They didn't need them; the lamp on the last plank was lit. They came out along the jetty in a row and stopped at the end of it, behind him, and he didn't turn round.

"We're going on the first," said Thea. "With Maren and Bram. The miller's lending the cart. Higu's done the moons."

"I know."

"You're coming."

"I'm coming."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Right," said Gigichi. Gruff. "Rule five, then."

He knew rule five. Everybody knew rule five. Alea had asked them for it in the first spring, in the militia hall, a fox girl of sixteen standing in front of five strangers with her ears flat: *please don't ever write my name in that thing you all write in*. They'd never once written it. And for a year and a half the same rule had kept a man's name and a lake and a basket of fish out of the channel too, because five girls had decided on their own that it wasn't theirs to put there; until the end of the Lists, when Higu had taken it down, because everybody knew.

"Rule five's retired," said Higu. "I took it down. At the Lists."

"Then put it back up," said Gigichi. "For this. We don't put him in it. Not her, not him. Not that he's coming. Not that he's on the road. Not that he's in the city. Nobody writes it." She looked at the others. "Thirty people at the Lists all reading the channel at breakfast, and one of them's going to go *oh she'll be so pleased* in front of her at the well. You know they are. Tiger's not even going to be there and he'd still manage it."

"Tiger would manage it from Thornwall," said Xola.

"So nobody writes it," said Gigichi. "Rule five."

"Rule five," said PenguChi. In the voice.

"Rule five," said Thea.

Higu wrote it down. He heard the pencil. *Rule five. Him too*.

He didn't say anything. He sat on the end of the jetty with the lamp lit and five people from another world standing in a row behind him in the dusk, deciding, between themselves, without asking him, to keep a thing for him the way they'd kept him for a year and a half before he ever said *it's me*: inside Group 1. Not on the wall. Not in the channel. Just in the five of them, and a book.

"Thank you," he said. To the water.

"Don't," said Gigichi. "You'll make Gu cry and then she'll write it down and then it's on the record."

"It's already on the record," said Higu.

They left Wrenhaven on the first day of the month, at dawn, by the south road.

The miller's cart, with the miller's grey and the miller's son driving and a great deal of Higu's paper in it in a box. Maren on the box beside him with her list. Bram walking at the tail, big and grey and grinning, with an apple. The five of them, walking, because the cart was for the paper and the food and Waddles, who'd walked a mile and then sat down in the road and looked at PenguChi until she picked him up.

And a man at the back with two buckets on a yoke.

Nobody had asked him to carry the water. Nobody had asked him last year either. He'd just picked up the yoke at the first well, out of habit, the way he'd picked up a sword once and a rod after it, and nobody had said anything, and by the third day it had been his and nobody would have dared take it off him. Maren had written it in her report, last year, to the Advisor, in her small precise hand: *He carries the water*. And under it, crossed out, then written again: *I think he is happy*.

He carried it now. The yoke on his shoulders and the buckets swinging and the rod across the top of the cart where he could reach it, and the south road going away in front of them over the first long hill in the grey, the same road he'd walked last summer with a girl on his left who didn't need him to carry anything.

At the top of the first hill he stopped. He didn't decide to. His feet did it, the way hers did at the end of the third form, pointing home.

He looked back.

Wrenhaven, small, in the dawn, with the smoke going up straight from the chimneys. The square, and the hall, and Gigichi's hedge, and the mill. And past them, down the lake road, at the bottom, the lake, flat and grey and enormous, with the mist on it. And the jetty. A thin dark line out into the grey. And on the end of it, very small, a light.

Rowan had lit it at dawn. The lamp on the last plank. Ars hadn't asked him to. He'd said *you don't have to*, and Rowan had said *I know I don't have to*, and come down the lake road at dawn with a taper and lit it anyway, and gone back up, and stood now at the top of the lake road with his chain on, very straight, the way he'd stood every morning for eight years at the top of that road so that nobody would go down it.

He wasn't stopping anybody now. He was just standing there. Watching a man go.

Ars lifted his hand. The empty one. Rowan lifted his.

"*Ars*," said Maren. From the cart. Not loud. Just the name, the way you'd say it to tell somebody the cart had gone on without them.

He turned round. He settled the yoke. He went on down the south road after them, at the back, with the water.

That night, at the first camp, at a farm called Pellow's Cross where they'd camped last year too and the farmer's wife remembered him and said nothing about it at all, very loudly, Higu sat by the fire with her book on her knees and wrote, and he could see the page from where he sat, if he let his eyes go to it. He didn't. He knew what it said. He could guess.

*Day 1. South. He carries the water.*

And under it, smaller, the way Maren had written it last year, crossed out and then written again:

*I think he's happy.*
