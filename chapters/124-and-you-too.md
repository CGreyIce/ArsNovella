Everybody had a beat.

Newish had been hearing them for nearly two years now, and she'd stopped being surprised by it some time in the first winter. Lucan's was a dance tune, a quick bouncing thing with too many flourishes. Ina's was a cello, low and warm and in no hurry. Winter's was water dripping in a cave, slow and clear and cold, with a long silence between each drop that you only realised was the point of it once you'd listened for a while. Hako's was a drum somebody was playing because they liked drums. Chris's was a held note, for the most part, and had been for three years, and since a wet afternoon in the winter when he wrote two words on a page, it had had a tune under it.

She heard them all the time. Walking down a street. Lying in bed. In the Lists, she heard them before people moved, a half-beat early, the way you hear the drummer's hand come up before the stick comes down. That was the magic, Lucan said. *Tempo*. *A support path*. You heard where the beat was going, and you nudged it. Your friends a little faster. The man trying to kill them a little slower. She'd shouted *down* once across the sand because she'd heard a beat in the stands that meant somebody was about to die, and they hadn't.

She'd been listening to his all day.

She'd heard it for the first time at the north gate, at about seven in the morning, when a girl in a borrowed coat sat down in the road. She'd been sitting on the edge of Tiger's bedsheet with her hands in her pockets, listening to the whole square, carters and horses and fifteen people she knew coming through the gate in a river of noise, and under all of it, at the back, behind a fox girl with a big sword, there'd been a beat she'd never heard before.

It was mostly rests.

That was the thing she couldn't get over. Everybody she'd ever listened to had a beat that was mostly *beats*. Notes. Something happening, and then something else happening. Even Winter's water, even Ina's cello, were mostly sound with a bit of silence between. His was the other way round. A single note, low and quiet, and then a rest. A long one. And then another rest. And then another. So long you'd think the music had stopped, that whoever was playing had put the instrument down and gone out for a walk. And then, just when you'd given up on it, the note again. Exactly on time. As if it had been there the whole time, waiting, and you just hadn't been patient enough to hear it.

*It is more like waiting for a fish*, she'd read, in one of Oswin's books, in the winter. *Most people miss the first tug, because they are too busy pulling.*

She'd sat on the bedsheet and listened to a beat that was mostly waiting, and gone white, and known.

***

He'd skipped her.

He'd gone round all of them, at the gate. Every single one. She'd watched him do it with her hands in her pockets and the word in her chest going round and round like a pebble in a stream. Aleaka. Hyvred. Hana, and Milli. Jared, at the wall. Cursiffe's ink. Tiger, who'd picked him up. Drew. Kei. Bee, who'd held his hand far too long. Hako. Chris and the thirty-sixth line. Winter's frost. Ina, *you're on support*, and Ina laughing behind the mask until he had to hold the wall.

And then he'd looked at her. Across the square. For a long moment.

And not said anything.

And turned round, and gone back to his buckets.

She'd sat there with her hands in her pockets and her face doing something she didn't let it do in public, and listened to his beat walk away from her across the cobbles, note and rest and rest and rest, and somewhere in the middle of the rests she'd heard it. Very faint. A thing in his beat she'd never have caught in anybody else's, because nobody else she knew had that much silence in theirs to hide it in.

He was laughing.

Not out loud. In the rests.

*He's saving it*, she'd thought. *He's skipped me on purpose.*

*Oh, he is so dead*.

***

The Broken Lance was full.

Lucan had taken the whole inn. He'd taken it at about nine in the morning, the moment thirty of the summoned came up the high street under ten thousand paper fishing rods with a crowd behind them and a squid waving from the front, and he'd stood in the door of the Lance in his red cloak with his arms out and wept, and paid the landlord for a week out of his own purse, and ordered everything. Everything. Every ham and cheese and loaf and pie and barrel in the house, and then sent boys to the market for more. The common room had six long tables in it and he'd had them pushed into one, a single enormous table down the middle of the room, and benches all round it, and lamps on every beam, and paper horns and paper squids and paper penguins hung from the rafters on threads.

And paper fishing rods. A hundred of them. Over the door. Over the hearth. Over the table, swaying in the warm air from the fire like reeds.

"*Just in case*," Lucan had said, to anyone who asked, all afternoon. And burst into tears again.

Nobody had told him. Newish was fairly sure about that. Nobody in Group 7 had said a word, and nobody else had either, and the guides and guards who knew, Maren and Bram and Emrys and Sabine and Petra and Kestra, were the kind of people who'd go to their graves with something rather than hand it to a man in a red cloak. Lucan didn't know who the water-carrier was. He'd watched thirty of the summoned cry round him in the street, and come back to his own inn and found them all sitting round him at the long table, and he still didn't know.

He'd asked her, once, in the kitchen passage, in the middle of the afternoon. "*Newish*. Who *is* he?"

"The water-carrier," Newish had said.

And Lucan had looked at her for a long moment, the master of the Lists for fifteen years, a man who'd sold a horned girl and an oni to a crowd that wanted to spit on them, who knew better than anyone alive when a thing was the kind you asked about and when it was the kind you didn't. And then he'd wiped his eyes on his red sleeve and said, "*Of course he is*," and gone off to order more cheese.

Ysolde had been sitting by the kitchen door. She hadn't said anything. But when the water-carrier had come in from the yard at noon with Jared, carrying the buckets between them, Ysolde had looked at him, once, and gone very still, the way Newish had seen her go still on the sand in front of a man with a real blade, and then looked away and not looked at him again all day. Ysolde was Reaching-tier. Ysolde had fought in the war. Newish didn't know what Ysolde had felt, looking at him. She hadn't asked, and she wasn't going to.

***

He'd spent the afternoon going round them all again. Properly, this time. Sitting down.

She'd watched him do it from the end of the long table, with a cup of Lucan's spiced wine going cold in her hands. He'd sat with Group 2 for an hour at the top end, with Hana's hand in his and Milli asking him questions in a rational voice and Jared beside him, still not saying anything, just sitting there close with his shoulder against his. He'd sat on the floor by the hearth with Group 5, with Tiger's head in his lap, literally, Tiger lying full length on the boards like a cat in the sun and refusing to move, and Bee drawing on the hearthstone with charcoal while she talked at him, and Hyvred beside him with her glasses up in her hair, just looking. He'd sat with Kei in the window, and Kei had told him what Hisui was saying, and he'd laughed.

He'd sat with Group 7 last.

Chris and Winter and Ina and Hako. And her. They'd had the corner table by the stairs, the one they always had, the one with Hako's knife marks in the edge from the first month. He'd come and sat down on the bench next to Chris, across from her, and she'd braced. Every single bit of her. She'd had the word in her mouth, behind her teeth, ready.

He'd talked to Chris about logging. He'd talked to Hako about head height. He'd talked to Winter about the trough at the east gate, and Winter had told him a whole story about a goat in Vietnam that Newish had never heard her tell anyone. He'd talked to Ina about the mask, quietly.

He hadn't said a word to her.

He'd looked at her. A couple of times. Across the table. And she'd heard it in his beat every time, in the rests, that faint thing she couldn't hear in anybody else's, the laugh. And she'd glared at him. And he'd turned and said something to Hako about crosshair placement.

"*Oh my god*," she'd said, at one point, out loud, to the table at large. "*Oh my god*."

"What?" said Chris.

"Nothing."

Winter had looked at her across the table with her pale-and-pink eyes, half-lidded, and her face doing nothing. And then, very slightly, the corner of Winter's mouth had gone up.

Winter knew. Winter noticed everything; it was the thing about her. She'd decided two years ago she was never going to be the last to know anything again, and she'd made herself into someone who noticed which way every secret in a room was moving. And she'd noticed this one. She'd noticed it all day.

"*Don't*," Newish had told her.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I'm never going to," Winter had said, with perfect calm, and drunk her wine. "I want to see what happens."

***

He went up the hill at dusk.

Newish heard him go. She didn't see it; she was in the kitchen passage arguing with Lucan about whether a cheese could be too big. But she heard his beat leave the room, the long slow rests going out of the door and down the step and away up the street toward the hill and the big house on it, and she stood in the kitchen passage with Lucan talking at her and listened to it go until she couldn't hear it any more, and found she'd stopped breathing.

"Newish?" said Lucan.

"He's gone to see somebody," said Newish.

"Who?"

She didn't know. She didn't know who was up on the hill except the king and the Advisor and four hundred people and a cook shouting about geese. But she'd heard his beat change as he went. Just slightly. The rests had got a little longer. A little heavier. The way a beat gets when somebody's walking toward a thing they've been not-walking-toward for a very long time.

"An old friend," said Newish, slowly. "I think."

The common room went quieter while he was gone. Not quiet. Thirty people and a lot of spiced wine couldn't be quiet. But quieter. People kept looking at the door. Tiger got up off the hearth and sat down again four times. Alea sat at the end of the long table with her sword propped against the wall behind her, a sword the size of a door, eating bread with great concentration, and every time Aleaka spoke anywhere in the room her ears went straight up and she had to look at the ceiling.

Newish went up to her room once, in the middle of it, and stood in front of the tin mirror.

It was a terrible mirror. It had been terrible for three months. It was warped down one side so that her face melted, and the tin had gone grey at the corners, and she'd stood in front of it every night since the winter and practised one word, until the landlord knocked on the wall.

She looked at herself in it. At her melting face.

"*Rude*," she said. Lightly. With her eyebrows up.

It was fine. It was perfect. She'd got it right weeks ago. It was exactly as light as it had been on a call at two in the morning in a room in Malacca, with the fan going, and the CS server lagging, and a man who'd just blamed the server for a shot he'd missed saying *love you guys, except Newish* and laughing.

*You can't say rude twice*, she'd told Chris, in the winter. *The second time it's just rude*.

She had one go.

She went back down.

***

He came back two hours after dark.

She heard him before anyone else did. The long rests, coming down the hill. Coming up the street. Coming up the step. They were different again. She stood in the middle of the common room with a jug in her hands, listening, and heard it: the rests had gone lighter. Not shorter. Lighter. As if the beat had been carrying something heavy up the hill and had set it down at the top, and now it was walking back down with its hands free.

The door opened.

The room went up like a bonfire.

Tiger got there first, again. Of course he did. He came off the hearth in one movement and across the room and into him in the doorway and hugged him, for the fourth time that day, and lifted him off the step.

"That's four," said Rachael, from the long table, without looking up from her bread. "Somebody should start charging."

"I *am* charging," said Dennis. "I've been charging since the gate. I'm keeping a ledger."

"Of course you are."

"Somebody has to think about the *economics*, Rae."

Tiger put him down. He came into the room. And Newish watched them all turn to him, every one of them, thirty people at a long table under a hundred paper fishing rods, the way plants turn to a window. She'd seen people do that for Lucan, in the Lists, when he stood up in the box in his red cloak and raised his arms. That was different. That was a crowd turning to a showman. This was thirty people turning to a man who'd come in out of the dark and stood in a doorway with his hands empty and his beat mostly rests.

He looked round at them. At all of them. Newish heard his beat do something she'd never heard a beat do: stop. Just for a moment. A rest so long that it wasn't a rest any more, it was the whole song holding its breath.

Then the note again. On time.

"Is there any of that pie left?" he said, in English.

There was a roar.

***

There was pie left. Lucan made sure there was pie left. Lucan, who didn't understand a word of English, understood *pie*, and went into the kitchen weeping and came out with three.

They put him at the middle of the long table, on the bench, between Alea and Gabe. People kept moving so they could sit nearer. Nobody could sit near enough. By the time the third pie was half gone the benches had given up being benches and people were sitting on the table and on the floor and on each other: Group 1 in a row along one side with Xola's squid sprawled across three plates; Group 4 opposite, Calvin with the kettle on the table in front of him like a trophy; Group 6 at the end in a heap, Azac taking up most of it, RX talking very fast about something to John, who had three little motes of light circling his head and was listening with his eyes shut.

Group 2 at the top. Group 5 on the floor. Group 7 in the corner by the stairs.

Newish sat on the stairs. Three steps up. Where she could see all of it, and hear all of it, and where she could get away if she needed to.

She heard all their beats.

That was the thing. She'd never heard so many in one room. Thirty of them. Thirty-one, with Alea, whose beat was quick and bright and exactly like a sword being swung by somebody who's just discovered it fits. Guides and guards, at the edges. Lucan's dance tune in the kitchen doorway. All of them, all at once, overlapping, tangling, fast and slow and hot and cold.

And then, some time after the second pie, without anyone doing anything, they started to line up.

She heard it happen. She'd never heard anything like it. Not in the Lists, not in the square, not on the night in the summer when ten thousand people held up paper horns and chanted Ina's name. Thirty beats in a room, all different, all their own, and one by one, without changing what they were, they were all finding the same place to put their weight. The same downbeat. As if there were a conductor somewhere in the room and they'd all just looked up.

She looked for the conductor.

He was sitting between his daughter and Gabe, eating pie. His beat was mostly rests. And every one of the thirty beats in the room was landing, now, exactly on the note he played between them.

Newish put her hands over her mouth on the stairs and sat there and listened to it for a long time.

***

Hako asked first.

He'd been working up to it all evening. Newish had heard it in his beat, the drum somebody was playing because they liked drums, getting faster and faster through the second pie and the third, the way a drum gets when somebody's about to do a solo. Then he got up off the corner bench and went round the table, past Group 4, past Calvin's kettle, and stood behind the bench where Ice was sitting, and said, loudly, cheerfully, to the back of his head:

"Teach me something."

Ice turned round.

"Anything," said Hako. "I don't care what. You were only getting started. Head height and peeking and that's it, that's all I got, eight months, and then—" He stopped. He went on, still cheerful, the way he always was, the kind of cheerful that made you believe things would work out because it so obviously believed it itself. "So. Teach me something. Here. With a spear. I've been doing head height with a spear for two years and Ysolde thinks I'm weird."

"You are weird," said Ysolde, from the kitchen door.

"*Thank you*, Ysolde."

Ice looked at him for a long moment. Newish heard his beat. A rest. A long one.

"Dawn," he said. "On the sand. Bring the spear."

Hako's whole face lit up like a lamp.

"There's a class," said Petra, from down the table. Petra was sitting with her sword on, like a soldier, between Xav and Alea, and had been all night. "On the road. Every morning. You'll have to queue."

"I'll queue."

"You'll queue behind *me*," said Petra.

"I'll queue behind *anybody*," said Hako, with tremendous happiness, and went back to the corner and sat down beside Chris and told him about it in detail, although Chris had been sitting right there and had heard the whole thing.

Chris wrote it down anyway.

He'd had the notebook out all night. Logging. The hour, and the light, and who was sitting where, and what they'd said. Newish watched him write. *Day 57. Broken Lance. Hako: lesson, dawn, sand.* And under it, after a moment, in his small square hand, *Thirty of thirty-five. Five on the road. Line thirty-six: filled.*

He showed it to Ice across the table. Ice read it, and nodded, and didn't say anything, and Chris put the notebook away under his arm and sat there with his face doing absolutely nothing, which was how Newish knew how much it meant.

***

Ina took the mask off at about midnight.

He'd had it on all day. Through the gate, and up the high street, and through the crowd, and then in the inn, too, at the long table, because Lucan had opened the windows and half of Valcrest had been looking in at them all afternoon. He wore it the way he always did in public: easily, with his hands behind his back, a gentleman in a demon's face, so that people looked at the oni instead of at Winter, and then looked away.

At midnight Lucan shut the shutters.

Ina sat at the corner table for a moment longer with the grey fanged mask over his face, looking across the room at the man between Alea and Gabe. Then he reached up and untied it, and took it off, and hung it at his shoulder by its red cords, where it grinned at the wall.

Underneath, his face was just his face. Teal eyes, and a fanged grin of his own, which was a lot less frightening than the mask's, and a single purple horn, and white hair going lavender at the ends. He looked tired. He looked, Newish thought, like a man who'd been standing between a crowd and someone he cared about for two years, and had just noticed, for the first time, that he didn't need to.

He got up and went round the table and sat down on the end of the bench next to Gabe, who moved over without being asked.

"You always thought I was cool," said Ina. Quite conversationally. In English.

"You are cool."

"I'm an *ogre*," said Ina. "I've got one horn and a demon's face on a string. Children cry. Lords say *demon* at dinner." He considered. "The paper-makers sell little white masks of my face. They've sold forty thousand."

"That's very cool," said Ice.

Ina looked at him. Then he laughed, the low warm cello of it, and put his elbow on the table and his chin in his hand, and said, "You know, you're the only person who ever said that to me. Back home. In all those calls. *Ina, that's so cool*. Every time. About anything. I'd tell you I'd made toast and you'd say *Ina, that's so cool*." He was quiet a moment. "I used to think you were taking the piss."

"I wasn't."

"I know," said Ina. "I worked it out, in the end. Here. On the sand. About a year too late." He looked down at the mask on his shoulder, grinning at the wall. "It was the nicest thing anyone ever said to me, and I thought it was a joke."

Ice didn't say anything. He just put his hand on Ina's shoulder, the one without the mask, for a moment. And Newish, on the stairs, heard Ina's beat do a thing it had never done in two years: the cello, the low warm cello that was in no hurry, played a note she'd never heard it play. High. Almost a laugh.

Newish read it on the ceiling of her window, on the stairs, and looked across the room.

He was reading it. She could tell. His eyes had gone to the place in front of his face that wasn't there, the way all their eyes went, and he was sitting very still between Alea and Gabe, and his shoulders were going, very slightly. Laughing. Without making a sound.

He didn't write anything. He never did. Even now, even tonight, with all thirty of them in one room and Maigo in capitals and Yan's single word at the bottom like a full stop. He read it all, every line, and closed his window, and didn't write a thing.

*Okay*, Yan had written.

Newish heard, three days out on an eastern road, in her head, a beat she'd never heard before but recognised anyway. Calm. Analytical. Counting to thirty-six.

***

Winter came and sat on the stairs beside her at about one in the morning.

She didn't say anything for a while. She just sat, with her long white hair down her back and her knees together and her hands in her lap, two steps above the noise, and listened to it with Newish. Her beat was the water in the cave. Slow. Clear. With the long silences that were the point of it.

"I was first," said Winter, eventually.

Newish looked at her.

"Today," said Winter. "At the gate. When he went round. I was the twelfth, or something, but I was *there*. I was in the square. I saw his face." She stopped. "I didn't see his face. Nobody can see his face. But I was there when the others did." She looked out at the room. "I've been last for three years. I was in Vietnam. I came home two months late and opened my messages and he'd been gone the whole time and I was the last of everyone to know." She paused. "Today I wasn't the last. I was at the gate."

"I know," said Newish. "I saw your frost."

"Everyone saw my frost."

"It was good frost."

"It was very good frost," said Winter, with complete seriousness. And then, after a while, looking out at the man between Alea and Gabe, who was listening to something Azac was telling him with both hands: "He's going to do it, isn't he."

"Do what?"

Winter just looked at her.

"*Don't*," said Newish.

"I'm not going to say anything," said Winter. "I told you. I want to see what happens." She was quiet. "You've got it ready."

"I've had it ready since the winter."

"I know," said Winter. "The landlord's complained twice."

***

It got late. Then it got later.

People started to go up. Not many, and not quickly. Lucan fell asleep in a chair by the kitchen door under his red cloak, weeping slightly in his sleep. Calvin fell asleep with his head on the table next to the kettle. Xola's squid had wrapped itself round the leg of a bench and refused to be moved. PenguChi went up with her penguin walking at her heels like a dog, and on the stairs she stopped beside Newish and said, very calmly, "Good luck," and went on up, and Newish stared after her and said "*What?*" and nobody answered.

Group 1 went up. Some of Group 6. Rachael, who said *goodnight* to the table in a voice that dared anyone to make anything of it, and then hugged Deston on the way past without breaking stride. Bee, who stopped at the bottom of the stairs and said to the water-carrier, quite loudly, "It's a *dog*," and went up without waiting for an answer.

And then, at about two in the morning, he stood up.

He stood up from the bench between Alea and Gabe, and stretched, the way a man does who's been sitting at a long table for a long time, and looked round at the ones who were left. There were still a lot of them. Group 2 at the top, Hana asleep on Milli's shoulder. Group 5 on the floor by the hearth, Tiger asleep with his head on Drew's knee. Group 7 in the corner. Gabe and Deston and Xav. Aleaka, and Alea, two fox girls at the end of the table who'd been not looking at each other all night. Kestra, on the stairs below Newish, who'd come down from the roof at some point without anybody noticing and was sitting there with her back against the wall and her eyes shut.

He stood there at the end of the long table, under the paper rods, with the fire going down behind him.

"Goodnight," he said.

Newish went very still.

Two in the morning. A room, and a fan going somewhere, and everybody on the call saying goodnight one at a time. That was how it had been. Exactly how. Yan had said goodnight, and Chris, and somebody else, and then Ice had said it, last, because he always said it last, and then he'd said the other thing.

Everybody in the corner knew. She'd told them. She'd told the whole table at the Broken Lance in the summer, the night after she shouted *down* across the sand: the last thing he'd ever said to her, and the last thing she'd ever said to him. Chris knew. Winter knew. Ina and Hako knew. They'd all gone very still too. Kestra on the stairs below her had opened her eyes.

She had it ready. She'd had it ready for three months, in a tin mirror, and for three years before that, without the mirror. It was right behind her teeth. Light. With her eyebrows up. The exact weight of a word said at two in the morning by somebody who's just lost thirteen to nine, partly because of her, to somebody who's just blamed the server.

*Love you guys. Except Newish.*

*Rude*.

She had one go.

"Love you guys," he said.

He looked round the room as he said it. At all of them. At the ones still awake, and the ones asleep on the benches and the floor and each other's shoulders, under the paper fishing rods, by the dying fire. And then he looked up the stairs.

At her.

And there was the laugh in his beat. In the rests. She heard it, as clear as she'd ever heard anything in her life.

"And you too, Newish," he said.

***

The word didn't have anywhere to go.

She'd had it ready for three years. She'd carried it across a world. She'd carried it the way you carry a single egg across a farmyard, so carefully, so as not to drop it before you got where you were going. And she'd got where she was going, and opened her hand, and he'd taken the farmyard away.

*And you too*.

He hadn't said *except*. He'd turned it round. The way he'd always turned things round, on every call, every time somebody thought they'd got him. He'd taken the joke she'd been waiting three years to finish, and finished it himself, the other way, so that there was nothing left for her to say at all.

She sat on the stairs with her hands over her mouth.

She could feel her face doing the thing she never let it do in public. She could feel Winter, beside her, very still. She could feel the whole corner of the room waiting, Chris and Ina and Hako, the way you wait at the top of a round when everybody's alive and the bomb's ticking and nobody knows who's going to peek first. And under all of it his beat, mostly rests, and in the rests the laugh, very kind.

She'd only brought the one word. That was the thing. She'd only ever brought the one.

So she said it anyway.

It came out of her round her hands, and it wasn't light, and her eyebrows weren't up, and it wasn't anything like the mirror. It came out cracked straight down the middle, wet, half a laugh and half the other thing, the voice of somebody who'd practised a word for three years and then found, when she finally got to say it, that it meant something completely different.

"...*Rude*."

***

Nobody laughed. Not at first.

Then Winter, beside her on the stairs, put her face into her hands and laughed so hard she nearly fell off the step. And Ina, at the end of the bench, put his head down on the table. And Hako said "*oh my god*," and Chris wrote something down very fast in the notebook and then put the pen down and covered his face. And Kestra, on the stairs below, who had not laughed out loud once in the whole of the two years Newish had known of her, made a short sharp sound like a door slamming that might have been a laugh, and then pretended it was a cough.

Newish got up.

She came down the stairs, past Kestra, and across the room, and stood in front of him. He was a lot taller than her. She couldn't see his face. She'd never be able to see his face. It didn't matter.

*I'm going to say rude*, she'd told the channel, on the night of the sum, last summer. *And then I'm going to hit him.*

She hit him.

On the arm. Hard. As hard as she could. It was like hitting a door.

"*You*," said Newish, "*are the worst person I have ever met*."

"I know," he said.

"*Three months*. In a *mirror*. The *landlord* complained."

"I know. I read it."

"You *skipped* me. At the *gate*. On *purpose*. You *laughed*."

"I did."

"*All day*. In your *rests*. I could *hear* you."

He didn't say anything to that. He just looked at her. And she heard it, in the long silence between his notes: not the laugh any more. Something else. Something so big and so quiet that she had no word for it at all, and didn't need one, and never would.

She put her arms round him, and her face into his coat, which still smelled very faintly of lake water, and held on.

***

She didn't know how long she stood there. Long enough for the fire to go down another notch. Long enough for Winter to come down the stairs and stand behind her, close, not touching, the way Winter stood behind people she cared about. Long enough for Chris to close the notebook.

When she let go he was still there. He'd been waiting. Of course he had. His whole beat was waiting.

"You can't say *rude* twice," she told him. Her voice still wasn't working. "That's the rule. I made it. The second time it's just rude."

"Then you've used yours."

"I've used mine," Newish agreed. She wiped her face on her sleeve. "I'm never saying it again. To you. Ever. As long as I live. You'll have to live without it."

"I'll manage."

"You *won't*," said Newish. "You'll miss it every day." And then, because she couldn't help it, because she was who she was and he'd always liked it, she put her chin up and narrowed her eyes at him across a room full of sleeping people in an inn at the bottom of another world, and said, in her most ragebaiting voice, the one that made Ysolde throw things: "*Love you too. Except you.*"

There was a pause.

Then he laughed. Out loud. Not in the rests. Properly, helplessly, the way Calvin had said he'd laughed on a hill the night before, so loud that Lucan woke up in his chair by the kitchen door and said *what* and Tiger lifted his head off Drew's knee and said *is it time to hug him* and Alea, at the end of the long table, put both hands over her face.

It was a good laugh. It was the best thing she'd ever heard.

She listened to it with everything she had. Note after note after note, no rests at all, not one. The whole song, all at once, for once in his life, out loud.

She'd tell Chris later to log it.

Chris already had.
