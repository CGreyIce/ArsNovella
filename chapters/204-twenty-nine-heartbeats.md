"Twenty-two," said Wendy.

She said it flatly, the way she always said it, without looking up from the arrow she was fletching. She was sitting on the wall walk with her back against the merlon and her silent headphones round her neck and a bundle of goose feathers in her lap, and she'd counted every one of his dawns for two years, and she had never once sounded impressed and never once sounded disappointed. It was one of the things Niko liked best about her. Wendy counted the way a clock counts. A clock doesn't mind what time it is.

He sat back against the parapet with blood on his lip.

Twenty-two. Again. It had been twenty-two at the first snow, on this wall, with the red going white for the first time anyone could remember, and he'd written it in the channel and Maigo had put eleven exclamation marks after it. It had been twenty-two at midwinter. Twenty-one in the thaw, twice. Twenty-three, once, on a morning in the spring when the wind dropped, and he'd thought *that's it, that's the wall gone*, and the next morning it had been twenty-two.

It was twenty-two now. The spring was nearly over. Out on the red the scrub at the edges had come up green this year, a thin green haze along the dry riverbeds toward the mountains, the first time in nine years, Aldous said. And on the knoll above the dry riverbed a long way out, past the markers, the young ash beside the black stone had every leaf out, bright, so that from the wall at dawn it looked like a small green flame.

He wiped his lip on the back of his hand.

"Again tomorrow," said Wendy.

"Yeah."

She looked up then. Not at his face. At his hands, on his knees. They were shaking. They always shook after, a bit. They were shaking more than a bit.

"You're pushing," said Wendy.

Niko didn't say anything.

"You worked that out," said Wendy. "In the summer. On this wall. With blood on your chin. You told us all. *I worked it out from a frying pan*." She went back to the arrow. "You stopped pushing, and you went from eleven to nineteen in one morning. And then twenty-two in the snow." She smoothed a feather. "And since the thaw you've been pushing again. I can hear it. You hold your breath at eighteen. You never used to hold your breath."

"I know," said Niko.

"So stop."

"I know," said Niko.

He looked at his window. It was still open, in front of his face, plain and white in the grey light.

And under the two of them, grey, unlit, the third line. With the faintest frost on it, if he let his eyes go down. And at the start of it, so faint you'd never see it if you didn't know it was there, a mark that went on, and off, and on.

"One left," said Niko.

Wendy's hands stopped on the arrow.

"I wrote that," he said. "In the channel. When Tiger went. *Nice one. Again. One left*." He looked at the line. "I meant it nicely. I meant it like, *well done, mate, now there's only me*. Like a joke." He was quiet. "And then I read it back. And it wasn't a joke. It was a number."

Wendy didn't say anything.

"Thirty-four," said Niko. "Chris has a book. With all of us in it. And a column at the end with what tier we are. And there's one line in the whole book with nothing in that column, and it's mine." He let his head go back against the stone. "I said I didn't mind being last. I said it to him on this wall. *Then be last*, he said. And I don't. I don't mind being last."

"But," said Wendy.

"But I'm not last any more," said Niko. "I'm *the* last. There's a difference." He shut his eyes. "Everyone's waiting."

***

Everyone was very kind about it. That was the worst part.

Nobody said anything. That was how he knew. On the channel, after Tiger, the whole thing had gone very careful round him, the way people go round somebody at a funeral. Maigo, who shouted everything, had written *NIKO YOU'RE GOING TO DO IT AND IT'S GOING TO BE SO COOL* in capitals, and then, an hour later, in lower case, which Maigo never used, *no pressure though*. Chris had put him in the bracket at *0–0 like everyone* and Niko had written *thanks* and meant it so much he'd had to put the window away. Gigichi had written *niko. you okay?* and he'd written *yeah gigi* and she'd written *liar* and then *that's fine. ask me if you want to know how*. Hyvred had sent a strip by Kei to say she'd looked at his window from Thornwall in her head, which she couldn't do, and it was *fine, it's fine, it's just very full*.

Very kind. All of them. And under all of it, the thing nobody said. Thirty-four. One left.

It followed him round the fort.

Down the stair from the wall, into the courtyard, where the morning was starting. Past the long hall, with the smoke of the cook's fires coming out of the louvre, and Teague on the step with his eggs and a spoon of Salty's pepper paste, who looked up and said *morning, Niko* in a voice that was exactly as kind as everyone else's. Past the infirmary, where Aldous was standing in the door of his little chapel with his book under his arm, and who didn't say anything at all but looked at Niko's lip, and then at his hands, and then took Niko's wrist in his fingers as he went by, without stopping him, and felt for the beat there for a count of ten, and let go.

"Well?" said Niko.

"Fast," said Aldous. "Still there." He opened the book. "Two paths. Half the speed of either. Maybe less." He looked at Niko over the top of it. He'd said it in the first week. He'd said it every week since. "I didn't say the heart couldn't take it. I said I didn't know if it could. Nobody does. Nobody's done it."

"I know."

"Then stop holding your breath at eighteen," said Aldous, and went back into the chapel.

Niko stood in the courtyard.

*Did Wendy tell him*, he thought, and then, *no, of course she didn't, she didn't need to*. Everyone in this fort could count. They'd all been counting him for two years.

***

The elves were at the forge.

They mostly were, in the mornings. Nobody had expected that. When three people walk out of the end of the world and kneel to a snake in the snow, you expect them to spend their days doing something mysterious and old, and they did, some of the time: Tavrin sat with Aldous over the book for hours and sang under her breath, and the three of them went out to the knoll every few days and stood at the edge of the green round the grave and didn't step on it. But mostly they were at the forge. Tavrin liked the hammer marks. Liraun liked the peppers. And Oen liked anything that could be lifted, thrown or arm-wrestled, and the forge had all three.

He was arm-wrestling Salty when Niko came across the yard.

On the anvil. Salty's elbow on one side and Oen's on the other, with Oakes standing over them looking pained, because it was his anvil. Salty's face had gone a deep slow red and his whole enormous arm was shaking. Oen, who was thinner than Salty's leg, was leaning on his other elbow with his chin on his fist, looking bored, with his long swept-back ears tilted forward with interest.

"You are using the bear," said Oen, in the kingdom's tongue, badly.

"I'm not," said Salty, through his teeth.

"You are. I feel it. It sits on your arm. Heavy." Oen smiled. "But you cannot move what the bear sits on. You told me. Yesterday." He put Salty's hand down on the anvil, gently, as if laying a cup on a table. "So."

"*AGAIN*," said Salty, which he'd got from Rhea, who'd lost to Oen eleven times since the winter and said it every time.

Liraun laughed. She was crouched at the back wall of the forge by the warm stones, where Salty's pepper plants stood in their row of pots, six of them now, all fruiting, red and green and one gone so dark it was nearly black. She was talking to them. She always was; in the singing tongue, very softly, the way you'd talk to someone in the next bed. Niko heard it as English, the way all of them did. *You're so angry*, she was saying. *You're so angry and so far from home. It's all right. It's all right to be angry*.

Tavrin was sitting on the forge step with her stave across her knees, watching all of it.

She looked up as Niko came. Winter-ice eyes, in a face like birch bark. She looked at him the way she looked at hammer marks.

"You are thinking with your breath," she said.

Niko stopped.

"Everyone tells me," he said.

"Everyone is right. That is very annoying. I know." Tavrin looked at his hands. "In the mornings. On the wall. You hold it in, at the top." She tapped her own chest, once, with two long fingers. "Like a smith who has hit the iron too hard and is afraid to hit it again." She looked at Salty's hammer, on the bench. "I can read that in the iron. I can read it in you."

"How?"

"You are a thing being made," said Tavrin, as if it were obvious. "Things that are being made remember it. I told the big one." She nodded at Salty, who was rubbing his elbow and glaring at Oen. "A thing that is made remembers being made. You will remember these mornings. Every one." She looked at him. "I would make them better mornings."

He went to find Yan after that, because when everybody in a fort is right about you there's only one person to go to, and it's the one who won't tell you.

Yan was on the east wall, at the eleventh merlon. The one with the cracked coping. He'd started sitting there in the winter, since the grave, since he went green. Not in the gap; nobody sat in the gap. That was the man's place, and had been since he was fifteen, and they all left it empty for him the way you leave a chair empty at a table. Yan sat just beside it, with his back against the twelfth, and his eyes shut, in the sun.

He was asleep.

Niko sat down beside him, quietly, and didn't wake him.

Yan slept a lot now. An hour more a night, Wendy said, since the grave; she kept it in her head the way she kept everything, like a spreadsheet. Kei had a slate about it, at Thornwall. Nobody said anything about it out loud. Yan didn't. He just slept in the sun on the wall in the afternoons sometimes, with his topknot coming loose and his silver-white hair going gold at the edges in the light and his long ears turned down, and the jade drops in them very still. Suzu lay along his collarbone, white, with her head on his chest, over his heart, and watched the world with one green eye.

She turned it on Niko.

He'd never got used to that. Two and a half years, and he'd never got used to being looked at by Suzu. It wasn't hostile. It wasn't anything. It was like being looked at by the weather.

"Hi," said Niko, to the snake. Quietly. Feeling stupid. "I'm not waking him. I just—" He didn't know what he just. "I just wanted to sit somewhere nobody's being kind."

Suzu looked at him.

Then she put her head down again, on Yan's chest, and shut the green eye. Which was, Niko had learned, the nearest thing to *fine* that Suzu had ever said to anyone who wasn't Yan.

He sat there in the sun on the east wall with his back to the merlon and looked out at the red.

It was green at the edges. Truly green. He hadn't believed it until he saw it. Out past the markers, in their long rows, all the way to the mountains, the dry riverbeds had a green line along them like somebody had drawn them in with a pen. And out on the knoll, the ash, small and bright. And nothing moving. Nothing at all. Rhea had sent scouts out every week since the thaw, and they'd come back every week with nothing, and Rhea had said, at supper, the last time, *I don't like nothing. Nothing's what you get before something*, and Wendy had said *that's not a sentence* and Rhea had said *it is on a border*.

He opened his window. He didn't decide to. It was a habit, like touching a sore tooth.

The two lines. Ninety-six. Ninety-six.

And the third.

He let his eyes go soft. Down. Past the light, the way Thea had written to him. And there it was, faint, under the grey: the frost. The shapes on the inside of the glass. And the little upright mark at the start of the line, going on, and off.

*Enter name.*

He'd been looking at it for nine months. On the wall, at dawn, at twenty-two heartbeats, every morning, when it lit. He'd seen it go from grey to white under his eyes a hundred and fifty times, and the frost-letters come up in it, and change, and change, and change, like a phone screen guessing what you're typing. He'd never been able to read them. They went too fast. They were too faint.

Except that once. At midwinter. At twenty-two. For half a heartbeat.

He hadn't told anyone.

"You're thinking very loud," said Yan, without opening his eyes.

Niko jumped.

"Sorry."

"It's fine. Suzu woke me. She says you've been sitting there doing sums at her for ten minutes." Yan yawned, enormously, and opened his eyes, and looked at Niko. Just at Niko. "What is it?"

Niko looked at the line.

"It guessed," he said.

Yan waited.

"At midwinter," said Niko. "At twenty-two. The frost-letters. They go too fast to read, they always have. They come up and they almost make a word and they go. Like it's trying things." He swallowed. "And at midwinter, for half a heartbeat, one of them stayed long enough. And I read it."

Niko didn't answer straight away.

He'd been embarrassed about it for four months. That was the stupid thing. It was such a stupid thing to be embarrassed about.

"*Battlemage*," he said.

Yan looked at him.

"In English. In capitals. Like a class name. Like the thing you pick on the character screen in a game, under the box where you put your name." Niko looked at his hands. "*BATTLEMAGE*. And then it went. And something else came up that I couldn't read."

"*No*," said Niko.

It came out of him much harder than he meant. Suzu opened one eye.

"No," said Niko again, quieter. "It's wrong. I knew it was wrong the moment I read it. It was like—" He looked for it. "It was like somebody calling you by your brother's name. Near enough. Wrong." He looked at the line. "A battlemage is a mage. Who fights. It's a mage first. The mage is the thing and the battle is what it does. It's got one in front of the other." He was quiet. "I'm not one in front of the other. That's the whole point. That's the only point. If I was one in front of the other I'd have been Drawn two years ago, or Spark, and I'd be in Chris's book with something in the column like everyone else."

Yan didn't say anything.

"I think that's why it keeps changing its mind," said Niko slowly. He'd never said it out loud before. He'd thought it on the wall, at dawn, a hundred times, and never said it. "It's guessing. Every word it's got for this puts one of them first. *Battlemage*. Probably *magic knight*, *spellsword*, *hybrid*, whatever. Every one. It tries one, and it's wrong, and it knows it's wrong, so it tries another. And they're all wrong." He looked at the little blinking mark at the start of the line. "Because nobody ever gave it a word for this. So it's guessing. And it can't guess right, because there isn't anything right to guess."

"Suzu says," said Yan, slowly, "it's rude."

"Guessing," said Yan. "Somebody's name." He was listening. His face had gone very still, the way it went when she said something he hadn't expected her to. Niko looked at the snake.

"*My name is the one he gave me*," said Yan, very quietly. Not his words. Hers. He'd said them before; they all knew them; Kei had written them in English on a strip in the winter, from a cord, from Hisui. "She says: tell the small one that. He'll know what to do with it."

Niko sat on the wall for a long time after that.

He didn't know what to do with it. Not yet. He held it in his head, very carefully, the way you'd hold a smoke grenade with the pin out, and didn't throw it.

Salty was trying to toss the skillet.

Niko heard it before he saw it. A sound from the forge like somebody dropping a cartwheel onto a cobbled street, and then Oakes shouting, and then a lot of laughing in at least two languages.

He went down.

The forge was full. It was always full when Salty cooked; it had been since the eleven dinners, the summer before, when a man had stood at the mouth of this fire with a skillet in one hand and the whole fort had come to the step. Rhea was in the door with her arms folded. Teague was on a barrel. Garth, the recruit who'd come east in the winter to see where the water came from, was sitting on the bench with three of the other new ones, very upright, the way recruits sit when they've been told to stay out of the way and are determined to obey with every muscle. Wendy was on the step. Maigo was in the corner by the peppers with Liraun, and the bells on his antlers were going because he couldn't keep still. And Cook, the fort's cook, who'd threatened to leave twice over the wrong peppers and not left, was standing by the bellows with her hands on her hips, watching her kitchen's pepper paste being treated with what she clearly felt was insufficient respect.

Salty was at the fire.

He had Oakes's skillet in one hand. The big one. The cartwheel, black iron, wide as a shield, the one Oakes had lent for the eleven dinners and never asked for back. It was on the fire, and it was red, and there were noodles in it, and egg, and shallots, and garlic, and a great deal of Salty's paste, made with Salty's peppers. The right peppers. For the first time. From the seeds he'd picked out of a paper bag of the man's leftovers, from a street by the ships in Valcrest, and grown on the warm stones behind this hearth all winter.

"Twelve dinners," said Wendy, beside Niko, on the step. "In the summer. He's practising."

"I know."

"He's been practising since the autumn. With the wrong peppers." She watched Salty. "These are the right ones. First time." She was quiet. "He was up at four picking them."

Salty heaved.

He did it the way Salty did everything: with all of him. He gripped the skillet's iron handle in his big fist and set his feet and lifted, to toss it, the way he'd watched the man do it, the whole lot up into the air over the fire—

And the skillet didn't go up.

It went *down*. All at once. Like a stone. Onto the fire-bed, *clang*, with a sound like a dropped cartwheel, and a great spurt of sparks, and the noodles leaping out of it sideways onto the hearth.

The forge exploded with laughter.

"*It's the bear*," said Salty, red to the ears. "It's the *bear*. Every time I try to throw it, it goes *heavy*. I can't—" He looked at the skillet with real hatred. "I can't move what I'm carrying. You can't *toss* a thing if you're *carrying* it."

"Let me," said Oen.

He came off the anvil. Rhea, in the door, said *oh no* in the voice of a woman who'd lost to him eleven times. Oen picked up the skillet, one-handed, as if it weighed nothing, and the noodles that were left in it, and looked at it with great interest. Then he flicked his wrist.

The noodles went up.

All of them. Very fast. Very high. Up out of the skillet, past the fire, past the chimney hood, and over the forge, in a great tangled arc, and out of the open door, and over Rhea's head, into the courtyard, where most of them landed on Garth.

Garth didn't move. He sat on the bench with noodles in his hair, very upright, with his eyes straight in front of him, obeying.

Liraun laughed so hard she sat down among the peppers.

"*Child*," said Tavrin, from the step, with her face in one long hand.

"It went up," said Oen, pleased.

"It is supposed to come *back*," said Tavrin.

"Nobody said."

***

Niko didn't laugh.

He stood on the forge step and looked at the skillet in Oen's hand, black and red, and at Salty glaring at it, and at the noodles in the courtyard. And at the fire.

He'd been here. The eleventh night. In the summer. He'd stood at that door with Wendy and watched a man he'd known since he was seventeen hold that skillet out into a fire hot enough to soften iron, bare-handed, and throw everything in it up. Into the flame. Through the smoke. And let it come back down. Twelve heartbeats. Salty had counted them on the bellows.

And he'd gone up to the wall the next morning, and stopped pushing, and done it. Eleven to nineteen. He'd *worked it out*. From a frying pan. He'd told everyone.

And then the thaw had come, and the channel had started counting down, and Tiger had gone, and he'd written *one left*; and since the thaw he'd gone up onto the wall every morning and held his breath at eighteen and *pushed*.

He'd forgotten.

Not the idea. He knew the idea; he could have explained it to anyone. He'd forgotten what it felt like. In his hands. In his wrist. He'd turned it into a thing he knew instead of a thing he did, and then the number had got loud, and when the number got loud he did what everybody does when they're frightened, which was hold on harder.

"Can I," said Niko.

The forge went quiet. They all turned round. He was standing on the step, small, thin, with blood still dried at the corner of his lip from the morning.

Salty looked at him a long moment. Then he took the skillet out of Oen's hand, and held it out to Niko, handle first.

"Mind," said Salty. "It's hot."

"I know," said Niko.

***

It was very heavy.

That was the first thing. He'd never held it. He'd only watched. It was black iron the size of a shield, and the handle was hot through the rag Salty gave him, and his arm, which was thin, went down with it at once, and he had to put his other hand under the handle to hold it at all.

Cook, without a word, put more noodles in. More egg. Shallots. Garlic. A spoon of the paste, the dark red one, the angry one. Oakes worked the bellows. The fire came up white.

He held it out into the fire.

It went red. He felt it go red; the heat came up the handle and through the rag into his palms. It was frightening. Everything in him said *pull back*. He didn't.

The food hissed, and spat, and the smell came up off it, garlic and the dark sweet heat of the peppers, and it started to catch on the iron.

And he tried to toss it, and pushed.

He knew he was doing it as he did it. His arms went *up*, straight up, the way you'd push a door, and the skillet went up an inch and came down again, *clang*, and half the noodles went on the hearth.

Nobody laughed. That was the worst thing. They were all being kind.

He tried again. He pushed again. Worse.

He stood there with the skillet out over the fire and his arms shaking and the noodles burning and the whole fort watching him in silence, and he was holding his breath. He noticed it. *Eighteen*, he thought, for no reason, and nearly laughed.

He let it out.

And he thought about the man's wrist.

Not his arms. His arms hadn't done much. Niko had watched; he'd been watching the man's hands since he was seventeen, on a mouse, on a keyboard, on a rifle in a game, holding an angle. He knew those hands better than anyone's. And at the fire, on the eleventh night, the arms had hardly moved. It was the *wrist*. A little flick, forward and up, and then a drop, a give, the hand going down a fraction just as the food left the pan, so the pan got out of the way. So it wasn't throwing the food up. It was taking the pan *away*. Letting go of it for a moment. Letting the food go up on its own, because it was already going, because it wanted to, out of the heat. And then bringing the pan back underneath, at the bottom, to catch it.

You didn't push it up.

You let it go up, and you were there when it came down.

He flicked his wrist.

The noodles went up. Not far. A hand's breadth. Out of the pan, into the air over the fire, into the edge of the flame, and he felt something happen to them there, a change, a smell like iron and smoke and the breath of something enormous; and his wrist dropped, and the pan was under them, and they came down into it.

Changed.

The forge made a sound. Not laughing.

He did it again. Up. Into the flame. Down. Again. Up, and the flame caught the edge of the cloud of food and went through it like a bright breath, and down into the iron, and the smell came off it, smoky and sweet and dark, the smell of every hawker stall he'd ever queued at in his life at eleven at night with his friends, and he was laughing. He didn't know when he'd started. He was standing at the mouth of a forge fire at the edge of the world with a skillet the size of a shield in both shaking hands, tossing noodles into a flame, laughing.

"Seven," said Wendy, on the step. Flatly. Counting.

Up. Down.

"Eight."

His arms burned. He didn't push. He let it go up, and was there when it came down. Over and over. *You just toss it*.

"Eleven. Twelve."

Off the fire. Onto the bench. Cook had plates out; she'd had plates out the whole time, Niko realised, and hadn't said anything, because she was Cook.

He put the skillet down.

He stood there with his hands shaking and his arms on fire and his face wet, and he didn't know if it was the smoke.

Salty came round the end of the hearth. He picked up a fork. He ate a mouthful, standing at the bench. He chewed it for a long time.

"That's it," said Salty, quietly. "That's the taste."

He didn't say anything else. He ate the whole plate standing up in silence, the way he had on the eleventh night, and nobody in the forge said a word.

Tavrin, on the step, looked at Niko for a long moment with her winter-ice eyes. Then she looked at his hands. And nodded, once, as if she'd read something in the iron.

***

He went up on the wall at dawn.

Wendy was already there. She was always already there. She was sitting against the merlon with her goose feathers and an arrow, and she didn't look up.

"Ready when you are," she said.

He sat down. Cross-legged. On the stone. Facing east, the way he always did, toward the red and the mountains and the small green flame on the knoll.

He didn't do anything for a while. He sat there and breathed and let the night go out of his arms, which still ached.

Then he began.

Aura first. It always was. Down in his chest, the warm thing, the swordsman's thing, the one that had taken him a year to find. He let it come up. And mana, on the other side, the cold bright one, the one that came in from outside like breath. Two candles, Yan called them. Two candles that keep almost touching.

He let them touch.

The circle closed. Out like a spell, in like breath, round, and round, no seam.

"One," said Wendy.

He felt the grey line come up in his window, under the other two. He didn't look.

"Six. Seven."

He didn't hold his breath. He noticed that. He just breathed. It felt like doing nothing. It felt like standing at the mouth of a fire with a pan.

"Eleven. Twelve."

The wall came.

It always came. At eleven, once. Now at eighteen, nineteen, somewhere round there: the moment the two candles stopped wanting to go round and wanted, with everything in them, to stop, to come apart, to be two things again. It came up through him like a tide. Everything in him said *hold it*. *Hold it together. Don't let go. You'll lose it*.

"Eighteen."

He didn't hold it.

He flicked his wrist.

That was what it felt like. Nothing else. A little flick, forward and up, from somewhere in the middle of his chest, and a drop, a give, the hand going down a fraction so the pan got out of the way. He let them go *up*. Both of them. Out. Away from each other, the way they wanted, out past the edge of him into the cold dawn air over the red, the aura and the mana both, apart, gone, and for half a heartbeat there was nothing in his chest at all.

It was still the most frightening thing he'd ever done.

And then he was there when they came down.

"Nineteen," said Wendy. "Twenty."

They came back changed. Through the edge of something. Already round each other, already going round, smoky and dark and sweet, the way the food came down into the iron. He felt the third line light under the other two, grey to white, like a lamp coming on in a window in a street you know.

"Twenty-two."

He tossed it again.

"Twenty-three."

The frost-letters came up on the line. He felt them. He didn't look. He didn't try to read them. He let his eyes stay soft, down, and watched them the way you'd watch somebody's hands across a table: coming up, almost a word, going; another; going. Guessing. Trying things on. Every one of them wrong, and knowing it, and going.
