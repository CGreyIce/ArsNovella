The coats came on the coldest morning of the year, on a cart pulled by a mule with frost in its whiskers, and Xola cried before she'd even tried hers on.

She couldn't help it. She'd been cold for three months. They all had. The militia hall had a fireplace at each end of the long room and draughts in every wall, and the clothes they'd arrived in had been made for a world with central heating: a jersey worn as a dress, a jellyfish skirt, a cardigan falling off one shoulder, a little lace camisole. They'd bought what they could in the Wrenhaven market with the stipend Dennis had somehow negotiated for them. But the stipend was small and the market was smaller, and by midwinter they'd been sleeping in everything they owned and still waking up with blue fingers.

And then a cart pulled up in the snow outside the gate, and a big grey man in a mayor's chain climbed down off it, and said, "Gift from the town," and began unloading.

Five coats. Heavy wool, lined with fleece, with deep hoods and horn buttons. Not new. Each one had been mended, carefully, in places. But each one was clean and thick and *warm*, and when Xola put hers on, it came down past her knees and swallowed her whole and she stood in the snowy yard with Squiddles peering out of the hood like a small purple sentry and burst into tears.

"Oh, no," said Rowan Hale, alarmed. "Is it the wrong size? I told him— I told *them*, at the hall, I said, she's small, she'll want—"

"It's *perfect,*" Xola wailed.

Rowan looked deeply relieved, and also as if he'd very much like to be somewhere else.

Behind the coats there was firewood. An entire cartload, split and stacked, enough to keep both fireplaces roaring until spring. Bram took one look at it and sat down on the step and put his face in his hands.

"Mayor's compliments," said Rowan, gruffly, to Maren. "The town's very grateful. For your being here. And so on."

Maren looked at the coats. Then at the wood. Then at Rowan, for a long, steady moment.

"Please thank the town," she said, "for us."

Something passed between them that Xola didn't understand. Rowan nodded once, sharply, climbed back up onto the cart, and drove away through the snow without looking back.

***

"The *town,*" said Higu, that evening, by the roaring fire.

She had her notebook open on her knees. Xola had seen the notebook before. Higu never let anyone read it, but everyone knew what was in it, more or less.

"What about the town?" said Xola, who was still wearing her coat indoors and had no intention of ever taking it off.

"The town didn't send them." Higu wrote something, very small. "The town doesn't know our sizes. The town didn't know PenguChi needed one with long sleeves, or that Gigichi's the tallest." She looked up. "Somebody measured us. With their eyes. From a distance."

There was a short silence.

"The fisherman," said Thea quietly, from the other end of the hearth.

Nobody said anything.

"You think everything's the fisherman," said Gigichi eventually, but she said it gently. She'd been saying everything gently since the night of the bonfire.

"I think a lot of things are the fisherman," said Higu. "I don't know why yet." She closed the notebook. "I'm just writing it down."

***

The water moved for Xola three days later.

Not still. *Moved.*

She'd been making the washbasin go still for months. It was her party trick: get upset, or get calm, or just look at it the right way, and the water in the basin would go flat as glass, no ripple, no tremble, as if it were holding its breath. Everyone had seen her do it. Bram called it "the axolotl stare."

But Thea had written to everyone after the bonfire. Not just to Chris and Cursiffe; to every group, every one of the thirty-five, a long letter in her careful hand that she'd sat up two nights writing. And it said, more or less: *You can't get over the edge by reading. You've all been reading. Sensing. Feeling. That's half of it. The other half is writing. You have to put something back into the world. One small line. In its language, not yours.*

Xola had read it eleven times.

On the third morning, at the washbasin, she didn't make the water go still. She looked at it, and felt for it, the way she always did, and then, for the first time, she *asked* it to do something.

*Turn,* she thought. *Please. Just once.*

And the water in the basin turned. A slow, lazy circle, like a whirlpool in a puddle, round and round, three times. Then it slopped over the edge and soaked her sleeve.

Xola stood there with water dripping off her elbow and her mouth wide open.

"*SQUIDDLES,*" she shrieked. "*SQUIDDLES, IT MOVED.*"

Squiddles, on her head, had seen it, and was vibrating.

Her window, when she opened it with shaking hands, said twenty-three. It had said nineteen the night before.

That was the biggest jump any of them had had in a single night since they'd arrived.

***

Thea went to the market alone that afternoon, because someone had to buy lamp oil, and because she couldn't sit still.

She hadn't been able to sit still since the bonfire. Not really. She had a little flame she could call to the tip of her finger now, any time she liked, and a skill called *Parse* that let her look at the lattice of the world the way she'd always looked at code: structure and syntax and intent, all laid out in front of her. She spent most of her waking hours doing it. She parsed the fire. She parsed the snow. She parsed the old grumpy tree by the gate and found something in it so slow and deep and ancient that she'd had to stop and sit down.

And she parsed her own window, every night, down at the bottom, where the root ran away toward the first window in the world. Still running, she was sure of that now. Still warm.

And the sealed value. *Medium: one blade.* And the door with no handle beside it.

She'd written to Chris about the root. She hadn't written to anyone about what she'd started to think. Not yet.

The market was quiet in the snow: half the stalls shuttered, the other half selling the few things you could still sell in midwinter. Firewood. Salt fish. Cheese. Eggs, from the woman at the corner stall with the red shawl. Thea bought her lamp oil from the chandler and turned to go back, and walked straight into somebody coming the other way.

"Sorry," she said, "sorry, I wasn't—"

She stopped.

It was him.

***

He was carrying a basket of eggs. That was the first thing she noticed, absurdly. A basket of eggs, and a coat with the collar turned up, and a long fishing rod over one shoulder, the way he'd had it on the road the first week. Plain. Ordinary. Somewhere in his thirties, with a patient, tired face, the kind of face you'd walk past in any market in any world and never look at twice.

And under her window, the sealed value slammed against its door so hard she nearly dropped the oil.

"No harm done," said the fisherman. He'd steadied the eggs. Not one of them had moved. "Mind the ice by the trough. It's bad today."

He started to step round her.

"Wait," said Thea.

He stopped. Politely. Patiently. The way you stop for a stranger who's dropped something.

Thea had thought about this. She'd thought about it a great deal, lying awake, in the weeks since the road. What she'd say, if she ever saw him close. She'd made lists. She'd drafted it like a letter. And now he was standing in front of her in the snow with a basket of eggs, and every word of it had gone straight out of her head.

"I'm Thea," she said. "I'm one of the summoned. At the militia hall." She held out her hand, because it was what you did, on Earth, and she didn't know what you did here. "I wanted to thank you. For the fish."

He looked at her hand for a moment. Then he shifted the basket of eggs to his other arm and shook it. Briefly. His grip was dry and warm and perfectly ordinary.

"Alea's the one who carries them," he said.

"But they're your fish."

"They're the lake's fish." The faintest crease at the corner of his eyes. "I just ask them politely."

***

She tried, after that. She'd promise herself later that she'd tried properly.

"Has the lake frozen yet?" she said, because it was the first thing she could think of that kept him standing there. "All the way?"

"Not yet. Another week, maybe. It always freezes from the north shore."

"Do you fish through the ice?"

"Sometimes. When it's thick enough. It's slow." He seemed to consider it. "I don't mind slow."

"Alea says that's what you always say." She watched his face. "She says you say a lot of things. She quotes you all the time. About the first tier. About waiting. She says you told her the first tier is the first time the world answers back."

"Sounds like a sensible man," said the fisherman.

Nothing. Not a flicker. Not a pause. Just the same patient, slightly tired politeness, as if she'd told him it was going to snow again.

"I reached the first tier," said Thea. "At the bonfire. You probably heard. Everyone heard."

"I did hear. Congratulations." He sounded as if he meant it. "That's early, for anyone. You must have worked very hard."

"I didn't, really. I just figured out how to write it." She took a breath. "In the world's language. Not mine. Everything here has a language underneath it. Did you know that? The fire, the snow. Even the windows." She paused, very deliberately. "Status windows. Do you know about those? The Advisor calls the words *the Hero's words*. Status. Inventory. Skills."

"I've heard the stories," said the fisherman.

"They're English," said Thea. "The words. They're from our language. From where we came from."

"Are they?" He shifted the eggs again. "I wouldn't know. I've never been anywhere but here."

The door at the bottom of her window was rattling so hard it felt like a heartbeat.

She looked at him: at his plain face and his patient eyes and the rod over his shoulder that wasn't quite a fishing rod. She'd seen that much, on the road. She'd Parsed it, just now, without meaning to. The shaft had an edge running all the way along it, folded into the wood like a secret.

And she tried one last thing.

She'd thought about it for weeks. It was stupid. It was probably nothing. But Higu had said it, round the fire, back in the autumn, telling stories about him: *and then he'd have said 'trust' at the end, and everyone would have known it was a lie.* And everyone who'd ever been in a call with Ice knew that bit. Every single one of them.

"Well," said Thea, as lightly as she could, "I'll pay you back for all the fish one day. I promise." She smiled. "*Trust.*"

The fisherman looked at her.

For one second, maybe less, she thought something moved in his face. Very deep down. Like a fish turning under ice.

Then it was gone, if it had ever been there.

"No need," he said pleasantly. "There's plenty of fish." He nodded toward the ice by the trough. "Mind that. It's bad today."

And he stepped round her, and walked away through the market with his eggs and his rod, unhurried, past the egg-woman, who called something after him. He answered her over his shoulder without stopping, something about her son's leg, and then he turned the corner toward the lake road, and was gone.

***

Thea stood in the snow with her lamp oil for a long time.

Under her window, slowly, the sealed value stopped rattling. The door went still. Shut. As if it had never moved at all.

*I've never been anywhere but here.*

She'd Parsed that, too. She hadn't meant to. It was getting harder and harder not to Parse things; the skill seemed to do it on its own now, the way your eyes read a sign before you've decided to look at it. And she'd felt something, in the structure of the sentence. Not a lie, exactly. Dennis, in Kingsferry, could hear lies like a cracked bell; Thea couldn't. But she could see the *shape* of things.

And that sentence had a shape like a door being closed very gently, by someone who'd closed it a great many times before.

She walked back to the militia hall slowly, through the snow, carrying the oil, thinking.

He hadn't reacted to anything. Not to Alea's sayings. Not to the Hero's words. Not to *English*, which should have meant nothing at all to a fisherman who'd never been anywhere. Not even to *trust*. He'd been patient and polite and perfectly ordinary, and he'd small-talked her about the ice for three full minutes as if he'd been doing it his whole life, and he'd walked away without once giving her anything to hold onto.

That was the thing. That was what stayed with her, all the way up the hill.

Nobody was *that* good at giving you nothing. Not unless they'd had a great deal of practice.

***

"Well?" said Higu, the moment she came through the door.

Thea looked at her. Higu was sitting by the fire with her notebook on her knees, as if she'd been waiting. She probably had.

"I bumped into him," said Thea. "At the market. The fisherman."

The long room went very quiet. Xola, in her coat, stopped trying to make the washbasin turn again. Gigichi looked up from her bed. PenguChi, in the corner with Waddles, went still.

"And?" said Higu.

Thea sat down by the fire. She held out her hands to it. They were shaking a little, and not from the cold.

"I thanked him for the fish," she said. "He said they were the lake's fish. We talked about the ice. I told him about the bonfire. I told him about the Hero's words being English." She swallowed. "I said *trust*."

"And?"

"Nothing," said Thea. "He said *no need, there's plenty of fish*. And told me to mind the ice by the trough."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That's it?" said Gigichi.

"That's it." Thea looked into the fire. "He gave me nothing. Nothing at all. Every single thing I tried, he just... stepped round it. Like it was ice by a trough."

Higu was writing. Very small, in the back of the notebook, on the page Thea knew was there: the one with the line she hadn't finished drawing.

"And your window?" said Higu, without looking up.

Thea was quiet for a long moment.

"It rattled the whole time," she said. "The whole time he was standing there. Harder than it ever has for Alea. And when he walked away, it stopped."

Higu stopped writing.

Across the room, the water in Xola's washbasin, which nobody was touching, gave one small, slow turn, all on its own, and went still.
