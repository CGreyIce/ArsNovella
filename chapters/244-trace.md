*Session 651*, Chris wrote. *Day 4 after. Still 99.95. Not rounding.*

He wrote it on the sand at the foot of the west wall of the Lists, at dawn, with his back against the stone and his notebook on his knee, the way he'd started every session for three years. Above him, forty feet up, thirty-six gold boxes in a long tree with the names in them, and the bye in the thirty-sixth box with no name, and in the corners of two of the boxes, in his own chalk, *looking* and *making her come down*, which nobody had rubbed out since the Lists, because nobody would. The lamps along the top of the wall were out. The sand was cold and grey and raked smooth, the way Lucan's boys raked it every evening whether anyone was going to use it or not.

He'd been at ninety-nine point nine since the Lists. Since a bout on the main sand against a thin quiet man with a short sword who'd told him, before it started, exactly where he was going to hit him, and then hit him there.

He'd been at ninety-nine point nine five since a quarter past midnight four nights ago, when he'd been sitting on this exact patch of sand with Lucan standing over him in a dressing-gown saying *Thea needs your eyes*, and he'd closed his notebook and given them.

He'd seen *when*.

That was the only way he could put it in the log, and he'd tried eleven ways. *Saw the order*. *Saw the sequence*. *Saw the timestamps*. None of them were right; they were all ways of writing down a thing after you'd seen it, and what he'd seen hadn't been after. For four hundred and six heartbeats, through Deston and Thea and six other people's eyes, he'd looked at a three-hundred-year-old register of people going home, and he'd known, for every mark on it, which came first. Which came after. How long after. Not because he'd worked it out. Because it was there, in front of him, laid out, like beads on a string, the way a page is there.

Two hundred and nine lines in. Two hundred and nine out. In order.

And then he'd come back into himself, on the sand, at the foot of the wall, and the beads had gone, and his window had said ninety-nine point nine five. And under it, still, grey:

*Trace · locked · requires Tier 2*.

*Not rounding*, he wrote again, and underlined it, and closed the notebook.

***

He'd been explaining *Trace* to people for a month and nobody understood it, including him.

It had come up grey in his window at the Lists, on the day he lost to Niko. He'd been standing on the main sand in front of forty thousand people with the touch still ringing in his teeth, and he'd looked at his window, and there it was, under *Release*: a word, greyed out, locked. *Trace*. And he'd known what it was, the way you know a word in your own language: it was the thing he'd nearly done. In the half a heartbeat before Niko's blade landed, he'd seen it coming. Not the blade. The *line*. A faint mark in the air in front of him, like a pencil line under ink, where the blade was going to go; and it had been there before the blade, a hair before, and he'd seen it, and his body hadn't had time to do anything about it.

*The skill was seeing it in time*, he'd written in the channel that night. *I saw it. Not in time*.

He'd been trying to see it in time ever since.

That was the problem. He knew what the problem was. He'd known since the first week. *Release* was his; *Release* was the thing he'd found in himself in the first year, the thing that let him change his mind halfway through a move and do something else without his body having to stop and start again. He'd debugged it for six hundred sessions. He knew every line of it. And *Trace* was the other half. *Release* let him change his mind. *Trace* would let him know when to.

He just had to see it sooner.

***

"You're seeing it sooner," said Winter, on the fourth morning, "and you're wrong."

She was sitting on the bottom step of the west stand with her knees up and her horns catching the first of the sun, eating an apple. She'd been watching him get knocked down for an hour. She did that most mornings now; she said it was restful.

He sat up on the sand. Hako, standing over him with the spear, put out a hand, and Chris took it, and got pulled up.

"I'm not wrong," said Chris. "I saw it."

"You saw *a* line," said Winter. "You saw it before it came. And you moved out of it." She took a bite of the apple. "And then Hako didn't do it."

Chris looked at Hako.

"I changed my mind," said Hako, a little apologetically. "Halfway. You moved, so I didn't go there. I went the other way."

"He feinted you," said Winter. "Hako. Hako who's never feinted anybody in his life. Hako feinted you." She pointed the apple at him. "Because you moved first. You saw the line and you jumped out of it before he'd committed to it, and he saw you jump, and he went somewhere else." She took another bite. "You're not seeing it in time, Chris. You're seeing it early. And early's the same as wrong."

Chris stood on the sand with his practice sword in his hand and didn't say anything.

She was right. He knew she was right the moment she said it. He'd logged it four times in the last two days without seeing it: *saw trace, moved, trace changed, hit*. He'd thought it was a bug in the seeing. It wasn't. It was a bug in the moving. He'd been seeing the line and treating it like a fact, and it wasn't a fact. It was a plan. Hako's plan, before Hako had finished making it. And if you moved out of a plan before somebody had finished making it, they just made another one.

"So I wait," said Chris.

"So you wait," said Winter. "Like a normal person."

"I'm not a normal person."

"No," agreed Winter, and threw him the apple core, and he caught it, and Hako laughed.

***

He didn't wait. Not well. He couldn't.

It wasn't in him. He'd spent six hundred sessions learning to change his mind halfway, which was the opposite of waiting: you started, you saw it was wrong, you let it go, you started something else, all in one breath, faster than anybody could see. *Release*. He was the fastest mind-changer in the Lists; Lucan said so; he'd beaten half the Swiss with it. And now he had to see a line in the air and *not move*. Not change his mind. Not start anything. Just stand there with the line in front of him, knowing where the spear was going, and let it keep going until it couldn't go anywhere else.

His whole body screamed at him not to.

He tried it on the fifth morning. Hako came at him the way Hako came at everyone, *Commit*, all of him down the shaft of the spear in one straight line, no feint, nothing held back; and Chris saw the line. Faint. Grey. A pencil mark in the air from the spear's point to a place just under his ribs, on the left. A hair before the spear moved.

He didn't move.

He stood there with the line in front of him and every part of him that had ever changed its mind halfway screaming *go, go, go*, and he didn't. He waited. He watched the line. And the spear came down it.

And hit him exactly where the line said, under the ribs, on the left, padded, hard enough to sit him down.

He sat on the sand.

"Better," said Winter, from the step.

"I waited too long."

"You waited exactly as long as he took to commit," said Winter. "Then you waited some more. Then he hit you." She was quiet a moment. "That's better. You saw it, and you didn't jump, and it was true. The line was true. That's the first time all week the line was true." She looked at him over her knees. "Now you just have to go."

"When?"

"I don't know," said Winter. "When it's true. Not before. Not after." She shrugged. "You're the one who's supposed to know when, Chris. You saw two hundred and nine of them."

***

He went into the town that afternoon. He shouldn't have.

He knew he shouldn't have. He'd been feeling it for two days: since the morning he'd sat on the sand and waited for the line to come true, something had come loose in him, the way a thing comes loose in a program when you change a setting you don't fully understand. The lines didn't go away when the bout ended. He kept seeing them. Faint. At the edges. Lucan reaching for a cup, and a pencil line in the air from his hand to the cup a hair before the hand moved. One of the raking boys turning round, and a faint grey curve where his shoulder was going to go. Ysolde drawing her sword in the yard to cut the oak post, and the line of the cut already there in the air, waiting for the steel.

Everywhere. Everybody's next thing. A hair before they did it.

He'd thought it would stop if he didn't look. It didn't stop.

He went into the market on the Street of Coopers to buy ink, because he was out, and because Winter had told him to get out of the Lists for an hour before he went strange; and he walked into the noon market, and it was full.

Hundreds of people. Carts. Children. Dogs. Women with baskets, men with barrows, a juggler at the corner, a boy running with a goose under his arm. Everybody going somewhere. Everybody about to do something.

And every one of them, a hair before they did it, drew a line.

He saw them all at once.

Thousands of lines. Grey, faint, crossing and recrossing, every hand reaching for something, every foot about to step, every head about to turn, every one of them drawn in the air a hair before it happened, so that the whole street was laced over with a fine grey net of things about to happen, moving, shifting, rewriting itself every heartbeat as people changed their minds. A woman's hand going to a loaf, the line going to the loaf, the line changing, going to the next loaf, the hand following it. A dog about to run, its line flicking out across the street ahead of it like a thrown rope. The juggler's balls, every one of them with a line going up and a line coming down, a dozen of them, crossing, crossing.

The street tilted.

He found out afterwards that he'd sat down. In the middle of the Street of Coopers, at noon, on the cobbles, with his ink money in his fist. A cooper's wife had come out and asked if he was all right, and he'd said *I can see what everybody's going to do* in English, and she'd gone and got her husband, and her husband had gone and got a boy, and the boy had run to the Lists.

Ysolde came for him.

She came down the street at the guard's pace with her hand on her sword hilt, and the crowd opened for her the way crowds opened for the Lists' guards in Valcrest, and she stopped in front of him and looked down.

He couldn't see her face properly. Her line was in the way. A grey curve from her hand down to his shoulder, a hair before her hand came down to his shoulder.

"Up," said Ysolde.

"I can't," said Chris. "There's too many."

"Too many what?"

"Lines."

Ysolde looked at him for a long moment. She was forty-one and she'd been Lucan's guard for eleven years and Reaching for nine, and she kept a ledger, in the kingdom's script, of a cut into an oak post, every week, measured with a chalk line. She'd shown it to him at the Lists. *I thought it was a number. It's a distance. You can measure a distance.*

"Then don't look at them all," said Ysolde.

"I can't not."

"You can," said Ysolde. "I can see every man in this street who's carrying a blade. Every one. I always can; it's what guards are for. Forty, in this street, at noon. I don't look at forty." She crouched down in front of him, on the cobbles, so that her face was level with his. "I look at one. The one nearest the thing I'm guarding. And I let the other thirty-nine be furniture." She held out her hand. "You're guarding yourself, right now. So look at the one nearest you."

Chris looked at her hand.

At its line. A faint grey mark, a hair ahead of it, from her palm to his.

Just that one. He let the others go, the way you'd let a page of numbers go out of focus when you only needed one of them. The juggler. The dog. The woman and her loaves. The whole street. Furniture.

One line. From her hand to his.

He took it.

*Session 652*, he wrote that night, on the sand at the foot of the wall, by the light of the one lamp Lucan's boys left burning for him. *Market. Saw all of them. Every next thing. Too many. Sat down in the street. Ysolde: look at one. Let the others be furniture. Did. Worked.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote, under it, in his old style, the pseudo-code he'd been writing in since the first morning on this sand because he couldn't write the kingdom's script and wasn't going to pretend he could:

*IF trace.count > 1 THEN filter(nearest). trace is a plan, not a fact. DO NOT act on plan. WAIT FOR commit. THEN release.*

He stopped.

He looked at it.

*Wait for commit. Then release*.

He'd written *Release* a thousand times in three years. He'd written *commit* a hundred, because it was Hako's word, Hako's skill, the thing Hako did. He'd never once written them next to each other.

*Trace* wasn't seeing the blow early. He'd had that wrong for a month. *Trace* was seeing the plan. The line in the air was what the other person meant to do, before they'd finished meaning it. And a plan wasn't anything; you couldn't dodge a plan; if you moved out of a plan, they made a new one. You had to wait until the plan stopped being a plan. Until the other person had committed: until they'd put enough of themselves into it that they couldn't take it back. Until the line was *true*.

And then you didn't have to see it sooner. You already had *Release*. You were the fastest mind-changer in the Lists. The moment the line went true, you changed your mind, all of it, in one breath, out of the line and into wherever it wasn't.

Not seeing it in time.

Seeing *when it's true*.

He sat on the sand for a long time with the notebook on his knee.

"Winter," he said, at last. Out loud. To nobody. The lamp, and the wall, and thirty-six gold boxes in the dark.

There wasn't anybody there.

There was a pause.

"What," said Winter, from the step of the west stand, where she'd been sitting in the dark for an hour with her knees up, watching him, because she'd known he'd be out here and he'd known she would be too.

"I've got it wrong for a month," said Chris. "It's not seeing early. It's seeing when it's true."

Winter didn't say anything for a while.

"I said that," she said. "On Tuesday. I said *when it's true*."

"I know," said Chris. "I just logged it."

Ina came out with them in the morning.

He didn't usually. He came out at night, mostly, to stand at the far end of the sand by himself with his mask in his hand and light lamps, one at a time, along the top of the west wall; forty of them, Lucan's forty, the ones he'd been trying to light all at once since the Swiss. He'd got to twenty-six once, at the Lists, in front of everybody, and then gone down on one knee with the twenty-seventh guttering out in his hand. Ninety-nine percent to Candle since. A grey line under his window, he'd told them: *Kagaribi · locked*.

He came out at dawn this morning and sat on the step beside Winter with his mask on his knee and didn't say anything. Newish came too, humming, with her hair in a scarf. Hako was already on the sand, with the spear, stretching.

"Chris says he's got it," said Winter.

"Chris always says he's got it," said Ina.

"This time I've *logged* it," said Chris.

Newish laughed. Then she tilted her head, the way she did when she was listening to something nobody else could hear: everybody's beat, she said, the thing under them that kept time. She listened to Chris for a long moment.

"You're early," said Newish. "Your beat. You've always been a bit early. Ever since I've known you. Half a beat ahead of everyone, like you're in a hurry to get to the next bar." She tilted her head the other way. "You're not, this morning. You're on it."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know yet," said Newish. "Let's find out."

***

Hako came at him the way Hako came at everyone.

Chris stood on the sand at the foot of the west wall with his practice sword low and his feet under his shoulders and the sun just coming up over the east stand into his eyes, and watched him come. Not the spear. Hako. The whole of him. A patient man who'd waited three years for someone to tell him to go, and been told, at the Lists last summer, on the side sand, by a man leaning on a rail with two bees on his head; and had gone, ever since, at everything, all the way, every time.

*Commit*.

The line came.

A pencil mark in the air. From the spear's point to a place on Chris's chest, just left of the middle. Faint. Grey. A hair before the spear moved.

He didn't move.

Everything in him said *go*. Six hundred sessions of *Release* said *go*, *change*, *now*, *out of it*. He let it say it. He didn't do it. He stood with the line in front of him and watched it, the way he'd watched the beads on the string at a quarter past midnight with seven people's eyes: not reading it, just letting it be what it was.

And the line moved.

A hair. To the right. Because Hako was still deciding. Because Hako, at the last moment, had seen him not move and thought *he's waiting for something* and his plan had shifted, the way a plan does, toward the left of Chris's chest instead of the middle.

Still a plan. Not true yet.

Chris didn't move.

The spear came forward. A foot. The line came with it, a hair ahead, steady now; not shifting; going exactly where it was going to go and nowhere else. And Chris felt it change. He didn't see it. He *felt* it, the way you feel a door click shut in another room: the line stopped being a thing Hako meant and became a thing Hako had done. All of him behind it. Down the shaft. Nothing held back. Nothing to take back.

True.

*Now*, thought Chris.

He changed his mind.

All of it, in one breath, the way he'd changed it a thousand times on this sand: out of the line, a half-step left and round, his weight going off his back foot and through, the practice sword coming up off the sand in a short flat arc. Not to block. There was nothing to block; the spear went past his chest a hand away, down the line where it had been going, into the empty air where he'd been standing a heartbeat ago. And he was already round the side of it. Inside it. At Hako's elbow.

He touched Hako on the ribs with the flat of the blade.

Very lightly. The way the man at the end of the rail had touched Milli on the ribs with the butt of a spear at the Lists, to say *there*.

Hako stopped.

He stood there with the spear out in front of him, all his weight down the shaft, into nothing, and a wooden sword resting very lightly on his ribs; and he turned his head, slowly, and looked at Chris, standing at his elbow, inside his reach, where nobody was supposed to be able to stand.

"You waited," said Hako.

"Until you meant it," said Chris.

Something happened in his chest.

***

He'd felt it before. Twice. Once on the fourth day of the Lists last summer, with a memory leak on his left side and Winter stepping into it, when *Release* came. And once at the Lists this summer, on the main sand, with Niko's touch ringing in his teeth, when a grey word came up under his name and didn't light.

It was the same feeling, the second time, as the first. Something that had been grey going white. Something that had been one thing and was about to be two.

He didn't look at the window. He looked at Hako. He'd learned that, if nothing else, from a man who never moved first: you didn't look at the window while you were still on the sand. You looked at the person in front of you.

Hako was grinning. Slowly, all the way across.

"Look at it," said Hako. "Go on. You've been at ninety-nine for three months. Look at it."

Chris looked at it.

[ Chris · Tier 2: Tempered · Skills: Release, Trace ]

No bar. The bar had gone into the middle line, the way everybody said it did. *Tempered*. And under *Release*, where for a month there'd been a grey word with *locked* after it, the same word, in the same plain white as all the others.

*Trace*.

He stood on the sand at the foot of the west wall of the Lists with a wooden sword in his hand and looked at it for a long time.

On the step, Winter had stood up. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Newish had both hands over her mouth. Ina had put his mask on, which he did when he didn't want anybody to see his face, which meant he was crying behind it; and then, after a moment, he took it off again, and didn't care.

"About time," said Winter.

Her voice cracked in the middle of it. She didn't seem to mind.

***

He logged it before he did anything else. He couldn't not.

He sat down on the sand where he'd been standing, with Hako's spear across his knees because Hako had sat down too and dropped it there, and opened the notebook, and wrote, with his hand shaking so that the letters went everywhere:

*Session 653. Day 6 after. Hako, Commit. Saw trace. Trace shifted (plan). Waited. Trace went true (commit). Released. Inside. Touch.*

*Tempered. Trace unlocked.*

*Trace is not seeing early. Trace is seeing when it's true.*

*(Winter said this on Tuesday. Logged Wednesday. Credit Winter.)*

Then he opened his window, and the channel, and wrote what he'd written for every one of them for three years, at the top of every count, the thing that had started as a list in a notebook in the first week and become, somehow, the thing thirty-four people checked first every morning:

He read them all. The way he always did.

Niko's had stopped him. *Nice one*. Two words, without the third. He knew where the third came from; everybody did, now, after the knoll. *Nice one. Again*. Three words, typed by a stranger at two in the morning to a boy of seventeen. Niko had given them away to Tiger in the spring, and got them back, and kept them.

He hadn't given Chris the *again*. Just the *nice one*. Chris looked at it for a long time and decided that was right. *Again* was for someone who'd lost.

***

He went up the west stair at noon, to the top of the wall, to the lamps.

Forty of them, along the top of the wall over the thirty-six gold boxes, iron, the old kind, with glass round three sides and the wicks trimmed every evening by Lucan's boys. He stood among them in the sun with his notebook in his pocket and looked down at the whole of the Lists from above: the empty bowl, the raked sand, the tiers going up all round in white stone, the city round it, the plain round that, the hills, and somewhere a long way north, past the hills, a lake.

His left side was empty.

It had been empty for three years; there'd been a man there once, on calls, at two in the morning, saying *I'm left*, and going left, first through the door, loudly. He'd found the gap last summer and Winter had stepped into it. She wasn't up here now. She was on the sand below with Ina, the two of them looking up at him, small.

He looked down at them, at the step, at the place on the sand where he'd stood that morning and waited until it was true.

He thought about the market. About a whole street laced over with things about to happen, every hand, every foot, every dog. Thousands of lines. All of them plans. All of them shifting. And in the middle of it a guard with forty years of blades in her eye saying *look at one*.

He looked at one.

Far off. North. Past the hills. Where a man sat on the end of a jetty, probably, with a rod across his knees, being still. He couldn't see him. Nobody could, from here. But Chris had seen his line, once, at the Lists. In the north stand, behind a pillar, the morning of the final, for half a heartbeat, through a crowd of forty thousand: one faint grey mark in the air, from a hand that hadn't moved, going nowhere. A plan that wasn't a plan. A man who'd sat in a crowd for nine days and never once meant to do anything except watch his daughter.

He hadn't known what it was, then. He'd logged it as a glitch.

He knew now. Some lines never went true, because the person who drew them never needed them to. They just sat there in the air, faint, ready, the way a man sits on the end of a jetty: knowing exactly where he'd go, and waiting for a reason.

Chris took his notebook out on the top of the west wall, among the forty lamps, in the sun, and wrote one more line at the bottom of the page. Not code. Not a count.

*Line 36: still there. Still waiting.*

Then he went down the stair to the sand, where Winter and Ina were, and Hako with the spear, and Newish humming, and the empty bye in the thirty-sixth box; and asked Hako to come at him again, because he wanted to know whether it would work twice, and because it was what you did, the morning after something worked: you checked it wasn't a fluke.

It wasn't.
