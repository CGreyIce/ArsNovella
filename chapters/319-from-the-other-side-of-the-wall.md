The lamp had been lit for twenty-eight nights.

He did it at dusk. Trimmed the wick a hair too high, the way he always had; struck the match; the small blue flame catching and going yellow; the chimney down over it; the lamp on the table in the window, where you could see it from the end of the jetty, and from the lake road, and from the top of the last hill on the way home. For her. She was the one who wasn't home. She was on a sand a long way south with a paper rod, and Chris had written in the channel that she'd held it up over her head on the fourth tier of the east stand and forty thousand others had gone up round it, and he'd read that sitting on the last plank with his cup, and put the cup down.

He didn't light it for himself any more. She'd said he was allowed. He'd said she was. They'd both been right, and she was the one on the road, so it was hers till the autumn.

Gigi came most evenings.

Not to the bench. Not since the morning Alea went. She came down the lake road from Wrenhaven at dusk and walked past the bench, two paces nearer the water than it had been in the spring, and down the shingle to the near end of the jetty, where the planks met the stones by the first post and the ash, and sat there. On the shingle. With her knees up and her back against the post. She didn't come on to the jetty. She didn't say anything, mostly, except *you okay*, once, at the start, and he said *no*, and sometimes *you?*, and she said *no*, or *a bit*, or once *yes actually* in a surprised voice, and then she sat there till the lamp had been lit an hour and went home.

The whole length of the jetty between them. Thirty planks. He'd counted.

He hadn't sat at that end. He'd thought about it. He knew what she was doing and he knew why she wasn't doing more. Alea had told her something; there was a new line on the list on its nail by the hearth, in the slant Alea had learned off her mother's slate, that he'd read the morning she left and not let himself read again. He knew what it said. *Let somebody sit with you*. And under it, smaller, the name.

He hadn't. Not yet. He sat on the last plank and she sat on the shingle and the lamp was lit behind them both, and that was where it was.

He was on the last plank at one in the morning on the twenty-ninth night.

He didn't sleep much in the summer. He never had. The nights were short and warm and the fish came up in the dark, and he'd sit out with the rod and not cast, mostly, and watch the stars come out upside down in the water. Gigi had gone home at eleven. The lamp was lit in the window behind him. The lake was very still.

He'd never seen that. The wall at Cinderhold was grey stone and forty feet high, and on the east side you could see a long way.

He'd been on it at noon. He remembered that. Not the morning; the morning had been the horns and the forward line and her going, and him on the gate with the black sword because that was where they'd put him; and the line coming back, slowly, in pieces, carrying people, and her not with them. And then noon. And somebody on the wall walk shouting. And he'd gone up the steps to the top of the wall and looked east, across the red, an hour's walk any other day, to the rise.

There'd been a ring on it.

He hadn't known what it was. Nobody had. Nobody had ever seen one. A ring on the ground, drawn, with lines going in, and fires at the end of every line, little ones, pale, burning in the middle of the day where you could see them from the wall. Lit for the wall. And in the middle, sitting, somebody small. He couldn't see who. He'd known who.

And between the wall and the rise, the host.

He'd gone over the wall.

Not through the gate. Over it. He didn't remember climbing down; Hesper said afterwards that he'd jumped, forty feet, on to the red, and got up, and walked. He'd walked into the host with the black sword at noon and come out the other side of it at dusk, an hour's walk, all afternoon, and she'd been half of herself when he got there. Because they'd lit it where he could see. Because they wanted him to come. Because he would.

They'd known he would. That was the whole of it.

He stood on the end of the jetty at the lake with the water black in front of him and the lamp lit behind him, and understood that he was standing, for the first time in his life, on the other side of that wall.

Not the side he'd been on. The other one. The one where somebody stands on the stones and watches somebody go out into the dark, and can't go after them, and counts.

***

Halvard had counted.

He'd told him that, in a way. Not in so many words. In the letter, the first one, the one that had come back with a worse fish and *Why did you go?*; and in what he hadn't said in it, which was more. Halvard had been on the wall over the gate the night a nineteen-year-old came back out of the black carrying a sword that sang, and he'd stood there with his hand on the bar, and counted his own heartbeats before he drew it. And he knew the number. He'd never written it down. He'd never said it.

Ars had never once wondered what that had been like. To be the one on the wall.

He wondered now.

He started counting.

He didn't mean to. He found he was doing it. One. Two. His own heart, under his shirt, going too fast; he could feel it in his throat and in the ends of his fingers. He counted it the way he'd count a fish's runs on a line, without numbers, just *one*, and *another*, and *another*.

There was somebody on the lake road.

He heard her before he saw her. Feet on the road in the dark, running, coming down from Wrenhaven, and breathing; and then a small shape coming round the turn by the alders at a run, in a coat over a nightdress, with no lantern. She'd read it at Wrenhaven. She'd read it in the loft of the militia hall with Higu at the window holding the channel open, and she'd got up and run. A long way, in the dark. He could hear how far in the way she was breathing.

She didn't come on to the jetty.

She came down the shingle past the bench, and stopped at the near end, by the first post and the ash. And sat down. On the stones. With her back against the post and her knees up and her chest going.

She didn't say anything. She didn't say *you okay*. She sat there in the dark at the near end of his jetty, thirty planks off, the way she'd sat every evening for four weeks, and got her breath back, and didn't look at him.

He walked down the jetty.

He didn't decide that either. He was walking. Off the last plank, along the boards, toward the shore, in the dark, the rod left behind on the end where he'd put it down. Toward the cabin. Toward the pegs and the new sword, and the road, and the hill, and the river.

He knew the sum. Higu had done it for Alea in the spring and Alea had told him, on the step, the night before she went: a hundred miles; five days by the post road; three if you killed a horse. A night, the thing on the steps. It would be over before he was past the Narrows. Whatever it was. It would be over and he'd be on a towpath somewhere above Duskhollow with a sword on his back and a dead horse, and they'd have watched him come. Because they'd lit it where she could see. Because they knew she'd come. And because they'd been watching her for two years, to see if *he* would.

He knew all of that. He'd written it to her himself, in English, in the spring. *If I come, I bring that with me*.

He walked down the jetty anyway. The old thing walked. It had walked into a host at noon with a black sword; it didn't care about sums. It was nearly at the shore.

And at the near end, by the first post, there was somebody sitting on the stones.

He stopped.

He was three planks from the end. She was below him, on the shingle, with her back against the post and her knees up, and she'd turned her head. She was looking up at him. In the dark he could only see the pale of her face and the shine of the gold watch in her hand.

She didn't say anything. She didn't say *don't*. She didn't say *sit down*. She didn't say anything at all. She sat on the stones at the near end of his jetty and looked up at him, and waited, the way he'd watched her learn to wait over nearly four years, from a girl who filled every quiet with something sharp to somebody who could sit outside a door all night with her hands in her lap.

*It's not a door shut*, she'd said to him, at ice-out, in the spring. *It's just a door. I'll sit outside it.*

He looked at her for a long time.

Then he came down off the last three planks on to the shingle, and turned, and sat down beside her.

Not close. Near. Near enough.

***

The stones moved under him.

He felt it before he understood it. The shingle at the foot of the first post, where the ash's roots went in under the jetty; something shifting under the stones, slow, like something turning over in its sleep. And then the stones under him weren't stones. They were wood. Living wood, smooth and warm, coming up through the shingle out of the ash's roots and taking the shape of him: a seat, low, against the post, with a back that came up exactly where his back wanted to go, and a place for his hand on the right side, where he'd have put it.

And it faced south. Down the lake. To where the Wren went out at the bottom, past the point, toward a city on the sea.

Gigi looked at it.

"*Oh*," she said. Very quietly. "Oh, shut up." And then, to the ash, because it was the ash's roots and not hers, not really, she'd only ever asked: "You didn't have to do it *that* fast."

He sat in it.

It was warm. It shouldn't have been. It had been a warm night but the stones had been cool. The seat was warm all through, like a bench in the sun, like the bench up the shingle that she said was warmer than it had any right to be. He sat in it with his hands on his knees, at the near end of his own jetty, facing south, with the lamp lit behind him in the window and the rod on the last plank thirty planks off where he'd left it, and didn't go anywhere.

His heart was still going. He was still counting.

"You okay," said Gigi.

"No," he said.

She didn't say anything.

"You?" he said.

"No," said Gigi.

They sat.

*Get on with it, girl*.

He'd never met Maud. He knew her the way you know people from letters, from the way other people say their names: an old woman with a stick she used mostly to point, who'd run a ward for forty years and given a girl from another world a needle case and said *come back*. Who'd frightened him from a hundred miles off; he'd said that to Alea, on the jetty, and meant it. Who was sitting in a ring on the customs-house steps with something's hands on her head, half of her already gone grey, and had been asked *can I*, and said *get on with it*.

The way Diwa had said *please*.

He shut his eyes.

He'd been asked. On the rise. He'd come through the host with the black sword at dusk and knelt by her in the middle of the ring with the fires going and her half of herself, and she'd asked him. Not *can I*. The other way round. She'd asked him to end it, before she stopped being herself. And there'd been nobody in the world who could do anything else. Nobody had ever closed one. Nobody had ever known you could.

And now somebody was going to try.

In the middle of one. On purpose. With four people round her and a needle case in her hands.

He didn't know if it would work. Nobody did. He opened his eyes and looked south down the black lake toward the place where the Wren went out, and he thought that he had never in his life wanted anything as much as he wanted a girl he'd known for a month in the spring to do a thing that nobody had ever done.

The sky went grey over the far shore at a quarter to five.

It did it the way it always did in the summer, early, from the bottom up: a line of something not quite dark along the top of the far hills, and then the stars going out one at a time from the east, and the lake going from black to iron. He'd watched it do that ten thousand times. He watched it now with his heart still going in his throat and his hands on his knees and his count somewhere past anything he could have said.

He sat there a long time.

The channel stayed quiet. Then it didn't; then everybody was in it at once, the way it went when a door opened, Thornwall and Valcrest and Solmere and Cinderhold and Wrenhaven, too many lines to read, and he didn't read them. He shut it. He sat in the seat at the near end of his jetty and looked at the sun on the lake.

He'd stayed.

That was all he'd done. He hadn't done anything. He'd sat on some stones beside a girl in a nightdress and counted his own heart for three hours while four people a hundred miles off went into a ring he'd have walked into a host to reach. He hadn't broken. He hadn't gone. He'd stood up on the last plank and walked down the jetty and stopped three planks short, because somebody was sitting at the end of it, and sat down.

It didn't feel like anything. It felt like the worst night of his life, nearly, and it had been nobody's but theirs, and he'd been on the other side of the wall for all of it.

He opened his window. He hadn't in a month.

He looked at it for a long time. Sixty-eight since the spring. Since the evening he'd written to Halvard first.

He didn't know what had moved it. He thought he might. He shut it.

"Gigi," he said.

She looked at him. Her face was blotched and swollen and she'd stopped pretending otherwise about an hour ago.

"Thank you," he said. "For sitting."

Gigi looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the seat, at the arm of it under his hand, the living wood, warm.

"I didn't do it," she said. "You sat down first. It comes up thorns, otherwise." She sniffed, enormously. "That's the rule. You have to sit down first."

He wrote to Rae at six.

At the table, in the cabin, with the lamp still lit beside him, because he wasn't going to put it out; it was Alea's till the autumn. In English. One sheet. He didn't draft it. He didn't burn anything. He'd stopped doing that, mostly, since the spring.

He wrote it the way you'd talk to somebody at the end of a very long night, plainly, and signed it the way he'd signed the last one. *D.* And folded it, and wrote her name on the outside, and *Kingsferry, the river-gate infirmary, by hand*. And walked up the lake road with it at seven to Wrenhaven, to Mistress Hennet at the post-house, who looked at his face and didn't say anything and put a boy on the best horse she had.

Then he walked back.

Gigi was asleep on the stones when he got there, curled up at the foot of the first post with her coat over her and her head on her arm. He didn't wake her. He went down the jetty, all thirty planks, and picked up the rod off the last one, where it had lain all night with the hook on the end. *RESURGAM*. He looked at it for a moment.

Then he came back down the jetty to the near end, and sat down in the seat, and put the rod across his knees, and didn't cast. And sat there in the morning with the sun on the lake and a girl asleep on the stones beside him and the lamp still burning in the window, where you could see it, if you knew where to look, from the top of the last hill on the way home.
