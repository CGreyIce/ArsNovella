He packed in the dark, the way he always had, because it was easier to leave a place you couldn't see.

Not much. He'd never carried much east. A blanket. The water skins, four of them, empty, rolled. A shirt that had been Diwa's choice and was mostly patches now. A twist of salt, a twist of Salty's pepper paste in waxed cloth that Alea had packed and then unpacked and then packed again in the night when she thought he was asleep. Bread. The rod, which wasn't packing, which was just the rod; it stood by the door where it always stood.

And the letters.

They were in the chest under the window, where he'd put them in the autumn. Thirty-eight of them, in a bundle tied with the same faded tape the man had tied them with in a drawer in Solmere, nine years of them, in order. He'd never untied it. He knelt in front of the chest in the dark and untied it now, and the tape came away soft in his fingers like old ribbon, and the bundle shifted and settled.

He took the top one.

It was the first. He knew that without lighting the lamp. It was thinner than the others, and the paper was a different paper, rougher, the kind the Advisor's office had used ten years ago before the king's cream paper came in; and when he ran his thumb over the front of it he could feel the shape of his own name pressed into it from the other side, hard, by somebody who'd written it with a pen held too tight.

He didn't open it. He'd said he wouldn't. *One a year. When I get back from the grave.* He wrapped it in oilcloth and put it inside his coat, in the inside pocket on the left, where the other thing was already: a child's drawing in blue crayon, folded in four, worn through at the folds. *MUM DAD ME. COME HOME SOON.* They sat there together against his ribs, a letter he hadn't read and a drawing he'd read so many times he didn't need to.

He tied the bundle up again. Thirty-seven. Put it back in the chest. Shut the lid.

***

Then he stood up and looked at the wall.

He'd known he would. He'd been not looking at it all night, the way you don't look at something that's in the room with you; and now there was a little grey in the window, and the wall over the table came up out of the dark the way the far shore came up out of the mist, slowly, a bit at a time.

The drawing on its nail. A jetty in blue crayon, forty-one planks, which was seven too many, because she'd been small and generous.

The list beside it.

And above them both, on two iron pegs a man's height apart, the blade.

It lay there along the logs, grey-silver, the length of a man, catching the little light there was. The frost in it was very still. He knew it wasn't. He'd felt it, the morning he put his hands on the grip, the whole long branching pattern of it shift through the steel and settle, the way a dog settles when it's decided you're staying. It had been settled ever since. Five nights. It didn't hum. It didn't do anything. It lay on its pegs over the table and over a drawing and a list, and waited, and was very patient about it, and he could feel the patience from across the room the way you can feel somebody looking at the back of your neck.

*Not for now*, Severin had said. *For the day you leave the lake.*

He was leaving the lake today.

"Not that day," he told it.

It didn't say anything. It was a sword.

He went to the wall. Not to the blade. To the list, beside the drawing, on the same nail: a page of Higu's small neat pencil, folded in half once a long time ago and opened out again, gone soft at the crease. *Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them.* Most of it crossed through now, in his hand, with a line each. *A tree*, at the land end of the jetty, at ice-out. *Answer them*, in the autumn, on a barge. *Gabe*, two days ago, with her pencil through the window.

*Twelve dinners* had eleven small ticks after it, neat, like a builder's marks on a beam, and a space, and a very small drawing of a briar. Three lines and some thorns. He'd found it the morning after the fifth dinner and not said anything and not rubbed it out.

*East* had nothing after it yet.

And at the bottom, in his own hand, where he'd written it again in the spring after crossing it off: *Fish*.

He took it down off the nail.

He hadn't decided to, until he did it. He stood there with it in his hand and looked at the nail, empty now beside the drawing, and thought about leaving it. It was the cabin's list. It lived here, on the wall, by the drawing, under the blade, where he could see it from the table every morning. He'd never taken it anywhere.

He'd never had two things left on it that were somewhere else.

He folded it in half, along the old crease, and put it in the inside pocket on the left with the letter and the drawing. Three things, now. They didn't take up any room.

***

Rowan came down the lake road at first light with two cups.

He always did, now. Since last year, when a letter had come up the river from Valcrest with two lines on it in a hand he knew, *Late pumpkins. A.* and under it *Both of us. A.*; and Rowan had read it at his kitchen table and set two cups on the table facing the door, and left them there sixty days, until the frost, when two people came back down the lake road in the dark and knocked.

He didn't wait for them to be gone, this time. He came down while they were still there. He came down the shingle in his chain, with a cup in each hand, old ones, the brown glazed kind from the potter in the square, and stood at the bottom of the cabin step, and held them up so that Ars could see them from the door.

That was all. He didn't say anything. He was past sixty, and mayor of Wrenhaven, and he'd known Ars thirteen years, and he'd learned, in the last two, that he didn't have to say anything to this man; he could hold up two cups.

"Both of us," said Ars.

"I know." Rowan lowered the cups. "I'll put them out when I'm back up. Facing the door." He looked past Ars into the cabin, at the wall. At the blade on its pegs. He looked at it for a while. "You're leaving that."

"Yes."

"Good," said Rowan. "I'd not want to carry it. Not on a walk." He looked at Ars. "Is it a walk?"

"It's a walk."

"Good," said Rowan again. And then, because he was Rowan, and there were things he'd been not saying for thirteen years and had only lately started to say: "Tell her I keep the step swept. Your wife. I never met her. Tell her anyway. I keep the step swept, and the girl comes up for her supper more than she lets on, and Odette's still got the bit of her blue cloth in the sewing box that she left behind the time she mended my coat." He stopped. "I don't know if you tell her things. At the grave. People do."

"I tell her the year," said Ars.

"Then tell her that." Rowan turned the cups over in his hands, once. "And the hens. Tell her about the hens. Women like hearing about hens."

***

The hens didn't want to go.

He'd known they wouldn't. They never had. Odette came down for them an hour after Rowan, in her apron, with a basket and a look, and the two brown ones went into the basket the way hens go into anything, with a great deal of complaint and no real objection; and the big red one went out along the jetty.

All thirty-four planks. At a run, for a hen, which was a sort of lurching waddle with her wings half open. To the end. And sat down on the last plank, by the lamp, where he sat every morning, and looked back down the jetty at all of them with one furious eye.

"She knows," said Odette.

"She doesn't know anything. She's a hen."

"She knows," said Odette, "that the last time you went, you came back with a girl and a pumpkin and a cold, and she wasn't asked." She put the basket down on the stones and went out along the jetty herself, a stout woman in a lace-edged apron with her skirts held up out of the wet, and stopped at the last plank and looked down at the hen. "Come on, then. Come on, you stupid great thing. You can sit in my window. You'll see the road."

The hen looked at her.

"You'll see him come back," said Odette. "Before anybody. I'll put you in the window that looks down the lake road and you'll see him come over the hill before the girl's even said it's him. You'll be first."

The hen thought about this.

Then it got up, with enormous dignity, and let itself be picked up, and was carried back along the jetty in Odette's apron like a loaf, making small offended noises the whole way. And as Odette went past him at the land end, the hen turned its head and looked at him, sideways, with one eye. And pecked his wrist. Once. Not hard.

"There," said Odette. "That's her told you."

***

The king went first.

He'd said he would. The oxen had been yoked since dawn, eight of them, pale southern beasts with a look of having been very patient for a very long time, and the dray behind them empty now, with only the sailcloth folded in the bed of it and the rollers lashed along the side. Kestra at the leaders, already mounted. And Severin standing by the near wheel, in his plain coat, with his hand on the rail, the way he'd walked beside it all the way up from Pellow's Cross.

He'd ride back. He'd said that too. *It doesn't need me now. It's only a cart.* But he stood with his hand on it anyway, as if he'd got used to it.

Ars came up the shingle to him.

They didn't say anything at first. They'd never been men who said things at the start. The king looked at him, and then past him, at the cabin, at the open door, at the wall inside it that you could just see from here if you knew where to look.

"It'll keep," said Severin.

"I know."

"It doesn't mind. I asked it." The king's beard moved. "That's not a joke. My father talked to iron. I thought he was mad. Then I was forty and I was doing it too." He was quiet. "It said it would keep. It said it's waited longer."

"It's a sword," said Ars.

"So's the other one," said Severin. "Ask her if it isn't."

Ars looked at him.

"Go and see her," said the king. "Your wife. And come home. That's all I want. I've a kingdom to run and seven things standing in a field outside Thornwall facing a pass, and I'll feel it if you need me, and you won't, and I'll feel it anyway." He took his hand off the rail at last. "Come home, Ars."

"I always come home."

"I know," said Severin. "I'm saying it so I've said it." He looked at Ars a moment longer. Then he put out his hand, the big burnt smith's hand, palm up; not to shake. The way he'd held it palms up over the hearth for five days, feeding the fire. As if there were something in it to give.

Ars put his own hand on it, flat.

The king closed his fingers over it for a count of three. Then he let go, and turned, and swung himself up onto the big grey Kestra had brought down for him, heavily, the way a man near seventy swings up onto anything, and gathered the reins.

"One pour," he said, to nobody, the way he said it.

And Kestra called to the leaders, and the oxen leaned, and the dray went up the lake road, slow and empty and creaking, with a king beside it on a grey horse, and Ars stood on the shingle and watched it until it went over the top.

***

Group 1 were at the top of the lake road.

All five. And Maren, in her guild coat, with her list. And Bram.

He saw Bram first, because Bram wasn't at the top of the lake road. He was on the shingle at the far end of the shore, where the stones gave out into reeds, in his old grey militia coat with his sword out; and he'd been there since before it was light, Ars knew, because he'd been there every morning since the ice went out. Ars hadn't asked him. Bram hadn't said.

He went along the shore to him. Not quickly. Not straight. The way you'd go along a shore anyway, if you were going that way.

Bram didn't stop. He didn't look round. He was a big grey man near fifty, with a soldier's hands and a fisherman's patience, which he'd got in the winter on a stool beside a hole in the ice; and he stood at the edge of the reeds with his sword held low, and the reeds standing up out of the water in front of him, tall, brown at the tops, green at the bottom, very still in the dawn.

He cut.

Not at the reeds. That was the thing. Ars saw it, coming along the shore. Bram's edge went across in front of him, slow, level, a hand short of the nearest reed; didn't touch it. And his weight went with it, not after it. Leaning. The way a man leans on a lead when the dog's already gone. And the sheen came up along the steel, the way it had come up along it since Bram was twenty-four, Gleaming, a soldier's silver; and at the end of the stroke, for a moment, it didn't stop at the edge.

It went on. A finger. Less. Off the steel, into the air, the way breath goes off a mouth on a cold morning.

And the reed bent.

As if somebody had blown on it. The whole long stem of it bowing away from the edge, slowly, its brown head going over, and over, and then coming back up, swaying, and standing still.

It didn't fall. It hadn't been cut. It had been leaned on.

Bram lowered his sword. He stood there breathing through his nose, looking at the reed, with his big grey face set, the face of a man who's done a thing two hundred times and got the same answer two hundred times and is going to do it a two hundred and first.

He knew Ars was there. Ars could see that in his shoulders.

Ars didn't say anything.

He stood on the shingle a pace behind Bram and to the side, where Bram could see him if he turned his head, and looked at the reed, the way he'd sat on a stool beside Bram on the ice for nine weeks looking at a hole, and didn't say anything at all. Not *wait*. Bram knew *wait*; he'd been saying it to the girls all spring. Not *closer*, or *further*, or *it's the edge*, or *it's not the edge*, or any of the things it was, because none of them would be Bram's if somebody else said them first.

After a while Bram said, not turning round, "It's the waiting."

Ars didn't say anything.

"It's the waiting," said Bram again. To the reed. "Isn't it. It's like the hole. You don't pull. You wait till it's on."

He brought the sword up again. Slow. Level. The lean. The sheen. The reed bowing away from the edge, over, over, and back.

Ars watched it come back up.

Then he turned round and went back along the shore toward the lake road, the way you'd go along a shore anyway; and behind him, at the edge of the reeds, he heard Bram say something very quietly to himself, a short word, the kind old soldiers say, and bring the sword up again.

***

"You didn't tell him," said Thea.

She was at the top of the lake road, with the others, with her arms folded. She'd watched the whole thing. They all had. Of course they had.

"No."

"You never tell anybody."

"I told Mashi to be last," said Ars.

"That's not telling. That's—" Thea stopped. She thought about it, with her head on one side, the way she thought about everything, as if it were a page of something and she was looking for the line where it went wrong. "That's a comment," she said. "In the code. Not the code. You leave comments. *Here's where it gets interesting.* And then you let them find it."

"Is that bad?"

"It's very annoying," said Thea. "It's very good. It's both." She unfolded her arms. "Don't do anything I'd have to patch."

"I won't."

"You will. You'll stand on something. I'll feel it from here." She looked at him. "Bring her home. Bring all of them home."

"Yes."

Higu didn't say anything. She stepped up to him, the small exact girl with her book under her arm and her white bow, and held out her hand; and in it there was a pencil. Not hers. A new one. A stub of good black graphite in a turned wooden case, the kind the clerks in Solmere used, that she must have got from Maren, or from Oswin by the cords, or from somewhere she wasn't going to say.

"For the list," said Higu.

He looked at her.

"You took it down," said Higu. "It's not on the nail. I looked through the window." She didn't smile. She very nearly never did. "You'll want to cross things off. You can't cross things off with nothing. And you're not taking mine."

He took the pencil.

"Thank you, Higu."

"Rule seven," said Higu. Which was a rule nobody had ever told him; it had been written in her book last summer, after the Lists, the night of the last basket, and he'd never once asked what it said, and she'd never once said. He thought, looking at her, that he knew anyway.

Xola hugged him. She always did; she was the only one of the five who did it without asking, all at once, the way a wave does, with her long hair going everywhere and her gills fluttering at the sides of her neck and the little glowing thing she called Squiddles going a pleased blue in her pocket. She said *come back* into his coat, very muffled, three times, and let go.

PenguChi looked at him. Just looked.

"Eat," she said.

He opened his mouth.

"On the road," said PenguChi. "Sit down and eat. With them. Not after. Not out of the pot. You did it last night." She didn't look away. "Do it every night."

The whole of Group 1 was looking at him now.

"Yes, ma'am," said Ars.

PenguChi nodded, once, satisfied, and stepped back, and the penguin at her feet, who'd come up the lake road with her as he went everywhere with her, looked up at him with an expression of enormous disapproval and didn't say anything, because it was a penguin.

And Gigichi.

She'd been at the back. She was always at the back, a little, in the gaps between people, the way her hedge was. She came forward now, in her cardigan, with the forget-me-nots in her hair gone a little limp from the morning damp, and stood in front of him, and looked up at him with her red-brown eyes.

She'd asked him, once. On a winter night two years ago, at a table in a cabin, in English, quietly, in front of everybody, when he'd only just said *it's me*. Two words. And nobody had asked him that, in thirty-four years, and he'd answered *yes* and it hadn't been true, and she'd known. And she'd asked him again at the end of the jetty in the spring, at ice-out, and he'd told her everything. All of it. The whole mess.

She didn't ask him now. She just looked.

"Yes," he said anyway.

Gigichi nodded. She believed him. He could see her believe him.

"Ask me again when I'm back," he said.

"I will," said Gigichi. "I'll ask you at the jetty. On the last plank." She thought about it. "I'll make a seat. In the hedge. Facing the road."

***

And then there were ten of them, on the lake road, walking.

Alea in front. She'd been in front since the cabin step, with Astra on her back and the good knife on her left hip and her ears forward, and she set the pace without looking back to see if anyone could keep it, because she knew they could, because she'd set it on this road before. Behind her Gabe, with his big sledge from Harl's slung over his shoulder in a sling of old sailcloth, because he'd said, at the Lists, that he'd bring it so nobody could tell him not to, and nobody had. Deston beside him. Xav. Then the four from Duskhollow, RX and John and Malcolm and Azac, in a loose knot, RX already invisible half the time out of boredom and reappearing to say things. And Petra, at the back of them, with her sword on her hip and the stick under her arm, walking like a woman on a march, very straight.

And him. Last. With the rod on his shoulder and the four water skins over the other, rolled, empty, to be filled at the first stream.

He carried the water.

He'd carried it on every road he'd walked in the last two years. Somebody had to. He was the one who didn't get thirsty, much, and didn't get tired, much, and could carry four full skins on a yoke all day without feeling it. And it put him at the back. Where he could see all of them. Where nobody had to look over their shoulder for him, because he was the one looking.

Maren had put it in a report, once. To the Advisor. In her small exact hand. *He carries the water*. And then, under it, the thing that Halvard had read on the gatehouse wall at Solmere and not been able to say anything about for an hour, Hana said: *I think he is happy*.

They went up the lake road. Past the bakehouse, with Edric in the door in his flour and his boy asleep in the window above. Past the militia hall, with the doors open, and inside it, on the long wall under an old map of the lake road in a girl's careful hand, fifty-six frames in chalk, four to a row; and Thea already back inside, he could see through the door, standing in front of them with her arms folded, before the ten of them were even out of the square. Past the square. Past Odette's window, the one that looked down the lake road, with a big red hen sitting in it on a cushion, looking out, very offended.

The town came out.

Not all of it. It didn't need to. The ones who'd always come out, when he went: the egg-woman and her boys, Robin at the front, who'd gone under the lake ice last winter and come back up in a fish girl's arms; the chandler's girl; the miller's men; the old woman who'd had the duck. They stood on their steps in the early sun and didn't say anything, and didn't wave, much, and a few of them, the old ones, the ones who'd been soldiers or had sons who were, put their hands flat on their chests as he went past.

He didn't look away from them. He'd stopped doing that.

Then the top of the town, and the last houses, and the road going on up between the fields toward the first of the hills, and the town behind them.

***

At the top of the first hill he stopped.

He always did. He'd stopped at the top of this hill every time he'd ever left; the first time at twenty-four, going back east to the garrison after his first week in the cabin, looking back at a roof with a woman and a small girl under it and deciding he'd come back to it, if he came back to anything. He'd stopped here with Diwa the last time, going east, at the end of a summer ten years ago, and she'd stood beside him and looked down at the town where a girl of eight was asleep in Rowan and Odette's spare bed, and said *it's very small*, and he'd said *it's very big*, and they'd both been right. He'd stopped here with Alea on his shoulders, before that. And coming home last autumn, with Alea a hill ahead, turning round at the top and waiting, her ears forward, not saying *come on*.

She was a hill ahead now. She'd stopped too. She turned round and waited.

He looked back.

The lake lay in its bowl of hills, grey going to blue as the sun got onto it. The mist nearly gone. The far shore with its trees. The town on its slope, with smoke going up from the bakehouse. And at the bottom of the slope below the town, where the shingle was, a cabin, very small, with a jetty going out from it, thirty-four planks, and a lamp on the end of it that somebody would light tonight and nobody would sit by.

And a hedge. A green ring on the shelf above the shingle, forty feet across, with no roof, and white flowers along the top of the walls, closing now in the sun. With a gap in it facing the lake road. And in the gap, very small, so small he wouldn't have seen it if he'd been anybody else, a girl in a cardigan, standing, looking up the road.

He lifted his hand.

Not the rod. His hand. He didn't know if she'd see. He thought she would.

Then he put his fingertips together. The tips of the fingers of his right hand on the tips of the fingers of his left, the way Diwa had done at a tent flap ten years ago, three times, light, before she went out into a night she didn't come back from. *Well, then*.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

And opened his window. He didn't decide to. It was there.

He looked at it for a while.

Sixty-four, yesterday. Sixty-four since the spring, since a morning on the end of a jetty when he'd laughed out loud at a word in a channel and crossed off *Fish* and written it again. It hadn't moved for the swords, or the dinners, or the Lists, or a cut that opened a lake to its bed. It hadn't moved for a star in a hearth.

It had moved last night. Some time between dusk and dawn. He hadn't been looking.

He knew when. He'd known when as soon as he saw it. A bench at the end of a table in a hedge, in the place nobody sat in; and a piece of Edric's bread, with butter, and a bit of the mayor's wife's damson cheese on it, eaten sitting down, with fifteen people round him and a king at the head and a hen on the table. That was all. He'd sat down and eaten bread.

*Slow living*, he'd worked out, the first winter, on the end of the jetty, at a percent a year. *And them*. He'd never been able to say it better than that.

He closed it.

"Dad," said Alea, from the next hill. Not *come on*. Just *Dad*. The way she said it when she wanted him to know she was there.

"Coming," he said, and settled the skins on his shoulder, and came.

***

They walked eleven miles the first day, which was too many, because Alea set the pace and Petra matched it and nobody else was going to be the one to say so.

Malcolm said so. At the fourth hour, sitting down at the side of the road on a milestone with his boots off and a page of his book open on his knee. *IF we go eleven miles a day THEN by the third day RX will be carried*. He showed it to everyone. RX, who was invisible at the time, made an outraged sound from the hedge.

They went eleven miles anyway. RX was not carried.

They camped on the edge of a beech wood above a stream, where Ars had camped before, where the ground was flat and dry and there was a fallen trunk to sit on and a place by the water where the bank had been worn into a step by thirty years of drovers. He filled the skins. Gabe made the fire, because Gabe always made the fire, with his big careful hands, blowing on it as if he were waking something. Deston lay on his back on the beech mast and listened to things nobody else could hear. John put up a little gold light on the end of his finger and let it go, and it went up through the beeches and hung there over the camp like a lamp nobody had to hold.

And they ate.

He cooked. Not much. Bread, toasted on sticks, and the last of the cheese, and some of the miller's smoked eel, which Rowan had put in Gabe's pack without telling anybody, and a pot of something with lentils and Salty's paste that Alea had been pretending not to want all afternoon.

And when it was done, and they all had their bowls, and he was standing where he always stood, at the edge of the fire, by the place nobody sat in, with the pot, he felt them look at him.

All nine. Not looking, quite. Not looking the way the whole of Group 1 had looked at him on the lake road, with PenguChi's eyes on him and the penguin's. Just: knowing where he was. The way you know where the edge of a roof is.

He sat down.

On the fallen trunk. Between Gabe and Deston. With the pot at his feet and his bowl in his hands, both of them, close against his chest, like somebody might take it.

Nobody said anything. Gabe moved up a little, without looking, to give him room. Deston, without opening his eyes, said "Hm," very quietly, the way he did when a note he'd been waiting for came in on time.

He ate.

***

Petra didn't sleep. He'd known she wouldn't.

He lay by the fire in his blanket with his hands under his head and the rod beside him, and Alea on the far side of the fire with her back against the beech trunk and Astra across her knees, on the first watch, because she'd taken the first watch on this road for two years and wasn't going to stop now; and he watched Petra get up, an hour after the fire went down, and take the stick, and go a little way off along the edge of the wood to where the ground fell away to the stream.

He didn't watch her, after that. Not with his eyes.

He felt her stand. Feet under her shoulders. Weight even. He'd taught her nothing about that; she'd taught herself, in ten years of yards, and then taught Xav, who'd taught him things about it he hadn't known. He felt her stand for a long time.

Then he felt her bring the stick across.

And the line came off it, thin and grey and cold, into the dark over the stream, and stayed. A count of one. Two.

And went.

He felt her rock on her feet when it went. He felt her stand there, afterwards, in the dark, with the stick at her side, looking at the place in the air where it had been, the way she'd looked at it at dusk at the end of the shore, as if it might come back if she looked hard enough. And then he felt her bring it across again, quickly, with her shoulders up. And nothing.

And again. Nothing.

He lay by the fire and didn't move.

*It's the stick*, she'd said, on the jetty. Quite firmly. *Something of him's in it.*

There wasn't anything of him in it. It was a bit of driftwood he'd picked up off the shingle the first week of the school, the spring before last, because Xav needed to see where a line went and he hadn't wanted to use a sword. He'd used it every morning after. He'd given it to her because she'd looked at it every morning as if it were something, and he'd thought she should have something.

He'd thought, then, that it was the right thing to give her. He still thought so. He'd just had no idea, then, what she'd do with it.

Across the fire, Alea turned her head, and looked at him in the dark, with the firelight in her eyes; and he could tell from her ears that she knew he was awake, and knew what he was listening to, and wasn't going to say anything about it either.

He shut his eyes.

Twenty days. Twenty-two, if Malcolm had anything to do with it.

And at the end of them, on the red, on the road in, a few hours short of the fort, a ditch. And four paces in front of it, on the red, the place where he'd stood the second night of the invasion with a black sword on his shoulder and forty children in the clay behind him and a girl of twenty-three at the end of them with her arm broken, who wouldn't put her sword down.

The place where he'd stood, and known, by midnight, that it was the wrong line.

And stayed.

He'd told three people that. In his whole life. Gabe and Deston and Xav, on the end of a jetty, at five in the morning, the night he told them all of it. And he'd asked Xav, who'd been her student, not to tell her. And Xav hadn't. And last night, on the end of the jetty, she'd asked him to stand there again, so that she could see it in the light.

He'd said yes. Of course he'd said yes.

He lay by the fire with his eyes shut and three things against his ribs, a letter and a drawing and a list, and listened to a woman at the edge of a beech wood bring a stick across in the dark, again and again, trying to make a line come that she didn't understand yet, because she thought it was in the stick.

It would come. He knew that much. It would come when she stopped trying to make it, and stood in front of something, the way she'd stood in front of forty children in a ditch with her arm broken; the way she stood, every morning, without knowing it, in front of five children on a quay wall.

He wasn't going to tell her.

He'd stand where he stood. She'd stand where she stood. And it would be hers.

Somewhere in the beech wood an owl went over, low, without a sound, and the little gold light that John had left hanging in the branches swayed, very slightly, in the wind it made going past; and on the far side of the fire, his daughter sat with her back to a tree and her sword across her knees and her ears turned toward the dark, keeping the watch, the way she'd learned, the way her mother would have; and he slept.
