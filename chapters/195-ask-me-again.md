Gigichi felt the ice go three hours before anybody heard it.

She was asleep. Then she wasn't. She lay in the dark in the long room at the top of the militia hall with her eyes open and Xola breathing in the next bed and the gold watch ticking under her pillow, and felt it, the way you feel somebody sit down on the end of your bed. Not a sound. A change. Down in the ground, under the town, under the lake road, under the shingle and the reeds at the lake's edge, where the roots went down into the cold: something letting go.

She took the watch out and opened it. Twelve minutes past three. The little gold leaves on the case caught what light there was.

She put it back. She took out the other thing, the one under it. A scrap of paper, folded in four, soft at the creases from a whole winter of being taken out and put back.

She didn't need to open it. She knew what it said. She'd written it the night of the first frost, in the dark, in English, the way she was doing everything tonight.

*Asked him. He said yes. Ask again in the spring.*

She held it in her fist and lay there and listened to the ground change its mind, and thought, very clearly: *oh no*.

***

The thing about keeping your word was that it didn't care whether you wanted to.

She'd always kept hers. Always. It was the one thing about her nobody had ever had to wonder about, on Earth or here. Gigichi was difficult and Gigichi was rude and Gigichi expected the worst of everything, which was only sensible, because the worst was what usually happened. But if Gigichi said she'd do a thing, she did it. You could build a house on it. People had.

So she'd ask. She'd known that all winter. She'd known it in the first snow, and at midwinter, walking up the lake road between forty paper lanterns with Xola's frostbitten fingers grown back. She'd known it every time she went down to the cabin, and every time she went into it and looked at the clay pot in the window with the bare stick in it and thought *not yet*.

She just hadn't thought about what she'd do if he said *yes* again.

That was the trouble. She lay in the dark and thought about it now. If she stood in front of him in the spring and said *you okay?* and he said *yes*, the way he'd said it in the frost, in that voice, with the lake going dark behind him. She'd have kept her word. She'd have asked. And it would be exactly as terrible as last time, and there'd be no more springs to put it off to, because she'd only said *spring*, she hadn't said *and the one after*.

And the other thing. The thing that was worse.

If he didn't say *yes*.

She didn't know what she'd do with that either. She'd asked Tiger, once, in the first spring, in the channel, and he'd said *no, not really*, and she'd sat with her hands over her mouth for an hour and then written *same* and nothing else, because nothing else was any good. She'd asked Thea, at a door, with a lamp. She knew what to do with *not really* from people her own age, who'd come through the same floor of the same hall on the same night.

She didn't know what to do with it from a man who'd been here thirty-six years.

The ground went on letting go under the town. Xola turned over in her sleep and said something about squid.

Gigichi put the paper back under the pillow, under the watch, and didn't sleep.

***

She counted the winter instead. It was that or think.

Seven weeks since the bonfire. Eight, nearly. It had been the hard kind of winter after midwinter, the kind the town called a proper one, as if there were an improper kind. The forty paper lanterns had stayed up along the lake road walls until the snow rotted them, and nobody took them down, and by the end of the first month they were grey rags on wire and Odette said they'd do for next year's frames.

Xola had a skill. That was the first thing. Xola had gone through the ice after a seven-year-old in a red cap and come up Spark, with *Tide*, and with frostbitten fingers that had grown back by the bonfire, and had spent every morning since with both arms in the horse trough in the militia yard, making the water stand up. It stood up a hand high now. It stood up for a count of ten. She cried every time. Gigichi had stopped making fun of it in the second week, which was a record.

Robin's mother sent eggs. Every day. Nobody could stop her.

Bram had gone on fishing. Five afternoons a week, on his stool beside the man's at the end of the jetty, two backs to the town. Gigichi had lost a bet about it to Higu and another to Xola and paid both in Edric's buns, and refused to bet a third time. Something had happened out there, before midwinter; she didn't know what, and nobody would say. But Bram drilled them differently now. He'd stopped saying *harder* in the yard and started saying *wait*. And once, after drill, he'd stood in the snow with his sword out and his eyes shut for so long that PenguChi had brought him a blanket, and he'd let her put it round him without opening his eyes.

Maren's number had gone up. Thea knew, so Higu knew, so everybody knew: sixty-two. Maren didn't say so, because Maren didn't say things. But she'd come into the kitchen one morning and set the kettle on the cold stove and started the words to warm it, under her breath, the way she had every morning for two years and a half, and stopped halfway. And the kettle had sung anyway. Maren had looked at it as if it had said something rude. Thea said it was the first time she'd ever seen Maren stop in the middle of a spell and have it work. Maren said it wasn't a spell, it was a kettle.

The channel had been elves for a month. Actual elves. Three of them, walking up out of the south to Cinderhold over the snow and kneeling to Yan's snake, which Suzu was being impossible about. Maigo had written ELVES in capitals so many times that Tiger had asked him to stop, and he hadn't. Gigichi had read every word and said *it's probably a trick* every night, and gone to sleep thinking about ears.

Thea sat up with Oswin's leaf most nights. Higu had started a new notebook. Waddles had fallen off the board on the trough about two hundred times and got back on two hundred times. Alea did her forms on the shingle in the dark every morning with a lamp on the step, and Gigichi had watched from the lake road once and seen where her feet ended up, and not said anything about that either.

And the stick in the clay pot in the cabin window had stayed a stick. Every Thursday all winter she'd gone down and put a finger on the soil and asked. Every Thursday it had said *not yet*, the way ground says things: like somebody asleep who knows you're in the room.

Everybody had moved. Even the ice. That was the trouble with ice. It kept its word too. It said *spring*, and here it was.

***

The whole town came down to see it.

It was a thing, in Wrenhaven. Odette had a list. Of course Odette had a list; there were eleven years of it in the back of her book of town-hall business, in her square black hand, *ice out*, and a date, and who'd won. Because people bet. Old Mabry bet every year on the same day, twelve days after the first rain, and had won twice in eleven years and said that proved it.

It wasn't twelve days. It was nine. Mabry stood at the top of the lake road in his good coat and said it was early, the lake was early, and he'd be speaking to it.

Gigichi stood on the shingle with the others and watched it go.

It didn't go all at once. She'd thought it would. She'd thought it would crack like a plate. It didn't. It had gone grey in the night, grey and soft and full of holes, the way snow goes at the end of the winter when it's been rained on, and in the morning there were dark lanes of open water across it from the reeds to the far shore, and the whole sheet of it moved. Slowly. All of it at once. Going. Toward the outflow at the far end, where the lake ran out into the Wren, in great grey slabs that ground against each other and against the shore with a sound like a long low breath.

Xola cried. Of course she did. She stood at the water's edge with Squiddles on her shoulder going a pale delighted blue, and her gills, under her hair, opening and shutting the way they did now when she was near the lake. "It's *turning*," she said. "It's all turning again. I can feel it. It was stuck all winter and now it's—" And couldn't finish, and didn't need to.

Higu stood beside her with her book open, writing. Thea stood with her hands in her sleeves, watching the water the way she watched everything now, as if it were written down somewhere under the surface and she could almost read it. PenguChi was further along, by the hedge, holding Waddles under one arm because he'd tried to go out on the grey ice three times since breakfast and she'd said *no* three times and he'd looked at her the third time as if she'd broken his heart.

And at the end of the jetty, the man.

Sitting on the last plank with his legs over the edge and his rod across his knees, the way he sat every morning, in the grey. Only this morning the grey was moving under him. The ice going out round the end of the jetty, past the posts, grinding, in slow slabs, and the open black water coming in behind it. He didn't move. The three hens sat on the planks behind him in a row, the big red one in front, and watched the ice go with an expression of deep personal grievance, as if the lake had done it at them.

Alea sat on the jetty halfway out, with her boots hanging over, skimming stones onto the slabs as they went past. Badly. On purpose, Gigichi thought.

Gigichi didn't look at the man for long. She looked at the cabin.

At the window. At the clay pot on the sill, behind the glass, with the bare grey stick in it.

***

She'd checked on it every week. All winter. Every Thursday, because it was Maigo's day and it seemed right.

She hadn't told anybody. It wasn't the sort of thing you told people. She'd just gone down, every Thursday, and gone in, because the door was never locked and nobody had ever once told her not to. And stood at the window and put one finger very lightly on the soil in the pot, and listened.

It had been asleep. Like the one on the grave. Maigo had told the channel about that one in capitals, the first snow, *THE TREE IS ASLEEP*, and then, underneath, very small, *it's fine. it's just asleep. I asked*. Gigichi had read it on the end of her bed and gone very still, because she'd felt the same thing, the same week, in a clay pot on a windowsill, and she hadn't asked. She hadn't needed to. You could tell asleep from the other thing. Asleep was warm, a long way down. The other thing wasn't anything.

Every Thursday. Asleep. *Not yet*.

She went up the slope now, while everyone else was watching the ice, through the gap in her own hedge, past the hen-house and the woodpile, and into the cabin.

It was very quiet inside. The kettle on the hook. The curtain, crooked, red with a gold fringe and a blot like a duck. The drawing over the table, of a jetty with forty-one planks, which was wrong, and nobody had ever fixed it. And beside it, pinned at the corner so the corners touched, a page torn out of a book, in Higu's hand.

*Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them.*

*Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)*

*Answer them* had a line through it. One pencil line, straight, at the start of the winter, the day he'd sent six fish down the river in a basket to Duskhollow instead of writing anything. Nothing else did.

Gigichi didn't look at her own name on it for long either.

She went to the window. She put her finger on the soil.

And felt it.

Not asleep. Not any more. Something a long way down, in the dark of the pot, in the roots that had been wrapped round each other all winter like hands in a muff, *turning over*. The way you turn over in bed in the morning, before you open your eyes, when you know it's light. Waking. Slowly. And all the way up the bare grey stick, under the bark, so faint she'd have missed it if she hadn't put her finger on this pot every week for four months, the sap. Coming up. Going toward the three places near the top where the leaves had been in the autumn, gold, before they fell.

Three buds. She looked closer. She hadn't seen them on Thursday. They were there now. Small and dark and tight, like the knuckles of a fist.

*Now*.

She stood at the window with her finger on the pot for a long time.

Then she went back out, down the slope, through the hedge, and onto the shingle, past Higu and Thea and Xola and PenguChi, and out along the jetty, past Alea, all the way to the end, where the man was sitting with the hens. And she stopped behind him, on the last plank but one, with the ice going out on both sides of her.

"It's awake," said Gigichi.

He didn't turn round. But his shoulders changed.

"The tree," said Gigichi. "In the window. It woke up last night. Same time as the ice." She heard her own voice, flat, the way it went when something mattered. "Today. Not tomorrow. It wants to go in today."

The man sat there on the end of the jetty, with the rod across his knees and the black water coming in round the posts.

Then he got up.

***

She'd thought she'd have to choose the place. She'd thought about it all winter, on and off, the way she thought about everything, badly, in the middle of the night. *By the water*, Maigo had said. In the autumn, at Cinderhold, when he'd put the clay pot in Alea's arms. *So she's got one at both ends*. He'd said it in the channel too, afterwards. *BY THE WATER. NOT IN THE SNOW*. And then, underneath: *gigichi will know where*.

She hadn't known where. That was the thing nobody understood about her. People thought she knew things. She didn't. She *asked*. She put her hand on the ground and asked, and waited, and the ground told her, and then she knew. That was all *Briar* was. That was all the hedge had ever been. Asking something to do what it already wanted to.

So she asked.

She walked along the shore, slowly, with her hand flat on the cold shingle and then on the cold mud where the shingle stopped, and the others watching her from the jetty and not saying anything. Alea, with the pot in her arms now, carried down from the window against her chest, the way she'd carried it all the way home from the east. The man, beside Alea, with the blue bucket. Pim's bucket, from the stable at Cinderhold, that a boy had given him at the gate with the cook's leave, and that he'd carried home a month across the kingdom full of water for a stick in a pot. Every evening on the road, Alea said. *The most important water he'd ever carried*.

Gigichi walked along the edge of the lake with her hand on the ground. And listened.

Not by the reeds. Too wet; it'd drown. Not by the hedge. Too much of her own in the ground there already; it'd fight. Not by the field. The pumpkins had the field. Not under the cabin window, where it could see in. She thought that was what the man would want, and she listened for it, and the ground didn't want it.

She came back along the shore. Toward the jetty. Toward the place at the landward end, where the planks began, where the first grey post went down into the shingle and the shingle went down into the water.

And stopped.

There. Under her hand. A long way down, under the stones, in the cold: room. Old roots, from something that had grown here once, a long time ago, and gone, and left the ground soft and open and waiting for something to come into it. And the lake, right there, a pace away, going into the ground and coming up into it, so that whatever went in here would always have its feet in the water. And the jetty beside it, going out. And the light coming down all morning over the hills on the far side.

She knelt. She put both hands flat on the stones.

"Here," said Gigichi.

***

Nobody said anything.

She didn't notice at first. She was listening to the ground, and the ground was very sure, and she was glad of that; she didn't often get *sure*. It was only when she looked up, to tell them to bring the spade, that she saw their faces. Or the ones she could see.

Alea had gone white. Standing on the shingle with the pot in her arms. White, and then pink, and her ears gone straight up the way they did when something went through her, and her mouth a little open.

And the man had stopped.

He was standing at the end of the jetty, the landward end, a pace from her, with the blue bucket in his hand. And he wasn't moving. She couldn't see his face. Nobody could. But she'd learned to read the rest of him, the way they all had: his shoulders, his hands, the way he stood. And the whole of him had gone still, the way the grey grass went still round his feet on the red, Wendy said. As if something had gone through him from his boots to the top of his head and he was waiting for it to finish.

"What?" said Gigichi. Too sharply. "What did I do?"

The man put the bucket down.

"Nothing," he said. "You didn't do anything." His voice was very quiet. "She said that."

Gigichi stared at him.

"Diwa," said the man. "When we came here. The first time. The jetty was half a ruin; there were more holes than planks. She walked out on it. On the planks that were left. All the way to the end. And stood there, and looked at the lake." He was looking at the far end of the jetty. At the last plank, where he sat every morning. "And said that. *Here*."

Gigichi didn't move.

"At the other end," said the man. He looked down at her, kneeling on the stones at the landward end with her hands flat on the ground. "You've put it at the other end."

"I didn't know," said Gigichi. Her voice had gone strange. "I didn't— nobody told me. I just asked. It's the ground. It's what the ground—"

"I know," said the man.

And then he laughed.

Not loudly. Not the helpless kind, the kind that made him sit down; Alea said that one was rare and you should write it down when it happened. This was smaller. A breath, out, through his nose, and his shoulders going down. The way you laugh when somebody does a thing so exactly right that there isn't anything else to do.

"Both ends," said the man. "Maigo's going to be impossible."

Alea made a sound that might have been a laugh and might have been the other thing, and put her face against the top of the clay pot for a moment, against the bare stick, and stood there like that.

Then she gave Gigichi the pot.

***

They planted it before noon.

Alea dug. She wouldn't let anyone else; she was Tempered and the stones were nothing to her and she said she'd carried it from the red and she'd put it in. So she knelt on the shingle and dug with her hands and then with the spade, down through the stones and into the soft old dark under them, while Higu held the lantern-moth over the hole for no reason except that it was a thing to do, and Thea watched with her hands in her sleeves and said, once, very quietly, *you knew exactly where*, and Gigichi said *I asked* and Thea looked at her and smiled.

Xola stood in the lake. Up to her knees, in the cold, in the black water where the ice had been an hour ago, with Squiddles glowing on her shoulder, because she said the water ought to know what was happening. Nobody argued. PenguChi put Waddles down at last and Waddles went straight into the lake beside Xola and floated there on his back, very pleased, with his yellow feet in the air.

The man filled the bucket.

He walked out along the jetty with it, to the end, and knelt on the last plank, and filled it from the lake. Where she'd stood. Gigichi saw him do it. She didn't think he knew she was watching. He knelt there a moment longer than he needed to with the full bucket beside him, and put his hand flat on the plank. Then he got up and carried it back along the jetty, thirty paces, plank by plank, not spilling any. The most important water he'd ever carried. Alea had said that about the road. Gigichi thought it was probably true about this too.

Then Alea took the stick out of the pot. Carefully. With the earth still round its roots, in a ball, the way you'd take a sleeping child out of a cart. And put it in the hole. And held it straight while Gigichi pushed the earth in round it with her hands.

And at the end, before the last of it, Gigichi stopped.

She put one finger in the soft dark earth at the foot of the stick. And wrote. The way she'd written in the gap among the roots in the frost, the night of the hedge. In English. Lowercase. Not loud. Into the ground, where only the ground would read it.

*here*

The ground took it. She felt it go down. Into the old roots, into the room somebody left, into the water coming up from the lake a pace away. And the stick, in her other hand, under Alea's hands, *moved*. Very slightly. The way you'd move in your sleep when somebody says your name.

The man poured the water.

All of it. Slowly. The whole blue bucket, round the foot of the stick, into the stones, into the earth, until it was dark and wet and the water stood on top for a moment and then went down. Gigichi felt it go down. Into the roots. Into the room.

At the top of the stick, the first of the three buds opened.

Not all the way. Not into a leaf. Just open. A split in the dark knuckle of it, and inside, folded very small, something green.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Higu wrote in her book. And Gigichi got up off her knees, with the mud on them, and wiped her hands on her skirt, and took her window out, and wrote in the channel, because somebody had to tell him.

***

She asked him at dusk.

She'd known she would. She'd known it all day, all through the afternoon, while the others went up to the cabin and Alea put the kettle on its two terrible notes and the hens came down off the jetty one at a time to inspect the tree and the big red one pecked it, once, and was shouted at by everyone. She'd known it at supper, which was fish, which was always fish. She'd known it when the first rider of the year came over the ridge from Hethe at sunset with the mud of the road on him and four letters in his bag addressed only *Ars*, in a thin grey hand, and the man took them on the step and stood there holding them for a while and then put them inside his coat without opening any.

She went down to the jetty when the others were inside.

He was there. Of course he was. On the end. Not the last plank tonight; the one before, so there was room beside him, though she didn't think he'd done it for that. The lake was open all the way to the far shore now, black and still and very cold, with a few last grey slabs going slowly toward the outflow and the stars coming out over the hills. At the land end, behind her, a stick in the stones with one bud open.

She walked out along the jetty. Thirty paces. She didn't count. She stopped beside him, and didn't sit down, and stood there with her hands in the pockets of her cardigan and the gold watch in one fist, ticking.

The thing about keeping your word was that it didn't care.

"You okay?" said Gigichi.

***

He didn't say anything for a long time.

She'd known he wouldn't. She'd known it would be like this: the lake, and the dark, and the long wait, the way it had been in the frost. She stood there and waited, and the watch ticked in her fist, and she thought *if he says yes I'm going to push him in the lake*, and meant it, and also knew she wouldn't, and also knew that if he said yes she'd have kept her word and that would have to be enough.

He didn't say yes.

"No," said the man.

In English. Very quietly. So that if anybody had been on the shore, Maren or Bram or a fisherman from the town, they wouldn't have understood a word.

Gigichi stood very still.

"No," he said again. "That's not right either. That's just the other half of *yes*." He put his hands flat on the plank on either side of him. "Sit down, Chi. It's going to be a mess. You said I was supposed to give you the mess."

She sat down. On the plank beside him. With her boots over the black water.

He was quiet for a while.

"I miss her," said the man. "Every day. I thought it would get smaller. Everybody says it gets smaller. It doesn't. It gets further off. Like the hills over there. It's always there when I look up. Today you knelt down at the end of my jetty and said *here* and it was right in front of me again, like the first day, and I didn't know if I was going to laugh or sit down. I laughed. I'm glad I laughed." He was quiet. "I don't know if it was good. Putting it there. I think it was good. I'll know in about twenty years."

The watch ticked.

"I've been saying goodbye to you," said the man. "All of you. Since the Lists. A little at a time, so it wouldn't be so bad at the end. Every time somebody writes *home* in the channel, I put a bit of them down. In advance. Higu caught me at it. Severin caught me at it. They both told me to stop." He breathed out. "I've stopped about half. That's the truth. About half. The other half I can't get my hands off."

Gigichi didn't say anything.

"I don't know if I want you to go," he said. "I want you to go. Home. Your mothers. Xola's mother, who she cries about at half past two in the morning. I want that for all of you so much I can't sleep some nights." He stopped. "And I want you to stay. Both. At the same time. All the time. And I'm ashamed of the second one, because wanting you to stay is wanting somebody's mother to wait." He was quiet. "So I don't say either. I say *yes*."

Out on the lake, a last slab of grey ice turned over, slowly, and went under.

"Alea's going south," he said. "In the summer. To the Lists. To beat Sorrel again; she's already decided. And I don't know if I'm going. I want to. I want to sit in a stand and watch my girl." His hands had come off the planks and were holding each other, between his knees. "And I'm frightened of it. I don't know why. I think Maren worked it out, last summer, at the Lists. She looked at me as if she had." He was quiet. "I'm frightened I'll be proud of her in front of people. That's all. That's the whole thing. I don't know why that frightens me. It does."

Gigichi looked at his hands. She couldn't see his face. She'd never been able to. She didn't need to.

"And my dad," said the man.

He stopped.

He didn't say anything for so long she thought that was it. That he'd got to the bottom and there wasn't any more. The stars came out the rest of the way. The cabin window, up the slope behind them, was yellow, with a red curtain hanging crooked in it.

"He's got somebody," said the man. "Gabe told me. At the funeral. Mine. She stood with her hand on his elbow the whole time, and he shook every hand in the room and asked after everybody by name and didn't cry. Gabe said she never moved her hand." He was quiet. "I'm glad. I'm so glad some nights I can't breathe."

"That's not the mess," said Gigichi. Very quietly. "That's the good bit."

"I know." He breathed out. "The mess is I did the sum. At midwinter. On this jetty, with the lanterns still up on the road. I went to sleep in Malacca when I was twenty-six. And I've been here thirty-six years. And I think—" He stopped again. "I think I'm older than him now. My dad. Inside. I think I'm older than my dad, and he's still there, on the other side of whatever it is, with her hand on his elbow. And I'm here. And I'm never going to—"

He didn't finish it.

Gigichi didn't make him. She sat on the end of the jetty beside him with her boots over the black water and the watch ticking in her fist, and let him not finish it, the way Higu had said once, on a staircase, that you were supposed to. Not fix it. Not say anything. Just be there, on the plank, while it was a mess.

After a long time he said, very quietly, "That's the mess."

***

"That's the answer," said Gigichi.

It came out of her rough. She hadn't known it was going to. She'd said it once before, at a table in the snow with a stew that was supposed to be assam pedas and wasn't, through tears, *that's the answer, it's a mess, I'm sorry, that's the answer*. To him. And he'd said *thank you*, and she hadn't known for a whole year what he'd been thanking her for.

She knew now.

"That's the answer," she said again. "It's a mess. Thank you."

He didn't say anything. His shoulders went down.

"Your dad one's the worst," said Gigichi.

"Yes."

"The rest of them are just normal bad. That one's the worst."

"Yes," said the man. And then, after a moment, she heard it in his voice: something that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite the other thing. "You're very bad at this, Chi."

"I know," said Gigichi. "You asked me first. Years. In my chat. Every stream. *You okay?* To everybody. And nobody ever asked you." She was looking at the lake. "I never answered. Not once. On Earth. And then I got here and you asked me again, at a table, and I gave you the mess, and you said *thank you*." She swallowed. "I'm not good at it. I've never done it. I've only been on the other end."

"You did it fine," said the man.

"I didn't do anything. I just sat here."

"Yes," said the man. "That's the whole thing."

***

He showed her before she went in.

She didn't ask. She was getting up off the plank, with her knees stiff from the cold and the mud from the planting still on them, and he put out his hand. Open. Palm up. And there it was, over it, in the dark, the way they came up for all of them: a window. Faint gold lines on the air. Gigichi looked at it.

"Sixty-three," she said.

"It moved," said the man. "Just now. While you weren't saying anything." He looked at it. "It does that when you put something down. Halvard says."

She looked at the number for a long time.

"How much did you put down?"

"About half," said the man. "The other half's still in my hands. I'm working on it."

He closed his hand. The window went.

He crossed them off that night. She saw him do it.

She'd gone up to the cabin to get her coat, and he came in behind her with the empty blue bucket and put it by the door, and went to the wall over the table where the drawing was, and the list beside it, corners touching. He took the stub of pencil off the shelf. And drew one line, straight, through *A tree*.

Then he stood there for a while.

Then he drew another, straight, through *Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)*

He didn't say anything. He put the pencil back on the shelf. Alea, on the ladder to the loft, with her hair down, watched him do it and didn't say anything either, and then looked at Gigichi, and her ears went down and up, and she went on up the ladder.

Three lines. *Answer them*. *A tree*. *Ask again*. Three of seven.

Gigichi walked back up the lake road in the dark with her coat on and the gold watch ticking in her pocket, past the hedge, past the forty iron hooks on the posts where the lanterns had hung at midwinter, up into the town. She went into the militia hall without waking anyone and up to the long room and sat on her bed.

She took out the scrap of paper. Soft at the creases. She unfolded it.

*Asked him. He said yes. Ask again in the spring.*

She took the pencil from the windowsill and wrote under it, in the dark, in English, in lowercase, very small:

*asked him again. he gave me the mess. 63.*

Then she folded it back up in four and put it under the pillow with the watch. And lay down. And slept, all night, for the first time since the ice.
