Winter knew three days before anyone.

She was on the roof of the Broken Lance at midnight, behind the chimney stack, on the flat leaded bit you got to through the hatch at the top of the back stair, with her back to the warm bricks and her coat under her and her bare feet on the lead. It had rained all week. It always rained in Valcrest in the winter; grey and warm and endless, coming up the river off the southern sea in long soft curtains, so that the streets ran and the Lists filled up at the bottom like a bowl. It had rained all day. And at a little before midnight, it stopped.

Not the way rain stops. The way a breath stops.

She sat very still. The roof was wet under her feet. The city went down the hill in front of her toward the river in the dark, wet slate and wet tile and lamps behind shutters, and the great pale ring of the Lists in the middle of it, with forty lamps along its west wall burning low; and over all of it the air had gone still. Not calm. Still. The way the air goes still in a room when someone in it has just heard something they weren't meant to.

And it smelled of iron.

She knew that smell. She'd smelled it on her own hands every time she said *still* to a trough and the water bloomed. Cold. Not the soft grey Valcrest cold that got into your clothes and stayed there. The other kind. The kind that came from somewhere and was going somewhere and didn't care what was in the way.

Under her bare feet, on the wet lead of the roof, without her asking, the frost came up.

Little feathery flowers of ice, opening out from her heels in a ring, white and fine and crackling faintly in the dark. They did that sometimes, when she felt something before she knew what it was. She looked down at them. She looked at the way they leaned.

Every one of them was leaning south. Toward the river. Toward the sea.

"Oh," said Winter. Out loud. To nobody.

***

She told Chris first, because Chris would write it down.

She went down the back stair and knocked on his door, and he opened it in his shirt with his notebook already in his hand, because Chris slept with his notebook the way other people slept with a cat. She told him on the landing in the dark: *it's coming. Real cold. Off the sea. Three days. Midwinter night.* He wrote it down without asking how she knew. Then he looked at her bare feet on the boards, and the frost on them, and said:

"How cold?"

"Cold enough," said Winter.

He didn't ask what that meant. He'd known her a long time now, here and before, and he'd learned, a long time ago, that when Winter said a thing like that she'd already thought about what it meant and decided you didn't need to hear it yet. He wrote *cold enough* and underlined it.

Then she told Lucan.

That was harder. Lucan was at the long table in the Lance at breakfast with his eggs and his tea and a letter from the council, and when she sat down opposite him and told him, he laughed. Not unkindly. Lucan was never unkind; he was the gentlest man in Valcrest and he wept at everything and he'd stood in the royal box in the frost the morning she made the flower with tears on his face. He laughed the way you laugh at a child who's told you the sky's going to fall.

"Valcrest doesn't freeze, my dear," said Lucan. "It rains. It's the sea. The sea keeps us warm. We get a frost or two in the hard winters, a skin on the troughs; you've seen it. You made a flower out of it." He smiled. "That's as cold as it gets. It's the saying. *Valcrest doesn't freeze.*"

"It's going to," said Winter.

"My dear—"

"Three days," said Winter. "Midwinter night. Off the sea, up the river. It's coming. I can smell it."

Lucan stopped smiling.

He sat there with his tea halfway to his mouth and looked at her. At her silver hair and her two black horns and one blue eye and one pink; at her hands, out of her sleeves for once, flat on the table; and at the table under them, where a thin white rime had come up out of the wood round her fingers without her meaning it to, the way it came up when she was sure.

He put the tea down.

"The black ice," said Lucan. Very quietly.

Nobody said anything. Hako, at the end of the table, put his spoon down. Newish stopped humming.

"I was sixteen," said Lucan. "Thirty-one years ago. A midwinter. It rained for a week and then it stopped, all at once, in the night." He was looking at the table, at the white round her fingers. "And the fog came up the river off the sea. Freezing fog. Nobody knew what it was; nobody'd seen it. It came up the river at dusk and it froze on everything it touched. The masts. The ropes. The roofs. It froze the harbour solid from wall to wall, and by midnight the river quarter was glass." His mouth moved. "Valcrest doesn't freeze. Nobody in the river quarter had a stove. You don't need a stove in Valcrest. They had braziers for the damp, and blankets, and they went to bed." He stopped. "We found them in the morning. In the Street of Coopers. In their beds."

"How many," said Winter.

"Two hundred and eleven," said Lucan. "My father counted. He was on the council." He looked up. "I helped him carry."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Then we'll tell them," said Winter.

***

That was the thing about seeing it first. He'd told her that, in a yard, at dawn, with her bare feet on the cobbles and a face she couldn't see. *It's no good unless you make sure nobody else is last.*

So she made sure.

She told the channel. She told Ysolde, who listened with her ram's horns tilted and her arms folded and said *how long* and *where* and then went to find the captain of the city watch. She went with Lucan to the council, in the hall on the square, and stood at the back with her hands in her sleeves while he told them, and when a man in a fur collar said *Valcrest doesn't freeze*, she took one hand out of her sleeve and laid it on the council table, and the rime came up out of the oak round her fingers, white, crackling, in a ring a foot across, and the man in the fur collar stopped talking.

She went down to the Street of Coopers with Newish and Hako on the second morning and went from door to door. Most of them knew her. All of Valcrest knew her; half the children in the river quarter wore paper horns at festivals, black, folded, tied under the chin, because of her. She'd never known what to do with her face about that. She didn't know now. She stood in doorways with her hands in her sleeves and told old women and old men and mothers with babies on their hips, *it's coming, midwinter night, real cold, come to the Lists*, and watched them look at her horns and her hair and her eyes and decide whether to believe the girl who looked like the enemy. Most of them did. Some didn't. She went back to the ones who didn't, the next morning, with Lucan, who'd carried their grandparents out of those same doors when he was sixteen.

They came, then.

The Lists opened its gates on the third day at noon. The great oval, with its stands going up in rings round the sand and its forty lamps along the west wall; the city's own bowl. The council sent braziers, every one they had, and charcoal, and the coopers sent barrels split for firewood, and the watch carried blankets out of the barracks in armfuls. Ina lit the wall. All forty lamps at once with a flick from the north end, the way he'd lit them on Lamp Night in the autumn, violet and then yellow; not for knowing, this time, he said, standing on the sand under them with his mask off, but for warm. And they were warm. You could feel them from the bottom tier, like standing near a hearth.

And the river quarter came up the hill in the afternoon, with its blankets and its children and its old people on handcarts, and filled the stands.

Nine thousand, the watch captain said. Nine thousand and some, in the lower and middle tiers, round the braziers, under blankets, packed in. Wil, who was ten now and had drawn the names out of the helmet at the Lists two summers ago, ran up and down the stairs with a bucket of charcoal, being important. Newish sat on the bottom tier with a ring of children round her and hummed, because children went quiet when Newish hummed; something in the beat of it. Hako carried old men up the stairs one at a time on his back and set them down by the braziers and went back for the next. Chris walked the tiers with his notebook, counting.

Winter stood at the top of the south stand and looked at the river.

***

She'd been stuck for a year.

Chris had a page for it. He had a page for everybody; he'd shown her hers in the autumn, on the edge of the sand, between bouts. *Winter. Spark. Rime.* And under it, a column of numbers in his neat hand, a year of them. *91. 91. 92. 92. 93. 93. 94. 94.*

She'd tried. She'd tried all year. She'd said *still* to the trough by the east gate a hundred times and made a hundred flowers, and every one of them was the same size as the first. She'd said it to the fountain in the square, which was bigger, and got a flower the same size in the middle of it and the rest of the water going on as if she hadn't said anything. She'd said it to the river, once, in the spring, standing on the quay steps, and the river hadn't noticed her at all. And she'd pushed. She'd stood on the quay and pushed the cold out of her the way Ina pushed his fire, harder and harder, *still*, *still*, and it had come out of her hands and gone into the water and the water had gone on; and the cold had stayed in her hands. And burned.

It did that. It always had, since the first month. *Cold that burns*, Chris had written, on the first page, in the first autumn. Her own cold, that she couldn't feel on her skin, in the wind, on bare feet on a winter roof; she could feel it when she pushed. It came back into her fingers and stayed there and went white, and then it hurt, the way a burn hurts. She'd had blisters on three fingers in the spring. Ysolde had seen them and said *no*, the way she'd said it to Ina when he burned his fingertips reaching for a twenty-seventh lamp; and Winter had stopped pushing, and the number had sat at ninety-four.

She didn't know what Rime was for. That was the truth of it. She'd made a flower, two winters ago, and it was beautiful, and it hadn't melted till she let it; and she'd thought that was the start of something. And it hadn't been. It had been a flower.

She'd sat on the roof of the Broken Lance most nights of the autumn and thought about it, and got nowhere. And then, a little before midnight three nights ago, the rain had stopped, and the frost had come up round her heels without her asking.

Leaning south.

She'd looked at them the next morning too. Not on the roof; on the posts. The old oak posts round the top of the south stand of the Lists, where the awnings hung in summer. There'd been a frost that morning, the first edge of it, the smell of iron in the air; and every post round the top of the stand had a little growth of rime on it, white, feathery, like moss. On one side.

The south side. The river side. All of them. Every feather of rime on every post pointing the same way, out, toward the river and the sea. Toward where the cold was coming from.

She'd stood in front of one for a long time.

"It grows into the wind," said Chris, at her shoulder. He'd followed her up. He had the notebook. "Rime. It's not like frost on a window. It's the fog. The fog's full of water that's colder than it should be, and when it hits something it freezes on, and the next bit freezes on that, so it grows. Toward where it's coming from." He looked at the post. "I read about it. Before. On a mountain somewhere; a weather station with feathers on it a metre long, all one way." He wrote something. "The windward side gets all of it. The other side gets nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Hardly anything. The first thing in the way takes it. That's why it's all on one side." He looked at her. "Why?"

She didn't answer. She was looking at the post. At the bare side of it, the north side, the side away from the river, where there was no rime at all. Just wet oak.

The first thing in the way takes it.

***

She tried it on the trough that afternoon. Her trough, by the east gate, the long stone one where she'd made the flower; full of rainwater, black. There was a thin fog coming up off the river, the first breath of it, the iron smell. She stood on the river side of the trough with her back to it, between it and the fog, facing south.

And she didn't say *still*.

She'd said *still* for a year. Like a command; like telling the water what to do. She didn't say anything. She stood there, between the fog and the trough, the first thing in its way, and let it come.

It came. Slow. The fog, white and fine and cold, drifting up off the river and through the east gate and onto her. And where it touched her, it froze. She felt it: on her coat, on her hair, on her lashes, on the curve of her horns; little points of cold, catching, holding, the next bit catching on that. Growing toward the river. Toward where it was coming from.

Behind her, the trough stayed black.

She could see it over her shoulder. A skin of ice at the edges, where the fog curled round her. But in the middle, behind her, where she was in the way, the water was black and wet and moving a little, and there was no frost on it at all. She'd taken it. All of it. The first thing in the way.

"Winter," said Chris, from the gate.

And then she did what she always did. She *pushed*. She didn't mean to. It was coming, and she was taking it, and she wanted to take more of it, all of it, the whole fog, so she reached out into it with the cold in her hands and pulled it to her, *come here*, the way you'd pull a rope; and it came, a lot of it, all at once, too much. It came onto her hands. Her bare hands, out of her sleeves. White. Thick. Growing. And it went into her fingers and stayed there, and her fingers went white, and then it hurt the way it always hurt, a burn, and she couldn't let go of it. That was the thing. She'd pulled it, and now it was on her and it wanted more and she couldn't make it stop.

"*No*," said Ysolde.

She was there. She'd come down from the stand; Winter hadn't seen her come. She stood three paces off, the way she always stood, with her ram's horns tilted and her face giving nothing away; and she didn't step in front of Winter, the way everybody always had. She just said *no*, the way she'd said it to Ina.

And Ina was there. He took Winter's hands in his.

Not gently. Oni hands, big and warm and hot right through, the way a stone is hot that's been by a fire all day; he held her white hands between his and the rime on them hissed and went to water and ran down both their wrists, and the burn went out of her fingers a joint at a time, the way it goes out of you after you've touched a stove. She stood there and let him. She didn't like being held. She let him.

"You pulled," said Ina. Quietly. With her hands in his.

"Yes."

"Don't pull. You're frost, not a fisherman." He looked at her hands. Pink now, wet, shaking a little. "It came to you on its own. Before you pulled. I saw. You didn't have to do anything. You just had to be there first."

She looked at him.

*You just had to be there first.*

She thought about a girl in Vietnam with her phone off. About two months of messages she'd scrolled through on the edge of a bed, in order, after. About a yard at dawn, and bare feet on the cobbles, and *it's no good unless you make sure nobody else is last*. About three years of watching which way things moved in a room so that she'd see it before it got to her; and every morning of this week, telling. She'd been first. She'd made herself first, for three years, so that nothing came at her from behind again. And she'd made sure the others weren't last. She'd stood in doorways in the Street of Coopers.

She'd never once thought it was the same thing.

The rime didn't need her to pull it. It didn't need *still*. It came off the sea and up the river toward the city, and it would land on the first thing in its way. That was all it did. It didn't care what was in the way. It would land on whatever was first.

So be first.

***

Midwinter came in at dusk.

The longest night of the year. She felt it go out of the day like a tide going out: the light, and then the warmth, and then the last of the wet; until the air over the Lists was so still and so dry that the smoke from the braziers went up in straight grey lines, and the lamps on the west wall stood up without flickering. Nine thousand people in the stands, under blankets, round the fires, with their breath going up. Newish humming on the bottom tier. Wil asleep on a sack of charcoal with his paper horns on crooked.

And then the fog came up the river.

She saw it from the top of the south stand. White. Coming up the river off the sea in the last of the light the way milk comes up in a pan: slow, then all at once, filling the river from bank to bank, and climbing out over the quays, and up the streets of the river quarter, empty now, between the dark houses. She heard it freeze. You could hear it, in the dead still air; a faint high crackling, like sugar, coming up the hill. The roofs of the river quarter went grey, then white, then glass. The masts in the harbour went white to the tops.

It came up the hill toward the Lists. Over the outer wall. Into the bowl.

She heard the stands see it. Nine thousand people at once, a sound going round the tiers like wind in a field, and a woman somewhere below her saying *oh god, oh god, it's the black ice*, in the voice of someone who'd been a child thirty-one years ago and remembered.

It came over the south rim. Over the top of the stand. Pouring, white, down the tiers toward the people and the fires.

Winter stepped into it.

To the very top of the south stand, the rim, the river side, the first thing in its way. She stood on the last bench with her back to the Lists and her face to the river and her hands out of her sleeves at her sides, and the fog came up over the edge of the bowl and met her.

She didn't pull. She didn't say anything. She stood there.

And it came to her.

It froze on her. All of it that she was in front of. On her coat, her hair, her lashes, the curve of her horns. She felt it catch and hold and grow, the next bit on that, the next on that; little feathers of ice standing out from her toward the river, toward where it was coming from, longer, and longer. Her silver hair went white and stiff and stood out behind her into the dark like a flag. Her horns went white. Her coat went white, then thick, then hard.

And behind her, nothing.

She couldn't turn round to see. She didn't need to. She could feel it, the way you can feel a door behind you that you've shut. The fog came over the rim of the south stand and met her and froze on her, and what went past her into the bowl was thin, and dry, and only cold. Not black ice. Not glass. Just a winter night in a stone bowl with fires in it. The way the north side of a post is just wet oak.

She wasn't wide enough. She knew that at once. She was one person on a bench, and the fog was coming over the whole rim of the stand; it went round her on either side, and down into the tiers to her left and her right, and she heard the crackle start on the benches there, and a child crying.

So she stood wider.

Not with her arms. She didn't know how she did it. The same way she'd known about the frost three days before anyone; she just knew, and then it was so. She stood on the rim of the south stand in the fog with her face to the river, and let herself be in the way of more of it. Of all of it. As far along the rim as she could feel. And the rime that was growing on the benches to her left and right stopped growing there, and started growing toward her instead, the way it grew on the river side of a post; a long white feathered line of it, along the top of the rim on either side of her, all of it leaning in toward where she stood, all of it pointing at the river.

She was the post. The whole south side of the Lists was the post. And she was the side the weather came from.

It didn't hurt. That was the thing. She waited for it. For the burn, the white fingers, the cold coming back into her hands and staying there, the way it always had when she pushed. It didn't come. She wasn't pushing. She wasn't pulling anything to her. It was coming on its own, the way weather comes; and she was only standing where it came first.

Her own cold, that she'd never been able to feel. She stood in it, and it didn't burn her, because for the first time in three years she wasn't trying to make it do anything.

***

She didn't know how long she stood there.

All night. Chris said afterwards. He'd sat on the bench below her with his notebook on his knee and a lamp at his feet and written down the time every hour, the way he logged everything; and then at some point after midnight he'd stopped writing the time and just sat there, with his hand on the back of her coat, which was a foot thick by then and hard as a wall. He said he'd been afraid she'd fall. She didn't fall.

Ysolde stood behind her. Not in front. Three paces back, on the bench below, between her and the bowl; facing the other way, toward the people, with her sword out of its sheath and her ram's horns going white too at the tips where the edge of the fog curled round. Like a door with someone standing behind it, holding it shut. All night. Winter could feel her there.

Hako came up at some point, with his spear, and stood at Ysolde's shoulder, and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say.

Newish came up later. She stood on the bench below Chris and hummed. Very quietly. And Winter found that she was breathing in the beat of it without meaning to; slow, and long, and steady, like the sea.

Ina didn't come up. He was on the sand at the bottom, under the forty lamps, with his mask off and his hands lit, keeping the wall warm. She saw him once, when she could still turn her head; a small figure in violet light, looking up at her. He lifted his hand. She didn't lift hers. She couldn't, by then. But he knew.

Below them, round the fires, in the cold, nine thousand people slept.

***

Dawn came up grey over the river.

The fog went out the way it had come in: slow, then all at once, sliding back down the hill and off the quays and into the river and away toward the sea, and the light coming up behind it, thin and cold and clean. And the city was white.

Every roof in the river quarter was glass. Every mast in the harbour was a white tree. The river ran black between banks of ice. And the Lists, the great pale bowl in the middle of the city, was white along its whole south rim, a long thick ridge of rime a yard high, feathered out toward the river like the edge of a wing.

And in the middle of it, where it was thickest, a girl.

She heard them see her. She couldn't turn round; she was frozen into it, her hair and her coat and her horns, standing out a forearm's length into the dawn in white feathers, pointing south. But she heard it, behind her, going round the tiers: people waking up under blankets by the burnt-down braziers, cold, alive, and sitting up, and turning round, and looking up at the top of the south stand. Nine thousand people. A sound she'd never heard a crowd make, in the Lists, in three years of bouts. Not cheering. Not anything. A whole bowl of people drawing breath at once, and not letting it out.

And then Lucan, somewhere below her, weeping. Of course it was Lucan.

"Nobody," he was saying. To someone. To everyone. "Nobody, nobody, they're all, the Street of Coopers, Ysolde, *nobody*—"

"I know," said Ysolde. Behind her. Closer than three paces. "I can see."

Ina came up the stairs. She heard him come; she knew his step. He came up the tiers through the crowd with his hands still faintly lit and stood on the bench below her and put them on her back, flat, through the foot of rime on her coat; and it hissed and ran, and the cold went out of her a little at a time, and she could move her head.

She turned it. Not much. Enough to see the bowl.

White, along the south rim. And below it, in the tiers, on the benches, round the braziers, people. Wrapped in blankets with frost on the tops of the blankets and none underneath. Standing up. Looking at her. A woman with a baby. An old man on a handcart, with his mouth open. Wil, awake now, on his sack of charcoal, with his paper horns on crooked, very white at the tips with frost, and the rest of them only paper.

She looked at the paper horns for a long time.

***

Her window opened while Ina was still melting her loose.

*Windward*. She read it twice. The side the weather comes from. The side that gets all of it, first, so the other side gets nothing.

She'd been the other side, all her life. In Vietnam with her phone off. On the sand at the midsummer bout, the first summer, when the thing with horns went for hers and everyone stood in front of her. In the channel, with *thank you, everyone, for standing in front*, because that was all she'd had to say. She'd been the north side of the post. Everybody stood in front of her.

*Windward*.

"Well," said Ysolde, behind her. She'd seen Winter's face; she couldn't see the window, but she'd seen the face. "There."

It was what she'd said at the trough. Winter heard her say it and knew she'd meant to.

"You're in front," said Ysolde.

"Yes."

"For once."

"Yes," said Winter. And then, because her face was doing something she couldn't stop, and she'd never in her life seen the point of going round a thing when you could go through it: "It's good. It's better than the other side."

Ysolde didn't say anything. But she came the last pace, the one she never came, and stood beside her on the rim of the south stand in the dawn, close enough that their shoulders touched through the rime; and stayed there.

Then Winter did the rule. With fingers Ina had only just melted, slowly.

They found nobody in their beds.

The watch went down to the Street of Coopers at noon with Lucan, who wouldn't stay away, and went from door to door the way he and his father had gone thirty-one years ago. Every door. Every bed. The beds were empty. The people who'd slept in them were up at the Lists, being given porridge by the coopers' wives out of the split barrels, and arguing with each other about whose blanket was whose.

Lucan sat down on a doorstep in the Street of Coopers, in the glass, and wept for an hour. Hako sat beside him. Nobody minded.

Winter went up on the roof of the Broken Lance that night.

The lead was dry. There was frost on it, a little, a thin white skin; the cold was going, but slowly. She sat with her back to the warm bricks of the chimney stack and her coat under her and her bare feet out in front of her, and looked at the city. White. Glass roofs, and the river black between its banks, and the Lists in the middle like a bowl somebody had set down, white along its south rim. Forty lamps on the west wall, burning low, warm, for nothing in particular now; Ina had left them lit.

The frost came up round her heels. A ring of little feathers, crackling faintly. She looked at them.

They were leaning north.

Away from the river. Toward her. All of them. A ring of rime round her bare feet on the roof of an inn, every feather of it leaning in, toward where she sat; as if she was the weather, now. As if she was the side it came from.

She sat there for a long time.

Somewhere a long way north of here, on a road over the downs in the snow, a man with a rod was walking home. The channel said so. Hyvred had written it, a fortnight ago, the morning the picture went out: *he's gone. at first light. on foot. east.* She thought about him. A man she'd known four months on Earth, on calls with Chris, who'd have made the joke about a girl called Winter with ice magic; who'd stood two paces off in a yard at dawn and told her that seeing it first was no good unless nobody else was last.

She'd done it. Both halves. She'd seen it first. And then she'd gone and stood where it came, so that it didn't come to anyone else.

She thought she might tell him. One day. When he was home. She'd sit two paces off on a step somewhere and tell him, bluntly, the way she told everything; and he'd be quiet for a moment, the way he was, and she wouldn't be able to see his face, and it wouldn't matter.

She opened the channel instead, because it was midnight, and it was cold, and somebody ought to.
