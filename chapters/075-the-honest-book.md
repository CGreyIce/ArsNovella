There was frost on the morning they left.

The first of the year. Kestra saw it from the cabin step at dawn, where she'd sat every night for eleven nights with a blanket round her shoulders and her sword across her knees: a thin white skin on everything, the hen-yard and the woodpile and the shingle and the long sailcloth roof over the forge, so that the whole shore looked as if someone had breathed on it. The lake was steaming. Not with heat. With cold. A low grey mist lying over the black water, moving very slowly, the way breath moves on a still morning.

The fisherman was on the end of the jetty in it, with his rod.

He'd been there since before light. He was always there before light. Kestra had learned that on the first night, and the second, and every night after. She'd learned a great many things, on that step, in eleven nights. She'd written down almost none of them.

She watched him fish. He caught nothing. He didn't seem to mind.

After a while, he reeled in, and stood up, and walked back along the jetty through the mist toward the cabin, and passed her on the step on his way in, and said, "Morning."

"Morning," said Kestra.

That was all he ever said to her, at dawn. *Morning.* Eleven times. She'd counted.

***

They loaded the stone first.

Garrow and four of Hale's militia, with a dray from the brewer and two oxen, down the lake road at first light. The black stone went onto the dray on its back, in straw, with its long spike lying out behind it and its face to the sky. Kestra watched them lift it. Four men and the smith, and a winch rigged off the jetty post, and still they grunted, and still the dray creaked on its axle when it took the weight.

The fisherman had carried the whole sword, both halves of this, on his shoulder from the Redmarch. Kestra had watched him lift the long half off the anvil with one hand.

She didn't write that down either.

The letters on the face of the stone were full of frost. White, in the black, so that for a moment, standing at the edge of the dray in the early light, Kestra could almost see them. Then her eyes slid off them, the way they always did, and came back, and slid off again. She'd stopped trying, on the second day of the carving. She'd sat on the jetty all that day with her book closed on her knee and watched him cut them, one tiny curl of iron at a time, and hadn't opened the book once.

She knew what they said. The girl had read them out loud, at sunset, on the shingle. Kestra had been close enough to hear.

Beside the stone, in the straw, the fisherman laid a sack. A heavy one, lumpy, tied at the neck. Stones, the size of a child's fist. Kestra had watched every one of them arrive.

And on top of the sack, a letter. Sealed. *Aldous, Cinderhold.*

"Four days to the crossroads at Hethe," said Garrow, to the fisherman, very seriously, with his hand on the side of the dray. "Then east. A month, the king says, the way the roads are in the autumn. Maybe more." He swallowed. "I'll take it all the way. I want to. If you'll let me. I want to see where it goes."

The fisherman looked at him.

"It goes to a grave in the middle of the Redmarch," he said. "Beside a tree. There's nothing else out there for miles."

"I know," said Garrow. "I want to see it."

The fisherman looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded.

"There's a chaplain at Cinderhold called Aldous," he said. "Give him the letter. And there are five people there who'll carry it the last stretch. A short one who's very strong, and a girl with a bow, and a tall one with a snake, and a thin one who never stops watching, and..." He stopped. Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "And a tree. You'll know them."

"A tree?"

"You'll know him," said the fisherman. "He'll be the one talking."

***

The king said goodbye to the hens first.

Kestra would put that in her book later, exactly as it happened, because she knew that if she didn't, nobody would ever believe her, including herself.

He went into the hen-yard in his travelling coat, with his saddlebags already on the horse at the gate, and squatted down in the frost, and the three hens came to him across the yard at a run. Not in a line, with their necks out. At a *run*, the way dogs run to a door. Captain got there first. He picked her up and tucked her under his arm and scratched her under the chin, and she made the purring noise, and the other two stood on his boots.

"Look after him," said the king to the hen, quite seriously. "He's not as clever as he thinks."

Captain looked at him with one round orange eye.

"I know," said the king. "But try."

He put her down. She followed him to the gate. All three of them did, in a line now, and when he went through it and shut it behind him, they stood at the bars, in the frost, and watched him go, and Captain made a noise Kestra had never heard a hen make. It sounded like grief.

The fisherman, standing on the step with his tea, looked at his hens with an expression of profound betrayal.

"Eight years," he said.

"Animals can tell," said the king, and very nearly smiled.

***

He said goodbye to the girl next.

She was standing by the forge, by the cold hearth, with the black sword across her back in Garrow's harness and her chin up and her ears very straight. She'd swung it every day since the naming. Kestra had watched her. Twenty times, then thirty, then fifty, on the shingle at dawn, with the town on the field wall cheering every one, and her arms shaking, and her face set. She'd nearly fallen over twice more. Not three times. She was never going to fall over three times.

The king stood in front of her and looked at her for a long time.

"Every day," he said.

"Every day."

"You'll curse it."

"I curse it already."

"Good. Keep cursing it. The day you stop cursing it is the day you'll have grown into it." He looked at the grip standing up over her shoulder. The old dark leather. "In two years, come and show me. In the capital. Swing it in the castle yard, in front of the Council, and watch their faces." Something moved in his own. "I'd pay a great deal to see their faces."

"I'll come," said the girl.

"And Alea." He'd never said her name before. Kestra noticed. The girl noticed too; her ears went flat for a moment, and then up again. "Your mother would have stitched my mouth shut for saying this. But I'm going to say it anyway, because she isn't here, and somebody should." He put one big scarred hand on her shoulder, beside the grip. "She'd be proud of you. Not because of the sword. Because of the bellows. Five days, and you never once complained."

The girl didn't say anything. She couldn't, Kestra saw. She just nodded, very hard, with her mouth pressed shut.

The king took his hand away.

***

And then he went to the fisherman.

They stood in front of each other at the bottom of the step, in the frost, two men of about the same size. The strongest man in the kingdom, and one who was halfway to being the same. Kestra stood by the horses and watched them, and wrote nothing, and remembered everything.

They didn't embrace. They weren't that kind of men. The king held out his right arm, and the fisherman took it, forearm to forearm, the way soldiers do, and they held on.

"In the spring," said the king.

"In the spring."

"Fish."

"I will."

"Grow your pumpkins."

"I will."

"Obey your orders."

"I've only got one."

"Then obey it," said the king. And he let go, and turned, and walked to his horse, and didn't look back. Kestra had served him twelve years. She'd never once seen him not look back at something he was leaving. She'd seen him look back at burning towns, and dead friends, and his own throne. He didn't look back at this.

She understood, after a moment, that he couldn't.

***

They rode up the lake road in the frost.

The king first, and Kestra behind him, three paces, on his left, where she always rode. The pack horses behind her. And behind them, slow, creaking, the brewer's dray with the oxen and Garrow and the four militiamen and the black stone on its back in the straw.

The town was waiting.

Not at the top of the lake road. Hale had stood there for eleven days and held them back, and he was there again now, big and grey with his chain on for once, at the front. But the town wasn't behind him. It was everywhere. All along the lake road, on both sides, standing by the long stone wall and in the stubble fields above it, in their hundreds, in the cold, with their breath going up white. And in the market square, and along the street to the south gate. The whole of Wrenhaven, out in the first frost of the year, to watch a king ride away.

Nobody cheered. They'd cheered every day at the forge. They didn't cheer now. They just stood there, in silence, as the king rode past. And as he passed each of them, they bowed. Not the deep bows of the capital, bent double, flourishing. Small ones. Heads down for a moment. The way you'd bow to a neighbour at a funeral.

The king bowed back. To every one of them. All the way up the lake road. Kestra watched him do it, and felt something in her throat that she hadn't felt since she was nineteen.

At the top of the road, Hale and the thin secretary, Odette, were standing together. Odette had a basket. She held it up to the king as he passed, without a word. He took it. Inside, Kestra saw, on top of what looked like enough bread and cheese and cold fish for a week, there was a napkin.

It was lace.

The king looked at the lace napkin for a long moment. Then he looked down at Odette, from the saddle.

"Thank you," he said. "For the bedding."

Odette's composure broke. Just for a moment. Then she was herself again, and curtseyed, and the king rode on.

***

Garrow got a hammer.

Kestra didn't see the king decide to do it. He was just suddenly reaching back into the leather roll behind his saddle, as they came into the market square, and taking something out, and leaning down from the horse toward the dray. Garrow, walking beside the oxen, looked up.

The king held it out. A hammer. Small, old, the handle dark and smooth with use, the head worn bright.

"My father's," he said. "From Brakewater. I've carried it fifty years." He held it out further. "Keep your thumb out of the way. You'll want it later."

Garrow took it as if it were made of glass. He couldn't speak. He walked on beside the dray holding the hammer in both hands against his chest, staring straight ahead, with tears running down his sooty face into his beard, and didn't wipe them away once, all the way across the square.

***

At the south gate, there were five girls.

Kestra had known there would be. She'd seen them on the hill road, coming down at a run, as the king's horse turned into the square. They were standing in a row beside the gate, in the frost, with their guide behind them, very straight, and the big veteran, Bram, behind her. They'd come down to see the king go. Everyone had. But they weren't looking at the king.

They were looking at the dray. At the black stone in the straw, with the frost in its letters.

The one with the pink and violet hair, Althea, was very pale. Kestra watched her look at the stone, and watched her eyes go along the letters, one by one, slowly, the way you read something in your own handwriting. They didn't slide off. They stayed. Her lips moved.

Kestra had forgotten. *Almost nobody born in this world.* These five hadn't been born in this world.

Althea's hand went to her mouth.

Beside her, the silver-haired one, Gigichi, read it too. And the one with the notebook, Higu, and the little lavender one with the creature on her head. Kestra watched each of their faces do the same thing, one after another, as the dray creaked past them through the south gate. And she didn't know what the stone said. Not really. Only the five words the girl had read out on the shingle at sunset. But she watched four of the summoned read it, at the south gate of Wrenhaven in the first frost, and saw what it did to them, and that was enough. She'd write *that* down. Feet first. Then hands. Then faces.

The fifth girl wasn't looking at the stone.

She was looking at Kestra.

The small one with the yellow heart in her hair. PenguChi. She had the penguin in her arms. She was standing at the end of the row, very still, with her golden eyes on Kestra's face, and when Kestra's horse came level with her, she said, in her calm, slow voice that did things to rooms:

"Again?"

Kestra looked down at her.

"Every day," she said. "At dawn. Until somebody tells you to stop."

"Will you come back?"

Kestra didn't answer straight away. She thought about it. Honestly. She'd been thinking about it for eleven nights, on a step, in the cold.

"If I'm told to," she said.

PenguChi looked at her a moment longer. "And if you're not?"

"Then perhaps anyway," said Kestra.

The penguin, in PenguChi's arms, leaned out as she passed, and put its beak very briefly on Kestra's boot in the stirrup. Kestra looked down at it. She didn't say anything. She didn't move her foot.

Then they were through the gate, and onto the south road, and Wrenhaven was behind them.

***

They camped that night at an inn at the edge of the lake country. The king took the hayloft again. Kestra didn't argue.

He lay on his back in the hay with his hands behind his head, the way he had on the way north, and looked at the rafters. Kestra sat with her back against a beam and her sword across her knees, the way she had. And when she was sure he was asleep, she took out the black book, and turned to the page she'd started on the north road, at the top of which she'd written only *The Hero of the Redmarch*, and nothing else, because she hadn't known what to write.

She knew now.

She wrote by the light of the lantern, slowly. Feet first. Then hands. Then where he stood. Then the face, last, because faces lied.

***

*The Hero of the Redmarch.*

*He fishes. Every night. I sat on his step eleven nights; he fished on nine. He caught something on four. He didn't seem to mind the other five.*

*He says "Morning." Nothing else, at dawn. Eleven times.*

*He grows pumpkins. A great many. He showed me, on the fourth day, without being asked. He was proud of them. He is right to be.*

*The hens hate him. They love the king. He minds.*

*He works a bellows too fast. He strikes exactly where he is told. The king says he has never had a better striker.*

*He asked a town for help. In the square, in front of everyone. The mayor told me it was the first time in eight years. The mayor had to go and look at the reeds afterward.*

*He took a stone from every child who brought one. On one knee. Every one. He said thank you to every one. He kept them all. They are on their way to the Redmarch now, in a sack, to be laid round the foot of a grave.*

*He cut his own sword in half, holding a chisel in his bare hands while the king struck. When it parted, he walked to the end of his jetty and sat there for an hour. His daughter went out and sat with him. Two hundred people watched and nobody said a word.*

*He cut letters nobody here can read into a stone, all day, for a woman who is dead. He went over one of them twice.*

*He laughed twice. Both times at the king.*

*Five of the summoned watch the lake road from a window every night. He knows. He says nothing to them. They say nothing to him. The little one on the hard path says they are waiting for him to come up it. I believe her.*

*I felt nothing from him. Not once. Eleven days. Standing beside him was like standing at the edge of his lake at night, when the water is so still you cannot tell where it starts. The king's pressure, which has been in every room I have stood in for twelve years, went quiet beside him. As if out of courtesy.*

*He is a plain man in a plain coat. I looked at his face every day for eleven days and I cannot tell you what it looks like.*

*He is going to be the second Ascendant alive. I heard it on the jetty, through the dark. I should not have been listening. I was.*

*He is a fisherman.*

***

She looked at it for a long time, by the lantern.

Then, at the bottom of the page, she wrote one more line. She thought about Oswin, at dinner, a year ago, going red and spluttering. She thought about Jared laughing into his soup, and Milli saying *what man?*, and the cat girl watching her, the way the cat girl always watched, as if she were deciding something. She thought about what she'd said, so certainly, with her spoon in her hand.

*The songs are better than the man. They always are.*

She wrote:

**The songs are worse than the man.**

And underlined it. Once. Not twice. Kestra never underlined anything twice.

***

"Read it to me," said the king, in the dark.

Kestra looked up. He hadn't moved. He was still lying on his back in the hay with his hands behind his head and his eyes closed. She'd been sure he was asleep.

"Majesty."

"I've read every page of yours for twelve years," said the king. "I've never once asked you to read one to me." He didn't open his eyes. "Read me that one."

So she did. Sitting against the beam in the hayloft of an inn at the edge of the lake country, by lantern light, in her flat, quiet voice, she read him the whole page, from *The Hero of the Redmarch* to the line at the bottom with its single underline.

When she'd finished, the king didn't say anything for a long time.

"*He minds*," he said at last. "About the hens."

"He does."

"Good." Something that might have been a laugh. Then quiet again. Then: "That's an honest book, Kestra."

"I told you I'd write what I saw."

"You did." The hay rustled as he turned onto his side, away from the lantern. "Oswin is going to be unbearable."

Kestra closed the book.

"I know," she said.

***

They reached the crossroads at Hethe four days later, at noon, in a thin cold rain.

The brewer's dray had kept up with them all the way, slow and creaking, with Garrow walking beside the oxen and his father's hammer, that was the king's father's hammer, tucked into his belt where he could touch it. At the crossroads, the south road went on to Solmere. The east road went to the Redmarch.

They stopped. The dray turned east.

Kestra watched it go. The oxen leaning into the yokes. The four militiamen walking alongside. Garrow at the front, with his hand on the side of the dray. And in the straw on the back of it, under a tarpaulin now against the rain, the long black shape with the spike, going east, to a grave a month away, where five people who'd carried a sword half a day across the red were waiting, though they didn't know it yet, to carry this the rest of the way.

The king sat his horse at the crossroads in the rain and watched it until it went over the hill and out of sight.

Then he turned south, toward the capital, and the Council, and a crown he'd left on a cushion in his rooms nearly a month ago without a second thought.

And on the south road, in the rain, after a mile or two, Kestra heard him begin to hum. An old river song, from a town called Brakewater. Under his breath, the way he'd hummed it at a village forge on the way north, and every night on the shingle by the lake, with a fisherman humming it back.

She didn't write that down. She didn't need to.

She'd remember.
