The tally was on the inside of the back cover of the fifth book, where nobody looked.

Malcolm had kept it since the frozen gravel bar. Since the morning on the Wren, two winters ago, when Petra leaned on Azac's post with a third of her fifth and he'd written *Hold* and then stopped where the backup went and written *Then* instead, and it had held, and the window had come up. He'd started counting that day. Every *Then* he'd ever written: on a ward, a blade, a strap, a bar, a boot. A stroke for every one he wrote. A ring round the stroke for every one that held.

Four hundred and twelve strokes. Two hundred and seventy-one rings.

Sixty-six in a hundred.

It wasn't bad. He'd have been pleased with it, on Earth, at anything. Most things in his life had gone about sixty-six in a hundred, and he'd been the one at the back of the class with a list for the other thirty-four. But Maren had said, in the first winter, to Thea, and Thea had said it in the channel, and it had gone into Malcolm's second book in capitals and never come out: *Candle is when it works every time. Ten out of ten. Not most. Every.*

Sixty-six wasn't ten out of ten. Sixty-six was a Spark who'd been a Spark for a year and seven months and was ninety-seven per cent of the way to something he couldn't do.

The thing he didn't like was this.

It had moved for watching. Ninety-four to ninety-five on the wall, watching Ossie run the race back the other way and put his flat hand on Drew's back. Ninety-five to ninety-six reading Hyvred's post about the seam, sitting on the mounting block with his book shut on his knee. Ninety-six to ninety-seven at seven in the morning the week before, in the Bell's kitchen with a ladle in his hand, reading *Xolalotl · Tier 2: Candle* and Chris's *34*, and not doing anything at all.

It hadn't moved once, since the winter, for anything he'd written.

He'd written a great deal. He did it every night: round the town after the lamps were lit, the way Azac did the long-room door and Hild did the wall. The postern bar. The north-gate hinges, which Agnes said were the worst in the kingdom and she should know, she'd made them. The rope on the tower stair that Hyvred held going up in the dark. The strap on Kei's satchel, which Kei had asked him not to and he'd done anyway. Azac's rim, every week, where the old *Hold*s had worn silver and needed going over. Bee's chalk box, once, as a joke, and she'd drawn the dog with it and the dog had stood on the slates so hard the slate cracked, and Bee had looked at him and said *don't do that again* in the voice she used for Tiger.

Sixty-six in a hundred. Every night. And the bar sat at ninety-seven like a stone in a shoe.

He wrote it down, on the mounting block, on the fourth morning, with the race going on at the bottom of the road and nobody coming down out of the pass to stand at the end of it.

*IF it moves when I watch THEN it's not about me.*

He looked at that for a long time.

*Backup: it's about me.*

"Why do you write a backup?" said Petra.

They were on the wall walk above the postern after the dawn class, the two of them, which was rare. Petra didn't talk to him much. She talked to Xav, and to Hild, and to Rui Xiang in a sort of sign language of half-sentences that Malcolm had never learned; and she watched the rest of them the way she watched the ditch, from a little way off, as if she were counting. She'd never thanked him for the sixth fish. She'd never thanked anybody for anything; she'd told him once it was a thing she was working on, and he'd written it down.

"Because the first thing fails," said Malcolm.

"Does it?"

"Thirty-four in a hundred." He'd brought the book. He showed her the back cover. She looked at the strokes and the rings for a long time with her head on one side.

"Then the backup's for the thirty-four," said Petra.

"No," said Malcolm. "It's for me."

She looked at him.

"I don't write them any more," he said. "Not on the thing. Not since the bar on the Wren. I write *Then* and stop where the backup would go and leave it empty, the way Pryce said. *A backup is something you do because you don't believe the first thing.*" He shut the book. "So I stopped. On paper. It still fails thirty-four in a hundred. So it's not the paper."

Petra was quiet a long time.

"When I was twenty-two," she said, "I stood in a ditch on the Redmarch for a day and a night, and a man I'd never met stood in front of me, and it held." She looked up the pass. "Afterwards, every time I stood in front of anything, I stood in front of it as if I'd be the one who wasn't enough. Ten years. I was very good. People said so. It held most of the time." She turned her head. "It's in your feet. Not your hands. The not believing. You can stop saying it and your feet still know."

"I write with my hands."

"Then it's in your hands," said Petra. "Ask somebody who reads."

*IF there's an* unless *in every* hold *THEN every hold fails when the unless happens.*

*IF the unless doesn't say what THEN it's anything.*

He wrote it on the mounting block in the morning with his breakfast going cold on the stone beside him.

That was it. That was the thirty-four. He'd never once written a backup on Azac's shield since the Wren; he'd been very proud of that. And all the time, under every word, in a hand he didn't know he had, he'd been writing *unless* and not finishing it. *Hold, unless*. Unless anything. Unless the rain. Unless the dark. Unless he'd got it wrong, which he always assumed he had. A *hold* that would give way for anything at all wasn't a hold. It was a door with no latch. It held till the first draught.

So: don't write it.

*IF I don't write the unless THEN it holds every time.*

He didn't write a backup under that one. He was very careful not to.

***

He tried it on the postern bar.

It was the obvious one. He did it every night; Old Pruett let him, the way Pruett let the Watch lean on his wall and Bee draw on his door, with a long-suffering grunt. A great square bar of black oak with iron at the ends, older than Pruett, which made it older than most things in Thornwall. He went down after the lamps were lit, at eleven, with the ink and the pen, and knelt on the cobbles, and wrote *Hold* on it.

And while he wrote it, he thought, as hard as he'd ever thought anything: *not unless. Not unless anything. Just hold*.

He felt it fight him.

That was the strange thing. He'd never felt a word fight him before. The *Hold* went on to the wood the way it always did, and he felt the little catch of it taking, the thing every enchanter felt, like a hook setting; and then under it, in his own hand, he felt the other word try to come. Small. Patient. The way it must have come every time, four hundred and twelve times, without him noticing. *Unless*. And he pushed it down. He held the pen hard and thought *no* and pushed.

The bar split.

It went with a noise like a musket shot in the dark: the whole length of it, end to end, straight down the grain, and the two halves fell off their irons on to the cobbles, one either side of him, and lay there.

His nose was bleeding. He hadn't noticed it start.

Lamps came on up and down the street. A window went up. Somebody shouted for the Watch. And Old Pruett came out of the gatehouse door in his nightshirt with a lamp in one hand and a cudgel in the other, and stood over the two halves of his bar, and over Malcolm kneeling between them with a pen in his hand and blood on his lip, and looked at the lot for a long time.

"Who broke my bar?" said Old Pruett.

Xav was on the wall walk above them. Malcolm didn't know how long he'd been there. He was leaning on the parapet with his arms folded, the way he leaned on everything, watching.

"I did," said Malcolm.

"With a *pen*?"

"Yes."

Pruett looked at the halves. He looked at the pen. Then, after a long time, he bent down, very slowly, because his back was old, and picked up the nearer half, and turned it over, and looked at the split. It was clean as an axe cut.

"Huh," said Old Pruett. He tucked the half under his arm. "That was my grandfather's."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Get Agnes up and get me another one before it's light." He went back in. At the door he stopped, without turning round. "Forty years I've had that gate. Nobody's broke it. Not the call, even. The call just had me lift it." The door shut.

Xav came down the steps.

He didn't say anything. He sat down on the bottom step and looked at the split bar and at Malcolm, and after a while he took a handkerchief out of his coat, which was the first time Malcolm had ever known Xav carry a handkerchief, and held it out. Not close. At arm's length. Where Malcolm could take it or not.

Malcolm took it. He held it under his nose.

"You pushed," said Xav.

"Yes."

"You don't push," said Xav. "You never have. Not since school. You write the list and you wait for the world to be as bad as the list says." He looked at the bar. "What were you pushing?"

"The unless."

Xav was quiet.

"It's mine," said Malcolm. "That's the thing. I can't not write it. It's in my hand. Thea says. I tried to write *Hold* without it and it came anyway, and I pushed it down, and the word broke the bar because there were two of them on it fighting." He took the handkerchief away and looked at the blood on it. "I'm a pessimist, Xav. I can't stop being one by not writing it down. I've been not writing it down for two years. It just writes itself underneath."

Xav looked at him for a long time.

"Then finish it," he said.

He sat on the roof of the Bell all the next day.

Not all of it. Agnes got him up at one in the morning, swearing, and they got the new bar out of the back of the forge, oak she'd been seasoning for a door, and Agnes put the irons on it and he didn't write anything on it at all, and Pruett said it was green and would warp and was the worst bar he'd ever had, and set it in the gate himself. Then Malcolm went up the ladder to the Bell's roof, where the five of them had sat in the warm wind the night they decided to stay, and sat on the ridge with his book.

He opened it at the page.

It was in the third book, near the back. The night on the roof, after the forge. The list: *IF we go back to Duskhollow*, *IF we stay*, the backups the same length both ways. And under it, on a line on its own at the very bottom of the page, in the dark, by feel, the thing Xav had said on this roof, looking down at the shed where Drew and Tiger were. No IF. No THEN. No backup. Two words, a little crooked, because he hadn't been able to see the page.

*Not twice.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he got the fifth book out and turned back through the channel, the bits he copied out every night in his small square hand because he liked having them on paper. A year of it. Going back to the winter. To the count, and the thaw, and the eight coming down. And all down the margins, every few pages, his own lines, the ones he put up in the channel without thinking about them, a joke, a habit, the way you say *bless you*.

*IF eight THEN thirty-one. IF thirty-one THEN Thea reads it. Backup: we're here.*

*IF it opens in a month THEN they come down in a month. Backup: we're here.*

*IF Gabe is a blade THEN Harl will want to hang him on a nail by the back door. Backup: there's room.*

*Backup: we're here.*

*Backup: we're here.*

He'd written it eleven times. He counted.

He sat on the ridge of the Bell's roof with the two books open on his knees and the town going on below him, the smoke from Agnes's forge, and Bee on the gatehouse roof, and Tiger's feet on the swept road, and Hyvred on the tower with the coat round her shoulders, and he understood something that he would have understood a year ago if he'd been anybody else.

He'd never been able to finish the unless. Because the unless was everything. *Hold, unless anything*. The rain, the dark, being wrong. You couldn't build on that. It was a whole world of worst cases, a hundred pages of them, and it was true, all of it could happen, and his hand knew it and wrote it under every word.

But there was one he knew wouldn't.

Not hoped. He didn't hope; he wasn't built for it. *Knew*. The way he knew the porridge took twenty minutes and Agnes's hinges were the worst in the kingdom. They weren't going. Xav had said it on this roof. *I'm not doing it twice*. They'd drifted from Declan once, all of them, from nobody's fault, from there always being another morning; and Xav had looked down at Group 5 on a shed roof and said *not twice*, and Malcolm had written it down by feel in the dark, and they'd stayed. All winter. All spring. Every one of them. Every night in the long room, every dawn on the wall. That wasn't a hope. It was a fact. He'd counted it. He counted it every hour.

*Backup: we're here*. He'd been writing it for a year. He'd thought it was a joke.

It was the end of the sentence.

He wrote it in the fifth book, on the mounting block, at dusk, in capitals.

*HOLD. UNLESS WE'VE GONE.*

And under that, where the backup would go, because he was who he was and he couldn't not:

*We haven't.*

He tried it on the new bar first. Old Pruett watched him, in his nightshirt, with the cudgel.

*Hold*, he wrote. And this time, when he felt the other word come up under it in his own hand, small and patient, he didn't push. He let it come. *Unless*. And then he finished it. Not on the wood; under it, in the place Thea said the word was, the underneath of the hold. *Unless we've gone*.

He felt it take. Not like a hook. Like a rope going tight. Like something at the other end of a line that had been slack for two years, and now wasn't; and the weight on the other end of it was all of them. Old Pruett in his nightshirt. Agnes asleep over the forge. Azac in the long-room door. Xav on the wall. The whole town with its lamps lit in every window, every night, since the autumn, for nobody coming.

He sat back on his heels. His nose wasn't bleeding.

"Well?" said Pruett.

"It'll hold," said Malcolm.

"How do you know?"

He thought about it.

"Because it's not about the bar," he said.

Pruett looked at him for a long time. Then he grunted, and went in.

He did nine.

Nine days, nine things, the way he'd have tested anything. He wrote them in the book, a stroke for each, and he didn't put the ring round until the thing had held through something.

The bar. It held through Brannoch's riders coming in at a canter at two in the morning and Pruett heaving on it. One.

Azac's rim. He went over every *Hold* on it, all the way round, two hundred and something, a night's work, and finished every one. *Unless we've gone*. Azac took it out on the north wall the next morning for Hild, who put her flat hand on it with her whole Shaped wall behind the hand, the wall she'd held across the arch all through the siege, and leaned. Azac went back one pace. The rim didn't. Hild took her hand off it and looked at the letters and said, "Hm." Two.

The tower rope. Hyvred went up it in the dark with her eyes shut, the way she went up when the sight was bad. Three.

The strap on Kei's satchel. Kei asked him not to. He did it anyway. Kei ran with it, from the Bell to the north gate and back, with every flask in it clinking, because Hisui said he was being *stupid in a way that tasted interesting*, and the strap didn't so much as creak. Four.

Agnes's hinges. Five. She said they were still the worst in the kingdom. They didn't squeak.

Bee's chalk box. Six. She drew the dog, on the slates, and it stood, and the slate didn't crack this time, and she looked at it and then at him and said "*oh*," very quietly.

The Bell's north-ward door, where the sick slept. Seven.

The lid of the porridge pot. That one was a joke. It held. Eight.

The ninth was Tiger's boots.

Tiger brought them to him himself, on the eighth night, at the long-room table, and put them down in front of him without saying anything, the way Tiger said most things now. He hadn't asked. The ring went round the ninth stroke.

He looked at the window that night. He didn't need to. He knew.

Ninety-nine. Nine out of nine.

He didn't post it. He didn't want Gabe in capitals. He shut it and lay on his pallet in the long room in the dark with Azac snoring in the door and thought about the tenth.

He didn't know what it would be. That was all right. He'd find out when it came. He'd written a hundred pages of worst cases about every morning of his life, and he lay there and found, very slowly, the way you'd find a step in the dark, that he didn't want to write one about tomorrow.

They came on the tenth morning.

Hild heard them first, the way she always did, at the first grey: *like people coming home from market*. And the town came up on to the wall, the way it always did, in coats over nightshirts, and the Watch with their lamps, and Bee on the gatehouse roof drawing the dog. And there they were, across the road, a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, in the grey, facing the town. Forty of them. The miller's widow in her shawl. Sergeant Dunmore with his coat buttoned wrong and his ten in a row. Ossie at the front. Hands open.

Drew stopped at the postern on his way out.

He didn't say anything. He never did, on these mornings; he'd go down the steps and out under the arch and along the swept road at a walk, and nobody talked to him, because what he was doing was hard and talking made it harder. But this morning he stopped inside the arch, where Malcolm was standing with the book, and looked at him. Then he got the pen out, and knelt, and wrote round the welt, small. *Hold*. And let the other word come up under it, the way it always would. And finished it. Drew put the foot down and lifted the other one, and Malcolm did that too, and Drew put it down, and stood on both, and went out under the arch without a word.

Ten.

Malcolm went up on the wall and counted it.

He always did. He'd counted it every dawn since the count, the way he'd counted the children into the granary with Corvin and the schoolmaster checking him; it was the one thing he was for, in the end, the man with the book who knew how many. Hild. Corvin. Brannoch. Fenwick. The Watch, nineteen. Pruett at the postern. Agnes. Olwen. Group 5, three, and Drew on the road. Group 6, five with him. Petra. The town.

One short.

He counted again. He knew who it was before he'd finished. There was a boy who came up on the wall every morning the ring came, and stood at the far end by the corner tower on his own, with his chin on the parapet, because he couldn't see over it. Seven years old. The miller's widow's youngest. Corin. Nobody had told him to stand there and nobody had told him not to. He came up and stood and looked at his mother across a quarter of a mile of road, and when she walked home at dusk he went home to his aunt's, and nobody ever said anything to him about it, because what would you say.

He wasn't on the wall.

Malcolm looked down.

Drew was on the bridge already, in the middle, turned round with his back to the forty and his face to the town, feet under him, the way he always stood. And in the ditch beside the swept road, below the level of the wall walk, where the grey was still thick, something small was running. It must have gone out under the arch behind Drew while Malcolm was on his knees with the pen. Nobody watches the ground behind a man going out to do something hard.

Malcolm opened his mouth to shout and didn't. Shouting wouldn't help. Nobody could get there; it was a quarter of a mile. And Tiger, at the well, hadn't seen; Tiger was crouched at the mark with his eyes on the road, waiting for the light.

The boy came up out of the ditch at the near end of the bridge, on his hands and knees on the stone. He got up. He went past Drew.

Drew didn't turn. Malcolm saw him not turn. He saw the whole of Drew's back go stiff, the way it had gone stiff on the long-room bench the day Kei splinted his knuckles; and saw him stand there, with his face to the town and his hands open at his sides, and not turn round, because that was the rule. That was the whole of it. Back to them. Every time. And a seven-year-old boy walking past him on the stone, to the far end of the bridge, thirty paces, on his own, in the grey, with his hands at his sides, and stopping at the last stone, a pace from the ring.

In front of the miller's widow.

"Mam," he said.

Malcolm heard it. Everybody on the wall heard it. Calvin's *Earshot* couldn't have carried it better. It was very small and very clear in the cold and it came up the whole quarter mile of the swept road to the wall as if the road had been built to bring it.

She didn't look down.

She stood with her shawl round her and her face patient and a little puzzled, the face of a woman who's walked to the end of the lane and can't remember why, and looked at the town. Over his head. The way they all looked at the town.

And then the forty took a step.

All of them. At once. Forward, the one pace, off the road and on to the bridge, the first time in all the mornings since the spring that any of them but Ossie had set foot on it. Like a tide coming in over a sill. And the boy was in front of his mother, and then he wasn't in front of anybody; he was in among them, in the front of the ring, with the miller's widow on one side of him and Ossie on the other and Sergeant Dunmore's ten closing up behind, and they were all still looking at the town, and they took another step.

*Come*. Thea had read it in every ring in the valley, the oldest hand in the leaves. It was all *come*; Drew had said it on the bridge in the spring. *It doesn't have the word for anything else*. A boy had walked up to it on his own and said *Mam*, and it had heard somebody come, and it was doing the only thing it knew.

Drew turned round.

He broke the rule. Malcolm saw him do it, and knew what it cost. Drew turned his back on the town and his face to the forty, and went down the bridge into them, three strides, and put his arms in among the coats and the shawls and came out with the boy, and turned again, with the boy against his chest, and had nowhere to go.

They came on.

Not fast. They didn't hurry; they never had. They came the way they'd come into the long room in the autumn, smiling, with their hands up and open and flat: forty people walking into a man on a bridge. The first hands found him. Ossie's. Dunmore's. The miller's widow's, flat on his shoulder, on the arm with her own son in it. And they pushed.

Not hard. They never pushed hard. Malcolm had been pushed once, in the long room, by Fenna who did the shirts, smiling, and he'd gone backwards three paces on his heels and sat down. That was one pair of hands. This was forty. Pressing, all together, patiently, the way a crowd presses at a gate. And the bridge was two carts wide and the parapet was at Drew's hip, and on the other side of the parapet was the millstream, in spate with the last of the snow-water, white and loud and four feet deep over stones.

Drew set.

*Post*. Malcolm saw it go into him, from the wall: the knees, and then the hips, and then the whole of him going still in a way a man doesn't go still, the way an oak post goes still when you lean a cart on it. He didn't put his hands up. He had the boy in them. He just stood, on the old stone, in the middle of forty pairs of open hands, and was a post.

And on a pair of boots on the old stone of the millstream bridge, round the welt, small, the words took the weight.

Malcolm felt it. A quarter of a mile off, on the wall walk, with his book in his hand. Not a sound. The other thing. The rope going tight. The line in his hand with a pike on the other end of it, a big one, the biggest he'd ever had on anything, forty people's weight coming on to two rings of letters he'd written kneeling on the cobbles ten minutes before. And under the *Hold*, in his own hand, the other word, finished. *Unless we've gone*.

They hadn't. They were all up here. Every one of them. On the wall.

It held.

The stone under Drew's boots cracked. Malcolm heard that one; everybody did. A flat hard crack like ice going on a lake, right across the bridge from one parapet to the other, under Drew's heels. And Drew went back one inch. Malcolm saw him go. One inch, and stopped, and stood, with his eyes shut and the boy's face in his shoulder and forty hands on him, and didn't go over.

Then Tiger came.

He'd seen. Somebody on the wall had shouted at last, and he'd gone. *Bolt*, the sound of it, the slap of a man's feet on cobbles going faster than a man's feet should, and then the arch, and then the swept road, and Tiger going down it at full stretch with his head down and his arms going, faster than Malcolm had ever seen him go, all of himself in it, toward a bridge with forty people on it and Drew in the middle.

He didn't slow. He didn't need to.

Three paces off the ring, at full *Bolt*, he turned.

In a pace. The way he'd turned the first morning the ring came down, the thing nobody's knees could do. The whole run went into him and rang; Malcolm heard it from the wall, a note like a struck bell, low; and Tiger was going back up the road the way he'd come, at full stretch, with his face over his shoulder.

The hands came off Drew.

All of them. At once. Malcolm had read it in Kei's post in the spring and never quite believed it: *the thing that moved them tasted Tiger turn and let go of the shoulder*. It let go. The forty stood on the bridge with their hands up and open in the air where Drew had been, as if they'd forgotten why they'd lifted them. And then, together, the way they'd come, they took one step back. And another. Off the stone. On to the road. A pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, where they always stood.

Facing the town.

Except one.

Ossie had turned his head. Not toward the town. Toward the boy, in Drew's arms, with his face in Drew's shoulder. Hyvred said afterwards, in the channel, that she saw it go along the seam: under the face, the other face, pressed flat, pushing up against the top of the join where it was loose, the way it had pushed since the race. Ossie looking at a boy he'd probably seen every day of his life in Thornwall, at the mill, at the well. Looking at him. And then the seam held, and the face was the face, and Ossie looked at the town again.

Drew came down the bridge. He didn't run. He carried the boy back up the swept road on his hip at a walk, and Tiger came back down it at a walk to meet him, and turned, and walked beside them, not touching, and the forty stood at the end of the bridge and watched them go and didn't move at all.

The boy didn't speak all morning.

He sat on the long-room bench at the Bell with a bowl of porridge on his knees that he didn't eat, and Drew beside him, and the women from the kitchen coming out every half hour to look at him and go back in. Nobody asked him anything. His aunt came, and sat on his other side, and cried, and he let her. He looked at the door.

Malcolm sat at the end of the bench.

Not near. He didn't touch people. He'd put his hands on somebody once, in the autumn, in the long room, because Drew had shouted his name: Fenna who did the shirts, leaning on Azac's ward, and she'd turned round in his arms smiling and pushed him over. He hadn't since. He sat at the end of the bench with his book shut on his knee and the boy two places along, the way Gabe sat with Rachael, which he'd watched Gabe do on the *Widow* for a hundred miles and never understood: near enough that you knew he was there, and not so near you had to do anything about it.

About noon the boy said, "She didn't look."

Nobody said anything.

"She didn't look at me," said the boy.

"No," said Malcolm.

He hadn't meant to. It came out. He was the one with the worst cases; he couldn't say *she will*. He didn't know that. He'd never known anything that wasn't true, and he couldn't start now, on a bench, to a seven-year-old.

The boy looked at him.

"Ossie did," said Malcolm. "A hair. Hyvred saw. He looked at you." He was quiet. "So somebody in there knows you're out here. And somebody out here—" He looked at the door. At the street. *IF she didn't look THEN she can't yet.*

*Unless we've gone.*
