There was a second line on the list.

He saw it in the grey before dawn, coming down the ladder in his socks with the stove to see to: the scrap of paper on its nail beside the drawing, under the sword on Garrow's pegs, her side out, the way she'd hung it on midwinter night. One line at the top, in her careful square hand, with a line drawn through it. *Come home.*

And under it, new, not crossed through:

*Tell me where you found it.*

He stood at the foot of the ladder and looked at it for a long time.

He hadn't heard her come down in the night. He always heard her; he'd heard her on that fourth rung every night of her life. But she'd written it, and hung the list back on its nail, and gone back up, and he'd slept through all of it, the way he'd slept through very little in thirty-seven years. He thought that probably meant something about the winter. He didn't think about what.

He did the stove. He put the kettle on, the cabin's old one, from the back of the cupboard, which made two notes too, as if it had learned them from the other. He stood with his back to the warm iron and his hands round a cup he hadn't drunk from yet and looked at the list.

He knew what *it* was. She hadn't needed to write it.

***

He'd read Gabe's post on the step, a fortnight ago, in the dark.

He'd been sitting there the way he sat every night, with his tea, looking at the lamp on the end of the jetty that wasn't his to light any more, not till the thaw; and the channel had opened in front of his face at twenty to one in the morning, the way it did when something came in that the window thought he'd want. Gabe. *the black sword. astra and the headstone. it's folded.* And then the rest. Fifteen folds. Kharvos five. Swords in museums behind glass, on the other side of a world he hadn't seen in thirty-seven years. *somebody from home made it.*

He'd read it three times. Then he'd read Thea's *how sure* and Gabe's *as sure as the heat*, and Malcolm, and Cursiffe, and Xav asking if it was still waiting, and Gabe saying he didn't know, it was at the lake. And Calvin, at the end, *rule four*, and Deston telling him off.

He hadn't written anything. He never had.

He'd sat on the step until his tea went cold, and then a while after that, and thought about a hill.

He hadn't thought about it properly in years. You didn't. It was a thing that had happened to a boy, a long way off, in a war that was over; and the boy had carried what he found out of it for eight years and swung it at everything that came over the red, and then stood it in the ground over the only woman he'd ever love, and walked away, and been glad. He'd told the story a handful of times in his life. To the king, at the forge, the autumn the king came; to Gabe and Deston and Xav on this jetty in the second spring; to Thea on the shingle, the day she read the ticks; and once, very short, to Alea, when she was small, as a thing that had happened in a bedtime story, which was the only way he'd ever been able to tell her anything true.

He'd never told it whole. He'd never once needed to. He'd found a sword. He'd brought it home.

And now a boy he'd sung with in a hot classroom in Singapore, who'd spent three months at a forge in Kingsferry barely touching a piece of a star, had put his hand on the thing a second time in his head and done the sums on an anvil, and said: *somebody from home*.

Somebody from home had stood on that hill.

***

She came down at first light with her hair wet from the bucket and Astra on her back, and saw him looking at the list, and didn't say anything. She got her porridge. She sat at the table and ate it with her ears forward and her tail round the leg of the stool, the way she'd sat at that table every morning of her life, and didn't look at him once.

He drank his tea. It was cold. He drank it anyway.

"Thea told you," he said.

"Thea told me what Gabe wrote." She didn't look up. "She reads it out. When I ask." She put her spoon down. "She said you wouldn't have told me."

"No."

"Why not?"

He thought about it. He'd always thought about her questions; she'd been asking them since she could talk, and the first time he'd given her an answer he hadn't thought about she'd known, at four, and looked at him with her mother's eyes until he gave her the real one.

"Because it's a story about a boy being stupid," he said, "a long way off, a long time ago. And I didn't think it mattered where."

"It matters now," said Alea. "Somebody from home made it." She looked at him at last. "Somebody like you."

He didn't say anything.

"I've carried half of it for more than two years," she said. "On my back. Every day. It's my sword. I sleep with it. I won the Fledgling with it, twice." She put her hand up, without seeming to notice, and touched the hilt over her shoulder: the old saddle leather, and the sharkskin over it where Gabe had redone the wrap. "And I don't know where it came from. I know *Kharvos*. I know *a hill*. I know you were nineteen and you cut that leather off your saddle. That's all I know." Her ears were very still. "That's not enough any more. Not now somebody made it."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

"So tell me."

"Now?"

"Not *now*." She stood up, and took her bowl to the bucket, and rinsed it, and put it on the shelf. "On the ice. Properly. All of it, from the start." She turned round. "You always tell things properly on the ice."

***

The lake had been frozen a month, solid from the reeds at the north end to the far shore, thick enough to take a cart. Bram had a hole out by the reed-beds with two stools at it that nobody else used, and Higu had a hole near the jetty where she sat with her notebook and caught nothing and wrote down that she'd caught nothing; and Ars had his own, a long way out, past the end of the jetty in the middle of the bay, where the water under the ice was deepest and the big slow fish lay on the bottom in the cold like sleeping dogs.

They walked out to it together. It was a white morning, very still, with the sky low and grey and the far shore gone soft in the haze, and the town on its hill behind them with its smoke going straight up. The ice boomed once under them, a long way off, the way it did on cold mornings; Alea didn't even look round.

He cleared the hole with the axe. She sat down on the second stool, the one she'd sat on since she was six, with her coat round her knees and Astra across them. He baited the hook, the bent one, the one he'd had since before she was born, that a big quiet man in Kingsferry was making him a new one of out of a star and thought he didn't know about; and dropped it in.

He didn't start for a while. She didn't make him.

"I was nineteen," he said at last.

***

"You weren't one yet.

"I'd been at Cinderhold four years by then. I came at fifteen with a borrowed sword and the worst temper on the Redmarch, and by nineteen I had a better sword and the same temper. I was Tempered, near Honed. I'd been in the line two summers. I was good at it. I knew I was good at it, which was most of the problem.

"I had you, and your mother. You were born in the infirmary, at the end of the summer before. You had ears like a lynx and no hair and you screamed at everybody but her. I'd sit on the cot at night with you on my chest, because it was the only place you'd sleep, and your mother would lie on the other cot with her cold hands under her chin and laugh at me. *The terror of the Redmarch*, she'd say. *Look at him. He daren't breathe.*

"I was happy. I want you to know that. It's important to the rest of it. I was happier than I'd been in either life.

"And one morning, at the start of the summer, I walked out of the east gate and kept going."

He stopped.

Alea didn't say anything. She sat on the stool with Astra across her knees and looked at the hole in the ice, where his line went down into the dark water, and waited, the way he'd taught her to wait for a fish.

"I don't know why," he said. "That's the truth. I've had eighteen years to think of a reason and I haven't got one. I've told everyone I was angry, and I was; I was angry at everything; but I'd been angry for four years and never walked off before. I think..." He turned the rod a little in his hands. "I think I'd been happy for a year, and I didn't know what to do with it. I'd been angry so long it was the shape I was. And then there was you, and her, and I was happy, and it didn't fit. And one morning I woke up and couldn't sit still in it."

"So you walked into Kharvos," said Alea.

"So I walked into Kharvos." He breathed out. "Like an idiot. With a sword and a horse and a bag of bread, past the last of the markers, past the line, past where anybody had ever been and come back. I told nobody. Not the sergeant, not her. I left before the bell." He was quiet. "She didn't know where I was for most of a summer. That's the worst thing I ever did to her, until the last thing. I want you to know that too."

The line twitched. He didn't strike. It was nothing; the cold, or a small fish nosing at the bait and thinking better of it.

"Go on," said Alea.

***

"It's black.

"That's the first thing. Everybody knows that, everybody who's stood on the wall and looked east. The red's red, and then past the red there's the black: black ground, black hills, black rock coming up through it like bones. You can see the start of it from the wall on a clear day. I'd seen it every day for four years. I thought it would be like the red, only black.

"It isn't. The red's a battlefield. It's dead because people made it dead, and the dead are in it; you can feel them, Yan says, in the soles of your feet. The black isn't like that. Nothing died there. Nothing was ever *there* to die. It's black the way the inside of a cave is black, as if the light had never got round to it.

"I rode for nine days. The horse didn't like it. Things didn't come near us; I'd see them, at night, a long way off, the hunters and the other things, standing on the black hills watching, and they didn't come. I didn't know why. I thought it was me. I was nineteen; I thought everything was me.

"On the tenth day the horse lay down and died. I don't know what of. It just lay down on the black ground in the morning and didn't get up. I sat by it all day. And then, because I was nineteen and stupid and the saddle was the army's and I'd have to pay for it, I picked the saddle up and carried it."

Alea made a sound. Not a laugh, quite.

"Three days," he said. "I carried an army saddle three days across Kharvos on my back, because I'd have to pay for it. And then I put it down on a rock, and cut one long strip off the skirt with my knife, I don't know why, I think I wanted something of it, and left the rest there. And walked."

"The strip," said Alea.

"The strip." He looked at her hand on the hilt. "Yes."

***

"I walked for weeks. I don't know how many. I'd stopped counting. I ate the bread and then I ate the things I could catch, which weren't many, and then I didn't eat much. I wasn't frightened. I should have been. I think I'd walked so far past being frightened that I'd come out the other side into something else. I just kept going east. There wasn't anything to go back for, I'd decided, by then. I'd decided I'd ruined it. You and her. I'd left without a word, and she'd never forgive me, and I'd keep walking till I fell over, and that was what I deserved.

"Nineteen," he said. "You'll forgive me for nineteen, I hope. I've had trouble."

"Go on, Dad."

"And then there was a hill."

He was looking at the hole in the ice. He could see it. He hadn't let himself see it in years, not properly; he'd told it to the king and to the boys and to Thea as a thing he knew, *a hill, on its own, like a marker*. But he'd never let himself stand on it again. Now he did.

"It was on its own," he said. "That's the thing about it. Kharvos is all hills, black hills one behind the other like waves, as far as you can see. And then there was this one, on its own, in the middle of a flat black plain, with nothing round it for miles. Not big. You could walk up it in a quarter of an hour. Round, like a bowl turned over.

"And it was green."

Alea looked up.

"Not much. Not like here. A pale green, the colour of grass that's grown under a stone. But it was the only green I'd seen in a month. The whole plain was black, and in the middle of it there was a round green hill, and I thought I'd gone mad. I thought I'd finally starved enough to see things. I walked toward it for most of a day, and it didn't go away.

"There were no birds. I noticed that after. There'd been no birds anywhere in Kharvos, but on the hill I noticed it, because grass ought to have birds. It was very quiet. And the grass..."

He stopped again.

The line had gone still in the water. Alea was very still on her stool.

"The grass grew in rings," he said slowly. "I'd forgotten that. I'd forgotten it until now. I thought it was the wind. Rings of the pale green, all the way round the hill, one inside the other, with black between them, like the rings on a cut tree, going up to the top. And lines across them. Straight lines, from the top down to the bottom, like the spokes of a wheel. I thought it was the wind. Or water. I didn't think anything. I was nineteen and starving and I walked up through them to the top the way you'd walk up a flight of stairs."

He was quiet a long time.

He'd seen that shape since. Not on a hill. On a rise a mile beyond the east wall of Cinderhold, ten years ago, burned into the ground in a ring thirty paces across with spokes and marks like a ruler's, where a general with a bull's horns had stood in the middle of it with a healer in his hands, pouring her out like a cup. He'd walked onto that rise the summer before last with Yan and knelt in the middle of it where nothing had grown in ten years. And he'd never once, in all that time, put the two together. A ring of burned ground on the red, and a ring of green on a hill a month east. They'd been in different drawers.

They weren't.

"Dad," said Alea.

"I'm all right."

"You've gone the colour of the ice."

"I'm all right," he said again, and found that he was, more or less; and that he was frightened, a little, sitting on a stool on a lake with a rod in his hand, the way he hadn't been at nineteen on the hill. "I've just remembered something I never let myself look at. Give me a minute."

She gave him a minute. Then she said, quietly, "What was at the top?"

***

"It was."

He meant the sword. She knew he did.

"Standing in the ground at the very top, in the middle of the rings, where the spokes all met. Point down. Black. Bigger than any sword I'd ever seen, bigger than I'd known you could make a sword, as long as I was tall and as broad as my hand at the hilt. No wrapping on the grip; it had rotted away, or there'd never been any. Just the bare iron, black, with the rain run off it for so long that there were tracks worn in the ground round it where the water went.

"No bones. No camp. Nothing. I walked all the way round it twice. Nobody had been there for a very long time. Hundreds of years, maybe. It looked like somebody had stood it there over something, and gone away, and never come back."

"And you pulled it out."

"I pulled it out." He breathed. "It took me all day. I couldn't lift it; I couldn't even shift it. I stood on the hill with both hands round the bare iron and pulled till I couldn't feel my arms, and it didn't move, and I sat down, and got up, and pulled again. I didn't think about why it wouldn't come. I didn't think anything. I'd walked a month to get there and it was the only thing in Kharvos that was any use and I was going to have it." He was quiet. "And at sunset it came."

"How?"

"All at once. The way a nail comes out of old wood. Nothing, nothing, nothing, and then it gives." He looked at the hole. "And the ground breathed out."

Alea didn't move.

"That's the only way I can say it. There was a hole where it had been, about the width of my hand, going down into the top of the hill. And air came up out of it. Cold. Very cold, colder than anything that summer, colder than the black at night. Like opening a cellar door in a house that's been shut up for years. It came up out of the hole for about as long as it takes to breathe out, and stopped." He turned the rod over in his hands. "And I didn't look in it. I never looked in it. I stood there with the sword fallen over on the grass beside me and my arms shaking and that cold on my face, and I didn't look, because I was tired and I was nineteen and I'd got what I came for. I sat down on the hill and slept. And in the morning I tied that strip of leather round the grip so I could hold it, and dragged it down the hill through the rings, and started west."

"You didn't go back up."

"No."

"You didn't look."

"No."

"Not once? In eighteen years?"

"Not once," he said. "Not even in my head. Not till this morning."

***

"It took me most of the summer to get home.

"I dragged it. I couldn't carry it at first; I put the point on the ground and walked backwards, pulling it by the grip, and it cut a groove behind me across Kharvos like a plough. Then after a week I could lift the point. After a month I could carry it on my shoulder for a mile before I had to put it down. Things watched, the whole way. They stood on the hills at night and watched me go by with it and didn't come near. I thought it was me, again. It wasn't. I know that now. It was the sword. They could never stand to touch it. All through the war, after; they'd take hold of it to pull it off me and their hands would smoke."

"It burns them," said Alea. She had her hand on the hilt. "I know. I've seen."

"It burns them," he said. "I never knew why. I never asked." He was quiet. "I was very good at not asking things, at nineteen. I kept it up for a long time."

He looked out over the white bay, at the haze over the far shore.

"I came back over the red at the very end of the summer. The sentries on the east wall saw me coming a long way off, a man walking backwards dragging a black thing; they thought I was one of *them*. They had the bows up. And then somebody said my name, and somebody else ran for the gate.

"Your mother was in the infirmary door when I came in. With you on her hip.

"She didn't say anything. She looked at me. At my face, and my arms, and the sword. And then she gave you to the orderly beside her, very carefully, and walked across the yard to me, and hit me in the chest with both fists as hard as she could, and then she sat down in the dust of the yard and cried."

Alea was looking at the ice.

"I sat down with her," he said. "I didn't know what else to do. We sat in the middle of the yard at Cinderhold with half the garrison watching, and she cried, and I held her hands, which were cold, they were always cold, and the sword lay in the dust beside us. And when she'd finished, she wiped her face and looked at it. At the sword. For a long time.

"And she said, *what is it?*

"And I said, *a sword*. Because I was nineteen.

"And she said, *whose?*

"And I said, *mine*."

He stopped.

"And what did she say?" said Alea, very quietly.

"She said *it was somebody's*." He turned the rod in his hands. "And then she got up, and went and got you back off the orderly, and went inside. And didn't speak to me for three days. And on the fourth day she came and stitched my hands, because they'd gone bad from the dragging, and she didn't say anything about it ever again. But she never touched it. Eight years. She never once laid a finger on that sword."

***

They sat on the ice for a long time.

A fish came, eventually. A perch, small and striped and cold, that took the bait as if it had been thinking about it all morning. He let it run a little and leaned, the way he'd shown Bram, and it was on; and he brought it up through the hole into the grey light and unhooked it with his thumb, and looked at it, and put it back down the hole. Alea watched it go.

"*It was somebody's*," she said.

"Yes."

"Mum knew."

"I think she felt it. The way she felt where it hurt, in a man on a cot. She never said what." He wound the line in. "I thought she meant it'd been some soldier's. Some Kharvos lord's, that he'd lost. I didn't think about it."

"Somebody from home," said Alea.

"Somebody from home." He set the rod across his knees. "Who folded it fifteen times when nobody here folds more than seven. Who stood it on a hill, in the middle of a ring, over something, and walked away from it, and never came back." He was quiet. "I did the same thing. Didn't I. Twenty years later. I stood it in the ground over the thing I loved most in either world, and walked away from it, and only ever went back to stand there once a year, on my own, at the end of the summer."

Alea didn't say anything. Her ears had gone flat, a little. Diwa's ears. When something hurt.

"I don't know what was under the hill," he said. "I never looked. Maybe it was a grave. Maybe whoever made it stood it over somebody, the way I did; maybe that's what *it was somebody's* means. Or maybe it was that cold." He looked at the hole in the ice. "I've felt that cold since. Under her grave. The same cold, Yan says, the thing the headstone holds down. I never put them together either. A hole on a hill, and a hole under a cairn. Different drawers."

"Are they?"

"I don't know." He looked at her. "That's the honest answer, Alea. I don't know. I didn't look, and I've never known, and I've told the king and the boys and Thea that I never found out, and that was true, and I let it be true for eighteen years because it was easier." He put his hand flat on the rod. "And now Gabe's done sums on an anvil, and you've written it on the list, and I've remembered the rings. And I can't let it be true any more."

***

She took the pencil out of her coat on the way back across the ice. Higu's pencil; she'd kept it since the summer, he'd found out on midwinter night, and it lived in her coat now the way the hook lived in his rod.

She didn't say anything. She walked beside him back to the jetty with Astra on her back and the pencil in her hand, and up the thirty-four planks, white with frost, and in at the cabin door; and took the list off its nail, and sat down at the table, and drew a line through the second line. Straight, from one end to the other. *Tell me where you found it.*

Then she looked at it for a moment.

"That's two," she said.

"That's two."

She didn't hang it back up straight away. She sat with it on the table in front of her and the pencil over it, not writing, the way she'd sat at Oakes's table on the edge of the red in the summer with the blank back of it in front of her; and he stood by the stove and didn't say anything, because it wasn't his.

"Thea wants to read it," she said at last. "Astra. She said so, in the channel, after Gabe. She asked me on the shingle if she could." She looked up. "I said I'd ask you."

"It's yours."

"I know it's mine." She put her hand on the hilt over her shoulder. "I'm asking you anyway."

He looked at her. At his daughter, nineteen, Honed, with the black half of a sword nobody had made on her back, that a boy of nineteen had pulled out of a hill without looking in the hole; asking him, at the table, the way you ask.

"Yes," he said. "Let her read it."

She nodded, once, the way he did. Then she hung the list back on its nail, her side out, two lines on it now, both crossed through, and a lot of room underneath; and went out to bring the axe in off the ice.

***

He sat on the step that night, late, with his tea.

The lamp was lit on the end of the jetty. She'd lit it at dusk, the way she had every dusk since the end of the autumn, and stood with her hand on the post until she knew it would stay lit, and come in. He didn't go and look at it. It wasn't his till the thaw.

Behind him, through the open door, the new sword lay on its pegs over the drawing and the list, long and grey-silver and patient, with frost laced through it. It didn't ask him anything. The other one had never asked him anything either, in eight years on his back; he'd never given it the chance. *Mine*, he'd said, in the dust of the yard, to a woman with cold hands who knew better. *It was somebody's*.

Somebody from home.

He thought about them. He'd never once let himself, before. Somebody who'd stood in the same stone hall, maybe, three hundred years ago, or six hundred, under the same painted suns, and been told the same things by somebody; and hadn't gone home. Or had. And had known, the way a smith knows, from a programme or a book or a museum on the other side of the world, that you could fold steel fifteen times if you were patient enough and good enough and didn't mind losing half of it in the fire. And had done it. Here. With nobody to strike for them, maybe, or with somebody. And put something in it that burned the things that came out of the black. And carried it a month east, the way he'd carried it a summer west. And stood it in the ground on a hill in the middle of a ring, over something, and walked away.

And a boy had come along, a long time after, and pulled it out without looking, because it was the only thing in Kharvos that was any use.

He drank his tea. It was cold.

He drank it anyway, and sat on the step a long time after that, looking at a small yellow light on the end of a jetty that his daughter had lit, on a lake under ice, with a cold breath on his face from a hole on a hill a month east that he'd never once looked into; and thought that he'd have to, now. Not with his feet. He wasn't going back. But with whatever it was in him that had stopped asking things at nineteen, and kept it up for eighteen years, and was very tired of it.

*It was somebody's*.

"I know," he said, out loud, to nobody. To a woman who'd been dead ten years and had been right about it all along. "I know it was. I'm sorry."

The lamp burned on the end of the jetty. The ice boomed once, a long way off.

He went in.
