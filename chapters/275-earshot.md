The wind brought him things. It always had.

That was the first thing Calvin had ever noticed about it, before the weather, before the scorecard on the wardrobe door, before *Carry us*. In the first month, at the top of a salt barge's mast on the Kingsferry quay with his legs wrapped round the crosstrees and nothing to do but sit, he'd heard two men arguing on the far bank. Not shouting. Arguing, in ordinary voices, about the price of eels, a quarter of a mile off across the river, with the whole noise of the quay in between. Every word. He'd sat up there for an hour listening to them like a play, and come down and told the others, and Dennis had said *the wind was blowing your way, genius, that's just sound*.

It was just sound. He knew that. Sound carried on water, and further with the wind behind it; any bargeman knew that. He'd just never thought much about it, the way you don't think much about the floor.

And then, in the autumn, there'd been the fog.

He'd been on the *Widow* coming home, the morning the river went white above the town and Haskett couldn't see the watergate stones and they'd all known, without anyone saying it, that a barge that missed the channel at the bend would go on the stones and open like an egg. And Calvin had stood at the bow and asked the wind for a hole. Not asked it to turn; he'd learned that much, the first spring, with his hands shaking on the ratlines. Asked it where it was already going, and gone with it, and pushed a little at the end. And it had torn a hole in the fog the size of a cart, for a heartbeat, and in the heartbeat Haskett had seen the stones.

That wasn't the thing he remembered. Everybody remembered the hole.

What he remembered was that, in the heartbeat the hole was open, he'd heard the city. All of it. Through the hole, like a door opening in a room full of people: the bells at the customs house, and a dog, and a woman calling a child in, and two carters swearing on the bridge, and a hammer at Harl's, and somebody laughing; all at once, clear as if he were standing among them, from half a mile off. And then the hole closed and it was gone, and Dennis was glamouring the quay lamps, and nobody had time to ask him why he'd gone white.

He hadn't told anyone. He'd thought about it all winter.

***

He'd thought about it on the masts, mostly. The *Widow* was on her winter mooring at the north quay, with the river frozen bank to bank above the city and black and quick below it, the way it went every year, and nobody needed a wind mage for anything; so he sat up her mast most afternoons in his coat, with the kettle tied to the crosstrees so it could see, and listened.

That was all he did, at first. Listened. With the wind from wherever it was coming from, the way you'd sit with your back to a fire. And he found out, over a month, that the wind did bring him things, if he let it. Not all the time. Only when it was coming toward him: a south wind brought him the south wharf, the forges and the net-menders and the chandlers' row, and a north wind brought him the frozen river above the city, the children skating, a dog barking on the ice. And the further off a thing was, the more it came in pieces. Words without the ones in between. A laugh without the joke.

It was like having very good ears in a crowd. That was all. It wasn't magic. He'd have said so, if anybody had asked him.

Nobody asked him. He was up a mast. That was the point of a mast.

***

The others were busy with rule four.

They'd been busy since the night at Harl's, with the number in soapstone on the anvil and Gabe standing beside it with his mouth open saying *somebody from home*. Deston had gone to Emrys the next morning and asked him, carefully, everything he was allowed to answer, and come back with less than he'd hoped and more than he'd expected. Dennis had started buying gossip in the market the way other people bought onions. Gabe was at the back of Harl's every night after midnight, cutting letters into a star a stroke at a time, and between strokes, Harl said, he'd started asking every old smith who came in out of the snow whether they'd ever seen a sword folded more than seven times. None of them had.

And Calvin asked the river.

He was good at it. He'd been asking the river things since the first month: who the Hero was, where he'd gone, whether he was seven feet tall. He'd had eleven versions of the Hero of the Redmarch by the first winter, and he still had the list. Bargemen knew everything, and told it to anyone who'd sit on a bollard and listen, and Calvin would sit on a bollard and listen to anybody. So he sat on bollards all over the north quay that winter, in the cold, with his kettle, and asked everybody who'd talk to him about a black sword on a hill in Kharvos.

Nobody knew anything.

That was the strange part. They knew everything else. They knew which captain on the river cheated at dice and which customs clerk could be bought and for how much, and they knew the Hero had eyes like coals and killed the general bare-handed. But not one bargeman on the whole of the Wren had ever heard a story about where his sword came from. He just *had* it. In every one of the eleven versions, the Hero of the Redmarch came out of nowhere with a black sword on his back, and nobody had ever wondered.

"Because it's not their story," said Deston, on the roof, when Calvin told him. "It's ours. Theirs starts with the war."

"Somebody's story starts with the sword."

"Somebody's." Deston looked east, the way he did. "Not anybody on this river, I think."

***

He tried it on purpose, in the end, because he couldn't not.

Not the hearing. The other thing. The thing that had happened in the fog, for a heartbeat, when the hole opened and the whole city came through it at once. He'd been thinking about it for two months on the mast, and he'd got as far as a theory, and the theory was this.

*Carry us* had been the first thing. Asking the wind where it was already going, and going with it; that was Tailwind, that was how he'd taken the *Widow* upriver in an afternoon against a current that should have taken a week. With the grain, it cost him nothing. Against it, he got three heartbeats and the shakes and slept through supper.

But a wind going somewhere wasn't only a thing you could ride. It was a thing that brought. Everything a wind passed through, it carried a little of: smoke, and leaves, and the smell of the forges, and sound. *Carry us* sent him where the wind went. What if he turned it round? What if he asked the wind, not to take him, but to bring?

*Bring it.*

He tried it on the mast in the second week of the winter, with a south wind coming across the river from the forges. He shut his eyes, and found the wind the way he always found it, the edge of it, the place where it was already going; and instead of going with it, he held still, and asked it to bring what it was carrying.

It brought everything.

That was the trouble. He'd half known it would; it was what had happened in the fog. The whole south wharf came across the river into his head at once, all of it, every voice and every hammer and every dog and every cart-wheel on every cobble, clear and loud and *right there*, as if he were standing in the middle of the street of forges with his head in a bell. He couldn't pick anything out of it. He couldn't think. He sat on the crosstrees with both hands clamped over his ears, which did nothing, because it wasn't coming in through his ears, and the noise went on and on until he let go of the wind with a jerk like dropping a hot pan.

And then there was nothing. No sound at all. Not even the river. Just a high thin ringing, like a glass somebody had flicked with a fingernail, and the mast swaying under him, and his own heart.

He couldn't hear anything for an hour.

He climbed down the mast very carefully, which took a long time, and walked back to the chandler's on Rope Street through a city with the sound turned off: barrows going by without a rumble, a woman's mouth moving at a door, a dog barking silently at a cat. Rachael was in the kitchen. She took one look at him and sat him on the bench and put her hand flat on the side of his head, without asking, which meant it was bad; and after a while the ringing went down to a whine and he could hear the kettle.

"What did you do?" said Rachael.

"Asked for too much."

"You always do." She took her hand away. "Your ears are fine. It's not your ears. It's the bit behind them." She looked at him the way she looked at a patient who was going to do it again. "Two a day. Like my *closes*. And not the whole of anything. You hear me?"

"I hear you," said Calvin. "Now."

***

He tried it again four days later, with less.

He'd thought about it. He'd thought about it the way Deston would have, in order, sitting in the window of the chandler's with the scorecard on the wardrobe door beside him. The fog had been a heartbeat. The mast had been everything, for as long as he could bear it. Neither of those was any use. What was any use was one thing, out of everything, the way the two men arguing about eels had come to him the first month, one voice out of a whole quay.

And he hadn't asked for that, that first time. He'd just been up a mast, and they'd been downwind of him, and they'd come.

*With the grain*.

He went up the mast with a south wind again, and didn't ask for anything. He just sat. He let the south wharf come across the river the way it always came, in pieces, a word here and a hammer there, the way he'd been letting it come all winter without calling it anything. And then, very gently, the way Gabe touched the star with a file, he leaned toward one of the pieces.

A woman selling chestnuts at the bottom of the forge street. *Hot, hot, two a copper, hot.* She was a long way off. Half a mile, maybe more. He'd heard her every afternoon for a month without listening. Now he listened, and asked the wind, not to bring the wharf, but to bring her; only her; a little more of her than it was already bringing.

*Hot. Hot. Two a copper, mind your fingers, love, hot.*

Clear. Right there. As if she were at the foot of the mast.

And the rest of the wharf stayed where it was, in its pieces, the way it had always been. The forges. The dogs. A cart. They didn't come. He hadn't asked for them.

He sat on the crosstrees with his mouth open, listening to a chestnut seller half a mile off telling a child to mind its fingers.

Then he let go, very gently, before he wanted to; and the chestnut woman went back to being a piece of the wharf; and there was no ringing. Just the river, and the quay, and the wind going past him toward the north.

His hands were shaking. Not much. The way they shook after *Carry us*.

He went down and wrote it on the wardrobe door, under the weather score and the boxed word *allowed*, in chalk. *ONE THING. WITH THE GRAIN. LEAN, DON'T PULL.* And under that, after a minute, because he was Calvin: *I'M CALLING IT.*

***

He did it twice a day for a fortnight, the way Rachael said.

He got better at it, slowly. He found out that it was easier with a tune than with words; a tune held together in the wind the way words didn't, so that a man whistling at the far end of the north quay came to him whole when the same man talking came in rags. He found out that he couldn't do it at all against the wind, not without paying for it in ringing and shakes; but with the wind, even a little, he could lean toward something a mile off and have it, for as long as he could hold it without pressing. He found out that the bit behind his ears got tired the way a muscle did, and that two a day was right, and three was a headache, and that Rachael always knew.

He didn't tell the channel. He told Deston, on the roof, and Deston listened to all of it with his whole face, and at the end said *that's a skill, Cal. That's going to be a skill.*

"It's ears," said Calvin.

"It's a wind mage hearing a chestnut seller half a mile off on purpose." Deston was smiling. "Bro. That's a skill."

His window said ninety-eight. It had said ninety-six in the autumn, after the fog. It moved a point the week he found the chestnut woman, and another the week he found out about tunes. He didn't look at it much. He'd got out of the habit of looking at it, the last year; it had sat in the nineties so long it had stopped feeling like a number and started feeling like weather.

***

The south wind came back on the twenty-second day after midwinter, at dusk, with snow in it.

He was up the *Widow*'s mast because he was always up the *Widow*'s mast at dusk, with the kettle on the crosstrees and his coat buttoned to the chin. The lamps were coming on along the south wharf across the river. The forges were banking down. And he was asking, the way he did every evening now, in the last of the light, with the wind coming toward him: *black sword. Kharvos. A hill.* Not asking anybody; nobody on the south wharf could hear him. Just listening, the way you'd listen for your own name in a crowd. Leaning, gently, toward anything that sounded like it might be.

Nothing. The same as every night. The chestnut woman packing up. Harl's big hammer, twice, then quiet. A dog. Two women arguing about a shawl. A child crying.

And then, a long way off, at the far end of the wharf where the net-menders worked under the last warehouse, a song.

He almost didn't lean toward it. It was so quiet; somebody singing to themselves, under their breath, the way you'd sing to a baby, or to your own hands while they worked. But it was a tune. And tunes held together.

So he leaned.

It came across the river to him through the snow, small and clear, an old woman's voice, cracked at the top and steady at the bottom.

*One hill, and one nail,*
*and one ring round the hill.*
*Two rings, and two hands*
*that hold the nail still.*

He sat very still on the crosstrees.

It wasn't the words. It was the way they came. Every word he'd ever heard in this world came to him the way speech did on Earth: in his own language, as if the people talking had been speaking it all along. The window did it. Deston had worked that out on the first night in Solmere; it was why they could talk to anybody and read nothing. But this came differently. It came with something underneath it, like an echo inside each word, a second set of sounds the words were lying on top of: harder, older, all corners. As if the window were translating as it went, and he could hear it working.

*Three rings, and three nights,*
*keep your back to the cold.*
*Four rings, and four hands,*
*and the nail holds hold.*

The old woman on the far wharf wasn't singing in Aurelan.

He knew it the way you know a wrong note. Every child in Kingsferry could have sung a song about a nail and a hill, if there'd been one; there wasn't. He'd sat on every bollard on the river. He'd have heard it. This was something else, in some other tongue, coming to him across half a mile of black water through a window that had never once, in three years, had to work this hard to tell him what somebody was saying.

*Five rings, and five hands,*
*and they're coming from the west.*
*Pull the nail, little one,*
*and the hill gets no rest.*

The song stopped.

Not finished. Stopped, the way a person stops singing when they realise they've been doing it out loud. He heard her breath, small and cold, half a mile away. He heard her needle go back into the net.

He didn't let go of the wind.

He couldn't have, for a moment. He sat on the crosstrees with his hands locked round the mast and the snow coming past him and the wind coming toward him across the river, carrying an old woman's breath and the click of a netting needle, and he held on to it without pressing, without pulling, leaning toward it the way you'd lean toward a voice in the next room; and he felt the thing behind his ears that had been tired for a fortnight go *quiet*.

Not tired. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when everybody in it stops talking at once and turns to look at the door.

And then something opened. Not in the wind. In him. As if the wind had always been bringing him things, every day of his life, on Earth and here, and he'd just never once stood still enough to take them; and now he was standing still, with a song about a nail in his hands, and he could take whatever it brought, from as far as it had come, and it would come.

He heard the whole of the far end of the wharf. Not all at once, the way the fog had been. In order. The way you'd hear a room if you walked round it. The net-menders packing up under their lamp. The water slapping on the stones under the last warehouse. A cat. Two men on a barge further down, talking about the thaw. And under the lamp, the old woman, alone, with a net across her knees and her needle stopped, breathing.

She was saying something. Very quietly. To nobody.

*Little one*, she was saying, in the other tongue, with the window working under it. *Little one. Fool. Forty years, and you still sing it in the snow.*

His window was open.

*Earshot*.

He started laughing. He couldn't help it. He sat on the top of the *Widow*'s mast in a snowstorm with the kettle tied to the crosstrees and laughed until he nearly fell off, because of course it was called that, of course it was, the window had a sense of humour and it had got it from somebody; and then he stopped laughing, and let go of the wind very gently, the way you'd put down something you'd been carrying a long way; and the old woman went back to being a piece of the far wharf, half a mile off, in the dark.

His hands were shaking. He didn't care.

He didn't put it in the channel. He thought about it, on the way down, rung by rung with his hands still shaking; and didn't. An old woman on the south wharf, singing in a language nobody in Kingsferry spoke, about a hill and a nail and rings. A Kharvos song, it had to be; where else would a song like that come from? And Kingsferry was a war port, and the war had been long, and there were people on this river who still spat when they said *Kharvos*.

Rule three. *Don't show them your whole window*. It had never been about the channel. But it had always been about who could get hurt.

***

He went across the river anyway.

He shouldn't have. He knew that halfway over the bridge, in the snow, with the kettle bumping on his hip and the lamps of the south wharf coming up through the white ahead of him. Deston would say *tomorrow, with Dennis, properly*. Gabe would say *bro, she's an old woman, don't scare her*. Rachael would say his ears weren't fine and he should be in bed.

But he'd heard her say *forty years*. And *fool*. And *little one*. And he was the one who asked things. He'd always been the one who asked things.

The net-menders' row was under the last warehouse at the far end of the south wharf, where the stones went down into the black water: a long low shelter with a roof of old sailcloth and three lamps, and nets hung on poles to dry that would never dry in this. Everybody else had gone. There was one lamp still lit, at the far end, and under it an old woman on a stool with a net across her knees.

She was very small. That was the first thing. Small and bent, in three shawls, with grey hair scraped back under a cap and hands like knotted rope, working the needle in and out of the net without looking at it. A face like a walnut. She could have been any old woman on any quay on the Wren. He'd walked past her a hundred times.

She looked up when he came under the sailcloth. Her eyes were very dark and very sharp.

"We're shut," she said. In Aurelan. Plain river Aurelan, with nothing under it at all.

"I know," said Calvin. "I'm sorry. I'm not buying anything." He stopped a few paces off, the way you'd stop near a dog you didn't know. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He put one on the kettle. "I heard you singing."

The needle stopped.

"Everybody hears me singing," she said, after a moment. "I've sung on this wharf forty years. Nobody minds. It's just an old woman's noise."

"It's not noise to me." He swallowed. "*One hill, and one nail, and one ring round the hill.*"

She didn't move. Her face didn't do anything. But he saw her hands go still on the net, all of them, every knotted finger, the way Rachael's went still when she felt something wrong under them.

"You're one of the summoned," she said.

"Yes."

"The window." She said it as if it were a word she'd heard but never needed. "They say you lot hear every tongue in the world in your own."

"I don't know about every," said Calvin. "But I heard yours."

She looked at him for a long time, under the lamp, in the snow. He couldn't tell what she was thinking. He'd never been able to tell, with old people; they'd had too long to get their faces the way they wanted them.

"What's your name?" she said at last.

"Calvin."

"Calvin." She tried it, like a coin. "I'm Yeva." She looked down at the net, and picked up the needle again, and put it through a mesh, slowly. "Nobody's said that song back to me in forty years, Calvin. Not since my mother."

"Where's it from?"

She didn't answer. She worked the needle. In, and round, and through, and pull.

"Not tonight," she said.

"I didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant. You meant where's it from, and what's the nail, and what's the hill, and what's coming from the west. I can see it all over you." She didn't look up. "You've got a face like a boy who's found a door. I had one too, once." She pulled the knot tight. "Not tonight. It's snowing and I'm old and you've frightened me, and I don't know you."

"I'm sorry," said Calvin. He meant it. "I'm really sorry."

"I know you are." She looked up at him, then, sharp and dark. "Come back in the daylight. Bring somebody who can tell whether an old woman's lying." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You've got one. I've heard about him. The little one with the coins, who glamoured the quay lamps in the fog. They say he knows a lie when he hears it, like a bell."

"Dennis," said Calvin.

"Bring Dennis," said Yeva. "And don't tell anybody else on this river what I sing. Not anybody." She put her needle back into the net. "There are people on this wharf who were on the wall in the long war. They don't know where I'm from. They think I'm from up the Sallow. I'd like them to go on thinking it."

"I won't," said Calvin. "I promise."

She nodded, once, and didn't say anything else; and after a moment he understood that he'd been sent away, and went.

***

He walked back over the bridge in the snow with the kettle on his hip.

Halfway across he stopped, and leaned on the parapet, and looked down at the black water going under, quick and cold, toward the sea; and then back at the south wharf, at the single lamp still lit at the far end under the last warehouse.

He didn't lean. He didn't ask for anything. He'd done his two for the day, and more; Rachael would know.

But the wind was still coming from the south, toward him, across the river, carrying snow. And on it, very faint, without him asking, an old woman's voice. Not singing. Just humming, under her breath, the way you'd hum to your own hands while they worked. The same tune. No words this time.

*Pull the nail, little one.*

He stood on the bridge until the lamp went out, and then a while after that.

Then he went home to the chandler's on Rope Street, and climbed the stair, and found Deston and Gabe and Dennis and Rachael at the kitchen table with their windows open and the soup on, waiting; and sat down, and took the kettle off his belt, and put it on the stove.

"Rule four," he said. "I found somebody on the edge."
