The thread had never been this bright.

Deston walked down the lake road with Gabe beside him and the low part coming in under his own, and felt it in the middle of his chest like a hand pressed flat against his breastbone. Not faded. Not grey. Not the old colourless rope that had hung in a window for thirty summers. Gold. As gold as Gabe's. Thicker, maybe. Running from his chest straight down the packed earth of the road, between the stone wall and the reeds, a quarter of a mile, to the end of a jetty.

To a man with his back to them.

He'd followed it for four months on a roof. Fifteen days up a river. Through a gorge where things came down the cliffs head first. He'd felt it get warmer every mile. He'd thought, in the Narrows, in the chorus, with every thread on the barge lit at once, that it couldn't get any brighter than that.

It was brighter than that now. Every step.

He kept humming. He didn't know how. His voice was shaking. Gabe's was too; he could hear it, the low part wobbling under his like a floor in an earthquake. They didn't stop. They'd never once stopped in the middle of this song, not at thirteen in a rehearsal room with bad carpet, not on a stage, not on a roof in Kingsferry with a gap in it every night for a year and a half.

They came to the place where the third part came in.

They left it.

***

It was a long gap.

Longer than they'd ever left it. They kept walking, both of them, down the lake road, humming their two parts with the hole in the middle of them, the way you'd hold a door open for somebody you could see coming up the path. Deston could feel Gabe beside him, holding it. Could feel, behind them, a few paces back, Xav, walking alone, not humming, holding it too. Could feel, further back, all the others, Groups 4 and 6 and Emrys and Sabine and Petra, coming down the road in a loose line, and every one of them, without knowing the song, holding the gap.

The man on the end of the jetty had gone very still.

Deston had seen it from halfway down the road. The man had been sitting the way Declan used to sit at his desk at three in the morning, hunched forward with his elbows on his knees, the rod held out over the water. And then, when the two parts reached him across the water and the reeds and the shingle, he'd stopped. Not moved. *Stopped.* The way a man stops when he hears his name called in a crowd.

He didn't turn round.

The gap went on. Twenty paces from the end of the road. Fifteen. The cabin on the left now, timber gone silver, with smoke going straight up from the chimney and three hens standing in a row in the yard behind the fence, watching. The shingle. The long low thing under the sailcloth roof that Deston could see now was a forge. The jetty, running out over the dark water on old grey posts, with nets hung along it.

Ten paces.

The gap. Open. A door held so long that your arm starts to shake.

And from the end of the jetty, without turning round, a voice came in.

***

It wasn't Declan's voice.

That was the first thing. Deston would remember it all his life, and that was the first thing. It wasn't his voice. Declan's voice had been light and quick and always too loud, always half laughing, the voice of a boy who sang the top part in a choir in Singapore and got told off every week for drowning out the sopranos. Deston had heard that voice for thirteen years. He'd have known it anywhere.

This wasn't it.

This was a man's voice. Lower. Rougher. Older, much older, the way a voice is older when it's been used for a long time in a lot of cold places. A voice from a different throat, in a different body, born in a cottage on the other side of the world.

It came in exactly where the third part went.

Not near it. Not close. *Exactly*. On the beat, on the note, in the gap they'd held open, without a breath of hesitation, the way a voice comes in when it has sung that part a thousand times and never once forgotten where it goes. And it was too loud.

Declan had always come in a fraction behind the beat. On purpose, so he could pull a face at them across the risers while the choirmaster wasn't looking. This voice came in dead on it. As if somebody, somewhere, on his own, had had a very long time to stop fooling about.

It was much too loud. It was so loud that the hens in the yard scattered. It came out across the water from the end of the jetty and filled the gap and spilled over it and drowned out both of them, the low part and the middle, the way it had drowned out the sopranos every week in a rehearsal room with a piano out of tune.

It was the wrong voice.

It was exactly right.

***

Deston stopped walking.

He didn't decide to. His legs stopped. He stood at the end of the lake road where it ran out onto the shingle, with Gabe beside him, and he kept singing. He didn't know how. He kept singing the middle part, because that was what you did. Because the song wasn't finished, and you never stopped in the middle.

Gabe kept singing too. The low part. Under both of them. His voice gone to pieces, cracked and wet and still, somehow, exactly on the note.

And the third voice, from the end of the jetty, too loud, on top.

Three parts.

For the first time in three years. Six weeks. Thirty-four years. Deston didn't know how to count it anymore. For the first time since a stage in Singapore and a car park after a concert, with a teacher telling Declan to keep it down. Three ordinary voices, and a chord.

He'd always thought he was the weak one. In the middle. On his own, nothing much.

He'd never once been on his own. Not in this song.

***

They finished it.

The whole thing. All the way to the end. Standing on the shingle at the end of a lake road in another world, in the spring morning, with the lake dark and still and the sky in it and the ice drifting white far out in the middle, and Group 1 and a fox girl and a whole town at the top of the road, and ten people behind them who didn't know the song and stood perfectly still. The three of them sang it to the last note, the way you sing it at the end of a concert, holding it, letting it go.

It went out over the lake.

Then it was quiet.

The man on the end of the jetty put down his rod.

***

He did it slowly. He laid it on the boards beside him, very carefully, the way you'd put down something you'd been holding a long time. Then he put his hands on the edge of the jetty, and stood up.

And turned round.

Deston looked at him.

He was a plain man. That was all. A man of about thirty-five, in a plain brown coat over a shirt, with his sleeves pushed up. Not tall, not short. His feet were bare on the grey boards. His face was a face Deston had never seen in his life. It wasn't Declan's face; it couldn't be. It was a stranger's face, a face you'd pass in a market and not look at twice.

Deston looked at it twice. Three times.

It slid. Everybody had said it would. Yan, after the Redmarch. Every letter that had ever come down the river about the fisherman: *a face you couldn't hold on to.* Deston's eyes went to it and slid off and came back and slid off again, like a hand on wet glass.

He found he didn't care. He'd never known Declan by his face. Not really. Not for years. He'd known him by a voice on a call at three in the morning, and a laugh in the middle of a sentence, and a third part in a song, a little too loud. He had all of that. He'd just heard it come across the water.

The man was crying.

He wasn't doing anything about it. He was standing at the end of his jetty in his bare feet with his hands at his sides, looking at the two of them on the shingle, with the tears running down his face, the way a man stands when he's been holding something up for so long that he's forgotten how to put it down, and someone's just taken it out of his hands.

He opened his mouth.

***

"Gabe," he said. "Deston."

In English. His own. Out of his own mouth.

The thread in Deston's chest went so bright that he couldn't see.

***

Gabe went first.

Deston didn't see him go. He felt him go, through the thread between them, the thickest he'd had since he was fifteen. Gabe walked down the shingle and onto the jetty. Not fast. Not slow. Like a wall walking. All the way out along the grey boards, past the nets, to the end, where the man was standing with his bare feet and his wet face.

He stopped in front of him.

They looked at each other. Gabe was bigger. Much bigger. Gabe was bigger than almost everyone. He stood there on the end of the jetty and looked down at a plain man in a brown coat, a stranger, a face he'd never seen, and Deston watched his back, and saw his shoulders.

Then Gabe hit him.

Not hard. In the chest. Flat-handed, the way you'd thump a door to see if anyone was home. Once. The man rocked back a step on his bare heels, and didn't do anything about it, and didn't look away.

"*Sleep*," said Gabe. His voice wasn't a voice anymore. "Sleep, Declan. *Seriously.*"

The man looked at him.

"I listened," he said. Very quietly. "That once."

***

Gabe sat down.

The way he'd sat on the roof in Kingsferry the night Deston told him about the thread. The way he'd sat on the wall at the corner of the square an hour ago when a fox girl said *I'm Alea*. All at once, very heavily, his legs just deciding for him. He sat down on the end of the jetty at the man's bare feet with his blade still on his back and put his face in his big hands and his shoulders started to shake.

The man knelt down.

He knelt on the grey boards beside Gabe, and put his arms round him. Round all of him, which was difficult, because there was a great deal of Gabe. He put his arms round him and his face against the side of Gabe's head and held on. And Gabe turned, without taking his hands from his face, and leaned into him, the whole enormous weight of him, the way a wall leans when the mortar goes.

Deston was on the jetty. He didn't remember walking.

He was standing over the two of them. He didn't know what to do with his hands. The thread in his chest was so bright he couldn't think. He stood there looking down at the back of a stranger's head, and a stranger's arms round Gabe, and a stranger's bare feet on the boards.

The man looked up at him. Over Gabe's shoulder. With his wet face.

"You sang the middle part," he said. "Like always. Holding it together."

"You sang it too loud," said Deston. His voice came out in pieces. "*Like always.*"

"I always do."

"The hens ran away."

"They hate me," said the man. "They've always hated me. They love the king." And something happened at the corner of his mouth. Something Deston had seen ten thousand times, on a call, at three in the morning, when Declan was about to say something stupid and was trying not to laugh before he'd said it. "Trust."

Deston went down on his knees on the boards.

He didn't know which of them he was holding on to. Both. All three. He didn't care.

***

He didn't know how long they stayed there. On the end of the jetty, in a heap, the three of them, on the grey boards, with the lake all round them and the sky in it. He didn't think anybody said anything. He didn't think anybody could.

At some point he felt the thread change.

He didn't have to look for it. It was just *there*, the way it had been there for four months on a roof, and fifteen days up a river, and every step of the lake road. From his own chest. Only it wasn't running north anymore. It wasn't running anywhere. It was right here. It went from his chest to a man kneeling beside him on a jetty with his arms round Gabe, a hand's width away, and it was gold, and thick, and taut, and it wasn't one thread pulled from one end anymore.

It was pulled from both.

He'd thought, on the roof, in the summer, that it was old. That it had faded because it had been there so long with nobody pulling on the other end. That distance didn't fade threads, but time did, and loneliness, and thirty-four years.

It wasn't faded. He could see that now. It had never been faded. It had been the same thread the whole time, the same one he'd had since he was thirteen, gold and thick, running from his chest to his brother's. It had just been pulled so tight, for so long, from only one end, that all the colour had gone out of it.

The colour was back.

***

Xav was standing at the land end of the jetty.

Deston saw him when he could see again. Standing on the shingle where the boards began, alone, a little way from the others. He hadn't come out along the jetty. He'd stopped where it started, with his hands at his sides, and waited. The way Xav always waited. The way Declan had taught him to wait, on a thousand late-night rounds, until everyone else got bored and moved.

He was waiting now.

The man on the end of the jetty lifted his head from Gabe's shoulder and saw him.

He went very still again. The way he'd gone still when the song reached him across the water.

Then he let go of Gabe, gently, and stood up. And walked back along the jetty. Barefoot on the grey boards, past the nets, the whole length of it, with his wet face, to where Xav was standing on the shingle at the end, waiting.

He stopped in front of him.

"Xav," he said.

Xav looked at him for a long time.

Deston watched his face. He'd known Xav for years, the way you know your best friend's other best friend: in lobbies, on calls, at the edges of things, with Declan in the middle. He'd seen Xav's face at the end of close rounds. He'd seen it on the roof at Duskhollow, in the dark, the night Deston told him everything. He'd seen it on the landing stage, when the barge came in.

He'd never seen it do this.

"Okay," said Xav.

His voice was perfectly flat. The voice he used on a call, after a lost round, when everybody else was shouting.

"Okay," he said again. And then, very quietly, in exactly the same voice, the thing he'd always said, every time, after every round they'd ever lost together, at three in the morning, when Declan said *one more*. The thing he'd written in the channel once, the night Hako asked everyone what Declan had been like. Two words.

"Again."

***

The man laughed.

It came out of him short and startled and wet, the way you laugh when somebody's said the exact thing you needed and didn't know you needed. He put his hand on the back of Xav's neck and pulled him in, and Xav let him, standing very stiff on the shingle at first, the way Xav always stood when somebody hugged him, and then not stiff at all.

"I've been waiting three years," said Xav, into his shoulder. Very flat. Very steady. "On a quay. In a yard. On a community server. I'm good at it now. You taught me."

"I know."

"You held banana for a minute and a half."

"I know."

"On Inferno. In the final. You never told anyone why."

"They were always going to come through banana," said the man. "Everyone gets bored. I just got bored last." He held on. "Trust."

Xav made a sound that Deston had never heard him make in all the years he'd known him.

***

The others were on the shingle.

They'd come down off the end of the road, the ten of them who weren't Gabe and Deston and Xav, and stopped there, in a loose line, in silence. Rachael with her hands over her mouth. Dennis beside her, very still, with his head tilted, listening the way he listened. Calvin. Malcolm, with his book held shut against his chest. Azac, with his shield on his back and his face doing something complicated. John. RX, who was there, visible, for once, not hushed, as if he didn't want to miss anything by not being anywhere. Emrys, with his blanket round his shoulders, very pale, looking at the man on the shingle as if he'd seen a ghost walk out of a song. Sabine. Petra, at the back, with her sword in her hand and her face perfectly still.

The man looked past Xav's shoulder at them.

At each of them. One after another. Slowly. The way Group 1 had said, in the channel, before they knew, that the fisherman had looked at them on the market road on the first day: as if he were reading something written on their faces and wanted to get every word. Rachael. Dennis. Calvin. Malcolm. Azac. John. RX.

His eyes stopped on Petra.

Deston saw them stop. Saw his face change. Just for a moment. Something going across it that was very old, and very far away, and had a ditch in it.

He didn't say anything. Not yet.

He looked back at the others.

"Give me a minute," he said. In English. To all of them. His voice was still wrecked. "I need a minute. And then I'll come up." He looked at Rachael, and Dennis, and Calvin, and Malcolm, and John, and Azac, and RX. "All of you. I'll come and see all of you. I promise."

Then something moved at the corner of his mouth again.

"I've got a kettle," he said.

***

Calvin made a noise like somebody had stood on him.

***

Deston opened his window that night.

He did it late, on the step of a cabin by a lake, in the dark, with Gabe and Xav asleep on the floor inside under every blanket the cabin owned, and Gabe snoring like a sawmill, and the hens muttering in the yard. The others were up in the town. Group 1 had come down the road in the afternoon and taken them up, without asking anybody, to Rowan's spare rooms and the beds above the bakery. *Tomorrow*, Thea had said, at the top of the road. *Let them have today.* Out of habit. The way everyone did, now. He hadn't looked at it all day.

He scrolled to the bottom, past everything, to the line most of the thirty-five had forgotten was there.

*Medium: one blade · parted.*

And beside it, where there had been a sealed value for a year and a half, a door with no handle, that rattled when you were near something you couldn't name:

[ Medium: one blade · parted · Declan ]

He looked at it for a long time.

Not *Ice*. Thea had told him, at dusk, when she came down the lake road with a basket of bread and went away again without coming in, that hers said *Ice*. The name he'd told them, at a supper in the winter. *It's me. It's Ice.*

He'd said *Gabe. Deston.* on the end of a jetty. And they'd known exactly who he was. He'd never had to tell them a name at all.

The window had known which one to write.

Deston closed it, and sat on the step in the dark, and listened to the lake, and to his brother snoring, and to his other brother, the one who'd been gone, moving about in the cabin behind him, quietly, putting a kettle on.

The thread in his chest was gold. Both ends.

He didn't need to point anymore.
