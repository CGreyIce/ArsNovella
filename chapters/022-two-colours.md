The first frost came to the Redmarch in the middle of the fourth month, and for one morning the whole of it was white.

Niko stood on the east wall of Cinderhold at dawn and looked at it. The red ground was gone. The grey grass was gone. Aldous's long rows of markers stood up out of a field of perfect white like the masts of sunken ships, and the light came up over the mountains of Kharvos and turned the whole thing pink and gold, and for about a quarter of an hour the Redmarch was the most beautiful place Niko had ever seen.

Then the sun got higher, and the frost melted, and the red came back through it like a bruise rising through skin.

"Huh," said Niko, to nobody.

He went down to breakfast thinking about it. He'd been thinking about things like that for four months: things that were one colour and then another, things that sat on top of each other without mixing, things that only showed what was underneath when the light changed. He suspected he'd be thinking about them for a long time yet.

***

He was still at the bottom of both ladders. He'd made his peace with that.

Mornings were aura. He did them with Rhea, in the yard, with Salty and Wendy: push-ups and sprints and forms with the practice sword, and then the long slow standing, eyes closed, breathing from the bottom of the chest the way Rhea said, waiting for the thing she called *the bruise* to come up on its own. Afternoons were magic. He did those with Aldous and Yan and Maigo, in the chapel with no priest: sitting, breathing, reaching out for the mana that moved in and around everything, slow in the stone, quick in the air, and trying to feel which way it was going.

Everyone else did one or the other. Niko did both, every day, twice as many hours, for half the results.

"You'd be at fifteen by now," Salty told him, at least once a week, "if you just picked one."

"I know," said Niko.

"So pick one."

"No," said Niko pleasantly, and went on doing both.

It wasn't stubbornness. Or it wasn't only stubbornness. It was the thing Hyvred had said, that night in the castle before they'd all split up. She'd gone round the room telling everyone what colour their light was, and when she got to Niko she'd looked at him, frowning, until he'd started to feel he'd done something wrong, and said: *yours is two colours. And they don't mix.*

He'd been thinking about that ever since. Not *you have two colours.* Plenty of things had two colours. *They don't mix.* As if they should. As if mixing were the natural thing, the thing they'd do if something weren't stopping them.

He wanted to know what was stopping them.

***

First, though, he did the obvious thing, because you always do the obvious thing first.

He used them as two.

He'd already found out, in the autumn, that it didn't matter which one he trained: a week of nothing but Rhea's mornings, and both bars went up a point; a week of nothing but Aldous's chapel, and both bars went up a point. So he stopped asking *which* and started asking the other question. If he had two of them, at four percent each, what could he actually do with two?

At Initiate, not much. Nobody could do much. But if he was patient, he could get a little of Rhea's bruise to come up into his forearm, a warmth that made the practice sword sit a fraction easier in his grip. And if he was patient in the chapel, he could draw a breath of mana close enough to feel it on his skin, a coolness, like standing near the mouth of a well. Two small things. So he tried using them both at once: the warmth in his sword arm and the coolness in his other hand, a sword and a spell, the way every story he'd ever loved had promised him he could.

He couldn't. Not at once. When he brought the warmth up, the coolness went out of his other hand like a candle in a draught. When he reached for the coolness, the warmth sank back down out of his arm. One or the other.

He could switch, he found, if he was quick. Warmth, then coolness, then warmth, a heartbeat each. But every time he switched, something in his chest *caught*: a jolt, a stall, like missing a step on a stair in the dark. On the twentieth switch, at the end of a long cold morning in the yard, the catch didn't let go. He sat down hard in the sand with his heart going like a rabbit's, and couldn't get up for a quarter of an hour.

Rhea crouched beside him. "What did you *do?*"

"Changed my mind too many times," said Niko, when he could talk.

He did it again the next day, more carefully, ten switches and no more, and the catch was there every time. Not a pain. A catch. As if each time one of them started, the other had to be stopped first, and stopping it cost something.

That was the part he couldn't let go of. Why should it cost anything to stop? Unless they weren't sitting side by side at all. Unless they were in each other's *way*.

So he watched. That was the thing he was good at. He'd always been good at it: in games, mostly, where he'd spent years learning other people's habits so he could beat them, or play next to them, or both. He watched Rhea in the mornings. He watched Aldous in the afternoons. And slowly, over weeks, he started to see the difference.

When Rhea used aura, she didn't do anything you could see. She was Gleaming-tier, and when she really wanted to show off, a faint sheen came up along the edge of her sword like light on water. But mostly it was *inside*. It went into her body. Her legs, when she sprinted. Her arms and back, when she wrestled Salty. It pooled in her, and gathered, and then went *out* through her: through her hands, through the sword, through her feet into the ground. Aura went in first, and then out.

Aldous was the other way round.

When Aldous used magic, the light didn't start inside him at all. It started *around* him. He'd breathe, and chant, and Niko could almost feel the mana in the air drawing toward him; once, in a sunbeam in the chapel, he saw the dust lean toward Aldous's hands. It gathered at his hands, outside his skin. And then it went *in*, into whatever he was casting at: the lamp-wick that caught, the water that warmed, the wound that closed. Magic came from outside, and went in.

Aura: in, then out. Magic: out, then in.

"They're the same shape," said Niko, out loud, in the middle of supper.

Everyone looked at him.

"What is?" said Wendy.

"Aura and magic." He put down his spoon. "They're the same shape, just backwards. Aura comes up from inside you and goes out through your body. Magic comes in from outside and goes into the thing you're casting at. It's one loop. They're just going round it in opposite directions." He stared at the table. "That's why they don't mix. They're both running, and they're running against each other. That's the catch, when I switch. One of them has to stop dead so the other can go the other way."

Nobody said anything.

"So stop one of them," said Salty, eventually, "and run the other."

"I've been doing that. It works. It's like fighting with one hand tied behind your back, and swapping which hand." Niko turned his cup round on the table. "Or," he said, "turn one of them round."

Maigo's pixel sunglasses slid down his nose. Yan put down his cup. Wendy, who believed nothing, looked at Niko with an expression of deep, reluctant interest.

"Can you do that?" she said.

"No idea," said Niko cheerfully. "But I'd like to find out."

Walking back across the yard to the barracks afterwards, Yan fell into step beside him.

"Mine's two as well," Yan said. "Me and her." He touched the white coils at his throat. "What she takes comes down the cord, and some of it stays in me. That's what moves my window. I'm sure of it now." He walked a few paces. "Maybe yours is like that. Maybe one of them's meant to feed the other."

Niko thought about it.

"Then which one's the snake?" he said.

"I don't know," said Yan. "Find out, and tell me."

***

He tried for three weeks.

He tried in the mornings, standing in the yard, waiting for the bruise of aura to come up through him, and then, at the exact moment it started to rise, reaching out with the other half of himself for the mana in the air. He tried in the afternoons, in the chapel, drawing the mana toward his hands the way Aldous did, and then, at the exact moment it touched his skin, letting the aura come up from underneath to meet it.

Mostly nothing happened.

Twice, something did. The first time, the two of them touched, for less than a heartbeat, right at the centre of his palms, and it felt like two hands pressing together, and then his nose started bleeding so hard he had to sit down. The second time, a week later, they touched for perhaps two heartbeats, and he went dizzy and grey and woke up on the chapel floor with Aldous standing over him and Yan holding a cold cloth to his forehead.

"No," said Aldous.

"I was close."

"You were *unconscious.*" The chaplain's face was very stern, and very frightened. "Whatever you're doing, the body isn't made for it. Not at Initiate. Maybe not at all." He crouched down, slowly. "Niko. Listen to me. Nobody has ever done both. I've read every account. Nobody, ever. People have died trying."

"How?"

Aldous hesitated. "Their hearts stopped."

Niko lay on the chapel floor and looked up at the roof beams and thought about that.

"Okay," he said finally. "Then I'll go slower."

"How slow?"

Niko sat up, carefully, with Yan's hand at his back. The chapel tilted and came level again. "Two, for now," he said. "I can do two. I'll use them as two. Swap, like I've been doing, ten times a morning and no more, until the catch gets smaller. That's safe; Rhea's been watching me do it for a month." He touched his upper lip, where the blood had dried. "And the other thing, the touching, one heartbeat at a time. Once a week. With you there."

Aldous looked at him without blinking.

"Once a month," he said.

"Once a fortnight."

The chaplain was quiet. "With me there," he said at last. "And Yan. And the day your nose bleeds, you stop, whatever you think is about to happen."

"Deal," said Niko.

***

They compared windows that night like always, in the corner of the mess hall. The score of Rhea and Salty's arm-wrestling stood at twenty-six to twenty-four. Salty's latest win had come after a forty-minute stalemate that the whole garrison had turned out to watch.

"Seventeen," said Maigo. "The ground likes the frost. I don't know why. It's like it can breathe easier." He hesitated, which Maigo never did. "And a root answered me. In the yard, under the well-head, where the old ash used to be. I put my hand flat on the ground and I *wanted* it to come up, and it came up. A finger's width. I felt it push my palm." He looked at his hand. "Then Rhea came over and I tried to show her and nothing. I tried six times. Nothing. It only listens when I want something. Not when I'm checking if it'll listen."

"Fourteen," said Salty. "The sword's light for whole sequences now. Not just a few swings." He flexed his hands. "Chris's thing works, but it costs. Every other day, like she said. But yesterday I wasn't worn down at all. I was annoyed at Wendy, and I wasn't thinking about it, and it went light for a whole form." He frowned at the table. "So it's not being tired. It's not pulling. You can't lift a thing that's bolted down, I've been pulling at it for three months. You've got to get under it and find where it's bolted."

"And have you?" said Maigo.

"One," said Salty. "Maybe." He flexed his hands again, pleased. "Rhea says by spring I'll be lifting things that'd break a normal man's back."

"Rhea says," said Wendy.

"Rhea's right," said Rhea, from the next table, without looking round.

"Twelve," said Wendy. "And I can feel where the arrow wants to go before I aim it. Every time, now. Not just sometimes." She frowned at the table. "It's like the arrow already knows, and I'm just agreeing with it. I tested it. Not-aiming, on purpose. Thirty yesterday. Twenty-two in the middle."

"That's better than aiming," said Salty.

"That's better than aiming," Wendy agreed, sounding personally offended by it.

"Thirteen," said Yan. "Two goats this week." Suzu lifted her head at the word, and put it down again.

Niko was last, as always.

"Four," he said. "And four."

"You went up," said Salty. "Both again."

"I did." Niko opened his window and looked at it. Two bars, side by side, creeping up together at exactly the same pace, as if they were holding hands. "They always go up together. Have you noticed? Both on the same day. Never one without the other."

"So?"

"So I think they *want* to," said Niko. "I keep saying it. I think they want to mix. I think something in me already knows how, and I'm just not strong enough yet to let it." He closed the window. "Aldous says go slower. So I'm going slower. Two for now. The swap's down to a little catch, not a fall. And once a fortnight, with Aldous watching, I try the other thing."

"How much slower?"

Niko thought about it.

"Years, maybe," he said. "That's fine. I've got years." He smiled. "We all do, apparently."

***

The parcel came in the fifth month, with a company of soldiers and a sealed letter from the Advisor, and Aldous went white when he opened it.

It was long and awkward and wrapped in old oilcloth, and two of the soldiers had to carry it between them from the wagon to the chapel. Aldous had them set it down on the front bench. Then he read the letter, alone, in the corner, twice. Then he unwrapped the parcel, and Niko, standing in the chapel doorway with the others, heard him draw a long, shaking breath.

Horns.

Two of them, black and ridged and curling back on themselves like a ram's, each one longer than a man's forearm, cracked at the bases where they'd been cut.

"Oh," said Aldous softly. "Oh, I know these."

"What are they?" said Yan.

Aldous didn't answer at first. He was looking at the horns with an expression Niko had never seen on his face before. It wasn't fear exactly, or grief exactly. It was the face of a man seeing something he'd buried come back up out of the ground.

"These belonged to the general," he said at last. "The demon general who led the last invasion. The one who took the healer." He touched one of the horns, very lightly, with two fingers. "I saw them the morning after the battle. On the Redmarch. The general was lying at the foot of the far ridge, and these were... gone. Cut off. And I never knew who'd taken them." He looked at the letter in his other hand. "The Advisor says they're to be hung above the east gate. Where the scouts will see them."

"Who sent them?" said Wendy. "The Advisor?"

"The Advisor sent them on." Aldous folded the letter. "He doesn't say who sent them to him." He was quiet for a moment. "He doesn't need to. There's only one man alive who could have cut these off that head."

Nobody asked who. They'd heard the stories. Bram's version, the eight-foot-tall version, had made its way east in a letter, by way of Thornwall. Oswin's version, the forty-one eyewitnesses version, had come from Group 2. And there was Aldous's own, which he'd never told them in full: a man going into the red alone, in the second night, and the general falling, and the sword left standing over a grave.

"So he's alive," said Salty slowly. "The Hero. And he sent these. On purpose."

"So it seems."

"Why?"

Aldous looked out of the chapel door, east, toward the gate and the red ground beyond it.

"To tell them he's still here," he said.

***

They hung the horns that afternoon, above the east gate of Cinderhold, on two iron spikes the smith hammered into the stone. Salty held the ladder. Rhea climbed it. When she came down, she stood at the bottom with her hands on her hips, looking up, until the smith asked if she wanted the ladder moved.

"I didn't believe it," she said quietly, to nobody. "Any of it. The Hero. The songs. I thought it was a story for recruits." She shook her head. "I believe that."

The scouts came back three nights later.

Niko was on the wall. He'd been on the wall every night since the horns went up. So had everyone. So had half the garrison, though they all pretended they just happened to be there. And when the shapes came out of the dark past the third row of markers, tall and thin and bent forward, moving in their short quick bursts across the frost-pale red, the whole wall went silent.

There were fourteen this time. More than before.

They came forward as they always did, to the edge of bowshot, and stopped. The one in the middle began to lift its arm. Niko watched it start to point, at the north tower, the way it had every time before.

Then its arm stopped.

It had seen the gate.

Niko would never forget what happened next. He'd watched a lot of people react to a lot of things, in games and out of them. He'd never seen anything like this. The pointing figure went absolutely rigid. Then all fourteen of them did, at once, as if a string had been pulled through every one of them. For three heartbeats nothing moved on the Redmarch at all.

Then the line broke.

Not in an orderly retreat. Not in their usual quick bursts. They *ran*: scattering, all of them, in every direction, back across the frost toward the mountains, as fast as Niko had ever seen anything move. Two of them collided in the dark and went down in a tangle and scrambled up again and kept running. Within thirty heartbeats the red ground was empty, and there was nothing out there but the markers standing in their rows and the moon on the frost.

Nobody on the wall said anything.

"They didn't count," said Maigo at last, very softly. "They didn't finish counting."

Yan had his eyes closed. Suzu was wound tight around his throat, but not as tight as before. "They were afraid," he said. "All of them. I could feel it. Even the cold one." He opened his eyes. "It was the most afraid of all."

On the battlements beside Niko, Wendy let out a long breath and lowered her bow. She hadn't drawn it. She'd just been holding it, the way she always did now.

Salty put his arm around her. She didn't shrug it off.

***

Later, much later, when the wall had emptied and the garrison had gone back to bed with a strange light-headed cheerfulness, Niko found them still up there: Salty and Wendy, sitting in an embrasure out of the wind, wrapped in Salty's coat, both of them, looking out at the empty dark.

He didn't go over. He just stood at the top of the stair for a moment.

"You cold?" Salty was saying, quietly.

"I'm always cold now," said Wendy. "Since the scouts. I told Yan." She was quiet a moment. "Not right now, though."

"Good."

"Don't get smug about it."

"I'm not."

"You are. I can hear it."

Salty didn't deny it. He just pulled the coat tighter around both of them. Wendy leaned her head on his shoulder, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"If you go," said Salty eventually, "I go. You said that. In the hall. First day."

"I remember."

"Works the other way too. You know that, right?" He was looking out at the dark, not at her. "Wherever this ends up. Home, or not. If you go, I go. If you stay, I stay."

Wendy didn't answer. Niko had his foot on the top stair when she spoke.

"I didn't believe in anything when we got here," she said. "Not the Advisor, not the way home, not any of it. I'm only here because you are." She paused. "I believe in the horns, though. And I believe in that."

"In what?"

"In you staying," said Wendy. "That one I believe."

Niko went back down the stairs very quietly, and went to bed, and lay in the dark thinking about two things that were different colours and sat on top of each other for a long time, and then, one night, without anyone deciding it, started to mix.
