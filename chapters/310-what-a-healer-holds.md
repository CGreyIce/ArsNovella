The letter came up the hill to the infirmary at noon on the fifth day, in the post-boy's bag, at the top, where it wouldn't get crushed; and Deston knew whose it was from the far end of the long ward, because he could hear the thread on it.

It was faint. Paper didn't hold much. But somebody had written on that paper very carefully, with their whole attention, for somebody, and folded it, and carried it to a post-house with their hand on their coat; and that was enough. A hair of thread came off it, gold, going north. Up the river. A hundred miles. To a lake.

He'd have known that thread anywhere. It was the thickest one he had.

Rachael was at the window at the end of the ward, where she always was now, with Ned Webb's notebook on her knee. She looked up when the post-boy came in. She saw what he was holding. She didn't get up.

The boy brought it to her. She took it, and turned it over, and looked at the outside, *Rachael. The infirmary. Kingsferry*, in the kingdom's hand; and Deston, coming down the ward between the beds, saw her see the other writing through the fold, the straight strange letters that only thirty-five people in the world could read.

She opened it. Her own dose slip fell out into her lap, folded, with her two pencilled words still on it.

She read the letter. Deston watched her read it. He'd known her twelve years and watched her read a great many things, and he'd never once seen her face do what it did now, which was nothing at all, very slowly, all the way down.

She read it twice. Then she held it out to him without looking up.

"Read it," she said. "He says your name."

***

It was English.

He hadn't seen Declan's writing in English since Earth. And this wasn't that hand. It couldn't be; it was a different body, thirty-eight years of a different body, a hand that had held a sword and a rod and a hammer and never once, in all that time, a pen that wrote this. The letters were stiff. Careful. Too upright, the way a man's walk goes when he's in boots he hasn't worn for years. But they were the same letters. A stranger's hand, making the shapes of somebody Deston knew.

*Rae,*

*Yes.*

*All of it. You don't have to make it small. Ask Gabe for anything you haven't got; he has it. Dest has the rest.*

*I'm not coming down the river. They did it on the rise where I could see, so I'd break or come running. If I come, I bring that with me, and they've been watching you to see if I will. You've got people in front of you who aren't what they're waiting for. That's better than me.*

*Tell Dest the other thing can go up too. Both of them. They should have it all at once.*

*D.*

Deston stood in the middle of the long ward with it in his hand.

*D.* Not *A.* Not the fish. He'd seen Declan sign things *A.* for two years, at the lake, on the notes he left on the cabin door. He'd never once seen the *D.*

"He's not coming," said Rachael.

"No."

"Good." She looked out of the window at the customs-house steps. "He's right. I'd have told him not to." She was quiet. "I was going to. I didn't, because I wanted him to." She picked up the dose slip from her lap, and unfolded it, and looked at her own two words, and folded it again, and put it in her apron pocket with the knife. "Don't tell anyone I said that."

"I won't."

"*Both of them*," said Rachael. "*All at once*." She looked up at him. "The Nail, and this."

"Yeah."

"That's a lot for one night."

"He's right about that too," said Deston. "If they get one, they'll want the other. They'll go looking. Better they get them both from us than half of each from somewhere else." He folded the letter along its creases and held it out to her. "Cursiffe's got most of the Nail. Gabe and I have got the rest of yours."

Rachael took the letter. She didn't put it away. She held it in both hands, flat, on top of Ned's notebook.

"Get Gabe," she said. "I want all of it. Not the outline. I've had the outline for two years. I want what he said."

***

They told her in the back room, with the door shut, at the end of the afternoon. Gabe and Deston on the bench and Rachael on the stool across from them, close enough to hear and no closer, with Sabine outside the door and the long ward going on beyond it the way it always went.

They'd never told anybody. Two springs ago, at the lake, three of them had sat with him through the strangest day of his two lives, after the song, after the jetty, after the minute he'd asked for; and at the end of it he'd told them. All of it. The rise. Quietly, with his hands round a bowl, as if he were telling them about a fish. And then he'd asked them not to, and they hadn't. Not Group 4. Not Xav's group. Not anybody. Rachael had worked out the outline for herself, from their faces, the way Rachael worked out everything. They'd never told her the rest.

They told her now. Gabe did most of it. Gabe remembered words; Gabe had always remembered words, from a choir at thirteen, from every song they'd ever sung; and he'd heard Declan tell it once, at the lake, and he gave it back now nearly the way it had been given, in a low voice, with his big hands hanging between his knees.

The rise. An hour's walk beyond the east wall. A low hill. The general on it, a big one, old, with horns like a bull's. And a circle drawn on the ground in something dark. And her, in the middle of it.

*They waited until I was on the wall. They'd lit fires*.

Rachael didn't move.

*It was a trap. Of course it was. A hero who sees that either breaks where he stands, or comes running on his own into whatever you've got waiting. They had the whole host between the wall and the rise*.

*I went through them. I was Shaped then. Tier six. I shouldn't have been able to, and I didn't care. It took all afternoon. An hour's walk, any other day*.

Deston watched her face. It did nothing. It did nothing so hard that he could see the muscles at the corner of her jaw.

Gabe got to the middle of the ring. To what they'd been doing to her. Emptying her out, Declan had said, and something going in instead, something cold; and her, half of her, still there. Still her. Enough to know.

*She asked me to end it*, Gabe said, in Declan's words, very quietly. *Before she stopped being herself. She gave me what she wanted to say to Alea. And then she asked me*.

*And I did*.

Nobody said anything.

Outside the door, in the ward, somebody was coughing, and Brother Tamsin's voice was saying *there, there*, and a cup went down on a table.

"Then the red," said Gabe. "He doesn't remember most of it. He went in Shaped and came out Dominion. Two tiers in a night. He killed the general. With his hands, at the end. The horns." He looked at his own hands. "And in the morning he carried her off the rise and west to the knoll above the old river and buried her himself, because nobody could get near him."

Rachael sat on the stool with the letter flat on her knee.

"*Because a healer holds things*," she said at last.

Gabe looked up.

"That's why her," said Rachael. "That's why a healer. I said it after the Narrows. I didn't know I knew it. They wanted something that would hold what they put in." She looked down at her hands. "And she held it long enough to ask him."

"Rae," said Deston.

"I'm all right." She wasn't. She sat very straight. "I'm going to write it now. While I've got it. In my words, not his. He said I didn't have to make it small, and I'm not going to." She looked at Gabe. "Did I get it all?"

"You got it all," said Gabe.

"Then get out," said Rachael. "Both of you. I'll tell you when it's done."

They got out.

***

Deston went up onto the roof of the infirmary, after. It was flat, at the back, over the drying room, where Brother Tamsin hung the linen; you could get out onto it through the loft window if you didn't mind your shins. He'd found it in the first year. He went up there to listen.

He sat on the lead with his back against the chimney stack and his knees up and the sun going down over the river, and shut his eyes, and let the threads come.

They came the way they always came now: a choir. Thousands of voices, every thread in Kingsferry, every bond between every person and every other, all of them sounding at once. The thick old ones, mothers and children, husbands and wives, forty years of a woman and her nets. The thin new ones, a man and a dog he'd bought at the market that morning. The ones that ran away out of the city along the river and the roads, to people far off; the ones that ran down into the ground, to graves.

He'd learned to hear them separately. It had taken him two years. He could pick one voice out of the choir now and follow it the way you'd follow a single singer in a crowd: Gabe's, to Harl at the forge, gold and steady; Dennis's, to Sabine, a funny crooked one that he'd never understood and had decided not to; Calvin's to the river, which wasn't a person, which nobody had told Calvin you couldn't have a thread to.

And the thirty-five.

He'd had those since the first day. Thirty-four threads, from him, out across the kingdom in seven directions, to people he'd stood in a hall with; some thick, some so thin he had to strain for them. Xav's, east to Thornwall, the oldest after Declan's. Gabe's, three feet away. Thea's, north, bright and fast like a struck string. Yan's, east, with something cold at the far end of it that wasn't Yan. And all the others, every one, going out from the long ward in Kingsferry to wherever they were tonight, the way spokes go out from a hub.

He didn't usually listen to them all at once. It was too much. It was like listening to the whole of a choir with your head inside the organ.

He listened to them now.

***

The channel had been quiet all afternoon. He'd felt it be quiet, the way you feel a held breath. He'd put four lines in it, at the end of the day, after he came down off the roof the first time: *Rae's got his answer. It's yes. And the other thing too. He says both*. And *Cursiffe, it's yours to put up. You've got the most of it. Then Rae*.

And then nobody had written anything. Thirty-three people across the kingdom sitting up and waiting.

At half past nine the Nail went up.

He read it on the roof, in his window, with the sun gone and the river going dark below him. All of it. The purse book, and the nine years, and the tenth, and the forge. *Stay*. The hill. *The boy is Ice*. He'd known all of it; Group 4 had found most of it; but he'd never seen it written down in one place, end to end, in Cursiffe's slow plain voice, and he read it the way you'd read a letter about your own family from a stranger.

And he felt the threads move.

All of them. At once. Thirty-four threads going out from him to thirty-four people, and every one of them, as they read it, *tightening*. Not toward him. Toward a lake. He felt it go through the whole choir like a gust through a field of barley: thirty-four voices all at once turning the same way, north, to one thread that wasn't any of theirs, the thickest one he had.

He'd never felt that before. Not even in the hall. In the hall they'd been seven groups of five, strangers half of them, pulling every way at once.

Tonight every thread he had was pointing at the same man.

He sat on the roof and listened to it with his eyes shut, and something in him went up a step, and he didn't look at it.

***

She'd come out of the back room at a quarter to ten with her eyes dry and her face very white and gone straight to the window at the end of the ward, and sat down, and put it in. He felt her do it; her thread went taut, the way a thread does when the person on the end of it does something with their whole self. He opened the channel on the roof and watched it come.

The threads stopped.

That was what it felt like. Deston had heard every one of them tighten when the Nail went up. When Rachael's went up, they stopped. All thirty-four at once, the whole choir, the way a choir stops at the end of a bar when the conductor's hand closes: not silence, the other thing, the held breath before the next note. He sat on the roof with his eyes shut and listened to thirty-four people across the kingdom stop breathing.

And then, one by one, he felt them turn.

Not north. Not to the lake. To *him*. To Kingsferry. To a girl at a window at the end of a long ward. Thirty-four threads, all at once, coming round from wherever they'd been pointing, the way heads come round in a room when somebody's name is said; and fastening, all of them, on the thread between Deston and Rachael, the one he'd had for twelve years, since she was seventeen and he'd brought a friend to meet her.

He felt it take the weight.

He'd never felt a thread take weight before. Threads didn't; they were just there, the way a song is just there in a room. This one did. Thirty-four people leaning on it at once, from seven towns, and it held them, and it went bright under them, gold, brighter than he'd ever heard it, like a single voice in a choir that's suddenly carrying the whole of the chord.

He opened his eyes.

Down through the loft window, at the far end of the long ward, Rachael was sitting at the window with her hands flat on Ned's notebook, very straight, not moving. He couldn't see her face. He could see her shoulders. They'd come down. An inch. The way a person's shoulders come down when something they've been holding up by themselves for a long time is suddenly being held up by somebody else as well.

She didn't know why. He could see that she didn't.

He did.

Gabe came up onto the roof at eleven, through the loft window, swearing at the window frame, which was too small for him and always had been. He sat down beside Deston against the chimney stack, carefully, the lead creaking under him, and didn't say anything for a while.

Below them the city was going to sleep. The lamps were going out along the waterfront. Down on the customs-house steps the night boats were tying up, and the water slapped the bottom stair, and there was nobody standing anywhere that Deston could see.

He listened, anyway. Threads went down there: boatmen's, to their families up the hill; a woman's, selling eels, to a sister in the south. And at the bottom of the steps, where Calvin had heard the hole in the sound two nights ago, and found a hook in the morning that Dennis couldn't see anything in:

Nothing. Not a gap. Not a hole. Just a place where no thread went, and none came out. You'd never have noticed it unless you knew to listen for it, the way you'd never notice a missing singer in a choir of a thousand unless you'd counted.

He'd counted.

"Is it there?" said Gabe.

"Not now. I don't think so." Deston kept listening. "I don't think I'd hear it if it was. That's the thing. Nothing's got no thread. I can only hear where it isn't."

Gabe looked down at the steps for a long time.

"She asked me to go and get you," he said at last. "Rae. She's done. She's sitting at the window. Sabine's on the door and Calvin's on the stairs and Dennis is in the street with his eyes doing the thing. And Emrys came in at ten and sat down at Maud's table and started burning his name off the back of his hand again, because Ned's grey got past the elbow this afternoon and he wants another go at it tomorrow, and Maud told him he was a fool and then brought him a basin."

"He got some of it out yesterday."

"Some. Not the bit past the seam. And he took a coin of the kid's skin with it at the wrist, where his aim went." Gabe looked at his own hands. "Rae mended it. It answered, she said. It's a real burn. It's trying to be a burn. She said that like it was good news."

"It is good news."

"I know." Gabe was quiet. "That's what's wrong with this year, Dest. A kid's got a burn on his arm the size of a coin and it's good news."

Deston didn't say anything.

"Rule four," said Gabe. "We find out for ourselves. That's what he taught us; that's what the novels taught you and Cal. Fine. We've found out." He looked down at the steps. "So what do we do with it?"

"We don't leave her on her own," said Deston. "Not for a minute. Not at night. Not at the window. Sabine's right; we've been standing behind her for four years. We stand in front now." He turned the thought over. "Emrys learns to burn a word. Cal listens at night. Dennis looks at everything that comes up those steps with *True*. You—" He looked at Gabe.

"I go and stand on the steps," said Gabe.

"You go and stand on the steps."

"With my back to the infirmary." Gabe nodded slowly. "Like he said he wanted to. Like Drew on his bridge." He was quiet. "He can't. So I will."

"And I listen," said Deston. "To all of it. All the time. For the place where there's nothing."

Gabe looked at him.

"You went up a step," he said. "Didn't you. Tonight. I saw your face when you came down for the bread."

Deston opened his window. He hadn't, all evening. He'd felt it go up, on the roof, when the threads all turned, and he hadn't looked at it, because it seemed like the wrong night to be thinking about himself.

***

They went down together, at midnight, through the loft window, Gabe swearing at the frame again.

Rachael was still at the window at the end of the ward. Sabine on her stool by the door with her sword across her knees. Ned asleep on the end bed with his mother asleep in the chair beside him and his right arm on a folded sheet, bandaged at the wrist where Emrys's aim had slipped, and above the bandage, very faint, under the skin, the grey going up past the elbow like a seam.

She didn't turn round when they came. She was looking at the steps.

Deston went and sat on the bench across from her. Near enough. Not too near. Gabe sat beside him.

"It's up," said Rachael. "All of it."

"Yeah."

"I put it in my words. I didn't make it small." She was very still. "Did I get it right? Was that what he said?"

"That was what he said," said Gabe.

She nodded. She looked down at her hands on the notebook. Then she said, so quietly he nearly didn't hear it:

"My shoulders have gone funny."

Deston didn't explain. He thought about it, and decided not to. There'd be a time; it wasn't now. He sat on the bench across from her near enough, and listened, without meaning to, to the thread between them, his and hers, twelve years old. It was still taking the weight. Thirty-four people across a kingdom, in their beds and on their towers and at their windows and on the end of a jetty at a lake, still leaning on it, and not one of them had let go.

"Go to sleep, Rae," said Gabe. "We're here."

"I know you're here." She didn't move. "That's why I can't."

But after a while, sitting at the window with her hands flat on the notebook, she did. And the three of them, and Sabine at the door, and Calvin on the stairs with his face to the wind, and Dennis in the street, stayed exactly where they were until the morning.
