"Your tail," said Sergeant Brask.

Milli was on his back on the flags of the east yard, again, with the sky going round. He'd been on his back on the flags of the east yard eleven times this morning. He knew because Jared was counting, from the wall, out loud, in a voice that was trying very hard not to enjoy it.

"Eleven," said Jared.

"I heard," said Milli, to the sky.

Brask stood over him with the iron-shod staff grounded beside his boot and looked down. He was sixty-one, and grey all over, beard and hair and eyebrows, like a man who'd been left out in the frost, and he moved about as much as a gatepost did. He'd come down to the east yard the week after the Council to see the lad who'd stood, because the king had said *buy the sergeant a drink* down a piece of string from the far end of the kingdom, and Jared had, and Brask had drunk it and said *I'll come and see you work in the morning*. And he'd come. And then he'd stayed, every morning, with his staff, because, he said, the Council sat at noon and there was nothing for a sergeant-at-arms to do before it but polish things.

He'd watched Jared twice. On the third morning he'd watched Milli.

On the fourth he'd picked up his staff and said *come on, then*.

"It goes before you do," said Brask. "Every time. I watch your feet, I've got nothing; you've got good feet, you're quiet, you're the stillest thing I've seen in a yard in forty year. And then the end of your tail goes." He lifted one grey finger and flicked it, a tiny movement, like a man shooing a fly. "Like that. Every time. And then you come."

Milli sat up.

"It doesn't," he said.

"It does, lad."

"I'd know."

"You wouldn't," said Brask, quite kindly. "That's the whole point of a tail. I kept barn cats forty year at my brother's, out at Ludd's Cross, before the Council took me. You put a bit of string on the floor and the cat goes flat and still as a stone, and you'd swear it was asleep, and the end of its tail's going like a bell-rope." He did the finger again. "That's when you lift the string. Just before. Cat comes, string's gone, cat looks at you like you've done something very unfair." He held his hand out. "You look at me like that too."

Milli took the hand and got up.

He was very good at waiting. That was the joke. He'd spent his whole first year here not being able to do it, a cat who couldn't sit still, *again, again, again*, Kestra standing across the yard from him saying one word until he wanted to throw his sword at her; and then one morning he'd waited, and she'd said *better*, and it had changed everything. *Pounce*: stillness turned into speed. The longer he waited, a breath, two, three, the faster he went when he went. He'd won bouts in the Lists with it. He'd beaten Dennis and Niko in the Swiss with it, by waiting.

And now an old sergeant with frost in his eyebrows was putting him on the flags of the east yard with a staff, eleven times in a morning, by watching the end of his tail.

"Again," said Milli.

Brask's eyebrows went up a hair. "You sound like the swordswoman."

"I learned it off her."

"Aye." Brask lifted the staff. "Well. She's not here. You've got me. *Again*."

He opened his window at the wall afterwards, sitting beside Jared with his back against the warm stone and his tail round his own ankle, holding it, which he didn't think anybody would notice.

[ Milli · Tier 1: Drawn · Skills: Pounce · Progress to Tempered: 98% ]

Ninety-eight. It had been ninety-eight since midwinter.

He'd been past ninety-five since the morning of the tenth, when Chris had posted the list at dawn. *Hako, Milli, Jared, Gabe: all past 95*. He'd read it in bed with Hana asleep against his back and thought, *a month*. Two at the outside. He'd been well over a year at Drawn by then and he'd thought he was nearly done with it.

That had been seven months ago.

And since then Jared had gone. In the winter, in the Council room, with Brask's staff on his breastbone, standing between Hana and twelve frightened people. And Gabe had gone, on the river at ice-out, with a floe on his chest. And the others, one at a time, all winter: Calvin, Azac, Aleaka. Chris's count going up in the channel. *Twenty-two of 35. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. Twenty-five*. He'd read every one. He'd been pleased for every one, properly, the way you're pleased for a friend; Aleaka's he'd shouted about, in the corridor, at the top of his voice, so loud a clerk had dropped a ledger.

And every one of them had been a little door shutting.

Not on him. That wasn't fair. Nobody was shutting anything on anybody. It was just that he was the last. Of Group 2's swords, again, the way he'd been the last in the first year. Jared at Tempered and Milli at Drawn, a cat at a door with his nose to the crack waiting for somebody to open it. And it hadn't opened, and it hadn't opened, and he didn't know what was on the other side of it or what it wanted him to do, and every morning he looked at ninety-eight.

"You're doing the thing," said Jared.

"What thing."

"With your tail." Jared didn't look at it. He was looking across the yard at Brask, who was sitting on the mounting block polishing the iron shoe of his staff with a rag, slowly, as if it were the most important thing in the kingdom. "You're holding it."

"I'm not."

"Milli."

Milli let go of his tail. It went, on its own, the moment he let go: a flick at the end, and another. He watched it do it. He'd never watched it before. It was like watching somebody else's hand drum on a table.

"Huh," he said.

***

He tried tying it.

He wasn't proud of that. It was a stupid idea and he'd known it was a stupid idea while he was doing it, in the corridor outside their rooms before dawn, with a strip of Hana's ribbon, tucking the end of his own tail into his belt at the back and knotting it so it couldn't go anywhere. *If it's the tail*, he thought, *then fix the tail*. Rational. One problem, one fix. Chris would have logged it.

Brask watched him walk into the east yard with his tail tied to his belt, and didn't say anything, and lifted the staff.

Milli lasted a count of four.

He'd never been put down so fast in his life. Not by Kestra, not by anybody. He went to wait, the way he always waited, low and still, and he couldn't. His whole body was wrong. He hadn't known. He hadn't known how much of standing still was the tail; how it moved under everything, all the time, the way a man's eyes move when he's looking at something still, little corrections, a hair this way and a hair that. Without it he was a jug on a shelf with one leg short. He swayed. He corrected with his shoulders. Brask's staff came in under the correction like a man stepping through a door somebody's just opened for him, and he was on his back.

"No," said Brask, standing over him. "That's not it either."

"I noticed," said Milli.

"You can't hold still by holding on, lad." Brask put the staff down and crouched, slowly, with a hand on his knee, and looked at the ribbon. He didn't touch it. "The cat's not holding still. A cat by a mousehole's the stillest thing in the world and it's not holding anything. It's just *there*. All of it." He stood up again, with a grunt. "You've got your feet there. You've got your hands there. You've got your face there, very nicely, I'll give you that, you've a good face for it. Everything there but the last inch." He shrugged. "And the last inch is where you live, I'd say. If I was guessing. Which I am. I'm a sergeant, not a priest."

He held his hand out.

Milli took it and got up and untied the ribbon, and his tail came loose and went straight to the flick, and he stood there in the east yard with Jared not laughing on the wall and let it.

*The last inch is where you live*.

***

Cursiffe had found something.

Milli had known for a month. He hadn't said anything. It wasn't his business; it was very clearly not his business, and he was a man who could tell when a thing wasn't his business, mostly. But he wasn't stupid either. He'd watched Cursiffe come up out of the archive under the old keep one evening after midwinter with Oswin behind him and a face Milli had never seen on him: white and very quiet and careful, like a man carrying a bowl filled to the brim. He'd watched him go straight to Hana's room without stopping to eat and ask for a cord. Not the wheel. One of the group cords, brown and grey, the one with the tin bell, that went to Kingsferry. To Deston.

And he'd watched Aleaka, on the day her window went, sitting on the floor of Hana's room testing *Heft* on everybody in turn, reach for the folded paper Cursiffe kept in his shirt and stop. And take her hand back. *It's somebody else's*, she'd said.

He'd read the channel every night since. Cursiffe hadn't posted it. Whatever it was. Group 4 hadn't posted it either; they'd gone upriver at ice-out on their barge, all of them, and posted that they were there, and posted Gabe's window, and posted *we're at the lake*, and then nothing. Nothing about anything. Group 4, who posted everything.

He knew what Cursiffe had been looking for. Everybody in the east wing did. Cursiffe had asked it at breakfast the morning after the Council, of nobody, with Aldemar's letter open beside his bowl for the hundredth time. *What happened in the tenth year*.

Aldemar's two. The man and the woman. *They asked me every day for nine years to send them home*.

Milli had stood in the room under the old keep on the night the king opened the door, and heard Oswin read that. He'd been the one who'd asked. He hadn't meant to. It had come out of him into the dust and the lamplight before he could stop it: *That's not going to happen to us. Is it*. And the king had looked at him, a big grey man at the bottom of ninety-one steps, and said, *No*.

He'd held on to that *no* for a year and a half. He'd held it the way you hold a rail on a ship. And now Cursiffe had come up out of the archive with a face like a full bowl, and found out what happened to the two who'd asked, and wasn't saying.

He found Cursiffe in the scriptorium on the fourth afternoon, at the end table under the window, writing in his own book in English the way he did every afternoon, and sat down across from him.

Cursiffe looked up, and saw his face, and put the pen down.

"You found it," said Milli.

He'd meant to say it calmly. He'd planned it. He'd sat on the edge of the bed for an hour that morning working out the order: say it calmly, give him room, don't push. He said it calmly. His tail was going under the bench like a whip.

Cursiffe didn't pretend. He'd never been any good at it. "Yes," he said.

"What happened to them."

"Some of it." Cursiffe looked at his hands. "Not all. Some."

"Did they go home."

Cursiffe didn't answer.

Milli sat very still. All of him. His feet and his hands and his face, very nicely, Brask would have said. "Cursiffe," he said. "Did they go home."

"I can't tell you," said Cursiffe. "Not yet."

"Why."

"Because it's somebody else's to hear first." Cursiffe's voice was very low. "Not mine. Not yours. It's about somebody, and they've only just heard it, and they asked for it to stay off the channel while they sit with it, and Group 4 said yes, and I said yes. Before they'd even told him." He looked up. "I'm sorry, Milli. I'm not doing it to you. I'd tell you if I could. It's not about home, mostly. It's about something else. It's just that home's in it."

"Home's in it," said Milli.

"Yes."

"And you're not telling me."

"Not yet."

"I asked the *king*," said Milli. He heard his voice go up and couldn't stop it. "I asked him, in that room, I asked him to his face, and he said no. And I've been holding on to that for a year and a half, and you've got it in your *shirt*—"

He stopped.

The scriptorium had gone quiet. Two of Oswin's clerks at the far table had their pens in the air. Cursiffe hadn't moved at all. He was sitting with his hands flat on the table either side of the book, looking at Milli, not looking away, and his face was the face of somebody being shouted at for a thing he'd have given anything not to be carrying.

Milli got up and walked out.

***

Hana found him on the roof.

Not the roof of the east wing; the roof of the old stable range across the yard, which you could get on to from the hayloft if you went out of the little door at the end and along the gutter, and which nobody else had ever been on as far as he knew, because nobody else was a cat. He'd found it in the first month. He went there when he needed to be somewhere nobody could follow him.

Hana could follow him. She came out of the hayloft door in her good dress with the hem tucked up into her belt and walked along the gutter without looking down, and sat beside him on the ridge with her feet in the leads, and didn't say anything.

They sat. The sun was going down over the city. From up here you could see most of Solmere: the old keep, the new palace, the river going through in loops like a dropped ribbon, the Lantern Quarter on the far bank with its thousand coloured windows starting to light up for the evening. Paper lanterns in every window, in every colour. It was the week before the spring market, and the paper-makers were hanging them in their own windows to show what they'd made, the way they did every year, Oswin said. From up here it looked like a field of flowers somebody had set on fire very gently.

"Your tail's been going since Thursday," said Hana.

"I know."

"I don't mean the yard. I mean all the time. At supper. In bed." She tucked her own tail round her feet. "You think I don't notice because I'm asleep. I'm not always asleep."

He didn't say anything.

"Cursiffe came to me," said Hana. "After. He was shaking. He's not angry with you; he said to tell you that first. He said, *tell him I'd have shouted too*."

Milli shut his eyes.

"He told me what he could," said Hana. "Which was what he told you. That it's somebody's, and they've only just heard it, and home's in it. That's all. I didn't ask for more." She was quiet. "I could have. You know that. I could have sat with him by the fire and been very warm and very kind and he'd have told me all of it, and he'd have felt better for it, and he'd never have known why."

"You didn't."

"No."

"Why not." He opened his eyes. "You do it when it's convenient. You said so. You charmed the kitchen clerk out of two pots of honey for me the first winter. You nearly did the whole Council."

"Nearly," said Hana. "I put it down. Jared said." She looked at the Lantern Quarter. "And Cursiffe's not a kitchen clerk. And it's not mine. Aleaka wouldn't pick it up either, and she can lift a horse's fear off it from across a field. When two people who could take it won't, Honey, you start to think maybe it's heavy."

Milli looked at his tail. It was going, on the ridge tiles, flick, flick, at the very end. He didn't try to stop it.

"I want to go home," he said.

He'd never said it like that. Not here. Not to her. He'd said it at the briefing, on the first day, in the hall, at the top of his voice: *prove it*. He'd said it to the king in a vault. He'd said it in a hundred ways, every one of them a question, every one of them rational. *Is there a way. Has anyone. What does it take*. He'd never said it like that, on a roof, as a thing he wanted.

"I know," said Hana.

"You don't. Not the way I do." He looked at his hands. "You came for this. You came for the way home and you got here and it turned out you were the middle of everything. The wheel. Seven groups. A whole kingdom talking down your strings. You're *needed* here. Jared's needed. Leaka's got a fox and a horse and she can feel what people carry. Cursiffe's got the archive. Everybody's got something." He stopped. "I barely knew him. Ice. You know that. I met him the last year. I knew him as the guy on your stream who made you laugh. I liked him. I didn't—" He stopped again. "Everybody here's got somebody they came for. He's at a lake. I came for *you*, and you're right here, and I'd go home tomorrow. I'd take you home tomorrow. That's what I came for. And there's an answer to whether I can in Cursiffe's shirt."

Hana didn't say anything for a long time.

"And if it's no," she said at last. Quietly. "The answer. If it's what happened to them. Nine years and then nothing."

"Then I want to know."

"Do you?"

He opened his mouth. Shut it.

"Because I don't think you do," said Hana. "I think you want to know it's yes. And Cursiffe's face says it isn't yes, Honey, or he'd have run up and down the east wing shouting it. And it isn't no either, or he'd have come to you first, because he's kind. It's something else. And you can't have it yet." She leaned against him. "So you're going to have to sit with not knowing. Like everybody does. Like I do. Like the king does about whatever's coming down that pass." She put her head on his shoulder. "You're very good at waiting in a yard. You're the best I've ever seen. You've just never had to do it with the last inch."

He didn't answer.

Below them, across the river, the Lantern Quarter lit up window by window, a thousand colours. And somewhere in the middle of it, very small, one window that was a colour none of the others were. Orange, and getting brighter.

He saw it before anyone.

Before the bells, before the shouting. He was looking right at it, with his head on Hana's head and his tail going on the tiles. One window in the third row back from the river, in the glue-boilers' street, where the paper-makers kept their size-kettles, orange instead of red or green or blue, and the orange getting bigger, and then the window wasn't there any more and the orange was coming out of the roof.

"Hana," he said.

She sat up. Looked. Went white.

"That's—"

"Fire," said Milli. He was already standing. "Get the cords. Get Jared." And he was off the ridge.

***

He went the way a cat goes. He didn't think about it; he hadn't the time, and it wasn't the kind of thing you thought about. Off the stable roof on to the yard wall, along it at a run, down into the lane behind the kitchens, over the kitchen-garden gate. Out of the postern by the river stair, which the guard on it didn't so much open for him as fall away from. Down the river stair three at a time. Across the stone bridge with the crowds on it already turning to look, and the bells starting behind him, all over the city at once, the way they did for fire. The way they'd done every night of the first week, Oswin said, the year the Lantern Quarter burned in his grandfather's time.

He could smell it from the bridge. Size. Glue. Hot paper. A sweet, choking smell like burnt sugar.

The glue-boilers' street was narrow, the way all the Lantern Quarter's streets were narrow, tall thin houses leaning together at the top so you could have shaken hands across from attic to attic, and every one of them stuffed to the rafters with paper. The fire was in the third house from the corner. All of it. It had come up through the floors faster than anybody could have believed, the way fire comes through paper, and it was out of the windows on every storey and up through the roof in a column, and the two houses either side of it were smoking at the eaves.

People in the street. A lot of people, and more coming. The city watch with buckets, a chain going down to the river, too short and too slow. A woman screaming. Men with ladders that wouldn't reach. A paper-maker on his knees in the gutter with his hands over his head, and his house going above him, and his whole year's work going with it, every lantern he'd made for the spring market.

And on the roof of the next house. The fourth from the corner, the one the fire hadn't reached yet but would. Up on the ridge, holding on to the chimney with both arms. A boy.

Nine, maybe. Ten. In a paper-maker's apron. The smoke going over him in a sheet.

"He went up," the woman was screaming. "He went up out of the smoke, he went *up*, he wouldn't come down—"

Milli was already on the wall.

He didn't decide. There wasn't anything to decide. He went up the front of the house opposite, the one across the narrow street, on the window ledges and the sign brackets and a drainpipe that came off in his hand halfway and didn't matter, and on to its roof. Along its ridge. And across: the gap at the top between the leaning houses, a pace and a half of nothing over a street full of fire and people, and he didn't even feel himself jump it.

He was on the boy's roof.

The heat hit him like a door. The fire was one house over, through the party wall, and the roof under his feet was hot through his boots, and the tiles were dry and loose and moving. Smoke, everywhere, black and sweet. He couldn't see the street. He could hear it: the woman, the bells, Jared's voice somewhere, very loud, saying *move, move, MOVE*.

The boy looked at him from the chimney with a face gone past fright into something flat and white.

"Hey," said Milli. "Hey. It's okay. I'm a cat."

The boy stared at his ears.

"I'm a cat," said Milli again. "Cats are good on roofs. You're going to hold on to me now instead of that. Okay? Like a sack. Arms round my neck."

He didn't know if the boy heard. He didn't wait. He took him off the chimney, arms and all, and the boy came the way a frightened thing comes, all at once, clamped, arms round Milli's neck so hard he could barely breathe, legs round his waist. Light. Paper-makers' boys were all bone.

Milli turned round on the ridge to go back the way he'd come.

The house he'd come from was on fire.

Not the house across the street; the one *beside* it, the fifth, the other side, which had caught from the eaves while he was climbing, and the gap he'd jumped was full of flame coming up from below like a chimney. Back was gone. The other way, past the chimney, was the third house, the one that had started it, with no roof left at all, just rafters and a column of fire going up into the dark. Down was a street full of people and a drop of four storeys on to cobbles.

He stood on the ridge with the boy round his neck and the roof getting hot under his feet.

*Go*.

Everything in him said it. Every single thing. *Go now, go anywhere, jump, the street, the next roof, the drop, take your chance, go before it goes*. He felt it in his legs, the spring, *Pounce* gathering on its own, the longer he waited the faster he'd go. And he knew where he'd go if he went: down, off the front of the roof, into the street. A four-storey drop with a boy in his arms. He'd land. Probably. He was a cat. He'd land on his feet and something would break, his ankles, his knees, and he'd fall forward on the cobbles on top of the boy, and the boy would—

*Not yet*.

He didn't know where it came from. It wasn't a thought. It was the thing under the yard, under all the mornings, under *again* and *better*. Not yet. The roof wasn't going yet. It was hot and it was moving but it wasn't going. And down in the street there were people who'd seen him, and Jared's voice, and the watch with a ladder that wouldn't reach and a blanket, maybe, if somebody thought of it.

He crouched on the ridge with the boy and waited.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever done. Harder than the first morning Kestra had said *better*. Harder than the vault. The roof was moving under him, tile by tile, sliding, going over the edge and smashing in the street; the heat was coming up through his boots and through his knees and the smoke was in his eyes so he couldn't see, and he waited. Low. Still. Feet. Hands round the boy. Face.

And his tail was going.

He felt it. For the first time in his life, crouched on a burning roof, he felt it go. Flick. Flick. At the very end. The last inch. Lashing on the tiles behind him like a thing with its own mind. Not waiting. Not still. Every bit of him on that roof was waiting except that, and that was saying *go* as loud as it could, the whole time, the way it had said it in the yard, and in the scriptorium, and on the stable roof with Hana's head on his shoulder. *Go. Know. Now. I want to go home*.

*The last inch is where you live*.

He didn't hold it. He didn't tie it. He didn't do anything to it at all.

He just let himself be there. All of him. The roof hot under him and the boy's arms round his neck and the street four storeys down and Cursiffe's folded paper in a shirt across the river and the king's *no* a year and a half ago and Hana on a stable roof saying *you're going to have to sit with not knowing*. He let it all be there at once, the way you let rain be rain. He didn't need to know if the roof would hold. He didn't need to know where he was going to go. He didn't need to know if Aldemar's two went home. He was on a roof. He had a boy. That was all there was, right now, and right now was enough to be going on with.

The end of his tail went still.

He felt it go. Like a bell that's been ringing and stops. Not held. Just *done*. And in the stillness, all of it, nose to tail, he felt the roof.

The whole roof. Every tile and rafter of it, under his feet and knees and the flat of his free hand, the way he'd never felt anything. Not with his eyes. With all of him. Where it was hot and where it was hotter. Where the rafters were going, at the back, by the party wall, the old dry timber catching all along its length. Which way it would go when it went. Not toward the fire. Away from it. Toward the street. The back wall was going to burn through and the whole roof would tip, slowly, like a table with its back legs sawn off, and slide toward the front, toward the street, and go.

Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.

*Now*.

The back of the roof went. He felt it a heartbeat before it did, the way Brask felt his tail: a crack, deep in the timber, and the whole roof under him tipping, slowly, toward the street, the tiles all starting to slide at once with a sound like a river. And he went *with* it. Not across. Not back. Down the slope, with the tilt, the boy clamped to his chest, three running steps down a roof that was falling, and off the eave, into the air, four storeys over the glue-boilers' street, with the whole roof sliding off behind him.

He'd never fallen so far. He'd never had time to think in a fall before. He did now. There was a lot of air. There was the street coming up, cobbles and faces, and a ladder lying where somebody had dropped it, and the watch with a blanket, four men, running, too late, too far.

He turned in the air. He didn't decide that either. The thing every cat does. Feet under. Back round the boy. And he was still. All of him. Nose to tail. Still in the air, the way the mousehole cat is still. Not braced. Not holding. Just there, all of him, all the way to the last inch, falling.

He landed.

Feet first on the cobbles of the glue-boilers' street, with a boy in his arms, from four storeys.

He felt it go into him. All of it. Four storeys of falling, and a boy, and his own weight, up through his heels into his ankles and his knees and his hips and his spine: a blow like being hit with a house. And it didn't go *through*. It went *in* and stopped. It went into him and his body took it the way the flags of the east yard took Brask's staff when it hit the ground: a ring, a deep low ring, all through him, from his heels to his ears, and then nothing. His feet were on the cobbles. His knees were bent. The cobbles under his boots had cracked, both of them, in stars.

Nothing in him had.

Behind him the roof came down into the street in a long roar of tiles and fire, and the crowd screamed and ran back, and the heat went over him like a wave; and he stood up, with the boy still round his neck, and walked out of it.

***

The boy's mother was on him before he'd gone three steps. She took the boy off him the way you'd take a thing out of a fire, which he supposed she was, and went down on the cobbles with him, and Milli stood there with his arms still bent the shape of a boy and nothing in them.

Then Jared.

Jared came through the crowd like a door coming through a wall, with soot on his face and a bucket in one hand that he'd forgotten he was holding, and stopped in front of Milli and looked at him. All of him. His feet. His knees. The cracked stones.

"You fell off a roof," said Jared.

"I jumped off a roof."

"You fell," said Jared, "four floors. I watched you. I watched the whole roof come off with you on it." He put the bucket down. His hands were shaking. "Milli. Are you—"

"I'm fine," said Milli.

And he was. That was the thing. He stood in the glue-boilers' street with the fire going up behind him and the watch's chain finally reaching, and the ash coming down like black snow, and he was fine. His ankles were fine. His knees were fine. His back. He felt it all, the way he'd felt the roof: whole, and heavy, and ringing very faintly still, like a bell after the hammer's gone. He'd fallen four floors with a boy and landed on his feet on stone, and it had gone into him, and he'd held.

Hana came through the crowd behind Jared with her dress tucked up and soot on her hands and her face doing several things at once. She didn't charm anybody. He watched her not. She stopped in front of him and looked at him, and didn't say *Honey*. She didn't say anything. She put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, the way you'd feel a wall to see if it was still standing.

Then she looked down, behind him.

"Your tail," she said.

He looked.

It was lying on the cobbles behind him in the ash. Still. Not held. Not tied. Just lying there, the end of it curled very slightly, the way a cat's tail lies when the cat's asleep in the sun.

He hadn't noticed.

***

They got the fire out a little before midnight. Five houses. The third, where it had started, and the fourth, the boy's, and the fifth, which had caught at the eaves, and two at the back. Nobody dead. He sat on the stair and looked at it.

*Poise*. It was a word from the other world, and it was the word people there used about cats: the thing a cat has that people don't. Not stillness. Balance. Being so completely where you are that nothing can knock you off it, because you're not leaning toward anywhere else.

He thought about the end of his tail going still on a burning roof. Not held. Just done. Because for one breath, crouched in the smoke with a boy round his neck, he hadn't been leaning toward anywhere else. Not home. Not the answer in Cursiffe's shirt. Not the king's *no*. He'd been on a roof, all of him, all the way to the last inch.

And then four floors of falling had gone into him and stopped, because there was nowhere in him for it to go through. No gap. No last inch, pointing somewhere else, for it to crack along.

*Aura hardens the body*. That was Tempered. It was on the first page of every primer in the kingdom. He'd always thought it meant harder. Like Jared's *Stand*, like Gabe's *Wall*: something you put up, something you braced. It wasn't, for him. It was the other thing. It was being all in one place. You couldn't break a thing that was all in one place. It had no weak end.

He sat on the stair, and the end of his tail lay on the step beside him, and for once he didn't look at it.

He found Cursiffe in the morning, in the scriptorium, at the end table under the window, writing in his book as if nothing had happened.

Milli sat down across from him. Cursiffe put the pen down.

"I'm sorry," said Milli. "For shouting."

"I'd have shouted," said Cursiffe. "I told Hana."

"She told me." Milli put his hands flat on the table. His tail was round the leg of the bench, loosely, not holding, just there. "I'm not going to ask you again."

Cursiffe looked at him.

"I want to know," said Milli. "I still want to know. I'm not going to pretend I don't. I want to go home, and there's something about whether people can in your shirt, and I want it more than I've wanted anything." He looked at his hands. "But it's not mine yet. And I don't need it yet. I'm here. Right now I'm here." He stopped. "So tell me when it's yours to tell. Or theirs. Whoever's. I'll be here."

Cursiffe didn't say anything for a while.

"That's very calm," he said at last.

"I know," said Milli. "I'm not calm. I'm just all in one place." He stood up. "It's different. I found out last night."

Cursiffe picked his pen up. Then he put it down again.

"Milli," he said.

"Yeah?"

"When I can tell you," said Cursiffe, "you'll be first. In the east wing. Before anybody. I'll come and find you." His voice was very quiet. "I promise. I'll write it down if you like."

"You don't have to write it down."

"I know," said Cursiffe. "I'm going to anyway." He pulled a scrap of paper toward him and wrote one word on it in English, very carefully, in his own hand, and folded it, and held it across the table.

Milli took it and didn't open it. He put it in his shirt. Where Cursiffe kept his.

He went down to the east yard. Brask was on the mounting block with his staff across his knees, polishing the iron shoe with a rag, and looked up when Milli came out of the arch, and looked at his feet, and his hands, and his face, and then, for a long moment, at the end of his tail.

He didn't say anything about it. He stood up, slowly, with a hand on his knee, and lifted the staff.

"Come on, then," said Brask.

Milli waited.

He didn't know how long. A long time. Long enough that Jared, on the wall, stopped counting and just watched. Long enough that Brask's eyebrows went up a hair, and then a hair more, and then the old sergeant shifted his weight, very slightly, to the other foot, the way a man does when he's been standing a long time and doesn't know what's coming.

Milli felt him do it. All of him.

And went.

Brask was on his back on the flags of the east yard with the sky going round and the iron-shod staff ringing on the stones beside him, and Milli was standing over him with his hand out, and the end of his tail curled very slightly on the flags behind him, the way a cat's does in the sun.

Brask lay there a while.

"Well," he said, to the sky. "That's a very unfair look you've got on your face, lad."

"I learned it off a cat," said Milli, and helped him up.
