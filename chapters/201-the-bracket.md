The paper came at dawn, in eleven carts, and Lucan met it in his dressing gown.

Chris saw it from the training yard. He was on the sand behind the Lists with his longsword in both hands and Hako's spear point a foot from his chest, in the grey half-light before the sun got over the east stand, and he was thinking about his left foot, which was behaving, and his right wrist, which wasn't. *Session 571*, he'd written at the top of the page an hour ago, in the cheap notebook on the fence post. *Spear drill, Hako. Release on the bind. Right wrist: late.* And under it, after the first ten passes, *still late*.

Hako came in again. He always came in the same way, which was the trick of it: no feint, no peek, nothing, just the whole of him going down the shaft of the spear in one straight line, *Commit*, the way he'd done it in the Singles last summer against Milli, so hard and so honest that you couldn't believe a man at Drawn could put that much behind a point. It was like standing in front of a door that had decided to be somewhere else.

Chris went to meet it. Blade up, on the bind, the way Ysolde had taught him two years ago with a wooden sword and a great deal of patience. And halfway there, with his weight already going and his arms already committed and every bit of him saying *this way*, he let go of it.

*Release*. Not the sword. The move. He dropped it halfway through like a line of code you comment out mid-run, and started a different one from exactly where his body was, with no hitch, no gap, no recovery: a half-step left and the flat of the blade coming round the other side of the shaft instead of into it. The spear went past his ribs with a sound like tearing cloth. His sword touched Hako's wrist. Lightly.

"*Ah*," said Hako, delighted, as if he'd been given a present. "That's the one. That was clean."

"Wrist was late," said Chris.

"It was not late."

"It was late by about a frame." Chris lowered the sword. "You didn't see it because you were committed. That's the point of you."

Hako laughed. He had the kind of laugh that made you believe everything was going to work out, which was dangerous in a sparring partner, because it was usually right. He grounded the spear and leaned on it, breathing, and looked past Chris, at the gate.

"Chris," he said. "Why is Lucan in his dressing gown in the street with a lot of carts?"

***

It was the purple one.

Chris recognised it at once. Everybody who'd been in Valcrest for the Lists last summer would have. It was the dressing gown from the goose: the long purple silk thing with the gold frogging that Lucan had come down in at one in the morning, one night in the Lists week, when a goose nobody ever owned had got onto the sand of the Lists and refused to leave. Lucan had walked out to it in the voice he used for kings and addressed it for some time. *Madam*. Drew told the story better. Drew told it to anyone who'd stand still, and had apparently told it, last week, according to the channel, to eleven people in a long room in Thornwall while something tried to call them up a mountain, and only finished it afterwards.

Lucan was standing in the middle of the road outside the Lists' south gate in the purple dressing gown with his arms out. The carts were in a line behind him, all the way down the hill. Eleven of them. Big ones, with high sides and canvas tied down over the tops, pulled by the slow heavy horses the paper-makers used, the ones with feathered feet. Their drivers sat on the boxes looking at Lucan the way people did look at Lucan: with the patience of people who were being paid.

Ysolde was standing in the gate.

She had her arms folded. Her ram's horns were catching the first of the light, curling back from her temples the colour of old bone, and her face was doing what it always did, which was nothing, at great length. She'd been a three-time champion of the Lists. She'd stood on this sand while crowds spat at her because her horns looked like something they were frightened of. She had, Chris had once worked out, a total vocabulary of about four hundred words, and used two hundred of them on Lucan.

"You may not order the paper," she said.

"I have ordered the paper," said Lucan.

"I said so in the autumn. I said so in writing. I had the cat girl send it."

"I *remember*." Lucan turned round. He had tears in his eyes already. He always had tears in his eyes; it was barely dawn. "Ysolde. Ysolde, look at them."

Ysolde looked at the carts.

"What's in them?" said Chris. He'd come up the yard with Hako behind him, the sword still in his hand. Lucan turned and saw them and his whole face lit up like a lamp, the way it did when an audience arrived.

"*Fishing rods*," he said.

There was a pause.

"How many fishing rods?" said Hako.

Lucan went to the nearest cart. He untied one corner of the canvas with tremendous ceremony, considering that he was in a dressing gown, and folded it back.

They were stacked to the top. Bundles. Each one tied with string, each one about as thick as a man's thigh, and in each bundle, folded flat and neat, a hundred paper fishing rods. Chris could see the ends of them. Long and thin, the length of an arm, with a little paper reel folded into the handle and a curl of paper line at the tip, and a little paper fish on the end of the line, which was new; last year there hadn't been a fish. They were every colour. The paper-makers of Valcrest had never in their lives been asked to make anything as stupid, and they had clearly decided, as a guild, to make it beautifully.

"Forty thousand," said Lucan.

The yard was very quiet.

"Last year," said Lucan, "we had ten thousand. I ordered ten thousand. *Just in case*. You remember." He looked at Hako, who'd been there; at Chris, who'd written it on a line in a notebook. "And by the second hour of the first day they had *sold out*. Every one. And on the last day, at the closing, in the sunset, when the girl wrote her mother's name in the box, I looked up at the stands, and the whole of Valcrest was holding up a fishing rod." His voice went. He let it. "Twenty thousand of them. And the ones who didn't have one were holding up *spoons*. Ladles. Walking sticks. A man in the north stand held up his *leg*. A wooden one. He took it off."

"I remember the leg," said Ysolde.

"*Forty thousand*," said Lucan. "One for every seat in the bowl. So that this year nobody has to take off their leg."

Ysolde looked at the carts for a long time.

"Where," she said, "are you going to put them?"

"In the Lists," said Lucan, as if it was obvious. "Under the stands. Where else would you put forty thousand fishing rods?"

"*Lucan*."

"There are a hundred days to midsummer. A *hundred*. The paper-makers have been folding since the leaves came down. They've had their children folding. I went to see them at midwinter and there was a grandmother in Tanner's Row folding them in her sleep." He put his hand flat on the nearest bundle, the way you'd put your hand on a horse. "They're *here*. They're done. And I had nowhere to put them except where they're going." He looked at Ysolde. "Please."

Ysolde unfolded her arms.

She looked at the bundle under his hand. At the little paper fish on the ends of the lines, every colour. And then, for some reason, at Chris, as if he were the one who ought to be asked.

Chris thought about it. He thought about a line in his notebook, at the bottom of a page, in his own small square hand, with *paper fishing rods* written at the end of it a year ago because Newish had said *for the Hero, just in case* and Lucan had wept.

"Under the north stand," said Chris. "It's dry. The south one floods."

Ysolde stood aside from the gate.

He'd written *ok* and then sat on the end of his bed for an hour with the notebook on his knees and not written anything else.

"You're doing the face," said Newish.

She was across the table, with her chin on her hand. She'd been watching him read it for some time. She did that. She said she could hear people's beats, the way other people heard footsteps, and Chris had stopped arguing with it in the first winter, because she was always right, and because it was easier to believe her than to explain how she kept knowing.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face you do when somebody's merged something into main without asking you."

Chris shut the window.

"It's Drew's thing," he said. "The bracket. It's always been Drew's. He ran brackets from his bedroom for ten years. He ran the whole of last summer. He turned the hall on its side." He looked at his porridge. "I count things."

"He knows you count things," said Winter.

She was beside him. She was always beside him, now. On his left. She'd been on his left since the third hour of the afternoon on the fourth day of the Lists last summer, in the final of the Trios, when he'd stopped holding the place open for someone who was sitting in the stands, and she'd stepped into it. She'd never stepped out. Neither of them had ever said anything about it. They didn't need to. It was like a line of code that ran so well you forgot it was there.

"He gave it to you because you count," said Winter. Bluntly, the way she said everything. "He's not giving you his thing. He's giving you yours."

Chris looked at her. She was eating toast, with her silver hair tied back off it and her eyes on the toast, one blue and one pink, half-lidded, and she didn't look up. She didn't need to.

"Thirty-five," said Ina, from the end of the table.

He'd come down late. He usually did; he'd been up late with the mask off, reading. He sat with his tea held in both hands under his chin, the single purple horn rising from his forehead, the grey oni mask hung at his shoulder by its red cords, grinning at the wall. He had the voice for mornings. Chris had never heard anyone else on earth or off it with a voice that made you want to listen to it at breakfast.

"That's the difficulty, isn't it," said Ina. "Not the drawing. The number." He drank his tea. "Thirty-five. All of us. In one bracket." He looked at Chris over the cup, teal-green and amused. "It's a frightful number."

"It's a terrible number," said Chris.

"Why?" said Hako, who'd been eating very fast so as not to miss anything.

And Chris opened his mouth to explain, and stopped, because the door of the Broken Lance had banged open and Lucan was standing in it, still in the dressing gown, with rain on his shoulders and his hair flat and both hands held up in front of him like a man about to announce a wedding.

"The west wall is white," said Lucan.

They looked at him.

"Forty feet." He came in. He dripped. The landlord, who had known Lucan for twenty years, put a towel on the end of the table without being asked and went back to the kitchen. "I had it done last week. Two coats. I didn't know who would draw on it. Drew is in the north with a valley. I thought perhaps nobody. I thought perhaps I'd have to do it myself, and it would be terrible, and everyone would know." He looked at Chris. "And then Ysolde said you'd been asked."

"How does Ysolde know I've been asked?"

"Ysolde knows everything," said Lucan. "She doesn't *say* anything. It's maddening." He picked up the towel and held it without using it. "Will you come and look at it?"

***

They went in by the south gate, under the stands, past the first of the bundles going in under the north side on the paper-makers' hand-carts: forty thousand paper fishing rods, all the colours of a market, being stacked very carefully in the dry by men in leather aprons who looked as if they'd been doing it in their sleep.

And then out onto the sand.

Chris had been on the sand of the Lists more mornings than he could count, which meant he could count them, because he'd logged every one. Five hundred and seventy-one. It was still a shock every time. The bowl went up all round you in great pale tiers, white stone, a hundred rows of it, and at midsummer it held forty thousand people and at dawn in the spring rain it held nobody at all, and the sound of your own feet came back to you off the far side a moment late.

The east wall first. They always looked at the east wall first. Everybody did.

It was still there. Lucan wouldn't let anybody touch it. He'd had a little sloped roof of tarred boards built over the top of it in the autumn so the rain ran off, and a rope across the front of it at knee height, as if it were a painting in a palace. Forty feet of whitewash under the east stand with the bracket of last summer on it in black, and the gold lines Drew had run between the boxes, and the names. All thirty-five of them, in the five squads, and the Trios tree, and the Singles tree with Xav's name at the end of it, and the Gate, and the Banner. And in the top corner, where the last box was, the spider. Drew's spider, from his bedroom, from ten years of brackets, painted in black with eight careful legs: top seed. And round it, painted on the last night in big black capitals by Drew himself, standing on a barrel with a brush in his teeth, a ring of words.

THE THIRTY-FIVE.

Hako stood in front of it with his hands behind his back. He always did that. He'd done it every time he came in for a year. Chris had never asked him why.

"Now," said Lucan. "Turn round."

They turned round.

The west wall was white.

Forty feet of it, under the west stand, from the foot of the stair at one end to the foot of the stair at the other: two coats of fresh lime, so white in the grey rain that it seemed to have its own light. There was nothing on it. Not a line. Not a mark. Just white, going away, very long, and Chris stood on the sand and looked at it and felt the thing he'd felt every time anybody had ever given him a blank file and said *build it*.

Not all of it was blank. At the bottom, along the foot of the wall, a strip about a man's height from the sand, Lucan had already painted something.

The Fledgling tree.

It was smaller than the bracket would be, and neater, because Lucan had done it himself and Lucan was neat about the things he loved. Sixty-four boxes in a tree, for the fledglings, any path, any guild in the kingdom, copper or iron, under twenty. Black on white, gold lines. And right at the far end of it, at the top, where the tree came together, in a box twice the size of the others with a gold edge round it, three words in Lucan's best capitals.

ALEA, OF WRENHAVEN.

And under it, smaller: HOLDER.

"I did it last week," said Lucan. He'd come up beside Chris. He was looking at the box, not at Chris. "Before the wall was dry. I couldn't wait." He was quiet. "She hasn't entered. Not yet. She doesn't need to; the holder's place is held till sunset on midsummer eve. That's the rule. Three hundred years." He touched the rope at the east wall behind him without looking at it, as if for luck. "If she comes, she's in. If she doesn't, the box stays like that, with her name in it. I'll not paint over it. I've decided."

"She'll come," said Hako.

"You don't know that."

"She'll come," said Hako, cheerfully, as if it were the weather. "Sorrel's coming. Isn't she?"

Lucan pointed.

There was one other name on the Fledgling tree. Only one, out of sixty-four. Halfway down the first column, in a plain box, in Lucan's plain capitals.

SORREL, OF GULLHAVEN.

"First entry in the kingdom," said Lucan. "Before the leaves were off the trees. I sent the notices out a week after the closing, last summer; I couldn't help it. Hers came back on the coast carrier in the autumn, with fish scales on it." He smiled at the box. "*Put me opposite her if you can*, she wrote. *If you can't, I'll get there*." He shook his head slowly. "I wrote back that it's a draw. That nobody can put anybody anywhere. That's the whole point of a draw." His smile went crooked. "She wrote back *I know. I'll get there*."

Chris looked at the two names. One at the bottom of the tree and one at the top, with sixty-two empty boxes between them, and five rounds.

Then he looked up. At the forty feet of white above them. Going away into the rain.

"Thirty-five," he said.

***

It took him three nights.

He did it at the long table at the Broken Lance after supper, under the lamp, with the notebook open and a lot of other paper round it, because the notebook wasn't big enough and he didn't want to put a bracket in it that didn't work. Lucan had sent round a stack of the paper-makers' scrap, thick cream sheets with torn edges, and a box of charcoal. The others sat round him. Not helping. Just there. It was what they'd done since the hall, when one of them was working something out: Ina reading, Hako with his spear across his knees, oiling the shaft for the third time, Newish with her boots up on the bench, and Winter on his left.

The trouble was the number.

"A bracket," said Chris, on the first night, "wants to be a power of two."

"A what," said Hako.

"Two. Four. Eight. Sixteen. Thirty-two. Sixty-four." Chris drew it. A box, and two lines going into it, and two boxes, and four lines. A tree, the way every bracket was a tree: halving and halving and halving until there was one. "Every round, half of you go out. So you need a number you can halve all the way down to one without anyone left over." He tapped the charcoal on *thirty-two*. "Thirty-two's a bracket." On *sixty-four*. "Sixty-four's a bracket." He wrote *35* in the middle of the page and drew a box round it. "Thirty-five is a bracket and three people standing outside it in the rain."

"You could have three people play their way in," said Ina. "Before it starts. The way they do with the fledglings, when too many come."

"Six people," said Chris. "Six play three matches. The three winners go into the thirty-two. Play-ins." He drew it. Six little boxes off to one side of the tree, with lines feeding in. It was a perfectly ordinary thing to draw. He'd seen it in a hundred tournaments. "That works."

"So who's in the play-in?" said Hako.

"Whoever's seeded lowest," said Chris. "Bottom six seeds."

"And who's seeded lowest?"

Chris opened his mouth to say *whoever's lowest on the*—

And stopped.

He looked down at his notebook. At the page it always fell open to now, because he'd opened it there every night for three years. Thirty-five lines, in his own small square hand, in English, with Lucan's script beside each one in Lucan's hand, because Lucan had asked to write them in the first month and Chris had let him. And beside each line, a column. The count. The tier. *Drawn*. *Spark*. *Candle*. *Tempered*. All the way down. Thirty-four of them filled in. One not.

*Nikorasu*, said the thirty-fifth line, at the bottom, where he'd moved it in the autumn when the others went past. *Tier 0. Initiate. Aura and mana. Both*.

The table had gone quiet.

"By the count," said Chris. "You'd seed by tier. Everybody does. Highest tier, top seed. Lowest tier—" He stopped again.

"Niko," said Newish. Not banter. Quietly.

"Niko," said Chris. "In a play-in. At the bottom. In front of forty thousand people, because he's last." He looked at the line. "And it's not even true. He's not the weakest of us. He's never been the weakest of us. He's doing two paths at once. He went nineteen heartbeats on a wall and then twenty-two in the snow. He's doing a thing nobody's ever done in two worlds, and he's at the bottom of my count because my count doesn't have a column for it."

He picked up the charcoal.

He crossed out the play-ins. Hard. All six boxes, and the lines, and the word *seeds*, until the charcoal broke.

"The count isn't a ranking," he said. "I'm not seeding anybody by it. I'm not seeding anybody by anything." He put the broken charcoal down. "It was never for that."

Nobody said anything for a while. Ina turned a page of his book without reading it. Hako put the oil cloth down.

"Then don't cut anyone," said Ina.

He'd closed his book. He was looking at Chris along the table in the lamplight, the mask at his shoulder grinning at the ceiling, his own face very serious, and when he spoke it was in the voice he used for the things he meant: not the public one, not the one he put on with the mask in front of crowds. The other one.

"You're trying to make thirty-five into thirty-two," said Ina, "by finding three people to stand outside in the rain. You've been trying to do it all evening, and you've hated every way you've found, and that's because every way you've found is somebody standing outside." He spread his hands. "We came here thirty-five. We'll go in thirty-five. If the number doesn't fit the tree, it's the tree that's wrong. Not the number."

"Byes," said Newish.

"Twenty-nine of them," said Chris. "In a sixty-four. That's not a bracket. That's a waiting room."

"Then don't use a tree," said Ina.

Chris looked at him.

"I don't know any other kind," said Ina, quite cheerfully. "I'm a gentleman. I'm only telling you the tree's wrong."

And Winter, on his left, who hadn't said a word all evening, who'd been sitting with her boots on the bench rail and her hands round her cup watching the page, said:

"Make it thirty-six."

The table went still.

Chris didn't look at her. Not straight away. He looked at the page. At *35*, in its box, in the middle of the crossed-out mess. And at his own hand, holding nothing, beside it.

"Don't look at me like that," said Winter, although he wasn't looking at her at all. "I said it. Not you."

***

He found her on the roof at midnight.

That was where she went. It had been since the first summer, when they'd found the hatch at the top of the back stair of the Broken Lance and the flat leaded bit of roof behind the chimney stack where you could sit with your back to the warm bricks and see the whole of Valcrest going down to the river, and the great pale ring of the Lists in the middle of it like a bowl somebody had set down and forgotten. She'd sat up here the night Thea's letter came, the year before last, and told him that a month on Earth was less than Vietnam. She'd sat up here a lot.

The rain had stopped. The roof was wet. She was sitting on her coat.

He sat down beside her. On her right. He noticed himself do it.

"You let go of the left," said Winter. "Last year. On the sand. In front of forty thousand people." She didn't look at him. She was looking at the Lists. "I was there. I stepped into it. I've been in it every day since."

"I know."

"So I'm asking." She said it bluntly, the way she said everything, but not unkindly; Winter was never unkind, it was just that she'd never in her life seen the point of going round something when you could go through it. "Thirty-six. One more box than there are of us. With nobody in it." She turned her head. One blue eye, one pink, in the dark. "Are you picking it back up?"

Chris thought about it.

He did it properly. He'd learned to, here. On Earth he'd have said *no* straight away, because it was the answer that made the question go away. Here he'd spent two years on the sand learning that the question doesn't go away; it just goes into your left leg and waits.

So he thought about it. The way he'd think about a bug. What was the slot. What was in it. What was holding it open, and for whom, and what happened to everything else while it was held.

"A memory leak," he said slowly, "is a slot you're holding for one process. One. Somebody asked for it, a long time ago, and then went away, and nobody ever told the system they'd gone, so it holds the slot for them forever. And nobody else can use it. Nothing else can go there. You just get less and less room, every day, and you don't know why." He looked at his hands. "That was the left. I was holding it for him. And you couldn't go there. And Newish kept stepping back into it, because she was waiting for him too."

Winter didn't say anything.

"This isn't that," said Chris.

"Explain."

"There's a kind of tournament," said Chris. "At home. The big ones. The Majors." He drew it in the air with his finger, because there wasn't any paper on the roof. "Everybody starts at nothing-nothing. Nobody's seeded. Nobody's bottom. Round one, you draw lots, and you play someone. If you win, you're one-nothing. If you lose, nothing-one. Round two, you play someone with the same record as you. Ones play ones. Nothings play nothings. And so on. Three wins, you're through. Three losses, you're out." He let his hand fall. "It's called a Swiss. Nobody knows why. It's the fairest thing anybody ever invented for a lot of people who don't know how good each other are yet."

"And thirty-five."

"Thirty-five's odd," said Chris. "So every round, somebody hasn't got anybody to play. There's always one left over." He looked at the Lists. "So you put in a bye. A thirty-sixth. An empty seat. Whoever draws it sits that round out. And the rule is—" He stopped, and found, saying it, that he'd known the rule for fifteen years and never once heard it. "The rule is nobody sits in it twice. It goes to somebody new every round. It moves."

Winter was very still beside him.

"It isn't a slot I'm holding for somebody," said Chris. "It's a slot everybody gets to sit in. Once. When it's their turn." He looked at his hands again. "It's not a leak. It's a rest."

A cart went by in the street below, late, with a lamp on it. Somewhere down by the river a dog was barking at nothing.

"And you'd have to paint it," said Winter. "On the wall. A thirty-sixth box."

"Yes."

"With nothing in it."

"Yes."

"In front of forty thousand people. Who all know exactly who isn't in it."

"Yes," said Chris.

Winter looked at the Lists for a long time.

"His beat's mostly rests," said somebody.

They both turned round. Newish was standing in the hatch, up to her waist, with her elbows on the leads and her chin on her hands and a blanket round her shoulders. Chris had no idea how long she'd been there. She did that too.

"I told you," said Newish. "Last summer. Everybody's got a beat. Lucan's is a march. Ysolde's is a big slow drum. Hako's is a pop song. Yours is a clock." She nodded at Chris. "And his was mostly rests. Long ones. The whole time I've ever heard it. Waiting." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I heard him laugh in them. When he skipped me, at the gate. In the rests."

Winter looked at her.

"You've just drawn him a rest," said Newish to Chris. "That's all. Not a slot. Not a seat with his name on. Just a rest, in the middle of everybody's beat, that moves along. The way they do." She shrugged, under the blanket. "It'll look right. On the wall. Trust me. I hear these things."

Nobody said anything.

Then Winter said, "Fine," and stood up, and picked up her wet coat, and went past Newish down the hatch, and on the way past, without stopping, she put her hand on top of Newish's head for a moment, the way you'd put your hand on a dog you liked. Newish went bright red in the dark. Chris saw it.

He sat on the roof for a while after they'd both gone. With his back to the chimney and the Lists down there in the middle of the town, pale, and empty, and forty feet of white on one side of it waiting.

Then he went down and drew it.

***

It took two days to paint. Chris did the lines himself.

Lucan had wanted his own painters, the ones who'd done the wall last year, and Chris had said yes to them for the letters and no to them for the lines, and Lucan had looked at him for a long moment with his eyes filling up, which meant yes. So Chris did the lines. With a chalk line first, snapped against the lime by Hako on the other end, and then with a ruler and a brush and a pot of black, up a ladder, in the rain, while Ysolde held the ladder and said nothing whatsoever for nine hours.

He'd never painted anything in his life. He found it was like writing a layout by hand. You did it once in your head, all the way through, and then you did it again with your hands, slowly, and the second time was the real one.

It came out left to right. That was how a Swiss read. At the far left, the first column: a long stack of boxes, from the top of the wall nearly to the Fledgling tree, in pairs, joined by short gold lines. *0–0*, Lucan's painter wrote above it in black, very carefully, in the kingdom's figures, copying off Chris's sheet, with Chris's own figures small underneath for the thirty-five. Then the second column, split down the middle: *1–0* above, *0–1* below. Then three stacks: *2–0*, *1–1*, *0–2*. Then the edges began to fall away. Top right, the ones who'd won three: *3–0*, *3–1*, *3–2*, and a gold line going on from each of them to the right, toward the end of the wall, where Chris had left room for Drew. Bottom right, the ones who'd lost three. Short black boxes, going nowhere.

It looked like a Major. It looked exactly like every Major Chris had ever watched, at three in the morning, on a second monitor, with Ice in his ear calling the rounds. He hadn't expected that. He stood at the foot of the ladder when the second column was done and looked at it and had to go and stand in the tunnel under the south stand for a few minutes.

Hako explained it to Lucan.

He did it on the second afternoon, sitting on the bottom tier with Lucan beside him and the wall in front of them, in his best upbeat voice, the one that made you believe it. Chris listened from the ladder.

"Everybody starts at nothing-nothing," said Hako. "See? That's the first column. Everybody. Xav and the girl with the moths and Salty. All the same. Nobody's better than anybody. You draw lots."

"I have the helmet," said Lucan.

"Then you play someone. Win, you go up. Lose, you go down." Hako traced it in the air. "Then you play someone else who's been doing as well as you. Or as badly. That's the clever bit. You always end up fighting someone your own size. And three wins, you're through. To the end. To Drew's bit." He pointed at the gold lines going off to the right. "And three losses, you're out. That's it. You've gone home."

Lucan was very quiet.

"*Gone home*," he said.

Hako stopped.

"That's what we say," said Lucan. "In the Lists. When you've lost. When it's over for you. *Go home*. The marshals say it at the gate. *Go home, lad, you fought well*. It's the oldest thing we have." He was looking at the wall. At the short black boxes at the bottom right, going nowhere. "I've said it ten thousand times. To boys. To girls. To men twice my age." He swallowed. "I never once heard it until you said it just now."

Hako didn't say anything. His smile had gone, the way it hardly ever did.

"It's just a way of talking," said Hako. "At home. In the game. You're out, that's all. You've lost. You go home." He looked at his own hands. "It doesn't mean—"

"I know what it doesn't mean," said Lucan. "I know about the ring at the edge of the circle with nothing in it. The whole city knew by the end of the Lists week." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, without embarrassment. Lucan was never embarrassed about crying; he said it was the one thing in his life he'd never once had to rehearse. "I only meant that in three hundred years nobody on this sand has ever minded going home. And this summer some of them will. And some of them won't." He looked at the wall. "And I'll say it to them at the gate anyway. *Go home. You fought well*. And I'll have to decide, each time, which way I mean it."

Up on the ladder, Chris found that he'd stopped painting.

He started again.

***

He did the thirty-sixth box last.

At the very bottom of the first column. Under the thirty-fifth. In its pair, joined to the box above it by a short gold line, the same as all the others. The same size. The same black edge. The same white inside.

He did it slowly. He did all four sides with the ruler, and went over each one once, and didn't go over any of them twice. Then he came down the ladder and stood back on the sand and looked at it.

It looked right. Newish had been right. It looked like a rest.

Lucan came and stood beside him.

He'd been very good for two days. He'd only cried four times, and only once on the ladder. He stood beside Chris with his hands clasped in front of him and looked at the box at the bottom of the first column for a long time, and then said, in a very small voice for Lucan:

"Gold."

"No."

"Just the edge. Just a little gold edge. The way I did the girl's."

"No," said Chris.

"Then a little fishing rod. A small one. In the corner. You'd hardly see it. Only the people who knew would—"

"Lucan." Chris didn't look away from the box. "It's the same as all the others. That's the point. If it's different, it's a slot. If it's the same, it's a rest." He looked at it. "It's a bye. Whoever draws it sits out a round, and gets a cup of water, and watches. And then next round it goes to somebody else. It moves." He pointed, and Lucan's eyes followed his finger across the wall. "There. And there. And there."

Because it did. He'd painted it in every column where the numbers came out odd: once in the *0–0*, once in the *0–1*, once lower down in the *1–2*, wherever the sums put it. Not one box. A thirty-sixth box that turned up again a column further on and a line lower, each time, the way the bye did, walking down the wall from left to right. Going to whoever hadn't had it yet. Always ending up, by the way the sums fell, somewhere near the bottom of things. At the end of the stack. On the last line.

He hadn't meant it to do that. That was just how a Swiss worked. The bye goes to the lowest record that hasn't sat in it. He'd known that for fifteen years.

He looked at it now, painted, on forty feet of white: a box with nothing in it, going down the wall a step at a time, always to the end of things, always on the last line, never in the same place twice. And he thought about a man who sat on the end of everything. On the bottom tier. On the end of a jetty. On the step of the stair by the gatehouse at Cinderhold, ten steps down, where you couldn't see him from the walk.

"There was a box last year," said Lucan quietly. "That he wasn't in."

"I know."

"Drew told him so. At the gate. *You're not in it*. I heard about it afterwards." Lucan looked at the east wall, behind them, under its little roof. At the spider in the top corner. "And then he came and sat in the east stand anyway, halfway up, every day, apart, where the casters used to sit. And watched every single match."

"I know," said Chris. "I was there."

Lucan was quiet a long time.

"Will he come?" he said. "This year."

Chris thought about the right answer, and the true one, and whether there was a difference.

"Not in the count," he said.

Lucan turned his head.

"I don't know," said Chris. "Nobody does. Not the girl. Not him, I think." He looked at the box. "I count the thirty-five. He's not one of the thirty-five. He's the line under them. And I've never put anything in the column next to it, because I don't know what goes there." He shrugged. "So it's blank. It's always been blank."

"But it's there," said Lucan.

"It's there."

Ysolde, who had held the ladder for two days and said nothing, came across the sand, and stopped, and looked at the box at the bottom of the first column. She looked at it the way she'd looked, last summer, once, at a man carrying water through the kitchen of the Broken Lance: very still, all at once, and then not again.

"Good," she said.

And went back to the ladder, and picked it up, and carried it away under the stand by herself, though it took two men to carry, and they heard it go down on top of the forty thousand fishing rods with a crash and a lot of swearing in a language none of them knew.

He read it all twice. The way he always did.

Then he closed the window, and opened the notebook.

Thirty-five lines, in his own small square hand, with Lucan's beside them. Thirty-four filled in, across, all the way to the column at the end. *Drawn*. *Spark*. *Candle*. *Tempered*. One not. *Nikorasu. Tier 0. Initiate. Aura and mana. Both*. And he looked at that one for a while and took his pencil and, very small, beside *Both*, wrote *0–0*, and nothing else.

And under it, the thirty-sixth.

He'd ruled it in the first summer, in a room at the Broken Lance, the night Thea's letter came with the slow date in it. He'd ruled a thirty-sixth line and left it blank, because he'd done a sum at the bottom of a page and didn't know yet what it meant. The winter after, in the rain, Newish had said *for the Hero, just in case*, and he'd written *paper fishing rods* at the end of it. Small. And then, the summer after that, at a north gate, with a man in front of him with two bees on his head, he'd written a name at the start of it.

He'd never written anything in the column at the end. The tier. It was the only line in the book with nothing in the column at the end.

He looked at it now. The name at the start. *Paper fishing rods* at the end. And nothing in the column.

He took the pencil, and after *paper fishing rods*, very small, he wrote: *the bye*.

He didn't write anything in the column.

Then he put the pencil down, and sat for a while with the notebook open under the lamp, with Winter on his left reading over his shoulder and saying nothing, and Ina turning pages, and Hako asleep sitting up with his cheek on the spear, and Newish humming something under her breath at the end of the bench that Chris didn't know, with long gaps in it.

He knew who read the channel. They all did, now. They'd known since the second winter, since a man at the bottom of a lake road had told five of them so, and the five had told the rest. Chris had never written to him. Nobody wrote *to* him; that was the rule nobody had ever made. But every night, at the end of the log, for three years, before he knew and after, Chris had written the same thing at the bottom.

*Line 36 still blank. Still there.*

It had started as a note to himself. A reminder that the sum at the bottom of the page wasn't finished. Somewhere in the second year it had turned into something else, without his noticing, the way a comment you leave in code for yourself turns, years later, into a comment for whoever comes after.

Now it was a ping. That was all. A packet you send into the dark to see if anything's there. You don't expect an answer. You don't need one. You're only checking the line's still up.

It was still up. He knew that. The way you know a server's alive at three in the morning when nothing's happening: not because it says so, but because nothing's come back to tell you it isn't.

A hundred days to midsummer. Forty thousand fishing rods under the north stand. Two names on the Fledgling tree, one at the bottom and one at the top. Thirty-five boxes on the west wall of the Lists, and a thirty-sixth, walking down it a step at a time, to the end of things.

Chris closed the notebook.

"Logging," he said, to nobody, and went up to bed.
