John had been on every one of Declan's streams.

Not on them. In them. That was the joke, back then, among the five of them: that if you opened one of Declan's facecam streams at any hour of the night you'd find John in the corner of it within ten minutes, in a call, saying something, laughing at something, telling Declan he was wrong about a thing he was right about just to watch him argue. Declan used to say John was a feature. *The John feature. Comes free. Can't be turned off*. John had liked that.

He'd liked the streams. He'd liked the idea of them more than anybody else in the poly class did, more than Declan did, probably. That you could sit in a room in one city and point a camera at yourself and somebody in another city, or another country, or a dozen of them in a dozen countries, could see you. Right now. As it happened. It had seemed to him, at seventeen, like the closest thing the world had to magic.

Now he had actual magic. And he still couldn't do it.

***

The first night on the road he held the square over the fire for an hour and nothing happened.

He hadn't expected anything to. He'd just wanted to try. He sat on the end of the fallen beech trunk with his knees up and his eyes shut, the way he held it best, and put the motes up into the branches and let them look down; and opened the square in the air over the fire, door-sized, gold, the way he'd opened it on the gravel bar at Duskhollow every dawn for a year. And inside it, from above, from where the motes were: the camp. The fire. Gabe's back, bent over it, blowing. Deston on his back on the beech mast. Declan by the pot, and then not by the pot, sitting down on the trunk between Gabe and Deston with his bowl in both hands, close against his chest.

John held that. Everyone round the fire could see it, if they looked up. RX did, and waved at himself.

Then John opened his window under it.

That was the idea. That was the whole idea, and it was a bad one, and he knew it. The channel was words. Thirty-five windows, and a long thin strip of words running through all of them, the same words in every one, that Thea had built in the first spring inside something older than any of them. You wrote in it, and the words came out everywhere. He'd written in it every night for three years.

And the square was light. His light. It went where the motes went and showed what the motes saw, and anybody standing near enough could see it, and nobody else could.

So he opened the window under the square, the way you'd hold a phone up under a thing to take a picture of it. And tried to put the square in.

He didn't know how. There was nothing to do it with. He just tried: the way you try to move your ears, if you can't, sitting very still and pushing at a place in your head that doesn't seem to be connected to anything.

Nothing.

He tried for an hour. Nothing at all. When he opened his eyes his window had the same last line in it as when he'd shut them, and the fire had gone down, and Declan was asleep, and the girl was on the watch on the far side with her back against a beech and her sword across her knees, looking at him with her ears turned toward him and an expression of polite fox interest.

"Was it supposed to do something?" said Alea.

"Yes," said John.

"It didn't."

"No," said John. "Thank you."

She nodded, and turned her ears back to the dark.

***

He wasn't the only one.

He'd seen Petra get up, an hour after the fire went down, and take the stick and go off along the edge of the wood toward the stream. He'd seen it with the motes; they were still up in the branches, he'd forgotten to bring them down. And he hadn't watched her, after, because it wasn't his to watch. He'd brought the motes down and gone to sleep.

But on the second night he saw her again. And the third.

Every night. After the fire. When the rest of them were in their blankets. She'd take the stick and go a little way off, out of the light, and stand for a long time, and then bring it across.

He knew what she was doing. Xav had told him on the lake. *She's going to try to make it come. She'll try every night. Don't help.* So he didn't help. He did what he did on the gravel bar every morning, which was watch, and keep, because that was what the motes were for and it was the thing he was best at in two worlds: being there while something happened, and keeping it, so that somebody could see it afterwards.

He kept it. Every night. He didn't show her.

Second night, on the edge of a field of stubble above the river road. The stick came across and a line stayed: a count of two. She did it again and it stayed for one. Then nothing, eleven times.

Third night, in a lane between hedges. Two again. And then, with her own sword, the long plain blade she'd carried since she was a recruit, nothing. Twenty times with the sword. Nothing. She put it back in its sheath like a woman putting away a tool that's broken.

Fifth night, in the lee of a barn. *Five*. He counted it in the motes. A thread of grey hanging in the dark at chest height for a count of five, the longest yet, and Petra standing in front of it, not breathing, until it went.

He felt something go through him when it did. He didn't know what. He wasn't a swordsman. He couldn't feel aura, much; he could feel the motes and the light and the place in his chest where Glimpse lived, and that was all. But he saw her shoulders after it went. And he knew what that was. He'd seen it on Declan's face on a stream at three in the morning when a clip had worked and he'd watched it back and then tried to do it again and it hadn't.

***

The fourth night something went into the channel.

Not the picture. He'd found that out in the morning, reading back. He'd sat with the square over the fire and the window under it for an hour again, pushing at the place in his head that wasn't connected to anything, and nothing had happened, the same as the first three nights; and then for a second, near the end, when he was tired and had stopped pushing and was just holding it there out of stubbornness, he'd felt something give.

Not much. The way a knot gives a hair when you've stopped pulling at it and started picking.

And Thea had written *something came through*. And Hana: *my cords went warm*. And Chris: *logging*.

He'd read it three times at breakfast with his porridge going cold.

"You've got a face," said Azac, across the fire.

"I haven't got a face."

"You've got the face you had when you found the motes could go round corners. In Duskhollow. In the first winter. You had that face for a week." Azac was the biggest of them, and the slowest to say anything, and when he said a thing it was usually a story, and usually about one of them. "You kept sending them round the corner of the chandler's and telling us what was in the window. Candles. Every time. It was a candle shop."

"It was a good test."

"It was a candle shop, John."

"Something went through," said John.

Azac looked at him over the fire for a while, with his big hands round his cup.

"Okay," said Azac. "Do it again."

***

So he did it again. Every night.

He made rules for it. He couldn't help it; he'd made rules for everything since he was a boy, and Malcolm made rules for everything too, and between the two of them the poly class had once had a four-page document about whose turn it was to buy the drinks. *Same time every night. Half past nine. Same height for the square. Window directly under it. Don't push. Hold.* He wrote them on the back of a page of Malcolm's book, and Malcolm read them over his shoulder and added a fifth in his own hand, *IF John holds it more than an hour THEN Azac takes it off him*, and Azac nodded gravely.

And every night, at half past nine, he held the square over the fire with his eyes shut and the window under it and didn't push, and every night, at some point, something gave a hair and went through, and in the morning there'd be lines.

Not the picture. Never the picture. Nobody saw the fire, or the camp, or the downs, or Declan with his bowl. They saw a colour. A warmth. A shape where a line should be, with nothing in it.

"It's like a thumbnail," he said to Xav, on the seventh night, after. "You know. When the picture won't load. You get the box, and the colour of it, and nothing in it."

"So it's loading," said Xav.

"It's not loading. It's never going to load. It's a box with a colour in it." He held his head. "I can't get the picture through. The channel's words. You can't put a picture in words."

Xav thought about that. He was sitting on the other side of the fire with his practice sword across his knees, the way he sat everywhere now, like a man waiting for a bus he wasn't in a hurry for.

"Deston," said Xav.

Deston was on his back, the way he always was, on the grass, with his eyes shut. "Hm."

"When John does it. Do you hear anything?"

Deston didn't open his eyes. He didn't say anything for a long time, which was how you knew he was listening; he listened to everything, all the time, the threads, the choir, thirty-five of them going out across the kingdom in every direction like strings on an instrument the size of the world. He'd told them once, on the lake, that he could hear the whole of the channel as a sort of hum under the threads. That it was like hearing a radio in another room.

"Yeah," said Deston.

John sat up.

"Not in the channel," said Deston. Slowly. "That's what's weird. It's not in the channel. When you do it, the channel doesn't do anything. It's the threads. Something goes along the threads. Like somebody running their thumb along the strings. All of them at once." He opened his eyes and looked at the sky. "Very quiet. And then it gets to wherever the threads go, the far end, each one, and it—" He thought about it. "It knocks. On the window. From outside. Like a moth on a lamp."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"So it's not going in the channel," said John.

"No."

"It's going along the threads. To the windows. And knocking."

"Yeah," said Deston. "And they can't open it from inside, so they see a moth."

John lay back on the grass. He looked up at the motes, which were still up there over the fire, very small, gold, against the dark.

"That's worse," he said. "That's so much worse. I don't know how to do that at all."

"No," said Deston, and closed his eyes again, and smiled. "But it's yours. It's not Thea's. She built the channel. Nobody built this." He was quiet. "You're the first one using the threads for anything. Except me."

***

The forcing came on the eighth night.

He'd known it was coming. He'd known since the fifth, when she'd held a line for a count of five and stood in front of it not breathing until it went, and then brought the stick across again so fast her shoulders went up round her ears, and nothing. He'd watched her shoulders get higher every night since.

The seventh night she got nine.

They were up on the first of the downs by then, the long green hills east of the river country with nothing on them but sheep and wind and the odd stone barn; and they'd camped in a fold of the hill out of the wind, and the grass up there was short and very green and the sky was enormous. She went off along the fold after the fire, the way she did. Up the slope a little. And stood. For a long time; longer than usual. And brought the stick across.

And the line stayed.

He counted it. *One. Two. Three*. A thread of grey over the short grass at chest height, very thin, very straight, against a sky so big it made you dizzy. *Four. Five. Six.* She didn't move. Nobody did. He realised half the camp was watching; they'd stopped pretending not to on the downs, where there was nowhere to look but each other. *Seven. Eight.*

*Nine*.

It went.

And from somewhere in the grass behind her, a little way off, where there was nobody, a small hard thing went *tock* against her boot.

An acorn.

RX came out of the air beside her, sheepish, with his hand still half raised.

"Sorry," said RX. "Sorry. I wanted to see. If it would stop it. I threw it through the line before it went. Sorry." He looked at the acorn by her boot. "It went straight through."

Petra looked at the acorn.

Then at the air where the line had been.

"A line isn't a wall," she said.

She said it flatly. To nobody. In the voice she used on the gravel bar for things the class had got wrong, which was a very level voice that made the class feel like they'd been hit with a plank. RX went pink to the ears and disappeared again, completely, the way he did when he wanted the ground to open.

And she brought the stick across again.

And again. Fast, this time. With her shoulders up. He saw it start in her shoulders and go down into her arms, a kind of locking, the way you lock your arms when you're pushing a cart up a hill and you think if you just push *harder*. She brought the stick across and nothing happened and she brought it across again and nothing, and again, and he saw her plant her feet and do the thing he'd never seen her do in a year of dawns on the gravel bar, which was *push*.

The line came.

Thicker. That was the first thing. Not a thread; a cord. Grey. And it didn't hang behind the stick; it came off it with a noise, a low harsh sound like a file on iron, and stood in the air, shaking.

And Petra went down on her knees in the grass.

All at once. As if somebody had cut the strings. The stick came out of her hand and the line went out like a lamp and she was on her knees on the downs with both hands flat on the grass and her head down, and when she lifted her head her nose was bleeding. Not a little. All down her mouth and her chin and the front of her coat, black in the firelight.

Declan was there before anybody else.

John didn't see him go. He was by the fire, sitting down, eating, the way he'd sat down and eaten every night since PenguChi told him to; and then he was on the slope, on one knee beside her, not touching her. Not saying anything. Just there, a pace off, the way he'd been there on the shingle beside Bram.

She didn't look at him.

"I'm all right," said Petra. Thickly. Into the grass.

"I know," said Declan.

"I pushed it."

"I know."

"It came. When I pushed." She spat blood into the grass. "It came thicker. It made a noise."

He didn't say anything to that.

She stayed on her knees for a long time. Then she got up, slowly, on her own, and picked up the stick, and stood there with it and the blood drying on her chin, and looked at the place where the line had been. And then she went back down the slope to the fire and sat at the end of the circle in her place and didn't say anything else that night, and nobody asked her to.

In the morning she couldn't close her hand.

Her right. The stick hand. She tried at breakfast, sitting on a stone with her porridge, and John watched her try: the fingers curling in a little way and stopping, as if there were something in her palm she couldn't see. She looked at it with a sort of distant soldierly interest, the way she'd looked at the perch on the jetty. And then picked up her spoon in her left, and ate.

Malcolm wrote something in his book, and tore the page out, and walked across the camp and gave it to her.

She couldn't read it. It was in English. She held it out to John, without looking at him.

"*IF Petra,*" John read. "*THEN sit down.*"

Petra looked at the page for a while.

Then she folded it, one-handed, and put it in her coat.

***

Her hand came back in three days. She didn't try with the stick again for those three days. She carried it under her arm and didn't take it out. At night she stood, the way she'd stood every morning for ten years, an hour at a time, out at the edge of wherever they'd camped, with her hands open at her sides; and when the hour was done she came back to the fire and sat down.

John didn't know what she was thinking. He thought, maybe, she didn't either.

On the eleventh day they came down off the downs into Ashby.

He'd heard of Ashby from Declan's side of the channel; Group 3 had passed through it on the way east last summer, and Wendy had written about it. A posting-house town where the north road came down off the hills and joined the east road from Valcrest, with a market cross and a long low inn with a yard, and a school. Small. Tidy. The kind of town that looks as if it's been the same for three hundred years and is mildly offended at the idea that it might be asked to change.

There was a woman called Annis who ran the posting-house. She came out into the yard when they came in, a short brisk woman with her sleeves rolled up and a cloth over her shoulder, and looked at all ten of them, and at the man at the back with the water skins, and put her hands on her hips.

"You," said Annis.

"Me," said Declan.

"Last year you came through with a cart and a girl and five strange children and a snake," said Annis. "And a boy saluted your buckets." She looked at Alea. "And you. You're taller."

"I'm Honed," said Alea.

"I don't know what that means and I don't want to," said Annis. "Is it catching?" She looked at the rest of them. At Gabe with his sledge. At RX, who'd just appeared out of nowhere at the back of the yard and made one of her stable boys drop a bucket. "Rooms," said Annis. "And the big table. And don't let that one near the horses."

***

The boy who'd saluted the buckets was called Garth, and he wasn't there. He was at Cinderhold. He'd gone east last summer and become a recruit, and stood right on a knoll in the spring in front of a horned thing, and had his ribs broken, and been carried home by an elf. Annis told them all of it at supper, because the whole of Ashby knew all of it; Garth's mother had been reading his letters out in the market every week since. Annis told it with a cloth over her shoulder and her chin up, the way people tell a thing about their own town that they're proud of and don't want to look proud of.

"And it was *his* school," said Annis. "Down the end of the street. Garth's. He was a terrible scholar. He used to stand at the back in the yard and salute the carts." She sniffed. "Mistress Marlow's got the little ones in it now. You'll see them in the morning. They come out to look at anybody with a sword."

They did come out.

In the morning, when the ten of them came down the street from the posting-house toward the east road, the school had let out early for it; there were twenty children in the lane outside a low stone building with a bell on the gable, standing in a ragged line along the wall, staring. The littlest at the front. A woman behind them in a grey dress, Mistress Marlow, with her arms folded, not stopping them.

They'd heard about the man with the rod. Of course they had. They stared at him the way you'd stare at the sea.

John put the motes up. He didn't decide to; he always did, coming into a new place, the way you'd look round a room. Up over the lane. Looking down. The school. The children. The ten of them coming down the street with Alea in front and Declan at the back with the skins. A carter's wagon coming up the street the other way, toward the market, a big one, loaded with sacks, and a heavy bay horse in the shafts with feathered feet and a white blaze, and the carter on the box half asleep.

And a child.

He saw it from above before anybody saw it from the street. One of the littlest, at the end of the line, a boy, four or five, in a smock too big for him, who'd seen something on the far side of the lane, a dog, maybe, or a cat, and broken out of the line and run.

Straight across. Under the horse.

Not under it. In front of it. Between the bay's front feet and the wall, where the lane narrowed, a small fast shape going across the cobbles in a smock, and the horse seeing it at the last moment the way horses do, and throwing its head up, and its front feet coming up off the ground, both of them, high, the great feathered hooves going up into the air over the boy's head as the horse reared—

He didn't see Petra move.

None of them did. He found that out afterwards; he asked every one of them, and none of them had seen her go. She'd been in the middle of the ten, walking, with her sword on her hip and the stick under her arm, and then she was in the lane in front of the horse with her back to the boy. Between them. And her sword was out. Not the stick. The stick was on the cobbles behind her where it had fallen out from under her arm. Her own sword, the long plain blade she'd carried since she was a recruit, the one she'd put back in its sheath like a broken tool on the third night.

She brought it across. Low and flat, at the height of the horse's chest. The way she brought it across a hundred times a morning on a gravel bar to show a class where the line went.

And a line stayed.

He saw it from above, in the motes. Grey. Thin. Hanging in the air across the lane at the height of a horse's chest, between the horse and the woman and the boy behind her, perfectly straight.

The horse's hooves came down.

Not on her. Not on the boy. They came down and the horse's whole great weight came down with them, and the horse *flinched*. Sideways. Away from the line. As if it had come down onto something it didn't like the feel of, the way a horse shies from a gate it can see and you can't. Its feet came down on the cobbles a pace to the left of where they should have, with a sound like a dropped anvil, and it slewed, and the wagon slewed behind it, and the carter woke up with a shout and hauled on the reins, and the whole thing came to a stop with the bay's head tossing and its eyes white and its near front hoof planted two feet from Petra's boots.

The line was gone.

The boy was standing in the lane behind her in his smock with his mouth open.

Nobody moved.

Then Mistress Marlow came down off the wall and across the lane at a pace John had never seen a schoolmistress move and picked the boy up under the arms and carried him back to the line like a sack of meal, saying a great many things to him in a very low voice that John was fairly sure weren't for children. And the carter climbed down off the box, white, and went to the horse's head. And Petra stood there in the middle of the lane with her sword in her hand, looking at it.

At the sword. Not the stick. The sword.

She looked round, after a while, and found the stick on the cobbles behind her, where it had fallen. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she looked up, along the lane, past all of them, at the back of the ten, where a man stood with four water skins on his shoulder and a rod, who hadn't moved at all. Who'd stood there with his hand on the strap of the skins and watched the whole thing happen and not moved.

He didn't say anything.

She sheathed her sword. Picked up the stick. Put it under her arm. And walked on down the lane toward the east road without a word, past the school and the twenty children against the wall who were all staring at her now and not at him; and the ten of them went after her.

At the end of the lane, the littlest children, the ones at the front of the line, put their hands flat on their chests.

John didn't know who'd taught them that. He thought, probably, Garth.

***

He showed her that night.

He didn't ask if she wanted it. He'd never once shown her anything she hadn't asked for; that was the rule, his own, from the first morning on the gravel bar. But they'd camped a few miles out of Ashby on the east road in an old drovers' ring of stones, and after the fire she came and sat down beside him on the grass, which she'd never done, and didn't say anything, and he knew.

He opened the square over the fire.

The lane at Ashby, from above. The school. The line of children. The wagon. The boy breaking out of the line. The horse's head going up and its front feet coming off the ground.

And her. In the lane, between them, with her back to the boy. And her own sword coming across.

And the line.

He slowed it down. He could do that now; he'd learned it on the bar, last winter. He held it, so the horse hung there in the gold square over the fire with its hooves in the air and the line across the lane under them, grey, thin, straight, at the height of its chest.

"That's your sword," said John.

Petra didn't say anything.

"Not the stick. The stick's on the cobbles. Look." He moved the square a little, so the motes looked back along the lane; and there it was, a length of grey driftwood lying in the gutter where it had fallen out from under her arm, a long way from her hand. "You'd dropped it. You didn't have it. It was your sword."

She looked at it for a long time.

Then he let the picture go on. The hooves came down. The horse flinched off the line, sideways, the way it had in the lane, and its feet came down a pace to the left, and the wagon slewed, and the line went.

"Again," said Petra.

He did it again. And again. Six times. She watched every one. Round the fire the others were watching too, not pretending not to, Gabe and Deston and Xav and Malcolm and Azac and RX, visible, for once, and the girl on the far side with her ears forward; and Declan, sitting on a stone at the edge of the ring with his bowl, eating, not looking at it. He'd seen it the first time. He didn't need to see it again.

"It's not the stick," said Petra.

She said it very quietly. To the square.

"No," said Xav.

She didn't look at him. She looked at the horse, hanging in the gold over the fire with its hooves up, and the line under them.

"I didn't decide," she said. "I didn't think. I didn't *try*. I didn't—" She stopped. "There was a boy. Behind me. I was in front of him."

Nobody said anything.

She sat there by the fire, with the stick across her knees and the folded page from Malcolm's book in her coat, and looked at herself in a lane in Ashby, from above, standing between a horse and a boy with her sword out and a line across the air in front of her, and her face was doing something John had never seen it do. He'd have said, about anybody else, that she was frightened.

He let the square go out.

She didn't move for a long time. Then she got up and went off a little way, out of the ring of stones, into the dark at the edge of the drovers' road, the way she did every night; and stood.

She didn't take the stick out. She stood with it under her arm, for an hour, with her hands at her sides. And then she came back and lay down by the fire and slept, for the first time on the road, all night.

He tried to send it.

Not then. Later. At half past nine, by his rules, with the square over the fire and his eyes shut and the window under it. He'd kept the horse in the motes; he could pull it back up whenever he liked. So he did. He put the lane at Ashby in the square, the horse rearing, the line, the boy, and held it there, and opened his window under it, and didn't push.

He held it for the whole hour. Azac didn't have to take it off him; he took it off himself, at a quarter past ten, because his head hurt.

In the morning there were lines.

RX beat Declan at cards on the fourteenth night.

It happened in a barn. A long stone barn on the last of the downs, the last before the grass went grey, where a shepherd let them sleep in the loft for a copper and a story; and Azac told the story, the whole of the Lists, in the right order, with voices, and the shepherd laughed at the bits that were funny and some that weren't, and gave them a lamp and a pack of cards.

They were the kingdom's cards. Five suits. Crowns and Ploughs and Ships and Keys and a fifth one nobody in the poly class could identify that Declan said was Bells, and pictures on the high ones that looked like woodcuts of kings who'd all been drawn by the same not very good artist. Declan taught them the game. It was the game everyone played on the border, he said, in the garrison, in the winter. *Ditch*, it was called. You laid cards in a line and tried to hold it.

RX learned it in ten minutes.

He'd always been like that. In the poly class, in every game they'd ever played: he'd sit there for ten minutes not saying anything, watching, and then he'd play once and lose and then he'd be better than you. He'd been better than everybody at everything except one thing, and the one thing was Declan, at the rhythm game, with Hidden on everything, for six years. RX had chased his scores for six years and never once caught them.

He beat Declan at Ditch in an hour.

Not once. Three times in a row, with the last of the lamp going and the shepherd's dog asleep on Malcolm's feet. The third time, he laid the last card down, the high Bell, a woodcut of a fat king holding what looked like a fish, on the end of a line that Declan couldn't break; and sat back on the hay; and looked at Declan.

Declan looked at the cards for a long time.

Then he laughed.

The real one. The one John had heard on a hundred streams at three in the morning, the one that came up out of him from somewhere he didn't control, too loud for the barn, so that the dog woke up and the shepherd swore from below. On the seventeenth day the grass went grey.

He'd known it would. They all had. Group 3 had written about it in the first month, and Wendy had written about it last summer, and Declan had said, at the lake, *there's a day where the grass goes grey and you'll know*. But knowing a thing and seeing it were two different things.

It didn't happen all at once. They came down off the last of the downs in the morning, and the grass on the lower slopes was green, and then a little further on it was paler, and then a little further on it was the colour of ash, of old rope, of a sky before rain; long and fine and dry, all the way to the horizon, under a white sky, and moving. All the time. Shivering, very slightly, with no wind. And whispering. A long low hush, everywhere, from every direction at once, like the sea heard from a long way inland.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Gabe, who never said anything first, said, "Oh, I don't like that."

"No," said Declan, from the back.

"Does it stop?"

"It stops round me," said Declan. "Mostly. You'll see."

On the nineteenth night, from the top of the last rise before the red, John held the square up over the plain and sent it.

He didn't mean to. That was the thing. He hadn't meant to send anything that night; it wasn't half past nine; it was an hour before sunset, and they'd only stopped because Alea had stopped, at the top of a long low rise in the grey grass, and stood there, and the rest of them had come up beside her one by one and stood there too.

And there it was.

The red.

He'd seen it in other people's light. In Group 3's lines, three years of them. In the scar on Rhea's scalp and the way Salty's hands shook sometimes and the way Wendy counted things. He'd never seen it with his own eyes. It lay in front of them all the way to the edge of the world: a plain the colour of rust, of old blood, of a kiln, flat, enormous, with the grey grass going down into it in long fingers and stopping at the edge as if it had been told not to go further. And far off across it, very small, on a low rise, a fort. Grey walls. A tower. Smoke. And beyond that, at the very edge, a black line of mountains against a sky that was going gold.

The sun was behind them. It laid their shadows out in front of them across the grey grass, ten long thin shadows going down the slope toward the red; and the red took the light and went up like a fire.

Nobody said anything.

He put the motes up without deciding to. High. Higher than he'd ever put them, up into the gold air over the rise, so that they could look down at the whole of it: the grey grass and the long fingers of it and the red going on and on to the mountains, and the fort, and the ten of them on the top of the rise, very small, with their shadows going out in front of them.

And opened the square. Not over a fire. Over the plain. Huge, the biggest he'd ever made it, gold, hanging in the air above the rise, with the red in it from above, burning.
