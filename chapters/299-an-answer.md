He didn't know how to start a letter that wasn't an answer.

He found that out on the first evening, at the table, with the lamp lit in the window behind him and a sheet of Alea's cream paper in front of him and a pen he'd borrowed from Thea, who had six. He sat there till the lamp needed trimming. He trimmed it. He sat there till the lamp needed trimming again.

He'd written a great many things. More than people thought. Books, under a name that wasn't his, that Oswin in Solmere had read till the spines broke. Years of letters to Garrow about hooks and to Rowan about fish and to the quartermaster at Cinderhold, once, about a saddle. Fish, to Group 6, when they'd asked. And Halvard.

Halvard had written to him a great many times, these last two years. Since the autumn night on the end of the jetty when he'd burned eleven letters he'd never sent in the lamp, with Ars on the boards beside him. By rider, at first; then down one of Hana's cords to the militia hall. *How are the hens*. That was how every one of them began. Sometimes there was more under it: the Summoners' Hall, the leaves, *Oswin is exact*. Sometimes there wasn't. *How are the hens*.

And that had been the shape of it. Halvard wrote, and asked something, and Ars answered. The hens. The lake. Whether the ice was in.

He'd never once written first. Not to Halvard. Not to anybody, really, since he was nineteen.

He looked at the paper.

He wrote *Halvard*, at the top, in the native hand, and then sat and looked at that for a long time.

***

The trouble was that everything he wanted to say was an answer.

He tried. He wrote the hill. All of it. The black ground for weeks, and the horse lying down on the tenth day, and the saddle he'd carried three days because he'd have to pay for it. The hill on its own on the plain, round like a bowl, pale green. The grass in rings with black between, and spokes from the top down. The sword at the top where the spokes met, point down, with rain-tracks round it. A day to pull it. The ground breathing out.

He wrote the purse book. A forge in the north yard of the old keep, and a man folding a star fifteen times, and a woman writing the same few strokes before every fold, ending in a loop. *To hold the mouth*. Two horses and bread for forty days and the road east.

He wrote Yeva's song. All six verses. He knew them now; Calvin had sung them at the table and he'd heard them once and that was enough. *Six rings, and no hands, and a boy from the west*.

He wrote the girl at the well.

He filled four sheets of Alea's cream paper. It was the longest thing he'd written since the last book. And he read it over, at midnight, with the lamp hissing behind him, and it was all true, and every word of it was an answer. To questions nobody had asked him. To *what happened*, and *where did you find it*, and *whose was it*. He'd written it the way he'd have answered Halvard if Halvard had asked, in a letter, very correctly, under a great deal of greeting.

Halvard hadn't asked. Halvard had never once asked, in eighteen years.

Because Ars had told him not to.

He put the four sheets in the stove.

***

He went fishing in the morning. He always did, when he didn't know what to do; it was what the lake was for.

The old rod, with the hook on it. *RESURGAM*. He'd cast it every morning since Gabe had put it in his palm on the shingle, and it had come up every morning, and he still didn't know why that mattered so much. It was a hook. It came up. Hooks did, mostly. But this one had a thread on it to Gabe, Deston said, the brightest thing on the lake, and when he cast it he could feel Gabe in it like a hand on the rod, and when it came up out of the dark water and swung in toward his hand with the word on the shank catching the light, he felt, every time, like somebody had said his name.

He cast it now. The line went out over the lake in the grey, a long way, and the hook went down into the dark.

He thought about the gate.

***

He didn't, much. He'd put it where you put things. But Alea had heard him on the jetty with Deston, from the bench, because the wind was right; and Aleaka had felt it in Solmere and said in the channel that she'd felt what somebody carried and wouldn't say whose. And he'd known, reading that, whose. There was only one person it could be.

The east gate of Cinderhold, at dawn, at the end of a summer. Eighteen years ago. Nearly nineteen.

He'd come in out of the red with the sword on a strap over his shoulder, dragging it, the point cutting a line in the road all the way back into the black. His hands in rags. He hadn't slept, really, in a month. He'd walked the last night through without stopping because he'd been able to see the fort's lights and couldn't make himself lie down with them in sight. And the gate had been shut, the way it always was at dawn, and there'd been a ward on it. A good one. Singing.

He'd stood under it and looked up.

Halvard had been on the wall over the gate. The garrison's strategist: the court mage from Solmere who'd come up behind a fifteen-year-old on the parapet one midnight four years before, and sat down on the stones, and stared at his window till dawn. Thin, in a good coat, with his hands on the parapet. Looking down. And Ars had looked up at him, and known, the way you know things after a month alone in the black, that Halvard was afraid of him.

Not of the sword. Of him.

*Open it, Halvard*, he'd said. Just that. The gate guard was in the gatehouse with the dogs under the wagons, all of them, the dogs and the guard, not looking at him.

And he hadn't opened it.

Ars had stood there. He hadn't counted. He'd never counted; he'd never been a man who counted things, it was other people who did that. But it had been a long time. Long enough to feel the ward singing in the iron of the gate, and in the iron on his back, the two of them saying something to each other he couldn't hear. Long enough to think: *he's not going to open it*. Long enough to not mind, very much, because he was nineteen and tired and he'd found a sword and nothing much mattered.

And then the bar had gone back. Halvard had come down off the wall and drawn it himself. And the gate had opened, and Diwa had come out of the infirmary with Alea on her hip, and put her down on the step, and hit him.

A week later, on the wall, Halvard had come and stood beside him on watch, and not said anything for a long time. And Ars had known he was going to ask. *Where did you find it. What is it. Why are the dogs afraid of you*. He'd felt it coming the way you feel weather.

*Don't ask me*, he'd said.

And Halvard hadn't. Not then. Not in eighteen years. Not when he was Advisor, not when he filled a hall with thirty-five people from the other world and a black sword lying in the middle of the circle as the medium. Not in all those letters about hens. Never once.

He'd done what he was told.

Ars stood on the end of the jetty with the line out and the hook down in the dark and understood, for the first time, standing there in the grey, that he'd done that to somebody. Said *don't ask me* to a frightened man on a wall, and walked away, and let him carry it for eighteen years. Whatever it was. The gate, and the count, and the not opening, and whatever he'd written in the log that morning. Eighteen years of not asking, because a boy with bleeding hands had told him not to, and he'd been too ashamed of the count to argue.

Ars hadn't asked either. He'd never once asked Halvard why the gate took so long.

They'd been standing on either side of the same shut gate for eighteen years.

The line went tight.

He reeled in. Not a fish. The hook, coming up out of the dark, bright, swinging in toward his hand with the word on the shank. *RESURGAM*. He caught it. He stood on the end of the jetty and looked at it a long time.

*I shall rise again*. Off a gravestone, Gabe said. When he was eleven.

He thought it might be a word for a letter, too.

***

He wrote it that night.

Not four sheets. He knew that, before he sat down. He'd written four sheets of answers and burned them, and he wasn't going to do it again. He sat at the table with the lamp behind him and one sheet of Alea's cream paper and Thea's pen, and he didn't think about it. He thought about the gate, and the man on the wall, and *don't ask me*. And he wrote what he'd have said, if he'd been the one on the wall.

*Halvard,*

*I pulled it out.*

*I didn't know.*

He stopped. He looked at it.

That was all of it. Everything else was in Group 4's hands, and Cursiffe's, and a purse book in an archive under the old keep, and a song an old woman on a wharf had kept for forty years. Halvard could find all of that. Halvard was very good at finding things. He'd found Ars, once, at a lake, when nobody else in the kingdom had thought to look; he'd find the rest. He didn't need it written down. He needed to be told that Ars knew.

That was the thing. Not *what happened*. That Ars knew it, now. That the sword hadn't been a sword. That there'd been something under it. That a boy of nineteen had pulled a nail out of a hill in the middle of the black and dragged it home on a strap, and stood under a gate with it, and asked to be let in; and that the man on the wall who'd been afraid to open the gate had been *right*.

He wrote:

*Tell the king.*

And then he sat there a long time.

The lamp hissed. Outside, on the lake road, Gigi was on her bench; he could hear her, very faintly, humming something with a tune. In the loft, Alea turned over, on the fourth rung, and was still. The kettle on the side of the stove ticked as it cooled.

He thought about the wait.

He'd never counted it. Aleaka had said in the channel only *eighteen years*, and *it's his*, and nothing else. But he could guess. It hadn't been long, under the gate, with the ward singing. Not really. He'd waited longer for a pike.

And Halvard had carried it for eighteen years.

He wrote one more line. He didn't decide to. It came out of him from the same place the third move had come out of Xav on the shingle, from under the deciding.

*You can ask me now.*

He looked at it.

Then he signed it, the way he signed everything, with the one letter. *A.* And under it, because Halvard had once drawn him a very bad fish and he'd never let him forget it, a small one. Better.

He folded it. He didn't seal it. He didn't have a seal; he'd never needed one. He wrote *Halvard* on the outside, and *Solmere, the west tower*, and put it inside his coat, flat, beside the place where he'd kept Group 4's letter all spring, the one on the back of a soup bill, and the hook when he wasn't fishing.

He sat there with his hand on his coat.

He'd written first.

***

He took it up to Wrenhaven himself in the morning.

He could have given it to anybody. Rowan would have taken it. The Tallow boy who'd brought Group 4's letter down at ice-out would have run it up the lake road for a copper and been proud of it all week. Alea would have taken it, and not read it, and put it in the post-house with her own hands. Higu would have taken it and written *SENT* in her notebook in capitals.

He didn't ask anybody. He walked up the lake road at first light with it inside his coat.

Gigi was on her bench. She looked at him as he went past, at his face, and at the flat place in his coat where his hand was, and didn't say anything. She didn't ask. She'd never once asked him anything except *you okay*, and she didn't ask that either.

She lifted her hand. A little. The way you'd lift it across a field.

He lifted his.

Up the lake road. Past the dairy at the top, where Dennis had bought his cheese. Over the rise, where you could see the town: the roofs and the chapel and the militia hall and the smoke going up straight in the still morning. Down the long hill into the market square, where Odette was putting out eggs and stopped, and looked at him, and didn't drop any this time. Past the Hollin brothers' yard, and Garrow's, where the old man was at the door with his spectacles on his forehead and nodded. To the post-house on the corner of the square, by the well, where the riders changed horses and the mail went south and west on the post road.

The post-mistress was a woman called Hennet who'd been at the post-house since before Ars came to the lake, and had taken in every one of the Advisor's letters that came by rider, and handed every one of them to somebody to take down to the cabin, and never once seen one go the other way. She looked at him over the counter.

He put the letter on it.

Hennet looked at it. At the one word on the outside, and the second line. *Halvard. Solmere, the west tower*. She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked up at him.

"Well," she said.

"How much."

"For that?" Hennet picked it up, very carefully, by the corners, the way you'd pick up something that might break. "Nothing. It's on the Crown. Anything to the west tower's on the Crown, has been since the year you came, I've the order in the drawer." She looked at him. "He sent it the first year you were here. *Anything the fisherman sends me, I'll pay for. Whatever it is*." She turned the letter over in her hands. "I've never had to use it."

Ars didn't say anything.

"The rider goes at noon," said Hennet. "He'll be in Solmere in nine days if the road's dry, and it is." She put the letter in the leather bag on the hook behind her, on top, by itself. "I'll tell him it's to go to the west tower by hand. Not the Chancellor's clerks. By hand."

"Thank you."

Hennet looked at him a moment longer.

"I'll not tell anybody," she said.

"You can," said Ars.

She looked at him.

"You can tell anybody you like," he said. "It's a letter. People send them." He found he didn't mind. He found, standing in the post-house at Wrenhaven with his hands empty, that he didn't mind at all. "I've just never sent one before."

***

He watched the rider go at noon.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to walk home. But he'd sat down on the edge of the well in the market square to let his legs decide, and they'd decided to stay, and he sat there in the sun with the town going about round him, and nobody bothered him, the way nobody in Wrenhaven ever bothered him. Odette brought him an egg, boiled, and a twist of salt, and went away again without saying anything. Rowan came out of the militia hall and saw him and stopped and lifted his cup, from across the square, the way he had on the morning Ars ran west, and went back in.

At noon the rider came out of the post-house yard on a big bay with the leather bag across the saddle, and Hennet came out after him and said something to him and pointed at the bag; and he looked across the square at Ars on the well, and touched his hat. And went out of the west road at a trot. Up the hill. Over the top. Gone.

Ars sat on the well.

He thought about Halvard opening it. In the west tower, at the top, in the study with the map on the wall, the blank inch in the south and the east past the Redmarch black to the edge. Nine days. Ten. A rider coming up the stairs with a letter by hand, because Hennet had said, and Halvard taking it, and seeing the outside, and the one word, and knowing the hand. He'd know the hand. He'd had all those answers in it.

He thought about Halvard seeing it wasn't an answer.

He didn't know what Halvard would do. He found he didn't need to. That was the strange thing. He'd always needed to know, before. What a thing would do when he let go of it. Whether a fish would run. Whether a man on a wall would open a gate. He'd spent his whole life here not asking, so he'd never have to find out.

He'd sent it anyway. He'd let go of it. He'd never find out exactly what it did, probably, the moment it landed, in a tower nine days away. And he was sitting on the edge of a well in the sun with an egg.

He opened his window. Not on purpose. His hand did it, the way it had on the jetty at midwinter.

Sixty-eight.

He looked at it for a long time. Sixty-seven since midwinter. Since the road home from Thornwall and *Come home* with a line through it. He hadn't looked since. He hadn't expected it to move; it hadn't moved for a hill, or a song, or a purse book, or a nail hole in the middle of a valley. It hadn't moved for *I pulled it out*.

It had moved for a letter. Three lines, and a fish.

He didn't know why. He thought he might, one day. He thought it might be the same thing that had moved it at midwinter, which he hadn't understood then either: a hand flat on a post beside a boy's, saying nothing. A lamp lit for himself. A letter written first. All the things he'd never done, because there'd always been somebody else to do them for, or a reason not to, or a gate he'd decided was shut.

*Find your own way to ascend*, the old saying went. Everybody knew it. He'd chosen his ten years ago, by a lake, without knowing he was choosing: fishing, and a garden, and a girl growing up, and tea going cold. Slow living. He'd thought it meant stopping.

He thought, sitting on the well in Wrenhaven in the sun, that it might mean the opposite. That the slow way up wasn't sitting still. It was doing, very slowly, one at a time, all the things he'd spent his whole life too fast to do.

He shut the window.

***

He walked home in the afternoon.

Down the long hill, past the dairy, along the lake road, with the lake going blue below him in the sun and the hens in the yard, and the red one in the cabin window looking at him the way she did. Gigi was still on her bench. She'd been there all day, by the look of the cup beside her, which was cold and full.

She looked up as he came.

"You okay?" she said.

He stopped.

He thought about it. Properly, the way he'd learned to that spring, because she'd asked and you answered what you were asked. He thought about the hill, and the girl at the well, and the two in the forge with nobody at the other end, and a mouth in the middle of a valley with nothing in it. He thought about twenty-seven people with nothing inside them standing in a curve at the top of a valley, and four more on the far side of a pass, and a hundred standing on the Redmarch all night with their backs to Cinderhold, facing east, toward a hill he'd stood on once at nineteen and never gone back to. He thought about a letter on a big bay horse going west up a hill toward a tower.

"Not yet," he said.

Gigi nodded slowly.

"But nearer," he said.

She looked at him for a long time. Then she moved along the bench, a little, the roots creaking, so there was room at the end.

He sat down on it.

It was the best bench he'd ever sat on. He'd said so. It had a back now, and leaves, and it was warm from the sun and from her sitting on it all day. They sat there side by side on the lake road and looked at the lake, and didn't say anything, and after a while Alea came out of the cabin with two cups and came up the shingle and gave him one and Gigi the other, and they were hot.

"Did you send it," said Alea.

"Yes."

"First?"

"First."

Alea didn't say anything. She stood beside the bench with her hand on his shoulder, and looked down at the jetty, and the lake, and the far shore.

"That's three," she said.

"That's three."

"I'll think of another one," said Alea.

"Take your time," said Ars.

At dusk he went down and lit the lamp. Himself. For himself. And put it in the window, where you could see it from the end of the jetty, and the lake road, and the top of the last hill on the way home. And went out on the last plank with his cup, and sat down with his legs over the edge, and looked back at it.

It was lit.

Somebody was home.
