Hana carried the sheets the whole way like a bird.

Not because they were fragile. They weren't; Higu's paper was the thick cream kind the Lance sold by the quire, and the ribbon was tied in a knot Higu had learned off Alea, and you could have dropped the whole bundle in the road and stepped on it and it would have been fine. She carried them like a bird because that was what she'd done with her hands when Higu held them out across the table, without thinking, and once she'd started she couldn't stop. In both hands on the cart. Against her chest at night, under the blanket. In her lap at the noon halt, with her tail round them.

She didn't look at them.

That was the other thing. She could have. Nobody had said she couldn't. Higu had untied the ribbon at the table at the Lance to show her, and Hana had seen the first sheet, five frames to a page, a small ring with lines going out of it and a short arc on the right, in Higu's careful pencil, with the frame's number in the corner and the ticks drawn across the arc like stitches. She'd seen that much and looked away, the way you'd look away from someone's diary that had fallen open.

*Tell him not to guess*, Higu had said.

Hana knew herself. If she looked, she'd guess. She'd look at the ticks for a whole afternoon on a jolting cart and make up a story about them, a kind one probably, with everyone in it, and by the time she got to Solmere she'd believe it, and she'd hand Oswin the sheets with the story still on her face. And Oswin would see it, because Oswin saw everything he pretended not to, and it would get into the work.

So she held them like a bird and looked at the road.

***

It was eight days north-west to Solmere. The same road as last summer, the other way. Milli sulked for the first two.

Not loudly. Milli never did anything loudly; he sulked the way he did everything else, with his tail very still and his face very pleasant. He'd gone out in the quarters to Niko. In front of forty thousand people, in the one fight in the whole Lists he'd wanted to win, because he'd beaten Niko in the Swiss and everyone had seen it, and then in the quarters Niko had just *not been there* when he pounced, and he'd landed on nothing, and Niko had touched him on the back of the neck very gently with the flat of a blunt sword, and said *again* in a voice that meant it as a gift.

"He meant it nicely," said Hana, on the second evening, at the fire.

"I know he meant it nicely," said Milli. "That's what's so annoying."

Jared laughed. Jared had gone out in the quarters too, to Wendy, and didn't seem to mind at all; he'd planted himself and she'd simply not been where he'd planted himself against, and three arrows had come down out of the air and tapped him on the head one after another like somebody knocking on a door. He'd laughed on the sand. He laughed now.

"Kestra'll say *again*," said Jared. "When we get back. To both of us."

"Kestra's at Thornwall with the king."

"Then she'll say it when *she* gets back," said Jared. "She'll have heard. She'll come in the door and look at us and say it. You know she will."

Milli thought about that, and his tail moved, a little, at the end. It was the first time it had moved in two days.

Leaka sat at the edge of the fire with a vixen asleep against her knee. The vixen had come out of the hedge at the noon halt and followed the cart for six miles and then simply got in. Nobody had said anything. Nobody ever did, now, with Leaka. The castle guards had looked at each other and decided it was above their pay.

Cursiffe sat beside Hana, writing.

He was always writing. He'd written all the way to Valcrest and all through the Lists and he was writing now, by the fire, on his knee, in the small neat hand he used for English: notes for the cords, things Oswin had asked him to ask. He looked up at the sheets in Hana's lap.

"You haven't looked," he said.

"No."

"I'd have looked by now." He went back to his writing. "I'd have looked on the first day. I'd have looked in the Lance, in the yard, before Higu had finished tying the ribbon." A pause. "That's why she gave them to you."

Hana didn't answer. She put her hand flat on the top sheet, over the ribbon, and felt the paper, warm from her lap.

***

Every night the cords.

Seven halves on a purple string round her left wrist, and a gold half-heart. She'd sat at the fire every night of the Lists with them warm and quiet, because everyone at the other ends had been in Valcrest with her and there'd been nothing to carry. Now they were all going away from each other, on four roads and one more along the sea, and the cords had woken up one by one as the roads came apart.

Maren's first. The blue one. On the first night out, an hour after dark, warm under her thumb the way it went when somebody at the other end had her half in their hand: and the meaning came through the way it always did, not words but the shape of words, already turned into hers.

*Group 1 on the north road. Ars cooked. Thea asks: are they safe*.

*They're safe*, Hana sent back. *I haven't looked*.

A pause. Then, in the same blue warmth, but different, quicker, the way meaning came when it was Thea's and not Maren's even though it was Maren's hand on the cord:

*Good. Don't*.

Then Corvin's, on the third night, from Thornwall, slow and plain as a stone: *The king is on the tower with Hyvred. Seven still turned. Basket still there. He asks after the holder*.

She'd told him. All of it. The heels on the chalk, the count to three, the silver going along Astra's edge. The paper rods. *Same as last year. And of Gullhaven*. She'd sent it on Corvin's cord at the third night's fire with her eyes shut, all at once, the way she told things, with every name in; and an hour later the cord had gone warm again, and it was the king, and it said only:

*Good. Tell me when Oswin lays it down*.

She'd looked at that one for a long time. *Lays it down*. The king knew. Of course the king knew. Oswin would have written to him in the spring, the day he understood what he wanted to do with Higu's copy, before Higu had even made it. Oswin wrote to the king about everything. The king had made the oak case.

Aldous's, on the fourth night, from the east road. Group 3 two days out. Maigo had said a word. One word. Nobody would tell her what it was. Yan was tired.

The Kingsferry cord, from the river. Dennis had hired a boat. Calvin had fallen in. Gabe had pulled him out with one hand. Rachael said Calvin was fine and was not to be told he was fine, because it would only encourage him.

The Duskhollow cord, in a voice like a door banging: Group 6 still on the road and nobody eating properly. Petra walking at the back with her stick. John filming the hedges for practice. The channel would have it.

Thornwall again. The Valcrest cord, where Group 7 had stayed: Lucan had stopped crying. Then started again.

And the gold half-heart, every night, last, just before she slept. Not words. Never words. Just warm, for as long as it takes to count to three, and then not.

She lay under her blanket at night with the sheets against her chest and the cords on her wrist and felt them all: seven roads and a gold one, going away from each other across the kingdom, and every one of them still tied to her.

She was the middle. She'd always been the middle. She'd kept a channel called *general* alive for six years in a server with four hundred people in it just by being in it every night, saying *hi* to whoever came in. She'd never minded. She'd liked it. It was the one thing she'd always been good at without trying.

She didn't mind now, either. But it was heavier, with the sheets.

***

Solmere came up out of the plain on the eighth afternoon, white, the way it always did, with the castle on its hill and the Summoners' Hall below the castle with its long grey roof, where thirty-five people had come up out of the floor in a burst of light two years and a half ago, all at once, holding their own breath.

Oswin was at the city gate.

He wasn't supposed to be. He was supposed to be in the archive, Edwy told them later, because the king had left him the keys and he'd promised not to leave them with anybody; and he'd left them with Edwy at noon and walked down through the whole city in his ink-stained archive coat to the north-west gate, and stood there for three hours in the sun, Edwy said, asking every carter that came in from the road if they'd passed a cart with a cat girl on it.

He didn't ask about the Lists. He didn't ask about the holder, or Gabe, or Niko, or the forty thousand paper rods, which he'd have heard about by cord anyway and would want to hear about from all five of them in turn, at great length, with questions, later. He came to the side of the cart while it was still moving and walked along beside it with his hand on the rail and looked at Hana's lap.

"Is that it?" said Oswin.

"Higu made a second copy," said Hana. "Over four nights. On the roof. She didn't tell anybody."

Oswin's face did something complicated.

"Of course she did," he said. "Of course she did." He looked at the ribbon. "She's tied it in a fisherman's knot."

"Alea taught her."

"Of course she did." He walked a few more paces beside the cart, with his hand on the rail. "Did she say anything? Higu? When she gave it to you?"

"She said tell you not to guess," said Hana. "She said put the frames down where they go and count what's there."

Oswin walked beside the cart in silence for a long time.

"Good," he said at last. "Yes. Good." And then, because he was Oswin and couldn't help it: "I'm going to *want* to, you know. Guess. I've wanted to since the spring. I've been crawling round that circle on my knees for four months wanting to." He looked up at her. "That's why I asked for Edwy."

***

The circle was on the floor of the long room.

Hana had seen it before, once. Last summer, in Valcrest, Oswin had brought it in a long roll and spread it in the archive room at the king's house, over the table and the floor, and Thea had walked round it reading the air above it, and Cursiffe had laid oiled sheets over it and written on them in blue English capitals, IT'S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX. Hana had only seen it from the door, half hidden under oiled sheets, with Thea on her knees in the middle of it.

It wasn't rolled up now.

Oswin had cleared the long room for it. The great table under the high windows, where the leaves lived in their tissue and Edwy checked lines, had been pushed against the wall, and the benches stacked on top of it; and on the bare boards in the middle, ten feet across, held flat at the edges by archive weights, books, two candlesticks, and an iron doorstop shaped like a sleeping dog, was the summoning circle.

All of it. Every ring. Fourteen of them, one inside the next, the way they were cut into the stone floor of the Hall at the foot of the hill, where the summoned had come through. Oswin had copied it in the first autumn, on his knees in the Hall, in ink, on linen paper; and every ring was full. Thirteen of them were full. Writing, all the way round each one, in the dense close script that Thea said wasn't script at all, but code: glyphs one after another in a band between two lines, round and round, like the words round the rim of a coin.

The fourteenth was outside the rest. The outermost ring.

Its left half was full, like the others. Written all the way round, the band crowded with glyphs.

Its right half was drawn. The two lines of the band were there, the inner and the outer, going on round from the top to the bottom, exactly as careful as everywhere else. But there was nothing between them. A long empty curve of bare paper between two inked lines, from the twelve o'clock to the six, on the right-hand side of the circle. Blank.

*A form with a blank box*, Cursiffe had written. *The way home*.

Hana had heard it described a hundred times. She'd never stood over it. She stood over it now, at the edge of the paper, in her travelling boots, with the sheets in her arms, and looked at the blank half of the outermost ring of the thing that had brought her here, and felt her tail go still.

"Shoes," said Edwy.

He was on the far side, by the windows. Kneeling, the way he always seemed to be kneeling when she saw him: a thin boy in an archive smock, with ink on both hands and his left sleeve rolled to the elbow. He wrote with his left. He hadn't always. Hana knew that from the cord and from the channel and from Oswin, who'd never said why and never had to.

"Sorry," said Hana, and took her boots off.

At the end of the long room, in a chair someone had brought from somewhere else, because it didn't match anything, the Warden of the Summoners' Hall sat with his hands in his lap and a pink bead on a linen thread between his fingers, and watched.

***

He came every morning. Edwy had told her by cord in the spring, and it was still true. He came at the second bell, up the hill from the Hall, slowly, because he was old and the hill was steep; and he came into the long room and sat at the end of the table, or in the chair now that the table was against the wall, and he watched Edwy work until noon, and then he went away again. He didn't speak much. He'd said *good morning, Edwy* on the hundred and tenth morning, after a hundred and nine mornings of Edwy saying it to him and getting nothing back; and he'd said it every morning since. That was most of what he said.

He was holding the bead.

Hana had made it in the spring. In this room. On a morning when he'd come in and stood at the end of the table looking at the leaves the way a man looks at a fire he's been told he mustn't put out, and she'd taken a bead off her own cuff and a bit of the linen thread off Oswin's spool and put a feeling into it. Not a charm. She'd been very careful about that. Just a feeling, the way a cord carried a word: *I'm not frightened of this*. Her own. Lent. She'd held it out, and he'd looked at it for a long time, and taken it.

*It isn't a charm on you*, she'd said. *You can put it down whenever you want*.

He hadn't put it down. Not once, that anybody had seen, in three months. He held it at the end of the table every morning between his finger and thumb, turning it, the way some old men turn a coin.

He looked at her now, across the circle, and at the sheets in her arms.

She went round the edge of the paper in her stockings, the long way, not crossing it, and stopped in front of his chair, and didn't know what to say.

"Good afternoon," she said.

The Warden looked at her for a long moment.

"Good afternoon, Hanarisu," he said.

***

They started the next morning. Oswin wouldn't start in the afternoon. "Bad light," he said, which wasn't true; the high windows let in light all day. What he meant was that he wanted to sleep on it, and lie awake on it, and get up at four and walk round the circle in the dark with a candle, and he did all three. Edwy told her. Edwy had been there at four too.

Oswin untied the ribbon at the second bell.

He did it on his knees at the edge of the circle, with the Warden in his chair and Edwy beside him and Group 2 standing round the walls, very quiet, because he'd asked them to be. He untied the fisherman's knot slowly, the way Alea did, and laid the twelve sheets out in a row on the boards, and looked at the first one for a long time without touching it.

Five frames. Small rings, a hand across, with lines going out of them through the ring and past it, and a mark in the middle of each, and a mark at the end of each line. And on the right of every ring, outside it, close against it, a short arc. And across every arc, ticks.

"Higu's numbers," said Oswin, softly. He touched the corner of the first sheet with one finger. "One to five. In the corners. The way I do the leaves." He looked up at Hana. "She copied my corners."

"She copies everything," said Hana.

Oswin got out his instruments.

He had a case of them. Hana had seen it once, in the winter: a long flat box of dark wood with a brass clasp, lined with green baize gone pale with age, and inside it, each in its own shaped place, a set of things she didn't have names for. A brass half-wheel with a line scored on it every finger's breadth and smaller ones between. Two pairs of dividers with steel points. A rule. A spool of fine silk cord, waxed. A pot of pins with glass heads. A little brass plumb on a thread, which Hana couldn't see the use of on a floor. It had been his master's, Oswin had said, in the winter, and his master's master's, and had been made by somebody in the archive for measuring the old maps, three hundred years ago.

*About the time*, he'd said, *somebody wrote the leaves*.

He opened it now and took out the half-wheel and the dividers, and bent over the first frame, and began.

***

It took four days.

Hana sent it on the cord every night. Not to everyone. To Maren's, the blue one, because Maren's went to Thea; and Thea was at the other end of it, every night, wherever Group 1 had stopped on the road north, a barn or an inn yard or the back of the miller's cart, with her hand over Maren's on the cord. Hana could feel her there. The blue cord went warm a quarter of an hour before Hana ever touched it, every night, and stayed warm; and when Hana sent, the meaning went into it like water into a dry riverbed.

*First day*, she sent. *He's measured frame one. He measured the ring. Then the arc. Where it starts and where it stops, on the half-wheel. Then every tick, where it sits along the arc. Edwy checked every number twice. He wrote it all down in a column. Then he stood up and went to the big circle with his silk and his pins*.

*And?*

*He scaled it*, Hana sent. *The ring in the frame to the thirteenth ring on the floor. He says the ring in a frame is the idea of the whole circle, like Thea said in the spring, so it should be the same as all thirteen together. The outside edge of the thirteenth. He pinned the silk round the thirteenth ring and measured it against the ring in the frame. Then he worked out how much bigger. He did it four times. Edwy did it twice*.

*And?*

*Then he took the arc*, Hana sent. *From the frame. Where it started and where it stopped. And put it on the big circle at the same size bigger. With the silk and the pins*.

She stopped. She hadn't meant to. Her thumb had come off the cord.

She put it back.

*It goes on the fourteenth ring*, she sent. *The blank half. Exactly. The top of the arc in frame one is a finger's breadth below the twelve o'clock on the big one, and the blank half on the big one starts a finger's breadth below the twelve o'clock. The bottom's the same. The width is the same. The inner line and the outer line. It's not like the blank half, Thea. It IS the blank half. It's just smaller*.

The blue cord was quiet for a long time. Warm. Quiet.

*Count*, it said at last. In Thea's quickness, through Maren's hand. *Not what it means. Count what's there*.

*He is*, Hana sent. *He's going to do the ticks tomorrow*.

***

He did the ticks on the second day.

There were nine on frame one. Nine small strokes across the arc, close together, unevenly spaced, like the marks a builder makes on a beam. Oswin measured where each one sat along the arc with the dividers and wrote it in his column and Edwy checked it; and then Oswin went to the big circle and walked the silk round the blank half of the fourteenth ring, very slowly, on his knees, with Edwy following with the pins, and put in a pin where each tick would be, if the arc in the frame were the size of the ring on the floor.

Nine glass-headed pins. In the blank band of the fourteenth ring. Between the inner line and the outer, in the bare paper where, on the left half of the same ring and on every one of the thirteen inside it, there was writing.

Oswin sat back on his heels.

"Nine lines out," said Edwy. Quietly. From the sheet, with his finger on it. "Frame one has nine lines going out of the ring. Nine ticks." He looked at his column. "It's the same as Higu's. And Thea's. One tick for every line. I checked."

"Twice," said Oswin.

"Twice."

Oswin got up off his knees, slowly, the way a man gets up who's been kneeling since dawn. He didn't say anything. He went back to the sheet and picked up the dividers and bent over frame two.

They did nine frames that day. Then ten the next. Then eighteen on the fourth day, because Oswin had stopped sleeping on it and was getting up at four and coming down with a candle and doing two before the bell, and Edwy was getting up at four too, and Hana found out on the fourth morning that Milli had been getting up at four as well, to sit on the stairs outside the long room and listen to them measure, and that Jared had been bringing him tea.

Frame after frame. The same ring. Different lines going out of it, different numbers, different directions, different marks at the ends. Six lines; six ticks. Three lines; three. One frame with eleven lines out, and eleven ticks across its arc crowded so close they nearly touched, and Oswin had to use the finer dividers. Every arc, scaled up, lay on the blank half of the fourteenth ring to a finger's breadth, top and bottom and width, the inner line and the outer. Every tick, scaled up, went into the blank band as a pin.

After the first day Oswin stopped putting the pins in. There wasn't room. The blank band would have been a hedge of glass heads by the tenth frame, all thirty-seven frames' worth of ticks pinned into the same half-ring on top of each other. He wrote the numbers in his column instead. Edwy checked them. And they went on.

The pins from frame one stayed. Nine glass heads in a half-ring of bare paper. Nobody moved them.

The Warden watched all of it from his chair. He came at the second bell every morning and didn't leave at noon any more. He stayed till dusk. He didn't speak. He held the bead.

***

*Frame thirty-seven*, Hana sent on the fourth night, late. *Eight lines out. Eight ticks. On the blank half. Same as all the others*.

*And thirty-eight?*

Hana sat on the step of the archive with the cord under her thumb and the castle quiet round her and the Hall's long roof below in the dark, and the city's lamps beyond it, and felt Thea's hand at the other end, a long way off, waiting, the way you'd feel somebody holding their breath in the next room.

*Thirty-eight's tomorrow*, she sent. *Oswin wanted to stop at thirty-seven. He said he wanted to sleep before the blank ones. He said it's different, measuring something that's there and measuring something that isn't*.

A long pause.

*He's right*, said the cord.

And then, after another pause, quieter, so quiet Hana almost didn't feel it: *Hana. Is anyone guessing?*

Hana thought about the long room. About Oswin on his knees with the silk, not saying anything, for four days. About Edwy following him with the pins and his column, checking every number twice, not saying anything either. About Milli on the stairs at four in the morning, and Jared with the tea, and Leaka's vixen asleep across the threshold, and Cursiffe not writing, for once, just sitting at the wall with his book shut, watching. About the Warden in his chair.

*Nobody's saying it*, she sent. *I think everybody's guessing*.

*Yes*, said the cord. *Me too*.

***

They did the blank ones on the fifth day.

It went fast. That was the strange thing. Thirty-seven frames had taken four days, because every tick had to be measured, and checked, and scaled, and checked; and the blank ones had no ticks. Oswin measured the ring, and the arc, and scaled the arc, and walked the silk round the fourteenth ring on his knees, and it lay on the blank half, exactly, top and bottom and width, like all the others. And there was nothing to put in it. And he wrote *none* in his column, and Edwy checked it, and wrote *none*, and Oswin bent over the next.

Thirty-eight. Four lines out. The arc, on the blank half. None.

Thirty-nine. None.

Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two. Lines going out, two, five, one, three; and the arc on the right of the ring, close against it, drawn as carefully as any of the others; and across it, nothing.

Hana stood at the wall with Group 2 and watched Oswin write *none* nineteen times.

She felt it in the room as it went. Not magic. Just the room. The way a room feels when a list is being read out and you're waiting for a name. Thirty-seven frames of ticks, and you'd stopped noticing them; and then nineteen frames of none, one after another, and every *none* landed a little harder than the last, until by fifty Milli had his tail wrapped round his own ankle and Leaka had the vixen in her arms and Jared had put his hand on Milli's shoulder and left it there.

Fifty-five. Two lines out. The arc. None.

Oswin picked up the twelfth sheet. The last. It had one frame on it, not four, because Higu had given it a page to itself.

Fifty-six.

It was the same ring as all the others, the same size, a hand across. But the lines going out of it were so many, and so close together, that the ring in the middle was almost hidden: a burst of fine pencil strokes going out through it in every direction, like the spokes of a wheel, or a dandelion clock, or the light that had come up out of the floor of the Hall two years and a half ago, all at once, with thirty-five people in it. Each one ending in a small mark. Each mark different.

Thirty-five lines.

And on the right of the ring, close against it, the arc. Drawn as carefully as all the others.

Blank.

Oswin measured it. Scaled it. Walked it round the fourteenth ring on his knees with the silk, the last time, slowly, past the nine glass pins from frame one that nobody had moved; and it lay on the blank half, top and bottom and width, the inner line and the outer, exactly.

He wrote *none*.

Edwy didn't check it.

***

Edwy was kneeling at the edge of the circle on the right-hand side, by the blank half of the fourteenth ring, with his column in his lap and his left hand flat on the boards beside the paper. He'd checked every number for five days. Every one. Twice. He was looking at the nine pins.

"They're people," said Edwy.

Nobody moved.

"The lines out," said Edwy. "Every frame. That's people coming in. That's what fifty-six is: thirty-five lines, and there's thirty-five of you. The marks at the ends are where you came from. Each one's different because you all came from somewhere different." He wasn't looking at anybody. He was looking at the pins. "And the arc's the way home. Cursiffe said. It's the blank half of the ring on the floor of the Hall. It's drawn and nobody's written in it. And in the old ones somebody's written in it. A tick for every line. Every person who came in on a line, there's a tick on the way home." His voice had gone very thin. "Thirty-seven times. Every person. And then nineteen times, nobody." He looked at fifty-six. "And then you."

The long room was silent.

"They're people going home," said Edwy.

Then he heard himself.

Hana watched it happen. Watched him hear it, the way you'd hear a plate go off a shelf in another room; watched his face go white from the top down, and his hands come off the boards and go together in his lap, and his eyes go to Oswin, and then, worse, past Oswin, to the end of the room. To the chair.

"I'm sorry," said Edwy. "I'm sorry, sir. I'm sorry, Master Oswin. We don't guess. I know we don't guess. Thea said. Higu said. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said it. It's not counting. It's not what's there. I'm sorry."

Oswin didn't say anything. He was on his knees at the edge of the circle with the dividers still in his hand and his face doing nothing at all, which on Oswin was the most frightening thing Hana had ever seen.

At the end of the room, the Warden stood up.

He did it slowly. He was old, and the chair was low. He stood up with his hands on its arms, and then without them, and stood there for a moment at the end of the long room, looking down the length of the boards at the circle. At the thirteen full rings and the half-full fourteenth. At nine glass pins in a band of bare paper. At fifty-six frames laid out in a row on twelve cream sheets, the last one on its own.

Then he walked down the room.

Nobody stopped him. He walked round the edge of the paper the long way, the way Hana had, not crossing it, in his soft indoor shoes, with the pink bead between his finger and thumb; round to the right-hand side, where Edwy was kneeling. To the blank half.

And sat down.

On the floor. On the bare boards at the very edge of the circle, beside Edwy, with his back to the windows and his knees drawn up like a much younger man's and his hands in his lap, the bead in them. An old man in an old robe, who'd stood on the third ring of the circle in the Hall two years and a half ago and felt thirty-five people come through it like hooks, and asked for the leaves to be burned, and lost. Sitting on the floor of the long room beside the boy who'd tried to do it for him, looking at a half-ring of blank paper with nine pins in it.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Edwy didn't either. He sat with his hands together and his face white and his eyes on the boards.

Then the Warden spoke, not loudly, the way he said *good morning*.

"Don't apologise, Edwy," he said.

***

She sent it on the blue cord that night, word for word. All of it. The nineteen *nones*. Fifty-six. The pins. What Edwy had said, every word, in order, with *(Edwy said this)* in front of it and *(then he said sorry, four times)* after it. The Warden. *Don't apologise, Edwy*.

She sent it with her eyes shut on the step of the archive, all at once, the way she told things, with every name in.

Then she waited.

The blue cord stayed warm a long time. It didn't say anything. Hana sat on the step with her thumb on it and felt Thea at the other end, a long way off, by some fire on the road north, with her hand over Maren's, and Higu beside her with the book, and the others; felt her sitting there, not saying anything, the way you'd sit very still at the end of a jetty when something has touched the line and you don't strike yet.

*Tell Edwy thank you*, said the cord, at last.

And then, quieter:

*Not yet*.

Hana knew what that meant. Everybody in the channel knew what that meant, now, since the spring; it was what Ars had said on the end of the jetty, the night Thea showed him the wall. *Somebody stopped*, he'd said. *Not yet*. It meant: I think so too. It meant: I'm not going to say it until I can read it. It meant: there are nineteen frames of nobody and then us, and I'm going to find out why before I let anybody say the thing Edwy said, because if I say it and I'm wrong, thirty-five people will have heard it.

She went in.

Nobody else wrote anything. Hana watched the channel for a long time, and nobody did. Thirty-five people, on seven roads and a tower, reading five lines and not answering. She'd never seen the channel so quiet with so many people in it. She could feel them in it. All of them. The way you can feel a room full of people holding their breath.

Then, at the bottom, a long time after, a new line.

[ 23:51 · Rachael: Good night, Edwy. ]

Edwy couldn't read it. He didn't have a window. Hana went and found him anyway, on the stairs outside the long room with a candle, sitting where Milli had sat at four in the morning, and told him: that a healer on the road to Kingsferry, who'd never met him, had said it to him.

He looked at her for a long time.

"I shouldn't have said it," said Edwy.

"Somebody had to," said Hana. She sat down on the stair beside him.

Edwy sat on the stair with the candle, and after a while he put his head down on his knees, and Hana put her tail round him, which you couldn't do with most people, but you could with Edwy, and they sat there.

***

The Warden was still in the long room at midnight.

Hana saw him through the door on her way up. He'd lit one candle. He was sitting where he'd sat down in the afternoon, on the bare boards at the edge of the circle on the right-hand side, by the blank half, with his knees drawn up and his hands in his lap. Nobody had moved him. Oswin had tried, Edwy said, at supper, gently, and been told no.

He was looking at frame fifty-six.

At thirty-five fine pencil lines going out through a small ring in every direction like the light that had come up out of the floor of his Hall. At thirty-five marks, each one different, at the ends of the lines. At an arc on the right, drawn very carefully, close against the ring, with nothing on it at all.

He had the bead between his finger and thumb. He was turning it.

He looked up and saw her in the doorway, in the dark, with her cords on her wrist and her tail round her ankles.

He didn't say *good night*. He didn't say anything. He looked at her, a long time, the way he'd looked at her across the table in the spring with the bead held out between them, before he took it.

Then he looked back at fifty-six.

And Hana went up to bed, and lay under her blanket with the cords on her wrist, seven halves and a gold one, and the place on her chest where the sheets had been for ten days empty and cool; and found that she was holding her own wrist, all of it at once, the way you'd hold a bird.
