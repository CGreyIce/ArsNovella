Alea went up on the east wall before dawn on the second morning, because she couldn't not.

She'd meant to wait. She'd told herself so, the night before, lying on a cot in the women's barracks with Wendy asleep in the next one and Rhea snoring like a bellows at the far end and Astra propped against the wall where she could see it. *Not yet. Tomorrow. When you've slept*. She hadn't slept. She'd lain there with her ears up, listening to the fort: the sentries' boots going along the walls over her head, the horses shifting in the stables, a door somewhere banging in a wind she couldn't feel. And somewhere above all of it, on the far side of the wall, the red. She could hear it. That was the thing nobody had told her. Even inside the walls, even through the stone, if you lay still enough, you could hear the grey grass on the red. *Hush. Hush. Hush*. All night. Like somebody in another room telling somebody else to be quiet.

So she got up in the dark and put her boots on and Astra on her back, and went out across the courtyard, and up.

The stair to the east wall was by the gatehouse. Stone, worn hollow in the middle by nine years of boots, and nine years before that, and more. She went up it slowly, with her hand on the wall. Her father had gone up these stairs. Thousands of times. At fifteen, and twenty, and twenty-seven. She thought about that on every step.

At the top, the wall walk went away from her in both directions, north and south, between the parapet on the outside and a low rail on the inside, with the courtyard below. The sky in the east was grey. The lamps were still lit along the parapet, every few merlons, burning down.

There was somebody on the wall.

An old man, coming along it toward her from the north, very slowly, with a lantern in one hand and a spear he was mostly leaning on in the other. Coming off the night watch. He was the oldest man she had ever seen on his feet. Bent nearly double. White-haired, what there was of it. His hand on the spear shook, all the time, a little, the way a leaf shakes on a tree with no wind.

He saw her, and stopped.

He looked at her for a long time in the grey light, the way the old ones on the wall had looked at her yesterday. At her ears, and her hair, and her face. His mouth worked.

"You'll be wanting his place," he said.

***

His name was Wat. He was eighty. He'd been at Cinderhold since before her father was born. He told her that on the way, very slowly, because everything Wat did was slow, and she walked beside him at his pace with her hand ready in case he fell, and he didn't.

"North of the gate," said Wat. "Eleven merlons. Not twelve. Folk get it wrong. The young ones count from the gatehouse door and they get twelve, but you don't count the door." He breathed. "Eleven."

They went along the wall. Past the gatehouse. Past the horns, hung on the outside of the gatehouse under the parapet, which she could just see if she leaned out: two great curved shapes, dark, like the horns of a bull the size of a cart, with frost on them. She didn't lean out far. She knew what they were. She didn't need to look.

Wat counted under his breath. Not out loud; she just saw his lips moving. *Seven. Eight*.

There was a gap.

That was the first thing she noticed. Not the place itself. The gap round it. Along the whole of the east wall, the lamps were set every three or four merlons, and the sentries' boots had worn a path in the stone of the wall walk, a long shallow trough down the middle where nine years of men had walked up and down at night. And at the eleventh merlon north of the gate, the path went round.

Not much. A step. The trough in the stone bent away from the parapet, toward the inside rail, for about the width of two men, and then came back. As if, for nine years, every sentry on the east wall of Cinderhold had stepped aside at that place without thinking about it. The way you step round a grave.

"Nobody stands there," said Wat.

"Ever?"

"Never." He leaned on his spear. "Nobody said not to. Nobody had to. The first night, after, the sentry went round it. And the next. And then the young ones came out, and the old ones told them *not that one*, and they never asked why." He breathed. "Some of them still don't know. They just go round."

Alea looked at it. Two merlons, and the embrasure between them. The gap in the parapet where you'd stand to look out. Ordinary stone. Grey. With the lamp-light on it.

And on the coping of the parapet, in the gap, on the flat top of the stone where you'd rest your hands if you were standing there looking out, two marks.

She went closer. She didn't step into the gap. She stood at the edge of it, where the worn path bent away, and looked.

They were cracks. Two of them, side by side, about a shoulder-width apart, in the top of the stone. Not marks at all. *Breaks*. Each one starting from a place about the size of a hand, where the stone had gone down a little, crushed, as if it had been mud and somebody had pressed into it; and from each of those places, cracks running out across the coping in every direction, fine and white and branching, the way cracks run out across ice when you put your weight on it too fast. The whole top of the stone between the two merlons was split with them. You could have put your fingers in the biggest.

She knew what they were before Wat said anything. She'd seen her father's hands on the end of the jetty every night of her life. She knew how far apart they went when he leaned on something. She knew the size.

"His hands," she said.

"His hands," said Wat.

***

"He stood here an hour," said Wat.

He'd sat down. On the step of the wall walk, with his back against the inside rail and his spear across his knees and his lantern beside him, because he'd been on his feet all night and he was eighty, he said, and he'd stand when he was dead. Alea sat beside him. Not in the gap. Beside it, with her back against the rail, where she could see the two cracked places in the stone against the grey sky.

"The third day," said Wat. "In the afternoon. When they lit the fires on the rise." He nodded east, over the parapet, without looking. "He came up those stairs at a run, and along the wall, and here. And put his hands on the stone. And looked." His mouth worked. "And then he didn't move."

Alea didn't say anything.

"An hour," said Wat. "I was two merlons down. On that side." He pointed, south, with his shaking hand. "I watched him. Everybody did. We'd all seen the fires. We'd all seen what was in the middle of them." He was quiet for a moment. "And he stood here, with his hands on the stone, an hour, and didn't move. And the stone went under his hands. You could hear it. Like ice on the river in the spring. *Crack*. And then another. And he didn't move."

"Why?" said Alea. It came out of her before she could stop it.

Wat looked at her for a long moment, with his old wet eyes.

"Nobody knows," he said. "Folk've said all sorts. That he was waiting for the general to show himself. That he was deciding. That he was praying. That he'd gone out of his head with it and didn't know where he was." He shook his head, slowly. "I don't hold with any of them. I was two merlons down. He knew where he was."

He was quiet for a while.

"The Advisor was beside him," said Wat. "Halvard. The whole hour. Stood right where you're sitting, near enough. Said something to him at the start, when he first put his hands on the stone. Nobody heard what. Then didn't say anything else. Just stood there. The whole hour. Beside him." He breathed. "And at the end of it, the Advisor said something again. And your father took his hands off the stone. And went down the stairs, and out of the gate, on his own, and into the host." He looked at the cracks. "And we didn't see him again until the morning."

Alea sat with her back against the rail and looked at the two broken places in the stone.

She knew everything. That was what she'd always been told, and it was true. Her father had told her all of it, when she was old enough, at the kitchen table, one winter: the invasion, the feint, the ditch, the infirmary, the fires, the rise. What was in the middle of the fires. What her mother had asked him. What he'd done. The last words, which were hers. She'd known all of it since she was thirteen.

He'd never told her about the hour.

She thought about asking him. She could feel the question in her, the shape of it, the way she'd felt it yesterday at the edge of a ditch on the south side of the fort. *What did Halvard say to you?* She could go down the stairs and find him, wherever he was, at the well with the blue bucket or at the forge door or in the chapel with the chaplain. She could ask him. He'd tell her. He always told her, when she asked.

She looked at the cracks.

She didn't ask. She closed it. The way she'd closed the other one, yesterday, at the edge of the ditch. Not because she was frightened of the answer. Because he'd carried it for nine years without putting it down in front of her, and that meant it was his, and he'd put it down when he was ready. She knew what that was like. She'd carried things too.

*Slower*, she thought.

"Thank you," she said to Wat.

"Don't thank me," said Wat. "I'm only telling you where. I don't know why." He reached out, slowly, with his shaking hand, and patted her knee, twice, the way you'd pat a dog. "You've got her face," he said. "Everyone's saying. I didn't believe them." His hand stayed on her knee a moment. "They're right."

Then he got up, which took a long time, and picked up his lantern and his spear, and went off along the wall toward the stairs and his bed. Very slowly. Going round the gap at the eleventh merlon without looking at it, the way he must have gone round it every night for nine years.

***

She stood up when he'd gone, and looked out.

Not from the gap. Beside it. At the twelfth merlon, with her hands on the parapet next to the cracked stone, not on it. The sun was coming up. The whole of the east was going gold over the mountains of Kharvos, and the light came across the red toward the wall, long and flat, and lit everything at once.

She'd been told it was ugly. Wendy had told her. Wendy didn't lie.

It was. It went away from the foot of the wall to the mountains, flat and rust-red, with the grey grass standing up out of it in tufts and the old dry riverbeds cutting through it in long crooked lines like scars. Nothing moving on it. No birds. And the markers. She saw them now. She'd heard about them for twenty days and she saw them now: rows of wooden posts going away from the wall, straight, very neat, row after row after row, catching the first light on their tops, like the teeth of a comb. Like a field of something that had been planted in lines and never come up. Hundreds of them. More. Going away and away across the red until they were too small to see.

She looked at them for a long time. She made herself. All of them.

And then she looked past them.

There was a tree.

One. On its own, a long way out, past the markers, on a low knoll above a dry riverbed, near a line of dead grey trees that stood up along the edge of the red like old bones. A small tree. Young. With leaves on it. Green. The only green thing on the whole of the red, as far as she could see in any direction, standing up very small and very bright in the first sun, with something round its foot that might have been flowers, and something dark beside it, low and rounded, that might have been a stone.

*It's taller than Salty*, Maigo had said. *It's the only tree on the whole of the red. You can see it from the fort wall*.

She could see it from the fort wall.

She didn't do anything. She didn't make a sound. She stood at the twelfth merlon with her hands on the parapet and looked at a tree an hour's walk away across the red, with a dark rounded stone beside it, and something in her chest that had been held very tight for twenty days, or nine years, went very slowly loose. Not all the way. Just enough to breathe round.

*Hi, Mum*, she thought.

She didn't say it out loud. She wasn't ready to say it out loud. That was for the night. For the full moon, and the stones, and the cloth the chaplain said he'd bring. She'd decided that on the road. She'd decided it in Ashby, writing to Sorrel. She wouldn't go out there before the night. She'd see it every morning from the wall, and wait, and go on the night, with him.

But she thought it.

*I can see you from here*.

***

Then she looked east, past the tree.

She'd known it would be there. He'd told her on the riverbank in Valcrest. *You can see it from the grave. It's just a rise. Grass on it now. The ring's still there, burned into the ground*.

You could see it from the wall too.

It was further out than the tree, and a little to the south. A long low hump in the red, like the back of something sleeping. Grey grass on it, the same as everywhere. Nothing special. You'd never look at it twice, if you didn't know. And then the sun came up a little higher and the light got under it, flat and low, and she saw the ring.

A circle. On the top of the rise. Darker than the ground round it, the way a ring is darker on a table where a hot pot has stood. Not big. You could have walked round it in thirty paces. Burned into the red so deep that nine years of rain and snow and grey grass hadn't touched it. And inside it, if she looked hard, in the long low light, other lines. Fainter. Going across. Like the spokes of a wheel.

She looked at it.

She'd thought, on the riverbank, that she'd look at it from the grave. *I'm going to stand there, and look east at the rise*. She'd said it as if it were the thing she had to do. The hard thing. The one that would prove something.

She stood on the wall now and looked at the ring on the rise in the first sun, and she found that she didn't want to go there. Not that it frightened her. It did. But not that. She just didn't want to. There was nothing there. It was where her mother had stopped being her mother. Her mother wasn't on the rise. Her mother was under a stone beside a tree, in a ring of flowers, an hour's walk away, where her father had carried her.

She'd look at the rise. From here. And from the grave, on the night, the way she'd said she would. She'd look at it as long as she needed to.

She wouldn't go.

She stood at the twelfth merlon, beside the gap where he'd stood, and looked at it until the sun was all the way up and the ring went flat and ordinary and grey and you couldn't see it any more unless you knew.

Then she went down to find the infirmary.

***

It was on the east side of the courtyard, against the wall.

A long low building of grey stone, the same as everything else in Cinderhold, with a slate roof and a row of small high windows and a door at one end with a lamp over it. Ordinary. She'd walked past it yesterday afternoon without knowing. It looked like a storehouse, or a barracks.

Except for the wall.

The back of the building was the fort's east wall. It was built into it, the way half the buildings in Cinderhold were built into the walls, to save stone. And in the back of it, in the long east side, there was a patch. She could see it from the courtyard, through the gap between the infirmary and the next building. A piece of the wall about the size and shape of a big double door, where the stone was different. Newer. Paler. Better cut. Fitted in very carefully, very neatly, where something had been taken out of the old wall and something else put back.

As if somebody had opened the wall like a door, once, and somebody else had come along after and bricked the door up.

She stood in the courtyard and looked at it for a long time.

Then she went to the door with the lamp over it, and counted to three, and went in.

***

There was only one person inside.

The infirmary was a long room with beds along both walls, a dozen each side, most of them empty, with clean grey blankets folded square on the ends. A table at the far end with jars on it, and a basin, and a lamp. The small high windows let in long bars of sunlight that lay across the floor. It smelled of vinegar and lye and dried herbs and, faintly, under everything, of blood, the way infirmaries always did, however much you scrubbed. She knew that smell. Rachael smelled of it, a little, always, and so did the back room at the militia hall where Maren kept the bandages.

An old man was sweeping the floor.

He was big. That was the first thing. Not tall, but broad and heavy, the way some men go broad when they get old, with a great soft barrel of a body and big soft hands on the broom. His hair was white and cropped close. He swept the way some people pray: slowly, very carefully, the whole floor, every board, as if it mattered which way the dust went.

He looked up when the door shut behind her.

And stopped sweeping.

He stood there in the bars of sunlight with the broom in his big hands and looked at her. At her face. And Alea saw it happen to him, the thing that had happened on the wall yesterday to twenty-five old soldiers, and to Teague by the fire, and to Wat that morning. All the colour going out of him at once. But he didn't say *Diwa*. He didn't say anything. He just stood there.

Then he leaned the broom very carefully against the end of the nearest bed, and sat down on the bed, heavily, as if his legs had stopped.

"Sorry," he said. His voice was soft, for such a big man. "Sorry. I'm all right. Give me a moment, miss."

Alea gave him a moment.

She stood just inside the door, with her mother's sword on her back, and waited, the way her father waited on the end of the jetty, and didn't say anything. And after a while the old man wiped his face with the back of his big hand and looked at her again, properly.

"They said," he said. "In the kitchen. Last night. That she'd sent you. The daughter." He breathed. "I didn't go up on the wall. Yesterday. I was in here. I'm always in here." He looked round at the empty beds. "I wasn't on the wall the first time, either."

"The first time?"

"The morning after." He looked at the floor, at the boards he'd been sweeping. "Everybody was up there. The whole fort. Every man still standing. Watching him come back." He was quiet. "I was in here. Washing the floor."

Alea didn't say anything.

"There were six of us," said the old man. "Orderlies. Me and five more. Carrying, washing, holding. We worked under the healers. And on the second night, the south line went, and they sent for stretchers, and I went. With the stretchers. Out to the ditch, all night, carrying the ones from the ditch back in through the west gate." He breathed. "The other five stayed in here. With the healers. With her."

He didn't say anything else about it. He didn't need to. Alea knew the rest. She'd known it since she was thirteen. *At dawn the infirmary wall had been opened. Six orderlies dead. Four healers gone*.

Six. Not five. She'd always been told six.

She looked at the big old man on the bed with his soft hands.

"They counted you," she said. "You were one of the six. Everyone thought—"

"For a day," he said. "Till they found me asleep in the stable, under a stretcher, with blood on me that wasn't mine." He looked at his hands. "Somebody else was the sixth. A boy from the ditch, they'd brought in at midnight and put on my cot. They found him on the floor and thought he was me, in the dark." He was quiet. "I've never known his name. The chaplain hasn't either. He's one of the ones in the back of the book. With a number." He turned his hands over. "I washed the floor, after. The morning after. While everyone was on the wall. Somebody had to." His voice didn't change. "I've been washing it since."

***

His name was Hesper.

He told her that after a while, when he could, and asked hers, though he knew it, and she told him. And he said it back to her, *Alea*, slowly, as if he were tasting it. And then he got up off the bed and went to the table at the far end, and put the kettle on the little spirit stove there, because, he said, you couldn't talk about anything in an infirmary without tea. It was a rule. Her mother's rule.

"She was particular about tea," said Hesper. "Very particular. Everything else, she'd drink out of a bucket if she had to. But tea had to be hot, and strong, and in a cup with a handle." He found two cups. One had a handle. He gave her that one. "Because of her hands."

"They were cold," said Alea. "Always. Dad said."

"Always." Hesper poured. "Cold as a frog's. Summer and winter. She'd come in on a morning in high summer, in the heat, when the whole fort was sweating, and put her hands on the back of your neck to say good morning, and you'd jump a foot." He nearly smiled. "She did it to me every day for four years. The first years. When she was young. Before your father took her north." He sat down at the table, across from Alea, with his cup in both big hands. "And then she'd go and warm them. Before she touched a patient. Every time. On the lamp, or the stove, or on somebody's tea. Mine, mostly." He looked at his cup. "She'd come and wrap her hands round my cup while I was drinking it. Never asked. Just did it. And I'd hold it for her while she warmed them, and then she'd go."

Alea held her own cup in both hands. It was very hot.

"She always came back," said Hesper. "Whenever there was trouble. Your father would come east, and she'd come with him, because she was the best healer there'd ever been on the border and she knew it, and she said she wasn't having a lot of boys die of things she could fix just because she'd moved to a lake. A month here. A season there." He breathed. "And that last summer, when the king called everybody east, she came again." "And she walked in that door, and put her hands on the back of my neck, and said *Hes, you've got fat*. And I said *you've got old*. And she laughed. And went and warmed her hands on my tea."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Two weeks," he said. "That was all. Two weeks, the last time."

***

"Tell me about the tent," said Alea.

She hadn't known she was going to say it. It came out of her the way the question had come out of her on the wall. But she didn't close this one. This one was hers to ask.

Hesper looked at her.

"Dad told me," said Alea. "When I was frightened. Before the Lists. He said she was frightened every time she went into an infirmary tent. Every single time. That she'd stand at the flap with her hands together and her ears flat, and count to three in her head, and then go in and be the best healer on the border for twelve hours." She held the cup. "I've been doing it. Since. Every round. Every time I'm frightened." She looked down. "I don't know if I'm doing it right."

Hesper put his cup down.

He didn't say anything for a while. Then he got up, slowly, and went to the door of the infirmary, the one with the lamp over it, and stood just inside it, facing out. With his back to her. A big old man in a doorway.

"Not this door," he said. "This was the fort. Out there, in the tents, when it was bad, when there were too many to bring in. We'd put the tents up outside the west gate in a row. Six of them. And the wounded would come in from the line all night on the stretchers, and lie in rows on the grass outside the tents, waiting. And the drums going." He was quiet. "And she'd come out of one tent, with her hands red to the elbow, and walk down the row to the next one. And stop. At the flap."

He put his hands together. In front of his chest. Not palm to palm. Just the fingertips, lightly, the way you'd hold something very small between them.

Alea's breath stopped.

"Like this," said Hesper. "Ears flat. Right back. Like a cat in the rain." He didn't have ears like that; he did something with his shoulders instead, hunching them, and somehow it was right. "And she'd count. In her head. You couldn't hear it. But you could see it. She tapped them." He moved his fingertips. Tapped them together, very lightly, the big soft fingers of his two hands, against each other. Once. "One." Again. "Two." Again. "Three."

He stopped.

"And then she'd say, *well, then*," said Hesper. "Out loud. Just that. *Well, then*. Like you'd say it at the end of a long day when there's still the washing up to do. And I'd hold the flap. And she'd go in."

He let his hands fall. He stood in the doorway with his back to her.

"Four years in the first years," he said. "And all the times after. And two weeks the last time. And every time, at every flap. *One, two, three, well, then*. I held the flap for her a thousand times." He was quiet. "I never once saw her not go in."

***

Alea looked down at her own hands.

They were round the cup. She took them off it. She put them together, in front of her chest, slowly, the way she'd been doing for weeks. Since a night above Valcrest when her father told her about the tent. Since round two, and round five, and the final, and the top of the rise outside this fort two nights ago, and the gate yesterday.

She'd always pressed them flat. Palm to palm, a little. Like praying. Because *hands together* was what he'd said, and that was what *hands together* meant, if you didn't know.

She moved them. Slowly. Until only the fingertips touched.

And tapped them. Once. Twice. Three times.

It felt different. She couldn't have said how. It felt like the difference between a song you've learned from somebody describing it, and the song.

Hesper had turned round. He was standing in the doorway watching her, and his big soft face was doing something it couldn't stop.

"Well, then," said Alea. Very quietly.

Hesper sat down on the end of the nearest bed, and put his face in his hands, and didn't make any sound at all.

***

He gave her the drawing before she went.

She didn't know it was there. She didn't know there was a drawing. He went to a cupboard at the far end of the room, behind the table, and opened it with a key from round his neck, and took out a book. Not a big one. An old ledger, the kind the infirmary must have kept the stores in once, with a cracked leather cover. And he opened it, near the back, and took out something that had been pressed between the pages, flat, for a long time.

A piece of paper. Folded in four. Brown at the edges, and soft, the way paper goes soft when it's been folded and unfolded too many times.

"It was on the wall," said Hesper. "Over her table. Where she kept the jars. She pinned it up the first day she came back, that last summer. She showed it to everybody. Every orderly. Every boy who came in with a cut. Every one of the healers. *Look what my girl did*." He held it out. "I came back from the ditch in the morning and the wall was open and she was gone and the table was over and the jars were broken on the floor, all of them. And that was still on the wall. Over the table. On its pin." He was quiet. "I took it down before anybody else came in. I don't know why. I didn't know where you were. Nobody did. Your father wasn't a man you could ask anything, that autumn. And then he was gone. And I had it." He looked at it in his hand. "Nine years. I didn't know who to give it to."

Alea took it.

She unfolded it. Very carefully, because it was very soft.

It was a lake.

A child's lake. Blue, in a wax crayon that had been pressed on very hard, a big blue oval taking up most of the page. And a brown line going out into it from the edge: a jetty, with planks drawn on it one by one, too many planks, every one of them counted. And on the end of the jetty, three people. A big one, with a stick, with a line going out from the stick into the blue and a fish on the end of the line, a big fish, bigger than the person, with a smile on it. And a middle one, with two orange triangles on top of its head and a tail. And a small one between them, with two orange triangles on top of its head and a tail, holding both their hands.

And over the top, in big careful letters, the way you write when you've only just learned:

*MUM DAD ME*

And under it, in smaller letters, crammed in at the bottom because she'd run out of room:

*COME HOME SOON*

Alea stood in the infirmary at Cinderhold in the bars of morning sunlight, with her mother's sword on her back, holding a drawing she'd made when she was eight, at the kitchen table at the cabin, on the night before her mother went east. She remembered making it. She'd forgotten. She remembered now. Her mother sitting across the table from her with her chin on her hand, watching. *More planks*, her mother had said. *Your father put in more planks than that. He's very proud of his planks*.

She'd put in more planks.

She didn't cry. She thought she might, and she didn't. She stood there and looked at the drawing for a long time, at the three people on the end of the jetty and the fish with the smile on it, and the thing in her chest that had come loose on the wall that morning came a little looser.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't," said Hesper. He'd picked up the broom again. He was holding it in both big hands, like something to hold on to. "Just take it home. That's all. It should go home."

***

She found her father on the wall at dusk.

She hadn't been looking for him. She'd gone up to look at the tree again before the light went, because she'd decided she was going to, every morning and every evening, until the night. And he was there.

Not in the gap. Beside it. At the tenth merlon, on the other side from where she'd stood that morning, with the rod leaning against the parapet beside him and his hands flat on the stone. Not on the cracked stone. On the next one. Looking east, at the red going dark, and the markers, and the tree, a long way out, small and black now against the last of the light.

He heard her coming. He always did. He didn't turn round.

She came and stood on the other side of the gap. At the twelfth merlon. With the eleventh between them, empty, with its cracked coping and the worn path going round it.

Neither of them stepped into it.

They stood there for a while, the two of them, one on each side of the place where he'd stood nine years ago, and looked at the red.

"Wat showed you," he said, eventually.

"Wat showed me."

"He's still on nights."

"He's eighty."

"He was seventy-one then," said her father. "And still on nights." He was quiet. "He was two merlons down."

"He said."

Her father didn't say anything else. He didn't say *what else did he say*. He didn't look at the cracks in the stone between them. And she didn't say anything about the hour, or Halvard, or the thing Wat said nobody knew. She'd closed that. She kept it closed. She could feel him knowing that she'd closed it, the way she could always feel him knowing things, and not saying anything about that either.

*When you're ready*, she thought. *It's yours*.

She took the drawing out of her coat instead.

She'd folded it back the way it had been, in four, along the old soft folds. She held it out across the gap. Across the eleventh merlon. He looked at it. Then he took his hands off the stone, and took it, and unfolded it.

She watched him look at it.

She couldn't see his face. She never had. But she'd been reading the rest of him all her life, on the jetty and the shingle and at the Lists, and she'd never seen him do what he did then. She'd thought she knew everything he could do. She hadn't.

"Hesper had it," she said. "In the infirmary. It was over her table. On the wall. On its pin." She looked at the red. "He took it down the morning after. Before anyone came in. He's kept it in a book."

Her father didn't say anything for a very long time. He stood at the tenth merlon with the drawing in his two hands, in the last of the light, looking at three people on the end of a jetty with too many planks.

"I saw it," he said at last. Very quietly. "Once. The first night we got here. She took me in there to look at it, with the whole infirmary watching, and made me say something nice about the planks." He breathed. "And then I never went in there again. Not after. I couldn't." He was quiet. "I thought it was gone. With the jars. I thought it was gone."

"More planks," said Alea. "She said. When I was drawing it. *Your father's very proud of his planks*."

Her father made a sound.

It wasn't a word. It wasn't a laugh, and it wasn't the other thing either. It was the sound something makes when it's been held very tight for a long time and somebody touches it in exactly the right place. He stood at the tenth merlon of the east wall of Cinderhold, beside the place where he'd stood for an hour with his hands breaking the stone, and held a child's drawing of a lake, and made that sound once. And then didn't make it again.

*COME HOME SOON*, said the drawing, in big careful letters, at the bottom, crammed in.

***

They stood there until it was dark.

The tree went out first. Then the markers, row by row, from the far ones in. Then the rise, with its ring, which she couldn't see any more but knew was there. Then the red itself, all of it, until there was nothing beyond the parapet but dark, and the *hush* of the grey grass, and the first stars coming out over Kharvos, very many and very bright.

The moon was up. Behind them, in the west, over the fort. Half of one, with its straight edge sharp as a cut, going down.

"Higu's chart," said Alea.

"Higu's chart," her father agreed.

Twice more. It would fill, and go, and fill again. Five weeks. And then the night.

"I'm not going out there before," said Alea. "To her. I decided. On the road." She looked at where the tree had been. "I want to go on the night. With you. With the cloth the chaplain's bringing, and the stones. Not before." She paused. "I'll look at her from here. Every morning. And every night. Until."

"All right."

"And I'm not going to the rise."

He was quiet for a moment.

"All right," he said.

"Not ever. Maybe." She thought about it. "Not this time. I'll look at it. From the grave, the way I said. As long as I need to. But I'm not going to stand on it." She looked at the dark where it was. "She's not there. She's under the stone. Where you carried her."

Her father didn't say anything. But he moved. Just slightly. She felt it more than saw it in the dark: his shoulders going, the way they'd gone at the end of the Lists, and at the gate yesterday, and on a road outside Valcrest when a short square man had said *eleven*. As if something he'd been holding up for a very long time had been taken off him, quietly, by somebody who'd come up behind without his noticing.

He folded the drawing. Along the old folds, carefully, in four. And held it out to her across the gap.

She shook her head.

"You keep it," she said. "For now. In your coat. With the good knife." She looked at him. "Hesper said it should go home. You're going home before me. Every night, you're on the end of the jetty before me." She was quiet. "Put it over the table. At the cabin. On a pin. When we're back."

He looked at her for a long time across the eleventh merlon. Across the empty place with the broken stone where he'd stood for an hour nine years ago, and that nobody in Cinderhold had stood in since.

Then he put the drawing in his coat. Inside. Over his heart. Where the good knife was.

"With the good knife," he said.

They stood there a while longer, one on each side of it, in the dark. Neither of them stepped into it. Neither of them needed to.

Below them, in the courtyard, somebody had lit the lamp over the infirmary door, the way somebody had lit it every night for longer than she'd been alive. And through the small high windows, very faintly, she could hear a broom.
