He didn't sleep.

He hadn't expected to. He sat by the fire after the door closed and the five lanterns went up the lake road and round the corner at the top, and Alea went up the stairs without a word and he heard her lie down on her bed in her clothes and not move. He sat there with the bowls still on the table, seven of them, and the borrowed white cloth with a stain on it in the shape of a fire in a swamp, and the pumpkin skins, and Squiddles's bowl licked clean, and he didn't clear any of it.

He sat there until the fire burned down. Then he went out to the jetty.

It was very cold, and very still, and the lake was white from shore to shore under a sky so full of stars that it looked frosted. He sat on the end of the jetty in the snow with his rod beside him, the way he had for thirty-four winters, and looked at the stars, which were the wrong ones, the way they'd always been.

They didn't look wrong tonight.

He thought about that for a long time. He'd looked at these stars every clear night for thirty-four years and every single time some small hard part of him had said *wrong*. Not loudly. The way you notice a picture hanging slightly crooked in a room you live in. *Wrong. Wrong. Not mine.* He'd stopped hearing it, mostly. It had never stopped saying it.

He sat on the jetty and listened for it, and it wasn't there.

They were just stars.

***

He opened his window at dawn, out of habit, and read the channel.

He'd read it every morning for a year. Mashi first, always, because Mashi posted his heartbeats last thing at night and they were the first thing at the bottom when you scrolled. *five. five heartbeats. no nosebleed this time. salty says i look like a man who's seen god.* Then Maigo, in capitals, replying. Then Kei. Tiger. Chris's numbers from the evening before, all thirty-five in a column, with the blank one at the end.

Nothing from Wrenhaven.

Not a word. Higu's three lines and Thea's one about the bonfire, two nights ago, and then nothing. Not *we had supper*. Not *we went down the lake road*. Not *it's him, he's here, he made us a stew*. The rest of the kingdom chattering away about goats and heartbeats and the tournament, and the five of them on the hill above his house, after the most important night of their lives in this world, saying nothing at all.

Rule five.

He'd known about it for a year without knowing it had a name. He knew it had a name now.

He closed the window and sat on the jetty with his face in his hands for a while.

***

He fished at first light, the way he always did. Through the hole in the ice off the end of the jetty, with a short line and the patience of a man who'd done it ten thousand times. He caught one. Then another. Then, after a long wait, a third, a big one, the best of the morning, and a fourth, and he laid them in the straw in the basket on the step.

Then he went back and waited for a fifth.

It took a long time. The lake didn't care that it was important. He sat there with the line in the black water and the cold coming up through the boards, and waited, and thought about the basket. Two, in the first autumn. Then three. Then four, the morning after Thea's first bonfire. Then five, since PenguChi went up a tier. And yesterday, four. Four, and an invitation.

He didn't want it to be four again. Ever.

The fifth came just as the sun cleared the hills on the far shore. A good one. He laid it in the straw with the others.

***

Alea came down at the usual time. She stopped in the doorway with her coat half on, the way she had yesterday, and looked at the basket on the step. She counted. He watched her count.

"Five," she said.

"The basket stays five," said Ars.

Alea looked at it for a moment longer. Then at him. She didn't say anything. She came down onto the step and picked up the basket and put her free arm round his neck, quickly, hard, the way she did when she didn't want him to see her face. And then she was off up the lake road in the snow with the basket and the black sword on her back, not running this time. Walking. Very fast.

He watched her go.

At the top of the road, Rowan was in his place, the way he always was. Ars watched Alea pass him. He watched Rowan turn his head and look down the lake road at the cabin, and lift a hand. Ars lifted his back.

Then he went in, and cleared the table.

***

They came back that evening.

He hadn't asked them to. He'd said *come tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever*, at the door, at midnight, and he'd meant it, and he hadn't known if they'd come. He'd spent the whole afternoon not knowing. Splitting wood he didn't need. Mending a net that didn't need mending. Telling himself that it was fine if they didn't, that one supper was more than he'd had any right to, that they had a militia hall and drills and a channel full of thirty people and their own lives, and didn't need to walk down a lake road in the snow every evening to sit at a fisherman's table.

At dusk, five lanterns came round the corner at the top of the road.

He was on the step. He didn't remember going out onto it.

They came down the lake road in a row, the way they had the night before, only faster. Xola was nearly running. Squiddles was bouncing on her head. And at the gate, the hens came across the yard at them in a line, with their necks out, and PenguChi said *it's all right*, and the hens sat down in the snow.

Gigichi was carrying something. A cloth bundle, held in front of her very carefully with both hands.

"Bread," she said, at the step, scowling, holding it out. "From Edric. Maren made us bring something. She says you don't go to someone's house with nothing in your hands." She shoved it at him. "It's not from *me*. It's from *Maren*."

"Thank you," said Ars. "Thank Maren."

"Tell her yourself," said Gigichi. "She'd die."

***

They came the next evening too. And the one after.

It became a thing. It happened very fast, the way things do when they're right. By the end of the first week, it was just what happened. Maren drilled them in the morning and they did whatever else they did in the afternoon, and at dusk five lanterns came round the corner at the top of the lake road, and Rowan stepped aside, and the hens sat down, and the door opened.

The town got used to it. Ars had thought it wouldn't; he'd thought there'd be talk. There was talk. There was always talk in Wrenhaven. But the talk, as far as he could tell from Alea, who heard everything, was mostly about whether the fisherman was feeding them properly and whether somebody ought to take down a ham.

Somebody took down a ham. It appeared on the step one morning with no note. Odette, Alea said. Odette denied it to everyone with a perfectly straight face.

***

The cabin changed.

He hadn't thought about that. He'd lived in it eight years with Alea, quietly, the two of them, and it had been the shape of two people: two chairs by the fire, two cups on the hook, two bowls on the shelf. Now there were five more people in it every evening, and the shape of it changed round them like water round stones.

Xola's coat on the third peg, every night. Her boots by the door, always one fallen over. Squiddles on the back of the chair nearest the fire, which became Squiddles's chair; Ars caught himself, by the end of the second week, not sitting in it.

PenguChi on the rag rug with Alea, cross-legged, the two of them comparing windows and arguing about the hard path in low voices, with Waddles asleep between them and Captain the hen, who'd been banished from the rug for exactly one night and had come back in a fury, sitting on Waddles.

Higu at the window. Always the window, the way she sat at the window in the upper room of the hall. Looking out at the lake with her notebook on her knees. She wrote in it sometimes, now. Not lists. He didn't ask what.

Gigichi at the table, sitting very straight, with her gold watch on the table in front of her, open, ticking. She never looked at it. She just liked it there. She argued with him about everything: the stew, the hens, the right way to stack wood, whether Captain was evil or just misunderstood. She was furious with him most evenings about something, and came back every single one.

And Thea.

Thea sat across the table from him and watched him. The way she watched windows. She asked questions. Hundreds. About the window, about the tiers, about the Redmarch and the king and the Advisor and the book about nets. About how a boy who'd died in his sleep in Malacca had woken up as a baby in a cottage in another world, and what that had been like, and when he'd first understood, and when he'd first seen the window, and what he'd called it, and why.

He answered all of them. In English. He hadn't spoken this much English in thirty years. It came back to him like a road you'd walked as a child: overgrown in places, but your feet knew it.

***

On the ninth evening, Xola asked to see his window.

It was late. The stew was gone and the bread was gone and Odette's ham was a bone that Captain was eyeing. Alea and PenguChi were on the rug. Higu was at the window. Gigichi was explaining, at length, why the hens were evil. And Xola, who'd been lying on the bench with her head in Thea's lap and Squiddles on her stomach, sat up all at once and said:

"Can we see it?"

They all looked at her.

"Your window," said Xola. "Higu saw it with the moth, but only the top. Thea's seen bits. We've never seen it. All of it." She went slightly pink. So did Squiddles. "You've seen all of ours. In the channel. Every number, every night, for a year. Chris posts them. You know Gigichi's at sixty-eight percent."

"Sixty-nine," said Gigichi. "As of this morning. Thank you."

"Sixty-nine," said Xola. "You know that. And we don't know anything about yours except it says *Dominion* and Alea says you're halfway to something." She looked at him. "It's only fair."

Ars looked at her for a moment.

Then he looked at Alea, on the rug. Alea had gone still. She knew. She was the only person in this world, now Diwa was gone, who'd ever seen all of it.

She nodded. Very slightly.

***

He opened it.

He did it the way he'd done it for thirty-four years, without thinking, the way you'd open your hand. And then he did something he hadn't done since he was fifteen, on a wall at a border garrison, with a young court mage beside him saying *what are you looking at?*

He turned it round, and let it open all the way.

***

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Higu, at the window, opened her notebook and started writing. Very fast. Ars watched her do it. She was copying it down, every word, in her small neat hand, the way she'd copied everything for sixteen months.

"*Annihilate*," said Xola.

"That's the eighth," said Ars.

"*Annihilate*?" said Xola again, higher. "Your skill is called *Annihilate*? Like, *that's* its *name*? It just *says* that?"

"It just says that."

"Who *names* them?"

"Not me," said Ars. "I named the window. When I was six. *Status*, because it was the only word I had for it. *Inventory*. *Skills*. I named the parts." He looked at the line of eight words. "I didn't name what went in them. The window did that. Or the world. I don't know. They just turned up, one at a time, every time I went up a tier. I'd open it and there'd be a new word I'd never chosen."

"*Maim*," said Gigichi. She'd come round the table to look, and she was staring at the third word with an expression of deep personal offence. "Your *third* skill was *Maim*? That's the third one? Before *Split*? Before you'd even—" She stopped. "How *old* were you?"

"Seventeen."

"*Seventeen.* And the world went, *here you go, have Maim*."

"It was a difficult year," said Ars.

Gigichi stared at him. Then she put her face in her hands and made a noise that might have been a laugh.

***

PenguChi had got up off the rug. She came and stood by Thea and looked at the window for a long time, quietly, the way she looked at everything. Her eyes went along the line of words, one at a time.

"*Split*," she said.

Ars looked at her.

"The fourth one," said PenguChi. "*Split*." She looked up at him. "Like the sword."

He hadn't thought of that. He'd had that word in his window since he was twenty-one, a Gleaming swordsman at a garrison who'd just found out he could put a sheen on a blade that would go through a shield, and he'd never once thought of it as anything but a word. A skill. The thing that came after *Maim*.

He thought about a chisel in his bare hands, and a king bringing down a hammer, and one clear note going out across the lake.

"Like the sword," he said.

***

Thea hadn't said anything.

She was looking at the window the way she'd looked at the bonfire. Not at the words. *Through* them. Her face had gone very still, and her lips were moving very slightly. Parsing. Reading the window the way only she could read windows, all the way down, past the words to whatever they were written in.

"It's the same," she said at last. Quietly.

"The same as what?"

"As ours. The skills line. It's built the same way. It's *where* ours come from." She looked up at him. "Every skill line in every window in the kingdom copies this one. Mine. Higu's. *Patch* and *Moth* and *Root* and *Mend*. They're all written in a line that's shaped exactly like this one, because this was the first one, and everything inherits from it." She looked back at the eight words. "We've all got skill lines because you had one first."

Ars didn't say anything.

"And yours have names like *Annihilate*," said Thea, "and ours have names like *Moth*."

"Moth's a good skill," said Higu, from the window, without looking up from her notebook.

"Moth's a *great* skill," said Thea. "That's not what I mean." She looked at him. "I mean the world looked at you, when you were seventeen, and said *Maim*. And it looked at Higu and said *Moth*. And at me, *Parse*, and *Patch*. And at Maigo, *Root*." She was quiet a moment. "It names you for what you do. What you're going to do. It looked at you and saw a war."

Nobody said anything.

"It wasn't wrong," said Ars.

***

Alea, on the rug, said: "There's a ninth."

They all looked at her.

"After *Annihilate*," said Alea. She wasn't looking at any of them. She was looking at Waddles, asleep in her lap, and stroking his head. "When he goes up. There'll be a ninth. And a tenth, if there's a tenth." She looked up at her father. "He doesn't know what they'll be called. He never knows, till it happens."

"What do you *think* they'll be called?" said Xola.

Ars looked at the line of eight words for a long moment. *Slice. Cut. Maim. Split. Pierce. Sunder. Rend. Annihilate.* A boy's first tier in a field behind a cottage, at fourteen, and the world saying *Slice*. And then nineteen years of it, each word bigger and worse than the one before, until the night on the Redmarch when the world had looked at him, with Diwa's blood still on the black sword, and said *Annihilate*.

"I don't know," he said. "Something worse, probably."

He closed the window.

***

Higu showed him the page, later. When the others were getting their coats on and Xola was looking for her other boot, which was, as always, under the bench. She came over from the window with her notebook open and held it out to him without a word.

He read it.

She'd copied the window. All of it, exactly, every word, in her small neat hand. And under it, a line she'd drawn, and under the line, in the same hand, a list. Eight items. The skills, one per line, numbered. And beside each one, in very small writing, a guess: the age he'd been when it came, as near as she could work out from everything he'd told them in nine evenings. *14. 16? 17. 21. 23? 25?* And beside the last two, *Rend* and *Annihilate*, the same thing twice: *27. The Redmarch.*

And at the bottom, two more lines. Numbered *9* and *10*. Empty.

"For when you find out," said Higu.

He looked at her.

"You don't have to tell us," said Higu. "But I'd like to write them down. When they come." She closed the notebook. "I've written everything else."

***

He walked them to the gate that night. He'd started doing that, the second week. The five of them in their coats with their lanterns, and Alea, and him, and the hens following PenguChi round the yard like ducklings.

At the gate, Thea stopped.

The others went on through, up onto the lake road. She stayed. She stood by the gatepost in the snow with her lantern, and looked up the road, at the lights of the town, and then back at him.

"Gabe said he's coming to find you," she said.

Ars didn't say anything.

"In the summer," said Thea. "The night of the sum. *Declan, if you can somehow read this. I'm coming to find you.*" She was watching his face. "And Deston said *me too*."

"I know," said Ars. "I read it."

"Are you going to tell him?"

He looked up the lake road. At the five lanterns going up it, and the lights of the town at the top, and past them, the dark country going away south, down the river, a long way, to a city with a forge on the wharves and a roof behind a chimney stack where two men hummed a song with a gap in it.

"Yes," he said. "Myself. Not in the channel. Not in a letter. I want to see his face."

"When?"

He thought about it.

"When I can," he said. "He always found me first. Every time. In every game, every city. He always turned up." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "He'll probably turn up."

Thea looked at him for a long moment.

She didn't know. None of them did. Groups 4 and 6 had kept it out of the channel as carefully as Wrenhaven had kept Wrenhaven. She had no idea that, a long way downriver, behind a chimney stack in Kingsferry, two men were sitting on a roof every night with their hands out over a parapet, pointing north, waiting for the river to open.

Neither did he.

"He probably will," said Thea.

And went up the road after the others.

***

He stood at the gate until the lanterns went round the corner at the top. Rowan stepped aside for them. Then Rowan stepped back. Ars watched the big man stand there a moment longer, looking down the lake road at him, and lift his hand. Ars lifted his.

Then he went back in, past the hens, who'd come back from the gate and were standing in a row in the snow looking at him with their usual expression of total contempt.

"Goodnight," he said to them. In English.

Captain turned her back on him.

He went in. Alea was on the rug, with the fire banked and her knees up, looking at him.

"Dad," she said.

"Mm."

"You're happy."

He thought about it. Honestly. The way she deserved. And found, to his surprise, that it didn't take very long.

"Yes," he said.

Alea smiled at the fire.

"Good," she said. "Then the basket stays five."
