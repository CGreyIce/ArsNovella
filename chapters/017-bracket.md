Drew had run eleven tournaments on Earth, and he'd known on the morning of every single one that something was going to go wrong.

That was the secret, if there was one. It wasn't that he was calm. People always said he was calm: the voice that turned a room toward it, the man who could get thirty strangers in a voice channel to agree on a schedule. But he wasn't calm, not really. He just knew, all the way down, that something always went wrong, and that the job wasn't stopping it. The job was being the person who was still standing there smiling when it did.

So when Chris's letter arrived at Thornwall in the eleventh week, by way of Hyvred, who'd read it first and then come running up the tower stairs two at a time, Drew read it, and smiled, and felt the old familiar flutter start somewhere under his ribs.

*A tournament of the summoned. All thirty-five. In the Lists at Valcrest, when the year turns.*

*Tell Drew. He's going to want to run it. Somebody should.*

"Well?" said Hyvred, bouncing on her toes. "*Well?*"

Drew folded the letter very carefully and put it in his pocket.

"I'm going to need paper," he said.

***

There wasn't any paper in Thornwall. Not spare paper. There was the quartermaster's ledger, which was guarded like a dragon's hoard, and the chaplain's prayer book, which was sacred, and there were Corvin's maps.

"No," said Corvin, when Drew asked.

"Just the old ones."

"There *aren't* any old ones. Every map on that wall is—" Corvin stopped. He looked at the wall of maps, with its dozens of pins, and then at Drew's face, and then at the wall again. "Some of them are duplicates," he admitted.

"Duplicates are perfect."

"You'll only use the backs."

"Only the backs," Drew promised.

So by the end of the week the long room at the top of the north tower had two walls of maps: Corvin's, on the east wall, with his pins and his tracks and his villages-acting-strangely; and Drew's, on the west wall, on the backs of the duplicates, covered in charcoal lines and boxes and arrows, which nobody but Drew could read.

"What *is* that?" asked Tiger, on the sixth day, staring at it.

"A bracket."

"It looks like a spider exploded."

"It's a *good* bracket," said Drew. "It's just not finished."

***

Everyone, it turned out, had opinions about the tournament.

"Duels," said Tiger, immediately. He was doing push-ups on the floor of the long room, which was where he did most of his thinking. "One on one. Best fighter wins. That's the only fair way."

"That's the only way *you'd* want," said Bee, from the hearth, where she was drawing on the stone. "Because you're fast."

"I'm not fast *yet*."

"Then you'd lose."

"I'd lose *nobly.*"

"Groups or individuals?" Bee asked Drew, ignoring him. She asked it the way she asked everything: straight out, no softening, as if the answer were already obvious and she was just checking.

"Both," said Drew.

"That's not an answer."

"It is if you do both."

Kei was by the window, as usual, with Hisui coiled around his wrist. "Hisui," he reported wearily, "wants to know if there's a snake division."

"There's no snake division."

Kei listened. "He says that's discrimination."

"He can file a complaint with the organiser."

Kei listened again. "He says you *are* the organiser."

"Then I've received it," said Drew, "and I'm taking it under advisement."

Hisui apparently found this acceptable, because Kei sighed with relief and went back to the window.

Hyvred was sitting cross-legged on the table in the middle of the room, looking at the west wall with her head tilted, the way she looked at things now: seeing more than was there. "You keep drawing lines between the groups," she said. "Across them. Why?"

Drew put down his charcoal.

"Because I don't want it to be seven groups," he said.

They all looked at him.

"Think about it." He gestured at the wall. "If we just do group against group, it's seven teams. Group 1 versus Group 3. Group 5 versus Group 7. And everyone goes home afterwards having fought people they don't know, standing with the same four people they've stood with all year." He picked the charcoal back up and drew a long line across three of the boxes. "But we're not seven teams. We never were. Half of us knew each other before any of this. You know Chris and Hako. Kei knows practically everyone. I know people in four other groups. Gigichi in Group 1 is a friend of mine and I haven't spoken to her since the hall."

"So?" said Bee.

"So there's a group round, because people will want it. And a singles bracket, because Tiger will cry if there isn't."

"I won't *cry*—"

"And then," said Drew, "a mixed round. Random teams. One from each group, or as near as I can make it. Fighting together. People who've never stood next to each other, standing next to each other." He looked at the wall. "So that when it's over, we're not seven groups anymore. We're thirty-five."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That's actually really nice," said Tiger, sounding betrayed.

"I know," said Drew. "I'm very good at this."

***

They compared windows that night, like every night, round the fire in the long room with the two walls of maps watching them.

"Fifteen," said Hyvred. "The sight's... I can see light at a distance now. Further every week. I stood on the wall this morning and I could see the shepherds' lights on the far side of the valley. Little ones. Like candles." She hesitated. "And I can see the bits where there *isn't* any. That's getting clearer too."

Nobody asked her to explain that. They all knew what she meant.

"Twelve," said Kei. "Hisui and I can name every plant in this valley that'll heal you and every one that'll kill you. Some of them are the same plant." He held up his hand, Hisui's head resting on his knuckles. "He's been teaching me. In his way. Mostly by saying *no, not that one, idiot.*"

"Eleven," said Bee. "And nothing's moved since the sparrow. Not once." She said it flatly, as if it didn't matter, and her Pollen Puffs pressed themselves against her neck on both sides the way they did when it did. "I draw every night. Nothing."

"It'll come back," said Hyvred.

"You don't know that."

"No," Hyvred agreed. "But I saw it the first time. So I know it's in there."

Bee looked at her for a long moment, and then down at her charcoal-stained hands, and didn't say anything else. But she drew another bird on the hearthstone before she went to bed, and stared at it for a long time.

"Eight," said Tiger, with great bitterness. Then, grudgingly: "Something happened, though. In the yard. With Hild. She swung at me and I moved before I'd decided to move. Like my legs knew before I did." He frowned. "It only happened once. I tried for an hour to make it happen again."

"That's speed," said Drew. "That's yours. That's the start of it."

"It was *once.*"

"Once is the hardest one."

"Nine," Drew said, when they looked at him. He'd chosen the brawler's path, the bladed knuckles, the counter-fighter who waited and read. Hild had been teaching him, in between Hyvred's blindfold sessions. "It's slow. But I don't mind slow. I've watched people play the same game for ten years to get one rank higher. This is just that."

***

They went back to Coldwater in the twelfth week. Hyvred, and Corvin, and Hild, and this time Drew as well, because Corvin had asked for "someone who's good at talking to people," and Drew had raised his hand before anybody else could.

It was worse than the first time.

Drew didn't have Hyvred's eyes. He couldn't see light, or the shapes of things that weren't there. But he could see a village, and a village was a lot like a crowd, and he'd been reading crowds his whole life. And the moment they rode down the lane into Coldwater, something in him went quiet and careful, the way it did before a match when he could already tell it was going to go wrong.

The man at the well was gone. Of course he was. But so were two more households: a farmhouse by the stream and a cottage at the end of the lane. Their doors were open. Inside, the tables were set. Bowls, spoons, a loaf of bread gone hard as stone, a pot on a cold hearth with something grey in the bottom of it. A child's wooden horse on the floor by the door, as if it had been dropped on the way out.

No dogs. No chickens. No sound at all, except the wind.

Hyvred stood in the doorway of the cottage for a long time without speaking.

"What do you see?" asked Hild quietly.

"Smears," said Hyvred. Her voice was very small. She pointed at the doorframe, at a place that looked, to Drew, like nothing but old wood. "Here. And on the table. On the horse." She swallowed. "The wrong light. Like the man at the well. Like a handprint, but the hand's the wrong shape." She stepped back out into the lane. "Someone came in here wearing someone. And then everyone left."

Corvin was writing, fast, his hand shaking.

Drew went to find someone to talk to. It was what he was for.

There was an old woman in the last house that still had smoke coming from its chimney, sitting on a bench in her garden with a shawl round her shoulders, shelling peas into a bowl. She looked up when Drew came to the gate. Her face was very calm, and very tired, and she didn't seem surprised to see him at all.

"Morning," said Drew, gently.

"Morning."

"I'm looking for the people from the cottage at the end of the lane. And the farm by the stream." He leaned on the gate. "Do you know where they went?"

"Visiting," said the old woman. "Family. In the hills." She went on shelling peas. "They all went at night. They said they'd be back."

"When was that?"

"Oh, a while ago now." She looked up at the ridge above the village, the one where Hyvred had seen the hole in the dark. "Everyone goes visiting, lately. I don't. My knees." She smiled at him, faintly. "They asked me. Very kindly. Lovely manners, they had, the new ones. But my knees."

Drew kept his face exactly as it was. He'd had a lot of practice.

"The new ones?" he said.

"The ones who came to stay. Last spring." She pointed with a pea pod. "In the miller's house. Ever so polite. They don't keep dogs." She went back to her bowl. "Nobody keeps dogs anymore. The dogs didn't like them."

***

Hild found the tracks at the top of the lane, where it turned into a sheep path and went up toward the ridge. Footprints in the soft earth: lots of them, people's boots, going up. A child's, small, among them. All going up. None coming back down.

And among the boots, here and there, something else. Long and narrow, with too many joints, and no heel.

Nobody said anything on the ride back to Thornwall. Not one word, the whole way.

***

Yan's letter was waiting when they got back.

Hyvred read it standing in the courtyard with her horse's reins still in her hand. Drew watched her face, and watched it go still.

"Scouts," she said. "In the east. On the Redmarch. Eleven of them. They stood out of range and counted the fort. Every tower." She looked up. "And one of them felt like the grave. To Yan. The same cold." She held out the letter. "*You saw someone wearing someone in the west. I think I felt one in the east.*"

Corvin made a sound like he'd been hit.

"The same thing," said Hild slowly. "East and west."

"Two sides," said Drew. He was thinking about the Advisor in the hall, the first day. *If they come from two sides, we cannot hold both.* "That's what he meant. That's what he was afraid of."

They stood in the courtyard for a long time, the four of them, with the mountains going dark behind the fortress and the valley below them very quiet.

"We tell everyone," said Drew at last. "Every group. Tonight. Everything we saw in Coldwater. Everything Yan saw. Everybody should know." He looked at Hyvred. "That's what the letters are for."

***

It took them half the night. Six letters, one to every other group, in English, with Hyvred's account and Corvin's tracks and the old woman's peas and Yan's eleven scouts all written down in the same place for the first time. Hild sat by the door the whole time with her sword across her knees, listening to the dark.

When they were done, and the others had gone to bed, Drew sat alone at the table under his bracket, the exploded spider of charcoal lines on the backs of Corvin's maps, and took out one more sheet of paper.

He thought for a while before he wrote. He thought about eleven tournaments on Earth, and the one he remembered best: a small one, a friendly one, an Apex tournament he'd hosted for a few dozen people. Yan had carried the whole final on his back. Yan's duo partner had spent the entire thing joking in voice chat, narrating his own deaths like a nature documentary. And when they won, he'd said, completely seriously, *I want everyone to know I was the reason we won. Trust.* Drew had laughed so hard on stream that he'd had to mute himself.

He'd only known Ice seven or eight months. He'd always thought there'd be more.

He picked up the pen.

*Lucan,*

*Chris tells me you're organising a tournament. I've run a few. I'd like to run this one.*

*I'll send you a bracket. Three rounds: groups, singles, and a mixed round where every team has someone from a different group. I want them standing next to people they don't know. I think, by the time this happens, they're going to need to.*

*And Lucan: make sure there's room. Not just in the stands. Make sure there's room for all thirty-five of us to be in the same place, at the same time, for as long as you can manage it.*

*I don't think we've got as long as everyone thinks.*

*I'll run it.*

*Drew.*

He sealed it, and set it with the others, and sat back in his chair and looked up at the bracket on the wall. It wasn't finished. He wasn't sure it ever would be. There were too many lines going across it now, between the boxes, from group to group, the way the roads went across the kingdom, the way the letters did.

Something was going to go wrong. Something always did.

The job was being the person still standing there, smiling, when it did.

He blew out the candle, and went to bed, and didn't sleep at all.
