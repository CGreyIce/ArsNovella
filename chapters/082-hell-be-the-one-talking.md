Garrow had never been anywhere.

He'd been born in Wrenhaven, over the forge on the square, in the room his father had been born in. He'd learned to shoe a horse at nine and taken over the forge at twenty when his father's back went, and he'd shod every horse in Wrenhaven for twenty years since: the miller's cart horses and the mayor's grey and the old pony the egg-woman's boy rode out to the farms. He'd made plough blades and gate hinges and pot hooks and once, memorably, a weathervane in the shape of a pike, for the inn, that the whole town still argued about. He'd been as far as the market at Hethe, twice, with his father, as a boy.

That was all. That was the whole of Garrow's life, until a king walked across the market square in a plain coat and asked if he could watch him work.

He'd been walking for a month now. East.

***

He'd thought he knew what the kingdom looked like. He'd seen maps. Odette had a big one on the wall of the town hall, with Wrenhaven at the top and the capital in the middle and everything else round the edges in small writing, and he'd looked at it a hundred times while he waited to be paid for the town hall's railings.

The map hadn't said anything about how big it was.

They'd come down out of the lake country at Hethe and turned east, and the land had gone flat, and stayed flat, for days. Fields, and then pasture, and then a kind of long grey grassland with nothing on it but sheep and wind and the road going away in front of them straight as a ruled line. Then hills. Then forest, dark and close, where the oxen didn't like the smell of something and the four militiamen walked with their hands on their swords. Then hills again, browner, harder, with the wind coming at them every morning out of the east with a smell in it that Garrow didn't know.

Iron, he'd thought, the first time. Old iron. Rust.

The militiamen had looked at each other. One of them, Wat, who was older and had been in the war, said: "That's the Redmarch."

***

He saw it on the last morning.

They'd come up over a long low ridge at dawn, the oxen leaning into the yokes and the dray creaking under its load the way it had creaked every step for a month, and Garrow was walking at the front with his hand on the side of the dray, the way he always walked, and he came up over the top of the ridge and stopped.

It was red.

He'd heard it was red. Everybody had. It was in the songs; it was in the name. He'd thought it meant *reddish*. Brown with a bit of red in it, the way some clay is. It didn't. It meant *red*. The whole land in front of them, from the foot of the ridge to the mountains on the horizon, flat and wide and endless, was the colour of rust, the colour of old blood dried on a blade, with a grey grass standing up out of it in tufts that didn't move in the wind. Nothing grew on it. Nothing walked on it. There were no birds.

And on the near edge of it, at the foot of the ridge, a fort. Grey stone walls and towers, very old, very patched. A flag. Smoke from chimneys. And over the east gate, the one that faced out across the red, something hanging. Big. Black. Two great curving shapes, like the horns of some enormous bull, nailed up over the gate where everyone could see them.

"Those are the general's horns," said Wat quietly, beside him. "From the war. He cut them off the morning after." He didn't say who *he* was. He didn't need to. "Sent them east last year, I heard, when the scouts started counting. Hung them up there so the things on the red could see them."

Garrow looked at the horns over the gate for a long time.

He thought about a man on the shingle by the lake, with his sleeves rolled up, striking for the king. Exactly where he was told. Not one hair more.

"Right," said Garrow. And walked on, down the ridge, toward Cinderhold.

***

The whole fort came out.

He hadn't expected that. He'd expected a guard on the gate and a sergeant and some forms to fill in. Instead, by the time the dray creaked in through the west gate at mid-morning, there were soldiers on the walls and in the doorways and lined up along both sides of the courtyard, dozens of them, in silence. A cook with flour on his arms. A smith, a real one, in a leather apron, who looked at Garrow's apron and nodded, one smith to another. An old woman from the laundry, very straight, with her hands folded.

They'd heard. Somehow, they'd heard. Garrow found out later that a rider had come a week ahead of them from Hethe, with a letter for the chaplain, and the chaplain had told the fort what was coming, and the fort had been out on the walls every morning since, watching the west road.

The chaplain came out last.

An old man, older than Garrow had expected, in a black coat gone grey at the elbows, with a book under his arm and a face like a church on a weekday: quiet, and worn, and kind. He came down the steps of a little chapel at the side of the courtyard and walked across the cobbles to the dray, slowly, and stopped beside it, and looked up at Garrow.

"You'll be the smith," he said.

"Garrow. From—" He stopped. He'd been told. Not in so many words. But he'd been told. "From the north," he said.

The old man's eyes did something. Then he smiled.

"Aldous," he said. "Thank you for bringing her."

***

Garrow gave him the letter.

He'd carried it in the inside pocket of his coat for a month, against his chest, next to the hammer. *Aldous, Cinderhold*, in a square careful hand. He'd taken it out every night by the fire and looked at it, and not opened it, and put it back. It was the most important thing he'd ever carried, and he knew it.

Aldous took it. He looked at the seal for a long moment. Then he broke it, standing there in the courtyard beside the dray, and read it.

Garrow watched his face while he read. He'd learned to do that from the king's guard, the tall dark woman who'd sat on the cabin step every night with her little black book. *Feet first*, she'd told him once, at the forge, when he asked what she wrote. *Then hands. Then the face, last.*

Aldous's feet didn't move. His hands, on the letter, started to shake. And his face, by the end of it, was wet.

He folded the letter up again, very carefully, along its creases, and put it inside his own coat.

"He asks," said Aldous, and stopped, and cleared his throat. "He asks that the five who carried the sword should carry this. The last stretch. By hand." He looked out across the courtyard, at somebody Garrow couldn't see yet. "He says they started it. They should finish."

***

And then Garrow heard it.

From the far side of the courtyard, behind the crowd of soldiers, coming closer. A voice. Young, and high, and very fast, and much too loud, the way a voice is when somebody's trying very hard to talk over something they don't want to feel.

"—no, I *know* it's from the north, Salty, everything's from the north, I'm saying *how far* north, because if it's the lake country that's like a month, and if it's a month then he must have sent it right after, which means he went *straight home* and *straight to a forge*, which means—"

"Maigo."

"—which means he had it *planned*, he had it planned the whole time, at the grave, when he said *half*, he already—"

"*Maigo.*"

The crowd of soldiers parted.

And Garrow saw them.

***

He knew them at once. He'd been told he would.

*There are five people there who'll carry it the last stretch*, the fisherman had said, on the shingle, the morning they left. Very quietly, looking at the black stone on the dray. *A short one who's very strong. And a girl with a bow. And a tall one with a snake. And a thin one who never stops watching. And a tree. You'll know him. He'll be the one talking.*

The short one who was very strong was at the front. He was shorter than Garrow by a head and nearly as wide, with arms like the beams of a mill and a blunt square face, and he was walking very carefully, the way a man walks when he's had his ribs broken this autumn and they still remember it.

The girl with the bow was beside him. Dark hair with green at the ends. A pair of strange purple ear-coverings round her neck, on a band. Eyes that didn't match: one brown, one violet. She was holding the short one's hand.

The tall one with the snake was behind them. Very tall, and very still, with long silver-white hair and pointed ears like nothing Garrow had ever seen, and a white snake wound round his neck like a scarf, with bright green eyes. He was looking at the dray. Not at Garrow. At the dray. At the shape under the tarpaulin.

The thin one who never stopped watching was beside the tall one. Small, slight, calm. His eyes were moving. Garrow felt them move over him, and the dray, and the oxen, and the militiamen, and Aldous, and come back, the way a man's eyes move over a forge he's never seen before, learning where everything is.

And the tree.

He was very small. Smaller than any of them. Bark-brown skin, and two branching antlers coming up out of the hood of a mustard-yellow coat, with little green leaves on them, and a pair of black spectacles pushed up on his forehead that seemed to be made of tiny black squares. One bare foot, with toes like roots. He was talking. He'd been talking the whole way across the courtyard. He was still talking when he saw the dray.

He stopped.

He stopped dead, in the middle of the cobbles, in the middle of a word, with his mouth still open. And the whole courtyard went quiet around him, because everybody in Cinderhold, Garrow would learn, knew what it meant when Maigo stopped talking.

***

Aldous pulled the tarpaulin off.

He did it himself. He climbed up onto the back of the dray, an old man, slowly, and took hold of the edge of the canvas, and drew it back, the whole length of it, the way you'd fold back a blanket from somebody sleeping.

And there it was. On its back in the straw, the way it had lain for a month. Black. A long gentle shape like a shield with a rounded top, and the light lying on it soft, like light on still water. The long black spike coming out of the bottom of it, lying back along the straw. And across the face of it, near the top, the letters.

Garrow couldn't read them. He'd tried every night for a month, by the fire, and his eyes had slid off them every time, the way water slides off oil. He knew what they said. The fox girl had read them out loud on the shingle at sunset, and he'd been close enough to hear. But he couldn't read them.

The five of them could.

***

He watched it happen.

He'd remember it for the rest of his life, and he'd tell it, once, years later, to his own apprentice, in the forge on the square in Wrenhaven, late at night, and the boy wouldn't believe him.

He watched the five of them come up to the side of the dray, one after another, and look at the stone. And he watched each of their faces read it. Not slide off it. *Read* it. Their eyes going along the letters from left to right, slowly, the way the fox girl's had. And he watched what it did to them.

The short one, Salty, read it, and his face didn't change at all for a long moment. And then it went, all at once, the way a wall goes when the mortar finally gives: from the top, straight down, all of it. He put his hand over his eyes. The girl with the bow caught his arm.

"He wrote it in English," said Salty. His voice was barely there. "He wrote it in *English*."

The girl with the bow read it. Garrow saw her lips move. She didn't say anything. She reached out, past Salty, and put her fingertips on the letters, very lightly, one after another, the way you'd touch something you weren't sure was real. Then she took her hand away and held it against her chest, against a little star on a chain round her neck, and closed her eyes.

The tall one with the snake read it. He read it twice. He was so still that the snake round his neck lifted its head and looked at him.

"For her," said the tall one, very quietly. "He wrote it for her. In the letters she could read." He was quiet a moment. "He taught her. Before. He must have. He taught her our letters, and she could read them, and nobody else in this world could." His voice went, slightly. "So he wrote her name in them."

The thin one who never stopped watching read it, and kept reading it, long after the others had stopped. His eyes went along the line and back, and along it again, and then stopped. On one letter. Near the end. Garrow followed his eyes and couldn't see what he was looking at; it was just a shape, sliding.

"One's gone over twice," said the thin one. Very softly. "The L. It's deeper than the rest. He went over it twice." He swallowed. "Like the sword. *Aspera.* One letter gone over twice."

Nobody said anything.

***

And the tree.

The tree had come up to the side of the dray last of all, and he was too small to see over it properly, so he'd climbed up onto the wheel, with his bare root-toed foot on the hub and his hands on the side-boards, and he was reading it. His lips were moving. Garrow could see him read it, letter by letter, slowly, the way a child reads.

"*To*," said the tree. "*Diwa*." He stopped. "*My*." He stopped again, for a long time. "*Love*."

And then the tree started to cry.

Not quietly. Not the way the others had. Like a child cries. Great gulping sobs, with his face screwed up and his black spectacles falling off his forehead into the straw, and his hands gripping the side of the dray so hard the wood creaked, and the little leaves on his antlers shaking.

The short one reached up and put a big hand on the tree's back. The tall one's snake slid down off his shoulders and along the side of the dray and wound itself, very gently, round the tree's ankle.

"It's in *English*," said the tree, through it. "He wrote it in *English*. On her *grave*. Where anyone could see." He gasped. "Where *we* could see. Where *we'd* be the ones carrying it. He knew we'd read it. He *knew*." He hit the side of the dray with his fist, not hard. "He didn't say it. On the Redmarch. Salty asked him straight out and he *didn't say it*. And then he sends us *this*."

Garrow stood there with his hand on the yoke of the near ox and didn't know where to look.

He'd been told, on the shingle. *You'll know him. He'll be the one talking.*

He'd been told the whole thing, he realised. The fisherman had known exactly what would happen when they pulled the tarpaulin back. He'd described five people Garrow had never met so well that Garrow had known every one of them across a courtyard. And he'd known that the tree would be talking, and that he'd stop.

***

They found out about the king from Rhea.

Or rather, Rhea found out from Garrow, and then everyone found out from Rhea, because Rhea had been standing at the back of the crowd with her arms folded and a new pink scar across her scalp, and she'd come forward when the tree was quieter, and looked at the stone, and looked at Garrow, and said, in a loud and very practical voice:

"Who made it? That's not a stone. That's iron. That's *forged*. Who forged that?"

And Garrow, who'd been carrying it for a month and hadn't said a word about it to anyone but the oxen, and whose chest was full to bursting with it, said:

"The king."

The courtyard went very quiet.

"The *king*," said Rhea.

"King Severin," said Garrow. "Himself. With his own hands. In a forge we built on the shore. I worked the bellows." He couldn't help it; it came out of him like steam out of a quench. "He made it in three days. He made the girl's half in five. He cut the sword with the fisherman holding the chisel bare-handed and him bringing the hammer down, once, and the whole lake rang like a bell, and he gave me this—"

He pulled the hammer out of his belt. Small, old, the handle dark with use.

"His father's," said Garrow. "From a town called Brakewater. He gave it me when he left. He said *keep your thumb out of the way*." He held it out, so they could see. His hand was shaking. "He was a smith, before. Nobody knows. He was the best smith I ever saw in my life. I didn't know you could hit iron like that."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"The *king*," said Rhea again, faintly, "forged a headstone."

"And the fisherman struck for him," said Garrow. "Better than anyone the king ever had. The king said so."

***

"Where?" said the short one.

He'd wiped his face. He was standing by the dray, square and blunt, looking at Garrow very steadily.

"Where's the forge?" said Salty. "Where's the shore? Where's he *live*?"

Garrow opened his mouth.

"In Wren—"

"Garrow," said Aldous.

Very gently. From the back of the dray, where he was still standing in the straw beside the stone. Garrow looked up at him. The old chaplain was shaking his head, very slightly. Not unkindly. The way you'd shake your head at a child who was about to say something at a funeral.

"That's his to tell," said Aldous.

Garrow shut his mouth.

The five of them were all looking at him now. All five. Even the tree, with his face blotched and his spectacles back on crooked.

Garrow thought about the shingle, the morning they left. The fisherman standing by the dray in the frost, with his hand on the black stone, looking down at it. And then looking up at Garrow, and saying, very quietly, as if he'd thought about it a long time: *They'll ask you where. When they do, tell them this.*

"He said you'd ask," said Garrow.

They waited.

"He said to tell you," said Garrow, "it's a long way. And it's cold." He swallowed. "And the fish are good."

***

There was a short silence.

Then the girl with the bow laughed.

It came out of her short and startled and very wet, as if it had been knocked out of her. She put her hand over her mouth. Beside her, the short one made a sound that might have been the same thing. The thin one who never stopped watching had shut his eyes. And the tall one with the snake, the one who'd said *for her*, smiled. Slowly. For the first time since he'd come across the courtyard.

"*The fish are good*," said the tall one.

"That's what he said," said Garrow helplessly. "I don't know what it means."

"We do," said the tall one.

The tree had stopped crying. He was looking up at Garrow from the wheel of the dray with his face blotched and his spectacles crooked, and something in his face had gone very bright.

"*The fish are good*," said the tree. "That's not an *answer*. That's *so* not an answer." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "That's the most him thing I've ever heard."

***

Garrow gave them the sack last.

He'd almost forgotten it. It had sat beside the stone in the straw for a month, lumpy and heavy, tied at the neck with twine. He lifted it down off the dray and set it on the cobbles in front of them, and untied it, and opened the neck so they could see.

Stones. Small ones. The size of a child's fist. Dozens of them. Grey field stone and brown river stone and one or two white ones, from the lake shore. All different. All just stones.

"Children brought them," said Garrow. "Down the lake road. For the forge, at first. One each. And then every morning. Every child in—" He caught Aldous's eye. "Every child in the town. One each, every morning, and he went down on one knee for every one of them, and took it, and said *thank you*. Every single one." He looked at the sack. "He didn't know what to do with them. And then he did. He said to set them round the foot of hers. Inside the flowers. In a ring." He swallowed. "So she'd know a whole town carried her stone."

The tree got down off the wheel of the dray.

He came and knelt on the cobbles in front of the sack, and put his hand in, very carefully, and took out one of the stones. A small grey one, the size of a walnut, worn smooth. He held it in his palm and looked at it for a long time.

"He said thank you to every one?" said the tree.

"Every one."

The tree closed his hand round the stone. He held it against his chest, against the front of his mustard-yellow coat, and bowed his head over it.

He didn't say anything else.

It was the first time, Aldous told Garrow that night in the chapel, in a year, that anyone in Cinderhold had ever known Maigo to be quiet two times in one day.

***

They'd carry it tomorrow. At dawn. Aldous said so. Across the red, the last stretch, by hand, the five of them, to the grave beside the tree.

Garrow was going with them. He hadn't asked. He'd just said, at supper, in the mess hall, in front of everybody, *I'm coming*, and Aldous had looked at him, and nodded.

He lay awake that night in a bunk in the barracks at Cinderhold, on the edge of the Redmarch, a month from home, with the king's father's hammer under his pillow. He could hear the wind outside, coming in off the red, with that smell in it. Old iron. Rust.

And through the wall, from the next room, very faintly, a voice. Young, and high, and fast. Talking. On and on, the way it had come across the courtyard that morning, filling the dark, about nothing at all: about stones, and fish, and kings, and somebody called Niko who was apparently up to three heartbeats now, and whether trees could be homesick for lakes they'd never seen.

Then, after a long time, it stopped.

And in the silence, a second voice. Deep and slow. Garrow couldn't hear the words. Just the sound of it. Saying something short.

And the first voice, very quietly, saying something back. Just one word, Garrow thought. He couldn't hear what.

He thought it might have been *okay*.
