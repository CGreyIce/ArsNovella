Four came in before breakfast.

Two from the north quay, a lighterman and his boy, with the same hook through the same two fingers, because the boy had been holding the line when his father pulled it in. One from the fish market, a gutting girl called Mags, fourteen, who'd found it in the bottom of a crate of herring and put her hand straight on it. And one from the customs house itself: a clerk, middle-aged, in a good coat, who'd picked it up off the bottom step on his way in at seven because it was lying there bright and new and he'd thought somebody had dropped it.

Rachael looked at the clerk's hand for a long time.

"The bottom step," she said.

"It was just lying there." He was very pale. He kept looking at his palm, at the little puncture in the meat of the thumb, and the hair-wide line of grey round it that hadn't been there at seven and was there now. "I thought it was a fishing hook. I was going to hand it in."

"It is a fishing hook," said Rachael. "Sit down."

He sat. On the end of Maud's bench, in the long ward, in the river smell and the early sun. The fourth bench they'd put out since the spring; there were eleven now, down both sides of the room and two across the end under the window, where Ned Webb had slept since the grey got to his wrist, with his mother in a chair.

She didn't look up at Sabine, in the door. She didn't need to. She knew what Sabine's face was doing. Gabe had been on those steps every night for five weeks, from dusk till the first grey, with his back to the infirmary and his arms folded, and nobody had come up them. Not the hat man. Not anybody. And every morning when he came off them at first light, there was a hook on the bottom one.

He'd stopped telling her. She'd stopped asking. Dennis looked at each one with *True* before anybody touched it, and it was always the same, the most nothing thing he'd ever looked at; and Gabe put it in a box under his bed at the chandler's, and the box was getting full.

"Maud," said Rachael.

"I see it." Maud came down the ward on her stick. She was slow this morning. She'd been slow all week; she'd been up three nights, with the dock cases, and she was seventy-one, and she wouldn't sit down. She looked at the clerk's hand without bending. "Emrys. You're on."

***

They did it the way they'd learned to do it. It had taken them six weeks to learn and two of Emrys's fingertips.

Maren had written it out. Not in the channel; she couldn't read the channel, she was a native. She'd written it in a letter by the post road, in her small formal hand, three sheets, to Emrys, guide to guide: what she'd done on Lissa Fowler's arm, the seventh word of her flame, what it meant to make it mean *what* and not *where*. And Emrys had sat at Maud's table every night for a month with a candle and a pot of iron-gall ink, writing his own name on the back of his left hand and burning it off.

He did it singing. Everything Emrys did, he did singing; he'd been a court musician before he was a guide, and he taught spellcraft as song, pitch and breath and rhythm, and a spell sung off-key failed. His flame-chant was a song. Seven words of it were the same as Maren's, in the old order, the way all the guides had learned it from the Advisor; and the seventh word was *to*. And he'd sung *to* for thirty years meaning *there*, *at*, *the place I'm looking*, and it had taken him till the week of the rhyme to sing it meaning *this*. *The word*. Every stroke of it.

He'd taken a coin of skin off Ned the first time, at the wrist. It had answered. A real burn, red and wet and ordinary, that Rachael had mended in a breath, and under it no grey. And then he'd taken skin off three more, smaller every time, and burned his own fingertips black on the fourth, and Maud had made him sit with his hands in a basin of cold water for an hour and called him a great soft fool, and he'd sung through it, quietly, so nobody could hear what the song was.

Now he could do it on a clerk's thumb in the time it took to say the clerk's name.

He sat on the stool in front of the man and took his hand, gently, palm up, and looked at the grey. And sang. Low. Seven words, the old ones, under his breath, so the ward didn't hear; and on the seventh, a thread of fire came out of the end of his finger, a hair wide, white at the heart, and went into the grey.

Rachael watched it go.

She always did. She couldn't not. It went round the puncture the way the grey went round it: following. Not the skin; the word. The tiny emptying word that Thea said was written there ten thousand times in a line, round and round the cut, like a seam. The fire followed every stroke of it. And the grey went up as smoke, cold, a thin grey thread of it, smelling of a cellar step, the way it had at Wrenhaven; and under it the skin was red.

Red. Hot. Hurting. A clerk's thumb with a hole in it, bleeding, ordinary.

"*Ow*," said the clerk, in surprise.

"Good," said Maud. "That's you. Rachael."

Rachael put her hands out.

"Can I?" she said.

The clerk looked at her. They always did, the first time; nobody in Kingsferry was used to a healer who asked. "Yes," he said. "Please. Yes."

She didn't go in at once. That was Xola's. *Don't push*. She held his hand in both of hers and let her own warmth sit by the cut, close, the way you'd put a hand by a sleeping animal, and waited. Listened. Till she felt what the flesh was already trying to do, which was close, which a cut always wanted, which was the most ordinary thing in the world. And then she helped it. *Mend*, slow, both hands, the way Maud taught her in the first winter, the way you'd pour water into a cup you couldn't see the bottom of.

It answered.

She felt it answer. The thing she'd missed so badly, those first weeks with Ned, that she'd lain awake for it: the tail thumping on the floor. The flesh taking what she gave it, and doing something, and knitting. The cut closed under her hands. Pink. Smooth. A clerk's thumb.

She let go. She was cold. She was always cold now; it was the price, and she paid it four times before breakfast and nine times before supper and didn't mind.

"That's all?" said the clerk.

"That's all," said Rachael. "Don't pick things up off steps."

***

Ned was the one they couldn't do.

He was on the end bench under the window, the way he'd been for nearly seven weeks. Ten years old. A rope-walk boy, with the tar ground into his knuckles and the rope-walk's squint, and a left arm that was grey from the palm to above the elbow. Not all over. In a line. A hair-wide line of it that had come up out of the hook-hole in the meat of his palm in the spring and gone round and round his arm like a seam on a sleeve, round and up, round and up, slowly, a hair a day, the way she'd written it down in his notebook every hour for the first week and then every morning.

And over the start of it, in the meat of his palm, over the hook-hole, her own seam.

That was the thing. That was what she couldn't stop looking at. In the spring, the first day, before Maud said *waiting*, before anybody knew what the grey was, she'd closed it. *Close*. Her own skill, the one she'd got on the back of Calvin's wind in her hands at the Lists, the thing that made a cut stop being a cut. She'd closed the cut over the grey, and the grey had stayed under it, and gone on going. And now there was a palm's breadth of Ned Webb's hand where the start of the word was *inside*, under a seam of whole pink skin that she'd put there, sealed in, and Emrys's fire couldn't follow a word to its end when the end was under glass.

He'd tried. He'd burned a coin of it off at the wrist, the first time, and it had answered; and a finger's length off the forearm in the week of the rhyme. And every time, by the next morning, it had grown back up out of the palm, from under her seam, a hair a day, the way it always had. He'd taken the fire down to the seam itself, twice, and it had stopped there the way water stops at glass.

"It's mine," she'd said, the second time. In the channel. At night. *it's mine. I put the lid on it. I've never opened anything on purpose in my life*.

Everybody had something to say about that. Thea said a seam was a join and you could get between. Hyvred said there was somebody under Ossie's. Malcolm said *IF somebody sewed it THEN it can be unpicked*, and *Backup: Rachael*, which had made her want to hit him, a long way off, in Thornwall. Xola said *listen first*.

None of them could tell her how to open her own *Close*.

***

Maud told her.

Not on purpose. Maud didn't do anything on purpose that looked like teaching; she just said things while she was doing something else and you had to catch them. It was nearly noon. The four from the morning had gone home with pink thumbs and instructions. Two more had come in, a carter and a girl from the rope walk who knew Ned, and Emrys had done them, and Rachael had mended them, and she was cold to the elbows and sitting on the stool by Ned's bench with her hands in her armpits, looking at his arm.

Maud was at the table, doing something with a boil.

A dock porter called Dob, with a neck like a bollard. He had a boil on the back of his neck the size of a hen's egg that had been coming for a week and that Maud had told him on Monday to come back with on Thursday, and it was Thursday. He sat on the stool with his head down and his hands on his knees and his face very brave, and Maud stood over him with a lancet.

"Hold still," she said, and opened it.

Rachael watched her do it. She'd seen it done a hundred times; she'd done it herself, in the first winter, with Maud's hand over hers. A quick small cut in the right place, not deep, and the badness coming out, and then you cleaned it, and you let it be open, and in a day or two it closed by itself from the bottom up and you never needed to mend it at all. The most ordinary thing in a healer's life.

"You open it," said Maud, wiping the lancet, not looking round, "to let out what shouldn't be in. Everybody knows that. Every washerwoman knows that." She put a pad on Dob's neck. "Then you leave it. And it closes from the bottom. Clean."

Rachael didn't say anything.

"You've been sitting there looking at that boy's arm for two months," said Maud, "like a girl who's never seen a boil."

"It's not a boil."

"No." Maud turned round. She leaned on her stick and looked at Ned's arm, and at the palm's breadth of whole pink skin over the hook-hole that Rachael had put there. "It's a seam. Yours. Very neat. You always did sew neat." She looked at Rachael. "Where's my case?"

Rachael went still.

She had it in her coat. She always did. She'd had it in her coat since the spring Group 4 first went up the river, two years ago: a needle case of black horn, worn smooth, forty years old, that Maud had pressed into her hands on the jetty and said *come back*. She'd carried it up the Wren and through the Narrows and to the lake and back. She'd held it against her chest on the *Widow* with twenty pairs of eyes on the bank. She'd never once opened it.

She took it out.

"Open it," said Maud.

She did. It came apart in her hands with a little dry click. Inside, on a strip of old red felt, there were needles. Eleven of them. Different sizes. Steel, very fine, a little dark with age, and one of bone, and one at the end, the smallest, that was silver.

"My teacher gave me that," said Maud. "Sister Merewen. Forty-one years ago, when I was thirty and thought I knew everything. She was the last of the old ward." She didn't sit down. "She said a healer needs two things. A hand that closes and a hand that opens. And most of us are born with one or the other and spend our whole lives pretending we've got both." She looked at the silver needle. "That one's for taking stitches out."

Rachael looked at it.

"You sewed it shut," said Maud. "Very neat. So take it out. Same way you put it in. Backwards." She turned back to Dob's neck. "Unpicking's not breaking, girl. It's just sewing the other way. You can't open what you closed by tearing at it. You'd tear the boy. You go back along it, the way it went, one stitch at a time, and you take each one out, and you thank it for holding." She tied off the pad. "Then you let Emrys in."

Dob, on the stool, said, "Can I go, Sister?"

"Thursday," said Maud.

"It is Thursday."

"Then next Thursday," said Maud. "Go."

***

She did it in the afternoon, with all of them there.

That was the rule now. Not because she was frightened of the boy. Because they'd made it, the night of the Nail and Diwa, in the back room of the chandler's, all five of them and Emrys and Sabine round the table: *never alone*. Not to the privy. Not to the end of the street. Maud's old rule, from the first winter, and they'd let it slide, and they'd made it again with their hands flat on the table like a promise. So they were all there. Sabine in the door with her sword across her knees. Calvin on the stairs where he could hear the street. Dennis at the window with his eyes doing the thing, looking at everybody who came up Tanner's Row. Deston on the bench beside her, close, with his hand not quite on her sleeve. Emrys on the stool across, singing nothing yet, with his blackened fingertips.

And Gabe in the corner by the door, on a chair too small for him, with his hands between his knees, near enough.

Ned's mother held his other hand. Ned held his own arm out on the bench, the grey one, the way he'd held it in the spring, like a bird.

"It's going to hurt a bit," said Rachael.

"You always say that," said Ned. "It never does."

"This time it might," said Rachael. "Can I?"

He looked at her for a long time with the rope-walk squint.

"Yeah," he said. "Go on."

She took out the silver needle.

She didn't put it in him. That was the thing she'd understood, sitting by his bench all through dinner with the needle case open on her knee. It wasn't a real needle for a real seam. Her *Close* wasn't thread. But she was a healer, and she could only do what she understood, and she understood a needle. She understood unpicking. She'd taken out a thousand stitches in this ward in four years, Maud's and Tamsin's and her own, on the day they were ready, snip and draw and snip. So she held the silver needle in her right hand, over his wrist, not touching, and put her left hand flat by the seam, close, and listened.

*Don't push*.

She listened to her own *Close*.

She'd never done that. She'd never had to. You closed a thing and it was closed and you went on to the next. But it was there, under her hand, a palm's breadth of it over the hook-hole, the thing she'd made in the spring, and she could feel it the way you can feel your own handwriting on a page in the dark. Neat. Tight. Every stitch exactly where she'd put it. Holding. It had been holding for eight weeks, holding what she'd shut under it, doing exactly what she'd asked it to.

*You thank it for holding*.

She went back along it.

From the heel of the hand, where she'd finished, the way you'd unpick anything: from the last stitch. And she found that she could. Not by pulling. By remembering. By going back along it the way it had gone, the way she'd put it in, and at each place where she'd said *close*, saying *thank you* instead, and letting it go. One stitch. Then the next. The silver needle moving over his skin a hair above it, not touching, the way her hand had moved in the spring.

The skin opened.

Not much. Not torn. A line, a hair wide, pink at the edges, from the heel of his hand toward the hook-hole, opening a stitch at a time under the needle the way a seam opens when you unpick it. And under it, in the line, grey.

Ned made a sound.

"Sorry," said Rachael. She didn't stop. "Nearly. Hold still."

"It's *cold*," said Ned.

"I know. That's the grey. That's what's been in there." She went on. Stitch. Stitch. Every one of them hers. Every one of them letting go when she thanked it, the way a hand lets go when you say *it's all right now*. "It's not you that's cold. It's that. Emrys."

Emrys sang.

She heard the seventh word go out of him, low. *This*. And the white thread of fire came out of his finger and went into the open line in Ned's palm, into the grey, where the seam had been; and followed it. Round. Into the old hook-hole, where it had started, the first stroke of the word, that no fire had ever reached; and back, and out of her opened seam, and up the arm past the wrist, into the rest of it: up the forearm, round and round, the way the grey went. Following the word. Every stroke.

Ned screamed.

His mother held him. Maud was there, suddenly, Rachael hadn't seen her come; she had Ned's head against her apron and her hand on his hair, and she was saying something to him in the river voice, the one she used for the dying, *there now, there now, there now*, and the grey was going up off his arm in a thin cold thread like smoke off a doused fire, smelling of a cellar step, all the way up, round and up, to above the elbow.

And it ran.

Rachael saw it. The way Maren had seen it on Lissa's arm. At the top, past the elbow, where the grey had got to that morning, the end of the word felt the fire coming and went. Up. Faster. A hair-wide line of grey going up the inside of a ten-year-old's arm toward the shoulder like a spider running up a thread.

"*Emrys*—"

"I know." Emrys's voice was very steady. He didn't sing louder. He didn't chase. "Maren said. Don't chase it. It's one word. It's got two ends." And he sent the fire up the other way, from the top, from the shoulder, down, a second thread, so that the grey running up met the fire coming down; and the word was caught between two ends of the same flame, the way Maren had caught it on Lissa, and had nowhere to run.

It went up as smoke. All of it.

And under it, all the way from Ned's palm to his elbow, a long thin raw red line, round and round his arm like the stripe on a stick of rock, bleeding.

Red.

"*Can I*," said Rachael. She didn't wait for him to answer. She couldn't; he was screaming into Maud's apron. She put both her hands on his arm. And didn't push.

She listened.

It was all trying. The whole length of it. Every hair of that raw red line was a body trying to close, the most ordinary thing in the world, and it had been trying for eight weeks under her lid with nothing to work on but cold. She put her warmth beside it and waited, one breath, two, the way Xola had stood knee-deep in a lake, till she felt it, all along his arm: what it was already doing.

She helped it do it.

It answered. The whole arm. Like a dog's tail on a floor, like a door opening, like Ned's mother's face when Rachael looked up, which she shouldn't have, because you don't look up at the mother; and she was cold, she was so cold, colder than she'd been since the Narrows, cold to the shoulders and the teeth and the back of her skull, and she didn't stop. She mended a ten-year-old's arm from the palm to the elbow, every hair of it, and it answered every hair of the way.

She took her hands off.

His arm lay on the bench. Pink. Thin. A long faint spiral of new skin round it from the palm to the elbow, like a scar from a rope. No grey.

Ned had stopped screaming. He was looking at it.

"It's my arm," he said.

"Yes," said Rachael. Her teeth were going. She couldn't stop them. "It always was."

***

Gabe gave her his coat.

He didn't ask. He didn't put it round her; she'd have hated that, and he knew. He just took it off and held it out, from the chair in the corner, at arm's length, the way he held things out to her, so she could take it or not. She took it. It was enormous and warm and smelled of the forge and of him, and she sat on the end of Ned's bench wrapped in it with her knees up and her teeth going, and didn't say anything for a long time.

Sabine looked at her from the door.

"Well," said Sabine. "That's one."

"Twenty-three," said Rachael. "Today. Counting the dock ones." Her teeth clicked. "That's the first one I closed in. That's the first one I had to *open*."

"How many others have you closed in?"

Rachael thought about it.

"Ned," she said. "Just Ned. In the spring. Before Maud said." She looked at the silver needle, still in her hand. "I never did it again."

"Then that's one," said Sabine.

Deston was looking at her. He'd been looking at her the whole time; he did that now, since the night of the post, the night all thirty-four threads had leaned on his. She knew what he was seeing. He'd told her once, the first year, that her threads were the brightest in Kingsferry, gold ones to all of them and to Maud and Tamsin and dozens of thin new ones out of the door to everybody she'd ever mended. She wondered what they looked like now. Twenty-three more of them, today.

"What," she said.

"There's a new one," said Deston. "To Ned." He was quiet. "And one to the needle."

"The needle doesn't have a thread. It's a needle."

"It does now," said Deston. "Somebody made it carefully for somebody. And somebody else gave it on. And you just used it." He looked at the case on her knee. "It's very old. It goes back. Past Maud." He tipped his head, the way he did when he was following one. "A long way."

Rachael looked at the black horn case in her lap.

Then she put the silver needle back in its place on the red felt, at the end, the smallest, and shut the case with its little dry click, and held it.

***

Calvin took her down to the north quay at dusk.

Not alone; never alone. Calvin and Dennis, and Gabe a pace behind, and Sabine somewhere she couldn't see, which was where Sabine was most of the time now. She'd asked. She'd been thinking about it all week, through the dock cases, and she'd been thinking about it all through Ned, and she wanted to hear it. Not from Calvin. From the woman.

Yeva was on the bench at the end of the north quay, where she'd come most evenings since the spring; since the morning she'd walked down to it for the first time in forty years with a little knotted net for Gabe to take up the river. Mending a net. She didn't look up when they came along the quay. Then she did, and looked at Rachael, and put the net down in her lap.

"The mender," said Yeva.

"Yes," said Rachael.

"I heard," said Yeva. "About the rope-walk boy. The whole quay heard. His mother came down crying at five and told the fish market." She looked at Rachael's hands, in Gabe's coat sleeves, which came down past her fingers. "You're cold."

"It's the price."

"Aye." Yeva looked at her a long time with her black net-mender's eyes. "You want the rest of it."

"There's a rest of it?"

"There's always a rest of it," said Yeva. "Girls never sing the rest. They sing the first bit and stop when the one in the middle sits down, because that's the fun part, everybody screaming." She picked the net up again. Her fingers went on with it without her looking, the way they had for forty years. "There's a second time round. For when the cup's sat. Nobody sings it but old women."

She sang it.

Not loud. In the cracked flat voice of an old woman singing to a net, on a quay, at dusk, with the river going past grey and gold. The first verse first, the one Calvin had brought up the stairs at three in the morning in the spring, the one Rachael had read in the channel so many times she could see the words in the dark.

*Empty hands from the cold side*

*came to the priests and stayed a night,*

*taught them how to pour.*

*Pour it out and pour it in,*

*what you were won't be again.*

*Who's the cup? Who's the cup?*

*Mender, mender, sit you up.*

*No one mends in all the black;*

*fetch a mender, bring her back.*

And then, without stopping, the same tune, the second time round:

*Round the ring the stranger ran,*

*wrote it with his empty hand;*

*hold it, hold it, all the night,*

*pour the dark and lose the light.*

*If the mender's in the ring*

*she can close up anything.*

*Who's the cup? Who's the cup?*

*Stranger, stranger, eat it up.*

The net stopped in her hands.

Nobody said anything. Calvin's wind had gone completely still round them, the way it went when he forgot about it. Dennis was looking at Yeva with *True* in his eyes, and Rachael knew what he was hearing, because his face didn't change at all. Nothing. No crack. Not a lie in it anywhere.

"*If the mender's in the ring*," said Rachael.

"Aye," said Yeva. "That's the bit that's a joke. For the girls. If the one in the middle's the mender, the game's spoiled. Because she can close it up." She looked at the net. "So you never put the mender in the middle. You make her sit at the edge. That's the rule. Every girl in the border villages knows it. Mender sits out."

"And the stranger," said Calvin. Very quietly. "*Eat it up*."

Yeva didn't answer for a while.

"My grandmother said that was the end," she said. "When the ring's round, and the dark's poured, and the light's lost. Whatever's in the cup by morning goes back to him that taught them." She shrugged, a small old shrug, and went on with the net. "It's a skipping rhyme. Girls sing it in the dust. You don't think about what it means; you think about not being the one in the middle when it stops." She looked up at Rachael. "I understood it once. Ten years ago, on this quay, listening to soldiers off the river wall talk about a healer on a hill. And I put it away. Then that boy with the wind came and sat on my quay at three in the morning, and I can't put it away again."

Rachael stood on the north quay in Gabe's coat with her hands lost in the sleeves.

*If the mender's in the ring, she can close up anything.*

"So that's why," she said. "That's why they want a healer. On the rise. And now." She heard her own voice, very flat, very exact, the voice she used for the bones of the hand. "Not because she holds it well. Because she's the one who could close it. You put the mender in the middle and fill her first, and then there's nobody left who can."

Nobody said anything.

Behind her, Gabe moved. Not much. A half step. So that he was between her and the end of the quay, where it went round toward the customs house and the steps.

Maud was still up at midnight. Of course she was. Maud didn't sleep. She sat at the table at the end of the long ward by the window with a lamp turned low and the dose book open in front of her, writing tomorrow's doses in her cramped old hand, and every so often she looked up and out of the window, down the hill, over the roofs of Tanner's Row, to the waterfront. To the customs house. To the steps, which you could see from here, from this window, if you knew where to look: a pale wedge of stone at the bottom of the hill, and on it, very small, a big dark shape with its arms folded.

Rachael came and sat on the end bench. Ned was asleep on it, on his back, with his new arm on the blanket and his mother asleep in the chair. She sat at his feet.

"You should be in bed," said Maud, without looking round.

"So should you."

"I'm seventy-one. I'll sleep when I'm dead." Maud wrote a dose. "You did well today, girl."

Rachael didn't say anything. Maud had never once said that to her. In nearly four years. *Hold it. Hard*. *Again*. *Worse*. *That'll do*. Never *well*.

"Sister Merewen," said Rachael. "Your teacher."

"What about her?"

"Who gave it to her? The case."

Maud stopped writing. She looked at the window for a long time.

"Her teacher," she said. "And hers before. I don't know how far. Merewen didn't. She said it was older than the ward, and the ward's older than the customs house." She turned her pen in her fingers. "She said there was a mender once who had it who'd been across the red. In a war, a long time ago. Who'd gone over into the border villages because there were no menders in the black and somebody had to." She was quiet. "That's just a story. Merewen liked stories."

Rachael looked at the case in her lap.

*No one mends in all the black*.

"Deston says it's got a thread," she said. "That goes back a long way."

"Deston says a great many things," said Maud. "Most of them true, more's the pity." She went back to her doses. "It's yours. It was always going to be yours. I gave it you so you'd come back, and you did, and now it's just yours." She wrote a line. "When I'm gone you'll have this ward. Don't argue. Tamsin's a good lad and he'll be a good healer and he'll never once in his life open a thing on purpose. You will. You did today." She didn't look round. "That's what the ward needs. Somebody with both hands."

"You're not going anywhere," said Rachael.

"No," said Maud. "Not tonight." She looked out of the window, down the hill, at the big dark shape on the steps. "Go to bed, girl. Your smith's on the steps and your wind boy's on the stairs and that Sabine's somewhere I can't see, which I don't like, and Dennis is in the street pretending to be a lamp post. You're the best-guarded woman in Kingsferry." She wrote a dose. "Go and sleep. I'll watch the window."

Rachael went.

She stopped at the door. She looked back down the long ward: eleven benches and the sleeping, and the lamp low at the end, and the old woman at the table by the window with her stick against the chair and her pen moving, and the window black behind her with the waterfront in it, and the steps.

"Maud."

"What."

"Thank you," said Rachael. "For holding."

Maud's pen stopped.

She didn't look round. She sat very still at the table with her back to the ward, and Rachael saw her shoulders, once, the way she'd seen them two springs ago at the corner of the customs house, when she'd given her the case and turned and gone without waiting to see the barge go.

"Go to *bed*," said Maud.

Rachael went to bed.

Down on the steps, Gabe stood with his arms folded and his back to the infirmary, and watched the dark, and there was nothing in it at all.
