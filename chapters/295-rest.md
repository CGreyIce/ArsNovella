Newish had never once in her life stopped humming, and then Hako walked into a hammer and she did.

She didn't notice at the time. Nobody notices when they stop doing a thing they've done since they could breathe. She was in the tunnel mouth with Winter and Ina and Chris, humming under the noise of the whole Lists the way she always hummed, low, to the beat of the place: the stamp of twenty thousand feet for Garran Stoke, *one, two*, and under it the beats of everybody she loved, Chris's quick even one and Winter's slow cold one and Ina's under the mask, which was always a little faster with the mask on. And Hako's, out on the sand. Bright and bouncy and a hair too fast, the way it had always been, like somebody running down a hill because it's fun.

And then Hako went *in*, under the hammer, and every beat in the Lists stopped.

Not stopped. She'd thought about it since; she'd thought about very little else. Ten thousand hearts didn't stop. But the *beat* did. The thing they were all doing together, the stamp, the breath, the crowd's one big pulse: it went silent. All at once. For a heartbeat, the whole of the Lists held still in the same place at the same time, waiting to find out.

And she'd stopped with it. Her humming. She'd felt it go out of her like a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

And then the flags went up and the crowd came back and she came back with it, humming, and didn't think about it again until that night, on the roof, when she opened her window to look at nothing in particular and it said ninety-seven.

It had said ninety-six for five months.

***

"Ninety-six," said Chris, at breakfast. He had the book open beside his bowl. "Since the ninth day after the black ice. I wrote it down. You said it at supper and then you said, *don't write that down*, and I wrote that down too."

"You're horrible," said Newish.

"I'm thorough." He turned the page. "Ninety-seven last night. At the bout. You didn't do anything."

"I did nothing," said Newish. "I stood in a tunnel. I didn't even slow anything. I was going to slow the hammer, actually, I was going to slow it right down so it hit him like a pillow, and then he went in and I forgot." She stopped. "I forgot to do anything. I just stood there."

Chris wrote something.

"Don't write that," said Newish.

"You stopped humming," said Chris, not looking up. "I noticed. You hum all the time. You hum when you're eating. You hum in your sleep; Winter says so. You stopped when he went in and you didn't start again for nine heartbeats. I counted." He put the pencil down. "That's the longest I've ever heard you not make a noise."

Newish opened her mouth to say something rude, and found that she didn't have anything, and shut it.

***

She went up on the Broken Lance's roof that afternoon, alone, and sat with her back against the chimney, and tried to think about it.

She'd heard beats all her life. Here, anyway; she'd heard them since the first day in the hall, when thirty-four people she loved had got up off a stone floor around her and every one of them had a rhythm she could have hummed. That was the Tempo, before it was a skill. Before she'd slowed a falling cup in Valcrest the summer before last and the window had said *Spark*. She heard what things were doing, in time. A heart. A horse at a trot. Chris's pencil. The river. And she could lean on it. Push a beat a little faster, so Chris's sword came round a hair sooner; pull one a little slower, so a man coming at Winter in a crowd took a breath too long to get there. That was the whole of Tempo. Faster, slower. She'd got very good at it.

Ninety-six for five months. She'd got very good at it, and it hadn't moved.

She thought about the Lists going silent.

She'd heard a beat go silent before. Once. On the road into Valcrest, two summers ago, in the crowd at the north gate, with a man at the back carrying a yoke of buckets. She'd heard him before she'd seen him; she'd heard him in the crowd the way you'd hear one instrument in a whole band that was playing something different. A beat so slow and so low it was barely there. Long gaps between the notes. Rests, mostly. She'd stood in the road with her mouth open listening to the rests, and in them, very faintly, she'd heard him laugh.

*His whole beat was waiting*. She'd thought that, on the Lance's stairs, with her face in his coat. She'd thought it and then she'd forgotten it, because she'd been busy crying.

She sat on the roof and hummed. She couldn't help it. A tune she didn't know the name of. And she made herself stop, in the middle of a bar, on purpose, the way you'd stop at a rest in a piece of music.

It was horrible.

She hadn't known it would be horrible. She sat there in the silence she'd made, with the gulls going over and the river below and the city doing its noises, and it was like standing on the edge of something. Like the bit after somebody's said a thing and you're waiting to see if they'll laugh. Like the bit after a message is sent and before it's read.

She started humming again before she could stop herself.

***

She knew why. She'd always known. She just hadn't put it in words, because she didn't like the words.

*Love you guys. Except Newish*.

*Rude*.

That had been the last thing. The last thing he ever wrote, in the chat; and the last thing she'd ever written back to him. One word. And then the silence after it, which she'd thought was just him going to bed, and which had gone on, and gone on, and turned out to be the rest of everything.

She'd filled every silence since. She knew that about herself. She talked. She bantered. She said the rude thing before anybody else could say the sad thing. She hummed. In Valcrest she'd made the crowd that called her friends demons laugh at itself until it chanted their names. She was good at it. It was most of what she was.

And a rest was a silence on purpose. In the middle of the music. Where you stopped, and waited, and trusted the music would come back.

She didn't trust it. That was all. She'd stopped once, after *rude*, and it hadn't.

***

She tried it anyway. She was stubborn; Ysolde said she was the most stubborn person in Valcrest, which in a city of arena champions was saying something.

She tried it in the yard behind the Lists, with Chris and Hako sparring, which they did every afternoon now that Hako's back had stopped ringing when you touched it. She stood at the fence and listened to them. Chris's beat, quick and even, *one-two-one-two*, a man counting. Hako's, bright and bouncy, slower than it used to be; it had slowed since the bout, she'd noticed, the way a man's walk slows when he's stopped being in a hurry to be somewhere else.

She thought: *a rest*. And she leaned on both of them, the way she leaned with Tempo. Not faster. Not slower. *Stop*. Right in the middle of an exchange. A rest, for both of them, the way you'd put a rest in a bar.

They stopped.

Not together. That was the first thing. Chris stopped on his beat and Hako stopped on his, and they weren't the same beat, and Chris stopped with his sword halfway round and Hako stopped with his spear halfway out, and both of them overbalanced in completely different directions, and Chris sat down on the sand and Hako fell over the fence.

And Newish's nose bled. All down her chin, hot, like a tap.

"Ow," said Hako, from the other side of the fence. "Was that you?"

"Yes," said Newish, through her sleeve. "Sorry."

"What was it?"

"I don't know," said Newish. "A rest. I tried to make you rest."

Chris was sitting on the sand looking at his sword as if it had said something to him. "That wasn't a rest," he said. "That was a stop. You stopped us. One each. Separately." He picked up the sword. "A rest isn't a stop. A rest is in the music. Everybody plays it together. That's what makes it a rest and not just somebody falling over."

"When did you learn music?"

"I didn't," said Chris. "I logged it. Lucan's band at the Lance, every night for three years. Four hundred and twelve rests." He stood up. "They never come in at different times. If one of them comes in early it's not a rest any more. It's a mistake."

Newish took her sleeve away from her nose and looked at the blood on it.

"You can't rest alone," she said. Slowly.

"No," said Chris. "I don't think you can."

***

Ninety-seven, that night. Not ninety-eight. She hadn't expected ninety-eight. You didn't get a point for falling over.

But she lay on her pallet in the long room of the Broken Lance with Winter asleep on one side of her and Ina breathing under the mask on the other, which he slept in, sometimes, when the city had been loud, and listened to them. And for the first time in her life she listened to the gaps.

Winter's beat was slow. She'd always known that. But she'd always listened to the notes, the slow cold heart, *one*, *one*, *one*. Tonight she listened to between. And between, there was nothing. And the nothing was full. It was Winter, asleep, safe, in a room with people she'd decided to trust. You could hear it, if you listened. The rest wasn't empty. It was the part where she wasn't afraid.

Ina's was faster. A gentleman's heart, eloquent, a little nervous, the way he'd been nervous every day here of what people would see when they looked at his face. And between the notes, a rest. Short. Very short. Like somebody who'd only just learned to stop, and wasn't sure yet that he was allowed.

She lay there and listened to the rests of the people she loved until she fell asleep. She didn't hum.

***

The hail came on the fifth day of the spring week.

It came in off the sea in the middle of the afternoon, out of a clear sky, the way spring storms did in Valcrest; Lucan said so, afterwards, weeping, *I should have known, I've seen it a dozen times, I should have known*. There'd been sun on the sand at noon. There'd been gulls. And then the gulls went, all at once, inland, and the light went green, and there was a sound out over the harbour like a cart full of gravel being emptied very slowly on to a roof, coming closer.

The Lists was full. It was the fifth day; the spear and sword finals were done and it was the trios, which the river quarter loved best, and they'd come in their thousands to the south stand. The steep one. The one with the rime ridge along the top all winter, and the paper lanterns, and the coopers' guild down at the bottom on the river side with their families, and the stair.

The south stand had one stair down to the river tunnel. One. Lucan had been saying for fifteen years it needed two. It was steep and stone and narrow, a stair for a crowd going down it slowly at the end of a day, talking, in no hurry. Not for one going down it all at once.

The hail hit the south stand like a thrown door.

Newish was on the stair. She'd been halfway down it, on her way back from the top with a jug of something for Ina, who was fighting in the trios and would want it after; she was on the eighth step, the one old Merrit had gone down on in the black ice with Hako underneath him. And the hail came in over the rim of the stand, stones of it the size of a thumb, a roar, a white wall, hitting the stone and the people and the paper lanterns all at once, the lanterns going to shreds, and everybody on the south stand did the same thing at the same time.

They ran for the stair.

***

She heard it before she saw it.

That was what Tempo was. She heard everything in time. And she heard the south stand go from a crowd to a stampede in the time it takes to draw a breath: a thousand beats, two thousand, all the hearts and feet of the river quarter, all at once going *fast*. Not together. That was the thing. A crowd sitting still has a beat, a big slow one, everybody breathing. A crowd running has none. Every heart its own, panicking, every pair of feet its own, and all of them going the same way, down, into a stair one person wide.

She saw it then. The top of the stair, filling. A man with a child on his shoulders. An old woman. Two boys. Coming down on top of each other, the hail behind them, the stair under them, the wall of people behind pushing, pushing, not because anybody meant to but because there was hail and the stair was the way out and everybody's body had decided at once.

Somebody fell. At the turn, ten steps above her. A woman. And the people behind her didn't stop, because they couldn't, because the people behind *them* didn't know she'd fallen; and they went over her, and somebody else fell, and the stair was a heap.

Newish did the only thing she knew how to do.

She slowed them.

She leaned on the whole stair with everything she had. *Slow*. The way she'd slowed the falling cup, the way she'd slowed the ram at the Gate in the Lists so it hit like a cushion. Every beat on the stair, every heart and every foot, *slower*.

It was the worst thing she could have done.

She knew it the moment she did it. They slowed. All of them, the whole of the stair, the way a river slows when it's dammed. But the people at the top didn't slow, because she couldn't reach them, because there were too many and they were too far; and the people on the stair slowed and the people behind didn't, and they *piled*. On to the slow ones. On to the heap at the turn. She heard a sound she'd hear for the rest of her life, a deep wet crunch of bodies against bodies against stone, and somebody screaming, high, a child, and under it, very fast, a heart she could hear going wrong.

She let go.

They came on again, all at once, faster for having been held.

She stood on the eighth step with the jug smashed at her feet and the hail hitting her and the whole south stand coming down the stair at her, and her nose bleeding again, and every beat she could hear going faster and faster and none of them together, and she thought, very clearly, the way you think things at the very end of something: *I can't stop them*.

And then: *I don't have to stop them*.

She didn't know where it came from. It came from the long room of the Broken Lance, at night, lying between Winter and Ina, listening to the gaps. It came from Chris on the sand with his sword. *A rest isn't a stop. A rest is in the music. Everybody plays it together.* It came from the Lists going silent when Hako walked into the hammer. Ten thousand people holding still in the same place at the same time.

Not because she'd made them. Because they'd all been waiting for the same thing.

It came from the north gate, two summers ago, and a man at the back of the crowd with a yoke of buckets, whose beat was so slow and so low it was barely there, mostly rests; and in the rests, very faintly, a laugh.

His whole beat was waiting. And it had never once been *empty*.

She stopped trying to do anything to them.

She stood on the eighth step in the hail and listened. Not to their beats. Under them. To the place where a beat would be, if there was one. The thing a crowd has when it's a crowd and not two thousand people who happen to be in the same place: the shared breath, the big slow pulse, the one the whole Lists had found together for a heartbeat when Hako went in. It wasn't there. They'd lost it. They'd each gone off on their own, every heart its own, in a panic, and nobody had a beat to come back to.

So she gave them one.

Not faster. Not slower. She didn't lean on anybody. She hummed.

Out loud, on the stair, in the hail, at the top of her voice, which wasn't very loud. One note. And then she stopped. On purpose. In the middle. A rest.

And she held it.

It was the hardest thing she'd ever done. Harder than the stair, harder than the hail. The silence after the note went on and on and she stood in it with her heart going like a bird's and everything in her screaming *fill it, fill it, say something, say the rude thing, hum, make a noise, don't let it go quiet, it doesn't come back, it never comes back*—

And she didn't fill it.

She trusted it.

She let the rest be a rest. Not empty. Not the end. The bit in the music where everybody stops, together, and waits, because they all know the next note's coming. She stood in it and *knew* the next note was coming. The way he'd known, at the back of the crowd at the north gate, with the buckets. The way you know a friend will laugh at a joke before they do.

And Tempo went out of her into it.

Not into them. Into the rest. Into the silence on the stair, the gap, the place between one beat and the next. She put the whole of her Tempo into a silence, and made it big enough for two thousand people to stand in.

The south stand stopped.

***

Hako told her afterwards what it looked like. She couldn't see it; she was in it.

Everybody on the stair stopped at once. All of them. Not slowed. Not frozen. *Stopped*, in the same breath, the way a band stops on a rest, the whole of the south stand from the rim to the river tunnel. A man with a child on his shoulders, mid-step, with his foot in the air, putting it down, gently, and standing. An old woman with her hand on the wall. Two boys. The people at the top, out of her reach, in the hail, who she couldn't have touched with Tempo if she'd tried: they stopped too. Because everybody in front of them had stopped, all together, and there's nothing in the world that makes a running crowd stop like a crowd in front of it standing still.

Nobody spoke. The hail roared on the stone. And for one long beat, the longest beat Hako said he'd ever lived through, two thousand people stood on a stair in a hailstorm and *waited*. Together. As if they'd all agreed.

He said their faces were the strangest thing. Not afraid. Not any more. *Listening*. All of them, at once, the way you'd listen in a church for the next note of a hymn.

And in the rest, Hako came up the stair.

He'd been at the bottom, by the tunnel, with Ysolde. He came up the stair through the stopped crowd the way you'd walk through a field of standing corn, sideways, fast, to the turn, to the heap, and started pulling people off it. Ysolde behind him. Chris behind her. A woman. A man. The two boys. And at the bottom of it, flat on the stone, a child of about six with her arm bent the wrong way and her face blue, not breathing, with the heart Newish had heard going wrong.

Ysolde picked her up. Turned her over her arm. Hit her between the shoulders, once, hard.

The child breathed.

***

Newish heard it. In the rest. One small heart, starting again, *thud*, *thud*, quick and frightened and alive, in the middle of the silence she was holding.

And she let the next note come.

She didn't push it. She didn't make it. She just stopped holding the rest, and the beat came back, the way a beat comes back after a rest in a piece of music, on the right count, all together. The south stand breathed in. Two thousand people. And then they went down the stair.

Slowly. Together. Not a stampede. A crowd. One step, and the next, and the next, at the pace of the slowest of them, in the hail, with their hands on each other's shoulders and their heads down, the way a crowd goes home at the end of a long day. And the heap at the turn was gone, and Hako and Ysolde and Chris were on the landing beside it passing people down, and nobody fell.

Newish stood on the eighth step and hummed. Low. Under her breath. A slow beat with long gaps in it, for them to walk to. She didn't know if they could hear it. She thought maybe they didn't need to.

It took the whole south stand a quarter of an hour to go down that stair. The hail stopped halfway through. The sun came out. The gulls came back.

When the last of them had gone down past her, an old man with a stick who touched his cap to her as he went, she sat down on the eighth step, in the wet, in the ice, with the bits of paper lantern stuck to the stone round her like petals, and couldn't hear anything at all.

***

It lasted an hour.

Not deaf. She could hear Winter, who came up the stair and sat down beside her without saying anything and put her hand flat on Newish's back. She could hear the gulls. But she couldn't hear *beats*. For the first time since the hall, she couldn't hear anybody's rhythm, not Winter's slow cold one under her hand, not her own. Just the world, plain, the way other people heard it.

It was the quietest she'd ever been.

She sat in it. She didn't fill it. She'd find out later from Chris that she didn't make a single sound for fifty-one minutes, which was, he said, carefully, writing it down, a record by fifty minutes and forty-two seconds.

At the end of the hour the beats came back. All at once, like somebody opening a door on a room where a band was playing. Winter's first. Slow. And between the notes, the rest. Full.

Newish opened her window.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Rest," said Winter, beside her. She couldn't see it. She could see Newish's face.

"Rest," said Newish. Her voice sounded strange to her, as if she hadn't used it in a year. "It's a rest. Not a stop. Everybody stops together and waits for the next note." She looked down the empty stair at the river tunnel, where the last of the crowd was going out into the sun. "And you have to believe it's coming. That's the whole thing. You have to stand in the silence and believe the next note's coming. Or it's not a rest. It's just quiet."

Winter didn't say anything.

"I've never believed it," said Newish. "Not since—" She stopped. "Not for a long time."

"And today?"

Newish thought about it.

"Today there were two thousand people on a stair," she said, "and I didn't have time not to."

Winter's hand stayed flat on her back.

She looked at it after she'd written it.

She'd said it. *Rude*. In the channel, to Winter, the way she'd said it a thousand times before, to everybody, about everything. She hadn't thought. It had just come out, the way it always did.

And somewhere a long way north, at a lake, a man who read every line of the channel and never wrote in it would be reading it.

She'd told him, on the stairs of the Broken Lance with her face in his coat, two summers ago. *You can't say rude twice. That's the rule. The second time it's just rude*. She'd used hers. She'd said she'd never say it to him again as long as she lived.

She hadn't. It was to Winter. It didn't count.

She sat on the eighth step in the sun and read it again, and found she was smiling, and didn't know why; and then she did.

After *rude*, last time, there'd been a silence that turned out to be the rest of everything.

This time there was a rest.
