*Roads*, Higu wrote, at the top of a clean page, at the end of the long table in the Broken Lance, on the morning after the closing.

She underlined it once. She didn't underline things twice. Twice was for Chris.

Last time it had been Drew. Last summer, at this table, on the back of a bracket, with everybody leaning over his shoulder telling him he'd drawn the river wrong. Drew drew things. Higu wrote them down. It was a different job. A drawing was what you thought was going to happen. A list was what people had told you, in their own words, with the time beside it, so that afterwards you could look back and see who'd said what and whether it had come true.

Drew had come down the stairs at the first bell and seen her with the book open and sat down across from her and said, "You do it this year."

"Why?"

"Because yours are always right," said Drew, "and mine are always nearly." He'd poured himself tea. "And because it's west for us again, at noon, twenty days to Thornwall, and I'd like somebody to write that down who isn't me, so I believe it."

She'd written it down. *West, noon. Drew, Bee, Kei. Thornwall. Twenty days*. And under it, because he'd said it: *(So I believe it.)*

After that they came to her one at a time, all morning, the way people came to Maren with a list; and sat down across the table, and told her where they were going, and watched her write it, and went away looking easier. She hadn't asked anybody to. She'd just sat at the end of the table with the book open, and they'd come.

She didn't mind. She liked it. She liked the sound a thing made when it went from being something somebody meant into something somebody had said, at a time, in front of a witness. It was a very small sound. Most people couldn't hear it.

Group 3 came first. All of them at once, the way they did everything, with Salty in front and Wendy holding his sleeve.

"East," said Salty. "First light tomorrow. Back to the fort." He looked at the book upside down. "Write *the fort*. Not Cinderhold. Everybody says Cinderhold. We say the fort."

*East, first light, day 432*, Higu wrote. *Group 3. The fort*.

"To wait at the grave," said Yan.

He said it quietly, from the back. He was standing with his arms folded and Suzu loose round his neck, and his eyes doing the thing they did now, since the winter, the long slow look that wasn't at your face but at something round it; and he looked tired. He always looked a little tired now. Higu had a page about it. She hadn't shown it to anybody.

*To wait at the grave*, she wrote.

"Two months," said Wendy. "Near enough. We'll have the knoll ready. And the green." She looked at Salty. "And somebody's going to fix the east gate hinge before the garrison sees it again, because somebody leaned on it."

"It was already loose," said Salty.

"It was loose. It wasn't *off*."

Maigo said nothing. He'd said nothing all week, Higu had noted; it was the second long silence of his she'd written down, and she'd started counting the days of it the way he counted things. He stood beside Yan with one hand on the table, flat, the way he'd put it on the ground at the fort, as if feeling for a root under the wood.

Niko was at the end. Not saying anything either. He had a cut over one eye from the final, from Gabe's wall, that Rachael had looked at and said *leave it*, and he was grinning.

"Write *again*," said Niko.

Higu looked up at him.

"For the grave," said Niko. "When he gets there. Write it down now, so it's true. *Again*."

She wrote it. *Niko: again*. She didn't know what it meant. She'd find out. She always did, eventually, if she wrote it down at the time.

Group 2 came at the third bell, without Hana, who was upstairs packing seven cords and a tray of beads.

"North-west," said Jared. "Second hour. Solmere. Home to the reading."

*North-west, second hour. Group 2. Solmere. The reading*.

That was what they called it now. Not the archive, not the leaves. *The reading*. Oswin's long table under the high windows, and the thirty-nine leaves in their tissue, and Edwy checking every line twice, and an old man sitting at the end of the table every morning holding a pink bead on a thread. Higu had never seen it. She had four pages on it anyway, from the channel, from Hana, who wrote the way Hana talked, all at once and kindly and with every name in.

"And the circle," said Milli. "Oswin wants to lay something on it. He said tell Higu."

"Tell Higu what?"

"That he wants to lay something on it," said Milli. "He didn't say what. He said Higu would know."

Higu put her pencil down.

She did know.

Hana came down at noon with her cords on her wrist and her tail going, and sat down across the table, and didn't say where she was going, because Higu already had it.

"He wants the frames," said Hana.

"I know."

"Not Thea's. Thea's are on your wall at home, in chalk, and Thea can't send chalk." Hana turned a bead on her wrist. "He's got the circle. The whole of it. Ten feet across, on the floor of the long room, his copy, every ring. He's been crawling round it on his knees all spring. He says there's a place on the outside ring where the lines stop." She looked up. "He wants to lay the frames against it. Where the ticks are. To see."

"He'll want them exact."

"He said you'd say that."

Higu turned back in her book. Past *Roads*. Past the moths for the week, and the moon for the week, and the page with *Alea is his. He has a window* on it, that she'd never torn out. To the copy.

Fifty-six frames. She'd made it on the stair at home, in the spring, the day Thea chalked them on the wall: every frame, every line, every tick, in pencil, the size they were, with the number of each frame in the corner; and then she'd checked it against Thea's hand on the wall, twice, once each, the way Gigichi had told them to. *Count what's there. Not what it means*. Fifty-six frames. One to thirty-seven with ticks across the little arc on the right of the ring, small, like the marks a builder makes on a beam, as many ticks as there were lines going out. Thirty-eight to fifty-six with the arc left blank. Fifty-five with two lines. Fifty-six with thirty-five.

*That's us*, Thea had said.

Higu had a second copy. She'd made it over the last four nights, on the roof, by the light of a moth, after Chris went down. She hadn't told anybody. She'd known since Milli said *Oswin*, and before, really; since Thea wrote *fourteen rings* in the channel in the spring. You didn't need to be told a thing if you'd written down everything that led up to it.

She took the second copy out from the back of the book, where she'd folded it. Twelve sheets, tied with a lavender ribbon off her own sleeve.

Hana looked at it.

"You already made it," said Hana.

"Over four nights."

"You didn't *say*."

"Nobody asked," said Higu. "And it wasn't finished." She held it out. "Tell him not to guess. Tell him to put the frames down where they go and count what's there. Thea's rule. Gigichi's, really."

Hana took the sheets, carefully, in both hands, the way she'd take a bird.

"I'll put it on the cord," she said. "Every night. Whatever he finds. To Maren's, and Thea'll have it." She was quiet. "Higu. If it's what everyone thinks it is."

"Nobody knows what it is."

"If it's people," said Hana. "Going home."

Higu didn't answer. She picked up her pencil and wrote, under *North-west, second hour*: *Hana. The copy. 12 sheets*. And the time.

That was all she could do with it. Write down that it had gone, and when, and who had it. The rest wasn't a list. The rest was a guess, and she didn't guess. She left that to Thea, who could read the thing, and couldn't read it yet, and was going to.

In the afternoon it was the north.

That was the long one. It had been the long one last time too: three groups and their guides and their carts, all going out the same gate at the third hour and up the same road to the river, and splitting there, three ways, like a hand opening.

Gabe came and sat down and filled the whole of the other side of the table.

"Kingsferry," said Gabe. "With Rachael and Dennis and Cal. Then up the river." He put his hands on the table. They were black again; he'd been at a forge in the city all morning, a borrowed one, for no reason he'd explain. "Write: *Gabe. Then the lake. With hammer*."

"Which hammer?"

"Mine," said Gabe. "The big one. From Harl's." He went red, which on Gabe was a whole weather. "He's doing twelve dinners on the forge on the shingle. You need a hammer for that. You need *somebody* with a hammer."

"For dinners."

"For the *fire*," said Gabe. "It's a forge. You've got to keep a forge right or it eats the coal." He looked at her book. "Write it down. Then it's true and he can't tell me not to come."

*North, third hour*, Higu wrote. *Group 4 to Kingsferry. Then Gabe up the river to the lake. With hammer. (So he can't be told not to)*.

Deston came after him, smiling at nothing. "Same," said Deston. "Kingsferry, then up. I want to hear the lake." He looked at the book. "Put me after Gabe. I'll be after Gabe anyway. I'm always after Gabe."

Then Xav, who didn't sit. He stood beside the table with his chin on his fist and looked at the book for a long time.

"Duskhollow," said Xav. "Group 6. Then up." A pause. "All five of us. Malcolm wants to see the jetty. RX wants to beat him at cards. John wants to film the hens." He thought. "Azac wants to come. He didn't say why. Write that he didn't say why."

*Group 6 to Duskhollow. Then all five up to the lake. (Azac didn't say why.)*

"And me," said Petra.

She'd come up behind Xav without anybody hearing, the way she did, with the driftwood stick under her arm. She didn't sit either.

"I'm going east," said Petra. "From the lake. With him. When he goes." She looked at Higu's pencil. "Write it twice."

"I only write things once."

"Then write it once, very hard."

Higu wrote it. *Petra: east, from the lake, with him*. She went over it once more, very lightly, so that nobody but her would see that she had; and Petra, who saw everything, saw, and nodded, and went away.

Gigichi found her at supper, still at the end of the table, with the book shut.

"You've been sitting there since the first bell," said Gigichi.

"People kept coming."

"You *let* them keep coming." Gigichi sat down, not across the table but on the bench beside her, with her silver hair coming out of its pins and a forget-me-not hanging off the end of one of them by its stalk. She looked tired too. The whole Lance looked tired. Nine days of the Lists and a closing and forty thousand paper rods; Higu had a page on how tired everybody was, with a column for each. "You're like a post office."

"A post office is a good thing."

"A post office doesn't eat," said Gigichi, and put a bowl in front of her.

Higu ate. Gigichi watched her do it, with her chin on her hand, the way she watched Thea eat on bad nights, and Xola, and PenguChi when PenguChi forgot; she watched all of them eat, and never said she was doing it, and if you said it she'd say she was just sitting there. Higu had a page on that too. She'd never shown it to Gigichi. She thought Gigichi would tear it out.

"What's left?" said Gigichi. "On your list."

Higu thought.

"The moon," she said.

***

She'd kept the moon since the first spring.

Every night. A line in the book: the date in their days and the date in the kingdom's, the phase, the time it rose, the time it set, where it stood at midnight over whatever roof she was under. Some nights she couldn't see it, and then she worked it out; she'd learned how in the first winter from an almanac Maren had borrowed off the miller and read out to her, a line at a time, because she couldn't read the hand it was written in. After a year she hadn't needed the almanac. After two she could tell you the moon for any night you liked, backward or forward, as near as an hour, without looking up.

Her magic liked it. That was the first reason. Moths came easier on a waxing moon and harder on a dark one, and everything she did was a little brighter when it was full and a little thinner when it wasn't, and she'd wanted to know why. It turned out there wasn't a why. It just did. So she kept the moon the way you'd keep a tide table if you lived by the sea.

That was the first reason. It wasn't the real one any more.

The real one was that it was the same moon.

She'd worked that out in the first winter too, on a night when Group 3 had written from the fort that they couldn't sleep and Group 5 had written from Thornwall that it was snowing and Hana had written from Solmere that the archive was cold, and she'd looked up from the roof of the militia hall and thought: *they can all see that*. Not the snow. The moon. Thirty-five people on seven roofs, a long way apart, and one moon over all of them. She'd put it in the channel that night. *Moon tonight: three days past full. Rises a little after dusk. It's the same one everywhere*. And six people had written back *thanks*.

She'd done it every night since. Nobody asked. Nobody had to.

And there was one night left that she hadn't done yet, that mattered more than the others.

The tenth.

***

She went up on the roof.

The Lance's roof was flat at the back, over the kitchen, with a low wall round it and a chimney stack that was warm all night because the Lance's cook never let the stove go out. Chris sat up there every night at four in the morning with his notebook. Higu sat up there earlier. They'd never once been there at the same time. Higu thought Chris had probably worked out her hours and was keeping out of them on purpose, and had written it down somewhere, the way she'd written down his.

She sat with her back against the warm chimney and her book on her knees and a pencil and a stub of candle she didn't light.

The moon wasn't up yet. It wouldn't be up till nearly midnight; it was nine days past full, half gone, and rising late. The sky over Valcrest was the dark blue that comes after the last of the light, with the stars coming through it one at a time and the city's lamps below, and the forty lamps along the great wall of the Lists still lit, a row of them, small and gold, across the rooftops.

She turned to a clean page. She wrote at the top: *The tenth*.

Day 493. Near enough. Aldous would know it to the day; Aldous knew it to the hour. She'd ask Yan to ask him. Day 493, at the fort, on the green below the knoll, and on the end of a jetty, and in a long room in Solmere, and on a tower at Thornwall, and on the roof of the Lists' great wall where Chris would be, and wherever else anybody was. One night. Thirty-five people and one more, under one sky.

She worked it.

Full on the hundred and twenty-seventh day, last summer's end: the ninth. She'd drawn that one for Alea two months ahead, on a page torn out of this book, and Alea had carried it against her chest all the way to the grave. Twenty-nine and a half days, near enough, from full to full; this world's moon kept the same time as theirs, which was one of the very few things here that did, and she'd never stopped being grateful for it. Count forward. Twelve fulls and a bit. The bit was what mattered.

She did it twice. Once each way.

*The tenth*, she wrote. *An old moon. Twelve days past full. A thin one, the wrong way round from a new one, like a fingernail paring*. She drew it. *Rises late. About the third hour after midnight, low in the east, over the downs if you're at the fort. Over the far shore if you're on the lake. Before that, nothing*. She drew the sky with nothing in it. *All the first half of the night is dark. Then a little light, from the east, very thin, until dawn*.

She looked at it.

*If you are doing anything on the green that needs light*, she wrote, smaller, under it, *you'll be doing it in the dark*.

She sat back against the chimney.

She'd thought it would be a full one. She didn't know why. She'd had a feeling, the way you have a feeling about a number before you work it out. A big bright full moon over the grave on the tenth, the way there'd been on the ninth: *The night. An hour after sunset. East, over the red*. She'd half wanted it. It would have been a good thing to put in the channel.

It wasn't. It was an old moon, rising late. Almost nothing.

She drew it again, more carefully. The thin bright curve of it, low, the wrong way round. She shaded in the dark round it. She drew the line of the downs under it, the way she remembered them from Group 3's descriptions, and Deston's map, and John's little pictures, which she'd never seen but had read about so often that she could see them. She drew the knoll. The ash on it. She drew it very small.

She found that she could see what she was drawing very clearly.

She didn't think about it. She was drawing. She drew the old moon a third time, in the margin, to check the curve, and shaded the dark, and the dark was very easy to shade, because the page was lit, faintly, all over, with a thin cold silver light that came from somewhere low and to the left and lay along the paper the way moonlight lies along water, the way an old moon's light would lie along the downs in the third hour after midnight, very thin, from the east.

She thought it was the moon.

She looked up, to see where it had risen.

It hadn't.

"Higu," said Gigichi.

She was at the top of the ladder. Higu hadn't heard her come up. She was standing on the top rung with her head and shoulders over the edge of the roof and two cups in her hands, steaming, and she wasn't looking at Higu's face. She was looking at Higu's hand.

"You didn't light the candle," said Gigichi.

"I could see."

"Yes," said Gigichi. "That's my point."

Higu looked down.

Her left hand was on the page, flat, the way she always held a page down when she was drawing. And the light was coming from it. Not from a moth; there was no moth. From her hand. From the whole of it, very faint, the way frost seems to give off light on a dark morning, a thin cold silver that lay out from her palm across the paper and over the edge of the book and onto the roof; and it was the exact light of the thing she'd been drawing. Not a full moon. Not gold. Thin, and cold, and low, and from the left, like the light of an old moon in the third hour after midnight over a long way of dark.

She'd been drawing it for half an hour. By it. Without knowing.

"I saw it from the yard," said Gigichi. She climbed the rest of the way up and came across the roof and put the cups down on the low wall, very carefully, and didn't sit. Higu looked at her hand.

She opened her window.

Below them, on the floor under the roof, a door opened and shut, and someone came out into the yard and stood looking up.

Higu didn't need to look. She knew the step. She'd written it down in the first winter: *he walks like he's already where he's going*. She went to the edge of the roof anyway, with her hand still lit, and looked over.

He was in the yard with the rod on his shoulder. Going out, or coming in; she couldn't tell. He'd stopped by the pump, under the roof, and was looking up at it. At her. At the thin silver light lying out over the edge of the low wall like the light of a moon that wasn't up.

She couldn't see his face. She'd never been able to.

He lifted his hand. Not the rod. The empty one. Up beside his face, open, for as long as it takes to count to three.

Then he went on out of the yard by the gate, toward the blue door, and she sat down again against the chimney with the others and found that she was smiling, which she didn't often do where people could see.

He didn't wake them. He sat down on the low wall at the far end, and opened his window, and wrote.

East at first light: Group 3, with their cart, and Salty walking at the tailboard with nothing in his hands, because the crock wasn't going east. Yan at the tailboard, with Suzu round his neck, looking back. Niko had said *again* to Ars at the gate, and Ars had said *at the grave, Mashi*, and Niko had grinned all the way down the road until the road went over a hill.

North-west at the second hour: Group 2, and two castle guards, and Hana at the back with her cords on her wrist and twelve sheets in a lavender ribbon held against her chest in both hands like a bird.

From the back of the miller's cart, as it went out through the north gate onto the road to the river, with Alea walking beside it with Astra on her back and her ears up, and Gigichi asleep against Higu's shoulder, and Thea sitting at the front beside Maren with her eyes shut, doing the thing she did now, reading something a long way off that nobody else could read.

*North gate, third hour, day 432*, Higu wrote. *Lucan: "Every summer." Ars: kept the key*.

And then, under it, because nobody else would and somebody should:

*Moon tonight: ten days past full. Rises after midnight. It's the same one everywhere*.

She didn't need it any more. She wrote it anyway.
