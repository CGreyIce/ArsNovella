There were a hundred and twelve steps to the top of the north tower, and Hyvred had counted them every morning since the thaw, and every morning it was a hundred and twelve, and every morning she counted anyway.

That was Hild's fault. Hild had said, on the parapet in the spring, with her one eye shut and her back to the drop, *counting tells you how bad it is; it doesn't tell you what to do*. And Hyvred had gone down the stairs that night and thought about it, and come up them the next morning and counted them, all hundred and twelve, out loud, because she'd decided that if counting didn't tell you what to do then at least it told you you'd got there.

She got there. A hundred and twelve. The door at the top stuck, the way it always stuck, and she put her shoulder to it the way she always did, and it opened onto the wind and the roof and the grey before dawn, and the whole valley of the Greyspine lying north under it like a bowl somebody had forgotten to wash.

She pushed her glasses up into her hair. She didn't need them. She hadn't needed them since the floor of the hall in Solmere, two years and a half ago, when she'd woken up and seen a ceiling for the first time in her life, the whole of a ceiling, every corner, all at once, and lain there on her back on the cold stone with her mouth open and cried so hard a guard had come over to see if she was hurt. She kept them anyway. They'd come through with her. They were the only thing in this world that had been on her face in the other one.

Then she looked.

On Earth she'd seen the world through a hole. That was how she'd explained it, on stream, once, with a clip she'd made of what it looked like: a little circle of picture in the middle of black, and everything outside the circle just not there, so that you turned your whole head to look at things other people saw out of the sides of their eyes. Ice had watched it and said, in the chat, *oh it's osu! Flashlight*, and then, about a minute later, when somebody had told him, *oh no. oh no I'm so sorry*, in capitals, eleven times. She'd laughed so hard she'd had to mute.

Here she saw it through a scope.

The left eye did it. It always had. The right one was just an eye, a golden one with dark rings in it, and it saw what eyes see, everything, the whole valley, more than she'd ever seen before she came. But the left one had a crosshair in it, a little red reticle set right in the gold, and when she really looked at something, it locked on. She felt it lock. Like a lens turning. And then she wasn't looking at the valley any more; she was looking at the thing, and the thing was close.

She looked at the rings.

Eleven miles. Fenwick had chained it, the first winter, with stakes, and sent it to the capital, and the capital had sent it back asking why. Nobody else in Thornwall could see that far. Not the sentries, not Hild, not the king, who'd stood beside her on this roof for a month now and felt what was out there the way you'd feel heat from a forge with the door shut, and asked her what it looked like. She could see it. She'd always been able to see it, since the first summer, since the night at the top of the pass when she'd looked down at it for the first time and Tiger had nearly gone. But she'd seen it the way you see something through a keyhole: there, and gone, and there. A glimpse. She'd lock on a face and hold it a breath, two, and then it would slip, and she'd have to find it again, and her head would ache, and by the time she came down the hundred and twelve steps she'd have a headache like a nail behind the crosshair that lasted till noon.

She held it now.

Thirteen rings. Round the burned circle at the bottom of the valley where nothing grew. Hundreds of them, standing, in rings, with their faces turned in toward the middle. Shepherds who'd walked up the valley one at a time over two years and not come back. A drover. Two sentries from the Western Watch. A girl of about Joss's age in a red shawl. The thirty-nine from Valcrest, last summer, in the thirteenth, the outermost, one at a time over ten days. All of them very still. All of them not breathing quite right, the way you could tell from a long way off if you were her: the little bit of the light in them that should have gone in and out on a breath, and didn't, and just sat there.

And the absence in the middle of them. Where the burned circle was. A place where her sight went and found nothing, not dark, not empty, *nothing*, the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. Facing the pass. It had faced the pass all spring, since the morning the king stood in front of her on this roof and opened his door and it had looked at him for as long as it takes an edge to go from straw to blue and then turned round. *The way you'd stand with your back to a door you knew somebody was behind*, the king had said. *It knows you're here now*.

It was still facing the pass. It hadn't moved.

She looked for Ossie.

Third ring. North side. A young soldier with freckles and big feet, in a Western Watch coat, with his face turned up toward the pass road, the way it had been turned for a year and a half, like a dog watching a door. Except a hair. Since the evening Tiger had run out into the valley after Joss and back, it had been turned a hair to the side, toward the road, the way you'd look sideways at the start of a race at the one beside you.

Still a hair. She held it. She could see the freckles.

She held it.

She'd been holding it, she realised, for a long time. Not a breath, not two. Twenty. Thirty. She'd stopped counting. Ossie's face was there in the crosshair, eleven miles off, every lash of it, and it wasn't slipping. It wasn't going anywhere. And her head didn't hurt.

She let go of Ossie and looked at the next face. A shepherd. She held him. The next. The girl in the red shawl. She held her. And the next and the next, round the third ring, face after face, every one of them close, every one of them staying where she put it, like coins on a table, like books on a shelf, like the stars on a clear night over the Redmarch, Yan had written once, when there was nothing between you and them and they just stayed where they were.

She did the third ring. All of it. Then the fourth.

She found she was crying. That was all right. She'd cried on the floor of the hall, too, about a ceiling.

"Your bar moved," said Hild.

Hyvred jumped. She hadn't heard her come up. Nobody ever heard Hild come up; she'd stopped making a noise on stairs when she lost the eye, she'd said once, because the eye had been the one that told her where her feet were, and after it was gone she'd had to learn to put them down where she meant to every time, and somewhere in learning that she'd stopped making a noise. She was sitting on the parapet with her back to the drop, the way nobody else ever sat, lean and brown and fifty-five, with her grey hair cropped close and the sword on her knees and her one eye on Hyvred.

"How do you know?" said Hyvred. "You can't see my window."

"I can see your face," said Hild. "Your face just did what Kei's did before the night he went up. And Drew's. And the boy's, out in the valley, with Joss over his shoulder." She tipped her head toward the rings. "How long have you been holding them?"

"I don't know. I stopped counting."

"Good," said Hild.

Hyvred opened her window.

She hadn't looked at it in a week. You didn't, when it sat still; you got into the habit of not looking, the way you got into the habit of not looking at a pot. It had sat at ninety-one percent since the first week of the thaw. It said ninety-eight now. And while she watched it, standing on the roof of the north tower in the wind with her glasses in her hair and the tears drying on her face, it said ninety-nine.

"Oh," said Hyvred.

"Go on, then," said Hild.

"I can't just *go on*. It doesn't work like that. You have to—"

"You've been doing it for an hour," said Hild. "You've just noticed. That's all a breakthrough is, mostly. Noticing you've already done it." She shut her eye. "Go on. Look at something. I'll wait."

So Hyvred looked.

Not at the rings. She'd done the rings. She turned round, on the roof, in the wind, and looked at everything else: at Thornwall, below her, grey and square and waking up, with the smoke going up straight from forty chimneys. At the well in the yard where they'd stopped Mother Ivey. At Old Tull's forge with the door open and the fire just going. At the stable, where a boy in a red wool scarf was carrying a bucket in both arms with his tongue out, and she could see the gap in his teeth. At the south gate, Tiger's gate, the one he'd built at Valcrest and pulled down and carted north and built again, where a tiger boy was sitting on the top bar with his knees up and his ears flat, looking north, the way he did every morning. At the road south, going away over the downs toward the rest of the kingdom, empty, with a hare on it. At the hare. The whiskers on the hare.

And then up. At the sky. The way she'd looked at it the first night, in the yard, on her back on the cobbles, with Bee beside her saying *Hyv, you're going to get a cold*, and her not caring, because there was all of it, all at once, and nobody had ever told her.

It was going pale in the east. There was one star left, very high, and she held it, and it stayed.

She looked at it for a long time.

"*Range*," said Hyvred.

"Is that a word that means something," said Hild, "or is it one of yours?"

"Both," said Hyvred. She was laughing now. She didn't know when she'd started. "It's a thing in games. How far something can reach. How far you can shoot. It's a thing for—" She stopped. "It's a thing for snipers. It's what you call it when you can hit something from far away. Ice would have—" She stopped again.

She looked at the window. *Range*. In a world where nobody had ever played a game with a scope in it. Where the window had picked the word out of her, the way the windows always picked the words out of them, from somewhere they didn't know they kept things. From an AWPer saying *oh it's osu! Flashlight*, eleven times, *I'm so sorry*.

"He'd have loved it," said Hyvred. "He'd have been so annoying about it."

Hild didn't say anything. But her mouth did the thing it did, at the corner, that wasn't quite a smile.

"Now," said Hild. "Look at the rings again. Properly. With this." She tipped her head at the window. "And tell me what's changed. Not your bar. Them."

Hyvred turned round.

It was in the thirteenth ring.

Not in it. Outside it. That was why she'd missed it. She'd been doing the rings one at a time, round and round, face after face, the way you'd read a page line by line, and she'd done the thirteenth and stopped, because the thirteenth was the edge, and there was nothing past the edge.

There was something past the edge.

Seven. Standing outside the thirteenth ring, on the north side, a little apart from it and from each other, the way people stand when they've arrived somewhere and haven't been told where to go yet. Not in a ring. Not facing in. Facing the pass, with the absence, as if they'd come to stand in the queue behind it.

She held them, one at a time.

A man in a long coat with the hem torn. An old woman with a stick. Two men in dockers' smocks, the kind you'd see on a wharf, with their sleeves rolled. A young woman with her hair in a net. A boy, older than Joss, younger than Tiger, in a sailor's jersey.

And a woman with a basket on her arm. A wicker basket, the market kind, with a cloth over it, and under the edge of the cloth, very small, very far, through a crosshair that had never held anything eleven miles off before today, something round. Something red.

Hyvred stopped breathing.

"Seven," she said. "New ones. Outside the thirteenth. They're not in a ring yet. They're just standing there, where the next one would go." She swallowed. "Hild. They're not from round here."

"How do you know?"

"Two of them are dockers," said Hyvred. "There aren't any docks here. One's in a sailor's jersey. And one's got a basket." She swallowed again. "With apples in it."

Hild opened her eye.

Drew had made a form.

Of course he had. Drew had run eleven tournaments on Earth and one in Valcrest, the one with the forty-foot wall and the spider and THE THIRTY-FIVE painted round it in letters as tall as a man, and he could no more go to a tournament without a form than Hyvred could go up a tower without counting the steps. It was pinned to the door of Kei's room, where they ate now, because Kei's room had the big table and the hearth and Nessa, who did the hearth and didn't hold with people eating on beds. NAME. GOING (Y/N). TAKING. NOT TAKING (and why). In his neat square capitals, with a column ruled down each side for the answers, and the 88EXP pendant on its chain hanging off the nail he'd pinned it with, because he'd taken it off to do the ruling and forgotten.

Tiger's line on the form was empty for two more days.

She knew why. Everybody knew why. Nobody said it. It sat in the middle of Kei's room at supper the way the absence sat in the middle of the rings, a thing everyone's eyes went round.

He'd built a gate. That was the thing about Tiger. He'd built a gate at Valcrest, for the Stronghold, out of planks and stubbornness, and it had held for six glasses against the champions of the Lists until Gabe pulled his sword out and the whole thing fell over flat; and he'd pulled it down after and carted it north, every plank, on a borrowed dray, and built it again across Thornwall's south gate, where it didn't do anything, because there was already a gate there. He sat on it every morning. And every morning he looked north.

On the third evening Hyvred found him on the shed.

OSSIE'S, it said, in Bee's chalk, on the back wall, under the roof. They went on the tenth day, at dawn. Drew and Bee and Kei, and Brannoch's riders as far as Ashby, and a cart. Corvin stayed. A guide went where his group's middle was, he said, and the middle was on the tower; and the king would want the cord.

She'd meant to see them off at the gate. She'd been at the gate, at the grey, with everyone; she'd hugged Bee until one of the Puffs got squashed and buzzed at her, and held Kei's hands while Hisui, round his neck, told her something in a voice only Kei could hear that made Kei laugh and say *he says don't look at anything he wouldn't*, and stood in front of Drew, who'd looked at her for a long time with his patient counter-fighter's face and then said, *I'll leave the box*, and she'd said, *I know*. And then Tiger had gone up onto his gate, the useless one, across the gate, and sat on the top bar, and she'd found she couldn't stand there and watch them go down the road from the ground, getting smaller, the way she'd have watched them on Earth, a little circle of picture shrinking in the middle of black until it was gone.

So she went up the hundred and twelve steps. She counted them. And she watched from the top.

She watched them all the way.

Down the south road, over the first ridge of the downs, and the second. Past the place where the hare had been. Past the milestone, and the ford, and the farm with the red door, and the long hill. Five miles. Eight. Eleven, which was further than anyone in Thornwall had ever seen anything. Fourteen. The cart a speck, and then a dot, and then three people beside it she could still tell apart: Bee's wings, catching the sun. Kei's snake, green. Drew's walk, the patient one. Twenty miles. She didn't know. She'd stopped counting.

Then they went over the last ridge she could see over, and down the other side, and the world curved, and they were gone. Not because she couldn't see them. Because there was a hill in the way, and even she couldn't see through a hill.

She stood there for a while.

"Did they wave?" said Hild. On the parapet. Hyvred hadn't heard her come up.

"Bee did. At the second ridge. She turned round and waved at the tower. She knew I'd be up here." Hyvred wiped her face. "She waved for about a mile."

"Of course she did," said Hild.

Hyvred turned round.

North. The valley, under the morning. The swept road going up it toward the pass between its walls of old snow, a furlong further out than it had been in the spring, as if somebody were sweeping it toward them. The rings. Thirteen of them, and seven more standing where a fourteenth would go, with a basket.

She locked on.
