Cursiffe had been at ninety-nine for a hundred and seven days, and he'd stopped minding, mostly, because he'd found something better to do with his evenings.

He wrote comments.

That was what Thea had called them, the first time, in Valcrest, on the floor of the archive room at the king's house, with Oswin's ten-foot copy of the circle spread out between them and a box of oiled sheets. *Comments*. The things you wrote in the margin of code, in plain language, so that whoever came after you would know what the code was doing and why. He'd laid the oiled sheets over Oswin's copy and written on them in blue ink as she Parsed it, ring by ring. *THE CALL*. *THE NET*. *LEGACY CODE. DO NOT TOUCH*. And on the last one, over a blank arc drawn with the same care as all the rest, *IT'S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX*.

He'd liked it more than anything he'd done in two years. More than the strips for Hana's cords, even, which were useful, and which the whole kingdom used now, and which nobody but him could write. The strips were plumbing. This was the other thing. Somebody had built something enormous, a long time ago, and never written down what any of it was for, and he was writing it down. In English. In the only script in the world no native could read.

So every night, now, he sat at the long table in the archive in the castle at Solmere, under the lamps, beside an archivist who was copying the second of forty grey leaves with a pair of steel dividers made by a king, and wrote comments on the copy.

Not on the meaning. Nobody knew the meaning. He wrote down where things were.

*Ring, closed, seven ticks*. *Line out from ring, returns at nine*. *Same shape as circle ring 11, outer half*. *Oswin: check this one twice, the ticks are close*.

It was like writing the index for a book in a language you couldn't read. He found it very soothing.

***

Oswin was asleep under the table.

He always ended up there. Cursiffe had stopped remarking on it in the first month. Oswin would copy until his hand shook, and then he'd copy until it stopped shaking, which it did, eventually, the way a horse stops shying if you keep it walking. And then at some point after the second bell past midnight he'd put the ruling pen down very carefully on its rest, and close the dividers, and say *I'll just*, and get down under the long table with his coat over him and go to sleep with his head on a box of old ledgers, and Cursiffe would go on writing.

He looked down at him now. A thin man of forty with ink on his chin, asleep on the stone floor of the archive with his knees drawn up and his mouth open, holding, in his sleep, the closed dividers against his chest the way a child holds a toy.

They'd come with the king, three weeks ago. In oilcloth, tied with a smith's twist of wire, with a note on cream paper in a big square hand. Oswin had read the note at the top of the archive stair, standing up, and then sat down on the top step quite suddenly and not said anything for a quarter of an hour.

*She says it parses. She says you're not wasting a year.*

*Three places a hair out. All where the lines run close. Not your eyes. Your pen. Use these.*

*Take two years if you need them.*

*S.*

The dividers had a hand on the hinge. A tiny open hand, stamped into the steel, cracked down the middle where the two legs met, so that when you opened them the hand split in two and when you closed them it came back together. Aldemar's hand. The king had cut the die himself, at an inn, on the road north.

Oswin had cried twice that evening and once the next morning, and then started the second leaf.

He'd done it in eleven nights. The first had taken eighty.

***

Cursiffe looked at the box.

It sat at the end of the table, where it always sat, in the lamplight. Grey, and plain, the size of a bread bin, with no lid until you touched it, the way it had been in the vault. Inside it, forty leaves. Thin, grey, cool, heavy as stone, bending like good paper. In a hand that slid off his eyes every time he looked at it, the way the kingdom's script slid off them, the way all handwriting did, for the summoned, except their own.

He'd tried to read it, once. In the first week after the door. He'd sat with the first leaf in his lap for an hour, and looked at it, and felt his eyes slide off, and slide off, and slide off, like trying to hold a fish in your hands. He'd given up. Not because it was hard. Because it was rude. It wasn't his. It was Thea's, or Oswin's, or nobody's yet. He was the index.

He looked at the box, and then at the Council minute on the corner of the table, which Hana had brought down three weeks ago and left there so they'd remember.

*His Majesty declined the petition*.

He'd been in the gallery. He'd heard the two words. He'd laughed, actually, very quietly, into his sleeve, and the clerk beside him had looked at him in horror. He'd been the only person in the gallery who laughed.

He'd also seen the boy.

Everybody had, after. Hana had told them all, that night, in their rooms, with the door shut. *Edwy. A third-year summoner. Kestra says his feet pointed at the archive stair the whole time*. And the king had put a guard on the stair the next day, a big quiet man from the castle watch who sat on a stool at the top of it all night with a lamp and a sword across his knees and nodded to Cursiffe every evening when he came down, and every morning when he went up.

Cursiffe had felt safer for it. He'd thought about the boy, for a week or so. Then he'd stopped.

He'd been thinking, he'd realise later, about the wrong stair.

***

He smelled it before he heard anything.

Lamp oil. Not the clean thin smell of the archive lamps, which Oswin filled himself every evening with the good oil that didn't smoke, because smoke was bad for paper. The other kind. Thick. Rank. The cheap oil they burned in the castle kitchens and the summoners burned in their Hall, that left a film on everything.

He'd been dozing. He hadn't known it. He was sitting at the long table with his pen in his hand and his head on his other hand and the comment he'd been writing stopped halfway through a word, *Same shape as circle ri*, and the lamps had burned down a little, and the smell was in his nose.

He looked up.

There was a door at the far end of the archive. Small. Low. Behind the last stack of shelves, where the old records were. It went down. Not to the vault; the vault stair was the other way, ninety-one steps, hand-cut by somebody very patient. This one went down to the old cellars under the castle, the ones that ran under the square, under half the old town. Nobody used it. It had been locked for a hundred years. Oswin had the key on his string, with the black one, and had never once turned it.

It was open.

And there was somebody in the archive.

***

He was at the end of the table. By the box.

A thin young man in grey, with a shaved head and a lamp in one hand and a clay jar in the other, tipped. Pouring. The rank oil going out of the jar in a thick dark rope over the grey box, and over the end of the table, and over Oswin's oiled sheets. The second leaf, finished, eleven nights of it, lying under its weights in the lamplight. The third, a quarter done, pinned to its board. The ruling pen on its rest. Cursiffe's comments.

All of it, shining, wet.

The young man was crying.

Cursiffe saw that before anything else. He'd remember it afterwards: not the jar, not the lamp, not the open door. The young man's face. Wet, all down it. Not sobbing. Just crying, steadily, the way you cry when you've decided something and it's the worst thing you've ever done and you're going to do it anyway because it's right.

"Don't," said Cursiffe.

The young man looked up.

He hadn't seen Cursiffe. Cursiffe understood that, in the half-second their eyes met. A man asleep at the far end of a long table in the lamplight, with his head on his hand. He hadn't looked. He'd come up through the cellars with his jar and his lamp and his key from the Hall, a hundred years old, that nobody had thought about, and he'd only been looking at the box.

"I'm sorry," said Edwy.

His voice was very young. It cracked.

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. It's for you. It's for all of you. You don't understand. Nobody understands, the king doesn't understand, she's going to *read* it, the girl in the north, she's going to read the hand that holds you and she's going to pull, and you'll all—" He stopped. He was shaking. "I felt it take you. On the fourth ring. I was seventeen. I felt every one of you come through, like hooks going into a fish. I've felt it holding you every day since. And if she reads it wrong it'll let go. All at once. All thirty-five." He was crying harder. "It wasn't meant to be read. It wasn't *meant* to be. That's what it means. That's what it's always meant."

"Edwy," said Cursiffe.

The young man flinched at his name.

"Put the lamp down," said Cursiffe. "Please. We can talk about it. I've read the circle. All fourteen rings. I wrote *don't touch* on the middle of it myself, in English, because I know exactly what you mean. I know what it's holding. I'm *in* it." He was standing up. He didn't remember deciding to. "Put the lamp down."

Edwy looked at him across the length of the long table, in the lamplight, with his face wet.

Then he put the jar down. Carefully. On the end of the table, by the box.

And lifted his other hand. The empty one. Flat. The way the mages in the kingdom lifted their hands when they chanted.

And began to sing.

***

Cursiffe knew the words. Not the meaning; the sound. Every summoned person in the kingdom knew that sound by now. The old tongue, the chant, the thing the native mages did with their voices instead of their hands, slow and low and going round and round. He'd heard Oswin do it, once, lighting a lamp. He'd heard the Advisor do it, the first night, in the hall, before any of them knew what it meant.

Fire. It was the fire one.

Edwy was a summoner. A mage. A third-year summoner was a Candle, at least, Oswin said, and the Hall didn't take anyone who couldn't light a room with a word. He sang, and the air over his open hand went hot, and bright, and a small flame came up in his palm, very steady, very clean, the way a candle flame stands up in a room with no draught.

And he turned his hand over.

The flame fell.

***

It went up all at once.

The oil caught where the flame touched it, on the end of the table, and the fire ran. Not like a candle. Like water poured out of a jug, along every line the oil had made: across the end of the table, over the edges of Oswin's oiled sheets, round the weights, up the board where the third leaf was pinned. Over the box. A sheet of it, yellow and orange and stinking, roaring, going up into the lamplight toward the beams of the archive ceiling, so bright and sudden that Cursiffe threw his arm over his face.

Under the table, Oswin woke up and screamed.

Cursiffe didn't think.

That was the thing he'd tell people afterwards, when they asked, and they asked a great many times. *I didn't think*. Which was strange, because thinking was all he'd ever done. He was the patient one. The slow one. The man who spent a quarter of an hour deciding which word to write in a margin. He'd sat at ninety-nine for a hundred and seven days because he'd thought about every line he'd ever tried to write until there was nothing left in it but thought.

He didn't think. He went for the vial.

It was on its chain round his neck, where it always was. Small, glass, dark blue. The ink he'd brought through the floor of the hall, the only thing in his inventory the first morning, that never ran out however much he used. He pulled the stopper with his teeth. He dipped his finger in it.

And went down the table into the fire.

***

It was hot. That was all he'd remember of that part. Not pain; that came later, a long time later, and Rachael would have something to say about it in the channel that made the whole kingdom laugh. Just heat, like opening an oven door in your face, and the light, and the noise, and Oswin screaming somewhere behind him, and the box.

He could see the box. In the middle of it. Grey, plain, the size of a bread bin, with the fire running over its top and down its sides in a sheet. Forty leaves inside it. Three hundred years under a door and four hundred under a stone, and a dead king who'd said *let no one burn them while you look*.

He put his hand on the lid.

It was cool. Even then. Even in the middle of the fire. The way the leaves were always cool. He felt the lid not be there, the way it never was until you touched it, and then be there, under his palm, grey, solid.

He wrote on it.

With his finger. In blue ink. In English. In capitals, the way he'd written *DO NOT TOUCH* on a ring of the circle and *IT'S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX* on an arc, because capitals were the way you wrote a thing you meant the reader not to argue with.

Four letters.

*KEEP*.

***

The fire went round it.

He saw it happen. He was a hand's width from it. The sheet of flame running over the lid of the grey box reached the first line of the *K*, and stopped. Not went out. Stopped. Parted. The way water parts round a stone in a river: going round, on both sides, and meeting again behind, and going on. All the way round the box. Leaving it there, grey and plain, in the middle of the roaring yellow, with four blue letters on its lid and not one lick of fire on it anywhere.

Like a stone in a river.

Like a word nobody in the kingdom could read, and so nobody could argue with.

Cursiffe stood in the fire with his hand flat on the box and looked at it.

And something opened.

Not in front of his face. Under him. Under everything. The way Thea had said it opened for her, at the bonfire, the first winter, and Maigo said it opened among the markers, and Kei said it opened over a cup. The place under the world where you could write. He'd been standing at the edge of it for a hundred and seven days, writing comments, and never once put his foot over. And he'd just walked into a fire and written four letters on a box and his foot had gone over without asking.

He felt the world read what he'd written.

It read it. All the way down. The way a reader reads a margin note and understands what the author meant. *KEEP*. Not *stop*. Not *don't burn*. Not *no*. *Keep*. The thing an archivist did. The thing a dead king had asked for. The thing a man did with something that mattered: kept it.

And the world, which had never had anybody write to it in English before, and couldn't argue, kept it.

***

His window opened in the middle of the fire.

He didn't look at it. Not then. He'd look at it later, sitting on the floor of the archive with his back against a shelf and Oswin's head in his lap and his right hand in a bucket of water that Jared had fetched, and read it, and laugh until Oswin asked him to stop.

He didn't look at it now. He turned round.

The table was burning. The end of it. Oswin's sheets. The second leaf, eleven nights, curling at the edges, going brown. The third, on its board. The ruling pen on its rest, with the fire round it. His own comments, going up in little black curls, *Same shape as circle ri*.

And under the table, Oswin. On his back. Trying to get out. Tangled in his coat, with the fire coming down over the edge of the table above him like a curtain, and the dividers still clutched to his chest.

Cursiffe dipped his finger in the vial again.

He didn't write on the sheets. There wasn't time. There were too many, and they were burning, and he could feel that the place under the world would only take so much from him tonight, the way your arms will only lift so much. He had one more word in him. Maybe two.

He went down on his knees in the fire, and reached under the table, and took Oswin's hand. The one with the dividers in it. Pulled it out toward him. And wrote on the back of it, in blue, in capitals, very fast, while Oswin stared up at him with his mouth open:

*KEEP*.

And the fire coming down over the edge of the table above Oswin's face reached the line of the archivist's raised hand, and parted, and went round.

***

The rest was quick.

Cursiffe didn't remember it in order. Jared, first. Jared, who'd been asleep two floors up and woken, he said afterwards, because Aleaka had hammered on his door in the dark and said *something's carrying fire*, with Brisk on her arm screaming. Jared coming down the archive stair past the guard on his stool, who'd heard nothing, who'd been watching the wrong stair all night the same as all of them. Jared in the doorway in his shirt with his red hair on end, taking it in. And then not standing in the doorway any more.

The guard behind him, shouting for the watch. Buckets. Sand. The castle coming awake round them in pieces, bells, feet on stairs. Somebody beating at the burning end of the table with a wet blanket. Somebody else at the cellar door, finding it open, and the key still in it.

And Edwy.

He hadn't run. Cursiffe would think about that for a long time. He'd had the cellar door behind him, open, and a hundred years of dark under the square to run into, and he hadn't run. He'd stood at the end of the long table with his empty hand still lifted and the flame gone out of it, staring at the grey box in the middle of the fire.

At the four blue letters on its lid.

He couldn't read them. Cursiffe saw his eyes go to them and slide off, and go back, and slide off. The way everybody's did. The way Cursiffe's own eyes slid off the kingdom's script, and off the leaves, and off every hand in the world but his own.

"What did you write?" said Edwy. He was whispering. "What did you *write*? Why won't it—" He stopped. "What did you write?"

Cursiffe was on his knees under the edge of the table, with Oswin's hand in his, and the fire going round the two of them, and his own right hand starting, now, finally, to tell him what it thought of all this.

"*Keep*," he said.

Edwy looked at him.

"It means keep," said Cursiffe. "In my language. The one nobody here can read." He breathed in smoke and coughed. "You said it wasn't meant to be read. You're right. It's not meant to be read by *you*. Or by me. It's meant to be read by somebody who can, in a long time, carefully, with a king's dividers, by people who'll take two years if they need them." He looked at the box. "And until then, it's meant to be kept. That's all. That's what I wrote."

Edwy stood there with his hand up.

Then Jared reached him. Not fast. Not hitting him. Just there, suddenly, the way Jared was always suddenly there, half a pace in front of whatever he'd decided to stand in front of. He put one hand flat on Edwy's chest, and the other round his raised wrist, and lowered it. Gently. The way you'd lower somebody's arm who'd been holding something up too long and couldn't remember how to put it down.

Edwy let him.

He sat down on the floor of the archive, very suddenly, in his grey robe, with the fire going out round him under wet blankets and sand. And put his shaved head in his hands. And didn't say anything else.

***

Cursiffe looked at his window at the third bell.

Sitting on the floor of the archive, against the end of a shelf, in the grey dawn coming in through the high windows, in the stink of wet char and cheap oil. With his right hand in a bucket of water, because Jared had said *in, now, don't argue*, and he hadn't argued. With Oswin's head in his lap, because Oswin had sat down beside him after the fire was out and then sort of folded, slowly, sideways, and not got up again, and Cursiffe had let him. Oswin was asleep. Or something like asleep. With the dividers still in his hand, and four blue letters on the back of it.

The end of the long table was black. The second leaf was gone. Eleven nights. The third, on its board, was a curl of brown. The ruling pen had come through, mostly; it was steel. His comments were ash.

The box was in the middle of the burnt end of the table, grey and plain and clean, with not a mark on it. Not a smudge of soot. *KEEP* on the lid in blue, like a label on a jar.

He lifted his left hand, the one that wasn't in the bucket, and did the thing at the air, and his window opened.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he laughed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him like the smoke had, from somewhere very low down, and he sat on the floor of the archive with his hand in a bucket and an archivist asleep on his knees and laughed until his ribs hurt, and Oswin woke up and said *what* and he couldn't tell him.

*Keep*.

A hundred and seven days at ninety-nine. A hundred and seven nights of writing comments in margins, slowly, carefully, one word at a time, choosing every one, the patient man, the diligent man, the man who thought about a line until there was nothing left in it but thought. And it had come in the one second of his life he hadn't thought at all. Four letters, in capitals, on a box, in a fire.

It was the most himself he'd ever been.

"What's funny?" said Oswin, from his lap, blearily.

"I wrote *keep*," said Cursiffe. "On the box. And on you."

Oswin lifted his hand and looked at the back of it. At the blue capitals. His eyes went to them and slid off, the way they always did, the way they slid off all of Cursiffe's English, and came back.

"What does it mean?" said Oswin.

"It means you're an archivist," said Cursiffe.

Oswin looked at the back of his hand for a long moment. Then he put it against his chest, with the dividers, and closed his eyes again.

***

The Advisor came down at dawn.

Cursiffe heard him on the stair. Everybody did; the archive went quiet the way rooms went quiet for him. Thin and grey and very straight, in his riding coat over his nightshirt, with his face doing nothing at all. He came down the last steps and stood in the doorway and looked at it. The black end of the table. The sand. The wet blankets. The open door to the cellars at the far end, with the guard from the castle watch standing beside it now, very pale.

And the box. Grey and clean, in the middle of the char, with four blue letters on its lid.

And the young man in grey, sitting on the floor against the far wall with his head in his hands, with Jared standing over him and Kestra, who'd come down at some point that Cursiffe hadn't noticed, standing over Jared.

The Advisor looked at all of it.

Then he looked at Cursiffe.

"Your hand," he said.

"It's fine."

"It is not fine. You will show it to a healer within the hour. That is not a request." The Advisor looked at the window still open in the air in front of Cursiffe's face. Cursiffe turned it round for him with two fingers, the way they all did now. He read it. Cursiffe watched him read it. Something moved at the corner of the thin grey mouth. "Thirty-one," said the Advisor.

"Thirty-one," said Cursiffe.

"I shall tell the king when he wakes. He has been asleep for four hours, which I am told has not happened since the summer, and I do not intend to be the one who ends it." He looked at the box again. "What does it say?"

"*Keep*."

The Advisor was quiet for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "That is the word."

***

The Warden came at the second bell of the morning.

He came down the archive stair on his own, slowly, an old man in grey with his hands in his sleeves, past the guard on the stool, past Kestra at the bottom, who let him by without a word and wrote something in her book as he passed. He stood in the doorway of the archive and looked at the black end of the table, and the sand, and the box.

And the young man on the floor against the far wall.

Cursiffe watched his face. He'd watched it in the Council chamber, three weeks ago, from the gallery, and thought it was a sincere face, and been frightened of it. He watched it now. It was still sincere. It was grey to the lips.

"Edwy," said the Warden.

The young man didn't look up.

"I didn't ask him," said the Warden. To the Advisor. Very quietly. "My lord. On my life. I did not ask him. I made the Hall's case in Council and lost it and I went home and said the evening prayer and went to bed." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I didn't ask him."

"I know you didn't," said the Advisor.

The Warden looked at him.

"You didn't need to," said the Advisor. He wasn't unkind. Cursiffe had never once heard him be unkind; it wasn't one of the things the Advisor did. He was just exact. "You kept a sentence for three hundred years, Warden. *A thing which cannot be read was not meant to be*. You taught it to every summoner who came through your door. You stood up in the Council chamber and said it in front of the king. And a boy of twenty who'd felt the binding take thirty-five people when he was seventeen, and has felt it holding them every day since, believed you." He looked at the young man on the floor. "He believed every word. That is what a sentence is for, if you keep it long enough. Somebody believes it."

The Warden didn't say anything. He stood in the doorway with his hands in his sleeves and his face grey.

"I know a little about being sure," said the Advisor. Quieter. "I was very sure, once, on a wall, of two orders. I would give them both again. I have also spent nine years in a tower with a drawer, finding out what being sure costs other people." He looked at Edwy. "He was sure. He came up through a hundred years of cellar with a jar of kitchen oil because he was sure he was saving thirty-five lives. He was not running when Jared reached him. He was weeping."

"What will happen to him?" said the Warden.

"The king will decide," said the Advisor. "And I will ask him for something neither of you will like." He turned and looked at the box, and the black table, and Oswin, asleep now properly on Cursiffe's knees. "I will ask that he spends the winter here. In this room. Every night. On a stool at the end of that table, with the guard, watching an archivist copy what he tried to burn. One line at a time. With a pair of dividers that a king made. For as long as it takes." He looked back at the young man. "Until he understands what he is afraid of. Or until he isn't."

Edwy lifted his head.

He looked at the Advisor. Then at the box, with its four blue letters. Then, for a long time, at Cursiffe, on the floor, with his hand in a bucket.

"I can't read it," said Edwy. Very small. "What you wrote."

"Nobody can," said Cursiffe. "That's why it works."

"Will you teach me?"

Cursiffe looked at him. A thin young man of twenty with a shaved head and a wet face and soot on his grey robe, sitting on the floor of an archive that he'd tried to burn, asking to learn the only script in the world that nobody could argue with.

He thought about it honestly. Properly. The way he thought about everything, slowly, from the bottom up, for once.

"Yes," said Cursiffe.

***

That night he went back down.

He couldn't write. Not with his right hand. He went down anyway, past the guard on the stool, who stood up when he came and said *sir*, which nobody had ever called him in his life. Into the archive. It smelled of wet char still, and the end of the long table was black, and somebody had swept the sand into a heap in the corner.

The box was where it had been. *KEEP* on its lid.

And at the other end of the table, under the lamps, Oswin. Copying. The second leaf again. From the beginning. With the king's dividers in his hand and the ruling pen on its rest and his face very close to the oiled sheet, holding his breath for the length of every line. Eleven nights' work, gone, and he'd started again at noon, and he'd said, when Cursiffe asked him why so soon, *because the king said take two years, and I don't want to need them*.

On a stool at the end of the table, by the door to the cellars, which was locked now, and barred, and had the castle guard's stool across it too: Edwy. In his grey robe. Very still. Watching Oswin copy. With his hands in his lap.

He looked up when Cursiffe came in. He didn't say anything. Neither did Cursiffe.

Cursiffe sat down at his place at the long table, at the unburnt end, beside Oswin. He put his bandaged hand on the table in front of him, and his ink vial beside it, on its chain. And after a while he picked up a pen in his left hand, which he'd never written with in his life, and pulled a clean oiled sheet toward him, and began, very slowly, very badly, in big shaky English capitals, to write the comments again.

*RING, CLOSED, SEVEN TICKS.*

It took him a quarter of an hour. It looked like a child had done it.

He slid it down the table. To the end. To the stool by the cellar door.

Edwy looked at it. At the shaky blue capitals. His eyes went to them and slid off, and came back, and slid off.

"That's an *R*," said Cursiffe. He pointed with the pen. "The first one. That's how it starts."

Edwy looked at the *R* for a long time.

Then he pulled the sheet a little closer, and got down off his stool, and came and sat at the end of the long table, at the black end, by the box, with his face very close to the paper. The way Oswin's was. Holding his breath.

Cursiffe went on writing.

Beside him, Oswin copied the second leaf. Behind them, on the burnt end of the table, the grey box sat in the lamplight with four blue letters on its lid, and kept.
