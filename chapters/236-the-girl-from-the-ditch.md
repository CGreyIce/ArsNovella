The red sounded wrong.

Deston had known it would. He'd heard it from the top of the west rise three days ago, when John put it in the channel for two seconds, and it had gone through him like a dropped tray. And he'd heard it in Group 3's lines for three years, under the words, the way you hear the room in a recording; a sort of low dissonance under everything they wrote from Cinderhold, that he'd thought for a long time was just them. It wasn't them. It was this.

He heard it properly on the last morning, when the grey grass gave out under their boots and the road went down onto the band of red.

Everything had a note. That was the thing he'd never been able to explain to anyone, not even Declan, not even on the lake. He'd tried. He'd said *threads* because it was the nearest word, and people heard *threads* and thought of string; and it was string, sort of, the way the strings of a piano are string. Every person had one. Every person was one: a note, held, all the time, under everything they did, that they didn't know they were singing. He'd heard thirty-five of them go out across the kingdom from the stone floor of a hall in Solmere on the first morning, a choir in thirty-five directions, and he'd been listening to it ever since. Natives had them too. Hens had them. Gigichi's hedge hummed with its mouth shut. The lake had one, very low, that Declan was always humming along to without knowing.

The red had one.

It was a lot of notes. That was what was wrong with it. Not a chord. A crowd. Hundreds of them, thousands, laid down on top of each other and none of them in tune, all of them a little sharp, all of them leaning; the way a choir sounds when every singer in it has been frightened at once. It came up out of the rust-coloured earth through his boots and through the grey grass on either side of the road, which shivered, all the time, with no wind; and it never stopped, and it never resolved, and it never got any louder or any quieter. It just went on. The way pain goes on in a room after the thing that caused it has left.

He knew what it was. Maigo had told them, in the first winter, from Cinderhold. *The ground remembers*. Salty had called it an imprint. Deston didn't have a word for it. He just heard it, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard, and he'd stood at Declan's funeral.

And then Declan walked onto it, at the back, with the water skins, and it stopped.

Not all of it. Round him. A ring a few paces across that went with him as he walked. Deston heard it go silent. Not quiet; silent. A rest in the music. The way a whole orchestra stops for one bar and you can hear the hall breathing. In a ring round a man walking along a red road with a rod on his shoulder, the frightened choir stopped singing, all of it, every voice, and waited.

And under the silence, Declan's note.

Deston had known it for sixteen years. He'd heard it first at thirteen in a choir room that smelled of floor polish, before he knew he could hear anything; a boy in the row in front who'd turned round in the middle of the warm-up and said *you're flat, bro*, quite kindly, *lift it*. Declan's note had been a baritone's then, a boy's baritone, a bit rough, a bit too loud, very sure of itself. It was lower now. Much lower. It had gone down, over thirty-four years in a world Deston had never seen, the way a river goes down into a gorge; it had got deep and slow and dark, and so big that most days you could only hear the top of it. Like the lake. He was always humming it with his mouth shut.

It was very steady this morning. And very low. The lowest Deston had ever heard it.

He knew why. So did Gabe, walking beside him with the sledge over his shoulder and his jaw set. So did Xav, ahead of them, with his practice sword on his back, not looking round.

Ahead of Xav, at the front, just behind Alea, Petra was walking very straight.

***

She'd been at the front since the grey grass gave out.

She hadn't asked. She'd just gone up the line, past Malcolm and Azac and Rui Xiang and John, past Xav, until she was walking a pace behind the girl, with her sword on her hip and the stick under her arm and her eyes on the road. Alea hadn't said anything. She'd let the pace slow a hair, so that Petra wouldn't have to match it, and Petra had matched it anyway.

Deston could hear her note.

He'd heard it for a year now. On the jetty, at the Lists, at a long table in a hedge. It was a hard note. Grey, if a note could be a colour; it was, for him, often, though he'd never told anyone that either. A soldier's note. A woman who'd stood in yards at dawn for ten years doing nothing until the nothing was a thing. It had been held so long and so tight that it had gone a little flat, the way a string goes flat when it's been under tension for years and the peg's slipped a hair without anybody noticing.

It was flat now. And shaking. Not much. The way a held note shakes when the singer's running out of breath and won't let go.

And under it, since Ashby, something else. Something he'd heard first in a lane, in the middle of a morning, when a horse went up on its hind legs over a boy in a smock: a second note, very faint, coming off hers. Not hers, quite. Like an overtone. The sound a glass makes when you run a wet finger round the rim; a thin high clear thing that comes out of the glass and isn't the glass.

He'd heard it four or five times since. Faint. It went when she noticed it.

He didn't tell her. He'd promised Xav he wouldn't, on the lake, the night before they left, in the dark at the end of the shingle where Xav had come to find him after he'd watched Petra ask Declan to stand where he stood. *Don't tell her anything*, Xav had said. *About any of it.* And Deston had known exactly what *any of it* meant, and said yes, and they'd stood there in the dark for a while not saying anything else, two men holding the same thing from either end.

The ditch had been a feint.

That was the thing they were holding. That was what Declan had told the three of them on the end of a jetty, the spring before last, at five in the morning, after telling them everything else. That on the second night of the invasion, the Advisor had read the attack on the south line as real and sent him to hold it. And he'd gone. Across the red in the dark to a ditch where forty recruits were in the clay and a girl of twenty-three at the end of them with her arm broken wouldn't put her sword down. And held it. All night.

And known, by midnight, that it was the wrong line.

That the real attack wasn't here. That the south line was a door they'd opened to get him out of the fort. That while he stood on the red in front of a ditch, something was coming in through the infirmary wall, on the far side of Cinderhold, where his wife was working.

And he'd stayed.

For the forty. Because if he left, they'd die, and he knew it, and he could see the girl at the end over his shoulder if he turned his head. He'd stayed till dawn. And at dawn he'd gone back to the fort, walking, not running, and found the infirmary wall opened like a door and six dead on the floor and four healers gone.

Deston had sat on the end of that jetty and listened to Declan's note while he said it, and it had been the lowest thing he'd ever heard, until this morning.

*Don't tell her*, Declan had said. *Xav. Don't tell her. It's not hers to carry.*

And Xav hadn't. Two years. And Petra had asked, on the last night at the lake, sitting beside him on the last plank, for him to stand where he stood. So that she could see it in the light.

He'd said yes.

***

They saw the ditch an hour before noon.

It wasn't anything. That was what Deston thought, first. He didn't know what he'd expected: something that looked like a battle. It looked like a ditch. A long low trench across the road, cut square into the red earth, a little deeper than a man's waist, with the grey grass grown down into it along both sides and the bottom gone soft and dark where the rain sat. It ran off the road on either side into the grass, a long way, a hundred paces, more, and stopped. Behind it, on the near side, the road went on toward a long rise, and beyond the rise, Deston knew, though you couldn't see it from here, the fort.

Petra stopped at the edge of the road where the ditch crossed it.

So did Alea. A pace beside her. She didn't say anything. She'd been here before, last summer. She'd stood at the edge of this ditch with her father and heard him say *a girl of twenty-three, with her arm broken*, and she'd closed a question in her face like a door, Wendy said, and not asked.

The rest of them came up and stopped behind them.

Nobody said anything.

Then Petra got down into the ditch.

She didn't jump. She sat down on the edge, on the grey grass, and swung her legs over and let herself down into it, the way you'd get down into a boat. And stood up in the bottom of it, in the soft dark earth, up to her waist. And turned, and began to walk along it. East. Off the road. Along the trench, through the grass grown down into it, away from them.

They watched her go.

She walked for a long time. Eighty paces. Ninety. To the end, where it ran out into the grass and the trench got shallower and stopped; where there'd been nobody on her left and nobody on her right, ten years ago, and the line gone on both sides. And stopped there. And stood. In the clay. With her back to them and her face to the east.

Deston heard her note.

It wasn't shaking any more.

***

Declan walked past them.

He didn't say anything. He put the water skins down on the road, carefully, one at a time, all four of them, side by side, the way you'd put down a thing you were going to come back for. He put the rod down beside them. And walked out along the near edge of the ditch, on the red, in his ring of silence, toward the far end where Petra was standing.

Deston heard the frightened choir stop as he passed, and start again behind him.

Ninety paces. He walked them the way he walked everything: not fast, not slow, as if there were no reason in the world to do either. He came to the far end of the ditch, where Petra was. He didn't look down at her. He stepped across the trench, one long stride, from the near side to the far side, from the side of the fort to the side of the dark; and walked out onto the red in front of it.

One pace. Two. Three.

Four.

And stopped. And turned to face east. With his back to the ditch, and to the woman standing in the end of it.

Exactly where he'd stood.

Deston didn't know how he knew it was exactly. Nobody had told him. There wasn't a mark. It was a place on a red plain four paces from the end of a ditch, like every other place on a red plain. But he heard it. Declan's note, when he stopped there, went down. Not lower; it couldn't go lower. *Down*. Into the earth, the way a note goes down into the floor of a room when you hit the right one and the whole building takes it. As if the ground under his boots had been waiting for that exact note for ten years and had just heard it.

The ring of silence round him went out.

Not far. A few paces. Wider. And in it the grey grass at the end of the ditch, that had been shivering all morning, all ten years, went still.

He stood there with his hands at his sides. No sword. No rod. Facing east.

At the end of the ditch, Petra looked up.

***

Deston watched her look.

He'd watched her face for a year and never once been able to read it. Nobody could, Xav said. She'd been doing it too long. But he could hear her, and he heard her look up at the man standing four paces out on the red with his back to her, in the light, in the middle of a morning, and her note went—

He didn't have a word for it. It went the way a held note goes when the singer finally hears the chord it's been holding against, after years, and realises it's been in tune the whole time.

She stood there looking for a long time. A minute. More. With her arm at her side, the left one, that had been broken in this ditch, and her sword in its sheath on her hip and the stick under her other arm. And nobody on the road moved. Gabe beside him had stopped breathing. Xav had his hand flat on the back of his own neck, the way he did when a thing was too much.

Then Petra climbed out of the ditch.

On the far side. The east side. The side of the dark.

She put her hands on the edge and pulled herself up, the way you'd climb out of a river, and got a knee onto the red and stood up. A pace behind him. She looked at his back. And Deston heard Declan's note not move at all, not by a hair, the way it didn't move on the end of the jetty when somebody came and sat down beside him and he knew who it was without turning round.

And she walked past him.

Deston heard himself make a sound.

She walked past him. Out of the ring of silence round his feet. One pace beyond him. Two. Into the grey grass at the edge of the red where the choir was still singing, frightened, out of tune, ten years of it, coming up through her boots. And stopped. And stood there. In front of him. Facing east.

Between him and the red.

***

Nobody had ever done that.

Deston knew it the way he knew his own note. Nobody had ever stood there. Not in thirty-four years. Not on this plain or any other. He'd been the one in front for all of it: in front of the ditch, in front of the fort, in front of the kingdom, on the Redmarch, for ten years, and on the end of a jetty for ten years after that, with a lake in front of him and a town behind him and everybody he loved behind him, holding. Deston had heard it in his note on the jetty, the spring before last. That was what the deep slow river was. It was a man who'd been standing in front of things so long that he'd forgotten there was any other way to stand.

And a woman he'd held a ditch for, ten years ago, with her arm broken, had just walked out past him onto the red and stood in front of him.

Declan's note didn't move.

But something round it did. Deston heard it. The way you hear a big bell's overtones change when somebody puts a hand flat on the bronze.

Petra drew her sword.

Not the stick. The stick was under her arm still. She drew her own sword, the long plain blade she'd carried since she was a recruit, that had been in her hand in this ditch with her arm broken; and held it low, in both hands, the way she held it on the gravel bar; and stood. Feet under her shoulders. Weight even. The east wind coming in off the red into her face, lifting her hair, laying the grey grass over in front of her in long shivering waves.

And brought it across.

Low and flat. At the height of a man's chest. The way she brought it across a hundred times a morning on a gravel bar, to show a class where the line went.

The line came.

Deston heard it before he saw it. The overtone. The wet finger on the rim of the glass. Only it wasn't faint now. It came off her blade at the end of the stroke, high and clear and absolutely steady, and *stayed*; and as it stayed it went down. Down out of the high thin place where it had always been into her own note, into the grey soldier's note that had been held flat for ten years, and the two of them met.

And her note lifted.

A hair. The way a string comes up into tune when somebody finally turns the peg the right way. *You're flat, bro. Lift it.*

He saw it, then.

A line across the air in front of her, grey, at chest height, the width of the stroke. And it didn't go. It stayed, and it widened. Up. Down. The way frost spreads across a window from one point. A sheet of grey going up from the line toward the sky and down from the line toward the red, very thin, the colour of rain seen through glass, until it stood there in front of her from the ground to above her head. As wide as the ditch was long, nearly. A hundred paces of grey, standing on the red, with a woman in front of it and a man behind her.

A wall.

The east wind hit it.

Deston saw the grass. In front of the wall, on the far side, the grey grass of the red was lying over flat in long waves under the wind, shivering, whispering, all of it, the frightened choir coming up out of the earth, the way it had all morning, all ten years. And it came up to the wall. And stopped.

On this side of the wall, the grass stood up straight.

All of it. From the wall back to the ditch, a hundred paces long and a few paces deep, the whole strip of grey grass between Petra's wall and the trench where she'd lain with her arm broken. Standing straight, and still, in no wind at all. Silent.

Not because of him. Deston heard that. Declan's ring of silence was round his feet, where it always was, a few paces. This was more. This was everything behind the wall. The choir stopped at the wall the way the wind did, the way the whispering did, and on this side of it there was nothing. Silence. And in the silence, holding, two notes.

Hers. Lifted. In tune.

And his. Deep and slow and dark, under it.

And they went to the same note.

Deston had never heard that happen. Not between a summoned and a native; not between anybody. Two notes a long way apart, a soldier's and a river's, going to the same pitch. Not the same note, quite. The way the stone and Astra weren't the same note, Yan said. The same note in two different octaves, so that if you were standing between them, the way he was, it sounded like one. Grey, if he'd had to say. The same grey. Her colour, which had been his for ten years without either of them knowing.

The wall stood there.

He didn't know how long. Nobody did, afterwards. Rui Xiang said a minute. Malcolm, who'd been counting, said a hundred and twelve heartbeats, and that his own had been going too fast to count right, so it was probably more. Alea, who'd been standing at the edge of the road with her ears all the way forward and her hand on Astra's grip and her face white, said she didn't know, she didn't care, she wasn't counting.

Petra stood in front of it with her sword level and her feet planted and her face to the wind, and held it, and didn't move.

Then she lowered the sword.

And the wall went. Not all at once. From the top down, the way frost goes off a window when the sun gets onto it. And the east wind came through where it had been, and laid the grass over, and the choir started again, frightened, out of tune.

But not behind her. Not quite. Not all the way back to the ditch. A few paces round her feet, for a few heartbeats longer, Deston heard it: the grass standing still, and a silence that wasn't his.

***

Declan sat down on the edge of the ditch.

He didn't say anything. He walked back the four paces from where he'd stood, with his back to her, and sat down on the lip of the trench on the far side with his boots in it, the way you'd sit on the edge of a jetty with your legs over the water, and put his hands on his knees.

He didn't look at her.

Petra stood out on the red with her sword in her hand for a long time. Then she turned round.

Deston saw her face. He couldn't read it. Nobody could. But her hands were shaking so hard that the point of her sword was drawing small circles in the air, and her mouth was open, and she was looking at the man sitting on the edge of the ditch with his boots in it.

At his face.

Deston knew the rule, or as much of it as anybody did. Alea had told them, at the lake, one night in the hedge after the dinner: what the Advisor had told her in the first snow last year. Nobody could hold his face. You looked at it and it went, the way a word goes off the tip of your tongue. The king could. The Advisor could. Hild, at Thornwall, could. Nobody knew why. *Nobody under the sixth tier*, the Advisor had said. *And nobody's ever been able to tell me why.*

Petra was looking at his face.

"I can see you," said Petra.

He looked up at her then.

She stood there on the red with her sword shaking and looked at him for a long time, the way she'd looked up out of the ditch; and Deston, who couldn't see it, who'd never been able to, not once in two years at the lake, watched a woman see his brother's face for the first time in ten years, and both of their notes go very still.

"You look tired," said Petra at last. Not steadily.

"I am," he said. "A bit."

She nodded, slowly, as if that were the thing she'd walked twenty days to find out.

Then she walked back across the red to the ditch, the four paces, the five, and sat down on the edge beside him. Not close. A little way off. The way she'd sat on the last plank of the jetty, with the hen between them. And laid her sword across her knees. And the stick, which had been under her arm the whole time, which she'd never taken out, which she'd forgotten she had, slid out from under it and onto the grass behind her, and she didn't notice.

They sat on the edge of the ditch side by side with their boots in it and looked east.

On the road, nobody moved.

Then Deston heard horses.

***

They came from the fort. Over the rise behind the road. Six of them, and a seventh: Group 3, all five, on the fort's horses, which none of them could ride properly except Wendy, and Rhea on a big black, at the front, riding like somebody who'd been born on one; and behind her, on a pony so small his feet nearly touched the ground, Aldous, in his chaplain's black, with his book.

They'd seen them from the west wall the day before, Wendy said later. Ten small shapes on the edge of the grey grass. And Rhea had said *I'm going out to meet them*, and everybody had said *we're coming*, and Rhea had said *you can't ride*, and Salty had said *watch me*, and fallen off twice in the yard.

They'd come over the rise at a canter, and seen.

Deston didn't know how much. Enough. They'd pulled up on the top of the rise, all of them, all at once, and not come any further. He could see them up there against the sky. Seven horses standing on a rise, and seven people on them not moving, looking down at a hundred paces of grey wall standing on the red in front of a ditch, and a woman in front of it, and a man behind her.

They'd stayed there until it went.

Then they came down. Slowly. At a walk. Rhea at the front, on the black, with her face set. She rode right down to the road and past the water skins and along the near side of the ditch to the end, where the two of them were sitting, and pulled up, and looked down at Petra from the saddle.

Nobody said anything.

Rhea swung down. She did it the way she did everything, in one movement, and landed on the red and stood there with the reins in her hand. Her left arm, Deston noticed, was a little stiff still, the way an arm is stiff that's been broken and come back.

She looked at Petra for a long time.

"That's a wall," said Rhea.

Petra looked up at her.

"That's a wall," said Rhea again. Very quietly, for Rhea. "That's Shaped. I've seen it once. In the war. Hild, on the Thornwall road. Held a gate with it for an hour." She was quiet. "A few dozen in the whole kingdom. Maybe." She looked at the red, where the wall had been. "One more, now."

Petra didn't say anything.

Rhea looked at her a moment longer. Then at Declan, sitting on the edge of the ditch with his boots in it, not looking at anybody.

"I'm going to arm-wrestle you," said Rhea. "Tonight. At Oakes's table."

"I know," said Declan.

"Three counts."

"We'll see."

Rhea nodded, once. Then she looked back at Petra, and her face did a thing Deston had never seen it do in a year of the Lists and a summer of letters.

"How," said Rhea.

Petra looked at her for a long time.

"I stood in front of him," said Petra.

That was all. She didn't say anything else. She looked down at the sword across her knees, and her hands on it, still shaking.

Rhea stood there with the black's reins in her hand. Deston heard her note. He'd heard it at the Lists, twice, a bonfire in a high wind, loud, always leaning toward the next fight; and he heard it now, and it wasn't loud. It went down. Into the red, through her boots, the way Yan said it did, like water into a floor.

And stopped there.

And then, for a moment, so faint he wasn't sure, it leaned. Not down. *Out*. Toward the place on the red where the wall had been.

"In front of him," said Rhea slowly.

"Yes."

Rhea looked at the red for a long time. Deston could see her thinking. He'd never seen Rhea think; she was the kind of woman who did a thing and then thought about whether she should have. She was thinking now. You could see it go across her face like weather.

"I've been going down," she said. To nobody. "All winter. Into whatever I trust. And it goes down. And it stops." She looked at her own hands on the reins. "And that's a wall. Going *out*. In front of somebody." She looked up. "Huh."

She didn't say anything else. She got back on the black, in one movement, a little stiff on the left, and turned it, and rode back along the ditch to the road without looking round.

***

Salty got to Declan first, of the five. He fell off his horse doing it. He fell off it on purpose, Wendy said after, because it was faster than getting off.

He didn't say anything. He walked along the edge of the ditch to where Declan was sitting and stood over him, short and square and enormous, with his greatsword on his back and his face red from the ride and the fall and the rest of it, and looked down at him.

"Twelve," said Salty.

Declan looked up at him.

"It's owed," said Salty. "I read it. In the channel. On the last night at the lake. Xola put it in. *It's owed. It's Salty's.*" He was going redder. "I've been practising. All year. I've got the right peppers. Mine. From the plants by the forge. I've got it right, I think. Mostly. Wendy says mostly."

"Wendy's right," said Wendy, from the rise.

"So it's owed," said Salty. "And I'm cooking it. Not you. You're not allowed near the forge. And you're sitting down."

"I sit down now," said Declan. "PenguChi said."

"Good," said Salty. "Good. She's right too." He stood there a moment longer, looking down at him. Then he looked at the red, where the wall had been. At Petra, sitting a little way along the ditch with her sword on her knees. And back at Declan. "You let her," he said. Not a question.

"I stood there," said Declan. "She did the rest."

Salty thought about that.

Then he sat down on the edge of the ditch on Declan's other side, heavily, with his boots in it, and the three of them sat there in a row, a soldier and a smith and a man who'd been both, looking east at the red, and nobody said anything for a long time.

***

They came in at the west gate at dusk.

Deston had heard about it. He'd heard about it from Wendy's lines and Yan's and from Declan himself, on the lake, in the autumn: the twenty-five. The old ones. The men and women who'd been at Cinderhold ten years ago, the second night and the third, and were still here; who'd stood on the wall the morning after and watched a man walk out across the red alone with a sword on his shoulder toward fires round a rise. Who put their hands on their chests.

He hadn't known what it would sound like.

They were on the wall over the west gate. All of them. In the last of the light, in a long row, the way they'd stood the first time, Wendy said, when the sword went out. Old soldiers in old coats. A woman from the laundry, Nell, very straight. A one-eyed man, Jessop, leaning on a spear. A man with three fingers on his hand, Jory Platt. A big slow old orderly with canvas bags over his shoulder, Hesper. And the smith, Oakes, in his leather apron, with his arms folded. And at the end of the row, a young man with a neat beard and a brass glass at his belt, the commander, who'd come from Solmere and not believed a word, until the spring.

They put their hands on their chests.

All twenty-five, and the commander, as the ten of them came in under the gate. Flat. And kept them there.

Deston heard them. Twenty-six notes on a wall. Old notes, most of them, worn, held a long time. And every one of them, when Declan came in under the gate at the back with the water skins on his shoulder and the rod, *leaned*. Toward him. The way Yan said souls leaned toward what they loved.

And then, when Petra came in under the gate, a little behind him, with her sword on her hip and the stick back under her arm where Alea had picked it up off the grass and handed it to her without a word, a few of them leaned toward her too.

Not many. The old ones. The ones who'd been on the south line that night, maybe. Who'd carried the stretchers out to the ditch at dawn. Who'd seen, from the rise, a hundred paces of grey wall stand up on the red in front of the ditch, because of course they'd seen it, the whole fort had been on the walls since Wendy saw them from the west wall the day before.

Petra looked up at the wall.

She didn't put her hand on her chest. She didn't do anything. She looked up at the row of old soldiers with their hands on their chests and her face was the same as it always was, and her note, Deston heard, was in tune.

Hesper, at the end of the row, the big old orderly, lifted his free hand off his chest and tapped the tips of his fingers together. Three times. Light. And put it back.

Declan, under the gate, stopped walking.

He stood there for a moment with the water skins on his shoulder, looking up at Hesper. And Deston heard his note do something it had never done in sixteen years, not in the choir room, not on the jetty. It *wavered*. A hair. The way the deepest string on a bass wavers when somebody walks past it in the dark and brushes it with a sleeve.

Then it was steady again, and he walked on into the yard.

***

Petra couldn't make it again.

She tried in the yard that night, after supper, with the whole of Cinderhold pretending not to watch from the walls and the doorways and the forge. Deston watched from the barracks step with Gabe and Xav. She stood in the corner of the yard by the old oak post with her own sword, and stood for a long time, and brought it across.

Nothing.

No line. No overtone. Nothing. The sword went across through the air and the air was just air.

She did it again. Nothing. She didn't push. Deston could hear that she didn't push; her note stayed level, in tune, the way it had been since the ditch. She just brought the sword across and nothing happened, and she stood there and looked at the place where nothing had happened, the way you'd look at a step you'd been sure was there in the dark.

"It's gone," said Petra.

She said it to the post.

"It's not gone," said Xav.

She turned her head.

"It's not gone," said Xav. He was sitting on the step with his practice sword across his knees and his chin on his fist. "It's tired. You held a hundred paces of it for a hundred and twelve heartbeats. Malcolm counted. You've never held anything for a hundred and twelve heartbeats." He was quiet. "*Now* went for a week after the first time. I told you. It came back."

Petra looked at him for a long time.

"There was nobody behind me," she said. "Just now."

"No," said Xav.

"There was him. On the red. Behind me." She looked at the post. "There's nobody here."

"There's a whole fort," said Gabe, from the step. He said it gently, the way Gabe said everything. "Behind you. Up on the walls. Pretending not to look."

Petra turned and looked up at the walls.

Then she laughed. The way she'd laughed on the shingle the first summer, startled, out loud, as if somebody had shoved it out of her. And put her sword away, and came and sat down on the barracks step at the end beside Xav, very straight, and didn't try again.

It came back four days later. Deston heard it from the barracks, in the middle of a morning, the wet finger on the rim of the glass. A count of twenty, in the yard, in front of a recruit who'd dropped a practice blade on his foot. But that was later.

***

He found Declan on the east wall, after.

He'd known he would. He'd heard him go up, an hour after the yard went dark. Thirty-nine steps; Deston had counted them on the way up, because Wendy had told everyone there were thirty-nine and one was a different height. The twenty-third. He'd tripped on it.

Declan was at the eleventh merlon. The one with the cracked coping. The one he'd sat in since he was fifteen, Yan said, and stood beside for an hour ten years ago with the Advisor next to him while a gate was barred. He was sitting in it now with his back against the stone and his legs up and the rod across his knees, looking out at the red under the stars.

Deston sat down on the walkway beside him with his back to the parapet.

He didn't say anything. He'd learned that, on the lake. You didn't have to say anything to Declan. You sat there and he knew you were there, and after a while, sometimes, he said something, and sometimes he didn't, and both were fine.

The red was singing. Down there, in the dark, all the way to the mountains; the frightened choir, out of tune, the way it always was. Deston could hear it from the wall. He could hear the knoll too, an hour out, very faint: something green and gold, two notes now where there'd been one, Maigo's ash and the elf's seedling, together. And under the knoll, a long way down, very low, a cold small dense thing, listening.

And Declan's note. Here. A pace away. Deep and slow and dark.

It was still very low.

"She stood in front of you," said Deston. After a long time.

"Yes."

"Nobody's ever done that."

Declan didn't say anything.

"I heard it," said Deston. "When she walked past you. Your note. It didn't move. But everything round it did." He looked at the stars. "Like somebody'd put their hand on a bell."

Declan was quiet for a long time.

"I didn't know," he said at last. "That she'd do that. I thought she'd stand in the ditch. Where she was. And look up. That's what she asked for. I thought that's what she'd do." He looked at the red. "I stood where I stood. I thought, that's all I can give her. Stand there again. In the light. So she can see it wasn't—" He stopped. "So she can see it was just a man."

"And she walked past you."

"And she walked past me," said Declan. "And stood in front."

Deston didn't say anything.

"That night," said Declan. "In the ditch. When I knew. By midnight. When I knew it was the wrong line." He wasn't looking at Deston. He was looking at the red, at the place out there in the dark, south and west, where a long low trench went across a road. "I stood there till dawn and I didn't move unless something came, and I thought, the whole time, *I'm in the wrong place*. Every heartbeat. All night. *Wrong place. Wrong place.* I could feel the fort behind me. I couldn't feel her. I didn't know yet. I just knew I was in the wrong place, and I couldn't leave, and if I left they'd die."

His note didn't move. Deston heard every word of it go down into the stone.

"And today," said Declan, "I stood in the same place. Four paces out. And she walked past me. And stood in front. And I thought—"

He stopped.

Deston waited.

"I thought, *oh*," said Declan. Very quietly. "*It was the right place for somebody.*"

They sat there.

The red sang under the stars. The knoll hummed, very faint, an hour out. Somewhere in the fort below them a door shut, and a dog barked once and stopped, and in the barracks Malcolm said something and Rui Xiang laughed.

Deston heard Declan's note.

It hadn't gone up. It wasn't going to; it was a river in a gorge, it had taken thirty-four years to get that deep and it wasn't going to come up for one day. But it had a new thing in it, very faint, somewhere near the top, that hadn't been there in the morning. An overtone. A thin high clear thing, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass. Grey.

Hers.

Deston didn't say anything about it. He'd promised.

He sat on the east wall beside his brother from a different mother, the way he'd sat beside him in a choir room at thirteen and on a jetty at twenty-nine, and listened to the two of them, his low note and the faint grey thing in it, holding together over the red in the dark; and an hour east of them, on a knoll, under a stone with his wife's name on it in English, something small and cold lay very still and listened too.
