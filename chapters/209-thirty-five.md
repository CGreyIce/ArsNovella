The heart went warm at a quarter to eight.

Hana was on the sill of her window in the east wing, with her knees up and her tail round her feet and her own window open in front of her face, reading Wendy's lines for the third time. *161 scouts. 4 big ones. counted twice*. She'd written *None of us are* under Thea's, at 19:47, because it was true and because she couldn't think of anything else, and then she'd sat there with her wrist in her lap and not written anything more.

And the heart went warm.

Half of a gold heart, on its own purple cord, beside the seven halves. She'd made it in the winter she found her skill, and cut it in two. It had been cool since Valcrest nearly all the time. Warm all one night in the autumn, at Cinderhold, when a man had sat in a merlon with a letter. Warm for a minute now and then since, the way a thing in a pocket goes warm when somebody closes a hand on it to check it's still there, and lets go.

This wasn't that. It was the other kind. The long kind. Steady, and patient, and not letting go.

She knew where. Not from the heart; the heart didn't tell you anything but *held*. From everything else. A month's ride west, on the end of a jetty, in the dark, with the channel open in front of him and Wendy's lines in it. *They're going for the grave*. And Yan's, under it. *Nobody come. You can't get here in time and we don't need you to. It's ours*.

He wouldn't write. He never had. He'd sit there.

And he'd put his hand in his coat, and found the only thing he owned that went to anybody without words, and closed his fist on it.

Hana put her other hand over her own half and held it.

"Honey," said Milli. From the bed, where he was lying on his back with his window open over his face and his tail hanging off the edge. "Is that him?"

"Yes."

Milli didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He'd been there in the autumn when it was warm all night. He reached out a long arm without looking, and found her ankle, and put his hand round it, and went back to reading the same nine lines she was.

***

She took it to the Advisor at eight.

Not the heart. That wasn't hers to take anywhere. The lines. She'd written them out fair on a sheet, the way she did every morning, Wendy's numbers and Yan's *it's ours*, and gone across the dark yard and up the hundred and four stairs of the west tower, and he'd said *come in* before she knocked.

He'd read the sheet standing up. He couldn't read the English, so she'd written it in the people's script as well as she could, which wasn't very well, under each line. He read it twice.

"The knoll," he'd said.

"The grave. Wendy says they're going for the grave. Not the fort."

"Yes." He was quiet. He'd been at Cinderhold, once. He'd been on the wall. "Thank you." And then, as she turned to go, something he'd never said before. "Hanarisu. When there's news. Whatever the hour." He'd stopped, as if he'd heard himself. "Come up."

She'd said she would.

His lamp was on now. She could see it from her sill, across the yard: one small steady light at the top of the west tower. It would be on all night. It was on all night most nights. She didn't think it would be the same kind of all night as usual.

***

The others didn't go to bed either. Nobody said they weren't going to. Nobody went.

Jared cooked. He didn't ask anybody if they were hungry; he went into the little kitchen at the end of their rooms, the one the king had given them in the first winter because, he said, people who cooked their own supper were people who meant to stay, and cooked. Big slow movements. Onions. He stood at the stove the way he stood everywhere, square, as if the floor had been put there for him to stand on, and every so often he looked at his window, open on the shelf above the stove between the salt and the pepper, and read whatever had come, and went back to the onions.

Aleaka sat at the table with Brisk on the back of her chair. The hawk slept with his head under his wing and woke whenever she moved, and glared at the room, and went back to sleep. Aleaka didn't move much. She had her window open and her hands flat on the table either side of it, the way you'd hold something still on a boat.

Cursiffe wrote. He always wrote. He had a sheet of good paper and the vial of ink on its chain, and he was copying the channel onto the paper by hand, line by line, as it came, in his small sharp hand. *KEEP*, small, in the corner of the sheet. He didn't say why. Hana knew why. Lines went off the top of the channel after two hundred. Ink didn't.

And Hana sat on the sill. The middle. With seven halves on her wrist and one heart, and the window of the west tower across the yard, and a night to get through.

The middle of the night was the worst. It always was. Hana had sat up with enough people, on Earth, in voice channels at four in the morning, to know that. The hour when the jokes stopped and nobody wanted to be the one to say goodnight, so nobody said anything, and you could hear everybody breathing.

You couldn't hear anybody breathing in a channel. You could see it, though. The gaps between lines. Twenty minutes. Forty. A line, and then nothing for an hour. Thirty-four people all over a kingdom, awake, with their windows open, not writing, because there was nothing to write, and not closing them, because Thea had said she wouldn't and none of them were going to be the first.

Thirty-four. And one more, who never wrote.

The heart on her wrist was warm. All night. It didn't flicker, didn't go cool for a minute, the way it did when somebody shifted their grip. It stayed exactly as warm as it had been at a quarter to eight. She thought about a fist closed on it for nine hours in the dark. About what that hand had held, once. A sword nobody else could carry. A daughter, the night her mother died. The whole of a kingdom's wall, for an hour, while a gate behind him stayed barred.

And now half a gold heart she'd made out of wire and lacquer at a kitchen table, because she'd been the middle all her life and didn't know how else to send anything to someone who wouldn't write back.

Milli had fallen asleep with his hand round her ankle. He woke up at four and didn't let go.

Nobody wrote for an hour and nine minutes.

She counted. Afterwards she could never not have counted. Cursiffe had his pen over the paper and nothing to copy. Aleaka had her hands flat on the table again. Jared took the pan off the stove because the onions were black, and stood there holding it, and didn't put it down.

The sun came up over Solmere. Hana watched it come, from the sill, where she'd gone back to. The grey first, and then the slates on the roofs of the old town going from black to blue to wet gold, one street at a time, down the long hill. The bells rang six. Down in the yard a groom came out yawning with two buckets and stopped and looked up at the west tower, because the lamp was still lit at the top of it in broad daylight, and it never was.

The heart was warm.

She thought: if it goes cold. If it goes cold now, all at once, the way a hand goes cold when it lets go of something because it can't hold it any more. She thought it over and over, the way you think the one thing you mustn't think. She didn't know what she'd do. She didn't know what anybody would do.

It didn't go cold.

At seven o'clock the bells rang again, and Milli said, very quietly, "Honey," and she looked down.

The heart went cool at a quarter past nine.

Slowly. She felt it go. Not the way she'd been afraid of all night, all at once, a hand letting go because it couldn't hold on any more. The other way.
