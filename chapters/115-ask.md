It was Calvin who asked. Of course it was.

They were at the fire on the twenty-second night, all of them, the way they were every night now. The pot was empty. Somebody had brought a jug of the brewer's cider up from the town, and somebody else had brought a second one, and the second one was nearly empty too. Emrys was humming, very quietly, in his new old voice, something from the hills above Valcrest that nobody else knew. Gabe was asleep sitting up. Xav was looking at the lake.

Calvin had been working up to it all evening. Ars had watched him do it. Had watched him look across the fire, and open his mouth, and shut it, and drink, and look again. The boy from a kitchen in Malacca, who'd held a dented kettle against his chest on a mayor's terrace and asked the one question nobody else would dare.

He dared again.

"Are you coming?" said Calvin. "To the Lists. At midsummer."

The fire went quiet.

Ars had known it was coming. He'd known for weeks. Since the first night, really, since a fox girl on a jetty asked Gabe which was his favourite story. Every one of them had a date in their heads. *Midsummer. Valcrest. All thirty-five.* The king's letter to the master of the Lists, last autumn, that Chris had copied into the channel line by line: *as long as it ends with all thirty-five standing on the sand at the same time.* Six weeks, now. Less. The fifteen would leave in a fortnight, to be sure of the road. Maren had already started packing the militia hall.

And not one of them had asked him. Not once, in three weeks. They'd talked around it the way they'd talked around Diwa on the first day, the way you walk around a hole in a floor.

Until Calvin.

"I don't know," said Ars.

It was the truth. Dennis, across the fire, tilted his head very slightly, listening, and then relaxed. No bell.

"Okay," said Calvin. He didn't push. He just nodded, and looked at the fire, and said, very quietly, the way he'd said everything since the kettle: "I hope you do."

***

He didn't sleep that night.

He sat on the end of the jetty, the way he had for eight years, with the rod across his knees and the lake black and still and the wrong stars on it. And he thought about leaving.

Not about Valcrest. About *leaving*.

He hadn't been south of Wrenhaven in eight years. He hadn't been anywhere, really. Once a year he packed his bag and picked up his rod and walked east, alone, a long way, to a cairn on red ground with flowers round it, and stood there for a day, and walked home. That was all. That was the whole of the world, for eight years: a lake, a town, a jetty, and a grave at the other end of a month's walk. He'd built it that way on purpose. Small enough to hold. Quiet enough to heal in.

The lake had done what he'd asked of it. He knew that now. He'd felt Rachael's warm hands find the place on his chest that wasn't on his skin and say *it's closing*. He'd felt his bar move, this last year, forty to fifty to fifty-four, the way it hadn't moved in six. The lake had held him while he healed. It had been exactly the right size.

And it wasn't anymore.

He knew that too. He'd known it since the morning on the jetty with Thea and Deston and Higu, a week ago, when Deston had stood on the boards and turned in a slow circle and said *there's so many*. Every window in the kingdom. Every thread, coming in, from further than Deston could see. Every guide and every mage and every chaplain on every frontier, hanging off one bored boy's word on a garrison wall.

He'd built a small world to hide in. And the whole time, without knowing, he'd been in the middle of a big one.

You couldn't hide in the middle of something. That was the thing. You could only pretend you weren't there.

***

He thought about Halvard.

Maren had told him, two days ago. She'd come down the lake road at dusk, alone, which she never did, and stood at the end of the jetty with her hands behind her back like a soldier giving a report, and told him about the letter. The Advisor's seal. The short plain lines. *I believe I know where they have gone.*

And the last one.

*Is he awake?*

"What did you tell him?" Ars had asked.

"That you were fishing," Maren had said. "And that I'd never seen you look less asleep."

He'd laughed. He hadn't been able to help it. And Maren had looked at him for a long moment, in the dusk, with the face she'd worn for twenty months, the plain hard face of a guide with an order. And then she'd done something she'd never done in twenty months.

She'd sat down on the end of the jetty beside him. And put her hands in her lap. And said, quietly, not as a guide, not as anything:

"He's asked that in every letter, you know. Twenty months. Every report I've sent him, somewhere near the end. *How is he.* I've never once known what to tell him."

Ars hadn't said anything.

"I think he's been waiting too," Maren had said. "The way the girls waited. The way you did. I think he put you on that wall when you were fifteen and gave you to the king, and the king gave you to the border, and the border took everything." She'd looked out at the lake. "And I think he's sat in that study every night for eight years wondering whether he broke you."

"He didn't."

"I know he didn't," said Maren. "*You* should tell him."

***

And he thought about Severin.

That was the real thing. He'd known it all along, under the rest of it.

*Ask me in the spring.*

He'd said it on this jetty, in the autumn, in the dark, with the king sitting beside him sixty-four years old and the strongest man alive. After twelve years of the king not asking. Twelve years of a garrison boy with a window and words nobody had ever heard and a sword from past where the maps stop, and the king looking at all of it, every time, and wondering, and never once asking. *I'm not going to be the first.*

And Ars had said: *ask me in the spring*.

It was spring. The ice had gone out of the lake a month ago. The hawthorns were out along the lake road. The hens were laying again. Spring had come and was nearly over, and the king hadn't asked.

Of course he hadn't. Severin had said he wouldn't be the first, and Severin had never once in his life said a thing and then not done it. He was sitting in a castle in Solmere, the strongest man alive, and he was waiting. The way everyone was waiting. For the fisherman to open the door himself.

Ars sat on the end of the jetty and thought about all of them. Waiting.

The girls, for twenty months, at the top of a road. Gabe and Deston on a roof, for a year and a half, with a gap in a song. Xav on a quay. Petra in a ditch, for eight years, with three words she couldn't say. Halvard in a study. The king in a castle. Twenty locked doors at the bottom of twenty windows, all over a kingdom, rattling.

And him, on a jetty, for eight years, waiting to be healed enough to stand up.

He thought: *I'm the one everyone's waiting for. And I'm the one who's still sitting down.*

The lake lapped at the posts. Somewhere up at the cabin a hen muttered in its sleep. And far out in the middle of the black water, very faintly, he heard the pike turn over.

He stood up.

***

He wrote it at dawn.

On the cabin table, with the good pen, the one he kept for letters to the king. On the good paper, the heavy cream sheets Severin had left behind in the autumn with a note that said *for when you've got something to say*. He'd never used them. He'd written to the king perhaps four times in twelve years, and every time on whatever was to hand.

He sat at the table in the grey light with the pen over the paper for a long time.

He thought about what to say. He could say a great deal. He could tell the king everything, here, on this paper, now. A cottage, a fan in a room in Malacca, a window at six, a word on a wall. He could fill every sheet Severin had left him. He could explain.

He didn't want to explain on paper. He'd done that for eight years. In a cheap notebook, at night, on the stairs. In books he'd written and never shown anyone. In a window that wrote itself into thirty-five other windows and never said his name.

He wanted to be asked.

So he wrote one word. In the middle of the heavy cream page, in plain clear letters, the way he'd cut the words on the stone. In the language of this world, not his. So that the king could read it himself, without anybody's help, and know it was meant.

*Ask.*

And underneath it, smaller, the one letter he'd always signed with.

*A.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded it once, and sealed it with a drop of candle wax and his thumb, because he'd never owned a seal in his life, and wrote on the outside: *Severin. Solmere.* And took it up the lake road in the early morning, past the fence and the hens and the wall, to the mayor's house at the top of the square.

Rowan was up. Rowan was always up. He opened the door in his shirtsleeves with a cup in his hand and looked at the folded paper, and at Ars's face, and didn't say anything for a moment.

"Fastest you've got," said Ars.

Rowan took it. He looked at the name on the outside. He looked at the shape of it in his hand: one sheet, folded once, no weight to it at all.

"That's not much of a letter," said Rowan.

"It's one word."

Rowan looked at him for a long moment. Eight years. More. A soldier who'd stayed with a nine-year-old girl so that her father could go east, and who'd never once let him pay it back.

"Good word?" said Rowan.

"I think so," said Ars.

Rowan nodded, once, slowly. Then he put the cup down on the step and went to wake the stable lad himself.

***

The rider went out of the south gate before the sun was up over the hills. Ars stood at the top of the lake road and watched him go. One man on a fast horse, on the long pale road south, getting smaller.

Nine days to Solmere, for the king riding slow. Five for a rider with changes of horse at every posting house. Five back, if the king answered at once.

Ten days.

He went back down to the lake and taught the morning class on the shingle, the way he did every morning, and nobody asked him where he'd been at dawn, though Alea looked at him for a long time over the heron.

***

They were good days.

He'd remember them, afterwards, for the rest of his life. Ten days at the end of spring, by the lake, with fifteen people he'd thought he'd never see again and a daughter he'd raised alone and three people who'd come up a river with them. Waiting for a letter. Not saying so.

Xav made him move twice again on the fourth morning. And again on the eighth, with the whole town on the wall, and the children cheering as if somebody had won something. Both times the stick found him on the second move. Both times Xav looked at it, and said "Okay. Again," and went and put his practice sword down, and Ars stood on the shingle with his heart going like a boy's and thought about the third move, the one that hadn't come yet, and found he was looking forward to being annoyed.

Petra moved first, in a spar against Alea, and Alea didn't see it coming, and sat down on the shingle very suddenly and said a word Ars had never taught her. Azac stood in front of a hit from Gabe's wooden fence post and let it go through him into the ground, and Gabe sat down instead, and looked at the ring of stones round Azac's feet, and said *okay* in a small voice for the second time in a month. PenguChi climbed six percent in ten days, more than she'd climbed all winter, and stopped telling anyone the number because, she said, it was getting embarrassing. Rachael cleared the queue at Rowan's side door every morning by noon and started on the town's backlog of bad knees. Calvin taught the fishermen to read the sky. Malcolm wrote. Emrys sang the morning office every day at the chapel, and the town came to hear it, and some mornings, now, the town sang back.

Thea and Deston sat on the jetty in the afternoons with their notebooks, drawing lines, and didn't show anyone.

And the lake was blue, and the hawthorn was out along the road, and the evenings got longer, and every evening there was a fire on the shingle and a basket with fifteen fish in it, and every night he sat on the end of the jetty and did not sleep, and was not tired.

On the ninth night he opened his window, out of habit. The way you check your ammo.

It had said fifty-four since the night on the jetty when he told his brothers about Diwa.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed it, and looked at the lake instead, and thought about a word on a heavy cream page going south on a fast horse, and found that he didn't mind waiting for this one at all.

***

The answer came on the tenth day, at dusk.

He heard it before he saw it. Everybody did. A horse on the south road coming fast, and then not fast, and then walking, very carefully, down through the square, the way you walk a horse that's been ridden too hard and knows it. And then the whole town going quiet in that particular way it went quiet when something mattered. And Rowan's voice, at the top of the lake road, saying something short.

It wasn't the rider.

It was a woman in a grey riding coat, travel-stained to the waist, with a swordswoman's long straight back and a sword on her hip and a small battered book sticking out of her coat pocket. She came down the lake road leading a tired grey horse, slowly, in the last of the light, with the whole of Wrenhaven watching her from the top of the road. Ars knew her walk before he could see her face. He'd watched it every night for eleven nights in the autumn, from the end of his jetty, while she sat on his step and wrote in that book.

Kestra.

She stopped at the bottom of the road, where it met the shingle. She looked at the fire. At the fifteen round it, who had all gone quiet, who were all looking at her. At Petra, sitting a little apart on the stones, who had stood up very slowly, the way one swordswoman stands up when another walks into a room. At Maren, who had come down with the girls tonight and was standing by the fire, and who looked at Kestra across the shingle the way she'd looked at her across the militia hall yard in the autumn. Two cats in a doorway.

Then Kestra looked at him.

"He said," said Kestra, "not to give it to anybody else. He said to put it in your hand myself." She came across the shingle, leading the horse, and stopped in front of him, and took a letter out of her coat. Heavy. Cream. The same paper. "He also said that if I lost it in a river, I needn't come back."

"Did you nearly?"

"Twice." Kestra held it out. "Your road south is a disgrace. Somebody should tell the king."

"I'll tell him," said Ars.

He took the letter.

***

It was one sheet, folded once. Sealed with the king's own ring, the small one, the one he used for things that were his and not the Crown's. *Ars. Wrenhaven. In his hand.*

He broke the seal.

The king's writing was big and square and leaned to the right, the hand of a man who'd learned his letters late, at a forge, between heats. Ars had read perhaps four letters in it in twelve years. He knew it the way he knew the king's walk.

It said:

*In person.*

*Midsummer. Valcrest.*

*Bring the girl.*

*Consider the order lifted for a week.*

And underneath, smaller, in the same hand, the one letter he'd always signed with too.

*S.*

***

He read it twice.

Then he stood there on the shingle in the last of the light with the letter in his hand, and the fire behind him, and the lake behind that, and found he didn't have anything to say at all.

"He read yours at breakfast," said Kestra.

Ars looked up.

"In the small hall. With the Advisor and two of the Council and a bowl of porridge." Kestra's face didn't change. Her voice didn't change. She was, Ars had learned in the autumn, the most honest person he had ever met, and she reported things exactly as they were and let you do the feeling. "He opened it. He looked at it for a long time. And then he laughed so hard he had to put his spoon down. And then he stopped laughing, and put his hand over his face, and sat there like that until his porridge went cold. And the Advisor asked him what it said."

"What did he say?"

"He held it up. So the Advisor could see. One word." Kestra paused. "The Advisor sat down too."

Ars didn't say anything.

"He wrote that," said Kestra, nodding at the letter in Ars's hand, "standing up, at the table, before he'd even wiped his eyes. He didn't think about it. He wrote it and folded it and gave it to me and said *Wrenhaven, now, in his hand*, and I was on the north road in an hour." She looked at him steadily. "That's all. That's what happened. I said I'd tell you what happened."

"Thank you," said Ars.

"I wrote it down," said Kestra. "In case you wanted it." She touched the battered book in her pocket. "I write everything down. You know that."

"I know," said Ars.

***

He turned round.

They were all looking at him. All of them, round the fire. The fifteen. Emrys and Sabine. Petra, still standing. Maren, by the pot. Gabe, awake now, very awake, with his big hands on his knees. Calvin, who'd asked. They were all looking at him, and none of them said anything, and he could feel, without needing Deston's eyes, every one of them holding their breath.

And Alea.

She was on her feet. She'd stood up when Kestra came down the road. She was standing on the far side of the fire with her ears straight up and Astra on her back and her face perfectly still, the way her mother's face went when something mattered more than anything.

He looked at her across the fire.

"*Bring the girl*," he said.

He held up the letter. So she could see it. So they all could, though most of them couldn't read the king's square hand.

Alea didn't move.

"The king wants you at Valcrest," said Ars. "At midsummer. At the Lists." He breathed. "And he's lifted the order. For a week. So I can bring you."

There was a very long silence.

"You're coming," said Alea. Not a question. She said it the way she'd said *finally*, on the cabin step in the winter, with a basket of fish at her feet.

"I'm coming," said Ars.

***

Calvin made the noise.

It was the same noise he'd made on the shingle on the first morning, when the man on the end of the jetty said *I've got a kettle*. It came out of him louder, and he didn't sit down this time; he stood up instead, very suddenly, and knocked the cider jug over, and nobody cared.

And then everybody was standing up. All of them. Round the fire, in the dusk, on the shingle. Gabe putting his arm round his neck from behind like a falling wall. Deston saying something that might have been *I knew it* and might have been nothing. Rachael laughing. Xola crying, again, with Squiddles patting her face. Higu with her counting book out, writing, very fast. Xav, on the far side of the fire, not moving, looking at him, with his face doing nothing and his eyes doing everything.

And Alea, coming round the fire.

She didn't run. She was seventeen and Tempered and she didn't run anywhere anymore. She walked round the fire to where he stood with the king's letter in his hand, and stopped in front of him, and looked up at him for a long moment the way her mother used to. Deciding whether he was worth the trouble.

Then she put her arms round him. Hard. The way she had at nine, on the end of the jetty, in the first autumn, after a month of not speaking to him. When she'd decided.

"It's about time," said Alea, into his coat.

"I know."

"Eight years, Dad."

"I know."

"Mum would've had you packed by now."

He laughed. It hurt, the good way. He put his hand on the back of her head, between her ears, and held on.

"She would," he said. "She'd have had me packed the morning Calvin asked. She'd have been at the top of the road waiting for me with the bags."

"I'll do it, then," said Alea. "I'll pack. You'll forget the good knife."

"I won't."

"You will," said Alea. "You always forget the good knife."

***

Later, much later, when the fire was embers and Kestra had been fed and put in the cabin's only bed, over her objections, and the others had gone up the road in a loud slow knot that took a long time to get anywhere, he sat on the end of the jetty with the king's letter in his lap.

*In person.*

He thought about the king at a table in a small hall, laughing so hard he put his spoon down. And then not laughing. And sitting there with his hand over his face until his porridge went cold.

Twelve years. Twelve years of not asking.

He thought about Halvard sitting down.

He thought about Valcrest. About the sand of the Lists, and the noise, and the paper horns that Chris said every child in the city was wearing, and thirty-five people from another world standing on it at the same time. About twenty locked doors at the bottom of twenty windows. About a boy called Hako who'd held his far angle for a year and a half without knowing whose it was. About Yan, who'd carried him in Apex and been carried by him across the red, and Mashi, who'd lost to him four hundred times on a community server and beaten him once. About Group 2, whom he'd never seen except as names in a channel. About Leaka, who'd teased him about a name he gave everything, and who'd find out, on a sandy floor in the south, that the name had grown up and was carrying half a black sword.

He thought: *I'm going to have to stand in front of every one of them, and say it.*

He waited to be afraid.

He'd been afraid for eight years. Of their faces. Of what they'd see. Of being the thing everyone was waiting for, and not being enough.

He sat there on the end of the jetty in the dark and waited for it to come, the way it always had.

It didn't come.

Somewhere far out on the lake, the pike turned over. The water went out in rings, slow and wide, all the way to the jetty, and broke softly against the posts. And Ars sat with the king's letter in his lap and his bare feet over the water, and listened to the lake, and the town behind him, and the fifteen threads he couldn't see but could feel, all pulled tight, all pointing at him from the beds of a small town at the top of the world.

He thought about one more. Thin and grey and thirty years old, going out of the world, not broken.

*I'm going south, Dad*, he thought. *First time in eight years. I'm going to stand on a sandy floor in front of thirty-five people and tell them all who I am. And then a king's going to ask me where I came from, and I'm going to tell him that too.*

*Wish me luck.*

He folded the letter, and put it inside his coat, against his chest. Over the place that wasn't on his skin.

Then he went in, and stepped over Gabe, and Deston, and Xav, asleep in their heap on the floor, and found the good knife on its hook by the door, and put it in his pack. So that Alea, in the morning, would have nothing to say.
