Nine hundred and thirty-one.

That was how many he had now. He'd had nine hundred and twelve at the balance, and he'd gone on, through the autumn, in Corvin's book, in the surveyor's neat columns, every evening at the long table with a candle: the carters who came in late with the last of the harvest, the two new babies on Tanners' Row, the trooper up from Ashby with the autumn draft who'd only been in Thornwall eight weeks and didn't know anybody's name yet himself. *Street, house, name, a word or two.* Nine hundred and thirty-one people in a grey stone town at the foot of the mountains.

Forty-one of them were in the long room.

He'd said every one of their names at the door at dusk, as they came in with their blankets over their arms. *Evening, Annet. Evening, Ivo*, on his right, because Ivo was deaf on the left. *Fenna.* Not *Aled*. Fenna didn't mention Aled, so he didn't. Old Linnet with her spindle. The Dacey sisters, holding hands already. Mother Ivey. Corporal Dunn, in his grey, who'd been called in the spring and stood on the long room floor every night since instead of on the wall, and minded. Iolo the cooper's boy, who'd let go at the balance and been barred by Hild's staff across the chest, and had come in tonight with his face set and said *I won't*. Joss, with his red scarf. Old Pruett, grumbling, last.

Forty-one. He'd counted them twice. Then the door had shut, and Azac had stood up in front of it with his arms held a little out from his sides, and the pale blue had come up round him, round, and stayed.

And Drew had stood in the middle of the long room with his chest bound under his jacket, two ribs on the left that had cracked at the balance and again on the millstream bridge and never had time to knit, and his blades on, both hands; and waited.

***

It came at full dark.

He'd known it would. Everybody had. *It likes the dark side.* He'd felt it coming all afternoon, down the valley, the way you feel a storm in your teeth, and at dusk he'd felt it stop at the north wall; and then, for a long time, nothing. Long enough for Annet to start baking. Long enough for Old Linnet's spindle to go. Long enough to think, the way you always think, *maybe not tonight*.

Then forty-one people stood up at once.

The benches went over. Old Linnet's spindle hit the floor and rolled. Annet stood up from her oven with flour to the elbow and her face going smooth, smiling, the way it went; and all round the room, by the hearths and the benches and the walls, forty-one people stood with their faces turned toward the north wall, and their eyes gone strange, and their hands coming up.

"*HANDS*," said Drew.

He'd told them all evening. At the door, ticking them in. *If you feel it, take the hand next to you and don't let go. Like the bow at the end of a show. That's all.* They'd heard him in the autumn. They'd heard him every night since. They took the hand next to them, all of them, without thinking, the way you'd catch a cup somebody had knocked off a table; and the chain was there, one long line of people round the long room from the far wall to the hearth, hand in hand in hand.

Annet was nearest. She came toward him across the floorboards with her eyes on the north wall and the whole chain behind her, leaning; and he put his open hands up, the blades on, palms out, the way he'd done it ten thousand times in the yard with Hild's staff coming at him; and she walked into them.

Into his chest.

*Come*, said the call.

***

It wasn't like the balance.

At the balance it had come in waves. Huge ones, a minute apart, each one with somebody's name in it, *there you are, oh, there you are*, so loud he'd felt them coming like a sea coming up a beach and had time, just, between them, to breathe. And it had lasted an hour. Then the king's hand on his back, and the forge door opening, and the whole of a ninth tier going into the turn; and the call had gone back up the road like a hammer and stopped.

The king wasn't here.

He was on the gatehouse roof. Drew could feel him there, through the floor, through the stone, the way you feel a fire in the next room: the door open, all of it, a forge with both doors wide, leaning on something at the foot of the north wall with everything he had. Holding it. Holding it off the tower. Drew had never felt anything hold like that. He knew, the way you know, that if the king took one hair of himself off that and put it anywhere else, it would be through.

So he didn't have the king.

And it didn't come in waves.

It came in like a tide. One word. Heavy and low and steady, over and over, without any gaps at all, through Annet's hands into his and into his chest and down; *come, come, come to this*. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. It just didn't stop. It leaned on forty-one people at once the way the tide leans on a sea wall, and the forty-one leaned on him, and he took it.

*Counter* took it. Turned it. Sent it back out off his open hands along exactly the line it had come in on, back through forty-one people, through the north wall, into whatever was standing at the foot of it in the dark.

And it came again before he'd finished sending it. And again.

He'd thought, at the balance, that it was like being on the wrong side of a door in a storm with somebody bigger than him on the other side who wasn't going to get tired. That had been a nice thought. That had been the warm-up.

This was like standing in the sea.

***

He didn't know how long. He never knew how long. Kei sat on the floor by the hearth with his back to the bricks and counted for him, out loud, every quarter of an hour by the candle Corvin had marked, so that he'd know; and Drew heard the numbers come and go and didn't hold on to any of them.

He held on to the names.

He said them. He didn't decide to. It just started coming out of him, under his breath, down the chain, the way he'd said them at the door: *Annet. Ivo. Mother Ivey. Bryn.* And he felt the chain hear. Not the people; the people were gone under, most of them, smiling. But something in them, somewhere, a long way down, under the call, heard its own name said in his voice and held on a little harder.

So he went on saying them.

*Perrin. Teo. Merrow. The Dacey girls. Old Linnet. Fenna.*

Somewhere in the second hour his nose went. He felt it come down over his mouth, hot, and tasted it, and didn't stop saying the names. Somewhere in the third his left eye went red at the edge again, where it had burst at the balance and never quite cleared, and the long room went pink on that side. The ribs went on the left. He felt them go, both of them, along the same lines, the way a crack in a pot goes along the same line every time you fire it; and he made a sound, and Annet, an inch from him, surfaced for a heartbeat and said *oh, love*, and went under again.

He didn't go down. He'd gone down at the balance, to his knees. He didn't, tonight. He didn't know why. He thought it was because nobody was going to put a hand on his back, and he knew it.

***

Tiger came in every hour.

That was the order. *Tiger on his legs, between all of them, carrying.* He went from the gate to the square to the tower to the Bell to the long room and back, round and round the town in the dark at a speed nobody else on the wall could follow, with messages in his head and his ears flat; and every hour, more or less, the long room door opened past Azac's shoulder and he came through it.

The first time, Drew didn't see him. He felt him. A hand, flat, between his shoulder blades, on his back. And the call, coming in through the chain, went into Tiger too, a little, through the hand; Drew felt it, and felt *Counter* take that too, and turn it, and send it back.

He heard Tiger breathe out behind him. A long breath. Like a man putting down something heavy.

"It's on me out there," said Tiger, very low, in his ear. "In the lanes. It knows my name. It says it all the time. *There you are.*" His hand stayed flat. "I can feel it pulling every step. Toward the wall. And I come in here and put my hand on you and it comes off." He was quiet. "Like getting out of a river."

"Then come in," said Drew, between the names. "Every time. Before you go out again."

"I am."

"Every time."

"I *said*." The hand pressed. "Hild says the doors went. The north gate. She's in the arch. She's holding. The king's holding. Petra's in the square; there's some got in, through the grating, by the laundry, they're in the lanes, walking up toward the square; they walked into her wall and they're leaning on it." He stopped. "And a soldier's dead."

Drew didn't stop saying the names. It was the only thing he could do with his mouth that was any use.

"Huw," said Tiger. "Huw Bevan. At the grating. The dog came up through the water. Kestra took its arm off and it went back."

*Huw Bevan. Tanners' Row, the end house with the blue door. Big ears. Carries everything two at a time. Da in the fourth ring, from Coldwater; doesn't talk about it; ask about the chain.* He'd written it in Corvin's book in the summer. Huw had told him about holding the end of Fenwick's chain, the summer he was eleven, all eleven miles of the valley, and Fenwick shouting *pull it straight, lad, it's not a washing line*, and Drew had laughed, and Huw had gone red to the tips of the ears.

"Okay," said Drew.

"It's not okay."

"No," said Drew. "Go. Come back."

The hand went. The door opened past Azac's shoulder and shut. And the call came in, and in, and he said *Annet, Ivo, Bryn* and didn't say *Huw*, because Huw wasn't in the chain, and the chain needed its own names.

He'd say it later. He'd say all of them later.

***

The second time Tiger came in, there was a smell on him.

Drew knew it. He'd smelled it once, at Coldwater, the first year, on Hild's sword when she came down off the sheep path; on Kei's coat; in the bracken. Not a smell, quite. Something underneath a smell, the way the circle in the hut had been something underneath a floor. Wet clay and something burnt.

"They're in the lanes," said Tiger. His hand on Drew's back was shaking. "Not the neighbours. The other things. The dogs. The ones like at Coldwater. With the mouths." He swallowed. "Two of them. They came in under the water at the grating with the neighbours, before Kestra got the cart up. Low. Nobody saw them. They went up the lanes at the east end. Away from the square. Away from Petra."

Drew said *Merrow, Teo*, and waited.

"The laundry," said Tiger. "The Callows. Both of them. He's eighty. She was getting him to the Bell." His voice went. "And the Pask boy. From the dye-yard. He went out to look. He's twelve. He went out the back of the dye-yard to look at the neighbours and one of them was in the lane."

*Callow, Tom and Bet, the laundry, by the grating. He's deaf as a post and proud of it; she does the talking. Davey Pask, the dye-yard, the youngest, wants to be a soldier, asks everybody how tall they are.*

He'd asked Drew how tall he was. In the summer. And then stood back to back with him in the dye-yard to see, and Drew had stood very straight so the boy could see how far there was to go.

"Okay," said Drew. His voice had gone somewhere. He found it. "Who's on them?"

"Xav got one." Tiger was breathing fast. "At the end of Tanners' Row. It came at him out of the alley and he wasn't there and then he was behind it and his sword was in its neck. I saw it. *Now*, he says. He just says *now* and it's done." A breath. "And Rui Xiang got the other. In the dye-yard. Nobody saw him. It was standing over—" He stopped. "It was standing in the yard and then it fell over with a knife in the back of its head and Rui Xiang was standing behind it and nobody'd seen him come. Not even it." Tiger's hand pressed harder. "He's being sick in the dye-yard now. He keeps saying *sorry*. To the dog."

Drew stood in the sea with forty-one people leaning on him and his ribs gone and the names coming out of his mouth, and thought about a slight polite young man being sick in a dye-yard, saying *sorry* to a thing with a mouth for a head, standing over a boy who'd wanted to be a soldier.

"Tell him," said Drew, "it's okay to be sick."

"I told him."

"Tell him I said."

"Okay." The hand stayed a moment longer. "Drew. It's not stopping. The neighbours. They're not stopping. They just keep leaning. They don't get tired. Hild's wall gives and throws them and they come back. At a walk. Every time. Like they've got all night."

"They have," said Drew.

Tiger didn't say anything.

"Go," said Drew. "Come back."

***

He heard her let go in the fifth hour.

*Listen for the one who lets go*, Hild had said. At the end of the summer, on the mounting block, with the staff across her knees. *If you hold thirty and it hits all thirty, it'll find the weakest hand. It always does. That's what a line is: the weakest hand in it. Listen for the one who lets go. You'll hear it a heartbeat before it happens.*

He heard it. A heartbeat before.

A hand, in the chain, about two-thirds of the way along, by the second hearth, going loose in another hand. Not pulling. Not tearing. Going *loose*. The way your fingers go loose on a cup when you're falling asleep. And he knew whose. He'd have known it anywhere; he'd said her name at the door.

*Fenna.*

The call had found her. Not her name; it had stopped saying names ten days ago, the long room said, and it hadn't said one since except Rui Xiang's. It had found something else. He didn't know what. Something under her name, in the place where you keep what you don't mention. *Don't mention Aled unless she does.* Something in the tide, coming in through forty people into her, had mentioned Aled.

Her hand went loose in Old Linnet's.

He had a heartbeat.

He could let go. One hand. The left; Annet was on the right. Let go of the end of the chain, and go across the room, and get Fenna. Take her hand himself. Turn it off her. He could do that. He'd done it for Joss at the balance, through Tiger's shoulder, and for Iolo, nearly.

And the chain would open.

The left hand was holding twenty people. If he let it go, the call would come in through twenty people with nothing at the end to turn it, and twenty people would stand up and walk to the north wall, and Azac's ward would hold them for a while, and then it wouldn't. And he'd have Fenna.

He had a heartbeat, and he spent it, and he didn't let go.

Fenna's hand came out of Old Linnet's.

Old Linnet, under, smiling, felt it go and closed her own hand on nothing and reached, without looking, for the next hand along, Perrin's, and found it; and the chain closed behind the gap, the way water closes behind a stone you've lifted out, and was whole. Forty.

And Fenna walked.

Across the long room, through the middle of it, between the line of people holding hands, at a walk. A woman of thirty with her hair tied up in a cloth and her sleeves pushed up, the way she did the shirts. Smiling. With her hands coming up, flat, palms out, the way the neighbours held theirs on the wall. Past the hearth. Past Kei, who got up off the floor and caught her arm and was thrown off it, gently, as if she'd brushed off a moth, and sat down hard against the bricks.

To the door.

Azac's ward was across it. Pale blue, round, the way Petra had shown him on the downs. Fenna walked into it and stopped. And leaned, with her flat hands on the blue, the way they leaned on the north wall.

"I'm sorry," said Azac. His voice cracked. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, Fenna, I'm sorry, you can't—"

The ward bowed in round her hands. Azac's arms shook.

She didn't push harder. She didn't need to. She just leaned, and smiled, and the ward bowed, and Azac stood there with his arms out and his face crumpling, saying *sorry*; and Drew, in the middle of the long room with forty people on his hands, said, as loud as he could, which wasn't very loud: "*Malcolm.*"

Malcolm was by the side door with his book. He put the book down. He came across the floor and took hold of Fenna from behind, round the waist, both arms, the way you'd catch somebody falling off a wall, and pulled.

She came off the ward. She didn't fight. She turned round in Malcolm's arms, smiling, and put her flat hands on his chest, and pushed him; once; not hard. And Malcolm went across the floorboards backwards on his heels three paces and sat down.

And she went out of the side door into the yard.

Into the dark.

Nobody went after her. There was nobody to go. Malcolm was on the floor. Azac was holding the door. Kei was against the bricks with his arm across his chest. Drew had forty people on his hands and the sea coming in, and he stood there and listened to her footsteps go across the frosted stones of the yard and out of the yard gate into the lane, at a walk, at a market-day pace, toward the east end of the town, toward the laundry, toward the grating where she did the shirts.

*Fenna. Shirts. Widow.*

He said her name. Out loud. The way he'd been saying it all night, under his breath, down the chain. It didn't go down the chain any more. It went out of the side door after her into the dark, and he didn't know if it reached.

Then he went on with the others. *Annet. Ivo. Old Linnet. Perrin.* Because they were still there, and they needed theirs.

***

Tiger came in an hour later and didn't put his hand on Drew's back.

Drew felt the door open past Azac's shoulder. He felt Tiger come in. He felt him stop, just inside, the way Tiger never stopped anywhere.

"Tiger."

Nothing.

"Tiger. Hand."

The hand came. Flat, between his shoulders. And the call that was on Tiger came off through it, and *Counter* turned it and sent it back, and Drew heard Tiger breathe out; and the breath went on going out, longer than a breath, and turned into something else.

"Fenna," said Tiger.

Drew waited.

"In the lane. By the laundry. Where the Callows were." Tiger's voice was very flat. Tiger's voice was never flat. "She was walking up from the grating end. Toward the wall. With her hands out. Like they do. And there was a soldier. From the reserve. One of the autumn lot, from Ashby." He stopped. "He'd just seen the dog. The one Rui Xiang got. He'd just seen what it did in the laundry. And he was standing in the lane on his own with his sword out, and a woman came out of the dark at him with her hands up flat like the neighbours do, smiling, and he—"

He stopped.

"He didn't know her," said Tiger. "He's been here eight weeks. He didn't know her face. He thought she was one of them." The hand on Drew's back closed into a fist, against his jacket, and opened again. "The order says don't kill them. He knows it says that. He said it to me. He was sitting in the laundry door with his sword on his knees and he kept saying it, *don't kill them if you can help it, I know, I know*. He said he couldn't help it. He said she came out of the dark. He said she was *smiling*."

Drew stood in the middle of the long room with forty people on his hands.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He said the names. *Annet. Ivo. Mother Ivey. Bryn. Perrin. Teo. Merrow. The Dacey girls. Old Linnet. Corporal Dunn. Iolo. Joss.* He went down the chain, all forty, the way he had all night. When he got to the place by the second hearth where Fenna had been, he didn't say anything, and went on.

"What's his name," said Drew. "The soldier."

"I don't know."

"Find out."

"Drew—"

"Find out," said Drew. "Please. Before you go back out. Ask Brannoch. Ask anybody." He swallowed blood. "I need to know his name."

Tiger's hand stayed on his back for a long moment.

"Okay," he said.

***

It went back at dawn.

He felt it go the way you feel a tide turn. Not all at once. The water doesn't go out all at once; it just stops coming in, and there's a moment when it's neither, and then you notice your feet are drier. The call didn't stop. It went on, *come, come*, through forty people into his hands. But a little less. And then a little less. And somewhere far off, through the stone, through the floor, he felt the king on the gatehouse roof go from holding to standing, the way a smith goes from holding the tongs to just standing at the anvil when the striker stops.

And the forty sat down.

Not all at once. One at a time, the way people sit down at the end of a long day. Annet first. She came up out of it a foot from his face and looked at him, and her face was wet, and she wasn't smiling; and she sat down on the floorboards in front of him with her floury hands in her lap and said *oh, love, oh, your face*. Then Ivo. Then the Dacey girls, back to back, still holding hands. Old Linnet, reaching for her spindle. Down the line.

He stood there with his hands up until the last one sat.

Then he found he was sitting too. On the floor. With his back against something; the bench. And his hands still up in front of him, open, with the blades on, shaking, and he couldn't make them close.

Kei was in front of him. Holding his wrists, one in each hand, the way you'd hold a bird that had flown into a window. Hisui was round Kei's neck, looking at him with the same face as Kei.

"Two," said Kei. "Left. Both through, this time. Not cracked; through." His thumbs moved on Drew's wrists. "Your eye's worse. Your nose is the same. Your hands won't close because there's nothing left in them." He swallowed. "You stood all night. You didn't kneel once. Hisui says you're the stupidest person he's ever met and he's met me."

"Forty," said Drew.

"Forty," said Kei.

"Forty-one," said Drew. "There were forty-one."

Kei didn't say anything. He held Drew's wrists.

Through the window, the sky over the roofs had gone grey. Somewhere outside, at the north wall, he could hear the soldiers. Not shouting. Talking, low, the way men talk on a wall at the end of a long watch. And under it, far off, through the stone, a sound he didn't know at first; and then knew. Hundreds of people walking. Away. At a market-day pace. Back across the frosted fields to the edge of the valley, where they'd stand all day in the cold with their faces turned toward the town, the way Hyvred said they would, and wait for dusk.

It had all winter.

***

Tiger came in at full light with the name.

He came and sat down on the floor beside Drew, against the bench, close, with his shoulder against Drew's broken side; and Drew didn't say *ow*, though he thought it so hard Tiger probably heard. Tiger's tail came round both of them.

"Alder," said Tiger. "Wyn Alder. From Ashby. He's nineteen. He's sitting in the laundry door. He won't go in to the Bell. Brannoch's taken his sword off him." He was quiet. "He keeps asking what her name was. Nobody'll tell him. They think it'll make it worse."

Drew sat there for a while.

Then he got up.

It took a long time. Kei said *no* and then didn't say anything, and got under his arm on the left, the side that was broken, and Tiger got under the right; and they went out of the long room, the three of them, past Azac, who was sitting on the step outside the door with his head in his hands and the pale blue gone, and Malcolm beside him with his book shut on his knees, not writing; and across the yard, and out of the yard gate, into the lane.

East. Toward the laundry. Down the lane in the grey light, where the frost was on the cobbles and on the doorsteps and on a dark place by the laundry wall that somebody had thrown a sack over. Past it. To the laundry door.

There was a boy sitting in it. A tall thin boy in Western Watch grey with his hands empty on his knees, looking at nothing, at the sack. Nineteen. A face Drew had seen on the wall for eight weeks and never put a name to, because the boy had only been in Thornwall eight weeks and Drew hadn't got to Ashby yet in the book.

Drew sat down beside him on the step. Slowly. Kei let him down.

The boy looked at him. At his face. At the eye and the nose and the blood down his front and the bound chest and the hands on his knees, open, that wouldn't close.

"Her name was Fenna," said Drew.

The boy didn't move.

"Fenna Rees. From the laundry. She did shirts. She did everybody's shirts, the whole garrison; she did yours, probably, you'd have had one back from her with the cuff mended and not known." He breathed. It hurt. "She was thirty. She was a widow. Her husband was called Aled; he was a carter; he went off the ridge road in a snowstorm two winters ago with a load of slate. She didn't talk about him. You didn't mention him unless she did." He looked at the sack by the wall. "She sang when she did the shirts. Badly. You could hear her from the guardhouse. Old songs, from before the kingdom, that nobody else knows the words to any more."

The boy's face had started to come apart.

"You didn't know her," said Drew. "That's not your fault. You've been here eight weeks. Nobody'd told you." He held the boy's eyes. "So I'm telling you. Fenna Rees. Shirts. Sang. Didn't mention Aled. That's her name. You carry it now. Not the other thing. That's not hers. This is."

The boy said *Fenna*.

Then he put his face in his hands on the laundry step and cried the way people cry when they've been holding it all night, with his whole body; and Drew sat beside him, and didn't put an arm round him, because his arms didn't work, and leaned on him instead, a little, shoulder to shoulder, the way you lean on somebody in a long room when there's nothing else to hold.

Behind them, Tiger sat on the cobbles with his back against the laundry wall and his tail round his knees. Kei stood. Nobody said anything.

Up the hill, from the tower, very small, a gold light went up off the roof into the grey, and hung there over Thornwall in the dawn like a lamp nobody had to hold. John. Not sending anything. Just there.

*Fenna*, Drew thought. *Huw. Tom Callow. Bet Callow. Davey Pask.*

Five. The first night.

He said them over in his head, all five, in order, with the word or two that went with each one, the way he'd said the forty all night down the chain. He'd say them again tonight. Every night. He'd say them until somebody wrote them on a stone, and then he'd go and stand in front of the stone and say them again, so that whoever was in charge of stones knew they were names and not just letters.

*Nine hundred and thirty-one*, he thought. And then, because he was who he was, and it was true, and somebody had to keep the book: *nine hundred and twenty-six.*
