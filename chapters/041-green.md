It was a known fact, in Group 3, that Maigo was the first to speak.

In any room. In any silence. If a game went quiet after a bad round, Maigo would fill it. If a call went quiet because someone had said something sad, Maigo would fill it. If five strangers from another world stood in a cold stone hall under a painted ceiling, staring at a grey man who'd just told them they couldn't go home, Maigo would be the one who said, "Okay, but do we get lunch?"

He couldn't help it. He'd tried. Silence felt to him like a held breath, and he'd never been able to stand there holding his.

So it was strange, that spring, how much of it he spent quiet.

***

It started in the ground.

He'd felt the Redmarch from the first day. They all had, in their way: Yan felt the dead, Wendy felt cold, Salty felt nothing at all and said so loudly. But Maigo, who was a tree now, or something like one, with bark for skin and two small branching antlers poking out through the hood of his hoodie and a pair of pixelated sunglasses that had somehow come through the floor with him, had felt it like a bruise. Standing barefoot on the red earth outside Cinderhold's walls was like standing on a sore tooth. The ground *hurt*. It had been hurt, a long time ago, so badly that it had never stopped, and every step he took on it, he felt it flinch.

All winter he'd stayed off it as much as he could. He'd trained inside the walls, on the patch of grey courtyard by the well, coaxing grass out of the cracks between the flagstones: a blade, two blades, a little tuft that lasted a day before it went brown. *Three percent a week*, Yan said, looking at his window. Slow. Fine.

And then, sometime in the first weeks of spring, something changed.

He noticed it at night first. He'd wake in the small hours in the barracks with an odd restless feeling in his arms and legs, like pins and needles, like his whole body had fallen asleep and was waking up. He'd lie there in the dark listening to Salty snore and Niko talk in his sleep, and feel it rising. Slow. Sweet. Up from his feet, through his legs, into his chest and out along his arms to the tips of his fingers, and up into the little antlers on his head.

He didn't know what it was for nearly a week. Then one morning he looked in the bucket of water by the well, and saw his own reflection, and stopped.

There were leaves on his antlers.

Not the tiny dry ones he'd had all winter. *New* ones. Small and bright and absurdly green, uncurling from the tips of the branches like fingers opening.

"Sap," said Wendy, when he showed them, at breakfast. She'd looked at the leaves for a long time, with her chin on her hand. "It's spring. You're a tree. Your sap's rising."

"That's not—" Maigo started, and stopped. "Is that a thing?"

"I don't know," said Wendy. "You're the tree."

He opened his window. It said forty-four. It had said thirty-one at the end of winter.

***

After that it went very fast.

Forty-four. Fifty-one. Fifty-eight. Every few days he'd open his window and it would have jumped again, three, four, five percent, the way nobody else's ever did. Yan said it was spring. Aldous said it was spring. Rhea, who didn't really understand mage tiers and didn't pretend to, said it was cheating and that she was going to report him to somebody.

He didn't feel like he was cheating. He felt like he was waking up.

He could feel the whole fort, now. Not just the grass in the cracks. The old ivy on the south wall that everyone thought was dead. The roots of the one stubborn rowan tree by the chapel door, going down and down and down, past the foundations, into the cold. The seeds in the cook's garden that hadn't come up yet, sleeping in the dark like held breaths. The mice in the granary. The swallows that had come back to the eaves. Cinderhold had been a heartbeat, in the autumn: one slow pulse, all of it at once. Now it was a hundred heartbeats, all different, all getting louder.

And out past the walls, the Redmarch. Still flinching. Still hurt. But every morning now he'd go and stand at the east gate with his bare feet on the threshold, just at the edge of the red, under the horns that hung over the gate, and feel it.

There was something under the hurt, he thought. He couldn't find it yet. But something.

Sixty-five. Seventy-two.

"You're going to be next," said Yan one evening, on the wall, quietly. Yan's own window said thirty-four. He didn't sound jealous. He never did. "After Thea. You're going to be the second."

"Don't say that," said Maigo. "That's a jinx. You can't say that. Now it won't happen."

"It'll happen," said Yan. He was looking at Maigo with his pale calm eyes, the way he looked at people now: not at their faces, but at something just behind them. Suzu was coiled round his neck, white against his dark robes. "I can see your candle. It's not a candle anymore. It's..." He paused, looking for the word. "It's green."

***

The letters came on a grey wet afternoon in the middle of the month, and they came all at once.

Four weeks of nothing. They'd stopped talking about it, the way you stop talking about a toothache. Yan had written to Group 7 every fortnight and had nothing back. Maigo had written to Hyvred and Kei and Tiger, at Thornwall, and had nothing back. They'd all started to think the same thing, and none of them had said it. *Something's happened. Something's happened to them and nobody's told us.*

Then a big quiet rider in the king's colours, one of the handful Kestra's friend Aubrey had picked, came through the west gate with a satchel that he wouldn't let out of his sight, not even to the gate sergeant, and put it into Yan's hands personally. And inside it there were nineteen letters.

They read them in the chapel, the five of them and Aldous and Rhea, sitting on the pews with the rain on the roof. Late letters, most of them. Weeks late. The seals broken and pressed back. Letters from Group 7, from Chris, every one of them, one a week, regular as a clock. Letters from Thornwall. And on top, in a packet of their own, thirty narrow strips of paper covered in tiny coiled blue-black writing, and a letter from Cursiffe marked *No. 1*.

Yan read that one aloud. About the courier clerk with the blue hands. About the steward with the heron ring, and the box under the floor in the Lantern Quarter, and the list of scholars with lines through their names. *Could not read. Could not read. Keep looking.*

Then he read the rest.

He read Chris's letter about the junior lists, and the sword inside a sword, and Newish screaming *down*. His voice didn't change. It never did. But his hand, holding the page, had gone very still.

Then he read Tiger's.

When he finished, nobody said anything for a long time. The rain came down on the chapel roof. Niko had his head in his hands. Salty was staring at the floor with his jaw set. Wendy had moved closer to Salty without seeming to, the way she always did when something was wrong, until her shoulder was against his.

"*Someone wearing someone,*" said Yan quietly. "Hyvred said it in the autumn. In Coldwater." He folded Tiger's letter very carefully. "I felt one. Out there. On the Redmarch. The cold one that counts the towers." He looked out of the chapel door, at the rain, east. "I've been telling myself for six months it was the same thing. Two sides. One shape."

"It knew about the roof," said Maigo. His voice came out small. "It knew what Tiger said in the dark."

Nobody answered.

Maigo sat in the cold chapel with the rain coming down and thought about Tiger, who he'd brought into all of this, really. Tiger and Hyvred and Drew: he'd met all three of them first, back on Earth, and brought them into the group, and then they'd met Ice through him, and then Ice had died. And now they were all here, on the other side of the kingdom from him, and something with a dead boy's face had walked up to their gate and asked for them by name.

He didn't fill the silence. He didn't know how.

***

That night he couldn't sleep, so he went out to the east gate.

The rain had stopped. The sky had cleared, cold and black and full of stars. The sentry on the gatehouse knew him, and nodded, and let him through the postern without a word; the soldiers of Cinderhold had got used to the little tree man going out to stand on the red at strange hours. He walked out past the first row of markers in the dark, barefoot, until he was standing among the stones. Aldous's thousand markers, in their long, neat, terrible rows, one for every soldier he'd buried.

The ground flinched under his feet. It always did.

Maigo stood there and let it.

He thought about Tiger. He thought about the thing at the gate. He thought about Chris's letter, and Newish screaming *down*, and Ina dropping flat on the sand. He thought about all of them, scattered across the kingdom, writing letters into a dark that somebody had been stealing from, for weeks, while they all sat alone thinking nobody was writing back.

And he thought about Ice.

He didn't let himself, mostly. It wasn't like it was for Yan, or Salty, who'd been closest. Maigo and Ice had been banter, mostly. Callouts and insults and the specific joy of two people who were both too loud in the same voice channel. Ice had called him Maigotron. Nobody else ever had. It had started in a CS match, years ago, when Maigo had come out of nowhere with a four-kill round on a pistol and Ice had screamed down the mic, *MAIGOTRON. THE MACHINE. THE TERMINATOR*, so loudly that Yan had taken off his headphones. After that it was just his name. *Maigotron, rotate. Maigotron, what are you doing. Maigotron, I'm so proud of you, never do that again.*

They'd never met. Not once. They'd always said they would, sometime, the way you say it. And then one morning Yan had called, at the wrong time of day, in the wrong voice, and Maigo had known before he picked up.

He stood in the dark among the markers, with his bare feet on the hurt ground, and missed his friend so much that his chest ached, the way it hadn't let itself ache in a long time.

And under his feet, the ground stopped flinching.

***

He felt it go still.

Not all of it. Just the bit under him. Just a little circle, a pace across, with him in the middle. The flinch went out of it, the way a muscle goes slack when someone stops pressing on a bruise. And under the hurt, where he'd been feeling for it all spring, he found what he'd been looking for.

It was so small. A thread. A thin, stubborn, living warmth, far down in the red earth, running away from him into the dark. East, toward the grave. Like a root. Like something that had been planted a long time ago and had been holding on, alone, with everything it had, ever since.

He knelt.

He didn't decide to. He just found himself on his knees in the cold red earth among the markers, with his hands flat on the ground. And he felt for the gap. The place Thea had written about. *One small line, in its language, not yours.* He'd been looking for it all spring, in the courtyard, in the cracks between the flagstones, and never found it.

It was here. It had been here all along. Right under the hurt.

He didn't have words. He didn't need them. He was a tree. He wrote into the gap the only thing a tree has ever really known how to say, all the way down from his hands into the dark.

*Stay.*

***

They found him at dawn.

The sentry had raised the alarm when the sky got light and the little tree man still hadn't come back through the postern. Yan came first, running, with Suzu tight around his neck, and then Salty with his sword, and Wendy with her bow, and Rhea, and Niko; and Aldous last of all, in his old chaplain's coat, out of breath, out past the first row of markers and the second and the third, into the grey light.

Maigo was sitting cross-legged in the red earth among the markers.

In front of him, in the ground, there was a tree.

A small one. A sapling, no taller than his knee. A thin pale stem, and a few small branches, and on the branches, uncurling in the first light, a scatter of bright new leaves so green that they looked like the only colour in the whole of the Redmarch. Its roots went down into the red, and the red, all around it, in a ring about a pace across, wasn't red anymore. It was brown. Ordinary brown earth. Dark and soft and damp from the rain.

Maigo looked up at them. His sunglasses were pushed up on his antlers. His glowing eyes were very bright.

"Hi," he said. "I've been sitting here for ages. I didn't want to leave it."

Nobody said anything. For once, Maigo didn't fill the silence. He just sat there, and let them look.

Aldous came forward, very slowly, between the markers. He stood over the little tree for a long time. Then he got down on his knees in the red earth, in his old coat, and reached out one hand, and touched one of the leaves with the tip of his finger, as if he thought it might not be real.

It was real. It moved under his finger, the way a leaf does.

Aldous began to cry.

He didn't make any noise. He just knelt there, in the grey dawn, among the stones he'd set over more than a thousand boys and men he'd buried with his own hands, with his finger on a green leaf, and tears running down into his beard.

"Seven years," he said at last. His voice was barely there. "I've walked this ground for seven years. Every week. Every one of these. I set every one of these." He didn't take his finger off the leaf. "Nothing grows here. Nothing. Not grass. Not moss. Not a weed. You can plant it and water it and it dies in a day." He swallowed. "Only her flowers. The ones on the grave. I always thought..." He stopped.

"Thought what?" said Maigo softly.

"I always thought that was a mercy someone had asked for," said Aldous. "For her. Just for her."

***

Maigo opened his window, sitting there in the red with the tree in front of him and everyone watching.

[ Maigo · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Root ]

He looked at it for a long time.

"Spark," said Yan, reading it over his shoulder. His deep slow voice had gone slightly unsteady. "You did it."

"I told you not to say it," said Maigo. "I told you it was a jinx."

"It wasn't."

"No," Maigo agreed. "It wasn't." And then, quite suddenly, he was laughing, and crying too, both at once, and Yan was crouched beside him in the red earth with his arm round his shoulders, and Salty was shouting, and Rhea was shouting louder than Salty out of sheer competitiveness, and Niko had sat down very hard on the ground and was saying "*the second, the second one, Maigo's the second*" over and over like he couldn't believe it, and Wendy was standing a little way back with her bow, smiling. Actually smiling. Which nobody ever saw.

"Root," said Wendy. "That's your skill."

"Yeah."

"What does it do?"

Maigo looked at the little tree. At the ring of brown earth around it, a pace across, in the middle of all that red.

"I think," he said slowly, "it makes things stay."

***

He couldn't leave it there.

He knew it as soon as he'd thought about it. Out here among the markers, on its own, in the middle of the Redmarch, the little tree was the only living thing for a mile in any direction. He could feel it already: the red pressing in at the edge of the brown, the hurt, waiting. The ring of soft earth he'd made would hold for a while. A season. Maybe two. But he couldn't come out and sit with it every night for the rest of his life, and without him, sooner or later, the red would win.

It needed somewhere it could stay. Somewhere it wouldn't be alone.

"The grave," he said.

They all looked at him.

"The flowers," said Maigo. "On the healer's grave. They grow. Every spring. Aldous said. Salty saw them; they're coming back right now, in a ring." He looked at the tree. "I felt it last night. Before. There's something under the ground out there. A thread. A root. Like something warm somebody left behind. Running all the way out to the grave." He swallowed. "That's why the flowers grow. Something's keeping them. Something's been keeping them all this time."

Aldous was looking at him very strangely.

"So I want to plant it there," said Maigo. "Inside the ring. With the flowers." He looked down at the leaves, very bright in the grey light. "So she isn't alone out here."

***

They carried it out that afternoon, all seven of them.

Maigo dug it up himself, with his hands, very carefully, the way you'd lift a sleeping baby. The roots came up out of the soft brown ring with a clump of earth around them, and he cradled it against his chest in the front pocket of his hoodie, with the leaves poking out at the top, and walked.

It was a long way. It always was. East from the markers, across the open red, with Rhea in front and Salty behind and Wendy with an arrow on the string, watching the horizon for the tall thin bent shapes that sometimes came and counted. None came. The red stretched out around them under the grey sky, flat and empty and hurt, and Maigo walked through it barefoot with the tree in his pocket and felt every step.

And then, at last, the grave.

He'd never been out this far. Only Salty had, and Aldous. He'd thought it would be something bigger. It wasn't. It was a low cairn of grey stones, in the middle of nowhere, in the middle of the red. And around it, in a ring, the flowers: white and blue, small and low, the buds just opening. The only colour for a mile. And in the middle of the cairn, a hole in the ground, full of rainwater, where the black sword had stood for seven years.

Maigo stood at the edge of the ring and felt it.

The thread. The warm stubborn root he'd felt last night, far down under the markers. It came here. It ran all the way out across the Redmarch, under the hurt, and it came here, and went down, under the cairn, and *held*. He could feel the flowers drinking from it. He could feel them growing because of it, every spring, against all the red in the world.

It felt like a hand. He didn't know how else to say it. Like a hand that had been laid on the ground here a long time ago, very gently, by somebody who didn't want to let go, and had never quite been lifted.

"Here," he said, very quietly.

He knelt inside the ring of flowers, beside the cairn, and dug a hole with his hands, and set the little tree in it, and pressed the earth down around its roots. Then he sat back on his heels and laid his palms flat on the ground on either side of it.

*Stay.*

The red around the flowers went brown. Not far. A hand's width. But it went. And the tree, the little pale sapling with its scatter of bright leaves, stood up a little straighter in the grey wind, the way plants do when they find water.

***

The others stood back, outside the ring, and let him have a moment. They were good like that. Maigo knelt by the cairn for a while in the quiet, with his hands on the earth, and didn't fill the silence.

Then, because he was Maigo, and because it felt rude not to, he did.

"Hi," he said to the grave. "Sorry. I don't know your name. They call you the healer." He looked at the cairn. "I'm Maigo. I'm a tree, kind of. It's a long story. I brought you a tree. I hope that's okay." He paused. "I know you've got flowers. They're really nice flowers. But I thought you might like something taller. Something that'll still be here in a long time. When we're... you know." He swallowed. "When we're wherever we end up."

The wind blew across the red. The flowers moved.

"Salty asked who you were," said Maigo. "In the spring. He came out on his own. Wendy told him off." He laughed, a little shakily. "She said you should ask whoever's there. She said somebody always is, at a grave." He looked at the flowers, and the hole full of rain, and the little tree. "So, I don't know. Whoever's there. Whoever planted these. Whoever's been keeping them." He touched the earth. "I'm not taking your spot. I promise. I just didn't want her to be on her own."

He sat there a moment longer. Then he stood up, and brushed the earth off his knees, and pulled his sunglasses back down over his eyes.

"Okay," he said, turning round to the others, much too loudly. "Okay. Who's hungry? I'm *starving*. Is anyone else starving? Rhea, you owe me two silver from the arm-wrestling, by the way, don't think I forgot."

"Thirty to twenty-eight," said Salty. "It was a fix."

"It was not a fix."

"It was a *bit* of a fix," said Rhea.

And they walked back across the red together, toward Cinderhold, with the horns over the gate catching the last of the light. Maigo walked in the middle, barefoot, talking the whole way, because it was easier than not.

He didn't look back. He didn't need to. He could feel it behind him, out there in the middle of the Redmarch, the whole way home: one small green thing, standing in a ring of flowers beside a grave, holding on.

***

They wrote that night, all of them. Letters numbered *No. 1* and sealed with Cursiffe's strips, to every group, with everything. The theft and the tree. Maigo's Spark. Yan wrote to Chris about the sword inside a sword, and to Kei and Hyvred about Ossie, and what he'd felt on the Redmarch, and how it was all one shape.

Their windows, at the end of the letter, in a column, the way they'd started doing in the winter. Yan 34. Salty 33. Wendy 32. Niko 12 and 12, *still trying to write two lines at once, still not dead*. And at the top, in Maigo's big loopy writing:

*MAIGO: TIER 1. SPARK. SKILL: ROOT. I PLANTED A TREE ON THE REDMARCH AND IT STAYED. TELL THEA I SAID HI AND THAT I'M SECOND, AND THAT SECOND IS JUST FIRST BUT LATER.*

In the last week of summer, when the eighth anniversary came round, a man would walk out alone across the Redmarch to sit with her, the way he did every year. And he would find, inside the ring of flowers he had planted with his own hands, a young tree that hadn't been there before, standing beside her in the wind.

But that was months away. And Maigo, asleep in the barracks at Cinderhold with new leaves on his antlers, didn't know it yet.
