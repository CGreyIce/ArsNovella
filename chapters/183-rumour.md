The songs got to Cinderhold before the snow did, which Teague said was always the way.

"Songs travel faster than wagons," he said, on the gatehouse, with his bad arm tucked inside his coat against the cold and his breath going up white. "Faster than riders, half the time. A song can go down a road in a week that a cart takes a month to. Nobody carries it. It just goes." He spat over the parapet into the red. "And it's always wrong when it gets here."

Wendy, beside him on the wall with her bow across her back and her silent headphones round her neck, didn't say anything.

She agreed with him. She'd agreed with him about most things since the summer. Teague was the only other person on the wall who didn't believe anything until he'd seen it twice, and then only on alternate days. They'd stood watches together all autumn. She liked him a great deal. He'd called her *the archer*, for a month, and then *Wendy*, and then, once, when she'd put an arrow through a crow on the far side of the ditch at a hundred and twenty paces without looking at it properly, *lass*, which she'd decided to take as a compliment.

"What song?" she said.

Teague looked at her sideways.

"You'll hear it," he said. "The relief's coming in tonight. Sixty of them, from Ashby, before the downs close. And every one of them will have learned it on the road." He looked south, at the long grey line of the downs under a low grey sky with snow in it. "I heard it from the carter who came in ahead of them. Last night. In the kitchen." His mouth did something at one corner. "I'm not going to tell you. I want to see your face."

***

The relief came in at dusk.

Wendy watched it from the wall. Everybody did. A column coming in off the downs in the last of the light, out of the south, with the first flakes of the snow just starting to come down round it: two wagons, a sergeant on a brown horse, and sixty young men and women in new grey coats from the military school at Ashby walking in a long double line with their packs on their backs and their breath going up. Recruits. Fresh, for the winter, the way they came every year before the snow shut the downs, to replace the ones who'd done their year and were going home.

Sixty faces, looking up at the wall.

Wendy had been one of those faces once. Not here. Not like this. But she'd come up a road in a column, more than two years ago, on a wagon, with four people from another world and a chaplain with a book, and looked up at this wall for the first time, and thought: *this is the worst place I have ever seen*. She'd been right. She still thought it was. She'd just stopped minding.

She watched the sixty come up the last of the road to the east gate and stop under it, and the gate open, and the old ones on the gatehouse look down at them the way old soldiers look at new ones, with a kind of patient, mournful affection. And one of the sixty, near the front, a tall straight young man with his pack square on his back and his feet set exactly the way the textbook said, stop dead under the gate, and look up, and go white.

Not at the wall. At the gatehouse. At the eleventh merlon north of it, with the gap in the coping where the stone had cracked under two hands.

He knew what it was. Wendy could see him know. He'd heard about it, somewhere, on a road. *That's where he stood*.

He put his fist to his chest.

Just like that, under the gate, in the snow, with sixty recruits jostling behind him and the sergeant shouting. Fist to the chest. Back straight. At an empty gap in a wall.

"Who's that?" said Wendy.

Teague looked down.

"Garth," he said. "Of Ashby. The cadet the girl beat in the second round." He sounded, very slightly, amused. "He asked for the posting. I've got the list. Top of his year at the school and he asked for Cinderhold, which nobody asks for. The drill-master wrote a note on the bottom of his papers." Teague's mouth twitched. "*Says he wants to see where the water came from*."

Wendy looked at the young man under the gate with his fist on his chest.

She remembered him. Of course she did. The Fledgling Lists in Valcrest, the second round, the summer: a boy from the military school with beautiful feet and no idea what to do when the fox girl came at him sideways. And then the road east, eight days out, at Ashby, in the street under the paper fishing rods, when he'd come down from the school at a run and asked Alea *is he here?*, and she'd pointed at a man with two buckets, and he'd looked at the man's face and his eyes had slid off it and landed on the buckets, and he'd saluted them.

Wendy had been on the cart. She'd seen the whole thing. She'd laughed about it for two days.

She watched him now, under the gate, saluting a gap in a wall.

She didn't laugh.

***

They heard the song at supper.

The long hall, after the change of the watch. All of it full, the way it was only full twice a year, when the relief came in and the old year hadn't gone out yet: two hundred people at the trestles, old ones and young ones, with the fires going at both ends and the steam off the stew and the noise like a market. Group 3 had their place by the east fire, where they'd sat since the first winter, because Aldous had sat there and then they had. Aldous at the end with his book. Salty with his elbows on the table. Maigo, with his antlers, which had been bare since the leaves came down at the end of the autumn and which he'd hung, for the winter, with small bells that a laundress called Nell had given him because she said she was sick of him walking into doorframes. Niko, who ate with one hand and held the other out flat beside his bowl the whole meal, feeling his heartbeats, the way he did everything now. Rhea, across from Salty, looking at his arm the way a cat looks at a bird.

And Yan, with Suzu round his neck like a white scarf, very quiet, eating slowly, the way he ate everything now.

Wendy watched him eat.

She'd been watching him since the anniversary. She didn't think anybody else had noticed. She'd noticed because she'd kept a spreadsheet on Earth, of everything, all the time, and she'd never stopped keeping it in her head. Hours slept. Meals eaten. Who laughed at what. And Yan's column had been doing something since the night on the green at the grave, when his hands had gone green and Aldous had said *Soul Eater* in the oldest voice Wendy had ever heard come out of a man.

He slept more. Not much more. An hour, most nights. He'd always been the last to bed and the first up, Yan; he'd always been the one who was awake when you came down. Since the grave he went up when Salty did. And in the mornings, sometimes, he was still asleep when Wendy went past his door. She'd never once in more than two years known him still asleep when she went past his door.

She hadn't said anything. Yan carried things. You didn't take them off him. You just kept the column.

At the far end of the hall, somebody stood up on a bench with a lute.

***

He was a recruit. One of the sixty. A thin red-haired boy with a cold, who'd plainly been told by everybody on the road from Ashby that he had a voice, and believed them. He stood up on the end of a trestle with a cheap lute and his new grey coat still on and his face very pink, and the hall, which had been doing what halls do on the first night of the relief, which was shout, went quiet in pieces, the way halls do when somebody stands on a bench.

"This one's from Pellow's Cross," said the boy. Loudly. Cracking a little. "The girl at the inn there made it. After the Lists. Everybody's singing it." He swallowed. "It's called *The Thirty-Five*."

Across the table, Salty put his spoon down.

Wendy felt the whole of Group 3 go still at once. Not visibly. Nobody else would have seen. But she knew them; she'd known them for years before this, in calls at two in the morning, and here, on a wall, for two and a half more. Five people going very still in a noisy hall, the way you'd go still if you heard your own name said in the next room.

Teague, two tables down, caught her eye. And grinned. Like a boy.

The red-haired boy began to sing.

***

It was terrible.

Wendy had been braced for terrible. She'd heard *The Ballad of the Black Blade*, more than once; you couldn't spend a summer in Valcrest without hearing it, and she'd heard the girl from Pellow's Cross sing it herself by the fountain, with the new verse about a girl by a lake so cold, and a man in a plain coat had stood at the edge of the crowd and paid her silver. She knew what the girl did to a true thing. She took it, and made it rhyme, and made it bigger, and put a princess in it.

*The Thirty-Five* was worse.

It went through the summoning. Wendy recognised that much. A hall, a light, thirty-five who fell out of the sky. It got the number right; she'd give it that. Then it went to the Lists, at length, with a forty-foot bracket and a spider in the last box and the king in the royal box in a plain coat, and a fox girl with a black sword who fought like a pike, which was true, and like a storm, which wasn't. It had a verse about a kettle. It had a verse about a girl who could make a whole arena furious just by talking, which had to be Newish, and which Newish would probably have framed. It had a verse about a girl with a squid on her head that was so bad that Wendy, despite herself, laughed out loud into her stew.

And then it had a verse about the east.

The red-haired boy came to it with his voice cracking and his face going pinker, and the whole hall leaning in, because it was the east and they were in it. And Wendy, braced, heard it come.

*From the east they came, from the red so wide,*
*with a sword on a cart and a chaplain to guide.*
*And the summoned brought an elf with ears like leaves,*
*and a treant who wore the forest in his sleeves.*

The hall turned round.

All of it. Two hundred people, at once, on the benches, turning round to look at the east fire. At the table where Group 3 had sat since the first winter. At a young man with a white snake round his neck and long silver-white hair gone up in a topknot, and two long pointed ears coming out of it like leaves, with jade drops hanging from them. And beside him a small person, half his height, with bark-brown skin and glowing white eyes and a pair of branch antlers hung with small bells, and one brown shoe, and one bare foot with wooden toes like roots.

The red-haired boy on the trestle saw them. He stopped singing. His mouth stayed open.

Nobody said anything.

Then Maigo stood up.

He stood up all at once, the way Maigo did everything, so that the bells on his antlers rang all together like a dropped tray. And looked round at the whole of the long hall of Cinderhold, every face turned toward him in the firelight. And said, at a volume that Wendy was fairly sure carried to the far end of the Redmarch:

"I'M A *TREANT*."

***

The hall came apart.

The old ones first. Teague went first, with his face in his hands, shaking. Then Hesper, at the infirmary table, who laughed the way he did everything, softly and enormously. Then Old Ansel, who was eighty and had been asleep, and woke up and didn't know why everybody was laughing and laughed anyway. Then the laundry table, and the kitchen, and the gate guard, and then the recruits, all sixty of them, very nervously and then not nervously at all, until the whole long hall was roaring, two hundred people, with the fires going and Maigo standing on the bench with his arms up and his bells ringing, bowing to every table in turn.

"I'M A TREANT," shouted Maigo, to the laundry table, who cheered. "I'VE GOT THE FOREST IN MY SLEEVES. I DON'T HAVE SLEEVES. NELL, WHERE ARE MY SLEEVES."

"In the wash," shouted Nell.

Wendy had her face in her hands. She couldn't stop. She was laughing so hard it hurt, at the end of the east table, with stew on her cardigan, and Salty beside her with his shoulders going and his big square hands flat on the table, and Rhea across from him making a noise like a kettle.

*The forest in his sleeves*.

She'd tell Gigichi. Gigichi would never recover.

And then, still laughing, with her eyes streaming, she looked down the table at Yan.

***

He wasn't laughing.

He was smiling. He was always smiling, Yan. He'd been smiling, on and off, all the way through it, at the kettle and the squid and Azac's verse. He was smiling now, faintly, up at Maigo on the bench. The trustworthy one. The one who kept everybody's spirits up. Yan smiled at everything and carried everything, and you never once saw the second one under the first.

But his hand was on Suzu.

Flat, on the white coil round his neck, the way you'd put your hand on somebody's arm to stop them getting up. And under his hand Suzu had gone completely still.

Not the stillness she had asleep. Wendy knew that one. She'd spent more than two years sitting next to that snake at this table and on the wall and on a cart, and Suzu asleep was a heavy loose softness, like a scarf left on a chair. This wasn't that. This was the other kind. The kind a cat has, on a windowsill, when something's moved in the garden. Every scale of her held. Her head up off Yan's shoulder, very slightly. Her green eyes open.

And turned south.

Not toward Maigo, on the bench. Not toward the singer. Not toward the hall at all. South. Through the wall of the long hall, through the stone and the snow and the night, toward the downs.

Wendy stopped laughing.

She didn't decide to. She just found it had stopped. In the middle of the roaring hall, with Maigo still bowing and the bells still ringing. She looked at a white snake looking at a wall.

Yan saw her look.

He didn't say anything. He just looked at her, down the table, with the faint smile still on, and his hand still flat on Suzu, and then looked back up at Maigo, and laughed, at last, at something Maigo said to the kitchen table about acorns. A real laugh. Yan didn't do fake ones.

But he didn't take his hand off the snake.

***

Garth found her on the wall at midnight.

She was on the late watch with Teague. North of the gate, the stretch she always took, with the snow coming down now properly, in big soft flakes out of the dark, settling on the parapet and on her shoulders and on the bow across her back. The red was white. That was the strange thing about the first snow here; she'd seen it twice and she still couldn't get used to it. The whole of the Redmarch, all the way to the mountains, all the markers and the ditch and the grey grass, gone white and soft and featureless under the snow, as if somebody had laid a cloth over the dead. Except that the grey grass came through. Here and there. Tufts of it, poking up out of the white, grey, the colour of ash, rattling very faintly when the wind took them. *Hush*.

She heard the cadet before she saw him. He walked like a textbook, too. Heel and toe, very even, the way they taught it at Ashby.

He stopped at a respectful distance and saluted her. Fist to the chest.

"Don't," said Wendy. "I'm not anyone."

"You're one of the thirty-five," said Garth.

"That's not anyone. That's just a number." She looked at him. He was very young. Nineteen, perhaps. With snow on his new grey coat and his face very serious and red with cold. "You're on the wrong stretch. Recruits do the south."

"I asked to do the north," said Garth. "Tonight. The sergeant said I could if I didn't touch anything." He looked at the parapet beside her. At the eleventh merlon, two along, with the gap in the coping. He didn't go near it. Nobody went near it. The sentries' path went round. "I wanted to see it."

Wendy didn't say anything.

"In the snow," said Garth. He was looking at the gap. "We heard about it. On the road. The carters talk about it at every posting house from Ashby to here. *He stood there an hour. With his hands in the stone*." He swallowed. "I didn't believe it. I thought it was a song." He looked at the crack in the coping, where the stone had broken in two places a hand's width apart, the shape of two hands gripping. "It isn't a song."

"No," said Wendy.

They stood there a while, in the snow.

"Is it true?" said Garth.

"Is what true?"

"The elf." He went red. Redder. "And the treant. The song. The boy from Pellow's Cross has been singing it the whole way from Ashby, every night, at every posting house. *The summoned brought an elf and a treant*." He looked at her, very seriously. "Everyone on the road believes it. Every inn. There were people at the posting house at Caddow who'd come in from the farms just to ask if we'd seen them." He hesitated. "Is the one with the ears an elf?"

Wendy looked at him.

"No," she said. "He's Yan. He's from a city with traffic lights and buses, and a mum whose kitchen he still thinks about every day. He's a man." She shifted the bow on her back. "His drawing had a story, that's all. That he died once and came back not quite an elf, with a snake's everything underneath. It was a bit. On stream. It was a good bit." "And Maigo's not a treant. Maigo's a man who looked like a tree on the internet and came out of a floor looking like a tree for real, and we're all very used to it."

Garth didn't know what the internet was. Wendy could see him not know. He didn't ask. He nodded, very seriously, as if she'd told him something important about the order of battle.

"So it's not true," he said.

"It's a song," said Wendy. "Songs aren't true. They're what people want to be true." She looked out at the white red. "Your boy from Pellow's Cross wants there to be elves. So there's an elf."

Garth thought about that.

"And the water?" he said.

Wendy looked at him.

"In Ashby," said Garth. "In the street. With the buckets." He was looking at the gap in the coping. "That wasn't a song. I was there. I saw it. A man with two buckets on a yoke. I couldn't see his face. I tried. My eyes kept going to the buckets." He swallowed. "And I saluted them. The buckets. In front of the whole street. And then I went back up to the school and sat on my bed and didn't know why I'd done it."

"I know," said Wendy. "I was on the cart."

"Everyone laughed."

"I laughed," said Wendy. "For two days."

Garth went red to the roots of his hair. He nodded, very stiffly.

"And then I stopped," said Wendy.

He looked at her.

"Because you were right," said Wendy. She didn't know why she was telling him. She was a skeptic. She didn't tell people things. "You looked at his face and it slid off and you saluted what was left. That's the right thing. That's what we all do. Everyone who can't see his face. You salute what he's carrying." She looked at the gap in the parapet, two merlons along. "Sometimes it's water."

Garth stood there in the snow for a long time.

Then he saluted her again. Fist to the chest. And this time she let him.

***

Yan was on the wall when Garth had gone.

She hadn't heard him come. Nobody ever did. He was just there, on the parapet a little way along, past the gap, sitting with his legs over the drop the way the man had sat in it once, in the autumn, reading a letter. Not in the gap. Beside it. With Suzu round his neck and the snow settling on both of them, on his silver hair and his long pointed ears and on her white coils, so that it was hard to tell where the snake stopped and the snow started.

He was looking south.

So was she.

Wendy went along the wall and stood beside him. She didn't sit. You didn't sit on that parapet unless you were very good at heights or very bad at caring, and Wendy was neither.

"She's still doing it," said Wendy.

"Since the song."

"Since the song," Wendy agreed. She looked at the snake. Suzu hadn't moved. Head up off his shoulder, a finger's width. Eyes open. South. "What's south?"

"The downs," said Yan. "Then Ashby. Then the river. Then the green country." He was quiet. "Then the south road, and Valcrest, and the sea." He looked down at the snake. "She's never been south. Not really. She's been to Valcrest. She's been on the road. She didn't look at it then."

"She's looking at it now."

"She's never looked at anywhere," said Yan. "That's the thing." He put his hand on her, very lightly, on the coil over his chest. "She looks at me. She looks at food. Sometimes she looks at Kei's snake, when he's there, and pretends she isn't. She doesn't look at *places*." He breathed out, white. "Two years and a half, Wen. She's never once looked at a place."

Wendy didn't say anything for a while.

She was a skeptic. It was what she was for. She'd come out of a floor in a hall in Solmere more than two years ago into a world with magic and windows and a demon kingdom, and spent the first year believing none of it, and the second year believing about half, and this autumn, on the grey grass, watching the red go quiet round a man's feet, she'd believed the rest. All at once. And it had been the worst feeling she'd ever had, and also the best, and she still hadn't decided which.

She didn't believe in songs. She believed in what she'd seen twice.

She'd seen this once.

"It's a stupid song," she said.

"It's a terrible song," Yan agreed.

"*The forest in his sleeves*."

Yan laughed. Short. Into the snow.

"The boy from Pellow's Cross sang it the whole way from Ashby," said Wendy. "Every posting house. Garth says people were coming in from the farms at Caddow to ask about the elf." She looked at the white downs going away south in the dark. "And every one of them told somebody. And every one of those told somebody. It's a song. It goes down a road in a week that a cart takes a month." She was quiet. "It's going south. Now. Tonight. While we sit here. Down every road out of the east. *The summoned brought an elf and a treant*."

"I know," said Yan.

"And she turned round when she heard it."

"I know."

Wendy looked at the snake.

"Yan," she said. "What does she know that you don't?"

Yan didn't answer for a long time. The snow came down on the white red, and the grey grass rattled under it, *hush*, and somewhere behind them in the fort a door banged and a recruit laughed and was told to be quiet.

"She won't tell me," he said, at last. "That's the thing, Wen. That's the whole thing." He looked down at the snake round his neck. "She tells me everything. Every day. Since the floor of the hall. What she thinks of the soup, and Rhea's boots, and the way Maigo breathes. She's never once not told me something." His hand moved, very lightly, on her coil. "I asked her. On the stair, after the song. At supper. Up here. I said *what's south*." He was quiet. "She said they're coming. And I said who. And she said *not yet, Yun*. That's all. Every time I ask. *Not yet*."

Wendy remembered the first winter. Yan at the fire, saying *Suzu says*, the way he always had on stream, and the white snake round his neck hissing at everybody, and all of them laughing, kindly, and humouring him, for a year and a half. *Suzu says the soup's too hot. Suzu says Maigo's standing on her tail.* Until a roof in Valcrest, and a green snake saying the same things back, word for word, in front of Kei. Wendy had sat at the long table in the Lance the next night with her hand over her mouth and apologised to him for a year and a half of humouring him, and he'd said *it's fine, Wen, I thought I was mad too*.

"And then Valcrest," said Yan. "On the roof. With Kei. And Hisui could hear her." He was quiet. "Somebody else. For the first time. And he heard her call me what she's called me every day since the hall. The thing I thought I'd made up."

"Yun," said Wendy.

Yan looked at her.

"Salty told me," said Wendy. "On the road east. He said you didn't sleep that night, after the roof. He said you sat on the end of your bed with her in your lap and said it over and over under your breath like you were checking it was still there."

Yan didn't say anything.

"So she tells you everything," said Wendy. "And now there's one thing she won't." She looked at the snake. "And she's looking at the south, and she won't say why."

"Yes," said Yan.

"And the only other one in the kingdom who can hear her is in Thornwall."

"Six weeks away," said Yan. "Over the passes. In the snow." He breathed out. "Hisui. Kei's snake. He's the only one."

Wendy thought about that.

She thought about it the way she'd thought about everything for more than two years, as a skeptic, from the bottom up. What she'd seen. What she knew. What she could put in a column. A snake in Thornwall who could hear a snake in Cinderhold, if they were in the same room. A cat girl in Solmere who made cords that warmed when the other half was held. Who'd made one for every guide in the kingdom, and they talked through them now, every day, a few words on a strip of blue ink, and the channel said the cords carried what you *meant*. Not the words. The meaning. That was why the binding had mistaken them for its own and nearly eaten the channel. Thea had explained it, at length, at two in the morning, with diagrams.

*They carry what you meant*.

"Yan," said Wendy.

"Mm."

"Does a snake mean things?"

Yan turned his head and looked at her.

***

Yan read it.

Then he put his hand on the white coil round his neck, very lightly, and leaned his head down, and said something into the snake's ear, if a snake had an ear, that Wendy didn't hear. In English. Very quietly.

Suzu didn't move. Her head stayed up, a finger's width. Her eyes stayed open. South.

But her tail, which had been lying along Yan's collarbone the whole time, very still, curled once. Just the tip. Round two of his fingers. And held on.

***

Salty was waiting when she came off the wall.

He always was. At the bottom of the north stair, in the snow, with a lantern, at the change of the watch, the way he'd waited for her at the bottom of every stair she'd come down for more than two years. He didn't say anything. He held the lantern up so she could see her feet on the last steps, and when she got to the bottom he put his free arm round her and she leaned on him, because she was cold and tired and it was two in the morning and there was nobody in the yard to see.

"Did Yan ask?" said Salty.

"He asked."

"Good." Salty was quiet. "She's been looking at the south since the song."

"You saw?"

"I saw." He held the lantern a bit higher. "I'm not stupid, Wen. I'm just slow."

Wendy laughed into his coat.

They walked across the yard toward the barracks in the snow. Past the forge, where Oakes's fire was banked for the night and the smell of the wrong peppers still hung in the doorway, because Salty had been practising again. *Twelve*. He'd said it at the gate when the man left, with his hand flat on his chest. *Twelve. At the lake. Next summer*. And he'd been practising the twelfth dinner every night since, at the second anvil, in a skillet the size of a cartwheel, with peppers from a trader who swore they were the right ones and weren't. The whole fort smelled of it. The cook had complained twice.

"It's a stupid song," said Wendy.

"It's a terrible song," said Salty. "*The forest in his sleeves*."

"Maigo's going to have it sewn on something."

"Nell's already doing it," said Salty. "She told me at the laundry. She's sewing leaves on his collar." He was quiet a moment. "Wen."

"Mm."

"You don't believe it."

"The song? No."

"Not the song," said Salty. "The other thing. That something's down there. In the south. That she can feel." He stopped at the barracks door and held the lantern up and looked at her in the light of it. Big and square and blunt, with the crooked ribs that would match the sword, with snow on his eyebrows. "You don't believe it."

Wendy looked at him.

She thought about it honestly. He'd asked her honestly. Salty always did; it was the best thing about him. He'd never once in his life asked her a question he didn't want the answer to.

"I've seen it once," she said.

Salty nodded slowly. He knew what that meant. He'd known her a long time.

"Then you'll be watching," he said, "for twice."

"I'll be watching," said Wendy.

He held the door for her, and she went in, and he went in after her with the lantern, and the door closed on the snow.

***

She didn't sleep.

She lay in her bunk in the women's barracks, under three blankets and her cardigan, with her silent headphones round her neck where they always were, and looked at the ceiling.

She was a skeptic. She'd been one all her life. On Earth she'd been the one in the call at two in the morning saying *that's not going to work* about every plan anyone had, and being right about half of them, and being loved for it anyway, which she'd never understood. She'd been the one who said *he's dead, Yan* when Yan didn't want to hear it, three years and a whole world ago. She'd been the one on the cart the first week who said *there's no way home, there's no Sun mage, this is a lie they tell you so you'll fight*.

She'd been wrong about that last one. Or about to be wrong. A girl in Wrenhaven had said *it parses* over a page of rings and lines, three weeks ago, and the channel had gone mad, and Wendy had sat at the east table and read it and felt, for the first time since the hall, the thing under her skepticism move. The thing she'd been sitting on for more than two years. The hope.

She didn't like it. Hope was a thing that could be taken off you.

She lay in the dark and thought about a white snake on a wall, looking south.

*She's never once looked at a place*.

At some point in the night she got up. She didn't know why. She put her boots on and her cardigan on and her coat over the cardigan and went out across the yard in the snow and up the south stair to the wall. The south wall, where the recruits walked. Where you could see the downs.

She stood on the south wall of Cinderhold at four in the morning in the snow and looked south.

Nothing. Of course nothing. The downs, white, going away in long low folds into the dark, under a sky with the snow thinning out of it now and a few stars showing in the gaps. No lights. No road; the road was under the snow. Nothing moving anywhere, all the way to the edge of the world.

There was nothing there. She was sure of it. She was a skeptic. She believed what she'd seen twice.

She stood there for an hour anyway.

At some point a recruit on the south watch came along the wall, a girl of eighteen in a new grey coat, very cold, and stopped, and looked at Wendy, and then looked south, where Wendy was looking.

"What is it?" said the girl. "What are you looking at?"

Wendy thought about it.

"I don't know," she said. "Nothing. Probably."

The girl looked south for a while, too. Then she went on along the wall. And at the end of her stretch, Wendy saw, she stopped again, and looked south again, for a long moment, before she turned round.

That was how it went, Wendy thought. That was how songs went down roads. Not because anybody carried them. Because somebody stood on a wall looking at something, and somebody else came along and looked too.

She went back down at dawn.

She didn't write anything in the channel. She didn't know what she'd write. *I looked south for an hour and there was nothing there*. It wasn't news. It wasn't anything.

But she put it in the column. In her head. The way she'd put everything in a column, all her life.

*Once*.
