The red was green.

Not all of it. Maigo wanted to be honest about that, because people back home were going to read the channel and get excited, and then one day some of them were going to come out here and look, and he didn't want them standing on the east wall going *that's not green, Maigo, that's red with a bit of green on it*. Which was true. It was red with a bit of green on it.

But the bit was *green*.

He stood on the wall walk at first light on Thursday with his hood down and his antlers out, because his antlers were doing the spring thing again and he wanted them to have the sun. New leaves. Little ones, the size of a fingernail, very bright, coming out of the bark along both antlers in a way that itched like nothing on Earth had ever itched. Nell had wanted to sew more leaves on his collar to match, and he'd said *no, Nell, these are real ones*, and she'd said *I know, love, that's why I want to match them*, and he'd let her, because you didn't argue with Nell about collars.

Out past the markers, in their long grey rows, the dry riverbeds went away across the plain toward the mountains. He'd looked at them for two and a half years. Old water-courses, cracked and pale, winding over the red the way veins go over the back of a hand, all the way out to where the mountains of Kharvos stood up black at the edge of everything.

Every one of them had a green line down the middle.

Thin. Brighter at this end and fading further out. As if somebody had gone along every riverbed on the red with a pen and drawn it in. Grass, Aldous said, and something low with small leaves he didn't have a name for, and in one place, two miles out, a clump of something that might, if you squinted, be a flower.

The first green on the red in nine years. The first that wasn't a grave.

"Thirty-one," said Maigo, to nobody.

"Thirty-one what?" said Niko.

Maigo jumped, and the bells on his antlers went. He hadn't heard Niko come along the walk. Nobody heard Niko come along anything. He was small and he moved like he'd been told off for making noise as a child and never got over it.

"Things I'm happy about," said Maigo. "Today. It's not even breakfast."

"What's number one?"

"*That*." Maigo pointed out at the red with his whole arm. "Look at it. Look at it, Niko. It's *green*."

"It's red with a bit of green on it," said Niko.

Maigo looked at him with his whole face.

"I'm joking," said Niko. "It's green. I've been looking at it every morning for a month." He leaned on the parapet beside Maigo. He had dried blood at the corner of his lip again, but only a little, and his hands were nearly still on the stone. Wendy had said *twenty-nine* again this morning; Maigo had heard her from the stair. Twenty-nine twice. No pushing. "Number two?"

"You."

Niko didn't say anything for a moment.

"Shut up," he said eventually, but nicely.

"I'm not shutting up. It's on the list. You can't take things off my list, it's my list." Maigo bounced on his heels. "Three's Salty's peppers. Four's Garth, because he still had a noodle behind his ear at supper and he wouldn't let anyone take it off because he hadn't been dismissed. Five's—"

"Maigo," said Niko. "Can I ask you something? About the tree?"

Maigo stopped bouncing.

***

"Its leaves," said Niko. "All of them. They've turned."

"They do that," said Maigo. "They turn to people. They turned to the man, when he came, and Alea, and to me, when I come. And they turned south for the elves all winter. She's very—" He looked for the word. "Friendly."

"East," said Niko.

Maigo looked at him.

"Since Monday night," said Niko. "I saw it from the wall. Every leaf. East. Toward the mountains. They were still like it yesterday." He looked out at the knoll, far out on the red, past the markers, where the young ash stood beside the black stone like a small green flame. From here you couldn't see leaves. You could just see green. "I was going to ask you on Thursday. It's Thursday."

"It's Thursday," Maigo agreed.

He looked at the knoll for a long moment.

Then he said, "There's nobody east."

"I know."

"There's nobody east to turn to. There's nothing east. There's just the red, and the mountains." He frowned. "She doesn't turn to *mountains*."

"I know," said Niko. "That's why I'm asking."

Maigo put his hand flat on the parapet. Stone. You couldn't feel anything through stone; it was the one thing his foot couldn't do, or his hands. Stone was like a closed door. He felt it anyway, a little, out of habit. Cold. Dry. Nothing.

"I'll ask her," he said.

***

Teague was on the gatehouse with his eggs.

"Thursday?" said Teague.

"Thursday," said Maigo.

Teague had a spoon of Salty's paste on each egg now. He said it was for his blood. Rhea said it was because he'd been dared. He lifted his good hand, the left one, the way he always did; his right one was the bad one, from the summer before last, and he kept it in his belt mostly, where it didn't have to do anything.

"Tell it the boy did twenty-nine," said Teague. "Twice."

"I will."

"Tell it the recruits are still useless."

"They're *not*—"

"Tell it anyway." Teague ate some egg. "It's a soldier's tree. It'll want something to complain about." He looked out over the gate at the red, the way old soldiers look at country they've fought over, not at any one thing, at all of it. "And tell it Rhea's taken the riders out every week since the thaw and found nothing. Six weeks. Not a track."

"Is that good?"

Teague chewed for a while.

"Ask the tree," he said.

***

He wasn't going alone. He found that out at the gate.

Liraun was waiting there.

She was sitting on the mounting block by the east gate in her long lichen-coloured coat with her knees pulled up and her pale hair loose, and she stood up when she saw him coming, quickly, the way she always did, as if she'd been caught somewhere she wasn't meant to be. She was the youngest of the three. She was taller than Rhea and she moved like grass moves, and she had the long swept-back ears of all of them, and the face of somebody who'd just been told a secret and was trying very hard not to look as if she had.

"Thursday," she said.

She said it in the singing tongue. He heard it in English, the way all of them did, a little musical round the edges. And she said it carefully, the way you say a word in a language you're learning, to see if it's right.

"Thursday," said Maigo, delighted. "Who told you about Thursday?"

"The old one on the gate." She looked at Teague, up on the gatehouse, who was pretending very hard to look at the red. "He told me you tell the tree things. On this day. And that it is a soldier's tree." She looked at Maigo, shy. "I would like to see the green on the water-roads. If I may walk with you. I will not step on hers."

Maigo had been at Cinderhold for two and a half years. In two and a half years, nobody had ever once asked to come with him on a Thursday. Wendy had come once, the first summer, and said it was very nice and very far. Salty had come twice. Yan came sometimes, when he was awake. But nobody had ever *asked*.

"Yes," said Maigo. "Yes. *Yes.* Come on. You'll love it. It's an hour. Well, it's two in the snow, but there isn't snow. Do you want to see where the first flower is? I think it's a flower. Aldous says it might be a weed. I said a weed's just a flower that nobody's said thank you to." He was already walking. "Do you like walking? I like walking. I didn't, before, back home, I didn't go outside for about three years, but it turns out I like it if there's somebody to talk to on the way."

Liraun walked beside him out of the east gate.

She didn't leave any tracks. Maigo had noticed that at the west gate in the winter, and he noticed it again now, on the soft red dust of Aldous's burial track: his own prints, one shoe and one bare root foot, going out east in a little uneven line, and beside them, nothing at all. As if he were walking out across the red by himself, talking to the air.

Teague watched them go from the gatehouse.

"Tell it," he called after them, "that it's got company."

***

The red didn't flinch.

He told Liraun that, on the way out, because she asked him what it felt like, and nobody ever asked him that either. He told her about the first winter. Every step from the walls out, the ground going tight and small under his foot, like walking on a bruise. Every marker. Every place where somebody had fallen. How he'd cried all the way out the first time and told nobody, and then told everybody.

"And then it stopped," said Maigo. "Two summers ago. A man walked across it with a fishing rod and it just went—" He made a gesture with both hands, like letting go of a rope. "Still. Round his feet. And it's been like that ever since. Not happy. It's not *happy*. It's still sad. But it's not frightened."

Liraun was quiet for a while. She was walking with her hand down at her side, trailing her long fingers through the tops of the grey grass at the edge of the track, the way you'd trail your hand in water from a boat.

"Grief and fear," she said at last, in the singing tongue, "are not the same weight."

"No," said Maigo.

"Grief is heavy and it lies down. Fear is light and it never lies down." She touched a grey tuft. It didn't move; the grey grass never moved; but Maigo thought, maybe, it leaned into her fingers a hair, the way a cat leans into a hand. "This lies down now. It is heavy. But it is lying down." She looked at him sideways, shy again. "Your man did that?"

"He's not my man," said Maigo. "He's—" He stopped.

What was he? He was Ice. He was the guy who'd called him Maigotron after a four-kill pistol round and never once called him anything else after. He was the guy who'd sat on the end of a voice call at four in the morning while Maigo cried about something stupid, and not said anything, and not hung up. He was the Black Blade. He was Alea's dad. He was a fisherman on a lake a month away who read the channel every night and never wrote in it.

"He's ours," said Maigo, in the end. "He's a lot of people's."

"Yes," said Liraun, as if that was obvious. "That is how it feels. On the ground. A great many hands, and one of them very large."

***

The first green was at the third row of markers.

Maigo stopped there, like he did every time now, and crouched down at the edge of the riverbed, and looked at it.

It wasn't much. If you'd been from anywhere else in the world you'd have walked past it. A thin fuzz of new grass down the middle of the old dry water-course, no wider than a cart track. Small, pale, the colour of the first leaves on his antlers. And a low creeping thing with round leaves, among the grass, that he didn't have a name for. It went away east down the riverbed from here, a green thread on the red, getting thinner, out past the last of the markers and on toward the mountains until he couldn't see it any more.

"Isn't it *good*," said Maigo.

Liraun crouched beside him. She didn't touch it. She looked at it for a long time, the way he'd seen her look at the pepper plants in Salty's forge, as if they were saying something.

"It is very young," she said.

"It came up after the thaw. Aldous says it's the first time since the war. The river used to run here, before. And then the war, and everything went grey and red, and nothing grew." Maigo touched the grass with one finger. It was cold and wet with the morning. "And now it's coming back. Down all the old rivers. Isn't it good? It's like the whole red's remembering it used to be somewhere."

"Mm," said Liraun.

She'd tilted her head, the way Yan tilted his when Suzu said something he hadn't expected.

"What?" said Maigo.

"Nothing." She stood up. "It is very young. That is all. Very young things do not know yet what to be afraid of."

Maigo looked at her.

She smiled at him, small and shy, and walked on.

***

The knoll came up out of the red the way it always did, slowly, a low hump of dark ground above the place where two of the old riverbeds met, with the cairn on the top of it and the black stone, and the tree.

Maigo saw the tree before he saw anything, because he always did. He'd planted it. You always see the thing you planted first.

It was in full leaf.

Taller than Rhea now. Taller than Rhea with her arms up, nearly. A slim straight young ash with pale grey bark and every branch full, every leaf out, bright and new and green against the enormous pale sky, standing up out of the knoll beside the black stone like somebody standing beside somebody they love.

And every leaf on it was turned east.

He'd known. Niko had told him. He'd known all the way out. It was still a shock, in his chest, a little cold drop, like stepping on a stair that isn't there.

Because it wasn't how she turned. When she turned to the man, two summers ago, she'd turned the way a face turns: all of her, softly, the leaves and the branches and the whole crown leaning. When she turned to Alea, the same. To the elves, in the winter, her branches had all gone south, but slowly, over a morning, the way a flower follows the sun.

This wasn't that.

Every leaf was flat to the east. Stiff. All of them. Exactly the same way, like a field of compass needles, and not moving, though there was a small cold wind off the mountains this morning and the grey grass round the foot of the knoll was hissing in it. The wind came over the knoll and through the ash, and the leaves didn't flutter. They just stayed pointed. East. At the mountains.

And when Maigo came up the slope onto the green, they didn't turn to him.

They always turned to him.

***

Liraun stopped at the edge of the green, where she always stopped. She didn't step on it. None of them did; they'd come out here every few days all winter and stood at the edge of it and never once put a foot on it. *It is his*, Tavrin had said. *The green is his hand. We do not stand on a man's hand*.

It had spread.

That was the other thing. Two summers ago, when the man first sat under her, the green had been a ring a long stride wide round the cairn, with the flowers in it. Small white flowers and small blue ones, the only things that had grown on the red in nine years. Last summer it had been wider. Now, with the spring, it had come out over the whole top of the knoll, soft and short and bright as a lawn, with the flowers all through it, white and blue, nodding. And at the east edge of the knoll, where the slope went down to the meeting of the two riverbeds, it didn't stop. It went on. Down the slope. Into the riverbeds. A green thread running out of the grave and away east along each of the old water-courses, toward the mountains.

Maigo stood on the green at the top of the knoll and looked at that for a long time.

"Oh," he said.

He looked back at the tree. At the leaves, all pointed the same way. At the green threads running out from under her, east.

"Oh," he said again. "It's *you*."

***

He went and put his hand on the bark.

Then he sat down at the foot of the cairn, with his back against the ash and his root foot flat on the green, the way he always did, and he didn't tell her anything.

He'd meant to. He had the whole week ready. He'd been saving it up since last Thursday like you save up a story for somebody who'll laugh at it. That Niko had done twenty-nine, twice, without pushing, because Salty couldn't toss a skillet and Niko had remembered how. That Oen had thrown the noodles over the forge and they'd landed on Garth, and Garth hadn't moved. That Chris had drawn the whole bracket for the summer on a wall in Valcrest as a Swiss, like a Major, with thirty-six boxes, so that there'd be a bye every round, and the bye would walk down the wall, and nobody would sit in it twice. That Alea's friend Sorrel had sent a letter by water. That Alea had stood on a floating mat of roots in a fog with no lamp and walked home anyway. That Thea had six leaves come in clean. That Rhea had lost to Oen a twelfth time. That Teague said the recruits were useless and to tell her so.

He'd been going to tell her all of it.

Instead he sat with his back against her and looked at the stiff green leaves above him, all turned one way, and said, "What are you looking at?"

And let his foot go down.

***

He knew her roots now the way you know the inside of your own pocket.

Down through the green. Down through the dark red earth under the green, into the cold, round the long black spike of the stone where it went into the ground like a key. And under the stone, the cold thing. Small and dense and wrong, a long way down, and the roots round it like fingers round a stone. Holding.

He didn't go near it. He never did.

He felt it, though. He made himself, for a moment, because something was different and he had to know what. And it was there, held, the way it always was. Asleep. But not *deep* asleep. It was lying the way a dog lies when it's heard something outside. Still. With its head up.

He went the other way. Fast. Out.

And there they were.

He'd never felt them before because they hadn't been there before. Roots. New ones. Young ones, very thin, very many, going out from under the ash into the earth on the east side of the knoll and down the slope and into the old riverbeds. Following the water-courses, where the ground was softer, where there had been water once. Out and out. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A whole spring's worth of roots, sent out east along every dry river on the red, so fast he could hardly believe it, the way roots go when they're not just growing but *going*.

And the grass was coming up behind them.

That was the green. That was all the green on the red. Not the red remembering anything. Just a young tree, at the end of the winter, putting out every root she had, east, as far as she could reach, along the oldest roads there were. And the grass coming up wherever the roots went, because roots make the ground soft and the ground had been waiting nine years for somebody to make it soft.

"You did this," said Maigo, out loud, with his eyes shut.

He followed them.

***

It was like running down a corridor in the dark with your hand on the wall.

The roots went on and on. Further than he'd ever felt anything. Past the last of the markers. Past where Aldous's burial track gave out. Out under the open red where nobody had walked for nine years except demons and a man with a fishing rod. The roots were so thin out here they were hardly anything, a hair, a thread, a thought, and he went along them anyway, east, following the green down the riverbeds, with the whole of him gone down into his foot and the rest of his body sitting forgotten against a tree on a knoll.

And he understood.

He understood it the way you understand a thing all at once, suddenly, the whole shape of it, like a map falling open.

They weren't roots. Not just. They were *pickets*.

Teague had taught him the word. The first spring, on the gatehouse, when Maigo asked why the riders went out every morning. *Pickets*, Teague had said. *You put men out. A long way out. Not to fight. Just to stand there and feel it if something comes. So you know before it gets to you*. And Maigo had gone out on the Thursday and told the tree, because he told the tree everything, and he'd said *Teague says you put pickets out, so you know*.

He'd told her that. Two springs ago.

And she'd put her pickets out.

Every root she had, east, as far as they'd go, along the old rivers, toward the mountains. Not looking for water. Not growing. *Standing there*. Feeling the ground. So she'd know before it got to her.

"Oh, you *clever* thing," said Maigo, with his eyes shut and his throat tight. "You clever, clever—"

The roots flinched.

***

He felt it at the very end of them. Out at the edge of everything. At the place where the riverbeds came down out of the foothills onto the red, where the green thread on the red was so thin it was hardly there, a day's walk east, maybe more, he had no idea, he'd never been this far.

The thinnest, youngest roots, out at the very tips.

Flinching.

Going tight and small and frightened, all at once, the way the whole red had gone under his foot the first winter. Like walking on a bruise. But not under *him*. Under something else. Something on top of them, going over them.

Step. And stop.

And step-step-step. And stop.

He knew that. He knew that rhythm. He'd stood on the wall the first winter with his hand on the cold stone and watched eleven of them come out of the dark across the red in exactly that rhythm, like hounds on a scent, quick and still and quick and still, and stop past the third row of markers and *point*. At the towers. At the gate. At the well. At the chapel. Counting.

Step-step. And stop.

Lots of them.

He couldn't count them. Roots didn't count; they just flinched. But he could feel how much of the green was flinching, out there at the tips, and it was a lot. A whole width of it. Every riverbed coming down out of the foothills, flinching at once, the way your whole skin goes when you walk into cold water. A great many feet. More than the summer. More than the summer before last, when the fifty had come out of the gullies round them and the three big ones with them and the man had come over the red with a rod and—

Step-step-step. Stop.

Coming down out of the hills onto the new grass.

The new grass that had never once in its life been walked on by anything like that, and didn't know yet what to be afraid of, and was finding out.

***

He didn't remember opening his eyes.

He was on his feet. He was on his feet on the green at the top of the knoll with both hands flat on the ash's bark and his heart going and the bells on his antlers ringing all at once, and the sky was huge and pale and empty, and the red was empty, and there was nothing out there. Nothing at all. Just the green threads going east down the riverbeds, very thin, very bright, toward the black line of the mountains, in the sun.

Nothing he could see.

"Maigo," said Liraun.

She was at the edge of the green. She'd gone down on her knees on the grey grass just outside it, with both her long hands flat on the ground, the way he put his foot. Her face had gone a strange colour. Not pale. Elves were pale already. It had gone *still*, the way grass goes still before a storm.

"You felt it," said Maigo.

"Yes."

"What are they? Do you know? Do you have a word?"

Liraun looked up at him. For a moment she looked very young. Younger than him. Younger than Garth.

"We have a word," she said. "We do not say it near green things." She took her hands off the ground, slowly, as if it were hot. "They are walking on the new grass. It has never been walked on by them. It does not know what they are. It is asking me." She swallowed. "I do not know how to tell it."

Maigo looked at the tree.

At every leaf on her, stiff and flat and pointed east, at the hills, at the place where her youngest roots were flinching under feet. Since Monday night. Three days. Three days she'd been standing out here on her own with her pickets out, feeling them come down out of the mountains, a step at a time, and pointing. Every leaf. As hard as a tree can point. *There. There. There*.

And nobody had come out until Thursday.

"I'm sorry," Maigo told her. His voice came out wrong. "I'm sorry. I didn't know. I'd have come. I'd have come Monday. You should have—" He stopped. You couldn't tell a tree it should have shouted. She *had* shouted. She'd shouted with every leaf she had, for three days, and Niko had heard her from the wall. "I'm here now," said Maigo. "I'm here now. I'll tell them. I'll go and tell them right now."

He put his hand on the bark.

"You did so well," he said.

Then he ran.

***

He wasn't fast. He'd never been fast. He was small and his legs were short and one of his feet was a root, and on Earth he'd once been out of breath going up the stairs to his own flat. But he ran. All the way back. Down off the knoll and across the red and along Aldous's track between the long rows of markers, with his hood bouncing and his bells going and his root foot slapping the dust, and the grey grass going by on either side, still, sad, lying down.

Liraun ran beside him.

She didn't look as if she was running. She looked as if she was walking quickly. Her long coat hardly moved and her feet didn't leave any marks at all, and every so often she looked down at him, worried, and slowed, and he said *I'm fine, I'm fine, go on*, and she didn't go on.

It took forty minutes. He knew because he counted. Maigo counted things when he was frightened. Questions, steps, markers. He'd counted a hundred and three questions once, walking behind a man across this exact plain, and not asked one. He counted the markers now. Rows of them. Aldous's names burned into the first ones, and then no names, only posts. He counted all of them. He got to four hundred and something and lost count and started again.

He was thinking, the whole way, about Rhea.

At supper. Not this week; the week before. *I don't like nothing. Nothing's what you get before something*. And Wendy saying *that's not a sentence*. And everybody laughing.

The gate came up out of the red. The walls. Teague on the gatehouse, standing up now, with his egg forgotten, watching them come.

"That was quick," said Teague.

"Get Rhea," said Maigo.

Teague looked at his face.

Then he turned round and bellowed it down into the courtyard, in a voice Maigo had only ever heard him use once before, two summers ago, on the red, with a ring of soldiers round a black sword: "*RHEA!*"

***

They were in the commander's room inside ten minutes. All of them.

Maigo had never been in the commander's room. It was small and very tidy and had a map on the wall that was much better than Aldous's map, with the riverbeds drawn in blue ink as if they still had water in them. Rhea stood at the window with her arms folded. Aldous sat on the one chair that wasn't the commander's, with his book on his knees. Yan was in the door with Suzu round his neck, half asleep still, his topknot coming down. Teague had come down off the gate. Liraun stood in the corner very straight and very quiet, as if she were trying to look like part of the wall.

And the commander sat behind his desk and listened.

He was young. Everybody said so. A careful young captain from Solmere, sent out three years ago, who'd heard all the stories about the Redmarch and the Black Blade and the healer and the sword and, Aldous said, never once believed a word of any of them. He had a neat beard and neat hands and a habit of straightening the things on his desk while people talked. He'd gone very red in the winter about the west gate. He'd said *regulations* a great many times. And then three tall grey people had walked out of the end of the world into his courtyard and knelt in the snow to a snake, and he'd stood there in his own gateway with his mouth open, and something in his face had never quite gone back.

He straightened his inkpot while Maigo talked. Then he stopped straightening it.

"Roots," he said, when Maigo had finished.

"Yes."

"You felt it. Through the roots. Of a tree." He wasn't saying it nastily. He was saying it the way you'd say a sum back to somebody, to check you'd heard the numbers right. "A day's walk east. Maybe more. At the foot of the hills."

"Her roots," said Maigo. "She put them out. Like pickets. Teague told her about pickets." He was still out of breath. "Well. I told her. Teague told me. And I told her."

Teague, at the door, made a small noise and looked at the floor.

The commander looked at Teague. Then at Maigo. Then he looked for a long time at the map on his wall, at the blue ink riverbeds going east.

"The grass," he said. "On the riverbeds. That's the tree?"

"Yes."

"The whole—" He stopped. He looked at Rhea.

"He asked me to open your west gate," said Rhea, from the window, without turning round. "At midnight. For three people nobody'd seen, who were walking out of somewhere nobody's been. And I asked Aldous, and Aldous said yes, and I did." She turned round. "And they came."

In the corner, Liraun looked at the floor.

"I remember," said the commander, a little stiffly.

"Then I'd listen to him about roots," said Rhea.

***

The commander listened to him about roots.

He sent two riders east inside the hour, on the two fastest horses in the stable, along the old riverbed: Collum, who was eighteen now and had grown out of all his clothes in the winter, and one of the older men, and told them to go as far as the foot of the hills and not one step further and to come back at the first sign of anything. He doubled the watch on the east wall. He sent a third rider west to Ashby with a letter, and a fourth with a letter for the king, wherever the king was, which was Thornwall, a very long way north.

He did it all very quickly and very neatly, and didn't say *regulations* once.

At the door, as they were going, he said, "Yan."

Yan stopped.

"Can you feel them?" said the commander. "The way you felt them the first winter?" He'd read the reports. He read everything. "Before anyone saw them?"

Yan stood in the door for a moment with his eyes half shut.

He'd been asleep when Teague shouted. Maigo knew because his hair was still flat on one side and his topknot was coming down, and because he hadn't been at breakfast, and because these days, on a lot of mornings, Yan was asleep when Teague shouted. He'd woken and come down with his eyes still heavy and stood in the door through all of it and not said anything. Suzu lay round his neck, white, with her green eyes open, both of them, which in the mornings she never had.

Now he shut his own eyes all the way, and tilted his head.

Maigo watched him do it. He'd watched Yan do it on Earth, too, on a voice call at three in the morning in a ranked game, when it had gone quiet and he wanted to know where the other squad was. *Hang on. Let me listen*.

The room waited.

"Yes," said Yan.

He opened his eyes.

"East," he said. "At the very edge. Where I stop being able to." His voice was slow and deep and very calm, the way it always was, the voice everybody in this fort had learned to trust in the first week. "I felt something at dawn. I thought I'd dreamed it." He looked at the map. "Cold candles. A lot. More than the summer."

"How many more?"

"I can't count them from here." Yan was quiet. "Wendy could. When we see them."

The commander nodded slowly. He looked down at his desk. Then he straightened his inkpot, very carefully, square to the edge.

"Thank you," he said. To Yan. And then, after a moment, as if it cost him something, to Maigo: "And thank your tree."

***

Tavrin was on the gatehouse stair when they came out.

Maigo didn't know how long she'd been there. Nobody ever knew how long the elves had been anywhere. She was sitting on the stone steps halfway up with her stave across her knees, in the sun, looking east over the wall at the red, at the green threads on the riverbeds going away toward the mountains. Her face, which was always the colour of birch bark, had gone the colour of birch bark in winter.

Liraun went straight up the stair to her and sat down beside her and put her head on the old woman's shoulder, the way a child does.

Tavrin put one long hand on her pale hair. She didn't take her eyes off the red.

"The tree told you," she said to Maigo.

"Yes."

"How?"

"She put her roots out," said Maigo. "East. All along the old rivers. To feel it if something came." He swallowed. "Like pickets. Soldiers. Who stand a long way out and don't fight. Just so you know."

Tavrin looked at him then.

For a long moment she didn't say anything. Her winter-ice eyes went over him, the way they went over Salty's hammer marks and Niko's hands, reading something. The antlers with the new leaves. The pixel sunglasses. The root foot, red with dust to the ankle.

"A tree," said Tavrin slowly, "that has learned soldiering."

"Teague taught me. I told her."

"Yes." Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The place a smile would have been on anybody younger than the oldest oak on the downs, looking at the edge of the world. "She is a good tree. And you are a good teacher." She looked back at the red. "And it is not ours."

Maigo had heard her say that before. In the winter. At the grave. *It is not ours to name*.

"What isn't?" he said.

But Tavrin only sat on the stair in the sun with her hand on Liraun's hair, looking east, and didn't answer; and after a while Maigo understood she wasn't going to, and went down.

***

He wrote it in the channel at noon.

He sat on the edge of the well in the courtyard with his window open and his root foot in the dust and wrote it, and it took him a long time, because he kept writing it in capitals and deleting it.

Maigo wrote everything in capitals. Everybody knew. He'd written in capitals since the first night of the channel, and he'd written in capitals when Thea went Candle and when Alea won the Fledgling and when Niko did twenty-nine. He'd written in capitals about the noodles. There had hardly been a time in the whole channel when Maigo hadn't. The one everybody remembered was two summers ago, the day after the man on the red. When everyone asked *was it him*, and Maigo had written *he didn't say* in lower case, and the whole channel had gone quiet, because everybody understood what that meant.

He wrote it in lower case.

***

The riders weren't back by dark.

He hadn't thought they would be. A day's ride east and a day back, if they went to the hills. Tomorrow night, maybe. Rhea said it at supper, in the long hall, in a voice that was loud and ordinary on purpose, so that the recruits could hear it and not be frightened: *tomorrow night, maybe, and they'll have found tracks and nothing else, and we'll all feel very silly*. Nobody felt silly. Garth ate his supper sitting very straight, with his eyes on his plate, and didn't have a noodle behind his ear any more. Cook had put out the good bread.

The elves didn't come to supper. Oakes said they were in the chapel with Aldous.

Salty sat beside Maigo on the bench and didn't say anything. That was Salty. He didn't talk when he was thinking; he just sat there being very solid, like a wall you could lean on. Halfway through supper he pushed his own plate along the table so it was in front of Maigo, and Maigo realised he hadn't eaten anything, and ate it.

Wendy, on Salty's other side, said, "A lot."

"A lot," said Maigo.

"More than the summer."

"Yan says."

"Yan says," said Wendy. She was turning her cup round and round on the table, slowly, a quarter turn at a time, the way she did when she was counting something in her head. "The summer was fifty-three scouts. And three big ones." She turned the cup. "I counted."

"I know."

"I'll count these," said Wendy.

She didn't say anything else. She didn't have to.

***

He couldn't sleep.

He didn't try very hard. Round about midnight he got up and put his hood on and went down through the dark fort, past the long hall and the forge with its banked fire glowing red through the shutters and Salty's peppers asleep on the warm stones, and out across the courtyard to the east gate.

There was a little postern beside it. A door in the wall, iron-bound, too small for anyone but a person on foot. The step in front of it was worn into a dip by a great many years of feet. And under the door, between the bottom of the iron and the step, there was a gap, a hand's width, where the ground came through. Red earth. The red, coming in under the wall.

He'd sat on the step of the other postern, the west one, all one night in the winter with his foot pushed under the door, feeling three people walk north. He sat on this one now.

He pushed his root foot through the gap under the door, into the earth on the other side, and let it go down.

***

It was further from here. Everything was. He was an hour's walk from the tree and the stone in the way, and the old dead roots under the red between here and the knoll were dry and grey and hardly carried anything. Like a phone call on a bad line. He had to sit very still and hold his breath to hear it at all.

But he heard it.

The old roots, thin and dry. Then her roots, the ash's, strong and warm and close, going down round the stone and round the cold thing. He felt the cold thing first, as he always did, the way you always notice the one cold tooth. It was still lying with its head up. Listening. Still held.

And past the knoll, out east, her pickets.

They'd stopped flinching at the very tips, out at the foot of the hills. Maigo didn't know why. Then he did. Because there was nothing on them there any more. Whatever had come down out of the hills had come *off* the hills. It was on the red now. On the long green threads down the middle of the old riverbeds, a long way out still, a very long way, but nearer than this morning.

Step-step. Stop.

And the roots under it flinching. All the way along. A long dark stretch of young green on every riverbed, flinching, tight and small, where they were. And then, as they moved, stopping flinching behind them and starting in front. So he could feel them come. Like feeling somebody walk toward you down a long corridor in the dark by the creak of the boards.

They weren't spread out.

That was the thing he noticed, after a while, sitting on the step with his foot under the door and his eyes shut. The first winter, the scouts had come out of the dark across the whole width of the red, spread out like a net, and stopped, and pointed at everything. At the towers. At the gate. At the well. Counting the fort. This wasn't that. They weren't spreading out. They were on the riverbeds, all of them, coming down the old water-courses, single file, every one of them on the green.

On *her* roots.

Coming down her own pickets toward her, as if they were following them home.

***

"Maigo."

He opened his eyes.

Yan was standing in the dark beside the postern, in his long robe, with Suzu round his shoulders and a lamp in his hand that he hadn't lit. Maigo didn't know how long he'd been there. He looked tired. He always looked tired now; it was just that in the dark, with nobody being kind to him about it, he let it show.

"Go to bed," said Yan.

"You go to bed," said Maigo. "You need it more than me."

Yan didn't say anything to that. He sat down on the worn step beside Maigo, slowly, with his back against the iron-bound door, and put the unlit lamp on the ground. Suzu lifted her head off his shoulder and looked down at Maigo's root foot going under the door, and then at Maigo, and then put her head back down.

They sat for a while in the dark.

"They're on her roots," said Maigo at last. "All of them. On the green. They're coming down the riverbeds on the green she put out. Like they're following it in."

Yan didn't answer straight away. He shut his eyes and tilted his head.

"Yes," he said after a while. "They lean."

"What?"

"Their candles. The cold ones." Yan's voice was very low. "I couldn't tell this morning. They were too far. I can tell now. Souls lean. Toward what they love. You've heard me say it. Suzu's leans toward me. Alea's leaned east all summer." He opened his eyes and looked out at the dark beyond the wall that neither of them could see through. "Theirs lean too. All of them. Exactly the same way. Like the ash's leaves."

Maigo's mouth had gone dry.

"Toward what?"

Yan looked at him.

He didn't say it. He didn't have to. There was only one thing out on the red that something cold could lean toward. A small dense coal of wrong, a long way down, under a stone that had been a sword, under a woman called Diwa, held by the roots of a tree Maigo had planted so she wouldn't be alone.

***

"He's not coming," said Maigo.

He said it to the dark. He hadn't meant to say it at all.

Yan didn't answer.

"I'm not *saying* he should," said Maigo quickly. "I'm not. He can't. He's a month away. He's ploughing. The king said. He's doing his list. He's got a jetty and three hens and Alea's going south and he's *happy*, Yan, he's actually happy, you could feel it in her roots in the autumn when he was here, he's—" He stopped. "I'm not saying he should come. I'm just saying he's not."

"I know," said Yan.

"Last time he came."

"Yes."

"Last time we were on the red and it all went wrong and Teague went down and Salty broke his ribs and a line came out of nowhere across a hundred paces and he just—" Maigo made the gesture again, the letting-go-of-a-rope one, in the dark. "Came. Over the red. With a fishing rod." He was quiet. "And he isn't going to, this time. Because he can't. And he'll read it. Tonight. Everything I wrote. On his jetty. And he'll sit there and read it and he won't be able to do anything."

The thought of that sat between them on the step, cold, like a third person.

"He came once," said Yan.

Maigo looked at him.

"Once," said Yan. "Two and a half years. The first winter, when they came to the edge of the red and pointed at the towers: that was us on the wall. The second winter, when they came back. Us. The summer they stood out there at dusk and watched the chapel. Us." He looked at his hands. "He came once, Maigo. On the one day. All the other nights, it was us. We just didn't notice, because nothing happened."

Maigo didn't say anything for a long time.

"That's very Yan of you," he said eventually, thickly.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"It was a bit," said Yan.

***

Yan went to bed in the end. Maigo made him. He walked him all the way to his door and stood there until he heard the bed creak, the way you'd see a friend home after a night out, and Suzu watched him do it over Yan's shoulder with one green eye and didn't say anything Yan passed on, which Maigo decided meant she approved.

Then he went back to the postern and sat down on the step and put his foot under the door again.

He didn't sleep. He didn't try. He sat there in the cold with his hood up and his antlers itching and his root foot in the red, and listened.

Step-step. Stop.

Far off. On the riverbeds. On the green.

And nearer, much nearer, steady, under everything, the ash. Her roots round the stone. Round the cold thing that was lying with its head up, listening. Holding. The way she'd held all winter, and all last summer, and the summer before. Two hands, he'd told Alea. Three, with the spike.

And under the green, faint, warm, enormous, the other one. The old root. The one that had been feeding the flowers for nine years, that had come back every spring and made them grow on dead ground where nothing grew. A great many hands, Liraun had said, and one of them very large. Still there. Even from a lake a month away. Still on the ground.

Four, then. With his.

He put his hand flat on the cold step beside his foot. Stone. You couldn't feel anything through stone. He kept it there anyway.

"I'm here," he told her. Out loud, in the dark, under the wall. "I'm right here. I'm listening now. You don't have to point any more."

Far off, out on the red, a long way east, on a thin green thread of new grass that had never in its life been walked on by anything like this, something went step-step-step.

And stopped.

And Maigo sat on the step until it was light, and counted.
