Aleaka could feel when someone was carrying something.

It had started with animals. The grey horse in the stables that bit everyone but her. The castle cats, six of them, who'd followed her into Lord Vane's banquet like a guard of honour. Brisk, the furious hawk, who sat on nobody's arm but hers and glared at the world as if it owed him money. She'd felt them before she'd ever touched them: a sort of warmth, or a tightness, or a hurt, sitting in them like a stone in a shoe. And she'd found that if she paid attention to it, really paid attention, they'd let her close.

Then, in the winter, it had spread to people.

Not all the time. Not clearly. But sometimes, if she was quiet and someone was close, she could feel the shape of what was in them. Hana, in the mews, with something bright and frightened held down under a lid. Oswin, at midnight, over his map, aching with a question he'd been told three times to leave alone. Kestra, who felt like a closed door with a fire behind it.

She could feel Hana now. Across the sitting room, at the window, the evening after the sword left the capital. Hana was carrying something so heavy that Aleaka could almost see her shoulders bend under it.

She'd been carrying it since the winter.

*Part of it,* she'd said, last week, when Aleaka asked. *Part of it.* And then she'd taken Aleaka's hands and promised again. *When I'm sure, you'll be first.*

Aleaka had believed her. She still did. But she'd sat in the window seat afterwards with her knees pulled up and her tail round her feet, and felt the shape of the thing in Hana, and thought: *She's never going to be sure. Not on her own. Not with something like this.*

And she'd wondered how long she could wait.

***

Hana came to her room at midnight, six days after the sword left.

Aleaka wasn't asleep. She'd been lying on her bed with Brisk on the bedpost and her window open on the ceiling, reading the channel. It had been busy all week, since Cursiffe's Latin: theories and arguments and people who'd never thought about Latin in their lives suddenly having opinions about it. And then, underneath it all, every so often, Gabe or Deston or Xav or John, quiet, adding another line about Declan. *He had a Steam group called something about the night. I never asked what it meant.* *He used to say it before matches. Some phrase. I thought he was just being weird.*

The knock was very soft.

"It's me," said Hana, through the door. "Can I come in?"

Aleaka sat up. Brisk ruffled on the bedpost and glared.

Hana came in with a candle. She wasn't alone. Jared was behind her, and Cursiffe, and they were all three dressed, as if they hadn't been to bed at all. Hana was holding two small books against her chest. Plain brown covers. Cheap paper. The kind that came up from the markets in the spring.

Aleaka knew, before anyone said a word. She could feel it. The lid was off.

"I'm still not sure," said Hana. She stood just inside the door with the candle in one hand and the books in the other, very straight, the way she stood when she was about to say something hard. "I want you to know that first. I'm not sure. I'm less sure than I was in the winter, if anything. Milli's right: it doesn't fit. It can't fit."

"But," said Aleaka.

"But I promised." Hana's voice wobbled, very slightly. "And if I wait until I'm sure, I'll be waiting forever. And sooner or later someone else will put it together, Thea, or Gabe, or anyone, and they'll put it in the channel at two in the morning, and you'll read it there. In a list of forty lines. From someone else." She swallowed. "I'm not having that. You were promised first. So you're first."

She held out the books.

***

They told her all of it.

Sitting on the end of her bed, the three of them, in the candlelight, while Brisk watched from the bedpost. How Oswin had lent Cursiffe the first book in the third week, because Oswin lent everyone everything. How Cursiffe had read it by the fire and gone very quiet, and given it to Jared. How Jared had read it, and gone very quiet too. How the two of them had taken it to Hana, in the winter, after the banquet, and shown her one page.

"This one," said Cursiffe. He opened the first book at a place he obviously knew by heart, and turned it round, and handed it to her.

Aleaka read it.

*On the question of bait, opinion in my town is bitterly divided. Some men swear by worms. Some men swear by bread. One old man swears by a secret paste his grandmother made, which I have tasted, and which is mostly mud. I use none of these. I have a method of my own. I have never once, in all my life, lost a fish.*

*Trust.*

She read it twice. Then she put the book down in her lap, very carefully, and sat there.

She'd known what was coming. She'd known since she felt the lid come off. It didn't help. It hit her anyway, right in the chest, so hard she couldn't breathe for a moment.

*Trust.*

She'd heard it a thousand times. On calls. In chat. In the middle of the night, when he'd said something completely untrustworthy and then added it at the end, in that voice, so that everyone in the call groaned at once. *I'll be on early tomorrow. Trust.* *I would never betray you in this game. Trust.* *It's a good name. One day I'm going to give it to someone who deserves it. Trust.*

"That's him," she said. Her voice came out very small. "That's *him*."

"That's what we thought," said Jared quietly.

"Then why—" She looked up at Hana. "Why didn't you *tell* me? In the winter? Why didn't you tell *anyone*?"

"Because it can't be," said Hana. She said it gently, the way you'd say something to a child who'd fallen over. "Leaka. Listen. The man who wrote this has been writing for years. Oswin has copies going back a long way. The trader brings a new one every spring. And the Hero of the Redmarch, whoever he is, has been the Hero for longer than that. Twenty years, the records say. More." She shook her head. "Ice died in his sleep in Malacca. We were there. All of us were there, in the chat, the morning after. You can't be in Malacca and here twenty years ago at the same time."

"But—"

"So it's someone else." Hana took a breath. "That's what I decided, in the winter. Someone from our world came here before us. A long time before us. Someone who gamed, maybe. Someone who says *trust*, because lots of people say trust. Someone who liked the same things he liked." She looked at the books in Aleaka's lap. "And then last week, Latin. And Gabe saying Ice loved Latin. And I don't know anymore. I just don't know." Her eyes were wet. "I didn't want to give you something to hold on to that turned out to be nothing. I watched you, after. When you didn't come to calls. When you didn't talk. I watched how long it took you to come back."

Aleaka looked down at the book.

"I know," she said. "I know you did." She touched the page, very lightly, with one finger, where it said *trust*. "Can I keep them? Tonight?"

"Of course," said Hana.

"I want to read them. All of them. On my own." Aleaka looked up. "I'll know. If I read them, I'll know. One way or the other."

***

She read all night.

Both books, beginning to end, by the light of one candle and then another, sitting up in bed with the blanket round her shoulders and Brisk asleep on the bedpost with his head under his wing. She read slowly. She'd always read slowly; she stumbled over words out loud and she stumbled over them on the page, too, a bit. But she wanted to read slowly. She wanted to hear it.

And she heard it.

That was the thing she hadn't expected. She'd expected to be looking for clues, the way Cursiffe must have, and Jared, and Hana, sifting every line for something that matched. Instead, about ten pages in, she stopped looking for anything at all, because she didn't need to. She could hear him. The voice in the books wasn't like his voice. It *was* his voice. Older, slower, quieter, as if it had been left out in the weather for a long time. But his. The rhythm of it. The way it set up a joke and then walked away from it without looking back. The way it said something very sad very lightly, so you didn't notice until three sentences later that your eyes were stinging.

She'd spent a year of her life talking to that voice. It had been in her chat every single stream. It had called her at two in the morning to tell her about a fish it had caught in a game. She'd have known it anywhere.

A fox chased off by hens. Spiteful pumpkins. *On Waiting*. The bait. *Trust.*

And about two thirds of the way through the first book, in a short chapter called *On Nets*, she found it.

***

*The small person who runs this house inspected my nets this morning,* it said, *and found them wanting.*

*She did not tell me this kindly. She stood on the end of the jetty with her hands on her hips, at a height somewhat below my elbow, and informed me at length that a man of my age ought to know better than to leave a net in that condition, that she had seen better nets on a scarecrow, and that if I could not be trusted with nets she did not know what I could be trusted with.*

*I said, "Yes, ma'am."*

*I was suitably terrified. I mended the nets.*

*Then, when I had finished, and she was standing over me to inspect the work, I told her that she was very cute when she was bossy.*

*She threw a fish at me.*

*It was a good fish. I ate it later. I have lived in this house a long time, and I have never once won an argument in it, and I have come to understand that this is the natural order of things.*

Aleaka stopped breathing.

She read it again. And again. Sitting very still in the candlelight, with the book in both hands, reading the same eleven lines over and over and over, while something enormous rose up in her chest and filled it and kept rising.

*Yes, ma'am.*

It was theirs. It was *their game*.

Nobody else knew it. Nobody else had ever known it. Not Hana, not Jared, not anyone. It was the thing they did, the two of them, on calls, in chat, in the middle of the night: she'd boss him about, all prim and strict like a terrible teacher, *you will sit up straight, you will finish your vegetables, you will not die to that man again*, and he'd go all small and meek and say *yes, ma'am* in a tiny frightened voice. And she'd get him properly cowed, really properly, and feel very pleased with herself.

And then, every single time, just when she'd won, he'd turn it round.

*You're cute when you're bossy.*

And she'd lose. Every time. She'd go red and splutter and he'd laugh at her, and that would be that, the game over, him winning without even trying. A year of it. Dozens of times. Hundreds. She'd told the channel about it, last week, the night Hako asked what he'd been like. She'd thought she was telling them something nobody else had.

And here it was. In a book. Written by a man by a lake, about his daughter.

The same rhythm. The same words. *Yes, ma'am.* The meek bit. The turn. *Very cute when she was bossy.* Even the losing. *I have never once won an argument in it.*

Aleaka put the book down on the blanket and put both her hands over her face and sat there in the candlelight, shaking.

On the bedpost, Brisk woke up, and looked at her, and for once in his whole furious life didn't glare. He just hopped down onto the bed beside her, awkwardly, and pressed his feathered side against her arm, and stayed there.

***

She went to them at dawn. All of them. She didn't wait to be fetched.

They were at the breakfast table in the sitting room, the four of them and Oswin, not eating much. Hana looked as if she hadn't slept either. Milli was beside her, with his arm along the back of her chair. Jared was by the fire. Cursiffe had the second book's twin open on the table in front of him, the copy Oswin had found in the archive, and wasn't reading it.

Aleaka put the first book down on the table in front of Hana, open at *On Nets*.

"Read that," she said.

Hana read it. Then she passed it to Milli. Milli read it, frowning, and passed it to Jared, and Jared read it and passed it to Cursiffe, and Cursiffe read it and looked up at Aleaka with his calm face gone not calm at all.

"I don't understand," said Milli slowly. "It's sweet. It's a man and his daughter. What is it?"

"It's ours," said Aleaka.

They all looked at her.

"*Yes, ma'am,*" said Aleaka. Her voice was shaking; she let it. "That's ours. Mine and his. That was our game. I'd boss him about, and he'd say *yes, ma'am* like he was terrified, and then he'd turn it round and say I was cute when I was bossy, and I'd lose." She swallowed. "Every time. For a year. *Nobody* knew that. I never told anyone. I told the channel last week, for Hako, and that was the first time." She put her finger on the page. "And here it is. Exactly. In a book that's been coming down from the north for years."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't care about the timeline," said Aleaka. "I'm sorry, Milli. I know it doesn't fit. I know you're right. I don't care." She looked round at them all. "I know his voice. I talked to it every day for a year. I'd know it in the dark. I'd know it underwater." Her eyes were streaming now. "It's him. I don't know how. I don't know when, or where, or *why*. But that's him."

Hana got up from the table, and came round it, and put her arms round her.

***

They argued about the channel all morning.

Not angrily. Just carefully, the way Group 2 did everything. Round and round the table, with Oswin taking notes he couldn't read back because half of it was in English.

Milli thought they should wait. *Until we know more. Until it fits.* Jared thought they couldn't. *The Latin's out. Gabe and Deston are already halfway there. If we sit on this, and they find out we had it all winter, how does that look?* Cursiffe thought they should share the books but not their conclusions: *let everyone read them and decide for themselves*. Oswin, who couldn't follow half of it, kept asking what the books had to do with the summoning and whether he could please be told, because he'd been told to leave it three times and was finding it very difficult.

Hana listened to all of it. Then she looked at Aleaka.

"You were first," she said. "It's your call."

Aleaka thought about it for a long time.

She thought about the months after. The grey time. She thought about all the people in the channel who were in it now: Gabe, quietly adding lines about Steam groups. Hako with his notebook. Gigichi, answering a message three years late. Newish, *it was the right answer*.

"Share them," she said. "All of it. The books, and the bits that sound like him. Let everyone see." She took a breath. "But I want to write *mine*. Myself."

***

So they did it that afternoon, Cursiffe first, in his careful lines.

[ Cursiffe: Something Group 2 should have shared a long time ago. I'm sorry we didn't.
Cursiffe: There are books. Small ones, no author. A trader brings them down from the north every spring. Oswin has two.
Cursiffe: They're about fishing, mostly. And a small town by the water. And they're funny. Very funny. In a way some of you will know.
Cursiffe: This is from the first one. About bait. ]

And then the bait passage. All of it, line by line, ending in the one word on its own.

[ Cursiffe: Trust. ]

He let it sit. Aleaka, watching her own window at the table, saw it sit there at the bottom of the channel with nothing under it, for a long time.

Then she wrote hers.

[ Aleaka: And this one is from a chapter about nets. He's writing about his daughter.
Aleaka: "I said, 'Yes, ma'am.' I was suitably terrified. I mended the nets."
Aleaka: "Then I told her that she was very cute when she was bossy. She threw a fish at me."
Aleaka: That was our game. Mine and Ice's. I told you about it last week. Nobody else ever knew it.
Aleaka: I've read both books now. It's his voice. I'm sorry. I know it can't be. It's his voice. ]

The channel went silent.

***

The channel filled up. People asking for more. People quoting lines back. Hako, who wrote: *Adding this to the book. A different book. You know what I mean.* Tiger, who wrote *the PUMPKINS are late out of SPITE?? that's the most him thing i've ever heard*. Kei, careful: *Aleaka, are you okay? This must be a lot.*

And someone, after a while, from the capital, the one thing nobody had said yet.

[ Milli: I believe Aleaka knows his voice. I do.
Milli: But he died. We were all there when we heard. Whoever wrote this, it can't be him.
Milli: I'm sorry. Someone has to say it. ]

Nobody answered that for a while. Then Gabe:

[ Gabe: i know. i went to the funeral.
Gabe: i still know his voice. ]

***

Aleaka noticed it in the evening, going back through what was left in the channel, which wasn't much. Forty lines. Most of the afternoon had scrolled off the top long ago. But she'd been watching, all day, for one particular thing, and she'd noticed it.

Group 1 hadn't said a word.

Xola, yes, once, early: *oh my goodness*. PenguChi: *☺ he sounds kind*. Gigichi, sharp: *Stop saying sorry, Aleaka. You didn't do anything wrong.* But Thea, who'd written the channel, who answered everything, who'd have had a theory by the second line. Nothing. And Higu, who'd made the rules, who always said something calm and exactly right at exactly the moment you needed it.

Nothing at all.

Aleaka looked at that for a long time, sitting in her window seat in the evening, with the city going gold below her. She didn't know what it meant. She thought about the way it felt when someone was carrying something. She thought that, if she'd been in Wrenhaven, standing in the same room as Thea and Higu, she might have been able to feel it from here.

She wasn't. So she couldn't.

But she wondered.

***

Far to the north, at the end of a jetty on a lake, a man sat in the late sun with a net across his knees that he'd stopped mending some time ago, and read.

All of it. The bait. *Trust.* The nets. *Yes, ma'am.* Gabe's *that's him*. Milli's *it can't be*. He read every line about his own books, the books he'd written by the fire on winter nights to pass the time, and sent south with a trader every spring, under no name at all, never once imagining who might read them.

He'd written *On Nets* when Alea was nine. The first winter after. She'd been so angry, that whole year. At him, at the world, at the Redmarch, at everything. And one morning she'd stood on the end of the jetty with her hands on her hips and told him off about the nets, the first time she'd told him off about anything in months, and he'd said *yes, ma'am* without thinking, the way he used to, a long time ago, to somebody else.

And she'd laughed. For the first time that year.

So he'd kept doing it.

He hadn't thought about where it came from in a long time. He'd forgotten, almost, that it had come from anywhere.

He sat on the jetty with his window open in front of him, and read Aleaka's lines over again. *That was our game. Nobody else ever knew it.* And then *I'm sorry. I know it can't be. It's his voice.*

Up at the cabin, a door banged, and Alea came down the path with a basket, calling something about supper.

He closed the window with two fingers. He picked up the net. And when she got to the end of the jetty and stood over him with her hands on her hips, looking at the net with deep disapproval, he looked up at her and said, very meekly, "Yes, ma'am."

She stared at him. Then she laughed, the way she always did.

He laughed with her. And didn't tell her why his eyes were wet.
