Xola was the last of Group 1, and everybody was being very kind about it, which was the worst part.

Not that she minded being last. She'd decided that in the autumn, crying, which was how she decided most things. Somebody had to be. Higu had been first, after Thea, in the spring of the first year, on a roof, with a moth. PenguChi in the summer, in the yard, with Alea's hands in hers. Gigichi in the frost, in a pumpkin field, in the dark, with a hedge. And Thea was at the third tier now, Lantern, the first of all thirty-five, and nobody even counted Thea any more; Thea was Thea.

And Xola was ninety-seven.

She'd been ninety-seven since the end of the summer. Since the road north. She'd watched it sit there in her window every morning, ninety-seven, like a word on the tip of her tongue, and every morning she'd put her hand in the washbasin in the upper room at the militia hall and asked the water to turn, and it had turned. It always turned. It had turned for her since the first winter. Round and round in the basin like a little whirlpool, while Squiddles on her head went pink with delight and reached down one tentacle to touch it.

It turned. It just never did anything else.

*It wants to go round*, she'd said to Thea, once, in the second winter, at two in the morning, when neither of them could sleep. *That's all water wants, in a basin. To go round. I ask it to do the thing it already wants and it does it, and I'm stuck there, and I don't know what else to ask.*

Thea had looked at her with her starry blue eyes for a long time, and said, very gently, *maybe it's not the water that's stuck*.

Xola had cried about that for a week.

***

It was midwinter eve, and Odette had the whole town working.

Xola loved midwinter in Wrenhaven. She'd loved it the first year, when she'd been frightened of everything and Thea had made a flame sit on her finger in the middle of the square and the whole town had gasped. She'd loved it the second year, when Thea wrote *Still here* into the fire and every lamp in Wrenhaven flared, and a man had stood at the edge of the light with a fox girl in front of him and looked at the five of them one by one. She'd cried at both. She was planning to cry at this one. She'd told everyone. Gigichi had said *you're going to cry at the bonfire, that's not news, that's weather*.

Odette had sent the five of them down to the lake for the afternoon to help Alea with the lanterns. That was the job. Forty paper lanterns for the lake road, to be lit at dusk tomorrow and set along the walls all the way from the square to the water, because last year the man had come up the lake road in the dark to the bonfire for the first time in eight years and Odette had decided, without asking anybody, that this year the road was going to be lit for him.

*He'll hate it*, Alea had said.

*He'll hate it*, Odette had agreed. *He'll walk up it anyway.*

So they were at the cabin, at the long table, with paper and paste and little wire frames and a great deal of mess, and Alea in the middle of it bossing everyone, and Gigichi complaining about the paste, and PenguChi folding lanterns very slowly and perfectly one at a time with Waddles asleep on her feet, and Higu at the window, where she always was, writing. And Thea trying to make the paper glow on its own with a line of code, and failing, and swearing, quietly, in English.

And the man on the end of the jetty, fishing through his hole in the ice, where he always was in the afternoons now. Two fish for the hall in the morning. And then, in the afternoon, he said, for himself. For the quiet. Xola could see him from the window, past Higu's shoulder: a dark shape on a stool at the end of the long white line of the jetty, with the frozen lake going away grey and flat on every side of him to the hills.

And the children.

There were always children on the lake now, in the afternoons. Since the ice had come in thick. The whole of Wrenhaven's children, it seemed like, on the ice at the bottom of the lake road and along the shore by the reeds, sliding, shrieking, falling over, being shouted at by older brothers. Odette said it was because of the man. Odette said children had always slid on the lake in the winter and then nine years ago they'd stopped, because their mothers didn't want them near the cabin at the bottom of the road with the soldier in it who didn't speak to anyone. And this winter, they'd come back.

Xola watched them through the window and smiled. She couldn't help it. Squiddles, on her head, went faintly gold.

There was one at the edge of all the others. Small, in a red cap, with a little wooden sled on a rope. Running. Further out than the rest. Toward the near end of the lake, where the reeds were, and past them, where the water went out under the ice toward the mill and the river.

Xola watched him run.

She didn't know why she was watching him. Something in her chest was watching him. The same thing that watched Thea when she went quiet, and Gigichi when she was about to snap, and the man on the jetty when he lifted his hand at the top of the lake road and his shoulders did the thing. The thing in her that was always watching for someone about to be hurt.

She put down her paste.

"Thea," she said.

***

The ice by the outflow was thin. Everybody in Wrenhaven knew that. Alea said so afterwards, over and over, as if it would help. It was the first thing every child in the town was told, before they were old enough to walk on the lake. *Not past the reeds. Never past the reeds*. Because past the reeds the water was moving, all winter, under the ice, going out of the lake toward the mill and the Wren and the sea, and moving water didn't freeze the way still water did. It made a skin. A pretty one. White on top, like the rest. And under it, nothing.

The boy in the red cap's sled had got away from him. That was all. He'd let go of the rope for one second, laughing, and the sled had gone sliding off on its own across the ice, past the reeds, toward the white, and he'd gone after it. Because it was his sled. Because he was seven. Because nobody had told him the thing everybody in Wrenhaven was told, or they had, and he'd been seven when they told him too.

Xola was out of the cabin door before Thea had turned round.

She'd never run like that. She didn't run; she was small and she cried and she had a squid on her head and long hair that went past her knees and got caught in everything. She ran. Down the shingle. Onto the ice, which sang under her boots the way it always did, a long low note going out in every direction. Toward the reeds. Shouting. She didn't know what. A name, she thought afterwards; she didn't know his name. *Stop*, probably. *Stop stop stop*.

He didn't stop. He was seven. He had his hand out for the sled's rope.

And the ice opened under him like a mouth.

***

There wasn't any sound.

That was the thing she'd remember. She'd thought there would be. A crack, a crash, a scream. There wasn't. There was a boy in a red cap running on white ice, and then there was a dark hole in the white where he'd been, and a red cap lying on the edge of it, and a wooden sled still sliding, slowly, on its own, away across the skin of the ice toward the mill.

And nothing.

Xola got to the hole.

She didn't remember the last of it. The running. She was just there, on her knees on the ice at the edge of a black hole the size of a cartwheel, with the cold coming up out of it like breath, and the red cap beside her hand. And the water in the hole was black, and moving. Not up and down. Sideways. Going out from under the ice, toward the mill, steady, the way a river goes, the way it had been going all winter under everybody's feet.

He wasn't in it.

He'd gone under. The current had taken him under the ice, sideways, toward the mill, the moment he went in. He was under the white now. Somewhere. Under her. Under the ice she was kneeling on, in the black, going away.

Behind her, a long way off, she heard Thea scream her name.

She heard the man on the jetty stand up.

She didn't know how she heard that. She heard it anyway. The stool going over, on the boards. And then nothing, because she wasn't listening any more.

She went in.

***

The cold was a hand closing.

Not on her skin. Inside her. It went through her coat and her dress and her skin all at once and closed round her heart and squeezed, and she opened her mouth to scream and the lake came in.

And she breathed it.

She didn't decide to. She'd have drowned, deciding. Her body did it. Behind her ears, under her hair, the two feathery purple things that had been on her model on Earth because the artist had thought they were pretty, that she'd come through the floor of the hall with and had thought, for two years and a half, were decoration: they opened. All the way. She felt them open, the way you feel your hands open when you've been clenching them all day. Fanning out, in the black water, behind her head.

And the water went in through them and out through them, cold, cold, so cold it burned, and there was air in it.

She was breathing.

Under the ice. In the black. In a lake in the north in the middle of winter. She was breathing water like a fish.

*Oh*, thought Xola. And then, quite clearly, with the part of her that was still twenty-six and from another world and had once been called an axolotl girl in a chat as a joke: *oh, I'm an* axolotl.

She'd have laughed, if she'd had air for it.

She didn't. She had water. She turned over in the black and looked for the boy.

***

She couldn't see anything.

It was black. Not dark; black. The ice over her a dim grey lid, very far up, or very close; she couldn't tell. And everything else black water, and cold, and moving. Pulling her sideways. Toward the mill. The current had her the way it had him, steady and patient, the way water is patient, going where it was going.

She couldn't see him.

And then, on her head, Squiddles lit up.

She'd forgotten him. She'd forgotten everything. He'd come in with her; of course he had, he went everywhere with her, he'd have gone into a fire with her. He was clinging to her hair with all his small arms, and he'd gone a colour she'd never seen him go. Not pink. Not gold. A deep clear shining blue, like the lake must be in summer, like a lamp behind blue glass. All of him. Glowing. Lighting the black water round her head in a soft blue ball the width of her arms.

She could see.

Ice, over her, grey and rough on the underside, with bubbles caught in it like coins. Black water, going sideways. Weed, far below, waving. And a little way off, downstream, against the underside of the ice where the current had pinned him, a small shape in a brown coat. Not moving. Arms out. Face up against the ice, as if he'd been trying to find the hole again and the current had pressed him flat against the lid of the world.

Xola went to him.

She didn't swim. She'd never been able to swim, on Earth; she'd been the one at the shallow end with the inflatable. She let the current take her. It was going his way anyway. Squiddles lit the water. And she reached him, and got her arms round him, round his middle, his brown coat, his small cold body, and held on.

He was so light. Like holding a coat with nobody in it.

He wasn't breathing.

***

She tried to go back.

She kicked. Against the current. Back the way she'd come, toward the hole, toward the grey and the light and Thea screaming her name. She kicked with her legs in her heavy skirts and her boots full of water and the boy in her arms, and the current didn't notice. It held her where she was. It pressed her and the boy against the underside of the ice, gently, steadily, the way it had been pressing everything all winter, and took them both a little further toward the mill.

She couldn't fight it. She wasn't strong. She'd never once in her life been strong. She was the one who cried.

*It wants to go round*, she'd told Thea. *That's all water wants*.

She stopped kicking.

She hung there, under the ice, in the black, in the blue light, with a seven-year-old boy who wasn't breathing in her arms and the lake going in and out through her gills, and the current taking them both toward the mill. And she thought about the washbasin. In the upper room at the militia hall. Every morning for two years. Her hand in it, and the water turning.

It hadn't been stuck. Thea was right. It had never been the water.

The water had always wanted to go round.

*She'd* been asking it to go round in a basin. Round and round, the same place, nowhere. Because a basin was safe, and small, and it was the only thing she'd ever been brave enough to ask.

She put her hand out. Flat. Into the black water, in front of her, in the blue light. And felt for it: the place under everything, the gap, the thing Thea had written to all of them about the first winter. *You have to write. One small line. In its language, not yours*. She felt for the water's language. Cold and patient and enormous, a whole lake of it, going out under the ice toward the mill and the river and the sea the way it went every winter, because that was where water went, eventually. Round. The biggest round there was. From the sky to the hills to the lake to the river to the sea and back up into the sky again.

Round.

And she wrote into it. Not *stop*. Not *turn*. Not *back*. She wrote the thing it already wanted, only bigger than she'd ever dared, and with one small change at the end of it, the way you'd ask a friend who was going somewhere anyway if they wouldn't mind dropping something off on the way.

*Round. Bring him home.*

***

The lake turned.

She felt it go. Not the water round her. All of it. The whole slow weight of the lake under the ice, every bit of it, from the reeds to the far shore, hesitating, for one long heartbeat. The way a person hesitates who's been walking somewhere for a long time and somebody they love says their name.

And then going round.

A current. A new one. Not out toward the mill. Round. A slow enormous wheel of water under the ice, turning, the way the water turned in her basin every morning, only the basin was the whole lake now and she was in the middle of it. It took her. It took the boy. It lifted them off the underside of the ice, gently, and carried them, turning, the long way round, through the black, under the grey lid of the world, with Squiddles lit blue on her head like a lantern. Away from the mill. Away from the river. Back into the lake.

Toward the jetty.

She didn't know that, then. She didn't know where it was taking them. She just held on to the boy and let it, the way you let somebody carry you who's stronger than you, and breathed the lake in and out, and thought, quite clearly, *I'm not stuck. I was never stuck. I was just asking for a basin.*

And then a hand came down through the water from above her and closed round her wrist.

***

He'd been at the hole.

His hole. The one at the end of the jetty, a pace out from the last post, that he cut every winter with an iron auger he'd made himself, for two fish in the morning and the quiet in the afternoon. He'd run to the end of the jetty when she went in, Alea said afterwards. Not toward the reeds. Away from them. To the end of the jetty, to his own hole, and gone down on his knees on the ice beside it and put his arm into the black water up to the shoulder, and waited.

Because he could feel them. Alea said. He could feel candles the way Yan could; he'd always been able to. And he'd felt hers go into the water, and his go out, and then hers stop, under the ice. And then, after a long time, after a very long time, he'd felt hers start moving again. Not toward the mill. Round. The long way. Across the whole of the lake. Toward him.

And he'd put his arm in the hole and waited for it.

He pulled her up out of the black so hard she came up out of the hole like a fish out of a stream, her and the boy together, onto the ice, onto the end of the jetty, into the grey daylight and the cold air and the noise. Everybody. Thea, screaming. Gigichi, on her knees, swearing in two languages. PenguChi, with her hands out. Higu, white, running down the jetty with her book still in her hand. Alea. The children from the ice, all of them, in a ring, silent, with their mouths open.

And the man, on his knees on the end of the jetty, with his arm soaked to the shoulder and water running off it, and his hand still round her wrist.

Xola coughed. The lake came out of her. Out of her mouth, out of her gills, which folded themselves back down behind her ears as if they were embarrassed to have been seen. She breathed air. It hurt.

She didn't care.

She let go of the man's hand and turned to the boy.

***

He was lying on the boards on his back with his red face gone white and his lips blue and his eyes shut. Not breathing. A small boy in a brown coat on the end of a jetty in the snow, with the lake running out of his clothes onto the boards.

Somebody was crying. A great many people were crying. Xola wasn't. That was the strange thing, afterwards. She was the one who cried at everything, at pumpkins and penguins and the snow, and she was kneeling on the end of a jetty over a boy who wasn't breathing and she wasn't crying at all.

She put her hands on his chest.

Flat. Both of them. The way she'd put them on the water under the ice. And felt.

She could feel it. The lake. Inside him. Not much; a cupful, two. Cold, and black, and still, sitting in his chest where the air should be, the way water sits in a basin. Patient. Not knowing it wasn't supposed to be there. It had come in with him when he went under, the way water goes into anything, because that's what water does. It goes where it can.

*Round*, she thought, to it. Not in the gap, this time. Just to it. The way you'd talk to somebody. *You're not home. This isn't the lake. Come home.*

And she pressed, once, with both hands. Not hard.

The water came out of him.

All of it. At once. Up out of his chest and out of his mouth in a long cold rush, onto the boards, and away between the planks of the jetty, down, into the hole at the end, into the lake. Going home. Round.

And the boy coughed.

***

He coughed, and then he screamed, the way small children scream when they wake up somewhere terrible and don't know why. And then a woman was there, on the jetty, a big woman in an apron with flour on her arms and her face gone grey, who'd come running all the way down the lake road from the square without stopping. The egg-woman. His mother. Robin, she was saying. *Robin. Robin*. She had him in her arms. She was on her knees on the jetty holding a soaking screaming seven-year-old against her chest so hard he couldn't breathe again, and nobody stopped her.

Xola sat back on her heels on the boards.

Her hands were white. She looked at them. Not pale; white. Dead white, the fingers, all of them, to the second knuckle, the way the man had told them hands went in the first winter if you stayed out on the ice too long. Frostbite. She'd read about it. She'd never seen it. She held her hands up in front of her face and looked at the dead white fingers and thought, very calmly, the way you think things when you're very cold, *oh. I'm going to lose those.*

And watched them come back.

Not fast. Not like magic in a game. Slowly. The white going, from the tips inward, the way frost goes off a window when the sun gets to it. Pink coming up under it. Then red. Then her own fingers, her own hands, aching, stinging, burning, alive. Ten fingers. All of them. Like they'd never been touched.

*Would you grow back a finger if you lost one?* Alea had asked her, the first week, in the militia hall kitchen, sixteen, curious, all ears.

*I don't* know, Xola had said.

She knew now.

***

She looked up.

The man was still on his knees on the end of the jetty. He hadn't moved. He was looking at her. At her hands, and then at her face, and then at the place on the boards where she'd knelt over the boy with both hands flat on his chest.

She couldn't see his face. Nobody could. But she could see his shoulders, and they'd gone. All the way. And his hand, the one that had pulled her out of the lake, was shaking.

"Ice?" said Xola.

He didn't answer for a long time.

"She used to do that," he said, at last. Very quietly. In English. So that only the five of them and Alea understood it. "Diwa. Just like that. Both hands. Flat." He was looking at the boards. "In the infirmary tent. On boys they brought in off the red who weren't breathing. She'd kneel on them like that and press once and say something to them under her breath, and they'd cough." His voice went. "I never knew what she said."

Alea had come down onto her knees beside him. She didn't say anything. She put her hand on his arm, the wet one.

Xola looked at her own hands. Pink, aching, alive.

"I said *come home*," she said. "To the water. I said *you're not home, this isn't the lake, come home*."

The man made a sound.

It wasn't a word. It was the sound something makes when it's been held very tight for a long time and somebody touches it in exactly the right place. He put his free hand over his face. Not his eyes. All of it. And knelt there on the end of his jetty in the snow with his daughter's hand on his arm, and didn't say anything else.

Xola didn't either.

She started crying, finally. Properly. The way she did everything. All at once, with her whole face, kneeling on the end of a jetty soaked to the skin with her hair frozen into ropes and a squid on her head gone the deepest, brightest gold she had ever seen him go.

***

Her window opened while she was still crying.

She didn't ask it to. It just came up, in front of her face, white and plain, on the end of the jetty, while Thea was wrapping a coat round her and Gigichi was wrapping another coat round the first one and PenguChi was saying *breathe, Xola, breathe* in the voice that did things to rooms.

She looked at it through her tears.

*Tide*.

She thought about it. She'd think about it for a long time, on the walk up the lake road, and in the bath Odette made Maren run for her at the militia hall, scalding, with Squiddles floating in it on his back. *Tide*. Not *Turn*. Not *Round*. Not anything small. The biggest round there was, that the sea did twice a day without anybody asking it. Going out, and coming back. Taking things away, and bringing them home.

*Bring him home*, she'd written.

It had.

"Thirty-two," said Higu.

She was standing at the end of the jetty with her book open, the way she always was, writing. Her hand wasn't steady. Xola had never once in her life seen Higu's hand not be steady. "Xola. You're thirty-two. You're the last of us."

"I know," said Xola, crying.

"Group 1," said Higu. "All five." She wrote it. Xola watched her write it, very small, in the book, and then underline it, which Higu never did. "All five of us."

"*I KNOW*," said Xola, and laughed, and cried harder, and Squiddles on her head went gold and then blue and then gold again as if he couldn't decide, and reached down one small arm and patted her on the forehead.

***

The egg-woman found her at the top of the lake road.

They were walking Xola up to the militia hall, all of them, in a crowd, with Thea holding one of her hands and Gigichi the other and PenguChi behind with Waddles and Higu in front with her book, and half the children of Wrenhaven trailing after them in silence. And the egg-woman was standing at the corner where the lake road came into the square, with Robin wrapped in a blanket in her arms, asleep, finally, with his red cap back on his head.

She stopped Xola in the road.

She didn't say anything. She was a big woman, red-faced, with flour on her arms, who'd sold eggs to the summoned every week for two years and a half and never once said more to them than the price. She stood in the road and looked at Xola. At her hair, frozen into ropes. At the purple gills behind her ears, folded down. At the squid on her head. At her hands, pink and whole.

And then she shifted the sleeping boy onto one arm and took Xola's hand in her free one, and held it.

Just held it. For a long time. In the road, in the snow, with the whole square watching.

"His leg," said the egg-woman, at last. Her voice was rough. "His brother's. The year before you came. The fisherman always asks after it. Every week. For two years, he's asked, *how's the boy's leg*, and I tell him." She looked down at the sleeping child in her arm. "I never knew why he asked. It was a broken leg. It mended."

Xola didn't say anything.

"I know now," said the egg-woman. "He asks because he's seen what doesn't mend." She squeezed Xola's hand once, hard, the way you squeeze the hand of somebody at a graveside. "And you've mended it. Mine. Today."

She let go. She turned and carried her son away across the square toward the bakery, where Edric was standing in his doorway with flour to his elbows and his face working.

Xola stood in the road and watched her go.

"I didn't mend anything," she said. To Thea. Very small. "I just asked the water to bring him back."

Thea held her hand.

"That's what mending is," said Thea. "You ask the thing to go where it was always supposed to go." She was quiet. "I didn't know that either. Till just now."

It went the way the channel went. All of it, at once, everybody, in capitals, through the night. Maigo first, larger than Xola's capitals, which Xola hadn't thought was possible. Tiger, who wrote *XOLA* forty times. Kei, careful: *Robin's lungs. Watch him for fever, two days.* Rachael: *That's not how lungs work. I don't care. Well done.* Chris: *count stands at 32*. Yan: *Okay*, and then, a moment later, *Suzu says the water must have liked you. She says water doesn't do that for everybody*. Hana: *Good*. Malcolm: *IF a lake THEN*, and then nothing, as if he'd given up.

And Gigichi, who'd been in the next room, and could have come in and said it, and wrote it in the channel instead, so that everybody would see:

*the last of us. all five. told you you'd get there. crying in the bath is fine. i'm crying on the stairs.*

Xola read it lying back in the hot water with her eyes running and her pink hands folded on her chest and her gills, behind her ears, fanning very slightly in the steam, as if they'd decided, now they'd been seen, that they didn't need to hide.

Tomorrow was midwinter. There would be a bonfire, and forty paper lanterns on the lake road, and a speech from the mayor that was longer than last year's. And she'd cry at it. She'd told everyone she would.

She was going to cry at it so much.
