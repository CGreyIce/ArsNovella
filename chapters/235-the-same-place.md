Three years to the day since the hall, Yan woke before it was light and lay in his bunk and did the sum.

He did it every year. He'd done it the first year, on the wall, and the second, at the grave, and he did it now in the dark of the barracks at Cinderhold with Suzu asleep on his collarbone, very small, the size of a scarf, her head tucked under his jaw. Three years here. Thirty-six months. And the slow clock, which Thea had found in the first spring and nobody had ever been able to make tick any faster: about eleven months here to one at home.

Three months, then. A little over.

Three months since a Tuesday evening in a flat with the curtains open. Three months since he'd sat at a desk with his headset on and his mother in the next room with the radio, and stood up to go and read her the gas bill, because it had come that morning and she'd been holding it all day waiting for him, the way she held all the post, in a neat pile on the kitchen table under the salt so it wouldn't blow away; and he'd got as far as the door.

He didn't know who'd read her the gas bill.

Nobody, probably. That was the thing he came back to, every year, doing the sum in the dark. His siblings wouldn't. They never had. And there was a pile of post on the kitchen table under the salt, three months of it, getting higher, with nobody to read it to her, and she'd be sitting at the table with her hand flat on the top of the pile, the way she did, feeling the edges of the envelopes. Knowing what most of them were by the shape. Waiting.

He lay in the dark and thought about that until he couldn't.

Then he thought about the other thing, which was worse. Which was that he didn't know if he'd go back.

He'd said it out loud once. To Ice, on the road east last summer, by a fire, the first night. *I don't know if I'd go.* And Ice had said *it's allowed*, and *you might get to choose*, and *that's a harder thing than not getting to*. Yan had thought about it every night since. He wanted the door. He wanted it more than anything he'd ever wanted. He wanted a Sun, whoever it was, to stand in the blank half of the fourteenth ring and fill it, so that there'd be a way back; so that somewhere in another world a woman with her hand on a pile of post could, one day, hear the door go, and his voice in the hall.

And he didn't know if he'd walk through it.

He didn't know if he'd go back to the flat and the radio and the gas bill and the siblings who didn't come, and the desk, and the headset, and the life that had been the only one he had until three years ago. Or stay. Here. With a fort on the edge of a red plain, and a chaplain who'd given him a quarterstaff and said *until*, and four friends who'd come out of the hall holding on to him, and a snake who wasn't a bit.

He'd decided not to decide. Until he was standing in it. He thought that was probably cowardice. He thought it was probably also the only honest thing he could do.

Suzu moved on his collarbone. Not awake. The small shift she made when she knew he was thinking about something in a way she didn't like.

*Sleep, Yun*, she said. In his head. Cool and low and very nearly asleep herself.

"In a minute."

*You always say a minute.*

"I always mean it."

*You never mean it*, said Suzu, and was asleep again, the way she could be, all at once, with her tail round two of his fingers.

***

He was tired.

He'd been tired since the grave. Last summer, on the night of the ninth, on the knoll under a full moon, when he'd stood beside Ice in front of a black stone and seen colours for the first time, Alea's green and Aldous's white and Suzu's, the same green; and something had gone out of him. Not much. A breath. And it hadn't come back. He'd thought it would. It hadn't.

It didn't hurt. It wasn't an illness; Hesper had looked at him, and Aldous had prayed over him, and Kei had written him eleven pages from Thornwall on a slate in Hisui's careful hand, and none of them had found anything. It was just that he was tired. That the east stair had thirty-nine steps and he stopped at the top now, every time, with his hand on the wall, and breathed. That he slept nine hours where he'd slept six. That some mornings, when he looked at people, at their lights, the candles in the middle of them that he'd been looking at every day for three years, the colours took a moment to come.

Suzu knew what it was. He was nearly sure. She'd said one word about it, once, in the winter, to the elves, so loudly that Hisui had heard it from the far side of the kingdom through a snake cord and gone white. *ENOUGH*. And then she hadn't said anything about it since, and he hadn't asked, because when she didn't want to say a thing she didn't, and asking only made her sad.

He went up the east stair, at dawn, on the third anniversary of the hall. Thirty-nine steps. He stopped at the top with his hand on the wall and breathed, and Suzu on his collarbone pressed her head against his jaw, very slightly, the way she did when he stopped.

Then he went along the wall to look at the knoll.

***

It was green.

It had been green since the spring. Since a morning on the last of the old moon when the scouts had turned round and gone east, and the stone had rung, and something under it had put its head down; and the next day, Maigo said, the green had started to go out from the knoll the way water goes out across a floor. Down the slopes. Into the riverbeds. Along the old dry channels that came out of the hills from Kharvos and went past the knoll and away to the south, that had been grey stripes through the red for ten years. All summer. A little every day.

From the east wall now, in the first light, Yan could see it all. The red, going away to the mountains. The riverbeds across it, three of them, curving, coming together below the knoll. And down every one of them, as far as he could see, a long ribbon of green. Not grass, quite. Something shorter and softer, with small white and blue flowers in it that the fort had stopped being able to name, because none of the old soldiers had ever seen them anywhere but there.

The knoll in the middle of it. A low green hill an hour out along the markers, with an ash tree on top.

He could see the ash from here. Everybody could. A small green flame on the knoll, against the red. And if you looked at it with the other eyes, the ones he'd had since the first winter, you could see its light: big, slow, old, older than it should have been for a tree that had gone in the ground in the first spring, because Maigo had asked it to grow and it had listened. Green. A deep still green with gold at the edges, like a pond with the sun on it.

And beside it, very close, on its east side, where the elf had knelt barefoot on the grass in the spring and put a seed in with her own hand because trees shouldn't carry seeds, a second light.

Very small. A spark. The light of something a hand high.

Liraun's seedling.

It had come up at midsummer. Maigo had felt it come up from the barracks, in the middle of the night, and sat up in his bunk and shouted *SHE'S UP* loudly enough to wake the whole of Group 3 and most of the fort, and then lain down again and cried for an hour into his hoodie. It had two leaves. Then four. Then a stem as thick as a pencil and a sort of bud at the top, and a light the size of a lamp-spark that Yan could see from the east wall if he looked for it.

And it was on its own.

That was what Maigo said. In the first week, and every week after. Sitting on the grass on the knoll beside it with his root foot flat on the green and his face screwed up behind his sunglasses. *It's on its own down there. It's a hand from her. A hand. And it can't hear her. And she can't hear it. They're the same kind and they're a hand apart and they can't hear each other.*

Yan had looked, with the other eyes. Maigo was right. Two lights, side by side on a knoll, one big and one small. And between them, a hand of ordinary ground, and nothing going across it at all.

***

Maigo had tried three times.

Yan had watched all three. He went out to the knoll every day; he'd done that since the first spring. It was an hour along the markers, at Salty's pace, which was the pace everyone walked out there now. He went with Aldous, mostly, or with Maigo, or alone, with Suzu, and sat with his back against the cairn and looked at things.

The first time was at the end of midsummer. Maigo had found a book. Not a book; a page, a single page in Aldous's chapel library, in a hand nobody could read but Aldous, about orchards. About how the old orchard men in the south made a good apple grow on a hardy root: you cut both, a clean slanting cut, and fitted them together, and bound them with bast and sealed them with wax, and waited, and if you'd done it right, they took.

Maigo had done it right. Yan had watched him. He'd got Hesper's smallest knife, the one for splinters, and dug down very carefully at the foot of the ash, on the east side, until he found a root of hers as thick as his finger running toward the seedling; and dug down beside the seedling until he found its root, white and thin as string. And cut them both. A clean slanting cut. And fitted them. And bound them with bast from the fort's stores and sealed them with wax from the chapel candles, and covered them up, and sat back on his heels.

And the ash had flinched.

Yan had seen it. With the other eyes and with his own. Every leaf on the tree, all at once, the whole green crown of it, shivered; the way skin shivers when you touch it with something cold. And her light went in, a little, at the edge, the way a candle flame goes in when you pass your hand over it.

And Maigo had screamed.

Not loudly. Not the way he'd screamed on the night of the scouts. A short sharp sound through his teeth, and his hand going to his root foot, the bare one, the one with the wooden toes that he kept flat on the green, and his whole small body curling round it.

"*It's fine*," Maigo had said, when he could. White. Grey under the bark. "It's fine. I felt it. I felt the cut. In my foot. It's fine. It's—" He'd looked at the place in the ground. "It's *her* foot. I cut her foot."

The graft had browned in a week. The seedling's root had died back from the cut, and the ash's had closed over, the way a tree closes over a wound, a little hard knot of bark; and the seedling had wilted for ten days and then, very slowly, come back. On its own. A hand from her. Not touching.

He'd tried again at the end of the summer. A different root. A better cut. The same thing. The flinch, the scream through his teeth, the limp; he'd limped for nine days the second time, and the fort's dogs had followed him round the yard because they could tell something was wrong. The graft had browned.

The third time, a fortnight ago, he hadn't even got the bast on. He'd cut the ash's root and felt it and stopped, with the little knife in his hand, and sat there on the knoll with his foot curled up under him and tears running down under his sunglasses, and said, very quietly, to the tree: "*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*"

And put the earth back. And come home.

He hadn't been out to the knoll since. A fortnight. It was the longest he'd ever stayed away.

***

"I'm doing it wrong," said Maigo.

It was the evening of the third anniversary. The five of them on the east wall, the way they sat most evenings, with their backs to the parapet and their legs out; Salty and Wendy at one end, with Wendy's head on Salty's shoulder and her silent headphones round her neck; Niko with his short sword across his knees, cleaning it with a rag he didn't need to clean it with; Maigo in the middle, with his knees up and his hood up and his sunglasses on, though the sun was down. And Yan at the end, with Suzu.

Nobody said anything.

"The orchard men," said Maigo. "They cut. That's how they do it. You cut and you bind and it takes. But they're doing it to apples. Apples don't—" He stopped. "Apples don't *know*. I asked one, once, at Valcrest. In the market. A crate of them. They didn't say anything. They're asleep. They don't mind." He pulled his hood further down. "She minds. She *knows*. When I cut her, she knows."

"So don't cut," said Salty.

Maigo looked at him.

"I'm not a tree," said Salty. "I don't know. But if a thing minds being cut, don't cut it." He shrugged, the big slow shrug of a man whose shoulders were a yard across. "Find another way."

"There isn't another way. That's what a graft *is*. You cut two things and stick them together."

"Is it?" said Niko.

They all looked at him. He hadn't looked up from his rag.

"That's what it is for apples," said Niko. "You said. For apples you cut them. But what is it, actually." He turned the short sword over. "It's two things being one thing. That's what you want. You don't want them cut. You want them to be one thing." He looked up. "The cut's just how the orchard men get there. Because apples are asleep and you have to force it."

Maigo didn't say anything.

"I didn't cut anything," said Niko. "On the knoll. In the spring. I had two things that kept coming apart. For two and a half years. And I tried holding them together, every morning, by force. Squeezing. And they came apart every time at eighteen." He put the rag down. "And then I stopped holding. I let them go round. Like food in a pan. I just let them get close and go round each other until they wanted to be one thing." He shrugged. "That's not a cut. That's—" He thought about it. "That's an introduction."

The wall was very quiet.

Maigo took his sunglasses off. He almost never did; he hated people seeing his eyes, white and glowing in the bark of his face. He sat there with them in his hand and looked at Niko, and his eyes were very bright.

"An introduction," said Maigo.

"I don't know," said Niko. "I'm not a tree either. I'm a spellblade."

"You keep *saying* that."

"I'm going to keep saying it," said Niko, "for the rest of my life," and Wendy laughed into Salty's shoulder.

Maigo put his sunglasses back on. He didn't say anything else. But Yan, at the end of the wall, with the other eyes, saw his light change. Maigo's light had always been the colour of the knoll: green, soft, alive, a little too bright, like new leaves in sun. It went brighter. Not much. The way a lamp brightens when somebody turns the wick up a hair.

***

He went out to the knoll in the morning, and Yan went with him.

They didn't say anything on the way. An hour along the markers at Salty's pace, which was Yan's pace now too, because he was tired. Four hundred and eighty-one markers between the east gate and the knoll, Wendy said, counting the ones with no names. Aldous's rows. The dead of the red, all of them, a stone for each one he'd found, and a name if he'd had one. Yan had stopped reading the names the first year. He hadn't stopped seeing them. Under every marker, if he looked, faint, so faint he'd stopped looking unless he had to, a sort of grey. Not a light. The place where a light had been.

*Not mine*, Aldous had said, in the first month. About those. And Yan had promised. He didn't take what wasn't given; he'd promised a chaplain on a wall three years ago, before he knew what it would cost, and he'd never once broken it, and it was why, Aldous said, he was weaker here than he'd have been anywhere else in the kingdom: because he walked every day through fourteen hundred graves and never put his hand out.

He didn't put his hand out now. He walked between the rows with Maigo, at Salty's pace, with Suzu on his collarbone, and didn't look down.

The knoll was green to the top. The ash was there, with every leaf turned in, the way it had been since the spring. Toward the cairn. Toward the black stone. Toward anybody who sat on the grass. Soft, all of it, the whole crown leaning a little toward the middle, the way a face turns.

And the seedling. A hand from her on the east side. A stem like a pencil, with six leaves now, and the bud, and its light like a spark in a lamp.

Maigo knelt down between them.

He didn't take the knife out. He didn't dig. He put his root foot flat on the green, the bare one, the wooden toes spreading out on the grass the way they did, going down; and put one hand flat on the ground at the foot of the ash, and the other hand flat on the ground at the foot of the seedling. A hand apart. And shut his eyes.

Yan sat down against the cairn and watched.

He didn't know what Maigo was doing. Not really. He'd never known, with Maigo's roots; it was a different lane from his, a different sense, the way Hyvred's light was different from his candles. But he could see the lights. He could see Maigo's, green, bright, kneeling on the grass; and the ash's, big and slow and gold at the edges, a hand to the left; and the seedling's, a spark, a hand to the right.

And he saw Maigo's light go down.

Out of his hands. Into the ground. Two threads of green, very thin, going down from each palm into the earth, the way roots go down. One toward the ash. One toward the seedling. And stop, a little way down, just touching each of them. The way you'd put a hand on somebody's shoulder.

"This is her," said Maigo.

Not to Yan. To the ground.

"This is her," said Maigo again. Very quietly. "She's from far away. Past the end of the maps. Liraun brought her. She's very small. She doesn't know anybody here." A breath. "This is the ash. She's mine. She's been on this knoll since the first spring. She was on watch all this spring, facing east, and she came off watch the morning the scouts turned, and she's very tired. She's—" His voice went. "She's the best tree in the world."

The seedling's light flickered.

Yan saw it. A spark in a lamp, going up a hair and back.

"You're the same kind," said Maigo. "You're a hand apart. You can't hear each other. I can hear both of you." His face was screwed up. "I'm not going to cut anybody. I'm just going to hold you. Here. Close. And you can decide."

And he held.

Yan had seen him hold before. On the red, two summers ago, on his knees with his hands flat, *stay, stay, stay*, for one heartbeat. On the knoll in the spring, all night, with his root foot down, holding a hundred scouts by the ankles. *Two hands*, Maigo always said. *Three, with the spike*. That was what Root was. You held a thing where it was. You told it *stay*.

This wasn't *stay*. Yan could see the difference. The two threads of green going down from Maigo's palms didn't grip. They just touched. A hand on a shoulder. And then, very slowly, so slowly that Yan wasn't sure for a long time that it was happening at all, they began to lean toward each other. Under the ground. In the hand of earth between the ash and the seedling. Not pulled. Leaning. The way the ash's leaves leaned toward the middle of the knoll. The way Suzu's light leaned toward him, all day, like a plant on a windowsill.

Maigo held it for an hour.

Then he took his hands off the ground and fell over sideways onto the grass and lay there, with his root foot still down, breathing.

"Nothing," he said. To the sky. "Nothing happened."

"Something happened," said Yan.

Maigo turned his head.

"The threads," said Yan. "Yours. They leaned. Toward each other. Under the ground." He looked at the hand of earth between the two lights. "Not much. A finger. Less."

Maigo lay there for a long time looking at him.

"Did they *decide*?" he said. Very small.

"No," said Yan. "Not yet. They leaned."

"Leaning's a start," said Maigo. And shut his eyes. And slept, right there on the knoll, in the sun, with his root foot in the ground; and Yan sat against the cairn beside him with Suzu on his collarbone, and watched the two lights, a hand apart, for the rest of the morning.

They didn't move. But he thought, once or twice, the seedling's flickered.

***

Rhea was in the yard when they got back.

She'd been in the yard every afternoon since the splint came off. In the spring, on the knoll, she'd had her left arm broken across her chest by the back of a horned thing's arm and fought the rest of the night one-handed, and put her sword into the back of a third one's neck, and then sat down on the grass and put her head between her knees; and Rachael had written in the channel at seven in the morning, *splint it and keep it still and if she says "again" to anyone in the next six weeks I'm coming out there*. Rhea had said *again* to Teague on the fourth day. Rachael hadn't come. Hesper had sat on her.

The splint had come off in the summer. The arm had come back slowly. She'd lost the strength in it, she said, the way you lose the strength in anything you don't use; she could hold a sword in it and not much more, for a month. And she'd been in the yard every afternoon since, putting it back.

Yan stopped at the gate and watched her.

She was cutting at the post. The old oak post in the corner of the yard, scarred white from forty years of recruits. One stroke at a time, slow, with the sword in both hands. Not hard. Rhea, who'd never in her life done anything that wasn't hard.

He looked at her light.

It had always been loud. That was what he'd thought, the first year, when he'd only just learned to see them: Rhea's was the loudest light in the fort. Bright and hot and restless, a bonfire in a high wind, always leaning toward the next fight the way Salty's leaned toward Wendy. You could feel it from across the yard.

It wasn't loud now.

He watched her bring the sword up. Slow. And as she did it, her light went down. Not out. Down. Out of the middle of her, where it had always sat, roaring; down through her legs, through her boots, into the packed earth of the yard. He could see it go. A thick slow column of warm gold, going down from her into the ground the way water goes down into a floor.

And stopping there. At the floor. Spreading out, a little, under her feet, like water does when it reaches the stone.

And she cut. And the stroke landed on the post with a sound he'd never heard a sword make on that post: low, and heavy, and short, like a bell dropped onto a flagstone. The whole post shook in its socket. The recruits on the far side of the yard looked round.

Rhea lowered the sword and stood there breathing, looking at the post.

"You can see it," she said. Without turning round.

Yan didn't say anything for a moment.

"Since last summer," said Rhea. "Since him. The eleventh night. I held him a count. One count, with my arm, on Oakes's table, and I felt where his went. *Down*. Into the table. Into the floor. Into everything. He didn't push. He let it go down into what he was standing on, and the whole fort held his arm up." She looked at the post. "So I've been doing that. All winter. All summer. Letting it go down. Into whatever I trust. The ground. The wall. The post." She was quiet. "Teague says I've moved more since the winter than in four years. I don't know. I can't see it." She turned round, at last, and looked at him. "You can."

"Yes," said Yan.

"What's it look like?"

He thought about it. He didn't want to tell her too much. He'd learned that from Ice too, watching him not tell people things on the shingle; it was one of the few things about Ice he'd never had to have explained. If he told her *it goes down and stops at the floor*, she'd hear *stops*, and she'd push against it, and she'd lose whatever it was.

"Like water," he said. "Going into a floor."

Rhea thought about that.

"Good?" she said.

"Yes."

"Good," said Rhea, and turned back to the post. "He's coming. A few days, Maigo says. He held me two counts at the gate last year when he left. *Next year*, I said." She brought the sword up. "It's next year."

He watched her light go down again, into the yard, and stop.

He didn't tell her it stopped.

***

On the third morning Maigo went out to the knoll alone.

Yan didn't know until he got up and found the bunk empty and the door of the barracks open, and Salty on the step, already up, already dressed, with his greatsword on his back, looking east along the markers.

"He went at four," said Salty. "Didn't wake anybody. I heard the gate." He looked at Yan. "You want to go?"

"Yes."

"I'll carry you if you get tired."

"I won't get tired."

"You get tired on the stairs, mate," said Salty, not unkindly, and they went.

Salty's pace. An hour. The sun coming up over Kharvos in front of them as they walked, gold over the black line of the mountains, and the riverbeds going green in it, and the knoll ahead with its little green flame on top.

Maigo was kneeling between them when they got there. The ash and the seedling. A hand apart. His root foot down, his hands flat on the ground, one at the foot of each. He'd been there since before dawn. His face was grey under the bark and his sunglasses were crooked and his lips were moving.

Yan looked with the other eyes.

The threads were there. Maigo's two threads of green, going down from his palms into the ground. Touching. A hand on a shoulder. And between them, under the hand of earth that separated the two lights, something else.

Two other threads. Very fine. Not Maigo's. One gold-edged and deep, from the ash. One the colour of a spark, from the seedling. Growing. Yan could see them grow; he'd never seen anything grow before, not with his eyes, not like that, so slowly and so certainly, the way the hour hand of a clock moves if you watch it long enough. Toward each other. Through the dark earth. Leaning.

A finger apart.

Less.

Maigo's lips were still moving. Yan couldn't hear what he was saying. He didn't need to.

Salty sat down on the grass beside the cairn, very quietly, and took his greatsword off his back and laid it on the green, and folded his big arms, and waited. He didn't know what he was waiting for. He waited anyway; he was the best in the world at it.

Half a finger.

A hair.

And they touched.

***

Yan felt it.

Not with the other eyes. With all of him. The way you feel a door close in a house you're standing in, in your feet and your ears at once. The two lights on the knoll, the big slow gold-edged one and the little spark, went *together*; not into each other, not one swallowing the other; together. The way two notes go together when somebody plays them at the same time and they're in tune, and suddenly there's a third thing in the room that wasn't there before, that neither of them was on its own.

And the ash heard.

Every leaf on her, the whole crown, shivered. Not the flinch. Not the cold-touch shiver of the cut. Something else. A long slow ripple from the top of the crown to the bottom, like wind going through barley, except there wasn't any wind. And then the leaves on the east side, the side toward the seedling, turned.

All of them. Away from the middle of the knoll. Away from the cairn and the stone. East. Toward the small thing a hand from her foot, with six leaves and a bud.

And the seedling put out a leaf.

Yan saw it happen. Everybody did; Salty made a sound beside him like a man who'd been punched. A seventh leaf, from the bud at the top of the pencil-thin stem, unfolding, right there, in the morning sun, in the time it took to breathe out. Small and green and creased. On the west side of the stem. Toward the ash.

Maigo opened his eyes.

He took his hands off the ground. Very slowly. As if he were taking them off something that might fall over. And sat back on his heels with his root foot still flat on the green, and looked at the two trees, a hand apart, with their leaves turned toward each other.

"Oh," said Maigo.

Then he started crying. Not like the night of the scouts. Quietly. With his sunglasses crooked and his mouth open and his hands in his lap, the way you'd cry at a wedding.

"They decided," said Maigo. "They *decided*. I didn't cut anybody. I didn't do anything. I just held them where they could hear and they—" He couldn't finish. "She can hear her. She can hear her, Yan."

"I know," said Yan. "I saw."

Maigo's window opened.

Yan saw it open. Not the window; the other eyes couldn't see windows. He saw Maigo's light jump, the way lights jumped when something changed in them, a green flare in the middle of him, and Maigo's head go back, and his mouth open, and his eyes go to the air in front of his face where his window was.

And he read it out. He always read them out. He'd read his first one out in capitals to the whole fort.

"*Maigo*," he said. Thickly. "*Tier two. Candle. Skills. Root.*" A breath. "*Graft.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*Graft*," said Maigo again. And laughed, wet and astonished, and fell over sideways onto the grass between the two trees and lay there with his arms out, laughing and crying at once, with his root foot in the ground and the ash's leaves and the seedling's leaves all turned toward each other over him.

Salty, by the cairn, put his face in his big hands.

Then he looked at Maigo's light, lying on the grass between the two trees.

It had always been the colour of the knoll. He'd said that, once, in the spring, to nobody. Green, soft, a little too bright. It still was. But it wasn't just the knoll's colour now. It had something else in it, at the edge, gold-edged, deep. The ash's. And something else, the colour of a spark in a lamp.

Three things. One light. Together.

The red came into his window that evening.

He was on the east wall. They all were, the five of them, and Aldous, with his book shut on his knee; and Rhea at the far end, leaning on the parapet, not sitting, because Rhea never sat on a wall if she could stand on it. They'd been watching the west road all afternoon, though there was nothing to see on it; the party was two days out, Maigo said, on the grey grass, coming, and you couldn't see the grey grass from the east wall. You couldn't see anything west from the east wall. They were watching it anyway, through the fort, through the walls, through the stone.

Yan had felt them since the morning. He'd felt them since the day before, really; but the day before it had been the way you feel a storm coming, the air going different, nothing you could put a hand on. This morning, after the knoll, it had been candles.

Ten of them. Far off. West. Moving.

He'd known them before he could see them properly. He'd met them all, at the Lists, twice; and Kin would have let him know the summoned ones anyway, the thread that went from him to every one of the thirty-five whether they'd met or not. Gabe's: big, steady, warm, like a forge banked for the night. Deston's, which sang, if a light could sing. Xav's, very still, very bright, a lamp in a window with nobody moving behind it. The four from Duskhollow, close together, a little knot. Petra's, which he'd seen at the Lists and not since, and which had changed: it had been a straight hard grey flame, like a lamp turned up too far, and now it was the same colour but it was *leaning*. Forward. Like a woman walking into a wind. Alea's, green. The same green as Suzu's. Loving.

And the sea.

He'd felt it first on the red, two summers ago, coming in over everything like a tide. He'd felt it on the road east last summer, at every fire. He felt it now, two days off, coming across the grey grass toward the fort; and even from two days off it was the biggest thing he'd ever felt, and it was very quiet, and it was coming home.

And then his window lit up, at twelve minutes past six, and there was red in it.

Not words. Red. For two seconds, in the place where the channel was, a plain the colour of rust going away under a gold sky to a black line of mountains; and on a low rise in the middle of it, very small, a fort. Grey walls. A tower. Smoke.

From the west. From high up. From somewhere on the far side of the grey grass, looking back across the whole of the red at the place where he was sitting.

It was gone before he understood it.

Beside him, Salty had stopped breathing. Wendy had her hand over her mouth. Niko had put the rag down. Maigo, in the middle, with his hood up and his new window still open in front of his face from the morning, because he'd been reading *Graft* over and over all day, had gone completely still.

The channel went mad.

That night, on the west wall, he heard the stone.

He'd gone round to the west wall after the others went down. He didn't know why. He couldn't see anything from it but the dark and the grey grass beginning, a long way off, and the stars. He stood there with his hands on the parapet and Suzu on his collarbone and looked west.

And felt the candles. Ten of them. Two days. Nearer than they'd been that morning.

And the green one, Alea's, nearest of all; out in front of the others, always, setting the pace. And on her back, where her light was, a second thing that wasn't a light.

Astra.

He'd seen it last summer. With the other eyes. A sword wasn't supposed to have a light; it wasn't alive. But Astra had something. Not a candle. More like the memory of one, the way a stone remembers heat after the sun's gone off it; and it was black, and dense, and very old, and it sat on Alea's back in his sight like a coal.

And behind him, east, through the whole of the fort, an hour out along the markers, on a green knoll in the dark, the other half.

He'd never been able to see them both at once before. They'd always been in the same place, or a long way apart. They were neither, tonight. They were a long way apart, two days west and an hour east, and in his sight, somehow, because of whatever it was that let him see the things he saw, they were a hand's width apart. As if the whole of the grey grass and the whole of the fort had folded up between them like a piece of paper and they were sitting on either side of the fold, almost touching.

And they hummed.

He didn't hear it. Not with his ears. He felt it the way he'd felt the knoll that morning, in his feet and his chest at once: a note, very low, so low it was almost under feeling, coming from the black stone on the knoll behind him. And another, a little higher, from the coal on the back of the girl two days west. The same note. Not the same. The way two bells from the same tower sound when somebody strikes one and the other answers.

They'd done it last summer. At the grave. On the night of the ninth, with Astra laid on the grass beside the stone she'd been cut from, under a full moon: they'd rung together, and the cold under the knoll had gone down. Ice said. Maigo said. Yan had felt it go.

They were doing it now. Two days apart. Humming.

Getting nearer.

He stood on the west wall in the dark with his hands on the cold stone and felt it for a long time. And under it, a long way under, under the black stone on the knoll and the spike that went down into the ground below it like a nail and the ash's roots wrapped round the spike like fingers, and the new roots, the seedling's, very small, that had reached across a hand of earth that morning and found hers; under all of it, something small and dense and cold, that had lifted its head in the spring and been told to lie down.

It wasn't lifting its head now.

It was listening. The way a dog listens to a sound in another room that it knows, and isn't sure yet whether it's frightened of.

*Yun*, said Suzu.

He didn't answer.

*Yun. Come down. You're tired.*

"They're humming," he said. Out loud, to the dark.

*I know.*

"Both of them. Two days apart. It's like they know they're coming."

*They do know*, said Suzu.

He looked down at her. She'd lifted her head off his collarbone and was looking west too, small, the size of a scarf, with her green eyes very bright in the starlight and her tongue going in and out, tasting the air.

*They always know*, said Suzu. *Halves always know.* She was quiet a moment. *So does she.*

He didn't ask who. He knew who. He could feel her too, if he let himself, the way he'd felt her last summer on the knoll: not a candle, not a light, nothing he could see. A grave. A black stone over a hole in the ground with nobody in it. A name, in English, in letters a fox girl couldn't read and knew anyway by the shape. *To Diwa, My Love*.

Two days.

"Do you think she'll come back up?" he said. "Alea. After. At night. Last year she went down with him."

Suzu didn't answer for a long time.

*Go to bed, Yun*, she said at last. Very gently. *It's the same place. It'll still be the same place in two days.*

He went to bed.

He slept nine hours, with Suzu round his neck and two notes humming somewhere under the floor of the world, a hand's width apart, getting nearer; and dreamed, for the first time in three years, of a kitchen table, and a pile of post under the salt, and his mother's hand flat on the top of it, feeling the edges, waiting. And in the dream he sat down across from her, and picked up the first envelope, and turned it over in his hands, and didn't open it, and didn't know why.

He woke at dawn with his face wet and didn't tell anybody.

Two mornings later, from the west wall, Wendy saw them.
