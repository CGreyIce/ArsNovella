Every morning, at the end of the third form, Alea looked down.

She hadn't meant to make a habit of it. Kestra had told her in the first snow, on the shingle, *it isn't a fault, I'm not telling you to fix it*, and then gone south with the king, and Alea had gone on doing the forms every morning on her own the way she had for a year, saying *again* under her breath. And at the end of the third, when the long low note had gone out over the water off Astra's flat and hung there, she'd looked down. Just to see.

They pointed at the end of the jetty. Every time.

All winter. On the ice, when the jetty was a white ridge running out into a white field and you couldn't tell where the boards stopped. In the dark, in the snow, with a lamp on the step. In the thaw, with the shingle running with meltwater and the hens complaining. On the morning the ice went out, at dawn, with the last grey slabs turning over in the black water and Gigichi standing on the shingle in her nightdress saying *it's going, it's going* to nobody. Every time. Her feet, at the end of the third form, pointing at the last plank.

It had stopped being strange by midwinter. By the thaw it had stopped being something she checked and become something she did, the way you touch a doorframe going through it.

This morning was the same.

The lake was flat and grey and steaming a little in the cold, the way it did in spring before the sun got on it. The cutting at the land end of the jetty, Gigichi's tree, the one they'd planted with Pim's blue bucket, had eleven leaves now; Alea had counted them yesterday, and today there were twelve. The hens were on the jetty in a row, watching her with the expressions of three hens who'd seen the third form four hundred times and had opinions about all of them.

She finished it. The note went out off the flat of the blade, low, like a bowed string, and hung over the water.

She looked down.

The jetty. The last plank. Where her father was sitting with the rod, with his back to her, the way he'd sat every morning of her life.

"Window," she said, to nobody. The way Kestra did.

Thirty-seven. Five since the snow. She'd written to Sorrel at thirty-two and told her so she'd be frightened. She thought about writing *thirty-seven*. She thought Sorrel probably wouldn't be.

She closed it, and put Astra on her back, and went down the jetty to sit beside her father, and the hens moved over for her. Which they did now. Since the first snow. Nobody knew why. She did.

"Barge," said her father.

She looked up.

He hadn't turned his head. He never did. But down at the far end of the lake, where the water went out between the reeds into the Wren, there was something on the grey that hadn't been there a moment ago: a mast, and a crosstree, and a brown sail being taken in, very slowly, by somebody who didn't trust the current yet.

The *Patient Widow*. The first one up the river since the ice.

***

Haskett took his cap off.

He always did. He'd done it the first time he saw her father, last spring, when he brought Gabe and Deston and Xav up the river and saw a man fishing on the end of a jetty and knew. He'd done it every time since. He did it on the landing below the mill, and on the shingle, and once, last autumn, standing in a barge full of snow with six perch in a basket, and he did it now, at the land end of the jetty, beside Gigichi's tree, with his old brown face red from the river wind.

"Late this year," said Haskett. "The Wren was in spate. Took three weeks to come down enough to tow." He looked at the jetty. At the man on the end of it. He'd never once come down the boards; he said they weren't his. "Brought the post."

He'd brought a lot of it. A sack, for the town, for Odette. A box of something heavy for the forge. A note from Kingsferry, from Rachael, folded very small, in English, which Alea couldn't read; she took it up the lake road to the militia hall later, and Thea read it twice and went very quiet, and then told her what it said. Three healers gone north to Thornwall. The threadless gone off the Kingsferry quays, all at once, the same morning. And Rachael hated it.

And a letter.

Haskett held it out to her. Not to her father. To her.

It was wrapped in oilcloth. Somebody had wrapped it in oilcloth and tied it with a bit of tarred twine in a knot Alea knew, because her father tied the same one on every line he'd ever made: a fisherman's knot. And on the oilcloth, in big leaning letters, with a blot on the *W* the shape of a crab:

*THE LAKE GIRL. WRENHAVEN. UP THE RIVER. BY WATER.*

And under it, in a different hand, small and careful, the way you'd add a note to a parcel so it didn't go astray:

*the fox one, on the jetty. — D.*

"Came up the coast in a fish-boat," said Haskett. "Into Kingsferry. Dennis had it out of the harbourmaster's sack before it was dry. Said there's only one lake girl on the whole river." He put his cap back on. Then he took it off again, because the man on the end of the jetty had turned his head. "He said to say she sent it by water on purpose. He said you'd know what that meant."

Alea held the letter in both hands.

She did know.

*My dad says water goes where it's going*, she'd written, in the first snow, in the loft, on her knees. *It takes its time*.

***

She read it on the end of the jetty, beside her father, with the hens.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to take it up to the loft and read it under the crooked curtain the way she'd read the others, where nobody could see her face. But she'd got as far as the last plank, untying the knot, and sat down without deciding to, and her father had moved over, and she'd unfolded it, and here she was.

Big leaning hand. Blots. Crossings-out. Three pages this time. The paper smelled of tar and, faintly, of fish.

*Lake girl,*

*I sent this one by water. You said water gets where it's going. I want to see if you're right. If you're reading this you are. If you're not I'll never know, so either way I win.*

*I got yours. The one with "Tobyn" in it. It came on the coast carrier just before midwinter, with a gull feather stuck in the seal that I think was there by accident.*

*I read it on the quay. Out loud. I didn't mean to. I read it to myself and then I got to the bit where you said his name off the jetty and it came out of my mouth and old Abner was at the bollard mending nets and he heard it.*

*He didn't say anything. He's been mending nets on our quay since before I was born. He was my dad's friend. He hasn't said his name once since. Not once. Two years.*

*He didn't say anything that day either.*

*The next morning there were four of them on the hard by NEXT YEAR when I came down. Abner and the two Hallams and their cousin from the salt-sheds. With oakum and a pitch pot and a brazier going. Nobody had asked them. They didn't say why. They just turned her over and caulked her. All day. Every seam. I sat on a bollard and watched because they wouldn't let me touch anything.*

*And at about noon, with his hands all black, Abner stood up and looked at her and said, "There. Tobyn's boat won't leak now."*

*Out loud.*

*And then the Hallams said it. And then their cousin. "Tobyn's boat." And then the harbourmaster, who came down to tell them they couldn't light a brazier on the hard, and saw what they were doing, and didn't tell them anything, and said "Tobyn's girl," to me, as if he'd always said it.*

*Now they all say it. On the whole quay. Tobyn's boat. Tobyn's girl. Tobyn would have. Like it's ordinary. Like it was always allowed and nobody noticed.*

*I didn't know I'd been waiting for it till they did.*

*She doesn't leak. NEXT YEAR. Not a drop. I'm almost sorry. I liked bailing. It gave me something to do while I was talking to him.*

Alea stopped.

She'd put her hand over her mouth at some point. She didn't remember doing it. She took it away.

Beside her, her father hadn't moved. He was looking at his float. But his shoulders had gone the way they went sometimes, still in a particular way, and she knew he'd been reading over her arm. He always could. He read the channel every night; he could read a letter upside down at a yard.

"They said his name," said Alea.

"I saw," said her father.

She read on.

*The second thing.*

*I told your mum.*

*Off the bar. In NEXT YEAR. The morning after they caulked her, the first morning she didn't leak, I went out past the bar before the guild boats and I sat there in the swell and I said it. "Diwa." Lucan read it out at the closing, off your box. "Daughter of Diwa, a healer." I remembered. I remember everything from that day. I'm still cross about most of it.*

*I said: Diwa. Your daughter's called Alea. She came at me like a pike with a sword the size of a door. She beat me by less than a heartbeat and then she sat on the sand and cried and so did I. She said my dad's name off a jetty in the snow so I'm saying yours off the bar.*

*I said: she's going to lose. This year. To me. I thought you should know. So you can tell her it's coming.*

*So that's both of them told. That's fair.*

Alea put the letter down on her knees.

She didn't make a sound. She was quite sure she didn't. But the big red hen, who'd been sitting at her elbow on the last plank pretending to be asleep, got up, and came round the back of her, and sat down again on the other side, against her leg, the way it had sat against her father's in the first snow. And didn't peck anybody.

Her father didn't say anything. He reeled in a little. He cast again. The float went out over the grey water and settled, and the rings went out from it, slowly, all the way to the reeds.

"She's cheeky," said Alea. Her voice did a thing. She let it.

"She's very cheeky," said her father.

"*So you can tell her it's coming*." Alea wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "That's mine. I said that. In the snow. To her dad." She laughed. It came out wet. "She's stolen it."

"You stole the pike from her," said her father. "Last summer. She came at you like a pike. You came back at her like a bigger one." He watched the float. "Fair's fair."

***

The third page was about the hooks.

*The thing.*

*I said in the autumn I had a new thing and I wasn't telling you what it was. It works now. I'm still not telling you.*

*But I'll tell you this, so it's fair, because you told me thirty-two percent so I'd be frightened.*

*I go out past the bar every morning in NEXT YEAR in the March swell. It's big in March. It comes in off the deep in long grey hills and the boat goes up them and down them and sideways. And I haul pots. Both hands. With the hooks. Standing up. And I don't hold on to anything.*

*Nothing. Not the mast. Not the gunwale. Both hands on the hooks and both feet on the boards and the whole sea going under me, and I stand there.*

*Think about that.*

*Lucan wrote back about the draw. He says nobody can put anybody opposite anybody. I know. I'll get there.*

*Next year is this year. Did you notice? I painted it on the stern at the end of last summer and now it's this year and it's still on the stern. Abner says I'll have to paint it out at midsummer. I said no. I said it's a name, not a date. He said that's what you said last time and I said yes.*

*Come to the Lists. Bring your dad if he'll come. Mine's coming. He's in the hooks.*

*Sorrel. (the boat-hook girl) (Tobyn's girl)*

*P.S. Tobyn with a y. Yes. Nobody's ever asked. You're the first.*

*P.P.S. I'm some percent to Honed. I'm not telling you. That should frighten you more.*

***

It did.

She thought about it all day. Up the lake road with Rachael's strip in her pocket, and in the militia hall kitchen while Thea read it twice, and in the yard at the hall with PenguChi, where she did the third form four times in a row and fell over on the fourth because she wasn't thinking about her feet.

*Both hands on the hooks and both feet on the boards and the whole sea going under me.*

Last summer, in the final, Sorrel had pulled her. That had been the whole of Sorrel's fight: the hook goes over your blade, or round your ankle, or into your belt, and she hauls, the way you haul a net, the way her father had hauled pots off the bar, and you go over. She'd pulled a Pemberton girl off her feet that way, and a guild boy, and a cadet from the king's school. She'd pulled Alea three times nearly.

And the fourth time Alea had stopped fighting the pull. She'd gone *with* it. The way a pike goes with the line when you strike, all the way in, faster than you're pulling, so the line goes slack in your hands and there's nothing on the end of it and then there's a fish in your face. She'd given Sorrel her own pull back, and more, and Sorrel had gone over backwards on the sand with a black sword at her collar and laughed.

It had worked because Sorrel was standing on sand. You pull, you lean back to pull, you're braced, and when the weight on the end of the line comes at you instead of away, you go over. Everybody does. It's what feet do.

Unless your feet have spent a winter on boards that never stop moving. Unless you've learned to haul with both hands while the whole sea goes out from under you, and not fall.

"She won't go over," said Alea.

She said it to PenguChi, in the yard, sitting in the dust where she'd fallen. PenguChi was sitting on the edge of the trough with Waddles in her lap, and she looked at Alea in the way she had, very gravely, as if everything anybody said to her deserved to be thought about properly.

"Who won't?"

"Sorrel. If I go with her, this year. Like last year. She won't go over." Alea looked at her own boots. "She's been standing up in a boat all winter. Hauling. She won't go over if I come at her. She'll just—" She thought about it. "She'll just stand there. On a moving deck. And let me come. And I'll be the one off balance."

PenguChi thought about it properly.

"Then you'll have to stand on something that moves," she said.

Waddles looked up at her. Then at Alea. Then he got out of PenguChi's lap, waddled across the yard to the water trough, climbed up the side of it with enormous difficulty, and stood on the floating board in the middle that the stable boy used to skim the leaves off. It tipped. He went in. He came out. He climbed up again and stood on it again and it tipped again, and he went in again, and came out, and stood on it.

They watched him do it five times.

"He does that," said PenguChi. "Every day. I don't know why. He's never once stayed on." She looked at Alea. "He's never once stopped, either."

***

She went to the Shiver.

She hadn't been there since she was small. Nobody went to the Shiver. It was at the north end of the lake, an hour round the shore path from the cabin, where the hill stream came down through the alders and lost itself in the reeds before it found the lake: a great flat floating mat of moss and reed-root and old dead stems and black water underneath, a quarter of a mile of it, green and brown and gold in the spring sun. It looked like a meadow. It wasn't. You stepped on it and the whole thing moved, all round you, slowly, for yards, the way a blanket moves when somebody sits down on the other end of the bed. Children dared each other to run on it. Every few years one of them went through.

Her mother had said *not the Shiver* the way she'd said *not the jetty end on your own* and *not the fire* and *not the knife drawer*. Firmly, and once, and you knew. *Not the Shiver, love. It eats boots. And then it eats whoever's in them*.

Alea took her boots off at the edge of it.

She did it on the first morning, with nobody there to see, at dawn, in the cold, with Astra on her back. She stood at the edge where the alders stopped and the moss began, barefoot, and put one foot out onto the green.

It gave. It went down under her a finger's depth, cold and wet and soft, and the moss all round it went down too, in a ring, and then the ring went out, a slow wave going away from her across the whole of the Shiver, through the reeds, so that a quarter of a mile away a heron she hadn't seen took off in disgust.

She put the other foot on it.

She stood there. It moved under her. Not much. Not all the time. It breathed. That was the only way to say it. The whole floating field went very slowly up and down under her feet like something asleep, and every time she shifted her weight it shifted back, a beat late, from somewhere a long way off.

She drew Astra.

She fell over on the first form.

***

It took four days.

She went every morning, before her father was on the jetty, and every afternoon, and came back muddy to the knees and the elbows and once, on the second day, to the ears, which she hated more than anything that had ever happened to her. Her father didn't ask. He looked at the mud once, on the first evening, and at her bare feet, and at the boots in her hand, and then he looked out at the lake for a long moment toward the north end, where the alders were, and didn't say anything at all. She'd thought he would. He didn't.

On the second evening there was a plate of fish on the table when she came in, cooked, with onions, and a towel on her chair. That was all.

The Shiver didn't care about forms.

That was the first thing she learned. You did the first form, the cast, and set your feet at the end of it the way you'd set them on the shingle, and the Shiver went down under the back foot because that was where your weight was, and you went over backwards into black water up to the waist. You did the second form, the draw, and pivoted, and the moss under the pivot foot slid, because moss slides, and you went over sideways. You did the third form—

You didn't do the third form. Not for two days. She never got that far.

On the second afternoon Gigichi came.

Alea didn't hear her. She came round the shore path through the alders in her scowl and her muddy boots and stood at the edge of the Shiver with her arms folded, watching Alea fall into it, and didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she took her own boots off, and walked out onto the moss, and knelt down, and put both hands flat on it.

"It's all roots," said Gigichi.

Alea, who was waist-deep in a hole in the Shiver and holding on to a clump of reed, looked at her.

"Underneath." Gigichi's eyes were shut. "It's not moss. Not really. It's roots. Reed roots and sedge roots and bog-bean and the alders at the edge, all grown into each other, for hundreds of years, all holding hands. Like a net." She opened her eyes. "It won't let you through if you go with it. It lets you through when you stamp. You're stamping."

"I'm *standing*."

"You're standing like it's the shingle. Like it owes you." Gigichi got up. The moss went down under her and came back up, slowly, and she didn't move at all; she just let it. "It doesn't owe you anything. It's a net. Stand on it like a net."

She went back to the alders and put her boots on.

"Chi," said Alea.

"I'm not discussing it," said Gigichi, and went back round the shore path without looking round, and Alea heard her say, very quietly, to the alders, *you're all right, she's all right, she's just stupid*, and the alders didn't say anything back.

***

On the third day she got through the first form without falling over.

On the fourth day she got through the second.

She stopped standing like it was the shingle. That was all it was, in the end; Gigichi had been right, the way Gigichi was always right, rudely. On the shingle you planted your feet and the ground took you. Here you didn't plant anything. You put your feet down on top of a net of roots that had been holding hands for three hundred years and let it hold you, and when it went down under the back foot you went down with it, a little, and when it came up you came up, and you didn't fight it, and you didn't hold on.

Like going with the pike.

She'd known that for a year. She'd done it with a rod and a hook on the end of a jetty at night, and with a black sword on the sand of the Lists in front of forty thousand people. You don't fight the thing that's pulling you. You go with it, faster than it, all the way in.

She hadn't known you could do it with your feet.

On the afternoon of the fourth day, with the sun going down red behind the hill and the reeds all gold, she got to the third form.

And the fog came.

***

She didn't see it come. Nobody ever did. That was the thing about lake smoke in the spring. One moment the sun was going down and the Shiver was gold and the alders were black against it and she could see all the way down the lake to the jetty, a thin dark line on the grey a long way off, with a speck on the end of it that was her father.

And then she turned, at the start of the third form, the long sweep, and turned back, and it was white.

All of it. All round her. Not mist; mist you could see through. This was the lake breathing out, the warm day going up off the cold water all at once and turning to cloud, the way it did every spring for a fortnight after the ice, and it came in over the Shiver from the lake side like a wall and went over her and past her and swallowed the alders, and then there was nothing. White. Close. A yard of moss under her bare feet, wet and green. A few reed heads, near, standing up out of nothing. And white.

She stood very still.

The Shiver moved under her. Up, and down. Slowly. Asleep.

She didn't know which way the shore was.

She knew she didn't. She knew it the way you know you've missed a step on a stair in the dark: all at once, in your stomach. She'd turned at the start of the form, and turned back, and turned again at the end of the second, and she'd been watching her feet and the moss and Astra's edge, and not the alders, and not the lake, and now there was no alders and no lake. Only white. And a quarter of a mile of floating roots in every direction, with black water under it, and here and there the holes where it had let her through, that she couldn't see.

Somewhere off to her left a bird called. Or to her right. In fog you couldn't tell. Sound came from everywhere.

*Not the Shiver, love. It eats boots. And then it eats whoever's in them.*

She was frightened.

She let herself be. She'd learned that on a hill above Valcrest, at midnight, from her father: *be frightened. Then count to three*. Her mother's trick, at the flap of every infirmary tent. She stood on the moss in the white with Astra in her hands and her heart going and her ears flat back against her skull, and was frightened, properly, all the way, for a count of three.

Then she thought about Kestra.

On the shingle in the snow, with her sword point-down and both hands on the pommel. Not looking at Alea's hands. Not at her shoulders.

*One day you'll be standing somewhere you don't know, in front of something you don't know, at the end of the third form, and you'll want to know which way's home without having to think about it.*

She was somewhere she didn't know. She'd known the Shiver all her life from the jetty, a gold strip at the end of the lake, and she didn't know it at all. In front of something she didn't know: white, and a net of roots, and black water. And she was at the start of the third form.

So she did it.

***

She didn't try to stand still.

That was the thing. She'd been trying for four days, she understood now, to make the Shiver into the shingle. To make it hold still for her, so she could do the forms the way she always did them. And it wouldn't, because it was a net, and a net doesn't hold still; it holds.

So she didn't make it. She stood on it the way Gigichi had said. Like a net. She let it go down under the back foot and went down with it, and let it come up and came up, and started the long sweep, the one Astra liked best, at the bottom of a breath of the Shiver, so that the whole floating field coming up under her lifted her into it.

Astra went round.

And sang.

The long low note went out off the flat of the blade into the fog. It was different. It was the same note, but it went out into the white and didn't come back off anything; there was no lake for it to go over, no shingle, no hill. It just went out, and hung there, all round her, as if the fog were holding it.

She finished the form. On the back foot. On the moss. Breathing.

She looked down.

Her feet were pointing into the fog.

Not at anything. There was nothing to point at. White, and a reed head, and the moss going away into white. But they were pointing, both of them, square, one a little behind the other, the way they pointed on the shingle and on the ice and in the yard at the hall. At something.

At the end of the jetty. An hour away, round the shore. Where a man was sitting with a rod.

She knew it the way she knew her own name. She didn't think it. She didn't work it out. She looked down, and her feet were pointing home, and she knew which way home was the way you know which way is down.

And if the jetty was there, then the lake was there, on that side, a bit to the left. And the alders were behind her and to the right. And the shore path went off from the alders, round, toward the jetty, along the edge of the lake.

She turned round. Carefully. On the moss. And walked.

***

She didn't fall through.

She didn't know how. She thought later it was because she walked the way she'd done the form: going with it, letting the Shiver take her, not stamping. Or because Gigichi had said something to the roots on her way out through the alders. Or because the net had decided, after four days of her falling into it, that she'd learned enough. She didn't know. She walked through the white with Astra in her hand and the moss going up and down under her feet in long slow breaths, and the reeds coming up out of the fog and going past, and after a long time something dark came up in front of her that was an alder, and then another, and then hard ground.

She put her boots on. Her hands were shaking. She did the laces twice.

Then she went home along the shore path in the fog.

It took an hour. She didn't see the lake once. She didn't see anything but the path in front of her, a few yards of it at a time, wet stones and old leaves and the roots of trees, and the white going on all round. She didn't stop. She didn't wonder, once, if she'd turned the wrong way. She didn't need to. At every bend, when the path forked, she knew which way to go without looking, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

The fog was thinning when she came out on the shingle. Just enough to see a little. The black line of the jetty going out into it. The boards. The posts.

And at the end of it, on the last plank, her father, on one knee, lighting the lamp.

He'd got the glass off. He had the flint in one hand and the lamp in the other, and he'd struck it once, she thought, and it hadn't caught, and he was striking it again. He was going to set it on the end of the jetty. On the last plank. The way people set lamps in windows in stories, so that whoever's out in the dark can see their way in.

The wick caught. He set the glass back on it. He stood up with it in his hand, and turned round, and saw her.

She was standing at the land end, by Gigichi's tree, with Astra in her hand and mud to her elbows and her hair full of fog.

He stood there with the lamp.

"You didn't need it," he said.

"No," said Alea.

He looked at the lamp in his hand. It was quite bright, in the last of the fog, yellow on the wet boards.

"I lit it anyway," he said.

"I know," said Alea.

***

Her window came up on its own, on the jetty, on the way down to him. She didn't ask it to.

Forty.

She stopped walking and looked at it. Three, in an hour. Three in one form, maybe. She'd never had three at once.

*It moves when I put something down*, her father had said, to Higu, on the end of this jetty, last autumn. *Not when I do something*. She'd thought, in the first snow, that it was true for her too, a little. That she'd put down a girl on a quay, talking to the sea on her own, with nobody to say the name back.

She thought about what she'd put down today.

Not the fog. Not the Shiver. She hadn't put those down; she'd walked out of them.

She'd put down the thing she'd been carrying since the thaw.

Not the question. She knew better than to ask him the question, and she knew he didn't know the answer; she'd watched him not know it all spring. On the jetty, at the forge, at the table, looking down the lake to the south at nothing in particular, the way he looked at things he hadn't decided. She'd been carrying the other thing, the one under it. *If he doesn't come, can I go?* Can I walk south three weeks away from this jetty, and stand on the sand in front of forty thousand people, and fight Tobyn's girl for a box with my own name in it, without him in the stand? Can I go somewhere he isn't?

She'd thought, all spring, without ever saying it, that maybe she couldn't. That he was what told her where home was. That without him at the end of the jetty she'd be standing somewhere she didn't know, in front of something she didn't know, with no way back.

And then she'd stood on the Shiver in the fog, a quarter of a mile of roots and black water, with no jetty and no lake and no him anywhere, and looked down, and known.

It wasn't him that told her. It was her feet. It was hers. Kestra had given it to her in the snow, and it was hers now, and it would be hers in Valcrest and in Gullhaven and on the far side of the red, if she ever went there, and at the bottom of the sea. It didn't matter where he was. Home didn't move.

So she could go.

She closed the window. She went down the rest of the jetty to the end, and sat down on the last plank beside the lamp, and her father sat down beside her, and the hens came down the boards in a row in the dusk and arranged themselves round the two of them and the lamp like people at a fire.

***

"I'm going south," said Alea. "In the summer. To the Lists."

"I know," said her father.

"To hold it. Lucan's put my name at the top. In gold. Thea says. Chris put it in the channel." She looked at the water. "I'm going to hold it."

"I know."

"You don't have to say anything." She said it quickly, before she lost it. "About you. Whether you're coming. I'm not asking. I'm never going to ask." She looked at her hands on her knees, muddy. "I just wanted you to know that I'm going. Whatever. On my own if I have to. And that it's all right. That I can."

Her father was quiet for a long time.

The fog went off the lake slowly, the way it came, all at once and then in pieces. The far shore came back, black. The first star.

"How do you know?" he said. Not doubting. Asking.

"My feet," said Alea. She laughed, a little. "Kestra. In the snow. They point here. At the end of the third form. Every time. Wherever I am." She looked down at them, in their muddy boots, on the last plank. "I was on the Shiver. In the fog. I couldn't see anything. And they pointed here." She was quiet. "So it doesn't matter where I go. I'll always know which way."

Her father didn't say anything.

But she felt him go still beside her. Not the jetty still. The other one. And after a while he put his hand on her shoulder, the way he had in the snow, and left it there. Warm. It was always warm.

They sat there until the lamp was the only light on the lake.

"Dad," said Alea.

"Mm."

"You say it. In the mornings."

He didn't move.

"When you cast," said Alea. "The first cast. Every morning. Since the snow. You say it under your breath. I've heard you. From the step." She didn't look at him. "*Tobyn*."

He didn't answer for a long time.

"Somebody should," he said.

***

She wrote back that night, in the loft, on her knees, with the paper on the lid of her chest and the candle in a saucer and mud still under her nails.

*Sorrel,*

*It got here. By water. Dennis had it out of the sack at Kingsferry and wrote "the fox one, on the jetty" on it so it wouldn't get lost, and Haskett brought it up the river on the first barge after the ice and took his cap off to my dad, which he always does. So you were right. And so was I. Water gets where it's going.*

*They said his name.*

*I read that bit on the end of the jetty and I didn't make a sound and our big red hen came and sat against my leg anyway. She knew. I don't know how. Tell Abner thank you. Tell him from the lake girl. Tell him the fox one, on the jetty, says thank you for the boat.*

*And thank you for my mum. I didn't know anybody else would ever say it. Out loud. Off a bar in the sea, a month away, to somebody she never met. I've read that part eleven times. I'm not telling you what my face did.*

*She knows now. That it's coming.*

*About the thing. I thought about it. I know what it is. Standing up in a boat in a March swell with both hands on the hooks and holding on to nothing. I thought about it for a whole day and fell over in the yard.*

*I've been standing on something that moves. That's all I'm telling you. You'll see.*

*I'm coming. To hold it. Lucan's put my name at the top of the tree in gold, Thea says, and yours in round one, because you entered first. You always enter first. I'll get there.*

*Next year is this year. Don't paint it out. It's a name.*

*Alea. (the lake girl) (the fox one, on the jetty) (holder)*

*P.S. I'm forty percent to Honed. Today. I went up three in an hour. I'm telling you so you're frightened, and also because I'm not as good at keeping secrets as you.*

*P.P.S. My dad says his name every morning when he casts. Tobyn. Under his breath. Since the snow. He doesn't know I know. Don't tell him.*

*P.P.P.S. He knows I know now. I told him. He said "somebody should." Tell Abner that too.*

She folded it. She wrapped it in a square of oilcloth from the cabin, and tied it with a bit of line, in a fisherman's knot, the one her father tied, because she'd watched him do it ten thousand times and had never once done it herself until tonight and found that her hands knew how.

On the outside she wrote: *TOBYN'S GIRL. THE QUAY, GULLHAVEN. DOWN THE RIVER. BY WATER.*

Then she blew the candle out and lay down under the crooked curtain with the duck facing in, and the lake beyond the little window, black and quiet, with a lamp on the end of the jetty that somebody had forgotten to put out. Or hadn't.

She lay and looked at it for a while.

Then she turned over, and slept, with her feet, under the blanket, pointing the way they always did.
