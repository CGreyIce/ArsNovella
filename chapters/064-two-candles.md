Mashi learned things by watching them.

Not by doing them. Everybody thought he learned by doing, because he was always doing something: two paths at once, twice the hours, half the results, every day for a year while the whole of Cinderhold told him it was a waste. But the doing wasn't how he learned. The doing was just how he checked. The learning happened before that, sitting very still somewhere with his chin on his fists, watching someone else do it, over and over, until he could see the shape of it with his eyes shut.

He'd learned Counter-Strike that way. Most of it. And most of what he'd learned, he'd learned from one player.

He'd been seventeen. A community server, the kind with a forty-player cap and a custom map rotation and an admin who banned people for spelling. And on the other team, nearly every night, an AWPer.

Mashi had lost to him hundreds of times. Hundreds. He'd kept a count, for a while, the way seventeen-year-olds do, and then stopped, because it was embarrassing. The AWPer never peeked first. That was the thing. Everyone else on that server peeked. They swung wide, they jiggled, they pushed, they got impatient and ran round corners into you. The AWPer didn't. He held an angle so long you forgot he was there. He held it so long you were sure he'd left, gone somewhere else, got bored, and you'd breathe out and step into the doorway, and he'd already be looking at the exact place you were stepping into. As if he'd known, a full minute earlier, where you'd go. As if he'd just been waiting for you to agree with him.

So Mashi had watched him. Every round, every night, for months. Watched where he held, and when he moved, and how long he waited, until he could see the shape of it with his eyes shut.

And one night he'd beaten him. Once. Waited longer than the AWPer did, for the first and only time, and caught him on the one step he took.

In all-chat, after, the AWPer had typed: *nice one. again.*

They'd been friends six years later. Close, by the end. Years of games together, and against. And in all that time, Mashi had never once seen anybody else fight like that. Hold like that. Wait like that.

Until yesterday.

***

They got back to Cinderhold after midnight.

The four soldiers had brought the news ahead, with Teague and Dace on their cloaks, and the whole fort was on the east wall again, with torches this time, when they came in out of the dark. Nobody cheered. Mashi thought they might, and they didn't. They just stood there along the top of the wall with their torches, in a long silent row, the way they'd stood when the sword went out. And when the gate opened and the escort came in, slowly, at Salty's pace, with Rhea limping and Aldous grey and the five of them all in one piece, the old woman from the laundry came down off the wall and walked straight across the courtyard to Aldous and put her arms round him.

Nobody said anything about the sword. Everybody could see it wasn't there.

Mashi didn't sleep. He hadn't expected to.

He lay on his bunk in the dark for an hour, listening to Salty breathe through his teeth in the next bunk, and Wendy whispering to him, and Maigo, for once, completely silent. Then he got up and went out and climbed the east wall and sat down on the parapet with his back against a merlon and looked out at the red under the stars.

And played it back.

***

He'd seen all of it. That was the thing. He'd been in his gap on the east side, the way he'd been all fight, holding it, and when the sea came in over the Redmarch he'd been the only one, apart from Yan, who wasn't looking at anything else. He'd turned his head and watched the man come. And he'd watched every single thing he did, the way he'd have watched a replay, and it was all still there, behind his eyes, frame by frame.

He played it back slowly now, sitting on the wall.

The first cast. The east one, in the air, two paces from Wendy. The line going out, a hundred paces, and the cut. Then the reel.

The second. The west one, over Yan. The hook going out, the catch, and then the man *reeling it in*. Pulling it to him. Across a hundred paces of air, off its feet, flailing. And then, as it came, the rod's shaft coming round once, short and flat, and the thing going past him in two pieces.

He never went to them. Mashi had noticed that at the time, and noticed it again now, and kept noticing it. Not once. Three hunters and fifty scouts, and the man hadn't taken one step toward any of them. He'd walked in a straight line from the north-west to the broken ring, the same steady pace from beginning to end, and everything he'd killed, he'd brought to himself.

He made them come to him.

That was the reel. Mashi sat very still on the wall and felt it go into him, cold and clear, the way things did when they were true. The hook was the shot; anybody could hit something with a hook. The *reel* was the point. The reel dragged the thing out of wherever it was and into the one place he was already looking, the one place he'd already decided it would be. You didn't swing wide on him. You didn't push. You didn't get to choose where you met him. He reeled you into his angle, and when you got there, he was already looking at it.

He made them peek.

And the north one. The one with Salty's sword in its side, that came at him on all fours across sixty paces faster than a horse. The man hadn't cast at it. He'd stopped walking, and held the rod low, and *waited*. Mashi had felt his own whole body screaming at the man to move, the way you scream at a teammate on a call, *he's right there, he's RIGHT THERE*. And he hadn't moved. Not until five paces. Not until the very last moment, when the thing was already committed, already in the air, already somewhere it couldn't come back from.

Then once.

Mashi had seen that before. On a community server, at seventeen, a few hundred times.

He sat on the wall and put his face in his hands for a while.

***

When he took them away again, he thought about the other thing.

Not the rod. The aura.

He couldn't feel aura the way Salty could, or Rhea. He was half a swordsman, the way he was half a mage. He felt everything half. But he'd felt the man's, yesterday, when the sea came in. Everyone had. And because he was half of each, and had spent a year trying to make the two halves touch, he'd felt something about it that he didn't think anybody else had.

It hadn't gone into the rod.

Aura went in, then out. That was how it worked. Mashi had worked it out in the winter, the night of the first frost, and nosebled on it for a year since. You drew aura in, from somewhere at the bottom of yourself, and then you put it out into whatever you trusted, a sword, an arrow, a fist, and it ran along the edge and came out the other side. In, then out. A line.

Magic was the other way. Mana came from outside. From the air, the ground, the world. You drew it in, and shaped it, and it came out as a spell. Outside, then in. Also a line. The same shape, backwards. And every time he'd tried to run both lines at once, they'd run straight into each other, head on, like two cars on a one-lane road, and his nose had bled, and once his heart had stopped for two beats and he'd woken up on the chapel floor with Aldous praying over him.

The man's aura hadn't run in a line.

It had gone *out*. Into everything. Not along an edge. Out of him like a spell does, filling the whole of the Redmarch, pinning fifty demons where they stood. That was aura behaving like magic. Mashi had felt it do that.

And then it had come back.

That was the part he'd almost missed. He'd only felt it at the very end, when the man let the scouts go: the sea opening on one side, east, and the fifty of them running. The aura going back. Not stopping. Not ending. Just turning round, at the far edge of where it had gone, and coming back in, the way a breath comes back in after you've breathed out. In, to the man. And then out again. And in.

Breathing.

Not a line. A circle.

Mashi sat on the wall in the dark with his eyes wide open.

*Put them end to end.*

He'd had it backwards. He'd been trying to run two lines side by side, at the same time, in the same direction. They'd never go. They ran against each other; he'd known that since the winter. But if you didn't run them side by side, if you put the end of one against the start of the other, so that the aura went out and kept going and became the mana coming in, and the mana came in and kept going and became the aura going out...

That wasn't two lines running into each other. That was one line, going round.

***

He tried it at dawn.

He waited for the light. He didn't know why. It just seemed like the kind of thing you should do in the light. He sat cross-legged on the parapet with his back against the merlon and his hands open on his knees and the sun coming up red over the mountains of Kharvos, the way it had come up yesterday, and closed his eyes, and breathed.

Out. And on the out-breath, he let the aura go. Not into a sword. Not into anything. Just *out*, into the air in front of him, the way the man's had gone out across the red. It didn't go far. A hand's width. Less. But it went.

In. And on the in-breath, he let the mana come. From outside, from the cold dawn air on the wall, in, the way it always came. And he didn't stop at the top of the breath. That was the trick. That was the thing he'd been doing wrong for a year: stopping at the top, and at the bottom, the way you do when you're careful. He didn't stop. He let the in-breath turn into the out-breath without a seam, and the mana coming in turned into the aura going out, and the aura going out turned into the mana coming in—

His two candles touched.

***

He'd wondered all year what it would feel like.

Yan had told him, in the spring, that they were always almost touching. Two candles, side by side, one warm and one cold, so close you could hardly fit a hair between them, never quite meeting. *I'll tell you,* Yan had said. *The second they touch, I'll tell you.*

It felt like two notes.

That was all he could think of, afterwards. Two notes that had been played together his whole life, very slightly out of tune with each other, so slightly that you'd stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing a fridge. And then, for one heartbeat, they weren't. They were the same note. And the whole world went quiet around it, and *clear*, the way the air goes clear after rain, and Mashi could see every single thing in front of him on the Redmarch, every marker, every tuft of grey grass, every crack in the red ground, as if he were holding an angle on all of them at once.

One heartbeat.

Then his nose started bleeding, and the two notes came apart, and he sat down hard on the stone of the parapet, which he was already sitting on, so mostly it was just his head hitting the merlon behind him.

He didn't faint.

That was the second thing he noticed. The first was the blood, which was everywhere, as usual. The second was that the sky was still the sky and the wall was still the wall, and he wasn't lying on a chapel floor with Aldous praying over him. He was sitting up. His heart was beating. Hard, and fast, but beating. He sat there with the blood running over his lip and down his chin, and laughed.

"They touched," said Yan.

He was standing at the top of the steps. Mashi didn't know how long he'd been there. Long enough, by his face.

"I know," said Mashi thickly, holding his nose. "I felt it."

"You promised me you'd go slower."

"That was slower."

Yan came and sat down on the parapet beside him, and handed him a handkerchief, because Yan always had a handkerchief, and looked out at the red. He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, very quietly:

"You watched him. The whole fight. I saw you."

"Yes."

"That's where you got it."

"Yes," said Mashi.

Yan nodded slowly.

"I need everybody," he said. "All five. Now, before breakfast. Before anyone writes anything." He stood up. "In the chapel."

Mashi opened his window before he followed him down.

[ Nikorasu · Tier 0: Initiate · Aura 18% · Mana 18% ]

Eighteen and eighteen. They always rose together. And for one heartbeat, between the two numbers, there was a third line he'd never seen before. Grey. Locked. No name on it at all. Then it was gone, and the window was the way it had always been.

He looked at the place where it had been for a long time.

***

The chapel was empty.

That was the first thing they all noticed, coming in, one after another, in the grey early light. The two trestles were still there in front of the altar, under the painted ceiling. There was nothing on them. Somebody had folded the canvas cover and left it on one of them, very neatly, and nobody had moved it.

They sat on the front bench, the five of them. Salty at the end, very carefully, with his ribs bound and his face grey. Wendy beside him. Then Maigo, who hadn't said a word since the grave. Then Mashi, with the bloody handkerchief still in his hand. Yan didn't sit. He stood in front of them, beside the empty trestles, with Suzu round his neck and his hands folded in front of him, so that they wouldn't see them shake. Mashi saw anyway.

"I'm going to tell you what I saw," said Yan. "And then I'm going to tell you what I think. And I want you to tell me if I'm wrong."

Nobody said anything.

So he told them.

***

He did it the way Yan did everything: slowly, carefully, one piece at a time, in order, the way he'd have laid out a plan on a call before a match. He told them about souls. About the grain. About a year of looking at every soldier and cook and carter in Cinderhold and seeing the same grain in every one of them, the grain of this world, the way wood from one forest has the same grain. And about the five of them, and himself, and all thirty-five, he thought, who had a different grain. *Earth*, he said. *That's the only word I've got for it. I've been looking at it every day for a year. I'd know it anywhere.*

And then he told them about the man.

"He's got it," said Yan. "Our grain. Earth. I'm as sure of that as I've ever been sure of anything." His deep voice didn't waver. "But it's taken. It's grown into him. All the way down, into every corner. The way an old tree grows through a wall. It's been in that body since the body was born. It's older than the body. It was somewhere else first." He paused. "Thirty-four years, the sum says. That's what Malcolm said. If it's three years at home, it's thirty-four here." He looked at them. "That's what I saw. A soul from home that's been here for thirty-four years."

Nobody said anything.

"And it's the size of the sea," said Yan. "I've felt that before, too. The night the cold one died. I felt it in the sword. I've told Wendy. I'm telling you now."

"And?" said Salty.

Yan looked at him.

"And when he looked at us," said Yan, more quietly, "it leaned."

***

"What does that mean?" said Wendy. "Leaned."

"Souls lean," said Yan. "Toward things. People. It's one of the first things I noticed, in the winter. Rhea's leans toward a fight. Aldous's leans toward the markers, every time he walks past them. Salty's leans toward you." He didn't look at Wendy when he said it, and Wendy didn't look at him, and Salty went slightly red under the grey. "They lean toward what they love. What they know. They don't lean toward strangers. Not ever. I've watched new soldiers come into this fort all year, and nobody's soul leans toward them for weeks."

He stopped. His hands had come apart in front of him. He put them back together.

"His leaned toward every one of us," said Yan. "When he looked at us, at the grave. One at a time. Salty. Wendy. Mashi. Me. Maigo." His voice went, very slightly, on the last name, and came back. "It leaned toward us the way you lean toward a fire, when you've come in from the cold. As if he'd been out in the cold for a very long time."

The chapel was very quiet.

"He's one of us," said Yan. "He's from home. And he knows us. Every one of us. He's known us a long time." He breathed out. "He's Ice."

***

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Mashi said, "He holds angles."

They all looked at him.

"He never peeks first," said Mashi. He was looking at the handkerchief in his hands, not at them. "He made them come to him. Every one. That's the reel. He reels them into the place he's already looking. And the north one, the one that ran at him. He waited till five paces. He held it till five paces." He swallowed. "I played against an AWPer for years, on a community server, when I was seventeen. He never peeked first. He held so long you forgot he was there. And when you moved, he was already looking at where you were going." He looked up. "Ice. That was Ice. I lost to him about four hundred times."

"You never told us that," said Maigo. Very quietly. The first thing he'd said all morning.

"It's embarrassing," said Mashi. "Four hundred."

Maigo laughed. It came out wet.

"He looked at my sunglasses," Maigo said. "At the grave. He looked right at them. And he nearly laughed." He pushed them up onto his antlers. "Nobody here thinks they're funny. Nobody in this whole world knows what they *are*. He nearly laughed."

"And the flowers were his," he said. "The hand under the ground. The one I felt in the spring. When he put his hand down by the cairn, I felt it. It was him. It's been him the whole time." He stopped. "That doesn't prove anything. About Ice. I know. I just... wanted you to know."

"Suzu put her head on his boot," said Yan. "She's never done that. Not to anyone. Not even to Salty, and she likes Salty." He touched the small white snake round his neck. "She knows what I know. Sometimes before I know it."

Salty shifted on the bench, very carefully, and winced.

"He didn't say no," he said. "I asked him. Straight. *Are you him.* He didn't say who. He didn't say no." He looked at the floor. "I played with him two years. Ice never once said no to anything. He just changed the subject."

"*Go home safely,*" said Maigo. "*Your friends will want to know you're all right.* That's changing the subject."

"Yeah," said Salty.

***

Wendy hadn't said anything.

She sat beside Salty with her hand on his knee and her silent headphones round her neck and her fingers on the little star on her necklace, turning it. She'd been turning it the whole time. Mashi watched her do it. She was looking at the empty trestles.

Yan looked at her.

"Wendy," he said.

She didn't look up.

"I told you in here," said Yan. "A week ago. About the sword. I told you because you'd never believe it unless it was true." He waited. "So."

Wendy turned the little star on its chain once more, and let it go.

"I believe it," she said.

She said it flatly, the way she said everything. As if it cost her nothing. Mashi could see exactly what it cost her.

Yan closed his eyes.

"Then it's true," he said.

***

They didn't say anything for a while after that. They just sat there, the five of them, on the front bench of the chapel, in front of two empty trestles, in the grey morning light, the way they'd sat in a hundred lobbies on Earth after a match they couldn't quite believe they'd won.

Then Maigo said, "We have to tell everyone."

And everything got complicated.

***

"We have to," said Maigo. "We *have* to. Gabe said he's coming to find him. In the channel. *Declan, if you can somehow read this, I'm coming to find you.* Everyone saw it. We can't just *not*—"

"Where?" said Salty.

Maigo stopped.

"Coming to find him where?" said Salty. He said it gently, for Salty. "He walked west. That's all we know. West, with a sword. We don't know where he lives. We don't know his name. We don't know anything."

"We know it's him."

"We think it's him."

"*Yan* knows it's him."

"And if we post it," said Wendy, "what happens?"

Everyone looked at her.

She'd folded her arms. Her mismatched eyes were very steady. "We post *the Hero is Ice and he walked west*. Gabe reads it. Deston reads it. Xav reads it." She shook her head. "They're on the Redmarch in a month. All three of them. Looking for him. And they walk straight into what we walked into yesterday. Fifty scouts and three hunters, in the open, with nobody to pull them out." Her voice didn't change. "I'm not posting something that gets Gabe killed looking for a man who's already walked away."

Maigo opened his mouth, and shut it.

"He let them go," said Mashi.

They looked at him.

"The scouts," said Mashi. "He could have taken all of them. I watched the line go out after them, four hundred paces, faster than they could run. And it stopped. And came back empty." He turned the handkerchief over in his hands. "He wanted them to run home. He wanted Kharvos to know the Black Blade came for his sword. That's all he wanted them to know. Not where he went. Not who he is." He paused. "If he'd wanted anyone to know who he is, he'd have said. He was standing right in front of us."

***

Yan had been quiet. He'd let them talk. He did that on calls, too: let everyone say their piece, and listened, and waited until they'd run out, and then said the thing they'd all been walking round.

He said it now.

"He looked at us," said Yan. "One at a time. He knows exactly who we are. He knew Maigo's sunglasses. He knew Suzu. He's known us the whole time we've been here." He looked down at his hands. "And he chose not to say. Salty asked him straight out, and he chose not to say."

"So?" said Maigo. Very small.

"So that's his," said Yan. "Not ours. It's the only thing he's got left that's his." He looked up. "He didn't answer, Maigo. When someone doesn't answer, that's an answer." He paused. "It just isn't *no*. It's *not yet*."

Maigo looked at him for a long time.

Then he pulled his sunglasses down over his eyes, and nodded, once.

"Not yet," he said.

***

They wrote it together. It took an hour. They argued over every line, the way they'd argued over callouts on Earth, and in the end Yan wrote it, because Yan always wrote it, slowly, with all five of them reading over his window.

[ Suzuyun: Group 3. All five of us are alive. Everyone in the escort is alive.
Suzuyun: We were carrying the sword across the Redmarch yesterday. Halfway to the grave, demon scouts came. Fifty or more. And three of the bigger ones, the kind that went for Winter at Valcrest.
Suzuyun: We couldn't hold them. We tried. Salty has two broken ribs. Rhea and our sergeant are hurt. Aldous burned himself out. They weren't after us. They were after the sword.
Suzuyun: The Hero came. The Black Blade. Across the red, on his own, with a fishing rod.
Suzuyun: He killed the three big ones. The scouts ran. He let them.
Suzuyun: Then he took his sword back. Out of Salty's hands. He's taken it home, wherever home is. He walked west.
Suzuyun: Salty asked him who she was. The woman in the grave.
Suzuyun: Her name was Diwa. She was a healer.
Suzuyun: That's all. We're safe. ]

The channel was still for a moment.

Then it broke.

***

It went the way Mashi had known it would. The way the channel always went now, since the night of the sum. Thirty people at once, all over the kingdom, all reading the same lines, all typing.

[ Tiger: WAIT
Tiger: WAS IT HIM
Rachael: are you all ok?? salty are you ok??
Kei: Hisui says broken ribs need to be bound tight but not too tight. Is someone there who knows?
Gabe: what did he look like
Gabe: yan. what did he look like
Hanarisu: Yan. Did he say anything else?
Newish: A FISHING ROD???
Cursiffe: Diwa. I'll write it down. Somebody should.
Gabe: did he say anything at all ]

Yan didn't answer straight away. He sat on the bench with his window open and looked at it for a long time, while the lines went up and vanished off the top, forty at a time.

Then he wrote:

[ Suzuyun: He didn't say. Salty asked him. He didn't say. ]

And Salty, beside him, opened his own window, slowly, with his ribs bound, and wrote:

[ Salty: he didn't say. ]

And Maigo, who had written every single line in that channel for a year in capitals, at full volume, because that was how Maigo wrote, opened his window and wrote:

[ Maigo: he didn't say ]

Lowercase. Quiet. Mashi looked at it, and looked at Maigo, and Maigo didn't look back.

The channel went on for a while. Questions. Guesses. Tiger in capitals. Malcolm with an IF. Milli, very carefully, from the capital: *If he took the sword back, he's alive and he's strong and he's close enough to the Redmarch to walk there. That's three things we didn't know yesterday.* Group 3 didn't add anything. They'd said what they were going to say. They held.

***

Mashi noticed two things, sitting there.

The first was a line from Duskhollow. It came in late, after most of the noise, on its own.

[ Xavier: how many times did he move ]

Mashi looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote:

[ Nikorasu: once. every time. ]

There was a long pause. Long enough that the line nearly went off the top.

[ Xavier: yeah.
Xavier: i know. ]

That was all. Mashi didn't know what Xav knew, or how. He thought about a sword-mistress Xav had written about, once, in a letter: forty recruits in a ditch on the Redmarch, and one man who came for them and *only ever moved once*. He thought about Petra telling Xav that Xav did it too. He didn't write anything else. Neither did Xav.

The second thing was the lines that didn't come.

All that noise, and every group in it. Thirty people asking *was it him*, one way or another, in capitals and in lowercase and in Malcolm's IFs. Every group but one. From Wrenhaven, from Group 1, all morning, there was only ever one line.

[ Higu: We're glad you're safe. All of you. ]

Nothing else. Not *was it him*. Not *what did he look like*. Not one question. Just that.

And from Valcrest, a little later, one more line, the same shape:

[ Winter: Glad you're safe. ]

Mashi looked at those two lines for a long time.

Then he closed his window, and didn't say anything to anyone about them. He thought, maybe, he wasn't the only one in the kingdom who'd learned to hold an angle.

***

Rhea found them at breakfast. She'd washed the blood off her face, mostly, and had a line of Aldous's stitches across her scalp, and she came into the mess hall and sat down opposite the five of them with her bowl and didn't eat it.

"Did you *see* him?" said Rhea.

They looked at her.

"Did you see him," she said again. She was very pale under the stitches. "He didn't look round. Not once. Two hundred paces, the last one. He reeled it in from two hundred paces, and he didn't even *look round*." She put her spoon down. "I've been Gleaming for six years. I trained every day of my life to get there. I thought I was good. I thought..." She stopped. "He didn't look round."

"No," said Salty.

Rhea stared at her porridge.

"I want to arm-wrestle him," she said, at last, very quietly.

There was a pause.

"You'd lose," said Salty.

"I *know* I'd lose. That's not the *point*." She picked her spoon back up. Her hand wasn't quite steady. "I just want to know what it feels like. Losing to that."

Maigo started laughing. It came out of him all at once, helplessly, the first real noise he'd made since the grave, and then Wendy was laughing too, and Salty, holding his ribs and swearing, and Rhea glaring at all of them and then not glaring, and Yan with his hand over his eyes. And Mashi sat in the middle of it with his porridge and his bloody handkerchief and thought: *yes.* That was exactly it. That was what it had felt like. Four hundred times.

He wouldn't have given back a single one.

***

That night he went back up on the wall.

He sat on the parapet with his back against the merlon and his hands open on his knees, and breathed. Out, and the aura going out. In, and the mana coming in. No seam. No stopping at the top.

They touched for two heartbeats this time.

His nose bled. He didn't faint. He sat there with the handkerchief pressed to his face and looked out at the red under the stars, and thought about a community server, a long time ago, and an AWPer who never peeked first, and a line in all-chat at the end of a round.

*nice one. again.*

"Again," said Mashi, to nobody, and breathed out.

***

A long way to the west, on a farm track between two fields of stubble, a man with a black sword on his shoulder stopped by a stile in the dark, and opened something only he could see, and read for a while.

Then he closed it, and stood there a moment longer, looking back east the way he'd come.

Then he walked on, toward the lake.
