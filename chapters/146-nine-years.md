Every year for eight years, he'd walked east on his own.

He'd gone at the end of summer. Always the same week. He'd pack on the first morning with the dew still on the pumpkins, and give Rowan the hens, and give Odette the key to the cabin, and walk up the lake road in the grey with the rod over his shoulder and the good knife in his pack where Diwa used to put it and Alea now did, and not look back. A month, there. A few days at the grave. A month back. He'd come down the lake road again in the first frost, and the hens would hate him, and Alea would be at the cabin door with her arms folded, and they wouldn't talk about it.

She'd stayed with Rowan and Odette. Every year. In the lace room at the top of Odette's house, the one with the curtains Odette had made out of a bridal veil, which Alea said was like sleeping inside a cloud. She'd been nine the first year. Ten the second. Eleven.

At eleven she'd asked.

He remembered it exactly. She'd been standing on the jetty in the evening the day before he left, with her little willow rod, not fishing, and she'd said, without looking at him, *can I come?*

And he'd said *when you're older*.

And she'd nodded, and not asked again. Not at twelve, or thirteen, or any year after. She'd packed the good knife instead, every year, secretly, the way her mother had. He'd found that out a week ago on a hill above this city. *I've been doing it since I was twelve. You never noticed.*

She'd never seen the Redmarch. She'd never seen the rise an hour's walk from the wall at Cinderhold, with the ring burned into it. She'd never seen the grave. A cairn of stones under a black iron headstone that had been half of a sword, with four words on it in a language nobody in this world could read, and a ring of flowers round it, and a young tree that a boy with antlers had planted so she wouldn't be alone.

She'd never once seen where her mother was.

***

He woke on the sixty-seventh day before it was light, on a bench in the common room of the Broken Lance, with his coat over him and somebody's boots under his head.

He knew straight away. The way you know, some mornings, that the weather's changed in the night without having to look out of the window. He lay there in the dark common room with thirty people asleep round him on benches and floors and each other, and knew it the way he'd known on the end of a jetty in the winter that *not yet* was over.

He was going to ask her.

He got up. He took the rod off the wall. He went upstairs, very quietly, past Group 7's rooms and Group 1's, to the small room at the end of the second floor that Alea had been sharing with Petra all week, and found the door open and Petra asleep with her sword on, like a soldier, and Alea's bed empty.

He knew where she'd be.

***

She was under the willow.

He came out of the west gate in the grey and along the river path through the water-meadows, and there she was, on the bank, where Group 2 had sat with him a week ago. With her knees up and her chin on them and Astra lying on the grass beside her, and a rod. Not her own; her own was at the lake. A cheap one, a cane and a line, that she must have borrowed from somebody at the Lance or bought at the market, cast out into the slow brown river in the half-dark, the float bobbing on the water by the reeds.

She didn't look round.

"You're up," she said.

"So are you."

"I couldn't sleep." She watched the float. "I keep waking up and thinking about the box. Lucan reading it out." She was quiet. "And everyone turning round to look at you."

He sat down on the bank beside her. He put his own rod across his knees and didn't cast it. They watched her float for a while.

"There's a pike in there," said Alea.

"There's always a pike."

"*Trust*," said Alea, and grinned at the water, and he laughed, quietly, so as not to scare the fish.

***

He didn't know how to ask.

That was the thing he'd think about later. He'd spent thirty-four years in this world and twenty-six in the other one, sixty years of words, and he'd talked his way out of tents and into kings' confidence and through more battle plans than he could count, and he sat on a riverbank beside his daughter at dawn and didn't know how to ask her one thing.

*When you're older*, he'd said. Six years ago. On a jetty.

She was seventeen now. Tempered. She'd won the Fledgling Lists two days ago by coming at a hook like a pike, and stood on a platform in front of forty thousand people and written her mother's name in a box.

She was older.

"Alea," he said.

"Mm."

"The anniversary's in less than two months."

Her float went still on the water. Not because a fish had taken it. Because her hands had gone still on the rod.

"I know," said Alea. "The ninth."

"I'm going east. With Group 3. They're going back to Cinderhold anyway. They asked me." He watched the float. "So I won't be walking on my own this year."

"I know," said Alea again. Her voice was very careful. "Yan told me. He said *with all of you*. I heard."

She didn't say anything else. She didn't look at him. She watched her float on the brown water, and her ears had gone, very slowly, flat.

He knew that look. She'd had it at eleven on a jetty, not looking at him.

*Can I come?*

He'd said *when you're older*. And she'd never asked again.

She wasn't going to ask now. He understood that, sitting there. She wasn't going to ask, because she'd asked once and been told no, and she was her mother's daughter, and her mother had never once in ten years asked him for the same thing twice. She'd just waited until he worked it out.

He'd worked it out.

"Come and see your mother," he said.

***

Alea didn't move.

The float sat on the water by the reeds. The river went past, slow and brown, the way the Wren went past the bottom of the lake road at home. A heron was standing in the shallows on the far bank, very still, watching the water, the way he'd told her to watch the whole thing every morning of her life.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she put the borrowed rod down on the grass, very carefully, the line still out in the river, and turned her head and looked at him. With her mother's face. Her mother's eyes. Deciding.

"Yes," she said.

That was all. One word. The same way her mother had said *here*, once, on the end of a half-ruined jetty, about a lake.

Then her face did something, and she turned it away, quickly, toward the river, and put both her hands over it, and sat there on the bank under the willow with her shoulders going and Astra lying in the grass beside her. Not making any sound. The way he'd taught her, without meaning to, by never once making any sound himself.

He didn't touch her. Not yet. He sat beside her with the rod across his knees and let her, the way you let a fish run.

On the water, her float went under.

Neither of them noticed.

***

"Why didn't you ever take me?" said Alea, eventually.

She'd taken her hands away from her face. She was looking at the river, at the place where her float had been. The line had gone slack; whatever had taken it had got off. She didn't seem to care.

"When I asked," she said. "At eleven. You said *when you're older*." She wiped her face on her sleeve. "I thought it was because I was too small. And then I wasn't too small any more, and you still went on your own, and I thought—" She stopped. "I thought it was because you wanted it to be yours. Just yours. Her. That I'd had her for nine years and you'd had her for ten and the grave was the only bit that was just yours."

He didn't say anything for a while.

He wanted to give her a true answer. She'd always been able to tell when he didn't. Her mother could too.

"Some of that," he said.

Alea looked at him.

"Some of it was that," he said. "Some of it was the Redmarch. It was still the front, the first years. Scouts. I didn't want you on it." He turned the rod in his hands. "And some of it was the rise."

Alea's ears went flat.

"It's an hour's walk from the wall," he said. "The place where they took her. Where the general put the ring of fires round it so I could see from the wall at Cinderhold." He was quiet. "You can see it from the grave. It's just a rise. Grass on it now. The ring's still there, burned into the ground. You can see it from the cairn if you look east." He breathed. "I couldn't carry it twice. Her, and the rise. And you seeing the rise. I couldn't carry that and watch you see it at the same time. So I carried her, on my own, and left you with Rowan, where you wouldn't have to see anything." He looked at the water. "That was the real reason. The rest were true. That one was the reason."

Alea didn't say anything for a long time.

"And now?" she said.

He thought about it.

"Now you wrote her name in front of a city," he said. "*Daughter of Diwa, a healer*. Lucan read it out. Forty thousand people heard it." He looked at her. "You're carrying her too. You've been carrying her the whole time. I just never saw it until you put it in a box." He was quiet. "I don't have to do it on my own any more. I don't think I ever did. I just didn't know you were carrying the other end."

Alea looked at him with her mother's face.

"I've been carrying the other end since I was nine," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," said Alea. She sounded exactly like Jared on a riverbank. "Be *slower*." And then, when he looked at her: "That's what she used to say. To you. When you were being stupid about something. Rowan told me. *Slower*."

He hadn't known Rowan knew that.

"She did," he said. "She said it all the time."

"Then I'm saying it," said Alea. "Slower. You've been carrying it on your own for nine years. Slower." She picked up the borrowed rod off the grass and began to reel in the slack line, very carefully, the way he'd taught her when she was four. "Let me take some."

***

He let her.

He sat on the bank beside her while she reeled in, and found the hook bare, and baited it again with a bit of bread from her pocket, and cast it out, and it went out over the brown water in a long easy line, like a cast going out over still water, like a sword going round. And landed by the reeds. And the float sat on the water, bobbing.

"Astra," said Alea.

"What about her?"

"She'll be there. At the grave. With the other half." Alea looked at the black sword lying in the grass beside her, the half of a slab of iron that a king had drawn narrower and given a new point and that she'd named for where she was going. "They haven't been in the same place since the cut. Since the king split them on the shore." She was quiet. "Will it feel anything? Do you think? When they're close?"

He looked at the sword.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "It came out of Kharvos. Off a hill, a long way past the border, standing in the ground on its own. It burned the things that touched it. Nobody knows who made it." He was quiet. "It's never been two things before. I don't know what it does when it's two things in the same place."

"We'll find out."

"We'll find out," he agreed.

Alea watched her float.

"I'm going to stand there," she said. "At the grave. With her half on my back. And Mum's half in the ground. And look east at the rise." Her voice didn't shake. "And then I'm going to tell her about the box."

He couldn't say anything to that. He sat on the riverbank with the rod across his knees and his daughter beside him and the heron on the far bank, and found that he couldn't say anything at all, and didn't need to.

Her float went under again.

This time she struck. Early, while it was still deciding to. And the line went taut, and the cane bent, and something in the brown water of a river in the south pulled back.

"*Pike*," said Alea.

"Told you," he said.

***

He told Gabe and Deston and Xav that afternoon.

In the yard behind the Broken Lance, where Salty had beaten Xav with sticks four nights ago, sitting on the edge of the horse trough in the sun. The three of them in a row on the edge of the trough, the way they'd sat on the end of the jetty in the spring, and him standing in front of them.

"East," said Gabe. "With Group 3."

"East."

"And Alea."

"And Alea."

Gabe looked at him for a long time. Then he looked at Deston, and Deston looked at Xav, and they all three looked back at him, with the same face, the one they'd had at thirteen in a choir room when the choirmaster said *who did that* and all three of them knew who'd done that.

"We're coming," said Gabe.

"No."

"*Declan*—"

"No," he said. Gently. "You're not."

***

He'd known they'd say it. He'd thought about what he'd say back since the riverbank.

"Go home," he said. "Your postings. Kingsferry. Duskhollow. The king's sending everyone back. Sister Maud's going to want Rachael, and Rachael's going to want all of you round her for three weeks on the road, after that apple." He looked at Gabe. "Harl's going to want his striker back. He thinks you're the only person in Kingsferry who doesn't burn his eyebrows." He looked at Xav. "Petra's going to want her dawn class. She's running it now. She needs people to run it at."

"She's got the whole of Group 6," said Xav.

"She's got you," he said. "You're the one she wants to beat."

Xav didn't say anything. His face did nothing at all.

"And," he said, and stopped.

"And?" said Deston.

"And somebody's got to look after the lake."

The three of them looked at him.

"It's on the way," he said. "Near enough. The river road north goes past the bottom of the Wren. Group 1's going home to Wrenhaven. You'll be with them as far as the turn." He looked at the three of them in a row on the horse trough, his brothers, his best friend from a polytechnic in Singapore. "Go up the lake road. Just for a night. Knock on Rowan's door. Tell him I'm coming back for the late pumpkins. Check the jetty's still standing. Check the hens hate somebody."

"They'll hate *me*," said Gabe.

"They'll hate you very much," he agreed. "They hated me for eight years. They'll be glad of the variety."

***

Gabe stood up off the trough.

He was a big man. Slightly overweight and very strong, the way he'd always been, since he was thirteen. He'd held a gate shut with a sword four days ago against a ram. He stood up off the horse trough and stood in front of the man he'd come upriver to find, and looked at him, at a face he couldn't see, the way he'd looked at it on a jetty in the spring when the song stopped.

"Nine years," said Gabe.

"Nine years."

"You walked it on your own. Every year. Eight times." Gabe's jaw was tight. "We didn't know. Any of us. We were in Singapore going to work and you were walking across a battlefield on your own to sit by a grave. Every year." He breathed. "I want to walk it with you. Once. Just once."

"I know."

"Then *let* me."

He looked at Gabe for a long moment.

"Next year," he said.

Gabe stared at him.

"Next year," he said again. "The tenth. You and Deston and Xav. All three. I'll walk it with you." He was quiet. "This year's Alea's. She's never been. She asked me at eleven and I said *when you're older* and she never asked again." He looked at Gabe. "This year she gets to be the one walking next to me. On her own. With her mother's sword on her back."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he put his arm round his neck from the side, like a falling wall, and didn't take it away.

"*Seriously*, Declan," said Gabe, into the side of his head. Thickly. "Next year. You'd better mean it."

"I mean it."

"You'd better put *trust* on it."

"I'm not putting *trust* on it," he said. "That'd make it a lie."

Gabe made a sound that was very nearly a laugh.

On the horse trough, Deston was humming. The first bar of the middle part. And stopping. And Xav, beside him, with his hands on his knees, said very quietly:

"Okay. Next year."

***

He wrote to Rowan that evening.

On the king's cream paper. The last sheet. He'd used the first one for *Ask*. He'd been keeping the rest since the spring, the way you keep good paper, for when you've got something to say. He sat at the end of the long table in the Broken Lance with the noise going on round him and Calvin's kettle at his elbow and wrote, in this world's script, in the plain narrow hand he'd written his anonymous books in for eight years, the whole of what he had to say:

*Late pumpkins. A.*

He looked at it.

It was enough. Rowan would know. Rowan would read it at his kitchen table with the cups on the shelf, and know that the man he'd stopped at his kitchen door one night a few weeks ago, *not tonight, come home and knock on that door like a stranger and I'll get the cups out*, was coming home. Late. After the anniversary. For the pumpkins. And that he'd knock.

He folded it.

Then Alea, beside him on the bench, with her chin on her hand, said, "Give it here."

He gave it to her.

She unfolded it. She read it. *Late pumpkins. A.* She looked at it for a long time with her mother's look.

Then she held out her hand for the pen, and he gave her that too, and she wrote, underneath, in her own hand, the round careful script Odette had taught her at the cabin table when she was six:

*Both of us. A.*

She folded it again along his folds and gave it back.

He looked at it. Two letters. *A*, and *A*.

"He'll cry," said Alea.

"He'll cry," he agreed.

"Odette will pretend she isn't."

"Odette will make you a new curtain," he said. "Out of something ridiculous. For the lace room. And you won't need it, because you won't be in the lace room this year."

Alea's ears went straight up.

"No," she said. "I won't."

***

He gave the letter to Maren.

She was in the yard, at dusk, with her list, going over the cart for the road north. The brewer's dray and the miller's cart, with their wheels greased and their bad shoes replaced, ready for Wrenhaven in three weeks and a bit. She looked at the folded cream paper he held out to her for a long moment.

"For Rowan," he said.

"Into his hands," said Maren.

"Into his hands."

She took it. She put it in the inside pocket of her coat, against her chest, the way Garrow had once carried a letter from Cinderhold, and buttoned it.

"I'll be on the road a month," said Maren. "And in Wrenhaven before you are, by two." She looked at the cart. "Who carries the water? On the way north?"

He thought about it.

"Gigichi," he said. "Ask Gigichi. She'll say no and do it anyway."

Maren looked at him. Then, for the first time in two years, Maren of the lists and the reports and the small exact hand, the guide who'd been given a description of a man by a lake and an order not to disturb him, and who'd written *I think he is happy* at the bottom of a report because it was the truest thing she'd ever written, laughed.

"Yes," she said. "She will."

***

Halvard found him on the roof, that night. The last night but one.

He'd gone up to the flat bit above the back stairs where Winter and Ina sat in the evenings, and Kei and Yan had sat with two snakes. Winter and Ina weren't there. He sat with his back to the chimney and the rod across his knees and looked at the city, and felt the Advisor come up the stairs a long time before he heard him. A thin careful flame. Not quite so careful any more.

Halvard came out onto the roof and stood there.

"You're going east," said Halvard.

"In two days."

"With the girl."

"With Alea."

Halvard nodded, slowly. He didn't sit down. He stood by the chimney with his hands behind his back and looked at the city, the way he'd looked at the Redmarch from a wall at Cinderhold twenty years ago before he found a boy on the parapet.

"The king and I go north-west," he said. "To Solmere. In two days also. The household's packing." He was quiet. "He's going down into the vault with Oswin the night we get back. To the door." He paused. "I'm going with him."

"Good."

"And then," said Halvard, and stopped.

He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. They both knew what was at the top of the west tower in Solmere. The bottom drawer, on the left, that stuck in wet weather. And the key, in Halvard's coat, beside a folded report.

"When you've opened it," he said, "write to me."

Halvard looked at him.

"I'll answer," he said. "This time."

Halvard didn't say anything for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, "You never answered. Not once. In eight years. The trader. Maren. Aldous. *Tell Halvard I've taken it*. That was the most you ever sent."

"You never sent," he said. "Not once. In eight years. They were all in a drawer."

Halvard went very still. Then, slowly, the thin grey face did something it hadn't done in a long time.

"That's fair," he said.

"It's fair," he agreed. "So send one. And I'll answer it."

***

He found Petra at the end of the evening, in the yard, sharpening her sword on the edge of the horse trough by lamplight, because she said she couldn't sleep the night before a road without doing it.

He sat down on the trough beside her. She didn't stop sharpening.

"I wanted to ask you something," he said.

"Ask."

"At the closing. When Lucan read it out. The king put his hand on his chest. And then the old soldiers on the third tier. And then other men, all round the stands. Older ones. Hand flat. Here." He touched his own chest. "I don't know what it means. I've never seen it."

Petra's whetstone stopped.

She sat on the edge of the horse trough with the sword across her knees and the stone in her hand, very still, for a long time. Then she put the stone down.

"You wouldn't have," she said.

He waited.

"The morning after," said Petra. Very quietly. "The Redmarch. You came back to the wall at Cinderhold from the rise. On foot. On your own. With the black sword." She didn't look at him. "I was on the wall. I'd been in the ditch two nights before, and you'd cut through to us, and I was on the wall that morning with my arm in a sling, with everyone. The whole garrison. Every man who was still standing."

He didn't say anything.

"Nobody told us to," said Petra. "Nobody gave an order. You came across the red toward the gate, walking, with the sword, and your face—" She stopped. "Nobody could look at your face. Even then. It wasn't the thing it is now. It was just that nobody could." She breathed. "And somebody on the wall put his hand on his chest. Like that. Flat. Over his heart. And then the man next to him. And then all of us. All the way along the wall. Every man. Hand on chest. While you walked to the gate." She looked down at her own hand on the sword. "You didn't look up. Not once. You walked in through the gate, and an hour later you walked out of it again with a spade, back across the red, and nobody but the king went near you for three days."

He sat on the horse trough very still.

"It's what the Redmarch men do," said Petra. "Since. When they talk about you. When they hear your name in a song. When somebody says *the Black Blade* in an inn. Not the young ones; they don't know. The ones who were there." She picked up the whetstone again. "The king was there. He was on the wall. He did it too, that morning. I saw him."

She went back to sharpening.

"I didn't know," he said, after a long time.

"I know you didn't," said Petra. "You were looking at the ground." The whetstone went down the blade. Up. Down. "That's why we did it. So somebody would be looking at you."

***

He went up to bed very late. The last night but one.

He didn't sleep. He lay on the bench in the common room with his coat over him and somebody's boots under his head, and thirty people asleep round him, and listened to them breathe.

Nine years.

He thought about a wall at Cinderhold. A whole garrison, every man still standing, with their hands on their chests. And him walking under them through the gate with the black sword and his face doing something nobody could look at, and never once looking up. For nine years he'd thought he'd walked back across the red alone.

He hadn't. He'd just been looking at the ground.

He opened his window. Out of habit.

He looked at it for a long time.

Still sixty. It hadn't moved since the closing. It didn't need to. Some days the bar moved and some days it didn't, and the days it didn't were sometimes the ones that mattered most: a riverbank at dawn, a girl saying *yes*; three brothers on a horse trough, *next year*; a wall he'd never looked up at.

He closed it.

At the other end of the common room, on a bench by the window, his daughter was asleep with Astra propped against the wall beside her and her mother's ears twitching in a dream. He listened to her breathe too, for a long time, the way he'd listened at the foot of the stairs every night of her life.

*She's coming, Diwa*, he thought. *This year. I'm bringing her*.

*Slower. I know. I'm going slower*.
