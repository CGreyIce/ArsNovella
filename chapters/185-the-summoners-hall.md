The petition came the morning after the king got home, which Hana thought was either very bad manners or very good timing, and probably both.

She heard about it the way she heard about everything now: through her wrist. Seven halves of seven cords on a purple string, and a gold half-heart beside them, and every one of them warm, most of the time, with somebody a long way off holding the other end. Maren in Wrenhaven. Aldous at Cinderhold. Emrys in Kingsferry, Garrick at Duskhollow, Corvin at Thornwall, Lucan in Valcrest. And Oswin, three floors down, in the archive, who held his half more than any of them, mostly by accident, because he'd tied it round his wrist and forgotten it was there.

It went warm at dawn. She was still in bed. Milli was asleep beside her with his tail over her ankle, the way he'd slept every night since they were married, in two worlds. She turned the strip round the knot of Oswin's half to the light from the window and read it.

*Hall's petition on the Council list. Today. Second bell. They want the leaves.*

She lay there for a while, looking at it.

Then she woke Milli, which took three tries, and went to find Kestra.

***

The Summoners' Hall stood across the old square from the castle gate, and had stood there for three hundred years, and looked it.

Hana had walked past it every day for two years and a half. Everybody in Solmere had. A tall narrow grey building with no windows on the street side and a door with an open hand carved on it, worn so smooth by weather that you had to know it was a hand to see it. Aldemar's device. The Open-Handed. She'd read about it in Oswin's books, the first winter, before she knew it mattered: King Aldemar had founded the Hall in the ninth year of his reign to keep the summoning circle and the records of every summoning, and given it his own mark, and a charter, and a seat on the Council that it had held ever since.

There were twelve summoners now. Mages, all of them, middling to good; the Warden was Beacon-tier, Oswin said, which made him one of the strongest mages in the capital after the Advisor. They lived in the Hall and kept the circle clean and kept the records, and once in a long while, when a king asked them, they stood in the rings of the circle on the floor of the summoning hall and did the thing the circle was for.

They'd done it two years and a half ago.

Hana had never thought about that. Not properly. She'd thought about the Advisor, who'd laid the sword in the circle, and about the king, who'd allowed it, and about the circle itself, which Thea said was code and Cursiffe said was a form with a blank box. She'd never once thought about twelve grey people standing in the rings round the edge of the floor that night, chanting, holding the circle open while thirty-five people fell through it.

She thought about it now, in the corridor outside the Council chamber, with Kestra at the door and Milli beside her with his tail very still and the strip on Oswin's cord gone cold, because Oswin had let go of it to come up the stairs.

They'd stood there. Twelve of them. And pulled.

***

They weren't supposed to be in the chamber. Only the strong spoke to the king; it was the oldest rule in Solmere, older than the Hall, and it applied to Councils most of all. But Kestra had come to Hana's door at first light with her book under her arm and said, without preamble, *the king says you'll want to hear this*, and taken them up to the gallery herself. Hana and Milli. Jared, who went where Aleaka went. Aleaka, with Brisk on her bare arm, glaring at everything. Cursiffe, with his vial of ink on its chain and his glasses pushed up and his face doing the sly half-smile it did when he was frightened and didn't want anyone to know. Oswin last, out of breath, with ink on his chin.

The gallery was a narrow stone balcony along one side of the chamber, high up, where clerks sat with their slates. Kestra put them at the end of it, out of the light, and stood behind them.

Below them, the Council.

Hana had never seen it sit. She'd imagined it, the way you imagine anything you've only heard about: a great hall, banners, a throne. It wasn't. It was a long room with a long table and a fire at one end and twenty people round the table in good coats, arguing in low voices about grain. The king at the head, in a plain coat, with soot still faintly in the creases of his knuckles, because he'd been at the forge at dawn, the way he always was the morning after a long ride. The Advisor on his right, thin and grey and very straight. And the rest. The Lord Treasurer, who Hana knew from Kestra's stories, a soft careful man who used the word *irregular* the way other people used *please*. The Lord Chancellor. A woman from the guilds. Two generals. A bishop. Names she'd half learned and faces she'd watched from windows for two years.

And at the far end of the table, in the seat that had been the Hall's for three hundred years, an old man in grey.

***

The Warden was called Aubrecht.

He was very old. Older than the king. Thin and upright, with white hair cropped close and a long grey robe with an open hand stitched on the breast in silver thread, and a face that Hana, who'd spent her whole life on Earth reading faces through a camera at a hundred frames a second, found she could read perfectly and didn't like at all.

Not because it was cruel. It wasn't cruel. It was sincere.

He waited until the grain was done. He waited until the king looked down the table at him and nodded. Then he stood up, slowly, with his hands folded in his sleeves, and the room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet for old men, and he spoke.

"Majesty," he said. "My lords. The Hall asks for the leaves."

His voice was dry and thin and very clear. It carried to the gallery without trying.

"Three hundred and twelve years ago," said the Warden, "King Aldemar's summoners found forty leaves under the first stone of the summoning hall, in a hand that drew the oldest rings of the circle, and asked him to burn them. They gave their reason. I will give it again, because it was true then and it is true now." He paused. "*A thing which cannot be read is a thing which was not meant to be*."

Beside Hana, Cursiffe went still.

She felt him do it. Not just still; the other kind, the kind that came over him when he was reading something in English in the archive and found a word that didn't belong. Cursiffe had written that sentence down himself, in blue ink, on oiled paper, on the floor of a vault, the night the door opened. *My summoners wished them burned. They said that a thing which cannot be read is a thing which was not meant to be.*

And here it was. Three hundred years later. In an old man's mouth. Word for word.

"Aldemar refused them," the Warden went on. "He shut the leaves behind a door that only a king asking for somebody else could open, so that no king would. And for three hundred years none did." He turned his head, very slightly, toward the head of the table. "Until this autumn."

The king didn't say anything.

***

He made his case well. Hana gave him that. She'd watched a great many people make a case, in two worlds; she'd made a great many herself, carefully, methodically, in a calm smooth voice, to sponsors and moderators and a cat-eared husband. She knew a good one.

This was a good one.

He didn't talk about doctrine, after the first sentence. He talked about the binding.

"The circle still runs," he said. "The Advisor knows it. The girl in the north who reads the circle like a ledger knows it; she has told the Advisor so, in his own reports. The binding that holds the thirty-five in this world, that gives them their windows and their tongue and their lives here, is still running. It has not stopped since the night we opened it. We kept it open. Twelve of us. On the rings." He was quiet for a moment. "I stood on the third ring that night, my lords. I felt it take them. I have felt it holding them every day since, the way you feel a weight you have been told to carry."

Hana found that her hands had closed in her lap.

"And the leaves," said the Warden, "are in the hand that drew the binding. The oldest hand. The hand nobody alive can read. If the girl in the north reads them, she reads the hand that holds thirty-five people in this world. And if she reads them wrong, my lords. If she touches what that hand wrote, the way she has already touched the circle's outer rings to make her channel. If she pulls on the wrong line." He let it sit. "The binding could stop. And everything it holds would stop with it."

The room was very quiet.

"I do not say she would. I say she could. I say that nobody has read that hand in four hundred years, and the last king who tried gave twenty years and the best minds in Aurelan to it and got guesses. And I say that what is at stake if the guess is wrong is not a book, or a door, or a road. It is thirty-five lives." He looked down the table. "We brought them here. The Hall. We stood on the rings. We are not asking to burn the leaves because we fear them. We are asking because we are responsible."

***

It was working.

Hana felt it work. She'd always been able to feel a room; it was what her charm was made of, the people half, the half she kept pushed down under her ribs like a hand over a lamp. She felt the Council lean. Not all at once. In pieces. The Lord Treasurer first, nodding very slightly, the way soft careful men nod at anything that sounds like caution. The bishop. One of the generals, who looked at the Warden, and then at the gallery, at the six of them along the rail, and Hana saw him think the other thing, the thing the Warden hadn't said and didn't need to. *If a door home opens before the war, the kingdom loses the only thirty-five weapons it has.*

The woman from the guilds. The Lord Chancellor, frowning.

Leaning. All down the table. Toward the old man in grey with his hands in his sleeves, who meant every word.

And under Hana's ribs, the charm woke up.

It did that, when a room leaned. It always had. On Earth it had been nothing; a knack, a way of saying the right thing to a chat at the right moment so they all laughed at once. Here it was a door. She'd found that out the first month, in the castle, and been so frightened she'd made herself a rule. *Charms, not people. Things, not minds.* Paired cords and keepsakes and little warm halves of a heart. Never the other thing.

She could feel the other thing now. Right there. Under her ribs, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. The Council was leaning, and she could lean it back. She knew exactly how. A breath, a thread, the smallest push, the way she'd push a chat that was turning ugly toward something kind. Twenty people at a long table, and every one of them a door, and she knew which ones were already half open. The Lord Treasurer. The bishop. The general who was thinking about weapons.

It would be so easy.

It would be so *right*. They were going to burn the only way home anyone had found in four hundred years, because an old man was frightened, and she could stop it from a gallery without saying a word, and nobody would ever know.

Her hand had come up. She found it, halfway to the rail. Open.

Milli's hand closed over her wrist.

Not hard. He didn't look at her. He was looking at the Warden, with his face perfectly calm, the way it always was, and his tail gone so still it might have been carved. He just put his hand round her wrist, over the cords, over the half-heart, and held it there.

*Charms, not people*.

She'd told him the rule the first month. He was the only person she'd ever told. He'd said *I know*, because he was immune, he said, because he was rational. She'd said it was because he was already charmed.

She put her hand back in her lap.

It was the hardest thing she'd done in two years.

***

The king let the room lean.

He let it go on leaning for a long time. Long enough that Hana began to be frightened, properly, in the stomach, the way she'd been frightened in the first week. He sat at the head of the table with his big scarred hands flat on the wood and his eyes on the Warden and let the old man finish, and let the room murmur, and let the Lord Treasurer say *perhaps a commission might be formed to consider*, and let the bishop say *the Hall has always had the keeping of such things*.

Then he looked up. At the gallery.

At the six of them along the rail.

"Well?" said the king.

The room turned round.

Hana hadn't expected that. Nobody had. Twenty faces turning up toward a gallery where clerks sat with slates, and finding, at the end of it, out of the light, five summoned and an archivist and a guard. A cat girl with a crown and one violet eye. A cat man with a still tail. A fox girl with a hawk on her bare arm. A red-haired man standing half a pace in front of her. A man with glasses and a vial of ink on a chain. Oswin, with ink on his chin.

"It's your door," said the king, quite mildly. "They're your lives. He's been talking about them for a quarter of an hour." He nodded at the Warden. "If you've got anything to say, say it. Only the strong speak to the king. I've decided you're strong. Nobody's going to argue."

Nobody argued.

Hana opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She'd spent two years being the calm smooth one, the hub, the one who knew what to say. She had nothing.

Milli let go of her wrist and stood up.

***

He didn't raise his voice. Milli never did. He stood at the rail of the gallery with his hands at his sides and his tail still and looked down the length of the Council chamber at the old man in grey, and spoke in the calm low voice that Hana had fallen in love with on a call, years ago, in a world where none of this was real.

"Warden," said Milli. "Were you on the third ring?"

The Warden looked up at him.

"I was."

"Then you were the one who felt it take us."

"Yes."

"I was on the floor," said Milli. "In the middle. I was nine feet from my wife, and I couldn't find her for a minute, because the light was in my eyes and the floor was cold and somebody was screaming. It was Tiger. I didn't know that then." He was quiet. "I was the one at the briefing who said *what's the catch*. I was the one who asked the king, in Valcrest, at his own table, if there was a way home. Everyone in this room's read the reports. I'm the skeptic. I'm the one who wants proof."

The Warden didn't say anything.

"You're right," said Milli. "It could go wrong. Thea could read it wrong. Nobody's ever read that hand. It could be dangerous." He nodded, slowly. "I believe you. I believe you mean it. I believe you've felt the weight of us every day for two years and a half and it frightens you." He paused. "But you didn't ask."

The Warden's face didn't move.

"Two years and a half ago," said Milli, "twelve of you stood on the rings and pulled thirty-five people out of their lives. You didn't ask us. There wasn't a way to. Fine." His voice didn't change. "Now there's a way. There's thirty-five of us. We're right here. And you've come to ask the king, and the Council, and the bishop, whether you can burn the only thing that might let us choose." He looked down at the old man. "You still haven't asked us."

The room was perfectly silent.

"Aldemar's two asked him every day for nine years," said Milli. "*Send us home*. And he asked the leaves every night for nine years. And then the page ends." He was quiet. "Nobody burned the leaves. They were still dead. Burning them wouldn't have saved anybody. It would just have meant nobody ever looked." He put his hands on the rail. "I'd rather look. I'd rather Thea looked, and was careful, and took twenty years if that's what it takes, than that a room full of people who've never once asked us decided for us that we shouldn't be allowed to." He paused. "That's all. That's my case."

He sat down.

Beside him, at the very end of his tail, the tip moved. Once. The way it had moved in a vault in the autumn, when Jared put a hand on his shoulder and said *trust*.

***

The Warden stood at the far end of the table for a long moment, looking up at the gallery.

Then he looked at the king.

"Majesty," he said. Very quietly, now. Not the voice he'd used for the room. "I have made the Hall's case. The young man has made his. I would ask you only to remember that the Hall has kept the circle for three hundred years, and kept it well, and has never once asked a king for anything." He bowed his head. "I ask now. Give us the leaves. Let the Hall keep them, under seal, as it keeps the circle. Let nobody read them until it is known to be safe."

"And when will it be known to be safe?" said the king.

The Warden didn't answer.

The king looked at him down the length of the table for a long time. Hana couldn't read his face. She never could, the king's; it was too big, and too old, and too used to being looked at. She could read the Advisor's, beside him, a little: thin and grey and very still, watching the king, the way you'd watch a smith with a hammer lifted, waiting to see where it fell.

Then the king said two words.

He said them quite pleasantly. Not loudly. In the voice of a smith's son from a river town in the south who'd been king of Aurelan a long time and had sat through a great many Councils and had, it seemed, decided that this was the last minute of this one he was going to spend.

Hana would remember them for the rest of her life. She would never write them down. Neither, she found out later, would anybody else in the room; the clerks in the gallery put down their styluses and looked at each other, and the minute for that item of business, in the Council roll for that winter, said only *His Majesty declined the petition*.

They were not words a king said in Council. They were words a man said across a forge to another man who'd put his thumb where the hammer was going.

The Lord Treasurer went white.

The bishop put his hand over his mouth.

And the Advisor, beside the king, on his right, thin and grey and upright in his chair, did something Hana had never seen him do in two years and a half, in any room, for any reason.

He smiled.

***

The Council broke up after that, the way Councils do, in pieces, everybody suddenly needing to be somewhere else. Hana sat in the gallery with her hands in her lap and watched it go. The Lord Treasurer, still white, leaving very fast. The bishop, not looking at anyone. The general who'd been thinking about weapons, who stopped at the door and looked up at the gallery, at the six of them, for a long moment, and then went out.

The Warden went last.

He gathered his grey robe round him, and bowed to the king, very correctly, the way a man bows who has lost and knows how to lose. The king nodded back. Not unkindly. Then the old man turned and walked down the long room toward the door, slowly, upright, with his hands in his sleeves.

There were others with him. Hana hadn't noticed them. Three summoners from the Hall, in plainer grey, standing against the wall by the door the whole time the way servants stand. Two older ones, a man and a woman, who fell in behind the Warden without looking up.

And a young one.

Hana noticed him because the charm noticed him. It had been awake all morning, under her ribs, and it went toward him the way a moth goes toward a lamp. A thin young man, nineteen or twenty, with a summoner's grey and a shaved head and his hands clenched at his sides so hard his knuckles were white. Not following the Warden. Standing. Looking up at the gallery.

At them.

She'd seen a great many faces look at the summoned. Wonder, mostly. Fear, at the start. Curiosity. Love, sometimes, now; children in Valcrest with paper rods. She'd never seen this one. He was looking at the six of them on the rail as if they were a fire somebody had lit in a house full of sleeping people, and he was the only one awake.

He was terrified.

Not of them. *For* them. She could feel it, with the charm, the way you feel heat from a stove across a room. He believed every word the Warden had said. Every one. He'd stood on a ring too, she thought, that night; he'd have been seventeen. He'd felt the binding take them. And he believed, with the whole of himself, that a girl in the north was going to read the wrong line and kill all thirty-five of them, and that the king had just said two words and let her.

The older woman touched his arm. He flinched, and looked down, and followed her out.

At the door he looked back. Once. Not at the gallery. Along the corridor. Down. Toward the stairs that went down through the castle, three floors, to the archive.

Hana sat very still.

"Kestra," she said. Quietly. Without turning round.

"I saw," said Kestra, behind her.

"What's his name?"

Kestra had her book open. She always had her book open. She wrote something in it, small and fast, the way she wrote everything, and Hana heard the pencil stop.

"Edwy," said Kestra. "Third-year summoner. His mother's a laundress in the Coopers' Row. He was on the fourth ring at the Great Summoning; the youngest they'd ever let stand." She closed the book. "His feet pointed at the archive stair. The whole time the king was speaking."

Hana looked at the empty doorway.

A name was a door. She'd always known that. That was what charm went through. Ama, Pellam, Hew: she learned every name in every room she walked into, and every name was a door she could open, if she wanted, and never did.

*Edwy*.

She didn't open it. She stood at the top of it, the way you'd stand at the top of a stair in the dark, and listened.

***

She went to the forge that night, the way she did every night the king was home.

He was at the anvil, in his shirtsleeves, making something that didn't need making. A hinge, she thought. He made a great many hinges when he was thinking. She sat on the stool by the door, where she always sat, with the cords on her wrist, and waited for him to finish the heat.

He finished it. He put the hinge in the quench, and the steam went up, and he leaned on the anvil and looked at her.

"Well," said the king.

"You said two words."

"I said two words." He didn't look sorry. "Halvard smiled. Did you see? The Treasurer's asked his secretary if he's ill."

"I saw."

The king turned the hinge over in the tongs.

"He's not wrong," he said, after a while. "Aubrecht. That's the trouble with him. He's a good man and he's not wrong. It could go wrong. The girl could pull the wrong line." He looked at the hinge. "But your husband's not wrong either, and he's righter. Nobody asked you. Nobody's going to decide for you again. Not while I'm king." He put the hinge down on the anvil, very carefully. "I spent the kingdom on it. I said so. I meant it."

Hana didn't say anything for a while.

"There was a boy," she said.

The king looked at her.

"With the Warden. A young summoner. Edwy." She turned the cords on her wrist. "He believes it. All of it. He thinks Thea's going to kill us all, and that you've let her. He's frightened for us." She swallowed. "Kestra says his feet pointed at the archive stair the whole time you were speaking."

The king didn't say anything for a long moment.

"Kestra's always right about feet," he said.

"I know."

"I'll put a guard on the stair."

"Thank you."

He looked at her a while longer, across the forge, with the red light on his big grey face.

"You didn't use it," he said. "Today. In the chamber."

Hana went very still.

She hadn't told him. She'd never told him. She'd never told anyone but Milli. *Charms, not people*. She'd shown the king keepsakes and cords and little warm halves of a heart, and never once the other thing, and she'd thought, the way you think about a thing you've kept very carefully, that he didn't know.

"Kestra," she said.

"Kestra wrote it down in the first week," said the king. "*The cat girl can do more than trinkets. She won't*." He picked up the tongs again. "I've read it every month since. She's never once had to write anything different." He put the hinge back in the fire. "You could have turned that table today. With your hands in your lap. I watched the Treasurer start to lean and I thought, *she could have him back in a breath*." He worked the bellows. "You didn't."

"Milli stopped me."

"Milli put his hand on your wrist," said the king. "I saw. You'd already stopped." He looked at her over the coals. "That's not the same, and you know it isn't."

Hana didn't say anything.

"Keep it," said the king. Quite gently. "The rule. Whatever it is. I don't need to know. Keep it. It's the reason I'd trust you with the whole kingdom on a string round your wrist." He watched the hinge come up red. "Your Warden's frightened of the leaves. Everybody's frightened of something you can't read. I'm frightened of very little, and most of it's things people *can* do and won't." He turned the hinge. "You're the other sort. You can, and you won't. That's rarer than Ascendant."

***

She wrote it to the guides that night, on seven strips, in Cursiffe's blue ink, one after another, by the lamp, with Milli asleep and his tail over her ankle again.

*The Summoners' Hall asked the Council for the leaves, to keep them sealed until it's safe to read them. The king said no.*

And then, because Maren would ask, and Lucan would ask in capitals, and Garrick would want to know exactly what was said so he could count it:

*He said it in two words. I'm not going to write them down either.*

They came back one at a time, through the night. Garrick: *Counted. Two.* Corvin: *Good*. Emrys: *Was it the one about the anvil? It was, wasn't it*. Lucan: *TELL ME*. Aldous: *I knew him at twenty-four. I can guess*. And Maren, last, near midnight, from a hall at the top of a lake road where five girls would be asleep and a basket on the step would have two fish in it by morning:

*He'll write to the lake about it. Halvard. He writes to the lake now.*

Hana read that one for a long time.

Then she turned the lamp down, and lay down beside her husband in the dark, and didn't sleep.

Three floors below, she knew without needing to know, Oswin was still awake too. At the long table in the archive, under the lamps, with forty grey leaves in a box beside him and the king's new dividers in his hand, copying the second leaf, one line at a time, holding his breath for the length of every line. With Cursiffe beside him, writing English in the margins, the way he did every night now.

And somewhere across the old square, in a tall grey building with no windows on the street and an open hand on the door worn smooth by three hundred years of weather, a young man of twenty was lying awake too.

She could feel him. She didn't want to. She lay in the dark with her hand flat over the cords on her wrist and felt a name on the other side of the square, burning, the way a lamp burns in a house where everyone else is asleep.

*Edwy*.

She didn't open the door.

She stood at the top of the stair and listened all night.
