"Show them," said Hyvred.

She said it on the gate step at first light, with Bee asleep against her knees and Corvin's board in Bee's lap and the king's coat round her own shoulders; and she said it to John, who was standing at the bottom of the long street with Malcolm's tray in his hands, because Malcolm had handed it to him to hold while he counted bowls.

John stood there with the tray.

"Show them what?" he said.

"This." She didn't look round. She was looking north, through the arch, at the fields under the snow. "It's quiet. They should see it's quiet. Gabe's asked forty times." She swallowed. "I want them to see it's quiet."

He looked at the rest of them. He'd said he wouldn't, until they asked; not Gabe, not the channel, not the king. *You lot.* So he looked. Tiger on the step beside Bee with his back against the oak post and his ears flat, not saying anything; who looked at John for a long moment and then nodded, once. Rui Xiang a hand away from him, who didn't nod, because it wasn't his to say. Drew, coming down the street from the Pells' roof with Xav, grey in the face, holding his ribs; who'd heard, and stopped, and said "Yeah," very quietly. Kei on the Bell step with the snake round his neck, who said "Yes," and then, a moment later, as if Hisui had said something: "He says yes too."

Bee was asleep. Bee had drawn a dog on the corner of Corvin's board in the night, a thumbnail of a dog in one line, and was sleeping with her mouth open. He decided that counted.

"Okay," said John.

***

He went up the tower.

A hundred and twelve steps; he'd counted them the first night, the night they came in, in the sleet, and he'd counted them every time since because he couldn't not. The leads at the top were white and soft with three days of snow, and Corvin's stool, and the north parapet with a dent in the snow along the top where two girls had sat for fifteen nights with their shoulders touching.

He sat down on the stool. He put a mote out.

They were still there; he'd only folded them in. They came out of him the way they always had, small and gold and curious, a dozen of them, like midges in a lamp, drifting; and he picked one and sent it north, off the parapet, out over the roofs. He felt it go. Down over the long street, white, with the trodden line down the middle. Over the arch, and the step, and the small dark shapes on it. Out across the fields. Up the valley, eleven miles, fast, the way light goes; to the top, and the rings standing in the snow, and the round place at the foot of the pass where the snow didn't lie.

He opened the square in the air in front of the parapet, the size of a book, and there it was.

All of it. From high up, the way a hawk would see it. The town at the bottom of the picture, grey roofs and white snow and the smoke going straight up; the north wall; the arch with its step, and people on it, too small to tell apart. The fields, white and empty. The valley going up into the mountains, white, with the swept road gone under the snow; and at the top, very small, in rings, standing, hundreds of people in a field. Dark specks on the white, all leaning the same way. And above them the round place, at the foot of the pass, where the white stopped.

It was quiet. Hyvred was right. It was the quietest thing he'd ever looked at.

He opened his window under the square. He didn't push; he'd learned that on the road east, on the rise. You held it there, the thing you wanted them to see, and you wanted them to see it, and you let it go along the threads the way Deston said, knocking on windows like a moth on a lamp.

It went.

And held. One heartbeat. Thirty-four windows, and the town in the snow, and the valley; held there, still, steady, for as long as a heart takes to beat once. The way it had held on the quay at Duskhollow and on the tower in the sleet and every night for fifteen nights.

Then it went.

Malcolm came up with a cloth and his book.

He sat on the parapet with his back to the north and held the cloth out until John took it, and then stood there, the way he stood with the bowls, until John had his head tipped back and the cloth pressed to his nose and the bleeding had more or less stopped. Then he sat down and opened the book.

"Read it to me," said John. Through the cloth.

Malcolm read it to him. He'd started the page on the road west, in the rain on the downs, the first night John's pictures held: *John: theory*. John's own words, in Malcolm's careful hand, with Malcolm's under them.

"*IF it's for them THEN it holds*," read Malcolm. "*IF it's for me THEN it doesn't.* That's yours." He turned the book a little to the light. "Under it, mine. *IF John is right THEN he'll be insufferable.* *Backup: he already is.*" He didn't smile. Malcolm didn't, much. "Then the quay. One heartbeat. Then the rain, then Rui Xiang's patch, then the bridge. One heartbeat, every time. Then the fifteen nights." He turned the page. "One heartbeat. Every night. Every time. Into every window. The rings walking, the lamps going out, the hunter at the grating. One heartbeat."

"It's for them," said John. "It *was* for them. All of it. I wasn't showing anybody anything for me. There wasn't anything in it I wanted to see." He took the cloth away from his nose and looked at it and put it back. "This morning wasn't for me. It's for Gabe. It's for her. And it still went in a heartbeat."

"So the theory's wrong."

"The theory's not *wrong*." He heard his own voice go up and made it come down. "It's not enough. It's right as far as it goes. It holds a heartbeat because it's for them. That's the bit that holds. Something else makes it stop."

Malcolm wrote that down.

*IF it's for them THEN it holds a heartbeat. IF ??? THEN it holds longer.* And then, because he was Malcolm, under it: *Backup: stop pushing before your head comes off.*

"I stopped," said John.

"You pushed eleven times first."

"I didn't push eleven times. I pushed once. The other ten were—" He stopped. They weren't pushing. He knew what pushing felt like; it felt like the twelfth. The other eleven had been exactly what he'd done every night for fifteen nights. Window, picture, want; and let it go.

And it went. Every time. A heartbeat.

"Go to sleep," said Malcolm. "You haven't, either. I've got a page for you too." He shut the book. "Then think about it. Not on the tower."

John didn't go to sleep. But he went down.

He sat on the gate step.

Not on the step itself; there wasn't room. Group 5 had it, the bottom of it, in a row, and the king had the middle, and Hild and Petra had the ends, the way they had every dusk. So he sat on the mounting block across the lane from the arch, with his back to the wall of the farrier's and a cup of something Agnes had sent up from the Bell going cold in his hands, and he watched them.

It was a strange thing to watch. He hadn't, really, for the three days since the snow; he'd been inside his own head, with his motes folded in, not looking. He looked now.

They weren't doing anything.

That was the strange part. Fifteen nights of everything happening at once. Every night, every hour, something: the rings walking, the lamps, the grating, the long room, the wall going, the line in the air, the rod. He'd been there for all of it. He'd sent all of it. One heartbeat at a time, into thirty-four windows, *look, now*. And now there was a row of people on a step in the snow, and an old man in the middle with a blanket over his knees, and a man beside him with a rod across his, and two women at either end with swords across theirs, and none of them were doing anything at all.

Hild had her one eye on the valley. Petra was looking at the same place. The king was looking north with his hands in his lap. Declan was looking north with his hands on the rod.

Declan.

John had known him since they were seventeen. A poly common room, a borrowed headset, two in the morning; telling him he was wrong about things he was right about. He'd known his face. He couldn't see it now. Nobody under the sixth tier could; it slid, like water off a waxed coat, and you could look at his shoulders and his hands and the old brown coat with the elbows gone, and that was all. He'd stopped minding, at the lake. He minded a little now. He'd have liked to know what his face was doing, sitting on a step in the snow with the king, looking at a valley.

He didn't know. He knew what his hands were doing. Nothing. They were on the rod. They'd been on the rod since dawn.

He sat there all morning.

At noon Agnes came out of the Bell with a pot and bread and gave it round. The step ate. At noon Tiger got up and walked once round the inside of the arch and sat down again in the same place. At noon the snow, which had stopped at dawn, started again, very fine, and settled on everybody's shoulders the same, and nobody brushed it off.

At noon they were still there.

He opened a square in the air, small, on his knee, for himself; he didn't send it. A mote on the wall over the arch, looking down. And there they were. The row on the step. The king. Declan. Hild and Petra. The oak post at the side of the arch with its cap of snow. The same picture as dawn. Nearly exactly the same; Bee was awake now, and Kei had moved along a little, and there was more snow. But the same.

He looked at it for a long time.

At dawn he'd sent the town. The town in the snow and the valley behind it, quiet. And it had gone in a heartbeat, every time, the way the lamps going out had gone in a heartbeat and the rings walking had gone in a heartbeat.

The lamps going out had been a heartbeat long. That was the thing. It had happened in a heartbeat. *Look, now*, and he'd sent it, and it had been true for exactly as long as it took to happen; and then it had happened, and the lamps were out, and the picture of them going out wasn't true any more. It was a picture of something that had been.

The rings walking. True for a heartbeat. Then they'd walked a step further, and the picture was of where they'd been.

Every picture he'd ever sent was a picture of *now*. And *now* was a heartbeat long. That was how long now was.

He looked at the square on his knee. At a row of people on a step in the snow, who'd been sitting there since dawn and would be sitting there at dusk.

"Oh," said John. Out loud. To the cup.

He tried it on the post.

He didn't want to try it on them. Not yet. If he was wrong, he didn't want it to be them that went in a heartbeat. So he put a mote on the wall over the arch and looked at the oak post at the side of it: the black oak post, scarred and old, with Tiger's hand-mark worn smooth on the inside of it at shoulder height and a cap of snow on the top, three days thick, rounded, like a loaf.

The snow wasn't going anywhere. There wasn't any wind. It would be there at dusk.

He opened the square. He opened his window under it.

And then he did the hard thing, which was nothing.

He didn't want them to look. That was it; that was the bit. Every picture he'd ever sent had *wanted*: *look, now, quickly, before it goes*, knocking on the windows like a moth, because it *was* going, it was a heartbeat and the heartbeat was ending. This wasn't going. It was a post with snow on it. You could look at it now or in an hour; it didn't matter, it'd still be there. So he didn't knock. He opened the window under it and let it lie there, the way a drawing lies on a wall. The way you'd leave a door open in summer. Not *look*. Just *it's here, if you want it*.

It went along the threads.

It held.

One heartbeat. Two. He felt it in thirty-four windows, a black post in the snow with a cap on it, and it didn't go. Five. Ten. He sat on the mounting block with his heart going hard and the cup forgotten in his hands and didn't push and didn't want; just left it there, true, and it stayed. Twenty. Thirty.

He waited till dusk.

He didn't have to. It had been true all afternoon; it'd be true at dusk. But dusk was the hour. Every night for fifteen nights, dusk had been when it came down the valley, when Hyvred wrote *they're moving* and he went up the tower and sent the worst thing anybody in the kingdom had ever seen into every window, one heartbeat at a time. He wanted to send this at dusk. He wanted dusk to have this in it instead.

He went up the hundred and twelve steps. Hyvred was there, on the north parapet, with her eyes shut and her back to the north and Bee beside her, counting; and Corvin with the board. *Twenty-three*, said Hyvred, and opened her eyes, and Corvin wrote it down. She looked round and saw him.

"Now?" she said.

"Now," said John.

He put the mote on the parapet. Not out over the valley; here. On the stone, beside Hyvred's hand, looking north. So that the picture was from where she sat. From where she'd sat every dusk for two years.

And there it was.

The roofs of the town going down the hill toward the north wall, white, with the lamps coming on in the windows one at a time, yellow, the ordinary way, because it was dusk and people wanted to see. The long street, with its trodden line. The north wall. The arch. And on the step inside the arch, small, in a row, in the snow: a boy with tiger ears with his back to a black post; a girl with folded wings; a boy with a snake; a big boy with his ribs bound, and Xav beside him; Rui Xiang; a woman with one eye at one end, and Petra at the other. And in the middle, an old man in a smith's apron with a blanket over his knees. And beside him, not close, a man with a fishing rod across his.

Beyond the arch, the fields. White and blue in the last of the light, flat, soft, with no tracks on them. The road going up the valley under the snow, not swept any more. The valley, white, going up into the mountains; and at the top of it, very small, the rings. Hundreds of people standing in a field in the snow, back where they'd always stood, far off, still. And above them, at the foot of the pass, the round place where the snow didn't lie, very small too, from here. A dark thumbprint at the top of a white page.

All of it true. All of it going to be true in an hour.

He opened his window under it.

He didn't knock. He didn't want anything. He sat on Corvin's stool on the top of the north tower at Thornwall at dusk on the third day after the snow, with his hands on his knees, and he let the picture lie in thirty-four windows the way the dog lay on the granary wall. *It's here. If you want it.*

It held.

He didn't count. He'd thought he would. He didn't. He sat there with his eyes open and felt it in the windows, all of them, steady, the town and the step and the valley, not going; and the channel began, slowly, the way a town wakes up.

The step was still there.

Group 5 had gone in, mostly; Azac had come down at full dark and stood at the bottom of the street until they went, one at a time, Kei and Drew and Xav, Tiger and Rui Xiang, so that it was only Hild and Petra at the ends of the arch, and the king, and Declan. And Bee's place, empty, at the bottom, with the snow filling it.

John stood at the bottom of the step in the dark. He didn't know what he'd come down for.

The king looked up. In the lamplight from the arch his face was grey and tired and very old, and the beard was dark down the front, and his eyes were the same as they'd been at the south gate in the sleet, the first evening, in the smith's apron: steady, and kind, and not surprised by anything.

"Azac told me," said the king. "The town. In all your windows." He couldn't see it; none of them could, the ones born here. Azac had come down at full dark and told him, standing in the lane with his shield, the way Azac told everything. "My town in all your windows, sitting down."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Good," said the king. He looked back north. "Good lad."

John looked at the other one. At Declan. At the old coat and the shoulders and the rod across his knees, with the line wound in and the bent hook in the cork. At the place where his face was, that you couldn't hold.

He'd known him since they were seventeen. He'd spent years of two-in-the-mornings telling him he was wrong about things he was right about. He'd drifted, when Declan moved; not on purpose; the way you do. He'd never said anything about the drift. He'd meant to, one day.

"Was that something?" said John. "To send. You said, on the rise. Whenever there's something you want them to see." He swallowed. "There wasn't anything happening. It was just you. Sitting on a step."

Declan didn't say anything for a while.

Then he turned his head. Not much. Toward John. And John couldn't see his face, he never could, nobody could; but he saw the shoulders change. The way shoulders do when somebody you've known since you were seventeen hears something you've said, and thinks about it properly, and doesn't laugh.

"Yes," said Declan.

That was all.

John stood there in the snow at the bottom of the step. Then he went in. Behind him, in the arch, three people and a man with a rod sat on, looking north; and in thirty-four windows across the kingdom, the same three people and the same man sat on a step in the snow at the bottom of a small square of gold, because it was still true.

It held until a little before dawn, when the man with the rod stood up.
