*They asked me every day for nine years to send them home. I could not. Neither of us was answered.*

Cursiffe had written it out in English three and a half years ago, on the night the king opened the door, with Oswin reading the old court hand aloud beside him by the light of one lamp at the bottom of ninety-one steps. He'd written it slowly, the way he wrote everything that mattered, in the blue ink from the vial on his chain, so that it would keep. He had it still. A single sheet, folded in four, in the inside pocket of his jacket, gone soft at the creases.

He read it again on the night of the Council, sitting at the little table in Hana's room with everybody asleep round him and Jared against the door. He didn't know why. He'd read it a hundred times. *Find the one who can, and let no one burn them. Aldemar. King, for what that was worth.*

Nine years, and then the letter ended.

He'd always thought of it as the end. Everybody had. Aldemar had written down the worst thing he knew, that he'd had two people from somewhere else in his house for nine years asking to go home and hadn't been able to do anything, and then he'd signed it and sealed it behind an iron door for three hundred years, and that was the story. Two people. Nine years. Nobody answered. *And then the page ends*, Milli had said once, at supper, in the first year, the way you'd say it about a book with the back torn out.

But a page ending wasn't the same as the thing ending. Cursiffe knew that better than most. He'd been writing things down all his life.

He sat at the little table in the dark and thought about Thea's lines in the channel, a month ago. *It isn't the hall's hand. Same language, different writer. Somebody who learned it. There's a flourish at the end of the word, like a beginner who got good.* And Gabe's, before that, at the anvil: *somebody from home made it*.

Somebody from home. Somebody who'd learned to write the hall's language. Somebody who'd folded a word into star-iron fifteen times the way smiths folded steel on Earth.

There were only two people it could have been. There had only ever been two. Frame fifty-five of the register, the last one before theirs: a ring, a middle mark, and two lines out.

And they'd had nine years in Aldemar's house to learn to read.

He folded the letter and put it back in his pocket, and sat there a while longer, with the lamp low and Jared breathing against the door and Hana's cords warm on the end of the bed where she slept, and thought: *what happened in the tenth year*.

***

"His purse book," said Oswin.

He said it the next morning, at the long table in the copying room in the east wing, with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead and ink on both cuffs and the look he got when somebody had asked him a question he'd been waiting eleven years for anybody to ask.

"His what?"

"His purse book. His own. Not the steward's rolls; the steward kept the household rolls, everybody's did, four hundred years of them, candles and horses and what the cook paid for eels, I've read them all, nobody else ever has." Oswin was already on his feet. "But the Open-Handed kept his own. A little book. For his private purse. What he gave and who he gave it to. In his own hand." He was pulling his coat on, one arm, the wrong arm. "It's the only one there is. No other king ever bothered. It's all alms, mostly. Coins to widows. A new roof for a chapel in the Fens. Nobody reads it because it's all alms."

"Where is it?"

"In the archive. Shelf sixty-one. Between the Wardens' minutes and a treatise on the breeding of carp." Oswin stopped with his coat half on and looked at Cursiffe over the top of his spectacles. "Why? What do you think's in it?"

"I don't know," said Cursiffe. "Something he paid for."

Oswin looked at him a moment longer.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. "*Oh.* In the tenth year."

"In the tenth year."

Oswin got the other arm into his coat.

***

The archive was under the old keep, two floors down from the Council chamber, in a long low vault with a stone roof like the inside of a barrel and shelves on both sides going away into the dark further than the lamp could reach. It smelled of dust and old leather and the faint sharp smell of the oil the archivists rubbed into the bindings every spring, which Oswin said he'd done himself for nine years and could still smell on his hands in his sleep.

Shelf sixty-one was a long way in.

The book was small. That was the first thing. Cursiffe had been picturing a ledger, something heavy, a king's book. It was the size of his hand. Bound in brown leather gone almost black, with a clasp that had rusted shut long ago and been cut by some archivist since, and on the front, very faint, pressed into the leather, an open hand.

Oswin carried it to the reading table at the end of the shelf as if it were alive.

"It's going," he said. "Look." He opened the cover. The first page came away at the edge, a sliver, and fell on the table like a dead leaf. "I haven't opened it in four years. It was going then. The ink's iron gall; it eats the page. Another fifty years and it'll be lace." He turned a page, very slowly. Another sliver came away. "I don't know if we can read it without losing it."

Cursiffe looked at the book.

Then he unscrewed the vial on his chain.

He'd done it a hundred times. He didn't think about it any more, mostly. He dipped the steel nib he kept in his jacket and leaned over the book and wrote, very small, along the inside edge of the front cover, where the leather met the first page, in English: *KEEP*.

The ink went in. Blue, and then darker, and then a kind of no-colour, the way his ink always went when it took; as if it had gone into the leather and the leather had decided to have it. And the first page, which had been lifting at the corner in the lamplight like a leaf about to fall, lay down.

Oswin turned the next page. Nothing came away.

He turned another. Nothing.

"I will never," said Oswin, "get used to that."

"It's only for a while," said Cursiffe. "It doesn't make it new. It just keeps it how it is while we're looking." He screwed the vial shut. "Like holding a page down with your hand."

"You say that like it's a small thing."

"It is a small thing," said Cursiffe. "Small things are what I do."

He sat down beside Oswin at the reading table with a sheet of paper in front of him and the pen in his hand, and Oswin put his spectacles back down on his nose, and they began.

***

It was alms, mostly. Oswin was right.

Oswin read it aloud, in the kingdom tongue, the old court hand turning into ordinary words in his mouth, slowly, page after page; and Cursiffe's window turned the words into English a heartbeat behind him, and Cursiffe wrote them down. A coin to a widow in Coopers' Row. A coin to a widow in the Fens. A cart of wood to the Summoners' Hall in a hard winter, *for my summoners, who are cold, and who would burn the leaves to be warm if I let them, and I will not*. A new roof for a chapel. A pair of boots for a boy at the stable gate *who has none, and who I am told is clever*. Year after year after year. A king's small book of what he'd given away, in a careful upright hand that leaned a little more each year, as if the man writing it had been getting slowly tired.

Cursiffe found he liked him. He hadn't expected to. He'd thought of Aldemar for three and a half years as a letter: a voice behind an iron door. He hadn't thought about the boots.

"The eleventh year," said Oswin, and turned a page.

They'd come.

It was there, in among the coins. Not a long entry. *To the two who came through the circle in my hall, a man and a woman: clothing, for they came in very little and very strange. Rooms in the east wing. A tutor in the tongue, Master Edrin, who says they are quick.* And in the margin, smaller, in the same hand, a line Oswin read twice before he read it out loud:

*They are frightened. So am I. I have told no one.*

Cursiffe wrote it down.

Year after year. Nine of them. The entries for the two came every few pages, small, among the widows and the boots. *Paper and ink for the woman, who is learning to write. A good knife for the man, who has asked for one, to whittle.* *The woman asks to see the leaves. I have shown her.* *Paper. More paper. She covers it in their circles.* *The man has made a whistle for the stable boy, the same that I gave boots. The boy follows him everywhere.* And in the margin, over and over, through the years, sometimes once a year, sometimes more, the same few words in the same careful hand, getting smaller:

*They asked again today.*

*They asked again.*

*Again.*

"Nine years," said Oswin. His voice wasn't steady. "He wrote it down every time."

Cursiffe didn't say anything. He wrote *again*, in English, in blue, under all the other *agains*, and his hand wasn't steady either.

"The twentieth year," said Oswin. And turned the page.

***

The margin was empty.

That was the first thing they noticed, both of them, at once. The twentieth year began with a coin to a widow and a cart of wood to the Hall, and the margin was clean. No *again*. The next entry, a month on: a coin, a roof. Clean. The next.

And then, in the spring of the twentieth year:

*To my two, at their asking: the old forge in the north yard, which has been cold since my father died. New bellows. Forty sacks of coal. A smith's striker, Tam Ashe, from the castle forge, for as long as they want him.*

And in the margin, in the careful hand, smaller than any of the others:

*They have stopped asking. I did not think I would grieve that. I do.*

Oswin put his hand flat on the reading table beside the book and kept it there. He didn't read anything for a while. Cursiffe sat beside him with the pen over the paper and waited.

"Go on," he said, after a while. Gently.

Oswin went on.

*Item: the star from the treasury. The black stone that fell on Wethering Down in my grandfather's reign, that no smith in the kingdom has ever been able to work; it will not take the fire. The man asked for it by name. I do not know how he knew of it.*

*Item: lamp oil for the north yard. They work at night.*

*Item: Tam Ashe's wages, the first quarter. He says the stone will not take the fire from the forge. He says the woman puts her hand on it before it goes in, and says something, and writes something on it with her finger, and then it takes the fire from her. He says he has been a striker thirty years and he has never seen it. Nor have I. I went down last night and stood in the door.*

*Item: Tam Ashe's wages, the second quarter. He says the man folds it. Heats it, and draws it out, and folds it back on itself, and hammers it flat, and draws it out again, and folds again. He says no smith in Aurelan folds more than a time or two. He says the man counts the folds aloud in their own tongue, and Tam has learned the sound of the counting, and the man counts to fifteen, and then they stop for the night, and the next night they begin a new bar and count to fifteen again.*

Cursiffe's pen had stopped.

"Fifteen," he said.

Oswin looked at him.

"Gabe," said Cursiffe. "In the autumn. At the forge in Kingsferry, with a bit of chalk. Fifteen folds is thirty-two thousand layers." He looked down at what he'd written. "*Somebody from home made it.*"

Oswin didn't say anything. He turned the page.

*Item: Tam Ashe's wages, the third quarter, and a coin over, for he has burned both hands and will not stop. He says before every fold the woman writes on it. He says it is the same thing every time. He says it is a short thing, a few strokes, and at the end of it her finger goes round once, like a girl finishing her name.*

Cursiffe put the pen down on the table.

*A flourish at the end of the word*, Thea had written. *Like a beginner who got good.*

He sat very still at the reading table in the archive with the lamp hissing and the shelves going away into the dark on both sides, and he thought about a woman three hundred years ago, in a cold forge in the north yard, at night, with her hand on a star that wouldn't take the fire from anyone but her. Learning to write. For nine years, in Aldemar's east wing, with paper and more paper, covering it in the hall's circles. Looking for the way home in the leaves, and asking every day, and not finding it. And then, in the tenth year, stopping. And going down to the forge, and writing one word on a bar of star-iron, over and over, a few strokes and a little loop at the end like a girl finishing her name, fifteen times a night, for the better part of a year.

He knew what that was. He knew it in his hands. He'd come into this world with a power that was writing and a world whose writing his eyes slid off like water off oil; he'd sat in the copying room for three and a half years writing everything in a script nobody here could read, because it was the only one he had. He'd written *Stay* once himself, in blue, on a dummy letter, in the first year, to see if it would hold. It hadn't held on him. Only on other people.

She'd learned theirs. All the way. Well enough to fold it into iron.

"She was a writer," he said.

Oswin looked at him.

"Not like me," said Cursiffe. "I mean she was somebody who had to write it down to make it real. And she couldn't, here. Not in their letters. So she learned theirs." He touched the page, very lightly, beside the line about the loop. "That's her. That little loop. That's the only thing of her that's left anywhere, and it's on Alea's back."

***

There were four more entries. Oswin read them slowly. He was crying by the second; he didn't stop reading, and his voice didn't change, but his face was wet, and he didn't wipe it, and Cursiffe didn't say anything.

*Item: to Tam Ashe, a pension, for life. His hands will not close. He says he would do it again. The work is finished. It is a sword, if a sword can be as long as a man and as black as the bottom of a well. It burns. Not to the touch. I touched it. It does not burn me. But the stable boy brought one of the castle's ratting dogs into the north yard and the dog would not go within ten paces of it, and lay down, and whined.*

*Item: two horses. Two saddles. Bread and oats for forty days. A sword-belt, made very long. The road east.*

*I asked them where. The woman said: to hold the mouth. I asked what mouth. She said: the one in the leaves. I asked how long. She said: as long as we can.*

*I did not ask again.*

And in the margin, beside it:

*I should have asked again.*

Oswin turned the page. His hand was shaking now. He turned it the way you'd turn a page in a letter you already knew the end of.

*Item: their places at table, which I have had laid every night for ten years. Taken away. The first day of the second winter after. They have not come back.*

*I have told the household they went home.*

That was all. The next entry was a coin to a widow in the Fens. The margin was clean, and stayed clean, for the rest of the book; and the hand went on getting smaller and more tired, through a few more years of boots and roofs and wood for the summoners, until it stopped.

***

They sat in the archive for a long time.

Oswin had taken his spectacles off and was holding them in his lap. Cursiffe sat with his sheet of paper in front of him, covered in his small careful English, *again*, *again*, *again*, all the way down, and then *fifteen*, and then *a girl finishing her name*, and then *as long as we can*.

"He told them they went home," said Oswin, at last.

"Yes."

"His whole household. For the rest of his life." Oswin turned the spectacles over. "And then he wrote the letter, and put it behind a door, and told the truth to whoever came next. *Neither of us was answered.*" He shook his head. "He never said. In the letter. About the forge. He said nine years and *find the one who can* and stopped."

"Maybe he didn't think it was his to say," said Cursiffe.

Oswin looked at him.

"It's theirs," said Cursiffe. "What they did. Where they went. He didn't know what it was for. He just paid for the coal." He looked at the little book on the table, lying open at the page about the places at table, flat, kept. "He wrote down what he paid for. That's all he could do. He couldn't go with them."

Oswin was quiet.

"The mouth," he said. "*The one in the leaves.*" He put his spectacles back on, slowly. "There's a mark in the middle of every frame of the register. Every summoning. A dense little mark, different every time, like a seal. I've drawn fifty-six of them. And on the method leaves, the ones that come after, there's a word that goes with it, the same mark, over and over." He looked at Cursiffe. "Thea's never read it. I've never asked her to. I didn't think it mattered. I called it *the middle* in my notes."

"She called it a mouth."

"She called it a mouth," said Oswin. "And took a sword east to hold it."

Neither of them said what both of them were thinking. It sat on the table between them like the little book.

A sword as long as a man and as black as the bottom of a well, that burned demons and made dogs lie down. That had come out of the east eighteen years ago on the back of a garrison boy nobody had ever asked about, and stood in a grave on the Redmarch, and been split on an anvil at a lake, and was now half a headstone and half a girl's sword.

*Somebody from home made it*, Gabe had said. *There's always one who came first*, Calvin had said, in the hall, the first day.

There had been two.

***

He didn't put it in the channel.

He'd meant to. He'd written it out, all of it, at the copying-room table that afternoon, in the English that was the only thing anyone in the channel could read: the forge, the star, Tam Ashe's hands, fifteen, the loop, the road east, *to hold the mouth*, the places at table. He had it ready. He sat with his window open over it and his finger over the place where you put a thing in, and didn't.

Because he'd been watching the channel. He watched everything; it was the only thing he'd ever been really good at. And for a month, since the night Thea read *Stay* in Astra, Group 4 had been quiet about the sword. Not quiet in general. Gabe posted about the hook, about Harl's files, about soup. Calvin posted about wind. Deston said *rule four* and *we're on it* and nothing else. And once, the night of Thea's post, Calvin had written *there's an old woman on the south wharf who sings about a nail*, and Deston had written *Cal*, and Calvin had written *sorry. no names. but there is*.

And then nothing. A month of nothing, from four people who'd never once in three and a half years been quiet about anything.

They had something. They were carrying it. And they'd decided who it was for.

He thought about Jared on the stair. *You don't stand in front of scared people. You stand in between.* He thought about a man on a jetty at a lake who read every line of the channel and never wrote, and who'd carried a black sword on his back for eight years before he ever hung it on a wall.

He didn't post it.

He went down to Hana's room instead, and asked her for the cord.

***

She had seven. One for every group, paired, the other halves out across the kingdom in seven pockets; the oldest of all her cords, the ones she'd made in the first winter before she knew what she was doing, knotted and re-knotted and bound with strips of Cursiffe's own ink so that they carried words. Group 4's was brown and grey, with a little tin bell on it that didn't ring. Deston had the other half.

Hana didn't ask him why. She looked at his face, and at the folded paper in his hand, and gave him the cord, and went back to the window.

He wrote it short. He wrote it on the cord's strip in blue, small, the way he wrote everything, and knew that at the other end, in Kingsferry, in Deston's pocket, the strip would warm and the words would come up on it in Deston's hand:

*Deston. Aldemar's own purse book, years 20–21. His two stopped asking to go home and asked for a forge, the star that fell on Wethering Down, and a striker. The man folded it fifteen times a night. The woman wrote on it before every fold, the same few strokes, with a loop at the end "like a girl finishing her name." It burned; dogs wouldn't go near it. Then two horses, forty days' bread, a long sword-belt, the road east. "To hold the mouth. The one in the leaves. As long as we can." They never came back. He told the household they'd gone home. I haven't put it in the channel. I think you've got the other half. I think it's somebody's. Tell me what to do with mine. C.*

He sat with the cord in his hand by Hana's fire and waited.

It warmed in the night. A little after the second hour, when everybody else was asleep: the strip went warm in his palm like a cup somebody had just poured, and the words came up on it in his own hand, which wasn't his.

*Cursiffe. Thank you. Yes, we've got the other half, and yes, it's somebody's, and he hasn't heard it yet. Please hold it. Just till we've told him. We're taking it to him at ice-out, all of it, ourselves. Then it's his to say who knows. I'm sorry to ask. Rule four, and three. D.*

And under it, in a different hand that wasn't anybody's either, squeezed in at the bottom:

*thank you bro. — G*

Cursiffe sat with the cord in his hand and read it twice.

Then he folded his sheet of English in four, and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, beside Aldemar's letter, so that the two of them were together. And held it there.

***

Oswin wrote his report that night.

He had to. He said so, at the copying-room table, with his pen over a clean sheet and his face still blotched from the archive. He was the Advisor's man. He'd been the Advisor's man for years, since long before any of them came; he'd been trained for it, the way the seven were all trained, and he reported what he found, every week, to the thin grey man at the top of the west tower. It was what he was for.

"I can not," he said. "If you ask me to. I'll tell him I've been in the carp treatises all week."

"Don't," said Cursiffe.

Oswin looked at him.

"He should know," said Cursiffe. "It's his sword too, a bit. He's known the Hero longer than anybody." He thought about it. "Tell him what's in the book. Not what Group 4 have. That's not ours. Just the book."

"He'll know what it means," said Oswin.

"He'll know what it means," said Cursiffe. "He's probably known what it means for eighteen years. He just never paid for the coal."

Oswin looked at him a long time. Then he bent his head and wrote, in the kingdom's court hand, slowly, in a script that slid off Cursiffe's eyes like water off oil.

***

He opened his window before he slept.

He didn't often. It didn't say much; it hadn't for a long time. *Keep*, and a bar he'd stopped looking at most weeks because it moved so slowly. He opened it that night lying on his cot in the room he shared with Jared, with Jared asleep and the lamp out and the cord's strip still faintly warm in his shirt pocket over Aldemar's letter.

It had been eighty-eight in the autumn. Ninety at midwinter. He looked at it for a while in the dark.

He thought he knew what had moved it. Not the ink on the book's cover, though that had been clean, the cleanest he'd ever done; the page had lain down under it like a dog. Something else. Sitting at the reading table with his pen stopped, understanding for the first time what *Keep* was. He'd always thought it was ink that wouldn't burn. Ink a fire wouldn't cross. That was how the window had found it, the first time, on a box in a burning room. A wall made of writing.

It wasn't a wall. It was what the woman had done. You wrote a thing down so that it would stay how it was while somebody needed it to. A page, so you could read it. A word, so a sword would say it. A truth, behind a door, for three hundred years, until somebody came who could use it. Aldemar had done it too, in his little book, with his *agains* in the margin. Writing a thing down so it wouldn't be lost before somebody could carry it.

And not writing a thing, sometimes. Holding it. Folded in four in an inside pocket, until the person it belonged to had heard it.

He didn't have the next bit yet. He could feel the shape of it, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. Not yet.

He shut the window. He lay on his back in the dark with his hand over the pocket where the two pieces of paper were, and the cord, and thought about a woman in a cold forge three hundred years ago with her hand on a star, writing one word on it, fifteen times a night, with a little loop at the end like a girl finishing her name.

*As long as we can.*

He hoped it had been long.
