In a green room, you learned every name.

That was the first rule, and Drew had never once broken it. Not in any show he'd ever run on the other side of a screen, and not here. You learned the name of the girl doing the lights and the man who'd brought the chairs and the boy who'd been sent out for sandwiches and forgotten what kind. You learned them before the show, not after, because after was too late; because the moment something went wrong, and something always went wrong, you needed to be able to say *Priya, the cable* or *Sam, mate, the door*, and have Priya or Sam turn round, because you'd said their name, and nobody ignores their own name.

Over the summer, he'd learned Thornwall's.

Not on purpose, at first. He'd walked the town every morning with Hyvred while she looked at people for the cold, and she'd say *that one's fine* or *that one's fine* and he'd say *what's his name*, out of habit, and she'd shrug, and he'd go and ask. And then he'd kept going after she stopped. The baker's, the cooper's, the Bell, the long street, Spindle Lane, the dyers' yard by the millstream with its vats of blue and its yard full of cloth hung out like flags. The garrison, one barrack at a time; Brannoch hadn't liked that, and then Brannoch had stopped minding, because Drew remembered the names of the boy soldiers' mothers too, and asked after them, and the boys stood up straighter on the wall afterwards.

By the equinox he had nine hundred and twelve.

He wrote them down every night, in a book Corvin had given him, in the surveyor's neat columns: street, house, name, a word or two. *Annet: bread, doesn't sleep. Ivo: deaf on the left, stand on his right. Fenna: shirts, widow, don't mention Aled unless she does.* Nine hundred and twelve people in a town of a thousand and some, between the mountains and the rest of the world, with the pass at its back.

He knew they couldn't all fit in the long room. He'd known that since the spring.

He'd learned their names anyway.

***

"It waits," said Kei, "until the nights are longer."

They were on the north wall, the five of them, the evening of the equinox, with the sun going down red behind the Greyspine and the valley going blue below them, all eleven miles of it, up to the place where the pass road went in between the shoulders of the mountains. Hisui was round Kei's neck with his head up, tasting the air. Kei had his hand flat on the snake's back, the way he did when he was listening.

"He says it came in the spring a fortnight after the thaw," said Kei. "When the days had just got longer than the nights. He says it'll come now when the nights have just got longer than the days." Hisui's tongue went in and out. Kei listened. "He says, *it likes the dark side of the balance*."

"How long?" said Tiger.

Kei listened.

"He says a week. Maybe less." Kei looked down at the snake. "He says he can taste it already. *Like somebody in the next room has started saying your name and hasn't got to the end of it.*"

Nobody said anything.

Bee had her arms folded and her wings flat against her back. The Pollen Puffs were in her hood, both of them, very still. Hyvred was at the parapet with her back to the valley and her eyes shut, the way she stood now, since the morning after One Pour, feeling it instead of looking. Drew watched her face. He'd watched her face every evening for a month. He'd learned it the way he learned names.

"Fourteen," said Hyvred. Without opening her eyes. "At the fourteenth place. There were twelve when the king came. There are fourteen now." She was quiet. "They're very patient."

Tiger, on the merlon, didn't look north either. He was looking down. At a small dark shed against the inside of the north wall, below them, where he sat every night and watched; and then, out of habit, up the valley, toward the third ring, where a trooper with freckles stood turned a hair toward the town.

"Tomorrow," said Tiger. To nobody. The way he said it every morning at the north gate with his hand flat on the post.

Drew put his hand on his back. Tiger leaned into it, a little, and didn't say anything else.

***

They had a plan. The king had made it with Brannoch and Corvin and Hild and Fenwick round the guardhouse table in the week after he came, and Drew had sat on the bench along the wall and listened and put his hand up once, and the king had looked at him and said *go on*.

The plan was the long room.

Everybody Hyvred named went in. That was the first part. Everybody who'd been called in the spring went in too, whether she named them or not: Annet and Ivo and the Dacey sisters and Bryn and Old Linnet and Fenna and Perrin and Nessa and Teo and Merrow, the eleven from Drew's road; and Old Pruett, the twelfth; and Corporal Dunn and Mother Ivey and the groom from the east stable and the three from the fifth day. And Joss, who'd gone with no window at all. And Tiger.

Tiger had argued about that. He'd stood in the guardhouse with his ears flat and said *I'm not one of them*, and the king had looked at him from across the table, very kindly, and said, "You were asked in. By name. At the third ring. I felt it from ten miles off." And Tiger had stopped arguing, because it was true, and because the king was the king; and he'd sat down on the bench next to Drew and put his face in his hands, and Drew had sat next to him and not said anything, which was the thing Drew was for.

Kei dosed them, the evening it came. That was the second part. He'd spent all summer making doses, out on the valley sides with Hisui tasting every plant, in the guardhouse kitchen at night with the shutters closed and the smell of it coming under the door; and by the equinox he had forty-one little flasks with green thread round their necks, in two satchels, and a look on his face of a man who hadn't slept properly since the thaw.

And Drew held them. That was the third part. The part Drew had put his hand up for.

"I held twelve," Drew had said, at the guardhouse table. "In the road. In the spring. They were leaning on me. It went through them and off my hands and back up the valley." He'd looked at his hands. "I don't know how many I can hold. I don't know if it's twelve or twenty or a hundred. But if they're touching me, or touching somebody who's touching me, I think it goes through."

"A chain," said Corvin.

"A green room," said Drew. "Everybody holds hands for the bow at the end of the show. That's all it is."

The king had looked at him for a long time.

"How much does it cost you?" said the king.

"I don't know," said Drew. "It was twelve. It felt like getting hit in the chest by a door." He'd smiled. "I've been hit by worse."

He'd been lying, a bit. He'd never been hit by worse. But you didn't tell the green room that.

***

He practised with Hild.

All summer, in the yard behind the guardhouse, at dawn, before the race. She'd stand in front of him with her one eye shut, so that she had none, and a quarterstaff in her hands, and hit him with it. Not lightly. She was fifty-five and Shaped and she'd learned to fight by hearing, and she could put the end of a staff into a man's breastbone so hard he sat down and couldn't remember sitting down.

And he'd give it back.

That was *Counter*. He'd found out what it did, over the summer, the way you find out what your hands can do. A blow came in, any blow, a staff, a fist, a voice from eleven miles off, and something in him turned it, without being asked, and sent it back along exactly the line it had come in on, exactly as hard. Hild's staff would hit his chest, and stop dead, and jump back out of her hands as if somebody had kicked the other end, and she'd stand there with her hands stinging, listening, and nod.

"Again," she'd say.

He'd learned that it worked through people. She'd put Bee in front of him, Bee with her arms folded and her wings flat, saying *this is stupid*, with his hand on her shoulder from behind; and Hild would put the staff into Bee's back, very gently, a tap, and it would go through Bee and into his hand and out of his chest, and back into the staff, and the staff would jump. Bee wouldn't feel it. Not at all. He would.

He'd learned it cost him the same as it would have cost them. Every time. The tap Bee didn't feel, he felt, exactly, in the middle of his chest, like a finger pushed hard into the bone. You gave it back; you didn't get rid of it. Something had to take it on the way through. And that something was him.

"So if you hold thirty," Hild had said, at the end of the summer, sitting on the mounting block with the staff across her knees and her one eye on nothing, "and it hits all thirty."

"I take thirty."

"And if it hits them all at once."

"I take them all at once." He'd rubbed his chest. It was green and yellow under his shirt from a summer of mornings. "And give them back."

Hild had listened to him for a while. The way she listened to everything.

"I lost an eye on the Redmarch," she'd said, "because I was holding a line, and a man on my blind side decided he didn't want to be in it any more. One man." She'd stood up. "You won't be able to see all thirty. Listen for the one who lets go."

***

Hyvred named them, that week.

Every morning she walked the town, from the north gate to the south and back by the dyers' yard, with Drew a pace behind her with his book; and every morning there were more. Two on the first day. Five on the second. A baker's boy. A soldier's wife. A clerk in the guardhouse. A woman in the Bell who'd never been called in the spring and stood behind the bar now with a cold at the edge of her light that Hyvred said was *like a window left open a crack in another room*; and Drew went and asked her name, and wrote it down, and told her very gently about the long room, and she went white and nodded.

She saw it a day early now. Sometimes two. That was new since the spring. In the spring she'd seen it on the morning; now she saw it on the evening before, a little cold coming into somebody's light, and she'd stop in the street and say *that one*, and Drew would go.

By the sixth day she had thirteen.

"It's asking more," she said, on the tower that night, with her eyes shut. "In the spring it only asked the ones nobody was watching. Now it's asking anyone." She was quiet. "It's louder. I can feel it at the back of everything. Like a note that's been held so long you've stopped hearing it."

"Thirteen," said Drew. He counted them in his book. "And the nineteen from the spring. And Tiger. Thirty-three."

"Seven of the spring ones have nothing on them this time," said Kei. "Hisui says."

"They go in anyway," said Drew. "King's orders."

Kei nodded. He looked at his satchels. Forty-one flasks. He didn't say anything about it, but Drew saw him count.

***

On the seventh day, he went to the north mill.

He'd been putting it off. That was the truth. He'd been putting it off all week, because he'd been once in the spring and once in the summer and he knew what she'd say.

The north mill was outside the walls. It always had been. It sat on the millstream a quarter of a mile up the valley from the north gate, where the water came down off the Greyspine fast enough to turn a wheel, an old grey stone mill with a mossy wheel and a yard with geese in it; and the Widow Cadogan lived in it alone, and had done since her husband died, and had done before that with him, and her mother before her, for longer than there'd been a north wall.

He knocked. The geese came at him across the yard with their necks out. He stood very still and let them; he'd met geese.

She opened the door with flour on her to the elbow. A big woman, sixty, with a face like a loaf and arms like a farrier, and grey hair pinned up under a cloth, and a look on her face that said she knew exactly why he'd come.

"No," said the Widow Cadogan.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You're going to ask me to come inside the wall for the night of it. Like you did in the spring." She leaned on the doorframe. "And I'm going to say no. Like I did in the spring."

"Hyvred hasn't named you," said Drew. "You're fine. You're not on the list. I'm not here about the list." He looked past her at the mill. The wheel was turning. He could feel it through the ground. "I'm here because you're a quarter of a mile outside the wall, on your own, on the night it comes. And I'd like you to be inside it. That's all."

"Nobody's ever called me in my life," said the Widow Cadogan. "Nobody calls a widow. Nobody's got anything to say to a widow." She said it without self-pity, the way she'd have said the stream was high. "It didn't come for me in the spring. It went past this door to get to Merrow on the ridge. It doesn't want me."

"That's exactly who it wants," said Drew. "The ones nobody's watching."

She looked at him for a long time.

"I've watched this mill for forty years," she said. "If I go inside the wall for a night, who watches the mill?"

He didn't have an answer.

She went back inside. He thought she'd shut the door. Then she came back with a sack over her shoulder and put it in his arms, so heavy he staggered; a sack of flour, fine, the best, milled that morning.

"For Annet," said the Widow Cadogan. "For the night of it. She'll be baking for your long room, I know her. She'll run out." She looked at him. "You're a kind boy. Go away."

And she shut the door.

He carried the flour all the way back to the north gate with the geese seeing him off and stopped at the gate and looked back. The wheel turning. The grey stone. The smoke from her chimney going straight up in the still air.

He wrote her name in his book that night anyway, at the bottom, under the thirty-three. *Widow Cadogan. North mill. Said no. Flour.*

He looked at it for a long time.

***

It came on the eighth night. Late.

They knew it was coming because Hyvred said so at noon; not a name, this time, but a feeling, all over, *like the whole town has got a window open in another room*. And because Hisui stopped talking. Kei said Hisui had never once stopped talking in three years, and at noon on the eighth day he went quiet and put his head under Kei's collar and stayed there.

So they went into the long room at dusk.

Thirty-three. Drew stood at the door with his book and ticked them in, one by one, by name, the way he'd ticked in every show he'd ever run. Annet with a basket. *Hello, Annet.* Ivo, and Drew stepped to his right. The Dacey sisters, arguing. Old Linnet with her spindle. Fenna. Old Pruett, in his leather apron, grumbling that somebody else was on his postern tonight and they'd better not leave the bar off. Corporal Dunn, Mother Ivey, the woman from the Bell. The thirteen. Tiger, last but one, with his ears flat and his face set, who walked in without looking at anybody and sat down on the floor by the north wall with his back against it.

Joss was last.

He came up the street in his red scarf with his sister. Drew knew her; he knew all of them. Elin, sixteen, the dyer's daughter, with blue to her wrists like her mother and her hair in one long plait, and a way of walking half a pace behind her brother all the time, with her hand not quite on his shoulder, that Drew had noticed in the summer and understood. She'd walked half a pace behind Joss everywhere since the spring. To the forge and back. To Tull's and back. Past the millstream, on the far side of the street from the grating, every time.

"He's to be in," said Elin. "Mam says."

"He's to be in," said Drew. "King's orders. Hello, Joss."

"Hello," said Joss. He had a gap in his teeth. He was looking past Drew at the long room, at the fire and Annet's bread and Tiger on the floor. "Is Tiger in?"

"Tiger's in."

Joss went in at a run. Elin watched him go. Then she looked at Drew.

"Mam's sitting up," she said. "She's got the shawl. Our nan's shawl. She wanted him to have it for the night, and she forgot to give it him." She held up her hands, blue to the wrist, empty. "I'll be back with it. Ten minutes."

Hyvred hadn't named her. Drew looked at her light, out of habit, the way you look at a thing you can't see. Nothing. A girl in the street, at dusk, with blue hands.

"Ten minutes," said Drew.

She went off down the street at a half-run, with her plait swinging. He watched her to the corner.

He went in, and shut the door.

***

He'd seen Rhys Hywel on the postern on his way in.

He'd remember that afterwards. On his way down from the tower with his book at dusk, past the north gate, past the postern in the wall beside it, the little iron door with the great bar across it that was Old Pruett's. Pruett was in the long room tonight. So there was a boy on it instead. Rhys Hywel, sixteen, one of Brannoch's, freckled, with a shield of the king's on his arm, one of the new ones from Agnes's forge, grey-black, with the hammer-marks still bright. *For holding*.

"Long night, Trooper," Drew had said.

"Yes, sir."

"I'm not a sir."

"No, sir," said Rhys Hywel; and Drew had laughed and gone on down the street with his book, and the boy had stood up a little straighter on the postern, the way they did.

He'd remember that. He'd remember all of it.

***

The long room, at night, was a green room.

He'd made it one. He couldn't help it. Annet was baking at the big hearth with the Widow Cadogan's flour, and the whole room smelled of it. Bee had chalk dogs at both doors, nine of them, sitting up, with their chalk tongues out. Old Linnet's spindle was going. The Dacey sisters were arguing about whether their mother had said *Tuesday* or *Thursday* in a year before Drew was born. Kei went round with the satchels and dosed them one at a time, a little flask each, *drink it all, it tastes of nettles*, and they drank it and made faces and he ticked them off in his own head.

And Drew talked. Of course he did. He went round the room, one by one, and asked after their mothers and their roofs and their sheep, and told them stories, and aimed for the laugh. It was the only thing he was good at, really. Getting a room through a long night. Getting it to the end of the show.

Toward midnight, Nessa said, from the hearth, with a chalk dog on her feet: "Tell the goose one."

He stopped.

The whole room went quiet. They all knew the goose one. They all knew he'd never finished it. Twice, now. Twice he'd got to *Champions have* and something had happened.

"After," said Drew.

"You always say after."

"This time I mean it." He smiled at her. "Dawn. When it's done. I'll stand right here and finish it. You can hold me to it."

"I will," said Nessa.

Elin hadn't come back.

He noticed it at midnight. He'd been noticing it, a little, at the back of his head, for an hour; he'd told himself her mother had wanted her to stay, that she'd sat down, that she'd fallen asleep. He looked at Joss on the floor by Tiger, with no shawl, asleep against Tiger's arm with his mouth open.

He opened his window and wrote to Hyvred.

[ 23:58 · Drew: Hyvred. can you see Elin? the dyer's girl. Joss's sister. she went home at dusk. ]

There was a long pause.

[ 00:01 · Hyvred: I'm looking. ]

[ 00:03 · Hyvred: not in the dyers' yard. ]

[ 00:03 · Hyvred: not in the street. ]

And then nothing, for a very long time.

***

He'd opened the door when Hyvred came.

Not into the room. She stood in the doorway in her blanket, with her glasses pushed up in her hair and her gold eyes very wide, and Corvin behind her with a lantern; she'd come down a hundred and twelve steps at a run. She didn't come in. She looked at him from the doorway, and he knew before she said anything, because he'd learned her face.

"Three," said Hyvred.

He didn't say anything.

"At the rings. The outside one. The thirteenth. They've just got there." Her voice was very steady. She'd stopped saying *you're fine* in the spring. "A soldier. With a shield on his arm. A new one. Grey." She took a breath. "A big woman with flour to the elbow. And a girl with a plait." She stopped. "I didn't see them. I looked at every light in this town every morning for a week. I didn't see anything on any of them. Nothing. No cold. They didn't have a window." Her voice went, a little, and came back. "It didn't knock."

Behind Drew, in the long room, Joss woke up.

"They went at dusk," said Hyvred. "They must have. It's eleven miles. They walked out at dusk, all three of them, while we were all looking at this room. The postern's open. Hild's just found it. The bar's on the stones."

"Rhys," said Drew.

"I don't know his name," said Hyvred. "I'm sorry. I didn't know any of their names."

"Rhys Hywel," said Drew. "The Widow Cadogan. Elin." He found he was holding his book. "I know their names."

Joss was standing up.

"Is Elin there?" said Joss. "Is that Elin? With the plait?"

And the call came.

***

Not a note through a wall, this time. Not *somebody saying your name in the next room*.

In this one.

Drew felt it hit the north wall of the long room like weather. Through the stone. Through the shutters. Through the floor under his feet and the air in his chest and the bones of his face; a voice, enormous, close, warm, so close it might have been his own, saying every name in the room at once, gently, *there you are*, *there you are*, *oh, there you are*, thirty-three times and once more for him, and something in him turned toward it like a plant to a window, and he held it, and it went past him like a wave past a post—

And thirty-three people stood up.

All at once. Smiling. Annet with bread in her hand. Old Linnet with the spindle falling out of her lap and rolling across the floor. The Dacey sisters, together. Corporal Dunn. The woman from the Bell. The thirteen. Tiger, by the north wall, and Joss beside him. Every face in the room lifting, the way you'd look up from a book if somebody said your name, and turning, all together, to the north wall, where the door was, where the voice was; and starting to walk.

"*HANDS*," said Drew.

He'd told them. All evening. At the door, ticking them in. *If you feel it, if you feel anything, take the hand next to you and don't let go*. And they'd laughed and said *like a dance* and he'd said *exactly like a dance*. They weren't thinking now. They were smiling. But somewhere under the smile, the way you'd remember a thing you'd been told at the door, their hands did it. Annet took Ivo's. Ivo took Fenna's. Fenna took one of the Dacey sisters' and the other Dacey sister took her sister's other hand without letting go of the argument, even now, even smiling. Down the room, hand to hand to hand, a chain going out from him through thirty-one people like a line of washing in the wind, all of them leaning north. And at his back, Tiger, up off the floor by the north wall with Joss's hand in his, smiling, and his other hand flat on Drew's shoulder blade, the way Drew had told him at the door: *if it comes, put your hand on me*.

Annet was nearest. He put his blades on, both hands, in one movement, the way he'd done it ten thousand times in the yard, and put his open hands up in front of him, and Annet walked into them.

Into his chest. And the whole chain behind her, leaning.

It hit him.

***

He'd thought he knew what it would be. Hild's staff, thirty-one times. He'd been wrong.

It came in through Annet's shoulder against his chest, and through her, through Ivo, through Fenna, through every one of them down the line, and it all came the same way, to the same place: into the middle of him. Thirty-three blows at once. Not taps. Not Hild's staff in the yard on a cool morning. The whole voice, the warm enormous voice, thirty-three times, landing on his breastbone all together like a cart going over him; and he heard it as it hit, a sound like somebody dropping a sack of grain onto a stone floor from a height, *whump*, inside his own chest.

And he gave it back.

He felt *Counter* take it. Turn it. Send it out off his open hands, along exactly the line it had come in on, back through thirty-three people, through the north wall, up the valley, eleven miles, like a ball thrown back to the hand that threw it.

The voice stopped.

Thirty-three people stopped leaning. He heard them stop: a long ragged sigh going down the room, hand to hand, and feet shuffling, and somebody saying *what*—

And it came again.

That was the difference. That was what the spring hadn't told him. In the spring he'd given it back and it had stopped like a door slammed, and tried again faint, and slid off. Now it didn't stop. He gave it back and it came again, at once, as loud as before, as if whatever was at the other end had caught it and thrown it straight back, harder; thirty-three more, *whump*, on the same place, on the bone, and he gave those back, and it came again.

And again.

He didn't know how long. Kei said afterwards it was a little under an hour. It didn't feel like an hour. It felt like being on the wrong side of a door in a storm with somebody on the other side who was bigger than him and wasn't going to get tired, and every time he shoved it shut it came back open into his chest. *Whump*. And give it back. *Whump*. And give it back. His hands were still up. Annet was still against him, smiling and not smiling, her face coming and going, the smile coming up every time it hit and going every time he gave it back, as if she was surfacing and going under, surfacing and going under; and behind her, all down the room, and at his back, thirty-two people doing the same.

Somewhere around the twentieth time, something in his chest went with a sound like a green stick.

He felt it go. On the left. Low. A rib. He didn't stop. He couldn't stop. He breathed in and it went white behind his eyes and he gave it back.

Somewhere around the thirtieth, his nose went. He felt it come down over his mouth, hot, and tasted it. Annet, surfacing, looked at him from an inch away and her face did something, and she said *oh, love*, and went under again.

He gave it back.

***

He didn't see Joss go. He heard him.

*Listen for the one who lets go.*

A small scuffle at his back. A hand tearing out of a hand. A boy's voice, surfacing, not smiling, just a boy: "*Elin*—" and feet on the floorboards, running, toward the north door, where Hyvred had been standing a moment ago with her gold eye open; and the chain was in front of Drew and his hands were full and he couldn't move them. Not an inch. If he moved his hands thirty-one people walked out of the room.

"*TIGER*," said Drew.

He couldn't turn his head. He heard it. Tiger's hand gone off his shoulder blade. A rush of air, so fast it was almost a sound by itself; and a thump; and the boy's voice, muffled, furious, *let me GO, she's there, she's THERE*—

"I've got him," said Tiger. "I've got him. Drew. I've got him."

Drew breathed. It went white. He gave it back.

"*There and back*," said Tiger. Behind him. Close. His voice was all wrong. "Drew. I can go. I can get there. There and back. I've done it. I did it in the spring. Eleven miles, I can do it in—"

"No," said Drew.

"She's in the *outside ring*, she's only just got there, she's still on the edge, I can—"

"You were asked in by name," said Drew. *Whump*. He gave it back. Blood went down his chin onto Annet's shoulder. "At the third ring. You get there, you're in the next one." He couldn't see Tiger. He could feel him, at his back; Tiger had Joss in both arms, a kicking boy in a red scarf, and he'd backed up against Drew without thinking, the way you back up against a wall, and his shoulder was against Drew's shoulder blade.

Touching.

Drew felt them come back into the chain through Tiger's shoulder, both of them, a heartbeat before the next one hit. Thirty-three again.

"Hold him," said Drew. "Just hold him. Don't let go of him. Don't let go of me."

Tiger made a sound. It wasn't a word.

But he didn't let go.

***

Somewhere around the fortieth time, Drew's knees went.

He didn't decide it. He found he was lower. Kneeling on the floorboards with his hands still up and open and Annet leaning down over him now, smiling, going under, surfacing, with her floury hands at her sides, and the chain behind her bent down toward him like a line of washing with too much on it.

He thought: *I can't*.

Not *I won't*. He'd never once in his life said *I won't* to a room. He'd stood up in front of crowds with the sound gone and the lights gone and the guest gone home with food poisoning, and said *right, then*, and got them to the end. But he thought now, very clearly, kneeling, with his rib grinding and the blood going down his face and the voice coming in again, warm and huge and patient, not in a hurry at all, *I can't*. Not thirty-three. Not again. Not for an hour more. Not till dawn.

The next one came.

He gave it back. Most of it.

Some of it stayed. He felt it stay. A little warm thread of the voice that didn't go back up the line, that went past him, into the room, and down the chain; and at the far end of the room, one of the thirteen, a cooper's apprentice called Iolo who'd been named on the second day, let go of the hand next to him.

Hild heard it. Of course she did. She was at the north door, where Hyvred had been until Corvin took her back up the tower, with her staff across it like a bar, and her one eye shut, listening. She heard one hand let go out of thirty-three, the way she'd heard a man on her blind side on the Redmarch, and she was there before Iolo had taken his second step, with the staff across his chest, and her free hand on his wrist, putting it back into the hand he'd let go of.

"Not you," said Hild, very calm. "Not tonight. Hold on, boy."

Iolo held on.

Drew knelt on the floor and knew it would happen again. Next time, or the time after. He couldn't hold the whole room. He'd known in the spring that he couldn't hold the whole town. He hadn't known, until now, that he couldn't hold the room.

*Whump*.

He gave it back.

And a hand came down flat on his back. Between the shoulder blades. Big, and hot, and rough as a file.

***

"Give it back," said the king. Behind him. Very quietly. "Once more. Just once. I'll lean."

Drew hadn't known he was there. He'd been in the room all night; he'd been at the hearth with Annet, eating bread, saying nothing, with Kestra at his side. Drew had forgotten. You forgot the king, sometimes. That was a thing about him.

"Majesty," said Drew. Through blood.

"I said *not yet*," said the king. "To myself. For a month. Not into the valley. Not against the pass. Not until I knew what was in there." His hand stayed flat on Drew's back. "But it's not in the valley tonight. It's in this room. On a floor. Thirty-three of mine." He leaned. "Give it back."

The next one came. Drew felt it start. The voice, coming in through the chain, warm and huge and patient. Through Annet. Through all of them. Through Tiger and Joss at his back.

And then the king opened his door.

Drew felt it. Everybody felt it. He'd heard about it, from Hyvred, from Corvin, from Bee who'd been on the tower in the spring: *like a forge door opening in a dark room*. They'd undersold it. It came into him through the king's hand between his shoulders like the whole of a hearth, heat and weight and an old man's patience, sixty-six years of it, twenty-seven at the top; not into the chain; into *him*; into the place where *Counter* lived, the place that turned things; and it didn't take it over. It leaned on it. The way a big man leans on the end of a lever that a small man's been holding.

Drew gave it back.

He'd never felt anything like it in his life. Not in the spring. Not in the yard. The blow came in, thirty-three people's worth of it, onto his broken chest, and *Counter* turned it, and the king's weight went into the turn; and it went out off Drew's open hands not like a ball thrown back to the hand that threw it, but like a hammer. Up the line, through thirty-three people, who didn't feel it. Through the north wall. Out across the valley in the dark, eleven miles, the way it had come, along exactly the line it had come in on, with the whole of a king behind it.

And at the far end, a long way off, something stopped.

He felt it stop. Not like a door slammed. Like a man who's been shouting through a door and has just had the door opened in his face.

The room went silent.

Annet's face came up out of the light and stayed up. She was looking at him from a foot away, kneeling on the floor in front of him, with her floury hands, and her face was wet, and she wasn't smiling.

"Drew?" said Annet.

"Hello, Annet," said Drew. And fell over.

***

He didn't pass out. He wanted to. He lay on his back on the floorboards of the long room with the ceiling going round and Kei's face over him, upside down, with Hisui looking out from under his collar, both of them looking at him with the same expression, and Kei's hands on his ribs, gently, feeling.

"Two," said Kei. "Two ribs. Left side. Cracked, not through." His hands moved. "Your nose isn't broken. It just thinks it is. You've burst the white of your left eye." He sat back on his heels. "Hisui says you're an idiot."

"Tell him thank you."

"He says he knows." Kei was quiet. "He says it's stopped. All of it. He says it's gone all the way back up the road and it's sitting very still at the far end. *Like somebody who's been slapped and is deciding whether to slap back*." He looked up. "He's talking again. He's never once stopped talking in three years, and he stopped at noon, and he's started again."

Drew laughed. It hurt so much he stopped.

Thirty-three people in the long room, sitting on the floor, on the benches, against the walls, holding the hands of the people next to them still, most of them, as if they'd forgotten how to let go. Annet crying into Ivo's good shoulder. Old Linnet looking for her spindle. The Dacey sisters, silent, both of them, for the second time in thirty years. Iolo, the cooper's boy, sitting on the floor by the north door with Hild standing over him, and Hild's hand on his head.

And Tiger. By the north wall. On the floor, with his back to the stone, and Joss in his arms, not kicking any more. Joss had his face in Tiger's coat and was making a noise like a much smaller boy.

Tiger was looking at Drew. Across the room. His face had nothing in it at all.

The king was sitting on the hearthstones beside Annet's oven. He was very grey. His big hands were in his lap, and they were shaking, very slightly, the way a smith's hands shake at the end of a long day. Kestra stood beside him and didn't touch him, and didn't leave.

"Majesty," said Corvin, in the doorway. He'd come down from the tower. "Was that—"

"No," said the king. "It wasn't wise." He looked at his hands. "It knows what I am now. It knew in the spring. Now it knows how much." He closed them. "But I'll not sit at a hearth and eat bread and watch a boy break on a floor for my town. My father'd have had my ears."

***

Hyvred came down at three.

She came into the long room and stood in the middle of it in her blanket and didn't look at anybody. She looked at the floor. And then she looked at Joss, in Tiger's arms, and her face went, and came back.

"They're there," she said. To Joss. Very gently. As gently as Drew had ever heard her say anything. "All three. In the thirteenth ring. On the edge. Standing." She knelt down in front of him on the floor. "Elin's there. She's standing up. Her plait's down her back. She's got her hands at her sides; they're blue to the wrist." She swallowed. "She's not hurt. Nobody's hurt her. She's smiling."

"Bring her back," said Joss. Into Tiger's coat.

"I can't," said Hyvred. "Nobody can. Not yet." She looked at her own hands. "They're not dead, Joss. I can feel them. I can feel all three. They're alive. They're just—" She stopped. She didn't have the word.

"Gone," said Old Pruett.

Everybody looked at him. He was sitting on the bench by the door in his leather apron. He'd been in the road in the spring, at the front of twelve, smiling. He knew.

"Not dead," said Old Pruett. "Gone. There's a difference. I was gone. In the spring. For an hour. The boy here brought me back." He nodded at Drew, on the floor. "You don't come back from dead. You can come back from gone." He looked at the shutters, at the north, at the valley. "Somebody just has to go and get you."

Nobody said anything.

Tiger put his face down into the top of Joss's head and stayed like that.

He sat by the door until dawn. Kei made him lie down twice and he sat up twice, and the third time Kei gave up and sat beside him instead, with Hisui looking out of his collar, talking, apparently, the whole time.

He watched the room. Every face. The way he watched the green rooms. Who wasn't sleeping. Who was holding a cup too tight. He wrote in his book on his knee, by the light of the hearth, with his left hand, because his right was swollen across the knuckles where the blades had been.

At the bottom, under the thirty-three, under *Widow Cadogan. North mill. Said no. Flour*, he wrote two more lines.

*Elin. Dyer's girl. Ten minutes.*

*Rhys Hywel. Postern. "No, sir."*

He looked at them for a long time.

He'd learned nine hundred and twelve names. He'd learned them so that when it went wrong, he could say a name and somebody would turn round. You learned them before. Not after. After was too late.

He'd learned these three before. All three. He'd said *ten minutes* to one and *long night, Trooper* to another, and the third had told him he was a kind boy and to go away. He'd known exactly who they were.

It hadn't helped.

*Counting tells you how bad it is*, Hild had told Hyvred, on the parapet in the spring; Hyvred had told him. *It doesn't tell you what to do.*

He closed the book.

***

Dawn came grey over the east wall, and then gold.

The long room was mostly asleep. Annet on the bench with her head on Ivo. The Dacey sisters back to back on the floor, holding hands. Joss asleep against Tiger's chest with his red scarf over his face, and Tiger awake, with his arms round him, looking at nothing. Bee had come down from the tower with Hyvred at some point in the night, and Hyvred was asleep in the corner with her glasses still pushed up in her hair and Bee sitting up beside her, very straight, with her wings out a little on either side, like a hen.

Nessa was awake. She was by the hearth, where she'd been all night, with a chalk dog on her feet. Bee's dogs had lasted. They always did, exactly as long as they were drawn to.

She looked at Drew.

"Dawn," said Nessa.

He'd forgotten. Then he hadn't.

He stood up. It took a long time. Kei said something sharp and Drew put his hand out, palm down, *I know*, and stood up anyway, with his hand on the doorframe, and his rib grinding, and his face stiff with dried blood, and one eye red all the way across. The room woke up. Not all at once. One at a time, the way a green room does at the end of a long night, when somebody stands up at the front. Annet lifted her head. The Dacey sisters. Old Pruett. Iolo by the north door, with Hild beside him, her one eye open now.

Tiger looked up.

"One night in the week of the Lists," said Drew, "in Valcrest, a goose got onto the sand."

Nobody said anything.

"Nobody knew how. It was just there, at midnight, in the middle of the sand. And it wouldn't leave. And they went and woke Lucan. And Lucan came down at one in the morning in a purple dressing gown with his hair standing up, and he walked out onto the sand, very slow, very grand, the way he walks out for the crowd, with his hands out. And he used the voice. The one for kings." He took a breath. It hurt. "And he said: *Madam*."

Nessa had her hands over her mouth.

"*Madam*," said Drew, in the voice. "*These are the Lists of Valcrest. Kings have stood upon this sand. Champions have bled upon it. You will kindly*—"

He stopped.

Nothing happened. No voice through the wall. No window in another room. Just the long room, at dawn, and thirty-three faces, and the fire, and Kei beside him holding his breath.

"—*and the goose*," said Drew, "*bit him*."

Somebody made a noise.

"On the dressing gown. Right on the backside. And held on. And Lucan, who ran the Lists, the voice of the kingdom, went off across the sand at one in the morning in a purple dressing gown with a goose hanging off the back of it by its beak, shouting *MADAM*, *MADAM*, *I MUST INSIST*—"

Nessa went. Then Annet, with her face still wet. Then the Dacey sisters, both at once, and then they were all going, the whole long room, crying and laughing at the same time, the terrible helpless way you laugh at the end of a night like that, when you didn't think you'd ever laugh again and here you are.

"And it lived in the royal box," said Drew, over them, "for the whole of the rest of the Lists. Lucan wouldn't let anybody move it. And every morning, at the opening, he had the herald announce it. *My lords, my ladies. In the royal box*." He held up his hand. "*Madam*."

Old Pruett was laughing so hard he'd had to sit down.

Tiger, by the north wall, with a sleeping boy in his arms, put his head back against the stone and shut his eyes, and his mouth moved; and it wasn't a laugh, not quite. But it was nearly.

Drew stood in the doorway of the long room with his hand on the frame and let them laugh.

He'd finished it. That was all. Third time. You got the room to the end of the show. That was the only rule that mattered more than the names. You got them to the end, all of them, every one, and then you stood at the door and said goodbye to each one by name as they went out into the morning.

He'd got thirty-three to the end.

Not thirty-six. Not nine hundred and twelve. Not a thousand and some.

Behind him, north, eleven miles off, in the thirteenth ring, a girl with a plait and blue hands and a boy with a new grey shield and a big woman with flour to the elbow stood with their faces turned toward the pass, smiling, alive, and didn't hear any of it.

*Somebody just has to go and get you.*

He wrote it in his book, at the bottom, under the three names. Then he put the book in his pocket, and went to help Annet up.
