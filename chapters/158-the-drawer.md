It began to rain a little after midnight, and the drawer stuck.

Halvard had known it would. He had known it all the way up the west tower stairs, a hundred and four of them, in the dark, with one hand on the wall and his riding gloves still on. He'd heard the first drops on the arrow-slits at about the sixtieth stair, and thought, quite calmly, *of course*. Of course it would rain. It had been dry for twenty days on the road from Valcrest, and dry in the vault, and dry when the seal cracked under the king's hand and fell on the floor in two halves. It would rain now. It would rain tonight, of all the nights in nine years, so that the bottom drawer on the left would swell in its runners the way it always did in wet weather, and stick.

He lit the lamp on the desk. He took off his gloves, finally, and laid them side by side on the blotter. He took off his riding coat and hung it on the back of the chair, and then took it down again, because the key was in it.

He found the key. Small and cold, beside a folded report in a woman's small exact hand that he did not need to unfold to read. He sat down at the desk. He put the key in the lock of the bottom drawer on the left, and turned it, and it turned.

And he pulled, and the drawer stuck.

***

He was a Comet-tier mage. He had been for twelve years. There were perhaps four people alive who could do what he could do with his hands. He could have moved the drawer without touching it. He could have moved the desk, the tower, a good part of the hill the tower stood on. It would have taken less effort than lifting a cup.

He sat at the desk in the lamplight with the rain on the window and his hand on the drawer pull, and did not.

He couldn't have said why. It seemed important. Thirty-eight letters, nine years, a drawer he'd locked every night and unlocked never, and he was not going to open it by magic, as if it were a seal, or a gate, or a thing that belonged to the kingdom. It was his. It was a drawer in a desk that stuck in wet weather. You opened it the way anybody opened a drawer that stuck.

He worked it.

He pulled, and it gave a little and stuck again. He pushed it back in, and pulled again, a little to the left, the way he'd seen his father do with the bread-bin in a kitchen in a village nobody had heard of, sixty years ago. It gave a little more. He took the pull in both hands. He put his foot against the leg of the desk, which was undignified, and was glad there was nobody to see. He pulled, steadily, the way you'd pull a boat in.

The drawer came out all at once, with a sound like a cough, and nearly into his lap.

He sat there with it in his hands, breathing a little hard, a thin grey man of sixty in his shirtsleeves at two in the morning in a tower with the rain coming down, holding a drawer.

Then he set it on the desk in front of him, under the lamp, and looked at what was in it.

***

They were exactly where he'd left them. They always were. That was the thing about a drawer nobody else had the key to.

Letters. Thick and thin, white and brown, the older ones gone yellow at the edges. Every one folded, every one sealed with his own seal in grey wax, every one with the same word on the outside in his own hand, in the plain upright script he'd learned at the garrison school when he was nine.

*Ars*.

Not *the Hero*. Not *the Black Blade*. Not *my lord*, or *sir*, or any of the things the kingdom called him. He had never once written any of those on the outside of these letters. In council, in reports, in the records, for nine years, he had written *the Hero of the Redmarch*, and *the one I was ordered not to disturb*, and once, in a fit of something he still couldn't name, *the fisherman*. But in this drawer, on the outside of every letter, only the name. As if somebody might one day come and collect them, and need to know whose they were.

Thirty-eight.

He'd counted them on the road to Valcrest, in his head, every night in the king's tent, and come to thirty-eight every time, and wondered if he'd got it wrong. He hadn't. He took them out one at a time and laid them on the desk in rows, in order, the oldest at the top left, the way he'd lay out a campaign. Thirty-eight.

And under the thirty-eight, at the very bottom of the drawer, flat, not folded, not sealed, not addressed, a thin stack of loose sheets held together with a strip of black ribbon.

He didn't take those out yet.

He picked up the first letter, and broke his own seal with his thumbnail, and read.

***

*Ars.*

*The king has asked me to write the account of the Redmarch for the archive. I have written it. Four pages. It says the south line was attacked in strength on the second night and held; that the east gate was barred on the advice of the Advisor during the afternoon of the third day; that the general was killed on the rise and the invasion broken by morning. It is accurate in every particular.*

*It does not say whose advice. Everyone who reads it will know. You know. I have not said it to you.*

*I am saying it now, to a drawer, which is a coward's way of saying anything, and I know that too.*

*I am told you have gone north. I am told you will not see anyone. I am told the girl is with you. I am glad of all three.*

*H.*

***

He read them all.

It took the rest of the night. He didn't hurry. He had thought, in the vault, standing at the back by the shelves while the king spoke to the seal, that he would open the drawer and go straight to the end, to the thing he'd been unable to write for nine years, and write it, and be done. He didn't. He found, when he sat down with the first one open in his hand, that he couldn't write the last one until he had read every one before it. The way you can't strike the last blow on a blade until you've struck all the others. He'd heard the king say that, once. He'd never understood it until now.

So he read them. In order. One at a time. Breaking his own seals with his thumbnail, one after another, and laying each one open on the desk when he'd read it, so that the rows of folded letters became rows of open ones, slowly, under the lamp, while the rain came down.

*The grave has flowers on it. Aldous writes that they came up in the spring, round it, in a ring. White and blue. He says nothing else grows on the red and these did. He does not know what to make of it. Neither do I.*

*The king asked me today whether I had heard from you. I said no. He said, "Have you written?" I said no. He looked at me for a long time, the way he looks at iron, and said, "Halvard, you're a fool." He has called me a fool eleven times in thirty years and been right every time.*

*Rowan Hale writes, in his annual report on the northern militia, which nobody but me reads, that you have rebuilt a jetty. He gives its length in feet. I do not know why he thought I needed to know its length in feet. I have read the sentence perhaps forty times.*

*Three years. I have started to write you the true letter four times this year. They are at the bottom of this drawer. They all stop at the same place.*

*Your sword is still on the grave. Aldous says the soldiers will not go near it. They say it hums in a high wind. I have not told the king that I sometimes think about it, the sword, at night. I do not like what I think. I am putting it away.*

*Five years. The scouts are counting Cinderhold again. They come to the edge of the red at dusk and stand there, twenty at a time, and go away. Corvin writes from Thornwall that the west is wrong. I have started to read the summoning records. I want you to know that I know what I am doing when I read them. I will not do it.*

And then, under that one, in different ink, later, in a hand less steady:

*I am going to do it.*

***

He sat back in the chair after that one.

The rain had settled into a long steady hiss on the tower roof. The lamp needed trimming. He trimmed it. His hands were not quite what they had been, these last years, and the wick took him three tries. He sat back again and looked at the rows of open letters on the desk, nineteen of them, and the nineteen still folded below, and thought about a man who had sat at this desk five years ago reading the summoning records by this lamp and deciding, over the course of a winter, to pull strangers out of the sky.

He had not asked anyone. That was the thing everybody said, and it was true. He had not asked the king, because the king would have said no. He had not asked the council, because the council would have argued for a year. He had not asked the strangers, because you could not.

He had not asked Ars.

He had taken the sword off the grave instead, with a winch and six men and a letter from the Advisor's office, and laid it at the centre of the circle in the hall with his own hands, and said the words. And the circle had reached along the sword's threads, the way a net follows the line it's tied to, and found, at the far end of them, not strangers.

*They were not strangers*. He'd written that in the margin of his own notes, in the vault, after. Oswin had found it. Oswin found everything.

He picked up the next letter.

***

*Seven years. The scouts have gone from twenty to forty. Corvin's reports are getting shorter, which is how I know they are getting worse. I have chosen the medium. I have not told the king what it is. I think you know what it is. I think you would understand. I think you would never forgive it.*

*I am doing it anyway.*

And the next. The one from the night after the hall. The night the floor of the summoning hall had gone white and then gold and then something that wasn't a colour, and thirty-five people had come up out of it screaming, and he'd stood at the edge of the circle with the king's guard round him and looked at them and thought, for the first time, *they know each other*.

*I'm sorry, Ars. I brought you thirty-five strangers.*

Only that. He'd sat at this desk at four in the morning, with thirty-five people asleep on cots in the long room below, and written one line, and sealed it, and put it in the drawer, and gone back down to stand in the corridor outside their door until it was light.

He'd told Ars about that one. In Valcrest, on the step of the tier at midnight, with his legs gone under him. *That's how it started*.

And the next. Short ones, now. The months after. *They are clever*. *They are writing to each other in a language no one can read*. *My archivist asked me if summonings ever bring people who know each other, and I told him to leave it*. *The scholar read your sword*. *I wrote to you. I sent it. I did not put it in the drawer*. And under that, a week later, very short: *You answered. "Let them." I laughed out loud in the gatehouse. Kestra heard. I have not explained.*

And the last few. The year since. The tournament. The lake road. *Maren writes that you carry the water. Maren writes that she thinks you are happy*. And the last one in the folded rows, from the night before the king rode south to Valcrest, two months ago. Three lines.

*I am bringing the key. I am not bringing these. If he asks, I will tell him. I do not think he will ask. He never has.*

*He is the only man I have ever known who never asked me for anything.*

*I think that is why I could never send them.*

***

That was thirty-eight.

He laid it open on the desk with the others. Thirty-eight letters, in rows, open, under the lamp. Nine years. He looked at them for a long time. They looked like what they were: the inside of a man, laid out on a desk to dry, the way you'd lay out a wet coat.

Then he took the ribbon off the loose sheets at the bottom of the drawer.

There were eleven of them.

He knew there were eleven. He'd written every one. Not folded, not sealed, not addressed, because none of them had ever been finished. Eleven attempts, over nine years, at the one letter that mattered. Some of them a page long. Some of them three lines. One of them a single word, *Ars*, and then nothing, and a blot where the pen had stood on the paper too long.

He spread them out. He read them, quickly this time, because he knew what they said. He knew exactly what they said, because they all said the same thing, in the same order, in more or less the same words, and they all stopped in the same place.

*On the second night, I read the attack on the south line as the real one—*

*On the second night, a little after the middle watch—*

*Ars. On the second night—*

Some of them got further than others. Some of them got through the south line, and the ditch, and the infirmary, and dawn. Two of them got as far as the afternoon. The fires on the rise. The wall.

And every one of them stopped there.

*And then you came up onto the wall*—

And nothing.

He had been trying to get past that sentence for nine years. He had stood at the edge of it eleven times with a pen in his hand, the way you stand at the edge of a drop in the dark, and every time something in him had turned round and walked away from it, and he'd put the sheet in the drawer, and tied the ribbon, and locked it.

*You'll do it when you open the drawer*, Ars had said, in the king's house in Valcrest, with the rod over his shoulder. *Yours is yours. Nobody can do your end for you*.

He picked up the pen.

***

It took him until the sky went grey.

Not because it was long. It wasn't long. It was shorter than any of the eleven. He wrote it slowly, in the plain upright hand from the garrison school, on a clean sheet of the good paper that he kept for the king, and he stopped often, and sat with the pen lifted, and listened to the rain. He crossed nothing out. He'd crossed out enough. He wrote each sentence in his head until it was right, and then put it on the paper, and then sat until the next one came.

And when he came to the sentence, he didn't stop.

He felt himself come to it. The edge of the drop. *And then you came up onto the wall*. He felt the thing in him turn round, the way it always turned round, and begin to walk away.

He didn't let it.

He thought about a king, sixty-five years old, stooping under the ceiling of a vault three hundred years old with his hand flat on a seal, saying *please* to a door. For somebody else. He thought about the seal cracking. *It put it down*, the girl with the hawk had said, with her hands over her mouth. *Whatever it was carrying*.

He kept writing.

***

When he had finished, he read it through once. Only once. Then he folded it, and sealed it with his seal in grey wax, and wrote the name on the outside, the way he'd written it thirty-eight times on letters nobody would ever read.

*Ars*.

And under it, for the first time in nine years, where it was going. *Cinderhold. By the hand of the king's fastest rider. To be put into his hand and no other*.

This is what it said.

*Ars.*

*This is the one I never finished. I have started it eleven times. They are in the drawer, and they all stop at the same place, which is the wall. I am going past the wall this time. If I stop, I will not send it, and you told me to send one. So.*

*On the second night, a little after the middle watch, the south line was attacked in strength. The king was on the east wall with the column's captains. The commander brought the scouts' reports to me, and I read them, and I judged the attack on the south line to be the real one and the movement on the east to be cover for it. I was wrong. It was the other way round.*

*I sent you to hold the south line, because you were the only thing in the fort that could hold anything. You went. You held it. There was a girl of twenty-three in a ditch with her arm broken, and she is alive because you held it, and I have never once been sorry for that part, and I will not pretend to be.*

*But I emptied the fort to do it. I sent you, and the reserve, and the stretcher-bearers with you. I left the east wall thin and the infirmary with five orderlies and four healers and nobody to stand in front of them. At dawn the wall of it was open. You know what was gone. I read the reports wrong, and that is why she was in a room with nobody in front of her when they came. That is the first thing.*

*The second is the hour.*

*When the fires went up round the rise that afternoon, and we saw what was in the middle of them, you came up onto the wall at a run. I had already given the order. The east gate was barred, on my word, and the gate captain had been told it was to stay barred until I said otherwise, and that no one was to go over the wall. I stood beside you, at the eleventh merlon, and told you it was an order, and that it was a trap. You knew it was a trap. You could see the whole host between the wall and the rise, waiting. They had lit those fires so that you would see. They wanted you to come alone, and you would have, and they would have had you, and then they would have had the fort, and the road west, and the kingdom.*

*So I held you. For an hour. You are the only man I have ever known who would obey an order of mine with his hands breaking the stone. I watched you do it. I was one of the few on that wall who could see your face, and I saw it, the whole hour. I have seen it every night since.*

*At the end of the hour I said go. Nothing had changed. The trap was still a trap. I said it because I could not stand beside you one minute longer and watch you obey me.*

*You went. You went through the whole of it, all afternoon, and you got there, and you know better than anyone what you found. Half. An hour sooner, and it might have been less than half. I have done that sum every night for nine years. I do not know the answer. No one does. But it was my hour. Not yours, and not the king's. Mine.*

*Both orders were right. I have gone over them as a strategist a thousand times, and I would give them both again, tomorrow, on the same wall, with the same reports in front of me. That is the worst of it. Not that I was wrong. That I was right, and she is under a stone, and you have a lake.*

*I am not asking you to forgive me. You have said already that I did not break you, and I believe you, and I am grateful past anything I can write. I am telling you because you asked me to send one, and this is the only one that was ever true.*

*I have missed you every day for nine years. That is the last thing in this letter, and the only part of it I never once had to work out.*

*Halvard.*

***

The rain had stopped.

He didn't notice when. He noticed only that the window had gone from black to grey, and that the hiss on the roof had gone, and that somewhere below, in the castle yard, a cock was crowing in the stables in the wet, and a door was banging.

He stood up. His knees told him he'd been sitting for six hours. He put his riding coat on again, over his shirtsleeves, and put the letter in the inside pocket, against his chest, beside Maren's report. Then, after a moment, he took Maren's report out again, and looked at it, and put it in the drawer.

In the drawer, on top of the thirty-eight open letters, which he'd gathered up and laid back in, in order, unsealed, the way you'd put away papers you were finished with. And the eleven loose sheets, with the ribbon round them. And on top of all of it, the small folded square in a woman's exact hand, that said, at the bottom, *He carries the water*, and under that, *I think he is happy*.

He didn't lock it.

He didn't close it, either. It would stick, in this weather, if he tried, and he found that he didn't want to try. He left it standing open in the desk under the dead lamp, the bottom drawer on the left, with nine years in it and nothing locked, and put the key in his coat, and went down the hundred and four stairs in the grey.

***

The king's fastest rider was a woman called Tamsin Vell.

She was forty, and small, and brown as a nut from twenty years on the roads, and she had ridden from Thornwall to Solmere once in eleven days with the news of the absence beyond the pass, which nobody had ever done before or since. She slept in the room over the stables. Halvard knocked on her door at dawn with the letter in his hand, and she opened it in her shirt with her boots already on, because the king's fastest rider always slept with her boots on, and looked at him.

"My lord," she said. Not surprised. Nothing surprised Tamsin.

"Cinderhold," said Halvard.

She looked at the letter in his hand. At the name on it. He saw her read it. Everyone in the castle knew whose that name was, now; the songs had seen to that, and the Lists. He saw her read it, and look up at him, and decide not to say anything.

"How fast?" she said.

"As fast as you can without killing a horse."

"Three weeks, then." She held out her hand. "Less, if the downs are dry."

He didn't give it to her at once. He stood in the doorway over the stables with the letter in his hand, in the grey, and found that his hand did not want to open. The way his hands had not wanted to lift off a table in Valcrest. The way the thing in him had turned round at the edge of a sentence eleven times and walked away.

*Send one*, Ars had said. *And I'll answer it*.

He gave it to her.

"Into his hand," he said. "His and no other. Not the commander's. Not the chaplain's. His."

"Into his hand," said Tamsin. She put it inside her shirt, flat, against her skin, the way the king's riders carried things that mattered, and buttoned the shirt over it, and picked up her saddle from the floor. "He'll be the one with the rod, my lord?"

"He'll be the one carrying the water," said Halvard.

Tamsin looked at him. Then she nodded, as if that made perfect sense, which from a woman who'd ridden to Thornwall in eleven days it very possibly did, and went past him down the stairs to the stables.

***

He watched her go from the top of the gatehouse.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to go to bed. But he found himself on the gatehouse stair instead, and then on the wall walk above the east gate of the castle, in the wet grey dawn, with his hands on the cold stone of the parapet. Not pressing. Just resting there. And below him the gate opened, and a single rider went out of it on a tall bay horse, at a canter and then a gallop, down the long hill through the waking city and out of the east gate of Solmere and onto the road. East. Toward the downs, and the river, and the grey grass, and a fort on the edge of a red plain three weeks away.

He watched her until she was a speck on the road, and then until she wasn't.

"Halvard," said somebody behind him.

He didn't turn round. He knew the voice. He'd known it forty years. He heard the king come along the wall walk behind him, heavily, in his shirtsleeves, with soot on his forearms, which meant he'd gone down to the castle forge at first light the way he did on the mornings he hadn't slept. The king came and stood beside him at the parapet, and put his own big hands on the stone, and looked east down the road where the rider had gone.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Your light was on all night," said the king.

"Yes."

"The archivist saw it. He told the guard. The guard told me." The king's mouth did something in his beard. "Everybody tells me everything, this morning."

"Yes."

The king looked at the empty road.

"Both of them?" he said.

Halvard looked at him.

He had never told the king. Not in nine years. The account in the archive said *on the advice of the Advisor*, and the king had read it and signed it and never once asked whose advice that was, though he had been on that wall, and seen who stood at the eleventh merlon for an hour, and who spoke at the start of the hour and at the end. Halvard had always assumed the king did not know. He had assumed it the way you assume a thing because the other thing is not bearable.

"You knew," he said.

"I was on the wall," said the king. "I was forty paces off, with the captains. I saw you go to him. I saw what he did to the stone." He looked at his own hands on the parapet. "I didn't stop you." He was quiet. "I've been waiting nine years for you to tell him. Not me. Him."

Halvard looked east.

"I've told him," he said.

The king nodded slowly. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He took one hand off the parapet and put it on Halvard's shoulder, heavily, for the second time in one night, the way you'd put your hand on a piece of iron to feel if it had cooled.

It had.

***

He slept, eventually. In the chair in his study, in his riding coat, with the drawer open beside him and the dawn coming up over Solmere.

And because a great many things had happened that night that had not happened in nine years, he dreamed. Not of the wall. He'd dreamed of the wall every night for nine years and expected to again. He dreamed instead, for the first time in a very long time, of an earlier one. Another wall, twenty years ago, on the same fort, at midnight on his fourth night there, when he'd been the youngest court mage the crown had ever sent to the border, with a commission and a good coat and nothing in any drawer at all. Going up on the walk to look at the Redmarch in the dark, which was not yet red. And finding, at the eleventh merlon north of the gate, sitting on the parapet with his legs over the drop, a boy of fifteen with a scowl and a borrowed sword too long for him, looking at something in the empty air in front of his own face as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

And the boy turning his head, not jumping, the way a boy should have when a court mage came up behind him in the dark. Looking at him. Not at his face. At something next to it.

*You've got one too*, the boy had said. *You just can't see it*.

In the dream, the way it had been, Halvard sat down on the stones beside him, and said, *show me*.
