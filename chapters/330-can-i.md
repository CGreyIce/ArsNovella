She tried every morning.

Before the healers came up the hill from their lodgings. Before Tamsin, who slept like a dead man and woke like one, grey and silent till his second cup. Before the dock cases came in by the side door. At six, at Maud's table by the window at the end of the long ward, with the dose book open and the lamp still lit from the night, she took the silver needle out of the case, the smallest one, and pricked the ball of her own left thumb with it, and watched a bead of blood come up.

And put her right hand over it.

*Mend*.

Nothing.

Not cold. Not the warmth going out of her hands, the price, the thing she'd paid ten thousand times. Not the sting of the wound in her own skin that came, a little, every time she mended. Nothing at all. Like putting your hand into a pocket and finding it empty. She sat at the table with her hand over her own thumb and reached for the first thing she'd ever been able to do, and there wasn't anything to reach.

She took her hand away. She looked at the bead of blood. She put the thumb in her mouth, the way you'd do with any pricked thumb, the way people did who weren't healers.

Then she put the needle back in its place on the red felt, and shut the case with its little dry click, and opened the dose book.

Sixty days.

***

Emrys had said weeks. *Weeks. Maybe. Not days.* She'd held on to that. It was what she'd have said to anybody: you burn your channels, they heal; they always heal; it takes what it takes. She'd said it herself, to Kei, once, when he'd drunk his own quietwort and couldn't feel his fingers for a week. *It takes what it takes. Then go to bed. That's a medical opinion*.

Nine weeks now.

She didn't say anything. She didn't put it in the channel. She taught. That was the thing she could do, so she did it: every day, at Maud's table, with thirteen healers from every order in the kingdom standing round her and the grey cases coming in from the docks. Fewer now. The criers had gone up and down the river all summer saying *don't pick up hooks*, and people had stopped picking them up, mostly, and the hooks that were still in the reeds and the mud from the spring were rusting where they lay. Three a week, now. Two. And every one of them done by somebody else's hands, with her at the table saying *left, a hair, there. Don't push. Listen first*.

They were good. That was the thing. They were better than good. The woman from Caddow had done eleven. The two Sisters of the Long Road had done a girl's arm from the palm to the shoulder together, one burning and one following, in forty heartbeats, and come up from it shaking and laughing. Even Doctor Aske, from the college in Solmere, with ink on his cuffs, who'd asked her on the first night *with respect, you can't mend*, had knelt on the floor of the long ward with a docker's grey palm in his clean hands and listened, for the first time in his life, Rachael thought, to something he couldn't read in a book; and when it answered him he'd looked up at her with a face like a boy's.

She'd nodded at him. *That'll do*.

She was very glad. She was. She sat at Maud's table every day and watched other people's hands do the thing hers couldn't, and was glad, all the way down, and at six every morning she pricked her thumb with a silver needle and nothing happened.

***

Gabe noticed, of course.

He didn't say anything. He wouldn't. He'd come up the hill every evening since the steps and sat at two paces on the bench by the window while she wrote the doses, and walked her down to the chandler's after, two paces, and walked back up in the morning; and she'd thought, for the first month, that she was hiding it.

She hadn't been. He'd known since the first week.

She found that out on the fifty-third evening, when she put her hand out to a cut on his knuckle, from Harl's forge, without thinking, the way she'd have done any evening for four years; and stopped, a hair off it, and took her hand back; and he said, very quietly, not looking at her, "You don't have to try on me."

She looked at him.

"Every morning," said Gabe. "At six. Tamsin told me. He's not asleep. He pretends." He looked at his knuckle. "You don't have to try on me as well. I'll just bleed. I'm good at it."

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"What if it doesn't come back," she said.

She hadn't said it out loud before. Not to anybody. Not to herself, at the table, at six, with the bead of blood on her thumb. It came out of her very flat and very exact, the way she said the bones of the hand.

Gabe didn't say *it will*. He didn't say *of course it will*. She'd have hit him if he had, and he knew that, the way he knew everything about her that she'd never told him.

"Then you teach," he said. "Like you're doing. And everybody you teach mends somebody. And everybody they mend's yours." He looked at her, then. "You did twenty-four, on the last day. Maud said. And then you did the one nobody ever did. And then you sat at her table and taught thirteen people to do it, and they've done a hundred." He was quiet. "If it doesn't come back, you did that."

She looked at the dose book.

"It's not enough," she said.

"I know," said Gabe. "It's not supposed to be. It's just true."

***

The cord was on the table too.

It had been there since the night it came, a week after the king's answer, carried up the hill in Emrys's hands with his face doing something strange. White and grey. A bead of black horn, the colour of a needle case. She couldn't read cords; nobody could but the guides. Emrys had read it to her in the long ward with the healers standing round, and his voice had gone on the last line.

*Not to come. To teach. Where she is. The Crown asks for her by name.*

She'd held it a long time, that night. She didn't know why. It was string. But it had felt, in her hands, like being held; and she'd sat at Maud's table with it against her chest until it went warm, and then put it down by the dose book, and it was still there.

*When somebody who knows what they're asking asks for you, by name, you'll go. And I'll pack your bag myself. Not before.*

Maud had said that. Two springs ago, on her step, when the king first asked for healers and Rachael had said *send me* and Maud had said *no*.

Somebody had asked. By name. Somebody who knew exactly what he was asking.

And she hadn't had to go anywhere. And there was nobody to pack her bag.

She looked at it every morning, after the needle. The cord and the case and the dose book in Maud's hand with her own under it. And told herself it was enough to be asked, even if she couldn't do the thing she'd been asked for.

It wasn't. Gabe was right. It was just true.

***

The bell rang at a quarter to three on the sixty-first night.

She'd never heard it at night. Gabe had hung it on the doorpost at seven in the morning nine weeks ago, a small rough iron bell made of thirty-seven hooks, with a rope pull low enough for a child; and it had rung a hundred times since, in the day, for the dock cases and the fevers and Ned Webb's mother coming in with the bandages. It had never once rung at night. People didn't bang on the door at night any more. The grey was going. The hooks were rusting in the reeds.

It rang.

She was at the table. She didn't sleep much now. She'd tried to stop; Gabe said she was turning into Maud and he'd tie her to a bed, and she'd said he'd have to find her first. She was at the table with the lamp low, writing, the way Maud had sat every night for forty years, and the bell rang below her, on the door, a plain small note, a bit flat, that went down Tanner's Row in the dark and came back off the net sheds.

She was on the stairs before she'd thought.

Calvin was on the bottom step. Calvin was always on the bottom step at night. He'd been on it since the night of the steps, and before that since the night of the Nail, and he'd stopped pretending it was because he liked the draught. He was up before she reached him, and had the bar off the door, and stood aside.

On the step outside, under the bell, in the dark, there were two children.

A girl of about nine, from the net sheds; Rachael knew her face, she'd been in the crowd on the morning they carried Maud up. In a nightdress and her mother's shawl and bare feet. And beside her, holding her hand, a boy of five or six, with his other hand wrapped in a rag, held out in front of him the way you'd carry a bird. The rag was black in the lamplight from the door. It was dripping.

"Please," said the girl. "Please, he cut it, he cut it on a shell, at the mussel beds, in the dark, he wasn't supposed to be there, Mam's asleep, she's had the fever, I didn't want to wake her, you said—" She stopped. She was shaking. "You said ring. If it went funny. You said even at night."

Rachael had said that. To every child on Tanner's Row. A hundred times.

"I said ring," said Rachael. "You rang. Come in."

***

She knelt down on the floor of the long ward under the lamp at the end, by Maud's table, and the boy sat on the end of Ned Webb's old bench, Ned was home now, with his sister holding his good hand; and Rachael unwrapped the rag.

A mussel shell. Of course. The beds below the north quay at low tide, where the children went for mussels when they weren't supposed to. A cut across the palm of his left hand, from the heel to the base of the first finger, deep, clean, bleeding hard. A shell cuts like a knife.

She looked at it.

Red. Wet. Hot. Bleeding. She put her face close to it in the lamplight, the way she'd learned to on the grey cases, and looked at the edges. Clean. No line, a hair wide. No cold. Nothing waiting.

An ordinary cut.

She could have cried. She didn't. She looked up at Calvin, who was standing behind the children with the lamp, and said, "It's just a cut," in a voice she didn't recognise.

"Good," said Calvin.

"Tamsin," she said. "Get Tamsin. He'll have to stitch it. I can't—"

She stopped.

The boy was looking at her. Five or six, with a cut across his palm and his face very white and very brave, the way they always were, the brave ones, the ones who held their hand out in front of them like a bird. He didn't know who she was. He knew she was the healer at the river gate. Every child on the Row knew that. The one at the end of the long ward who asked first.

He was waiting for her to ask.

She looked at his hand.

She thought about the needle, at six every morning. Sixty mornings. The bead of blood on her own thumb and her hand over it and nothing. She thought about the stone of the customs-house landing and an old woman's face, a hundred thousand strokes, from the palm to the hairline, every one of them opened and closed, until there was nothing left in her at all. Not even the price.

She thought about Maud, on her step, telling her you help what's already there do what it's already trying to do.

The cut was trying. She could see it. Even bleeding, even this deep, every edge of it was doing the most ordinary thing in the world, which was trying to close.

She didn't reach for *Mend*.

That was the thing. She'd reached for it every morning for sixty days, at six, at the table, the way you'd reach for a cup on a shelf, and every morning the shelf was bare. She'd been reaching for *hers*. For the thing she did. And it wasn't there, because she'd used it all on a landing at dawn, and it was growing back, slowly, the way burned channels grew back, from the bottom up, and you couldn't hurry a thing that was growing back by reaching for it.

She didn't reach.

She put her hands out. A hair from the boy's palm. Close enough to work and no closer.

"Can I?" she said.

The boy looked at her hands. Then at her face.

"Will it hurt?" he said.

"I don't know," said Rachael. "It might. It hasn't for a while." She swallowed. "I'll tell you before the bit that hurts."

He thought about it.

"Okay," he said.

***

She listened.

Not to herself. To him. To the cut. She put her hands by it the way Xola had stood in a lake, and didn't ask for anything, and listened for what it was already doing. And it was there, at once, in the lamplight, the way it always was: a body trying. A five-year-old's palm, with all of a five-year-old's furious hurry in it, trying to close.

She didn't push.

She just let her hands be near it. Warm. The way you'd put a hand by a sleeping animal. The way a lake is near a burn and the burn remembers how to breathe. She didn't reach for *Mend*. She didn't reach for anything. She knelt on the floor of the long ward with her hands a hair from a boy's cut and *agreed* with it; with what it wanted, which was to close; with the plain ordinary hurry of a small body to be whole.

And something went out of her.

She felt it go. Warmth. From her palms, the way it always had. The first warmth that had gone out of her hands in sixty-one days; not much, a little, a thread of it, the way the first water comes into a dry well, slowly, from the bottom. It went out of her hands into his. Into the cut.

The cut took it.

She felt it take. She felt it *answer*. The thing she'd lain awake for, those first weeks with Ned; the thing she'd lost on the landing and reached for every morning since; the dog's tail thumping on the floor when you come in. A body taking what she gave it and doing something with it. The edges of a five-year-old's palm, red and wet, drawing in. Knitting. From the bottom up. Under her hands, in the lamplight, closing.

And the price.

It came up into her own palm, sharp, a line from the heel of her hand to the base of her first finger: the echo of the wound, the sting, the thing that came, a little, every time she mended. She'd forgotten it. She'd forgotten what it felt like to have a cut that wasn't hers hurt in her own hand. She knelt on the floor with a line of fire across her palm and her teeth set and the cold going out of her, warmth first, then strength, the way Maud said, the way it always had; and she had never in her life been so glad to hurt.

She took her hands away.

The boy's palm was pink. A thin pink line from the heel to the first finger, like a crease. Closed.

He looked at it.

"Did it hurt," said Rachael. Her voice wasn't working properly.

"No," said the boy. He turned his hand over and looked at the back of it, as if the cut might have gone round there. "It was warm." He looked at her. "Did it hurt *you*?"

She looked at her own palm. There was nothing on it. There never was. Just the sting, going, like the end of a bell.

"A bit," she said.

"Sorry," said the boy.

"Don't be," said Rachael. "That's what it's for."

***

She sat on the step of the river-gate infirmary at a quarter past three in the morning, after, with Calvin's coat round her because she was shaking, and the two children asleep on Ned Webb's old bench inside with a blanket over both of them, because she wasn't sending them back down the Row in the dark and their mother was asleep with the fever and wouldn't know they'd gone.

She was cold. Properly cold, the healer's cold, the price. For the first time in nine weeks. It went all the way up her arms to the shoulders.

She'd never been so glad of anything.

Calvin sat on the step beside her. Not close. He was the one who asked things, Calvin; he'd asked Declan once on a terrace at Wrenhaven if he was happy, which nobody else would have dared, and Declan had answered. He didn't ask her anything now. He sat on the step with his arms on his knees and his wind gone very still round them both, and looked down the hill at the steps, pale in the moon at the bottom of Tanner's Row.

"It came back," he said at last. Not a question.

"It came back."

"How?"

She thought about it. Properly, the way she thought about everything, with the bones of it.

"I stopped reaching for it," she said. "I'd been reaching for mine. Every morning. Like it was a thing on a shelf." She looked at her hands. "It wasn't on the shelf. It was growing back. From the bottom, like a burn. And I kept trying to take it before it was there." She was quiet. "Tonight I didn't reach. I just listened to him. And agreed with him. And it was there. Under. It had been coming back for weeks, a bit at a time. I just hadn't let it come out."

Calvin didn't say anything for a while.

"That's what Thea said," he said. "About *Close*. That it doesn't tell anything. It agrees."

"I know," said Rachael. "I read it." She pulled his coat tighter. "I thought it was about the turning. It's about everything. It's about a cut on a five-year-old's hand at three in the morning." She shut her eyes. "Maud said it the first week. *You don't make anything, girl. You help what's already there do what it's already trying to do.* I wrote it in the back of my notebook in English. I've read it a thousand times." She opened them. "I didn't know it meant me as well."

***

She opened her window on the step, after a while, with the cold still in her arms.

She didn't look at the window. She knew what it said; it hadn't changed in a year and it wasn't going to change because she'd mended a cut. She opened the channel.

It was quiet. It had been quiet since the anniversary, mostly. Two nights ago, past midnight, Yan's line had come up from the knoll, *Tier 3: Lantern*, *Carry*, and *one. it went up*, and she'd written *good*, at Maud's table, and meant it more than she'd meant most things. And then nothing much. People were tired. It had been a long summer.

She wrote it plain.

[ 03:24 · Rachael: it answered. ]

[ 03:24 · Rachael: a boy from the net sheds. a mussel shell. an ordinary cut. no grey. I put my hands by it and listened and didn't reach and it was there. ]

[ 03:25 · Rachael: sixty-one days. ]

Nobody answered at once. It was three in the morning. Then:

[ 03:26 · Gabe: RAE ]

He was at the chandler's. He'd been asleep. He wasn't now.

[ 03:26 · Gabe: RAE RAE RAE ]

[ 03:27 · Gabe: I'm coming up ]

[ 03:27 · Rachael: you don't have to come up. it's three in the morning. ]

[ 03:27 · Gabe: I know. I'm coming up. ]

[ 03:28 · Xolalotl: you LISTENED ]

[ 03:28 · Deston: there's a new thread. to a boy. it's very bright. ]

[ 03:29 · Rachael: I know. I can feel it. ]

[ 03:29 · Higu: Everybody's fine. ]

[ 03:30 · Higu: Rachael's fine. ]

She sat on the step and looked at that for a long time.

Then Gabe came up Tanner's Row at a run in his shirt with his boots unlaced, and stopped at the infirmary gate, breathing, with his face doing the thing it did, wet and loud and too much, and didn't come any further. Two paces. He stood at the gate at two paces and looked at her on the step.

She held up her hand. The right one. The one that had been cold for nine weeks and was cold now for a better reason.

He looked at it.

She didn't say anything. She held it up a moment longer, and then put it out, sideways, and took hold of his sleeve, where he'd come up the last two paces without being told and sat down on the step beside her. At the wrist. And held on.

***

The line came up at a quarter to five, with the sky going grey over the river.

From Cinderhold. From Wendy, who never wrote anything at night, who said the channel was for people who couldn't sleep and she slept fine. She'd been on the band, Rachael thought. With the Marshal's two hundred. Standing on a line in the red, a man's width, the way Salty had stood on it in the summer with blood coming out of his ears.

[ 04:44 · Wendy: something happened on the band. ]

[ 04:44 · Wendy: the threadless came back. not where the soldiers are. north and south of them, past the ends of the line we're holding, as far as I can see in the dark. hundreds. standing on the ring. ]

[ 04:45 · Wendy: they stood there from midnight. facing in. like the spring. ]

[ 04:45 · Wendy: and at four they all stepped one pace in. ]

[ 04:46 · Wendy: all of them. at once. toward the middle. off the line. ]

[ 04:46 · Wendy: then they stopped. ]

[ 04:47 · Wendy: they're still standing there. one pace in. ]

Nobody wrote anything for a long time.

Rachael sat on the step of the river-gate infirmary with Gabe's sleeve in one hand and the window open in front of her and the cold of a boy's cut still in her arms, and read it twice.

[ 04:49 · Althea: a ring that steps in is a smaller ring. ]

[ 04:50 · Althea: I don't know what that means yet. ]

[ 04:50 · Suzuyun: the cold under her didn't lean. it listened. ]

[ 04:51 · Malcolm: IF they stepped in THEN the line's moved. IF the line's moved THEN the gaps are outside it. Backup: stand on the new one. ]

[ 04:52 · Wendy: Rhea's already moving the men. ]

[ 04:52 · Higu: Written down. ]

She shut it.

Gabe didn't say anything. He'd read it too. He sat on the step beside her with his sleeve in her hand and looked down the hill at the steps, and past them at the river, and past that at the hills to the west, where a hair of thread the colour of nothing ran out of Kingsferry straight as a rule to somewhere nobody could see the end of.

"Rae," he said.

"I know."

"It's not tonight."

"No," said Rachael. "It's not tonight."

She looked at her hand on his sleeve. Cold. Hers. Working.

Behind them, in the long ward, a boy of five woke up on Ned Webb's old bench and looked at his palm in the grey light coming through the window, and turned it over, and turned it back, and lay down again with it held against his chest.

And down on the doorpost, very quietly, the bell rang. Once. A plain small note. Somebody on the step, in the first light, who'd cut themselves on something at the mussel beds, or burned a hand on the rope-walk pitch, or woken with a fever and remembered the girl at the end of the long ward saying *ring, even at night*.

Rachael let go of Gabe's sleeve.

She got up. She was still cold. She went to the door.

"Can I?" she said.
