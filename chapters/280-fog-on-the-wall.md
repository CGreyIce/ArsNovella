The fog came in off the red at noon, and by the second hour Rhea couldn't see the end of her own sword.

She'd never seen it like this. Nobody had; she asked, on the wall, and the old sergeants shook their heads. Fog on the Redmarch was a spring thing, a thaw thing, the ground breathing out the winter in white sheets that lay in the hollows at dawn and were gone by the second bell. Not this. Not in the dead middle of the winter, with the red frozen iron-hard under a foot of old snow. This came in from the east all at once, like a wall walking, so thick and white and cold that it didn't drift; it stood. It stood against the fort the way water stands against a dam. It froze on everything it touched. By the third hour there was rime a finger thick on the merlons, and on the shafts of the spears in the racks, and on Rhea's eyebrows; and when she put her hand flat on the stone of the parapet it came away white.

The commander shut the gates at the first hour after noon. Both of them, the east and the postern. Nobody went out on the red in this; not to the markers, not to the burial track. Not to the knoll.

"Not tonight," he said to Yan, in the gate passage, over the seam of cracked flags. "I'm sorry, lad. Not in this."

Yan didn't argue. Yan never argued. He stood in the passage with his staff and Suzu down inside his collar and his face doing the thing it did when he'd already decided something and was waiting for it to be all right; and then he nodded, and went up the gate-tower stair to Wendy, instead of out.

Rhea went up the east wall.

***

It was the old wall. That was why she'd asked for it.

The commander had looked at her when she asked, the week before, at the morning muster; and then looked at the duty board; and then put her on the south stretch of the east wall walk, from the gate tower to the second turret, with eleven of the autumn draft, every night for a fortnight. Nobody wanted the east wall in winter. It faced the red and the wind came straight off it. But it was the oldest thing in Cinderhold, the bottom courses older than the kingdom, older than Aurelan's name; and it had been knocked down and built up and cracked and patched so many times that if you looked at it from the red in a low sun it was every colour of grey there was, like an old man's hands.

She'd stood on it every night since the chapel. A count of ten, on the old stones, without thinking. Then twenty. Then a hundred. She'd stood on it at dusk with her sword on her back and her hands loose and let her weight go down into it, through her boots, into the walk, into the patched courses under the walk, and not let anything else go down. Just her weight. Just *standing*. Learning it the way you learn a horse, Aldous had said, before you ask it anything.

It hadn't cracked. Not once. Not a flag. Not a hair.

She hadn't let the other thing go down yet. Not on purpose. She'd felt it want to, every night, the way you feel your legs want to sit when you've stood a watch, and she'd held it, up, in the sword, where she'd held it all winter, and stood on the old wall like a woman on ice.

*This winter*, she'd said, on the stair. The whole of the winter was nearly gone.

Tobin Ferris was beside her. The boy with his father's sword. He'd been on the south stretch since the start of the fortnight, because she'd asked for him by name, which had made him go red to the ears and the rest of the draft laugh at him for a week. He stood at the next embrasure with the rime on his helmet and his sword drawn too early, in both hands, held out in front of him at the fog as if the fog were going to fight him.

"Put it down a bit," said Rhea.

"Ma'am?"

"You'll be tired by dark. Put the point down. Rest it on the stone."

He put the point down on the stone. He didn't look any less frightened. None of them did, down the whole stretch, eleven boys and girls from the hill farms in their new grey coats, standing at their embrasures with the fog in front of them like the side of a cloud. She didn't blame them. She couldn't see the knoll. She couldn't see the burial track or the markers or the red. She couldn't see the second turret, at the end of her own stretch, eighty paces off.

She could see a boy with his sword point on the stone. And the next embrasure. And the rime.

***

Niko came along the wall walk at dusk, from the gate tower. She heard him before she saw him: the scrape of his boots on the frozen walk and the little clink his sword made against his belt when he walked fast. Then he was there, coming out of the white a pace off, thin and pale, with his breath smoking and his eyes on the parapet.

Not on the fog. On the stone.

"Rhea," he said. "Look."

She looked. Rime on the merlon. White, finger-thick, the same as every merlon on the wall.

"I can't see anything," she said.

"No." He put his glove out, not touching, an inch from the stone. "It's on the stone. The letters. On the frost, on the wall. I've never seen them on anything but the red." His voice was very quiet. "They're coming up all along it. Here. And there. On every one." He looked along the wall into the fog. "They're on the fort."

The boy at the next embrasure was staring at him.

Rhea didn't know what the letters were. She'd never seen one; nobody had but Niko. She knew Niko saw them out on the red at night, in the frost, the way Yan felt candles, the way the girl at Thornwall saw lights; and that a week ago he'd walked a ring round the knoll in the snow with Wendy's rope and said they stopped at a hundred and forty paces. She knew Yan had sat at the cairn every night since, with his hand on the snow.

She knew the letters had never once come to the fort.

"Go and tell Yan," she said.

"Yan knows," said Niko. "Yan says—"

He stopped.

Out in the fog, east, a long way off, something had made a sound.

Rhea heard it. The whole stretch heard it; she felt eleven boys and girls go still down the wall in the white, all at once, the way a field of hares goes still. A soft sound. Rhythmic. Very far. *Hush*, *hush*. *Hush*, *hush*. Like somebody sweeping a floor in another house. Like long arms swinging, going past dry grass.

She'd heard it once before in her life. On the Redmarch, at nineteen, on her first probe, a sound out of the dark like that, and the old sergeant beside her putting his hand on her shoulder and saying, very calmly, *those are the big ones, girl. Count your breaths*.

*Hush*, *hush*. Closer.

"Draw," said Rhea. Not loud. "All of you. Points up. Nobody shouts. Nobody looks over." She heard the swords come up down the wall in the fog, eleven of them, ragged, and Tobin's last. "Niko. Go back to the tower. Tell Yan the big ones are coming."

"He knows," said Niko again. And then, from the gate tower behind them in the fog, high and clear, Wendy's voice:

"*Six.*"

***

The first light came out of the fog over her head.

Blue. Small. A finger of pale blue light, hanging in the white thirty paces out from the wall and ten paces up, very still, like a lamp somebody had hung on a hook in the middle of the air. She'd seen it before: Wendy's arrows, on the morning of the ten, going away down the west road. The *Aloft*. An arrow that went up and stopped and hung over the thing it was meant for, and stayed there as long as Wendy held it.

Then another, further along. And another. And another, to the left, nearly at the second turret.

"*Six*," called Wendy, from the gate tower. "*Six big ones. Yan's got them. I've put one over every one. Don't look at the light. Look under it.*"

Rhea looked under the nearest one.

Fog. White. Nothing. And then, as she looked, the fog under the light moved, the way water moves when something big turns over under it; and the *hush*, *hush* was very close now, and she could hear what was under it, a heavy padding, four feet and something else, and a smell came through the fog at her, sharp and wrong, like hot iron left out in the rain.

Under the blue light, the fog went dark. A shape. Low and heavy, coming at the wall at a trot, the way a big dog trots, with its head down and something swinging on either side of it that was too long to be arms and was arms.

It came out of the fog at the foot of the wall.

She saw it then. Plain, in the last grey light, from directly above, with Wendy's arrow hanging over it like a lamp. A horned thing the size of a cart-horse, grey-black, with the rime already forming on the ridge of its back; with two horns curling back off a skull that had no face on the front of it, only a smooth plate like a beetle's; and the arms. Each as long as a man is tall. Jointed twice. Ending in hands that were too many fingers and all of them hooked. They swung as it ran, forward and back, *hush*, *hush*, sweeping the snow at the foot of the wall.

It reached the wall and didn't stop. It put both hands flat on the old stones and *climbed*. Straight up. The long arms reaching up the face of the wall over its own head and hooking into the cracks between the courses, the patched cracks, the old ones, and pulling, so that the whole weight of it came up the wall in two heaves, like a man going up a ladder three rungs at a time.

At Tobin Ferris's embrasure.

***

She went for it.

She didn't think. She went along the walk at a run with her sword up and the aura up in it, where she'd held it all winter, bright, in the blade, in her hands, so full that the steel had a sheen on it in the fog like wet slate. And her feet weren't on anything.

That was what it was. She felt it every step. The walk under her boots was there, frozen and hard, and her feet were on it, and she wasn't. She was in the sword. She'd put all of herself up into the sword so that nothing would go down into the floor and crack it, and so there was nothing in her legs, nothing under her, and she ran along the old wall like a bird running along a branch with its wings open, not trusting it.

The hunter came over the parapet.

Both arms first. They came over the merlons on either side of Tobin's embrasure and hooked into the inside of the parapet, and the faceless head came up between them, and the boy stood in front of it with his father's sword in both hands and his mouth open and didn't move.

Rhea cut.

It was a good cut. It was the best cut she had; she'd been making it for fifteen years. Down, from the right, at the place where the left arm joined the shoulder, with the whole sheen of the blade behind it. It hit.

And didn't go in.

It hit the arm a hand below the shoulder and the edge bit, a finger's depth, through the grey-black hide into something under it that was hard as horn; and stopped. Like cutting a tree. The shock of it went back up her arms into her shoulders, and there was nothing under her shoulders to take it, nothing in her legs, nothing going down. It went back up into her and had nowhere to go.

The arm moved.

She didn't see it move. She felt it. The long arm came off the parapet and swung, backhand, the way you'd swat a wasp, with her sword still stuck in it; and it hit her across the ribs and the left side and lifted her off her feet and threw her.

She hit the merlon behind her with her back. Hard. She heard something go: not bone, the rime, cracking off the stone in a sheet. Then she was on the walk on her hands and knees, with the breath gone out of her and her sword gone, and her ears ringing, and the stone under her palms.

*Count of ten*, she thought. *That was about four.*

***

Tobin Ferris screamed.

She heard it. She looked up. The hunter was on the walk. It had come all the way over, and it was bigger on the walk than it had been on the wall, filling the space between the parapet and the drop to the yard, with its back to her and the rime steaming off it; and the boy was on his back under it, at the foot of the embrasure, with his father's sword skittered away along the stones and one of the long arms on his chest, flat, pressing him down, the way you'd hold a puppy down to look at it.

The other arm went up.

Out along the wall, in the fog, there was shouting now. Wendy's voice from the tower, high and steady: *Seven. Eight. Two at the second turret. One on the gate.* Somebody screaming further along, not Tobin. A crash, like a cart going over, and a sound she knew from the yard, a deep wooden *thock*, which was Salty, which was *Bear*, which was something big hitting something it couldn't move. And down by the gate, very sharp, a single clear ringing note like a struck bell, with light in it, orange, flashing in the fog for a heartbeat like lightning inside a cloud: Niko.

None of them were here.

Her sword was four paces off. On the walk. Where the arm had thrown it.

She couldn't get up. She tried. She put her hands under her and pushed and her legs didn't come; there was nothing in them. She'd put it all in the sword, and the sword was four paces off, and there was nothing left in her to stand with. She knelt on the old wall with her palms flat on its stones and her ribs on fire, and looked at the boy under the hunter's hand, and couldn't stand up.

And the wall was under her hands.

She felt it. Not the way she'd felt it all fortnight, standing on it with her weight. She felt it with her palms, which were the only part of her that was on anything. Cold, under the rime. Rough. The walk stones were new, a hundred years new; but she was kneeling near the parapet, where the walk ran over the top of the old wall, and under her right hand there was a crack. An old one. A long one, running across the stone from the parapet to the edge, with mortar in it, grey, packed hard, gone smooth with a hundred years of feet. Patched. Broken, and patched, and still there.

*Found something that had already been broken*, Aldous had said, in the chapel. *Over and over. And was still there.*

*Floor's not for trusting*, old Oakes had said. *It's for walking on*.

She'd been holding it up all winter so it wouldn't break the floor.

You couldn't break this. It was already broken. It had been broken a hundred times and somebody had put mortar in it every time, and it had held a fort on the end of the world for longer than the kingdom had had a name. It didn't need her to be careful of it. It didn't need anything from her at all.

She let go.

***

It went down.

All of it. All winter's worth. Out of the middle of her, out of her chest and her belly, out of her arms where she'd held it up, down through her palms on the stone and her knees on the walk, into the old wall. Into the crack under her right hand. Down the crack, into the course under it, and the course under that, and the old patched courses going down and down into the dark under the walk, a long way, further than the gate passage, further than the flags, older and older, into stone that had been laid before anybody's grandmother.

And the wall didn't crack.

She'd braced for it. She'd been bracing for it all winter, the way you brace for ice to go. It didn't come. The old wall took everything she had and didn't crack and didn't give and didn't move; it just *took* it. All the way down. Like a well takes a stone.

And at the bottom, it pushed back.

She felt it come up. The way you feel the ground come up under your foot when you push off it to run. The whole weight of her, everything she'd put down, hitting the bottom of something that had been standing there for a thousand years, and the thousand years pushing it back up at her. Up the courses. Up the crack. Into her palms and her knees, into her legs, her belly, her chest, her arms.

Her legs came.

She was on her feet. She didn't remember getting there. She was on her feet on the old wall with the stone pushing up through her boots like a spring coming up through sand, and she'd taken three steps and had her sword in her hand off the walk, and the hunter's other arm was coming down at the boy's face.

She was four paces off. Three, with the steps. She was three paces off, and her sword was a pace and a bit of steel, and it was the wrong side of the hunter to reach.

She cut anyway.

She didn't think about the edge. She'd thought about her edge every day of her life. She didn't think about it now. She thought about the wall. She cut down from the right with the stone pushing up through her and her feet on the oldest thing in the fort, and it came up through her legs and her belly and her arms and went out along the blade the way water goes out along a gutter when the roof's full, and *didn't stop at the end of it*.

She felt it go past the edge. Like breath going past your lips. A hand. More than a hand.

The arm came off.

At the elbow. The first joint. Three paces from her, the wrong side of the hunter, where her steel had never been. The long grey-black arm, coming down at Tobin Ferris's face with its hooked fingers spread, came apart at the elbow as if somebody had drawn a line across it, clean, and the forearm and the hand went on down on their own and hit the stones beside the boy's head with a sound like a sack of wet sand dropped off a cart. And lay there. The fingers opened and shut once, slowly, on the rime.

Black blood came out of the stump in a sheet. It steamed. It hit the frozen walk and hissed, and the smell of it came off in the fog like a smithy's quench trough, hot iron and something rotten under it.

The hunter turned round.

It turned on the walk with its one arm still flat on Tobin's chest and its faceless head swinging toward her, and she saw the place on the beetle-plate of its face where a face would have been, and it made a sound for the first time, high and thin, like a kettle.

She didn't wait for it.

She stepped in. One step, on the old wall, with the push coming up through her. And cut again, from the left, low, at the place under the faceless head where the thing's neck went into its shoulders. Her blade was a pace from it. It didn't matter. It went out past the edge in the fog, a hand, a hand and a finger, a line in the air like a draught under a door; and the head came off.

Not all the way. Most. It went over sideways on a flap of hide and the whole heavy body sagged, slowly, the way a tent sags when somebody pulls the pole, and fell against the parapet with all its weight, and the rime cracked off the merlon in a sheet; and the last long arm came off Tobin Ferris's chest, and slid down him, and lay still.

The boy lay on his back and looked up at the fog.

"Ma'am," he said. Very small.

"Get up."

"Ma'am, you were three paces off."

"I know." Her voice didn't sound like hers. "Get up. Get your sword. There's eight."

***

There were eight. Wendy said so, from the gate tower, every few breaths, the way she'd said the ten on the morning of the walk east. *Eight. Two at the second turret. One on the gate. Salty's got the gate. Seven. Niko's got one. Six*. And every time she said a number, a blue light out in the fog went out, or moved, or a new one came up over something new climbing the wall; and Rhea ran along the walk under them, from embrasure to embrasure, with the old wall pushing up through her boots.

She didn't trust it at first. She felt herself start to hold it up again, out of habit, the second time, running, and her feet go light on the stones like on ice; and she stopped dead on the walk and put her boot down flat and let it go down again, on purpose, into the wall. And it pushed back. Every time. Every step. It was there every step, like the ground is there when you stop thinking about it.

The second was a scout. Tall and thin, faceless, coming over the merlon at the second turret in a burst, the way scouts moved, nothing and then there, all at once, like a thing on a string being jerked. She didn't see it. She heard Wendy: *at the turret, high, left*; and saw the blue light hanging over the merlon; and cut at the place under the light from four paces off, and felt it go out past the edge, a hand, and heard the thing come apart in the fog with a sound like a wet branch going, and fall back off the wall into the white.

The third she never saw at all. She cut where Yan's voice said, down the stair from the gate tower, shouting for the first time she'd ever heard Yan shout: *Rhea, in front of you, it's in front of you, it's cold, it's right—*; and felt the cut land, out in the fog, a hand and a finger past the edge, on something hard that broke.

Down by the gate, there was a crash like a wall falling, and a laugh. Salty's laugh. Big and stupid and all at once. She found out afterwards that a hunter had come over the gate-tower parapet straight on to him, and that Salty had made himself heavy, the *Bear*, and stood there, and let it hit him; and that it had hit him with its whole weight at a run, the way the first one had hit her, and had broken both its own arms on him like a man running into a tree, and fallen off the parapet into the passage, and that Salty had followed it down the stair and stood on it.

And Niko. She heard Niko twice more. The bell. A single clear note in the fog, like a struck bell, with a flash of orange in it; and the second time, very close, at the gate tower, a sound after it like a log splitting in a hot fire.

Then the *hush*, *hush* went away.

It went east. Back into the fog. Slow, and then fast. Wendy's lights went out one at a time in the white, *four, three, two*, and then hung there, the last one, over something going away at a trot, getting smaller, until Wendy let it go too.

"*None*," said Wendy, from the tower. "*None on the wall*."

Rhea stood on the walk with her sword in her hand.

She was breathing as if she'd run up the gate-tower stair ten times. Her ribs on the left were on fire. There was black blood on her sword and her boots and her coat, steaming in the cold, and rime in her hair. And under her boots, through the walk, through the old cracked patched courses, all the way down, the wall was still there. Pushing up. Not because she was asking it to. Because it was a wall, and she was standing on it, and that was what walls did.

Tobin Ferris was sitting against the parapet beside the dead hunter with his father's sword across his knees and his left arm held against his chest. His collarbone, the fort's surgeon said later; the long arm on his chest had broken it the way you'd break a stick across your knee. He was looking at the forearm on the stones a pace from his head. At the clean line across the elbow.

"You were three paces off," he said again. To the arm, not to her.

***

The fog lifted at dawn.

It went the way it had come, all at once, as if somebody had picked it up. One moment the wall walk was a white room ten paces long; the next the light came grey and clean out of the east over the red, and you could see. The burial track, going away east between its rows of stones with the snow on them. The markers. The red, white to the edge of the world.

And the knoll.

Rhea saw it from the east wall. They all did. The whole stretch, the eleven, standing at their embrasures with their swords down and their mouths open, and Niko beside her, and Yan and Wendy come along the walk from the gate tower, and Salty with blood on him and his hands hanging open at his sides.

There were people round the knoll.

An hour east. Small, at that distance. Dark against the snow, standing. Not hunters; hunters didn't stand like that. People. Upright, with their arms at their sides, in coats and shawls and a hat or two, the way people stand at a fair. Forty of them, perhaps. More. In a ring. All the way round the knoll, at the bottom of the slope, in a circle so even you could have drawn it with a cord; and every one of them facing in. Toward the top. Toward the cairn and the ash and the black stone at the head of the cairn.

Not moving.

And here and there among them, low and dark on the snow, the big ones. Sitting. Three of them, four. The way the cold thing at Thornwall had lain down beside the absence in the autumn; like dogs beside their masters' chairs.

Nobody on the wall said anything.

"They're at the stakes," said Wendy.

Her voice was very flat. She had her slate in her hand. She didn't look at it.

"They're at the hundred and forty," she said. "All the way round. That's where they're standing." She swallowed. "They came for it. And they got to the stakes. And they stopped."

Yan had his hand up at his neck. At Suzu. His face was grey.

"I can't feel them," he said. "Any of them. The people. There's nobody in them." He was looking at the ring. "Forty holes in the snow, facing the grave. The big ones I can feel. Cold. Lying down. But the people—" He stopped. "It's like the curve at Thornwall. The holes."

"Threadless," said Salty.

Nobody said anything.

"Walking with the big ones," said Salty. "Together. On the red." He wiped his face with the back of his hand and looked at the black blood on it. "That's the two sides. Isn't it. That's both of them at once, out in the open."

"Yes," said Yan.

They stood on the wall and watched. The sun came up behind the knoll, red over the white, and threw the ring's shadows across the snow toward the fort, long; and Rhea saw, and didn't say, that some of the people in the ring didn't throw any.

At the second bell, all at once, as if somebody had rung one, the ring turned round. Every one of them. Away from the knoll, east. And walked. At a walk, at a market-day pace, with their hands at their sides, east across the red into the white; and the big ones got up off the snow and trotted after them, *hush*, *hush*, their long arms swinging. And went over the rise, and were gone.

The black stone stood at the head of the cairn, facing west. The snow round its foot was untouched.

***

They went out at noon. Yan and Wendy, with the rope. Salty and Niko. Rhea, with her ribs strapped and her sword on her back, because the commander said nobody went out on the red without a sword, and she was a sword.

The prints were at the stakes.

All the way round. In the old snow, where the rime had frozen the crust: two rows of footprints, side by side, a pace apart, going round the knoll in a ring, as even as a hem. Boots and bare feet and clogs and one pair of small shoes like a child's. Right up to the stakes. Not a hand past them. And inside the ring, the snow went up the knoll to the cairn without a mark on it except the little three-toed prints of a crow.

Wendy walked it with the rope from the black stone. A hundred and forty, north. A hundred and forty, west. South.

East, a hundred and thirty-nine.

The prints on the east side were a pace inside the stake. Right where Niko had said the frost-letters stopped, the week before, on the fifth night, a pace in. They'd come up to exactly where it stopped them. Not a hand further.

Wendy didn't say anything. She knelt in the snow by the east stake with the rope in her hands and her slate on her knee, and wrote it down, and her hand was shaking.

Yan went up the knoll to the cairn and sat down with his back against it, and his shoulder against the black stone, and put his hand flat on the snow beside it, the way he had every night for a fortnight. He sat there a long time. Suzu came down out of his collar into the snow and lay along the foot of the stone with her head up.

Rhea stood at the edge of the knoll, facing east, the way she always stood, and didn't watch him. She looked at the prints instead. Forty pairs, all the way round, to the line and not over it. She thought about the old wall under her boots in the fog. About a thing that had been broken and broken and was still there, and pushed back.

*It's tired*, Yan had said, at supper, the week before, in that voice he used for things he'd decided to say out loud. *Suzu says it's tired. It's been saying one word for ten years with nobody saying it back.*

It hadn't looked tired, from the wall, at dawn. It had looked like a line in the snow that forty people couldn't cross.

***

Aldous wrote to the king that night.

He did it in the chapel, at the long table, with the fort's book of the dead pushed to one side for once and a clean sheet in front of him and the brazier going; and he made Rhea sit across from him while he did it, which she didn't want, because her ribs hurt and she wanted to lie down and she was a soldier and soldiers didn't sit in chapels being written about.

"He told the girl at Thornwall something," said Aldous, writing. "In the autumn. Wendy read it to me. When she was writing to Solmere about the dog." He didn't look up. "*Don't write we think. Write Hyvred saw it.*" He dipped the pen. "So."

He wrote it in the kingdom's court hand, which Rhea could read, slowly, upside down.

*Majesty. In the night just gone, in fog, which we have never seen on the Redmarch in winter, Kharvos probed the east wall: eight of the big ones and some forty scouts. The wall held. One dead of ours, Trooper Ennis on the north stretch; six hurt. At dawn there were people standing round the grave on the knoll, an hour east, forty or more, in a ring, facing in. Not hunters. People, in coats. The soul mage Yan says there is nobody in them. They had four of the big ones lying among them like dogs. They came as far as a line a hundred and forty paces from the stone and no further, all the way round, and at the second bell they turned and walked east with the hunters. Both together, on the red, in the open.*

*I do not write that we think so. Rhea saw it, from the east wall. So did I.*

He signed it. He blotted it. He folded it and put the commander's seal on it, and only then looked up at her across the table.

"And the other thing," he said.

"What other thing."

"Teague says you cut a hunter's arm off at the elbow from three paces." He put the pen down. "The Ferris boy says it too. He says it to anyone who'll stand still. He's told the cook." He looked at her for a long time with his old grave face. "And Niko and the big lad went up on the east wall at noon with Wendy's rope and measured the mark on the merlon behind where it fell. The last cut. Where the line went into the stone after it went through the thing's neck." He held up his hand, palm flat, and then the edge of a second finger. "A hand and a finger past the end of your blade. In stone."

Rhea didn't say anything.

"I can't see your window, girl," said Aldous. "You haven't got one. Hardly anybody born here has. So nobody can tell you what you are." He picked the pen up again and turned it in his fingers. "But I've been on the Redmarch twenty years, and I've seen a few people in my life cut past the edge of their own steel. Not many. You're one of them now."

She sat across the table from him with her ribs strapped and her hands in her lap. Then she looked down at her hands. At the palms. There was still rime-burn on them, red, from the stones.

"Reaching," she said.

It came out of her plain. Not loud. Not the way she said things in the yard; she said everything in the yard loud, because it was a yard, and because if you said a thing loud enough nobody noticed you hadn't been sure. She said this one quiet, in a chapel, to an old man and a brazier. She'd been Gleaming for years. She'd held the Black Blade one count across a barrel-head on the eleventh night of his dinners, and she'd wanted, more than anything in the two years since, to do one thing that was more than one count.

"Reaching," said Rhea again. "That's Reaching. It went past the edge. I felt it go." She swallowed. "Into the wall first. Then out."

"Yes," said Aldous.

"Tell Petra."

"I'll have Wendy tell the girl at Thornwall, and the girl at Thornwall can tell Petra." Aldous almost smiled. "Petra won't believe it."

"She'll believe it," said Rhea. "She'll be furious." She stood up. Her ribs hurt; she didn't care. "Is that all? I want to lie down."

"That's all."

She went to the door. And stopped, with her hand on it, and turned round.

"It was the wall," she said. "It wasn't me. I was on my knees. I couldn't stand up. I put my hands on the wall, and it was already broken, so I couldn't break it, so I stopped being careful of it." She was quiet for a moment. "That's all I did. I stopped being careful of it."

Aldous looked at her across the chapel.

"That's all anybody does," he said. "Go and lie down."

Rhea didn't read any of it. She couldn't; she had no window, and she couldn't read English if she had. Wendy read it to her the next morning, standing by her bunk in the women's barracks with her slate in one hand and a cup of something hot in the other, every line, flat, the way Wendy read things; and when she got to *she owes the draft a beer*, Rhea put her arm over her face and laughed, and it hurt her ribs so much that she had to stop, and then she laughed again anyway.

She bought it at the thaw. The whole draft, forty of them, at the canteen in the yard, the first night the red went soft. She stood on the bench and said it was because she'd gone down first, the morning after the snow, with a boy from the hill farms standing over her saying *count of ten*; and everybody cheered, and Tobin Ferris went red to the ears and had to be held up on the bench by two of the others because his arm was still in a sling.

She didn't say the other thing. She didn't need to. Everybody in the fort knew.

She went up on the east wall after, on her own, in the dark, with the red going soft and black below and the smell of the thaw coming off it, and stood on the old stones by the second turret where the mark was. She put her hand on it. On the cut in the merlon, a hand and a finger past where her blade had been; clean, a line in the stone, already starting to darken at the edges with the weather.

Under her boots, all the way down, the wall pushed back.
