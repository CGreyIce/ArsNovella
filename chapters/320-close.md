It was quiet inside.

That was the first thing. She'd stepped over a line drawn on stone and the night had stopped. Not gone dark; it was the same moon, the same white columns, the same pale sick fires on the edge of the ring, twelve of them, burning straight up like candles in a shut room. But the sound had stopped. The fire on the waterfront, the shouting, the bell at the river gate, the crowd at the fish-market door, the river: gone. As if somebody had shut a door on all of it. She could hear her own breath. She could hear Gabe's, beside her, heavy. She could hear the stone under her bare feet, which was cold, colder than stone should be in summer, a cellar step.

She could hear Maud breathing. Two different breaths, at once. One on the right side, old and short and stubborn. One on the left, slow, and not quite in time with anything.

"Don't let go of my sleeve," she said to Deston.

"I'm not," said Deston.

She walked across the ring to the middle.

***

The lines went in under her feet like the spokes of a wheel, and she didn't step on them. She didn't know why. It just seemed wrong, the way you don't step on a grave. Five paces. Six. Gabe on her left and Deston on her right and Emrys and Dennis behind, and nobody saying anything, and the tall thing at the far side of the middle with its long grey hands on Maud's head, watching her come with the two places where its eyes should have been.

It didn't move. It was pouring. She could see the dark coming off its fingers, slow, down over the backs of them like water off a ledge, into Maud's hair.

Maud sat on the hub of the lines on the stone with her stick across her knees and her back straight, and the grey had come down the whole of her left side now. From her hairline round her eye round her cheek round her jaw and down into the collar of her dress, a seam, a hair-wide line going round and round the way it had gone round Ned's arm. And inside the line, on the left, her face was smooth. Patient. A little puzzled. A woman who's walked to the end of the lane and can't remember why.

And on the right, Maud.

Rachael knelt down in front of her. On the cold stone. Close enough to work and no closer.

"Can I?" she said.

The right side of Maud's mouth moved. The left didn't.

"Get on with it, girl," said Maud.

***

She put her hands out.

Not on her. Near. By her face, on the left, a hair from the grey, the way she'd put them by the clerk's thumb that morning. The way Xola had stood in a lake. *Don't push*. And she listened.

She'd never listened to anything like it.

It was all there at once. That was what she hadn't been ready for. With Ned it had been one thing, one word, going round. With this it was everything. Under her hands, on the left side of an old woman's face, she could feel the grey: the word, the tiny emptying word, written round and round in a line like a seam, ten thousand times, a hundred thousand, going down into her. And under the word, *in* the word, coming in through it the way water comes in through a crack in a cup, the pour. Cold. So cold her hands ached a hair off it. Not one cold; many. Hundreds of small packed colds pressed together like coal in a scuttle, like Yan had said about the cold under the grave. Coming in. Filling the places the word had emptied.

And under all of it, on the right side, Maud. Trying. A body trying, the most ordinary thing in the world, an old body that had been trying for seventy-one years and was trying now, against all of that, to be Maud.

She took her hands away.

"Rae," said Deston.

"I know." Her voice came out level. She was glad of that. "It's both."

"Both what?"

"It's a word and a pour." She didn't look round. "Thea said. The cup and the closer are the same word with the sign turned round. And Maud said. A hand that closes and a hand that opens." She looked at her own hands, in Gabe's sleeves, with the cuffs pushed back. "If I close it, I close the pour in. Like Ned. Everything you close over, you close in." She swallowed. "So I have to open where it's poured. Let it out. And close the word behind it. At the same time. Every stroke."

Nobody said anything.

"How many strokes," said Dennis.

"I don't know," said Rachael. "All of them."

She didn't see that. She didn't see anything for a long time but Maud's face.

She started at the top. At the hairline, on the left, where the grey had started when the long hands first went on. The first stroke of the word. She put her left hand there, flat, by it, and her right hand just below, and she did what Maud had done to Dob the porter's neck, and what she'd done to her own seam on Ned's palm that afternoon, and what every washerwoman in the world knew how to do: she opened it.

Not with a lancet. With *thank you*. The way she'd unpicked her own *Close* with the silver needle: going along it the way it had gone, and at each place where it said what it said, saying the other thing instead. She found the first stroke of the emptying word, under her left hand, and she didn't fight it. She went back along it. And the place opened, a hair, at Maud's hairline. And something came out.

Dark. Cold. A thread of it, like smoke off a doused fire, coming up out of the hair-wide line on Maud's forehead and going up into the air and *back*. Not away. Back up into the long grey hands on Maud's head, where it had come from. As if it knew the way.

Her right hand came down behind it, and closed.

*Close*. The thing she'd had since the Lists. The thing that made a cut stop being a cut. She put it on the place her left hand had opened, a stroke behind, and the hair-wide line at Maud's hairline went from grey to pink. To skin. To Maud's.

One.

The tall thing behind Maud moved its head.

It looked down at its own hands. At the thread of dark that had come back up into them. Then at Rachael.

She didn't look at it. She found the second stroke.

***

It was slow. She'd known it would be. She hadn't known it would be like this.

Every stroke was the same. Find it. Listen to it. Open it, with *thank you*, the way you'd unpick a stitch, and let out what had been poured into the place behind it, and feel it go up past her face like cold smoke, back into the hands. Close behind it. Pink. One more. And the next was a hair further round, a hair further down, and it was the same, and the one after, and she couldn't see them. That was the worst of it. She was working on a word she couldn't read. Thea could have read it. Thea was a hundred miles away at a lake, and Rachael had only her hands.

"Left," said Dennis. "A hair. Down. There."

She stopped.

He was kneeling beside her. She hadn't heard him kneel. He was looking at Maud's face with his eyes doing the thing, and his face was grey and very tired and absolutely steady.

"It rings," he said. "The word. Every bit of it. It's a lie. *What you were won't be again*." He swallowed. "Every stroke of it rings cracked. Like a bell with a split in it. I can hear where the next one is before you get there." He pointed, not touching. "There. Under the eye. And then round. It goes round, three times, and then down."

She put her left hand where he pointed.

He was right. It was there.

"Keep telling me," said Rachael.

"I'll keep telling you."

And behind her, very low, so low she thought at first it was the blood in her own ears, Emrys started to sing.

Not a spell. She knew his spells; she'd heard him sing the seven words a hundred times that summer, burning his name off his hand. This wasn't that. It was a tune, very simple, with no words, the kind of tune you'd hum to a child who couldn't sleep; and it had a beat. Slow. Steady. Like a heart at rest. And she found, without deciding, that she was opening on the beat and closing on the off; that her hands had found the time and gone into it and stopped having to think about it, the way your feet stop thinking on a long road when somebody walks beside you.

"Don't stop," she said.

"I won't," said Emrys, in the tune, and didn't.

***

She was cold by the fortieth stroke.

She'd known that would come too. It was the price; it always had been. Warmth first, then strength. Maud had told her that in the first week, in this city, with a soldier's head in her lap. And every stroke she closed took a little of the warmth out of her hands and put it into Maud's skin, and there were thousands of strokes, and she was one person.

Her hands started to shake.

"Rae," said Deston.

"I'm fine."

"You're not," said Deston. "Your thread's gone thin. You're putting it all in." He was kneeling on her other side, now, with his hand on her sleeve, where she'd told him to keep it. "Let me."

She didn't know what he meant. Then she felt it.

*Harmony*. She'd felt it once before, at the Lists, with Calvin's wind coming into her hands through Deston's thread, the first time she ever closed anything. Deston didn't have warmth of his own to give. He wasn't a healer. But he had threads to everybody. And she felt him reach along them, the gold ones, the thick ones, to the people kneeling and standing round her in a ring on a customs house in the middle of the night: to Dennis, and to Emrys, and to Gabe. And take a little of what they had, the warmth of them, the plain ordinary heat of four bodies that weren't hers. And bring it along the thread on her sleeve, into her.

Her hands stopped shaking.

"Oh," said Dennis, beside her. "That's what that feels like." He sounded surprised. He'd gone a bit paler. "Take it. Take more. I've got loads, I'm very warm-blooded, I'm told it's my best quality."

She nearly laughed. She didn't have the warmth to spare for it.

She found the forty-first stroke.

***

The tall thing took one hand off.

She didn't see it. She heard Gabe move. A big man moving fast on stone, and then a sound like two heavy things meeting; and she looked up, past Maud's head, and the priest's left hand was off Maud's hair and coming down, long and grey and open, at her. At Rachael. At her face.

And Gabe had it by the wrist.

He'd been standing at Maud's shoulder the whole time. She hadn't looked at him once. He'd stood there, with his burned arm and his torn shirt and the blood on his face, at the shoulder of an old woman on the stone, facing the thing behind her, a pace from it, with his hands at his sides; not doing anything; just there. And when the hand came off he'd put his own up and taken it by the wrist, the way you'd take hold of a door that was swinging shut on somebody's fingers.

It pulled.

She saw Gabe's feet go. The thing was as strong as Sabine; it had put Sabine into a stone column. It pulled once and Gabe went forward half a pace on the stone, sliding, and his whole body rang, low, the note he made, *Wall*; and then the note changed. Went higher. Thinner. *Temper*. He gave, a hair. And then he didn't.

He held it.

He stood at Maud's shoulder with the priest's long grey wrist in both his hands and his feet set on the stone and his face a foot from the two places where its eyes should have been, and held it, and the hand an inch from Rachael's face didn't come any closer.

"Keep going, Rae," said Gabe. Through his teeth. Not loud. "I've got it. Keep going."

She looked at the hand. An inch from her face. Long and grey, with the dark still coming off the fingertips in slow cold drops, falling on the stone between her knees.

She kept going.

***

It went on.

She didn't know how long. Nobody inside had any way of knowing; the moon went down behind the customs house at some point and she didn't see it go. There was only Maud's face, and the next stroke, and Dennis's voice saying *left, down, there, round again*, and Emrys's tune going on and on behind her without a break, and Deston's hand on her sleeve with four people's warmth coming down it, and Gabe at Maud's shoulder holding a demon's wrist in both hands and ringing, very softly, all the time, like a bell somebody keeps touching.

The grey came off Maud's face.

Stroke by stroke. Round the eye, three times. Down the cheek. Round the jaw. Every stroke opened, and the pour behind it let out in a thread of cold that went up past Rachael's face back into the one grey hand still on Maud's head; and every stroke closed behind it, pink, Maud's. Down the neck. Into the collar. Rachael unbuttoned the top of Maud's dress with fingers she couldn't feel, *can I*, *get on with it*, and the grey went on down, round and round, the line of it like the stripe on a stick of rock, down the left shoulder and the left arm, and she followed it down, a stroke at a time, and it ended at the left hand.

In the palm.

Of course it did. A hair-wide line of grey going round and round the ball of the thumb, the way it had gone round the hook-hole in Ned's palm, and in the middle of it, the first stroke. Not at the hairline. *Here*. She'd had it backwards. The long hands had poured in at the top but the word had been started here, at the bottom, in the palm, where you'd put your hand to take something you were given.

Where you'd take a hook off a bottom step and think somebody had dropped it.

"*When*," said Rachael. Her voice didn't work properly. "Maud. When did you—"

The right side of Maud's mouth moved.

"Thursday," said Maud. Very faint. "Off the step. On the way in. I thought somebody'd dropped it."

Rachael looked at the palm for a long time.

Four days. She'd been walking up and down the long ward for four days with a hook-hole in her palm, doing the dock cases. Saying nothing. Because there were twenty-three to do and the girl was cold to the bone and no use to anybody till she'd slept.

"You stupid old woman," said Rachael.

"Get *on* with it," said Maud.

She opened the first stroke.

She felt them go.

All twelve. At once. She was kneeling on the stone with her left hand open on Maud's palm and her right hand closing behind it on the last stroke of the word, the very first one, the one that had been put in on Thursday off a step; and it closed, pink, Maud's; and there wasn't another. There was nothing left to open. The word was gone. All of it. A hundred thousand strokes, from the palm to the hairline, closed. And the pour had nowhere to go.

And the ring went out like a breath.

The pale fires on the edge of it didn't flicker. They were burning, and then they weren't, all twelve in the same instant, the way a row of candles goes out when somebody opens a door at the end of a long room. And the sound came back. All of it, all at once: the waterfront, and the fire, still burning at the east end, and the bell, and the crowd, and the river, so loud after the quiet that she put her hands over her ears and couldn't, because her hands weren't working.

The tall thing made a noise.

The second noise it had ever made. Not a crack this time. A sound like a full jug cracking. Like something with too much in it.

Gabe let go of its wrist.

He didn't mean to. It came out of his hands. And the long grey hands came off Maud's head, both of them, and Rachael saw them, for the first time, full. All the dark she'd let out of Maud, stroke by stroke for three hours, all the pour, the hundreds of small packed colds, gone back up into the hands that had held them; and the hands couldn't hold them now. They were overflowing. The dark was coming off them in sheets, off the fingers, off the wrists, down the long sleeves of the robe, and where it touched the stone it hissed.

The thing stepped back.

One step. Off the hub of the lines, where there weren't any lines any more; just stone, wet with something. And another. To the edge of the landing, the side where it dropped twelve feet to the quay, the side Benno had gone over. And it stood there with its hands held out in front of it, streaming, and looked at Rachael with the places where its eyes should be.

And went backwards off the edge.

Not falling. Stepping. Off the landing into the air, the way you'd step off a stair, and down, past the quay, into the river; and the black water took it, the tide running hard on the turn, and it went under.

Maud fell over.

Sideways, toward her, off the hub, the way you'd fall asleep sitting up. Rachael caught her. She didn't know how. She had no strength left in her arms at all, and she caught her anyway, and held her, an old woman's weight across her knees on the cold stone, with the stick fallen and the shawl gone and her face, the whole of her face, Maud's.

Both sides.

Her eyes were open. Both of them. Grey and sharp and seventy-one years old. They looked up at Rachael.

"There you are," said Maud.

She tried to mend Gabe's arm, on the stone, after.

She didn't think about it. It was there, black to the elbow from the rope walk, the skin gone in places, and he hadn't said anything about it all night; and she put her hands out, the way she always did, and said *can I*, and he said *Rae, don't*, and she put them on anyway.

Nothing came.

Not cold. Not warmth going out. Nothing. Like putting your hand into a pocket and finding it empty. She sat there with her hands on his burned arm and reached for *Mend*, the first thing she'd ever been able to do, the thing she'd done ten thousand times, and there was nothing to reach. Not even the price. She'd paid it all. Every stroke. And Deston's thread and Dennis's warmth and Emrys's tune had kept her going long enough to pay it to the end, and now it was the end, and there wasn't any left.

She took her hands away and looked at them.

"It'll come back," said Emrys. He'd stopped singing. His voice was nearly gone. "It does. The channels heal. It takes—" He stopped.

"How long?"

Emrys didn't answer for a while.

"For that," he said at last. "I don't know. Nobody's ever done that." He looked at the stone where the ring had been. "Weeks. Maybe. Not days."

"I closed it," she said. "The thing that was in her. All of it. She was herself at the end." Her voice did something. She let it. "That's all you can do, sometimes. That's the most you can do. You help what's already there do what it's trying to do." She looked at the old woman on the table. "She was trying to be Maud. So I helped."

Ned looked at her for a long time with the rope-walk squint.

Then he moved his feet, under the blanket. Not much. Just enough that there was room on the end of the bench. And didn't say anything.

Rachael sat there, in the room he'd made, and didn't cry, for a long time; and then she did.
