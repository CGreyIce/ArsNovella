Maigo was always the first to speak.

He couldn't help it. He'd tried. He'd tried his whole life, on and off, mostly after somebody told him to. Silences made him itch. They sat in a room like a smell, and everybody pretended they couldn't notice it, and Maigo always noticed it, and always, every time, a little voice in the back of his head said *somebody should say something*, and then he'd said it. Usually something stupid. Usually much too loud.

On calls, on Earth, he'd been the one who filled the gaps. Between rounds. Between matches. When somebody died and the call went quiet. When it was three in the morning and nobody wanted to be the one to say goodnight. Maigo filled it. And Ice, nine times out of ten, had filled it right back, louder and stupider, and then they'd both be going, back and forth, until Yan said *can we please play the game*, and Wendy laughed despite herself, and the silence was gone.

He'd been filling them for a year here, too. In the barracks, in the chapel, on the wall. He'd filled the silence at the grave in the spring, when he'd planted the tree. Talked to it. Introduced himself. It had felt rude not to.

He didn't fill this one.

He couldn't. He opened his mouth, three or four times, walking across the red behind the man with the sword on his shoulder, and nothing came out. Not one word. And after the fourth time he stopped trying, and just walked, and let the silence sit there, and found, to his own surprise, that it wasn't the itching kind.

It was the other kind. The kind you get in a church.

***

They'd sorted the wounded first.

That had taken a while. Aldous had got his breath back, or some of it, and gone round the soldiers on his knees with his book shut and his sleeves rolled up and his hands steady, binding and splinting and stitching, the way he must have done out here eight years ago a thousand times. Sergeant Teague's arm was the worst. Then Dace's leg. Then Salty's ribs, which Aldous felt along with his fingertips while Salty lay on the red and swore through his teeth, and pronounced *broken, two of them, clean, he'll live, keep him still*, and bound tight with strips of somebody's cloak.

Rhea's face was mostly a cut across the scalp, which bled the way scalp cuts do, and looked much worse than it was. She wouldn't let Aldous near it until he'd finished with everyone else.

Suzu's side Yan saw to himself. He'd knelt with her across his lap, full-size still, with her head in his hands, and done something Maigo couldn't see, with his eyes closed. When he opened them, the black streaks on her white scales had stopped spreading, and she'd shrunk back down to scarf size, slowly, and climbed up his arm and round his neck and gone very still there.

Nobody had died. Maigo kept saying it to himself, inside his head, the way you say something you can't believe. *Nobody died. Nobody died.* Fifty of them, and three of the big ones, and ten soldiers and Rhea and Aldous and the five of them on open ground in the middle of the Redmarch, and nobody had died.

The man had stood a little apart, while they did all that, with the sword on his shoulder and the rod in his hand. He hadn't helped. He hadn't left. He'd just stood there on the red, quite still, looking east, toward the line of dead trees on the low rise where the river had once run. Waiting, Maigo thought. Patient. As if he had all the time in the world, and was happy to spend some of it here.

When Aldous stood up from the last of the soldiers, the man turned and started walking.

East. Toward the trees.

Nobody told them to follow him. Nobody said anything at all. Aldous sent four of the soldiers back toward Cinderhold with Teague and Dace on two cloaks slung between spears, and the rest of them just went. After the man. Across the red. Rhea limping, with her sword over her shoulder and the blood drying on her face. Salty on his feet somehow, with one arm over Wendy's shoulders and his jaw clamped shut. Aldous with his book. Mashi with his hands in his pockets. Yan, with Suzu round his neck. Maigo.

It was the way you followed someone in a game, Maigo thought. When the round started and somebody just went, and you didn't ask where; you just went with them, because they'd started walking, and they'd started walking like they knew.

***

The ground was different around him.

Maigo noticed it after the first hundred paces, and then couldn't stop noticing. He was barefoot, the way he always was out here, with one root-toed foot flat on the red at every step, and he'd felt the Redmarch flinch under him every single day for a year. It flinched at everything. At the scouts. At the soldiers. At the five of them, a little, in the beginning, until it got used to them. It was hurt all the way down, this ground, so deep and for so long that it didn't know how to do anything else.

It didn't flinch at the man.

The man walked across it in his red-dusted boots, ten paces ahead, with the black sword on his shoulder, and wherever he put his feet, the red ground went *quiet*. Not still. Quiet. The way a dog goes quiet when it hears a particular step on the stair, and knows whose it is, and lies back down.

Maigo walked behind him in the quiet, and his chest hurt.

He had a hundred questions. More. He counted them, to give himself something to do, walking across the red behind the man's back. *Who are you. Where did you come from. How did you know we were here. How did you do that, with the rod. Why did you let them go. Why did you take the sword. Are you the Black Blade. Are you the one who killed the cold one. Are you the one who carved the letters. Do you know what they mean. Do you know what Latin is. Have you ever heard of a game called Counter-Strike. Have you ever—*

He stopped counting at a hundred and three. He didn't ask any of them.

He just walked. And the red went quiet, and the dead trees on the rise came closer, and closer, and then they were there.

***

The man stopped at the edge of the flowers.

They were still out. That was the first thing Maigo saw, and it made something in his throat go tight. In the spring, when he'd come out with the tree, they'd been buds. Aldous said they always came up in the spring and flowered for a few weeks and went over, every year, the only colour on the whole Redmarch for a few weeks in the spring, and then they were gone till next year.

They hadn't gone over this year.

They were still out. All of them. In the last week of summer, in the dry heat, on the dead red ground, a ring of small white and blue flowers round a low grey cairn, open and bright and moving in the wind. More of them than in the spring. Maigo had counted twelve in the spring. There were thirty now, at least. Spreading out from the ring a little, here and there, as if they'd been feeling brave.

And in the middle of them, beside the cairn, the tree.

It had grown. Maigo had felt it growing, all summer, from Cinderhold, an hour away: a small green warmth out on the red, holding on, getting stronger. He hadn't been back to see it. He'd wanted to, every day. He'd thought it might be bad luck. He hadn't known how much it had grown.

It was taller than him. Not by much; Maigo wasn't tall. But it was. A slim young tree with pale bark and a crown of bright leaves, standing straight up out of the red beside a grave in the wind, in a ring of brown earth a long stride wide where the red had gone back, and the flowers were growing right up to its roots.

And at the head of the cairn, the hole. Dry now. Crumbling at the edges. Where the sword had stood.

The man stood at the edge of the ring and looked at all of it, for a long time.

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. Maigo stood behind him with his bare foot on the red, feeling it be quiet, and watched his back. The plain brown coat. The pack. The black iron on his shoulder. The rod hanging from his other hand, the hook swinging gently on its line.

And then the man said, very quietly, to nobody, "I didn't plant that."

***

Maigo answered.

He didn't decide to. He hadn't said a word since the fight, not one, and he'd thought he wasn't going to say one all day, and then it was just there, in his mouth, the way it always was, and out before he could stop it. Only it didn't come out loud. For the first time in his life, it came out quiet.

"I did," said Maigo. "In the spring."

The man turned his head and looked at him.

Maigo felt it land. He didn't know how else to say it. It was like standing in a field in the late afternoon when the sun comes out from behind a cloud, and lands on you, all at once, warm all the way through. It went into his bark. It went into his antlers. He felt the new leaves on them lift, all by themselves, the way leaves lift toward the light.

The man's eyes went to his antlers. Then to his face. Then, for a moment, to the pixel sunglasses pushed up on his forehead, the stupid black *deal with it* sunglasses that had come through the floor with him because he'd been wearing them, and that he'd refused to take off for a year.

Something moved at the corner of the man's mouth. It wasn't quite a smile. It was there and then it was gone.

"So she wouldn't be alone out here," said Maigo. His voice cracked in the middle. He didn't care. "That's why. I felt something. Under the ground. Like a hand. Holding on to her. And I thought it must be lonely, out here, just holding on. On its own. So I brought her a tree." He swallowed. "I hope that's okay."

The man looked at him for a long moment.

Then he went down on one knee at the edge of the ring, and leaned the black sword carefully against the cairn, and reached out past the flowers, and touched one of the tree's leaves with the tip of his finger. Very gently. As if it might not be real. The exact way Aldous had touched it, in the spring, among the markers, the morning it came up out of the ground.

"Thank you," he said.

***

He sat down by her after that, and he didn't get up again until the sun went down.

He sat on the red ground inside the ring of flowers, with his back against the cairn and the black sword lying across his knees. He put the rod down beside him, and the pack. He didn't look at them again.

Aldous took them back. Thirty paces, to the edge of the rise, where a dead tree had fallen years ago and lay grey and smooth along the ground like a bench. He didn't say anything. He just put his hand on Yan's shoulder, and on Maigo's, and turned them round, gently, and walked them back, and the rest followed. And they sat down on the fallen tree and the red ground beside it, all of them, in a row, with their backs to the grave, or nearly. And waited.

Maigo didn't fill the silence.

He sat there with his bare feet on the red and his hands in the front pocket of his hoodie and his sunglasses pulled down over his eyes, and listened to the wind, and didn't say one word. Nobody did. Not for a long time.

Salty lay on the ground with his head in Wendy's lap and his bound ribs going up and down, very shallowly, and looked at the sky. Once, a long while in, he said something under his breath. Maigo thought it was *Diwa*. Wendy put her hand on his hair.

Rhea sat on the end of the fallen tree with her sword across her knees and her face caked with dried blood, and stared straight ahead, at nothing, with an expression Maigo had never seen on her before. She opened her mouth, once, an hour in, and Wendy looked at her, and she shut it again.

Aldous sat with his book open on his knees and didn't read it.

Mashi didn't look away from the man once. Not once, the whole afternoon. He sat cross-legged on the red at the very end of the row, with his elbows on his knees and his chin on his fists, and watched him. Not his face. His hands, and his shoulders, and the way he sat. The way Mashi had watched games on Earth, other people's games, replays, hunched forward, for hours, until he could have played them himself with his eyes shut.

And Yan sat beside Maigo with his eyes closed and Suzu round his neck, and didn't say anything either. But his hands, in his lap, hadn't stopped shaking. Not since the fight. Maigo could see them.

***

The man talked to her.

They could see him do it, if they looked round, and after a while they all did, a little, every now and then, the way you can't help looking at something you've been told not to. He sat there with his back against the cairn and the sword across his knees and his head tipped back against the stones, and talked. Quietly. Not all the time. A few words, and then a long silence, and then a few more. They were too far away to hear any of it. Maigo was glad. He didn't think he could have stood it.

Once, the man laughed. Maigo heard that. It was a short low sound, carried on the wind, gone almost before it came. As if she'd said something.

Once, he put his hand down flat on the earth beside the cairn, among the flowers, and left it there.

And Maigo felt it.

He was sitting on the fallen tree with his bare feet on the red, the way he always sat, and he felt it come up through the soles of his feet like warm water. The thread. The root. The warm stubborn thing he'd felt in the spring, far down under the markers, running out under the whole Redmarch to this grave, and going down under the cairn, and *holding*. Like a hand. Like a hand that had been laid on the ground here a long time ago, by somebody who didn't want to let go, and had never quite been lifted.

It was *his*.

Maigo knew it the moment the man's palm touched the earth. The way you know a voice on the phone before they say their name. The hand on the ground and the hand under the ground were the same hand. The warm thing that had been feeding those flowers for eight years, that had come back every spring and made them grow on dead ground where nothing grew, was this man. Was whatever was in this man. Soaked into the earth around her, a little more every year, every time he came out here and sat down and put his hand on the ground and talked to her.

The flowers had never been planted, exactly. Not just planted. They'd been *held*.

Maigo pushed his sunglasses up, and then pulled them back down again, hard, and put his face in his hands.

Beside him, very quietly, Yan said, "It's gone quiet."

"What?" said Maigo, into his hands.

"The cold. Under the grave." Yan's deep voice was barely a murmur. "The thing I felt, the first time Aldous brought us here. The thing that was done to someone. It's been there all year. Every time I've reached for it." He was quiet for a moment. "It's gone quiet. Since he sat down. Like it's asleep."

Maigo didn't say anything. He couldn't.

***

He thought about Ice.

He didn't mean to. It just happened, sitting there, with the warm root under his feet and the wind going through the tree's leaves behind him. He thought about a pistol round, years ago, in a game that didn't matter. Four kills. A *pistol*. He'd been so surprised he'd just sat there in his chair with his hands in the air. And on the call, Ice screaming *THE MACHINE, MAIGOTRON, THE MACHINE*, so loud Yan had taken his headphones off.

Nobody else had ever called him that. He'd written it in the Ice book, for Hako, in the channel. *Nobody else ever called me that. I miss it.*

He sat on the fallen tree with his face in his hands and wanted, so badly that it frightened him, for the man by the grave to say it. Just once. *Maigotron.* That was all it would take. One word, and he'd know, and he could stop sitting here with a hundred and three questions in his chest, and get up, and run across the red, and—

And he was afraid. That was the other thing. He was so afraid that the man would say it, and so afraid that he wouldn't, that he couldn't tell which one he was more afraid of.

The man didn't say it. The man sat with his back against the cairn and talked to the woman under it, quietly, every now and then, and the sun went down the sky behind him, and the shadow of the young tree got longer and longer across the ring of flowers, until it reached the fallen tree where they were sitting, and touched Maigo's bare foot.

***

He stood up at dusk.

The sun had gone down behind the Redmarch in the west, the way it did here, enormous and red, so that for a few minutes the whole dead plain was the colour of a coal. And when it had gone, and the light was going grey and blue, the man stood up.

He picked up the rod, and the pack, and slung the pack on his back. He stood looking down at the cairn for a moment. Then he bent and picked up the black sword, and swung it up onto his shoulder, the way he had on the red that morning, like a bundle of sticks for the stove. And he touched the tree's leaves once more, in passing, with the back of his free hand. And came out of the ring of flowers, and across the thirty paces to where they were sitting.

They all stood up. Even Salty. Wendy tried to stop him, and he gave her a look, and she let him, and he got up off the red with his teeth clamped and one arm round his ribs, and stood there.

The man stopped in front of them.

Nobody said anything.

And then Salty said it, because of course he did. Because he was Salty, and it was there, and he never decided to say things; he just said them. He stood there on the red in the dusk with his broken ribs and his blunt square face, and looked the man in the eye, and asked.

"Are you him?"

He didn't say who. He didn't have to. Maigo felt everyone go still around him, all at once, the way a room goes still when somebody says the thing nobody's been saying.

***

The man didn't answer.

He looked at Salty. For a long time. And then he looked at Wendy. And then at Mashi. And then at Yan. One after another, slowly, the way you'd look at people across a room if you'd been away from that room for a very long time and wanted to be sure they were all still there. He looked at each of them as if he were reading something written on their faces, and wanted to get every word.

He looked at Maigo last.

It landed on Maigo the way it had at the edge of the ring. Like late sun. All the way through. He felt the leaves on his antlers lift again, all on their own, toward it. And he stood there in the dusk on the red and looked back, and didn't look away, and didn't say anything, and waited for the word.

The man didn't say it.

"Go home safely," he said instead. Quietly. His voice was the same as it had been all day: nobody's voice, a stranger's, rough and low, with that accent from somewhere north. "Your friends will want to know you're all right."

***

He turned to Aldous.

"Tell Halvard I've taken it," he said.

Aldous's face was wet again. It had been wet on and off all afternoon. He bowed his head.

"He'll understand," he said. "I think he hoped you would."

"I know he did." The man shifted the sword on his shoulder, very slightly. He looked back past them, at the cairn, and the flowers, and the young tree, dark now against the last of the light. "Half of it will come back to her."

Aldous looked up. "Half?"

"She should have a stone," said the man. "A proper one. Not a hole in the ground." He looked at the cairn a moment longer. "Half of it for her. The other half for someone who needs a sword."

Aldous didn't ask who. Maigo saw him not ask. He just bowed his head again, lower this time, and stayed bowed.

The man nodded to him. Then to Rhea, who'd gone very red under the dried blood and didn't seem to know what to do with her face. Then to the four soldiers, standing in a row at the back, who saluted all at once, badly, as if they'd forgotten how.

Then he turned and walked away, west, across the Redmarch, into the dusk.

***

They watched him go.

A plain man in a plain coat, with a pack on his back and a fishing rod in one hand and a black sword on his shoulder, walking across the red in the last of the light, getting smaller. The ground went quiet under him wherever he walked. Maigo could feel it, the quiet, moving away across the Redmarch like the wake of a boat, getting fainter and fainter, a long way, until it was just a line of stillness going west into the dark.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The first stars came out. The wind got up. Behind them, the young tree's leaves moved in it with a sound like someone whispering.

Then Maigo said, very quietly, "He didn't say no."

They all looked at him.

He was crying. He didn't care. He was standing on the red in the dark, barefoot, with his sunglasses pushed up on his antlers and the tears running down his bark, and he didn't care at all.

"Salty asked him," said Maigo. "Salty asked him, straight out. *Are you him.* And he looked at us. All of us. Like..." He stopped. He didn't know like what. "And he didn't say no. He could have said no. It would have been so easy to say no. Anybody else would have said *who?*" He wiped his face with the sleeve of his hoodie. "He didn't say *who*. He didn't say no."

Nobody answered him.

Then Wendy, of all people, Wendy who didn't believe in much, reached out in the dark and took his hand. And held it.

And for once in his life, Maigo didn't say anything else. He didn't need to.

They walked home together across the red, under the stars, all of them, slowly, at Salty's pace, toward the lights of Cinderhold and the horns over the gate. And the silence came with them the whole way.

It was the other kind. The kind you get in a church.
