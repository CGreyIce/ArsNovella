Niko had lost to him about four hundred times.

He'd counted, once, when he was seventeen. Not on purpose. He'd just noticed, one night, on a community server, after the fortieth or fiftieth time in a week that he'd walked into mid on a map he knew better than his own bedroom and been killed by the same AWPer from the same angle, that he'd started keeping a tally in his head. And once you start a tally you can't stop. So he hadn't.

Four hundred and something. Over two years. The same AWPer. The same mid. The same patience.

He'd never once seen him peek first. That was the thing about him that had driven a seventeen-year-old Niko up the wall. Everybody peeked, eventually. Everybody got bored, or nervous, or greedy, and stuck their head out to see where you were, and you shot it. This one never did. He sat in mid with his AWP and waited, so long you'd think he'd gone to make tea, and then you'd get bored, or nervous, or greedy, and stick your head out to see where he'd gone, and you'd be dead.

Four hundred times.

And once, Niko had beaten him.

***

He was thinking about that now, sitting on the bottom tier of the east stand of the Lists in the grey before dawn on the sixty-eighth day, with his hands in his pockets and his knees up, waiting.

The Lists were empty. Really empty, for the first time since he'd come over the hill eight days ago with a cart and a blanket full of horseshoes. The Lists were over. The bracket on the east wall was full, every box, all forty feet of it, with *THE THIRTY-FIVE* painted round a spider in the top corner. The paper fishing rods were still tied along the rails, a bit limp now in the dew. The sand had been raked one last time, the evening before, by a marshal who'd stood at the end of it afterwards with his rake and looked at it for a long time, the way you look at something you won't see again for a year.

Nobody was here. The dawn class was on the practice ground behind the Lists, the way it always was: Niko had walked past it on the way in. Seven of them in a loose ring on the wet sand in the mist, very still. Petra first.

The man in the middle of them hadn't been there.

He'd stopped coming to the middle of the class two days ago. Petra ran it now, at dawn, with Xav. The man came, and stood at the fence with his rod on his shoulder, and watched, and said one word now and then. But he didn't stand in the middle. *They don't need me in the middle*, he'd said, when Hako asked. *They need me at the fence. So they know I'm watching*.

Niko had asked him last night, at the long table, quietly, under the noise.

*Can I see you move? Once more. Properly. Not at the class*.

And the man had looked at him for a long time and said *dawn*.

***

He came in by the side gate at the end of the grey, when the sky over the top of the stands was going the colour of a fish's belly. On his own. With the rod on his shoulder and, for once, no bees on his head; Bee had finally got them back the night before, by bribing them with honey, and they'd gone to her looking guilty.

He came down onto the sand. He stopped about ten paces from the bottom tier. He looked at Niko.

"You wanted to see," he said.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

Niko took his hands out of his pockets. He looked at them.

"Because I'm stuck," he said.

***

He'd been stuck for a year.

Not stuck like Thea had been, at ninety-nine percent for eleven days before a bonfire. Stuck sideways. He was Initiate. He was still Initiate. He was the slowest of the thirty-five to rank up; everybody knew it; he'd known it since the first week at Cinderhold, when Aldous looked at his window and frowned and said *you're training both*. Aura and mana. Swordsman and mage. Everybody said it was a waste. You picked one. Everybody picked one. You put everything you had into one path, and you climbed it.

Niko had put half into each, and climbed both, very slowly, exactly together.

They'd always risen together. From the first week. One and one. Three and three. Nine and nine. Eighteen and eighteen. He'd never once been able to make one go up without the other. He'd tried. In the first winter he'd done nothing but aura for a month, every waking hour, sword drills till his arms shook, and the aura line had gone up two, and the mana line had gone up two with it, as if they were tied.

*I think they want to mix*, he'd said, then. He hadn't known what he meant.

And then, a year ago, on the red, on the road to a grave, demon scouts had come out of the grey grass at an escort carrying a black sword, and a man with a fishing rod had walked into the middle of them.

Niko had watched him fight. Once. For about a minute. From behind a fallen wagon, with a split lip and his sword in the wrong hand.

He'd never seen anything like it. Not the speed. Not the hook and the reel and the cut, though that was frightening enough. The other thing. The thing underneath. He'd felt the man's aura go out. Not like Salty's, a hard bright edge, or Wendy's, sharp at the tip of an arrow. Out like a *spell*. Filling the space round him the way a mage's fire fills a room. Pressing everything flat. And then, the moment after, coming back *in*. Like breath.

Out like a spell. In like breath. A circle. Not two lines. One thing, going round.

He'd sat on the wall at Cinderhold the next morning with his two lines in front of his face, aura and mana, eighteen and eighteen, and thought: *what if I put them end to end*.

Aura out. Mana in. On the breath. A circle.

They'd touched. His two candles. That was what Yan called them: *two candles that keep almost touching*. They'd touched for one heartbeat, at dawn, on the wall, and everything had gone clear and in tune, like two notes finding each other, and his nose had bled all down his chin and Yan had come running.

And a third line had appeared in his window. Under the other two. For a heartbeat. Grey. Locked. With no name.

He'd seen it every time since. Every time the candles touched. One heartbeat, then two. By the winter, five. Five heartbeats of two candles touching and everything in tune and the grey line sitting there under the others, locked, nameless, waiting.

*Waiting for something I haven't seen yet*, he'd written in a letter from the fort, in the snow. *I think you know what*.

***

"I've got to five," said Niko. "Five heartbeats. Since the winter. I can't get past it." He looked at his hands. "It's not that it hurts. It doesn't, any more. It's that I don't know what comes after. I've only ever seen it once. The real thing. The circle. You, on the red, for about a minute, from behind a wagon." He looked up. "I learned it from that. I learned the whole thing from one minute behind a wagon. And I've been doing it on my own for a year and I don't know if I'm doing it *right*."

The man didn't say anything.

"I learned you before," said Niko. "On the server. Four hundred times. I learned how you waited. I learned your angles. I learned where you'd be before you were there." He was quiet. "I learned you by losing to you. That's the only way I've ever learned anything from you." He looked at him. "I want to lose to you once more. Properly. Close. So I can see."

The man looked at him for a long time.

"You won't lose," he said. "I'm not going to fight you."

"Then what?"

"I'm going to move," said the man. "Once. The way I moved on the red. Not at you. Just move." He set the rod down against the bottom of the stand. Very carefully. The way you'd put down something you didn't want to scare. "You watch. With both."

***

He walked out into the middle of the sand.

Niko watched him go. Across the raked gold, in the grey light, with his hands empty, a man with a face nobody could see, walking out into the middle of the great bowl of the Lists on his own. He stopped where the mound had been, and the gate after it. Where thirty-five people had stood in a ring at midnight, eight days ago, and said *here*.

He stood there. Not doing anything.

Niko watched with both.

That was what the man had said, and it was the only way Niko knew how to watch any more. He'd been training both for two years; he couldn't turn either one off. He watched with his eyes, a man standing on sand. He watched with his aura, which was a kind of feeling in his skin and his bones, the way you feel the weight of something before you lift it. And he watched with his mana, which was a kind of seeing, the way you see shapes in the dark when your eyes have got used to it.

With his aura, the man in the middle of the sand was very heavy. The way a mountain is heavy, if you put your hand on it. Banked.

With his mana, he was very quiet.

"Ready?" said the man. Not loudly. It carried across the whole empty bowl anyway.

"Ready," said Niko.

The man moved.

***

Niko would try to describe it afterwards, for years. To Yan, who'd seen it on the red. To Thea, who wanted the right word for it. To Wendy, who said *just say what happened*. He never got it right. He'd say it like this, in the end, because it was the closest he could get:

*He didn't do anything. He just stopped holding it in*.

That was all. The man stood in the middle of the sand and didn't move his hands or his feet or anything Niko could see with his eyes. He just stopped holding it in. And it came out of him.

Out like a spell.

Niko felt it go. With his aura first: a weight, coming off the man in the middle of the sand, out across the raked gold in every direction at once, like water poured out of a bowl. Not fast. Not slow. Just *out*. It went across the sand and up the stands, tier on tier, and over Niko on the bottom tier, and through him, and he felt himself pressed flat against the stone behind him, gently, the way a big wind presses you against a wall, and he couldn't have lifted his hand if he'd tried. It went up past him and up and up to the top of the stands and out over the rim of the bowl into the grey sky.

Dominion. *Aura fills an area. Presence alone pins weaker fighters*. He'd read it on a list, in the first week. He'd thought it was just a description.

It filled the Lists. The whole bowl. Every tier. Every bench. Every paper fishing rod on every rail lifting very slightly, the way grass lifts before a storm.

And with his mana, at the same moment, Niko *saw* it.

Not the weight. The shape. He saw what it was doing. It wasn't a wall, going out. It wasn't an edge. It was a pattern. Rings inside rings, going out from the man in the middle like ripples from a stone, each ring the same shape as the last, nested, turning, the way a spell turns when Thea pulls it apart in the air.

A spell. Made of aura.

The two things he'd been training for two years, the thing everyone said you had to choose between. They weren't two things. Not at the top. At the top, aura stopped being a sword and started being a spell. It filled a room the way fire fills a room. It had a *shape*.

And then it came back.

***

It came back in like breath.

That was the part he'd seen on the red and never understood. The part he'd been trying to copy for a year on a wall at Cinderhold, in the snow, with his two lines end to end. He saw it now. Close. With both.

The rings went out to the rim of the bowl and stopped. Hung there, for a moment, filling everything, pressing everything flat. And then, without the man in the middle of the sand doing anything at all, they turned round.

And came back.

In. Toward the middle. The same rings, the same shape, going the other way. Folding back in on themselves like a tide going out, like a breath being drawn, all the way across the sand and the stands and through Niko on the bottom tier, so that the weight came off him as gently as it had come on, and back to the man standing on his own in the middle of the empty Lists.

And there was no seam.

That was the thing. That was the thing he'd never been able to do. He could put aura-out and mana-in end to end, and they'd touch, and go round, and hold, for five heartbeats. But there was always a seam. A place where one stopped and the other started. A join. He could feel it every time, like a knot in a rope, and the circle always broke at the knot.

The man's had no knot. It went out like a spell and came back like breath and there was no place where one stopped and the other started. It was one thing. Going round. A circle drawn in a single line, the way Bee drew her dogs.

*Oh*, thought Niko.

*That's what I haven't seen*.

***

His candles touched.

He didn't decide to. He didn't do anything. He was sitting on the bottom tier with his back against the stone and the weight just lifted off him, and he was watching with both, and something in him saw the circle with no seam and simply went *oh*, and copied it.

The way you copy an angle you've died to four hundred times. Without thinking. Because you've seen it so often your body knows where to stand before your head does.

Aura out. Mana in. On the breath.

No knot.

His two candles, the ones Yan had seen in him in the first spring, *two candles that keep almost touching*, touched. And didn't stop. They didn't go round and hold and break at the seam. They went round and kept going round, because there was no seam to break at. One thing. Going round. Like breathing.

One heartbeat.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

He'd never got past five. He felt the place where five had always been, the place where it always broke, and he felt himself come up to it, and he felt it not break.

Six.

His window was open. He hadn't opened it. It had opened itself, in front of his face, in the grey light, on the bottom tier of the Lists.

And under it, the third line.

***

It wasn't grey.

For a year it had been grey. Every time the candles touched, it had appeared, under the other two, the way a ghost appears in a photograph: a line, with nothing on it, the colour of ash, locked. No name. No number. Just *there*, waiting, for one heartbeat, for two, for five, and then gone when the circle broke.

It wasn't grey now.

It was light. The same light as the other two lines, the plain white light of every status window in the kingdom. Lit. As if somebody had turned it on.

And there was something on it.

Not a name. Not yet. Niko stared at it and couldn't read it, the way you can't read a word written in frost on a window from the inside. There were letters. He could see that. The shapes of letters, very faint, under the light, the way you can see the shape of writing on the back of a page held up to a lamp. In English. He was almost sure it was English. A word, or two words. He couldn't read them. They were there and they weren't, like a word on the tip of your tongue.

Seven heartbeats.

Eight.

*Look*, he thought. *Look. Look at it. Read it*.

He couldn't. It wasn't ready. He could feel it not being ready, the way the king said you could feel iron that wasn't ready: you could see it in the colour. The line was lit. The word was there. It just wasn't finished being written.

Nine.

He held it as long as he could.

The circle broke at eleven.

***

He came back to himself on the bottom tier of the Lists with his back against the stone and blood all down his chin.

His nose. Again. He hadn't had a nosebleed since the first winter. He wiped it with the back of his hand and looked at it and laughed, a little, because it was so stupid, and then looked up.

The window was still open. The third line was grey again. Locked. Nameless. Ash.

But he'd seen it lit.

The man had come back across the sand. He was standing at the foot of the bottom tier, a few paces away, with his hands empty, looking at Niko. He didn't say anything. He took a cloth out of his coat, a fisherman's cloth, for wiping hands on, and held it out.

Niko took it. He held it against his nose.

"Eleven," he said, through the cloth.

"I counted."

"I've never got past five."

"I know," said the man. "Yan told me. In the letter. *Five heartbeats*." He sat down on the bottom tier, beside Niko, close, the way he'd sat on the end of a jetty with Group 1 in the winter. "What did you see?"

Niko thought about it. He wanted to get it right.

"No seam," he said. "Yours has no seam. It goes out and comes back and there's no place where one stops and the other starts. Mine always had a knot. I've been trying to get past the knot for a year." He took the cloth away from his nose and looked at the blood on it. "There isn't a knot. There's never been a knot. I was *putting* it there. Because I thought they were two things."

"They are two things," said the man.

"Not at the top."

The man was quiet for a long time.

"No," he said. "Not at the top."

***

They sat there while the sky went from grey to pale over the rim of the stands. Somewhere behind the Lists the dawn class was finishing; Niko heard Petra's voice say something sharp, and Tiger laugh, and Hako say *again*.

"The third line lit," said Niko.

The man turned his head.

"In my window. The grey one. With no name. It lit. For about six heartbeats. From five to eleven." Niko looked at his window, still open in front of his face, the grey line under the other two. "There was something written on it. I couldn't read it. Like frost on a window from the inside. Letters. English, I think." He swallowed. "Two words, maybe. It wasn't finished."

The man didn't say anything for a long time.

"What do you think it is?" said Niko.

"I don't know," said the man honestly. "I've never seen a third line. Not in thirty-four years. Not in my window or anyone's. Nobody's ever trained both." He looked at the grey line. "Nobody's ever been stupid enough."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"I know," said Niko. "That's why I'm saying thank you."

The man laughed, quietly.

***

"There's a thing," said Niko. "That I've been thinking about. Since the winter."

"Go on."

"The demon king." Niko put the cloth down on the stone beside him. "Everyone says you need two people to kill him. A Sun mage and a Zenith swordsman. *In tandem*. That's what the books say. Aldous told us, in the first week. Thea read it in the circle yesterday, sort of, the blank half needs a Sun." He looked at his hands. "Everyone thinks *in tandem* means two people. Standing next to each other. One with a sword and one with a spell. Hitting him at the same time."

"That's what it means."

"Does it?" Niko turned his hands over. "What if it means this? Two things going round with no seam. Out like a spell. In like breath. What if *in tandem* doesn't mean two people next to each other. What if it means two things that aren't two things any more."

The man was very still.

"I don't know," said Niko. "I'm Initiate. I'm the slowest of the thirty-five. I've got a nosebleed. I'm probably wrong." He looked at the grey line in his window. "But I saw you do it. Just now. Aura that was a spell. On your own. With no seam." He looked up. "You're a swordsman. You're the strongest swordsman alive except the king. And at the top, yours stopped being a sword."

The man didn't answer for a long time.

"It's a question for later," he said eventually. "A lot later."

"I know."

"Years."

"I know," said Niko. "I'm not in a hurry. I've been Initiate for two years. I'm very good at not being in a hurry." He wiped his nose again. "I just wanted to say it to somebody. Out loud. Before I go back to the fort and say it to a wall."

"You've said it."

"I've said it."

"Then hold onto it," said the man. "Don't say it to anyone else yet. Not Thea. Not Yan. Not the king." He paused. "Not because it's wrong. Because it's yours. And you haven't finished writing it."

Niko looked at the grey line in his window. Under the other two. Locked. Nameless. With something written on it he couldn't read yet.

"Okay," he said.

***

The sun came up over the rim of the Lists.

It came up gold, the way it had come up every morning of the week, and laid itself across the raked sand and the forty-foot bracket on the east wall and the paper fishing rods along the rails, limp in the dew. It caught the spider in the top corner of the bracket, in the middle of *THE THIRTY-FIVE*, and the web lit up, every strand of it, for a moment, silver.

Niko closed his window.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Go on."

"Do you remember it? The one time." He didn't look round. "On the server. When I was seventeen. Four hundred times you killed me in mid, and once I got you."

The man was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said.

Niko turned his head. He hadn't expected that. He'd thought the man would say *no*, gently, the way you say no to somebody who remembers something you don't. Thirty-four years. A whole life, in between. He'd have understood.

"You remember?"

"I remember," said the man. "You didn't peek."

Niko stared at him.

"Everybody peeked," said the man. "Everybody, eventually. Four hundred times you peeked. And then one night you didn't. You didn't wait, either. You did both. You threw something over to make me look and you came through behind it at the same time, both at once, and I was looking at the wrong thing." He was quiet. "Nobody had ever done both at once. Not to me. I sat there dead in mid and I thought: *that kid's going to be dangerous*."

Niko didn't say anything. He couldn't.

"And I typed something in all-chat," said the man.

"I know," said Niko. His voice had gone. "I know what you typed."

He'd kept it. For eleven years. On Earth, and then here. He'd never told anyone what it was, not even Yan, because it was stupid, and it was three words, and it was the only thing that AWPer had ever said to him in two years of killing him four hundred times.

The man didn't make him say it.

He stood up off the bottom tier. He picked up his rod from where he'd leaned it against the stand. He put it on his shoulder. And he looked down at Niko on the stone, with a cloth full of blood beside him and a nosebleed and a grey line in his window with a word on it he couldn't read yet, and said it, in English, in a voice that wasn't the voice of a boy in a community server at two in the morning any more and was exactly the same:

"Nice one. Again."

***

Niko sat on the bottom tier of the empty Lists for a long time after the man had gone.

He watched the sun come all the way up. He watched the marshals come in through the main gate to start taking the paper fishing rods down off the rails, very carefully, one at a time, folding them, putting them in baskets. One of them stopped at the rail of the royal box and looked at a rod for a long time, and then didn't fold it. He put it inside his coat instead.

Niko opened his window again.

One each. Together. The way they always went.

And under them, grey and locked and nameless, the third line. He looked at it for a long time. He could still see, if he looked hard, if he let his mana-sight go *down* the way Thea had taught him once in a letter, the faintest shape of something on it. Frost on a window from the inside. Two words. Not finished.

*Not yet*, he thought. That was Yan's word. *When someone doesn't answer, it isn't no. It's not yet*.

He closed his window. He put his hands in his pockets. He got up off the bottom tier and went out by the side gate into the city, where Group 3 would be packing a cart for the road east, with a scythe and a hammer and a horseshoe and eleven empty pie dishes, and a man who was coming with them this year, for the first time, to a grave.

He'd say it to a wall, at Cinderhold, in the snow. And the wall would say nothing back, and that would be fine.

He'd keep losing. He was good at it. It was how he learned.
