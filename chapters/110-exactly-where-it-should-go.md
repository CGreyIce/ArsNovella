Emrys knew every song in the kingdom.

That wasn't a boast. It was simply the shape of his life. He'd been born in a chapter house in the hills above Valcrest, to a chantress and a stonemason, and he'd been singing before he could walk. At seven they'd given him to the choir. At twelve, to the chanters. At twenty he'd lit his first torch with a song, a real one, a little flame that sat on the end of his finger and burned without burning. And for twenty-seven years after that he'd walked the roads of Aurelan from the Redmarch to the western sea, learning every song he could find. Harvest songs. Rowing songs. Lullabies. Songs for the dead and songs for the newly married, songs for calling sheep and songs for cursing the tax man. Old songs in the hill tongue that nobody spoke anymore. Songs so holy you sang them only once a year, in the dark, with the doors shut.

He knew them all. Every one. It was what he was for.

And then, a year and a half ago, in Kingsferry, in the first weeks, he'd climbed the ladder to the roof above the chandler's in the dark to tell the five strangers he was guiding that supper was ready. And found two of them sitting on the leads behind the chimney, humming something. Low. Without the words. Two parts, a low one and a middle one, the way men hum when they've known a tune so long it hums itself.

He'd never heard it before in his life.

He'd lain awake that whole night, trying to place it. Turning it over. Trying to fit it to every song he'd ever learned. It didn't fit anything. It wasn't from anywhere. It wasn't built the way songs in this world were built, it turned corners that no song turned, it climbed in places where songs here fell. And it had a hole in it.

That was the thing that kept him awake. Halfway through each phrase, the two of them went quiet. Both at once. For a breath or two. As if they were waiting for something. And then went on.

He'd asked, the next morning. Carefully. *The song you hum. There's a gap in it.*

Deston had looked at Gabe. Gabe had looked at the fire.

*There's a third part*, Deston had said. *We leave room for it.*

He hadn't asked who sang the third part. He'd been a chanter for twenty-seven years. He knew what it sounded like when somebody kept a space at a table for someone who wasn't coming.

***

He'd carried that gap for a year and a half.

He'd only heard the song the once. He'd never climbed that ladder at that hour again; it wasn't his to listen to. But he hadn't needed to hear it twice. He never did. He'd carried it in his head ever since, every turn, every corner, and the hole in the middle. He'd never once sung it. It wasn't his to sing.

And then, at midwinter, at the night market, a man with no thread had come for Rachael through the crowd, and Emrys had done the only thing he knew how to do. He'd sung. Everything. Every note he had, all at once, in one great blaze of light, like throwing a whole year's oil onto one lamp. It had worked. The man had gone back. Rachael had lived.

And his voice had gone with it.

Not all of it. He could speak. A rasp, a croak, that hurt to listen to and hurt more to make. He could hum, if he was careful, cracked and thin, like wind in a broken bottle. But he couldn't *sing*. Not a chant. Not a note that held. Every time he tried, it broke in his throat like a dry stick, and the little flame on the end of his finger guttered and went out.

A chanter who couldn't sing.

He'd told nobody how much it frightened him. He was forty-seven. He'd been singing for forty-six of them. He didn't know who he was without it.

So he'd carried the song instead. All that year. The gap and all. He'd hummed it once, in the Narrows, at the stern of a barge, after the fight, with his cracked voice that sounded like a broken bottle, and he'd left the gap, and nobody had said anything.

And then, four days ago, on a shingle beach at the end of a lake road, a stranger's voice had come in across the water, from the end of a jetty, too loud.

Exactly where it should go.

***

They lit a fire on the shingle on the fifth night.

It was the first time they'd all been in one place. All of them. Not just the thirteen off the barge, but Group 1 as well, who'd been coming down the lake road every evening since the winter, Emrys was given to understand, and had stayed politely up in the town for four days to leave the fisherman to his brothers. They came down that evening with bread and a ham that Odette denied having anything to do with, and a pot of something red that the fisherman had made and that everybody ate anyway, crying, because it was hotter than anything Emrys had ever put in his mouth.

Fifteen of them. Fifteen of the thirty-five, round one fire. Emrys counted. He'd been counting heads since the river road; it was what a guide did.

And the fisherman. And his daughter. And Petra, and Sabine, and Maren, the guide of Group 1, who looked at Emrys across the fire and nodded once in a way that said *you too, then*. And at the top of the shingle, on the low stone wall along the lake road, a little line of townsfolk had appeared as it got dark. Not coming down. Just sitting on the wall in the dusk, the way you'd sit on a wall to watch boats come in. The mayor among them, with his arms folded.

Emrys sat a little apart, on a log at the edge of the light, with his blanket round his shoulders, and watched.

He'd seen a lot of fires in his life. A lot of people round them. He didn't think he'd ever seen faces like these. Firelit and wet and laughing, all of them, all at once. Passing bread. Talking over each other. A small one with a strange creature on her head, sitting in the fisherman's lap as if she'd been doing it for years, telling a story with her hands. A quiet one beside her, watching everyone, very still, and smiling at nothing. A girl with a penguin. A penguin.

He'd stopped being surprised by things, mostly, around the summoned. He was still a little surprised by the penguin.

The fisherman sat in the middle of it. On a log by the fire with his back to the lake and his daughter beside him and a cup in his hand and Gabe's arm across his shoulders, and the girl with the creature on his knee, and somebody always talking to him. He didn't say much. He listened. He laughed, often, suddenly, too loud. And every so often, Emrys saw, he looked round at all of them, at every face, one after another, the way he'd looked at them on the mayor's terrace. As if he were counting too.

***

It was Alea who asked.

She'd been quiet most of the evening. Sitting by her father with her knees up and her tail round her feet and her chin on her knees, watching the fire. And then, in a lull, she lifted her head.

"Sing it," she said.

The fire went quiet.

"All the way through," said Alea. "Properly. The three of you." She looked at Gabe, and Deston, and her father. "I've only ever heard it with one voice."

Emrys saw Gabe look at her.

"One voice?"

"He sang it to me," said Alea. "Every night. When I was small. When I couldn't sleep." She looked at the fire. "All three parts. One after another. The low one, then the middle one, then his. Because he only had one voice and he wanted me to hear all of it." Her ears went back, a little. "That's how I knew you sang the middle part, Deston. He always sang the middle part *properly*. He said he had to, or you'd be cross."

Deston made a sound.

"It's the only song from his world," said Alea. "In this one. He never sang me anything else from there. Just that." She looked at her father. "I want to hear it the way it's supposed to be. With all three."

The fisherman looked at his daughter for a long time.

Then he looked at Gabe. And Gabe looked at Deston. And Deston nodded.

***

They didn't stand up. They didn't go anywhere. They stayed where they were, on their logs round the fire, the fisherman in the middle with Gabe on one side and Deston on the other. The girl with the creature on her head slid off his knee without a word and sat on the shingle at his feet.

Deston started.

He always started. Emrys had heard that on the roof, the one time. Low, a breath, just the first note of the middle part, so quiet you'd barely hear it, like someone testing whether ice would take his weight.

Gabe came in under him. The low part. The floor.

Emrys held his breath. He couldn't help it. A year and a half of carrying it, and his body knew exactly what came next. The two parts climbing together towards the place, towards the corner, towards the gap, and both of them going quiet, and the hole opening, and the long breath of nothing where a third voice should be.

They reached it.

And the fisherman came in.

***

Not too loud, this time.

That was what Emrys noticed first. On the jetty, four days ago, the stranger's voice had come across the water like a man shouting a name across a crowded market, too loud, too much, spilling over everything, because he'd been holding it in for thirty-four years and it all came out at once.

Not now.

Now it came in exactly where it should go, and exactly as loud as it should be. Rough. Low for a top part. Older than the others, much older, a voice that had been used in a lot of cold places. But *right*. Sitting on top of the other two the way a roof sits on a house. Not drowning them. Holding them.

Three parts.

Emrys had heard a thousand choirs. He'd sung in hundreds. He'd stood in the great chapel at Valcrest at midwinter with two hundred voices round him and felt the roof shake.

He'd never heard anything like three ordinary voices round a fire on a shingle beach, singing a song from another world, with nothing missing.

***

It went round the fire like warmth.

He watched it go. He couldn't help watching; it was what he did. He watched it reach each face. Rachael, with her hands over her mouth. Calvin, with the dented kettle in his lap, holding on to it. John, on his back on the shingle, looking straight up at the stars. Xav, very still, with his eyes shut. The girl with the creature on her head, at the fisherman's feet, with her face crumpled up and the creature patting her cheek with one soft tentacle. The quiet one, who had started to cry without moving any other part of her face at all.

And Thea. The one with the code. Emrys had barely spoken to her. She was sitting across the fire with her knees drawn up, and her lips were moving. Silently. On the melody. As if she'd heard it once, a long time ago, and had forgotten she knew it until now.

And Alea.

Alea was sitting beside her father with her eyes closed and her face turned up to the dark, and she was mouthing the words. All of them. Every one. The words to her father's part, the top part, the one he'd sung to her alone every night when she was small. She knew it better than anyone at that fire except the man singing it.

Something happened in Emrys's chest.

He didn't know what it was. He put his hand flat on his breastbone, the way you would if you'd felt your heart skip. And then he felt it.

Not in his chest. *Round* him. Between everyone. Thin bright lines, like threads of light, running from every person at the fire to every other person. From Gabe to the fisherman. From Deston to Gabe. From the fisherman to his daughter, a line so thick and bright it hurt to feel. From Rachael to Dennis to Calvin. From the quiet one to all four of her friends. Dozens of them. Criss-crossing over the fire, over the shingle, out to the townsfolk on the wall. And one of them, thin and faint and grey, from his own chest.

He'd felt this once before. In the Narrows. In the gorge, with the turned coming down the cliffs and the barge swinging toward the rocks, when Deston had written something in his window and every thread on the barge had lit at once.

*Listen.*

Deston wasn't doing it on purpose. Emrys could see that. Deston was singing, with his eyes shut, holding the middle. It had just happened. The way it happens in a choir, when the choir is good. When every voice can hear every other voice, and nobody has to look at the conductor.

And Emrys, sitting on a log at the edge of the light, could hear all of them.

***

He could hear the song from inside.

That was the only way he'd ever be able to say it, afterwards. He wasn't listening to it anymore. He was *in* it. He could hear Gabe's low part as if it were coming out of his own ribs. He could hear Deston's middle part as if it were his own breath. He could hear the fisherman's top part, rough and old and exactly right, as if it were the roof over his own head.

And above it, he could hear the space.

He'd never noticed it before. A year and a half of carrying the gap in the middle, and he'd never once noticed the space at the top. Above the third part. Above the roof. The way there's sky above a house. Not a hole. Not something missing. Just room. Air. The place where, in a song like this, in a choir like this, a fourth voice might go. If someone was there. If someone heard it.

He heard it.

He heard exactly where it went. He could feel the shape of it, in the air above the fire, the way you can feel the shape of a word on the tip of your tongue. A line of notes. High and thin and clear, running above everything else, like light on the top of water.

He opened his mouth.

He didn't decide to. He'd have stopped himself, if he'd thought about it. He'd tried to sing a hundred times in a year and a half and it had broken in his throat like a dry stick every time. He'd have been too frightened.

He didn't think about it.

He sang.

***

It came out.

That was all. It just came out. Not cracked. Not broken. Not a rasp, not wind in a bottle. Clear. Thin and high and clear and true, a little out of practice, a little rough at the edges, like a door that hasn't been opened in a long time. But *his*. His voice. The voice he'd had for forty-six years and lost on a night at midwinter, coming up out of his chest as if it had never been gone.

It went up above the three parts and sat there, in the space at the top, exactly where it should go.

He felt the whole fire turn and look at him.

He didn't stop. You never stopped, in the middle. He'd learned that at seven, in the choir in the hills, from a choirmaster with a stick. *You never stop in the middle. Whatever happens. The song isn't finished.* He sat on his log with his blanket round his shoulders and the tears running into his beard and sang the fourth part, the one he'd only just heard, the one that hadn't existed until a moment ago. Above Gabe's floor. Above Deston's breath. Above the fisherman's roof.

The fisherman had turned his head. He was looking straight at Emrys, across the fire, still singing. Emrys couldn't hold on to his face. Nobody could. But he didn't need to. He could hear the fisherman's voice. And the fisherman's voice, still singing, still holding the top of the house, had changed. Just slightly. Opened. The way a man's voice opens when he's singing and smiling at the same time.

*I think you just haven't heard the right thing yet.*

Emrys sang.

***

The light came on the second phrase.

He didn't call it. He didn't chant for it. He hadn't been able to chant for a year and a half; he'd forgotten, almost, what it felt like. He was just singing. But the song went out of him clear and true and full, the way a chant goes when it's right, and something came with it, the way light always came for him when he sang properly. He felt it gather in his chest and go out through his voice and up.

Above the fire, a light.

Small. The size of a candle flame. Warm and gold, floating in the air above the shingle, burning without burning.

Then another. Then another.

They rose off the song as he sang it. One for each phrase. Little gold lights, drifting up from the fire on the warm air and out over the shingle and over the end of the jetty and out across the black water of the lake, slowly, one after another, the way the candle-boats went down the river at Kingsferry at midwinter, the night he lost his voice.

He heard the townsfolk on the wall go quiet.

He heard the mayor say something, very low, and stop.

He didn't stop singing. He couldn't have. The lights went out across the water, dozens of them now, gold on black, and their reflections went with them on the still surface, so that there were two of every light, one above and one below, going out across the lake towards the far shore. The whole lake lit up with them. Like the sky had come down and lay on the water. Like every star had a twin.

And round the fire, one by one, everybody else started to sing.

***

Alea first. Out loud now, not mouthing. Her father's part, the top part, under her father's voice, so that it was doubled. A girl's voice, low and steady, singing her lullaby back to the man who'd sung it to her.

Then Thea, on the melody, a little shaky, getting stronger. The quiet one. The small one with the creature on her head, who clearly didn't know the words and was singing anyway, loudly, on something approximately like the tune. Rachael, laughing and crying at once, on Deston's part. Calvin with his kettle. John, on his back, singing straight up at the stars. RX, who'd appeared beside Emrys on the log without Emrys noticing, singing with his arm round Emrys's shoulders.

Not Xav. Xav sat with his eyes shut and listened. Emrys understood. Somebody had to.

Not Petra. Not Sabine. Not Maren. They didn't know it. They sat with their hands still and their faces turned to the lights going out over the water, and listened too.

And on the wall at the top of the shingle, the townsfolk. Who didn't know the words, or the tune, or what world it came from. Humming. Very softly. Whatever they could catch of it. Twenty people on a wall in the dark, humming a song from another world because a fisherman they'd lived beside for seven years was singing it at last.

Emrys held the top.

He held it all the way to the end. Above all of them. In the space where the fourth part went.

***

They finished it.

The last chord. All of it, every voice, held, the way you hold the end of a song at the end of a concert. Held. Held. Let go.

It went out over the lake after the lights.

Then it was quiet.

The lights hung over the water, gold, doubled, going slowly out. The fire cracked. Somebody on the wall blew their nose. A hen, somewhere up by the cabin, muttered in its sleep and settled.

Emrys sat on his log with his hand on his throat.

It didn't hurt. That was the thing. That was the thing he couldn't get past. He'd sung for the length of a song, full voice, holding a line he'd never heard before above three others and a fire full of people, and it didn't *hurt*. Not a scrape. Not a rasp. He swallowed, and it was just a throat, swallowing.

Rachael was in front of him.

He hadn't seen her move. She was kneeling on the shingle in front of his log, with her hands out, and her eyes were enormous.

"Can I," she said.

He nodded.

She put her fingers on his throat. Gently. The way she'd put them there in Kingsferry, a dozen times, all that year, trying to find what was wrong, and shaking her head every time, and saying *I can feel where it hurts, I just can't reach it, it's not in the meat of you, it's further in*. He felt her hands go warm. Felt her go quiet. Listening.

"It's closed," said Rachael.

She said it like a question.

"It's *closed*," she said again. "It's... I couldn't find it before. It was a hole. Right there. All year. Like something burned out. And it's..." She looked up at him. "It's just *gone*. It's healed. There isn't even a scar."

Emrys looked at her.

He opened his mouth.

"Thank you," he said.

Out loud. In his own voice. Clear as water.

Rachael burst into tears.

***

He looked across the fire at the fisherman.

The fisherman was looking back at him. Still sitting on his log, with Gabe's arm across his shoulders and his daughter against his side, very still. Watching him. And Emrys knew, without knowing how he knew, that the fisherman had heard every word.

He thought about the river road. The first week. A fire like this one, smaller, with five frightened strangers round it and a dark road behind them. The boy with the questions, Calvin, asking straight out: *Who's the Hero of the Redmarch? The one everybody talks about?*

And Emrys, who knew every song in the kingdom and every story, had said the only true thing he knew.

*A man who has earned his rest.*

He'd thought that was the end of the story. He'd thought that was the kindest thing you could say about anybody.

He'd been wrong. Rest wasn't the end. Rest was just the gap in the middle, where you waited. Where you left room. Where somebody, a long way off, held a door open for you without knowing whether you'd ever come through it.

He stood up.

He walked round the fire, past all of them, to where the fisherman was sitting. And he didn't kneel. He'd been told not to. He wouldn't do it again. He stood in front of the fisherman, and looked down at the face he couldn't hold on to, and said, in his own voice, the first thing he'd said in it in a year and a half that wasn't *thank you*:

"You were right."

"I know," said the fisherman. Quietly. "I'm sorry it took so long."

"It took exactly as long as it took." Emrys found that he was smiling. His beard was wet. He didn't care. "You came in exactly where you should. I just had to wait until I could hear where I went."

The fisherman looked up at him for a long moment.

Then he moved along the log, a little, and Gabe moved along too, and there was room. Just enough. Emrys sat down in it. Between the fisherman and the fire. Among them.

***

Later, much later, when the fire had gone to embers and the lights had all gone out over the lake and Group 1 had gone up the road in a sleepy, quarrelling knot with the townsfolk, Emrys sat on the end of the jetty with Deston and Gabe and the fisherman, and his voice, and watched the stars.

Deston had his window open. Emrys could see the faint light of it on his face. He was writing something. Emrys didn't ask what.

"It's only two words," said Deston, to nobody in particular. "Group 1 and Group 3 will know. Nobody else needs to."

"Go on, then," said Gabe.

Deston wrote it. Then he sat back, and waited, and after a minute he started to laugh. Quietly. Wetly.

"Yan's put three exclamation marks," he said. "Just that. Three. Mashi says *nice one. again*." He wiped his eyes. "Hako says he told everyone it'd work out. Gigichi says it's nearly midnight and we should all go to sleep. Maigo has written *WHAT* in capitals eleven times and then *OH* and then *I KNOW WHAT IT MEANS* and then a lot of crying." He laughed again. "Thea's put *Good.* With a full stop."

Beside Emrys, the fisherman had gone very still.

He didn't say anything. He didn't write anything; he never did, Emrys had been told, though nobody seemed to know quite why. He sat on the end of his jetty with his feet over the black water and looked out at the lake, at nothing, at the place where the last of the lights had gone out.

"What did you write?" said Emrys.

Deston closed his window.

"*Three parts*," he said.

Out on the lake, very far away, a fish jumped. The rings went out across the still water, slowly, one after another, wider and wider, until they reached the jetty and broke softly against the posts.

Emrys hummed. Very quietly. In his own voice. The fourth part, the top part, the one he'd only just found.

After a moment, without anybody deciding anything, the other three came in under him.
