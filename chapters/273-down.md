The floor of the east gate passage had a seam in it now, and Rhea had made it.

She stood over it in the grey before the morning drill with her sword on her back and her hands on her hips and looked at it, the way she'd looked at it every morning since the snow. Five flagstones, down the middle of the passage, from the foot of the post she cut at to the step where Yan sat in the evenings. Every one of them split. Not shattered; split, clean, the way ice goes on a pond, a single line through each one, and the lines joining up, flag to flag, so that it looked as if somebody had drawn a crack down the passage with a ruler.

Forty years, those flags. The quartermaster had said so, at a volume that brought the commander down the stair. Laid when the fort was laid; nobody this side of Ashby could cut new ones to match. And Rhea had put a seam down the middle of them in fifteen nights, with a practice sword, cutting at a post.

*Leave her*, the commander had said.

She'd have liked it better if he'd shouted.

***

It went down. That was the problem. That had always been the problem, and for three years it hadn't been a problem at all; it had been the thing she was best at.

*Aura goes into whatever you trust*. Aldous had said it in the yard in the first week the summoned came, to five strangers with snakes and antlers and a boy who wanted to do both paths, and Rhea had been standing at the back with her arms folded thinking *obviously*. She'd known it since she was nine. You trusted your sword, so it went into the sword. You trusted your feet, so it went into your feet. And if you were very good, if you'd stood on the same ground long enough to know it, you could let it go all the way down, through your boots, into the stones, and the stones gave it back to you a hair harder than you'd given it, and your stroke landed like a dropped bell.

She'd held the Black Blade one count like that. In the yard, on the eleventh night of his dinners, across a barrel-head with their elbows on it, because she'd challenged him to arm-wrestle the way she challenged everybody; and she'd let it go down, through her elbow into the barrel and through the barrel into the packed red earth of the yard, and the yard had held him. Not her arm. The yard. One count. She'd spent the whole of the next winter finding out what she'd done, and the summer after that cutting at a post with both hands, slow, letting it go down, and down, a little further every time, until Yan sitting on the step said he could see her light going into the floor *like water, and stopping there, at the bottom of something*.

And then in the autumn, the night the thing at Thornwall came down the pass, it hadn't stopped.

It had gone down, and down, a hand into the flags, and through; and the flag in front of her right boot had split from side to side with a sound like a branch going in a frost. And the next night, and the next. Five flags. A seam. And Yan, who never lied to her, sitting on the step with his staff across his knees and his white snake gone stiff round his neck, saying he didn't know whether it was her or the cold under the grave, an hour east. Because it happened at the same minute, every time. The thing at Thornwall moved, and the cold under the knoll lifted its head, and Rhea's light went through the floor.

*Is it me*, she'd said, on the twelfth night, on the knoll. *Or is it that.*

*I don't know.*

*Then it's me.*

She'd meant it. She'd decided, standing at the edge of the knoll in the dark with her sword on, facing east. Whatever was under that grave, it wasn't getting the credit. If her light had gone through the floor, it was because she'd put it there; and if she'd put it there, she could learn to do it on purpose; and then she'd find out what *through* was for.

That had been a month ago.

She'd found out what *through* was for. It was for breaking floors.

***

Petra had told her. In the summer, on the red, the day after the ditch, when Rhea had stood twenty paces off and watched a guard her own age walk out of a hole in the ground past the Black Blade and stand in front of him and make a wall the width of the ditch out of nothing, and had gone and found her afterwards by the horse lines and asked her how. Straight out. Rhea didn't know how to ask things any other way.

*I stood in front of him*, Petra had said.

That was all. Petra didn't explain things. But Rhea had walked back to the fort that night along the burial track turning it over and over, and somewhere around the third marker she'd understood it, and it had gone through her like cold water. She'd spent three years going *down*. Into the stones. Into the ground. Into whatever she trusted. And Reaching wasn't down. Reaching was the edge cutting past the steel; it was the stroke going on after the sword stopped, out there, a hand past the point, into the air in front of you. Reaching was *out*.

Petra's wall had gone out. In front of her. Into a place with nothing in it to trust at all.

And Rhea's had gone down. And when it went down far enough, it had come out the bottom, into the floor.

*So bring it up*, she'd thought. A month ago, standing over the first crack. *If down comes out the bottom, stop going down. Bring it up. Hold it up, in the sword, and push it out the front.*

It was a good theory. It was the first theory she'd ever had in her life that wasn't *hit it harder*, and she'd been proud of it, privately, for about a week.

***

The morning drill was on the wall walk above the east gate, because the yard was iron and the wall walk was at least swept.

Rhea ran it. She always had. The autumn draft had come in from Ashby and the hill farms in the last weeks before the snow, forty of them, raw as turnips, and the commander had given them to her the way you'd give a dog a bone, to keep her busy; and she'd done to them what she did to everybody, which was make it a contest. Who could hold the low guard longest. Who could cut the post twenty times without the edge wandering. Who could last against her for a count of ten. They'd hated her for a week and loved her for a month and now they came up the wall stair every morning in the dark shoving each other to be first, which was exactly how she liked them.

"Pairs," she said. "Practice swords. First one down buys the beer at the thaw."

They paired. She walked the line. She did it every morning, and every morning she felt it, the way you'd feel a tooth you'd stopped trusting: the thing she was doing now, since the theory. Holding it up.

It was like standing on ice.

That was the only way she could describe it, and she'd tried to Yan, and to Salty, and once to Wendy, who'd looked at her the way Wendy looked at most things and said *that sounds unpleasant* and gone back to counting. All her life there'd been a floor under her aura. She'd never thought about it, any more than you think about the floor under your boots. You drew it up from the bottom of yourself and it went into your sword and through your sword and the bit that was left over went down, into the ground, and the ground held it. It was where the leftover went. It was what made a stroke heavy.

And now she wasn't letting it go down. She was holding all of it up, in the sword, the way you'd hold a full bucket at arm's length so as not to slop it, and pushing at the front, and nothing was coming out of the front, and nothing was going down, and there was no floor. There was just her, holding a bucket, on ice.

"Ma'am," said one of the recruits. "You want a go?"

It was the tall one. Tobin Ferris, from a hill farm two days south, seventeen, all wrists and elbows, who'd come in with the draft carrying his father's old sword in a sack and had never once in five weeks won a count of ten against anybody. He was grinning. They all grinned at her now, a bit; that was the month of loving her.

"You?" said Rhea.

"Count of ten, ma'am."

"Count of ten," said Rhea, and took a practice sword off the rack, because it was a contest, and she'd never in her life said no to one.

She held it up. She couldn't stop herself; she'd been doing it for a month and it was a habit now, the way the other had been a habit for twenty years. The aura in the sword and none of it going down. She came at Tobin Ferris the way she'd have come at anybody, low and fast and heavy.

Only it wasn't heavy.

She felt it go wrong the instant the blades met. There was nothing under it. She'd struck a thousand strokes in her life that had the whole of the fort under them, the stones and the wall and the red under the wall, and this one had nothing. It was a stroke made of arm. Tobin Ferris, who'd been bracing for a stroke made of the whole fort, took it on his practice blade and it *didn't move him*, and his face did a thing of astonishment; and then, because he was seventeen and had been taught for five weeks by a woman who made everything a contest, he shoved.

It wasn't a good shove. It was a farm boy's shove, the kind you'd give a heifer that had stepped on your foot. It came in under her guard with his shoulder behind it, and she went to meet it the way she'd met every shove of her life, by letting it go down; and there was nowhere for it to go. She was standing on ice. She'd made it ice.

Her feet went out from under her on the swept stones of the wall walk, and she went down on her back on the frost so hard it knocked the breath out of her, with her practice sword skittering away along the wall and the grey sky over her and forty recruits standing very, very still.

Tobin Ferris stood over her with his practice blade in his hand and his mouth open.

"Ma'am," he said. "Ma'am, I didn't— are you—"

She lay there.

It didn't hurt. Not much. She'd been knocked down by better than a farm boy; she'd had her arm broken on the knoll last spring by a thing out of Kharvos with too many joints. It wasn't the fall. It was that it hadn't been anything. It had been a boy pushing a woman who'd forgotten how to stand.

"Count of ten," she said, to the sky. "That was about four."

Somebody laughed, nervously. Then a few more. Then all of them, because she was lying on her back on the wall walk saying *that was about four*, and it was funny, it was the funniest thing that had happened on that wall all winter; and she laughed too, loudly, the way she laughed at everything, and got up, and slapped Tobin Ferris on the shoulder so hard he staggered, and told him he'd bought the beer.

Then she sent them all down early, and stood on the wall walk on her own with her hands on the coping, and didn't laugh.

***

Old Oakes re-laid the flags that afternoon.

The quartermaster hadn't asked him. Nobody had. Oakes had been the fort's smith since before the war, before the last war, before anybody could remember; an old grey man with a bad hip and a forge in the corner of the yard where he kept a fire banked day and night for whoever needed it. He came into the east gate passage after the noon bell with a barrow and a crowbar and a bucket of sand, and five flagstones on the barrow, grey and worn and old, that he'd dug up from somewhere, and he didn't say anything to Rhea, who was sitting on the step where Yan usually sat. He just started.

He levered up the first cracked flag with the crowbar, groaning. It came up in two halves. He set them against the wall.

"Where'd you get those?" said Rhea.

"Under my floor." He didn't look up. "Old forge floor. Laid same year as these. Been under my anvil forty year." He bedded the first of them in the sand, and tapped it down with the heel of his hand, and knelt there looking at it, with his bad hip out to the side. "Not a mason this side of Ashby can cut you new ones. Quartermaster's right." He looked at the four cracked flags still in the floor. "So I'm giving you mine."

"You didn't have to."

"No," said Oakes. He levered up the second. "Forty year these held a fort. Wagons. Horses. The whole garrison coming in off the red at a run, the last morning of the war; I stood in this passage and watched them come, and not one of these so much as rang." He set the halves against the wall. "Four nights of you."

"Fifteen."

"Four that cracked." He bedded the second flag. He was slow. He was very old. "What did you do to 'em?"

She told him. She didn't know why; Oakes had never once in three years asked her a question that wasn't *where's my good tongs*. She told him about down, and through, and the floor, and the theory, and the ice, and Tobin Ferris and the count of four, and he knelt in the passage with his crowbar across his knees and his face doing nothing at all, and listened.

When she'd finished he didn't say anything for a long time.

"Huh," he said at last. "Floor's not for trusting."

"What?"

"A floor." He tapped the new flag with his knuckle. "It's not for trusting. It's for walking on. You trust a thing, you lean on it. You don't lean on a floor. You stand on it." He got up, slowly, with the crowbar, the way old men got up. "That's what I'd say. But I'm a smith. What do I know."

He went to lever up the third.

Rhea sat on the step and watched him, and didn't understand it, and was angry that she didn't. She'd always understood things by doing them. You didn't explain a sword. You swung it. And here was an old man with a bad hip telling her a floor wasn't for trusting, as if that meant something, and she'd spent twenty years trusting floors and it had been the best thing she had.

"Everything breaks," she said. "If you lean on it hard enough."

"Aye," said Oakes. He bedded the third flag. "Most things."

***

She tested it that night. She had to know.

She'd told Yan *then it's me*, a month ago, on the knoll, and meant it; but meaning it wasn't knowing it, and she was a soldier, and you didn't go into the next thing without knowing what the last thing had been. Yan said the cold under the grave had gone down on the last dawn, the morning the black iron rang by itself; further down than anything had ever put it, past where the spike reached. *Asleep*, he said. *A long way down. Nothing lifts its head.* He'd sat on the knoll every evening since and felt it lying there like a stone at the bottom of a well.

So if it was the cold that had pulled her through the floor, it couldn't now.

And if it was her, it would.

She went down to the passage after supper, when the fort was quiet and the step was empty, with a practice sword. Oakes's five old flags lay in a row down the middle of the floor, bedded in sand, a shade darker than the ones round them. The post stood at the end. Snow on the sill of the gate, and the lamp in its bracket, and the cold coming in under the gate like water.

She stood in front of the post. Both hands. Slow.

And she let it go down.

It was the hardest thing she'd ever done, which was ridiculous; it was the thing she'd done ten thousand times without thinking. But she'd spent a month holding it up, standing on ice, and her whole body had learned to be afraid of the floor. She stood there with the practice sword up and her aura held in it, hard, like a breath, and made herself let it go. Down. Through her arms, through her body, through her boots, the way water goes out of a bucket when you tip it.

Into Oakes's old flag. Into his forty years under an anvil.

Down.

She brought the sword down on the post.

The flag in front of her right boot split from side to side with a sound like a branch going in a frost.

She stood there.

The fort was quiet. The cold under the grave, an hour east, was asleep at the bottom of its well; Yan had said so at supper, and Yan didn't lie. Nothing at Thornwall was moving; the channel had said so at dusk, *23. same.*, and a boy with lights had put a picture of the valley in ten windows that would go out if anything so much as turned its head. Nothing anywhere had lifted its head tonight.

And Oakes's flag had cracked under her.

*Then it's me*.

It was. It had always been. And she'd broken an old man's floor to find out.

She put the practice sword back on its rack very carefully, and went and sat on the step, and put her face in her hands.

***

Yan found her there an hour later. He'd been on the knoll with Salty; he came in at the east gate with his staff and his coat white with snow and Suzu down inside his collar for the cold, and saw her, and saw the floor, and stopped.

He didn't say anything. He looked at the new crack for a long time. Then he sat down on the step beside her, not close, the way Yan sat beside people.

"It was me," said Rhea, through her hands.

"I know."

"You can *see* that?"

"I could see it going down." He was quiet. "Like before. Like water. Only it didn't stop at the bottom, it went through." He turned his staff over on his knees. "It was always you, I think. The cold was just loud at the same time. Two loud things at once. I couldn't tell which was which." He looked at the floor. "I'm sorry. I should have—"

"You said you didn't know. You didn't know." She took her hands off her face. "That's not your fault. That's just true."

They sat there.

"I'm floating," said Rhea, after a while. "All day. Since the theory. I've been holding it up so it wouldn't go through, and now there's nothing under it, and I'm *floating*. A boy from a hill farm put me on my back this morning with a shove you'd give a cow." She laughed, short, not loud for once. "And the only way I know how to stand is the way that breaks things."

Yan didn't say anything for a while.

"Suzu says you're like a bird that's forgotten it can land," he said. "Up there, flapping."

"Tell Suzu thank you very much."

"She says it nicely." He considered. "For her."

***

Aldous was in the chapel. He usually was, at that hour; he kept the lamps there himself, a slow quiet man with a Bonfire mage's hands, who'd been chaplain to the Redmarch garrison through the whole of the last war and buried more people than anybody alive. Rhea went in without knocking and stood at the back by the door with snow melting on her boots.

"Aura goes into whatever you trust," she said.

He looked up from the lamp he was trimming. "So I've said."

"I trusted the floor. Twenty years. And I broke it." She told him. All of it, the way she'd told Oakes, faster. "So what do I trust now? Not the floor. It breaks. Not my sword; my sword's just arm, without the floor, a boy shoved straight through it. Not *up*." She spread her hands. "Everything breaks, if you put enough into it. So what do you put it into, if you've got more than anything can hold?"

Aldous trimmed the wick. He took his time. He always did; it was the thing about him that had driven her mad for three years, and was driving her mad now.

"I don't know," he said at last.

"You're the *chaplain*."

"I'm a mage," said Aldous mildly. "I've never once put aura into anything in my life. I just say what the old swordsmen said to me." He put the lamp down. "But I've watched a great many of them. And the ones who got past where you are didn't find something that wouldn't break." He looked at her. "They found something that had already been broken. Over and over. And was still there."

Rhea stood by the door.

"That's a riddle," she said.

"It's an old man in a chapel at the end of a long day," said Aldous, "who doesn't know the answer, and is trying not to say so." He picked up the next lamp. "Go to bed, Rhea."

***

She didn't go to bed.

She went up onto the east wall, because it was where she went when she didn't know what to do, and had been since the first week she came here, with five strangers and a chaplain and a sword she was too proud of and no idea what the red was. The wall walk was empty except for the sentries at the towers, who knew her and nodded and didn't come over. The snow had stopped. There was no moon. The red lay out under the stars white from the foot of the wall to the edge of the world, flat and silent, with the markers standing up out of it in their long rows like masts, and the burial track going out between them toward a knoll an hour off where a black iron headstone stood over a grave with a cold thing asleep at the bottom of it.

She put her hands flat on the coping. Frozen stone, rough and old, with frost in its pits.

She didn't trust it. That was the thing. She'd have to, to stand on it, to fight on it; she'd fought on this wall a dozen times, against probes out of Kharvos in the spring with the hunters coming up the face in the dark. But she didn't trust it the way she'd trusted the floor, or the yard, or her own two feet. She couldn't. Her body had learned in a month that the thing under you breaks if you put yourself into it, and her body didn't care how many times she told it otherwise.

*Floor's not for trusting. It's for walking on.*

*They found something that had already been broken.*

She stood with her hands on the coping and looked east, out over the red, toward a country she'd never seen except as a line of black hills on the far side of the white, where the things came from.

The wall went down a long way under her hands. She'd never thought about it before. It went down past the wall walk and the guardrooms and the gate passage with its seam; past the foundations; into the red. She didn't know how far. Nobody did. It had been here before the fort, Oakes said once, the bottom courses, older than the kingdom; the fort had just been built on top. It had been hit by everything Kharvos had, for longer than anybody had kept count. There were stones in it she could see from here that had been cracked clean through, by rams and by hunters and by whatever had come over the red in the long war, and patched, and cracked again, and patched again, with newer stone mortared into the old in seams and scars all the way down the face, so that the wall in the starlight looked like an old man's hands.

Broken. Over and over.

Still there.

She didn't feel anything. She was honest with herself about that, later. She stood on the east wall of Cinderhold in the snow with her hands flat on the coping and her aura held up in her, hard, floating, the way it had been all month, and she didn't let it go down, because she was afraid to; and nothing happened. No bell. No floor coming up to meet her. Just cold stone under her hands, and the stars, and the red.

But she stood there a long time. Longer than she'd meant to. Until the sentry at the north tower changed and the new one looked along the wall at her and decided not to come over either.

And when she took her hands off the coping at last, and went down the stair to her bed, she found that for the first time in a month she'd been standing on something without thinking about it. For about the length of a count of ten.

She didn't know what that meant. She'd find out.

Not next year, she thought, on the stair. She'd said *next year* to Yan, on the knoll, in the autumn. Next year was too long.

This winter.
