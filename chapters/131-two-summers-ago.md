Lucan had been rehearsing the verb for six months.

Not the speech. The speech was easy. He'd made a hundred speeches on that sand, fifteen midsummers' worth, in a red cloak with his arms out: speeches about champions and lances and the glory of the Lists and the generosity of whichever lord had paid for the prizes that year. He could make a speech in his sleep. He sometimes had.

It was the verb.

*Two summers ago, in a hall in Solmere, thirty-five people*—and then what? *Arrived*? They hadn't arrived. You arrived somewhere you'd meant to go. *Came*? Same problem. *Were summoned*? That was the official word, the Advisor's word, the word on every proclamation; and Lucan had stood on that sand two summers ago and heard a crowd shout *demon* at a girl with horns, and he'd known, watching her face do nothing at all, that *summoned* was a word for something you did to a dog.

He'd tried them all. In the winter, in the rain, on his own, in the drained and empty Lists, walking up and down the sand saying the sentence to the stands. *Arrived*. *Came*. *Were called*. *Appeared*. *Fell*. The pigeons had heard every one.

And then one wet afternoon he'd said *were brought*, and stopped, and stood very still in the middle of the sand with the rain coming down on him, and wept.

Because that was it. That was the true one. Nobody had asked them. Nobody had let them choose. They'd been picked up out of their own lives by somebody else's hand and carried a world away and set down in a hall in front of strangers, and told to save a kingdom that wasn't theirs. They hadn't come. They'd been *brought*.

And they'd stayed anyway.

He'd written it on a card. He had it in his sleeve now. He didn't need the card. He'd said that sentence to the pigeons four hundred times.

***

The Lists filled from the first hour.

Lucan had seen it fill fifteen times before, from the master's platform, and it never once stopped making his chest go tight. It started as a trickle at the gates at dawn: the keen ones, the ones who wanted the front tier, families with baskets and children on shoulders. Then a stream. Then a flood, so that by the second hour every gate was a river and the stands were going up dark with people, tier on tier on tier, a whole city and half a kingdom pouring into a bowl of pale stone until it brimmed.

Forty thousand, the steward said. Lucan thought more.

And the paper.

That was new. That had never happened in fifteen years. Every second person coming in through the gates was wearing something made of paper. Horns, mostly. Little paper crowns of black horns, with a white mask stitched on the front, the way they'd started doing last summer after a boy stood up on a bench. But not only horns. Antlers with paper leaves. Cat ears with little crowns. Paper squids on hats, waving paper arms. Paper penguins on children's shoulders. Moths. Bees. Kettles, which Lucan was still very proud of. Thirty-five kinds of paper, from the warehouse on the river where he'd had the paper-makers working since the winter, sold at every gate for a copper apiece, and he'd sold out by the second hour.

And in every hand, it seemed to Lucan, up on the master's platform, looking out over the whole roaring bowl of it, a paper fishing rod.

Nobody knew what they were for. People kept asking him. *Lucan, what are the fishing rods? Lucan, why fishing rods? Lucan, are they for the Hero?* And he'd said *just in case*, every time, and smiled, and walked off, and nobody had ever got anything else out of him, because there wasn't anything else. He didn't know either.

A girl with a ragebait voice had asked him for them, in the winter. *For the Hero. Just in case*. And he'd thought it was the most beautiful idea anyone had ever had, and ordered ten thousand, and never once asked what she meant.

He was a showman. Showmen didn't ask what a thing meant. They asked whether people would hold it up.

***

He looked for the water-carrier in the stands.

He hadn't meant to. He'd told himself, in the night, lying awake at the Lance with the inn full of the summoned asleep round him on benches, that he wasn't going to. It wasn't his business. Newish had said *the water-carrier* in the kitchen passage, with her face perfectly straight, and Lucan had said *of course he is*, and that had been the end of it.

He looked anyway. It took him a long time to find him. Forty thousand people.

He found him by the summoned.

That was how. Lucan had been watching the thirty-five all week, the way he'd watched champions for fifteen years: where they stood, who they looked at, how they moved in a room. And all week, every single one of them, every time anything happened, had looked at the same place first. Before they looked at the bracket, or the king, or each other. The way a team looks at its captain before the bell.

They were down in the tunnel now, under the east stand, waiting to walk out. But the guides and guards weren't. And Lucan saw Maren, on the fourth tier of the east stand near the royal box, turn her head and look at something three places along. And Kestra, on the rail above her, look at the same thing. And a fox girl with a sword the size of a door, sitting on the bottom tier waiting for her bout at the third hour, turn round on her bench and look up at it, and then turn back.

A man. On the fourth tier. On the end of a bench, with a fishing rod across his knees.

Nobody near him looked at him. Lucan watched for a long time. The people on either side of him were talking and pointing and eating and waving paper horns and not one of them, in all that time, so much as glanced at him. As if he weren't there. As if he were a gap in the crowd that their eyes went round.

Lucan looked at him and found that his own eyes went round too.

He couldn't see the man's face. He tried. He'd spent fifteen years on that platform reading faces at a hundred yards, the champions' and the crowd's, and he couldn't see this one. His eyes went to it and slid off, like water off a cloak, and he found himself looking at the rod instead. At the man's hands on it. At two small round yellow things asleep on the man's head, which were Bee's.

He stood on the master's platform with forty thousand people roaring round him and looked at a man nobody could see, holding a fishing rod, in a stand full of ten thousand paper fishing rods.

*Just in case*, he thought.

And then he made himself look away, because it wasn't his business, and he was a showman, and the bell was about to go.

***

The bell went at the end of the second hour.

It was a great bronze thing in the tower over the west gate, older than the Lists, and when it rang the whole bowl went quiet in a wave, from the top tier down, forty thousand people stopping talking one row at a time until the only sound in the Lists was the bell itself, rolling out over the sand and the stands and the city, and the wind in the paper.

The king was in the royal box.

He'd come in quietly, without trumpets, the way he did everything. A big grey man in a plain dark coat that might have been a smith's Sunday best, sitting in the centre seat of the box with his hands on his knees, and the Advisor beside him, thin and grey, and Kestra on the rail behind. Lucan had seen that box hold a dozen lords and their ladies and their wine. He'd never seen it hold a king who looked like he'd rather be sitting in the stands.

Drew was in the master's chair.

He'd set it on the platform himself, at dawn, beside Lucan's old place. It rocked very slightly on its short leg. He was sitting in it now with his arms on its pale-worn arms and his 88EXP nameplate catching the sun, perfectly calm, the way he'd been calm all week, looking out at the bowl of the Lists as if it were a lobby that had just finished loading.

He turned his head and looked at Lucan.

"Your speech," said Drew.

"It's your Lists."

"It's your speech," said Drew. "You've been saying it to pigeons since the winter. Tiger told me." He smiled. "I'm running it. You open it. That's the deal."

Lucan looked at him.

Then he stepped forward, to the front of the master's platform, to the rail, where he'd stood fifteen midsummers in a row, and put his hands on it. The red cloak. The gold on the hem. Forty thousand faces, and a king, and a man nobody could see.

He didn't take the card out of his sleeve.

***

"Two summers ago," said Lucan.

His voice went out over the Lists. He'd learned that in his first year: how to stand and breathe so that a voice went all the way to the top tier without shouting. You didn't push it. You let it go, like a kite.

"Two summers ago, in a hall in Solmere, thirty-five people were brought."

He let the verb sit. He felt it go out over the bowl and land.

"Not *came*. Not *arrived*. Not *summoned*, though that's the word on the proclamations, and some of you have heard it so often you think it's a name." He looked round the stands. "*Brought*. Picked up out of their own lives. Out of their homes, and their families, and their own beds, in the middle of the night, in a world none of us will ever see. Without being asked. Without being told. And set down in a hall full of soldiers with their hands on their swords, and told that the kingdom needed them."

The Lists were very quiet.

"Two summers ago," said Lucan, "this city stood on this sand and looked at two of them, and shouted *demon*."

He let that sit too. He didn't look at anyone in particular. He didn't need to. Half the city had been there.

"They didn't have to stay," said Lucan. "Nobody's chained them. They've had two years. They could have walked into the hills. They could have sat in their rooms. They could have said *it isn't ours, it was never ours, you brought us, you fix it*." His voice was beginning to go. He let it. It always went; the crowd liked it when it went. "And instead they went where they were sent. To the edges. To the red, and the mountains, and the river, and the north. And they trained. And they bled. And one of them stood on this sand last summer and wrote *catch* in the air in fire, and the thing that had come for his friend died."

There was a sound from somewhere high in the east stand. A small sound. A child.

"This is the Tournament of the Thirty-Five," said Lucan. "Not because they're the strongest. They're not. One of my old champions would beat any of them on any morning. Not because we need to know what they're worth. We know what they're worth." He took a breath. "Because for two years they've been scattered across a kingdom in fives, and they've never once been in one place, and this morning, for the first time since a hall in Solmere, they are."

He turned. He put out his arm toward the great dark mouth of the tunnel under the east stand.

"Valcrest," said Lucan. "*The Thirty-Five*."

***

They walked out in squads.

Drew had insisted on that. Not in groups, the way they'd been sorted in the hall. In the squads he'd drawn, five of seven, one from every posting, sideways. So that nobody walked out beside anyone they'd come with.

Squad A first. The Kettle. A man with crimson hair and a slow easy walk at the front, a big man behind him, a boy with a spear, a girl with a gold watch, a man with a green snake, an archer with lavender headphones round her neck, and a cheerful man carrying an actual kettle over his head like a trophy. The crowd didn't know what the kettle was for. It roared anyway.

The Stronghold. A tiger boy who ran out in front of it and turned a full circle on the sand with his arms up. A penguin girl with a penguin. A short broad man with a king's horseshoe in his belt. The biggest of the thirty-five, a man with forge-black hands, walking like a wall. A cat. A thin quick one with daggers.

And an oni.

He walked out last of the Stronghold, with his hands behind his back, in his long indigo coat with the wisteria on the hem and the red cords hanging from his shoulder. He had the mask on.

The Committee: seven people who all, visibly, from the master's platform, wanted to be the one walking in front, and had settled it by walking out in a line abreast like a row of generals, a soul mage with a scythe and a white snake, a cow girl with a scope in her eye, a tall man, a quiet one, a pink-and-violet-haired girl looking at everything, a cat princess, and Chris with his notebook.

Wolf. A bee girl with a chalk dog trotting beside her that had come off a wall in the tunnel. A moth girl. A fox with a hawk. A walking tree with a crown of summer leaves, waving. A small dry woman with her sleeves rolled. A man with a notebook of contingencies.

And a girl with horns.

Winter walked out in the middle of Wolf, very straight, with her long white hair down her back and her two black horns and her face doing nothing at all.

Except Newish came last. Under a banner. Dennis had got a banner made, overnight, out of his own pocket, which meant he was planning to make it back somehow: a long strip of white cloth on two poles that said, in enormous black letters, EXCEPT NEWISH. Nobody in the crowd could read it. The squad walked out under it anyway: a girl with an axolotl's frills and a squid on her shoulder, a man with blue ink at his throat, a thin chill man with his hands in his pockets, a man in a very good coat waving like royalty, a man with lights going round his head, Drew's own place empty because Drew was in the master's chair, and Newish, at the front, under her own name, with her eyebrows up.

Thirty-five. On the sand. In five squads of seven, sideways.

And the Lists roared.

***

It came from the north stand.

Lucan heard it start. He'd heard it start before. Two summers ago, from every stand at once. It was smaller now. One section, high on the north side, where the visitors sat, the ones who'd come in from the far provinces for the Lists and never seen the summoned, and had heard about them only in rumours. A knot of liveried men. Some lord's retinue, in grey and blue. Lucan didn't know whose. Somebody's.

*Demon*.

One voice. Then three. Then a dozen.

*Demon*. *Demon*. *Get them off the sand*.

It went out across the Lists. Not loud, against forty thousand. But it went. Lucan heard it go. He saw it land on the sand, on Winter in the middle of Wolf, who didn't move, whose face did absolutely nothing; and on Ina at the back of the Stronghold, with the mask on, who stood very still with his hands behind his back.

He'd heard this before. He'd stood on this platform two summers ago and heard a whole stand shout it at a girl with horns, and he'd done his best, and it hadn't been enough, and he'd gone home that night and wept for an hour.

He opened his mouth.

He didn't need to.

***

On the front tier of the east stand, right at the rail, a small boy stood up on his bench.

He was about eight. He had a patched shirt, and a battered old cavalry helmet on the bench beside him that Lucan had very pointedly not asked for back, and on his head a paper crown of horns. Old horns, from last summer, soft and creased, with a little white mask stitched on the front.

Wil took the horns off his head.

And held them up. Over his head. In both hands. As high as he could reach.

He didn't say anything. He just stood there on the front tier of the east stand, under the master's platform, with his paper horns held up over his head, facing the north stand.

And the east stand stood up.

Lucan watched it happen. He'd seen it once before, last summer, on a night when something had come over the stands for Winter and Ina had written *catch* in fire. He'd never thought he'd see it again. It went out from the boy in a wave: the people round him first, standing up on the benches, taking off their own paper horns and holding them up. Then the tier behind. Then the tier behind that. Then the whole east stand, all the way to the top. Then the south. Then the west.

Paper horns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, held up in silence over forty thousand heads, in the morning sun, turned toward the north stand. And paper antlers, and paper squids, and paper bees and moths and kettles, and between them, everywhere, paper fishing rods, held up too, because people had them in their hands and didn't know what else to do.

The north stand stopped.

The knot of liveried men in grey and blue stood in the middle of it, very small, looking round them at a bowl of forty thousand people standing up in silence holding paper over their heads. Their voices had gone. One of them sat down. Then another. After a while they all sat down, and somebody in the seats behind them, a farmer, by the look of him, in from the plain, reached forward without a word and put a paper crown of horns on the head of the man in front.

The man didn't take it off.

***

On the sand, Winter's face did something.

Lucan saw it from the platform. Very small. The corner of her mouth. And then she turned her head and looked across the sand, past the Committee and the Kettle, to the back of the Stronghold, where an oni stood in a mask.

Ina reached up, slowly, and pushed the mask to the side of his head.

He was smiling. His own fanged grin. He looked up at the east stand, at the front tier, at a small boy in a patched shirt standing on a bench with his paper horns held up over his head. And he took his hands from behind his back and lifted one, gloved, and put the tip of his finger to the air in front of him, and wrote one word, very small, in violet fire.

*Thanks*.

It hung there for a moment over the sand, small and wisteria-coloured and burning. Then it went out.

Wil burst into tears. So did Lucan.

***

Emrys sang, after.

Lucan had asked him to. By letter, weeks ago, to a lake in the north, when nobody yet knew for certain who'd be coming south. *If you come, sing at the opening. Anything. Whatever you like.* And Emrys had written back one line: *I'll come*.

He came out on the sand alone, a thin pale man with a lute on his back, Group 4's guide, the man who knew every song in the kingdom. He didn't use the lute. He stood in the middle of the sand, between the five squads, in the silence after the paper, and lifted his face to the stands, and sang.

Lucan didn't know the song. He knew every song in Valcrest and he didn't know this one. It was very old. It had no words that he could catch, or perhaps it had words in a language nobody spoke any more, the way some of the oldest midsummer songs did. It was slow and plain and it went up at the end of every line, like a question, and then came down again, like an answer. Emrys's voice went up into the bowl of the Lists the way Lucan's had, without pushing, letting it go like a kite, and went all the way to the top tier, and further, out over the city, to wherever songs go.

When it ended, nobody clapped. Nobody moved. Forty thousand people stood in the Lists with paper still in their hands, in silence, for a long time.

Lucan found that he was looking at the fourth tier.

The man with the rod hadn't stood up when the stands stood up. He'd been the only one. He'd sat on the end of his bench with the rod across his knees and two bees on his head while every person round him rose, and he'd stayed sitting, very still, the way a stone stays still when a river goes over it. Lucan had seen him not move.

He hadn't moved during the song either. But at the end of it, when it came down on its last line like an answer, Lucan saw him put his hand over his face.

Nobody near him noticed. Nobody ever did.

Lucan looked away. It wasn't his business. He wiped his eyes on his red sleeve, and went back to the master's chair, where Drew was sitting very still, and said, in a voice he barely recognised as his own:

"The Fledglings."

***

The Fledgling Lists went on four rings.

Lucan had run them for nine years before he handed them to his deputy, and he'd run them this year himself, because he'd wanted to; because of a notice in a saddlebag and a note in his own hand at the bottom of it, *I want them ALL*. And he'd got them all. A hundred and forty, under twenty, any tier, any path, upon a guild mark. Cadets from the military schools in their grey. Guild children with copper marks and borrowed swords. Noble boys with private masters standing behind them like shadows. A coast girl with two boat-hooks, who'd won her first bout before Lucan had finished reading her name.

Four rings on the sand, chalked, side by side, under the east stand. Seventy bouts in the first round. The crowd watching all four at once, the way a crowd at the Lists learned to: shouting at one, groaning at another, standing up for a third.

At the third hour, on the near ring, right under the master's platform, Lucan read out the next names from the roll himself.

He hadn't meant to. He had a herald for it. But he'd seen the name on the roll at breakfast, in Maren's guild hand, and the box beside it, and he'd taken the roll off the herald without a word.

"In the near ring," said Lucan. His voice went out over the sand. "Edmund Halloway, of Solmere. Son of Lord Halloway of the Council. Iron. Aura. Drawn. Pupil of Ser Garret Holm, three times champion of these Lists."

A tall boy of eighteen came out onto the near ring, in good boots and a better coat, with a sword that had cost more than most of the families in the east stand earned in a year. Behind him, at the edge of the ring, a broad grey man in a champion's sash folded his arms. Ser Garret. Lucan had crowned him twice himself, in his first years on the platform. He had a very good face for a champion. He was looking at his pupil the way a man looks at a horse he isn't sure about.

The boy was standing on his heels.

Lucan saw it from the platform. He'd seen it on a hundred boys. The good boots, the good sword, the good master, and the weight all the way back on the heels, the way a boy stands when he's been told all his life he's going to win.

"And against him," said Lucan, and looked down at the roll.

*Alea, of Wrenhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered. Sponsor: Maren, guild guide.*

*Father:* and then nothing. A box, left blank.

Lucan had seen blanks before. Plenty of fledglings had blanks in that box: orphans, foundlings, guild children who didn't know, or didn't want to say. But they were never this blank. They were scratched out, or smudged, or had *unknown* written in them by a clerk. This one had been left. Clean. A space in a guild guide's careful hand where someone had stopped, deliberately, and decided not to write, yet.

A showman knew what a blank was. A blank was a story that hadn't been told.

"Alea," said Lucan, "of Wrenhaven."

He didn't read the rest.

***

She came out from the tunnel under the east stand with Astra on her back.

The crowd didn't know her. Nobody in Valcrest did. A fox girl of seventeen in plain leathers and a worn shirt, with red-gold hair tied up and black-tipped ears, walking out onto a chalked ring on the great sand of the Lists. They knew the summoned. They didn't know her.

They saw the sword.

Lucan heard them see it. A murmur, going round the stands near the near ring like a breeze through barley. Because it was a sword the size of a door. A slab of black iron, broad as a man's back, coming up over the girl's shoulder in its harness, and the girl was walking under it as if it were nothing at all. Lucan had seen a lot of fledglings with swords too big for them. He'd never seen one carry a sword that size as if it had been made for her.

She stopped in the near ring. She drew it. It came off her back and into her two hands and she stood there, very still, with the point resting on the sand.

Behind her, at the edge of the ring, a soldier-looking girl with a sword on her hip and a quiet man with nothing in his hands stood together. Petra. Xavier. Her corner.

Edmund Halloway looked at the sword. Then at the girl. Then over his shoulder at Ser Garret, who was no longer looking at his pupil at all. He was looking at the girl's feet.

"Begin," said the marshal.

***

The boy came in.

He came in well. Lucan would give him that, afterwards, to anyone who asked. He'd been taught well, by a champion, for years; he came in fast and straight with a good high cut that would have opened anyone on the near ring who wasn't ready for it. It was a beautiful cut. Lucan had seen Ser Garret throw the same one in a final, sixteen years ago, and win with it.

The girl didn't move.

She stood in the ring with the black sword in her hands and the point on the sand, and watched him come, and didn't move at all. Not a step. Not a breath. Lucan, on the platform, found that he'd stopped breathing himself. He'd seen fledglings freeze. This wasn't freezing. This was something else. This was a heron on the edge of a lake, watching the water, while something came toward it under the surface.

And then, at the last possible moment, just before the boy's good high cut came down, while it was still deciding to, she lifted the sword.

Not fast. That was what Lucan would never forget. It wasn't fast at all. It came up off the sand and round in a long easy line, like a fisherman lifting a rod, like somebody casting a line out over water, heavy and patient and unhurried, and at the top of it, she simply let it go.

Let it fall.

The black sword came down. Not swung. *Fell*. Its own weight, all of it, half a slab of iron that had taken six men and a winch to move, dropping out of the sky onto the boy's good cut the way a tree drops onto a fence. Lucan heard it land. The whole near side of the Lists heard it land: one great flat *clang*, like a bell with a crack in it, and the boy's beautiful expensive sword simply went. Out of his hands. Up. Turning over and over in the morning sun, high over the ring, glittering, and coming down point-first in the sand on the far side of the chalk line, three yards outside it, quivering.

Edmund Halloway stood in the middle of the ring with his empty hands still up in front of him, where the sword had been.

The girl's black point was resting on the sand again. Exactly where it had started. As if it had never moved.

One stroke.

***

There was a silence over the near side of the Lists.

Then the marshal, a little late, raised his flag. And the near stand went up.

Lucan had heard the Lists roar for fifteen years. He'd heard it roar for champions and for kings and for a horned girl and an oni and a boy with a helmet. He'd never heard it roar quite like this: surprised, delighted, the roar of a crowd that has just seen something it didn't know it wanted to see. They didn't know who she was. They didn't know her name. They'd seen a fox girl with a door for a sword stand still while a lord's son came at her, and lift it like a fishing rod, and let it fall, and that had been the end of it.

Behind the ring, Ser Garret Holm, three times champion of the Lists, had unfolded his arms. He was looking at the girl with an expression Lucan had never once seen on his face in twenty years. Then he did something Lucan had also never seen him do.

He bowed. To her. A small bow, from the waist, a champion's bow, the one you gave an opponent at the end of a final.

The girl looked at him, startled. Then she bowed back, awkwardly, with the sword still in her hands.

At the edge of the ring, the quiet man with nothing in his hands, Xavier, said something to the soldier girl beside him. Lucan couldn't hear what. The soldier girl laughed.

***

Lucan looked up at the fourth tier.

He told himself not to. It wasn't his business. He looked anyway.

The man with the rod was on his feet.

He was the only one in that whole section of the stand who'd stood up. Everyone round him had been on their feet for the paper horns and sat down again; now they were cheering from their benches, shouting, waving horns and rods and penguins, for a fox girl none of them knew. And in the middle of them, on the end of the fourth tier, one man was standing. Very still. With the fishing rod in one hand, and the other hand at his side, and two small yellow bees on his head that had woken up and were buzzing.

Nobody near him looked at him. Nobody saw him standing up.

Lucan saw.

He couldn't see the man's face. He never would. But he could see the way he stood. He'd spent fifteen years on that platform watching people stand in the stands when someone they loved won on the sand: mothers, fathers, old masters, sweethearts. He knew exactly what it looked like. It always looked the same. It looked like somebody trying very hard not to look the way they felt, and failing completely.

On the near ring, the girl had turned round. Before the marshal could say anything, before she'd even put the sword back on her shoulder, she'd turned round and looked up. Straight up. Past the master's platform, past the royal box, to the fourth tier of the east stand, to the end of a bench, to a man standing up on his own in a crowd that couldn't see him.

She didn't wave. She didn't do anything. She just looked. For a moment. The way you'd look across a room at the one person in it you were checking was still there.

Then she put Astra on her shoulder and walked off the ring.

***

Lucan sat down in his old seat on the master's platform, beside the master's chair, very slowly.

Drew didn't say anything. He was watching the next bouts on the other three rings, and the herald calling the next names, and the marshals with their flags. He sat in the rocking chair with his hands on its arms and his face calm. But after a while, without looking round, he passed Lucan a handkerchief.

Lucan took it.

"I'm not going to ask," he said, eventually. To nobody. Through the handkerchief. "I want you to know that. I'm not going to ask. It isn't my business."

"I know," said Drew.

"Whose business is it?"

Drew didn't answer that. He looked out at the sand. At the near ring, where the marshals were chalking the line fresh for the next bout. At the stands, still roaring. At ten thousand paper fishing rods.

"Hers," said Drew, eventually. "At the end. If she wins."

Lucan looked at him.

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then next year," said Drew. "That's what she told the king."

Lucan didn't ask how Drew knew what the girl had told the king. He wasn't going to ask anything. He sat on the master's platform with Drew's handkerchief in his hand and the whole roaring bowl of the Lists round him, his Lists, which weren't his any more and never would be again and had never been better, and looked down at the roll in his lap. At a name in a guild guide's hand, and a blank box beside it.

A story that hadn't been told yet.

He picked up his pen. He looked at the box for a long time.

Then he put the pen down again, and left it blank. And wept, quite happily, for most of the rest of the morning.

It was midsummer. He was entitled.
