He didn't teach the way anyone had ever taught Petra.

She'd had teachers. A sergeant on the Redmarch who taught by shouting. A guild master in Duskhollow who taught by making you do a thing four hundred times until your arms gave out and then telling you to do it again. A woman in a yard in the south, when Petra was fifteen, who'd taught her to hold a sword by hitting her across the knuckles with a cane every time she held it wrong. They'd all talked. They'd all had forms, and drills, and words for things. *Guard. Bind. Thrust. Recover.* You learned the words and then you learned what the words meant, and then, if you were lucky and stubborn, one day you didn't need the words anymore.

He didn't have any words.

That was the first thing she noticed, on the first morning, on the shingle in the grey light before the sun came over the hills. Five of them, standing in a loose line at the edge of the water with practice swords. Her. Xav. The fox girl, Alea, with the black slab of a sword on her back that she carried the way other girls carried a basket. The small one from Group 1, PenguChi, who'd turned up at dawn with a stuffed penguin under one arm and put it down very carefully on a dry stone, facing the lake, as if it were going to watch. And him.

He stood in front of them in his bare feet with a stick of driftwood in his hand, and looked at them for a long time.

Then he said, "Watch the heron."

And went and sat down on the end of the jetty, and fished.

***

They watched the heron.

All morning. Petra did it because she'd been told to, and because she was a soldier, and a soldier who'd been told to watch something watched it. The others did it because he had. Xav did it the way Xav did everything, completely still, without complaint, as if he'd been waiting his whole life for somebody to tell him to stand on a beach and look at a bird. Alea did it with her arms folded and her tail twitching, because, Petra suspected, she'd been made to do it before, when she was thirteen, and hadn't liked it then either. The small one did it with her lips moving, very slightly, as if she were counting.

The heron stood in the reeds at the edge of the shingle on one leg.

It didn't move for a very long time.

Then it moved. Once. The neck went out like a thrown spear and came back with something silver. And it swallowed, and stood on one leg again, and didn't move.

"Again," said Xav, under his breath, to nobody.

By the time the sun was up, the heron had moved eleven times. It had caught something nine of them. Petra had seen every one. She'd seen the neck go out and come back. She'd seen the fish.

She hadn't once seen it decide.

That was what she thought about, walking back up the lake road with the others to breakfast. Eleven strikes. She'd watched every one, from twenty paces, in good light. She was the best watcher in Duskhollow; she'd spent eight years learning to see a man decide before his feet did. And she had not once seen that bird decide anything. It had just been still, and then it had been moving, and there'd been nothing at all in between.

***

The second morning he came off the jetty.

He walked along the boards with his stick and stood in front of them on the shingle, and looked at them the way he'd looked at them the first morning. At each of them. One after another.

"Did anybody see it decide?" he said.

Nobody said anything.

"No," he said. "Nobody does. That's why it eats." He tapped the stick against the shingle, once. "Everything you've ever been taught is about seeing the other man decide. Watch his feet. Watch his shoulders. Watch his eyes. Wait for him to commit. And then punish it." He looked at Petra. "That's what you do. Xav says you never move first. Not once. In more than a year of dawns."

"Never," said Petra.

"It's good. It's very good. It kept you alive. It kept forty of you alive in a ditch, once, because I did it too." He turned the stick in his hand. "But it means you're always half a breath behind. Always. You can never be faster than the man you're waiting for. The best you can ever be is exactly as fast, plus however long it takes you to see it." He shrugged. "Most men are slow enough that that's fine. Some aren't."

"The heron isn't," said Alea, sourly. She'd heard this before. You could tell.

"The heron isn't," he agreed. "The heron doesn't wait for the fish to decide. The heron goes in the breath *before* the fish decides. When the fish doesn't even know yet that it's going to move. When it's still only *thinking* about thinking about it." He looked at Petra again. "There's always a breath before. In everything. In fish, in men, in demons. A breath where the decision's being made, and it isn't made yet, and the feet don't know. If you move in that breath, you're not first, and you're not second. You're just... there. Already. When they arrive."

Petra stood very still.

"That's what you did," she said. "In the ditch."

"That's what I did."

"You only ever moved once."

"You only ever need to," he said, "if you move in the right breath."

***

So that was what he taught her.

Not forms. Not drills. He didn't correct her grip or her stance; he looked at them on the first morning and said *those are fine*, and never mentioned them again. He just stood in front of her on the wet shingle every dawn, in his bare feet, with his stick held low, and waited. And she stood in front of him with her practice sword, and tried to find the breath.

She couldn't find it.

She tried for six mornings. She stood across from him and watched him the way she'd watched every man she'd ever faced. Feet. Shoulders. Eyes. Hands. And every single time, without warning, the stick was touching her somewhere. Her wrist. Her collarbone. The side of her neck, very lightly, like a fingertip. And she hadn't seen him decide. She hadn't seen him *start*. There was nothing in between. He was still, and then the stick was there, and he was still again.

"Again," he said. Every time.

On the sixth morning she lost her temper.

She hadn't lost her temper in years. She'd thought she'd lost the ability. But she stood on the shingle with the stick resting against the side of her neck for the twentieth time that morning, and something hot came up in her chest, and she said, through her teeth:

"I can't *see* it."

"No," he said. "You can't."

"Then how am I supposed to—"

"You can't see it," he said, "because you're looking at me."

She stopped.

He lowered the stick. "You're looking at my feet and my shoulders and my eyes. Waiting for them to tell you. They're not going to tell you; they don't know yet. That's the whole point. That's what the breath *before* means." He tilted his head. "Stop looking at me. Look at the whole thing. Me, and you, and the space between us, and the shingle, and the water. All of it. The way you'd look at a lake for a fish." He almost smiled. "The way the heron does. It isn't looking at the fish. It's looking at the water. And when the water's about to change, it knows. Before the fish does."

Petra stared at him.

"That's fishing," she said.

"It's all fishing," he said. "Everything I know is fishing. I just didn't know that's what it was called until I came here."

***

PenguChi got it before she did.

Petra wouldn't have believed it if she hadn't been there. The small one, on the hard path, aura, who'd been at it for twenty months and had told Petra on the second morning, very seriously, that she climbed one percent a week, sometimes, if she was lucky. Who carried a penguin. Who'd been Drawn for less than a year.

She didn't spar. He didn't make her. She stood a little way along the shingle with her hands loose at her sides and her eyes half closed, and *breathed*, and he stood beside her and talked to her very quietly, so quietly that Petra only caught bits of it. *Lower. Lower than that. Like you're waiting for a bite. Don't pull. Let it come up.* And the small one breathed, and her lips moved, counting, and nothing happened, and nothing happened.

And then on the seventh morning, while Petra stood across from the stick and failed for the thirtieth time, the small one said, out loud, in a clear calm voice that carried along the whole shore:

"Oh. *There* it is."

Everyone looked round.

She was standing with her eyes open now, and her hands open at her sides, and the air round her was doing something. Not much. Petra wouldn't have noticed if she hadn't been trained for eight years to notice things. A stillness. As if the morning had paused round her, very slightly, the way water goes still round a stone.

"It's lower," said PenguChi, wonderingly. "Alea always said. *It's lower. Like waiting for a fish.*" She looked at him. "I thought she meant it was down in my stomach or something. She didn't. She meant it's *under*. Under the thinking. You don't go and get it. You just stop being on top of it."

He was smiling. Properly. Petra had seen him smile perhaps four times in two weeks, and every time it had changed his face so completely that she'd had to look away.

"Alea got that from me," he said. "I got it from a pike."

PenguChi laughed. She opened her window, there on the shingle, in front of everyone, without seeming to think about it; the summoned did that, Petra had learned. Looked at the empty air in front of their faces and read something only they could see. Her face did something complicated.

"Four," she said.

"Four what?" said Alea.

"Four percent. This morning. Just now." PenguChi looked up. "I've never done more than one in a week in my life."

She read it out, the way the summoned always read them out to each other, as if saying it made it more true.

The stuffed penguin, on its dry stone, faced the lake and said nothing.

"Good," said the fisherman softly. "Again tomorrow. Don't chase it. It'll be there."

***

That night Petra couldn't sleep.

She was sharing a room above the bakery with Sabine, who slept like a soldier, instantly and completely, on her back with her arms folded. Petra lay in the dark and listened to the lake through the open window and thought about water.

She thought about the ditch.

Eight years. She'd thought about it every night for eight years, nearly. She'd tried to remember his face and couldn't. She'd tried to remember the words he said and couldn't, until a beach two weeks ago. But she'd always remembered the way he moved. She'd built her whole life on it. On *waiting*. On standing still while the thing came, until it committed, and then.

She'd got it wrong.

Not wrong. Half. She'd watched him from the bottom of a ditch, twenty-three and terrified, and seen a man who waited, and thought that was the whole of it. She hadn't seen the other half. She couldn't have. From the bottom of a ditch you could see the waiting. You couldn't see the breath before.

She thought about the heron. Not looking at the fish. Looking at the water.

She thought about the four demons that had come over the lip of the ditch, one after another, the last one so close she'd felt its breath. And him, at the top of the bank, against the red sky, not moving. Not moving. And then moving, once, and the demon falling, and him still again.

She'd always thought he'd waited for it to commit.

She lay in the dark above the bakery and saw it again, for the thousandth time, and for the first time she saw it properly.

He hadn't waited for it to commit.

He'd moved while it was still deciding to.

***

On the ninth morning she found it.

Not against him. Against Xav.

The fisherman did that sometimes, when one of them had been failing too long: he'd step back and put two of them across from each other instead, and go and stand on the jetty with his rod and watch. Petra and Xav had sparred a hundred times in the yard behind Mother Annick's. They knew each other's every habit. Two people who never moved first, standing across from each other on the wet shingle, practice swords low, waiting.

They'd stood like that for an hour, in Duskhollow, more than once. Neither of them moving. Until somebody called it a draw.

Petra stood across from Xav and stopped looking at him.

She didn't know how she did it. She'd tried for nine mornings and failed, and then she just did it, the way you stop trying to remember a word and it arrives. She stopped looking at Xav's feet and his shoulders and his eyes. She looked at the whole thing instead. Xav, and her, and the space between them, and the wet shingle, and the grey water behind him, and the heron standing in the reeds at the edge of her sight. All of it at once. Like a lake.

And the lake changed.

Not Xav. Xav didn't move. Xav was perfectly still, the way she'd taught him to be. But something in the whole of it, the space, the morning, shifted. Very slightly. The way the surface of water shifts a moment before a fish breaks it. Like the breath you take before you speak.

She moved.

She didn't decide to. That was the thing. She was still, and then she was moving, and there was nothing in between. One step. The practice sword coming up and across. And when it arrived, at the side of Xav's neck, very lightly, like a fingertip, Xav's sword was only just beginning to lift. He'd been about to move. He'd been deciding. He hadn't decided yet.

She'd been there before he arrived.

Xav looked at her, along the length of her practice sword. His face didn't change. His eyes did.

"Oh," he said.

Petra lowered the sword.

She found that her hands were shaking. Not with fear. Not with anything she had a name for. She looked down at them, and then she looked along the shingle, to the end of the jetty, where the fisherman was sitting with his rod, watching.

He didn't say anything. He didn't call out, or clap, or come down off the jetty. He just looked at her across the water, for a long moment. And then he nodded. Once.

The way he'd nodded to her on the shingle on the first day. The way, she thought, he might have nodded to a girl at the end of a ditch, if she'd ever looked up.

She found she had to sit down on the stones for a while.

Nobody bothered her. Sabine, who'd come down to watch that morning for the first time and was sitting on the wall at the top of the shingle with her arms folded, looked at Petra across the beach and raised one eyebrow, very slightly. Which was, from Sabine, a speech.

***

There was a rule on the shore.

Nobody had made it. It had just happened, on the third morning, and then it was a rule. Anybody could try him. Once a morning. With a practice sword, against his stick. Anybody who wanted.

Everybody had.

Gabe had tried on the third morning, because he was Gabe, with a wooden blade the size of a fence post, and had come at him like a wall falling down. The fisherman had moved once. Gabe had sat down on the shingle very suddenly with the stick resting on his collarbone and said *okay* in a small voice. Azac had tried with his shield. Once. The stick had come round the edge of the shield, somehow, from a direction a stick shouldn't be able to come from, and tapped him on the ear. Rachael had tried, laughing, and been tapped on the nose, and laughed harder. Calvin had tried with the wind behind him, which was cheating, and been tapped anyway, before the wind arrived. RX had tried hushed, which was *definitely* cheating, and the stick had found him in the empty air as if he'd been standing there waving.

Alea tried every morning. She'd been trying, she told Petra, since she was thirteen. She had never once made him move twice.

Petra tried every morning. Once. And every morning: still, and then the stick, and nothing in between.

And Xav.

Xav tried every morning.

He didn't say anything about it. He didn't need to. Every dawn, after the heron and the breath and the rest of it, he'd pick up his practice sword and walk out onto the wet shingle and stand across from the fisherman. And wait. The way he waited for everything. And every morning, after a minute, or ten, or once nearly half an hour while the whole beach held its breath, the stick would be there, at his throat, at his wrist, at his temple, and Xav would look at it, and say the same thing. Flat. Patient. Every time.

"Okay. Again."

And go back, and put the sword down, and come back the next morning.

Eleven mornings.

***

The twelfth morning was grey.

Mist on the lake, lying low and white on the water so that you couldn't see the far shore, and the jetty went out into nothing. The heron was a shape in the reeds, grey on grey. Everybody was there; it had become a thing the town did, a little, coming down to the wall at the top of the shingle at dawn to watch the fisherman's school. Rowan, some mornings. The baker's boys. Children, sitting on the wall in a row with their legs swinging.

Petra had done her morning. Once. The stick at her wrist. She'd seen something, this time. Not enough. A shift in the water, a breath, half a heartbeat too late. He'd nodded as if she'd done something.

Then Xav walked out onto the shingle.

He stood across from the fisherman in the mist, practice sword low, and was still.

Petra watched them. She did what she'd learned to do. She didn't watch Xav. She didn't watch the fisherman. She watched the whole of it. Two men on a grey beach in the mist, very still, and the space between them, and the water, and the heron, and the town on the wall holding its breath.

It went on for a long time.

She felt the whole of it settle. The way a lake settles at dusk when the wind drops. Everything going still, and stiller, until there was nothing moving on the whole shore but the mist.

And then she felt the lake change.

She felt it before anything happened. The breath before. A shift in the whole of it, very faint, like the surface of water a moment before something breaks it. And she knew, the way she'd known on the ninth morning with Xav, that the fisherman was about to move.

Xav knew too.

She saw him know it. She saw it go through him, the same thing she'd learned on the ninth morning, from inside, in front of him. He'd been standing across from her when she found it. He'd seen it from the other side. And he'd gone home that night and, she realised now, he'd done what Xav always did. He'd waited. He'd thought about it. He'd let it settle.

Xav moved.

Not to strike. That was what she'd remember. Not forward. *Sideways*. One step. Small. In the breath before. Not where the fisherman was going to be, but out of where the stick was going to arrive.

The stick arrived.

The place where Xav's throat had been was empty.

And for the first time in Petra's life, for the first time in a ditch or a yard or a beach, for the first time, she suspected, in anybody's life, the fisherman had to move again.

***

She counted it.

She couldn't help it. She'd counted it in the ditch, eight years ago, from the bottom of a ditch with her sword shaking in her hands. *One.* Every time. *One.* Every demon that came over the lip. *One. One. One.* He'd only ever moved once.

*One*, she counted now, on the misty shingle.

And then:

*Two.*

The stick came round. It wasn't fast, the second time. It didn't need to be. It came round in a short flat arc, from a direction that shouldn't have been possible, and touched Xav very lightly on the side of the neck, exactly where Petra's practice sword had touched him on the ninth morning.

It was over.

The whole beach was silent.

***

Xav stood there with the stick against his neck.

He didn't say anything. He looked at the fisherman, along the length of the driftwood. His face didn't change. His face never changed. But Petra had spent more than a year of dawns across from that face, and she saw his eyes. She saw them go very bright.

The fisherman lowered the stick.

He stood there in his bare feet on the wet shingle and looked at Xav for a long time. On the wall, the children had stopped swinging their legs. Alea, on the stones by the water, had both hands over her mouth. Gabe had stood up.

"Once," said the fisherman.

Xav looked at him.

"Once what?"

"Once more than anyone," said the fisherman. He said it very quietly. "Ever. Anybody who's ever stood across from me. In thirty-five years. On the Redmarch, or in Kharvos, or in a yard, or a ditch." He turned the stick over in his hand and looked at it, as if it had surprised him too. "Nobody's ever made me move twice. Not the general. Nobody."

He looked up.

"You made me move twice, Xav."

Xav didn't say anything.

For a long moment, he didn't say anything at all. He stood on the shingle in the mist with his practice sword low and his face perfectly still and his eyes very bright, and the whole beach waited to see what he would say. The town on the wall. The children. Gabe, and Alea, and Petra, and PenguChi with her penguin, and Sabine on the wall with her eyebrow.

And then he said what Petra would have bet her sword he would say. What he'd said every morning for eleven mornings. What he'd said, she'd learned, after every round he'd ever lost with this man, on the other side of the world, in a game, at three in the morning, for nine years.

"Okay," said Xav. "Again."

***

The fisherman laughed.

It came out of him the way it had on the jetty that first morning, short and startled and wet, the laugh of a man who's heard exactly the thing he needed and didn't know he needed. He put his free hand on the back of Xav's neck, the way Petra had seen him do with Gabe, and pulled him in until their foreheads nearly touched, and held him there.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Again tomorrow."

"Okay."

"You're going to do it again."

"Okay."

"And then you're going to make me move three times. One day. And I'm going to be so annoyed."

"Okay," said Xav. And then, very quietly, so quietly that only Petra, who was nearest, heard it: "You taught me."

"No," said the fisherman. "I taught you to wait. That was me. That was a long time ago." He let go of Xav's neck and stepped back, and looked along the shingle, at Petra, sitting on the stones with her practice sword across her knees. "*She* taught you the breath before. On the ninth morning. In front of you. You just watched her do it."

Xav turned and looked at Petra too.

And Petra, who had not cried at the ditch, or at the funeral pyres after, or in eight years of standing in yards, sat on a cold beach at the top of the world in the mist and found that she was crying. Quietly. With her face perfectly still. The way her student did everything.

***

They walked back up the lake road to breakfast, all of them, the way they did every morning. The mist was lifting off the water. The heron had caught something. The children ran ahead, shouting, to tell the rest of the town what had happened, and by noon everybody in Wrenhaven would know that the quiet boy from the south had made the fisherman move twice, and none of them would quite understand what it meant, and all of them would tell it anyway.

Petra walked at the back.

Xav fell in beside her. He didn't say anything for a while. Then:

"The ninth morning."

"Yes."

"You moved before I'd decided."

"Yes."

"I felt it," said Xav. "From the other side. I felt you go before I'd even..." He stopped. "I lay awake all night. Thinking about what it felt like. Being the fish."

Petra looked at him.

"I've been the fish for eight years," she said. "In a ditch. Every night. It was good of you to try it for one."

Xav almost smiled. It was the closest Petra had ever seen him come.

Ahead of them, on the road, the fisherman was walking with his daughter, the stick still in his hand, saying something to her that made her shove him with her shoulder. And as Petra watched, he lifted the stick and, without looking, without breaking stride, tossed it sideways over the stone wall into the reeds, the way you'd throw away a stick after a walk. As if it were nothing. As if it had never been anything.

Petra stopped on the road.

She went and climbed over the wall, into the wet reeds, and looked until she found it. A piece of grey driftwood, the length of her forearm, worn smooth by the lake. Nothing. Firewood.

She put it in her belt.

When she came back over the wall, Xav was waiting for her on the road. He looked at the stick in her belt. He didn't say anything.

They walked on up the lake road, the two of them, at the back, in the lifting mist. Two people who'd waited all their lives.

Petra found she didn't mind going first.
