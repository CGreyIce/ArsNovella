For the first time in a year, the Advisor read the reports out of order.

He noticed himself doing it. That was the worst of it. He had always read them in the order he'd trained their writers: Maren first, then Oswin, then Aldous and Emrys and Corvin and Garrick and Lucan. A small vanity, and a kind of promise. He had kept it through the whole of the first year, through Coldwater and the Hollow and the night market and the Council, every month, by lamplight, in his study, in order.

This month, a rider came to the castle gate at dusk on a lathered horse, wearing the mayor of Wrenhaven's colours, with a letter he would put into no hands but the Advisor's own. And the Advisor took it from him in the gatehouse, standing up, still in his outdoor coat, and broke the seal with his thumb, and read it right there, by the light of the gate-lantern, with the rider watching.

It was from Rowan Hale. It was very short.

***

*Halvard.*

*Something came over the ridge from the east, nine nights ago, and sat down in the old orchard on the hill above Wrenhaven, where you can see into the upper windows of the militia hall. It was big. Twice a man. Too many joints. Older and heavier than the scouts. He says fourth tier, maybe fifth. He says the kind Kharvos keeps back for things that matter.*

*It watched the girls for an hour.*

*It is dead. It is in two pieces. I burned them. I have also, on his instruction, sawn off an apple bough that was cut through in the same stroke, so that nobody looks at it twice.*

*He asked me to tell you three things.*

*That it came for them. Not for him. For the summoned, while they are still weak.*

*That every posting is being hunted. All seven. Not just this one.*

*And that if anyone asks how it died, you are to tell them it met a fisherman.*

*R.*

*He said something else, at the end, when I asked him the old question. I don't know what to make of it. I'll tell you when I see you.*

***

The Advisor stood in the gatehouse for a long time.

Then he went up to his study, still in his coat, and sat down at his desk, and wrote seven letters, one to each of his guides, all the same, all short. *Something came for the summoned at Wrenhaven. A hunter, out of Kharvos, of the fourth tier or the fifth. It is dead. It will not be the last. Every posting must be watched. Every night.* He sealed them and sent them by seven riders before midnight, out of seven gates, on seven roads.

He didn't write how it had died. He couldn't think how to put it.

Only when the riders had gone did he take off his coat, and pour the cup of wine he would not finish, and sit down again at the desk, and read Rowan's letter a second time, slowly. *It met a fisherman.*

*Nine nights ago*. Nine nights since a man who had not drawn his edge in seven years had drawn it again, on a hill above a small town, in the dark, because something had come to look at five girls through a window. Five girls who were there because the Advisor had put them there. Who were in this world at all because the Advisor had reached across the sky and pulled them through it.

Kharvos was hunting them because they existed. They existed here because of him.

And Ars had cleaned up after him. Again. Without a word. And told Rowan to say *a fisherman*.

The Advisor put the letter down, and pressed his fingers to his eyes, and sat that way for a while.

***

The other reports came in over the next fortnight. He read them all.

**Kestra**, through the king, the way her reports always came. She wrote in the same small terse hand she'd always used, every line a fact.

*The winch party took the sword east on the fourteenth. Before it went, by the king's leave, the summoned scholar Cursiffe examined the carving near the hilt. He could read it. His face changed. He told Oswin he needed time.*

*I asked him later, privately, what it said. He considered for some time before he answered. He said it was a motto. From their world. An old one, in an old language. About climbing.*

*He did not say more. I did not press. I judged that he was telling me the truth, and that it was not all of the truth.*

*The cat girl did not use her charm on him to find out, though she was in the room and it would have been easy. I record this as before.*

The Advisor read that paragraph four times.

*From their world.*

He had suspected. Of course he had. He'd sat at this desk in the autumn and written it, in a letter he never sent: *they are yours, aren't they*. He'd known it the way you know the weather's turning, in your joints, without being able to point at a cloud. Thirty-five strangers opening their eyes with windows exactly like the one a young swordsman had shown him at a border garrison twenty years ago. Speaking the Hero's words as if they'd been born to them. Group 6 calling them *English*, as if they had a name.

But suspecting was one thing.

He'd stood beside that sword a hundred times. He'd watched it pulled out of the red earth over the healer's grave by six men and a winch. He'd laid it at the centre of the circle with his own hands. He'd looked at the scratches near the hilt more times than he could count, and his eyes had slid off them every time, the way everyone's did, the way the scribes' eyes slid off Cursiffe's ink. He'd written *an unknown script* in his records and left it there.

And a summoned boy with a vial of ink round his neck had knelt down in front of it and *read* it.

*A motto. From their world. About climbing.*

Ars had stood over the grave of the woman he loved, seven years ago, on the morning after the worst night of his life, and carved something into his sword. Not in the kingdom's letters. In theirs.

The Advisor sat very still in the lamplight.

*I have never asked you where you came from,* he had written once. *You never offered.*

He thought he knew, now. He thought he'd known for a year. He just hadn't let himself look straight at it.

***

**Lucan**, from Valcrest. Long, breathless, enthusiastic, with too many exclamation marks, the way Lucan's reports always were, even this one.

A hunter. At the midsummer bout, in front of ten thousand people. Out of the east stand, thirty feet down onto the sand. It had gone for the horned girl first. Ysolde had met it, and been cut, and held. And the summoned had fought it. All five, as a team, the way they fought in the Lists: the spear holding the angle, the small loud girl calling its timing, the horned girl freezing the sand under its feet. And the oni.

*He put on the mask, Halvard, and his fire CAUGHT. Violet. Like wisteria. He hit it in the face at the exact moment the girl called it, and it was blind for one breath, and Ysolde took its head. He is Spark now. The seventh.*

*And then the city stood up and held up its paper horns. All of them. Ten thousand. In silence. For him and the horned girl. I have run the Lists for fifteen years and I have never seen anything like it in my life. I wept. I am not ashamed of it. I will be in the morning, probably, but not tonight.*

The Advisor found, to his considerable surprise, that he was smiling.

***

**Fenwick**, from Thornwall. Eleven pages. Corvin's habits had rubbed off on him.

The Advisor read them twice through, and then sat for a long time with the last page in his hand, looking at nothing.

A pass over the Greyspine that nobody had crossed in a century. A road on the far side, old and paved, built for armies. A valley. A town of the turned, hundreds of them, standing in rings round a circle burned into the earth, the size of a fort. And at the far end, *something with no light at all, the size of a hill, which turned and looked at us*.

*The girl with the true sight saw it. She reached Spark at the top of the pass in the act of seeing it. Her skill is called Scope. I believe her. I have believed everything Corvin wrote for two years and I believe this. We came back, all forty-six. I do not know why it let us.*

*It knows we are here, Halvard. It knows exactly where Thornwall is.*

*It does not seem to be in a hurry.*

***

He took all of it to the king at dawn.

Severin was in the private yard, the way he always was at that hour, in a plain shirt with the sleeves rolled up, moving through his forms in the grey light with a practice sword. Sixty-four now. The ninth tier, for twenty-five years. He moved like a man of thirty.

The Advisor waited by the wall, the way he always did, and watched him.

He'd known Severin forty years. Longer than anyone alive, probably. He'd known him before the crown, before the war, before the ninth tier, when he'd been a broad young man with a smith's shoulders and a smith's hands, standing at a forge in a river town, hammering out plough blades and horseshoes. You could still see it in him. Not in his face; his face was a king's face now, lined and still and impossible to read. In his hands. Thick fingers. Square palms. And across the backs of both of them, faded almost white with age, a scatter of small round scars: old burns, from sparks off the anvil, from a lifetime ago.

He'd been a blacksmith before he was a king. Most people at court had forgotten. Severin hadn't. Sometimes, late at night, when the council had been very trying, he still went down to the castle smithy and sent the smith to bed and worked the bellows himself, for an hour, alone.

The king finished his forms. He came over and took the towel off its peg and looked at the Advisor's face.

"Kharvos," he said.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Two that we know of. One at Wrenhaven, nine nights before midsummer. One at Valcrest, at midsummer, at the Lists." The Advisor gave him the reports. Severin read them standing up, towel round his neck, the way he read everything. Rowan's first. Then Lucan's. Then Fenwick's eleven pages, which took him a long time.

Then Kestra's. Which he read twice.

He didn't say anything for a while.

"It met a fisherman," he said at last.

"Yes."

"In two pieces. From where? Did Rowan say?"

"He didn't say. I don't think he knew." The Advisor paused. "The orchard is on the hill above the town. The lake is more than a mile below it."

Severin looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked away, east, over the castle wall, at the sky going pale.

"I saw him do that once," he said quietly. "On the Redmarch. The morning after. From the wall of Cinderhold, with a spyglass. Something big came at him out of the smoke, across the red. Three hundred paces off. He didn't even turn round." The king's thick scarred hands were very still on the ends of the towel. "I was Ascendant by then. Nearly twenty years. And I stood on that wall and thought: *I couldn't do that*."

The Advisor didn't say anything.

***

"They're hunting the summoned," said Severin. "All of them. Every posting."

"Yes."

"Because they're weak now, and won't be later."

"Yes."

"Then they'll keep coming." The king turned back from the wall. "What do you want to do? Bring them in? All thirty-five? Here, behind these walls, where I can stand in front of them myself?"

The Advisor had thought about it all night. He'd thought about little else for a fortnight.

"No," he said. "Not yet. Thirty-five of them in one place is one target. Kharvos would only have to send one hunter, and get lucky once." He shook his head. "They're safer scattered. With their guides and guards, and more men. Every posting doubled. Every night watched." He paused. "And they're learning. Faster than I thought. Seven at the first tier, now. They're learning from each other."

"How? Their letters go a month on the road."

"Not anymore." The Advisor hesitated. "Maren tells me the girl at Wrenhaven, Althea, the one who reached the first tier first. She's done something to the windows. All of theirs. They can talk to each other through them. All thirty-five, at once, across the whole kingdom. Instantly."

Severin stared at him.

"I can't see it," said the Advisor. "Nor can Maren, nor any of us. It's only in theirs." He spread his hands. "But I know it's true. The news from Duskhollow reached Valcrest three nights after it happened. By road it should have taken three weeks."

The king was quiet for a long time.

"Thirty-five people," he said, "who can talk across the whole kingdom without a courier. Who can see exactly how far they've climbed. Who learn from each other faster than any school I've ever seen." He shook his head slowly. "And Kharvos knows they exist."

"Yes."

"No wonder they're frightened." Severin put the towel back on its peg. "Double every posting. Treble Wrenhaven, if Hale will have it, which he won't, because he knows who's already there." Something that might have been a smile went across his face and was gone. "And Halvard."

"Majesty."

"The sword. The one they're carrying back to the Redmarch." He looked down at his own hands. At the scatter of old white scars. "I looked at it properly, once. When you laid it in the hall. Before the summoning. I put my hand on it." He turned his hands over, palms up, and looked at them. "That's good iron. Whoever forged it knew exactly what he was doing. I'd like to see it again, some day. With more time."

"It's going back to her grave," said the Advisor. "On the anniversary."

"I know where it's going." The king picked up his practice sword again. "I said some day."

***

That night, the Advisor wrote to Ars.

He did it at the desk in his study, with the lamp turned low, the way he had written perhaps thirty letters over seven years. He knew where they all were. In the second drawer on the left, under a blotter, every one of them sealed, not one of them sent.

He wrote slowly, crossing out more than he kept.

*Ars.*

*Rowan has told me what met a fisherman. Thank you. I know what it cost you to draw it again. I know whose fault it was that you had to.*

*There was a second, at Valcrest, at midsummer. The summoned killed it, with their guard. All five of them, together. The oni boy put on his mask and his fire caught. The city stood up for them. I wish you could have seen it. I suspect you could have, if you'd been there; I suspect you'd have seen more of it than any of us.*

*They have read your sword.*

*The scholar in the capital. The one with the ink. He knelt in front of it before it left and read the carving, and his face changed, and he told my guard only that it was a motto from their world, about climbing.*

*Their world.*

*I have never asked you where you came from. I am still not asking. I am telling you that I think I know, and that I have told no one, and that I will tell no one. Not the summoned. Not the council. The king knows what I know, which is very little, and he will say nothing either. That is his promise as much as mine.*

*But you should know that they are clever, Ars. Far cleverer than I gave them credit for, in the hall. They talk to each other now, all thirty-five, through their windows, instantly, across the whole kingdom. I can't see how. I only know it works. They have your books, the ones that come down from the lake. They have the carving. They have the boy in Duskhollow who fights like you, and the boy in Kingsferry who sees ties, and the girl at Wrenhaven who reads windows like scripture.*

*And there are thirty-five of them.*

*I could stop it. I could close the archive to them, take away the books, send the scholar somewhere far from anything written. I could tell them a story about the Hero that would keep them busy for years. You know I could. I have done worse, for less.*

*I am asking you what you want me to do.*

*H.*

He read it through three times.

Then, instead of folding it into the drawer with the others, he sealed it, and went down to the gatehouse in the middle of the night in his slippers, and put it into the hand of Rowan Hale's rider, who was sleeping on a bench by the fire, waiting to go home.

***

The answer came back nine days later, with the same rider.

It was a single small sheet of paper, folded once, not sealed. Rough paper. The kind you'd buy at a market stall for a copper a sheaf. It smelled, very faintly, of woodsmoke and lake water.

The Advisor unfolded it in the gatehouse, standing up, by the light of the gate-lantern, the way he'd unfolded Rowan's letter a month before.

There were two words on it, in the kingdom's letters. In the same plain unhurried hand that had written him a single line a year ago, about a girl who brought fish to a militia hall, when he'd written to ask whether he should stop her.

*Let them.*

***

He stood in the gatehouse for a long time.

Then he laughed. He didn't mean to. It came out of him all at once, short and helpless, startling the gate sergeant half out of his skin. A grey old man in slippers, standing in a gatehouse at midnight, laughing at a scrap of market paper with two words on it.

*Let them.*

Not *stop them*. Not *tell them*. Not *keep them away from me*. Not anything he'd expected, or feared, or half hoped. Just *let them*. As if it were the simplest thing in the world. As if thirty-five clever frightened people working out a secret Ars had kept for thirty-four years were weather, or a tide, or a fish on a line: something you didn't fight, but let run.

He'd written *let her*, a year ago. About Alea. And the Advisor had let her, and she'd gone up the hill every day with fish, and a girl on the aura path who'd been stuck for months had found her feet, and the whole militia hall in Wrenhaven had grown a little warmer.

*Let them.*

The Advisor folded the scrap of paper very carefully, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, against his chest, where he would carry it for a long time.

He thought about the drawer. Thirty letters, sealed, unsent. Seven years of things he had never said.

He thought that perhaps, now, some of them could be.

He went back up to his study, and sat down at his desk, and did not open the drawer. Not yet. But he put his hand on it, for a moment, in the lamplight, the way you'd put your hand on a door you've decided you're going to open one day.

Then he blew out the lamp, and went to bed, and for the first time in a month, he slept.
