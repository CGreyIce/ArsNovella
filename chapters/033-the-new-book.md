The trader came up the north road in the first week of the thaw, with mud to his axles and a cart full of things nobody in the capital needed, and Oswin bought every book he had before he'd finished unloading.

Cursiffe watched it happen from the archive window. He'd been watching for it for a month. Oswin had told him, in the dead of winter, that the trader from the lake country came every spring, regular as the swallows, and always had a new one: a new little brown book with no name on the spine. Oswin had said it the way other men talked about the first warm day. And Cursiffe, who had read the first book four times now, and could recite the passage about bait from memory, had found himself counting the weeks.

Now the cart was in the courtyard, and Oswin was running across it in his slippers, waving his arms, and the trader was laughing and holding up a stack of small brown books tied with string.

Cursiffe went down to meet him at the archive door.

"Six copies," said Oswin, breathless, triumphant, his cheeks pink with cold. "*Six.* He brought six this year. Somebody up north must be selling more." He pressed one into Cursiffe's hands. "Here. Yours. Read it first. Don't tell me anything until I've read it too."

The title page said the same as before: *Written down by someone who should be fishing.*

Under it, in the same plain hand, someone had added: *Second year. Still fishing.*

Cursiffe stood in the archive doorway with the book in his hands and felt something go very still in his chest.

***

He read it that night by the fire in the prince's sitting room, with the others drifting off to bed one by one, the way he'd read the first one.

It was the same voice. He'd have known it anywhere now. Wry, and patient, and very slightly mischievous: stories about the lake, and the weather, and the people in a small town by the water, and fish. A great deal about fish. A chapter about a fox who'd tried to get into a henhouse and been chased off by the hens themselves, which made Cursiffe laugh out loud, alone, at midnight. A chapter about growing pumpkins that were late out of spite.

And then, two thirds of the way through, a chapter that wasn't about fish at all.

*On Waiting.*

Cursiffe sat up.

*There is a thing the young ask me, more and more lately, which I am not qualified to answer, so I will answer it anyway: how long does it take to reach the first tier?*

*The honest answer is that it takes as long as it takes. But that is not a useful answer, and young people deserve useful answers, so here is what I know.*

*You cannot chase it. Everyone tries. Everyone sits in a cold yard with their eyes screwed shut, pushing, pushing, as if aura or mana were a stubborn door and they could break it down by leaning. It is not a door. It will not be broken. The harder you push, the further it goes.*

*It is more like waiting for a fish.*

*You breathe from the very bottom of you, lower than you think. You let your line down. And then you stop trying to make anything happen. You sit, and you are patient, and you pay attention, and you wait. And one day, when you are not expecting it, there is a tug.*

*Most people miss the first tug, because they are too busy pulling.*

*I have never once, in all my life, caught a fish by shouting at the water.*

There was no *trust* at the end of it. Cursiffe checked twice. Just that last line, and then the next chapter, which was about the best way to smoke trout.

He read *On Waiting* four times. Then he got up and went to find Milli.

***

Milli was in the practice yard, at midnight, in the cold, alone. Cursiffe had known he would be. He was there most nights now, since the Council; standing in the middle of the sand under the lanterns with a practice sword, doing forms until he couldn't lift his arms.

He was at fourteen percent. He'd been at fourteen for three weeks. Kestra said *again* to him less often now, but she still said it. And the frustration had got into him so deep that Cursiffe could see it in the way he moved: every swing a little too hard, every breath a little too high, his tail lashing.

"Read this," said Cursiffe, and held out the book.

Milli looked at him as if he were mad. "It's midnight."

"I know. Read it anyway. The marked page."

Milli read it under the lantern. Cursiffe watched his face. Watched him start to frown, the impatient frown, the *this is a book about fish* frown. And then watched it change, slowly, as he got further down the page.

*Most people miss the first tug, because they are too busy pulling.*

Milli read it again.

Then he put the book down very carefully on the fence, and walked back into the middle of the yard, and stood there. He didn't lift the sword. He didn't take a stance. He just stood with his arms at his sides and his eyes half closed, and breathed. Low. From the very bottom of him. In, and out, and in.

His tail stopped lashing.

Cursiffe stood by the fence and watched him for a long time. Ten minutes. Fifteen. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere in the castle a bell rang the half hour.

And then Milli's shoulders dropped, very slightly, and he made a small sound, the kind of sound you make when you step into a warm room out of the cold.

"Oh," he said quietly. "*Oh.* There it is."

He didn't move. He didn't lift the sword or try to do anything with it. He just stood in the middle of the yard with his eyes shut, very still, and let whatever it was come up through him on its own. And Cursiffe, who couldn't see aura, could see Milli's face, which was the face of a man who has just felt the first tug on a line.

When he opened his eyes, he looked at his window, and laughed out loud.

"*Seventeen.* It was fourteen for three weeks. *Seventeen.*" He turned round. "Who *wrote* this?"

"Nobody knows," said Cursiffe.

He didn't say the rest. He didn't say: *somebody who sounds exactly like someone we lost*. He didn't say: *somebody who used to make jokes on the fly faster than anyone I ever met*. He'd promised Hana. Until they were sure.

But he went back up to the sitting room afterwards, and sat by the dying fire with the book open on his knees, and read *On Waiting* one more time. And he thought about how strange it was, that a man in a cabin somewhere in the lake country had written down, for nobody in particular, exactly what Milli needed to hear.

As if he'd known.

***

The summoning circle was the other thing.

Thea had asked him for it months ago, in one of her long, dense, wonderful letters: *Can you draw me the circle? In the hall? You're the closest. I need to see the structure.* And he'd tried, in the winter. He'd gone down to the summoning hall with Oswin, who had the key, and stood at the edge of the great circle carved into the floor and tried to copy it.

He couldn't. Not in front of it. It was the strangest thing. He'd stand there with his pen and his paper, looking down at the rings and spokes cut into the stone, and the moment he tried to put a line on the page his hand would lose its place. Like the scribes' eyes sliding off his English. The circle didn't want to be copied.

And at the centre of it, the black sword stood in the stone where it had stood since the day they arrived. Nobody had moved it. Oswin said nobody could; it had taken six men and a winch to bring it there. Cursiffe hadn't looked at it for long. He'd found he didn't like to. There was something carved on it, near the hilt, very small. He'd glimpsed it once, from the edge of the circle, and had to look away, because his eyes had done something strange when they touched it, as if it were trying to be read.

So he'd given up, that winter, and written to Thea to say he couldn't.

But now, in the spring, with *On Waiting* in his coat and Milli at seventeen, he thought about Thea's letter after the bonfire. *You can't get over the edge by reading. The other half is writing.*

He'd been trying to *copy* the circle. To read it off the floor and put it on the page. And it didn't want to be read.

So that night, very late, at the table in the sitting room, he didn't try to copy it. He shut his eyes and *remembered* it: the shape it had made in him, standing at its edge in the cold, the way a word you've heard once stays in your ear. And he unstoppered the little vial of ink on the chain around his neck, and wet his pen, and *wrote* it. Not drew. Wrote. In English. In the only script he had.

*Ring. Ring inside ring. Seven spokes. Where the spokes cross the third ring, a knot. At the centre, a gap the shape of a blade.*

He wrote it down line by line, in words, the way you'd describe a map to someone over the phone. And the ink, his own English ink, that held on skin and wouldn't wash off and that no scribe in the castle could copy, went down onto the page warm and steady and stayed there, and when he'd finished there were three pages of it. A circle written in words.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded it into his letter to Thea, along with everything else.

And at the end, on its own page, he copied out *On Waiting*. All of it. Every word.

*This is from the book. The new one. I don't know why I'm sending it, except that Milli read it at midnight and his aura came up for the first time in three weeks, and I thought, Thea would want to know that somebody out there is writing things that help.*

*I don't know who he is. Nobody does. Oswin says the books come from the north.*

*From the lake country.*

***

The letter reached Wrenhaven twelve days later.

Thea read it at the long table in the militia hall, by the window, with the spring light coming in. The circle first. Three pages of it, in Cursiffe's narrow English hand. She read it the way she read code: slowly, carefully, building the structure in her head line by line. And somewhere in the middle of the second page she started to *see* it, the way she'd seen the lattice in the bonfire: rings and spokes and knots, turning, and at the centre a gap the shape of a blade.

The medium. That was where it went. Of course it was.

She'd need weeks to Parse the rest of it. Months, maybe. She set it aside very carefully, the way you'd set aside something that might break.

Then she turned to the last page.

*On Waiting.*

She read it once, quickly, the way you read a letter from a friend.

Then she stopped.

She read it again. Slowly, this time. Every word.

*You breathe from the very bottom of you, lower than you think. You let your line down. And then you stop trying to make anything happen.*

*It is more like waiting for a fish.*

Thea sat at the table with the letter in her hands and the spring light on the page, and didn't move for a long time.

She'd heard those words before. Not read them. *Heard* them. In the autumn, in the yard of this very hall, on a cold morning in the second week. Alea, in her good boots and her copper guild token, standing in the far corner with PenguChi, explaining how to find your aura. Thea had been sitting cross-legged on the flagstones, supposed to be sensing, actually listening. And Alea had said, slowly, as if she were trying to remember exactly how someone else had said it to her:

*It's lower. You breathe from the very bottom of you, and you wait, and you stop trying to make it come, because it won't come if you chase it. It's like waiting for a fish.*

The same words. Almost exactly. In Alea's voice, in the yard. And in a book written by nobody, from the lake country, that a trader brought to the capital every spring.

*Someone told me. Someone who could see it.*

*My dad always said.*

*They're the lake's fish. I just ask them politely.*

Thea put the letter down on the table and went to find Higu.

Higu was in the yard, sitting on the wall in the sun with her notebook on her knees. She looked up when Thea came out, and saw her face, and closed the notebook without a word, and moved over to make room.

Thea sat down beside her. She held out the last page of Cursiffe's letter.

Higu read it. Then she read it again. Then she opened her notebook to the page at the back, the one with the line she hadn't finished, and looked at it for a long time.

"The book," she said quietly. "From the lake country."

"Yes."

"And Alea said the same words. In the autumn."

"Almost exactly."

Higu was silent for a while, looking out over the wall at the town and the hills and, far off, the glint of the lake.

"He writes books," she said at last. "The fisherman."

It wasn't a question. Thea didn't answer it.

Higu took out her pencil, and on the page at the back of the notebook, very small, under the list of things Alea said that weren't answers, she wrote one more line.

*The books come from the lake.*

She didn't draw anything else. Not yet. But Thea, watching her, saw her pencil hover for a long moment over the line she'd never finished: the one that ran off the edge of the page, toward a name she still hadn't written.

Then Higu closed the notebook, and they sat together on the wall in the spring sun, saying nothing, looking at the lake.
