Odette had organised eleven midwinters for the town of Wrenhaven, and this was going to be the best one, and nobody was going to thank her for it, and that was exactly how she liked it.

She started at five. She always started at five. In the town hall, at her desk, with the fire lit and the lamp trimmed and her keys on the blotter and the list in front of her, the long one, in her sharp neat hand, that she'd been writing and rewriting since the first frost.

*Wood: the Hollin brothers, from the north road copse, Tuesday. Confirmed. Again.*

*Bread: Edric. Forty loaves. Tell him forty, he'll make fifty, let him.*

*Ale: the brewer. Two barrels. Not three. Last year three.*

*Chairs for the old ones: from the church. Ask the vicar nicely. Then take them.*

*The mayor's speech: he will want to make it longer. Let him make it longer.*

And at the bottom, underlined twice, the thing she'd added on the morning after the first snow, when the king's horses had gone up the lake road and the red hen had cried and run after them and a girl with fox ears had carried it home under her chin:

*Lanterns. The lake road. All of it. Forty.*

She looked at it in the lamplight for a long time.

Then she took her pen and added, very small, beside it, in a hand nobody but her could read: *He'll hate it.*

And under that: *Good.*

***

There were people who thought Odette was a hard woman. She knew who they were. She had a list of them too, in the back of the ledger, though she'd never once used it for anything but making sure they got the worst seats at the harvest supper.

She wasn't hard. She was *organised*. It was different, and almost nobody understood the difference, and she'd stopped explaining it at about the age of twenty, when the old mayor had made her keeper of the town hall because she was the only person in Wrenhaven who could find anything in it.

Rowan understood. He was the only one who ever had. He'd been made mayor nine years ago, the winter after the war, with a look on his face she'd seen on men before, because nobody else would be, and sat down across the desk from her on his first morning and said *I don't know how to do any of this*. And she'd said *I know. I'll do it. You stand in front of things*.

He'd been standing in front of things ever since. She'd been doing the rest.

She'd never once told him that she'd kept that first morning in the ledger. *Rowan, mayor. Doesn't know how. Will stand in front.* She'd never told him she'd underlined it.

***

The town came awake round her the way it always did on midwinter morning: badly, and then all at once.

The Hollin brothers with the wood, two hours late, arguing. Edric with fifty loaves, as foretold, and flour to his elbows, singing a hymn to his oven and denying it. The brewer with three barrels, which she sent one of straight back up the hill, and he went, because it was Odette. The vicar's chairs, which she'd asked for nicely on Tuesday, and which she now took. Children everywhere, underfoot, shrieking, being told off and doing it again. Bram from the militia hall carrying benches, crying slightly, the way Bram did at most things, because Bram always cried at midwinter and nobody remarked on it any more.

And the five girls from the militia hall, down at the lake, making lanterns. Odette had sent them yesterday. She'd heard about yesterday.

Everybody had heard about yesterday.

***

She'd been at her desk when the egg-woman's boy came running. Not Robin. The other one, the older one, whose leg had broken the year before the summoned came and mended. He'd come up the town hall steps three at a time and burst in through her door without knocking, which nobody in eleven years had ever done, and stood there with his face white saying *Robin, Robin, the lake, Robin*, and she'd been on her feet and out of the door and halfway across the square before he'd finished.

She didn't run. Odette never ran. She walked very fast.

She'd got to the top of the lake road in time to see it. Not the ice; she'd been too late for the ice. The end of it. A crowd on the jetty, at the far end, by the hole. Children in a ring. The egg-woman, who must have been closer, running down the shingle in her apron. And on the end of the jetty, on his knees, a man with his arm soaked to the shoulder and water running off it. And beside him, on her knees too, over a small brown shape on the boards, a little girl with long lavender hair frozen into ropes and a squid on her head glowing like a lamp.

Odette had stood at the top of the lake road and watched the small brown shape on the boards cough.

She'd turned round, then, and walked very fast back up to the town hall, and gone in, and shut her door, and sat down at her desk, and put her face in her hands for exactly as long as it took. Which was some time.

Then she'd written in the ledger, in her sharp neat hand: *Robin, the egg-woman's youngest, through the ice by the outflow. Brought out by the axolotl girl. Breathing.*

And under it, after a moment, because it was the honest book and somebody had to keep it: *The axolotl girl went under the ice after him. Nobody asked her to.*

***

By noon she had the bonfire built in the middle of the square, taller than last year's, which was the Hollin brothers showing off, and the benches in a ring round it, and the vicar's chairs at the front for the old ones, with Mother Breck's at the very front, with a blanket on it. Ninety-one, now, Mother Breck. She'd said at the harvest that she wasn't going to bother with another midwinter. She'd said that every harvest for six years.

By one, the bread. By two, the ale, two barrels, and the brewer sulking.

By three, Old Mabry.

He came across the square toward her at the slow determined pace of a man who'd been on the town council for thirty-one years and had a question, and Odette, who'd known the question was coming for two months and a half, put down her list and folded her hands and waited for it with her face perfectly composed.

"Odette," said Old Mabry.

"Councillor."

"The tablecloth."

"Yes?"

"The council tablecloth," said Old Mabry. "The red one. With the gold fringe. It's not on the council table." He frowned at her. "It hasn't been on the council table since the autumn. There's a brown one on it. A *brown* one."

"It's at the cleaner's."

"Since the *autumn*?"

"It's a large cloth," said Odette.

Old Mabry looked at her for a long time. He was not a clever man, Mabry. He'd talked about the drains in council for eleven years and never once said anything about them anybody needed to hear. But he'd sat at that table for thirty-one years, looking at that cloth. And thirty years ago, in his first month on the council, he'd knocked over an inkpot onto it in the middle of a vote and left a blot the shape of a duck, and everybody had laughed, and he'd been mortified, and then over thirty years it had become his blot, and he'd started touching it with one finger at the start of every meeting, the way you touch a lucky stone.

He hadn't touched it since the autumn.

"Odette," he said. Quietly. "Where's my duck?"

Odette looked at him.

She thought about a cabin by the lake, in the autumn, with a red curtain hanging crookedly in the loft window under the eaves. A curtain with a gold fringe, and a duck-shaped blot on it, low down on the left, that she'd turned to the inside so it would face the girl in the loft and not the lake. Because a duck was a cheerful thing to wake up to.

She'd told the man *tell Mabry and I'll deny it*.

"At the cleaner's," said Odette. "Councillor."

Old Mabry looked at her a moment longer. Then something happened to his long dull face that Odette had never seen it do in thirty-one years. He smiled. Not much. A little. At the corner.

"Good," he said. "Tell the cleaner I hope it's warm."

And went away across the square, at his slow determined pace, to tell the vicar about the drains.

Odette sat on the bench with her list and looked after him for some time.

Then she wrote in the margin, very small, where nobody would ever see it: *Mabry knows.*

***

The lanterns went up at dusk.

She went down to see it. She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to stay in the square, where she was needed, where the bread was and the children were and the vicar was going to try to take his chairs back. She found herself at the top of the lake road instead, with her keys in her pocket and her good coat on, at the corner where the road left the square, in the dusk.

Forty lanterns. On both walls. All the way down.

The girls had done it. The five of them and Alea, in the last of the light, going down the lake road with a taper from the bonfire, lighting them one at a time. Small paper lanterns on little wire frames, red and yellow and white, on every second stone of the field walls on both sides of the road, all the way from the square to the shingle. Glowing. Lighting the snow on the walls gold, and the frozen ruts of the road between them, and the white fields on either side going away into the dark. A road of light, going down to the water. To a cabin with one window lit, and a red curtain in its loft, and smoke going straight up.

To the end of the jetty, where somebody had hung the last lantern on the last post. Over the hole in the ice.

Odette stood at the top of it and looked down it for a long time.

Nine years ago she'd walked down this road in the dark with a basket on her arm, in the autumn, in a war, to a cabin by the lake where a little fox girl of eight was sitting in the loft by the window, looking east. Because the girl's mother had gone east with the girl's father to the war, and somebody had to keep her fed and warm, and Odette was the only woman in the town hall who didn't have children of her own to keep.

She'd done it for a season. Kept her fed. Kept her warm. Kept the cabin like a barracks, with the beds made square and the fire never out and the porridge on at six. Rowan, who wasn't mayor then, had stood in the doorway every night with a sword across his knees because somebody had to, and had not once in the whole season been able to boil an egg without burning it, and she'd told him so every morning. And the girl had sat in the loft window every evening and looked east, at the road the soldiers would come home by.

And then one morning, at the end of the autumn, a man had come home down that road on his own.

Odette had been in the kitchen. Making porridge. She'd heard the door. She'd known, from the sound of it, before she turned round. And the girl had come down the ladder from the loft in her nightgown and stood at the bottom of it and said *where's Mum*, and Rowan had said *your dad'll tell you*, and gone and sat on the end of the bed, and Odette had stood at the stove with the porridge spoon in her hand and not turned round. Because she couldn't. Because if she'd turned round she'd have had to look at him. Not his face; nobody in Wrenhaven had ever been able to hold his face. Him. His shoulders. His hands. The way he was standing in the doorway. She'd known exactly what she'd see, and that she'd never in her life be able to stop seeing it.

She'd stirred the porridge. She'd stirred it until it burned. She'd given it to them anyway, and nobody had eaten it, and she'd scraped it into the hens' bucket and gone home.

Nine years. She'd never once walked down the lake road again until the autumn the king came, and the stones.

***

He came up it at full dark.

She saw him from the top. The way you'd see a fish rise at dusk on a still pond, a long way off: a small dark shape at the bottom of the lake road, at the gate of the cabin, between the last two lanterns. Standing there. For a long time. Looking up the road.

At forty lanterns. Lit. All the way up. For him.

She watched him look at it.

He'd hate it. She'd known he'd hate it; she'd written it in the margin. He was a man who'd walked up this road in the dark for nine years without a lamp, with his collar up, so nobody would see him go by. Who'd come to the bonfire last year for the first time in eight and stood at the very edge of the light, at the back, behind his daughter, where nobody would look at him. And now there was a road of light, forty lanterns long, going all the way from his gate to the square, with every child in Wrenhaven knowing exactly who it was for.

He stood at the bottom of it a long time.

Then the girl was beside him. Alea. Coming out of the cabin gate behind him with her black sword on her back and her good knife on her hip and something in her hand. She stood beside him at the bottom of the lit road, and said something, and Odette couldn't hear what. She didn't need to.

And then the five girls from the militia hall. Coming down the lake road from the square, past Odette, at the top, without seeing her. In their coats, with their lanterns, in a row, the way they'd come down it the first time in the snow last winter when nobody in the whole of Wrenhaven had known what was going to happen. The pink-and-violet one. The one with the white bow. The silver-haired one, scowling. The small one with the penguin. And the axolotl girl, in front, with her hair dry now and loose and down past her knees and a squid on her head glowing faintly gold in the dark.

They went down between the lanterns to the bottom of the road. To the gate. To him.

And turned round. All of them. The five girls, and the fox girl, and the man. And came back up.

Together.

Odette stood at the top of the lake road and watched seven people walk up between forty lanterns in the snow, slowly, not talking much, toward the square and the bonfire and the whole of Wrenhaven waiting with its back very carefully turned. The man in the middle. Not at the edge. Not behind. In the middle, with his daughter on one side of him and the axolotl girl on the other, holding his hand, which nobody in the town had ever once seen anybody do.

She went back to the square before they got to the top.

She didn't want him to see her face. Odette had a very good face; she'd spent forty years making it do nothing. It wasn't doing nothing.

***

Rowan's speech was longer than last year's.

It was always longer than last year's. That was midwinter. Odette sat on the front bench beside Mother Breck's chair with her list on her knee and her hands folded on it and listened to him do it, the way she'd listened to him do it for nine years, standing on the steps of the town hall in his chain in the firelight, with the whole town round the bonfire in the cold and their faces lit gold.

He talked about the harvest. He always did. He talked about the late pumpkins, at length, and the frost, and the silver-haired girl from the militia hall who'd grown a hedge round half of them in one night, and the town clapped and the silver-haired girl went scarlet and glared at everybody. He talked about Garrow's hammer, back in its forge, and Garrow at the edge of the crowd went red to the roots of his hair. He talked about a king who had come to the lake twice now, and wanted to see the hens, and a thin grey man from the capital who'd come too, and carried a basket of fish up the lake road, which the town had seen and not mentioned. He talked about the snow.

He talked about yesterday.

Not much. Rowan was a good speaker, Odette thought, which was something she'd never tell him; he knew when to stop. He said *and yesterday, at the outflow*, and stopped, and the whole square went still, and the egg-woman on the third bench put her arm round a small boy in a blanket with a red cap on his head, who was asleep, with a fever, against her side, because Odette had said he could come for an hour if he slept through it. And Rowan looked down from the town hall steps at a little girl with a squid on her head, sitting on the front bench three along from Odette, between the pink-and-violet one and the man.

"Yesterday," said Rowan, "somebody went under the ice. And somebody else went after him." He was quiet a moment. "That's all I'm going to say about it. You were all there. You all know." He looked at the axolotl girl. "Thank you."

The whole square stood up.

Odette didn't see it start. She didn't think anybody did. It was the way the stones had gone, in the autumn, the king's autumn, or the way the town had come down the lake road for the pumpkins without being asked. Nobody decided. One of the Hollin brothers was standing, and then the miller's wife, and then the egg-woman with her boy in her arms, and then all of them. The whole of Wrenhaven. On its feet round the bonfire in the snow, not clapping, not shouting, just standing. Looking at a little girl from another world on the front bench, with gills behind her ears that she'd hidden for two years and a half, and a squid on her head.

The little girl burst into tears.

Of course she did. She'd told everyone she was going to. Gigichi had said *that's not news, that's weather*. She sat on the front bench with her face in her hands and cried, and the squid on her head went gold, the deepest brightest gold Odette had ever seen, so that the whole front row was lit with it. And the man beside her put his arm round her shoulders, in front of the whole town.

Odette looked at her list.

She found that she couldn't read it. She'd written it herself, in her own sharp neat hand, and she couldn't read a single line.

***

The fire was the girls'.

Odette hadn't arranged that. She'd arranged everything else; she'd arranged where the vicar's chairs went and how many loaves and which barrel. She hadn't arranged this. She'd learned, the first winter, that you didn't arrange what the girls did at the fire. The first winter, the pink-and-violet one had made a flame sit on her fingertip in front of everyone and the town had gasped, and the second winter she'd written something into the bonfire and every lamp in Wrenhaven had flared up white, the cabin's too, and Odette had stood in the square with her list and felt it go through her like a bell.

This winter there were five of them.

All five. The axolotl girl had made it five, yesterday, on the end of a jetty. Odette had heard the quiet one with the white bow say it, on the walk up the lake road, very low: *all five*. She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, watching them get up off the front bench when Rowan had finished, one at a time, and go to the fire.

The pink-and-violet one first. Thea. She stood at the edge of the bonfire with the light on her face and put out one hand, and did something with her fingers, and wrote something in the air in front of the flames that nobody in the square could read. And the fire went white. Not as bright as last year. Steadier. The way a lamp goes steady when somebody trims the wick.

The quiet one with the bow. Higu. She held up her hand, and three moths came off her shoulders, pale, and then five, and then a dozen, white moths, glowing, going up off her into the dark over the fire in a slow spiral, like sparks that had decided to stay.

The silver-haired one, scowling. She knelt at the edge of the ring and put her hand on the snow, and out of the ground round the bonfire, all the way round, a ring of briar came up. Not high. Knee-high. Thorn and leaf and a few small white flowers, in the middle of winter, in the snow, holding the fire in a ring the way your arms would hold somebody.

The small one with the penguin. She didn't do anything with her hands. She just stood at the edge of the fire with her penguin at her feet, and opened her mouth, and began to sing. Low and slow and steady. Odette didn't know the song. Nobody in Wrenhaven did. It was in their language, the summoned's. But everybody in the square went quiet for it, the way a room goes quiet when somebody you trust starts to speak.

And the axolotl girl.

She came last. Still crying, a little. Wiping her face on her sleeve. She went to the edge of the fire, inside the ring of briar, and knelt in the snow, and put both her hands flat on the ground.

And the snow round the bonfire melted.

All of it. In a ring. Not to mud; to water. Clean, clear water, a hand deep, lying on the flagstones of the square all the way round the fire inside the briar, with the white flame and the moths and the thorns reflected in it upside down. Still. Perfectly still. And then, very slowly, turning. Round. The whole ring of it, going round the bonfire the way water goes round in a basin, the way the lake had gone round yesterday under the ice, with the firelight going round on it like a wheel.

Nobody in the square said anything.

Odette sat on the front bench with her list on her knee and watched five girls from another world stand round a bonfire in the middle of her town, with light and moths and thorn and song and water, all of it at once, all five of them, the way they'd stood at the top of the lake road last winter with lanterns, frightened, not knowing anything.

They weren't frightened now.

She looked across the fire at the man.

He was standing at the edge of the light. Where he'd stood last year. But not behind his daughter this time. Beside her. With his hands at his sides. Looking at the five of them round the fire, one by one, the way he'd looked at them at the edge of the light last year, as if he were reading something written on each of their faces and wanted to get every word.

Odette couldn't see his face. Nobody in Wrenhaven could.

She didn't need to. She'd stood at a stove once with a porridge spoon in her hand and not turned round.

She could see his shoulders.

***

He found her afterwards.

She'd known he would. She'd tried to be busy. She was busy. The vicar wanted his chairs back and the brewer wanted to know if he could bring the third barrel now, and Mother Breck needed taking home, and Robin needed taking home before the hour was up, and the Hollin brothers were arguing about whose turn it was to bank the fire. She did all of it. She did it very fast. She was at the corner of the town hall with her keys in her hand and her list folded in her pocket, sending the last of the children home, when he was there.

Beside her. In the snow. With his hands in his coat.

"Odette," he said.

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to." She didn't look at him. She looked at the children going away across the square in the dark with their lanterns. "You're going to say *thank you for the lanterns*. And I'm going to say I don't know what you mean, and you're going to say you know it was me, and I'm going to say it was the girls, and you're going to say the girls said it was me. And then we're both going to stand here." She put her keys in her pocket. "I've got the vicar's chairs to see to."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"I wasn't going to say that," he said.

Odette looked at him, then. She couldn't help it.

He was looking at the town hall. At its steps, and its door, and the window of the room on the left where her desk was, where the lamp was still lit, because she always left it lit on midwinter night, in case.

"Nine years ago," he said. "The autumn. When we went east."

Odette went very still.

"You kept her," he said. "The whole season. She was eight. Her mother had gone to the war and her father had gone with her mother and the whole town was frightened, and you walked down that road every morning before light with a basket, and kept her warm. And fed. And the fire never out." He was quiet. "And Rowan in the doorway, burning eggs."

"He couldn't boil one," said Odette. Her voice wasn't quite right. "Not one. The whole season."

"I know. Alea told me. She was eight. She said *Odette's porridge was better than yours and Rowan's eggs were worse*." He breathed out, white, in the cold. "And then I came home. On my own. In the morning. And you were at the stove."

Odette didn't say anything. She couldn't.

"You didn't turn round," said the man. "I remember that. I came in the door and Alea came down the ladder and Rowan went to the bed and you stood at the stove with your back to me and stirred the porridge until it burned." He was looking at the town hall window. "I thought, for years, you couldn't bear to look at me. I thought it was because of what I'd come home without."

"It was," said Odette.

"It wasn't," said the man. "Rowan told me. At his table. In the autumn. When I knocked." He turned his head and looked at her. She couldn't see his face. She never had. "He said you came home that morning and sat at your desk in the town hall and didn't move till the next day. He said you said *I couldn't turn round, Rowan. If I'd turned round I'd have seen him. Not his face. Him. The way he was standing*."

Odette looked at the snow.

"I'd have seen it," she said. Very low. "All of it. In your back. I knew I would." She swallowed. "I didn't want to. I didn't want to be the one who saw it first. It wasn't mine. It was hers. The girl's." She stopped. "So I stirred the porridge."

The man didn't say anything for a long time.

"Thank you," he said.

"For burning the porridge."

"For not turning round," he said.

***

They stood there a while longer. At the corner of the town hall in the snow, with the square emptying round them and the bonfire going down to red behind its ring of briar and the ring of water turning, still, slowly, round it, with nobody watching. The forty lanterns on the lake road going out one by one, down the hill, as the girls went back down it with a snuffer, laughing.

"The curtain," said the man, eventually.

"What about it."

"It's crooked."

"It's meant to be crooked," said Odette. "I hung it crooked. So the duck faces in." She sniffed. "A duck's a cheerful thing to wake up to."

"Mabry asked you."

"Mabry asked me." She looked at the town hall door. "I said it was at the cleaner's. He said *tell the cleaner I hope it's warm*." She was quiet. "He knows. He's not a clever man, Mabry. But he's sat at that table thirty-one years, and he's nobody's fool about his own duck."

The man laughed.

Out loud, at the corner of the town hall, in the snow, so that the last of the children going home across the square turned round to see what it was. Odette had heard him laugh like that perhaps twice in twelve years. She'd never once been the reason.

"And the lining," he said. "The blue flowers."

Odette stopped.

She'd thought nobody would notice. She'd thought he wouldn't. Small blue flowers, on the inside of a red curtain, sewn in by lamplight at her kitchen table in the autumn out of a cloth she'd had in a drawer for nine years. A cloth she'd bought at the market at Hethe the summer before the war, for no reason, because it was pretty. And never used. And put away, nine years ago, the morning after the porridge, because she couldn't look at it.

Because there was a grave in the east, Rowan had told her once, years later, in a rare moment. On a knoll on the Redmarch. Where nothing grew. And every year, when the man went east alone and came back, he'd say nothing, and Rowan would ask nothing, and once, only once, the man had said, at Rowan's gate, *the flowers came up again*. White ones, and blue.

Odette had never seen them. She'd never been east in her life.

She'd lined the curtain with them anyway. So the girl would have them in her window, here, at the lake. At both ends.

"I had the cloth," said Odette. Very stiffly. "In a drawer. It was going to waste."

The man didn't say anything.

He put his hand on her shoulder. Briefly. The way you'd touch a gatepost you'd known all your life, going past it in the dark. And then took it away.

"Good night, Odette," he said.

"Good night," said Odette.

He went across the square toward the lake road. Not hurrying. The way he walked everywhere. And at the top of the road, by the last lantern still lit, five girls and a fox girl were waiting for him, with a snuffer and a squid glowing gold, and they went down together into the dark.

***

She went back into the town hall.

Up the steps, through the door, into the room on the left, where the lamp was still lit, the way she always left it on midwinter night. She sat down at her desk. She took off her good coat and hung it on the back of the chair and took out her list, folded, from the pocket, and smoothed it flat on the blotter.

It was all ticked. Every line. Wood, bread, ale, chairs, the speech. She'd ticked them as they went.

All but the last.

*Lanterns. The lake road. All of it. Forty.*

*He'll hate it.*

*Good.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she took her pen and drew a line through *He'll hate it*. Not hard. You could still read it under the line.

And beside *Good*, very small, in her sharp neat hand, where nobody but her would ever see it, she wrote:

*He walked up the middle.*

She ticked it. She put the list in the ledger, at the back, with the other one, the list of people who thought she was a hard woman, which she noticed for the first time in years had got quite short.

Then she turned the lamp down, and sat in the dark for a while, in the town hall of Wrenhaven on midwinter night, with her hands folded on the ledger, and let her face do whatever it liked.

There was nobody there to see.
