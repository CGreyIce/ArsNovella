Salty walked him out at dusk, the way he had every night since the autumn, and stopped at the foot of the slope.

Yan stopped too.

He hadn't meant to, the first time he thought about it, in the summer. He'd thought about it for weeks. On the wall, at night, with Suzu round his neck and the band a mile west of the fort full of men now, two hundred of the Marshal's foot in grey and blue who'd marched in eight days ago and gone out at dusk the same evening to stand on a line in the red, a man's width apart, all night, all along the west side of a ring forty miles round. He'd stood on the wall and felt their candles go out along the band in the dark, one after another, a long string of small warm lights on a line that had been cold all summer; and felt the cold under the grave, a mile and a half behind him, lean toward the band, and find it full of holes, and lean less.

And he'd thought about Salty, at the foot of the slope. Every night. For ten months.

"Will you come up," said Yan.

Salty didn't move.

He was standing in the grass at the bottom of the knoll where he always stood, with his hands in his pockets and his face to the hill and the ash on the top of it against the last of the light. He'd never once come up. He'd said why, once, on the wall, the only time Yan asked: *it's his. It's not mine to walk up. Not without being asked.*

"He's not here," said Salty.

"No," said Yan. "He's at the lake. In a seat a tree grew him. He didn't go east this year." He looked up the hill. "So I'm asking."

Salty looked at him for a long time.

"Why," he said.

"Because I need somebody who carries things," said Yan.

***

He'd worked it out in the summer. Slowly, the way he worked everything out: sitting with it, on the knoll, every night, with his hand on the grass by the spike, not reaching. He hadn't reached again since the night of the frost. Aldous had said *you were right to look and wrong to reach* and he'd been both, and his arm had been wrapped to the shoulder in Maigo's leaves for a week, and he'd lain on the cot in the infirmary with the smell of bread in his nose that wouldn't go away and thought.

*Too cold to move*.

That had been the trouble. He'd had it in his hands, the one at the edge, pressed against the iron: grey under the black, an ordinary person's light, put out and kept so long it had forgotten how to go anywhere. And he'd tried to carry it, and it hadn't come. Not because it was stuck. Because it was cold. Because something that's been put out can't move until somebody lights it, a little, enough. And the thing in him that might have lit it had been behind a lock.

So he'd asked.

Not out loud. In the way he asked anything: by watching. The channel all summer had been full of people warming things.

Rachael, on the steps, with four people's warmth coming down Deston's thread into her hands so she didn't run out halfway. *Halfway is worse than not starting.*

Thornwall, a quarter of a mile of a town holding hands from the postern to the bridge, the warmth going down arm after arm into a healer's back, into a boy coming up out of three years under the ice.

Thea, at the lake, in the middle of the night, writing *it doesn't tell anything. it agrees.*

And Ossie. Pushing. From underneath. With his hands flat on the inside of his own face for half a year, so that when the thing holding him let go, he went *up*.

He'd sat on the knoll the night after Ossie came home with his hand on the grass and *Hue* down in the dark, not reaching. Just looking. At the knot. At the hundreds of small packed lights, put out and kept. At the one at the edge, pressed against the iron.

It was still there. It was still pressed against the iron. It had been pressed there, he thought, since before he'd come; since before the spike went in, maybe; since whenever it was poured into a woman on a rise who had no one to fill, and sank into the ground under her with all the others.

Pressed against the iron. The way Ossie had been pressed against his own face.

*Pushing*.

He'd sat there for a long time, that night.

Then he'd written it down. In his own book, the one he'd kept since the first year in a hand nobody else could read, at the back, on the page where he'd written *Carry* in the winter and nothing under it.

*It's not too cold to move. It's too cold to move on its own. It's been pushing against the iron the whole time. It wants up. I don't pull it. Pulling is what the ring does. I warm it enough to go where it's going, and I carry it the last bit, and I open my hands.*

*I can't warm it with mine. I tried. It took my arm.*

*Somebody warm.*

***

Salty came up the hill.

Slowly. Behind Yan, a pace back, the way he walked behind him everywhere; up through the long grass on the side of the knoll in the dusk, past the ring of children's stones, past the place where Maigo's seedling had been grafted on to the ash's root last summer and grown into a sapling of its own, to the top. To the cairn, and the headstone, black, half a sword, with the English on it that Ice had cut himself. *TO DIWA, MY LOVE*. To the iron point going down into the ground in front of it, saying its word into the dark. To the ash, with its leaves all turned one way in no wind.

Toward the lake.

Salty stopped at the headstone. He stood there with his hands out of his pockets now, at his sides, and looked at it, and Yan saw his shoulders do something.

He'd carried it. Salty. Out of the fort on his back the first winter, with his ribs knitted crooked from the last probe, a mile, and wouldn't put it down. *Not till it's there*. And then he'd stood at the foot of the slope every night for ten months after and never once come back up to see it standing.

"It's right," said Salty. His voice was strange. "It's standing right."

"Garrow said that," said Yan. "When it went in."

"Yeah." Salty didn't look away from it. "He was right."

Aldous was at the foot of the knoll. Yan could feel him there: a Bonfire burning low and steady like a hearth at night, old, patient, with his stick and his chaplain's black, sitting on a stone in the grass where Salty usually stood. He'd come out after them. He hadn't come up. He said, when Yan told him what he meant to do, *I'll be at the bottom. In case you need words said*.

Maigo was by the trunk of the ash with his root foot in the earth, very still. He'd been there since noon. He said the tree was awake, and listening, and had been all day.

And the channel was quiet.

Yan had noticed at noon, the way everybody had. Thirty-four people, not writing. Holding it open. On the one day of the year that had always been Ice's, on his own, on this hill, they'd all of them said nothing at all.

He sat down by the headstone. Cross-legged, on the grass, the way he always sat. He put his right hand flat on the ground beside the spike.

Suzu came down off his collar on to his shoulder. Her cool weight. Her tongue going and going against his neck.

"Salty," he said.

"Yeah."

"Put your hand on my back."

Salty looked at him.

"Flat," said Yan. "Between my shoulders. And don't take it off. Whatever happens. If it goes the way it went in the spring, if I yell, if my arm goes, don't take it off and don't pull me away. Suzu'll pull me away if I need pulling." He didn't look round. "I need you to stay."

Salty didn't say anything for a while.

Then he knelt down in the grass behind Yan, a big short man with arms like a wrestler's and a greatsword on his back he'd taken off and laid down by the cairn, and put his hand flat between Yan's shoulders.

It was warm.

Yan had known it would be. He'd felt Salty's candle since the first day, on the road east: a furnace. That was the word for it. Not a candle; a furnace, big and stupid and all at once, the biggest light of any of the thirty-five, too big to hold steady, which was why Salty had always been slow and always would be and why, when it came, it came big. He'd felt it from across the fort. He'd felt it every night from the top of this hill, a mile and a half off, at the foot of the slope, burning.

Now it was a hand on his back.

He took his notebook out and opened his window, the way he'd promised himself he would, before.

***

The iron first.

The old point, saying its word into the dark. *Stay*. A hand flat on a lid. It was tired. It had been tired for two years, and saying it anyway, the way a man on a wall says *hold* to himself at four in the morning with nobody listening.

He felt it notice him. The way it always did. Not unfriendly. The way a dog at a gate notices someone it knows.

Under the iron, the cold.

The knot. Small and dense and very still, under the grave, in the old hole where the point went in. Hundreds of them, more, packed like coal in a scuttle, put out and kept. He'd felt it lean every night all summer: west, toward the band, toward the edge of a ring being written deeper every midnight, the way a dog leans at a door when it hears somebody on the step. It wasn't leaning now. Not much. The band was full of holes. Two hundred small warm lights standing on a line in the red, and the writing couldn't get round them, and the call that the writing made was broken in two hundred places, and the cold under the grave could hardly hear it.

*Longer's not nothing*.

And at the edge of the knot, pressed against the iron, the one.

He found it at once. He'd found it every night since the spring. Grey under the black; the plain grey of an ordinary person living an ordinary life. Very faint. Very cold. Pressed up against the point where it went into the ground, the way it had been the first time. Held there, he'd thought, by all the others behind it.

He'd been wrong.

He felt it now, properly, the way he'd never let himself feel it in the spring because he'd been too busy reaching. It wasn't being held there. It was *pushing*. Very slowly, with whatever it had left, which was almost nothing, against the iron. Up. The way a seed pushes against a stone. The way a boy at Thornwall had pushed against the underneath of his own face for half a year. It had been pushing up against the word *stay* for ten years, and the word had held it, because that was what the word was for, and it had gone on pushing anyway.

It wanted up.

It didn't want west, toward the ring. It didn't want to be poured. It didn't want to stay. Of all the lights packed into that dark, put out and kept, it was the one that had never once stopped trying to go where lights were supposed to go when somebody died.

*Find what it's already doing. And agree.*

He didn't pull.

***

He cupped it.

The way he'd done it in the spring. The way he cupped a colour with the dying. Very gently, in the hollow of his attention, like a moth in his hands. And the cold came up off it into him at once, the way it had: into his palm, his wrist, the absence of feeling, the place where his arm stopped being there.

He let it.

He didn't fight it this time. He didn't pull against it. He let the cold come up his wrist, and held the light in his cupped attention, steady, not moving it anywhere; and with the rest of himself, with *Kin*, with the thing that felt the threads between people, he reached back.

Not down. Back. Behind him. To the hand between his shoulders.

To the furnace.

It came all at once.

That was the trouble with Salty. It always had been. Big and stupid and all at once. Yan reached along the thread for a candle's worth of warmth and got the whole forge door thrown open: heat, enormous, roaring, more than he'd ever felt in one person, coming down Salty's arm into his back and through him like a tide coming up a beach, so much of it that for a moment he couldn't feel the cold at all, or the light in his hands, or anything but heat.

Too much.

He felt the light flinch from it. The small grey cold thing in his cupped attention, pressed against the iron, flinching, the way your eyes flinch from a lamp brought too close in the dark. If he let all of it through, it would be too much. It would be like pouring a kettle on a frozen hand.

*Lantern*. Hold a thing steady while you do something else.

He held it.

Salty's heat, roaring into him from behind. The cold, coming up his wrist from the light in front. Both at once. And he held them, the way you'd hold two ends of a rope in a current, steady, not letting either go, not letting either have all of him; and with the hollow in the middle of himself where the two met, very carefully, a hair at a time, he let a candle's worth of Salty's warmth go down his arm.

Into his hand.

Into the light.

***

It lit.

Not much. Not like a candle. Like an ember when you blow on it in the morning: a faint, faint orange coming up under the grey, from the inside, the way colour comes back into a cold hand. He felt it happen in his cupped attention, the smallest thing, a light that had been put out for longer than he could tell catching, just barely, at the very bottom, the way a wick catches.

And it moved.

On its own. That was the thing. He didn't move it. He held it, and warmed it, and it moved: up. The way it had been trying to move for ten years. Against the iron. Against *stay*.

And the iron—

He'd been afraid of this. He'd written it in the book and underlined it. *The point's job is to hold them. If one goes past it, what does the point do?* He'd lain awake on it. Aldous had said, in the spring: *if it ever stops holding them, they'll go where they're called*. And for ten years every one of them would have been called west, to the ring, to be poured.

The iron noticed.

He felt it notice. The tired old word in the point, saying *stay* into the dark the way it had for ten years, feeling something press up against it that was warm, now, a little, and moving on its own, and not going west. Not going toward the ring. Going *up*. Where lights went.

And the iron let it.

He didn't know how else to say it, afterwards. To Aldous, to the channel, to Ice, a long time after. The word didn't change. *Stay* went on saying *stay*. But a word that holds a door shut against a crowd isn't a word against a person going out the right way. The iron had been holding them in so that they couldn't be called out to the ring. It had never been holding them in so that they couldn't go up.

It let one go.

***

He carried it.

Up. Past the point. Through the dark of the old hole, through the earth, through the roots of the ash that were round the iron like fingers, through the grass under his palm. Not pulling. Carrying. The way you'd carry somebody who's trying to walk and can't quite, with your arm under theirs, going where they're going, at their pace. It went up through him, his hand and his wrist and the frost, and he let it, and Salty's heat came down his back into him to meet it, and he held both, steady, the hollow in the middle.

And then it was in his palm.

Not his attention. His actual palm. He felt it there: a weight that wasn't a weight, a warmth that was very small and very new, lying in the hollow of his right hand on the grass by the spike like an ember somebody had passed him. Grey, with the faint orange under it, coming up.

He opened his eyes.

He could see it. He'd never been able to see them; he felt them, he'd always felt them, it was his lane. But he could see this one. A small faint light in his hand in the dark on the top of the knoll, grey and gold, like a candle seen through a window a long way off. Somebody. A person. He couldn't have said who, or when, or where they'd lived; he'd never been able to, with anybody, and he wasn't going to start now. But he knew the smallest thing about it, the way he always knew the smallest thing.

Bread. An oven with a cracked door. Somebody who'd got up in the dark every morning of their life to light it, and stood there in the warm with their hands on the dough while the town was still asleep, and thought about nothing at all.

Suzu looked at it.

He felt her look. She was on his shoulder, her head up, her tongue going; she'd been on his shoulder for the whole of it, and she was hungry, she was always hungry, and here was a light in his palm, an ordinary grey light, the kind she'd eaten a hundred times off the dying on the road and the dead after the probes. Warm, now. Lit. Food.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she put her head down against his neck, and didn't.

*Not mine*, Yan thought. Aldous's word. The promise. *Not the ones under the red*. These had never been the red's. Aldous had said that too. *Those were never ours*.

Not anybody's.

He opened his hand.

***

It went up.

Slowly, at first. Off his palm, a hair, the way a spark comes off a fire and hangs for a moment as if it's deciding. Grey and gold in the dark over the knoll, at the height of his face, smelling of bread.

Then faster.

Up. Past the top of the headstone. Past the ash, whose leaves turned, all of them, at once, with no wind, not toward the lake this time but up. Past the top of the ash. Up into the dark over the red, a small faint light going up and up the way a spark goes up a chimney on a still night, getting smaller, and smaller, until it was a star among the stars, and then he couldn't tell which one.

Gone where it was going.

At the foot of the knoll, very quietly, Aldous was saying the words. The old church words, the ones he'd said over every grave on the Redmarch the year after, eleven years ago, the ones Yan had never understood and never asked about. Over somebody he'd never known. Somebody who'd never been his.

Under the grave, the cold was quieter.

By one.

Yan felt it. The knot, the packed dark, the hundreds; and one fewer of them. A gap at the edge, pressed against the iron, where something had been pushing for ten years and wasn't now. And the iron, saying *stay*, a hair less tired. As if one less thing were leaning on the lid.

He sat on the grass with his right hand open on the ground and frost on it to the wrist, not past, and blood coming out of his nose, and Salty's hand still flat between his shoulders, shaking.

"*Yan*," said Salty.

"I know."

"I'm *cold*." Salty sounded astonished. "I'm never cold. I've never been cold in my life. I'm *cold*."

"I know," said Yan. "I took some." He wiped his nose with the back of his frozen hand. "You've got loads. You'll be warm by breakfast."

Salty didn't take his hand off his back. He'd said he wouldn't.

***

He looked at his window on the grass, with Suzu's head against his neck and the ash's leaves coming slowly back round, all of them, to the lake.

He knew what it would say. He'd known when he opened his hand.

Nothing else.

Nobody else wrote anything. He'd thought they might. They didn't. The door stayed open, and the stair stayed empty, and it was past midnight on the first day after the eleventh, and thirty-four people had said nothing all day and nothing now; and Yan sat on the top of the knoll with his frozen hand on the grass and understood that they were leaving the last of it for somebody who never wrote.

Who didn't. He never had.

But a long way west, Yan thought he felt it anyway. Not in the channel. Down *Kin*, down the thread he'd felt since the north gate at Valcrest two summers ago, the longest thread he had, that went from this hill all the way across the kingdom to a lake; a thread that had been taut all day, all ten years, like a line with something heavy on the other end.

It went slack. A little. The way a line goes slack when whatever's on the other end stops pulling, and sits down.

***

They went down the hill at one. Salty first, this time, a pace ahead, with the greatsword on his back again and his hands in his pockets because they were cold; and Yan behind him, with his arm wrapped to the wrist in his own scarf and Suzu round his neck.

At the foot of the slope Aldous was on his stone. He got up when they came down. He looked at Yan's hand. At Salty. At the hill behind them, and the ash on it with its leaves turned toward the lake.

"I said the words," said Aldous.

"I heard," said Yan.

"I've never said them for somebody who wasn't mine." Aldous leaned on his stick. "Eleven years I've said them over the red. Every one of them was mine. I buried them." He looked up at the top of the knoll for a long time. "That one had been waiting a great deal longer than eleven years for somebody to say them."

"It smelled of bread," said Yan.

Aldous looked at him.

"Somebody baked," said Yan. "Every morning. In an oven with a cracked door." He held his wrapped hand against his chest. "I'll keep that. I always keep the smallest thing. I'll smell bread for the rest of my life."

Aldous didn't say anything for a while.

"There are worse things to keep," he said.

They walked back to the fort along the track in the dark, the three of them, past the burned stump of the old signal post, with the band a mile west of them full of men and holes and the cold under the grave a mile and a half behind them, quieter by one, and the iron over it saying its word a little less tiredly into the dark.

Hundreds more. One at a time. It would take years.

*Longer's not nothing*, Yan thought. *Longer's how anybody gets there*.

He'd go up tomorrow night, and see if another one was pushing.
