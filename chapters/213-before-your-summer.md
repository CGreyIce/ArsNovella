The elves folded their blankets every morning, and on the last morning Yan watched them do it for the last time.

They'd done it for a hundred and twenty mornings. He'd counted; he couldn't help it. In the chapel, on the stone floor between the benches, where Aldous had put down straw and blankets for them on the first night because there was nowhere else in the fort that three people who'd walked out of a song could sleep without half the garrison coming to look. They'd never once lain under the blankets. They slept on the straw in their lichen-coloured coats, the three of them in a row with their staves beside them, and in the morning they folded the blankets they hadn't used into neat squares and stacked them on the end of the bench, and Aldous unstacked them at night and laid them out again. Neither side had ever said anything about it. It had become, Yan thought, a kind of conversation. *We know you meant it kindly. We know you know we won't*.

This morning Tavrin folded hers, and Oen his, and Liraun hers, and they stacked them on the end of the bench the way they always had. And then Liraun, who was the youngest and had pale hair and whom the roots loved best, picked up the top one again, and shook it out, and spread it over the back of the bench, open, the way you'd leave a blanket for somebody who was coming in later and might be cold.

Aldous, in the doorway, saw her do it. He didn't say anything. He put his hand over his mouth.

*She has learned your manners*, said Suzu, in Yan's head, in her cool low voice, from his collarbone. *It took her four months. That is fast, for them*.

"Is it?"

*Very*, said Suzu. *They are not quick people. They are thorough*. She was quiet a moment. *The old one is looking at you*.

He knew. He'd felt it. He could always feel Tavrin looking; it was like standing near a lamp that's been lit for a very long time and gone down low and steady, a colour he'd had no name for when they came and still hadn't. Old silver. Burning slowly. All three of them burned like that, the slow cold silver of something that had been alight for a hundred years and meant to be alight for a hundred more, and all three of them leaned, always, a little, the way all souls leaned toward what they loved. Toward the white snake on his collarbone.

He looked up. Tavrin was on the far side of the chapel, by the chest where Aldous kept the white cloths, with her stave in her hand and her silver ear-rings catching the window, looking at him with her winter-ice eyes. Not at Suzu. At him.

She'd looked at him like that on the first night, at the west gate, in the snow. She'd put out one long grey finger, without asking, and touched the point of his ear, and said, to nobody, in a voice like somebody reading something written very small: *a man. Made over in an elf's shape. With a snake under it. It is a very good drawing*. And then she'd knelt to Suzu, all three of them had, in the snow, and said a word that had made Aldous drop his book.

She'd looked at him a good deal, since. Not often. But for a long time, each time.

"Today," said Tavrin. Across the chapel, to all of them, in the kingdom's tongue, rusty and careful, the way she'd learned it a very long time ago from people who were dead. "At the evening. We go at the evening."

"You said before our summer," said Aldous. From the door, through his fingers.

"It is nearly your summer," said Tavrin. "We are slow. If we go at your summer, we will be home at your autumn. Our forest is a long way past the end of your maps." She looked at the blanket Liraun had left open on the bench. "We have stayed longer than we meant."

"Why?"

Tavrin was quiet for a moment.

"Because something here was held," she said, "and we wanted to see if it would stay held without us."

***

Nobody had quite forgiven them.

That was the truth, and everyone in Cinderhold knew it, and nobody said it, which was how things worked in a fort on a border: you lived with people for months in a space the size of a field, and you didn't say the thing, because you'd still have to eat breakfast with them tomorrow.

The night on the knoll. Twenty-six days ago. Yan had been against the cairn with Suzu in his lap, torn, going grey as ash himself from what he'd given her; he hadn't seen most of it. But he'd felt it. Everyone's light, all night, in a ring on the green round the black stone: the forty, and Group 3, and Rhea's going white with pain when her arm went, and Garth's going small and thin when the hunter's arm took him in the ribs, and Salty's, bearing, all night, a furnace with the doors shut, steady. And outside the ring, on the north edge of the green, three lights of old slow silver, burning, watching.

They'd watched it all. They hadn't moved. Oen had moved once, when the second one went past them close enough to touch, on its way to Wendy; Yan had felt his light jump toward it like a dog to a door. And Tavrin's hand had come down on his arm. And he'd stopped.

*If we had held it, it would be ours*. She'd said it to Salty, on the knoll, at dawn, with the stone still ringing. Slowly, in the kingdom's tongue, so there'd be no mistake. *Then every spring it would wait for us. And so would you. And we go home before your summer*.

Salty had said *that's a stupid rule*, the way he'd said it in the forge in the winter about their other rule, the one about not making anything that would last longer than its maker. And Tavrin, in the forge, had laughed. On the knoll she hadn't.

Yan understood it. He'd understood it on the knoll, with Suzu in his lap; he'd heard her say it and thought, *yes*, and then thought about Garth's ribs and felt sick. He'd said so, later, in the channel, to Kei, in a strip down the snake cord, because there was nobody else he could say it to who'd understand both halves at once. *She's right. It was the only way it'd stay ours. And Garth could have died and she knew that and stood there. Both of those are true and I hate it*.

And Kei had written back, from Thornwall, in his small careful hand: *Hisui says that's what being right feels like, mostly*.

So nobody said it. And the elves knew nobody said it, because the elves knew everything, slowly. And Oen, who was young and whose ears went red when he was angry and flat when he was ashamed, had carried Garth back across the red that morning on his back like a sack, an hour's walk, without being asked and without saying a word, and set him down at the infirmary door in front of Hesper, and gone and stood in the yard with his ears flat for the rest of the day.

His ears had been flat, on and off, for twenty-six days.

***

Maigo took Liraun out to the knoll in the afternoon, and Yan went with them, because it was Thursday.

It had been a Thursday the first time. Maigo had told him. The first spring, when he'd carried a sapling out across the red in a ring of brown earth with the thaw still in the ground, and planted it in the ring of flowers at the head of a grave nobody had told him about, so that whoever was there wouldn't be alone; and every Thursday since, for two years, he'd walked out and told it the week. Two hours there and two hours back in the snow. An hour in the summer. In the snow last winter it had been asleep, every leaf laid in a ring on the black stone, and he'd talked to it anyway.

It was awake now. It had been awake since the night three walkers came north out of the south and the roots of the whole downs leaned toward them, and it had woken with one green leaf turned south. It was in full leaf now, the whole crown of it. And since the knoll, since the stone rang, every leaf on it had been turned in. Toward the middle. Toward the cairn and the stone and whoever was standing there. *She's come off watch*, Maigo had said, at dawn, with his bells ringing.

The green went all the way down the slope now, to the meeting of the two dry riverbeds, and out along them. Two summers ago it had been a ring a stride wide round a cairn. Liraun walked onto it barefoot, and Yan watched the grass under her feet go a shade greener where she stepped, the way it had in the snow on the first night, and stay that way.

She'd never walked onto the green before. None of them had. On the first morning Aldous had walked them out, they'd stopped at the edge of it and wouldn't step on, and Tavrin had said *it is not ours*, and they'd stood at the edge all morning looking at the cold under it and gone grey.

Liraun walked onto it now. All the way. To the ash. And put her hand flat on the bark, the way Maigo did, the way the man had, two summers ago, on the day he sat against the cairn with the sword across his knees.

"Hello," said Liraun. To the tree. In the kingdom's tongue, which she spoke worst of the three of them, worse even than Oen. "We go today."

The leaves moved. There wasn't any wind.

Maigo was standing beside her with his root foot sunk an inch into the green and his antlers full of leaves, very still, which on Maigo was the loudest thing in the world. He'd been quiet all the way out. He'd asked Yan four questions on the walk, which was the fewest Yan had ever heard him ask in an hour, and none of them had been about the elves.

"Is she going to miss you?" said Maigo. Small. To Liraun. "The tree."

Liraun thought about it. She thought about everything for a long time; she was the youngest, she'd told them once, and the young ones were the slowest, because they hadn't learned yet which things you could answer quickly.

"No," she said. "She has you."

Maigo's face did something.

Liraun took her hand off the bark. She put it into her coat, and took something out, and held it in her palm for Maigo to see.

A seed. Small, brown, the size of a fingernail, the shape of a teardrop with a little wing on one side, like the ones that came off the sycamores along the road at Ashby and spun down.

"From home," said Liraun. "From the tree I was born under. I have carried it since I left." She looked at Maigo. "Tavrin says trees should not carry seeds. A tree drops them. That is what trees are for. She gave the old one's seed to the strong one, in the forge, because of that. You are a tree. You should not carry this."

"I'm not a tree," said Maigo. "I'm a treant. It's different. There's a song."

"I know the song," said Liraun. "I am not a tree either. So I will not give it to you to carry. I will drop it." She knelt down on the green at the foot of the ash, on the east side, where the roots went into the ground like fingers into dough, and pressed the seed into the earth with her thumb, a finger's depth, and smoothed the grass back over it. "There. It is not carried. It is dropped." She looked up at Maigo. "Now it is not yours. It is the knoll's. You only have to tell it things on your Thursdays."

Maigo sat down.

Not all at once. His root foot stayed where it was, sunk in the green, and the rest of him folded down round it the way a sapling folds in a high wind, until he was sitting on the grass beside the place where the seed was with his knees up and his antlers drooping and his face in his hands.

"I'm going to tell it so many things," said Maigo, through his fingers. "It's going to get so bored."

"Good," said Liraun. "Bored trees grow." And she put her arm round him, the way she'd put it round him in the forge in the winter when Tavrin told them about the old kind, the dying trees that walked once to the edge of the forest and fell. *Nothing is ending*, she'd said then. *Something is beginning. That is why we are glad*.

Yan stood at the edge of the green and watched them.

He looked at their lights. He couldn't help that either; since the grave, since Candle, he saw them all the time, the colours, whether he wanted to or not. Liraun's old slow silver, leaning toward the white on his collarbone, the way it always did. And Maigo's.

He'd seen Maigo's light for two years and a half and never had a word for it. Before the grave he'd only seen grain and lean: native or Earth, toward or away. Since the grave he'd seen colours, and Maigo's had been green, mostly, like Alea's, like Suzu's eyes, the green that meant somebody who loved things. But there'd always been something else in it, under the green, that he couldn't name. A warmth. A slowness. A thing that went down instead of out.

He saw what it was now. Looking at Maigo sitting on the green with his root foot in the ground beside a seed that wasn't his.

It was the same colour as the knoll.

*Yes*, said Suzu, very quietly, from his collarbone. *He is not one of the old kind. He is something new. The old one said so at the gate. A tree that walked out of a song*. She was quiet. *I have seen a great many trees, Yun. I have never seen one sit down and cry*.

***

Rhea found Oen at the west gate at the end of the afternoon, and Yan was on the wall, and saw it.

The elves were ready. They had very little to be ready with: their staves, their coats, a bag each of the dried fish they ate instead of bread, which Cook had been drying for them on the roof of the long hall for a week without being asked, the way Cook did things. They were standing in the arch of the west gate in the long gold light, the gate Rhea had kept open after dusk for the first time in four years on the night they came, because a tree person had asked her at midnight to leave it open and sat on the postern step all night with his foot under it.

Half the fort was on the west wall. Not to say goodbye; you didn't, in a fort. To look. The way they'd looked on the first night, when three people out of a song had stopped at the edge of the gate's shadow in the snow and Rhea had said *state your business at the king's gate* and Tavrin had said *names are not given at gates* and Rhea had said *they are at mine*.

Rhea came down the gate-stair with her left arm strapped across her chest in a sling of grey army cloth, where it had been for twenty-six days, and a barrel on her right shoulder.

She set the barrel down in the middle of the arch, in front of Oen. *Thump*. She put her right elbow on it.

Oen looked at it. His ears, which had been flat, went up.

"You'll lose," said Oen. In the kingdom's tongue, badly. He spoke it badly about everything except arm-wrestling, which he spoke fluently.

"I've lost every time," said Rhea. "Every time since the gate. Eleven before the knoll and four since, with the other one, after this went." She lifted her strapped left arm an inch in its sling. "Fifteen. I've counted. The whole garrison's counted." She put her right hand up, open, on the barrel. "Names are given at gates. So are rematches. At mine."

Oen looked at Tavrin.

Tavrin was leaning on her stave in the shadow of the arch with her face doing nothing at all, the way it did nothing when something was about to happen that she'd already decided not to stop.

"Both of you are very stupid," said Tavrin. "Go on."

Oen put his elbow on the barrel.

They locked hands. Rhea's big, square, scarred, red to the wrist. Oen's long and grey and thin as a branch, with the knuckles like knots in wood. Everyone on the west wall leaned forward at once; Yan felt it, the whole wall, every light on it, leaning toward the arch.

He knew what was going to happen. Everybody knew. Oen didn't move. Oen never moved; that was the thing about Oen and arm-wrestling. Rhea would put everything she had into it, all of it, a lifetime on a border, her whole Gleaming aura into the bones of her arm, and Oen's arm would simply not go anywhere, the way a tree doesn't go anywhere when you push it, and then after a while, when she'd emptied herself into it, he'd put her hand down on the barrel, gently, as if he were laying something on a table, and say *again*, which was the word he said best.

Rhea pushed.

Oen's arm didn't move.

Rhea pushed. Her face went red, then darker. The cords in her neck stood out like ropes. The barrel creaked. Yan saw her light, the Gleaming one, the silver sheen she put on a blade, go down her arm into her hand and into Oen's, the way it went down the steel; he'd seen her do it on the knoll with a sword in a hunter's ribs.

Oen's arm didn't move.

And then it did.

Not much. A finger's width. Toward the barrel. Oen's eyes went wide.

Yan looked at his light, because he couldn't help it.

Oen wasn't letting her. That was the first thing he saw, and he was glad of it, because Rhea would have known and it would have been worse than losing. Oen's light was all there, all of it, the old slow silver pushing back down his arm with everything it had, the way it had pushed back fifteen times. He wasn't holding back. He was trying.

But it was flat. That was the second thing. It had been flat, on and off, for twenty-six days, the way his ears had. Ashamed. Something in it that had gone toward a hunter on a knoll like a dog to a door and been stopped, and hadn't come back up.

And Rhea's wasn't. Rhea's was the colour Yan had seen it go on the knoll, white with pain and then something past white, when her arm broke and she'd said *AGAIN* to a thing twice her size and gone back in one-handed. She'd been carrying that for twenty-six days too. Not flat. Up.

Oen's hand went down.

Slowly. A finger. Two. Oen pushing with everything, his ears straight up now, his long grey face doing something Yan had never seen an elf's face do, which was strain. Rhea not saying anything. Rhea's eyes on his. Three fingers. A hand's width.

The back of Oen's hand touched the barrel.

Nobody on the west wall said anything at all.

Then the whole wall made a noise Yan had only ever heard once before, on the day a fisherman walked across the red with a rod over his shoulder and the scouts ran: not a cheer, not quite, a sort of roar that started low and went up, two hundred people at once, and banged off the arch, and went out across the red.

Rhea didn't let go.

She held Oen's hand flat on the barrel, his long grey fingers in her big red ones, and looked at him across it, and her face didn't do anything triumphant at all. It did the other thing, the thing Yan had seen it do perhaps three times in two years and a half: on the red, on her knees, with her sword laid down in front of her, the day the man came. And at Valcrest, in the arena, when she'd looked up at the fourth tier and put her hand on her chest.

"You carried Garth," said Rhea.

Oen's ears went flat again.

"An hour across the red," said Rhea. "On your back. With his ribs going. I saw you. You didn't say a word and you set him down in front of Hesper and you went and stood in my yard all day like a horse that's been shouted at." She didn't let go of his hand. "I'd have had the old woman's arm off if she'd put it on mine. I'd have gone. Course I would. I'd have gone and got killed and it'd have been the stupidest thing I ever did and I'd have done it anyway." She looked at Tavrin, in the shadow, and then back at Oen. "She was right. I hate it. She was right."

Oen didn't say anything. Elves didn't cry, Niko had said, on the knoll; they did something else instead. Yan watched Oen do it. It was a long slow breath, all the way down, and his light going up, slowly, the way a fire does when somebody opens the vent.

"Again," said Oen. The word he said best.

"No," said Rhea. "That's the last one. That's the one I keep." She let go of his hand. She stood up. She picked up the barrel on her one good shoulder and stood there in the arch with it, red-faced, sweating, with her left arm strapped to her chest and her hair coming out of its tie. "Next time," she said, "you come back. And I'll have two arms."

Oen looked at her.

"Yes," said Oen. Which Yan had never once heard him say. He was fairly sure nobody had taught him it.

***

Tavrin sang it once more, at dusk, in the arch, before they went.

Not all of it. Not over the names; that had taken all night, in the chapel, in the winter, all fourteen hundred and twenty-three of them, the names and then the numbers where there weren't names any more, with Liraun singing the low part and Group 3 sitting on the cold benches listening until dawn. Aldous had written *Sung* under the last line of the book the next morning, and at the top of the first page, in his own hand, the line that had been the elves' all along: *We lay white over them, for the Vael, who carries*.

She sang the rite. Just the rite, the old one, the words Aldous had said over every soldier he'd buried on the red for nine years and over a healer called Diwa at the end of every summer, that had worn down over generations of masters and their masters' masters until there was no tune left in it at all. She sang it the way it had been meant to be sung. In the arch of the west gate, with her stave in her hand and the red going dark behind her and the first stars coming out over it, and Liraun beside her singing the low part, and Oen, after a while, very quietly, joining in under them both, with his ears up.

Aldous stood at the foot of the gate-stair with his book shut against his chest and his eyes shut.

Yan knew the rite. He'd heard Aldous say it over the turned on the red, in the first year, over the ones nobody had names for. He'd heard it over the forty on the knoll, the night before, when Aldous lit a fire the size of the green and said it for the living, who weren't dead yet, just in case. He knew every line. He could have said it with Aldous.

So he knew, at once, when Tavrin sang a verse that wasn't in it.

Near the end. Before the last line, the elves' line, *for the Vael, who carries*. A verse Aldous had never said, that wasn't in the book, that had worn off the rite with the tune, generations ago, or had never been in the kingdom's half of it at all. Four lines, in the elves' own tongue, the old one Yan couldn't follow, and then, because Tavrin was looking at Aldous while she sang it and wanted him to have it, the same four again in the kingdom's, rusty and careful:

*And the one who walked with the white, / who sang her the way across, / who had no name we kept, / we lay white over him too.*

Suzu turned her head.

On his collarbone. She'd been lying there quietly since the chapel, cool and heavy and still, her head on the hollow of his throat, her eyes half shut. She lifted her head, at that, an inch, and turned it, toward the arch, toward the old grey woman singing, and her green eyes opened all the way.

She didn't say anything.

Yan waited. He'd learned that, in two years and a half: Suzu talked when she wanted to talk, and if you asked her a thing before she was ready, she'd say *not yet, Yun* and you'd have to wait twice as long. He waited. He felt her lying very still on his collarbone with her head turned toward the arch, the whole length of her, from his throat down under his collar and round his ribs, gone still in a way he'd never felt her go still. Not asleep. Not hunting. Listening. The way you'd listen to a song you'd heard once, a long time ago, from the next room.

The verse ended. Tavrin sang the last line. *We lay white over them, for the Vael, who carries*. And bowed her head, to the white snake on his collarbone, the way she'd knelt to it in the snow on the first night.

Suzu put her head back down. In the hollow of his throat.

She didn't say anything at all.

Aldous had opened his eyes. He was looking at Tavrin. He'd heard it too. He'd have heard it if it had been sung in the next valley; he'd said the rite for thirty-five years.

"Who is that one for?" said Aldous. Very quietly. "That verse. Who walked with the white?"

"Nobody knows," said Tavrin. "It has no name in it. It never had." She looked at him, and then, for a long moment, past him, at Yan, on the wall, with the snake round his neck. "It is the oldest part of the song. Older than the forest. We sing it because it is there. We do not know who it was for." She was quiet. "Perhaps that is why it lasted. Things with names wear off. You have seen that, chaplain. You said the wrong word with the right meaning for thirty-five years, and she heard you anyway."

Aldous opened his book. He took the pencil out of the spine, where it lived. He wrote something, on the inside of the back cover, under the line he'd written there after the knoll, *The knoll. The last morning of the old moon. Nobody*. Yan couldn't see what. It was four lines long.

***

Tavrin came up onto the wall to say goodbye to him.

She didn't need the stair. She went up the gate-stair the way the elves went up everything, without the steps creaking, and stood beside him at the parapet with her stave and looked out at the red, going dark, and the knoll, an hour off, a darker shape against the dark, with the green on it that you couldn't see from here any more but he could, because the green had a light of its own now.

"You are tired," said Tavrin.

She'd said it before. In the chapel, on the first night, with the fire going: *you are tired, the one who carries her*—. And Suzu had shouted. The first time Yan had ever heard her shout. *ENOUGH*. Tavrin had stopped at once, and bowed her head, and said, *it is hers to tell. I am sorry, Vael*.

Suzu didn't shout now. She lay on his collarbone, still, with her head down, and didn't say anything.

"Yes," said Yan.

"Since the grave," said Tavrin. "Since the colours."

"Since the colours."

Tavrin looked at the knoll for a long time.

"You will carry more than she can, soon," she said. "Not tomorrow. Soon. More than she has carried, in all the time she has carried." She didn't look at him. She looked at the knoll. "When you do, let her see you put it down."

Yan didn't say anything. He didn't know what to say. He felt Suzu, on his collarbone, very still.

"She has never had anybody carry anything for her," said Tavrin. "Not once. Not in all the long time. She does not know what it looks like, when somebody puts a thing down and is not hurt by it. She will think it costs you what it costs her." She was quiet. "Show her it doesn't. That is all. It is the only thing I know that you do not." She turned her head, and looked at him, the long look, the one she'd given him a dozen times in four months, as if she were reading something written very small. "And I do not know very much that you do not. Which is strange. For a man made over in an elf's shape, out of a drawing, not three years ago."

*Old woman*, said Suzu.

Very quietly. In his head. Not angry. Not *ENOUGH*. Just that. *Old woman*. The way you'd say someone's name to tell them they'd gone far enough, kindly, the way you'd put a hand on someone's arm.

Tavrin couldn't hear her. Nobody could hear her except Yan and a green snake at Thornwall. But Tavrin stopped, as if she had. She bowed her head, to the white on his collarbone, the way she'd bowed in the arch.

"Yes," said Tavrin. "I know. It is yours to tell." She straightened up. "I am glad I came this far to hear you say so little, Vael. Most of your words were worth the walk."

And she went back down the stair without it creaking.

***

They went at full dark, out of the west gate, the way they'd come in, and south.

Rhea held the gate. She stood in the arch with her strapped arm and her one good hand on the bar and didn't close it until they'd gone past the first row of markers on the downs road, which was the last place you could see anything from the arch with a lantern, and then for a while after that. The old woman had said, on the first night, that a gate should be loud. Rhea wasn't going to slam it on anybody's back.

They left almost no tracks. They never had. Yan watched them go from the wall, with his sight and not his eyes; the slow old silver of the three of them going away south across the dark, small, and smaller, toward the end of the maps. Tavrin in front, with her stave. Oen in the middle, with his ears up. Liraun last.

Liraun looked back once.

At the knoll. Not at the fort. An hour east across the red, where a seed from home was a finger's depth under the green at the foot of an ash, on the east side, where the roots went down.

Then she turned round and went on, and the three of them went over the first rise of the downs, and the grass behind them, all the way back to the west gate, was a shade greener than the grass on either side, a line of it across the dark like a path somebody had drawn with a finger, and it stayed that way. In the morning the whole fort came out to look at it. By the summer you could still see it, faintly, from the wall, if you knew where to look.

Group 3 sat on the west wall after the gate was shut.

Nobody went to bed. Maigo sat with his root foot hooked round the bottom of a merlon and his antlers drooping and didn't say anything for a whole hour, which had never happened. Wendy sat with her lavender headphones round her neck and her bow across her knees and counted, under her breath, nothing, just numbers, the way she did when she didn't know what else to do with her mouth. Niko sat with his hands in his pockets, chill, and looked south, and every so often closed his eyes and did something with his breathing that Tavrin had taught him in the winter, slowly, *you are thinking with your breath*, and opened them again.

And Salty sat with Oakes's rivet tin in his lap.

He'd brought it up from the forge. Yan hadn't seen him go. It was a small square tin with a dented lid that Oakes kept rivets in, and since the winter it had kept two other things: a list in Wendy's hand, on the back of a ration chit, that said *ELEVEN*, which was the dinners; and a seed.

Not Liraun's. The other one. The one Tavrin had given him in the forge in the winter, the first seed of a hundred-and-forty-year-old tree that had grown where the last of the old kind fell. *Plant it where you are going to stay*. And when Salty had said *I don't know where that is*, Tavrin had said *good. Then you will not plant it in the wrong place*.

He opened the tin. He looked at the seed for a long time.

"I don't know," said Salty. To nobody. "I still don't know." He shut the tin. "It weighs something. You can't lie to a weight."

Wendy stopped counting. She put her hand on the back of his neck.

"You'll know when you know," said Wendy. Which was his own line, from the winter, said back to him.

"Yeah," said Salty.

Yan sat at the end of the row with Suzu on his collarbone.

He looked at all of them, because he couldn't help it. The colours. Maigo's, the colour of the knoll. Wendy's, the green, with the counting going through it like a pulse. Niko's, two candles that weren't two any more, that had gone into one on a knoll at dawn and rung. Salty's, a furnace banked for the night, with a weight in it. Rhea's, down in the arch, with her one good hand still on the bar of the shut gate, gone up.

And his own.

He'd never been able to see his own. Nobody could, Tavrin had said; the eye doesn't see the eye. But he could feel it, tonight, for the first time, the way you feel your own pulse in your fingertips if you hold them still. Tired. The way it had been since the grave. Something in it that went out every day and didn't all come back, the way something had gone out of him into Suzu on the knoll and come back slowly, and not all of it.

And something else, under the tired, that he'd never felt before. Not a colour. A weight. Like a hand, held out, open, waiting for something to be put in it.

*You will carry more than she can, soon*.

*Yun*, said Suzu.

He waited.

*The old woman talks too much*, said Suzu. Cool and low, in his head, from the hollow of his throat. *She always did*.

He went very still.

*Always*.

She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. She put her head back down, and he felt her settle along his collarbone and down under his collar and round his ribs, cool and heavy, the whole length of her, and go to sleep, properly, for the first time since the knoll; and he sat on the west wall of Cinderhold with his friends in a row beside him and the stars out over the red and a line of green going away south across the dark, and didn't ask her what she meant.

Not yet.
