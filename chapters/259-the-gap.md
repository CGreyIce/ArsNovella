"A candle under a cup," said Hyvred.

Rui Xiang thought about that.

They were on the tower stair, about two-thirds of the way up, where there was a slit window facing east and a step wide enough for two people to sit on if one of them was small. He was small. Hyvred had sat down on it because her legs had stopped, which they did now, about two-thirds of the way up, since the evening the thing in the valley turned round. She said it was the steps. It wasn't the steps.

"Can you say that again?" he said. "Slower. Please."

"When you're doing your thing." She had her eyes shut. She'd been keeping them shut a lot, the last two days, when she wasn't on the roof. "I can see you. I always could. I see through stuff, it's what I do, I saw you on the first day in the hall when you were trying to hide behind John. You're a light. Quick. Sort of greeny-gold. Like a fish in a stream when the sun's on it." Her tail moved on the step. "When you do *Unseen*, you're still there. You just go small. Pulled in. Like somebody put a cup over a candle."

"And the cup?"

"What about it?"

"Can you see the cup?"

Hyvred opened her eyes and looked at him. The gold one with the rings in it and the gold one with the red mark. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she looked at the valley.

"Yes," she said slowly. "Yes. Huh. I never thought about it. The cup's the thing I see. Not you. The shape of you, all pulled in tight, with a hard edge round it." She frowned. "Everything else has soft edges. People. Sheep. The hillside. Light comes off them and goes out into everything else and you can't tell where they stop. You've got an edge. All the way round. Like a cup."

He sat on the step and turned his hands over in his lap.

*A man standing in a doorway in the dark*, Deston had said, on the road east. *You leave a hole in the threads the shape of you.* And John's motes, on the downs: a patch of firelit grass where he should have been. And now this. A candle under a cup.

Three different people, three different kinds of seeing, and every one of them had found the same thing. Not him. The edge of him.

"Thank you," he said.

"Why does it matter?"

He looked out of the slit window. East, over the roofs, over the long street and the south gate and the road going away into the hills. You couldn't see the valley from here. That was why he'd sat on this step.

"Because it's facing the town now," he said. "And it's in front of them. And from the tower you can't see past it."

Hyvred didn't say anything.

"You counted thirty last night," he said. "With your eyes shut. You said *thirty, I think, thirty or thirty-one, I can't tell, it's behind it.*" He said it politely. He said everything politely. It made people tell him things. "Since it moved, the fourteenth place is behind it. From here. Your sight goes in and doesn't come back. So you can't count them properly any more. And Thea can't read it without it looking down the line at her. And John's motes see them as people." He turned his hands over again. "So nobody knows how many there are. Or how fast they're coming. Or how long it is until the arc's done."

"Rui Xiang," said Hyvred.

"Somebody has to go and count it," he said. "Close. With a hand."

***

He asked the king that afternoon.

He'd asked Xav first. That was only fair. Xav was the one who'd stood up on the quay at Duskhollow and written *WE ASK* in the channel in capitals, and Rui Xiang had wanted *please*, and been outvoted four to one, and Xav had looked at him afterwards and said *you can put please on the next one*. He told Xav on the north wall, by Old Pruett's postern, where Xav stood now for most of the day because Pruett had asked for him.

Xav listened to all of it without moving. He was good at that. He'd always been good at that: the one who listened to the end of everything and then said one word.

"Okay," said Xav. "Ask him."

So he went down to the forge.

The king was at the anvil with Agnes, making a hinge. Not a shield; the shields were done, twelve on the first day and nearly fifty since, stacked along the back wall of the guardhouse, grey-black, with the hammer-marks still bright. A hinge. For the granary door, Agnes said, because the old one had a crack in it you could put your thumb in and the king had put his thumb in it and gone quiet. The forge was hot and red and loud, and the king was in his apron with his sleeves rolled, and his arms had more scars on them than Rui Xiang had known arms could hold.

He stood in the door and waited for the hammer to stop.

When it did, the king didn't look up. "You've been standing there a quarter of an hour."

"Yes, Majesty. Sorry."

"You weren't doing your thing." The king turned the hinge over in the tongs, looked at it, put it back in the fire. "I'd have felt you either way. I felt you on the first night on the bench, a hand from the tiger, all pulled in. Like a fist." He worked the bellows twice. "What do you want, son?"

Rui Xiang told him.

All of it. The cup and the edge, and Deston's doorway, and John's empty grass. The thirty or thirty-one behind the thing. The arc nobody could count. And the other part, the part he hadn't told Hyvred, which was that he wanted to know whether it could be stood in. Whether there was a way to be near the call and not be named. Because if there was, then a man could stand in the valley with it. And if a man could stand in the valley with it, then maybe one day a man could stand in the valley and bring somebody back.

He said it fast, the way he said everything. He said it clearly, so it could be followed. He said *please* twice, near the end, without meaning to.

The king took the hinge out of the fire and looked at it for a long time. Orange going to straw.

"No," said the king.

Rui Xiang nodded. He'd thought he would.

"Not *no*," said the king. He put the hinge on the anvil and didn't hit it. "*Not yet.* It's a word I've been borrowing all year from a man by a lake. It means the same thing when I say it." He looked up, finally. His eyes looked at Rui Xiang the way they looked at iron. "You want to walk eleven miles up a valley at night past a thing that's turned round to face us, with a dog from Kharvos asleep beside it, and count holes with your hand. And you've just told me the only trick you've got is one the girl on the tower can see through from a hundred and twelve steps up."

"Yes, Majesty."

"Then no. Not yet." He picked the hammer up. "When you can stand in front of her, on that roof, close enough to touch, with her looking straight at you with the red eye, and she can't find you. Then come and ask me again."

He hit the hinge. Once. Clean.

"And Rui Xiang."

"Majesty?"

"If you go without asking," said the king, "I'll know. And I won't send anybody after you. Because I won't have anybody to send who could find you." He hit it again. "So don't."

"I wouldn't," said Rui Xiang. "Please. I asked."

The king looked at him over the anvil for a moment longer. Then something happened in his face, under the beard. Not a smile. Something near it.

"You did," he said. "Go on."

***

He couldn't do it.

He tried for three days. He tried it the obvious way first, the way anybody would. *Unseen* was pulling in; the cup was the pulling in; so he'd stop pulling in. He'd do *Unseen* and then let go of the edge of it, only the edge, and keep the rest.

It didn't work like that. There wasn't a rest to keep. He let go of the edge and the whole thing went, all at once, like a held breath, and he was standing in the guardhouse yard in the middle of the afternoon in plain view, a slight young man with quick hands and a polite face, and a soldier coming past with a bucket said *afternoon* and went on.

He tried it slower. He tried it in pieces. He tried letting go of the left side and holding the right, and got a headache that lasted until supper, and Malcolm watching him from the mounting block, writing.

*IF Rui Xiang lets go THEN he's just Rui Xiang.*
*IF he holds THEN he's a cup.*
*Backup:*

Malcolm hadn't written a backup. He'd left the line under it empty, which he never did, and Rui Xiang had looked at the empty line for a long time.

On the second day he went into the forge while doing his thing, to test whether the king could really feel him, and the king said *afternoon, son* without looking up. He went out again and tried the edge on the stair, and got a nosebleed at the slit window, and sat on Hyvred's step with his head back and his sleeve to his nose and thought: *it's not supposed to be this. It's not supposed to be harder.*

On the third night he tried to get past Hild.

He hadn't meant to. It wasn't a test; it was just that she was in the yard, sitting on the rim of the well in the dark with her staff across her knees, listening north, the way she sat every night, and he had to cross the yard to get to the long room door, and he thought *I'll just see*. He did his thing. He pulled in, all the way, until there was nothing of him for anybody to feel; and walked. Slowly. On the frosted stones, along the far side of the yard from her, by the wall, where the shadow was.

"Evening, Rui Xiang," said Hild.

He stopped.

She hadn't turned her head. She had it a little on one side, the side with the eye, the way she always did; but the eye was shut.

"How?" he said. It came out before he could make it polite.

"You're the quietest thing in the yard."

He didn't understand. He came over, and let go of his thing, and stood in front of her, in the dark, by the well.

"Everything makes a little noise," said Hild. "All the time. You don't hear it. I do. I've had twenty-six years of listening harder than other people." She lifted one hand off the staff, a finger at a time. "The well. It breathes, a bit, down the shaft. The horses in the end stall. Pruett on the postern, up there, sucking his teeth. The frost on the stones, if you listen. The town." She put the hand back. "And then a place by the wall where none of it is. A hole in the noise. The shape of a person walking." She opened the eye. Grey, steady, on him. "That's you."

He stood there in the cold.

*A hole in the noise.* A cup. A gap in the threads. An empty patch of grass. A fourth kind of seeing, and the same thing again.

"The ones up there," he said. "At the fourteenth place. The threadless. Can you hear them?"

Hild didn't answer for a while.

"I stood near one, once," she said. "At Coldwater. The first year. The miller's wife, walking along a shepherd's track. Hyvred said there was nobody in her. A hole." She turned the staff over on her knees. "I heard her. A woman walking. Shoes on stone. Skirts. Breathing. Exactly the noise a woman makes, walking along a track on a cold afternoon. Not one bit more. Not one bit less."

"Then how did you know?"

"I didn't," said Hild. "Not by listening. That's what was wrong with her. There was nothing wrong with her at all." She looked north. "A real woman makes a bit more noise than that. Or a bit less. She's tired, or she's cross, or she's thinking about her husband. There's always something extra. The miller's wife had nothing extra. She sounded exactly like what she looked like." She was quiet. "Like a chair sounds like a chair."

Rui Xiang stood by the well for a long time.

Then he said *thank you*, and went in, and didn't go to bed. He sat on the floor of the long room by Tiger's pallet, a hand away, with his back against the wall and his knees up, and thought about it all night.

***

He'd had it backwards.

That was the thing. He'd thought, for three years, that being unseen was a matter of being *less*. Less to look at, less to feel, less of him; pull it all in, tighter, smaller, until there was nothing left for anybody's eyes to catch on. And it worked. It had always worked, on eyes. People's eyes went past him like water past a stone.

But a stone in a stream isn't nothing. A stone in a stream is the one thing in the stream the water has to go *round*.

The threadless weren't less. They weren't pulled in. They weren't hiding at all. They stood in a field in the frost the way furniture stands in a room: there, plain, exactly what they looked like and nothing extra, and everything went past them. Not round. *Past*. Eyes, threads, light, noise, the call. Nothing caught on them because there was nothing to catch on. No edge. No cup.

He'd been saying *don't look at me* for three years. Very loudly. With his whole aura. And everybody who knew how to listen had heard him say it.

The threadless didn't say anything.

He sat on the floor of the long room in the dark and thought about the hall in Solmere, the first morning. Thirty-five people from another world standing on a silver circle, shouting. He'd been behind John. Not hiding. He hadn't known how to hide, then. He'd just been standing there, quietly, the way he stood everywhere, a slight young man nobody looked at twice; and nobody had looked at him twice. Until Hyvred, crying at the ceiling, had looked at everyone.

He hadn't needed a cup then.

*Be exactly what's there*, he thought. *Not less. Not more. Exactly.*

He didn't know how to do that. He didn't know what it would feel like. But he knew, sitting on the floor with his back to the wall, that it was the right shape for a thing to be; the way you know a sentence is going to be right before you've got to the end of it.

Beside him, on the pallet, Tiger said, without opening his eyes: "You're thinking really loud."

"Sorry."

"S'fine." Tiger's tail moved on the blanket. "Thought you'd gone. You go so quiet when you do your thing. I always know when you've gone. It's like somebody shut a door."

Rui Xiang sat very still.

"Is it," he said.

"Yeah," said Tiger. "Like a door. Night."

***

On the fourth day he stopped trying to hold anything.

He sat in the guardhouse yard on the mounting block at noon with his hands in his lap and did nothing. Not *Unseen*. Not anything. He sat there and listened, the way Hild listened; to the well, and the horses, and Agnes's hammer in the forge, and the soldiers on the wall, and the town. And he let himself be the same as all of it. Not quieter. Not louder. A man sitting on a mounting block at noon. The cold coming up through the stone into him the way it came up into the stone. His breath going up the way the horses' breath went up, in the end stall, and the soldiers' on the wall.

It wasn't like pulling in. It was the opposite. It was like letting go of something he hadn't known he was holding, the way you let your shoulders down when somebody tells you they're up round your ears.

Malcolm came out of the long room with his book, and walked past the mounting block, and stopped at the well, and looked round the yard, and frowned.

"Has anybody seen Rui Xiang?" said Malcolm.

He was a pace away. Less.

"I'm here," said Rui Xiang.

Malcolm jumped so hard he dropped the book.

"You weren't—" He picked it up. He looked at Rui Xiang, sitting on the mounting block in plain daylight with his hands in his lap. "You weren't doing your thing. I'd have felt it. You always feel like a draught when you're doing your thing." He opened the book, and shut it, and opened it again. "You were just *sitting there*."

"Yes."

"I looked straight at you."

"Yes," said Rui Xiang. "Please write that down."

Malcolm wrote it down.

*IF Rui Xiang just sits there THEN he's not there.*

He looked at it. Then he crossed out *not there* and wrote *furniture*, and underlined it, and looked up at Rui Xiang with his face doing something it didn't do very often, which was not know what to say next.

"That's not a proper one," said Rui Xiang.

"I know," said Malcolm. "I haven't got a proper one. Go and show the king."

***

He went up the tower at dusk, a hundred and twelve steps, behind Hyvred.

She'd stopped two-thirds of the way up again, on the step by the slit window, and he'd waited with her until her legs came back. Then the rest of the way up, and out onto the roof, into the cold. The sun going down red behind the mountains. Bee on the parapet with her chalk. John at the north side with his hand in his pocket. Corvin with his board. And the king, in his coat, sitting against the north parapet with his back to the mountains, the way he always sat, who looked up when they came out of the trapdoor and didn't say anything.

Rui Xiang walked out into the middle of the roof and stood there.

"Hyvred," he said. "Please could you look at me. With the red one."

She turned round. She'd been about to sit down on the parapet with her back to the north and her eyes shut; she stopped. She looked at him. Then at the king. The king nodded, a little.

She let her eye go.

He felt it. He'd never felt anybody look at him before. Not the way she looked, with the red mark; it came onto him like a hand laid flat on the top of his head. And he did nothing. That was all. He stood in the middle of the tower roof at sunset with Hyvred's red eye on him, an arm's length away, and he didn't pull in, and he didn't push out, and he didn't say *don't look at me*. He let the cold come up through the stones into his feet the way it came up into the parapet. He let his breath go up the way Bee's went up, and Corvin's. He was a man on a roof at sunset. Exactly. Nothing extra.

Hyvred frowned.

The red mark went over him. Across him. Back. He watched it go; you could see it, if you knew to look, a small bright point in her eye moving like a pin on a map. It went over him again. It went to the parapet behind him and the trapdoor and Bee and back.

"Where's he gone?" said Hyvred.

Nobody said anything.

"I can see the roof," said Hyvred. Her voice had gone high. "I can see the parapet. I can see Bee. I can see you, Corvin, you're doing the thing with your thumb on the board. I can see the town. I can see—" She stopped. Her red eye went over him for the fourth time, and through him, and out the other side, to the south, to the town going down the hill in the dusk with all its lights. "There's a candle in a window down there. In the daytime. That nobody's noticed because it's daytime." Her tail went still. "There's a candle in a window in the—"

She stopped.

"Oh," said Hyvred. "*Oh.* That's you."

Her eye came back and locked onto him. The hand on his head. He felt it close.

"Forty," said Corvin, quietly, from his board. "I counted. A count of forty."

The king had got up.

He came across the roof slowly, in his coat, with his big scarred hands loose at his sides, and stopped in front of Rui Xiang, and looked down at him. He was a great deal taller. He looked at him for a long time, the way he'd looked at the hinge going from orange to straw.

"I didn't feel you either," said the king. "For about the same. I thought you'd gone down the stair." He was quiet. "Forty's enough."

"Majesty."

"Go," said the king. "Tomorrow night. The moon's a night off full; she'll be able to see the whole valley but you. Back before it sets." He turned away. "And Rui Xiang. Take the tiger to the bridge. He'll want to be there. Somebody'll need to sit on him."

***

They sat on him.

Not really. But Tiger was on the millstream bridge at the first milestone when Rui Xiang got there at dusk the next day, and so was Drew, on the parapet, where he'd sat three nights in the autumn waiting for exactly this; and so was Xav, standing in the middle of the road with his hands in his coat. Drew had his chest bound under his jacket. Tiger had his ears flat and his tail wrapped round his own leg, and his legs did a thing, when he saw Rui Xiang come down the road from the north gate, that Rui Xiang had seen them do in the yard before every morning race.

"Please don't," said Rui Xiang.

"I'm not," said Tiger. "I'm just *here*."

"Thank you."

"I'm not going to *do* anything." Tiger's ears went flatter. "Drew already told me. *Not here. Not yet.* I know." He looked up the valley, at the pale floor of it going away into the mountains in the dusk, white with frost, with the swept road a dark line up the middle of it. "Will you look at him?"

Rui Xiang didn't have to ask who.

"Yes," he said. "If I get that far. I'll look at him for you."

"Don't say anything to him."

"I won't."

"It's not—" Tiger stopped. "It's not that I don't want you to. It's that it's mine. It's my race."

"I know," said Rui Xiang. "I wouldn't. Please don't worry. It's not mine to say."

Tiger looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and went and sat down on the parapet beside Drew, close, with his shoulder against Drew's bound side, and Drew didn't say *ow*, though Rui Xiang saw him think it.

Xav hadn't moved.

"Come back," said Xav.

That was all. One word, then another. He'd always been the one who said one word.

"I will," said Rui Xiang. "Please don't come after me."

"I'm not Tiger," said Xav.

"I know. Thank you."

He went up the road.

***

The swept road started a furlong past the first milestone.

He'd heard about it in the channel for two years and read it in Corvin's letters and seen it from the tower, and he still wasn't ready for it. The frost on the valley floor, white and thick and furred on every tussock, crunching under his boots as he left the bridge behind; and then, at a line across the road as straight as if somebody had drawn it with a rule, nothing. The road ahead of him dark and bare and dry. The old paving, grey, each stone clean, as if somebody had walked down it ahead of the frost with a broom. All the way up the valley. As far as he could see in the moonlight.

He walked on it. There wasn't anywhere else to walk.

The moon came up behind him over the hills. A night off full, white, enormous, laying his shadow out in front of him on the swept stones, long and thin and pointing north. He didn't do his thing. Not yet. There was nobody to hide from. He just walked. Two miles. The second milestone, frosted on its north face. Three. The valley narrowing a little, the mountains on either side coming up black against the stars.

At four miles he started to hear it.

He'd been told about it. Kei had told him, and Drew, and the long room, the thirty-three who'd stood up in the autumn with their eyes gone strange, and Joss, who couldn't describe it at all and had just put his hands over his ears. *Like somebody in the next room*, they said. *Saying names.*

It had stopped saying names. Five days ago, in the middle of one. Hyvred had seen every candle in the valley stand up straight at once. And for a day after that there'd been nothing at all, and the long room had slept, the whole of it, for the first time since the spring; and then it had come back. Different. Not names. Just the one word, over and over, Kei said, quieter and heavier, the way a tide comes in. *Come.*

He heard it at four miles. He wasn't anyone it had ever called. He heard it anyway. Not in his ears. Somewhere behind his ribs, low, the way you hear a cart on the road through the floor before you hear it through the window.

*Come. Come. Come here. Come to this.*

He walked.

At six miles it was loud enough that he had to think about walking. At seven he could see them.

***

The rings, first. You couldn't miss them, in that moon. A long way up the valley, still, at the far end where it widened again under the pass, a great round darkness on the white floor; and when he got nearer, the darkness was people. Hundreds of them. Standing in rings in the frost, close, the way a crowd stands when it's been told to wait, with their breath going up off them into the moonlight in a single faint haze, like smoke over a village on a still morning.

And between him and them, on the road, the other thing.

He'd known it would be there. He'd known it for five days, since it came out of the mouth of the pass and through the rings and sat down on the near side of them, facing down the valley. Facing the town. He'd known he'd have to pass it.

He hadn't known what it would look like to his own eyes.

It looked like nothing. That was the trouble. He'd thought it might look like a darkness, the way it looked like a burn in John's picture, a hole in the world. It didn't. It looked like the valley. Frosted ground, and the dark line of the road going into it, and beyond it the rings. Except that the frost stopped. In a curve, the size of a hill. Inside the curve the ground was dark and bare and dry, like the road. And his eyes wouldn't stay on it. He'd look at it, and find he was looking at the rings beyond, or the mountain, or his own feet; and look back, and find he was looking at the rings again. Like trying to look at the place in the middle of your eye where there's nothing.

The road went into it. He didn't think the road came out.

He stopped on the road four hundred paces short of the curve and stood there, with the call going *come, come, come* behind his ribs like a tide on a beach, and looked at the thing for the first time, and found he was looking at something else.

At the dog.

It was lying on the frost at the edge of the curve, just outside the line where the frost stopped, on the right of the road. Curled up. Grey, the colour of a wet stone, a long low body bigger than any dog, with its back to him and its long arms folded under its chest, the way Hild had said. He could see its breath go up. Slow. Its side going up and down. One long hand, grey, with too many joints in the fingers, lying on the frost with the fingers curled, like a sleeping man's hand on a blanket.

A hunter. Out of Kharvos. Lying at the edge of the thing in the valley, asleep. Like a dog by its master's chair.

He'd never seen one. Niko had; Salty had, Wendy, Yan, all of Group 3, on the red, every spring. They'd put a greatsword through one. He'd read about it. He'd read about Sabine going into a well head first.

He was very frightened.

He noticed it the way you'd notice it was raining. *Oh. I'm frightened.* His hands had gone cold. His breath had gone short. He thought about turning round. He thought about it properly, the way he'd been taught to think about things; and then he thought about thirty, or thirty-one, behind the thing, that nobody could count. And a freckled boy in the third ring. And a tiger on a bridge with his shoulder against his friend's cracked ribs, saying *it's my race*.

He did his thing.

He pulled in. All the way. Until there was nothing left of him for anybody to feel. And left the road, to the left, the side away from the dog, off the swept stones and onto the frost, and began to walk round.

***

He was a hundred paces into it when the dog lifted its head.

He stopped.

It didn't get up. It just lifted its head, the way a dog by a fire does when it hears a step on the path, and turned it, slowly, on the end of its long neck. Not toward him. Not quite. Toward the valley between them. Toward the frost on the left of the road, where he was standing very still with his breath held and nothing of him to feel.

He saw its face. He wished he hadn't. It was mostly mouth.

And the thing beside it moved.

Not moved. *Turned*. The way Hyvred had said, the way a compass needle turns when you bring a knife up to the glass; he felt it. Not with his eyes. With all of him. Something the size of a hill, beside him on the valley floor, that had been facing the town, turning, very slightly, very slowly, a hair, toward the left of the road. Toward a place in the frost where none of the noise was. A place with an edge round it.

A cup.

*Oh*, he thought. *Oh, no. It can feel the cup.*

He'd known that. He'd known it for four days, on the floor of the long room. He just hadn't believed it, the way you don't believe the ice is thin until you're standing on it. Hyvred could see the cup. Hild could hear it. Deston could see the gap. And the thing in the valley, which didn't have eyes or ears or anything at all, which was nothing but *facing*, could feel it too. A stone in its stream. A hole in its noise. The shape of a person, standing in the frost, pulled in tight, saying *don't look at me* with his whole body as loudly as he could.

It was turning toward the shout.

The call changed.

It had been *come*, the tide, the one word, heavy and low behind his ribs since four miles. Now it stopped. In the middle. And started again, and it wasn't *come*.

*Rui Xiang.*

His name.

The first name it had said in five days. It had stopped saying names in the middle of somebody's, the evening it turned round, and it hadn't said one since; and now it said his. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. It came down on him from the side, from the hill of nothing beside him, the way a hand comes down on your shoulder in a crowd. *Rui Xiang. There you are. Oh, there you are.*

It hurt.

He hadn't known it would. Nobody had said. Drew and Kei and the long room had said it was like somebody in the next room; they'd never said it was like being *pulled*. It took hold of him somewhere under the breastbone and pulled, gently, toward the left, toward itself, the way a current takes your legs in a river before you know you're in it. His foot moved. He hadn't moved it. His right foot, on the frost, half a step, toward the thing.

The dog was getting up.

***

He had a heartbeat. Maybe two.

He could run. He was fast; not Tiger's fast, nobody was Tiger's fast, but fast. He could let go of the cup and turn round and run back to the road and seven miles down it with a hunter behind him and his name in his chest, and probably not make it.

Or he could do the other thing.

He didn't know if it would work. He'd done it twice. Once on a mounting block at noon in a yard with a well and horses, and once on a tower roof at sunset with his friends round him, for a count of forty. Never out here. Never alone. Never with anything turning toward him.

*Take a blow that should hurt*, Hild had said once, in the yard, to Azac, about the second tier. *On purpose. And have it not.*

He let go.

Not of the cup. Of all of it. Of *Unseen*, the whole of it, three years of it, all at once, in the middle of the valley, off the road, two hundred paces from a hunter getting up off the frost. He let it go the way you let your shoulders down. And he was there. A slight young man standing in the frost in a valley in the moonlight, in plain view, with his breath going up.

And he didn't pull in. And he didn't push out. And he didn't say anything.

He stood there exactly. A man in a valley at night. The frost coming up through his boots into him the way it came up into the tussocks. His breath going up into the moonlight the way the rings' breath went up, four hundred paces off, in a single faint haze. The cold on his face the way it was on the stones. Nothing extra. No edge. Not less. Not more. Exactly what was there.

Like a chair sounds like a chair.

*Rui Xiang*, said the call. *There you are.*

He let it come.

He didn't brace for it. That was the thing; that was all of it, in the end. He'd have braced, before. He'd have pulled in tighter, made the cup smaller and harder, so it couldn't get a grip. He didn't. He stood in the frost and let his own name come down on him from the side like a hand on his shoulder in a crowd, on purpose, the whole weight of it, *there you are, oh, there you are*; and it took hold of him under the breastbone where it had taken hold before.

And there was nothing there for it to hold.

It went through. Not round. *Through*. The way a hand goes into the pocket of a coat hanging on a hook, a coat that's just a coat, with nobody in it; and finds the lining; and comes out again, empty. He felt it go in. He felt it find nothing. He felt it come out the other side, and go on, past him, down the valley, and away, like a wave going up a beach past a post.

It didn't hurt.

It should have. He knew exactly how much it should have; he'd felt it for one heartbeat, under the breastbone, the pull. And now it went through the same place with all its weight and it didn't hurt at all, any more than the frost hurt the stones.

*There you*— said the call. And stopped. In the middle.

The dog was on its feet.

It stood on the frost at the edge of the curve, on its long arms and its short back legs, with its head up and its mouth a little open, and looked across the valley. At the left of the road. At him. He saw its eyes find him. In the moonlight, two hundred paces off, a slight young man standing in plain view in the frost with nothing pulled in at all.

He didn't move. He didn't breathe differently. He stood there exactly.

The dog looked at him for a long time.

Then it looked away. It looked at the rings, and at the mountain, and at the road; the way your eyes go past a post on a hillside, or a stone, or a chair in a room you've sat in all your life. It turned round once on the frost, the way a dog does. And lay down again, with its long arms folded under its chest and its back to him, and put its head down.

Beside it, the thing turned back. A hair. Toward the town.

And the call came back too, behind his ribs, the tide, heavy and low and steady. *Come. Come. Come here.* Not his name. Not anybody's.

He stood in the frost for a long time.

Then his window opened.

Not touching. An inch off. He walked along the curve outside the thirteenth ring, slowly, and put his hand up an inch from each one's shoulder as he passed, the way you'd count fence-posts in the dark; and counted out loud under his breath, very quietly, because counting under your breath on a cold night is what a man does, and it wasn't extra.

*One.* A man in a farmer's coat.

*Two.* A woman.

And then, at *five*, he saw it.

They didn't breathe.

Everybody else did. Every one of the hundreds in the thirteen rings, the turned, the called, Elin and Rhys Hywel and the Widow Cadogan: their breath went up off them in the cold, white, the way breath does, a faint haze over the whole crowd. And the curve didn't. Not one of them. They stood in the frost, close enough to touch, and nothing came out of their mouths at all. No breath. No haze. The air in front of their faces was clear and still and full of moonlight.

He stood an inch from the shoulder of the fifth one, a woman in a shawl, and looked at the clear air in front of her mouth for a long time.

Nobody had seen that. Hyvred couldn't; she saw lights and holes, not breath. John's motes saw them as people. Nobody had ever been close enough on a cold night to look.

*Six*, he counted. *Seven.*

Between the shoulders of people who'd walked eleven miles up a valley in the night, some of them two years ago, and stood here ever since in the rain and the frost and the summer with their faces turned up toward the pass, and now down toward the town. A woman with a baby's bonnet tied to her wrist by its strings. A man in a carter's smock. He didn't look at their faces for long. He'd promised himself that on the road. He'd look at one.

The third ring.

He found him the way Hyvred had always found him: by the face. A soldier. Young; younger than Rui Xiang had thought. Freckles, and big feet, and a Western Watch coat gone pale at the shoulders with two years of weather. Frost on his eyelashes and in his hair. His breath going up.

His face was turned toward the town. They all were now. But Rui Xiang thought, standing an arm from him in the moonlight, that his was turned a hair further than the others. As if he'd been turned that way already, before everyone else, and was still ahead of them.

Ossie.

He stood an arm from him for a long time.

He stood there in the third ring in the moonlight with the call going through him and out the other side and looked at a freckled boy he'd never met, whom a tiger had raced to the north gate every morning for two years, alone, slapping the post for both of them, while he stood here and turned his face a hair to see who'd won.

And then he saw the hand.

Ossie's right hand. Down at his side, like everybody's. But not hanging, the way everybody else's hung. Open. The palm flat, facing out, the fingers together. Held there, an inch or two from his hip, at the height of the top bar of a gate.

He didn't do his thing once. He didn't need it.
