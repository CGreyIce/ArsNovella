Petra stood on the shingle for an hour at dawn, the way she had every morning for ten years, and on the last morning at the lake she couldn't do it.

She tried. She went down at first light with her sword on her hip and the driftwood stick under her arm, past the cold forge and the girl's hedge with its white flowers still shut, to the flat rock at the top of the stones, and put the stick down on it, and stood. Feet under her shoulders. Weight even. Hands open at her sides. The lake grey and flat in front of her, and the mist lying on it in long low banks, and the far shore not there yet.

She lasted about as long as it takes to boil an egg.

Then she found she'd turned her head and was looking east, up the slope, over the cabin roof and the trees above it, at a sky going pale over hills she couldn't see past; and she turned it back, and stood, and a little later found she'd done it again.

She gave up. She didn't think she'd ever given up on the standing before, not once, not with a fever, not the winter her mother died. She picked up the stick and sat down on the rock instead with it across her knees, like an old woman on a doorstep, and looked at the lake, and let her head turn east whenever it wanted to.

Tomorrow, then.

Tomorrow they walked. Twenty days, the girl said, or twenty-two if her father insisted on carrying all the water, which he would. Twenty-two days east across the lake country and the downs and the grey grass, and then the red. And on the red, on the road in, a few hours short of the fort, a long low trench across the way, which she hadn't seen in ten years and had seen every night of them.

She'd never gone back.

She'd been offered it. Twice. A posting east, the spring after, with the rest of the ones who'd lived; she'd asked for Duskhollow instead, and got it, and nobody had asked her why. And once, three years ago, a captain at the Hollow who'd been at Cinderhold had said he was riding out with the autumn remounts and she could come, and she'd said she had a class, and she hadn't had a class then. She'd had nothing. She'd had a yard in the dew and an hour of standing every morning and a sound in her mouth she couldn't make.

Now she had a class. Now she had the sound. And the man.

And tomorrow she was going back.

***

He was on the end of the jetty.

He was always on the end of the jetty, at dawn. She'd learned that in the first week. He went out before it was light, with the rod, and sat on the last plank with his legs over the water, and he didn't fish, mostly, or not the way you'd think. He sat. And the hens came down the planks after him one at a time and sat too, along the edge, like a row of old men on a bench outside an inn.

There were three of them this morning, and then there weren't, because the big red one got up and came back along the jetty and down onto the shingle and up the stones to her rock, and looked at her, sideways, with one eye. And then sat down against her boot.

Petra let her.

She'd never liked hens. She'd grown up with them, on a farm, in the hills above the Wren, and they'd been nothing but work and noise and the stupidest creatures God ever set on legs. This one had sat on the end of the table at a dinner three nights ago and walked down it through fifteen people's bowls into the penguin girl's lap, and the fast one who could disappear had laughed so hard he fell off the bench into the briar.

"You're very stupid," Petra told the hen.

The hen settled against her boot and shut its eyes.

Out on the end of the jetty, the man didn't turn round. But his shoulders moved, very slightly, the way they did when somebody had said something he thought was funny and he wasn't going to say so. He couldn't have heard her. It was thirty-four planks, and the water lapping. She'd stopped wondering, a long time ago, what he could and couldn't hear.

***

"I said I'd point," said Xav.

He'd come down the shingle without her hearing him, which he could do now, and she didn't like it. He'd learned it from the man. He sat down on the stones below her rock with his practice sword across his knees, and didn't look at her, and looked at the lake.

She waited.

"In the spring," said Xav. "On the bar. You didn't see it. John and I did." He was quiet. "I said if you didn't find it yourself I'd point. And not yet." He turned his head and looked up at her. "It's tomorrow. You're going back. I think it's yet."

"Point at what?"

"John," said Xav. Not loudly.

And John was there. Of course he was. Sitting on the big flat stone at the water's edge a little way along the shingle with his knees up and his eyes shut, the way he always sat, as if he'd been there an hour. Maybe he had. He lifted one hand off his knee without opening his eyes, and the light came up off his fingers.

She'd seen it a hundred times now. She still didn't like it, quite. A square of gold the size of a door, hanging in the air over the shingle a little above head height, with nothing holding it up, and inside it, like looking down through clear water at the bottom of a pool, a picture.

The gravel bar at Duskhollow.

From above. From where the little lights went, the ones John sent up over the river at dawn and called *motes*, which wasn't a word anybody she knew used for anything. She saw the long low tongue of stones going out into the Wren where it bent below the town quay; and the river going past it, brown with the thaw; and the dozen of them standing on it in the grey light. Her class. Their heads from above. Rui Xiang's, very still, at the back. Dunstan, the big slow Hollow guard in his grey coat, near the front, with his sword up. And at the near end, in a hat she'd been meaning to throw in the river all spring, herself.

It was strange, seeing yourself from above. You looked smaller. You looked like anybody.

"Watch your stick," said Xav.

She watched the stick.

In the picture, she was teaching. She could see that from the shape of the class: the ones at the front turned toward her, the ones at the back pretending not to be. She'd have been saying *heels*. She was always saying *heels*. She saw herself step forward, one step, and bring the stick across in front of her, flat, at chest height, the way she brought it across a hundred times a morning to show them where a cut should go, where a line should be, where the edge should be when it got there.

And when the stick had gone past, a line stayed in the air behind it.

She didn't breathe.

It was very faint. Grey. Thinner than a hair, it looked, from above, from where the motes were; a pencil line on the morning, perfectly straight, hanging over the gravel at chest height, exactly where the stick had been. It stayed there for a count of one. Two. And then it wasn't there, the way breath on a cold window isn't there.

In the picture, she was already turning away. Already saying something to the front of the class. She hadn't seen it. She'd had her back to it.

"Again," said Petra.

John's hand moved, a little, on his knee, and the picture went back like a tide going out, and came in again. She stepped forward. The stick came across. The line stayed.

"Again."

It stayed.

"There's four," said Xav quietly. "Since the thaw. That one's the first." The picture changed. The bar again, a different morning, the river higher, her hat gone; a wet morning, the stones shining. She stepped forward and the stick came across, and the line stayed, a count of two. "That's the second. Then two more, on the ninth day and the nineteenth. John's got all of them." He paused. "Only with the stick. Never with your sword. We watched."

"You watched for a whole spring," said Petra.

"Yes."

"And didn't say."

"Yes," said Xav. He didn't look away. "You'd have tried to make it come. And it would have stopped. That's what happens. I know. I did it with *Now* for three months, after I found it. Tried to make it come, every bout, and it went away, and I had to go and stand in the yard for a week doing nothing before it came back." He looked at the lake. "He points. He doesn't hand it to you. Then it's yours."

The picture had stopped. The square of gold hung over the shingle with the line frozen in it, thin and grey, on a wet morning on a gravel bar two days down the river, behind a woman who'd had her back to it.

Petra looked at it for a long time.

"What is it?" she said.

Neither of them answered. She hadn't thought they would. She'd been a soldier long enough to know when two men were not going to answer a question because they'd agreed beforehand they wouldn't.

"It's the stick," she said.

She looked at it in her hands. A length of grey driftwood, as long as her arm, a little bent near the end, smooth as bone where hands had held it. He'd picked it up off this shingle the spring before last, the first week of the school, to show Xav where a line went, and used it every morning after, and when she went back down the river to Duskhollow he'd given it to her. Not said anything. Just held it out. She'd carried it ever since. She'd taught with nothing else.

"It's his," said Petra. "It's the stick. Something of him's in it."

"Maybe," said Xav.

That was all. *Maybe*. In the calm voice that made you trust him, that he'd had at seventeen, the man said, and had in a yard at Duskhollow on the first morning, when he'd stood still for forty minutes doing nothing because she'd told him to and asked afterwards, quite seriously, whether he was allowed to breathe.

John closed his hand. The gold went out like a lamp.

The hen against her boot hadn't moved.

***

She tried it once, at dusk, when nobody was looking.

That was a lie. Somebody was always looking, on this shore. But she went along the shingle to the far end, where the stones gave out into reeds and the shore path went up into the trees, where the cabin couldn't see and the hedge couldn't see and the jetty couldn't see unless the man on the end of it turned round, and he didn't. And she stood, with the stick, and the lake going dark and gold in front of her.

She brought it across. Flat. At chest height. The way she did a hundred times a morning.

Nothing.

She brought it across again. Watching it, this time. Watching the place in the air behind it.

Nothing. The reeds. The water. Midges.

She did it twenty times. She did it slowly, and fast, and from the left, and with her eyes shut, and with her weight on her heels and then off them, and a voice in her that sounded like Xav said *you'd have tried to make it come*, and she told it to be quiet. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Her shoulders had gone tight. She could feel them. She'd have told any one of her class, *you're holding it in your shoulders, put it down*, and she couldn't put it down.

She stopped.

She stood there with the stick down at her side and her breath going, and looked at the lake, and thought about nothing, the way she'd been taught. The way she'd been taught by *him*. And when she'd thought about nothing for long enough, she found she was thinking about the bar. Not the line. The bar. The grey morning on the picture. The front of the class turned toward her. And behind them, up on the quay wall, where she'd never once looked because you didn't look at the people watching, you looked at the people learning, the children.

There were always children on the quay wall. The town's. Four or five, every dawn, in their nightshirts, swinging their legs, waiting for the big summoned boys to fall over. She'd never paid them any attention. She'd known they were there. She'd known, without looking, the way you know where the edge of a roof is, that they were behind her and above, and that if anything ever came out of the river at that end of the bar, which it wouldn't, they were in the way.

She brought the stick across.

And a line stayed in the air.

She saw it. Herself, for the first time, with her own eyes and not a mote's. A thread of grey, hanging over the shingle at chest height, at the end of the stick's stroke, perfectly straight. It was there for a count of one. She felt it, too, which she hadn't expected. Not in the stick. In her. A thin cold pull, all along the inside of her arm, as if something had gone out of her down the stick and into the air and was still attached.

Then it was gone. And the pull went with it, so suddenly that she rocked on her feet.

Petra stood on the shingle in the dusk and looked at the air where it had been.

*It's the stick*, she thought.

She looked at the stick in her hand for a while. Then at the lake. Then, because she couldn't help it, along the whole length of the shore, past the hedge and the cold forge and the cabin, to the end of the jetty, where a man sat on the last plank with his back to her and a rod across his knees, looking at the water.

He hadn't turned round.

***

She asked him on the jetty, at the end of the evening.

Not then. There was the dinner first. There was always the dinner. It had been a dinner every night for eleven nights, in the girl's hedge, under the stars, at the long table that wasn't a table, it was trestles and a bakehouse board and three of the mayor's wife's tablecloths with lace on them that had been a bridal veil once. Fifteen places, and a chair carried down from the cabin for the king at the head since he came, with Kestra at his elbow. Petra at the end, because she'd sat at the end the first night and nobody had moved her since.

And tonight was the twelfth.

They'd all been waiting for it. She'd watched them wait all day. Eleven dinners, each with a name that she couldn't say and a list beside it in Higu's book in English that she couldn't read, eleven dishes from the man's other life, from a city by a sea she'd never see, that he'd promised his friends before he died and cooked for them here instead, thirty-four years late. They'd cried at the second one. Not from sadness. From the peppers. And the twelfth wasn't on the list. Everybody knew that. Everybody had asked him what it was, every night, for eleven nights, the fish girl and the fox girl and the fast one who could disappear, and he'd said the same thing every time.

The forge on the shingle was cold.

Petra noticed that first. She'd come up the stones at dusk from the far end of the shore with the stick under her arm, and her arm still faintly cold on the inside, and seen the hedge lit up, the lamps along the trestles and the white flowers open along the top of the walls; and past it, down on the stones, the forge where he'd cooked every dinner, dark. No fire. The big black skillet the size of a cartwheel hanging on its hook, clean.

She went in through the gap. The table was laid. Seventeen bowls. Seventeen spoons. Bread on the board, a great deal of it, Edric's, from the bakehouse in the town, still warm, and butter, and the last of the mayor's wife's damson cheese, and a jug of water from the lake, and that was all.

The others came in after her. She watched them come in and see it. The fox girl first, with her sword on her back; she stopped in the gap and her ears went flat, and then up, and then she looked at her father, who was standing where he always stood, at the far end of the table, by the place nobody sat in, with his hands at his sides. Then the fish girl, who said "*Oh*" very loudly. Then all the rest of them, in ones and twos, the three brothers from Kingsferry and Duskhollow and the five from the militia hall and the five from Duskhollow; and every one of them stopped in the gap and looked at the bread and the empty bowls and the cold forge and then at him.

Nobody said anything.

Then the fish girl, Xola, who never could not say anything, said: "Is it bread? Is the twelfth *bread*?"

"No," he said.

"Then what—"

"Owed," he said.

The same word. The same voice. The one he used for things that weren't jokes. Petra had heard him use it eleven nights in a row, and she'd thought it was a joke all the same, a game he was playing with them, the way he played with the hens. It wasn't. She could see that now, looking at his feet. They were very still.

"Owed to who?" said Xola.

He didn't answer.

And the dark girl, Higu, who kept the book and never asked him anything, opened the book on the table in front of her and turned back through it, a long way back, past the eleven dinners, past the summer, past the spring, to the winter. Petra watched her do it. She didn't know what was in it; she couldn't have read it if she'd been holding it. She watched the girl's finger go down a page and stop.

"*Twelve*," said Higu.

The table went quiet.

"Salty," said Higu. Not to him. To the page. "In the channel. First snow. *Twelve. At the lake. Next summer.*" She looked up. "He said it at the gate. When you left Cinderhold. Wendy said he was practising. With the wrong peppers. All winter. The whole fort smelled of it."

"He didn't come to the lake," said Gabe. Slowly. "He went east. From the Lists. To wait at the—" He stopped.

"So it's owed," said Higu.

She looked at the man at the end of the table. He looked back at her. And Petra, who'd been watching feet for ten years, saw his shift. Very slightly. The weight going back onto his heels, the way it did when somebody had got to a thing before he'd had to say it.

"It's not mine to cook," he said. "It's his."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then the big Kingsferry boy, Gabe, sat down on the bench, very heavily, and took a piece of Edric's bread off the board and tore it in half, and put butter on it, and said, with his mouth full, "Cinderhold, then."

"Cinderhold," said Deston.

At the head of the table the king looked at the bread for a long moment. Then he laughed, quietly, the way he did, into his beard, and reached for a piece.

"I'm not eating Salty's cooking," said Rui Xiang. "I've seen Salty's cooking. On a stream. He set a pan on fire making *toast*—"

"You'll eat it," said the man.

And sat down.

Petra had never seen him sit at the table. Not once, in eleven nights. He cooked, and he watched them eat, standing at the end by the empty place, and when they'd all gone he ate on his own, last, out of the pot at the forge, standing up. She'd thought it was a soldier's thing. She'd seen old sergeants do it, in the war, eat last and on their own, after the men. It wasn't that either. The girl who'd grown the hedge had written it on her wrist in ink the first week, and Petra had seen it there, and not been able to read it, and known anyway what it said.

He sat down on the bench at the end, in the place nobody sat in. And took a piece of bread. And ate it.

That was the twelfth dinner. Bread and butter and the mayor's wife's damson cheese and a jug of water from the lake, in a hedge, with seventeen people round a table that wasn't a table. It was owed. So it was bread.

The penguin girl put the red hen on the table and nobody stopped her.

***

She asked him on the jetty, after.

She went out along it when the others had gone up the slope, thirty-four planks in the dark, with the stick, and sat down on the last one beside him with her legs over the edge. The lamp was lit by his elbow. The rod was across his knees. The water under her boots was black and very still, and full of stars, and she could hear the hens settling somewhere behind them on the shingle with little annoyed sounds.

She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did he. They'd done that before, on this jetty, for an hour at a time. It wasn't hard. It was the easiest thing she knew how to do, sitting next to him and not saying anything.

"I made a line," she said at last. "Tonight. At the end of the shore."

"I know."

Of course he knew.

"Xav showed me the others. In John's light. Four of them, on the bar. I never saw them." She turned the stick over on her knees. "I made one. With the stick. It stayed for a count of one."

He didn't say anything.

"What is it?" said Petra.

He looked at the water.

She'd known he wouldn't say. She'd asked anyway. It had been worth asking, to know for certain; the way it's worth putting your foot on the ice once before you walk out on it, even when you know it'll hold. She waited.

"What were you thinking," he said, "when you made it?"

She opened her mouth to say *nothing*. That was the truth. She'd been thinking nothing; that was what he'd taught her; that was the whole of the standing, the hour every morning for ten years, the thing she'd given her class. And then she closed it again, because it wasn't the truth, quite, and he'd know.

"The children," she said.

"Which children?"

"On the quay wall. At Duskhollow. Behind the class. There's always four or five. In their nightshirts." She stopped. "I don't look at them. You don't look at the ones watching. But they're there. Behind me. If anything came out of the river at that end of the bar, they'd be in the way."

He didn't say anything to that either.

He just nodded. Once. Looking at the water. As if she'd said something about the weather, or the price of eggs, and he agreed.

Petra sat there with the stick on her knees and felt the thing go through her that she'd felt the first summer, on the shingle, when he'd said *I'll teach you when to*. Not an answer. The shape of where an answer would be, if you went and stood in it.

She didn't go and stand in it. Not yet. She sat there.

*It's the stick*, she thought. Quite firmly. *Something of him's in it.*

***

The other thing she asked him came out of her before she'd decided to ask it, which was how she knew it was the real one.

"When we get there," she said. "The ditch."

He went still.

She felt him do it, beside her. Not his shoulders. Not his feet. Something in the air round him. The way the water goes still in a pool when a pike comes up under it. It only lasted a heartbeat, and then it was gone, and he was a man sitting on a jetty with a rod again, and she wasn't sure she hadn't imagined it.

She went on anyway. She'd been holding it in her mouth for twenty days. Since the Lists. Since she'd stood in front of Higu's table in a painters' yard in Valcrest and said *I'm going east* and *write it twice*.

"I've never been back," she said. "Ten years. I was offered it. I said no. I didn't want to see it." She looked at the water. "Now I want to see it. And when I see it, I want—" She stopped.

He waited.

"I want you to stand where you stood," said Petra. "That night. When you came across. I want to stand in it where I was, and look up, and see you standing where you were." She found her hands were tight on the stick, and made them open. "I don't remember your face. I never saw it; nobody could. I remember where you stood. In the dark. I've seen it every night. I want to see it in the light."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Out on the lake something jumped, a fish, a small one, and the rings went out from it across the black water and through the stars, and he watched them go all the way out and stop.

"On the red," he said.

She didn't understand, for a moment.

"That's where I stood," he said. "Not on the edge. You'd remember the edge. On the red. In front of it. Four paces out." He was quiet. "You were on the far end. I could see you over my shoulder if I turned my head. I didn't turn my head much."

"No," said Petra. "You didn't."

"I'll stand there," he said.

That was all. He didn't say *yes*. He didn't say *if you like*, or *are you sure*, or anything that people said when you asked them for something that would cost them. He said where he'd stood, and that he'd stand there, the way you'd tell somebody where you'd left a thing.

"Thank you," said Petra.

He nodded.

And she saw, out of the side of her eye, Xav.

He was on the shingle. At the land end of the jetty, where the planks met the stones. He must have come down for his practice sword, which he'd left on her rock; he had it in his hand. He'd stopped there. He was standing quite still at the end of the jetty with the sword in his hand, looking at the two of them on the last plank, and his face, in the lamplight that just reached him, was doing something Petra had never seen it do.

She didn't know what it was. She thought, at first, that it was grief. Then she thought it wasn't grief, exactly; it was the face of somebody who knew something and had promised not to say it, and had just watched someone walk up to the edge of it without knowing it was there.

Then he turned round and went back up the shingle, and was gone.

She looked at the man beside her.

He was looking at the water. His face was the way it always was. She couldn't hold it; nobody could; she'd stopped trying. But his feet, on the edge of the last plank, over the black water, had gone very slightly back. Onto his heels. The way they had at the table, when Higu said *twelve*.

She didn't ask. She'd been a soldier long enough to know what not to ask, and when.

***

She remembered it in pieces. She always had. Never the whole of it at once; she didn't think anybody who'd been there could hold the whole of it at once. Pieces, coming up out of the dark at night the way fish come up, one at a time, and turning, and going down again.

The ditch. That was the first piece and the clearest. A long low trench across the road, a little deeper than a man's waist, dug the summer before by men who'd never thought anybody would have to stand in it. Clay at the bottom, wet. The smell of the clay. She could smell it now, sitting on a jetty two hundred miles away, if she let herself.

The forty of them. Recruits. The youngest sixteen, the oldest twenty-five. They'd been on the south line six weeks. They'd been told the south line was the quiet one. They'd been told that by a sergeant who was dead by midnight.

The second night. They'd come at the line in the dark. Not the tall thin ones, at first; the ones that came first were low, and fast, and in a great many places at once, and you heard them before you saw them, a sound in the grass like rain. And the line had held, mostly. Except here. Here it had gone. The men on her left had gone, and the men on her right had gone, and she'd been at the end, at the far end of the ditch where it ran out into the grass, with her left arm broken from something she never saw and her sword in her right hand. And thirty-nine recruits behind her in the clay, because they'd all come down into the ditch, the way frightened men go down into anything that's lower than where they are.

And the line gone on both sides.

She'd held it. That was the next piece. She didn't remember holding it. She remembered that she had, because the sergeant at Duskhollow had told her afterwards, reading it out of a report, that a recruit called Petra had held the east end of the ditch on her own for most of an hour before relief came. She'd laughed at him. Not because it was funny. Because *relief* was such a strange word for it.

He'd come across from the fort.

That was the next piece. The biggest. She'd been on her knees at the end of the ditch, she thought, by then, with her sword still up because her hand wouldn't open; and something had come down out of the dark at her that was twice the height of a man, horned, grey, with its arms out on either side of it like a man about to catch something; and she'd seen its shape against the stars and known it was the last thing she'd see.

And then it wasn't there.

She'd heard it go. A sound like a sail tearing, very loud, very close, and then two heavy things hitting the clay at the far end of the ditch, one, two, and the recruits screaming. And a man standing on the red in front of the ditch where the thing had been. Four paces out. With his back to her. With a sword on his shoulder as long as he was tall, a black slab of iron as broad as a man's back. She'd seen the shape of it against the fires on the far side of the plain.

He hadn't said anything to her. Not then. He'd turned his head once, a little, so that she saw the side of his face in the firelight, or would have, if she'd been able to look at it. She'd never been able to say, after, what she'd seen. She'd seen that he'd seen her. At the end of the ditch, on her knees, with her sword up. And then he'd turned his head back and looked at the dark.

And they'd come.

All night. That was the next piece. Not in a rush; that was what she hadn't understood, then, and understood now, after ten years of standing in yards. They came in ones and twos and threes, the way water comes through a dam once the dam's gone, finding the place. And every one of them that came out of the dark came at the man on the red, because he was in front of the ditch, and the ditch was the place.

And he didn't move.

Not until they were there. She'd watched him. Lying in the clay at the end of the ditch, with her arm broken and her sword up, she'd watched him not move, again and again, while things came across the red at him out of the dark at a run, close, closer, and every part of her screamed at him to *move*. And he didn't, and he didn't, and then, at the very last, when the thing was already in the air or already committed or already somewhere it couldn't come back from, he said three words she didn't know, very quietly, in a language nobody in the world spoke.

And moved. Once.

And the thing was in two pieces on the red behind him, and he hadn't moved again.

Every time. All night. She'd stopped being frightened at some point in the night, she didn't know when, and just watched. Lying in the clay. Watching the one place in the whole of the Redmarch where nothing could get past, which was the four paces of red in front of the ditch where a man was standing with his back to her.

And in the morning, when it was grey, they'd stopped coming. And he'd turned round. And looked down at the forty of them in the clay. And then he'd turned and gone back toward the fort, walking, not running, with the black sword on his shoulder, and she'd watched him go until she couldn't.

She'd never known his name, for eight years. She'd known the three words. She'd said them in the dark every night, trying to remember the shapes, until a summer on a shingle beach when he said them out loud for her, and a boy on the end of the jetty called across the water what they meant.

*Fortune favours the bold.*

A joke, she'd said. *And a prayer*, he'd said. *Both*.

***

"You're thinking about it," he said.

She came back. The jetty. The lamp. The stars in the water.

"Yes," said Petra.

"You can stop, if you want. I'll stand there either way."

"I don't want to stop." She looked down at the stick across her knees. "I've thought about it every night for ten years. In pieces. I thought, if I went back and saw it, I'd stop. And I didn't want to stop, so I didn't go." She was quiet. "I want to now. Go. I don't know if I'll stop."

"You won't," he said.

She looked at him.

"You won't stop," he said. "It doesn't stop. You just get—" He thought about it. He was always careful with words, she'd noticed, the way some men are careful with knives. "You get somewhere to put it. That's all. It's still there. You've just got a shelf."

"Have you got a shelf?"

He didn't answer that. He looked at the water.

And she thought about Xav's face at the land end of the jetty, and the man's feet going back onto his heels, and the way the air had gone still round him for a heartbeat when she'd said *the ditch*; and she understood, sitting there, not what it was, but that there was a thing. That the second night of the invasion, the night he'd stood on the red in front of the ditch and held it till dawn, was a night that had been something else to him too. Something she didn't know. Something that his friends knew, and had promised him not to say.

She didn't ask.

She'd come to say thank you, the first summer. She'd said it. She'd come east now to look at a ditch in the light. She'd look at it. And whatever the rest of the night had been, it was his, and he'd carried it ten years without putting it down in front of her, and that was his to do.

"Twenty-two days," she said instead.

"Twenty. If she sets the pace."

"She'll set the pace."

"She always sets the pace," he said, and there it was: his shoulders, moving, very slightly, the way they did. "I carry the water."

"I know," said Petra. "Everyone knows. Maren put it in a report."

"Maren puts everything in a report."

They sat there.

After a while the red hen came back along the jetty in the dark, thirty-four planks, with little indignant noises, as if they'd been keeping her up, and sat down between the two of them on the last plank, against Petra's hip on one side and his on the other. And Petra let her, and so did he.

***

She slept badly, above the bakery, in the bed the five from the militia hall had found for her in the first week, and woke an hour before dawn, and lay there.

Then she got up and went down to the shingle in the dark with the stick, the way she had every morning, and stood on the flat rock at the top of the stones for an hour, and didn't turn her head east once.

She didn't know why she could, today, when she couldn't yesterday. She stood. Feet under her shoulders. Weight even. Hands open at her sides, with the stick in one of them, the point resting on the rock. The lake grey and flat and the mist on it. And the cabin behind her and above, up the slope, dark, with a lamp just being lit in the window; and in it, she knew, though she couldn't see it from here, a long grey-silver blade on two iron pegs over the table, that had been made for the day he left the lake and wasn't going today.

He'd leave it on the wall. She knew that too. He'd take the rod.

When the hour was done she brought the stick up, without deciding to, and across, flat, in front of her, at chest height, the way she did a hundred times a morning. Not to make anything. Just because it was what you did, at the end of the standing, to show the yard where the line went.

She didn't look to see if anything stayed.

She thought about the bakery behind her, up the shingle, with Edric's boy asleep over the ovens; and the hedge, with the fifteen bowls washed and stacked on the trestles; and the cabin, with its lamp; and the jetty, with a man just walking out along it to the end with a rod and three hens following him in a line. All of it behind her. All of it, if anything came up out of the lake at this end of the shore, which it wouldn't, in the way.

The pull went down the inside of her arm, cold and thin.

She didn't look.

She put the stick under her arm, and went up the shingle to get her pack.
