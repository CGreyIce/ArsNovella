Salty was bad at secrets.

He knew it. Everybody knew it. He'd been bad at them on Earth, where he'd once spent three weeks planning a surprise for Wendy's birthday and then told her about it on the first day because she'd asked him what he was thinking about and he couldn't think of a lie fast enough. He was bad at them here. He didn't lie. He'd never seen the point. If somebody asked you a thing, you either told them or you said *not telling*, and *not telling* was the same as telling, really, everybody knew that.

So he'd decided, back in the winter, at the second anvil in the fort's forge at Cinderhold, with the snow coming down outside and Garrow beside him saying *slower*, that he wasn't going to tell anyone. He wasn't going to say *not telling*. He was just going to make it, and not say anything at all, and then one day hand it over.

It had worked for about four days.

"It's horseshoes," he said now, for perhaps the hundredth time since the winter, to nobody, on the road.

"Nobody asked," said Niko.

"In case anyone was wondering."

"Nobody," said Niko, with great patience, "was wondering."

It was in the back of the cart, wrapped in a blanket. It had been in the back of the cart for twenty days, all the way from Cinderhold, along the east road and over the downs and across the plain. A long, thin, awkward shape, taller than Salty, which was not difficult, wrapped in Wendy's second-best blanket and tied with cord at both ends and in the middle, lying along the side of the cart like a body. People in villages had looked at it. One man in a market at Ashby had asked if it was a body. Salty had said *horseshoes*.

The man had looked at the shape. It was nearly seven feet long.

"Big horseshoes," Salty had said.

He was very bad at secrets.

***

They came to the last hill at the end of the afternoon.

Salty had been watching for it since noon. Aldous had told them about it at breakfast, at the inn in the last village: a low long hill where the east road came up out of the market gardens, and from the top of it you'd see the whole of Valcrest at once, the walls and the roofs and the great pale bowl of the Lists in the middle. *You'll know it*, Aldous had said. *There's a shrine at the top. A little one. Travellers leave a coin*.

He could see the shrine now. A stone box on a post at the top of the rise, with the late sun on it.

He'd been walking beside the cart all day. He'd walked beside the cart most of the way from Cinderhold, because his ribs didn't like the cart, and because Rhea kept trying to race the cart and somebody had to stop her. His ribs had knitted crooked, from the stone, in the autumn. They'd match the sword, he'd said at the time. He'd meant it as a joke. They ached when it rained and when he lifted things and when he laughed, and he still did all three.

"Rhea," said Aldous, from the front of the cart, without looking round.

Rhea was behind the cart. Rhea was always behind the cart. She had both hands flat on the tailboard and her boots dug into the road and she was pushing, red in the face, the whole cart and the horse and Aldous and all their baggage and Maigo, who was sitting on the baggage, up the last slope of the hill, faster than the horse wanted to go.

"I'm helping," said Rhea.

"The horse doesn't need help."

"The horse is *slow*." She pushed harder. The cart lurched. Maigo, on top of the baggage, said *WHOA* and grabbed the blanket. "I can beat it. I can beat the cart to the top."

"You're *pushing* the cart."

"I can still *beat* it."

Salty didn't say anything. He went round the back of the cart and put his own hands on the tailboard next to Rhea's, and pushed.

His ribs said something about that. He ignored them. Rhea looked at him sideways, red-faced, and grinned, the grin she'd been grinning at him across an arm-wrestling table for two years, fourteen to thirteen, and then a decider, and then a decider for the decider, and then nobody knew any more because Maigo had lost the tally. And they pushed the cart up the last of the hill together, the two strongest people in Cinderhold, while the horse trotted in front of it in deep offence and Aldous sat on the box with his book open on his knee and his eyes shut, praying, probably, for patience.

They got to the top.

The cart stopped by the shrine. Salty straightened up, with one hand on his ribs.

And there was Valcrest.

***

Aldous had been right. You'd know it.

It lay on the plain below them in the late gold light like something poured out: walls and roofs and towers and roads going in from every side, and smoke going up straight in the still air from ten thousand chimneys, and in the middle of it all the Lists, a long oval of pale stone, bigger than anything Salty had ever seen built by hand. There were tents outside the walls. Hundreds. Flags on poles. People on every road. It looked, Salty thought, like a LAN. Like the biggest LAN in the world, on the morning of the final, when everybody's arrived and nobody's playing yet.

And there was something in every window.

He couldn't see what from up here. Just that there was something. Something thin and long, hung up in every window on every street going up to the Lists, catching the light. Thousands of them.

"Fishing rods," said Yan, behind him.

Salty turned round.

Yan was standing by the shrine, with Suzu round his neck like a white scarf and his long silver hair loose down his back, looking at the city with his calm half-lidded eyes. Suzu had her head up. She'd had her head up since noon. She hadn't done that since the red.

"Paper ones," said Yan. "Newish's. In every window. Chris said." He was quiet. "*For the Hero. Just in case*."

Nobody said anything.

They'd read it. All of them. Every night for a week, on the road, by the fire, with their windows open on the dark and the channel scrolling up through them. They'd read Tiger's capitals and Hyvred's *I'm going to look at all of you* and Aleaka's *I'll be at the north gate*. They'd read Chris on the morning of the fifty-seventh day: *He's here*. They'd read Newish, the night after, and nobody in Group 3 had said anything for a long time after they read Newish. They'd read *He's eating pie*.

They'd known since the red. Salty had asked him straight out, on the red, at the edge of a ring of flowers, with his ribs broken: *are you him*. And the man had looked at each of them in turn and not said no. That had been the answer. Everybody knew that had been the answer. You didn't need a yes when a man like that didn't say no.

But Salty had asked. And nobody had ever answered.

He was bad at secrets. He was worse at questions without answers.

"There's somebody at the gate," said Wendy.

***

She'd seen it first. She always did. She was an archer; she saw things at a distance and didn't trust them until she'd looked twice. She was standing at the edge of the road with her lavender headphones round her neck, silent the way they'd been silent for two years, and her hand up shading her eyes, looking down the long slope of the road to the city.

Salty looked.

The east gate was a long way off. Half a mile, more, down the hill and through the market gardens. Smaller than he'd expected, an old square gatehouse in the wall with the road going into it. People going in and out. Carts.

And on the road outside it. A little way out, past the last of the gardens, where the road came off the hill. Standing on his own, very still, with something thin and long over his shoulder.

Salty knew him.

He couldn't see his face. He'd never been able to see his face; nobody could; on the red it had slid off your eyes like rain. But he'd stood thirty paces from that man at the edge of a ring of flowers in the dusk and watched him take a sword the size of a door out of his hands and carry it home like firewood. He knew the way he stood. Weight on the back foot. Easy. Ready to go either way. Like a man holding an angle.

"Oh," said Maigo.

Maigo was always the first to speak. Always. In every call, in every channel, in every room. He'd said *WHOA* on the cart a minute ago. He'd talked to sheep all the way across the downs. He'd talked to the shrine when they got to the top.

He didn't say anything else.

He sat on top of the baggage on the cart, very small, in his big mustard hoodie with the hood up and his antlers poking through it and his pixel sunglasses on, and looked down the hill at the man on the road, and didn't say a single word. And the little leaves on his antlers, which had been dry and quiet since the heat of the downs, lifted. All of them. All at once. Toward the city, like something turning to the sun.

"He's been there all day," said Yan quietly.

Salty looked at him.

"Since the morning," said Yan. "I felt him go. From the Lists. Out to the east gate. He stopped there and he didn't move." His hand had come up to Suzu, resting on her. "All day. On his own. Waiting." He was quiet. "He wanted to be standing there when we came over the hill. This time. Instead of us coming over the hill at him."

"How do you know that?"

"I know him," said Yan. "I've known him longer than any of you." And then, quite calmly, the way he said everything: "Thirty-six. I counted."

***

They went down the hill.

Nobody decided to. Aldous just picked up the reins and clicked his tongue at the horse, and the cart rolled off the top of the rise and down the long slope of the east road, and they walked beside it. Rhea at the back. Wendy on the near side with her hand on the edge of the cart. Niko on the far side, with his hands in his pockets, chill, the way he always was, except that Salty could see his jaw. Yan in front with Suzu.

Maigo got down off the baggage halfway down. He didn't say why. He just climbed down and walked, in his one shoe and his one bare root-toed foot, on the road, beside Salty. Very close.

Salty kept walking.

The man on the road didn't move as they came down. He stood there in the late sun with the rod over his shoulder and watched them come, the way you'd watch a team walk out of a tunnel. The people going in and out of the gate went round him without looking at him. Nobody looked at him. Salty had forgotten that. On the red there'd been nobody to not look at him. Here there were hundreds, and not one of them saw him.

There were others at the gate. Further back, under the arch. Salty saw them as they came closer. A girl with a sword the size of a door on her back, a fox girl, with her ears forward. A small dark woman with a list. A man in a green robe with a green snake round his neck, standing very still, with his head tipped as if he were listening to something. And up on the wall over the gate, among the paper rods, a long row of heads, thirty of them, trying very hard to look as if they just happened to be standing on a wall.

Salty heard Tiger, up there, say *shh*, very loudly.

They came to the bottom of the hill. To the last of the market gardens. To the place on the road where he was standing.

Aldous stopped the cart.

Nobody said anything.

***

The man looked at them.

The way he had on the red. One after another, slowly, as if he were reading something written on their faces and wanted to get every word. Rhea, at the back, red from the hill. Aldous, on the box, with his book closed on his knee and his old grave face gone very still. Wendy. Niko. Yan, with Suzu.

Maigo last.

He looked at Maigo for a long time. Maigo stood in the road in his big hoodie with his hood up and his sunglasses on and his leaves all lifted toward the man, every one of them, and didn't say anything, and didn't look away. Salty could feel him beside him, trembling.

And the man said it.

"Maigotron."

Just that. In English. Not loud.

And Maigo's antlers burst into leaf.

Salty had never seen it happen like that. He'd seen Maigo's antlers grow in the spring sap, new leaves coming one at a time over weeks, *31, 44, 72*. He'd seen them go dry on the downs in the heat. He'd never seen them do this. Every bud on both antlers opening at once, a soft green explosion all over Maigo's head, leaves unfurling and unfurling until his hood was pushed right back and he was standing in the road with a crown of fresh green summer on him like a tree in a single afternoon of May.

"*THE MACHINE*," said Maigo.

It came out of him at the top of his lungs. Cracked. The callout. The thing Ice had screamed in a voice chat four years ago when Maigo went four-for-four on a pistol round and saved the half, *THE MACHINE, MAIGOTRON, THE MACHINE*, the thing that had been his nickname ever since, that only one person in two worlds had ever called him. And then Maigo didn't say anything else at all. He went. Straight down the road and into the man with his arms out and his face in his coat, and held on, and the leaves on his antlers shook.

The man put one hand on the back of Maigo's hood, between the antlers, and held it there.

"Hi, Maigo," he said quietly.

On the wall over the gate somebody made a noise like a kettle going off.

***

"Mashi," said the man.

Niko hadn't moved. He was still standing by the far side of the cart with his hands in his pockets, very chill, very well put together, with his jaw tight. He looked at the man over the top of Maigo's leaves.

"Took you long enough," said Niko.

"I know."

"I knew on the red." Niko didn't take his hands out of his pockets. His voice was perfectly level. "I watched you hold the angle on the rise. Before you came down. You stood exactly where I'd have stood. Same timing. Same patience." He paused. "There was a guy on a community server, years ago. When I was seventeen. Used to sit in mid with an AWP and wait for me. Every single round. Every map. He'd wait so long you'd think he'd gone to make tea, and then you'd peek, and you'd be dead." He looked at the man. "Same guy."

"Same guy," said the man.

"You were *so* annoying," said Niko.

"I know."

Niko took one hand out of his pocket. Then he looked at it, as if he didn't know what it was for. Then he crossed the road in three steps and put his arms round the man's neck from the side, Maigo and all, and held on, with his face turned away so nobody could see it, and didn't say anything else at all.

***

Wendy didn't move.

She stood by the cart with her hand on its edge and her silent headphones round her neck and her star-and-moon necklace catching the sun, and looked at him. She'd written one line in the letter, in the winter. *Thank you for the stone. I believe it now*. She'd carried a handful of children's stones across the red for a woman she'd never met, and set one white one from a lake shore by the head of a grave. She'd been the one who didn't believe. The one who was here because Salty was here.

"You could've said yes," she said. "On the red."

"I know."

"Would've saved us a year." Her voice was defensive, the way it went when she was frightened. "A whole year. Of not knowing. Of Yan counting. Of Maigo talking to a tree."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

Wendy looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Salty. Then back.

"You're not," she said. "Not really." And then, quieter: "That's okay. I wouldn't be either. I'd have wanted to do it properly." She let go of the cart. "Is this properly?"

"It's as properly as I could manage."

"Fine," said Wendy, and walked across the road, and put her arm round Niko from behind, so that there were four of them now in a heap in the road outside the east gate of Valcrest, and the leaves on Maigo's antlers brushed her cheek.

***

Then the man looked at Salty.

Salty was standing beside the cart with one hand on his ribs. He'd been standing there the whole time. He wasn't good at this. He wasn't good at secrets and he wasn't good at waiting and he wasn't good at standing in a road while the people he'd come across a country with fell on a man one at a time. He was good at lifting things. He was good at saying what he meant.

He'd asked a question on the red. Nobody had answered it.

The man looked at him over the top of the others. Salty couldn't see his face. He could see that he was looking.

"You asked me something," said the man. "On the red. At the flowers."

"Yeah."

"I didn't answer."

"No," said Salty. "You said *go home safely*." He paused. "We did. Mostly. Rhea fell off a wall."

"I did *not*," said Rhea, from behind the cart.

"She fell off a wall."

"It was a *small* wall."

The man didn't laugh. Salty felt him not laugh. Then he said, quietly, in English, looking straight at him:

"Yes."

Just that. One word. The answer to a question asked a year ago on the red with broken ribs, at the edge of a ring of flowers, in the dusk. *Are you him*. *Yes*.

Salty stood there.

He'd thought he'd know what to do. He'd thought about it on the road for twenty days. He'd thought he'd go in, like Maigo. Or say something, like Niko. Or be fine, like Wendy pretended to be. He found he couldn't do any of those. He found he was just standing in the road with his hand on his crooked ribs and his face doing something he couldn't stop, and there was only one thing in his head, and it was the thing he'd written in the letter, because it was the only thing he'd ever had to say.

"Eleven," said Salty.

The man went still.

"*Dinners*," he said. "I know."

***

It had been a running thing. Two years of it. Salty had never told anyone else what it meant, not even Wendy, who'd guessed.

Every time Salty clutched a round in a game with Ice watching, a one-versus-two or worse, Ice had said the same thing in voice. *Okay. That's a dinner. When you come out, dinner's on me*. And Salty would say *that's two*, or *that's five*, and Ice would say *I know, I know, I'm keeping count, trust*. And it had gone up. Every clutch. Six. Eight. Ten. *When you come out*. Salty and Wendy had been going to fly out. Yan too. They'd been looking at dates.

Eleven. The last one had been about a month before. A one-versus-three on a map Salty hated, with Ice dead in the corner narrating it. *That's eleven. Eleven dinners. I'm going to be broke*.

And then the call in the night, and the trip never happened, and nobody had ever bought anybody dinner.

"I'm going to be broke," said the man, very quietly, in English.

And Salty's face finally stopped doing what it was doing and did the other thing, the thing he hadn't done since the night he found out, and he put his hand over his eyes, there in the road, and stood like that. Not moving. His shoulders going. Wendy came back out of the heap without a word and stood in front of him so nobody on the wall could see.

After a while the man came round the cart. He stood beside Salty. He didn't hug him; Salty had never been a hugger, and he knew it. He just put one hand flat on Salty's back, between the shoulder blades, the way you'd steady somebody carrying something heavy up a slope.

"I'll pay," he said. "All eleven."

"You'd better," said Salty, through his hand.

"I don't know what they eat here. For a nice dinner."

"I don't *care*," said Salty. "Pie. Lucan's pie. Chris says it's good." He took his hand away from his eyes. They were red. He didn't care. "Eleven pies."

"Eleven pies," the man agreed.

***

Yan was last.

He'd stood at the front of the cart the whole time, where he'd been when it stopped. He hadn't moved. He'd stood in the road with Suzu round his neck and his long hair down his back and his hands at his sides, and watched the others go one at a time, Maigo and Niko and Wendy and Salty, the way a captain watches his team load into a round. Calm. Half-lidded. Steady. The one who'd kept everyone together for two years on the edge of the red.

He'd been the first to know. Salty knew that now. Yan had known before anyone. He'd seen it on the red, with whatever it was he saw with: the man's soul, older than his body, not from this world. And he'd said nothing. He'd pondered it alone. He'd counted thirty-six from a grave, in the winter, at the moment he reached his first tier, and written it in a letter. *Thirty-six. I counted*.

The man went to him.

They stood in front of each other in the road. Yan was taller. Salty had forgotten that. Not by much.

Suzu lifted her head off Yan's collarbone and looked at the man for a long time, with her bright green eyes, very still. Her tongue went in and out once. Then she put her head back down on Yan's shoulder, and closed her eyes, the way she had on the red, a year ago, after looking at him for a long time. As if she'd decided something and wasn't going to discuss it.

"Hello, Suzu," said the man.

Suzu didn't open her eyes.

"She says hello," said Yan.

"Does she?"

"No," said Yan. "I don't know what she says. I never have." It was the closest Salty had ever heard him come to a joke about it. He'd translated for her on every stream for years. Here, where she was real and couldn't speak, he'd stopped. "I'm guessing."

The man looked at him.

"You counted," he said.

"I counted."

"From the grave. In the winter. When you went up a tier." The man was quiet. "Thirty-five across the kingdom. And one more. North-west. The biggest."

"That was you."

"That was me." A pause. "You got the right number."

Yan didn't say anything for a moment. His face did nothing. His face always did nothing; it was what made him a good leader. But Salty, who'd known him six years, who'd sat next to him in a hundred calls, saw his hand come up to Suzu, and rest on her, and hold on.

"Okay," said Yan.

That was all. His word. The one he'd written at the bottom of the channel every night that week, a full stop under Maigo's capitals. *Okay*.

The man laughed, very quietly. Then he put his hand on Yan's shoulder, the one without the snake on it, and Yan bent his head, just slightly, the way you bend your head when you've been carrying something a long way and someone finally takes the other end of it.

On the wall over the east gate of Valcrest, thirty people stopped pretending they weren't watching, and cheered.

***

Aldous got down off the cart.

He'd sat on the box through all of it, with his book closed on his knee and his old grave chaplain's face very still. He'd buried the dead on the Redmarch after the battle. He'd seen a black sword standing over a grave for eight years, and noticed the morning it was gone, and led the escort that took it back. He'd stood at the edge of the ring of flowers a year ago and watched the man take it out of Salty's hands, and not stopped him.

He came round the cart now and stood in front of him.

"Ars," said Aldous.

"Aldous."

"You were by the grave," said Aldous, "when I came out to bury them. The morning after. You didn't look up." He was quiet. "I've wondered for eight years whether I should have gone over to you."

"You had work to do."

"I did." Aldous looked at him for a long time. Then, very slowly, he put out both hands, palms up, the way Salty had seen him do over the dead on the red, giving them back: an old chaplain's gesture, from a funeral rite. "Thank you," said Aldous, "for carrying my five home."

The man looked at the old man's hands.

Then he put his own in them. Just for a moment.

Behind the cart, Rhea cleared her throat very loudly.

"*You're* the one who carried us," she said. She'd come round the side of the cart and was standing with her fists on her hips, red-faced, glaring. "On the red. All five. And me. You carried *me*. I had a concussion. I don't *remember* it." She glared harder. "I want a rematch."

"Of what?"

"*Anything*," said Rhea. "Arm-wrestling. Running. Pushing a cart. I don't *care*. You carried me and I don't remember it and I want to *win* something."

The man considered her.

"Tomorrow," he said. "After the Banner. If you've still got arms."

Rhea's grin went all the way across her face.

***

Salty didn't untie the blanket at the gate.

He'd thought about it. On the road, for twenty days. Where to do it. He'd thought about doing it at the gate, in the road, the moment he saw him; that was the obvious place. But standing in the road with his face still wet and his hand on his ribs, he found he couldn't. Not here. Not on a road, in the dust, with people going round them and a wall full of their friends cheering.

It had been made at an anvil. It ought to be given at one.

"Is there a forge?" said Salty.

The man looked at him. At the cart. At the long thin shape in Wendy's second-best blanket, tied at both ends and in the middle, nearly seven feet long.

"Horseshoes," said the man gravely.

"*Shut up*," said Salty.

***

There was a forge at the bottom of the Street of Armourers, the fox girl said. Hesk's. A small one. And the king was in it.

"The *king*?" said Salty.

"He's been there all afternoon," said Alea. She'd come out from under the gate arch at some point, with the small dark woman and the man with the green snake, and was walking beside her father with her terrible sword on her back. "He went down to look at Gabe's blade and then he just stayed. Hesk's had a horse in for shoeing and he said he'd do it. He's been shoeing horses all afternoon. There's a queue."

"A queue of *horses*?"

"A queue of people with horses," said Alea. "They don't know who he is. They think he's very good."

So they went down the Street of Armourers in the dusk. All of them. Group 3, and Aldous with the cart, and Rhea, and the man, and Alea, and Maren with her list, and Kei with his snake, who hadn't said one word yet and kept looking at Yan's neck with his head tipped. And behind them, in a loose crowd that pretended not to be following, most of the thirty from the wall, who'd come down off it the moment the cart moved.

The forge was at the bottom of the street, small and smoky, with its doors open on the dusk and a red glow inside and the sound of a hammer. A woman in a leather apron was sitting on an upturned bucket in the street outside, eating an apple, with an expression of deep peace. There were three horses tied to a rail and their owners standing beside them, waiting.

Inside, at the anvil, with his coat off and his sleeves rolled and soot to the elbow, a big grey man was fitting a shoe.

He looked up when they came to the door. He looked at all of them. At the cart, and the blanket, and Salty. At the man with the rod. Salty felt it come off him then, the thing the others had talked about in the channel, *the king came to the lake and the hens went mad*: a weight, like weather, the whole of the ninth tier sitting down on the forge at once. Salty had felt Rhea's Gleaming and Aldous's Bonfire and, once, on the red, the man with the rod. He'd never felt anything like this.

"Ah," said the king. "You're the one who carried the stone."

Salty didn't know what to say. He'd never been spoken to by a king.

"Yes," he said.

"Good," said the king, and went back to the shoe.

***

Salty untied the cord. Both ends, and the middle. His hands were not as steady as he wanted. Wendy came and held one end of the blanket and Maigo held the other, without being asked, and they lifted it back between them, and it was there, on Hesk's bench in the red light of the forge.

He'd made it all winter. Since the first snow. Since Garrow came to the fort's forge with an anvil's worth of patience and said *you'll want to start with something small*, and Salty had said *no*. He'd made it at the second anvil while the snow came down and the pass shut and the war stayed asleep across the red. Garrow had taught him how to draw the steel out long and thin without it going brittle, and how to put the curve in, slow, a little every heat, and how to listen to it. *The heat stops being angry*, Garrow said, *if you wait for it*. Salty had never waited for anything in his life. He'd waited for this.

A scythe.

Not a farmer's. A long blade, nearly the length of Salty's arm and more, curved like a new moon, dark and grey and grained, with an edge he'd spent three weeks on and a spine he'd spent three more. The whole length of it, both faces, covered in hammer marks. Small, even, overlapping, like the scales on a fish. And on the shaft, a long pole of pale wood, straight and smooth and very slightly green under the varnish, with two grips on it at the places Yan's hands would go.

Maigo had grown the shaft. That was the other secret. A single straight branch, out of a cutting from the fort's one ash tree, coaxed up through a whole spring in a pot in the barracks window, a hand's width a week, while Maigo talked to it every night. *It wanted to be straight*, Maigo had said. *I just told it to keep going*. Niko had shaped the grips. Wendy had wrapped them, in strips of lavender cloth from the fort market that matched her headphones, because she'd said if Yan was going to hold something every day it ought to be something soft.

Five of them had made it. Salty had only done the iron.

He turned round.

"Yan," he said. His voice came out rough. "It's not horseshoes."

***

Yan looked at it.

He stood in the door of Hesk's forge with Suzu round his neck, and looked at the scythe on the bench. At the dark curved blade and the pale green-varnished shaft and the lavender grips. He looked at it for a long moment, with his calm half-lidded face.

And then he said, in a voice like a man reading a shopping list:

"Oh. Wow. A scythe. For me. What a surprise."

There was a silence in the forge.

Wendy put her hand over her face.

"*HE KNEW*," said Maigo.

Salty looked at Yan. Yan looked back, very calmly, with absolutely nothing at all on his face. Then his eyes went, very slightly, to Wendy.

"*Wendy*," said Salty.

"He'd have cried," said Wendy, from behind her hand. "In front of you. In the barracks. In the *winter*. If he'd found out by accident. So I told him. In the first week. So he could practise." She took her hand down. "He's been practising for *six months*. That was the best he could do."

"That was my best," said Yan.

"It was *terrible*," said Niko.

"I know," said Yan. "I'm not good at faces."

And then he stopped trying to have a face at all. He came across the forge, slowly, and put his hand on the scythe's shaft, where Maigo's branch was, and closed his fingers round Wendy's lavender grip. And lifted it. It came up off the bench light and long and balanced, the dark blade swinging round in the red light of the forge, and Yan stood in the middle of Hesk's forge holding it, a tall silver-haired man with a white snake round his neck and a scythe in his hands, looking for the first time in two years exactly like the drawing he'd been when they all came through the hall.

Suzu lifted her head.

She uncoiled from his neck. Slowly. Salty had seen her do it before, on the red, when it mattered: growing, the white length of her pouring off Yan's shoulders and down his arm, longer and longer, until she was nearly six feet of pale scales with a green belly. She went down Yan's arm and onto the scythe's shaft, and wound herself round it. Once, twice, three times, up the pale wood, to the top, where the blade met the shaft. And settled there. With her head resting on the curve of the blade, and her bright green eyes half shut.

Yan stood very still.

"She likes it," said Yan. His voice wasn't steady now. "She—I don't know what she says. But she likes it."

"She's not translating?" said Salty.

"No," said Yan. And then, after a moment, very quietly: "I think I'm going to stop guessing."

He didn't cry. Salty had been sure he would. He stood in the forge with the scythe in his hands and Suzu wound round its shaft, and his face did nothing at all, and Wendy had been wrong, he didn't cry. He just stood there holding it as if somebody had handed him something he'd been waiting for since before he was born.

Over by the door, Kei made a small sound. He had his hand up at his own neck, on his own snake, and his head tipped. As if something round his neck was saying something very fast and very loudly and he was trying not to laugh.

***

The king had stopped hammering.

Salty didn't notice when. But the forge had gone quiet, and when he looked round, the big grey man had put down Hesk's hammer and the horseshoe and come away from the anvil, and was standing beside Yan, looking at the scythe.

Not at Suzu. At the blade.

"May I?" said the king.

Yan held it out. Suzu didn't move off the shaft. The king didn't seem to mind. He took the scythe in his two enormous scarred hands, snake and all, and held the blade up into the light of the forge fire, and looked at it, very close.

At the hammer marks.

Small, even, overlapping, like the scales on a fish. Thousands of them. Salty had made every one. And in every one, very faint, unless you held it to the light and looked very close, a little mark. A tiny crescent, like the edge of a fingernail, pressed into the steel. In every single strike. All down both faces of the blade, thousands of tiny moons.

The king stopped breathing.

Salty saw him do it. He saw the whole of that great weight, the ninth tier that had sat down on the forge when they came in, go perfectly still. The king turned the blade slowly in the light. He put out one finger, very carefully, and touched one of the little crescents. Then another. Then he closed his eyes and ran his fingertips along the flat of the blade, down the whole length of it, the way a blind man reads a face.

"That's my father's hammer," said the king.

Nobody said anything.

"That's my father's hammer," he said again. Quietly. Not to anyone. "That's the nick in the face. He chipped it on a hardy when he was fourteen and wouldn't grind it out, because it was his master's and you don't." He opened his eyes. "I'd know that mark anywhere. I learned on it. I carried it fifty years." He looked at Salty. "I gave it to a smith in Wrenhaven in the autumn."

"Garrow," said Salty.

His voice came out strange. He reached into his belt. He'd had it there since the spring, since Garrow went home. He'd carried it every day. He took it out and held it up in the red light: a small old cross-peen hammer, the handle dark and smooth with use, a burn on the haft near the head where somebody had left it in the coals once, long ago.

"He taught me on it," said Salty. "All winter. At Cinderhold. He said—" He stopped. He said it the way Garrow had said it to him, in the spring, at the fort gate, with the dray loaded and the oxen waiting and his eyes wet: "He said *a good hammer ought to be working, not riding about in somebody's coat*. He said finish it. He said bring it back to Wrenhaven when you're done."

The king looked at the hammer in Salty's hand.

He didn't take it. Salty held it out, and the king looked at it for a long time, and didn't take it.

"My father used to say that," said the king.

"Garrow said a king told him."

"A king did tell him," said the king. "The king's father told the king. In Brakewater. When the king was ten, and wanted to hang it on the wall." He looked at the scythe in his hands, at the thousands of tiny moons. "And it's been working. All winter. In the snow. On the edge of the red. Making this." His voice did something. "For a soul mage. With a snake on it."

He gave the scythe back to Yan. Carefully. Suzu shifted on the shaft and settled again.

Then he put his hand on Salty's shoulder. It was like having a mountain put its hand on your shoulder.

"Take it back to Garrow," said the king. "When you go north. Tell him it did good work." He paused. "Tell him the king's father would have liked the edge."

Salty couldn't say anything. He nodded.

"And the man who carried the stone," said the king, "is very good at a long blade, for a man who's been smithing six months. Who taught you to put the curve in a heat at a time?"

"Garrow."

"And who taught Garrow?" said the king, and something in his beard was very nearly a smile.

Salty didn't know. He looked round.

At the door of the forge, leaning on the frame with a fishing rod over his shoulder and two Pollen Puffs asleep on his head and his daughter beside him, the man he'd asked a question on the red cleared his throat, very slightly.

"You," said the king, without turning round, "taught nobody anything. You held a chisel."

"I held it very well," said the man.

"You held it *adequately*," said the king, and laughed, the short deep sound like a hammer landing once on an anvil, and went back across the forge to the horse that was still waiting, very patiently, with one bare hoof.

***

They went back up the Street of Armourers in the dark, all of them, toward the Broken Lance and its long table and whatever Lucan had decided to put on it.

Salty walked at the back. He had his hands in his pockets, and the king's father's hammer back in his belt, and his ribs aching the way they did when he'd laughed or cried or lifted something, and he'd done all three. Ahead of him Yan walked with the scythe over his shoulder and Suzu wound round its shaft, and Kei walked beside Yan, very close, with his own snake round his neck saying something so fast and so loud that Kei had given up trying not to laugh. Maigo was telling Alea about the tree. Niko was walking beside the man with the rod and not saying anything, very chill, with his shoulder touching his. Wendy had her arm through Salty's.

"Eleven pies," said Wendy.

"Eleven pies."

"You're going to be sick."

"I'm going to eat every one," said Salty. "Slowly. In front of him."

Wendy laughed and put her head on his shoulder as they walked.

At the corner of the street, under a lamp, with a paper fishing rod in the window above it, somebody was waiting for them. The king, in his shirtsleeves, with soot to the elbow, who must have finished the horse and come up the street the back way, faster than any of them. He was holding something.

He held it out to Salty as he came past.

A horseshoe. New. Still faintly warm. Fitted, cut, punched, every nail-hole clean. A good one. On the face of it, along the curve, in a row, small and even, were the marks of somebody's hammer.

Not his father's. His own.

"For the cart," said the king. "So it's not a lie any more."

Salty looked at the horseshoe in his hand.

Then he laughed. Out loud, in the street, under the paper rods, until his ribs hurt and he had to hold on to Wendy. And the king went off up the hill toward his house without another word, in his shirtsleeves, and nobody in the street looked at him either.

Salty carried the horseshoe the rest of the way to the Broken Lance. He'd put it on the cart in the morning.

He was very bad at secrets. He was getting better at horseshoes.
