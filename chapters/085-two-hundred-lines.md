Xola was the one who brought people to people.

She'd always been. She didn't know how it had started. It was just the shape she was. On Earth, she'd been the one who said *oh, you HAVE to meet my friend*, and dragged somebody into a call, and stayed there, chattering, until the two of them were talking to each other instead of to her, and then sat back and watched it happen with her chin in her hands, delighted. She'd brought Thea to Gigichi. Thea to Ice. Ice to all of them.

Higu had drawn it on the back of a letter, the first winter. Five names, and lines, and all the lines running into the middle of the page, like spokes into a hub. Into her. Xola had beamed at it. She hadn't been able to help it.

And then Higu had drawn one more line, across the rim, that didn't go through the middle at all, from herself to Gigichi. Six years. Xola had beamed at that too. That was the best part of being the hub. Sometimes the spokes found each other without you.

Here, in this world, she was mostly the one who cried first.

She didn't mind. Somebody had to. It let everybody else know it was all right.

***

The snow came, and stayed, and the militia hall became a ship.

That was what it felt like. The town buried to its windowsills, and the paths dug out between the drifts like trenches, and the lake white from shore to shore, and the long timber hall up on its hill with its fires going and its shutters closed, riding out the winter like a ship riding out a storm. Maren drilled them in the morning, in the yard, on the hard-packed snow. Afternoons were for the upper room: windows open, numbers compared, the channel.

The channel was different now.

Two hundred lines. Times on every one. Xola loved it so much she couldn't stop scrolling. She'd lie on her cot in the afternoons with Squiddles on her chest and her window open and scroll back and back and back, through yesterday, and the day before, all the way to the top, just because she could. Reading things she'd missed. Mashi's heartbeats, every night, *three, still no faint*, *four*, *four again*. Maigo in capitals. Kei's mushrooms. Rachael's patients. Tiger's goat, which had now eaten four of Drew's boots and one of Hild's. Malcolm's IFs. Chris's numbers, every evening at the same time, *21:00 · Chris: Logging.*, all thirty-five, in a column, with a blank one at the end that he never filled in.

Everybody loved it. Maigo had written *WHOEVER DID THIS I LOVE YOU* in the first week, and then again in the second, and then started writing it every few days, as a sort of greeting. Other people joined in. Tiger. Newish. Rachael. *WHOEVER DID THIS I LOVE YOU.*

And every time somebody wrote it, Xola noticed, Thea and Higu looked at each other.

***

She noticed it the first time without knowing she'd noticed. She was lying on her cot with her window open and Maigo's line came up, and she laughed, and turned her head to say *look what Maigo's done*, and saw Thea, at the table, look up from her own window. At Higu. And Higu, at the window, look back.

Just for a moment. Not long. The kind of look two people give each other across a room when somebody's said a thing that means something to them and nothing to anybody else.

The second time, she noticed on purpose.

By the tenth, she'd started keeping count. Like Higu kept lists. She didn't write it down. She didn't need to. Every time somebody in the channel mentioned the two hundred lines, or the times, or Thea saying *it wasn't me*, Thea and Higu looked at each other. And then looked away. And then didn't say anything.

They'd been not saying anything for three weeks.

Xola was the hub. She brought people to people. She couldn't bear it when two people had a thing between them and everyone else was outside it, looking in, not knowing what it was. It made her skin itch.

On the twenty-second night, snowed in, with all five of them in the upper room and the fire going and nobody even pretending to sleep, she sat up on her cot with Squiddles on her head and said:

"You two keep doing the look."

***

Everybody went still.

Gigichi, on the floor with her back against the wall, looked up. PenguChi, sitting on the end of her cot with Waddles in her lap, looked up. Thea, at the table, went very slightly pink. And Higu, at the window, where she always sat, didn't move at all. Which was how Xola knew she'd been right.

"What look?" said Thea.

"*The* look," said Xola. "Every time someone in the channel says *whoever did this*. Every time. You look at Gu, and she looks at you, and then you both go quiet and pretend you're reading." She folded her arms. Squiddles, on her head, folded his tentacles, in solidarity. "Twenty-two times. I counted."

"You counted," said Higu.

"I learned it from you," said Xola.

Higu looked at her for a long moment. Then, very slightly, at the corner of her mouth, something that might have been a smile.

"That's fair," she said.

***

Thea told them.

She did it the way Thea did everything about the windows: fast, with her hands, as if the thing she was describing were hanging in the air in front of her and she could point to the parts of it. Higu didn't say anything. She sat at the window and listened and, every now and then, nodded.

"The channel's hooked into the first window," said Thea. "You know that. I told you in the spring, the night I built it. Every window copies the first one. Mine, yours, everybody's. And the channel runs underneath all of them, in the shared part, the part they all inherit." She drew a shape in the air: a tree, upside down, with roots. "I built the channel in the middle. I can reach down into it a bit. Not all the way. The forty-line limit was down at the bottom, where the first window is, and I couldn't touch it. I tried. For months. It wouldn't let me."

"You said," said Gigichi. "To Dennis. *Written too deep.*"

"And then, the night Dennis asked, somebody rewrote it." Thea put her hand flat on the table. "Two hundred lines. Times on everything. Overnight. In the same writing the whole window's written in. Not my writing. *Its* writing. The writing the window was made in, in the first place."

She looked at them.

"Somebody who could write at the very bottom," said Thea. "Below me. Below all of us. In the first window." She swallowed. "Somebody who was reading the channel at two in the morning when Dennis asked. And answered him by dawn."

Nobody said anything.

"The man who saw his window first," said Higu quietly, from the window. "That's what the Advisor said. In the hall, the first day. *The Hero's words. The man who saw his window first gave it that name.*" She didn't look round. "The first window is his. It's always been his. We worked that out in the summer, at this table. The Hero. The fisherman. Ice." She was quiet a moment. "Whoever owns the first window can write at the bottom. And he did."

***

"We think he can read it," said Thea. "The channel." Her voice had gone very small. "All of it."

The fire cracked.

"All of it," said Xola.

"Every line," said Higu. "Since the first night." She turned her head, finally, and looked at them. "I saw him, the first night. With the moth. I told you. On the jetty, reading a window, scrolling. I didn't know what he was reading. I thought it was his own window. His numbers." She looked down at her notebook in her lap. "It was us."

Xola sat very still on her cot.

She thought about the channel. A year of it. A year and more. Everything any of them had ever written in it.

She thought about the Ice book.

***

They all thought about it at the same time. She saw it go across their faces, one after another, the way a shadow goes across a field when a cloud goes over.

The Ice book. Hako's book. *What was he like?* Twenty-three pages of it, in the channel, in the summer, every one of the thirty-five, one after another, telling stories about a boy who'd died. Stories they'd never have told if they'd known he was listening. Higu's ban poem. Xola's subs. Thea's calls, every night, the singing. PenguChi's word games. Gigichi—

Xola looked at Gigichi.

Gigichi had gone white.

She'd written it in the Ice book. All of them had read it. *He sent me "you okay?" once, when nobody else noticed I'd gone quiet. I never answered. I'm answering now. I'm okay. Mostly.*

She'd written that to a dead boy. In a channel she thought only thirty-five people could see.

"He read it," said Gigichi. Barely a whisper.

"Chi," said Higu.

"He *read* it. My *answer*. I wrote it to him because I thought he'd never—" She stopped. She put her hands over her face. "Oh, god. Oh, *god*. And the poem. And the night of the sum. *Declan, if you can somehow read this.* Gabe wrote that. Gabe *wrote* that, and he—"

"He could," said Thea. Very quietly. "He could somehow read it."

***

Nobody said anything for a long time.

PenguChi had both arms round Waddles. Gigichi had her hands over her face. Thea was staring at the table. Higu was looking out of the window, at the lake, at the one lamp at the end of the lake road, burning in the snow.

Xola sat on her cot and felt it come up in her the way it always did. The crying. First, before anyone. She let it. Somebody had to.

But it wasn't the kind she'd thought it would be. That was the strange thing. She'd thought, if anyone ever told her that he'd read the Ice book, read her story about the subs, about how he'd gifted them in every stream even when she barely streamed, about how she'd wished she'd talked to him more in the run-up, at the end, she'd thought she'd die of it. Of embarrassment. Of sadness.

She didn't. What came up in her instead was something else. Something warm and enormous and very sore, like a bruise on your heart that you'd forgotten about, being pressed, and finding that it didn't hurt the way you'd thought it would. It hurt the other way.

"Then he knows," said Xola.

They looked at her.

"He knows we know," said Xola. Her voice was wobbling. She didn't care. "If he's been reading it. All of it. Since the first night." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Then he read us do the sum. In the summer. All of us, at once, working it out. He read Gabe. He read Milli say *it fits*. He read Yan say *whoever killed that demon held that sword*." She swallowed. "And he read *us*."

"We didn't write anything," said Gigichi, through her hands. "The night of the sum. We didn't write anything at all. Not one of us. Not Thea, not Gu."

"*Exactly*," said Xola.

***

"Rule five," said Higu softly.

They all looked at her.

"We never wrote her name," said Higu. "Alea. Not once. Not in a year. Not her name, not her dad, not the fish, not Wrenhaven, not the cabin, not the lake. Not anything. Rule five. She asked us, in the yard, the week the channel started, and we said yes, and we never did." She looked out at the lamp. "And then rule six. *We wait for him.*"

"He'd have seen that," said PenguChi slowly. Her calm voice was very quiet. "If he was reading. He'd have seen all thirty-five of us talking about him every night for a year. And the five of us, in Wrenhaven, where he actually is..." She stopped.

"Saying nothing," said Thea.

"Saying nothing," said PenguChi. "Every night. For a year. Not where. Not who. Not anything." She looked down at Waddles. "He watched us not tell anyone."

Xola thought about that. A man on the end of a jetty, in the dark, every night, reading thirty-five people talk about him. And five of them, on the hill above his house, writing *we're glad you're safe* and nothing else. Never once writing the name of his daughter, or his town, or the thing they'd worked out at a table in the summer.

Watching them protect him. Without knowing they were doing it for him. Without being able to say thank you.

"Then he knows we know," said Xola again. "And he knows we're waiting. He's known the whole time."

***

"Then why hasn't he—" said Gigichi.

She'd taken her hands off her face. Her eyes were red. She was angry, the way Gigichi got angry when she was frightened: all at once, at everything, sitting up very straight against the wall.

"If he knows we know," said Gigichi, "and he knows we're waiting, and he's been reading every word we've ever written about him for a *year*, including my *stupid answer*, then why hasn't he just come up the hill? Why hasn't he said *hi*? Why is he down there in the snow every night with his *rod*, reading, when we're *right here*, we're *right up here*, it's a *ten-minute walk*—"

"He's waiting too," said PenguChi.

Gigichi stopped.

PenguChi was looking at the lamp. Her golden eyes very steady. She said it the way she said everything, slow and calm and low, the voice that did things to rooms.

"When he walked past the gate in the autumn," said PenguChi. "On his way to the Redmarch. When he looked at each of us." She stroked Waddles's head. "I've seen that face before. In my chat, on Earth. On people who've been holding something for a long time and are about to say it, and can't, quite." She was quiet. "He was frightened. I didn't know of what. I do now." She looked round at them. "We've been waiting for him to be ready. And he's been waiting to be brave enough." She was quiet. "It's the same wait. It's just from the other side of the door."

Nobody said anything.

Gigichi looked at PenguChi for a long time. Then she put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes.

"Rule six is stupid," she said.

"Rule six is stupid," Higu agreed.

"It's the only rule that matters," said Gigichi.

"It's the only rule that matters," said Higu.

***

"We could write to him," said Xola.

They all looked at her.

"In the channel," said Xola. She was sitting up very straight on her cot, with Squiddles on her head gone a hopeful sort of pink. "If he's reading it. We could just. Write. *Hi. We know. Come for supper.* It's ten words. He'd read it tonight."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"No," said Thea.

"*Why*—"

"Because everyone else would read it too," said Thea. "All thirty-five. And then they'd know where he is. And he hasn't said they can." She looked at her hands. "Rule five, Xola. It isn't ours."

Xola deflated. Squiddles went grey.

"And because," said Higu, from the window, "if we write it, then he comes up the hill because we asked." She was looking at the lamp. "I don't want him to come because we asked. I want him to come because he's ready. Because he wants to." She was quiet. "He's been doing things for us for a year without being asked. The fish. The coats. The lessons. The two hundred lines. He's never once waited to be asked." She closed her notebook. "This one should be his too."

***

But Thea wrote something.

Later. After the others had lain down, and the fire had burned low, and Xola was lying on her cot with Squiddles on her chest pretending to be asleep and watching her through her lashes. Thea, at the table, alone, with her window open in front of her and her face lit faintly blue by it. She sat there for a long time without writing anything.

Then she wrote one line. Xola saw it come up in her own window a moment later, in the channel, in the middle of the night, among Mashi's heartbeats and Kei's mushrooms:

[ 01:14 · Althea: Whoever did the two hundred lines: thank you. ]

That was all. The kind of thing anybody might have written. The kind of thing Maigo wrote every few days, in capitals.

A few minutes later, from somewhere on the eastern frontier, half asleep:

[ 01:19 · Maigo: WHOEVER DID THIS I LOVE YOU ]

And then, from Thornwall:

[ 01:22 · Tiger: whoever did this i love you ]

And then Rachael. And Newish. And Hako, of all people. And Dennis, who wrote *whoever did this, I love you, and I'd like to discuss your rates*. One after another, in the middle of the night, half the kingdom waking up for a moment to say the same thing to nobody, and going back to sleep.

Thea sat at the table and watched them come up, one by one, with her hand over her mouth.

Xola lay on her cot and watched Thea.

And then she looked past her. Out of the window. Down the hill, over the snowbound roofs of Wrenhaven, to the lake, white from shore to shore under the stars. To the one lamp at the end of the lake road. And below it, on the end of the jetty, so small and so far away she could only just make it out, a dark shape, sitting in the snow. With the faint shine of a window open on its knees.

She watched it for a long time.

She didn't know what he was reading. She knew exactly what he was reading.

***

Xola had always been the one who brought people to people.

She'd brought Thea to Ice. Ice to all of them. She'd sat back with her chin in her hands and watched it happen, every time, delighted.

She couldn't bring this one. She knew that. It wasn't hers. It was his.

But she lay on her cot in the dark with Squiddles on her chest and, very quietly, so that nobody would see, lifted one hand up off the blanket, and waved. Down the hill. At the lamp. At the small dark shape on the end of the jetty in the snow, reading thirty-five people tell nobody that they loved him.

She didn't know if he could see it. He couldn't, probably. It was dark, and a long way, and she was just a girl on a cot behind a shuttered window.

She waved anyway.

On her chest, Squiddles had turned a colour she'd never seen him go before. Not pink. Not grey. A soft warm gold, like a lamp in a window, in the snow.
