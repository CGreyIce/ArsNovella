He kept his hand up until she put hers down.

That took a while. She stood in the middle of the main sand with Astra in her right hand and her left up beside her face, open, the way his was, and the bowl went on making the noise it was making all round her; and she didn't put it down, and he didn't, and forty thousand people looked from one of them to the other and back again, across forty paces of sand and four tiers of stand, as if they were watching a line go out over water and couldn't see the end of it.

He could feel all of them. He always could. Forty thousand candles in a bowl of stone, every one of them leaning the same way at once, the way a field of barley leans; and in the middle of them, on the sand, one he'd known since it was a spark in a cot by a stove.

It was different.

He'd felt it change. He'd been sitting very still on the end of the fourth tier with the woman's little girl holding his finger in her fist, and he'd felt it go: down, all of it, out of Alea's feet and out of the whole of her and into the steel, the way a line goes down into deep water when a big fish takes it and runs for the bottom. He'd felt it go into the sword he'd carried through a war and cut in half on an anvil with a king. And he'd felt it stay.

Honed. She was Honed.

It sat in her now like a stone sits in a river. Not loud. Not bright. Just there, and the water going round it. He'd never felt anything sit in anyone so quietly. It hadn't sat in him like that, when it came. It had sat in him like a coal.

She put her hand down.

So did he. He sat down on the bench. The little girl beside him, who'd been a baby last year, who'd been asleep on her mother's shoulder the whole of the closing while twenty thousand paper rods went up round her, took hold of his finger again as if she'd only lent it to him. Her mother looked at him, and slid off him, and came back, the way everybody's eyes did, and didn't say anything. She'd moved over for him at noon without being asked. She'd moved over for him last year.

Then Sorrel came over the rail.

***

She came over it barefoot, from the near stand, with her hooks over her shoulder, the way you'd come over the gunwale of a boat; dropped six feet onto the sand without breaking stride, ran the whole width of the main sand with the marshals shouting at her, and hit Alea in the middle of the chalk ring so hard they both sat down.

The bowl laughed. Forty thousand people at once. It went round the stands like a wave going round a harbour.

Then Group 1. Not over the rail; through the tunnel, the five of them in a row, the way they always seemed to end up, Thea in front walking fast and Xola running and Gigichi walking very slowly on purpose with her face doing nothing at all, and Higu at the back with her notebook shut. And Gabe behind them, enormous, redder than anything had ever been, who had won the summoned's bracket at noon and still didn't know what to do with his hands, and who solved it now by picking Alea up off the sand with Sorrel still attached to her, both of them, and putting them on his shoulders.

Ars watched her go up. Astra too. Gabe didn't seem to notice the extra weight.

She looked up at the fourth tier once more, from Gabe's shoulder. Found him. Didn't wave. Then Lucan got to the chalk ring in his good coat, crying so hard he couldn't see where he was going, and walked into Gabe from behind, and the whole lot of them disappeared into marshals and summoned and fledglings and the near stand coming over the rail in a body, and he lost sight of her.

He didn't go down.

He'd known he wouldn't. It was hers. All of it: the sand, the noise, the boy from the guard school saluting with his back very straight, the girl with the hooks sitting on her in the ring. It was the first thing in her life she'd done without anybody watching her do it, and he wasn't going to walk down four tiers in front of forty thousand people and stand in the middle of it.

He'd done what he came for. He'd been where she'd know to find him, after.

The little girl let go of his finger. He stood up. He nodded to her mother, who nodded back, at the place beside him, the way people did. And he went along the fourth tier and down the back stair of the east stand, behind everybody, while every face in the bowl was turned to the sand; and nobody looked round.

***

He'd sat in the north stand for nine days.

High up. At the back, under the overhang, in the shade, where the seats were cheapest and the stone was cracked and the view of the near ring was half blocked by a pillar. He'd chosen it for the pillar. He'd come in every morning by the painters' yard before the gates opened, with the rod left behind the blue door, and sat in the same seat with nothing in his hands, and gone out the same way after dark.

Deston had heard him on the first day. He'd felt Deston hear him: a boy on the healers' bench with his eyes shut, listening to a bowl full of people like a choir, finding one voice in the north stand that he knew, and very carefully turning his whole head the other way. Deston had kept it turned all week. Ars had watched him do it every morning and been more grateful than he could have said, if anybody had asked, which nobody had.

Rachael had known by the second. She hadn't looked. She'd just folded her linen a little more precisely.

He'd watched all of it. The Swiss. Dennis going down with his forehead open and Rachael kneeling, and Deston taking Calvin's wind in one hand and Rachael's in the other, and the cut closing, and two candles going up a step at once, one bout, so close together he'd felt them like two notes. He'd gone up with them. A little. He hadn't meant to. Deston had felt that too; he'd seen the boy's head come up, and smile, and not turn round.

Xav, standing in the ring, deciding late. He'd been behind the rail for that one; he'd come down for it, because he'd known what it would be, and he'd said *save one for the jetty* at the back of Xav's neck, and Xav hadn't turned round, and had said *I am*.

The sand jumping. He'd felt that in his feet, in the north stand, like the anvil at Garrow's when the king hit it.

And the bell.

He'd let it out. On midsummer day, when Niko's blunt steel touched Chris under the left arm and rang through the whole bowl like a bell in a valley, and every candle in the Lists jumped. He'd let it out one heartbeat. Not on purpose. Something had come up out of the bottom of him at the sound, the way the lake comes up through a hole in the ice; and he'd pulled it back in before the bell had stopped, and sat very still under the overhang with his hands flat on his knees, and felt a boy on the main sand with a notebook in his pocket turn round and look straight up at the north stand.

Chris hadn't written it in the channel. Chris had written *line 36 still blank. still there*, that night, the way he did every night. True. He just didn't say where.

Ars had read it on the step of the blue door and laughed out loud, alone, in a stranger's yard.

***

Then the bracket. Three days of it. Eight boxes at the right-hand end of the great wall, lettered in gold.

Salty had filled the eighth one in the last light on midsummer day. The decider: two and two against two and two, a long grim one, the boy on the other side very fast and Salty very patient, until the patience ran out on the other side first; and Salty had stood in the middle of the main sand with the armoury's heaviest blunted greatsword over his shoulder and looked up at the north stand. Straight up. Not at Ars. At the overhang. As if he'd been told where to look and was doing it once, for the dinners, and wouldn't again.

Then the quarters. Gabe over Salty, the wall and the forge, neither of them moving for a long time and the sand jumping and Gabe not. Wendy over Jared, three arrows held in the air and counted, and Jared's *Stand* going wrong for the first time anyone had seen it, because Wendy wasn't where he'd planted himself against. Xav over Hako again, *now* and *third*, the door shut. And Niko over Milli.

That one he'd watched with his hand over his mouth.

Milli had beaten him in the second round of the Swiss. *Pounce*: there and then not there, through the place where Niko had been a heartbeat before. Niko had lost and grinned and said *again*. And in the quarters he'd stood in the ring across from Milli with his blunt steel low and his breath very quiet, two candles in him, the one that wasn't magic and the one that was, and found the gap. Not in Milli. In himself. The off-beat. Where he was nobody, and *Pounce* had nothing to pounce on.

Ars had said it. Under the overhang. Alone. Not loud. *Mashi*.

He'd watched him beat Xav in the semi the same way. Xav deciding late, and Niko not deciding at all; and *now* reaching for something to feel and finding a gap where a boy should be. Xav had laughed on the sand. Ars had seen him. Laughed, and put his sword up, and said something Ars couldn't hear and could guess. *Gg*. Then he'd looked up at the north stand too.

Everybody did, by the end of the week. Not at him. Near him. The way you'd look toward a lamp you'd been told not to mention.

Then the final. Noon today. Gabe and Niko on the main sand, and nobody in the Lists could get past Gabe when he planted his feet behind a blunt armoury sword and put *Wall* in front of it and leaned. Niko had tried for a long time. The gap, the off-beat, the bell; and the bell had rung on Gabe's wall like a hammer on an anvil and the wall hadn't moved, because Gabe didn't move, because Gabe had never moved for anything in his life that he didn't want to move for, except once, on a jetty, at midnight, to sit down.

Gabe had touched him on the shoulder. Very lightly. And then stood there with his hands, not knowing what to do with them.

And Niko had sat on the sand with his blunt sword across his knees, Tier 1, the one Chris couldn't rank, beaten in the final of the whole Lists by a boy who'd made his own sword, and grinned up at Gabe and said *again*.

Ars had felt that one all the way to the north stand. Not a bell. Not a candle going up. Something else. The thing that made a boy say *again* every time.

He'd had it once.

***

The blue door was at the end of a yard off the Street of Coopers, with a cooper's shop on one side and a chandler's on the other and a narrow house between them that Lucan's aunt had lived in and nobody had since. Two rooms. A kitchen with a stove that drew badly. A table. On the table, a crock with a cloth tied over it that Lucan had brought round himself on the fourth night, after the sand jumped, and put down without a word, and gone away crying.

He didn't go in. He went round. Through the yard, past the cooper's barrels, to the back gate; through the back gate into the painters' yard, which ran behind the south stand of the Lists all the way along under the wall, where Lucan's painters kept their ladders and their pots of gold and their tarred sheets, and where, this week, under a lean-to roofed with sailcloth, there'd been forty thousand paper fishing rods stacked in bundles to keep them dry.

The bundles were gone. Given out at the gates that morning. There was a bare place on the cobbles where they'd been, and a few paper fish that had come off their lines lying in it.

The rod was where he'd left it. Leaning in the corner by the painters' gate, behind a stack of ladders, where nobody would look because nobody looked at ladders.

He picked it up.

It was good to have it in his hand. He hadn't held it in nine days. He'd held it every day for nine years before that, and he'd left it in a corner behind some ladders for nine days so that he could sit in a stand with his hands empty, and his hands had felt wrong the whole time, the way your mouth feels wrong with a tooth out. He sat down on an upturned paint pot with the rod across his knees and his hands on it.

The noise of the bowl came over the wall. Still going. It would go on for an hour, Lucan had told him; they always did, for a holder.

He sat and listened to it.

He didn't hear her come. He felt her. He'd felt her coming since the south tunnel, a stone in a river, moving; down under the south stand, along the dark passage behind the marshals' room, through a door that only painters used; and he didn't look up from the rod until she was in the painters' yard with Astra on her back and chalk on her boots and her hair coming out of its tie, standing by the bare place where the bundles had been, looking at him.

She'd got away from them. He didn't know how. He'd ask Sorrel later; Sorrel would know.

They looked at each other.

"You came," said Alea.

"You didn't ask."

"No."

"That's why," said Ars.

***

She sat down on the cobbles next to the paint pot.

Not on another pot. On the cobbles, with her back against the wall of the south stand, and her knees up, and Astra laid across them, the way she'd sat on the end of the jetty when she was small, with a stick across hers. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did he. The bowl roared on the other side of the wall.

"I didn't look," she said.

"I know. I watched you not look." He was quiet. "All week."

"Was it hard?"

He thought about it. About the north stand and the pillar and his empty hands. About watching her eyes go to the fourth tier of the east stand on the first morning, and find a woman with a baby, and come back, and come back, and come back, for four days, and never once go anywhere near the overhang where he was, because she wasn't asking.

"Yes," said Ars.

She nodded. As if that was the right answer. As if it was the one she'd needed.

"I felt you," she said. "At the end. On the chalk. When my heels were on it." She didn't look at him. She looked at the bare place on the cobbles where the bundles had been. "I didn't know you'd moved. I didn't look. I just knew where you were. Like the end of the jetty in the dark."

"I moved this morning," said Ars. "Before the bracket."

"Why?"

He looked at the rod.

"So you'd know where to find me," he said. "After. If you wanted to." A pause. "Not before. I'd not have been any use before. Before was yours."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Your weight went back," said Ars.

"I know."

"On the chalk. Ten cuts."

"I know." She looked at the sword across her knees. "I let it."

"You let it." He thought about the gravel bar at Duskhollow, which he hadn't seen, and which Petra had told him about one night at the blue door, in four words, the way Petra told you things: *heels. a hand. fixed?* "Where'd you get that?"

"A boat," said Alea. "It's called NEXT YEAR. You don't hold it. It holds you."

He laughed.

It came out of him short and surprised, the way hers had on the fourth tier last summer, and went up the wall of the south stand and over it into the noise. She looked round at him. She'd heard him laugh perhaps a dozen times in her life. He knew, because he'd counted them, the year she was twelve, when he'd realised it was a number you could count.

"Your ears," he said.

"What about them."

"Flat. Then up. On three." He looked at the rod. "She did that."

Alea didn't say anything.

"At the tent flap," said Ars. "Every time. I'd stand outside and watch them go flat, and then I'd count. And on three they'd come up, and she'd go in." He turned the rod over in his hands. "I could tell from forty paces what she was going to do. From her ears. I never told her." He was quiet. "I saw yours go flat on the chalk, and I counted. And on three."

"On three," said Alea.

"On three."

She put her head against his arm. Not a hug. She'd done that on the quay below the mill, in front of the whole town, and he'd gone still for a heartbeat and then held on. This was different. Just her head, against his arm, on the side away from the rod, the way she'd put it there on the end of the jetty when she was small and tired and the fish weren't coming.

"It's *Wait*," she said, into his sleeve. "The new one. The word."

He started laughing again. He couldn't help it. She thumped him.

"I know," she said. "I *know*."

***

They sat there until the noise over the wall began to change. Not stopping. Changing. Forty thousand people who'd been making a noise for an hour beginning, all at once, to go somewhere, the way a crowd does when it remembers there's another thing coming.

The closing. At sunset. Lucan would be on the platform already, in the gold fringe, with the roll.

Alea stood up. She put Astra on her back. She looked down at him on his paint pot.

"Are you coming?" she said. "To the closing."

It was a question. She heard it. He saw her hear it, saw her ears go back a little, and then decide.

"I'm asking," she said. "This one I'm asking."

"I'll be in my place," said Ars.

She nodded. She went to the painters' door. At the door she stopped and looked back at him, at the rod across his knees.

"Bring that," said Alea, and went.

***

The closing was the same as last year. Lucan made sure of that. He'd told Ars so on the fourth night, standing in the kitchen behind the blue door with the crock on the table between them and his eyes running: *it's the same every year, it has to be, that's what makes it a closing*. The platform at the edge of the sand under the royal box, hung with red cloth and gold fringe and paper fishing rods. The marshals in a row. The rocking chair. The roll. The sun going down over the west stand, red and slow, and the whole bowl of the Lists full and gold.

The royal box was empty. The king was at Thornwall, standing on a tower with a girl who could hold thirteen rings at eleven miles, looking at seven threadless and a basket; and he'd sent word to Lucan in the spring that he wouldn't be at the Lists this year, and that Lucan should not hold the closing for him, and that there was a box at the top of a Fledgling tree that he would very much like to hear about.

Ars sat on the end of the fourth tier of the east stand with the rod across his knees.

The woman with the little girl was there. She'd kept his place. She'd put her shawl on it, folded, and she took it off when he came along the tier, and moved over, and didn't look at him. The little girl took his finger.

Nobody else looked at him either. Not yet.

They read the champions in.

Lucan did it from the edge of the platform, with the roll held up and his voice going out over the bowl like a kite, the way he'd done it for fifteen years; and every champion came up the steps when their name was called and wrote in the box, who they were and where they were from and who their people were, so that the city knew. The spear and the bow and the long-sword, and the Gate, and the Banner: guild hands and garrison captains, and a woman from the river towns with a bow taller than she was.

Then the summoned's bracket.

"*Gabe*," read Lucan.

Gabe came up the steps. All of him. He'd washed, Ars saw, and put on a clean shirt that was too small, and his hands were black again anyway, from somewhere; he'd been at a forge. Of course he had. Lucan held out the pen. Gabe took it the way he'd take a pair of tongs, and stood looking at the box for a long time, enormous, red, with forty thousand people waiting; and then he wrote. Not long. In this world's capitals, slowly, one letter at a time, the way Drew had last year.

Lucan read it. His eyes went along the line, and stopped, and went back.

"*Gabe*," read Lucan, "*of Kingsferry. A smith*." A breath. "*Of Harl's forge. And of the end of a jetty*."

The bowl didn't know what that meant. It cheered anyway, because it was the Lists and it was sunset and he was enormous and red and had held a wall against a bell.

Ars sat very still on the fourth tier.

He'd never been called a jetty before. He'd been called a lot of things. He found he had his hand over his mouth again, and took it away, and the little girl took the finger back.

Gabe went down the steps without looking at the east stand. He didn't need to. Ars felt him not look. All the way down.

***

"*The Fledgling*," said Lucan.

His voice went. Of course it did. It had held last year, for the only time that week, and Ars had always remembered it; and this year it went on the first word, and he stood on the edge of the platform with the roll in both hands and his face running and couldn't get any further, and forty thousand people waited for him the way you'd wait for an old dog to get up a step.

"*The holder*," said Lucan, at last.

She came up the steps.

Astra on her back. Chalk on her boots. Her hair still coming out of its tie; she hadn't fixed it. She walked to the edge of the platform where Lucan was standing and took the pen out of his hand, gently, because he'd forgotten he was holding it, and turned to the roll.

The box under her name was blank. It always was, the second year. Lucan had explained it on the fourth night. A holder's people had been read once; they didn't need reading twice; the city knew. The box was there in case. In case there was anything else.

She held the pen the way her mother had held a needle. For a moment. Thinking.

Ars watched her ears.

Flat.

Then she wrote. Not much. Short. She held the roll out to Lucan, and he took it, and looked down at it.

And laughed.

Out loud. On the platform. Through all of it, the crying and the voice gone, Lucan laughed, a great surprised bark of it, the way a man laughs when a child says something at a funeral; and the bowl, which had been holding its breath, didn't know what to do and so laughed too, a little, all round, nervous, waiting to be told.

"*Alea*," read Lucan, "*of Wrenhaven. Holder. Twice*." He wiped his face with his whole sleeve. "*Same as last year*."

The bowl laughed properly then.

"*And of Gullhaven*," read Lucan.

The near stand went off like a gun.

Ars couldn't see Sorrel. He didn't need to. He felt her: a hot fierce candle in the middle of the near stand, standing up on a bench, going off like a gun with all the rest of it. *And of Gullhaven*. A fishing town that faced out. A boat with a name painted on the stern by an angry sixteen-year-old, and an old man with three fingers, and a woman who'd dropped a basket of herring and picked every one of them up herself.

Alea's ears came up.

***

Then Lucan looked at the east stand.

He didn't mean to. Ars saw that. He saw Lucan's eyes go up past the royal box, along the east stand, to the fourth tier, to the end of a bench, the way they'd gone last year; and saw him try to stop them, because the thing was hers, and he'd promised, and he'd kept a secret for a week from a holder for the first time in fifteen years and wasn't going to spoil it now. And saw them go anyway. And stay.

The bowl followed them.

It had learned how, last summer. It went round the stands the way it had then, from a point outward. From the woman beside him with the little girl, who turned on the bench and looked at the rod across his knees. To the people next to her. To the tier below. To the whole east stand. To the whole bowl. Forty thousand people with forty thousand paper fishing rods in their hands, that Lucan had given out at the gates that morning for a girl, looking at the real one.

Waiting.

Last year he hadn't done anything. He'd sat there with it across his knees and let them understand, and that had been enough, and more than he'd wanted.

He didn't want this either. He'd never wanted it. He'd spent nine years on a jetty not wanting it.

But she'd said *bring that*.

He stood up.

The little girl let go of his finger and looked up at him. He put the rod in both hands. The real one. Old ash, and a line that had been out over Lake Ilse every morning for nine years. And he lifted it.

Once.

Not high. Not over his head. Just up, in both hands, level with his face, the way you'd lift a rod off the water when you'd felt something on the end of the line and wanted to be sure. Toward the platform. Toward the girl on it with the pen still in her hand.

He held it there for as long as it takes to count to three.

And forty thousand paper fishing rods went up.

All at once. Not the slow way, from a point, the way they'd gone last year when the city was finding something out. This was the other way. The city already knew. It had known for a week, and kept it, very kindly, for a girl who wasn't asking; and now it didn't have to. The rods went up all round the great bowl of the Lists in the last red light of midsummer, forty thousand of them, every colour of a market, with paper fish turning on the ends of their lines, over every head in every tier, all of them pointing the same way.

Not at him.

At her.

He'd lifted his toward her, and they'd all followed it. Every one. The whole of Valcrest, holding up paper rods to a girl on a platform with chalk on her boots, because a man on the fourth tier had shown them which way to point.

That was all he'd wanted. He found that out standing there. That was the whole of what he'd come south for, carrying the water; that if the city was going to lift anything at all this year, it should lift it to her, and know why.

He put the rod down.

On the platform, Lucan had sat down on the edge of it with his legs over the side and his face in his hands and the roll in his lap, and was crying so hard the marshals had come and stood round him in a ring, the way you'd stand round a fire.

And Alea was standing at the edge of the platform above him, with the pen still in her hand, looking up at the fourth tier of the east stand, with her ears all the way up.

***

Sorrel found him after dark.

Not at the blue door. At the Lance. He'd gone there because Alea had said *the end table, by the window* in the painters' yard on her way out, the way you'd tell somebody the time; and he'd gone, because she'd asked, and sat at the end table by the window of the Broken Lance with the rod leaning on the wall behind him, while thirty-three summoned and Maren and Bram and the whole kitchen very carefully didn't make a thing of it, and made a thing of it anyway, the way they did.

Bram had put a plate in front of him without asking what he wanted. Maren had sat down at the next table and written in her book, and looked up every so often, and looked down again. Calvin had come and stood beside the table for a while with his kettle, not saying anything, which was very hard for Calvin, and then said *HOLDER ADJACENT* very quietly and gone away.

Sorrel came in from the yard with her hooks and her face scrubbed red and stood at the end of his table.

She looked at him for a long time. Her eyes slid off, and she put them back. Slid off. She put them back, and held them there, the way you'd hold a boat's head into a swell, with her jaw set.

"You're the one who says it every morning," said Sorrel.

"You're Tobyn's girl," said Ars.

Sorrel stood very still.

Nobody said it outside Gullhaven. He knew that. Alea had told him, in a letter that came down the lake road in Maren's voice: that it had been three winters before anybody on the quay could say it, and now the whole quay said it, *Tobyn's boat, Tobyn's girl*, like it was ordinary, like it had always been allowed. But not here. Not in Valcrest, in a tavern full of strangers, from a man she'd never met.

"She told me," said Sorrel. Her voice came out rough. "Alea. On the boat. Where she got it from. Saying names at the first cast. She said her dad does it. Every morning. On a lake. The ones nobody says." She swallowed. "She said he'd be saying my dad's. She said he'd have started the day her letter got there." She looked at him. "Is he?"

"Since the snow," said Ars.

"You didn't know him."

"No."

"You never met him. You never sailed with him. You don't know what he looked like." Her voice was going. She didn't let it. "Why?"

He thought about it. He thought about the jetty, every morning, five fish. *Tobyn* at the first cast.

"Somebody should," said Ars.

Sorrel's face did something. He watched it. It did it for quite a long time, with her jaw set and her eyes on him, sliding off and coming back, until it had finished; and then she sat down across the table from him, hard, the way she'd sat down on the sand when a cadet stood on her anchor, and put her hooks on the table between them, and her hands on the hooks.

"Abner said that," she said. "The old one on the slip. To Alea. *I didn't know him*. *That's why it worked*." She looked at the hooks. "He said we couldn't say it because we knew him. Somebody had to say it who didn't."

"Abner's right."

"Abner's always right. It's horrible." She wiped her face with her wrist. "I lost."

"I saw. He stood on your anchor."

"He *stood* on it."

"He'd been looking at the whole thing," said Ars. "For a year. Because she told him to." He looked at her hooks on the table. Old ash shafts, dark with hands. Tobyn's. "You hooked the ground. Nobody's ever done that. Not here. Not in three hundred years. I've asked Lucan."

Sorrel looked at him.

"Next year," she said.

"Next year," said Ars.

"It stays on the stern. NEXT YEAR. I said if I lost it stays." She was quiet. "It's a name. Not a date."

"I know," said Ars. "It's a good name for a boat."

Across the room, at the long table, Alea was sitting with Group 1 and Gabe and not looking at the end table by the window, very carefully, with her ears straight up.

***

He read the channel at midnight on the step of the blue door, with the crock on the table behind him in the dark and the rod across his knees and the noise of Valcrest going on over the rooftops, a city that wasn't going to bed tonight.

The whole of the evening was in it. He read it from the top.

In the morning, at first light, he went down to the sea gate.

He hadn't fished the sea in a long time. He went down through the empty city with the rod over his shoulder, past the Street of Coopers and the fish market with its shutters down and the gulls already shouting on the roofs, to the sea gate, where the city wall came down to the harbour and there was a stone stair going into the water, green at the bottom.

Alea was on the stair. Sorrel was beside her, with her hooks. They were sitting on the third step from the water with their boots off and their feet in the sea, not saying anything, waiting, the way you wait for a man you know is coming.

He sat down on the step above them.

Nobody said good morning. He baited the hook. He stood up on the step, and they stood up on theirs, one either side of him, below, with the sea going in and out over their feet; and he cast, out over the harbour, a long easy line like a rod lifting, into water he'd never fished.

"Tobyn," said Ars.

"Tobyn," said Sorrel. Not to him. To the sea.

He reeled in. He cast again.

"Diwa," said Alea.

He didn't say anything for a moment. The line went out. The sea came in over her feet and went out again.

"Diwa," said Ars.

Sorrel looked up at him, and then at Alea, and then out at the harbour, where the line was, and the light coming up behind it.

"Diwa," said Sorrel, who'd never met her either.
