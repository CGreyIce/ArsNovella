"Tell me something true," said Yeva.

She said it without looking up. She was on her stool under the last warehouse on the south wharf, with the net across her knees and the needle going in and round and through, in a grey daylight that smelled of tar and river and old snow; and Dennis was standing in front of her in his good coat, the blue one, with Calvin a step behind him holding the kettle like a lamp.

He'd come dressed. He always came dressed. You didn't walk up to anybody who had something you wanted looking like you'd slept in a hedge; that was the first rule of every market in two worlds, and his own.

"I'm very handsome," said Dennis.

The needle didn't stop. "Something true."

He thought about it. He thought about the things he usually said when somebody asked him for something true, which were all true and all useless, the way a coin is true and useless when you're thirsty in a desert. *I'm the treasurer. I keep the books. I've never lost a copper of anybody's in three and a half years.* He looked at her hands. Knotted, every finger, like rope that's been wet and dried a thousand times. Working.

"I came because Cal asked," he said. "And because I want to know what the song is. And because some part of me, I'm not proud of it, has already started working out what it's worth."

The needle stopped.

Yeva looked up at him. Very dark eyes, very sharp, in a face like a walnut. She looked at him for a long time.

Then she laughed. Short, through her nose, an old woman's laugh, surprised out of her.

"That one's true," she said.

"I know," said Dennis. "I can hear it."

"Can you." She went back to the net. "Calvin says you hear a lie like a bell."

"Like a bell," said Dennis. "Little ones ring thin. Kind ones ring soft. The ones you tell yourself ring so quietly you'd only hear them in a church." He put his hands in his coat. "True things don't ring at all. That's how I know them. By the quiet."

"Show me."

"I can't show you. It's in my ears."

"Then I'll show you." She put the needle through a mesh and pulled the knot tight and didn't look up. "I'm from up the Sallow," she said, in plain river Aurelan with nothing under it. "My father kept eels."

It rang.

Not loud. He'd been braced for loud. It rang the way a step rings that's been walked on for forty years: worn so smooth in the middle that there was hardly anything left to ring, a dull low note like a cup set down on a table. A lie that had been told so many times that it had nearly turned into the truth from the outside in, and hadn't, quite.

"Rang," said Dennis.

"How?"

He told her. A step. A cup on a table. Forty years.

Yeva sat with the needle still for a moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she'd nodded at Calvin in the snow.

"Good," she said. "Sit down. Not there, that's wet. On the coil." She held out the edge of the net to him without looking. "Hold that. Not like that. Like you'd hold a cat's tail. You've never mended anything in your life, have you."

"I mend *money*."

"Hold the net," said Yeva.

***

She didn't tell it all at once. She told it the way she mended: a mesh at a time, with a knot at the end of each one, pulled tight. Calvin said, on the walk home over the bridge that first afternoon, that he'd thought she'd tell it in one go, like the song; and Dennis said that nobody who'd kept something for forty years told it in one go, and Calvin said *how do you know*, and Dennis didn't answer that.

They went every afternoon for two weeks. In the snow, and in the sleet, and once in a fog so thick that the far end of the wharf went out like a lamp and they found her by the needle-sound. They brought bread, because Dennis said you didn't go empty-handed, and the kettle, because Calvin went nowhere without it now. Dennis learned to hold a net. He learned to hold it badly and then less badly; and on the ninth day, under her eye, to tie a mesh-knot, which came undone, and then another, which didn't. "That's one," said Yeva. "You won't starve."

She told them about the village first. That was the easy part; you could tell he was the easy part, because she told it looking at the river instead of at the net.

It was at the edge. Right at the edge, where the black stopped. The black was east of it, all the way to the end of the world, and west of it was the red, and then you. "We called it a word that means the last well," she said. "Because it was. There was a well in the middle and nothing after it. You could stand at the well and look east and there'd be nothing, all the way, no trees, no birds. Not dead. Just never anything. And you could look west across the red on a clear morning and see the smoke from your forts."

"Under the—" Calvin stopped.

"Under the king," said Yeva. "Theirs. Ours, then." She worked the needle. "We never saw him. Nobody did, at the edge. We saw the hunters. They came through twice a year, east to west, west to east, the way weather comes. Long arms. You stood in your doorway and looked at the floor until they'd gone. We paid in barley and goats, and we looked at the floor, and they mostly went past." She pulled a knot tight. "That's all it was. People think it was something. It was barley and goats and looking at the floor."

It didn't ring. None of it. Dennis sat on the coil of wet rope with the net in his hands and listened to her not ring, and it was the strangest thing he'd heard in this world, which was a world with talking snakes in it.

Because he'd heard Kharvos talked about for three and a half years. On this river, in this city, every day. The bargemen spat when they said it. Sabine said it like the name of a debt. The soldiers on the river wall said it the way you'd say the name of something under the bed. And nobody, not once, in three and a half years, had ever said *barley and goats*.

"There was a man," said Yeva, on the fourth day.

***

He'd come over the red in the long war with a raiding party, she said. She was nineteen. Aurelan soldiers, fifteen of them, at night, to burn the barley the hunters took. They'd burned it; they'd burned the last well's barley along with the hunters' tribute because in the dark you couldn't tell which was which, and the village had stood in its doorways and watched its own winter go up; and on the way back west across the red the hunters had caught them, and only some of them had got home.

"He didn't," said Yeva. "He was in our well-house in the morning. With his leg broken in two places and a hole in his side. He'd crawled back to the only roof for a mile." She put the needle through. "I found him. I'd gone for water."

"What did you do?"

"I gave him some." She didn't look up. "What would you do?"

Dennis didn't say anything.

She'd hidden him in the well-house for nineteen days. Fed him. Set the leg the way her mother had taught her to set a goat's, which he said afterwards was the worst thing that had ever happened to him, worse than the hole. The hunters came through on the twelfth day, west to east, and she stood in her doorway and looked at the floor with him under the well-house boards forty paces off, and they went past.

"And on the twentieth night I walked him west," said Yeva. "Across the red. With a stick for his leg and my arm for the other one. Two weeks. To the forts. I was going to leave him at the edge of the red where your soldiers could find him and go home before anyone missed me." She stopped. Her needle stopped. "I got to the edge of the red."

The fog was coming up off the river. Out on the water, a long way off, a barge was going past with a lamp in the bow, very slow.

"And I didn't," said Yeva.

It didn't ring.

"His name was Emmet," she said. "Emmet Rook. His father had barges. He had a laugh like a goose and he couldn't mend a net to save his life and he told every soul on this river for forty years that I was from up the Sallow and my father kept eels. Forty years. He never told a single person where he'd found me." She started the needle again. "He died eleven winters ago. In the fever year. And I've been from up the Sallow on my own since."

"I'm sorry," said Calvin.

"Don't be. Forty years." She pulled a knot tight. "That's more than most. That's more than anybody at the last well ever got, I'd think. I don't know. I never went back." She looked up at Dennis. "He said it every day. *Yeva's from up the Sallow.* Forty years. Did it ring, do you think?"

Dennis thought about it.

"Yes," he said. "Every time. A bit."

"Good," said Yeva, and looked down. "Don't tell me what it sounded like."

He didn't.

***

The song came on the tenth day.

Not the song; they had the song. Calvin had it written on the wardrobe door at the chandler's in chalk, all three verses, and Deston had sung it to Emrys, in the kingdom tongue as near as he could get it, because Emrys had heard every song in the kingdom and sung most of them; and Emrys had made him sing it three times and gone very quiet and said *that's not one of ours* and asked where it came from, and Deston had said *rule four* and Emrys had said *good lad* and not asked again. They had the words.

What they didn't have was what it meant.

"It's a counting song," said Yeva. "For little ones. Every child at the edge knows it. You sing it when you're skipping, or doing the stairs, or going to sleep." She worked the needle. "*One hill and one nail*. That's the first thing you learn, before you can count. There's a hill. A long way in. Further than anybody goes. Further than the hunters go. And there's a nail in the top of it."

"A nail," said Dennis.

"That's what they call it. *The Nail*. My mother said it was black, and as long as a man, and that nothing that walked at the edge could go near it." She didn't look up. "The hunters wouldn't go that way. Never. Even the birds didn't, my mother said, except there weren't any birds anyway."

Calvin had gone very still.

"And the rings," said Yeva. "That's the counting. *One ring round the hill. Two rings and two hands that hold the nail still*. My mother said that a long time ago, before the king, before anybody, two came from the west with the Nail and put it in the hill. Two people. From your side. And then they held it." She put the needle through a mesh. "Walked round and round the hill, holding it, saying something. For years. Till they died. And the grass grew in rings where they'd walked. That's what the song's counting. Their walking."

"Saying what?" said Calvin.

"Nobody knows. That's the point." She pulled the knot tight. "Don't ask me what's under the hill, Calvin. I know you want to. Everybody at the edge knows not to. You say *the hill*. You don't say what's in it. You don't name what's under a nail. You say the hill, and you look at the floor."

"Did your mother know?"

"My mother said it gets no rest," said Yeva. "If the nail comes out. That's the last line. *Pull the nail, little one, and the hill gets no rest*. And she said that was all anybody needed to know, and I was to go to sleep." She looked out at the river. "I did. I was six."

Dennis sat with the net across his knees.

It hadn't rung. Not one word. Not *black*, not *as long as a man*, not *two from the west*, not *walked round and round*. Not a thread. He'd been listening with everything he had, the way he'd listened on the river wall in the autumn with four true lamps over a mile of fog: listening for the crack, the buzz, the thin high ring of an old woman telling three strangers a story to make them go away.

Nothing. The quiet. All the way down.

Which didn't mean it was true. He knew that. It had taken him two years and a forehead opened above the eyebrow at the Lists to know it, and he knew it now the way he knew his own name. It meant *she* thought it was true. It meant a woman who'd been six at the last well had been told a thing by her mother, and believed it, and kept it for fifty-odd years in a city where she couldn't say it out loud, and was saying it now, and believed every word.

That was all True could tell you. Whether somebody believed it.

But then, he thought, sitting on the coil of rope in the fog, that was all anybody could ever tell you about anything.

***

"There's another verse," said Yeva, on the last day.

The twelfth day. It wasn't snowing. It was cold and bright and still, with the river going past black and quick under a sky like a bowl of milk, and the far end of the wharf so sharp you could see the rust on the bollards. She'd been quiet all afternoon. She'd mended with her eyes on the net, not the river; and Dennis, who'd learned by now which way she looked when, had known something was coming and hadn't said anything, and nor had Calvin, who'd learned too.

"My mother didn't know it," said Yeva. "It wasn't there when I was a girl. I heard it here."

"Here?"

"Eleven, twelve winters ago. At the end of the short war." She put the needle through. "They brought prisoners down the river. Not soldiers. Soldiers they kept on the Redmarch. These were the ones they took at the crossings, carrying water, driving carts, people from the edge the hunters had made walk with them. They brought the sick ones down here, to the infirmary. Maud's. She was young then." She pulled a knot tight. "There was a girl. Thirteen. From a village two days north of the last well. She'd been carrying water for them at the river and somebody's horse had kicked her, and she had the fever from it. And somebody on this wharf said there was a Kharvos child dying in Maud's and good riddance, and I went."

"You went in?"

"I said I was from up the Sallow and I sat with the dying." She didn't look up. "Maud let me. Maud let anybody, then. She didn't have the beds." The needle went in, and round. "The girl didn't know the kingdom tongue. She thought she'd died and gone wherever you go, because there was a woman beside her bed talking to her in her own. She held my hand. And in the night, in the fever, she sang."

Yeva stopped mending.

She sat on her stool with her knotted hands lying in the net, still, the way Calvin had said they'd gone still in the snow; and she looked at the river; and she sang it. Very low. In the old tongue, the hard one with the older echo under every word. Dennis's window took it apart for him the way it took everything apart, so that it came into his head in English a heartbeat behind her, in a voice that wasn't anybody's:

*Six rings, and no hands,*
*and a boy from the west.*
*He pulled out the nail, little one,*
*and the hill gets no rest.*

The river went past.

"She said a boy came out of the black," said Yeva. "When she was small. Six or seven. She was at the well in her village at dawn, and he came out of the east, walking. On his own. Out of the black, where nobody comes from. With the Nail behind him." She was looking at the river. "That's how she said it. Not *a sword*. *The Nail*. Black, as long as a man, dragging on a strap over his shoulder so it cut a line in the ground behind him all the way, and his hands all bound up in rags and bleeding through. And nothing would go near him. The dogs ran under the houses. And he walked past the well and west across the red and he never looked at any of them."

"How did they know it was the Nail?" said Calvin. His voice wasn't right.

"Because the priest at their well went out east the next week. On his own. All the way in. Further than anybody goes." Yeva put her hands back on the net. "And came back a month after, and sat down in the doorway of the well-house, and put his hands flat on the ground, and wept. In front of everybody. And told them the hill was empty." She started mending again, slowly. "She said the old women at every well from there to the last one came out of their doors and put their hands on the ground, the same, for days. And the children made up a new verse. Because that's what children do. Something happens and they make up a verse to skip to."

"A boy from the west," said Dennis.

"From your side." Yeva pulled the knot tight. "A garrison boy, the girl said. They knew because of his coat. A soldier's coat, grey, like the soldiers at the fort across the red. She'd seen them on the walls on clear mornings. Your fort." She looked up at Dennis at last. "She died two days after. I buried her. She's in the paupers' ground behind the rope-walk, with a stone I paid for, with a name on it in the kingdom tongue that isn't her name, because I never knew it."

Dennis didn't say anything for a long time.

It hadn't rung. Any of it.

He sat on the coil of rope with the net over his knees and looked at Calvin. Calvin was looking at the river. His face was white, and his jaw was tight, and his hand was on the kettle on his hip, holding it the way you'd hold someone's sleeve.

A soldier's coat. The fort across the red. There was only one fort across the red from the black that Dennis had ever heard of, and Yan's group had been posting from it for two years.

A garrison boy. Eighteen years ago, or nineteen. With a black sword as long as a man on his back, and his hands bleeding through rags, walking west out of the black on his own. And nothing would go near him.

A sword that burned demons. That had been folded fifteen times by somebody from home. That said *stay*, thirty-two thousand times, to whatever it touched.

That had stood in a hill a long way in for longer than anybody's mother could remember, holding it, with two people walking round and round it till they died.

And a boy had pulled it out.

***

"In the novels," said Deston, "the enemy always has the other half of the story."

The chandler's kitchen, on Rope Street, that night. The five of them round the table with the lamp in the middle and the soup going cold and the shutters closed, and nobody's window open, which had never happened at that table in three and a half years. Deston had his elbows on the table and his hands round his cup. Gabe sat with his arms folded and his jaw tight, very still, the way Gabe went still when something had hit him somewhere he couldn't put a hand on. Rachael was by the stove with her back to it, arms folded too. Calvin had told it. Dennis had sat and said *true* at the end of each piece of it, the way he'd been asked to, the way they'd always divided it: Cal the river, Gabe the iron, Deston Emrys, Dennis which bits were true.

*True*. *True*. *True*. All the way to the paupers' ground.

"It's always the bit you get last," said Deston. "The hero goes into the dark place and gets the sword and nobody ever asks who it belonged to before. Who was holding it. What it was doing there." He turned the cup in his hands. "And it's never just lying around. It's never just *there*. It's always holding something."

"It was," said Gabe.

Everybody looked at him.

"Folded fifteen times," said Gabe. "By somebody from home. Somebody who learned the hall's writing and put *stay* in it thirty-two thousand times." He didn't unfold his arms. "And took it east. Two of them. Over the red, into the black, as far as anybody goes. And put it in a hill. And walked round it till they died." He looked at the table. "That's not a sword. That's a nail. They made a nail."

"And he pulled it out," said Calvin.

Nobody said anything.

"He'd have been nineteen," said Deston, after a while. Quietly. "At Cinderhold four years. Alea a baby." He put the cup down. "He's never told any of us. He told Gabe and me about Diwa, the first summer. All of it. He never once said where the sword came from."

"Maybe he doesn't know," said Rachael.

They looked at her. She hadn't said anything all evening. She stood with her back to the stove and her arms folded, and her face was the face she wore at Maud's bench when a thing was bad and she'd decided to be the one who said so.

"He walked into the black at nineteen, on his own, for no reason anybody's ever said, and came out with it," said Rachael. "Maybe he doesn't know what it was doing there. Maybe nobody ever told him there was a song. Maybe he's carried it half his life thinking it was a sword." She looked at Dennis. "Did she say he knew?"

"No."

"Did she say he didn't?"

"No."

"Then we don't know," said Rachael. "Rule four. You find out yourself. You don't decide." She unfolded her arms. "And it's his. Before it's anybody's. Before it's the channel's, or Chris's log, or a hundred people in Solmere arguing about what it means. It's his, and Alea's. It's his hill. He gets told first. By somebody's face."

It didn't ring. Of course it didn't; it was Rachael. Rachael didn't do lies. She barely did kindness, out loud. But Dennis had never once heard her be wrong about who a thing belonged to.

"She's right," said Gabe.

"I'm always right," said Rachael. "It's very tiring."

"Off the channel," said Deston. He looked round the table. "All of it. The Nail, the hill, the boy, Yeva. Until he says. Rule three: who can get hurt. Yeva can, on this wharf, if one word of it gets down the river with her name on. And he can." He was quiet. "He can most."

"We take it to him," said Calvin. "Ourselves. At the thaw."

"At ice-out," said Gabe. "First boat up the Wren." He unfolded his arms at last and put his big hands flat on the table. "I've got seven letters to cut on the hook. And the eye and the point. I was going to have it done by the thaw anyway." He looked at Deston. "We take it up together. The hook and all of it. And we sit at his table and tell it to his face."

"And we write now," said Deston. "Short. By the post road, while the river's shut. So he's not sitting there in the spring wondering why five people turned up on his jetty with faces." He took a pencil out of his coat. "Not what. Just that."

He wrote it on the back of the soup bill, in the end, because there wasn't any other paper in the kitchen, and read it out, and they all said *yes*:

*Declan. We found the other half of the story. It's yours to hear first. We'll bring it at ice-out, all of us, and the hook. Nothing's wrong. Nobody's hurt. It'll keep till then. D, G, C, D, R.*

"Nothing's wrong," said Dennis.

Deston looked at him.

"It rang," said Dennis. "Soft. *Nothing's wrong*." He shrugged. "Kind one. I'd leave it in."

Deston looked at the soup bill for a long time. Then he left it in.

***

Dennis lay awake that night in the room at the top of the chandler's with the roof sloping over him and Calvin snoring across the floor, and thought about what it was worth.

He'd said he would. On the first day. *Some part of me's already working out what it's worth*. And it had been true, and Yeva had laughed. So he lay there and did the sums, because he always did the sums, because that was who he was and he'd stopped apologising for it in the first year.

A Kharvos woman on the south wharf of Kingsferry, forty years under a false name. That was worth something to somebody. To the men on the river wall who'd been in the long war. To a lord in Solmere who wanted somebody to point at.

The story of where the Hero's sword came from. Told by the other side. That was worth more than anything he'd ever sold. Half the Council would pay for it. The Advisor would pay for it, probably, or already knew, which was its own kind of price.

And the Hero himself, whose name nobody on this river knew, sitting on a jetty on a frozen lake with no idea that five people from his old life were about to come up the river and tell him that the thing he'd carried for eight years, and stood in his wife's grave, and split on an anvil, had been a nail, holding a hill, and he'd pulled it out at nineteen.

What was that worth.

Dennis lay on his back and looked at the roof. He thought about the soup bill. About *nothing's wrong*, soft, a kind one, and Deston leaving it in.

"Nothing," he said, out loud, in the dark. "Nobody hears it from me."

He listened.

It didn't ring.

He lay there a while longer, a little surprised, and then turned over and went to sleep.

***

Rachael came to the wharf the next afternoon.

She hadn't before. It wasn't hers, she'd said; it was Cal's and Dennis's, and you didn't crowd an old woman. But she came down the south wharf in the cold with her infirmary apron still on under her coat and her hair tied back, and stopped a few paces off from the stool, the way Calvin had in the snow, and looked at Yeva's hands.

"These are my friends," said Yeva, not looking up. "They don't bring girls."

"I'm not a girl. I'm a healer. At Maud's." Rachael didn't come closer. "Dennis says your hands hurt."

The needle stopped.

"Dennis says a lot."

"Dennis says almost nothing that's true," said Rachael, "which is how I know when he does." She looked at the hands. "They hurt in the cold. Every joint. Worse in the morning. You've got a way of holding the needle so the third finger on the right doesn't have to bend, because it doesn't, any more." She waited. "Can I?"

Yeva looked up at her for a long time. At the apron. At Rachael's face, which was not a kind face, exactly; it was a face that had been the patient for twenty-six years on another world and didn't have the patience for anybody else being one if it could help it.

"Maud's," said Yeva.

"Yes."

"She let me sit with a child once. When nobody else would."

"She would," said Rachael. "She still does." She held out her hand, palm up, not touching. "Can I?"

Yeva put the needle down in the net. And after a moment she held out her right hand, the knotted one, the one with the third finger that didn't bend; and Rachael took it in both of hers, the way she did at the bench, as if it were a tool she needed to look at properly, and shut her eyes.

Dennis watched her go cold.

He'd seen it a hundred times. It never got easier. The colour went out of her face from the top down, like a blind coming down a window, and her lips went pale, and her shoulders came up as if she'd stepped into a wind. *Mend* took from her as well as from her mana; it always had. She'd feel it in her own hands afterwards, for an hour. She'd feel forty years of rope in every joint of her own fingers, an echo, a sting, the way she felt every cut she closed.

She didn't let go.

It wasn't much. Dennis could see that. Rachael couldn't undo forty years; she'd said so on the way down, in the cold, *I can't make her new, I'm not a miracle, I'm a woman with warm hands*. But she held the old hand for a slow count of a hundred, and when she opened her eyes and let go, Yeva sat looking at her own fingers, and closed them, slowly. All of them. The third one too. Not all the way. Further than it had gone in a long time.

"Why," said Yeva.

"Because they hurt," said Rachael. Her voice had gone thin with cold, and she put her own hands under her arms. "That's the whole reason. And because you've been mending nets in the snow with them for forty years on a wharf full of people and nobody's said anything, which is stupid."

Yeva laughed.

It was a different laugh from the one on the first day. Longer. Out of her chest, not her nose. An old woman's laugh with something under it that Dennis couldn't name, except that it was nothing like a lie at all.

"Sit down, healer," she said. "On the coil. Not that end, it's wet."

Rachael sat on the coil. Not close. A little way along from Dennis, near enough that Yeva knew she was there and not so near she had to do anything about it.

And Yeva picked up the needle with her right hand, the third finger bent round it the way it was meant to go, and started mending. And after a while, without being asked, she sang.

All of it. All six verses. In the old hard tongue with the older echo under every word, low, to her own hands, the way you'd sing a child up the stairs. *One hill, and one nail.* The rings, and the hands, and the walking. *They're coming from the west*. And then the new one, the children's one, the one a girl had sung with a fever in Maud's infirmary twelve winters ago holding the hand of a woman who'd told her she was from up the Sallow. *Six rings, and no hands, and a boy from the west*.

Calvin didn't lean. He sat on the bollard with the kettle on his knee and his eyes shut and just listened, the plain way, with his ears.

Dennis listened too.

He listened to all six verses, all the way through, the way he'd listened to everything she'd said for twelve days, braced for the crack. For the buzz. For the thin high ring of a thing that wasn't so.

It didn't come.

Out on the river a barge went by with its lamp lit early, very slow, going down toward the sea. The south wharf went gold, and then grey. And an old woman from the last well sang a song about a nail to three people from another world on a coil of wet rope, and none of it rang at all. Not one word. The quiet, all the way down.

Which didn't mean it was true. It meant she believed it.

He was beginning to think that was most of what anybody had.
