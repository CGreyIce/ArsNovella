Milli went on a Tuesday, off a burning roof, and that left Hako.

He read it on the roof of the Broken Lance a little before one in the morning, where he'd gone because Winter was up there and he couldn't sleep, and he read it twice, and then he laughed out loud, so that Winter looked round from the parapet with frost on her eyelashes.

"Milli," he said. "Off a *roof*. Four floors. With a kid."

"I saw." Winter turned back to the river. "*Poise*."

"*Poise*." He read it again. *[ Milli · Tier 2: Tempered · Skills: Pounce, Poise ]*. He was grinning. He could feel it on his face, the way it always was; Ysolde said he grinned at things the way other people breathed. "He's been at it since before the tenth. Same as me. We were on Chris's list the same morning. *Hako, Milli, Jared, Gabe, all past 95*."

"I remember."

"And Jared went in the winter, and Gabe on the river, and now Milli." He laughed again. It came out a little different, and he heard it. "That's the whole list. That's everybody on it but me."

Winter didn't say anything. She was very good at not saying anything; she'd been better at it than anybody he'd ever met when they first came, and she'd got less good at it over three years, and then this winter she'd got good at it again in a different way. The old way had been a wall. The new way was more like standing next to you.

"It'll be fine," said Hako. "It'll come."

"Yes," said Winter.

"It will."

"I said yes."

"I know." He shut the window. He was still grinning. He didn't know how to stop. "I'm just saying it so it's said."

***

Chris showed him the page in the morning.

Chris had pages on everybody. That was what Chris did; he'd done it since the first week, in the corner of the Broken Lance's long room with a stub of pencil and a book he'd bought off a paper-maker, writing everything down in English in neat square letters nobody in Valcrest could read. Sessions. Bouts. Bars. He'd started with his own and then, because he couldn't help it, everybody else's: what they'd said their bar was and when, what they'd been doing that day, what the weather was. Over a thousand sessions now. He called it his log. Lucan called it *the book of spells* and wouldn't touch it.

He sat across from Hako at the long table with the book open and turned it round.

"I want to show you something," he said. "Don't take it the wrong way."

That was how Chris started most things. Hako didn't take it any way. He looked at the page.

It was his. HAKO along the top, underlined. Down the side, dates, in Chris's square hand. Across, numbers. His bar, every time he'd said it out loud for nearly two years; Chris must have been listening every time. *96*. *97*. *97*. *98*. *98*. *98*. And beside each one, small, a note.

"It's moved six times since the tenth," said Chris. "Six. In seven months." He put his finger on the first. "Here. Ninety-six to ninety-seven. That's the night of the black ice. You carried old Merrit up the south stand stairs with his chair and slipped on the eighth step and went down the whole flight on your back with him on top of you, and got up, and carried him the rest of the way."

"I remember," said Hako. He did. It had hurt.

"Here." The finger moved. "The next morning. You sat with Lucan on the doorstep in the Street of Coopers for an hour while he cried. Ninety-seven, and a bit. You said *a bit*. I wrote it down." The finger moved. "Here, the bout with Ysolde in the second week of the winter where she put you through the practice fence. Here, when Wil fell off the roof of the coopers' shed and you caught him and he broke your nose with his head. Here, when Newish told you she was stuck."

"She told you that?"

"She tells me everything. I write it down." Chris didn't look up. "Her bar. She cried, and you said *that's okay, it'll come*, and you sat with her."

Hako didn't say anything.

"And here," said Chris. "Ninety-eight. Six weeks ago. The day you went out of the north gate on your own to meet the Kingsferry carrier, because Ina's mother's letter was in the bag and Ina couldn't go, and the carrier's mule went into the ditch on the river road and the cart went over on you." He looked up. "You held it off the carrier's leg till the watch came. Ten minutes. With your back."

"That wasn't—" Hako stopped. "It was a small cart."

"It was a cart," said Chris. He turned the book back round. "I've been looking at this for a month. I've done it for everybody. I did it for Jared before he went, I did it for Milli, I did it for Gabe; I sent Gabe his. Everybody's is different. Jared's moved every time he stood still for somebody. Milli's moved every time he waited and didn't know what for." He tapped the page. "Yours has moved six times in seven months, and every single one of them, something hit you."

Hako looked at him.

"The stairs," said Chris. "Wil's head. The fence. The cart. Lucan crying on you, which I'm counting. Newish." He shut the book. "You spend all day in the yard with the spear keeping everything at the end of it. Every bout, every drill. Nothing touches you. You're the best I've ever seen at that; nothing gets past your point. And your bar sits there." He looked at Hako. "And then something gets inside, by accident, and you get hit, and you get up, and it moves."

***

He didn't believe it, at first.

He told himself he didn't believe it. He went out to the practice ground behind the Lists with his spear and did the morning drills on his own, the way he'd done them for three years: the long thrust, the short, the sweep, the recover. *Wait, then go*. Somebody had said that to him once, a long time ago, in another life. *You've got the wait. Now you've got the go*. He'd carried it ever since. He'd found *Commit* on it, in the Lists, against Milli, the day he'd stopped holding back the last tenth of every thrust and put all of himself down the shaft.

He was good. He knew he was good. He'd been the first spear among the thirty-five and he was still the best of them, and the spear was the right thing for him the way the sword was right for Xav and the knives were right for Rui Xiang. It was reach. It was the end of a long stick between you and whatever was coming, and he was very, very good at keeping it there.

Nothing touched him. Chris was right.

He thought about that, doing the long thrust for the hundredth time on the empty sand in the early sun with the gulls going over.

He thought: *is that bad*.

***

He asked Ysolde.

She was on the west wall of the Lists, where she always was at that hour, standing with her arms folded and her horns catching the sun, watching the practice ground the way she'd watched it every morning for three years. Three-time champion of the Lists. Reaching. A goat demihuman with curled ram's horns in a city that had spat at her in the war for having them. She didn't say much. She didn't need to.

He told her what Chris had said. All of it. The stairs and the fence and the cart.

Ysolde listened. When he was done she looked at him for a long time.

"You've never been hit," she said. "Have you."

"I've been hit loads," said Hako. "You put me through the fence."

"By accident," said Ysolde. "By me. In a drill. Because you were looking at Newish." She didn't smile. "I mean in a fight. When you meant it. When you'd chosen to be there." She unfolded her arms. "You keep them out. That's what a spear's for, they'll tell you. That's what every spearman in the south'll tell you, and they're right, mostly. You put a long stick between you and what's coming and you keep it there."

"And that's wrong?"

"No," said Ysolde. "It's half." She looked down at the practice ground. "I watched you in the black ice. Carrying. Up and down those stairs all night with an old man on your back, and you were smiling the whole way, every step. Telling them it'd be fine. Telling them they'd be warm in a minute." She was quiet. "And you meant it. That's the thing about you. You always mean it. You think it'll be fine, and you go round making it fine, and mostly it is."

"Mostly," said Hako.

"Mostly," said Ysolde. "And you do it from the end of a spear." She looked at him. "Even with people. You're kind to everybody in this city and you've never once let anybody close enough to hit you. You smile at them from three paces off and keep them there. It's a very nice smile. It's a very long spear."

He opened his mouth. Shut it.

He hadn't known that. He didn't think it was true. He thought about Lucan on the doorstep in the Street of Coopers, crying, and himself beside him, a hand on his back, not saying anything, because there wasn't anything to say. That had been close.

*That had been an accident*, he thought. *He sat down and I sat down next to him*.

"Try it," said Ysolde. "Tomorrow. In the yard. With me." She turned back to the wall. "Let me in."

***

He couldn't.

He tried. Two mornings. He stood on the practice ground with the spear in his hands and Ysolde across from him with her sword, and he tried to let her in.

She came at him the way she always came, straight and fast and with no fuss, and every time she got within a spear's length of him his feet went back. Not his choice. He felt them go. He'd decide *stay*, and she'd come, and the spear would come up and his feet would take him back a pace, two, out of her reach, and the point would be there in front of her face like a door shutting, and she'd stop.

"Again," said Ysolde.

He tried leaving the spear low. She came in over it and his feet went back.

He tried standing with his back to the practice fence so he couldn't go back. She came, and he went sideways, along the fence, like water finding the way out of a bucket.

He tried grounding the spear entirely and standing there with his hands empty. She walked up to him, sword down, and put her hand flat on his chest, and he flinched so hard he sat down.

He sat on the sand with his heart going like a bird's and looked up at her.

"I don't know why I did that," he said.

"I do," said Ysolde.

She crouched down in front of him, which she never did; she was a tall woman and she didn't like crouching. She looked at him, level, from about a foot away.

"You're the happiest person I've ever met," she said. "You get up every morning and grin at the gulls. You've grinned at everything this city's thrown at you for three years. The crowd that called your friends demons. The black ice. Two hundred and eleven people dead in their beds in the Street of Coopers before you came, and nine thousand alive in the stands, and you carried half of them up the stairs." She was quiet. "And you've never let one thing touch you. Not one. You keep it at the end of the spear. So it can't get in. So you can go on grinning."

He didn't say anything.

"I'm not telling you to stop," said Ysolde. "For pity's sake, lad, don't stop. This city needs somebody to grin at it. I'm telling you that's why it doesn't move. *Tempered*. Aura hardens the body." She stood up. "A body that's never been hit doesn't know what it's hardening for."

***

The spring opening of the Lists was on the ninth day after Milli's roof.

It was the first since the black ice. Valcrest had never had a winter like it, and the council had nearly cancelled the spring bouts, and Lucan had gone to the council with his red cloak on and stood in the middle of the council chamber and talked for an hour and a half without stopping until they didn't. *The river quarter slept in those stands*, he'd said. *Nine thousand of them. They'll want to come back and sit in them in the sun and watch somebody hit somebody else with a stick. Let them*.

So it was full. The whole of the river quarter came, and most of the rest of the city, in the first warm sun of the year; and the stands were full from the sand to the rim, and the paper-makers had hung paper lanterns all round the top of the south stand, the one that had a ridge of rime along it all winter like the edge of a wing, and the coopers' guild had bought the whole of the lowest tier on the river side and filled it with their families. Wil was down there, in the front row, with a paper spear.

Hako had entered the open spear.

He hadn't meant to. Lucan had put his name down. Lucan put everybody's name down for everything; it was one of the ways he loved you. And Hako had said *sure*, grinning, because it was the spring and the sun was out and why not. And he'd gone through the first three rounds without anybody touching him once, the way he always did. Point, point, point. A docker from the river quarter with a boathook. A soldier from the south garrison. A big young farmer from up the valley who'd come in on a cart for it and lost in four breaths and shook his hand afterwards like he'd been given a present.

And then the last bout of the day, the spear final, was Garran Stoke.

Hako had watched him all afternoon from the tunnel. Everybody had. He wasn't a spearman, strictly; the open spear let you bring whatever you liked as long as it had a long haft, and Stoke brought a hammer. A war-hammer with a shaft as long as a man was tall and a head the size of a loaf, blunted for the Lists with a leather cap, which didn't make anybody feel better. And a shield. A great round iron-rimmed shield like a cartwheel. He was forty-four, the champion of the open three springs running, Gleaming-tier, a quarryman's son from the hills with arms like hawsers, and he fought every bout exactly the same way.

He walked in.

That was all he did. He held the shield up and walked at you. Whatever you did. You put your spear on his shield and he walked. You put it on his leg and he walked. You backed off and he walked, and the crowd stamped in time with his feet, *one, two, one, two*, because they knew what came next. You couldn't keep him out. Nobody ever had. He walked into your reach and through it, and when he was inside, where the spear was no use to you, he hit you with the hammer.

Hako had watched him do it to three men that afternoon. They'd all been better spearmen than they had any right to be, and Stoke had walked into all of them, and they'd all gone down on the sand and not got up for a while.

"He's going to walk at you," said Chris, in the tunnel.

"I know."

"You're going to back off."

"I know," said Hako. He was grinning. He couldn't help it. His hands were cold on the spear. "It'll be fine."

Chris looked at him, and didn't say anything, and wrote something in his book.

***

It was first to two touches. A touch was a clean blow to the body that would have hurt if it had meant it; the marshals called it, and there were three of them, and Lucan wasn't one of them this year because the council had said he wasn't to be trusted not to weep.

They called them out.

The crowd made the noise it made for Stoke, a deep stamp all round the bowl like a heart going. And then the noise it made for Hako, which was different. Higher. A lot of the river quarter knew him. He'd carried their grandmothers. *HA-KO*, somebody shouted, on the coopers' tier, and Wil's voice, very high, *HAKO, HAKO*, waving the paper spear.

Hako waved back. Grinning.

He took his place on the sand. Stoke took his, thirty paces off, and settled the shield on his arm, and the hammer on his shoulder, and looked at Hako over the iron rim with the face of a man who'd done this a great many times and didn't mind doing it again.

The marshal dropped the flag.

Stoke walked.

***

The first touch took eleven heartbeats.

Hako counted. He couldn't help that either. He waited, the way he always waited, low, with the point out in front of him and his weight on the back foot, and Stoke came across the sand at a walk, *one, two*, with the crowd stamping it, and Hako put the spear on the shield. A clean hit, dead centre, that would have stopped most men in their tracks. *Tunk*. Stoke walked. The spear slid off the iron rim and Hako drew it back and put it on the shield again, high, *tunk*, and Stoke walked, and he was six paces off, and Hako's feet went back.

He felt them go. He'd known they would. Two paces. Three. The point out in front, steady, like a door between him and the iron. Stoke walked. *One, two*. The crowd was roaring now. He went back again, and felt the fence of the Lists coming up behind him through the soles of his feet before he got to it, and went sideways, along it, the way he had with Ysolde, the way water goes—

And Stoke had known he would. Everybody went sideways. The shield came round to meet him, flat, like a door slamming, and the rim of it took him across the chest so hard his whole body rang, and he was on his back on the sand looking at the sky with the spear somewhere else.

The marshals' flags went up. All three.

*Touch*.

***

He got up. He was grinning. He could taste blood; he'd bitten his tongue. The crowd was making a long *oooh*, the way it did, and the coopers' tier was shouting something, and he couldn't hear what.

Stoke had stepped back to his mark. He was watching Hako over the shield. Not unkindly. Just watching.

Hako found his spear and picked it up.

*It'll be fine*, he thought. And then, for the first time he could remember: *will it?*

***

He took the second.

He didn't know how. He was never sure, afterwards. He went back to his mark with his chest ringing and his tongue bleeding and the crowd stamping for Stoke again, and the flag dropped, and Stoke walked, and Hako did the only thing he knew how to do, which was keep him out.

But this time he went *early*. Not back; forward, a half-step, a heartbeat before Stoke expected it, because a man who's walked through every spear in the south for three springs isn't expecting the spear to come to *him*. *Wait, then go*. He went. Low, long, the whole of himself down the shaft, *Commit*, not holding back one tenth of one hair; under the rim of the shield where it was coming down to meet him, and on to the front of Stoke's thigh, *crack*, through the leather and on to the plate, hard enough to stop him walking.

Stoke stopped.

The flags went up. Two of three.

*Touch*.

The crowd went mad. Hako heard Wil, over all of it, screaming. He heard Lucan, somewhere, sobbing *my boy, my boy* to anybody who'd listen.

He'd stepped back already. Out of reach. He felt his feet do it.

Stoke stood on his mark, and put his weight on the leg, carefully, and looked at Hako over the shield for a long moment. And nodded. Once.

Then he settled the shield, and lifted the hammer, and waited for the flag.

***

One each.

Hako stood on his mark with his chest ringing and the sun on the sand and the whole of the Lists making a sound like the sea, and knew he couldn't do it again.

Stoke wouldn't let him. He'd seen it now. A man doesn't win the open three springs running by being surprised twice. He'd walk in again and this time he'd be waiting for the spear to come early, and he'd take it on the shield, and walk, and Hako's feet would go back, and he'd go sideways, and the shield would come round like a door—

He looked at the coopers' tier. Wil, in the front row, with his paper spear, very white. Behind him, three rows back, old Merrit, in his chair that Hako had carried up the stairs on the night of the black ice, eighth step and all. And further along, in the river quarter seats, faces. A lot of them. People he'd carried. People he'd sat with. People he'd grinned at, every morning for three years, from three paces off.

He looked at the tunnel. Chris, with his book, not writing. Winter beside him, with her arms folded. Ina in his mask, for the crowd. Newish, very small, humming. He could hear it, under the noise: a slow beat, with long gaps in it, the way she'd been humming all spring.

And Ysolde, on the west wall, where she always was, with her horns in the sun.

*A body that's never been hit doesn't know what it's hardening for*.

The flag dropped.

Stoke walked.

***

He didn't go back.

He felt his feet want to. All of it. Every morning for three years of drills, every bout he'd ever won, every time he'd put a long stick between himself and something coming. His whole body said *back*. *Keep it out. Keep it at the end of the spear where it can't touch you, where you can go on grinning at it.* And under that, so far under it he'd never once in his life heard it until now: *if it touches me, it won't be fine*.

That was it. That was the whole thing. He stood on the sand with the hammer coming and heard it for the first time, and it was so small. So frightened. *If it touches me, it won't be fine. So nothing can touch me. So it'll always be fine*.

He thought about old Merrit on the stairs. Going down the whole flight on his back with the old man on top of him, every step, *bang, bang, bang*, and lying at the bottom in the dark with the breath knocked out of him, and old Merrit saying *are you all right, son*, frightened, and Hako saying, from the bottom of the stairs, flat on his back, with his ribs on fire, *yeah, it's fine*.

And it had been.

It had touched him. Every step of it. And it had been fine.

Stoke was five paces off. Four. The shield up. The hammer going back.

*Wait*.

Three.

*Go*.

He went forward.

Not early, this time. Not under the rim. *In*. Straight into the walk, the way nobody ever had, into the space where Stoke was coming, where a spear was no use to anybody. He let the spear slide back through his hands as he went, the whole long shaft of it, till he had it short, a hand from the head, like a knife. And he went inside the shield, inside the reach, inside the hammer, so close he could smell the leather of the shield straps and the sweat on Stoke's neck, so close the hammer was already past him on its way round.

The hammer wasn't past him.

The head was. The shaft wasn't. A shaft as long as a man was tall, coming round at the end of a quarryman's arm, and Hako was inside the swing, and it took him across the back of the shoulders like a falling beam.

He felt it go in.

All of it. The whole weight of Stoke's arm and the hammer and the walk, three springs of it, into his shoulders and his back and his spine and down through his legs into the sand. He'd never been hit like it. Nobody had ever hit him like it. And it didn't go *through*. It went in, and he let it in, he didn't brace, he didn't go away from it, he didn't do anything at all except be there, inside, where it was; and his body *took* it. Like a bell takes the hammer. A deep ring, all through him, from his heels to the top of his head. And then nothing.

He was still standing.

He was inside Stoke's shield with the hammer's shaft lying across his shoulders and the head of it somewhere behind him, and he was still standing, and the short end of his spear was against the front of Stoke's mail, under the breastbone, right on the place where a man's breath lives.

He didn't push it. He didn't need to. It was there.

Stoke looked down at it.

The whole of the Lists had gone silent. All those people, and not one sound. Hako could hear the gulls. He could hear the river.

"Well," said Garran Stoke, at last, very quietly, from about a hand away. "That's not what you're supposed to do with a spear, lad."

"No," said Hako.

He was grinning. He could feel it. But it was different. He didn't know how; he couldn't have said. It wasn't on the front of his face, holding something off. It was all the way in.

"Never had anybody come *in*," said Stoke. "Three springs. They all go back." He took the hammer off Hako's shoulders, carefully, the way you'd lift a beam off somebody. "Did that hurt?"

Hako thought about it.

"Yes," he said. "It's fine."

The flags went up. All three.

***

He didn't remember much of after. The noise, mostly. The coopers' tier coming over the barrier. Wil hitting him in the stomach at a full run with his head, again, so that Hako sat down on the sand laughing with a ten-year-old on top of him, and the sun in his eyes. Lucan weeping. Somebody, he never found out who, putting a garland of paper flowers round his neck that fell to pieces almost at once.

And Garran Stoke, before he walked off, standing over him on the sand with the hammer on his shoulder and the shield on his arm, and putting his hand down.

Hako took it. Stoke pulled him up.

"Next spring," said Stoke.

"Next spring," said Hako.

Stoke looked at him a moment longer, from about a hand away. And then he nodded, and turned, and walked off across the sand to the tunnel the same way he'd walked at everybody that day, at a walk, *one, two*, and the crowd stamped it for him all the way out.

***

He opened his window that night on the roof of the Broken Lance, where Winter was, because Winter was always there.

[ Hako · Tier 2: Tempered · Skills: Commit, Inside ]

He looked at it for a long time.

*Inside*. He didn't need anybody to tell him what it meant. He'd been there. Inside the reach. Inside the walk, inside the hammer, so close he could smell the shield straps. The place a spearman never goes, because it's where you can be hit.

He thought about Ysolde's hand on his chest in the yard, and how he'd sat down.

He thought about Lucan on the doorstep.

He thought, for a while, about the water-carrier. The man at the Lists two summers ago with a yoke on his shoulders and a different face, who'd come up the south stand stairs with buckets and sat at the end of Lucan's long table at the Broken Lance and talked to everybody, and looked at Hako once across the room and nodded, and that had been all. Hako had wanted to know him. On the other side, the first time, there'd only been nine months of it before there wasn't any more; and here there'd been a week of a man carrying water, and he'd grinned back, and never gone across it.

He'd been three paces off. He'd always been three paces off. He hadn't known.

Winter was at the parapet. She didn't look round.

"Inside," she said. She'd read it in the channel, then. He hadn't posted it. He looked, and he had; he didn't remember doing it.

"Yeah," said Hako.

"Good," said Winter. And then, after a moment, still not looking round: "You can sit here if you like."

He went and sat down next to her. Not three paces off. Next to her, shoulder to shoulder, with the river going dark below them toward the sea and the paper lanterns lit all round the rim of the south stand.

She didn't move away.
