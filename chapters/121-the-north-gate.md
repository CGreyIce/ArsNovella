Aleaka could feel when someone was carrying something.

She'd always been able to, a little. On Earth it had been the ordinary kind: a friend going quiet in a call, a voice that was too bright, someone in her chat typing *lol* about a thing that wasn't funny. Here it was more than that. Here it was nearly a sense, the way smell is a sense. She'd walk into a room in the castle at Solmere and feel the weight on everyone in it, the way you feel the warmth coming off a hearth: the cook carrying a sick child at home, the Keeper of the archive carrying forty years of being ignored, Oswin carrying a question so big he'd had to put it down in a locked room. Animals carried things too, but simpler ones. Hunger. A sore foot. Being angry about a gull.

She'd decided, somewhere in the first winter, that it was what her magic was really made of. Not taming. You couldn't tame anything you couldn't feel. You just found out what it was carrying, and you offered to carry a bit of it, and after that it would sit on your arm.

It was still dark when she got to the north gate. She'd known it would be. She'd spent the night at the Broken Lance, because it was nearer the north gate than the king's house on the hill and Winter had a spare bed and hadn't asked why. She hadn't slept in it. She'd stopped pretending she might at about two in the morning, and at four she'd got up and dressed and gone down through the Broken Lance in her stockings with her boots in her hand, past Lucan asleep in a chair in the common room under his own red cloak, and let herself out into the street.

Valcrest at four in the morning smelled of bread and wet stone and horses. The lamps were still lit along the high street, and under every one of them, in every window above every shop, there was a paper fishing rod.

She'd seen them yesterday in daylight. She'd seen the paper-makers' carts going round with them all week. It was different in the dark. In the dark they were just shapes: a long thin line with a little curl at the end, hung in a window, a lamp behind it, and the next window, and the next, all the way up the street to the north gate and over it, so that when she came out into the gate square and looked up, the gatehouse itself was bristling with them. Someone had tied a hundred along the top of the wall. Someone else had tied one to the gate sergeant's hut. They moved a little in the wind off the plain, all together, like reeds.

*For the Hero*, Newish had said to Lucan, in the autumn. *Just in case*.

Aleaka stood in the middle of the square in the dark, with her tail round her ankles against the cold, and looked up at them for a long time.

Then she went and sat on the mounting block by the gate, where the carters tied up their horses before the gate opened, and waited.

***

Brisk found her about half an hour later.

She felt him before she saw him. She always could, now, since Hana had tied the other half of a charm round his leg in the winter and he'd bitten her for it. A small hard knot of furious attention somewhere up over the rooftops behind her, coming closer. Then a rush of air, and claws, and he was on her shoulder, glaring at the gate as if it had personally insulted him.

"Good morning," said Aleaka.

Brisk didn't say good morning. Brisk never did. He was a hawk the colour of old bark, with a bent primary he'd come to her with and wouldn't let anyone straighten, and he'd been angry about something every day of his life. She'd found him in the castle mews in the first autumn, in a corner, refusing to eat for the falconer, and she'd sat on the floor of the mews for four hours until she'd found out what he was carrying. It had been very simple. He hadn't wanted to be there. So she'd opened the mews door, and he'd gone, and he'd come back the next morning on his own and sat on the back of her chair, and he'd been coming back ever since. He still bit everybody. He bit Aleaka about once a week, on principle. But he came back.

"They're coming today," she told him.

Brisk ruffled his feathers and said nothing.

"The fifteen. Group 1, and Deston's lot, and Xavier's." She looked at the gate. It was still shut, the big double doors barred, the little door in the left-hand one shut too. "Maren and Bram and Emrys and Sabine. And Petra. And some friends."

*Some friends*, Higu had written, last night.

Aleaka had read it in her bed at the Broken Lance with her window open on the ceiling, and lain there for a long time looking at it.

Higu didn't write things she didn't mean. Higu wrote the rules. Higu had written *be kind, everyone here is scared* on the first night anyone had ever written anything, and every word she'd put in the channel since then had been exact, the way a moon chart is exact. If Higu had meant *the guides and the guards*, she'd have stopped there. She hadn't.

*And some friends*.

Group 1 had kept Wrenhaven out of the channel for a year. Aleaka had noticed. Everybody had noticed. Five girls who'd written every single day about everything, the market and the hens and Thea's lattice and PenguChi's one percent a week, and who had never once, not once, written the name of the man who lived by the lake. They'd written *the fisherman*, at the beginning. Then they'd stopped writing even that. Then, in the winter, they'd stopped writing about their evenings at all. Groups 4 and 6 had gone upriver after a thread in the spring and written *we're here* and then nothing much else, either.

And she'd sat in the room above the archive in Solmere with Hana and Milli and Jared and Cursiffe, all through the winter, and they'd looked at the shape of all that silence and known exactly what it was the shape of.

They'd known since last summer. Since a book about nets. Since *yes, ma'am*.

They'd known on paper, since a night in the autumn when Oswin had come up the stairs with a map and his calm face gone, and written three letters under a drawing of a black sword, and sat down in the doorway.

Knowing wasn't the same.

Aleaka put her hand up and touched Brisk's feet, very lightly, where they gripped her shoulder. He let her. Which meant he could feel it too.

***

Tiger was next. Of course Tiger was next.

He came round the corner of the high street at a run, in the grey before dawn, with something enormous and white flapping over his shoulder, and stopped dead when he saw her, and said "*No*," in a tone of real betrayal.

"Good morning," said Aleaka.

"I wanted to be *first*," said Tiger. "I set an *alarm*. I got Drew to *hit* me." He came across the square and dumped the white thing on the cobbles at her feet. It was a bedsheet. It was a whole bedsheet, from the inn, which was going to be a conversation with the landlord later, and it had letters painted on it in big wobbly black capitals that took up the whole thing and still didn't fit, so that the last word went round the corner and up the side:

WELCOME GROUPS 1 4 AND 6!!!!

There was a dog under the words. A cheerful lopsided chalk dog, with its tongue out, and a stick.

"Bee did the dog," said Tiger.

"It's a good dog."

"It's a *great* dog. She said if anybody asks it's a wolf." He looked up at the gatehouse, at the paper rods along the wall. "How long have you been here?"

"A while."

Tiger looked at her properly then. He had a way of doing that, under all the noise, that people didn't expect. His ears went forward. He sat down on the cobbles next to the mounting block with his back against it and his knees up, close enough that his shoulder was against her leg, warm, and didn't say anything for a bit.

"Me too," he said eventually. "Kind of. I've been up since two." He picked at the edge of the bedsheet. "I'm going to hug every single one of them."

"I know. You said."

"I'm going to hug Maren."

"She'll kill you."

"She can try," said Tiger happily.

The others came while the sky went from grey to pale. Drew, unhurried, with two cups of something hot from the bakery on the corner and a third one he handed to Aleaka without a word, because he'd seen her from the end of the street and done the sum. He sat down on the mounting block's other side and drank his and looked at the gate as if he were reading it. Then Hyvred and Bee, together, the way they always were, Hyvred in a borrowed coat with her glasses pushed up into her hair and Bee yawning and furious about it, with the two Pollen Puffs riding in her collar like a pair of very small, very round scarves. Then Kei, quiet, with the long green shape of Hisui looped twice round his neck and his head tipped a little, the way it was when Hisui was talking.

"He says it's too early," said Kei, to nobody in particular, "and he hates it here, and why is there no sun, and where's the other snake."

"The other snake's three days out," said Drew.

"I told him." Kei listened. "He says three days is too long and he's going to go and find it himself." He listened again. "He isn't."

Bee looked at the bedsheet on the cobbles.

"You spelled *welcome* right," she told Tiger. "I'm proud of you."

"I had help."

"I know. I helped." Bee sat down on the cobbles on his other side, and the Puffs came out of her collar and went and sat on top of the dog, as if they'd been asked to guard it. "If anybody asks," she said, "it's a wolf."

Hyvred didn't sit down.

She stood in the middle of the square in her borrowed coat, with her tail going slowly side to side behind her, and looked at the gate. Not at it. Through it. Aleaka could see her left eye doing the thing it did, the gold of it gone sharp and still, the little red mark in it like a crosshair locking on something a long way off. Up the road. Past the gate and the farms and the market gardens, toward the Last Look, three hours away for a wagon with a wheel that talked.

"Can you see them?" said Aleaka.

"Not yet," said Hyvred. She didn't look round. "Too far. Too many walls." She was quiet. "I said I'd look at all of them properly. In the channel. I said."

"You did."

"I'm going to," said Hyvred. She said it the way you'd say a vow. And Aleaka, who could feel what people carried, felt it in her: a thing she'd been carrying since the winter, since Thornwall, since a shed roof and a shape of absence over a mountain pass. A promise. That when she got here she'd look at every one of them with the eyes she'd been given in a hall in Solmere, the eyes that saw through things, and not let anything at all get in the way.

Aleaka didn't ask. Hyvred didn't say.

***

The gate opened at sunrise with a lot of shouting, the way it did every morning. The sergeant in his hut, who had a paper fishing rod tied to his roof and clearly didn't want to talk about it, came out and lifted the bar with two of his men, and the big doors swung in, and the carters who'd been camped outside all night came rolling through with their carts of cabbages and turnips and early peas and a pig, and the square filled up with noise and steam and people, and the fifteen of them sat in the middle of it all on a bedsheet like an island.

The rest came while the carts did. Group 7 from the Broken Lance, the way they always moved through the city, five of them in a loose line and the city parting round them like water round stones. Chris first, with his notebook under his arm and his pen behind his ear, saying "Morning, morning, logging," to everyone he passed. Hako behind him, cheerful, spear on his shoulder and wrapped against the crowd, telling a carter's boy who'd stopped to stare that it would be fine, whatever it was, it would be fine. Winter, very straight, with her long white hair down her back and her two black horns, and her face doing the thing it did when people looked at her: nothing, very hard. Newish, with her hands in her pockets.

And Ina.

Ina had the mask on.

Aleaka had seen him put it on before, at the Lists, and in the street when the looks got too much, and once at the king's own table when a lord had said *demon* too loudly. She'd never seen him wear it for a gate square at dawn. But there were already people gathering. There had been since the gate opened: carters and bakers' girls and the sergeant's men and people coming out of the houses round the square to see what fifteen of the summoned were doing sitting on a bedsheet at six in the morning. And Ina had looked at them, and at Winter beside him with her face doing nothing, and put the grey fanged mask on over his own face without a word, and walked into the square between Winter and the crowd with his hands behind his back like a gentleman on a promenade.

Nobody said *demon*. Nobody said anything at all. They looked at the oni in the indigo coat, with wisteria on the hem and red cords hanging from the mask's grin, and then they looked somewhere else, carefully, and Winter walked beside him in the space he'd made, and her face did something very small that might have been a smile.

"Good morning, Leaka," said Ina, from behind the mask. His voice came out of it the way it always did: warm and British and amused, the voice of a man at a garden party. "You're early."

"I couldn't sleep."

"None of us could. Newish practised in the mirror until the landlord knocked on the wall."

"I did *not*."

"She did," said Hako, sitting down on the bedsheet. "It'll be fine."

Newish sat down on the edge of the sheet with her knees up and her hands still in her pockets and didn't say anything. And Aleaka, who could feel what people carried, felt what was in Newish's pocket. Not in her pocket. In her. A small hard thing, the size of a pebble, that she'd been turning over and over for months until it had gone smooth. One word. Carried very carefully, the way you carry a single egg across a farmyard, so as not to drop it before you got where you were going.

Aleaka looked away, because it seemed rude to look at it.

Group 2 came last. They'd been on the other side of the city, in the king's house on the hill, where they'd been lodged since they came in two days ago with the king and four hundred people and eleven wagons. Hana and Milli together, Hana's hand in his and her tail and his tail doing the thing they did when they walked, round each other like two ribbons. Jared a pace behind them, the way he always was, close enough to step in front. Cursiffe, slow, with his ink at his throat on its chain and a book under his arm he was clearly not going to read. And Oswin, carrying a writing case and talking.

Aleaka got up off the mounting block and went and hugged Hana without a word.

Hana hugged her back. She didn't ask. She could feel things too, in her way; she knew what people wanted before they said it, which was what made her dangerous, and what made her kind. She held on for a long time.

"You didn't sleep," said Hana, into her hair.

"No."

"Nor did I. Milli did. Milli can sleep through anything."

"I was being rational," said Milli. "Somebody had to be."

"He was awake at three," said Hana. "I felt him."

"I was being rational with my eyes open."

Jared didn't say anything. Jared looked at the gate. Then he went and stood beside it, on the left, a little in front of where everybody else was sitting, with his arms folded and his weight forward, the way Kestra had told him in the first week he stood: *who taught you to stand there?* And Aleaka felt what he was carrying too, and had to look away from that one as well.

Three years of being too far away. Half a world. A phone in his hand at the wrong time of day, in America, with nothing he could do. It had sat on him the whole time she'd known him here, like a coat that was too heavy for the weather, and he'd never once put it down.

Kestra wasn't with them.

Aleaka looked for her, and then looked up, and found her: on the wall above the gate, among the paper fishing rods, sitting on the parapet with one boot up and her back against a merlon, the way she sat on the step outside their rooms in Solmere at night. She wasn't looking at the square. She was looking up the north road.

Kestra had been carrying something for ten months.

Aleaka had felt it the day she came back from the north with the king in the autumn. A small, heavy, *closed* thing, like a sealed letter in an inside pocket. She'd felt it at every meal since. She'd felt it a month ago, the morning the king laughed at breakfast in the small hall until he put his spoon down, and then sat with his hand over his face, and then gave Kestra a letter, and she rode north with it that same hour without telling anyone where. And she'd felt it two days ago, when Group 2 came into Valcrest with the king's household and found Kestra already there, waiting on the steps of the king's house, brown from the road, with the thing in her heavier than ever and a look on her face Aleaka had never seen there before. She'd gone and sat on the step that night and not said anything to anybody.

Kestra caught her looking. Up on the wall, among the rods. She looked back for a moment, with her dry, terse, dangerous face. Then she looked up the road again.

She didn't say anything. She never did.

*We'll be there when we get there*.

Gigichi, who'd shoved a lamp at somebody once at a door in the snow so she wouldn't have to look at his face. Group 1 had kept that out of the channel too. But Aleaka had felt it, all the way from Solmere, without knowing what it was: the night in the winter when Group 1's silence had changed shape. It had been a held breath for a year, their silence. And then one night in the snow it had let go, all at once, and become something else. Something warm. Something that stayed.

She'd known, then, too.

"Leaka," said Hana quietly, beside her.

"I know."

"You're shaking."

"I know," said Aleaka. "I know. I know." She didn't stop.

***

Hyvred saw the dust first.

She'd been standing in the gateway for an hour, half in and half out, with the carts going round her, while the sun came up over the plain and the paper rods on the wall threw long thin shadows across the cobbles like the lines of a staff. She hadn't moved. Then she moved. Her tail stopped. She took one step forward, out of the gate, onto the road, and said, "There."

Tiger was on his feet before the word was finished. He had the bedsheet up off the cobbles and over his head in one motion, held at the corners, and Bee caught the other end without being asked, swearing, and between them it went up across the gateway like a sail: WELCOME GROUPS 1 4 AND 6!!!! and a wolf. The Puffs came off it in a protesting yellow cloud and settled on Drew's head. Drew let them.

Aleaka couldn't see anything. Then she could. A low smudge on the north road where it came down between the market gardens, a mile off, gold in the morning sun. The smudge got bigger. It became shapes.

She'd read about it in the channel for sixteen days. The miller's cart and the brewer's dray. Four horses. Twenty-two people on the road from a lake in the north. She'd read Calvin's kettle and Dennis's goose and Xavier's *we're slow*, and Maren's nobody-looks-at-him, and the thing in Pellow's Cross about a girl singing a ballad, and she'd built it in her head, the way you do, out of words. It didn't look like what she'd built. It looked like a lot of tired people walking down a road.

In front, a small dark woman with a list. Maren. Walking as if the road owed her money. Behind her the dray, high and square, with a big quiet man at the lead horse's head, talking to it. Then a cart. Then people. A lot of people.

The crowd in the square had got bigger without her noticing. It came out of the gate behind her now in a slow wave, carters and bakers' girls and the sergeant's men and children, and stood along the road on both sides to look, the way crowds did for the summoned, in this city that had booed two of them on the sand their first summer and was going to cheer them all into the Lists in four days. Somebody said *the squid lady*. Somebody said *the moth, look, look, the moth*. A little boy on his father's shoulders held up a paper penguin.

They came closer. Aleaka could see faces now.

Xola, in front of the cart, with a round purple shape on her shoulder that waved two of its arms at the crowd with tremendous dignity. PenguChi beside her, small and calm, with a penguin walking at her heels like a dog. Higu, with three pale moths circling over her head in the sunlight as if they were tied to her on threads. Thea, a little apart, looking at everything at once, the way Thea did. Gigichi with her hand closed round something in her coat pocket and her chin up.

And behind Group 1, the ones Aleaka had only ever known as words. A tall man in the middle, taller than anyone, walking slowly, as if he were listening to something. Deston. A big man beside him with forge-black on his hands that would never come off now. Gabe. A small dry-looking woman with her sleeves rolled. Rachael. A man in a very good coat for a road, waving at the crowd like royalty. Dennis. A man with a kettle tied to his pack, already grinning. Calvin. And Group 6 behind them: a huge broad one who had to be Azac, a thin quick one talking with both hands who had to be RX, one with a notebook, one with little points of light going round his head in the sun like gnats, and one at the very edge, walking silently, who looked at the crowd and the gate and the wall and the people on it, all of it, once, the way a soldier looks at a room. Xavier.

And a soldier-looking girl beside him with a sword. And a pale singer with a lute on his back, already crying. And a woman with a tired, sharp face walking by the cart's tail who had to be Sabine.

And at the back.

At the back of all of them, a fox girl, about seventeen, with a sword on her back so big it looked like a door. Walking beside a man carrying water.

***

Aleaka didn't look at the man with the water. She found, afterwards, that she couldn't have described him to save her life. Her eyes went to him and slid off, the way water slides off an oiled cloth, and went somewhere else: to the girl. To the sword. To the buckets, which were wooden, and full, and hanging from a yoke across his shoulders, and slopping a little at every step.

He was carrying something.

She felt it before she knew she was feeling for it. She'd felt for everyone in the procession as they came down the road, without meaning to, the way you can't help hearing voices in a crowded room. Tiredness. A blister. Rachael carrying something sharp and old that she'd put down, mostly. Gabe carrying something enormous and *glad*. Deston carrying a held note. Ordinary things. Human-sized.

The man with the water was carrying something so heavy she nearly sat down.

She had never felt anything like it. Not in the castle. Not in the archive. Not in the king, who carried a country and a dead father's hammer and was very good at it. It was like walking into a room and finding a mountain in it. Thirty-four years of something. A field. A war. A red plain and a grave and a hill and the smell of smoke. Eight years of a jetty. Thirty-five names. A girl. A woman with cold hands.

And he was carrying it like water. Easily. Two buckets on a yoke, slopping a little at every step.

And under it. Under all of it, so faint she nearly missed it, the way you nearly miss a single high note in a choir: something light. Something terrified. Something she knew. She'd felt it a hundred times in her chat at two in the morning, in a voice that was trying very hard not to sound pleased that you'd turned up.

Aleaka stopped breathing.

***

Tiger got to them first.

He'd given up on the bedsheet. He'd let go of his end of it, and Bee had said a word Aleaka had never heard Bee say, and he'd gone off down the road at a run, into the procession like a stone into a pond, with his arms out. He hugged Xola, and the squid on her shoulder, which wrapped an arm round his ear. He hugged PenguChi, who let him, and the penguin, which didn't. He hugged Higu through a cloud of moths, and Thea, who said "*Tiger*," and Gigichi, who said "*don't*," and got hugged anyway. He hugged Deston, who was so tall that Tiger's face went into his chest, and Gabe, who picked him up off the ground. He hugged Rachael and Dennis and Calvin and the kettle. He hugged all of Group 6 in one go, which shouldn't have been possible, and Azac laughed so loudly the horses looked round. He hugged Maren.

Maren stood very still and endured it, with her list against her chest, like a cat being held by a child.

He hugged Bram, and the horse Bram was talking to. He hugged Emrys, who was crying anyway. He hugged Sabine, who swore at him. He hugged Petra, who put a hand on his chest and pushed him off, and then hugged him back. He hugged the fox girl with the sword, who was so surprised she laughed.

Then he got to the man with the water, at the very back, and stopped.

Aleaka watched him stop. Tiger, who never stopped for anything. He stood in the road in front of the water-carrier with his arms half up and his ears going forward and then back, as if he'd smelled something and couldn't place it. As if, under the noise, under all of it, some part of Tiger that nobody ever saw had looked at the man with the buckets and said *wait*.

Then he shook his head, the way you shake water out of your ears, and grinned his enormous grin, and said, "You too, whoever you are."

And hugged him. Buckets and yoke and all. The water went everywhere. Down Tiger's back, down the man's front, over the fox girl's boots.

The man with the water stood in the road, soaked, with Tiger's arms round him and the yoke knocked crooked on his shoulders, and didn't move. And Aleaka, who could feel what people carried, felt the mountain in him shift. Not get lighter. Shift. The way a load shifts when someone you didn't expect takes the other end.

Then Tiger let go and went off up the road again, shouting, to hug Maren a second time.

***

They came in to the gate.

Maren first, with her list. The sergeant came out of his hut to meet her, the way sergeants did, with his own list, and they looked at each other's lists with deep mutual suspicion. The crowd pressed in along both sides. Group 1 came through the gateway, under the bedsheet, which Bee was still holding up on her own with a face like thunder, and the square went up in noise.

Aleaka didn't move. She stood in the middle of it with Brisk on her shoulder, and couldn't.

Somebody said, "Alea, your strap's caught."

Xola. Reaching back to the fox girl with the big sword, who'd stopped in the gateway to look up at the paper rods along the wall, and whose sword-strap had caught on the corner of the cart. "*Alea*," said Xola again, patiently. "Hold still."

The fox girl held still.

And the name landed.

It landed the first time like a stone landing in water: a small clean sound, *Alea*, in Xola's voice, in a gate square full of carters. The name. *His* name. The name he'd given every companion in every game she'd ever watched him play: the horse in one, the dog in another, the little robot that followed you about in that terrible space thing he'd played for a fortnight and then abandoned. *Alea*. She'd teased him about it for a year. *You've got no imagination. You've got one name. You're going to name your children Alea*. And he'd said *maybe I will*, in a voice like a man being bossed about, and she'd lost the game, the way she always lost.

It landed the second time a breath later, deeper, where she couldn't hear it.

A book about nets. *The small person who runs this house inspected my nets*. A man by a lake, writing about his daughter.

A fox girl.

Aleaka looked at her. She couldn't help it.

The girl looked back.

She had a fox's ears, black-tipped like Aleaka's own, and a fox's red-gold tail, and a face that wasn't his, because nobody's face was his any more. Seventeen. A sword on her back that no seventeen-year-old in the world should have been able to stand up under, standing up under it. And she looked at Aleaka. Not the way the crowd looked at the summoned. Not the way you look at a stranger. She looked at her the way you look at someone you've heard about all your life, in bedtime stories, from a voice in the dark at the top of a stair. *There was a girl called Leaka, and she used to tease me*.

The girl's face did nothing at all. It was a very good nothing. It was nearly as good as Winter's.

Her ears were doing the opposite. Her ears had gone straight up and forward, the way a fox's ears go when it's trying very hard not to laugh, and were quivering.

And Aleaka, who could feel what people carried, felt what the girl was carrying, and it was the funniest thing in the world, and it was the size of the whole sky.

"Good morning," said the girl politely.

"Good," said Aleaka. Her voice did the thing it did when she was nervous, stumbling, going round a word twice before it found the door. "Good. Morning. Good morning."

The girl's ears quivered harder.

Then Xola said "there," and the strap was free, and the girl turned and went on into the square, into the noise, very straight, with her terrible sword and her tail held carefully still. And she didn't look back. And Aleaka knew, the way you know a thing with your whole body, that the moment she was round the corner she was going to put both hands over her face and laugh until she fell over.

***

That was when Hyvred looked.

She'd come back through the gate with the crowd. She'd been standing just inside it, against the wall, under the rods, with her glasses up in her hair, while Group 1 and Group 4 and Group 6 came through past her in a river of noise. She'd looked at all of them. Properly. The way she'd promised. Aleaka had seen her do it: each face, one after another, the gold eye going sharp and still and the crosshair in it locking on and letting go, locking on and letting go, like somebody counting. Deston. Gabe. Xavier. Thea. She'd smiled at every one. She'd said their names, quietly, one at a time, under the noise, as if she were writing them down.

The man with the water came through the gate last. Behind the girl. With his buckets half empty now and his front soaked and the yoke still a little crooked.

Hyvred looked at him properly.

Aleaka saw the crosshair lock.

And it didn't let go.

Hyvred stood against the wall with her tail gone still and her face gone still and her gold eye fixed on the water-carrier, on something nobody else in the square could see, and Aleaka watched her see it. Whatever it was. Whatever true sight did when it looked at a man everyone else's eyes slid off. Whatever was there, under the face that wasn't his.

She was looking right at it.

"*Oh*," said Hyvred.

And sat down in the road.

Just like that. Her legs went, and she sat down on the cobbles of the gate square in her borrowed coat with her back against the wall and her hands in her lap, and looked up at him, and her face was the face of somebody watching the sun come up who had spent most of her life in the dark.

The man with the water stopped.

The crowd went round them. Carters and children and Tiger, somewhere, still shouting. Nobody noticed. Nobody ever noticed him; that was the whole point of him. A water-carrier and a girl in a coat sitting down against a wall. But Aleaka noticed. And Drew noticed, on the mounting block, with the Puffs on his head; he'd gone very still. And Kei, with his head tipped, listening to something Hisui was saying very fast. And Jared by the gate, who'd turned round. And Newish, on the edge of the bedsheet, with her hands in her pockets, and her face gone white.

The man with the water looked down at Hyvred.

Then he bent his knees, slowly, the way you do with a yoke, and set the two buckets down on the cobbles, one and then the other, carefully, so that they didn't spill. And slid the yoke off his shoulders, and leaned it against the wall. And straightened up.

And turned round.

He was looking at Aleaka.

She couldn't see his face. She could see that he was looking at her. She could feel it, the way you feel the sun, or a hand on your shoulder in the dark. The mountain in him, and under it, the high faint frightened note, very close now. Right there. Ten feet away, across a gate square, in the sun, in front of a bedsheet with a wolf on it.

Every bit of her had gone still. Brisk on her shoulder had gone still, flattened, the way animals went for something very big, and was pressing himself against her neck.

"Hi, Leaka," said the water-carrier.

In English.

***

She hadn't heard English spoken out loud by a stranger in two years.

She'd heard it from her friends, every day, in rooms with the door shut. She'd written it in the channel. She'd read it in books by a lake that nobody else in this world could read the jokes in. But nobody in this world had ever said a word to her in English who hadn't come through a hall in Solmere with her, and this man had said two. Two words, in a voice that wasn't his voice, because nothing was his any more, not his face or his voice or his hands. Except the way he'd said them.

*Hi, Leaka*.

A little too quiet. A little too pleased that you'd turned up. Trying very hard not to sound it.

She'd heard it at two in the morning in her chat a hundred times. She'd have known it in the dark. She'd have known it underwater.

Aleaka stood in the middle of the gate square of Valcrest, in the sun, with a hawk pressed against her neck and the noise of the crowd going on round her like weather, and found that she wasn't shaking any more. That something in her had gone very calm and very clear, the way it had gone on the night she read a chapter about nets and put both hands over her face.

She could have said a hundred things. She'd rehearsed none of them. She wasn't Newish. She'd decided, somewhere in the winter, that when it happened she'd say whatever came, and let it be clumsy, because she always was, and he'd always liked it.

What came was the game.

It came up out of her the way it always had, before she could stop it. The rhythm of a hundred calls. She'd boss him about, and he'd go meek, and she'd win, and then he'd turn it round on her and she'd lose. Every time. For a year. She put her chin up. She folded her arms. She looked at a man whose face she couldn't see, in a city at the bottom of another world, and gave it to him in her slowest, sternest, most British voice, the one that had always made him laugh.

"You're late," said Aleaka.

There was a pause.

Then the water-carrier ducked his head, very slightly, the way a man does when he's being told off by somebody he'd walk into a fire for, and said, meekly, in English, in a voice like a man suitably terrified:

"Yes, ma'am."
