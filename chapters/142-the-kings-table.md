Milli had been Drawn for three days, and it still felt like wearing somebody else's body.

He'd been told it would. Everybody who'd gone up a tier had said so, in the channel, in the first year, when it was still new: *it's like your body got an update*, Calvin had written, and RX had written *it's like when you change your sensitivity and everything's slightly wrong for a week*. Milli had read it and thought: *that's not proof of anything. That's just people describing feelings*.

He'd been wrong. He was often wrong, these days. It was an interesting change.

He noticed things now. That was the main difference. Not big things. Small ones. The way a person's weight shifted on their feet a breath before they turned round. The sound a door made before it opened. The way Hana's tail moved when she was about to laugh, which he'd watched for two years and never once *seen*, not like this, not as a thing that happened in time, a beat before the laugh, like a drummer's hand coming up.

And his own tail had stopped moving.

That was the strangest part. For twenty-nine years, on Earth in a body without one and for two years here in a body with one, he'd been impatient. Rational, realistic, analytical, and impatient. His tail had shown it. It twitched and lashed and wrapped itself round things without asking him. Kestra had watched it for two years, every morning, in the courtyard at Solmere, and said *again*.

Since the side sand, three days ago, flat on his back, no, standing over Hako, who was flat on his back, laughing, his tail had been still. Not held still. Just still. As if it had finally been told what it was waiting for, and could stop looking.

"You're doing the thing," said Hana.

"What thing?"

"The noticing thing." She was walking beside him down the hill from the king's house, in the morning, with her arm through his. "You've been doing it since the side sand. You look at things like you're reading them."

"I'm noticing."

"I know, Honey," said Hana. "It's very attractive."

Milli's tail did something, then. Just once. He decided not to notice it.

***

Valcrest knew.

You could feel it in the streets. Milli had walked these streets every day for a week and they'd been loud every day, midsummer loud, Lists loud, a city that had doubled overnight and was trying to be in every square at once. This was different. This morning the city was loud the way a hive is loud when somebody's knocked it. Everyone talking. Everyone talking about the same thing.

*Daughter of Diwa, a healer. And of Ars.*

They'd heard it at sunset. Forty thousand of them, in the Lists, read out by Lucan in a voice that for once hadn't broken. And then they'd gone home and told everyone who hadn't been there, and those people had told everyone they knew, and by dawn there wasn't a baker's boy or a cooper's wife or a chicken-seller in the whole of Valcrest who didn't know that the fox girl with the sword the size of a door, the lake girl, *let it fall*, was the daughter of the Hero of the Redmarch.

And that he was here. Somewhere. In the city.

And that nobody could find him.

"There's a crowd at the Lance," said Hana. "Look."

There was. Milli could see it from the top of the high street: a solid knot of people outside the Broken Lance, hundreds, filling the street, spilling into the side streets. Craning. Shouting questions at the windows. Lucan was on the step, in his red cloak, with his arms out, saying something nobody could hear over the noise, and weeping.

"What are they asking?"

"Where he is," said Hana. "What he looks like." She listened. Hana could listen to a crowd the way she listened to a room, and pick out the threads in it. "They want to know where the seven-foot man is. With the eyes like coals. And the horse."

"There isn't a horse."

"I know," said Hana. "There was, for a month, apparently. When he was nineteen. He cut its saddle up for a sword grip." She paused. "I don't think that's going to help."

***

They walked down through it. Through the crowd, through the city, toward the market square below the Lists. Nobody stopped them. People looked at them, the way people always looked at Hana, the cat princess, the summoned girl with the crown and the purple tail; and at Milli, beside her, the cat. But they weren't looking for Hana this morning. They were looking for a man seven feet tall.

They looked straight past the water-carrier.

Milli saw him before Hana did. He was coming up the side of the market square from the bakers' row, on his own, with a sack of bread over one shoulder and a fishing rod over the other, the way he'd been fetching bread for the long table at the Lance every morning this week because Lucan kept forgetting to order enough. He was walking through the crowd. Through the middle of it. Through hundreds of people who were all, every one of them, looking for him.

Nobody looked at him.

A man bumped into him and said *sorry* without turning his head. A woman stepped round him with her basket. A boy ran past him shouting *HE'S AT THE LISTS, SOMEONE SAW HIM AT THE LISTS*, and nearly knocked the bread off his shoulder, and didn't stop.

He reached them. He stopped.

"Morning," he said.

"They're looking for you," said Milli.

"I know."

"All of them. The whole city." Milli looked round at it. At hundreds of people craning, pointing, shouting. "And you're walking through the middle of them with *bread*."

"Somebody has to get the bread."

"They're looking right *at* you," said Milli. He couldn't help it. It was the most irrational thing he'd ever seen and he'd seen a snake talk. "That woman just *stepped round* you. And she's holding a paper fishing rod. *For you*. And she stepped round you."

The water-carrier looked at the woman with the basket, going away across the square. She had a paper fishing rod sticking up out of the basket among the onions.

"I know," he said. "It's very restful."

***

There was a girl singing in the square.

Milli heard her before he saw her. Over the noise of the crowd, from the far side, by the fountain: a girl's voice, high and clear and very slightly flat, singing something with a lot of verses. A ballad. He knew it was a ballad from the rhythm, the way the lines went up and came down and went up again, like a cart on a hill road.

People were stopping to listen. Quite a lot of them.

"*Oh*," said Hana, very quietly, beside him. "Oh, it's *her*."

"Who?"

"Maren wrote about her. In the channel. On the road south." Hana was looking at the fountain with her one violet eye very wide. "The innkeeper's daughter. From Pellow's Cross. The one who sang the Black Blade ballad, and he paid her silver to sing it again."

Milli looked at the water-carrier.

He'd gone very still. He was standing at the edge of the square with the bread over his shoulder, looking at the fountain. At a girl of about fifteen in a plain dress with her hair tied back, standing on the edge of the fountain basin with her hands clasped in front of her, singing to a crowd.

She'd come to Valcrest for midsummer. Everybody had. And she'd been singing in the square all week, probably, for coppers, the way girls from inns did. And last night she'd heard what everyone heard.

She'd written a new verse.

*And the girl he raised by the lake so cold*

*with her mother's ears and a sword of old*

*came down to the Lists where the brave ones go*

*and she let it fall, and she let it go*

It was terrible. The rhymes were terrible and the tune was slightly flat and *a sword of old* didn't mean anything. Milli winced. He was rational; he couldn't help it. *Let it fall* and *let it go* weren't even the same thing.

The crowd round the fountain loved it.

They were joining in. On the last line of every verse. *And she let it fall, and she let it go*. A hundred people, and then two hundred, standing round a fountain in the market square of Valcrest at ten in the morning, singing a terrible ballad about a fox girl with a sword, while the man the ballad was about stood at the back of the crowd with a sack of bread over his shoulder and nobody looked at him.

The girl went on. More verses. The Hero was seven feet tall again, with eyes like coals. The horse called Midnight came back. There was a new verse about the healer, who in this version had been a princess with golden hair who'd healed the Hero's wounds with a kiss, and Milli watched the water-carrier's shoulders go very slightly at that one, and then go still.

The girl sang the last verse. The old last verse, from Pellow's Cross.

*And the flowers come up blue and white*

*that he planted on his own*.

She stopped.

The crowd cheered. People threw coppers into the basin of the fountain. The girl bobbed a curtsey, very red, and got down off the edge to collect them.

***

The water-carrier went across the square.

Milli watched him go. Through the crowd round the fountain, which parted for him without anyone noticing it was parting, the way water parts round a stone. He went up to the girl, who was crouched at the edge of the basin fishing coppers out of the water, and stood beside her, and held out his hand.

There was something in it. Milli saw it catch the light. Silver.

The girl looked up. She looked at the silver. Then at the man holding it out. Her eyes went to his face and slid off, the way everyone's did, and she frowned, and looked again.

"You," she said. Milli heard it, from across the square; he could hear everything now. "You're the one from Pellow's Cross. With the buckets. You paid me to sing it again."

"I did."

"Nobody pays silver." The girl was staring at the coin. "Not for the Black Blade. Everybody knows the Black Blade. Everyone sings it."

"I liked it," said the water-carrier.

"It's not a very good song."

"It's a very good song," said the water-carrier. "It's just about somebody else." He held the silver out a little further. "Sing it again. The new verse too."

The girl looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the coin, very carefully, and stood up, and got back up onto the edge of the fountain, and looked out at the crowd, which had started to drift away.

"*Again*," she said. Loudly. "*Somebody paid. Again*."

And she sang it again.

***

Milli waited for him at the edge of the square.

He'd been rational his whole life. It was the first thing anybody ever said about him, after *impatient*. He wanted proof. He wanted things to make sense. He'd stood in a hall in Solmere two years ago, the first day, while thirty-four other people panicked, and asked the Advisor for proof that the way home existed, because it was the only sensible question to ask.

He didn't understand this at all.

"Why?" said Milli, when the water-carrier came back across the square with the bread still over his shoulder.

"Why what?"

"Why pay her?" Milli looked at the fountain. At the girl on the edge of it, singing the terrible new verse to a crowd that was drifting back. "She's got everything wrong. Seven feet tall. Eyes like coals. A horse. A princess with *golden hair*." He looked at the water-carrier. "Diwa was a fox. With cold hands. Who stitched you up and told you you were stupid. Alea told Hana. She wasn't a princess. She didn't heal you with a *kiss*."

"No," said the water-carrier.

"Then why pay her to sing it *wrong*?"

The water-carrier looked at the fountain for a long time.

"Because she's kind," he said. "The girl. She doesn't know anything. She's never met anyone in it. She heard it from somebody who heard it from somebody, and she made it up as she went, and she made it kind." He turned the rod on his shoulder. "Everyone in it's brave. The healer heals people. The girl comes down to the Lists. The flowers come up." He was quiet. "The real one's not a song anybody'd want to sing. This one's better."

Milli thought about that.

"It's not true," he said.

"No," said the water-carrier. "Let her."

And he went off up the high street toward the Broken Lance with the bread, through a crowd of hundreds of people looking for a seven-foot man with eyes like coals, and not one of them turned round.

Hana put her arm back through Milli's.

"*Let her*," she said quietly. "He always says that."

"It's not rational."

"No, Honey," said Hana. "It's better."

***

The king's supper was at dusk.

All thirty-five. That was the promise. *After the Lists. At my table. All of you. I'll feed you properly.* He'd made it in the common room of the Broken Lance with a paper penguin on the table in front of him, and Milli had been holding him to it for four days, privately, without saying anything to anyone, the way you hold onto a thing you've been waiting two years for and are frightened to look at directly.

The king's house on the hill had a great hall. Milli had walked past it every day for ten days without going in. It was the old part of the house, older than the rest: a long high room with a beamed roof and a hearth at each end big enough to stand up in, and windows down one side looking out over the city. Tonight there were tables in it. Three long tables down the length of the room, and a short one across the top, and lamps on every beam, and both hearths lit even though it was midsummer, because the king said a hall without a fire in it was just a barn.

And food.

Milli had been to Vane's banquet in Solmere, in the first autumn, where a lord had called Hana *the cat princess* and there'd been swans. This wasn't that. There were no swans. There were geese, a great many of them, because the cook had been shouting about geese for ten days and had finally been allowed to roast them. There was bread from every bakery in the city. There were pies, because somebody had told the king about Lucan's pie, and there were eleven of them on a separate table at the end with a card propped against them that said, in the king's big rough hand, *SALTY*. There was cheese. There were early peas and new potatoes and something with onions in it that tasted of somebody's grandmother. There was ale in jugs and wine in jugs and water in jugs, and nobody poured for anybody; you poured for yourself, or for the person next to you, the way you would in a farmhouse kitchen.

The king served the first goose himself.

He stood at the top of the hall in his shirtsleeves with a carving knife and carved it, very well, the way a smith does everything with his hands, and put the first slice on a plate, and carried it down the length of the hall himself, and put it in front of Maigo, who'd been talking to the man next to him about trees and stopped, mid-word, for possibly the first time in his life.

"You planted the tree," said the king. "By her grave."

Maigo looked up at him with his white glowing eyes behind his pixel sunglasses, and his antlers full of leaves.

"Yes," said Maigo. Very small.

"Then you get the first slice," said the king, and went back up the hall to carve the second.

***

Milli sat between Hana and Jared, halfway down the middle table, and didn't eat very much.

He looked at the room instead. He noticed things now. He noticed that the king had put them all at the long tables in no particular order, groups mixed, the way Drew had mixed them for the trials: Group 1 next to Group 6, Group 3 next to Group 7, a squid on a plate next to a kettle. He noticed that the guides and guards were at the tables too, not standing at the walls: Maren beside Lucan, who was weeping into a goose; Kestra beside Bram, who was talking; Ysolde and Rhea arm-wrestling at the end of the far table while Sabine refereed. He noticed that the coast girl, Sorrel, was there, at the end of the near table, between Alea and Gigichi, eating everything and talking with her mouth full, and that nobody had invited her except Alea, and nobody had minded.

He noticed that the water-carrier was at the top table. Not at the top. At the end of it. Beside the king. With Halvard on the king's other side, very thin and very upright, eating almost nothing, and looking, Milli thought, like a man who'd been told something four nights ago and was still finding out what it weighed.

He noticed that the king kept looking at him.

At Milli.

Not often. A glance, now and then, down the length of the hall. While he carved, and while he talked to the water-carrier, and while he laughed at something Tiger shouted from the far end. Each time, the king's eyes came down the hall and found Milli and stayed a moment and went away again.

He was waiting, Milli understood. The way you wait for iron.

***

He waited until the geese were gone and the pies were going and the room had got to the loud warm stage of a long supper where everyone's talking at once and nobody's listening to anybody in particular. Then he put his hands flat on the table.

He'd thought about this for two years. Every morning, in the courtyard at Solmere, under Kestra's *again*. Every night, in the rooms above the archive, reading Oswin's books. He'd thought about what he'd say, and how he'd say it, and he'd rehearsed it, the way Newish had rehearsed a word in a tin mirror, except he'd rehearsed a whole speech. Rational. Clear. A list of questions, in order, each one following from the last.

He'd thought he'd be frightened. He wasn't. His tail was perfectly still.

He stood up.

The noise in the hall didn't stop. Not at first. But the people nearest him noticed, and went quiet, and the people near them noticed them going quiet, and it went out across the room the way a ripple goes out across a pond, until the whole great hall was silent and every face at every table was turned toward a cat-eared man standing up halfway down the middle table with his hands at his sides.

At the top table, the king put down his cup.

"Majesty," said Milli.

"Milli," said the king.

"You promised," said Milli. "At the Lance. *After the Lists. At my table. Ask me then, and I'll answer you as well as I can*."

"I did."

"I'm asking," said Milli. He took a breath. He let all the rehearsed questions go, every one, the whole list, and said the only one that mattered. "Is there a way home?"

***

The king didn't answer straight away.

He sat at the top table with his big scarred hands flat on the wood, the way Halvard sat at a table when he couldn't lift them, and looked down the length of the hall at Milli for a long time. The fires burned at both ends. Nobody moved.

Milli noticed that Yan, at the far end of the near table, had put his hand on the white snake round his neck.

"I'll tell you what I know," said the king. "And what I don't. And which is which." He paused. "That's the best answer anyone can give you. My father used to say a smith who tells you he knows what's inside a piece of iron before he's cut it is lying, or a fool."

"Okay," said Milli.

"What I know," said the king. "Thirty-five of you were brought here through a circle in my capital. By my Advisor. With my permission." He didn't look at Halvard. He didn't need to. "The circle is old. Older than the kingdom. Nobody knows who first drew it. It's been used before, a handful of times in four hundred years, and it's never brought more than three. It brought thirty-five because of what was in the middle of it." He glanced, just once, at the man at the end of the top table. "You know about that now. All of you."

The hall was very quiet.

"What I know," said the king again. "The records of the ones who came before. Oswin's been reading them for two years, with a key Halvard should have given him sooner." He nodded down the hall to where Oswin was sitting beside Cursiffe, very still, with a goose bone in his hand. "They come. They stay. Some of them do great things. And then the records stop. Every time. They don't say the summoned went home. They don't say they died. They just stop." He paused. "I don't know why. Nobody does."

Milli didn't move.

"What I believe," said the king. "Not what I know. What I believe." He turned his cup on the table, slowly. "The oldest books say only one kind of hand could ever send someone back the way they came. A mage at the tenth tier. A Sun." He was quiet. "Nobody alive has been a Sun. Nobody in any record I've read has been one for certain. There are songs. There are always songs." He looked down the hall. "I'm the closest thing to the top this kingdom has on the swordsman's side, and I'm one tier short. On the mage's side, Halvard's three short. There's never been a Sun, in my lifetime, or my father's, or his."

"Then it doesn't exist," said Milli. Very quietly. He'd always said the rational thing. He said it now.

"I didn't say that," said the king.

***

He stood up.

Not like a king. Like a man getting up from a table to go and look at something in the forge. He came round the end of the top table, and down the length of the hall, between the long tables, past Group 1 and Group 6 and a squid on a plate and a kettle, until he was standing in front of Milli. A big grey man in his shirtsleeves, with goose fat on his fingers and soot in the creases of his knuckles. Ascendant. The only one alive.

"When I was ten," said the king, "my father put a bar of iron in the fire in Brakewater and told me to make a plough blade out of it. And I said *I can't. There's no plough blade in there. It's just a bar*." He looked at Milli. "And he said: *there's a plough blade in every bar of iron in the world. Nobody's ever seen it. You can't see it until you've made it. That doesn't mean it isn't there*."

Milli didn't say anything.

"I've never seen a Sun," said the king. "Nobody has. That doesn't mean there isn't one in the fire." He paused. "Thirty-five of you came through that circle. You climb faster than any of my people ever have. You can see your windows. You can see exactly how far you've come and how far you've got to go. You're two years in, and twenty-four of you are past the first tier already, and one of you is past the second." He didn't look at Thea. Everybody else did. "My Advisor brought you here because he believed one of you might get to the top. I let him, because I believed it too." He was quiet. "I still believe it. I believed it more this week than I ever have."

"Belief isn't proof," said Milli.

"No," said the king. "It isn't. Iron in the fire isn't a plough blade." He looked at Milli for a long time. "There's a door in my archive in Solmere. Sealed by a king who died three hundred years ago. I've never opened it. I'm going to, when I go back. I don't know what's behind it." He paused. "And if there's a way home, any way at all, for any of you, behind that door or in that circle or at the top of a tier nobody's reached, I will spend this kingdom finding it."

Milli stood very still.

"That's not proof either," said the king. "That's a promise." He put his goose-fat hand on Milli's shoulder. It was like having a mountain put its hand on you. "You were brought here without being asked. If there's a way to send you back, you'll be sent. Every one of you who wants to go." He glanced, once, down the hall. At the far end of the near table. At a silver-haired man with a white snake round his neck, whose hand was resting on it. "As fast as I can."

***

Milli didn't sit down for a long moment.

He stood there, with the king's hand on his shoulder, and thought about it. Rationally. Clearly. The way he thought about everything. A circle nobody understood. Records that stopped. A tier nobody had ever held. A door nobody had opened. And a king, in his shirtsleeves, saying *I believe*.

It wasn't proof. It wasn't anything like proof. He'd asked for proof two years ago, in a hall in Solmere, and been given a briefing. He'd asked for it every week since, and been given *again*.

He looked at Hana. She was looking up at him with her one violet eye, very calm, with her hand on the half-heart at her wrist. Not asking him anything. Just there.

He looked down the hall at the far end of the near table. At Yan. At Yan's hand on Suzu. At his face, doing nothing, the way it always did nothing. Yan, who had a mother at home, waiting. Two months, Earth time. Who'd wanted to reach the tenth tier since the first winter, for her. Who'd never once put it on anyone else.

He looked at the top table. At the man at the end of it, beside an empty chair where the king had been. Who'd died in his sleep in another world, and woken up in this one, and lived thirty-four years here with no way back, and stopped looking for the window. Who'd said, on a riverbank, four days ago, when Milli asked him: *No. Nobody does. Not yet. Ask the king.*

*Not yet*.

"Okay," said Milli.

The king took his hand off his shoulder.

"Okay?"

"It's not proof," said Milli. "But it's honest. And it's the first time anybody's told me which parts they know and which parts they don't." He breathed. "That's better than proof. Proof would've been a lie."

The king looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed, the short deep sound like a hammer landing once on an anvil, and turned round, and went back up the hall to the top table, and sat down, and picked up his cup.

Milli sat down.

His tail, under the table, went round Hana's ankle. He let it.

***

The king didn't let them go back to the noise. Not quite yet.

He stood up again at the top table, after a while, with his cup in his hand. Not to make a speech. He didn't make speeches. He stood the way a man stands at the end of a day's work, to say what happens tomorrow.

"Two things," said the king.

The hall went quiet again.

"One," said the king. "You go back. To your postings. In a few days. Wrenhaven, and Kingsferry, and Duskhollow, and Cinderhold, and Thornwall, and Solmere, and here." He looked round the hall. At all of them. "There are thirty-nine people in this city who aren't people. You know. Yan wrote it in your channel; I read it twice. They've been counting you all week. Thirty-five of you, in one bowl, in one place, for the first time." He was quiet for a moment. "Halvard was right. He's been right for two years. Together you're one target. That's why there was an order."

At the top table, Halvard didn't move.

"And I was right too," said the king. "To lift it. For a week." He looked at the man at the end of the top table. "Because you needed to be in one place. Once. All of you. With him." He paused. "Now you have been. Now they've counted you. And now you go back to the edges, where you were sent, and you hold them. In fives. The way you've held them for two years."

Nobody said anything. Milli noticed that nobody looked surprised, either. They'd known. They'd all known since Yan's message, last night, at the long table. *Don't go anywhere alone. Not till we're all out of the city*.

"Two," said the king.

He put his cup down.

"The order's gone," he said. "*No gathering*. For good. Not lifted for a week. Gone." He looked round the hall. "You go back to your postings. And when you need to be together, you come. Any of you. All of you. Whenever you choose. You don't ask me. You don't ask Halvard. You decide." He glanced, once, at Hana, halfway down the middle table, with her hand on her wrist. "I hear there's a girl in Solmere who can make two halves of a charm that warm when the other's held. I imagine that'll help."

Hana went very red.

"That's all," said the king, and sat down, and picked up a pie.

***

They walked back down the hill very late.

The whole of the thirty-five, more or less, in a long loose crowd, in pairs, never alone, under the lamps, with the city quiet now round them and the paper fishing rods still in every window. The threadless were out there somewhere. Milli could feel the shape of that, now; not the way Yan could, not candles, just a kind of wrongness in certain dark doorways that he noticed and didn't look at.

Hana walked beside him with her arm through his.

"He didn't say yes," said Milli.

"No."

"He didn't say there's a way. He said he *believes* there might be. He said a Sun *might* exist. He said a door *might* have something behind it." Milli looked at the lamps going down the hill. "That's not a yes."

"He didn't say no, either," said Hana.

"That's not an answer."

Hana was quiet for a moment.

"Yan said something," she said. "In the channel. Last summer. After the Redmarch. When everyone was asking Group 3 whether the man on the red was him, and Group 3 kept saying *he didn't say*." She looked up at Milli. "Yan said: *when someone doesn't answer, that's an answer. It just isn't no. It's not yet*."

Milli didn't say anything.

He thought about it, all the way down the hill. Rationally. Clearly. The way he thought about everything.

*Not yet*.

At the bottom of the hill, outside the Broken Lance, under a paper fishing rod in the window, he stopped.

"I've been waiting two years for proof," he said.

"I know, Honey."

"I've been bad at waiting my whole life. Kestra said *again* to me every morning for two years." He looked at his own tail, very still, round Hana's ankle. "And three days ago on the side sand I finally learned how. And then the king stood up and told me the thing I've been waiting for doesn't exist yet."

"How do you feel?"

Milli thought about it.

"Patient," he said. And was surprised, saying it, that it was true.

Hana laughed, and put her head on his shoulder, and they went into the Lance together, where thirty people were already arguing about who'd got the last of Salty's pies, and Salty was saying *I've still got four*, and somewhere in the middle of it all, at the end of the long table, a man with a rod across his knees was reading the channel and laughing at something, and not writing anything at all.
