The king of Aurelan stood in Ars's hen-yard with a roll of hammers at his feet, and the hens fell in love with him.

Ars watched it happen. There was nothing else to do. One moment the big red one was savaging the boot of the king's guard with the cold, personal fury she'd been turning on Ars every single evening for five years, and the next she'd stopped, and turned her head on one side, and looked up at Severin. And then she walked over to him. Not at a run, with her neck out. *Walked*. Quite slowly. And stood beside his boot, and leaned against it, the way a cat leans against a leg, and closed her eyes.

The other two came over and did the same.

"Well," said the king, looking down at them.

Ars looked at his hens. He'd built them a house with a shingled roof and a ramp. He'd fed them every morning for five years. He'd once, at real personal risk, gone into the reeds at night after a fox that had taken the speckled one, and come out with the speckled one, alive, and been pecked for it on the way back.

"Eight years," he said, "I've been feeding them."

"Animals can tell," said Alea, from the doorway, very faintly.

The king bent down, with a grunt, and picked up the big red one, and tucked her under his arm like a loaf, and scratched her under the chin with one thick scarred finger. She made a noise Ars had never once heard her make. It was very like purring.

"What's her name?"

"Captain," said Alea.

"He named her?"

"*I* named her. He's not allowed to name anything." Alea had come down off the step. She'd taken her hands away from her mouth. She was looking at the king with an expression Ars knew well, because it was the one she'd worn at six when he brought home a hedgehog: equal parts terror and delight, and the delight winning. "He'd call them all the same thing."

"Would he," said the king, and looked at Ars over the top of the hen, and for just a moment the king's face wasn't a king's face at all.

"Come in," said Ars. "Before she decides she wants to go back to the capital with you."

***

He'd known Severin twelve years.

Not well. Not the way Halvard had known him, every day, at the garrison and after. But he'd known him. He'd stood in front of him the first time at twenty-two, on a parade ground at Cinderhold, a sweating Honed swordsman with a slab of black iron on his back and a temper, while the Ascendant who'd been sent from the capital to see what all the fuss was about walked round him once in a circle and said nothing at all. He'd fought under him. He'd stood in a tent with him on the night before the Redmarch, with a map on the table between them, and argued, and lost.

And afterwards, the morning after, when it was over and the ground was red, Severin had come out to find him. Ars didn't remember much of that morning. He remembered the king walking across the red alone, a long way, in his armour. Sitting down on the ground beside him by the grave without a word. And sitting there, for an hour, not saying anything, while the sun came up.

He'd offered Ars half the east, later. A lordship. A seat on the Council. A guard of his own. Ars had said he'd rather have a lake. Severin had looked at him for a long time and said, *Then have a lake*, and given him one, more or less, by quietly telling the lords of the north to leave the lake country alone. And for eight years they'd written perhaps three letters, and none of them had been about anything that mattered.

Ars had always liked him. He'd never quite known why. Severin didn't say much, and when he did, it was usually something you didn't want to hear. But he'd sat on the red ground for an hour that morning and not said anything, and that was more than anyone else had managed.

***

The king's guard stood just inside the door with her back to the wall, and didn't sit.

Ars knew who she was. He'd known since the gate, before she ever opened her mouth. She'd been in the channel all year, in other people's lines, the way all of them were. *Kestra says again. Kestra wrote it down. Kestra caught us with the map and told us to lock up.* A tall dark woman in her thirties, Reaching, very still, with a sword on her hip and a small black book in her coat that she touched, every so often, with two fingers, the way some people touch a charm.

She watched his feet. Then his hands. Then where he stood. Then, last, his face, and her eyes slid off it, the way everybody's did, and came back, and slid off again.

He didn't say her name. He didn't know it. He couldn't.

"You'll want to sit," he said instead. "It's a long way from the capital, and Alea's made too much stew."

"I'll stand."

"Kestra," said the king, without looking round. He'd sat down at the table, which groaned, and was letting Captain walk up and down his forearm. "Sit down."

She sat. On the very end of the bench, nearest the door, with her sword across her knees. She ate the stew. Ars saw her taste it and look down at the bowl in surprise.

"It's the fish," said Alea. Very politely, very carefully. The way she spoke to people when she was trying hard not to look at them as if they'd walked out of a story, which was what Kestra had done. "He catches good fish."

"They're the lake's fish," said Ars. "I just ask them politely."

Kestra looked at him. And then took the small black book out of her coat, and opened it on her knee, under the edge of the table where she thought nobody could see, and wrote something in it. Ars didn't try to see what.

***

After supper the king got down on the floor.

The sword was lying where it had lain since Ars came home: on the rag rug, in front of the hearth, wrapped in its grey sailcloth. The king knelt beside it, slowly, the way a big man kneels, one knee and then the other. He didn't ask. He just reached out and took the edge of the canvas and folded it back, the whole length of it, the way Ars had folded it back for Alea.

And then he sat there on his heels, with his hands on his thighs, and looked at it. For a long time.

Nobody spoke. Alea had stopped clearing the bowls. Kestra had stopped writing. Even Captain, on the back of the king's chair, had gone quiet.

Then Severin put out one hand and laid it on the flat of the black blade. Palm down. And moved it. Slowly, along the length of it, from the point toward the grip, the way you'd run your hand along a horse's neck. His fingers stopped at the first notch, a deep one, a hand's width from the point, and felt round the edges of it.

"Bone," he said. "Something big. With the grain going across." He moved on. Another notch. "Armour. Black iron, theirs. Good iron." Another. "That's old. That's older than the war. Years older." He looked up at Ars. "How old is this one?"

Ars looked at the notch under the king's fingertip.

"Old," he said.

The king's hand moved on. Notch after notch, all the way along the edge, reading them the way Kestra read people. He stopped at the last one, the deepest, just above the grip, where the iron had been bitten nearly a third of the way through and had held anyway.

He didn't say anything about that one. He knew what it was. He'd been on the wall.

His hand came to rest on the crooked letters by the grip.

"And this," he said.

"That's mine."

"I know it's yours. It isn't a smith's mark. A smith would have done it properly." The king's thumb moved over the scratched letters, once. "Halvard's scholar read it, I'm told. Something about stars." He didn't ask what it meant. He took his hand away. "Ars."

"Mm."

"Who forged this?"

***

Ars didn't answer straight away.

He'd known the question was coming. He'd known since he wrote the letter, sitting at this table with the split-nibbed pen, that Severin would look at the sword and ask it, because Severin had been asking it, in one way or another, for twelve years. On the parade ground at Cinderhold, walking round him in a circle: *where did a garrison boy get a blade like that?* In the tent, the night before the Redmarch. In a letter, years ago, among the three letters, a single line at the end: *I still want to know who made it.*

Ars had never told him. He'd never told anybody. Not Halvard. Not Rowan. Only Diwa, and Alea.

"I'll tell you at the anvil," he said.

The king looked at him for a long moment, from the floor. Ars waited for him to push. He didn't. He just nodded, slowly, the way he'd nodded on the red ground eight years ago when Ars had finally said something, and turned back to the sword.

"At the anvil, then," he said.

***

"There's something else," said Ars.

He told him about the Redmarch. He told it plainly, the way you'd give a report: the escort, and the gullies, and fifty scouts and three of the big ones. The ring of fire, and the ring breaking. A sergeant's arm, a boy's leg, a short square man with two broken ribs standing in the middle of all of it holding a sword he couldn't swing.

"They went past the soldiers," said Ars. "Past the summoned. Past everybody. They didn't want any of them." He looked at the black iron on the rug. "They went for the sword. Three of them had it by the blade. It was burning them, and they didn't let go. They were dragging it away."

The king was very still.

"They wanted it," he said.

"Yes."

"Ask yourself why."

"I have," said Ars.

He had. Every night, on the walk home, three days of it, with the sword on his shoulder. He'd read Malcolm's book, a page at a time, as the boy posted it in the channel in the spring: the circle under the Hollow, rings and spokes, people standing on the lines, waiting. *IF the circle under the Hollow is the same kind of circle as the one that brought us here, THEN somebody is summoning. And they didn't ask either.* He'd read Oswin's words, passed on in somebody's letter. *A summoning is a net. It catches what is tangled in it.*

Halvard had used this sword, and his net had caught thirty-five people, every one of them tangled in the man who'd carried it. Ars didn't know what somebody else's net would catch with it. He didn't want to find out.

"It caught them," said Ars quietly. "Halvard's circle. Everyone it caught was..." He stopped. He looked at the king. "It caught what was tangled in it," he said instead. "I'd rather nobody else got the chance to find out what that is."

The king looked at him for a long time. Ars watched him not ask the question that was sitting right there, in the middle of that sentence, the way he'd watched him not ask it on the floor a moment ago.

"Then it stops being one blade," said Severin.

"It was going to anyway."

"Now there are two reasons." The king put the canvas back over the sword, very carefully, the whole length of it, and stood up off the floor with one hand on the table. "Half for the girl. Half for the grave. And neither of them any use to anyone else."

***

They talked about the forge after that. Alea made tea, and Kestra sat on the end of the bench with her book closed on her knee, listening, and the king and Ars sat at the table and argued, the way they'd argued in the tent, except that this time it was about bellows.

The town forge was no good. Ars had known that before he wrote. It was a horseshoe forge, built for horseshoes and plough blades and the odd gate hinge. The sword wouldn't fit in its fire. You couldn't heat a slab of iron that size in a hearth built for horseshoes; you'd heat a foot of it and the rest would sit there cold and laugh at you.

"Stone," said the king. "A long hearth. Two bellows, one each end. And the lake for quenching." He nodded at the window, at the grey water going dark beyond the jetty. "You can't quench a piece that size in a trough. You need a lot of water and you need it all at once."

"Then we build it by the jetty."

"With what?"

"Stones," said Ars. "There are plenty on the shore. Not enough. We'll need more." He thought about it. "And the town anvil. Ours wouldn't take it. I've only got a little one, for hooks."

"You'll need help," said the king.

"I know."

"From the town."

"I know," said Ars again.

The king looked at him across the table. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Ars had lived here eight years and asked the town for nothing. Not once. Bread came from Odette because Odette sent it. Coats went up the hill *from the town* because Rowan said so. Everything went one way.

"I'll ask," said Ars.

"You'll ask," said the king, "in person. Tomorrow. Not Hale. You."

Alea, at the hearth with the kettle, had gone very still.

"Tomorrow," said Ars.

***

The king slept in the shed.

He insisted. Odette had sent the egg-woman's boy down the lake road at dusk with a handcart, piled with bedding, the good bedding, the town house's bedding, lace and all, and a note in her sharp neat hand that said *for His Majesty* and was underlined twice. The king looked at the handcart, and at the lace, and at Ars.

"Where do you keep the nets in winter?"

"The shed."

"I'll sleep in the shed."

"Majesty," said Kestra.

"I've slept in worse," said the king. "I've sat through Council." He took one blanket off the handcart, the plainest, and tucked it under his arm, and walked off across the hen-yard toward the shed with Captain trotting behind him like a dog. At the door he stopped and looked back. "You'll want to send the rest of that back to Odette. With my thanks. She'll be hurt otherwise."

"She'll be hurt anyway," said Ars.

"Yes," said the king. "But less."

Kestra took the step. Ars had known she would. She sat on the top step of the cabin with her back against the doorpost and her sword across her knees, facing the lake road, the way a guard sits. She'd sit there all night. Ars brought her a blanket and a cup of tea, and she looked at both of them as if he'd handed her something she didn't know how to hold, and then took them.

"Thank you," she said.

"He's safer here than anywhere in the kingdom," said Ars. "You know that."

"I know that," said Kestra. "I'm not sitting here for him."

Ars looked at her.

"I'm sitting here," said Kestra, looking out at the lake road in the dark, "because I'd like to know what you do at night. When nobody's watching." She drank some of the tea. "I write things down."

"I fish," said Ars.

"Then I'll write that down."

***

Alea caught him at the top of the stairs.

"They saw him come," she said, very low, so that Kestra on the step wouldn't hear. "Up on the hill. All five of them were at the window. I saw them when he walked past the hall."

"Everyone saw him come."

"Dad." She looked at him. "*Everyone* saw him come. The whole town. Odette's been telling people since the seventh. Tomorrow you're going to walk into the market and ask for *stones*."

"Yes."

"In front of everybody."

"Yes."

Alea opened her mouth. Shut it. Looked at him, for a long time, in the dark at the top of the stairs. And then something happened to her face that he'd seen happen to it only a few times, and every one of them recently.

"Good," she said. And went to bed.

***

He went down to the jetty.

It was a clear night, still, cold for the first week of autumn, with the stars out over the lake in their thousands and doubled in the water, the wrong stars, the way they always were. He sat on the end of the jetty with his feet over the edge and his rod beside him and didn't fish.

He could feel them.

Five candles on the hill above the town. He always could, if he reached for them, and tonight he didn't need to reach. They were awake. All five of them. Sitting up, close together, in the upper room of the militia hall, the way they sat when something had happened that they couldn't put in the channel. He could feel Thea's, bright and quick and turning things over. Higu's, quiet and very watchful. Gigichi's, warm and fierce and worried. Xola's, soft. And PenguChi's, the newest flame on the hard path, slow and steady and stubborn, the way Alea's had been at the start.

He opened his window and looked at the channel.

The usual. Forty lines, going up and away. Tiger and Drew arguing about whose turn it was to cook. Rachael's progress, Calvin's weather. Mashi, every night now: *two heartbeats again. no faint.* Maigo, in capitals again, mostly, though not always. Somebody in the capital asking if anyone knew where Kestra had gone, and Jared saying *away*, and Milli saying *she'll be back*, and Hana not saying anything.

Nothing from Wrenhaven. Nothing at all.

The king of Aurelan had ridden into their town that afternoon without a crown, and walked down the lake road to a fisherman's cabin with a roll of hammers on his shoulder, and every person in Wrenhaven had seen it, and the five of them had seen it from their window. And not one of them had written a single word.

Ars sat on the end of the jetty and looked at that for a long time.

*They're better than me*, he'd told Alea. *They always were.*

***

Then, because he hadn't in a while, and because the quiet had been coming and going all evening like a cat that couldn't decide whether to stay, he scrolled to the top of his own window, past the channel, past everything, and looked at the number.

Forty-eight.

It had been forty-six in the summer, the night he read the stories. It had been forty for six years before that. It had moved twice since he last looked, and he hadn't noticed either time.

He sat there and tried to work out when.

The red ground. A small cocky voice in a ring of flowers, cracking in the middle: *I hope that's okay.* A leaf under his fingertip. A short square man's hands, opening on the grip because somebody had finally told them they could. A sentence he hadn't been able to finish, at a grave, and a laugh he'd laughed for someone who couldn't. Alea crying on the bench with his arm round her. *Maigotron.*

Humming. He'd been humming on the jetty, the morning after he came home. Alea had looked at him as if he'd grown a second head. He hadn't known he was doing it.

None of it was fighting. Not one of the things that had moved it was a fight. He'd killed three hunters on the Redmarch in front of fifty scouts, and it hadn't moved for that. It had moved for a tree.

The quiet came, then, and stayed. He sat in it for a long time, on the end of the jetty, with the window open on his knees and the wrong stars doubled in the water, and five candles awake on the hill, and a king asleep in his net shed with a hen.

Behind him, on the step, a pencil moved across a page, very softly, and stopped.

He didn't look round. Let her write it down. He fished.
