The hook was done.

Gabe had it in a twist of oilcloth inside his shirt, against his chest, where he could feel it every time he breathed: a finger-length of star-iron, cold, with frost in it if you looked close, bent round to a shank and an eye and a barb so fine you could hang a hair on it. Three months of filing. Eleven of Harl's files. And down the shank, in letters you'd need a lamp and a good eye to read, seven of them cut a stroke at a time with Harl's finest graver and a breath between every stroke, the eighth finished on the last night before the river went:

*RESURGAM*.

He'd cut the M at the anvil at two in the morning with Harl asleep in the chair by the cold hearth and Deston asleep on a sack in the corner and his own hands shaking so badly by the end that he'd had to stop between every stroke and hold them under his arms. And then it was done, and he'd sat there looking at it in the lamplight, and he hadn't known what to do with his face.

*I shall rise again*. Off a gravestone, when he was eleven, in a churchyard. He didn't know whose. He'd never forgotten it. He'd never known why, until he'd been at Harl's anvil for three months with a bit of a fallen star in the tongs, and needed a word.

Harl had woken up and come and looked at it over his shoulder. A long time. And then gone back to the chair without saying anything, which from Harl was a speech.

***

They went on the fourth day after the Wren broke.

The *Patient Widow* was at the end of the long jetty where she'd been tied up all winter, with the river going past her black and quick and full of grey ice. Old Haskett had his two boys on the poles and was standing at the steering oar in his oilskins saying, to anybody who'd listen, that ice-out was no time for a salt barge, that nobody with any sense went upriver the first week, that there'd be floes the size of barns coming down off the lake and through the Narrows and he'd seen a barge stove in by one in his father's time.

"We've got a wind," said Calvin.

"You've got a *boy*," said Old Haskett, "who says he's got a wind." He spat over the side. "I've seen his wind. It's a good wind. It's not a good enough wind for that."

"It got us four days in an afternoon," said Calvin.

"In summer."

"It's a better wind now," said Calvin, and didn't explain, because he couldn't, not without saying *Earshot* and *Candle* and a lot of other words Old Haskett would have spat at.

Rachael came down the jetty last. With her bag, and Sister Maud's forty-year needle case in her coat, and the face she wore when she'd decided something and was daring the world to argue. Deston had told her, a week ago, at the kitchen table, that it might be better if she stayed; that it was a long way, and the river was bad at ice-out, and Maud needed her.

She'd looked at him.

"Rachael comes," she'd said, "or nobody goes."

So Rachael came.

Sabine came too. Nobody had asked her; she was just on the barge when they got there, sitting on a coil of rope at the stern with her sword across her knees and her feet up, eating an apple. "I'm your guard," she'd said, when Dennis looked at her. "I guard. And I want to see this lake. Three and a half years you lot have been going on about a lake." She'd taken another bite of the apple. "Also it's your boat and I want my cut."

And at the last moment, as Haskett's boys were casting off, a very small old woman in three shawls came down the long jetty, slowly, with a stick, and stopped at the end of it.

Yeva.

She'd never come to the north quay. Not in forty years, Calvin said. It was the soldiers' end; it was where the men from the river wall drank. She stood at the end of the jetty in her shawls with her walnut face and her dark sharp eyes, and held something out over the gap of black water between the planks and the barge's side.

A net. A small one. Folded tight, the way she folded them, with a knot at each corner.

"For whoever's at the end of the river," she said. In plain river Aurelan.

Dennis took it. Leaned out over the water and took it from her knotted hand, the one Rachael had mended, the third finger bending round the net the way it was meant to.

"We didn't say who," said Dennis.

"You didn't need to." Yeva looked at the five of them on the barge. At Gabe, last, with his hand on his chest over the oilcloth. "Nobody packs like that for a lake. You're going to see somebody." She put both hands on the head of her stick. "Tell him an old woman at the last well said a song back to him. That's all. He'll know what it means or he won't."

The barge moved. The current took it, and Haskett's boys leaned on their poles, and the black water opened up between the planks and the hull, a pace, two, ten. Calvin stood in the bow with his eyes shut and his hands open. And the wind came round.

It came round the barge the way it had in the summer, on the first trip, the way it did now whenever Calvin asked it: not pushing, but bending. Leaning into the old patched sail from behind as if somebody had put a hand there. The *Widow* turned her nose upriver into the current and the floes and began, slowly, and then not slowly, to walk.

Gabe looked back. Yeva was still at the end of the jetty, very small. She didn't wave. She stood there with her hands on her stick until the bend took her.

***

The first floe hit them on the second day.

Not a big one. The big ones were in the middle of the river, where the current was strongest, going down past them in a slow grey procession like carts going to market, turning, grinding against each other, sometimes riding up one on another with a long groan and going under. Haskett kept them out of the middle. He kept them in along the edge where the water was slacker, with Calvin's wind holding the sail and the boys poling off the bank; and the floes that came down the edge were small, the size of tables, of doors.

Gabe was in the bow with a pole. Fending. That was the job; he'd asked for it. He was the biggest, and the strongest, and the bow was where the ice came first. He stood there all morning with his feet planted on the wet planks and the long ash pole in his hands, and every time a slab came down the edge at them he put the iron-shod end of the pole against it and leaned, and *Wall* came up in him, the way it always did, his aura going into his arms and his back and his legs until he was a big hard thing a slab of ice couldn't move. And the slab turned off the pole and went past.

It worked forty times. Fifty.

On the fifty-first, the slab was bigger than it looked.

It came down out of the shadow under the far bank, low in the water, so that only a hand's breadth of it showed; and under that hand's breadth was a yard of grey ice, a cart's worth, turning slowly as it came. Gabe put the pole against it and leaned.

The pole broke.

Clean, in the middle, with a crack like a branch in a frost. And the slab came on, the whole cart's weight of it, and Gabe was standing in the bow with half a pole in his hands and nothing between him and it.

He didn't think. He never did, in a fight; that was the point of *Wall*. He threw the broken pole down and put his hands out and his shoulder down and let *Wall* come up in him all at once, everything, every bit of aura he had into every part of him, hard, harder than he'd ever made it, a wall, a big man made of stone standing in the bow of a barge. And the slab hit him.

It hit him in the chest and the left shoulder. He felt the ice crack against him. He felt it stop. He was a wall; it didn't move him an inch; he stood in the bow with his feet planted and his hands flat on the slab and the barge's nose held off it, and the slab turned, slowly, on the current, and slid off along the side of the hull and went past.

And something in his chest went *tick*.

He felt it. Very small. Like a bit of glass cracking in your pocket. Left side, low, under the place where the slab had hit. He stood in the bow with his hands still out and his *Wall* still up and felt it, and then the *Wall* went down, and the pain came up, all at once, so hard that he sat down on the wet planks without deciding to.

***

"You're an idiot," said Rachael.

He was lying on his back on the deck with his coat open and his shirt pulled up and the oilcloth with the hook in it moved carefully to one side, and she was kneeling beside him with her hands an inch off his ribs, not touching. Not yet. She hadn't asked yet. She was looking at him.

"I held it," said Gabe.

"You held it." She didn't move her hands. "And you cracked a rib holding it. One. Left side, the seventh. I can feel it from here. I don't even need to touch you." She looked at the rib. "Can I?"

"Yeah."

She put her hands on him. Flat, on his side, cold. He watched her go cold. The colour went out of her face from the top down, like a blind coming down, and her lips went pale, and she shut her eyes; and he felt the warmth go into the rib, into the crack, and the crack close, slowly, like a door being pushed to against a draught. It took a long time. She didn't move. When she took her hands away she was shivering, and she put them under her arms, and sat back on her heels and looked at him.

"Don't do it again," she said.

"It held, though."

"I don't care if it held. You held a cart of ice off the bow with your chest and your rib broke." She was still shivering. "If the wall held, why did *you* break?"

Gabe lay on the deck and looked at the sky.

He didn't know. That was the thing. He'd been a wall for nearly two years. *Wall* had come to him in the Lists, in the second summer, the first time Sabine hit him hard enough to mean it and he'd stood there and not gone down. It had never broken. Things broke on it. He'd stood in front of people with it a hundred times: Rachael, on the cliffs at the Narrows, the spring before last; Calvin, Dennis. He'd stood in front of Declan's driftwood stick on the shingle at the lake once and sat down, but that was different, that was Declan, everybody sat down.

It held. And something under it had gone *tick*, like glass.

"I don't know," he said.

Rachael looked at him a while longer. Then she stood up, slowly, still shivering, and went to the stern to sit in the sun with her hands under her arms. Not near him. Near enough that he'd know she was there.

***

He lay on the deck all afternoon. Rachael said he had to; the rib was closed but it would be sore for days, and if he stood in the bow and did that again she'd let it stay broken and he could explain it to Declan. So he lay there on a folded sail with the oilcloth on his chest and the river going past, and thought.

He thought about Harl's files.

He'd broken eleven of them. Over the winter, on the star-iron, filing the hook. He hadn't meant to. A file was hard; that was the point of a file, it was harder than what you filed. Harl made his own, from good steel, and quenched them so hard they'd scratch glass. And the star-iron was harder still, and cold, and wouldn't take the fire, and he'd filed it every night for three months; and eleven times, a file had gone in his hands. Not worn out. *Gone*. Snapped across, with a little high *tick*, like glass, and the two halves in his hands.

They hung on a nail by the back door at Harl's, all eleven, in a row. Harl had hung them there. *To remind you*, he'd said. Gabe had thought he meant *remind you to be careful*. He'd been careful. They'd broken anyway.

*Hard things break*, Harl had said, the first week, before any of them had. *That's what nobody tells you. You quench a thing hard enough and it'll cut anything. And then you drop it on the floor and it goes like a teacup.*

*So what do you do?*

*You temper it.*

He'd watched Harl do it a hundred times. A blade, or a chisel, or a spring for a cart: you hardened it, quenched it in the trough so it hissed and went grey and hard as glass; and then you cleaned it bright, and put it back near the fire, not in, near, and watched the colours run along the bright steel. Pale yellow first. Straw. Then darker, bronze, then purple, then blue. And you took it off when it was the colour you wanted, and let it cool, and it wasn't quite as hard any more. A hair less. It gave a little, when you hit it.

And it didn't break.

*A sword's no good hard*, Harl had said. *A hard sword shatters on the first good blow. You want a sword that gives a hair and comes back. That's tempered. That's all tempered is. Hard enough to hold an edge and soft enough to take a knock.*

Gabe lay on the deck of the *Widow* with the sky going past.

*Tempered*. It was the name of the tier. It had been in front of him for nearly two years. In the window, on the line under *Drawn*, every time he opened it. *Progress to Tempered*. And he'd been at an anvil every day of those years and never once put the two together.

He'd been quenching himself. Every time. Hard as he could. A file. A wall. And walls broke; Declan had told Azac that, on the shingle, a long time ago, and Azac had told everybody. *Don't be a wall. Walls break*.

His bar was ninety-seven. It had been ninety-seven since midwinter.

He opened his window anyway.

He tried it the next morning, with Sabine.

He asked her. He stood up off the sail with his rib aching and went down the barge to where she was sitting on her coil of rope with her sword across her knees and said, "Hit me." And she looked at him over the top of her apple and said, "Where," and he said, "Anywhere. Not the rib."

She hit him across the shoulder with the flat of her sword. Hard. She'd drilled him for three and a half years; she knew exactly how hard he could take.

He tried to temper it. He didn't know how. He let *Wall* come up, and then tried to let it go, a little, to make it a hair less hard, the way the colour ran down the steel; and he got it wrong, and let it go too much, and the flat of Sabine's sword went into his shoulder like a plank into a sack of flour, and he grunted and staggered and nearly went over the side.

"That hurt," said Gabe.

"That's what it does," said Sabine.

"Again."

She hit him again. He didn't let it go at all; it was *Wall*, hard as stone; and the sword rang on his shoulder like a bell and the shock went through him and down his arm and his fingers went numb, and something in the shoulder joint went *tick*. Not broken. Not quite. Close.

"Again," said Gabe.

Sabine lowered her sword. She looked at him for a long time with her dry unsentimental mercenary's face.

"You're trying to be soft," she said. "Then hard. You're going back and forth." She put the point of the sword down on the deck. "Neither of those is anything. A soft man gets hurt and a hard man gets broken. I've seen a thousand of both."

"Then what?"

"I don't know what you call it." She shrugged. "The ones who last. They're not soft and they're not hard. They're both at once. They give, and then they don't. Like a good pike shaft. Like a bow." She looked at him. "You're a smith. Don't you have a word?"

He did. He didn't say it. He stood on the deck of the *Widow* with his shoulder numb and thought about a bright blade by a fire, and the colours running.

"Not yet," he said. "I'll tell you when I've got it."

***

The Narrows came on the sixth day.

He knew them. They all did. They'd come up through them the spring before last, on the way to the lake for the first time, and it had been the worst day of that year. The river went into a gorge there, between two cliffs of grey rock with trees hanging off them, and narrowed to a third of its width, and ran fast and deep and loud between the walls. And in the first spring, on the east cliff, the turned had come down the rock face head first like spiders. And a man in a hat had stood on the top of the cliff and watched Rachael, and then dropped.

Rachael had cut his hand with Gabe's knife. He'd gone grey. He hadn't smiled.

Nobody said anything as the cliffs came up. Haskett took them in along the west side, where the water was a little slower. Calvin stood in the bow with his eyes shut, holding the wind steady in the sail against the current coming down the gorge like a wall. The boys leaned on their poles. Sabine stood up off her rope and put her sword on.

Gabe was at the stern. Rachael was beside him. Not near. Near enough. She was sitting on the stern rail with Haskett's younger boy beside her, the one with the hand he'd cut on a cleat the day before, and she had the boy's hand in both of hers and was looking at it, not mending, just looking, the way she did when she was deciding whether to.

Gabe looked up at the east cliff.

He didn't mean to. He'd been telling himself for a day not to; he'd been telling himself it was a long time ago, the spring before last, two years, and the man in the hat had gone west with the others over the downs and down the pass at Thornwall into the valley, Hyvred had seen him, and that was that. He looked up anyway. At the top of the east cliff, where the trees hung over. Where the man in the hat had stood.

He was standing there.

***

Gabe didn't move.

He didn't know how long he stood at the stern of the *Widow* looking up at the top of the east cliff. Not long. The barge was moving; Calvin's wind had it walking up the gorge against the current at a good pace, and the cliffs were going past on both sides. A count of five, maybe. Ten.

A man in a hat. At the top of the cliff, among the trees, standing at the edge with his hands at his sides. A plain brown coat. A wide-brimmed hat pulled down. Not moving. The way they didn't move, the ones Deston said had no thread; the way a coat on a peg doesn't move.

Looking down.

Not at the barge. Not at Gabe. At the stern rail, where Rachael was sitting with a boy's hand in both of hers, not looking up.

He didn't drop. He didn't do anything. He stood at the top of the cliff in the grey light and looked at her, and the barge went on up the gorge, and his head turned, very slowly, to follow her, the way the frost-letters had followed things in Niko's window, the way the faces in the rings at Thornwall turned to follow the town.

Gabe moved.

Not fast. He didn't want her to look up. He stepped across the stern, one step, two, quietly, the way you'd step across a room where somebody was asleep, until he was standing between the rail and the east cliff. Between her and the man at the top of it. In front.

"Gabe," said Rachael. Without looking up from the boy's hand. "You're in my light."

"Stay there a minute," said Gabe.

She looked up then. At him. At his face, which he knew was doing something, because he'd never once in his life been able to make his face not do something.

She didn't ask what. She looked at him a moment longer with her sharp face, and then she looked back down at the boy's hand, and stayed there.

Gabe stood in front of her and looked up at the man on the cliff.

The man looked at him.

He didn't think it had before. Not once, at the Narrows, the spring before last; it had looked at Rachael the whole time and never at anybody else, not even when Gabe had put his knife in her hand. It looked at him now. From the top of the cliff, under the hat, with nothing in it; he couldn't see a face; he couldn't see if there was one. But he felt it look. Like a draught on the back of your neck from a door you know is shut.

And then the bend took them, and the cliff went behind the trees, and the man in the hat was gone.

***

That was when the floe came.

He'd find out afterwards that it had been jammed at the top of the gorge, where the river came round a rock; a great raft of ice, three slabs ridden up one on another, stuck since dawn, with the whole weight of the river building up behind it. And that a little before the *Widow* came round the bend, it had come loose. All at once, the way a log jam comes loose. And come down the gorge on the current at the pace of a galloping horse.

Haskett saw it first. He shouted. Something Gabe didn't hear; the river was too loud in the gorge, between the cliffs. He saw Haskett throw his whole weight on the steering oar and the *Widow*'s nose come round, toward the west cliff, out of the middle, slowly, too slowly. He saw Calvin in the bow open his eyes and throw his hands out and the wind come round so hard the sail cracked like a whip. He saw the boys drop their poles and run.

And he saw the floe.

Coming down the middle of the gorge at them, grey and white and as long as the barge, three slabs ridden up on each other so that the top one stood out of the water higher than the deck. Turning. Slowly. As it came. Swinging its front end round on the current toward the *Widow*'s stern.

Toward the rail. Where Rachael was sitting with Haskett's boy's hand in hers.

Where Gabe was standing. In front.

There wasn't time to move her. He knew it the way he knew the river was loud. There wasn't time for anything except what he was already doing, which was standing there.

So he stood there.

***

He didn't let *Wall* come up hard.

He thought about it. Every bit of him wanted to. Every fight of his life, every slab of ice, every time Sabine had come at him with a sword in three and a half years: the aura coming up into every part of him, hard, a big man made of stone, a wall. *Quench it*. Hard as glass.

And walls broke. And files went *tick* in your hands. And a rib he could still feel.

He thought about the bright blade by Harl's fire. The colours running along it. Straw, and then bronze, and then blue.

He let it come up. *Wall*. All of it, into every part of him. And then, as it came, he didn't hold it there. He let it run. The way the colour runs along steel by the fire, from the hot end to the cold; he let it go through him and *not stop*, not set, not go hard. A hair less than stone. A hair. Hard enough to hold. Not so hard it couldn't give.

Tempered.

The floe hit him.

***

It hit him in the chest. The front end of the top slab, as it swung, a hand thick and a yard wide, grey, with the whole weight of three slabs and the river behind it. It hit him square, at the height of his chest, standing in front of the stern rail with his feet planted on the wet planks and his hands out; and he felt it go *in*.

A hair. That was all. He felt his chest give under it, the way a good blade gives when you strike it on the anvil to test it; a hair, the width of a thought. Not a wall. A spring. The whole weight of the floe going into him a hair, and stopping, and him not stopping, him going back the other way, the way a tempered blade comes back, *ringing*.

He felt himself ring. Like a bell. Like the star had rung under Declan's sledge at the lake, a note going out across the water. A low clear note, all through him, from his chest to his feet, and out through his feet into the planks, and the planks rang too.

And the floe broke.

Not him. The floe. The front end of the top slab, where it had hit him, split from side to side with a crack louder than the river, and the front yard of it fell off and went into the water beside the stern in a great grey splash that came over all of them; and the rest of the raft, swinging, hit nothing, because the piece that would have hit the rail was gone, and slid on past the stern a hand's breadth from the rudder, grinding, groaning, three slabs and a river, and went away down the gorge toward Kingsferry and the sea.

The *Widow* rocked. Hard. Then less. Then she was in the slack water under the west cliff with her sail full and Haskett on the oar swearing in a voice like a cracked bell, and she was fine.

Gabe was standing at the stern.

Wet through. With river water running out of his hair and down his face and the oilcloth with the hook in it soaked against his chest. His hands were still out. His feet were still where they'd been.

His chest didn't hurt.

He waited for it. He stood there with the water running off him and waited for the pain to come up out of the place where the ice had hit, the way it had come up two days ago, all at once, and sat him down. He looked down.

Rachael was sitting on the stern rail behind him. Exactly where she'd been. Soaked, with Haskett's boy beside her soaked too and his mouth open, and her hands still round the boy's hand. She hadn't moved. He'd told her to stay there, and she'd stayed there.

She was looking at him.

"Gabe," she said.

"Yeah."

"Are you hurt."

"No," said Gabe. And then, because he was straightforward and it was true and it was the strangest thing that had ever been true: "No. It didn't hurt."

She looked at him a long time with river water dripping off her nose.

"Can I?" she said.

"Yeah."

She let go of the boy's hand. She put her own flat on Gabe's chest, on the wet shirt, over the place where the ice had hit, over the oilcloth with the hook in it; and shut her eyes. He watched her not go cold. She didn't need to. She opened her eyes again almost at once.

"Nothing," she said. "There's nothing. Not a bruise." She took her hand away, and looked at it, as if it had told her something she didn't believe. "The ice broke. On you. And there's nothing."

"How?"

He thought about it. Standing at the stern of the *Widow* in the gorge, with the river going past and the cliffs going up on both sides, and the place on the east cliff where a man in a hat had been, behind the trees now, gone.

"I stopped being a wall," said Gabe.

Deston didn't say anything for a while.

"We tell her," he said. "Now. She'd kill us if we didn't."

So they told her. By the fire, with Sabine listening and Dennis listening and Calvin with the kettle on. Gabe told it straight, the way he told everything, because he didn't know another way: the cliff, the hat, the brown coat. Looking at her. Then at him. Then the bend.

Rachael listened with her arms folded and her face doing nothing at all.

"So that's why I had to stay there," she said, when he'd finished.

"Yeah."

"You could have said."

"You'd have looked up," said Gabe. "And it'd have seen you look."

Rachael was quiet a long time.

"Good," she said at last. "Now I know." She looked into the fire. "He's not in the valley, then. At Thornwall. With the others."

"No."

"He's here. On the river." She didn't unfold her arms. "Looking at me." She was quiet again. "Last time I cut his hand and he went grey. Like water stopping." She looked at her own hands, folded in her elbows. "*Mend* closes things. I closed something in him. I've thought about that for two years."

Nobody said anything.

"I'm not frightened," said Rachael. "I want that written down somewhere. I'm not." She sniffed. "I'm annoyed. He keeps turning up when I'm busy."

Dennis looked at her across the fire.

He didn't say anything. But Gabe saw him look, and knew what he'd heard, and knew that Dennis would never in his life tell her whether it had rung. And that it didn't matter, because Gabe was going to sit on her left on the barge for the rest of the way up the river, near enough that she knew he was there and not so near she had to do anything about it; and in front, if he had to be.

***

They came out of the Narrows on the eighth day, into the wide water below Wrenhaven, and the river went flat and slow and gold in the evening light, and the floes were small and few and white, like geese.

Gabe was in the bow. Not fending. There was nothing to fend. Just standing there, with the hook in its oilcloth against his chest, dried now, and his hand on it.

Four days. Five. Haskett said five; Calvin said four. Up past Wrenhaven, and on up the Wren into the hills, to the place where it came out of the lake.

He thought about the man at the end of it. On a jetty. Who'd read every line of the channel, the whole way up the river, tonight, *Tempered*, *Temper*, *a floe broke on him*, and not written anything, the way he never wrote anything. And who'd got a letter by the road, three weeks ago, in a big square hand, that said *we found the other half of the story* and *nothing's wrong*.

Deston had written *nothing's wrong*. Dennis had said it rang soft, a kind one, and Deston had left it in.

He stood in the bow and thought about what they were bringing up the river. The folds. The word. Aldemar's two in a cold forge, and the striker's hands, and a road east. A song about a nail. A hill. A boy with bleeding hands, dragging something west out of the black, cutting a line in the ground behind him all the way.

*Somebody from home made it*. He'd said that. At the anvil, with a bit of chalk, in the autumn. He'd been so pleased.

And somebody from home had pulled it out.

He put his hand flat on his chest, on the hook, on the word cut a stroke at a time into a bit of fallen star. *RESURGAM*.

He'd picked it off a gravestone. He'd thought it was a good word for a hook. A thing that goes down into the dark water and comes up again.

He hadn't thought, until the river, until a floe broke on his chest and he rang like a bell and didn't break, that it might be a word for a man.
