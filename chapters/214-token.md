The Warden of the Summoners' Hall had not said good morning to Edwy for a hundred and nine days, and on the hundred and tenth Hana decided to do something about it.

She hadn't counted. Edwy had. Edwy counted everything now; it was the archive's fault, Cursiffe said, and Oswin's, and the glass. You sat on a stool beside a man copying a grey leaf line by line for long enough, with a lens in your eye and your finger on the original and his finger on the copy, saying *yes* and *yes* and *wait* and *yes*, two by two, making a little red tick in the margin for every line that matched, and after a while you started making little red ticks in your head for everything else. Edwy ticked the stairs. He ticked his meals. He ticked, in a small book he kept in his sleeve and didn't think anyone knew about, every morning since the last week of the winter that he'd met the Warden in the corridor outside the Hall's archive door and said *good morning, Warden*, and every morning that the Warden had stopped, and looked at him, and not known what to do, and walked on.

A hundred and nine ticks. In a row. Hana had seen the page by accident, at supper, when it fell out of his sleeve into the soup.

"He doesn't hate me," Edwy said. He'd fished the book out and was blotting it with his sleeve, red to the ears. He was twenty and had a shaved head and a mother who did laundry in Coopers' Row, and in the autumn he'd come up through the old cellars by a door nobody had used in a hundred years and poured kitchen oil over forty grey leaves and chanted fire, weeping, *it's for you, it wasn't meant to be*. "He doesn't hate me. He just doesn't know which thing I am. The one who tried to burn them or the one who checks the lines. He's waiting to see which one wins."

"They're the same one," said Milli. Across the table. With his tail round the leg of his chair.

"I know," said Edwy. "That's what he doesn't know what to do about."

It was spring in Solmere, which meant rain.

It rained in Solmere the way it snowed in Wrenhaven, Thea had written once: all at once and for weeks and as if it had been personally insulted. It rained on the old square and the Hall across it, the tall grey building with no windows on the street side and a weathered open hand carved over the door. It rained on the castle and on the west tower, where the Advisor's lamp was lit at all hours, a hundred and four stairs up. It rained on the east wing, where Group 2 had lived since the first week, in four rooms at the end of a long corridor and a little kitchen the king had given them in the first winter, because, he'd said, people who cooked their own supper were people who meant to stay.

Jared cooked. Jared always cooked. He stood at the little stove with his back to the room and his sleeves rolled up and made whatever there was, and didn't talk while he did it, and the four rooms smelled of onions from noon till midnight. Aleaka sat in the window with Brisk on her arm, the grey hawk who'd chosen her in the first winter and since last summer had chosen, two days in three, to sit on the Advisor's arm instead, in the west tower, and come back at dusk smelling of lamp oil and looking pleased with himself. Cursiffe sat at the table with his ink and his left hand, teaching Edwy to make the letter R, because Edwy had asked, in the autumn, *will you teach me*, and Cursiffe's right hand had still been bandaged from the fire, so he'd taught him left-handed, and now the burns had healed to a pale shine across the knuckles and he still taught him left-handed, because, he said, it was the hand Edwy had learned on and you didn't change a man's hand halfway.

And Hana sat at the end of the table with her wrist on the wood and her cords on her wrist and listened to all of them.

Seven halves on a purple string. A blue one with a white bead, Maren's, at the lake. A brown knot with no bead, Aldous's, at Cinderhold. Green with a grey bead, Corvin's, at Thornwall. Emrys's at Kingsferry. Lucan's at Valcrest. Garrick's at Duskhollow. Oswin's, the closest, who was two floors down and a hundred yards along in the archive with his nose an inch from a grey leaf and had been since dawn. And beside them, on its own, a gold half-heart of wire and lacquer, the other half of which was in the inside pocket of a fisherman's coat, over his heart, with a stone.

It had gone warm one night, in the spring, at a quarter to eight, and stayed warm all night until a quarter past nine in the morning, and shaken once, very hard, at five past seven, when somebody had laughed out loud on the end of a jetty a week's ride north. It had been cool since. Cool the ordinary way, the way it always was, which meant he was all right and not thinking about her particularly, which was how she liked it.

She'd been the middle all her life. On Earth, in a Discord server with four hundred people in it, in a channel called *general* she'd kept alive for six years just by being in it every night. Here, with seven halves on her wrist, she was the middle of the wheel; Milli had said so, the night everyone made it. *It's not hanging off you any more. You're the middle of the wheel*. And the wheel had moved over, that night, when she sent on all seven at once for the first time since the summer she broke it, and nothing had broken.

She'd sent on all seven at once four times since. Every time, the binding moved over for it. Like somebody stepping aside on a pavement.

She hadn't gone up.

That was the thing. She'd been Spark since the winter of the second year, since the night on the end of a bed with a cut cord in her lap and *remember* written on it and the two halves going warm and knowing each other. Fifteenth of thirty-five. Hyvred had been sixth. Hyvred had gone Candle a fortnight ago on the top of a tower at Thornwall, holding a valley in her eye; Hana had felt Corvin's cord go warm with it, green and grey, like a lamp being turned up in the next room. And Hana had written *Hyv!!* in the channel and meant it with her whole heart, and then sat on the end of her bed for an hour with her wrist in her lap and not known what she felt.

She knew what she felt. She was methodical. She knew exactly what she felt; she just didn't like it.

Her bar was at ninety-four. It had been at ninety-four since the night of the bell.

***

The Warden came to the archive on the hundred and tenth morning.

He'd asked. Through the Advisor, properly, in writing, on Hall paper with the open hand in the corner, in the old formal script that took Oswin a quarter of an hour to read aloud: *The Warden of the Hall of Summoners begs the courtesy of the Advisor to the Crown to be permitted to observe, for one morning, the copying of the leaves, so that the Hall may know with its own eyes what is being done with them, and in what manner*. Halvard had read it in the west tower with Brisk on his arm and passed it to Hana without a word, and she'd read it, or rather heard Oswin read it, and understood it the way she understood most things, which was by hearing what it didn't say.

*So that the Hall may know*. Not *so that I may see*. He was asking for the Hall, because the Hall was a sentence three hundred and twelve years old, *a thing that cannot be read was not meant to be*, and he was its Warden, and he'd stood up in the Council in the winter in front of the king and the whole of the court and asked for the leaves to be burned. And lost. And then, a fortnight later, one of his own summoners had come up through the cellars with a lamp and a jar of oil to do it anyway, without asking him; and the Warden hadn't ordered it, and everybody knew he hadn't, and everybody knew it didn't matter.

He wanted to see that they were being careful.

He was frightened. That was what the letter didn't say. Hana had heard it in every line.

"Yes," said Halvard. "Tell him yes. Tomorrow. And tell Oswin to wear a clean shirt." He stroked Brisk's back with one finger. The hawk let him. "He won't."

Oswin didn't.

***

The archive was at the bottom of the castle, below the old kitchens, three rooms of stone with high small windows at the level of the yard outside, so that all day you saw boots going past in the rain at the top of the walls, and smelled wet stone and old paper and candle smoke and, faintly, from a box on a shelf in the corner that nobody went near, lamp oil. Oswin's long table was under the biggest window. Oswin was at it. He'd been at it, more or less, for a year and a half: since the second autumn, when the Advisor had given him the key, and then the vault, and then the box, and then the leaves.

He slept under the table. There was a blanket there, and a pillow, and a cat that wasn't anyone's. When the king was in Solmere, the king came down at midnight and looked under the table and if Oswin wasn't in it sent him to bed; since the thaw, with the king at Thornwall, the Advisor came down instead, and stood at the top of the stair and said *Oswin* in a particular voice, and Oswin went.

He was copying the sixteenth leaf.

Hana had watched him do it a hundred times and it still made the back of her neck go cold. The grey leaf on the left, in its tissue, in the shallow tray the king had made: thin as slate, heavy as stone, bending like paper if you let it, covered edge to edge on both sides in rings and lines and little ruler-marks in a hand that was older than the kingdom and wasn't writing, Thea said, the way music isn't writing. The oiled paper on the right. And between them, in Oswin's hand, the dividers the king had made for him out of Kharvos-folded steel at a forge on a lake, with Aldemar's open hand cut into the hinge and split down the middle along its crack, and the ruling pen, adjustable, with a screw. Oswin set the dividers on a line on the grey and turned them, and set them on the paper and turned them, and drew, and drew, and lifted his head.

"Yes?" said Oswin.

"Yes," said Edwy, at his elbow, on the stool, with the lens in his eye and his finger on the grey. A red tick.

"Yes?"

"Wait." A long pause. Edwy's breath held. "Yes." A red tick.

The Warden stood at the end of the table and watched them.

He'd come on time, which Hana had expected, and alone, which she hadn't; she'd thought he'd bring a summoner, or two, the way a lord brings witnesses. He'd come down the archive stair by himself, slowly, with one hand on the wall, a very old man in the grey robe of the Hall with the open hand embroidered on the breast, Beacon-tier, one of the strongest mages in the capital after the Advisor, who'd stood on the third ring of the circle at the Great Summoning, two years and a half ago, and felt thirty-five people come through it like hooks into his chest. He'd stood at the bottom of the stair and looked round the archive, at the boots going past in the rain at the top of the walls, at the cat, at the blanket under the table, at the box on the shelf in the corner with *KEEP* written on its lid in blue capitals in a language he couldn't read. He'd looked at the box for a long time.

Then he'd come and stood at the end of the table, and Edwy had looked up from the lens, and gone white, and then red, and said, "Good morning, Warden."

And the Warden had looked at him, and opened his mouth, and shut it again.

A hundred and ten.

***

He watched for three hours.

He didn't say anything. He stood at the end of the table, and then, when Hana brought him a chair, because she couldn't stand watching an old man stand for three hours, he sat on it, at the end of the table, with his hands folded in his lap, and watched Oswin copy a line, and Edwy check it, and Oswin copy the next.

Hana sat at the other end and watched him.

She could feel him. She'd always been able to feel people; it was the thing her window had found in her in the first week and turned into a skill, and the thing she'd been afraid of in herself since long before that. Not their thoughts. The shape of them. Where the doors were, and whether they were open, and which way the person behind them was leaning. On Earth she'd just called it being good with people. Here it was called *charm*, and it worked; it had worked in a council chamber in the first winter, when twelve lords had wanted to foster the summoned out to noble houses like spare horses, and she'd felt the whole room lean toward her and known that if she pushed, just a little, through all those open doors, they'd lean the rest of the way. She hadn't pushed. She'd made a rule that night, on the end of her bed, with her hands shaking: *charms, not people*. Things. Knots, beads, ribbon. Things you could give, that somebody could take or not take, and put down.

She'd kept the rule for two years and a half. She'd kept it in the Council again, in the winter, when the Warden asked for the leaves to be burned and the room had leaned toward him and she'd felt her hand go out toward the rail on its own, and Milli's hand had come down on her wrist, and she'd already stopped. She'd kept it with the Advisor, in the first autumn, when it would have been so easy. She'd kept it every day.

She could feel the Warden's doors now. All of them. He was very old and very tired and very frightened, and the frightened was so big it had pushed everything else to the edges. It wasn't for himself. That was the thing that made her throat go tight. He wasn't frightened of the leaves, or of the summoned, or of the king, or of being wrong. He was frightened *for* them. For the thirty-five. For a binding that was still running, holding all of them, that he'd stood on the third ring and felt take hold. For a hand older than the kingdom that had drawn it, and somebody in a cellar at the bottom of a castle reading that hand line by line, and getting one line wrong, and the whole thing letting go.

He'd felt it take them. He'd felt every one of them come through, like hooks. And every day since, for two years and a half, he'd felt it holding.

He thought that if he was wrong, and they read it, and it stopped, he'd feel that too.

She could have pushed. She could feel exactly where. A door, open a crack, at the bottom of all that fright, that said *I want to be told it's all right*. If she leaned on it, just a little, the way you'd lean on a door that was already swinging, he'd feel all right. He'd go back across the square in the rain and sit in the Hall with his hands folded and feel, for the first time in two years and a half, all right. And it wouldn't be his. It'd be hers, put into him, the way you'd put a coin into somebody's pocket while they weren't looking. And he'd never know.

Her hand had gone to the cords on her wrist. She didn't remember putting it there.

She took it away.

At noon Jared brought bread and cheese down the archive stair in a cloth, because Jared fed everyone, and set it on the end of the table by the Warden without a word, the way he set it in front of everyone, and went back up. The Warden looked at the bread for a long time, the way he'd looked at the box.

Oswin stopped. He did, at noon, for a quarter of an hour; the Advisor had made him promise. He put the dividers down very carefully on their felt, and sat back, and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and noticed the Warden as if for the first time.

"Warden," said Oswin. Surprised. "You're still here."

"I am still here," said the Warden.

"Do you want to look?" said Oswin. "At the copy. Against it." He pushed the oiled paper an inch toward the old man. "You can. You can't read it. Nobody can, not even Thea yet. But you can see it's the same."

The Warden looked at the paper. Then at the grey leaf, in its tissue, in the tray. Then at Edwy, on the stool, with the lens pushed up on his shaved head and his red pencil in his fingers and his face doing its best not to do anything at all.

"How many have you checked?" said the Warden. To Edwy. The first thing he'd said to him in a hundred and ten mornings that wasn't nothing.

Edwy swallowed.

"Every line on every leaf since the fire," said Edwy. "Both sides. Twice. Fourteen leaves and most of the sixteenth. I don't know how many lines." He looked at his book. The other one, the red-ticked one, on the table. "Eleven thousand, four hundred and six. I counted. And none of them were wrong." He swallowed again. "Two were. In the winter. On the ninth. I found them, and Master Oswin did them again, and then they weren't."

The Warden didn't say anything.

"I'm not reading it," said Edwy. Fast, all at once, the way it had come out of him in the autumn with the lamp. "None of us are. I know that's what you're afraid of. That's what I was afraid of. That's why I—" He stopped. "We're not reading it. We're copying it. A copy can't touch the binding. It's paper. If I get a line wrong on paper the binding doesn't know. I know that now. I didn't know it then. I thought—" He stopped again. "I thought because I couldn't read it, nobody should. That's what the sentence says. I thought it was true."

"It is the Hall's sentence," said the Warden.

"I know," said Edwy. "I used to say it every morning."

The Warden looked at him for a long time.

Hana felt it. The door, at the bottom of everything, open a crack. *I want to be told it's all right*. Leaning, so hard she could almost hear the hinge.

She put her hand on the cords.

And she didn't push. She knew she wouldn't. She'd known for two years and a half. She sat there at the end of the table with her hand on her wrist and the whole of her wanting to, because he was old and frightened and she could make it stop, and she didn't, and she felt something come up in her that wasn't the charm and wasn't the rule, from somewhere under both of them.

She wasn't frightened.

That was what it was. She looked at it, sitting there, the way Thea said she looked at a line of code when it did something she hadn't told it to. She wasn't frightened. Not of the leaves. Not of the reading. Not of the hand older than the kingdom. Not of the binding letting go. She'd been the middle of the wheel for a year and a half, with seven halves on her wrist and thirty-four people on the other end of a channel in her head every night, and she'd felt the binding move over for her four times like somebody stepping aside on a pavement, and she knew it. She knew what it felt like. It didn't feel like something that was going to let go. It felt like something that had been holding on to them for two years and a half on purpose, carefully, the way you'd carry somebody else's child across a ford.

She wasn't frightened at all.

And he was so frightened he couldn't say good morning to a boy.

She could give him that. Not push it. Give it. A feeling, the way you'd give somebody a thing. Not hers put into him. Hers, held out, so he could hold it if he wanted, and feel what it felt like to sit in this archive and not be afraid, and know it wasn't his, and put it down whenever he liked.

That wasn't a person. That was a charm.

She made it at the end of the table, while they all watched.

She hadn't brought anything. She never needed to; there was always something. A length of the plain linen thread Oswin used to tie the tissue round the leaves, off the spool by the tray. A bead off the cuff of her own sleeve, a pink one, that had been sewn there since the first winter by a seamstress in the Lantern Quarter who'd called her *princess* and meant it. She cut the thread with the little knife Jared had given her in the second winter, the one she'd cut every cord with since, and tied the bead into the middle of it with the knot Ama had shown her in the kitchens, the one that didn't slip. She didn't write anything on it. Cursiffe had written on all her cords, in his ink, *remember*, *keep*, *carry*, the one script in this world the summoned could write that natives couldn't read; the cords carried words, that was what they were for. This didn't need a word. This was the other thing.

She held it in her hand and put into it what she felt.

Not the rule. Not the charm, the people one; she kept her other hand flat on the table the whole time, where she could see it. Just what she felt. Sitting in this archive with the rain going past the window at the top of the wall and Oswin's dividers on their felt and Edwy's eleven thousand red ticks and a box on the shelf with *KEEP* on the lid. Not afraid. Not *it's all right*; she didn't know if it was all right. She wasn't going to tell him something she didn't know. Just: *I'm not frightened of this. I've felt it hold. It holds on purpose*.

She felt it go into the bead the way the meaning went into a cord when Cursiffe wrote on it. Not a word. A warmth. The way the half-heart went warm, at the other end, when somebody was holding it.

She looked at the bead in her palm. Pink, on a bit of linen thread. Nothing. Something you'd find in a drawer.

She got up, and walked down the length of Oswin's table, past Oswin and past Edwy, and stood in front of the Warden of the Hall of Summoners, and held it out.

"It isn't a charm on you," said Hana. "I want you to know that first. I don't do that. I've never done it to anyone, not once, and I'm not going to start with you." She kept her hand out, open, with the thread lying across her palm. "It's a thing. It's got what I feel in it. About this. About them." She nodded at the leaves. "You can hold it, if you want, and feel it. It'll be mine, not yours. You'll know it's mine. And you can put it down whenever you want, and it'll stop."

The Warden looked at the bead.

"What do you feel?" he said. Very quietly. "About them?"

"I'm not afraid," said Hana. "I've felt the binding. Every night. It moves over for me. I don't think it's going to let go." She was quiet. "I might be wrong. I'm not telling you I'm right. I'm telling you I'm not afraid. You've been afraid for us for two years and a half. You can put it down for a minute, if you like. And pick it up again after. It's yours to pick up."

The Warden didn't move for a long time.

Then he put out his hand. Old, spotted, with a Beacon's ring on the forefinger, shaking very slightly the way old men's hands do. And took the thread off her palm, by one end, the way you'd take something you weren't sure was safe, and let the bead lie in his own.

Hana watched his face.

She watched him feel it. She knew exactly what it was like; it was hers. The archive. The rain. The leaves in their tissue and the copies beside them, line for line. Not frightened. The warmth of something holding on to you carefully on purpose. She watched it go into him, not through any door, not pushed, just sitting there in his palm the way warmth sits in your hand from a cup, so that he could feel it and know it was a cup.

His face didn't do anything for a long moment.

Then it crumpled. Just for a breath. The way an old man's face does when he's been holding something for two years and a half and somebody shows him what it would be like to put it down.

He closed his hand round the bead.

He didn't put it down.

She looked at her window on the archive stair, on the way up, with her back against the cold wall and her tail round her ankles and nobody watching except a boot going past the window at the top of the stair in the rain.

*Token*.

She said it out loud. Quietly, so the boot wouldn't hear.

On Earth a token had been a lot of things. A coin for a machine. A thing you got for logging in every day. A thing you gave somebody to say you'd been somewhere, a keepsake, a ticket stub. *A token of my esteem*, people said, in old books, which meant *a thing that isn't the thing, but stands for it*. She'd made tokens for her stream, once, little badges, for people who'd been there a year, and Ice had got one, and put it on his profile, and never taken it off. She hadn't thought about that in two years and a half.

The window had found it. The way they always found the words. *Keepsake* for the cords that carried words; *Token* for the thing that carried what you felt, and stood for it, and wasn't it, and could be put down.

Oswin finished the sixteenth leaf at dusk, and packed the seven.

Not the sixteenth. Leaves eight to fourteen. The rest of the first part, the part with the open ring and the tick in the corner on his sheet: the list, Thea called it, with nothing to call it but that. Thea had the first seven at the lake. She'd chalked them on the wall of the militia hall in Wrenhaven, Maren wrote, frame after frame, the same ring over and over with different things in it, and stood in front of the wall every night refusing to guess. These were the rest. After fourteen the corner mark changed, and whatever the second part was, it was a different kind of thing, and Oswin was only two leaves into it.

He packed them in the oak case the king had made to hold six, in felt and tissue, the copies only; the leaves themselves didn't leave the castle, and wouldn't, ever, he said, not while he had a key. Six fitted. The seventh he wrapped in his own oilcloth, and tied with a twist of wire, and laid on top. And a note, in his hand, which Cursiffe read out to the table in English while Oswin stood there pink: *Eight to fourteen. Both sides. Edwy checked every line twice. That is the end of the first part. I do not know what it is a list of. I have stopped guessing too. O.* And under it, in a different hand, small and careful, left-handed, the letters a little uneven, the way your handwriting goes when you learned it late and the hand you learned it in wasn't the one you were born with:

*Every line. Twice. E.*

"He's never signed one," said Oswin. Pinker. "He didn't think he was allowed."

"Is he?" said Hana.

"I've no idea," said Oswin. "I didn't ask anyone. I just said *sign it*."

Kit took them at dawn. Kit, solemn, nineteen, in the king's grey, who'd ridden the first case north in the winter and come back with a story about a red hen that had looked him in the eye; he put the case in his saddle-bag and the seventh leaf in his coat, against his chest, and stood in the yard in the rain with his horse's reins in his hand and looked at Hana.

"Five days," said Kit. "Six if the fords are up."

"Tell her to take her time," said Hana. "Tell her there's no hurry. Tell her Oswin's stopped guessing too."

"I'll tell her," said Kit. And then, after a moment, because he was nineteen and solemn and had been told something in the kitchen at midnight that he hadn't known what to do with: "And the Warden, miss. The cook at the Hall told the cook here, and the cook here told the guard, and the guard told me. He's been sitting in the Hall since noon yesterday. In his chair. With his hand shut." Kit swallowed. "He hasn't put it down."

Hana didn't say anything.

Kit got on his horse, and went out of the yard in the rain, north, with seven grey frames in his saddle-bag and on his chest.

She stood there after he'd gone. On the cobbles, in the rain, with her tail getting wet and her cords on her wrist, seven halves on a purple string and a gold half-heart. She put her other hand over them. All of them at once.

They were warm. All seven. The ordinary way. Seven people at the far ends of the kingdom, holding the other halves, not thinking about her particularly. Maren at the lake. Aldous on the red. Corvin on a tower. Emrys at Kingsferry, humming. Lucan in Valcrest, probably crying about something. Garrick at Duskhollow with a lantern. Oswin two floors down, already back under the table with the seventeenth leaf, in a clean shirt, because the Advisor had sent one down at midnight and stood at the top of the stair until he put it on.

And the heart. Cool. The ordinary way. A fisherman on a jetty a week's ride north, all right, not thinking about her particularly.

She stood in the middle of all of it, with her hand over her wrist in the rain, and wasn't frightened of any of it.

Then she went in, because Jared had made onions.
