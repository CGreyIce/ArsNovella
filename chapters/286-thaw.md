The millstream woke up on a Tuesday.

Tiger heard it from the well. He was at the well in the guardhouse yard in the grey before dawn, the way he was every morning, with his hand on the cold stone rim and his feet ready and his breath smoking; and under the usual morning sounds, the gulls and the dripping eaves and somebody's shutter going back, there was a new one. Low. A long way off, outside the walls, up the valley. Like somebody talking in the next room with their mouth full.

He knew it. He'd sat on it all autumn. On the parapet of the little stone bridge at the first milestone, a quarter mile out from the north gate, with the black water going under him talking to itself in the dark and Drew beside him with his ribs strapped.

It had been ice since the first snow. Nearly three months. He'd stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing a clock.

He stood at the well with his hand on the rim and listened to it, and something in his chest did a thing it hadn't done since the autumn.

Then he ran.

***

He ran the race the way he'd run it every morning for more than two years. Well to gate. Down the guardhouse lane, round the corner by the Bell, where the new slates were going on the old wing in a stack by the yard door; up the long street with the lamps going out one at a time in the windows as people woke and didn't need them; past the farrier's, where Joss was in the door with a broom he wasn't using; into the north arch, and the dark of it, and the old black oak post on the inside of it, just before the gate doors.

Flat hand on the wood. Or it doesn't count.

That was Ossie's rule. Ossie had made it up the first week, when Tiger kept beating him to the gate by a body's length and slapping the stone of the arch, which Ossie said was cheating because stone was nearer. *Flat hand on the wood*, he'd said, red in the face, with his freckles standing out and his breath gone. *On the post. Or it doesn't count*. And then lost anyway, every morning, for a month. And then hadn't come back from the passes with Sergeant Dunmore's ten.

Tiger put his hand flat on the post.

It was wet. That was new. All winter it had been frozen, so cold that the first morning after the snow his palm had stuck to it and he'd had to peel it off, and Drew had said *you're an idiot* and wrapped it. Now it was wet and dark and soft at the surface, the way old oak goes in a thaw, and there were two marks on it, at shoulder height, side by side. His own: a dark patch of wood worn smooth by two years of hands. And beside it, a hand's width off, a second, newer, fainter; where a man with a fishing rod had put his hand flat on the post, once, at first light, in the first week of the winter, and said nothing, and gone home.

Tiger looked at the two marks.

"Tomorrow," he said.

***

Hyvred said Ossie turned.

She always said it, every morning, from the tower, on the channel, so that Tiger would know: *he turned*. A heartbeat after the hand hit the wood. A hair. In the third ring, on the north side, eleven miles up the valley in the snow, a boy with freckles and his hands open at his sides, standing the way they all stood, with his face turned toward the town a little further than any of the others. Toward the post.

He'd been turned all winter. Not moving. Just turned. The snow had come down on the rings and lain on their shoulders and Ossie's face had stayed turned toward the gate under it, the only one, like a sunflower that hadn't been told the sun had gone.

*You can see their coats again.*

And this was the part he'd added. Since the autumn. Since the night on the bridge, and the night at the gate, with Drew's hands on his chest. He'd run *there* every morning for two years; to the gate, to the post, and stopped, and walked back, because there wasn't any point running back; there wasn't anybody to race to the well.

Since the autumn he ran back.

Not as fast. That wasn't the point. He turned round in the arch with his palm wet from the post and ran back the way he'd come, down the long street, past the farrier's, past Joss and his broom, round the corner by the Bell, down the guardhouse lane, to the well. And put his hand on the rim. Stone. Stone was fine for the well; it wasn't Ossie's rule, it was his.

*There and back*, the king had said, in the autumn, on the gatehouse. *And if it takes your hand, you let go.*

*There and back*, Drew had said, on his knees in the frozen field with his bladed hands on Tiger's chest. *You've done there. Now you have to do back. That's the half nobody ever does.*

So he did back. Every morning. On his own, with nobody watching but Joss with his broom and the gulls on the guardhouse roof. It was the stupidest thing he'd ever done, he'd thought, the first week; running back to a well. And then on the ninth morning his bar had moved, and he'd stopped thinking it was stupid.

He stood at the well with his hand on the rim and got his breath.

His window was open in front of him before he'd meant to open it.

Eighty-six. It had been eighty-three in the autumn, at the gate, after he'd let go of Ossie's hand. It had gone to eighty-four on the ninth morning of running back. Eighty-five at midwinter. Eighty-six last week.

***

The thaw took nine days to get properly going, and then it went all at once.

Thornwall had never been a dry town; it sat in a fold of the Greyspine with mountains on three sides, and everything that melted on all three sides came down through it. The eaves dripped all day. The long street turned to brown slush and then brown water, ankle-deep at the bottom by the square, and somebody laid planks across it from the Bell to the guardhouse, and Bee drew a dog on every plank in charcoal so that you could tell which ones you'd walked on. The snow went off the roofs in slabs that came down into the lanes at night like somebody dropping sacks. The schoolroom flooded. The children were delighted.

And the Bell got its roof back.

The whole Watch did it, in the end. Brannoch put twenty men on it, and Agnes made the nails, and Old Pruett, who'd been a slater before he was a postern-keeper, sat on a barrel in the Bell's little yard with his bad leg out in front of him and shouted at everybody. It rained on the third day; hard, a cold spring rain off the mountains, the kind that went sideways. And Azac went up the ladder with his shield on his back and stood on the ridge in the middle of the half-done roof, with the twenty men on the battens round him, and opened his hand, and the *Roof* came up over all of them. Pale blue, round, humming. The rain came down on the top of it and slid off, down the round sides, and went into the gutters, and the twenty men slated the old wing of the Bell dry, under a roof that was holding up the roof.

"And the rain's coming in off the north," said Azac, on the ridge, to the twenty men. "And it's heavy, it's very heavy, and Pruett's shouting, as he does, and Rhys has dropped a slate, and that's fine, we've got a lot of slates—"

"I did *not* drop a slate," said Rhys Hywel, from the battens.

"Rhys has dropped a slate," said Azac, "and is denying it, folks."

Tiger watched it from the yard door with Olwen of Coldwater, who'd come out of the Bell to see, with her shawl over her head. She didn't say anything. She stood with her arms folded and watched the blue dome over the men on the roof, and when it was done, at dusk, and the last slate was on and Azac let it down and came down the ladder grey in the face and grinning, she went across the yard and put her hand flat on his chest, once, and went back inside.

***

The swept road came out from under the snow on the sixth day.

Tiger saw it from the north wall. Everybody did; people came up on to the wall to look, the way they'd come up to look at the rings in the autumn, and stood along the parapet in the wet with their hands in their armpits and didn't say anything. The road out of the north gate and up the valley: the one that had been swept clean all the last year and the year before, by nobody, by the feet of people walking up it in the night. It had gone under four feet of snow on the last dawn of the autumn and stayed there all winter, and now it was coming out again, the way a stone comes out of a stream when the water goes down.

Brown. Clean. A swept line, going north up the valley through the white, as far as anybody could see.

Nobody had walked on it all winter. Nobody could have. And it was still swept.

"That's not right," said somebody on the wall. Quietly.

"No," said Hild.

She was at the north gate, where she always was, on the walk over the arch with her one eye on the valley and her hands flat on the stone. She didn't look round.

"The mountains are waking up," she said. "Not all the way. Like a dog that's heard its name in its sleep." She took her hands off the stone. "The pass is still shut. It'll be shut a month. The snow up there's twelve feet, Fenwick says." She looked north at it. At the white notch at the top of the valley, where the switchbacks went up under the snow to the gap in the Greyspine and over. "A month."

Tiger looked at the swept road going up the valley toward it.

At the top, at the foot of the pass, the round place where the snow had never settled all winter, facing the town. He couldn't see it. Hyvred could. She'd said it was still there. Still facing them.

And in front of it, the rings, coming out of the snow. Coats again. And in the third ring, on the north side, a boy with freckles, turned.

***

He went to the bridge with Drew on the seventh day.

He'd asked. That was the thing he'd learned, this winter, maybe more than anything: to ask. He'd never asked anybody anything in his life before Thornwall; he'd just gone. Gone up on the shed roof, gone over the north wall in the dark, gone up the pass road alone at full *Bolt*. And every time, somebody had been standing in the way with their hands out. Drew, on the bridge. Drew, in the field. The king, on the gatehouse, saying *there and back, son*.

So he asked. At supper, in the long room, with his bowl in his lap.

"I want to go to the bridge," he said. "The milestone. Tomorrow. Before the race." He looked at Drew. "And come back. I'm not going past it. I just want to hear it."

Drew looked at him across the long room for a long time.

Drew was better. He'd been better since midwinter; his ribs had knitted, all three, the third one last, and he walked without holding his side now and laughed without stopping halfway. But he still sat on Ossie's roof most nights, the roof of the shed that had stood two years now, the roof Ossie's father had put on it, and still came down off it in the grey with frost on his coat. And he still looked at Tiger, sometimes, across a room, the way you'd look at a door you weren't sure was shut.

"Okay," said Drew.

"Okay?"

"I'll come," said Drew. "I'll stand on the bridge. You go to the bridge and you come back to me. Not to the post. To me." He picked up his spoon. "I'm the one who stays."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Drew. "I'm saying it anyway. It's my thing. I say where everybody's going." He ate a spoonful. "Azac's got his own now. I'm keeping mine."

***

They went out at the first grey. Through the postern beside the north gate, which Old Pruett unbarred for them with his face saying everything he wasn't saying, and on to the swept road.

It felt wrong under his feet. Tiger had known it would. Clean, and hard, and swept, in the middle of a valley of slush and brown snow, as if somebody had walked it last night; and nobody had. He didn't run. Drew walked beside him, a little behind, on his left, the side Tiger always forgot. A quarter of a mile. The milestone, grey and leaning, with the snow gone off its top. And the bridge.

The millstream was running.

Loud. Brown and white, high under the little stone arch, so high it was nearly at the top of it, coming down off the Greyspine full of the whole winter at once and going under the bridge with a sound like a crowd, and away down the valley toward the mill and the town. The parapet was wet with its spray. The stones of the bridge were shaking, very slightly, under his hand.

Tiger stood in the middle of the bridge and listened to it.

He didn't know why he'd wanted to. He'd lain awake thinking about it since the Tuesday, and he still didn't know. It was just a stream. It had been ice all winter and now it was water, the way it was every spring. Ossie would have heard it every spring of his life; he'd been born two valleys over, in Harrowford, where his dad was a carpenter. He'd have come up here as a boy, probably, with the other garrison boys, and thrown sticks off the bridge and raced them.

*Race you*, Tiger thought. To nobody. To the stream.

Drew had stopped at the near end of the bridge. On the town side. He stood with his hands loose at his sides and his feet planted and his face doing nothing at all, and waited.

Tiger looked north. Up the swept road. Eleven miles, up the valley, to the rings. He couldn't see them. He knew where they were.

He thought about going. He did. He wasn't going to lie to himself about that; he'd stopped lying to himself in the autumn, in the field, with Ossie's hand going cold in his. He stood on the bridge with the millstream roaring under him and the swept road going north, and the *Bolt* came up in his legs on its own, the way it always did, a held breath, a spring wound tight; and he thought: *I could be there by the time the sun's up*.

He thought about Drew's hands on his chest. *Back. That's the half nobody ever does.*

He turned round.

And walked back. Across the bridge. To Drew, at the town end, with his feet planted, who didn't move as Tiger came, didn't put his hands out, didn't do anything at all except stand there and be the one who stayed. And Tiger stopped in front of him.

"I came back," said Tiger.

"Yeah," said Drew. "You did."

They stood there a moment on the end of the bridge with the water going under.

"It moves for back," said Tiger. "My bar. Did you know that? Only back. Never for there."

"No," said Drew. "But I'm not surprised."

"Why?"

Drew thought about it. He looked past Tiger at the bridge, and the water, and the swept road going north up the valley.

"Because anybody can go," he said. "Going's easy. You just have to be brave. Coming back's the hard bit. You have to be brave, and then you have to want to be somewhere more than you want to be brave." He looked at Tiger. "That's what it's measuring, I think. Not your legs."

Tiger didn't say anything.

"Race you to the gate," said Drew.

"You'll lose."

"I know," said Drew. "I always lose. I'm the one who stays." He started walking. "Flat hand on the wood, or it doesn't count."

***

He ran the race that morning from the bridge.

Not from the well. From the milestone, a quarter mile out; down the swept road to the north gate, through the postern, into the arch, with Drew walking behind him a long way back, the way he'd said. Flat hand on the oak post. Wet. Two marks.

"Tomorrow," said Tiger.

And then back. Not to the well this time. Out through the postern again, on to the swept road, a quarter mile back up to the bridge, where Drew had got to by then, walking, and was standing in the middle of it with his hands in his pockets. That night he sat on the shed roof with Drew.

Ossie's shed. The third one, the one that had stood two years, with the roof his father had come all the way from Harrowford to put on it. The roof was wet and the moss on it was coming green again under the old snow, and from up there you could see over the north wall, up the valley, a long way, in the starlight. The swept road, a pale line going north through the brown and white. The bridge, and the stream, which you could hear from here if you listened. And past all of it, very far, the dark at the top of the valley where the pass was.

Drew lay on his back with his hands behind his head. Tiger sat with his knees up and his tail round his ankles.

They lay there.
