They came over the pass in the middle of the night, all four at once, and Hyvred was the only person in Thornwall awake to see it.

She hadn't meant to be. It wasn't her night. Since the winter they'd had a rota for the tower, Drew's rota, chalked on the inside of the gatehouse door in Drew's square hand with Bee's dog drawn beside every name, and tonight the name with the dog beside it was Rui Xiang's, who couldn't see anything in the dark but could sit very still at the top of a tower for nine hours without anybody knowing he was there, which counted. Hyvred was meant to be asleep. She'd been asleep, on her cot in the long room, in Bee's coat, with her glasses folded on the floor beside her where she could find them.

She woke at the second hour because her left eye was hot.

Not sore. Hot, the way it went when the red mark in it locked onto something a long way off before she'd decided to look. She lay on her back in the dark of the long room and felt it lock, and felt it pull, north, through the wall, through the town and the mill and the eleven miles of swept road and the valley and the rings, up, to the notch.

She got up. She didn't put her glasses on; she never needed them. She'd kept them anyway, for four years, because they'd been on her face for most of her life before that and her face didn't know what to do without them. She put them in her pocket and went out into the yard in Bee's coat and her stockinged feet, and up the hundred and twelve steps of the tower in the dark, quickly, with her hand on the wall.

Rui Xiang was at the top. She knew he was there because she knew he'd be there, and because her eye went past the place where he was sitting and didn't stop, the way it always did with him, the way you'd look past a chair.

"It's me," she said.

"I know," said the place. "Your feet are very loud. What is it?"

"The notch."

She put her hands on the parapet and looked.

***

Eleven miles to the valley. Another nine up the switchbacks to the top of the pass. Twenty miles in the dark, in a country without a single lit window in it, and in the winter when she'd first come up here with *Range* new in her, she couldn't have seen a horse at the top of the pass at noon.

She could see them now.

Four. At the top of the notch, where the road came over out of the north from the far side, from the country nobody had crossed in a century. Four lights.

Not lights. She'd never had a good word for it and she'd stopped trying. Everything that lived had a light to her: not the candle Yan saw, the soul, the thing inside, that was Yan's and she'd never once seen it. Hers was the other thing. The light that came *off* people. The edge of them against the world. The way a person sat in the air. She could see it on a man at eleven miles now, the shape of him, held, the way you'd see a lamp in a window across a valley; and she could see when it wasn't there.

That was what these were. Four places at the top of the pass where the edge was missing. Four shapes in the dark that the night went *round*, the way water goes round a stone, because there was nothing in them for the dark to touch.

She'd watched all twenty-seven of them come down that road. One at a time, mostly, days or weeks apart. Twenty-seven holes walking down nine miles of switchbacks into the valley and along the floor of it to the rings, and standing at the end of the curve, in a line, a pace apart, facing in.

Four at once was new.

They came down the switchbacks together. She watched them all the way. A pace apart, the way the others always were, walking in step though nobody had told them to, down the long zigzags of the pass road in the dark. It took them an hour. She didn't look away once. Her eye was so hot by the end that she had tears running down that side of her face and didn't wipe them, and Rui Xiang, who couldn't see what she was seeing, sat beside her the whole hour and didn't say anything, which was the thing about Rui Xiang that nobody ever believed until it happened to them.

They came off the bottom of the pass onto the valley floor. They walked the three miles to the rings. Along the outside of the thirteenth, the outermost, where the townspeople stood who'd been called first and longest. To the end of the curve.

And stopped. And turned, and faced in.

Thirty-one.

"Four," said Hyvred. Her voice came out rough. "All four. That's all of them. That's the last of the eight."

"The last of the eight," said Rui Xiang.

"Thirty-one in the curve." She took her hands off the parapet. They'd gone stiff. "That's the lot. Hisui said there were eight still coming, in the autumn. The last four. There's nobody left on the far side."

"That we know of."

"That we know of," said Hyvred.

She stood there, and looked at the curve. Thirty-one holes in a line, round the outside of the rings, in the dark, eleven miles off. Last spring it had been a scratch, a few marks at the end of the thirteenth ring like the start of a letter. Corvin had measured it every week since, on the big survey sheet in the long room: how far round it went, how much of the circle it would make. It went a long way round now.

She didn't know what happened when it went all the way round.

Nobody did.

***

Kei came up at the third hour, because Hisui had woken him.

He came up the stair slowly, with the green snake round his shoulders and his tongue going, and stood at the parapet a long time without saying anything. Then he said, "Hisui tasted them come over. He says they tasted like the others. Nothing. Like a cellar." He was quiet. "He says the air in the valley's different tonight. Fuller."

"Fuller how?"

Kei listened to something she couldn't hear.

"Like a room with everybody in it," he said. "When you've been waiting for the last person and they've come." He stroked the snake's head. "He doesn't like it."

"No," said Hyvred.

They stood there till the first grey, the three of them, Kei and the snake and the place where Rui Xiang was. Below them the town woke up a bit at a time, the way Thornwall did: the mill wheel first, then the bakery, Annet's chimney going, then the Watch changing on the wall and somebody shouting for Old Pruett. Drew came out of the long room with his splinted hand held against his chest, and looked up at the tower, and saw her, and lifted the other hand.

And at the first grey, on the swept road, eleven miles off, Hyvred saw the third ring come out of the valley.

***

She'd known they would.

She didn't know how. Nobody knew how. Every few days, since the morning Tiger turned, the third ring came down: forty of them, in the clothes they'd been called in, walking down the swept road in the dark to stand a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, at the end of Tiger's race. And every time, it was a morning Tiger was going to run, though Tiger ran every morning.

But she'd known it this morning before the third ring moved. She'd felt it in the valley, in the *fullness* Hisui didn't like, like a held breath. Something in there had been waiting for the last four to come in. And now they had.

The third ring came down the swept road in the grey, walking. Forty of them. Ossie at the front.

Hyvred watched them come the whole eleven miles. By the time they were halfway the town was on the wall: the Watch, and Group 6, and Petra in the square with the children who always came, and Bee on the gatehouse roof drawing dogs. Corvin came up the tower with his survey sheet under his arm and looked at the curve and wrote *31* on it in his small precise hand, and underlined it, and didn't say anything. John came up after him and sat on the top step with his fingers moving and put the picture up into thirty-four windows across the kingdom, so that the other thirty sat up in their beds in Solmere and Valcrest and Cinderhold and a lake in the north and saw what she saw: forty people walking down a road in the grey toward a bridge.

Drew went down to the postern.

She watched him walk out to the bridge, a quarter of a mile at a walk, with his splint against his chest. She watched him go to the middle and turn round with his back to them and his hands down. *Post*. She'd leaned on him on the gate step eleven nights ago and he'd been as hard as a wall, and he'd stood on that bridge every time since, and the third ring had come and stood a pace off the far end and looked, she'd swear it, at *him*. Not at the gate. Not at the town. At the post in the middle of the bridge.

The third ring came to the far end and stopped. A pace off the last stone.

Ossie at the front, with his hands open at his sides.

And Hyvred, for the first time since the thaw, went down the tower.

***

"No," said Hild.

She was at the foot of the tower stair. She'd known. Of course she'd known; Hild had known what Hyvred was going to do before Hyvred did since the first week, since a girl with a red mark in her eye had come up this stair for the first time and stood at the top of it and said *oh* at the size of the world. Hild stood in the door with her big quiet hands and filled it.

"I need to see his face," said Hyvred.

"You can see his face from up there. You can see a sparrow's face from up there."

"Not close." Hyvred held her ground. "I've seen him from eleven miles. I've seen him from the wall. I've never been close. Not a pace. Not since the first spring." She put her hand up to her left eye without meaning to. "It doesn't work the same from a long way off. From a long way off I see what's wearing him. I see the thing. I've always seen the thing." She swallowed. "I want to see *him*."

Hild looked at her.

"There's a difference," said Hyvred. "Between seeing a mask from across a room and seeing it from a hand away. From across a room you see the mask. From a hand away you see where it stops."

Hild was quiet for a long time.

"Drew's on the bridge," she said at last.

"I know."

"You stand behind him. Not beside him. Behind." Hild stepped out of the door. "And you look, and you come back. You don't touch anything. You don't say anything to anybody. You look, and you come back to me." She looked at Hyvred's face. "I mean it, girl."

"I know you do," said Hyvred, and went.

***

The swept road was cold under her feet. She'd forgotten her boots. She didn't go back for them.

She'd never walked it. She'd looked down it from the tower every day for a year, and watched Tiger run it, and watched the turned come down it and go back up, and she'd never once set foot on it, because Hild said no and because, if she was honest, she'd been afraid. It was wrong under your feet. Everybody said so. Swept clean, every stone, by something that had walked it in the night, in a town where nobody swept the roads.

She walked it. A quarter of a mile. Not fast. Drew walked it slowly, so she did.

The bridge came up out of the grey with the millstream roaring white under it, and Drew in the middle with his back to the far end, and his face to her; and when he saw who it was coming his face did a thing she'd never seen Drew's face do, which was not know what to say.

"Hild said behind you," said Hyvred.

"Then behind me," said Drew. He didn't turn round. He didn't move at all. "I'll stand here."

She walked onto the bridge. Thirty paces. The spray came up cold onto her face, and her glasses in her pocket knocked against her hip. She came to Drew in the middle, and stepped round him, carefully, and stood behind him. Between him and the far end. A pace from his back.

And a pace from the far end of the bridge, beyond the last stone, the third ring.

***

She'd seen faces wearing faces since the first spring. Since a night on the north gate with Bee's chalk dog barking, when she'd looked down at a boy in a watch coat smiling up at the gate and seen, under the smile, *something else*. Patient. Interested. Wearing him. She'd seen it on every one of the forty since, from the tower, from the wall, from eleven miles: forty people with something standing up inside them like a hand inside a glove.

That was what she'd always seen. The hand. Because from a long way off, the hand was all there was to see. It filled them. You looked at Agnes the miller's widow from the tower and you saw a thing wearing a woman, and the woman was just the shape of the glove.

From a pace away, it was different.

She looked at Ossie.

He was standing a pace off the last stone, at the front, the way he always stood. Freckles. A grey watch coat with the collar turned up. His hands open at his sides. His face, which was his face, patient and a little puzzled; and his eyes on Drew's back. Not on the gate. On Drew.

And her left eye went hot, and the red mark locked, and she looked *through*.

There was the hand. She'd known there would be. Standing up inside him, filling him, cold and patient and interested, the thing that had walked him down eleven miles of road in the dark. She looked at it, and it didn't look back, because it had never once in four years looked at her; it didn't look at anybody; it looked at the gate.

And round the edge of it, she saw the seam.

***

It ran round his face.

Not on the skin. Under it, a hair under, the way a stone shows under clear water. A line. From the corner of his jaw on the left, up under the ear, along the edge of his hair at the temple, across the top of his forehead under the fringe, down the other side, along the jaw, under the chin, and back. All the way round. Fine and even and very neat, like the line round the edge of a mask where it meets the skin, or a seam where two pieces of cloth have been sewn together by somebody who knew how.

Where the thing had been put on.

She stared at it. She forgot to breathe. She forgot Hild and the swept road and her bare feet on the cold stone. There was a line round Ossie's face, a seam, and inside the seam was the hand, the thing, the cold patient interested thing that filled him; and *outside* the seam—

No. Under. Under the seam, under the hand, pressed flat between the thing and the skin the way a flower's pressed flat in a book, so thin she'd never once seen it from eleven miles away:

Ossie.

His own face. Not the one he was wearing. The other one, underneath. Squashed flat and thin and very faint, like a drawing in pencil that somebody's rubbed out and you can still see the dents in the paper. But there. Freckles. A mouth that went up at one corner. The face of a boy who'd been running, a long time ago, and looked back over his shoulder to see if you were coming.

There was somebody inside.

She'd thought there wasn't. Everybody had. She'd sat on the tower one night in the autumn, weeks before the battle, and the king had come up with his hands black from the forge and sat down beside her against the north parapet with his back to the mountains, his knees complaining, and said, quite quietly, *I'd rather hold them than open them. Until somebody tells me there's nobody inside*. And she'd said nothing, because she'd thought there wasn't, and she hadn't wanted to be the one to say it.

There was.

She looked at the seam. She looked at it so hard her eye began to bleed; she felt it, a warm thread down her cheek from the corner of the left eye, and didn't wipe it. She followed the line all the way round, twice, the way you'd follow a stitch in a coat you were going to unpick. And she saw something else.

It wasn't tight.

Not all the way round. On the left, at the corner of the jaw, where it started, the seam lay close, tight as a stitch. But along the top, under the fringe, where Ossie's hair fell forward over his forehead, it was looser. A hair looser. The line wasn't quite flat there; it stood up a little from what was under it, the way a stitch stands up when the thread's been pulled.

It had been pulled. She was sure of it, the way you're sure of things you can't prove. Something had pulled on it from the inside. Not the hand. The hand didn't want anything but the gate.

From underneath.

Eleven mornings ago, a boy had run across this bridge by himself and put a flat hand on Drew's back, and turned round, and run home. And Hisui had said nothing called him. Nothing moved Ossie but Ossie.

He'd pulled. From underneath. And the seam had given, a hair, along the top, where his face would have gone up when he ran.

***

"Hyvred," said Drew. Very quietly, without turning round. "You're bleeding."

"I know."

"Hild's going to kill me."

"I know." She didn't look away. "Drew. There's a seam."

He didn't say anything.

"Round his face. Where it was put on. Like where a mask stops." Her voice was doing something she couldn't stop. "And he's under it. He's *under* it. Flat. Like a pressed flower. He's still there."

Drew stood very still.

"And it's loose," said Hyvred. "At the top. Where he'd smile. He's been pulling on it."

At the far end of the bridge, Ossie's eyes moved.

Off Drew's back. To her. For a heartbeat. The patient puzzled face, and the eyes in it, finding her behind Drew, a pace away, bleeding from one eye in Bee's coat with no shoes on.

And under the seam, flat, faint as a pencil line rubbed out, the other face looked at her too.

She didn't know how she knew. She just did. The hand didn't look at her; it had never looked at her in four years; it looked at the gate. But the thing under the seam, the boy, the pressed flower, looked at her, for one heartbeat, from very far down; and she saw him see that she could see him.

Then it went, the way a face goes under water. And Ossie's eyes went back to Drew.

Hyvred stood there.

Then she did what Hild had said. She turned round, and walked back across the bridge, past Drew, thirty paces, and onto the swept road, and up it, a quarter of a mile, in her stockinged feet, with the blood drying on her cheek, and didn't look back once, because she'd promised.

Hild was standing in the postern. She took one look at Hyvred's face and her own did nothing at all, very hard, the way Old Pruett's did. She took Hyvred's arm.

"Well?" said Hild.

"There's somebody inside," said Hyvred, and sat down on the cobbles, and found she was crying.

Corvin had put it down the cord.

She didn't see him do it; she was on the cobbles at the postern with Hild's hand on her back. But she saw him afterwards, at the foot of the tower, with the half of the cord round his wrist that went to a girl in Solmere and through her to the king, holding it, with his eyes shut and his thin careful surveyor's face very still.

He'd been the one who believed her, in the beginning. Before anybody. A surveyor who'd come home from the west before the summoning with eleven pages nobody wanted to read about villages going quiet, and an Advisor who'd read them anyway and sent the girl with true sight to stand where Corvin pointed. He'd believed her when she said there was nothing in the faces. He'd written it down.

He opened his eyes and saw her looking.

"Four words," said Corvin. "To the king. I didn't put any more. He'll want it plain." He looked down at the cord on his wrist. "*There is somebody inside*."

Hyvred nodded.

"He said a thing to you," said Corvin. "In the autumn. On the tower. You told me." He was quiet. "*I'd rather hold them than open them*. *Until somebody tells me*." He let go of the cord. "Well. Somebody's told him."

***

She went up the tower again at noon, when her eye had stopped bleeding and Hild had stopped looking at her like that.

The third ring was still at the far end of the bridge, the way it always was until dusk. Drew had come in; there was nobody in the middle. Forty people a pace off the last stone, in the sun, facing the town.

She looked at Ossie from the tower. Eleven miles was nothing to her now; a quarter of a mile was like standing next to him. And from up here, she saw what she'd always seen. The hand. The thing standing up inside him, filling him, patient, interested, looking at the gate.

She couldn't see the seam from here. It was too fine. She'd never have seen it from up here if she lived to be a hundred.

But she knew where it was now.

That was the thing about seeing something once. You couldn't stop knowing it was there. She looked at the hand inside Ossie from the top of the tower, and she knew, the way you know there's a floor under a carpet, that it stopped. That it had an edge. That under it, flat and faint and pressed and still trying, there was a boy who'd raced Tiger to an oak post every morning one spring, and put a flat hand on the wood, and pulled, from underneath, on the stitches of a thing that had been sewn over his face, until one of them gave a hair.

She looked at her window. She hadn't looked at it in a while.

Fifty-one, at the thaw. She'd seen through faces for four years and the number had sat there. It had moved today for seeing where one stopped.

She looked at it, and then at the bridge, and then at the curve in the valley, thirty-one long, all the way round the rings except the last of it; and she thought about a girl in Kingsferry who'd never opened anything on purpose in her life.

At dusk the third ring turned round and walked home up the swept road, eleven miles, to its place in the circle. Ossie last, with his head turned over his shoulder.

He was looking at the bridge. She'd known he would.

And then, for a heartbeat, at the top of the tower. At her.

She lifted her hand. She didn't know if he could see it. She did it anyway, the way you'd wave to somebody on a boat going out, so they'd know you'd seen them go, and would be there when they came in.
