Oswin had been told to leave it three times.

He counted. He was an archivist; he counted everything. The first time had been in the first month after the summoning, in the corridor outside the Advisor's study, when he'd said *thirty-five people who all know each other, sir, summonings don't do that*, and the Advisor had said *leave it, Oswin* without even looking round. The second had been in the winter, after the map. The third had been in the spring, in a letter, two lines long.

He'd left it. He was a good archivist. A good archivist did as he was told, and filed what he wasn't allowed to read, and didn't ask what was in the locked rooms.

And then, one evening in the autumn, the Advisor had held out a black iron key with a bit like a little castle on the end of it, and said *don't leave it, Oswin. Not anymore.*

Oswin had worn it on a string round his neck ever since. He slept with it. He touched it, without meaning to, forty or fifty times a day, the way you'd touch a sore tooth.

He'd been in the sealed room every night for five weeks.

***

It wasn't a room, really. It was a vault.

Below the archive, below the old cellars, down a stair so narrow and steep that you went down it sideways, with a lantern, counting the steps. Ninety-one. At the bottom, a door of black oak bound with iron, with two seals on it: the king's, and the Advisor's, in wax gone dark with age. Oswin had broken neither. The key went into a lock beside them, and the door swung in on hinges that hadn't been oiled in a year, since the Advisor himself had come down here before the summoning, alone, and shut it behind him.

Inside: shelves. Floor to ceiling, on every wall, in a long low room that smelled of old paper and stone and candle smoke from a hundred years ago. Books, scrolls, bundles of letters tied with faded tape, wax tablets, a few things in boxes that Oswin hadn't dared open yet. Four hundred years of them. Every summoning the kingdom of Aurelan had ever attempted, and the men who'd attempted them, and what had happened after.

He'd read every surviving record of past summonings that existed outside this room. That was why he'd got the capital group; the Advisor had said so. *You know more about summonings than anyone alive, except me.*

He'd been wrong. Oswin had known about a tenth of it. The rest had been down here.

***

He didn't do it alone. He couldn't have.

Cursiffe came down with him. Every night, after supper, with a lantern and a bottle of the blue ink and a stack of paper. Oswin had asked him in the first week. He'd needed someone who could read fast, and write faster, and keep his mouth shut, and Cursiffe could do all three.

He couldn't read the old hands, of course. None of the summoned could read handwriting; it slid off their eyes the way their English slid off Oswin's. But he could read print, through the window, and a great deal of what was down here had been copied out in print by archivists before Oswin's time. And what he couldn't read, Oswin read aloud, and Cursiffe wrote it down in English as fast as Oswin spoke, in his small calm hand, in the blue ink that stayed on the page when nothing else would. Notes Oswin couldn't read, on records Cursiffe couldn't read. Between the two of them, they could read everything.

"We're like one very slow scholar," Cursiffe said, the first night, "cut in half."

Oswin had laughed so hard he'd had to put the lantern down.

***

He found the shape of it in the third week.

That was how it happened, with records. You didn't find the answer on one page. You read a thousand pages, and somewhere around the eight hundredth, you started to see the shape the thousand pages made together, the way you'd see a face in a crowd of dots if you stood far enough back.

Four hundred years. Thirty-one attempts. Twenty of them had failed outright: nothing, a circle that didn't light, a mage who burned himself out on the floor and was carried away. Of the eleven that had worked, nine had brought one person. Two had brought two. None had ever brought more.

Eleven summoned, in four hundred years. Thirteen people.

And not one of them had ever had a window. Oswin checked twice. Not one record, in four hundred years, of a summoned man or woman seeing words in the air. As far as anything in this room knew, windows had begun with one man.

And then, a year ago, thirty-five.

"Read me the mediums," said Cursiffe, on the twentieth night. He'd been quiet for a while, writing. "All of them. The ones that worked. Just the mediums."

So Oswin read them. He'd been reading them for weeks, scattered through the records; he'd never read them together.

A ring of a metal nobody could name, found in a riverbed. A mirror, very old, that showed nothing. A book with blank pages. A crown, from a king three dynasties dead. A comb. A shell. A bell that wouldn't ring. A knife, found in a field. A cup. A stone with a hole in it. A child's shoe, nobody knew whose.

"They're all *things*," said Cursiffe slowly. "Strange things. Old things. Things nobody knew where they'd come from."

"Yes."

"Nobody *owned* any of them."

Oswin stopped.

He went back. He read them again, slowly, one at a time, with the lantern held close. The ring in the riverbed: found, not owned. The mirror: in a vault, a hundred years, nobody's. The book, the comb, the shell, the bell, the knife, the cup, the stone, the shoe. Relics. Curiosities. Things in boxes. Not one of them had been carried by one person for years. Not one of them had been *anybody's*.

And every one of them had caught strangers. One, or two. Who didn't know each other. Who didn't know anyone.

"And then," said Cursiffe, very quietly, "the Advisor used a sword."

***

The Advisor's notes were in the last box.

Oswin found them on the twenty-second night. A thin bundle of pages tied with black tape, in a small careful hand he knew better than his own, at the very back of the room, on its own shelf. The Advisor's working notes. From before. He hadn't meant to read them. He'd known they'd be here, somewhere, and he'd been half avoiding them for three weeks, the way you avoid a letter you know is going to be bad.

He read them standing up, by the lantern, with Cursiffe waiting.

Most of it was technical. Circles and rings and spokes, diagrams Oswin half understood. The chant, written out in the old tongue. Calculations of how much of himself the Advisor would have to pour into the floor, and how much he'd have left after. (Not much. He'd underlined *not much* twice.)

And then, on the last page, by itself:

*Every medium that has ever worked has been a stranger's thing, and has caught strangers. A thing that belongs to no one catches no one in particular.*

*I mean to use a thing that belongs to someone. The most someone of any thing I know. Carried, every day, for years. Through a war. Stood over a grave. If the old summoners were right, that a medium remembers its hands, then this one remembers nothing else.*

*I do not know what it will catch. Whatever was tangled in him, I suppose. Whatever he left behind, wherever he came from before he was a garrison boy with a window and no one to explain it.*

*I am sorry, Ars. I will bring you strangers. Forgive me.*

Below that, in different ink, later, shakier, as if written by a man who'd come back down the ninety-one steps after it was all over and sat on the floor of this room with a lantern:

*They were not strangers.*

***

Oswin sat down on the floor of the sealed room.

He didn't decide to. His knees just went. He sat on the cold stone with the Advisor's last page in his hands and the lantern beside him and looked at it for a long time.

Cursiffe didn't say anything. He sat down too, on a box, and waited.

"A medium remembers its hands," said Oswin at last. Very slowly. "That's the old summoners. Two hundred years ago. A mage called Teodric, I read him last week, I thought it was poetry." He looked at the page. "It isn't poetry. It's the *rule*. A thing nobody's held catches nobody in particular. A thing one man's held for years catches..."

"What was tangled in him," said Cursiffe.

"What was tangled in him," said Oswin. "Thirty-five people. All of whom knew each other. Every one of them caught by one sword. Because they were all..." He stopped. He looked up at Cursiffe. "Because they were all *his*."

Cursiffe didn't say anything.

"Tangled in him," said Oswin. His voice had gone up. He couldn't help it. "Not tangled in each other. Tangled in *him*. Thirty-five threads, all running into the same man. Aleaka's map. The river with a hole in the middle where all the lines point and nobody's standing." He was on his feet now. He didn't remember getting up. "And he's a garrison boy who had a window when nobody else in the world did. Who used words nobody had ever heard. Who carved letters on his sword that nobody here can read." He was almost shouting. "And it caught *you*. Thirty-five people from wherever you come from. Because that's where *he* comes from. That's where he left them. Before he was a garrison boy. He *came from there*."

He stood in the middle of the sealed room with the Advisor's page in his hand and the lantern on the floor, breathing hard.

"The Hero of the Redmarch," said Oswin, "is from your world."

***

He ran.

Up the ninety-one steps, two at a time, with the lantern swinging and the key bouncing on its string against his chest. Through the old cellars. Through the archive, past the Keeper, who said *Oswin!* in a scandalised voice. Up the stairs to the rooms where Group 2 lived, at a run, at midnight, with Cursiffe somewhere behind him on the stairs saying *Oswin, wait*.

He burst in without knocking.

They were all there. Hana on the end of the bed with her tail round her ankles and her ledger of names. Milli at the window. Jared on the floor by the door, as always. Aleaka on the rug with one of the castle cats in her lap and the furious hawk on the back of her chair. They all looked up.

"The Hero," said Oswin. He was so out of breath he could barely speak. "The Black Blade. The sword in the hall. The medium. It remembers its hands. Teodric. Two hundred years. The Advisor wrote it down, it caught what was tangled in *him*, all thirty-five of you, all of you, all tangled in one man, and he had a window when nobody did, and he carved letters on the sword in your—"

He stopped. He took a breath.

"He's from your world," said Oswin. "The Hero of the Redmarch. He came from where you came from. He's *one of you*. He *knew* you. Before." He held up the Advisor's page, as if it would help, though none of them could read it. "I've worked it out. Five weeks. I've *worked it out*."

There was a short silence.

Then Hana said, very gently, "We know, Oswin."

***

He stood in the doorway with the page in his hand.

"You know," he said.

"We know," said Hana.

"How long," said Oswin, "have you known?"

They looked at each other. Hana, and Milli, and Jared, and Aleaka. The way people look at each other when they're trying to decide who should say a thing nobody wants to say.

"Some of us since the spring," said Hana. "Leaka since the winter, really. Me since..." She stopped. "I thought it last winter. After the first book. And then I talked myself out of it."

"*All* of us," said Jared, from the floor, "since the end of the summer. Since the night of the sum. In the windows. You'd have seen us. We were all crying."

Oswin had seen them. He'd thought it was homesickness.

Behind him, Cursiffe had arrived at the top of the stairs. Oswin heard him stop. He heard him say, very quietly, "Sorry, Oswin."

Oswin turned round and looked at him.

"*You* knew," he said.

"I read the carving," said Cursiffe. "On the sword. Before it left. I knew what it said." He looked at the floor. "I'd have told you. If it had been mine to tell."

***

Oswin sat down on the floor of Group 2's room, in the doorway, with his back against the frame and the Advisor's page on his knees.

He didn't know what he felt. He felt about seventeen things at once. He felt like a man who'd climbed a mountain for five weeks and got to the top and found five people already sitting there, having a picnic. He felt furious. He felt foolish. He felt, underneath both of those, something enormous and bright that was rising in him like a tide, and that he couldn't put a name to.

"You could have *told* me," he said.

"It wasn't ours," said Hana. "It's his."

Oswin opened his mouth to argue.

And then didn't. Because she was right. Because the Advisor had said the same thing, a few weeks ago, at the top of the west tower: *where he is isn't mine to give*. Because Oswin had spent twenty years collecting every account of the Redmarch that existed, every song, every soldier's story, every scrap, and never once, not once, in all those years, had it occurred to him to ask where the man had come from.

"No," he said slowly. "No. I suppose it wasn't."

***

Aleaka got up off the rug.

She put the cat down, and came over, and sat on the floor beside him in the doorway, close, the way she sat with the biting grey horse in the mews when it was frightened. She didn't say anything at first. Then she put her arms round him.

Oswin sat there in the doorway and was hugged by a fox girl from another world, and found, to his considerable surprise, that he was crying.

"You worked it out," said Aleaka, into his shoulder. Slowly, the way she said everything, stumbling a little. "On your own. From the *records*. With nobody telling you." She pulled back and looked at him. "We had his voice. We had his jokes. We had a word he used to say that nobody else says, in a book, and a name he used to give everything. We had *everything*. You had a vault full of old paper and a sword you'd never met the owner of." She smiled at him, wetly. "And you got there anyway. That's not foolish. That's the best thing I've ever heard."

Oswin wiped his face on his sleeve.

"What was he like?" he said. "Before. When he was yours."

Nobody answered for a moment.

"Funny," said Jared.

They all looked at him.

"He was funny," said Jared again. He was looking at the ceiling. "That's what he was, to us. The funny one. He made me laugh so hard once, on a call, I fell off my chair. He'd do bits. He'd say the most ridiculous thing you ever heard, and then he'd say *trust*, and you'd lose it." He was quiet. "He was half a world away from all of us. We never once met him. We only ever heard his voice. And it was always laughing."

"Then he's changed," said Oswin quietly. "The songs say the Black Blade never laughed."

"The songs," said Hana, "are probably wrong."

***

The next morning, Kestra came back.

She rode in at the north gate an hour after dawn, with the king beside her, both of them in plain travelling coats, mud to the knee. No banners. No heralds. Just two riders and two pack horses coming in out of a thin cold rain, as if they'd been out for a ride and got caught in the weather.

Half the castle saw it anyway. By breakfast, the whole court knew the king was back from his hunting trip. By the second hour, three different Council lords had sent to ask what he'd caught. The king's answer, relayed by a page who was trying very hard not to laugh, was: *A cold.*

Kestra came to Group 2's rooms at noon.

She stood in the doorway in her plain dark coat, with her sword on her hip and her little black book in her pocket, and looked at the five of them one after another, the way she always did. Then at Oswin, who'd come up from the archive to be there, and hadn't slept, and had ink on his face.

Nobody asked where she'd been. They didn't need to. Hana had watched her ride out by the north gate a month ago with a king and a roll of hammers. They'd all done that sum.

So Oswin asked the other thing. He couldn't help it. He'd been waiting a year.

"Well?" he said.

Kestra looked at him.

"The Hero," said Oswin. "You've met him. Haven't you. You've *met* him." He leaned forward. "You said, a year ago. At dinner. At that table, right there. You said *the songs are better than the man. They always are.*" He was nearly vibrating. "So? *Well?* Were they?"

Kestra didn't answer for a long time.

Hana was watching her. Oswin saw it. Hana always watched Kestra; it was how she'd learned to read her. Not her face. Her feet. And Kestra's feet, Oswin saw, following Hana's eyes, had turned, very slightly, without Kestra seeming to know it, toward the window. Toward the north.

"No," said Kestra.

Oswin stared at her.

"The songs," said Kestra, "are worse than the man."

***

There was a short silence.

Then Oswin stood up, very slowly, from the end of the bed, and pointed at her.

"*Worse*," he said.

"Oswin," said Kestra.

"You said *better*. A *year* ago. At *dinner*. With your *spoon in your hand*." His voice was rising. "You rolled your eyes. You said *half propaganda*. You said a man who carries a sword that size is overcompensating for something. You said—"

"I know what I said."

"*Worse!*"

"Oswin."

"I want it in writing," said Oswin. "I want it in your *book*. I want it *underlined*."

Kestra looked at him for a long moment, with no expression whatsoever.

Then she took the little black book out of her coat, and opened it to a page near the end, and turned it round, and held it out to him.

He couldn't read it, of course. It was her hand, cramped and fast, and his eyes were an archivist's eyes, used to every hand in the kingdom; they didn't slide off it. He read it. The whole page. *The Hero of the Redmarch. He fishes.* All the way down. *He minds. He laughed twice. He is a fisherman.*

And at the bottom, underlined once:

*The songs are worse than the man.*

Oswin read it twice. Then he handed the book back to her, very carefully, and sat down on the end of the bed, and put his face in his hands.

"He *fishes*," he said, through his fingers.

"He fishes," said Kestra.

"He grows *pumpkins*."

"He's very proud of them."

"The *hens* hate him."

"He minds," said Kestra.

And Oswin, archivist, who'd collected every song about the Black Blade for twenty years and never once thought to ask where he came from, sat on the end of Hana's bed with his face in his hands and laughed until he couldn't breathe.

***

He was unbearable for a week. Everybody said so. Kestra said so most of all.

He followed her round the castle. He asked her questions at breakfast. *What kind of rod? What kind of fish? What colour are the hens?* She answered almost none of them. She'd come back from the north with her book full and her mouth shut, and she kept it shut, the way she kept everything. Where the man lived, she never said. Not to Oswin, not to Hana, not to anyone. Oswin didn't push. He'd learned that much, at least, in a year. *It isn't ours. It's his.*

But on the seventh day, at dinner, at the same table where she'd said *the songs are better than the man*, Kestra looked across at him and said, without any expression at all:

"His hens love the king."

Oswin put his spoon down.

"They *what*?"

"Follow him round the yard," said Kestra. "Sit on his boots. The big red one cried when he left." She went back to her soup. "That's all you're getting."

Oswin wrote it down. On the back of his hand. In ink. Cursiffe's blue ink, which stayed.

***

That night, late, he unrolled the map.

The one he'd made with Aleaka, a year ago, on the back of a kingdom map, in the archive, at night, when nobody had told him to stop. Thirty-five names. Lines between them, *who brought you to who*, in his own marks beside the English he couldn't read. Knots at Yan, at Gabe, at Kei. And in the middle, where all the lines pointed, a small empty circle he'd drawn in the winter, with no lines going to it, because he hadn't known what to put there.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he dipped his pen and wrote inside the circle, in his own small archivist's hand, in the script of Aurelan: *the Black Blade*.

Aleaka was beside him. She'd come down with a lantern, because she couldn't sleep either. She looked at what he'd written. She couldn't read it. He told her.

She held out her hand for the pen.

He gave it to her. She bent over the map, and under his words, in the middle of the empty circle, she wrote something in her own hand. In English. Three letters. Oswin's eyes went to it, and slid off it, and came back, and slid off again.

"What does it say?" he said.

Aleaka looked at the three letters for a long time.

"His name," she said. "His other one. The one we knew him by."

"Will you tell me it?"

She thought about it. Honestly. The way they all did, now, about anything that had to do with him.

"When he does," said Aleaka.

Oswin nodded slowly. He looked at the circle. His words, and under them, three letters he couldn't read. All thirty-five lines on the map, pointing in.

It wasn't empty anymore.
