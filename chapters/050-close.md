The last thing Gabe ever said to Declan was *go to sleep*.

He'd thought about that a lot, in three years. More than he'd ever told anyone. More than he'd told Deston, even, and he told Deston most things. It had been past two in the morning, the end of a bad CS match, five of them in a call. Declan had whiffed the easiest AWP shot of his life and blamed the server, and then said he was going to bed, and everyone had called him a coward. And Gabe had said, because he always said it, because he'd been saying it for eleven years, since he was thirteen in the back row of a school choir:

*Sleep, Declan. Seriously.*

And Declan had.

It wasn't why. Gabe knew it wasn't why. You didn't die in your sleep at twenty-six because your friend told you to go to bed. The doctors had said things Gabe had never fully understood and never wanted to, and it had been nobody's fault, and there was nothing anyone could have done. He knew all that.

He still thought about it. Some nights. In the dark, in the room above the chandler's on Rope Street, with Calvin talking in his sleep and Deston breathing on the other side of the room.

*Sleep, Declan. Seriously.*

He'd never say it to anyone again. He'd decided that, quietly, the week of the funeral. Whatever else he said to anyone for the rest of his life, it wasn't going to be that.

***

The forge on the south wharf had been his for months now.

Not his. Harl's. Harl was the smith: a short, wide, scarred old man with no eyebrows, burned off years ago, and forearms like hams, who'd looked at Gabe on his first morning in Kingsferry standing in the doorway watching, and said *watching's free, bellows aren't*. Gabe had worked the bellows. Then he'd swept the floor. Then, by the third week, he'd been holding the tongs, and by the winter, drawing out bar on the anvil while Harl watched and grunted.

But it felt like his. That was the thing. The heat and the noise and the smell of it, coal smoke and hot iron and quench oil. The ring of the hammer. The way the metal changed under your hands, if you paid attention. Gabe had always been good with his hands, back home: fixing things, building things, taking things apart to see how they worked. But he'd never had *this*. He'd never stood in front of a fire and felt a piece of iron tell him it was ready.

That was what it was like. He'd tried to explain it to the others and never managed. You put the bar in the fire, and you watched it go red, and then orange, and then a deep bright yellow, and at some point, if you were paying attention, the heat in it stopped being *angry*. It went from fighting you to waiting for you. And that was when you took it out.

Harl had never heard of anyone being able to feel that. Not below Honed, anyway. Gabe was nowhere near Honed. He was barely an Initiate. Twenty-seven percent, at the start of the summer, which was slow; he knew it was slow.

But he could feel when the metal was ready. And he'd been making knives since the winter, and they were good. Rachael carried his first one everywhere, still. She'd cut a man's hand with it at the night market, and the man's skin had moved like water.

He'd told everyone, in the spring, that he'd have his own blade by summer.

It was summer.

***

He'd been working on it for three weeks.

Not a big sword. He wasn't a big-sword person, whatever Sabine said about walls. A short heavy blade, a forearm and a half long, broad at the base, with a single edge and a thick spine. The kind of thing you could use to stop something, not just cut it. A wall-and-forge sort of blade, Harl said, approving. Sabine, when she saw the drawing, said it looked like a butcher's cleaver had married a gate.

"That's the idea," said Gabe.

Sabine looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded. "Good," she said. "That's the right idea."

He'd forged the blade itself the week before. Drawn it, folded it, drawn it again. Hardened it in Harl's quench and tempered it in the oven, slowly, over a whole afternoon, while Calvin sat on an upturned barrel outside the forge door and kept him company and asked questions about everything. Ground the edge, all of one long evening, until it would take the hair off his arm. It was good. It was *right*. He'd picked it up when it was done and held it, and felt it settle into his hand like it had been waiting for him.

All it needed now was a guard. A simple crossbar of bronze between the blade and the grip, to stop your hand sliding down onto the edge. Harl said to cast it, rather than forge it; bronze cast cleaner than it hammered. So Gabe had made a mould, out of sand and clay, and on a hot bright afternoon at the beginning of summer he put a crucible of bronze in the fire to melt.

He was thinking about Declan when the crucible cracked.

He'd been thinking about him all morning. He didn't know why. The stories, maybe: the night the week before, when that boy in Valcrest had asked what Declan was like and thirty-four people had told him. Gabe had written about the choir. The back row. *He never once got caught.* He'd stayed up till three, reading everyone else's, and felt better and worse than he had in a year.

And he'd been thinking, all morning, holding the finished blade: *Declan would have named this.*

Declan named everything. Not with real names. In Latin. He'd had a thing for Latin, ever since school, for no reason anybody had ever got out of him. Every Discord status he'd ever had was some Latin phrase he'd found somewhere. Every Steam group he'd ever made had a Latin name, half of them jokes, half of them deadly serious, all of them pretentious. Gabe and Deston had given him so much grief for it, for years. *Bro, nobody can read that. Bro, you don't even speak Latin.* And Declan had just smiled and said something in Latin back at them, and refused to translate.

Gabe didn't know any Latin. He'd never bothered to learn. He'd thought there'd be time to ask.

He was standing at the fire, thinking that, with the long tongs in his hands, lifting the crucible out. White-hot. Brimming. And there was a small, sharp *tick*, like a fingernail on glass.

Then the side of the crucible came away.

***

It wasn't much bronze. A cupful, maybe. But it was a cupful of liquid metal hot enough to melt through a boot, and it came out of the cracked side of the crucible in a single bright gout and went down the length of Gabe's left forearm, from the elbow almost to the wrist.

He didn't feel it at first. That was the strange thing. He saw it: a bright orange stripe, running down his arm. He heard it: a hiss, like fat in a pan. He smelled it.

Then he felt it.

He'd never felt anything like it. He'd broken his wrist once, at fifteen, falling off a wall. He'd thought that was pain. This was something else. This was the whole world going white and narrow and silent, all at once, as if somebody had pulled a sack over his head.

He didn't scream. He found out afterwards that he didn't. He just dropped the tongs, very carefully, so they wouldn't fall on his foot. And then he sat down on the floor of the forge, because his legs had stopped working.

Harl was shouting. Somebody was shouting. Calvin, outside the door, on his barrel, had seen it through the doorway and was on his feet, and then inside, and then *gone*, out of the door again, so fast that the wind of him blew the coals up in the forge.

Gabe sat on the floor of the forge with his arm in his lap, and looked at it, and thought, very clearly and very calmly: *Oh. That's bad.*

It was very bad. He could see that even through the white. The bronze had cooled and was sitting on his arm in a long dull crust, and round it and under it there wasn't skin anymore. There was something red and black and wrong, all the way down, and in one place, near the elbow, there was something white that Gabe decided he wasn't going to look at.

Harl was kneeling beside him, pouring cold water over it from a bucket. It didn't help. It didn't help at all.

"Stay with me, lad," Harl was saying, over and over. "Stay with me. Stay with me."

*Sleep*, Gabe thought, stupidly. *Don't say sleep.*

***

Rachael got there in four minutes.

He found that out afterwards too. Calvin had run the whole length of the waterfront to the river-gate infirmary, and the wind had come with him, behind him, pushing, the way it did now when he asked it to. He'd burst into Sister Maud's long white room and grabbed Rachael by the hand and pulled her out of the door without a word, and the two of them had come back along the quays so fast that the fishwives were still talking about it a week later.

Gabe didn't see her come in. He only knew she was there because suddenly there were hands on either side of his face, small and warm and very steady, and a voice he'd known since he was fifteen saying, "Gabe. Gabe. Look at me."

He looked at her.

Rachael was kneeling on the forge floor in front of him in her infirmary apron, with her hair coming down and her face very white and very calm. She'd been the patient for twenty-six years. She'd told them that, once: needles and hospitals and other people's hands, all her life. She knew what it was like on the floor. And she'd decided, the first week here, that she was never going to make anyone feel the way the bad nurses had made her feel.

So she didn't say *this won't hurt*. She didn't say *it's not that bad*. She looked at his arm for one second, all of it, the bronze and the black and the white thing near the elbow, and her face didn't move at all.

"Okay," she said. "That's bad."

"Yeah," said Gabe.

"I'm going to fix it."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah." She took her hands off his face and laid them, very carefully, on either side of his arm. Not touching. A finger's width away. "It's going to sting, and then it's going to stop. I promise."

***

He'd seen her do it before. Everyone had. Rope burns, cuts, a bargeman's arm in the spring. A finger's width at a time, slowly, sweating, gold light running out of her hands, the skin creeping closed across a wound like frost across a window. *Close*, she called it. Her line. She said it again every time, a little further each time. A finger's width more.

This wasn't a finger's width. This was his whole forearm.

He watched her face as she began. He saw her feel it. All of it, all the way down: the burn and the black and the white thing near the elbow. He saw her flinch, very slightly, at the size of it. And then he saw her decide something.

She closed her eyes.

The gold came. Soft at first, the way it always did, running from her palms into the edges of the burn like water into dry ground. He felt it: warm, so warm, a warmth that went in under the white noise of the pain and started, very gently, to push it back. He watched the edge of the burn at his wrist go pink, and then pale. A finger's width. Two.

And then he felt her stop.

Not stop. *Pause*. As if she'd come to the end of something. The end of the road she'd been walking every day all spring, a finger's width at a time. He saw her face tighten. He saw her, kneeling there with her eyes shut, feel for whatever came next, and not find it, and keep feeling.

*Come on, Rae*, Gabe thought. *Come on.*

And then her face changed.

He'd never seen it before. Not on her. Not on anyone. It was the face you make when you've been trying to remember a word for weeks and it suddenly arrives, all at once, out of nowhere, on the tip of your tongue. Surprise. And then something that wasn't surprise at all. Something very sure.

She didn't say *close*. Not this time.

He watched her lips move, very slightly, around a different word. He didn't hear it. He found out afterwards what it was.

*Mend.*

***

The gold went *everywhere*.

Not a finger's width. Not two. All of it, at once. It poured out of her hands like light coming through a door that's just been thrown open, and it went into his arm from the wrist to the elbow, all the way down, under the bronze and through the black and into the white thing that he wasn't looking at. And he felt it go. He felt it, all of it, the whole terrible white roar of it, go quiet. Not fade. *Stop.* As if somebody had closed a door on it.

The bronze crust fell off his arm and rattled on the floor of the forge.

Under it there was skin.

New skin. Pink and thin and shiny, from his wrist to his elbow, all the way round. No black. No white. No red. Just skin, a little tight, a little tender, the way skin is after a sunburn. He could see the hairs on his arm starting, very faintly, to come back.

Rachael opened her eyes.

She looked at his arm. Then at her own hands. Then at his arm again.

Then she sat back on her heels on the floor of the forge, very suddenly, the way you sit down when your legs give out, and opened her window, and stared at it.

[ Rachael · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Mend ]

"Oh," she said. In a very small voice. "Oh."

Gabe looked at his arm. He moved his fingers. They moved. He made a fist. It made a fist. He turned his arm over. Nothing hurt. Nothing at all.

"Rae," he said.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were swimming.

"I didn't mean to," she said. "I mean, I meant to. I just... I got to the end of *close*. I could feel it. And there was so much more, and I couldn't get there a finger at a time. There wasn't *time*. So I just..." She swallowed. "I asked for all of it. At once."

"You mended me," said Gabe.

"Yeah." Rachael laughed, a little wildly, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist. "Yeah. I mended you." She looked at her window again. "Fifth. I'm *fifth*. Thea, Maigo, Higu, Salty. And me." She laughed again. "I'm going to be *so* insufferable."

Gabe looked at her, kneeling on the floor of Harl's forge in her infirmary apron with her hair coming down and gold light still fading in the lines of her palms.

He thought about a girl he'd known since he was fifteen. Who'd spent her whole life being careful. Being the fragile one. Never going anywhere without a mask and a bottle of hand gel and a list of the nearest hospitals. Who'd come through the floor into another world and been healed in the first hour by a grey man with two fingers on her wrist. Who'd decided, that very same week, that she was going to spend the rest of her life doing that for everyone else.

He leaned forward and put his arms round her. His left arm too. It didn't hurt.

"You're already insufferable," he said, into her hair.

"I know," said Rachael. "Isn't it great."

***

Sister Maud came that evening, to the rooms above the chandler's. She'd heard. Half of Kingsferry had heard. She climbed the stairs slowly, on her stick, and sat down at their table, and asked to see Gabe's arm. She looked at it for a long time, turning it over in her old fierce hands, pressing here and there with her thumb.

"Not a mark," she said at last. "Not a mark on it. From a bronze pour." She let go of his arm and looked at Rachael, across the table. Her face was very serious. "Forty years," she said. "Forty years I've been a healer. Candle tier, and proud of it. And I couldn't have done that in a week. Not with every healer in this city helping me."

Rachael didn't say anything. She'd gone a little pale again.

"Listen to me, girl," said Sister Maud. "I told you in the autumn. Healers are one in a few hundred. Healers who can do *that*..." She shook her head. "Every order in the kingdom will want you now. Every temple. Every lord with a sick child. Every army." She leaned forward. "And worse."

"Worse," said Rachael.

"You know what I mean." Maud's eyes went to the window, to the river, to the west. "The ones who came for you at the night market. The ones in the hills. They wanted you before. When you could close a cut a finger at a time. Now?" She sat back. "Now you're the best thing they've ever seen."

Nobody said anything.

"So you stay watched," said Sister Maud. "Every hour. Every day. You don't go anywhere alone. Not to the privy. Not to the end of the street." She looked round the table at all of them, at Gabe and Deston and Dennis and Calvin, and at Emrys by the fire and Sabine by the door. "You hear me? *Every* hour."

"We hear you," said Gabe.

He'd been hearing it since the autumn. He found he was holding his left arm against his chest, the new skin warm under his other hand.

***

Deston saw the man the next morning.

They were walking Rachael to the infirmary, the four of them, the way they always did now. Along the quay, past the fishwives and the moored barges, in the bright early sun. Rachael in the middle. Calvin in front, with the wind at his back. Dennis beside her, chatting, listening, as always, for anything that rang like a cracked bell. Deston at the back, scanning faces.

Gabe was beside Deston. He'd been beside Deston since Gabe was thirteen, more or less. He knew all his faces.

So he saw it when Deston's face went still.

Deston had stopped walking. He was staring across the river. Not at the river. At the far quay, on the other bank, where the grain barges tied up: a hundred yards of water away, crowded with carts and porters and sacks. He was staring at one particular place on it, very hard, with his eyes narrowed, the way he looked when he was reading threads.

Gabe looked where he was looking. He saw carts. Porters. Sacks. A woman with a basket. A dog.

Nothing.

"What?" said Gabe.

Deston didn't answer straight away. He was still staring. Then he blinked, and his face went back to normal, mostly, and he started walking again, a little faster than before.

"There was a man," he said quietly. "On the far quay. By the grain barge with the red sail. Standing still. Looking this way." He paused. "No threads."

Gabe's stomach dropped.

"None?"

"None. Not a single one. Not grey, not gold. Nothing." Deston was walking very fast now, eyes on Rachael's back. "I looked at him for maybe two seconds. Then a cart went past in front of him. And when it had gone by, he wasn't there."

Gabe looked back over his shoulder at the far quay. At the grain barge with the red sail. Carts, and porters, and sacks.

Nobody standing still.

"Since the winter," said Deston. "That's the first one since the winter."

"The day after," said Gabe.

Deston looked at him.

"The day after she went up a tier," said Gabe. "The day after half of Kingsferry heard about it."

Deston didn't say anything. He didn't need to. They walked Rachael the rest of the way to the infirmary in silence, both of them watching every face, and when they got there Gabe stood outside the door of Sister Maud's long white room for the whole of the morning, with his new skin itching and his short heavy blade, with no guard on it yet, wrapped in sacking under his arm.

***

He finished the guard the next day. In Harl's forge, with a new crucible, which he checked over three times before he put it in the fire, and with Harl watching from the doorway with his arms folded, and Calvin on his barrel outside, and Rachael sitting on the bench by the door with her lunch, refusing to leave until it was done.

It went fine. The bronze poured clean into the mould, and set, and he broke the mould and cleaned the guard and fitted it to the blade. Wrapped the grip in leather. Pinned it. Polished the whole thing, all one long evening, until it shone.

Then he held it out in front of him, in the light of Harl's forge, and looked at it.

A short heavy blade, a forearm and a half long. Broad at the base. A thick spine and a single edge. A plain bronze crossbar and a dark leather grip. A wall and a forge. It was the best thing he'd ever made.

He felt something, holding it. The way he felt metal, when it was ready. Something in the blade settling, the way the heat in a bar settled when it stopped being angry. As if it had been waiting for his hand.

And something in him settled with it. His window, that night, said thirty-one. Four, in two days. He hadn't been trying.

He didn't name it. He thought about it. He sat on the bench in Harl's forge with the blade across his knees and thought about it for a long time. And in the end he didn't, because the only person he'd ever known who could have given it the right name was the one person he couldn't ask.

*Declan would have named this,* he thought. *In Latin. And refused to tell me what it meant.*

He thought about Declan's dad, then. He didn't know why. Maybe because of the blade across his knees: a thing made by hand, to last.

He'd been at the funeral. All of Group 4 had, and most of Group 6. Declan's dad had stood at the front the whole time, in a dark suit that didn't quite fit, shaking every hand. Thanking every single person for coming. Asking after their parents, their jobs, their studies, by name, as if it were a wedding and not what it was. He hadn't cried once. Not once, all day. The woman he'd been with for the last couple of years had stood beside him with her hand on his elbow, not saying much, just there.

Later, Gabe had gone out the back for some air and found him sitting on a low wall in the car park with his tie pulled loose, on his own. Not crying. Just sitting there, looking at nothing, the way you look at nothing when you've been holding something up all day and finally put it down.

Gabe had stood in the doorway, not knowing whether to go to him.

Then the woman had come out of the side door, and walked across the car park, and sat down on the wall beside him. She hadn't said anything. She'd just taken his hand. And Declan's dad had let her, and the two of them had sat there together, looking at nothing, in the evening.

Gabe had gone back inside before either of them saw him. He remembered standing in the corridor, thinking: *Good. He's not on his own. Declan would want that.*

Three years. They'd probably married by now. Gabe hoped so. He thought he'd like to be able to tell Declan that, one day, somehow, even though there was no way he ever could.

***

They sang that night, on the roof. He and Deston. They always did.

He'd thought, after the forge, after the man on the far quay, that they might not. But Deston came up the ladder after supper, the way he always did, and sat down among the chimney pots, and after a while, without either of them saying anything, Gabe started.

Low. Without the words. The song they'd learned at thirteen, in the back row, trying not to laugh in the quiet part. A song about how you measure a year in someone's life.

Gabe took the low line. Deston took the middle.

And they left the gap. The third part, the one that went over the top and held it all together. Declan's part. They always left it. They'd been leaving it every night since the first week in Kingsferry, without ever once saying why.

But tonight, Gabe noticed something.

Deston was leaving it longer.

Not much. A beat. Half a beat. At the place where Declan's line would have come in, over the top, Deston stopped singing his own line a little early, and held the silence open a little longer than he used to. As if he were listening for something. As if he were waiting to see whether anything came in to fill it.

Nothing did. Of course nothing did. Then Deston came back in, on the middle line, a beat late.

Gabe stopped singing.

Deston stopped too, a moment after. They sat there in the dark, on the roof above the chandler's, with the river going by below and the lamps along the quays, and Gabe looked at him.

"You've been doing that," said Gabe. "For a couple of weeks."

Deston didn't answer.

"Leaving the gap longer." Gabe kept his voice easy. Straightforward. Saying things how they were. That was how he'd always talked to Deston; it was how they'd always talked to each other. "Since the night on the roof. When you had the thread face. You've been weird since then, bro. Not bad weird. Just..." He shrugged. "Listening for something."

Deston was looking out at the river. North. Upriver, past the bend, where the Wren came down from wherever the Wren came from.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Is it the thread?" said Gabe. "The faded one?"

Deston closed his eyes.

"Not yet," he said.

Gabe looked at him.

It was an odd thing to say. Not *no*. Not *I don't want to talk about it*. Deston had known him for nearly fifteen years; he could have said either of those, and Gabe would have let it go. *Not yet.* As if there were something there. As if he knew what it was, or nearly. As if he were waiting until he was sure.

Gabe thought about pushing. He was good at pushing, when it mattered. He thought about it for a long moment, sitting on the roof in the dark.

Then he didn't.

"Okay," he said. "When you're ready."

Deston opened his eyes and looked at him. Something in his face eased.

"Thanks," he said.

"Don't thank me. Just don't leave it too long." Gabe picked up the line again, low, from the bridge. "You know I'm bad at waiting."

Deston laughed, a little shakily, and came back in on the middle line.

And they left the gap, the way they always did. But Gabe found, without quite meaning to, that he was leaving it a little longer too.
