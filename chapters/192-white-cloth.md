Aldous had laid white cloth over eleven hundred and four people on the Redmarch whose names he knew, and three hundred and nineteen whose names he didn't, and a great many more in the years before that, in other places, and he'd said the same line over every one of them, and he had never once known what it meant.

He'd thought he did. That was the worst of it. He'd thought, for thirty-five years, that he knew exactly what it meant. His master had told him, in a cold chapel in the south when he was nineteen, with a cloth over a dead child on the table between them. *We lay white over them, for the Soul Eater, who carries*. And Aldous had asked who the Soul Eater was, and his master had said *nobody knows, boy, it's very old, say it anyway*. And he'd said it anyway. Over children and soldiers and old women. Over a boy from a ditch that nobody could name. Over a man from Ashby who'd owed somebody a shilling.

He'd said *Soul Eater*. Every time. Because that was the word.

And a woman out of a song had sat on a bench in his chapel last night, by his fire, and looked at his chest of folded white cloths for a long time, and said it was hers.

***

He didn't sleep.

He hadn't expected to. He sat up at the end of the bench nearest the fire, in his old chaplain's coat, with his book on his knees, and watched them sleep. The three of them. On the floor in front of the coals, in their lichen-coloured coats, with the blankets he'd given them folded very neatly in a pile to one side. The old one, Tavrin, on her back with her hands folded on her stave like a figure on a tomb. The young man, Oen, curled on his side toward the door, the way a soldier sleeps who's been told to watch it. And the girl with the pale hair, Liraun, sitting up, with her back against the end of the bench where the little tree man had fallen asleep, and her long hand resting on one of his bare antlers.

Somebody had put the folded blankets over Maigo in the night. Aldous hadn't seen who.

The girl didn't sleep at all, as far as he could tell. She sat there with her eyes half closed and her hand on the antler, the way you'd sit by somebody's bed.

Aldous sat across the fire from her with his book, and didn't open it.

He knew what was in it. One thousand one hundred and four names, in his own small hand, in rows, and beside each one a number: the row and the post. Then three hundred and nineteen numbers with no names at all, where he'd stopped knowing names and kept counting. He'd told Group 3, on their first week, that he'd stopped counting. It was the only lie he'd ever told them. He'd never stopped. He'd just stopped saying the numbers out loud.

And forty pages in, on the page with the healers, one line at the bottom on its own. *A healer. Diwa. The rise beyond the east wall; then the grave on the knoll, under the sword*. The name in newer ink than the rest, blacker, a little uneven, written on a rise a mile west of the fort on the morning the man came back. And under it, in his hand, from the morning the man went west again:

*The rites said.*

He'd written that because of the green. Because a young man with a white snake round his neck had sat on a fallen tree at the foot of a woman's grave in the moonlight and gone green, all of him, like a lamp in a wood, and Aldous had said a word out of the oldest part of the rites without thinking. And the snake had lifted her head and looked at him. As if he'd said her name in a crowd.

He'd thought, writing it, that it was the strangest thing that would ever happen to him.

He'd been wrong about that too.

***

Tavrin woke at the grey.

Aldous saw her wake. She didn't move, at first. She opened her eyes and lay there on the flags of his chapel looking up at the beams, the way you'd look at the ceiling of a house you'd been told about and never seen. Then she sat up. All of her at once, the long grey length of her folding up off the stones. And looked across the coals at him.

"Chaplain," she said.

In the kingdom's tongue. Not well. Each word set down by itself, like a stone across a stream, with the weight in odd places. The way you'd speak a language you'd learned once, a long time ago, and put away in a chest, and not taken out since. She'd let the young man do the talking at the gate. Aldous understood, now, that it hadn't been because she couldn't.

"Lady," said Aldous.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked at the book on his knees.

"The dead," she said.

"Yes."

"May I?"

Aldous looked at her.

Nobody had ever asked. Not in nine years. The garrison thought the book was scripture. Group 3 had thought it was prayers, once. Rhea thought, he suspected, that it was a very long and very boring novel. Nobody had ever asked to read it.

He got up. He went round the coals, slowly, because his knees were fifty-four. And sat down on the stones beside her, on the floor of his own chapel, and put the book in her long grey hands.

She opened it.

She couldn't read it. He saw that at once. Her pale eyes went to the first page, to his hand, the kingdom's script, row on row of names, and stayed there, not moving along the lines the way eyes do when they read. She looked at it the way you'd look at a field of stones. Not reading. Counting.

She turned the pages. Slowly. One at a time. All of them. Every page of names, and then every page of numbers. She didn't hurry. The grey light came up in the high window over the altar while she did it and lay across the book in her lap, and outside, very faintly, the fort began to wake: a door, a voice, the cook banging a pot.

At the page with the healers on it, she stopped.

Five orderlies. A boy from the ditch, brought in at midnight and put on somebody else's cot. Three healers taken and never found. And the line at the bottom on its own, with the newer ink in it, and the three words under that.

She put one long finger on the name.

"This one," she said. "New."

"Yes," said Aldous.

"Her name."

"Diwa," said Aldous. "A healer. She was taken from the infirmary on the last night. Her husband found her on the rise. He buried her on the knoll, under his sword." He was quiet. "I didn't know her name for nine years. I knew where she was. I knew what was done to her. I didn't know her name." He looked at the line. "Her husband told me. At the end of the summer. On the rise."

Tavrin looked at the name for a long time.

Then she looked at the three words under it. *The rites said*.

"And this?"

Aldous didn't answer at once.

"It means," he said, slowly, so she'd get every word, "that I said the rites over her. At the end of the summer, at the grave, for the first time. The old words, the whole hour, with a white cloth over the stones. Her husband had buried her with nobody to say anything over her at all. And that night a young man went green on a fallen tree, and the snake looked at me, and I knew—" He stopped. "I wrote it so I'd remember that it happened. That the rites *said* something. That they weren't only words."

Tavrin closed the book. Gently. And held it on her knees, in both hands, the way he held it.

"They were never only words," she said.

***

Group 3 came at the first bell, all of them, the way they came everywhere, and the little tree man woke up under a pile of folded blankets and sat up and said *oh* and looked round and saw the girl with the pale hair still sitting beside him and went very pink under the bark.

"You stayed," said Maigo.

The girl said something, in the singing tongue. Low. The summoned understood it; Aldous saw it go across their faces, Wendy's and Salty's and Yan's, the way you see wind go across a field. He didn't. He never would.

"She says," said Maigo, very carefully, "she always stays with trees when they're asleep." He looked at her. "She says it's rude not to."

Aldous found he had to look at the fire for a moment.

***

They asked to see the grave.

Aldous had known they would. It was why they'd come, the old woman had said: for the white. And the white had been at the grave, at the end of the summer, on the night of the full moon, round the neck of a young man glowing green on a fallen tree. And under the grave, a long way down, held, was the thing that Yan had felt since his first week on the red, and that the two halves of the sword had rung over, that night, and that the ash's roots had closed round like fingers round a stone.

He didn't know if they knew about that.

He took them anyway. Out of the east gate, under the horns, onto the red, in the morning, with the snow still lying on the plain and the sky high and pale and the wind gone round to the north. Group 3 behind. Rhea at the back, with her sword, not saying anything, because the rematch had been put off to the evening and Rhea was saving her voice. The old ones on the east wall, watching them go, with their fists to their chests. Not for the three tall grey people. For the white snake round Yan's neck.

For nine years the red had been the red. Grey grass rattling. The markers in their rows. The feeling under your feet of walking across somebody's grief. And then a man with a rod had walked across it, and the grass had gone still round his boots. And at the end of the summer, round his daughter's too.

And round the three tall grey people, walking ahead of him in their lichen coats across the snow, Aldous saw something else.

The grass didn't go still round them.

It *turned*.

He saw it. Every tuft of grey grass they passed, poking up out of the snow, ash-coloured and dead and rattling the way it had rattled for nine years. As they went by, it turned. Very slightly. Toward them. The way the ash leaned toward the black stone. The way the little tree man said the roots on the downs had leaned, under the snow, toward three people walking north.

As if the red, after nine years, had been waiting for somebody who knew what it was.

***

The old woman stopped at the edge of the green.

Exactly at it. The way the three of them had stopped at the edge of the shadow in the west gate. At the line on the knoll where the grey grass ended and the real grass began, green under the snow, in a ring round the long low cairn and the black stone and the young ash.

She didn't step onto it.

None of them did. They stood at the edge of the ring in a row, the three of them, with their staves upright, and looked.

At the cairn, the length of a person, with the snow on it. At the ring of children's stones round its foot, and the scorched one, and Mother Breck's on the top. At the white one at its head, that Wendy had carried east in her pack without knowing whose it was, and that the man had gone into a lake up to his chest for, in the dark, at the end of a jetty. At the black stone behind it, broad and flat in the face, that had been half a sword, with four words on it in the summoned's letters that Wendy had read out to Aldous once and he had not been able to answer. At the bare place beside it, where the other half had stood in the summer and wasn't now; it had gone west, on a fox girl's back.

At the ash.

Aldous had heard about the leaf. Yan had told Maigo at dawn, on the morning of the gate: *she says it's awake, she says there's a leaf on it*. And Maigo had told the whole long hall at breakfast, crying into his porridge, and Aldous had nodded, and not quite believed it, the way you don't quite believe a thing you're told about a tree in the middle of winter.

He believed it now.

The ash stood on the knoll in the snow with every one of its branches turned. Not south, the way Yan said they'd been. Toward the edge of the green. Toward the three of them. Every branch, every twig, the whole young tree, leaning the way a person leans toward somebody they've been waiting for at a door. And on the lowest branch, on the end of it, on the edge of the Redmarch where nothing grew:

One leaf.

Green.

Small, and new, and folded still, the way a leaf is in the first days of spring. Bright as a lamp against the white. Moving very slightly, in no wind.

Behind him, Maigo made a sound.

***

The girl went first. Liraun.

Not onto the green. To the edge of it, at the place on the line nearest the ash. She put out her hand, long and pale, across the line, without stepping over it, toward the lowest branch. Toward the one green leaf.

She didn't touch it.

She held her hand there, a finger's width from it, very still. And the branch moved. Aldous saw it move. Not in the wind; there was no wind. The branch came down, very slowly, the way a branch comes down under the weight of snow. Toward her hand. Until the green leaf on the end of it was lying in her open palm.

She closed her eyes.

She stayed like that for a long time. The little tree man had come up beside her and was standing with his root foot on the very edge of the green, where the grass started and the red stopped hurting, with his hand over his mouth. Nobody else moved. The wind came back, a little, off the mountains, and moved the snow on the cairn, and didn't move the leaf.

Then Liraun opened her eyes and said something. To the tree. Very low. In the singing tongue.

And took her hand away.

The branch went back up. Slowly. And stayed turned toward her.

"What did she say?" said Aldous. He didn't mean to. It came out of him. He'd stood at a great many graves and never once asked what anybody said to the dead.

Maigo wiped his face on his sleeve.

"She said," said Maigo, "*thank you for holding*."

***

The old woman hadn't moved.

She was standing at the edge of the green with her stave upright and her face very still, and she wasn't looking at the ash. She was looking at the cairn. At the black stone. And then, Aldous saw, at neither of them.

Down.

At the ground under the cairn. Through the snow and the stones and the red earth. At something a long way down.

Her face had gone a colour he'd never seen it go. Grey. Not the birch-grey of her skin; the other kind. The grey a face goes when its owner has put a hand into the dark and touched something they didn't expect to be there.

She took one step back from the edge of the green.

So did the young man. So did Liraun, who'd been nearest, who'd had her hand across the line a moment ago. She drew it back against her chest and stepped back, once, with her pale eyes very wide.

"You feel it," said Yan. Quietly.

He'd come up on Aldous's other side, with the scythe on his shoulder and the snake round his neck. And the snake had gone tight and still round his throat, the way she did on the wall when the scouts came.

The old woman didn't look at him. She was still looking down.

"I've had a thread of it since my first week here," said Yan. "Under her. Asleep. Held." He was quiet. "It isn't her. It's under her. Something they put in, that didn't go when she did."

The old woman said something.

In the singing tongue. Very low. Aldous watched it reach the summoned: Yan's face, and Wendy's, and Salty's. He saw all three of them go still.

"She says," said Yan, slowly, "*it is not ours to name*."

Aldous looked at the old woman.

"Do you know what it is?" he said. Plainly. The way he'd ask a dying man if he wanted his mother sent for.

She turned her head and looked at him.

"We know it is not ours," she said. In the kingdom's tongue, each word by itself. "We know it was put. We know it is held." She looked at the ash, leaning toward her, and at the black stone, and at the place under it where its long spike went down into the ground like a key into a lock. "Held well. The stone that was a sword. The tree. The green, which is his hand, still on the ground." She was quiet. "And words. Said over it at the end of the summer. Under a white cloth."

Aldous didn't understand.

"You," said the old woman. "Chaplain. Nine years, nobody said anything over her. Then you came with your cloth and your wrong word, and said it anyway, for an hour." She looked at him with her winter eyes. "That holds too."

"Who put it?" said Wendy. From behind. Very quietly. "The cold thing. Who put it in her?"

The old woman looked at her for a long time.

"Not ours to name," she said again.

And turned, and walked away from the edge of the green, back across the red the way they'd come, with her stave, without looking round. After a moment the other two went after her.

***

They didn't speak on the way back. Not the three of them. Aldous walked behind them across the red with Group 3, and watched the grey grass turn toward them as they went, every tuft, and turn back after.

At the east gate, under the horns, the old woman stopped.

She looked up. At the horns. The great black curving horns of a demon general that a man had cut off on the red on the morning after, and carried home, and kept wrapped in oilcloth beside a lake for seven years, and sent east in a box to be hung where the scouts would see them. They'd hung over the east gate of Cinderhold for two years.

She looked at them for a long time.

"His," she said. Not a question.

"The man with the rod," said Aldous. "The Black Blade. He took them off the general. The morning after."

The old woman nodded slowly.

"The one who buried her," she said. "Under the sword."

"Yes."

"Is he coming?"

Aldous blinked.

"At the end of the summer," he said. "To the grave. He comes every year."

The old woman was quiet for a long moment, under the horns.

"We will not be here," she said. "We go home before your summer." She looked at the snake round Yan's neck. Then, for some reason, at Aldous. "Tell him," she said. "When he comes. Tell him the old ones came to see the Vael, and asked nothing, and went home. Tell him the thing under his wife is not ours to name. But it is held. And he holds it." A pause. "Even from his lake."

"The king will want to know you came," said Aldous. "I'll have to write to Solmere. If there's anything you'd ask of him—"

"Nothing," said the old woman.

Not unkindly. The way you'd tell a child that the river didn't want anything from them either.

"Tell your king we came," she said. "Tell him we went. Kings are short." She looked up at the horns again. "We have outlived a great many of them. We have never asked one for anything. We will not start with a good one."

And she went in under the arch, and the other two after her, and the old ones on the wall turned to watch them cross the yard to the chapel, every one, the way the grass had.

***

She asked him for the rite that evening.

In the chapel. After the rematch, which Rhea lost again, in one count, to the young man, and roared *AGAIN* so loudly that the old ones said afterwards they'd heard it in the north-west tower. After supper, which the three of them ate in the chapel because the long hall was too loud: dried fish, and water, and nothing else. After the summoned had gone up to the barracks, all of them except Yan, who sat on the bench by the door with the snake round his neck and his eyes half closed, and the little tree man, who'd fallen asleep at the end of the bench again because he said the chapel was warm.

The old woman sat on the floor in front of the fire with her stave across her knees and looked at Aldous, on his bench, with his book.

"The words," she said. "Say them."

Aldous looked at her.

"Over who?" he said.

She looked at the book in his lap.

***

He said them.

He opened the book at the first page, where the first name was, a boy of nineteen from the Western Watch who'd died on the wall on the first night, and he said the old words over him in the old tongue, the way his master had taught him in a cold chapel in the south. The whole rite. The whole hour. Low and slow, going round and round, rising a little and falling, the same shapes coming back. He'd always thought it rose and fell because that was how old words went.

And at the end, the last line.

*We lay white over them, for the—*

He stopped. It was in his mouth. *Soul Eater*. It had been in his mouth for thirty-five years.

He looked at the old woman.

She didn't say anything. She was waiting.

He said it the way she'd said it, in the gate, kneeling in the snow. The word his master had told him meant *Soul Eater*. The word she'd said to a white snake as if it were a name.

"*Vael*," said Aldous.

The old woman closed her eyes.

And then, very softly, she began to sing.

***

The same words. His words. The whole rite, from the beginning, the whole hour, over the boy from the Western Watch. But sung.

He'd never known there was a tune. There was. It had been under the words all along, the way a riverbed is under the water. Somewhere in thirty-five years of masters and their masters' masters, it had worn off, like the face off an old coin, and left only the shape: the rise and the fall and the going round. He'd been saying the shape of a song for thirty-five years and never once heard the song.

The old woman sang it. Sitting on the floor of his chapel in front of the fire, with her eyes shut and her stave across her knees, in a voice so low and so old that it didn't sound like a voice at all. It sounded like wind in a forest a long way off. Going up and down the way her tongue went up and down, like water over stones. Round, and round, and back, and round again.

And at the end of it, the last line. And the name.

*Vael*.

Like a breath let go.

Aldous sat on his bench with the book open in his lap and his hands flat on the first page, on the boy of nineteen, and wept.

He didn't hide it. He'd stopped hiding it, at the grave, at the end of the summer. He sat there in his old chaplain's coat and let it come, the way you let rain come when you're out in a field with nowhere to go, and listened to a woman out of a song sing the words he'd said wrong for thirty-five years the way they'd been meant to be sung.

When she stopped, she opened her eyes and looked at him.

"Again," said Aldous. He couldn't help it. "Please. The next one."

She looked at the book.

"All of them?" she said.

"All of them," said Aldous.

***

Not the whole rite. There wasn't time; there wasn't time in a year. The rite was an hour, and there were fourteen hundred and twenty-three of them. The last line. Only that. The last line, sung, and the name.

It took them all night.

He didn't remember all of it. He remembered pieces. The fire going down and the young man, Oen, getting up without a word to build it again. The girl with the pale hair, Liraun, sitting by the little tree man's bench with her hand on his antler, singing the low part under the old woman's voice without being asked, the way the second voice comes into a song. The summoned, who'd gone up, coming back down one at a time in the dark, because they'd heard it through the walls. Wendy first, sitting on the floor by the door with her headphones round her neck and her eyes wet. Then Salty, very quietly, beside her. Then Niko, who made no sound coming in at all. And last, from across the yard, Rhea, who didn't say anything all night, which nobody would ever believe.

He remembered turning the pages.

One name. The last line, and the tune, and *Vael*. And the next. A boy from the Western Watch. A cook from the south kitchen. A woman from the laundry who'd gone up on the wall with a pot of boiling water on the second night because there was nobody else. Men from Harrowmere, a whole page of them, together, the way they'd been found. Eleven hundred and four. And then the numbers, three hundred and nineteen, where he'd stopped knowing names. For those he said the number, and she sang the line, the same, without a name, and it sounded no less for it.

He remembered the healers' page.

He remembered reaching it, and the whole chapel going quiet. The orderlies. The boy from the ditch. The three healers never found. And the line at the bottom on its own.

He said her name.

*Diwa*.

The old woman didn't sing.

He looked up. She was looking at him. Not at the book. At him. Waiting, the way she'd waited at the end of the first one.

And on the bench by the door, the snake lifted her head.

She'd been still all night, round Yan's neck, with her chin on his collarbone. She lifted her head off it now and looked across the chapel. At Aldous. With her two green eyes. Not cold. Not hissing. Just looking.

"She says," said Yan. Very quietly. Into the quiet of the chapel. "She says *you do this one*."

Aldous looked at the snake.

"She says you said the rite over her first," said Yan. "Before anyone. With the wrong word. She says the old woman's got the tune, but you've got her." He swallowed. "She says do it yourself."

Aldous's hands were shaking on the page.

He sang it.

He hadn't sung anything since he was nineteen. His voice was an old man's voice, low and cracked, and it didn't go up and down the way the old woman's went. It went up, a little, and came down too soon, and caught on the last word. But it was the tune. He'd heard it fourteen hundred times that night. He had it.

*We lay white over them, for the Vael, who carries*.

And then the name, in his own voice, on the end of the tune.

*Diwa*.

Nobody moved.

Then the old woman bowed her head, very slightly, the way she'd bowed it to him at the gate. And across the chapel, the snake laid her chin back down on Yan's collarbone and closed her eyes.

"She says *better*," said Yan.

***

Aldous wrote it down at dawn.

He sat on his bench in the grey light with the fire out and the chapel full of people asleep on its floor. The three tall grey ones in front of the coals, the blankets folded beside them. Group 3 and Rhea against the walls, in a heap, the way soldiers sleep. The little tree man at the end of the bench. Yan on the bench by the door, asleep, sitting up, with the snake round his neck, and his face doing something quiet.

He opened the book at the healers' page.

Under *A healer. Diwa*. Under *The rites said*. He wrote, very carefully, in his own hand, the way he'd written every line in that book for nine years:

*Sung.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he turned back to the first page. To the boy of nineteen from the Western Watch. And at the top of it, above his name, above all the names, where there'd never been anything, he wrote the last line of the rite. Not the way his master had taught him. The way it was meant to be said.

*We lay white over them, for the Vael, who carries.*

And under it, smaller, because it was the honest book and somebody had to keep it:

*Vael: the Carrier. Not the Soul Eater. I said it wrong for thirty-five years. She heard me anyway.*

He closed the book. He held it on his knees, in both hands, the way he always held it. And sat there in his chapel at Cinderhold on the edge of the Redmarch, in the grey light, with eleven hundred and four names and three hundred and nineteen numbers on his knees, every one of them sung, for the first time, the way they'd been meant to be.

He didn't sleep. He didn't need to.

Outside, very faintly, from the east wall, he heard somebody salute. A fist, on a coat. He didn't know how you could hear that from a chapel. He heard it anyway.
