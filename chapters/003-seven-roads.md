The night before, after the hall had emptied, the Advisor called seven people to his study.

They came in the order he had trained them, and stood in a line in front of his desk the way they had when they were students: the seven guides of the summoned. He looked at them for a long moment. Then he took a folded paper from his sleeve and passed it down the line, and each of them read it and passed it on.

It was a description of a man. Height. Build. Hair. A way of standing. A fishing rod he was never seen without.

"If you ever see this man," the Advisor said, "he is a fisherman, and nothing more. He is not to be disturbed. Not by you, not by your guards, not by the people in your care. If he chooses to speak to them, that is his choice. If he doesn't, you will tell them nothing."

Six of them nodded without asking. The seventh, the only woman in the line, folded the paper very precisely along its creases and handed it back.

"Where does he live?" Maren asked.

The Advisor looked at her with something like regret.

"Near you," he said.

***

Down the corridor, in the long room where the thirty-five had been given cots for the night, nobody slept much.

Niko gave up first. He'd been lying on his back reading his status window for an hour, and it had stopped telling him anything new, so he got up to find out what everyone else's said.

It turned out everyone else had the same idea.

By midnight the cots had been dragged into rough circles and the room looked like the last hour of a LAN party: people sitting cross-legged on each other's blankets, windows open, comparing. The layout was the same for all of them. Name, tier, path, skills, inventory. Every *Path* read *none*. Every *Skills* tab was empty. Most of the progress bars sat at zero, or one, or two.

"Forty-eight," said Thea, when Niko asked, and the room went briefly quiet.

"*How?*" said Malcolm, from two circles over, sounding personally betrayed.

"I don't know." Thea hesitated, then seemed to decide something. "I see it differently. Under the window there's structure. Like code. I can't read most of it yet, but I can see it."

"Show me," said Chris at once, and came over with his blanket around his shoulders like a cape. Cursiffe drifted after him, the ink on the back of his hand already smudged. Within ten minutes the three of them were bent over Thea's window together, Chris asking questions in a language that was mostly programming, Cursiffe asking them in a language that was mostly grammar, and Thea answering both. Niko listened until he understood about a third of it, which was more than he'd expected.

Over by the windows, Hyvred was going from cot to cot telling people what their light looked like. Everyone had some, she said, very faint, just under the skin. Rachael's was the brightest of anyone who'd arrived as themselves, warm and gold where the Advisor had touched her wrist. Salty's was "like a stove." Niko's, she told him, was two colours that didn't mix, and she didn't know what that meant.

"Neither do I," said Niko, and kept it.

Kei explained Hisui to a growing audience, with the weary patience of a man who'd already explained it four times. Only he could hear the snake. The snake had opinions. The snake, at the moment, thought Suzu was very rude for not saying hello.

"She can't talk," said Yan.

Kei listened to something nobody else could hear. "He says that's no excuse."

Suzu hissed. Hisui, apparently, hissed back. Maigo laughed so hard he fell off a cot.

A little later, Niko looked over and saw Yan sitting on the edge of his cot with a heel of bread from supper in one gloved hand and a strip of cold mutton in the other, holding them up to the white snake round his neck one after the other. She tasted each one with her tongue and turned her head away.

"She's hungry," said Yan quietly, when he saw Niko looking. "I can feel it. She won't eat."

"Mice?" said Niko. "Snakes eat mice."

"I asked one of the guards. He looked at me like I was mad." Yan put the bread down on the blanket. "It isn't that. It's not that kind of hungry."

Across the room, Kei listened to his wrist for a moment, and sighed. "Hisui says she's not that kind of snake," he said. "He says you'll work it out." A pause. "He says he's not telling you what kind, because she was rude to him."

It was nearly two in the morning when Deston said the thing that mattered.

"We're being split up tomorrow," he said. "Seven groups, seven directions. We need a way to keep talking."

"Letters?" said Higu.

"Letters." Deston looked around the room. "The window translates what they say, and what we read. It doesn't help us write their script. So everything we write, we'll write in English." He let that sit. "And nobody in this kingdom can read English."

The room went quiet again, differently.

"So we write to each other," said Xav slowly. "About all of it. What we find. What our windows do. What they don't tell us."

"And nobody reads it but us," said Hanarisu, from her corner. It was the first thing she'd said all night.

They spent the last hour writing down who knew whom, so that every group would have someone to write to. Niko fell asleep at last with Thea's forty-eight percent still turning over in his head, and the odd certainty that, for the first time since they'd woken on that floor, they had done something the kingdom hadn't planned for.

***

In the morning there were seven wagons in the castle courtyard, and seven pairs of strangers waiting beside them.

At breakfast Deston had asked the Advisor whether letters could travel between the postings, and the Advisor had agreed that they could go with the royal couriers. He hadn't asked what language they would be written in.

Chris liked theirs immediately, because the guide was waving.

"Group seven! My stars! Over here, over here, look at you all!" He was a broad, bright man in his forties with a voice built for filling stadiums, and a short cloak of a red so loud it was almost a sound. "Lucan. Torch-tier, at your service, and for fifteen years the master of the Lists at Valcrest, which you'll be seeing very soon, because that's where we're going."

"What are the Lists?" asked Hako.

"Only the finest arena in the kingdom." Lucan's eyes found Ina, and the mask at his shoulder, and lit up like a man finding money in an old coat. "Oh. Oh, they are going to *hate* you."

"Thanks," said Ina.

"And then they're going to *love* you. Trust me. I've done this before."

Their guard hadn't said anything. She stood a little apart, leaning on the wagon wheel: a tall woman in plain leathers, with a pair of curled grey horns sweeping back from her temples like a ram's. Chris saw Winter notice them. He saw Winter go very still.

The woman pushed off the wheel and came over. She didn't look at Chris, or Lucan, or anybody else. She looked at Winter.

"Ysolde," she said. "Goat. Not a demon either." She tapped one of her own horns. "They'll stare at you in Valcrest. They stared at me for nine years. Let them. Then win."

Winter studied her for a long moment. Then, for the first time since any of them had woken up, she nodded.

"Is there a ranked mode," said Newish, "or is it all casual?"

Lucan beamed at her. "Oh, I *like* you."

***

Xav's group had a guide who looked like he'd been carved out of an old door.

"Garrick," he said. He was nearly fifty, grey-stubbled and weathered, with a staff across his shoulders and the steady, unimpressed eyes of a man who had seen a great many young people walk into dangerous places and fewer walk out. "Torch-tier. I ran parties in the Hollow for twenty years before the Advisor dragged me out of retirement, so I'll be teaching you how a group survives a fight. First lesson. When something comes at us, who stands at the front?"

"Azac," said Xav.

"Me," said Azac, at the same time, and stepped forward.

"Good. Who keeps him standing?"

"Mal," said four voices.

"Who goes in quiet and finishes it?"

"RX."

"Who's watching for what we haven't seen yet?"

"John."

"And who calls it?"

They all looked at Xav. Garrick looked at Xav too, then back at the five of them, and his eyebrows went up a slow quarter of an inch.

"Where," he said, "did five city boys learn party roles?"

"Practice," said John, with a straight face.

Their guard was a lean, quiet woman of about thirty with a sword at her hip and a scar along her jaw. She'd introduced herself as Petra and then said nothing else at all. But Xav caught her watching him as he climbed into the wagon. She wasn't watching his face. She was watching his hands, and his feet, and the way he'd turned without thinking to keep the courtyard gate in his eye line.

He filed that away too.

***

Groups four and six would share the road for the first three days, as far as the river. Deston found that out when he saw Xav waving at him from the next wagon, and something in his chest unclenched that he hadn't known was clenched.

Their own guide was a soft-spoken man with ink-dark hair going grey, who introduced himself as Emrys, a Torch-tier mage and, until last year, a musician at the royal court.

"Here's my first confession," he said, as the wagon rocked into motion. "Most mages will tell you a chant is just words. I don't believe that. When I teach, a chant has pitch. Breath. Rhythm. A spell sung off-key fails." He smiled ruefully around at them. "My last students found that ridiculous."

Deston and Gabe looked at each other.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them needed to. Years ago, in the back row of a school choir, they'd learned to breathe on the same count.

"We won't find it ridiculous," said Deston.

The guard was a different story. Sabine was in her forties, wiry and sunburnt, with a mercenary's mismatched armour and the patient eyes of someone who had spent fifteen years guarding merchant caravans from things in ditches. "Let me be honest with you," she said. "I'm not here for the kingdom. I'm here for the pay."

Dennis sat up. "*Finally.* Someone honest."

"How much did you negotiate yesterday?" Sabine asked him.

Dennis told her. Sabine whistled. Within an hour they were deep in conversation about tier-based pay scales, and the rest of Group 4 had started looking at them both with mild alarm.

Calvin leaned forward across the wagon toward Emrys. "Okay, real question. Who's the Hero of the Redmarch? Everyone keeps not saying."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. "A man who has earned his rest," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It isn't."

Rachael sat at the back of the wagon with her face turned up to the sun, breathing, and didn't join in. Every so often she laughed at nothing, very quietly, as if she couldn't help it.

***

Hyvred's group wasn't supposed to go west.

"You were meant for the south," their guide said, a little breathlessly, for the third time. "Originally. He changed it last night. To me." He was a thin, earnest man in his thirties called Corvin, with a satchel full of maps and the slightly desperate air of someone who had been trying to get people to listen to him for a long time. "Which I'm delighted about. Truly. It's only that nobody ever wants to come to Thornwall."

"What's at Thornwall?" asked Tiger.

"Mountains. Sheep. A fortress nobody's attacked in a hundred years." Corvin hesitated. "And some things I've been writing reports about that nobody at court reads."

"What things?"

"Tracks," said Corvin. "Mostly."

He didn't elaborate, and Hyvred, watching him, saw the faint light under his skin flicker the way a candle does when a door opens somewhere. He was frightened. Not of them.

Their guard was the oldest of the seven, a broad, grey-braided woman with a patch over one eye and a sword across her back so heavy it made Hyvred's shoulders ache to look at. She'd given her name as Hild and nothing more. She sat on the driver's bench with her face turned slightly sideways, her good ear toward the road.

Hyvred climbed up next to her. She couldn't help it. She'd never been able to help things like that.

"You fight with one eye," she said.

"I fight with my ears," said Hild. "The eye is a courtesy." Her remaining eye moved to Hyvred's face, to the golden horns, to the glasses pushed up in her hair. "And you're the one who sees."

"I only just started."

"Mm," said Hild. "I only just stopped." She looked back at the road. "We'll manage."

Behind them, Bee was sitting with her arms crossed and both Pollen Puffs tucked under her chin, glaring at the back of Hild's head as if daring her to be unkind. Kei was having a low, furious argument with his wrist. Drew had fallen asleep against the side of the wagon and was smiling.

Hyvred looked west, toward the mountains she couldn't see yet, and felt something she didn't have a name for. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was the feeling of a door opening somewhere.

***

Group 3's guide was a big, slow, gentle man of about fifty, who looked at Suzu around Yan's neck and made a small, involuntary gesture with two fingers, the way an old woman might cross herself.

"Forgive me," he said, at once. "Old habit. I was a chaplain for a long time. Aldous. Bonfire-tier." He held out a hand to Yan, and Yan shook it, and Aldous held on just a moment longer than he needed to, his eyes on the white snake. "Where I served, we laid white cloth over the dead. It has been a long time since I saw something so white so close to a living man."

"She bites," said Yan mildly.

"I would expect nothing less."

"Where are we going?" asked Wendy.

"East." Aldous let go. "To the frontier, near the Redmarch. The demons came across there seven years ago, and they will come across there again. If you are going to learn to fight, it should be where the fighting will be." He paused. "And I know that land better than anyone living. I buried a great many people there."

Nobody said anything for a moment after that.

Then their guard leaned over the side of the wagon and slapped a hand down on the boards in front of Salty. She was young, not yet thirty, with cropped hair and an enormous grin.

"Rhea," she said. "Gleaming-tier. You. Arm-wrestle me."

Salty looked at her hand. Then at her face. Then he put his elbow on the boards.

"Oh no," said Wendy, to nobody. "There's two of them."

Maigo started a betting pool. Niko, who had been reading his status window with his chin in his hand, quietly put money on Rhea. Yan watched them all with something like peace.

But when the wagon pulled away, he saw Aldous turn on the bench and look back. Not at the castle. At the doors of the great hall, where the black sword still stood in its circle of dead silver. The chaplain looked at those doors for a long time, until the gate cut them off.

Yan didn't ask. He just noticed, and kept it.

***

Group 2 was the only one not leaving.

"The capital is where the answers are," their guide explained to Jared, with enormous enthusiasm, as they stood together at the top of the courtyard steps and watched the other wagons go. He was a cheerful, untidy man in his late thirties called Oswin, with ink on his fingers and a notebook he hadn't stopped writing in since he arrived. "Every record of every summoning in Aurelan's history is in the archive here, and I've read all of them, and I can tell you without the slightest doubt that none of them were anything like this."

"How so?" said Jared.

"Thirty-five people," said Oswin, "who all know each other. Summonings don't *do* that." He wrote something down. "Do you mind if I ask about the tail?"

"Not my tail," said Jared. "Ask him."

Milli, who did have a tail, gave Oswin a look of such pure impatience that Oswin wrote that down too.

Their guard stood at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded. Jared recognised her the moment he saw her. She was the woman who'd stepped in front of Newish yesterday, so fast nobody had seen her move.

"Kestra," she said, when he came down to her. "Reaching-tier. I'm from the king's guard. I'll be training you."

"You're the one who said only the strong can talk to him."

"I am."

"So if I get strong, I can talk to the king?"

Kestra looked him up and down: once, slow, like a merchant weighing a horse. Whatever she saw, she seemed to find it acceptable.

"Get strong first," she said. "Then we'll discuss it."

Behind him, Milli muttered something about the king owing them answers. Kestra's eyes slid to him. "Again," she said, flatly, and Jared had the strong feeling that word was going to follow Milli around for months.

Hanarisu stood at the top of the steps, watching the wagons leave, and said nothing. Kestra watched her doing it.

***

Group 1 left last, because they had the furthest to go.

"Wrenhaven," said their guide, Maren, when Higu asked. She was a neat, serious woman of about thirty, with her hair pinned so tightly that not a strand escaped. She'd checked their names off a list twice. "A town in the north, on the edge of the lake country. It's quiet. You'll be able to train without distraction."

"Lake country," said Xola dreamily. "That sounds so *pretty.*"

Gigichi had taken the seat at the very back of the wagon, as far from Thea as it was possible to get. Thea had taken the seat at the very front. PenguChi sat in the middle with Waddles in her lap, looking between them with an expression of infinite, patient sorrow.

Thea was looking at her window. Forty-eight percent. Under it, the line that led to the sword, and the sealed value she still couldn't read.

*Whose are you.*

Their guard was a big, scarred, cheerful man named Bram, who had introduced himself by saying he'd been a soldier at the border during the last war and was now, gods help him, a babysitter. Once the wagon had cleared the city gates and Maren had moved up to sit beside the driver, he leaned toward Higu and PenguChi and lowered his voice.

"You want to know about the Hero?" he whispered. "The one the Advisor wouldn't talk about?"

They did.

"Eight feet tall," said Bram. "Swear it. Lives in a fortress out on the eastern border, where he can keep an eye on the demons. They say he hasn't slept since the battle. They say he never puts his sword down."

"Bram," said Maren, without turning around.

"I'm not saying anything."

He winked at them.

The wagon rolled north, toward the lake country, toward Wrenhaven and the long road to its market, and five girls in clothes from another world watched the capital shrink behind them.

None of them looked at the paper folded in their guide's sleeve. None of them knew it was there.

***

When the courtyard was empty, the Advisor went back to the hall.

It was very quiet without them. The light through the high windows lay across the floor in long gold bars, and the circle carved into the stone had gone grey and dull, and in the middle of it the black sword stood exactly where it had stood all night. It had taken six soldiers and a winch to bring it here from the Redmarch. No one in the castle could lift it alone.

He didn't try.

He stood in front of it instead, with his hands folded in his sleeves, and looked at the notched edge, and the worn leather of the grip, and the place near the hilt where something had been scratched into the iron in letters nobody in this world could read.

On the anniversary, Aldous would take it back to the grave it had been borrowed from. Until then it would stand here, and he would come and look at it, and he would not ask its owner's forgiveness. He hadn't earned that yet.

"I'm sorry, Ars," he said anyway, very quietly, to the empty hall. "I brought you thirty-five strangers."

He did not know how wrong he was.
