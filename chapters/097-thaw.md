Calvin had known Declan for a year.

Not even. He'd met him on the trip to Malacca, the summer before, with the others. Deston and Gabe and Rachael and Dennis, who'd known him half their lives, and Calvin, who'd known Deston for two years and come along because Deston said *you'll like him*. An hour after they'd met, Declan had walked Calvin into his kitchen and pointed at a kettle and said *this is the kettle*, like it was a trophy.

It was the worst kettle Calvin had ever seen. Dented. Stained. The handle held on with wire. Declan had been so proud of it.

And Calvin had thought: *I like this guy.*

He'd thought there'd be more time. More trips. More kettles. He'd thought, the way you do when you're twenty-five and somebody makes you laugh in a kitchen, that he'd just met someone he was going to know for the rest of his life.

A year later, Deston had rung him at three in the morning.

He'd been the newest of them. He'd always been the one asking. *Who's the Hero? Why not ask him? What's the catch?* He'd asked Emrys on the river road, the first week, *who's the Hero of the Redmarch?*, and Emrys had said *a man who has earned his rest*, and Calvin had thought that was a strange way to talk about a legend.

He knew why, now.

He knew exactly what he was going to ask him. He'd known for a year. Since a night on a roof in the winter, when Deston said *it's him*, and Calvin had sat there in the dark and thought, very clearly, about one question. The one nobody else would think to ask. The one nobody else would *dare* to.

He was going to ask it the minute he saw him.

***

The Wren broke up in the first week of spring.

Calvin heard it before he saw it. He was asleep in the rooms above the chandler's on Rope Street, and he woke in the dark to a sound like the whole city groaning. Long and low, from the river, and then a crack like a tree going over, and then another, and then a roar.

He was on the roof before he was properly awake. So was everyone. The five of them in their shirts behind the chimney stack in the grey dawn, and Sabine in her coat with her sword, and Emrys with a blanket round his shoulders, looking down at the river.

It was moving.

All winter it had been white. Frozen from bank to bank above the city, from the lakes down, the way Emrys said it did every year, with the barges locked in at the quays and the children skating and the ferrymen out of work. Now it was breaking. Great grey slabs of ice as big as houses, coming down out of the north on the black water, turning, grinding against each other, riding up over the jetties and crashing down again. The whole river, as far as Calvin could see upstream, was a moving field of broken ice, roaring.

"Every year," said Emrys, hoarsely. "It comes down from the lakes. A week of this, and then it's gone, and the river's open all the way to the hills."

Deston had his hand out over the parapet. Flat. Palm down. Pointing north, up the river, the way he'd pointed every night all winter.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

***

Dennis bought the *Patient Widow* on the third day of the ice.

Calvin hadn't known he was going to. Nobody had. Dennis went out in the morning in his good coat, with Sabine, and came back at noon with a piece of paper, and put it on the table in the rooms above the chandler's, and sat down, and folded his arms.

"What's that?" said Gabe.

"A boat," said Dennis.

Calvin stared at him.

"The *Patient Widow*," said Dennis. "Salt barge. Forty feet. One mast, with a crosstree." He looked at Calvin. "Her owner died two years ago and nobody's been able to work out who she belongs to, so she's been tied up at the end of the long jetty rotting, and the harbourmaster's been trying to get rid of her for a year. He wanted her off the jetty before the ice brought another one down on top of her." He spread his hands. "I offered him a fair price. He said that was insulting. I listened to him say it. Then I offered him the same price again, and he took it."

"You *bought a boat*," said Rachael.

"I've been saving," said Dennis. "For a year and a half. Out of the stipend. A little every week." He looked round the table at them. "I knew we'd need something, one day. I didn't know what. I thought it might be a boat."

"How did you know it'd be a *boat*?"

"I didn't," said Dennis. "I had a feeling." He looked at Deston. "Somebody's been pointing north every night for four months. You can't walk north. Not with Rachael. Not with what's on the banks." He picked up the paper and looked at it. "So I bought a boat."

"I'll want a cut," said Sabine, from the doorway.

"Of what?"

"Of whatever you do with it after."

Dennis looked at her. Sabine looked back. And something passed between them that made Gabe, beside Calvin, put his face in his hands.

***

Calvin climbed the mast that afternoon.

He hadn't been up since the winter. The ice had made the long jetty too dangerous; half of it had gone in the first night, and the rest groaned every time a slab hit it. But the *Patient Widow* was still there at the end of it, tied up the way she'd been tied up for two years, with her one mast and her crosstree and her paint peeling off in long grey strips, riding the floes.

His boat. Their boat. He couldn't get over it.

He sat on the crosstree with his legs round the mast and the whole river spread out below him, the way he'd sat all last spring, feeling for the wind. The ice going past on the black water. The city behind him, the quays and the warehouses and the roof of the river-gate infirmary. And north, up the river, past the last houses and the long brown fields, the hills, and past the hills, very faint against the pale sky, nothing. Just a direction.

The wind was coming from the north.

It always did, this time of year. Calvin could feel it. Down off the lakes, cold and steady and strong, straight down the river into his face. Straight against them.

He sat there for a long time, feeling it.

***

They found a crew in a week.

Old Haskett, of Fallow's Reach, who'd been running grain up and down the western tributaries for forty years and had told Sabine, in the first spring, about the villages going quiet. *Visiting*, the people said. *Come back the same, except the dogs won't go near them.* Haskett knew the upper Wren better than anyone in Kingsferry. He took one look at the *Patient Widow* and spat over the side and said *she'll do*, and then looked at the five of them and Emrys and Sabine and said *where to?*

"North," said Deston.

"How far north?"

Deston didn't answer straight away. He looked up the river, at nothing.

"I'll know when we get there," he said.

Haskett looked at him for a long moment, the way an old bargeman looks at a young man who's said something mad. Then he spat over the side again.

"Two of my boys," he said. "And me. And you buy the food." He looked at Dennis. "And don't haggle with me, boy. I've heard about you."

Dennis smiled at him. Haskett, very slightly, smiled back.

***

Sister Maud came down to the jetty on the last morning.

The ice had gone. The river was high and brown and fast, running south with a few last grey floes turning in it, and the *Patient Widow* sat low in the water at the end of the long jetty with her sail furled and her hold full of salt beef and hard bread and Dennis's careful lists. The infirmary had come to see them off. Brother Tamsin, and three of the nurses, and a bargeman called Hob whose rope burn Rachael had closed in the spring, standing at the back with his cap in his hands.

And Maud. Old, fierce, Candle-tier, with her grey hair scraped back so tight it pulled her eyebrows up. She walked straight down the jetty to Rachael, through all of them, the way she walked through the infirmary, and stopped in front of her.

"You're going," said Maud.

"I'm going," said Rachael.

Maud looked at her for a long time.

"You remember what I told you," she said. "When you went up the tier. Every order in the kingdom will want you. And worse." She looked out at the river, north. "You're going up that river into country I've heard things about. Villages with no dogs. People who come back the same." She looked back at Rachael. "You'll be watched every hour. You'll *let* yourself be watched. You won't go anywhere alone. Not to wash. Not to pee. Not anywhere."

"I won't."

"Swear it."

"I swear it," said Rachael.

Maud reached into her apron. She took out a small leather case, old and worn, the kind a healer carries her needles in, and held it out.

"Mine," she said. "Forty years. My teacher gave it to me." She pushed it into Rachael's hands. "You'll need it more than I will, where you're going."

Rachael looked at the needle case in her hands. Her face did something Calvin had seen it do once before, in the hall, the first morning, when the Advisor put two fingers on her wrist and said *that will hold*.

"Sister," she said.

"Come back," said Maud. Very gruffly. "That's all. You come back to my infirmary and you finish learning how to set a collarbone properly, because you've been doing it wrong for a year and I've been too kind to say." She turned round. "Tamsin. We have patients."

She walked back up the jetty without looking round.

Rachael stood there with the needle case in her hands and watched her go, and Calvin saw Maud's shoulders, at the top of the jetty, and saw them shake, just once, before she went round the corner of the customs house.

***

They pushed off at noon.

Haskett at the tiller, roaring at his boys. The boys on the poles, pushing them out from the jetty into the current. Sabine at the bow with her sword across her knees, watching the banks. Emrys on a coil of rope amidships, with his blanket. Rachael beside him, with the needle case still in her hands. Dennis at the rail with a ledger, already writing. Gabe in the stern, very large, very quiet, looking back at Kingsferry going away behind them, the quays and the warehouses and the south-wharf forges where he'd made his first blade.

And Deston at the bow, beside Sabine. With his hand out over the water. Pointing north.

The current took them. South.

That was the problem. Calvin felt it the moment the poles came out of the mud: the whole brown weight of the Wren, high with the melt, running south to the sea, and the *Patient Widow* sitting on top of it like a leaf. The boys poled. Haskett roared. They went north, slowly, against it, a few yards at a time, hugging the bank where the current was weakest.

"Sail," said Haskett. "Get that sail up, boys, and we'll see what the wind does."

They got the sail up. It filled.

With the north wind. Straight down the river, straight into it, straight against them. The sail bellied backwards and the mast creaked and the *Patient Widow* stopped going north at all and started, very gently, to go south.

"Get it down," roared Haskett. "*Down*, you idiots, before we're back at the jetty!"

They got it down.

Haskett looked at the river. He looked at the sky. He spat over the side.

"North wind this time of year," he said. "Always is. Off the lakes. Blows for a month, sometimes two." He looked at the five of them. "Poles and the towpath. That's how we'll go. Two months to the lake country, maybe. If we're lucky. If the towpath's not under water."

Deston's hand, at the bow, didn't move.

Two months.

***

Calvin climbed the mast.

He didn't ask anyone. He just went, up the ratlines, the way he'd gone up every mast on the Kingsferry waterfront by the end of the first winter, and sat on the crosstree with his legs round the mast and the river below him, brown and fast and going the wrong way.

The wind hit him in the face. Cold. Off the lakes. Straight down the river. He could feel all of it from up here, the way he'd always been able to: the whole long body of it, coming down out of the north, pushing.

He'd been asking it things for a year and a half.

*Turn.* That had been his word. Since the first spring, up here, on this same crosstree. He'd felt the wind getting ready to change, a breath being drawn in another room, and instead of waiting for it, he'd asked. *Turn north.* And it had. For three heartbeats. Then six. Then, by the end of the summer, a whole minute, round his head, on the crosstree. He'd turned it in the courtyard of the infirmary on the night the hunter came, for three heartbeats, enough to shove a thing the size of a cart three paces sideways.

Never long enough to matter. Never more than a breath.

He sat on the crosstree and felt the north wind come down the river, cold and steady, a month of it, two months, the way Haskett said. And he looked at Deston, at the bow, small from up here, with his hand out flat over the water, pointing north, the way he'd pointed every night for four months.

Two months.

Calvin had known Declan for a year. Not even. He'd thought there'd be more time.

He wasn't going to wait two more months.

***

He didn't ask it to turn.

That was the thing he understood, sitting there. He'd been asking the wind to turn for a year and a half. *Turn. Turn. Go the other way.* And the wind had done it, a little, for a breath, because he'd asked nicely. And then gone back to what it wanted, because what it wanted was to blow.

He'd been asking it to stop being itself.

Chris had said it, in the summer, in his list: *they're all asking something to do what it already wants to*. Maigo's *Stay*. Rachael's *Mend*. Higu's *Go and see*.

The wind didn't want to turn. The wind wanted to *carry* something. That was all wind was. It wanted to pick things up and take them somewhere. Leaves. Seeds. Sails. It was the most generous thing in the world. It just didn't care where.

Calvin found the edge. It was right there, at the top of the mast, in the cold, where it had been all along. He didn't ask the wind to turn.

He asked it where he wanted to go.

*Carry us.*

***

The wind changed.

Not turned. *Changed*. Calvin felt it happen, all the way down the river, the whole long cold body of it, coming down out of the north. It didn't stop. It didn't go the other way. It came down the river the way it always had, and then, round the *Patient Widow*, just round her, round her sail and her mast and her crosstree and the young man sitting on it, it *bent*. Like water bending round a rock. Like a hand cupping round a flame.

And came at her from behind.

"*SAIL*," Calvin screamed. "*HASKETT. GET THE SAIL UP.*"

Haskett looked up at him. Haskett, who'd been on the river forty years, looked up at a young man on a crosstree screaming at him, and then looked at the water round the boat, at the little ripples on it, and saw them going the wrong way. North. Upriver. Against the current, against the whole rest of the river, where everywhere else the wind was blowing south.

Haskett didn't say anything. He just roared at his boys.

The sail went up.

It filled.

***

It filled *forward*.

The canvas bellied out ahead of the mast, the right way, the way a sail is supposed to fill, with the wind behind it. The mast groaned. The ropes sang. And the *Patient Widow*, forty feet of salt barge with peeling paint and a hold full of Dennis's lists, leaned forward into the brown water and *went*. North. Up the river. Against the whole weight of the Wren running south. Faster than any boat on that river had ever gone north in spring.

Calvin clung to the mast with both arms and screamed with laughter.

He felt it the whole time. The wind round them, bent, cupped, carrying. He wasn't pushing it. He couldn't have pushed it; it was the whole north wind. He was just *there*, at the top of the mast, where it could feel him, and it was doing what it had always wanted to do. Carrying something. And he'd told it where.

Below him, on the deck, everyone was shouting. Haskett's boys, staring at the water going past. Dennis at the rail with his ledger forgotten in his hand. Emrys on his feet, with his blanket fallen off. Rachael, laughing. Sabine at the bow with her sword across her knees, looking up at him, with the faintest smile on her face that Calvin had ever seen there.

Gabe, in the stern, with his mouth open.

And Deston, at the bow, with his hand still out over the water, pointing north. Looking up at Calvin on the mast. With his face wet.

***

He opened his window when he could let go of the mast with one hand.

[ Calvin · Tier 1: Spark · Skills: Tailwind ]

The eighteenth.

*Tailwind.* He laughed so hard he nearly fell off.

It lasted all afternoon. He sat on the crosstree until his legs went numb, and the wind went on carrying them, bent round the *Patient Widow* like a hand round a flame, while everywhere else on the Wren it blew south and the other barges on the river stopped poling and stared at the salt barge going past them upstream under full sail with a young man on the mast laughing. It lasted until the sun went down. And then it let go, gently, the way you'd set something down, and the *Patient Widow* coasted into the bank at a village called Ferrow's Bend, half a day's poling upriver of Kingsferry, at the end of the first afternoon.

Haskett tied her up. He stood on the bank in the dusk and looked at the boat, and at the river, and up at the mast. Calvin was still on it. He couldn't feel his legs.

"Two months," said Haskett, to nobody. "I said two months." He spat in the river. "We've done four days in one afternoon."

"Can you do it again?" said Dennis, from the deck, looking up at the mast. "Tomorrow?"

Calvin thought about it. He felt for the wind. It was still there, all round him, coming down out of the north, cold and generous and waiting.

"Yes," he said.

***

He came down, eventually. His legs didn't work, and Gabe had to lift him off the ratlines like a child. They sat him on the deck with a blanket and Rachael felt his hands, which were blue, and told him off, and rubbed them until they weren't.

Deston sat down beside him on the deck.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He just sat there in the dusk, with the river going past, brown and fast and south, and his hand, for once, in his lap.

"How far is it?" said Calvin. "The thread. Can you tell yet?"

"No," said Deston. "Distance doesn't thin them. I told you." He was quiet a moment. "But it's warmer. Today. It got warmer all afternoon." He looked north up the river, at the dark. "Every time we moved."

Calvin didn't say anything. He pulled the blanket round himself and looked north too.

"You know what you're going to say to him?" said Deston. "When we get there?"

"Yes," said Calvin.

Deston looked at him. "What?"

Calvin thought about a kitchen in Malacca. A dented kettle with its handle held on with wire. A man he'd known for an hour, so proud of it.

"It's a question," he said.

"What question?"

Calvin smiled at the dark.

"You'll see," he said.

***

He posted it in the channel that night, from the deck of the *Patient Widow*, tied up at Ferrow's Bend under the stars. *Spark. Tailwind. Eighteenth. I asked the wind to carry us instead of asking it to turn, and it did, all afternoon.*

He didn't say where. He didn't say *boat*. He didn't say *north*. None of them did.

The channel went the way it always went. Maigo in capitals, *EIGHTEENTH*. Rachael, from three feet away, who wrote *he couldn't feel his legs* because she couldn't resist. Tiger: *TAILWIND IS SUCH A GOOD NAME*. Yan, from Cinderhold: *I felt your candle go up.*

Then Xav, from Duskhollow, upriver, alone, one line:

[ 22:41 · Xavier: see you soon. ]

Nobody in the whole channel knew what it meant, except five people on a salt barge tied up at a village called Ferrow's Bend.

Calvin closed his window, and lay back on the deck under the stars with the blanket round him, and felt the north wind coming down the river over his face, cold, generous, waiting for the morning.

*Carry us*, he thought.

He thought about a kettle. And a question.

He fell asleep smiling.
