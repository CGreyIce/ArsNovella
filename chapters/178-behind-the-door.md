Severin had never once in his life come to a forge and found it ready for him.

He'd lit his own fires since he was six. In Brakewater, in his father's smithy, before light, with the cold coming in under the door; in the army, in field forges under canvas in the rain, with a sergeant shouting; in the castle forge at Solmere, which had a smith of its own and three apprentices and a great many people who would have lit it for him if he'd let them, and which he lit himself every morning he couldn't sleep, because lighting it was half the point.

He came down from the cabin on the second morning with his roll over his shoulder and the red hen under his arm, and stopped on the shingle.

The sailcloth was up.

He'd left it folded. A year ago, on the last morning, he and Ars had taken the long roof down off its poles and folded it and given it to Garrow to keep dry in his loft over the winter, because the man had no use for a forge on his shore and the king had no business expecting one. He'd thought, if he thought about it at all, that it would still be in Garrow's loft, or cut up for a cart cover, or gone.

It was up. On its poles, over the long stone hearth the town of Wrenhaven had carried down the lake road a stone at a time. Taut and pale in the grey light, with the frost on it. And under it, the hearth swept. A stack of charcoal against the end wall, dry, under a tarpaulin. The anvil, uncovered, oiled. Water in the quench. The bellows mended, new leather on the left one. Two hammers on the bench, a sledge and a hand hammer, both his, that he'd left behind a year ago and never missed. Cleaned.

And on a sack by the bellows, asleep sitting up with his arms folded and his chin on his chest, a gangling boy of fifteen or so with soot on his face and a blanket round him.

Severin stood on the shingle with the hen under his arm and looked at it all for a long time.

"Garrow's boy," said Ars, behind him. He'd come down with the cups. "Pip. They came down in the night. Garrow and Pip and the Tallow boys. I heard them. I didn't go out." He held out a cup. "Garrow said, at the gate, last night, that a good forge ought to be working. Not folded up in somebody's loft."

Severin took the cup. He didn't drink it. He stood there looking at a forge somebody had made ready for him in the dark without being asked, and found that his throat had gone tight.

"He got that from me," he said, after a while.

"He got it from your father."

"He got it from my father," Severin agreed.

The boy on the sack woke up. He saw who was standing over him. He went the colour of a hot iron and got up so fast he fell over the bellows.

***

The leaf was in the roll.

He'd carried it nine days. Between two thin boards, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a smith's twist of wire, in the middle of the roll between the tongs and the fullers where nothing could bend it. He hadn't told anybody. Halvard knew; Halvard knew everything. Kestra knew, because Kestra had watched him pack it, and written down that he'd packed it, and he'd read that page on the second night on the road and said nothing and she'd seen him read it and said nothing back, which was how they did things.

He took it out at the bench under the sailcloth, while Pip lit the fire because it was very plainly the only thing in the world the boy wanted to do. He undid the wire. He unwrapped the oilcloth. He lifted the top board.

One sheet. Oiled paper, the thin hard kind Oswin used for his copies, that you could see a lamp through. Both sides covered, edge to edge, in lines.

He'd watched Oswin make it. Not all of it; he'd come down the ninety-one steps perhaps one night in five, late, after council, and sat on the bottom step out of the light and watched. The archivist at the long table under the lamps, with a grey leaf on a stand in front of him and the oiled sheet laid over a board, and a reed pen, and three different inks, and a glass. Copying. A line at a time. Holding his breath, Severin had thought, for the length of every line, and letting it out when the line ended, and taking it again for the next. Eighty nights, for one leaf. Both sides.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing Severin had ever seen a man make with a pen.

He couldn't read a mark of it.

Nobody could. That was the point. Three hundred years ago a king called Aldemar had given twenty years and the best scholars in Aurelan to the trying, and got bad guesses, and shut the leaves behind a door that only opened for a king who was asking for somebody else, so that nobody would burn them while somebody looked for the one who could.

Severin had found the one who could. He'd found her in a hall in Solmere, two years ago, in a heap on the floor in the middle of a circle, with thirty-four other people from another world, and not known it.

She was coming down the lake road now. He could feel her. A candle at the third tier, steady and bright, the brightest of the five coming down the road from the militia hall in the cold.

He put the board back over the leaf, gently, and waited.

***

He'd said *no* in the vault.

He thought about that while he waited. He'd thought about it most nights on the road north and a good many nights before that. The cat girl's husband, the tall still one with the tail, standing at the end of the shelves with the girl's hand in his, very calm, saying *that's not going to happen to us. Is it*. And not asking Oswin. Not asking anybody. Asking him.

And Severin had said *no*.

He didn't make promises. He'd said so, at his own table, in Valcrest, in front of all thirty-five of them. *Proof would've been a lie*. A smith didn't promise a blade before the iron was in the fire; he said *I'll see what it wants to be*. A king didn't promise a war. He'd been a king a long time, and a soldier longer, and he had learned in both trades that a promise was the most expensive thing a man could make, because somebody else always paid for it if you were wrong.

He'd said *no* anyway. In a vault, at midnight, with a letter from a dead king in Oswin's hand. *They asked me every day for nine years to send them home. Neither of us was answered*. And then the record stopped. Not *went home*. Not *died*. The page ended. Thirteen people, in four hundred years. And the tall still man with the tail had looked at him, and Severin had looked back, and said the only word there was.

He'd carried it seventy-five days. It was heavier than the leaf.

***

Group 1 came under the sailcloth in a row, the way they did everything.

The tall one with the pink and violet streaks in her hair. The little one with the penguin, who bowed to him, gravely, and the penguin bowed too, or fell forward; he couldn't tell. The one with the squid, already crying, apparently about the forge. The quiet one with the white bow and the notebook, who looked at the leaf on the bench before she looked at anything else, and then at him, and then wrote something down. And the silver-haired one, last, with mud on her boots and her arms folded and a scowl, who'd grown a hedge round a man's pumpkins in one night and was, the mayor had told him, *not discussing it*.

He looked at her. She glared back at him. He liked her immediately.

"Majesty," said the tall one. Thea. Althea. He'd learned their names at a long table in Valcrest and never forgotten one. She was looking at the board on the bench. She'd felt it. He could see that she'd felt it, coming down the road, the way he'd felt her. "Is that—"

"Oswin's," said Severin. "The first one. Both sides. Eighty nights." He lifted the board off. "He asked me to bring it. He said—" He stopped, and said it the way Oswin had said it, at the top of the ninety-one steps at dawn, with ink on his chin and his hands shaking. "*Ask her if I'm wasting a year*."

Thea looked at the leaf.

She didn't touch it. Not at first. She stood over the bench with her hands at her sides and looked down at it, the way Severin had seen her look at the circle, at Oswin's ten-foot copy on the floor of the archive room in Valcrest: all of her going still, and quiet, and inward, the way a smith goes still when he's listening to a blade cool. Her eyes moved. Not over the lines. Somewhere just in front of them. Somewhere he couldn't see.

The others didn't move. Even the squid.

Behind him, Severin felt Ars come and stand at the end of the bench. Not close. Just there. With the rod on his shoulder, though he wasn't going fishing, because he never put it down.

Thea put out one hand, and laid two fingers on the edge of the oiled paper.

***

It took a long time. Severin didn't know how long. The fire came up behind him in the hearth, red and then orange, Pip working the bellows with tremendous concentration and his eyes on the girl. The lake was flat and white under the frost. A heron stood in the reeds at the near end and didn't move either.

Thea's lips moved. She didn't say anything out loud. She was reading something. Not the leaf. Something the leaf was saying to her, through her window, in a language she'd learned by writing in it.

Then she breathed out.

"It parses," she said.

Severin didn't know what that meant. He'd heard her say it before, in Valcrest, over the circle. He knew it was good from the way the quiet one with the notebook let her breath go.

"It's real," said Thea. She didn't look up. "It's not noise. It's not pictures. It's the same language as the middle of the circle. Rings nine to thirteen, the oldest ones, the ones Cursiffe wrote *don't touch* on." Her fingers moved on the edge of the paper. "Same hand. Same everything. It's like—" She stopped. She looked for the word. "It's like finding the notebook of the person who built the house. In their own writing. With all their working in it."

"Can you read it?" said Severin.

"No," said Thea.

She said it quite simply. The way Oswin had said it in the vault, with a grey leaf in his hands. *No*.

"Not yet," she said. "Not at Lantern. It's enormous. It's—" She looked up at him, finally, and her face was doing something he'd seen on young smiths' faces the first time they put their hand on a really good blade and felt how much they didn't know. "I can see that it's *saying* something. All of it, edge to edge, both sides. Not decoration. Not a prayer. Instructions. I can see the shape of instructions, the way you can see a recipe in a language you can't read, because there's a list and then there's a method." She swallowed. "I can't read what it says. I'm three tiers in. It's going to take more than three."

"How many more?"

"I don't know." She looked at the leaf. "More. But it's not *impossible*. That's the thing. Last year I didn't know if anything like this was even in the language. Now I've got one page of it. In my hand. And it parses."

She was quiet a moment.

"Tell Oswin he's not wasting a year," said Thea.

***

"There's something else," said Thea.

She'd bent over the leaf again. She'd stopped looking at it the way you look at a cathedral and started looking at it the way you look at a column of sums. Her finger went to a place near the top, on the left. And another, halfway down. And a third, near the edge, on the right, where the lines ran close.

"Here," she said. "And here. And here."

Severin leaned over. He looked where she was pointing. Lines, going round. Rings with lines in them. Small careful marks along the lines, like a ruler. He couldn't see anything wrong. It looked like the rest.

"It doesn't parse there," said Thea. "Just there. It's like—" She hunted for it again. "It's like a typo. A wrong letter in a word. The rest of the line's fine, and then there's one place where it's not quite what it should be, and my window stops and goes *what?* and then picks up again after." She traced one of them with the very tip of her finger, not touching. "He's off. By a hair. The line goes this way, and it should go—" She moved her finger the smallest amount Severin had ever seen a finger move. "That way. That's all."

"Can you mend it?" said the silver-haired one, from the back. "With your Patch thing."

"No." Thea shook her head. "I don't know what it's supposed to say. I'd just be guessing. That's what Aldemar's scholars did." She looked at the three places. "Patch only fixes things when I can see what they meant to be."

"Then how—"

"He has to get it right," said Thea. "Oswin. Exactly. Every line. Thirty-nine more leaves, both sides. If he's off by a hair in three places a leaf, that's—" She did the sum in her head; Severin watched her do it. "That's a hundred and twenty places where my window goes *what?*. I'll be able to read round some of them. Not all." She looked at the three faults. "And the ones I can't read round might be the ones that matter."

The quiet one with the notebook said, very calmly: "Where are they?"

Thea looked at her.

"The three," said Higu. "Where on the page. Are they where the lines are closest?"

Thea looked down. She looked for a long time.

"Yes," she said slowly. "All three. Where the lines run closest together. Where he'd have had to hold the pen stillest."

"Then it isn't his eyes," said Higu. "It's his hand." She wrote something in her book. "Or his pen."

Severin looked at the three places where the lines ran closest together. Where the archivist, at the long table under the lamps, on his eightieth night, holding his breath for the length of every line, had had to hold a reed pen stiller than a reed pen could be held.

He knew that problem. He'd known it since he was ten. When the work was finer than the tool.

"His pen," said Severin.

***

He sent the five of them up to the cabin for breakfast, because the squid had started crying again for reasons nobody could establish and the penguin was eating the charcoal. Thea went last. She stopped at the edge of the sailcloth and looked back at the leaf on the bench, the way you'd look back at somebody you were leaving in a room.

"Majesty," she said.

"Thea."

"Thank you for carrying it." She hesitated. "Oswin said you said you'd spend the kingdom. On it. On us." She looked at him. "That's a lot of kingdom."

"It's a lot of you," said Severin.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she went.

Severin stood under the sailcloth with the leaf. Pip was at the bellows, watching him with his mouth open, waiting to be told something. Ars was at the end of the bench with the rod on his shoulder. He hadn't said anything all morning. He was looking at the leaf.

Severin could see his face. He'd been able to since the first time he walked round him on the parade ground at Cinderhold, thirteen years ago; he'd been Ascendant thirteen years by then, and he'd thought, the first day, that the boy with the slab of black iron was the most ordinary-looking young man he'd ever seen, and wondered why the whole garrison said they couldn't hold his face. He'd learned later. He'd never got used to it. That he could look at a face nobody else in a room could see.

He looked at it now.

It was doing two things at once. He'd seen faces do that, on battlefields and at forges and once, in Brakewater, in a mirror. The first was simple. It was a man watching the best news of his life happen to somebody else. *It parses*. A page of instructions in a hand nobody alive could read, and a girl he'd known in another life standing over it saying *it's real*. Severin had seen that look on fathers at the Lists. He'd seen it on this face, on the east stand, the day a girl filled in a box on a roll.

The second thing was underneath. And it was older, and quieter, and Severin knew it the way he knew the sound of a blade with a flaw in it before it broke.

He didn't say anything about it. Not yet.

"I'll want a striker," he said instead.

Ars looked at him.

"Dividers," said Severin. "A pair, as fine as I can make them. Steel. Points you can split a hair with. And a ruling pen, with a screw in the nib to set the width, so the line comes out the same at the end as it went in at the start, every time, whatever his hand's doing." He put the board back over the leaf. "Reed's for letters. Reed's for poems. You don't copy the house-builder's notebook with a reed."

Ars looked at the leaf for a long moment.

Then he put the rod down.

Severin watched him do it. He leaned it against the end post of the sailcloth, carefully, where it wouldn't fall. It was the first time Severin had ever seen him put it down when he wasn't fishing with it.

"Where do you want me," said Ars.

***

They worked all day.

Not the dividers. Not yet. You didn't make fine work from a bar; you made the bar first, and the bar had to be right, and that took the morning. Severin had brought the steel in the roll, a short length of the best he had, Kharvos-folded, from a blade that had come over the red in the long war and been broken on a wall, that he'd kept in the bottom of a chest ever since because it was too good to waste on anything that wasn't worth it.

He'd decided, in the vault, that Oswin was worth it. He hadn't known then what for.

He drew it out under the sailcloth, in the frost-light, with Ars on the sledge and Pip on the bellows. The old rhythm. *Ting*, and the long pause, and *ting*. The king's small hammer telling the sledge where to fall, and the sledge falling, every time, exactly, the way it had a year ago on this shingle over a black sword. Ars struck the way he did everything now. Slowly. Without hurry. With the whole of the eighth tier in his shoulders held so carefully that the sledge came down on the steel no harder than it needed to and not a feather softer.

Severin had worked with a great many strikers. He'd never had one like that. A year ago he'd thought it was because the man had been a garrison boy who'd hung round the armoury and been chased out of the smithy at Cinderhold for touching the bellows. He knew better now. It wasn't the armoury. It was the fishing. Thirty years of waiting for a float to go down and not pulling until it was time.

They didn't talk much. You didn't, at a forge. You said *heat* and *now* and *again* and *good*, and the rest was the hammer.

But at noon, with the bar drawn and cooling on the hearth and Pip sent up to the cabin for bread, they sat on the bench under the sailcloth with their backs to the warmth and their hands on their knees, and looked at the lake.

Severin said: "You're doing it already."

Ars didn't ask what. Severin had known he wouldn't.

"Saying goodbye," said Severin. "A bit at a time. Every time one of them goes up a tier. Every time the girl with the code says *it parses*." He looked at the lake. "So that when it comes it won't be all at once."

Ars didn't say anything.

"I can see your face," said Severin. "I'm one of a few dozen people alive who can. I've watched it do that since the Lists. On the east stand, every time one of them won a round. At my table in Valcrest, when the tall still one asked me if he was going home. This morning, over the leaf." He was quiet. "You've been doing it since spring."

The heron in the reeds moved one foot, and put it down again, and was still.

"It isn't my door," said Ars, at last. Quietly. "The way home. Nobody brought me. I came the other way." He looked at his hands. "When it opens, they'll go back along the threads. All of them. Every one that wants to. And I'll be at the end of it, so they're not on their own. I told Rowan that. I meant it." He was quiet. "And then I'll be on the end of a jetty. The way I was before. Thirty-four years."

"With a girl," said Severin.

"With a girl," Ars agreed. "With a lake. And a cabin. And three hens that hate me." He looked at the water. "That's more than I had the first time. I know it is. It's a great deal more."

"But."

Ars didn't answer.

Severin let it sit. He'd learned that from his father, at the anvil. You didn't hurry a heat. You didn't hurry a man either, when the iron in him was that colour.

"I did that," said Severin. "Once."

Ars looked at him.

***

He'd never told anyone.

He'd thought about it, on the road north. Whether he would. He'd thought he probably wouldn't; he'd been not telling it for twenty-six years, and a thing you've not told for that long gets a kind of crust on it, like scale on a bar left in the rain. You have to knock it off before you can see what it is.

He knocked it off.

"My father," he said. "In Brakewater. He was dying for a whole winter. The year I was thirty-nine. I was Dominion. I'd been Dominion four years and stuck the whole time, and everyone saying *he'll be Ascendant before he's fifty* and me knowing I wasn't going to be. I got the letter at the front. *Come home, he's going*." He looked at his hands. "And I went home, and he wasn't gone. He went on not going. All winter. And I sat in that house and I did what you're doing. A bit at a time. Every morning. So it wouldn't all come at once."

"Did it work?"

"No," said Severin. "It never works. It just means you lose him twice. Once a bit at a time, all winter, while he's still sitting there across the table from you wanting to talk about the price of charcoal. And then once more at the end, all at once, the same as if you'd never bothered." He was quiet. "I lost a whole winter of him that way. I was so busy saying goodbye I didn't have him."

Ars didn't say anything.

"He died at the end of the winter," said Severin. "And I went into his forge the morning after the burial, because I didn't know where else to go. And there was an order on the wall. In his hand. Crooked. *Twelve plough blades, Ashdown farm, by the thaw. Half paid*." He breathed out. "So I made them. One a day. Twelve days. On his anvil, with his hammer, at his fire. I didn't think about the war. I didn't think about tiers. I didn't think about him, even, much. I just made the plough blades. Because they were the work he'd left, and somebody had paid half, and Ashdown would want them by the thaw."

He looked at the lake.

"On the twelfth night," said Severin, "with the last blade on the anvil, the world went quiet. And then it bent."

The fire ticked in the hearth behind them.

Ars had gone very still beside him.

"The songs say it was the last charge of the long war," said Severin. "I let them. It's a better song." He looked at his own hands on his knees. "It was twelve plough blades for a farmer called Ashdown in a dead man's forge. I've never told anyone. Halvard guessed. I never told him he was right."

He turned his head and looked at the face beside him that nobody else at this lake could see.

"I'm telling you," he said, "because you're doing what I did all that winter. And it doesn't work. And I'd rather you didn't lose a whole winter of them." He looked at the leaf on the bench, under its board. "They're here. All thirty-five. It's going to take years. The girl just said so. More than three tiers. A year to copy. Another to read. And then somebody's got to get to the top of everything, which nobody's ever done." He looked back at Ars. "That's years of them. Sitting across the table wanting to talk about the price of charcoal. Don't spend them saying goodbye."

Ars didn't say anything for a long time.

"What do I spend them doing?" he said.

Severin nearly laughed.

"Plough blades," he said.

***

They made the dividers in the afternoon.

That was fine work, and it was Severin's. He sent Ars back to the sledge for the forging of the legs, and then off it, and did the rest himself at the bench, with files and a stone and a glass, the way his father had taught him to do the fine work on a lock: slowly, a breath at a time, in the good light. Two legs, long and slender, tapering to points so fine you could hardly see where the steel stopped. A hinge at the top, round, with a pin through it that he turned on the bench by hand until it moved without a sound and stayed wherever you left it. And then the ruling pen, which was harder: two blades of steel like the two halves of a bird's beak, with a tiny screw through them that set how far apart they stood, so that the ink between them came out in a line exactly as wide as you'd set it and no wider, every time, whatever the hand above it was doing.

Ars watched him do it. He didn't talk. He sat on the end of the bench with his hands on his knees and watched the king of Aurelan file a hinge-pin by hand in the last of the light, and Severin felt him watch, and didn't mind.

At the end, when the light was nearly gone and Pip had lit the lamps without being asked, Severin took a small punch from the roll. A die. He'd cut it on the road, at the inn at Hethe, at night, in his room by a candle, after he'd gone up early and left Halvard and Kestra by the fire. A tiny thing, no bigger than a fingernail. An open hand.

He set it on the flat of the hinge, and tapped it once with the small hammer. *Ting*.

When he lifted it, there was an open hand on the hinge of the dividers. Small. Clean. With a crack down the middle of it, where the two legs of the dividers met and parted, so that when you opened them the hand split in two along the line of the crack, the way the seal had split on the door in the vault and fallen in halves on the floor.

He hadn't planned the crack. He'd cut the die whole. He looked at it now, the hand on the hinge, broken exactly down the middle where the steel parted, and didn't know whether he'd done that or the steel had.

"Aldemar's," said Ars.

"Aldemar's." Severin closed the dividers. The hand closed. Whole. He opened them. It parted. "He kept those leaves three hundred years so somebody could do this. He'd want it working." He turned the dividers over in his fingers. "Not riding about in somebody's coat."

He wrapped them in oilcloth with the ruling pen, and tied it with a twist of wire, and wrote on a scrap of his cream paper, the thick kind he kept for when he had something to say, in his big square smith's hand:

*Oswin.*

*She says it parses. She says you're not wasting a year.*

*Three places a hair out. All where the lines run close. Not your eyes. Your pen. Use these.*

*Take two years if you need them.*

*S.*

And tucked it under the wire.

"That's the second coin," he said.

***

He wrote the other letter after supper.

At the table in the cabin, with the terrible stew cleared away and the red hen asleep on his knee and Halvard across from him reading something of Kestra's that Kestra had apparently decided he could read, which Severin noted and said nothing about. Ars was on the hearth with the girl, mending a net. The girl was telling him about a pike. She'd told the whole cabin about the pike four times. Nobody minded.

Severin wrote on the cream paper. Slowly. He didn't write many letters with his own hand; he had secretaries for that, and he didn't enjoy it, and his hand looked like what it was, a smith's hand holding a pen as if it were a punch.

*Gabe.*

*I saw the grip you drew for the girl. In Valcrest, in my yard. I put my hand round it and I knew who'd made it before anyone told me. It fits how you hold a cup. Nobody taught you that. I didn't know anybody could.*

*Harl tells me you're the best striker on the south wharf and the only man in Kingsferry who doesn't burn his eyebrows. I've written to Harl. He says take him, he talks too much anyway.*

*Next autumn you're walking east with your friend for the tenth year. Come to the lake a week before. Bring your hammer. There's a forge on the shingle under a sailcloth roof, and an anvil, and a fire somebody in this town will have lit before either of us is up.*

*Strike for me.*

*I'll tell you what we're making at the anvil.*

*Severin.*

He read it over. He didn't change anything. He folded it and sealed it with a drop of wax off the lamp and pressed it with his thumb, because he'd left his seal in Solmere on a cushion with the crown, and wrote on the outside, *Gabe, Rope Street, Kingsferry, by the barge*.

Ars was looking at him from the hearth. Over the net.

"What are we making?" said Ars.

"I'll tell you at the anvil," said Severin.

Ars looked at him a moment longer. Then something happened to his face that nobody at the table but Severin and Halvard could see, and that Severin would remember, he thought, on the cold mornings in the castle forge for a long time.

He went back to the net.

***

Severin went down to the forge again at midnight.

He didn't know why. He hadn't been able to sleep, which was usual, and the rug by the fire was very hard, which was also usual, and the hen had gone to sleep on his chest and he'd had to lift her off very carefully onto the rug, where she'd glared at him with one eye and gone to sleep again. He went down the shingle in his shirtsleeves with a coat over his shoulders and stood under the sailcloth by the banked fire, and looked at the lake.

The jetty was dark. Nobody on the end of it. Halvard was asleep in the bed; Severin had heard him snore, faintly, for the first time in forty years, and been so astonished he'd lain awake listening to it. Ars was in the loft, he thought, or on the floor beside it. Kestra was on the step. She was always on the step.

He stood by the banked fire and thought about next year.

He hadn't thought about next year in a long time. Not really. A king thought about next year all the time: harvests, taxes, the border, the Council, the west. But that wasn't next year. That was the work. He'd stopped having a next year of his own somewhere round about the time he stopped having a father, and he'd never quite noticed it had gone.

Everybody in this kingdom had next year in their mouths now. He'd heard it all the way north. The cat girl had read it to him out of the channel, line by line, the night before they left, at his request, in his study, while he sat with his eyes shut.

*Next year*, Gabe had written, to the man with the rod, at the east gate of Valcrest. *The tenth. You and Deston and Xav*.

*NEXT MIDSUMMER*, the Master of the Lists had written, in capitals, by the cat girl's cords. *ALL THIRTY-FIVE. I HAVE ALREADY ORDERED THE PAPER*.

*AGAIN*, a swordswoman at Cinderhold with a scar across her scalp had said, at every dinner, by every account, which Severin understood perfectly.

*Twelve*, a short square man with crooked ribs had said, at a forge on the edge of the red. Just that. A list of dinners that had stopped at eleven and was going to go on. At the lake. With fresh peppers.

*NEXT YEAR*, on the stern of a leaking boat off the Gullhaven bar, in big letters, that a harbourmaster said was not a name but a threat.

*In the spring*, he'd said himself, to the captain of Thornwall, through the cat girl, about a pass and a ring of turned people facing it. *I'll come myself*.

And a forty-foot bracket in Valcrest with *The Thirty-Five* in the top box, and a box at the bottom of a roll that a girl had filled in in front of forty thousand people, and an archivist on the ninety-first step with a box of grey leaves in his arms saying *a year, more than a year*. And a girl with the code, this morning, under this sailcloth, saying *more than three tiers*.

Years of them. All of them. Sitting across the table wanting to talk about the price of charcoal.

He found that he had next year in his mouth too. He'd put it there himself, after supper, on cream paper, in his own hand. *Come to the lake a week before. Bring your hammer*. He hadn't written *next year* in a letter of his own in twenty-six years.

He stood there by the banked fire with his coat over his shoulders and the frost coming down on the sailcloth, and tasted it.

It tasted like the first morning in his father's forge after the burial. With the order on the wall, in a crooked hand. And the fire to light.

***

Behind him, on the shingle, somebody came down from the cabin.

He didn't turn round. He knew the walk. Unhurried, flat-footed, the way a man walks down to the water to see if anything's biting.

Ars came and stood beside him under the sailcloth. In his shirt, with a coat over his shoulders too. They stood side by side and looked at the lake, the two of them, the only two Ascendant-class swordsmen in four hundred years of records, if you counted the one who wasn't quite there yet, in their shirtsleeves at midnight by a banked fire, not saying anything. The way they'd stood a year ago on this shingle the night before the cut.

"You couldn't sleep either," said Severin.

"I was thinking," said Ars.

"About?"

Ars was quiet for a long time.

"Plough blades," he said.

Severin didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He put his hand on the man's shoulder, the way he put it on everyone's, the way you put your hand on a bar to feel if it's cooled.

It hadn't. Not all the way. But it was a better colour than it had been in the morning.

He left it there.
