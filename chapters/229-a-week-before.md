Gabe heard the oxen before anybody.

It wasn't magic. He wasn't Deston; he couldn't hear a thread from a mile off. He just knew the sound. He'd grown up, in the other life, two streets from a builder's yard where the lorries came in at six every morning, and he'd lain in bed every morning of his childhood listening to something very heavy being brought very slowly somewhere it didn't want to go. The groan of an axle under a load it wasn't built for. The way the whole sound of a thing changes when it starts going downhill and everything wants to run.

He was at the forge on the shingle, at dawn, keeping the fire. He'd kept it every morning since he came. You had to; a forge left to itself went sulky and ate coal and took half the morning to come right again, and Declan had dinner nine to cook that night on it, and Gabe wasn't having dinner nine cooked on a sulky fire. He was raking the coals over with the long iron and talking to them under his breath, the way Harl did, the way he'd never let anyone hear him do; and he stopped with the rake in his hand and listened.

Up the lake road. A long way off. Past the top of the hill, past the militia hall, past the square.

Oxen. A lot of them. And an axle, groaning.

And under the groan, something he'd never heard an axle groan about before. The particular low complaining note of a cart carrying something much heavier than anything that size had any right to be.

He put the rake down.

"He's here," said Gabe, to the fire.

***

It took them all morning to come down the lake road.

Everybody in Wrenhaven came out to watch. Gabe went up to the top of the shingle with the others, and the hedge emptied, and the militia hall emptied, and the square emptied too, he found out later, the whole town walking down the lake road behind the cart at the pace of the oxen, the way you'd walk behind a band.

Eight oxen. Gabe counted them twice. Big pale southern beasts, the kind they bred for the stone carts on the coast road, yoked in pairs, four pairs, leaning into it with their heads down and their great slow shoulders working. And behind them a cart. Not a big cart. That was the strange thing. A low flat iron-shod dray, no bigger than the miller's, built so heavy in every timber that it looked like it had been made by somebody angry; and on it, lashed down with chains and ropes and a web of leather straps, under a sailcloth gone grey with road dust, a long low shape. The length of a man, about. Not much more. Lying on its side, the way a man lies down.

Eight oxen for that.

Kestra walked at the head of the leading pair, with a goad in one hand and her other hand on her sword, and her face doing absolutely nothing at all, which Gabe had learned meant she was very, very tired of oxen.

And beside the cart, at the near back wheel, with his hand on the rail, walking, was the king.

Not riding. Walking. In a plain coat with the dust of the road on it, and boots, and nothing on his head; an old man with a smith's shoulders and a smith's hands, walking beside a cart with his hand on the rail the way you'd walk beside a horse you were bringing home. As if he'd walked all the way. Gabe found out later that he had. From Pellow's Cross, at the pace of the oxen, with his hand on the rail. He'd said the thing on the cart didn't like the road, and somebody ought to be with it.

The cart came down the last of the lake road to the top of the shingle and stopped, with all eight oxen blowing, and the axle gave one last long groan, like an old man sitting down.

The king looked at the forge.

Then he looked along the shingle, to the jetty, thirty-four planks, where a man was standing on the end with a fishing rod, with his back to all of it, fishing.

"I felt that," said the king, loudly, along the shingle. "At Pellow's Cross. Two mornings ago. At dawn."

The man on the end of the jetty didn't turn round.

"Everybody at Pellow's Cross felt it," said the king. "The innkeeper dropped a tray. Kestra drew her sword at the breakfast table and couldn't tell me why." He paused. "Was it the first one?"

On the end of the jetty, the rod dipped. The man reeled in, unhurried.

"Yes," said Declan, without turning round.

The king laughed. He put his head back and laughed, a big rough forge-shed laugh that went out over the lake and came back off the far hills, and the oxen shifted in their yokes, and the whole of Wrenhaven standing on the lake road behind the cart looked at each other.

"*Slice*," said the king, wiping his eyes. "He cuts a lake in half with *Slice*." He turned round, still laughing, and his eyes went along the people at the top of the shingle and stopped.

On Gabe.

"Smith," said the king.

***

Gabe had had the letter since the winter.

It had come down the river on the *Patient Widow* in the first week of the snow, with Haskett's elder boy, in a packet with the royal seal on it that the whole of the Kingsferry wharf had come to look at; and Sabine had read it out to him on the infirmary step, with Rachael sitting beside him, because it was in the king's own hand and Gabe couldn't read the king's own hand. Nobody from Earth could. It was the joined-up kind. Sabine had squinted at it for a long time and then read it in a voice, because Sabine always did voices, and Rachael had told her to stop and read it properly, and she had.

*Gabe of Kingsferry, smith*.

*Come to the lake a week before they go east for the tenth. Bring your hammer. Tell Harl I asked; he will understand. I'll tell you what we're making when you get there*.

*S*.

That was all. Sabine had read it twice and handed it back to him, and Rachael had said *well*, and Gabe had sat on the infirmary step with it in his hands for an hour without moving, and then gone back to the forge and worked till midnight on nothing in particular, because he couldn't sit still.

He'd guessed. Of course he'd guessed. He'd spent all winter guessing. Gabe at the anvil, a week before; Declan had said it himself, on the end of a jetty, to Higu, and Higu had written it on a list, and the list was on the cabin wall over the table. *Gabe*. Just his name. Between *Twelve dinners* and *East*. He'd seen it the first night he came, in the lamplight, and had to go outside.

He'd guessed a sword. Of course he had. What else did you make, with a king, at a forge on a shingle where a black sword had been cut in half? But he hadn't let himself think it all the way through, the way you don't let yourself look straight at something too bright.

Now the king was looking at him.

"Come here, smith," said the king.

Gabe went.

***

The king put his hand on the sailcloth.

He didn't pull it off. Not yet. He stood beside the cart with one big scarred hand flat on the grey cloth, on the long shape under it, the way you'd put your hand on a horse's neck; and looked at Gabe.

"Put your hand on it," he said. "Next to mine. Don't look. Just put it there."

Gabe put his hand on the sailcloth.

It was cold.

That was the first thing. It was high summer, nearly noon, the sun on the shingle hot enough to feel through your boots; the sailcloth had been lying in that sun on the road all morning and should have been warm as bread. It wasn't. It was cold. Not cold like stone; cold like the bottom of a well. A deep, steady, all-the-way-through cold that came up through the canvas into his palm and didn't stop at the skin.

And heavy. He couldn't have said how he knew that through a cloth. He knew. The way you know, picking up a box, before you've lifted it, that there's lead in it. The thing under the sailcloth was heavier than it had any right to be, in a way that went further down than weight; as if it were pulling, very slightly, on everything round it. On the cart. On the shingle. On his hand.

He'd felt something like it once before. On this shingle. At this forge. The second spring, the morning after Declan slept five hours, with Astra on the bench and his hands on the black steel. *Folded thousands of times. Not from here. Waiting*.

This wasn't Astra. It wasn't folded. It wasn't anything. That was the strange part. Astra, under his hands, had been full: full of work, full of somebody's hands, a thousand foldings, a long patient will. This was empty. Not empty like a box; empty like the sky at night. It had never been worked. It had never been hot. It had never been anything since it was made, wherever it was made, a very long way off; it had just been cold, and heavy, and quiet, for longer than Gabe could think about.

And it wasn't waiting.

It was asleep.

He took his hand off. He didn't mean to. He looked at it, his own hand, black from the morning's coal, and it was shaking.

"Yes," said the king, quietly. "That's what my father said."

***

He pulled the sailcloth off.

It came away in a long grey sheet, with the road dust going up off it in the sun; and the whole of Wrenhaven standing on the lake road made a sound, and then didn't.

It was a stone.

That was what it looked like, at first. A long rough stone, the length of a man lying down and about as thick as one, lying on its side on the bed of the dray in its web of chains. Dark. Not black; grey, a deep dark grey with a bloom on it like the bloom on a plum, and pitted all over, the whole surface of it, in little round dimples and runnels and cups, the way a stone gets in a river after a thousand years, except that it wasn't smooth the way river stones were smooth. It looked melted. As if it had been very hot once, all over, for a very short time, a long time ago, and run, and set.

And at one end, the near end, somebody had cut into it.

A long time ago. Gabe could tell. A single cut, a hand deep, as if with a hot chisel, and then given up on; and in the face of the cut, where the bloom was gone, the metal showed. Bright. Grey-silver. And through the grey-silver, inside it, a pattern. Fine lines, crossing and branching and crossing again, all the way through the face of the cut, like frost on a window on a winter morning. Like the frost on the inside of the cabin window that Alea had told him about, the first winter, that her father used to draw in with his fingernail when she was small.

"A star," said the king.

Nobody said anything.

"It fell when I was eight," said the king. "Into the river at Brakewater. At night. In the autumn. I saw it." He was looking at the cut, at the frost in the metal. "The whole sky went white and then there was a sound like the end of the world, and then the river was boiling, a mile below the town, by the old ford. Everybody ran. My father ran the other way. Toward it." He put his hand back on it, on the bare grey-silver of the cut. "He dragged it out of the Brakewater with every mule in the parish, three days after, when the river had stopped steaming. He was the only smith for twenty miles. He thought it was iron."

"It's iron," said Gabe. He couldn't help it.

"It's iron," the king agreed. "Of a sort. Not our sort. Too dense for any fire he had. He tried for a year. He broke his best hammer on it. He built a new hearth for it, and it didn't care. He cut that." He touched the cut. "With a hot chisel, a week's work, a hand deep, and then the chisel went soft, and he stopped." He looked at the frost-pattern for a long time. "*The iron tells you when it's ready*, he said. *If you let it*. And it hadn't told him. So he put a sailcloth over it, in the back of the forge yard, and left it. And it lay there for fifty-eight years."

"You didn't try," said Gabe. "When you were Ascendant. You could have—"

"I could have," said the king. "I thought about it every winter for twenty-seven years. I'd go home to Brakewater for the dead days and stand in the yard and look at it under its cloth. I could have got it hot. I could have made it do something." He shook his head. "It hadn't told me."

He looked down the shingle at the forge under its sailcloth roof. At the round hearth of stones that the whole town had brought, the autumn before last, children too, so that a king and a fisherman could cut a black sword in half.

"It told me last winter," he said. "In the yard at Brakewater, in the dead days, standing in the snow looking at it under its cloth, the way I did every year." He was quiet. "I don't know why then. I didn't ask. You don't ask. I wrote to you that night. And in the spring, when the roads opened, I had them dig it out of the yard and put it on the heaviest cart in the south." He looked at Gabe. "And I've walked beside it from Pellow's Cross, because it doesn't like roads."

Gabe looked at the star.

At the long dark melted shape of it, and the bloom like a plum's, and the one bright cut at the end with the frost inside.

"I'll tell you what we're making," said the king.

"A sword," said Gabe.

The king looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked past him. Down the shingle. Along thirty-four planks, to the end of the jetty, where a man was standing with a fishing rod with his back to all of it, not fishing any more. Just standing.

"His," said the king.

***

Declan didn't come up off the jetty.

Gabe watched him not come. All afternoon, while they got the star off the cart; all evening; he stood on the end of the jetty, or sat on the last plank with the hens, with the rod across his knees, and didn't come up the shingle once. Didn't look round. Gabe knew he'd heard. Everybody on the lake shore had heard; the king hadn't lowered his voice. Declan had heard *his*, and gone still, and then sat down, and stayed there.

He didn't say no.

That was the thing Gabe kept thinking, all afternoon, with his shoulder against a beam. He'd known Declan for eleven years in one world and two and a half in this one, and he'd heard him say no to a great many things. To the Lists, once. To coming back. To the king himself, on this shingle, the autumn before last: *you could come back*, and *not yet*, and once, a long time ago, *no*. He said no the way he did everything else, quietly, without fuss, and then it was no.

He didn't say it now. He sat on the end of the jetty and didn't say anything at all.

Getting the star off the cart took nine men, two ox teams, the brewer's dray's rollers, four greased oak beams from Garrow's yard, and the whole afternoon. They couldn't take the cart down the shingle; the shingle would have swallowed the wheels to the hubs. So they built a road. Beams laid end to end from the bed of the cart down the stones to the forge, and the rollers on the beams, and the star on the rollers, and every man on the lake shore with a rope or a shoulder or a pry-bar, and Kestra at the top with her goad calling the pulls in a voice like a gate shutting.

Gabe was on a rope. Then a beam. Then on the rollers, at the front, on his knees, sliding them back under as the star came off them, which was the most dangerous job and the one he'd been told not to do, by four people, and did.

Petra was on a rope. So was Alea, with Astra on her back. So was Garrow, sweating in his apron, and Rowan, in his chain, who was past sixty and had been told by Odette at the top of the lake road that if he so much as touched that rope she'd have the chain off him and give it to the hens, and who'd touched it anyway. RX was on a rope invisible, which helped no one and made Kestra so angry she said four words in a row. Azac stood between the beams and the water without noticing he was doing it. Xav pulled without speaking. Malcolm had a plan and nobody listened to it and it turned out to be right, about halfway down, when the beams started to spread on the shingle and he made them put cross-pieces in.

Deston didn't pull. Deston sat on the stones at the top with his eyes shut and his head on one side and called the pulls before Kestra did. Half a breath before. Every time. *Now*. And Kestra, after the third time, stopped calling them and let him.

"How," said Kestra, at the bottom, when it was done.

"It's got a note," said Deston. He opened his eyes. "Very low. Lower than the lake. It hums when it's about to move. You just listen for the hum." He looked at it, lying on the shingle by the forge in the evening light, on its rollers, dark and pitted and melted, with the frost in the cut. "It's asleep. It hums in its sleep."

Kestra looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the king.

"Yes," said the king. "They're like that. All thirty-five. You get used to it."

"I haven't," said Kestra.

***

Garrow brought the hammer down at dusk.

Gabe saw him come. Down the last of the lake road from the square, slowly, in his apron, alone, with his beard gone grey all through since the winter he was snowed in at Cinderhold, carrying something wrapped in a cloth in both hands the way you'd carry a loaf to a neighbour.

He came down the shingle to the forge, where the king was sitting on Gabe's bucket by the hearth with a cup of the lake's water, looking at the star on its rollers. And stopped in front of him. And unwrapped the cloth.

A hammer. A small one. A cross-peen, old, the head worn bright where it struck and dark everywhere else, on a handle of something pale and close-grained that had been oiled by hands for so long it had gone the colour of honey. Nothing special about it. Gabe had seen a hundred like it on a hundred benches. He'd have walked past it in a smithy without looking twice.

He didn't walk past it. He couldn't. He was standing three paces off and he could feel it from there. The way you can feel a fire through a wall. Not hot. *Used*. Used for a very long time, by people who'd known exactly what they were doing, for good honest slow work, plough blades and horseshoes and gate hinges, ten thousand mornings, until the handle had taken the shape of a hand and the head had taken the shape of the work.

The king looked at it.

"Salty brought it back," said Garrow. "From the fort. In the autumn, by your fisherman, because Salty said a good hammer ought to be working, not riding about in somebody's coat." His voice was gruff. "And the fisherman put it on my bench and said *Salty says it's a good hammer*, and I said *I know*, and he said *he said to tell you anyway*." He held it out. "So. It's a good hammer, majesty."

The king took it.

He held it the way a smith holds a hammer. Loose, at the end of the handle, with the weight hanging. He turned it over, once, and looked at the face of it, and at the worn place on the handle where a thumb went; and put his own thumb in it.

It fitted.

"My father's," said the king. Very quietly. To nobody. To the hammer. "He'd have hated this." He looked at the star. "He'd have loved it."

Garrow didn't say anything. He nodded, once, and turned round, and went back up the shingle to the lake road without another word; and at the top, Gabe saw, he stopped, and stood there in the dusk for a long time, looking back down at the forge, before he went on.

***

The king talked to him that night.

Not at the dinner. At the dinner the king sat at the long table in Gigichi's hedge, in the place at the head that nobody sat in, because Declan had put him there without a word and then gone back down to the forge; and ate nasi lemak for the second time in three days, *nine*, with Higu ticking it on the list on the wall at last because this time it was dinner; and laughed at RX and was laughed at by Xola and was settled, along with everybody else, by PenguChi, at about the point where Malcolm and Thea started on the queue system again. The king had looked at PenguChi for a long time after that, with an expression Gabe had seen on him once before, in the autumn before last, looking at a fox girl lifting a sword too big for her.

After the dinner. At the forge. When the hedge had gone quiet and the fire was down to a red eye in the middle of the hearth, and the star lay on its rollers beside it in the dark, cold, dense, humming in its sleep where only Deston could hear it.

Gabe was banking the fire. The king was sitting on the bucket with his father's hammer across his knees.

"You'll want to know why you," said the king.

Gabe didn't stop banking the fire. He'd been waiting all day for somebody to ask that, and had decided, round about the third roller, that he wasn't going to ask it himself.

"I'm Tier 1," said Gabe. "Drawn. I've made one sword. I've made it badly. I've made a knife and a sword and about four hundred hinges." He raked the coals over. "Harl's better than me. Garrow's better than me. You're better than anybody. He's—" He stopped. "You don't need me."

"No," said the king. "I don't."

Gabe looked up.

"I don't need you," said the king. "I can get it hot. Probably. It'll take everything I've got and a day of it, but I can do it; I can feel that I can, now, the way I couldn't for fifty-eight years. And he can strike. He's the best striker I've ever had; I told him so the autumn before last and I meant it." He turned the hammer over on his knees. "We could do it, the two of us. A week of it. We'd make a sword."

"Then why—"

"Because it wouldn't be *his*," said the king.

Gabe sat back on his heels.

"I made the last one smaller," said the king. "The black one. I cut it in half for a girl, so she could carry it. I did it well. It was right. But it wasn't making; it was cutting. It was taking something that was already somebody's and making it somebody else's." He looked at the star. "This has never been anybody's. It's never been anything. It fell out of the sky into a river when I was eight and lay in a yard for fifty-eight years being nothing. And whatever we make of it is going to be the first thing it ever is. For the rest of whatever time it's got, which is longer than any of us." He looked at Gabe. "I can make it into a sword. I can't make it into *his*."

"And I can?"

"You did it to hers," said the king. "Alea's. In the second spring. On this shingle. I'd drawn that blade, and he'd struck it, and it was a good blade, and it was still too big for her. And you sat on that bench with it for a morning and made her a grip from the way she holds a cup." He was quiet. "I've been making things for more than fifty years and I never once thought to look at how somebody holds a cup."

Gabe didn't say anything.

"And he put you on his list," said the king. "*Gabe*. Between the dinners and the east. Not *the king*. Not *the sword*. You." He held out his father's hammer. "So you're the smith on this one. I'll get it hot. He'll strike. You'll tell us both what it wants to be. And at the end you'll make it his."

Gabe looked at the hammer in the king's hand.

He didn't take it. He couldn't. It was the king's father's. He sat on his heels on the shingle by the red eye of the fire and looked at it held out to him in the dark, and felt his face go hot all the way to the hair, and didn't know what to do with his hands.

"Do it anyway," said Gabe. Out loud. To himself. The way he said it to himself at Harl's forge on the bad mornings, the way Declan had cut it on Sto with the king's chisel. *Audentes fortuna juvat*. Fortune helps the bold. It didn't mean that. It meant *do it anyway*.

He took the hammer.

It fitted his hand. Not like it fitted the king's; it had never known his. But it didn't mind him. He could feel it not minding.

"Good," said the king. He stood up off the bucket, slowly, an old man at the end of a long road. "We start at dawn. Bring yours too. The big one. You'll want it." He looked at the star in the dark. "And Gabe."

"Yes?"

"Go and tell him," said the king. "He's on the end of the jetty. He's been there since noon. He won't come up for me." He paused. "He'll come up for you."

***

Gabe went.

Down the shingle, past the land end of the jetty, past Gigichi's little tree in its ring of stones. Out along the planks. Thirty-four. He counted them, because everybody did. The lamp was lit on the last one. The hens were asleep in their row. Declan was sitting on the edge of the last plank with his legs over the water and the rod across his knees, looking at the lake.

Gabe sat down beside him.

They sat there.

He didn't know how to start. He'd sat on the end of this jetty with Declan at midnight once before, with Deston and Xav, the second spring, and said *we're not going to ask*, and Declan had told them everything. Gabe had never known how to start anything. He'd always just been there, and then whatever it was had started round him.

"It's asleep," he said, in the end.

Declan didn't say anything.

"The star," said Gabe. "It's never been hot. It's never been anything. It's cold all the way through and it's heavier than it should be and it's asleep." He looked at his hands. "The black one was waiting. When I had my hands on it. Astra. It was waiting for something. I didn't know what." He swallowed. "This one isn't waiting for anything. It's never had anybody to wait for."

The lake moved against the posts under them, very quietly.

"He wants to make it yours," said Gabe. "He said I've got to make it yours. At the end. Like the grip on Astra. From how you hold a cup."

Declan was silent for a long time.

"I don't need a sword," he said at last. Very quietly. "I've got the rod."

"I know."

"I gave the black one away. Half to the grave. Half to her. I was glad to." He turned the rod over on his knees. "I've not wanted a sword for ten years, Gabe. I didn't want the last one. I found it on a hill in Kharvos, standing in the ground, and I picked it up because I was twenty-one and stupid and there was a war, and I carried it through all of it, and then I put it in the ground over her and walked away from it, and I was glad." He was quiet. "I don't want a sword."

"I know," said Gabe.

He did know. He'd known since noon, watching Declan stand on the end of the jetty with his back to the cart. He'd known, really, since a night on this jetty in the second spring, when Declan had told them about the hill in Kharvos, and the black sword in the ground, and the general, and the red.

"He said it's not for now," said Gabe. "He hasn't said that yet. But he's going to. I can tell. He's going to make it and put it somewhere and say it's not for now."

"For when?"

"I don't know," said Gabe. "For when you need it. For when you leave the lake. For if." He looked at the water. "You said yourself. On the list. *East*. And then whatever comes after east. Thea's frames, and the seven at Thornwall who don't know their own names, and the Hollow, and the thing behind the bricked door in Duskhollow that's gone the colour of nothing. The king walked beside a cart for three days because something told him in the spring." He stopped. "I don't know what's coming. Nobody does. But if it comes, and you have to go, I don't want you going with a fishing rod." His voice had gone rough. "I want you going with something I made."

Declan didn't answer.

Gabe sat beside him on the last plank with his hands between his knees and his face hot and didn't say anything else, because he didn't have anything else. That was the whole of it. That was all he'd come down the jetty with.

The lamp burned on the plank between them. Across the lake, the far hills were black against the stars.

"From how I hold a cup," said Declan, at last.

"That's how I did hers."

"I hold a cup badly."

"I know," said Gabe. "In both hands. Close. Like somebody's going to take it." He'd watched him do it for two weeks, at the long table, at the forge, on the end of the jetty in the mornings. "Alea does it too. She got it off you."

Declan was quiet for a very long time.

Then he laughed. Not the big one, the one Xav had got out of him on the shingle, that had made him sit down in the lake. The short one. The one that went up at the end. Very quiet, in the dark, on the end of a jetty, at a big shy boy from Kingsferry who'd come down thirty-four planks to tell him he couldn't go with a fishing rod.

"Okay," said Declan.

Gabe looked at him.

"Okay," Declan said again. He put the rod down along the planks, carefully, beside the lamp. "Dawn?"

"Dawn," said Gabe. "He says bring the big hammer."

"Which one of us is he talking to?"

"Both of us, I think."

Declan stood up. He held a hand down to Gabe, and Gabe took it, and was pulled up off the last plank as if he weighed nothing at all.

They walked back along the jetty together in the dark. Thirty-four planks. At the land end, by Gigichi's tree, Declan stopped, and looked up the shingle at the forge, where the king was sitting on the bucket by the red eye of the fire with his father's hammer across his knees, and the star lay beside him on its rollers in the dark.

He looked at it for a long time.

"It's asleep," said Declan.

"Yes."

"I can feel it," said Declan. "From here." He was quiet. "It's very cold."

"It won't be," said Gabe, "by Friday."

***

In the morning, when Gabe came down to the forge before the light to start the fire, there was somebody sitting on the shingle beside the star.

Not the king. The king was asleep in the cabin; Declan had given him the bed, and slept on the floor by the hearth, and the king had let him, which Gabe thought was probably the only time anybody had ever won that argument with Declan. Not Declan. Declan was on the end of the jetty with the lamp, the way he always was at this hour.

It was Alea.

She was sitting on the stones with her back against the star, in the dark, with Astra across her knees. The black blade. Her half. The one the king had drawn narrow and Declan had struck, on this shingle, the autumn before last, out of a sword that had been standing in the ground on a hill in Kharvos when her father was twenty-one.

She had one hand flat on the star behind her. On the cold grey bloom of it.

She looked up when he came.

"It's cold," she said.

"Yes."

"Astra's humming," said Alea. "Feel."

Gabe crouched down beside her and put his hand on the black steel across her knees.

It was. Very faintly. A low steady note in the folded metal, the way it had hummed on the shingle two mornings ago when her father cut the lake.

But this was different. Gabe sat on his heels with one hand on Astra and felt it, and then, carefully, reached past Alea and put his other hand on the star.

Cold. Dense. Asleep.

And under the cold, so faint he'd never have felt it if he hadn't had his other hand on Astra at the same time, so that he could feel the two of them together, one in each hand, like two strings: something answering.

Not waking. Not yet. Turning over, a little, in its sleep. The way a big dog turns over when somebody it knows comes into the room.

"It knows her," said Gabe. He didn't know why he said *her*. It came out.

"Astra?"

"I think so." He took his hands away. They were shaking again. "Not from here. Neither of them." He looked at the star, and at the black blade across her knees. "I don't think they're from the same place. I don't think they're from anywhere near each other. But they're both not from here." He swallowed. "And it's lonely. I think. It's been lonely for a very long time."

Alea didn't say anything. She sat with her back against the star and her hand flat on it and her sword across her knees, and her ears, in the dark, went slowly flat, and then up.

Gabe stood, and went to the hearth, and started the fire.
