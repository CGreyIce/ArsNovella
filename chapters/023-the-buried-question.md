The hawk's name was Brisk, and she was the angriest creature Aleaka had ever loved.

She lived in the castle mews, on the third perch from the end, and she had bitten the falconer twice, the stable boy four times, and Oswin once, memorably, on the nose. She hated gloves. She hated hoods. She hated being looked at, and being talked to, and the sound of the bell in the courtyard, and other hawks, and rain.

She did not hate Aleaka.

Nobody understood it, least of all Aleaka. She'd walked into the mews in the second month to look at the birds, the way she'd walked into the stables and the kennels and the kitchen yard where the castle cats lived. And Brisk had looked at her from the third perch with one furious golden eye, and then, without being asked, dropped down onto her bare forearm and sat there. No talons. No grip. Just weight, and warmth, and that fierce, considering stare.

"That's impossible," the falconer had said.

"I'm s-sorry," Aleaka had said, because she always said sorry when things happened that she didn't understand. "Should I— do you want her back?"

The falconer had looked at the hawk, and the hawk had looked at the falconer, and the falconer had very wisely decided that he didn't.

Now, in the fifth month, Aleaka came to the mews every morning before training, and Brisk sat on her arm while she fed her, and Aleaka could feel her. Not just the weight. *Her.* A hot, bright, impatient knot of feeling, all edges: *hungry, bored, the light's wrong, that pigeon, that pigeon, I hate that pigeon.* It was like standing next to a fire you could hear thinking.

She'd always been able to tell how people were feeling. It was the thing everyone said about her, on Earth, in calls: *Leaka always knows.* She'd know someone was sad before they'd said anything, from the gap before they spoke, or the way they laughed. Here, it had spread. It had gone past people, into the cats and the horses and the hawks and the hounds. She'd stopped being surprised by it.

It was why she knew something was wrong with Hanarisu.

***

It had been going on for weeks. Since the end of the third month, maybe, around the time of Lord Vane's banquet.

It wasn't anything big. That was what made it so hard to point at. It was small things. Conversations that stopped when Aleaka came into the room, and then started again a little too brightly. Jared looking at Cursiffe across the sitting room, and Cursiffe looking back, and both of them looking away. Cursiffe's hand going to the inside of his coat, over and over, the way you touch a pocket to check something's still there. Hana watching Aleaka with a small, careful, worried look she'd quickly smooth away.

They had a secret. The three of them. And whatever it was, it was about her. Or it was something they'd decided she shouldn't have.

She could feel it every time she walked into a room. It was like walking into a draught.

She didn't say anything for a long time. That was her way. She was patient. She waited, and watched, and turned it over, and tried not to let it hurt. But it did hurt, in a small, persistent way, like a splinter.

So in the fifth month, on a cold morning, when Hanarisu came into the mews to find her, she didn't wait any longer.

"You're keeping something from me," Aleaka said. She didn't look away from Brisk. "You and Jared and Cursiffe."

Hana stopped in the doorway.

For a long moment neither of them said anything. Brisk shifted on Aleaka's arm, sensing something, her feathers rising.

"Yes," said Hanarisu quietly.

Aleaka hadn't expected her to say it. She'd expected a deflection, or a pleasant face. Hana was very good at pleasant faces. She looked up.

Hana was standing in the doorway with her purple tail very still and her hands clasped in front of her, and she didn't look pleasant at all. She looked tired, and unhappy, and very honest.

"I'm not sure of something," Hana said. "We found something, a while ago, and I don't know what it means. It might be nothing. It's probably nothing." She took a breath. "And if it's something, it's going to matter to you more than to anyone. So I'm not going to hand it to you until I know."

"That's not fair."

"No," said Hana. "It isn't. I'm sorry." She came a step closer. "But I remember the months after, Leaka. When you didn't come to calls. When you did come and didn't talk. I remember how long it took you to come back." Her voice wobbled, very slightly. "I'm not going to give you something to hope for that I can't keep. Not until I'm sure."

Aleaka looked at her for a long time.

The months after. She didn't like thinking about them. A grey time, a long flat stretch of days where nothing had colour and nothing tasted of anything. A friend she'd only known for a year, gone in his sleep in Malaysia, and somehow it had knocked the bottom out of everything. Her group had pulled her out of it. Hana, mostly. Hana and Jared, an inch at a time.

She could feel Hana now, standing in front of her. Not her face. Her *self*: a tight, worried, aching knot, full of something heavy and something else underneath it. Something bright. Something Hana was holding down very hard, the way you hold down a lid on a pot.

Hope. That was what it was. Hana was afraid of something, and underneath the fear she was hoping.

"When you're sure," said Aleaka slowly. "Whatever it is. I want to be first."

"You'll be first," said Hana. "I promise."

Aleaka nodded. Then she looked back at Brisk, because she didn't trust her face.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. I trust you."

She did. It still hurt. But she found, standing there in the cold mews with a furious hawk on her arm, that the two things could sit together in her without either one winning.

***

Oswin found her that afternoon, in the cat yard, and he had the expression of a man about to do something he'd been specifically told not to.

"Aleaka," he said. "Can I ask you something? Privately?"

"You're asking it in a yard full of cats."

"The cats won't tell." He glanced over his shoulder, which was ridiculous, because there was nobody there. "I need help. With a question the Advisor has asked me to leave alone. Twice." He hesitated. "Three times, if you count the look."

Aleaka put down the cat she was holding. "What question?"

"Who knows whom," said Oswin. "Among the thirty-five."

She stared at him.

"Every summoning in four hundred years," said Oswin, very quickly, the way he always talked when he was excited and nervous at the same time. "I've read all of them. One person. Two, sometimes. Three, once. Never more. And never, *ever* people who knew each other before. And then the Advisor reaches out and pulls in thirty-five, and every one of you can name somebody in another group, and some of you can name ten." He was turning his ink-stained hands over and over. "A summoning is a net. It catches what's tangled in it. Something tied all of you together *before* we reached for you. I want to know what."

"Why?"

"Because I'm an archivist," said Oswin, as if that explained everything. "And because nobody else is asking. And because the Advisor wants it buried, and I've known him for fifteen years, and he has never once wanted anything buried that didn't matter." He looked at her. "You know everyone. More than anyone in this group. The others told me. You were in everybody's chat."

"Not *everybody's*," said Aleaka. But it was mostly true.

"Will you help me?"

She thought about Hana in the doorway that morning. *I'm not sure of something.* She thought about the draught in every room, and Cursiffe's hand on his coat.

"Yes," she said.

***

They did it in the archive, at night, with Oswin's key and a lamp and the biggest sheet of paper Oswin could find, which turned out to be the back of an old map of the kingdom that took up most of the table.

Aleaka wrote the names. All thirty-five of them, in English, in a big loose ring around the edge of the paper, grouped by seven. Oswin couldn't read a letter of it. His eyes kept sliding off the words the way the scribes' had with Cursiffe's writing. So beside each name he wrote a little mark of his own in the native script, a sound, a shape, so that he could follow along.

Then they drew the lines.

She started with what she knew. Hana to Jared, Hana to Milli, Hana to Cursiffe: the inside of her own group, all of them tied to each other. Then out. Jared to Xav, in Group 6. Hana to Tiger and Drew and Kei, in Group 5. Herself to Kei, and Gabe, and Newish, and all of Group 3.

Then she got stuck.

"I don't know Group 1," she said. "Not really. Or most of Group 4. Or Group 6, except Xav." She stared at the paper. "I know who *I* know. I don't know who everyone else knows."

"Then put down what you can," said Oswin. "And we'll find the rest."

So she put down what she could. And when she'd finished, it was a mess: a ring of thirty-five names with lines going every which way across the middle, thick in some places and bare in others, like a spider's web that a cat had been at.

Oswin leaned over it with the lamp. He was quiet for a very long time.

"There are knots," he said at last. "Look. Here, and here, and here." He touched three names with his inky finger. Yan. Gabe. Kei. "Lines everywhere, out of these. They're... crossroads. People who connect groups to each other." He moved his finger. "And these, over here, have almost none. These three in Group 4. These two in Group 1."

"Rachael," said Aleaka, reading. "Dennis. Calvin. Xola. PenguChi." She frowned. "I don't think I've ever spoken to any of them."

"So they're tied to their own groups and nothing else." Oswin was writing notes in the margin, fast. "But their groups are tied to the knots. And the knots are tied to each other." He sat back. "It's not a web. It's a... a tree. Or a river. Lots of little streams, running into bigger ones, running into..." He stopped.

"Into what?" said Aleaka.

Oswin looked at the paper for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the trouble. If it's a river, it should run into something. A sea. A single place where it all comes together." He spread his hands over the middle of the map, where the lines crossed and tangled and didn't quite meet. "And there's nothing there. Just a hole in the middle where the lines all point and nobody's standing."

Aleaka looked at the hole in the middle of the paper.

Something moved in her chest. Not a thought. Not yet. More like the feeling you get when you walk into a familiar room and something's been moved and you can't tell what.

"Is there anyone," said Oswin carefully, "outside the thirty-five, that a lot of you knew? Someone who isn't here?"

Aleaka opened her mouth.

And closed it again.

"I don't know," she said slowly. "I'd... I'd have to ask. I don't know who everyone knew before. Not really. I know my own." She swallowed. "I'd have to ask them all."

Oswin nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected.

"Then ask," he said.

***

The door opened behind them.

Aleaka jumped so hard she knocked the lamp, and Oswin caught it, and they both turned round.

Kestra was standing in the archive doorway with her arms folded.

She looked at them: at Oswin, ink-stained and frozen; at Aleaka, red to the tips of her fox ears; at the enormous sheet of paper on the table with thirty-five names on it in a script nobody in the castle could read, and lines going everywhere. Her face did absolutely nothing.

"The Advisor," said Kestra, "told you to leave this."

"He did," said Oswin, and swallowed, and lifted his chin. "Three times."

"And here you are."

"Here I am."

Kestra came into the room. She walked to the table and looked down at the map for a long moment, at the knots and the bare places and the hole in the middle. Then she took out her little book, and her pencil, and wrote something in it, slowly, without hiding it.

"The king," she said, not looking up, "told the Advisor to find out why the one called Xavier fights like the Black Blade." She closed the book. "Nobody's told me to stop you."

She turned and walked out. At the door she paused.

"Lock up when you're finished," she said. "The Keeper will have a fit."

The door closed behind her.

Aleaka and Oswin looked at each other across the lamp.

"Did she just..." said Aleaka.

"I think she did," said Oswin faintly. "I think the king's guard just gave us permission." He sat down very suddenly on a stool. "I need a drink."

***

She wrote the letters that night, by the fire in the prince's sitting room, after everyone else had gone to bed.

Six of them. One to every other group. She wrote them slowly, in her round careful hand, and she asked every one of them the same thing.

*Oswin and I are trying to work out how we're all connected. Not just who's friends with who now. Who you knew BEFORE. On Earth.*

*Who brought you to who? Who did you meet first? If you had to draw a line back from everyone you know here, where would it go?*

*Please be honest. Please be specific. I think it matters. I don't know why yet.*

She sealed them, one by one, and stacked them on the table for the morning courier.

Then she sat by the fire with her knees pulled up, and let herself think about him.

She didn't do that very often. She'd learned not to, in the grey months. But tonight, with the hole in the middle of Oswin's map still sitting behind her eyes, she couldn't help it.

He'd been in her chat almost every stream, for most of a year. He'd been the one who made her laugh at the worst moments. She'd teased him without mercy, and called him every name she could think of. He'd taken all of it with a kind of exaggerated, pleading delight, like a puppy rolling over, and called her cute in return, which had made her teasing worse, which had been the point. They'd both known exactly what it was and wasn't. She was aromantic, and he knew it, and he'd never once pushed. It had been a game. The best kind. The kind where both people know the rules and love them anyway.

And he named everything Alea.

She smiled, a little, by the fire, remembering. Every pet, in every game. Every companion. Every horse, every dog, every slime he'd tamed in some terrible pixelated RPG at two in the morning. *Alea.* Over and over. She'd teased him about that too, endlessly. *Alea again? You have no imagination. You're the least creative man alive.*

And he'd said, once, laughing, in that voice he used when he was about to say something ridiculous:

*It's a good name. One day I'm going to give it to someone who deserves it. Trust.*

She'd laughed. Everyone had laughed. It was the kind of thing he said.

Aleaka sat by the fire in a castle in another world, with a hawk asleep in the mews below her and six cats curled on the hearth and a stack of letters on the table asking everyone she knew who they'd known before, and she let the memory sit with her for a while. Warm, and sore, and his.

Then she put it away again, carefully, the way you put away something that might break.

She didn't let herself look at the hole in the middle of the map. Not tonight.

Not yet.
