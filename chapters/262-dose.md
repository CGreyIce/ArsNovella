There was no such thing as a poison. There was only a dose.

Kei had believed that since he was old enough to read the back of a bottle. He believed it on the second night, standing in the long room with a cup of water for Drew that Drew couldn't hold, while forty people leaned on a boy with two broken ribs and the boy said their names. He believed it on the third night, when the king's nose started to bleed on the gatehouse roof and didn't stop until dawn, and Kestra stood a length behind him and wiped it with her sleeve every quarter of an hour without being asked. He believed it on the fourth, when Hild's wall in the arch came up a hand narrower than it had the night before, and she didn't say anything about it, and Hyvred, on the tower, did.

There was only a dose. Everything was a poison if you got the amount wrong. Everything was a medicine if you got it right.

The trouble was the amount.

***

*It bit*, said Hisui.

The fifth morning. Grey. The neighbours gone back across the fields at first light to stand at the edge of the valley in the cold, facing the town, the way they'd stood every day; the long room asleep in heaps; Drew on his back on a pallet by the hearth, not asleep, looking at the ceiling with the red eye and the clear one, breathing in small careful sips. Kei was sitting on the floor beside him with the snake round his neck and his notebook on his knee.

"What did?"

*The green root. At the edge. When you did it in your little room with the shutters open, and the girl on the tower said it flinched.* Hisui's tongue went in and out against his jaw. *I've been thinking.*

"You don't think. You taste."

*Same thing. I've been tasting it for five nights.* The snake shifted. *It doesn't go into them all over. The call. You thought it did. You thought it came in like water into a cloth, everywhere at once, so you made a dose for everywhere, and it went everywhere, and you didn't know Hild for half an hour.* A pause. *It doesn't. It comes in at the edge. At the crack. One place. Like a draught under a door. And then it's in.*

Kei looked at the notebook. Eleven days of it. Doses, distances, the taste of his own name in the next room.

"I know that," he said. "That's why I tried the knitwort at the edge. That's what flinched."

*Yes. For a hair.* Hisui was quiet. *But you took it in the afternoon. In your little room. And it sat in you all evening doing nothing, and you were sick, and then you opened the shutters and the call came in, and it flinched.*

"Yes."

*So what was it doing all evening?*

Kei didn't answer.

*Nothing*, said Hisui. *It was doing nothing. It was waiting. Sitting at the edge of you like a cat at a mousehole. You don't notice a styptic until you cut yourself.* His tongue flickered. *That's what the green root does. It doesn't do anything to the whole of you. It only does something where there's a cut. It waits for the cut.*

Kei sat on the floor of the long room with the notebook on his knee and didn't move for a long time.

He knew that. It was in every herbal Corvin had found him since the first winter. A styptic does nothing to whole skin. You can rub yarrow on your arm all day and nothing happens. It's only when there's a cut that it closes the cut. That was why you could pack a wound with it and not kill the man. That was why it was a medicine and not a poison.

He'd known it. He'd been so busy thinking about the call that he'd forgotten what the knitwort was.

"It waits," he said.

*It waits*, said Hisui. *So you don't have to time it. The call times it. The call comes in at the crack, and the root's there, waiting, and it closes the crack. On its own. When the call presses.* The snake settled. *And the call presses on all of them at once. Every night. At full dark. When the first ones put their hands on the wall. All of them, all forty, in the same heartbeat.*

On the pallet, Drew had turned his head. He was looking at them both with the red eye.

"If all forty had it," said Drew. Very carefully, around the ribs. "At the edge. Waiting. And the call came in at full dark and hit all forty edges at once. And they all closed." He breathed. "What does it do?"

"I don't know," said Kei.

*It flinches*, said Hisui. *It flinched at one. In a little room. For a hair.* His tongue went in and out, slowly, tasting the long room, the forty asleep, the cold coming in at the shutters from the valley where the neighbours stood. *Forty is more than one.*

***

He asked them first.

That was the rule. His own rule. He'd made it in the spring, when the first of them came into the long room with the cold at the edge of their light, and Corporal Dunn had stood in front of him in his grey and said *try it on me, I don't mind*, and Kei had said *no*. *I don't test on people who can't say no.* Under the call, they couldn't say anything. At noon, when the call was only a tide in the next room and they were themselves, sitting on the benches eating Annet's bread, they could.

So he went down the long room at noon with his notebook and asked them. Each one. Sitting on the end of the bench beside them, the way Drew sat, and saying their names first, the way Drew said them.

"It's a root," he said. "A styptic. It closes cuts. I've taken it myself; Hyvred watched. It made me sick for two days the first time, because I took too much, and the second time a little less, and the third time a grain the size of barley and I was only sick for an afternoon. I'll make yours smaller. I'll make yours *exactly*. And it won't do anything at all to you unless the call comes in. And then it'll close the place where it comes in. For a heartbeat. That's all I know." He looked at them. "I don't know what the call does when forty doors shut on it at once. Nobody does. It might do nothing. It might be angry. I don't think it gets angry. I don't know."

Annet said yes before he'd finished. Ivo said yes on his right side. Old Linnet looked at him for a long time over her spindle and said *you're a good lad, you ask*, and yes. The Dacey sisters looked at each other and said yes together. Mother Ivey said *is it bitter* and he said *very* and she said *good, then it's working*, and yes. Iolo said yes with his jaw set. Corporal Dunn said *I said yes in the spring*, and Kei said *I know, I'm asking again*, and Dunn said *yes*, louder, and the long room laughed, the first time it had laughed in five days.

Joss said yes.

Kei sat on the bench beside him for a long time.

He was a child. Thirteen or fourteen now; a farrier's apprentice with a red scarf. He'd been called in the spring, by name, and stood up in the long room every night since. He'd broken out of the chain at the balance, running for his sister, and Tiger had caught him. He was looking at Kei now with Elin's face, which Kei knew from the tower, from Hyvred's description, and from the thirteenth ring.

"You can say no," said Kei.

"I know," said Joss. "Drew said. He said you'd say that." He looked at his hands. "If it works. If it does the thing. Does it go up the line? To her?"

Kei didn't answer straight away. He thought about it, properly, the way the boy deserved.

"I don't know," he said. "It's one call. It's one thing, coming in at forty doors. If it flinches here, maybe it flinches everywhere. Maybe it lets go of everything at once, for a heartbeat." He looked at the boy. "I don't know. I'm sorry. I'm telling you because you asked and I don't lie to people I'm giving things to."

"Then yes," said Joss.

So he gave Joss the smallest.

***

He made them in the afternoon.

Forty. In the little room off the long room with the shutters closed and the brazier going and the apothecaries from Ashby and Kingsferry boiling in the Bell's kitchen next door, the way the king had ordered in the spring. The apothecaries did the boiling. Kei did the word.

That was what *Dose* was. It had been since the first winter. Anybody could boil a root; the apothecaries had been boiling roots for thirty years. What Kei did was say what it was *for*. Hold the cup in his two hands and understand, as exactly as he could, what was in it and what it was going to do and to whom, and ask, very clearly, the way the court mages had told them all in the first month a spell was asked: *this*. And the thing in the cup did *this*, and not anything else, as near as he could make it.

He'd been doing it for three years. He'd never done it forty times in an afternoon, for forty different people.

They were all different. That was the thing. That was why it had to be forty and not one big pot. Annet was a big woman who baked all night and never slept, and the cold at the edge of her was wide, a long crack, like a door left open on a hook; Hisui had tasted it every night for a week. Old Linnet was small and old and the cold at her edge was narrow and deep. Corporal Dunn was a soldier; his was where a soldier's is, he said, behind the breastbone, where the fear goes. Joss was a child and his was thin and new and frightened. Forty people. Forty cracks. Forty doses, each one exactly the size of the place it was going into, and not a grain more, because a grain more was numbing, and numbing wasn't closing; Rachael had said so, down the cords, from Kingsferry, from a girl who'd been the patient for twenty-six years. *Numbing isn't closing. That's a wound.*

He held each cup in his two hands and said its name.

*Annet. The long one. At the left side, low, where it opens like a door on a hook. Close the edge when it presses. Nothing else.*

*Ivo.*

*Mother Ivey.*

By the twentieth his hands were shaking. By the thirtieth, Hisui had stopped talking and lay round his neck with his tongue going in and out against the pulse in Kei's throat, very fast, which meant *I'm watching*. By the thirty-eighth the brazier had gone low and nobody had come in to feed it, because Hild was sitting in the doorway with her staff across her knees and her one eye shut and wouldn't let anybody in.

The thirty-ninth was Joss.

He held the cup a long time. The smallest. A grain. Less than a grain. He said the boy's name, and the place, and *close the edge when it presses, nothing else*; and then he said, because he couldn't not, *and if you can, if there's a line, if it goes up the line*—

He stopped.

That wasn't a dose. That was a wish. You couldn't put a wish in a cup. He'd learned that in the first winter, the hard way, with a fever he'd wanted to break and couldn't.

He took the wish out. He said the name again, and the place, and *nothing else*; and put the cup down.

The fortieth was Drew. He hadn't asked to be in it. Kei hadn't asked him. Drew wasn't called; Drew was the end of the chain, the hands it all came into. But he'd been standing at the end of the call for six nights with it going into his chest, and Hisui said he tasted of it now, a little, at the edge, like everybody who'd stood in the long room too long. So Kei made one for him too. Small. *Close the edge. Nothing else. Let him keep his hands.*

Then he put his own hands flat on the table between the forty cups and sat there until they stopped shaking.

***

He tested them.

Of course he did. He was Kei. He didn't give anybody anything he hadn't tested. He couldn't test them the whole way; only the call could do that, at full dark. But he could test whether they did anything they shouldn't.

At the hour before dusk, the forty drank. In the long room, all together, at Drew's word, because Drew said it should be all together; *like a toast*, he said, from his pallet, and Annet laughed and lifted hers, and so they all lifted theirs, forty small cups of bitter water, and Old Linnet said *to the lad who asks*, and drank.

And then Kei went down the line with Hyvred.

She'd come down from the tower for it. She hadn't come down from the tower before dusk in six days. She came down a hundred and twelve steps with Bee behind her, and stood in the long room with her red eye open and looked at forty people one at a time while Kei watched her face.

"Nothing," she said, at Annet. "Same. The cold at the edge. Same as last night." She frowned. "No. Not the same. There's something sitting in it. Green. Like moss in a crack in a wall."

"Is it doing anything?"

"No. It's just sitting there." She moved on. "Ivo. Same. Moss in the crack." And on. "Mother Ivey. Same." And on. "Old Linnet. Hers is narrow. The green's narrow too. It's exactly as narrow." She looked at Kei. "How did you get it exactly as narrow?"

"Hisui told me how narrow."

"Oh," said Hyvred.

She went all the way down. Forty. At every one she stopped, and looked, and said *same, moss in the crack, sitting there, not doing anything*; and at every one Kei wrote a mark in his notebook. A small one. A tick. Nothing to the whole of them. Nothing they'd notice. Only at the edge, where the cut was, waiting.

Forty ticks.

He looked at them on the page for a long time. Ten out of ten, Corvin had said once, about the second tier of the magic path. *When it works every time. Not nine. Ten.* He'd never had ten. He'd had eight, and nine, and one terrible week in the first winter, two. He'd never once in three years made a dose that did exactly what he'd asked, every time, to everyone, and nothing else.

Forty out of forty.

He didn't open his window. He didn't want to know yet. It didn't matter yet. The test wasn't the moss. The test was the dark.

***

He went up on the north wall at dusk.

He'd thought about staying in the long room. That was where the forty were, and Drew. But Drew had the forty; Drew had had them for six nights and would have them tonight whether the doses worked or not, and if they didn't, the chain would need its hands, and Kei's hands were no good in a chain. And whatever the doses did, if they did anything, they'd do it out there. At the wall. Where the call came from.

So he went up the stair by Old Pruett's postern with Hisui round his neck, and stood on the wall-walk beside Xav, with the lamps lit all along the parapet, and looked down.

They were already there.

They'd come across the fields in the last of the light, the way they did every evening now, at a walk, in their rings, with the thing in front of them that Kei couldn't see and Hisui couldn't taste, only the edge of. The first ring at the foot of the wall, close, with their faces turned up. Then the second behind them. The third. Thirteen deep. The curve on the left, by the west end, that didn't breathe. The whole of the ground under the north wall from end to end, in the lamplight, a crowd of people in coats and shawls with their faces turned up toward the town, silent, breathing, waiting for full dark.

He'd never looked at them this close. He'd seen them from the tower, small, through Hyvred's description. He looked at them now from the wall, thirty feet up, in the lamplight. A woman in an apron. A man in a carter's smock with frost on his shoulders. An old man with a white beard. A girl with a plait and an old grey shawl, at the edge of the crowd by the gate, in the thirteenth ring; and beside her a boy in Western Watch grey with a shield; and a big woman with her sleeves rolled and flour in the creases of her hands.

*There they are*, said Hisui. Very quietly. *The three.*

The dark came.

Kei felt it come the way the long room felt it, now, after six nights: not the dark. The thing in the dark. The pressure going up. And at the foot of the wall, all along it, the first ring lifted their hands.

All at once. Like one person.

And laid them flat on the stone.

*Now*, said Hisui.

***

He felt it.

Not in himself. He hadn't dosed himself; he hadn't been called. In Hisui. The snake went rigid round his neck, the whole length of him at once, the way he'd gone rigid on the sheep path above Coldwater in the first year; and his tongue came out and stayed out.

*It bit*, said Hisui.

Behind them, across the town, through the stone of the wall under Kei's feet, something happened in the long room. He couldn't feel it. He didn't have to. He heard it, later, a hundred times, from forty people who'd all felt it at once: the call coming in, at full dark, at the edge, the way it always did, *come*, through the crack under the door—

And the door was shut.

Forty doors. All at once. Every one of them closed at the edge by a little green moss of bitter root, exactly the size of the crack, that had been sitting there all evening doing nothing, waiting for something to press on it.

*It flinched*, said Hisui.

The call stopped.

Not in the long room. Everywhere. Kei heard it stop. He'd been hearing it for six nights, faintly, the way you hear the sea from a house on a cliff; and it stopped. In the middle. As if somebody had put their hand on a hot iron. As if a dog had put its nose into a nettle.

And at the foot of the north wall, hundreds of hands came off the stone.

All at once. Like one person. The whole first ring, from end to end of the wall in the lamplight, took its hands off the stone and stood there, with its hands in the air in front of it, an inch from the wall, not leaning.

Swaying.

Xav said something beside him. Kei didn't hear it.

He was looking at the gate.

***

The arch. Hild's wall across it, pale, curved, with the first of the neighbours pressed against it, the way they'd pressed every night. And among them, at the front, where the thirteenth ring had come up against it: a boy in Western Watch grey.

He'd stopped leaning. They all had. He was standing in the arch with his hands up, an inch from Hild's wall, and his face—

His face had changed.

Kei saw it from the wall, thirty feet up, in the light of the gate lamps. He'd never seen it before and he knew exactly what it was, the way you know a word in a language you don't speak because somebody says it in the right voice. The smile went. Not slowly. All at once, like a cloth pulled off a table. And under it there was a boy of sixteen with freckles and frost in his eyebrows and his hands up in front of him in an arch he knew, at a gate he'd stood on, looking at a pale shimmering wall with a one-eyed woman behind it.

He blinked.

"Ma'am?" said Rhys Hywel.

Hild dropped the wall.

Kei had never seen her do anything fast. He saw it now. The pale light fell off her blade like rain, and she went through the place where it had been in one step, and took Rhys Hywel by the front of his grey coat in her left fist, and *pulled*; and he came. Off his feet. Back through the arch, past her, into the soldiers behind her, who caught him, and went down with him in a heap on the cobbles; and Hild had already stepped back into the arch and the wall was up again before the neighbour behind him had finished swaying.

A heartbeat. Less.

Kei looked along the wall.

At the grating, at the east end, where Kestra had built a barricade out of a laundry cart and two farrier's doors: a woman in a brown shawl had her face. He could see it from here. She was standing in the black water with her hands up an inch from the cart, and her face had come up out of the smile, and she was saying something. Kei couldn't hear it. Kestra could. Kestra came over the top of the cart into the water and picked her up bodily, the way you'd pick up a sack, and threw her over the cart to the reserve.

Further along. A third. An old man near the front of the crowd under the wall, close to the gate, with his face turned up toward the parapet, his own face, bewildered, the way you'd look up if you'd woken in a field and didn't know how you'd got there. Looking up at the lamps. At Kei.

"*Rope*," said Xav.

There was a coil on the wall by the postern. Old Pruett's, for hauling. Xav had it over the parapet before Kei had moved. It went down the wall and hit the old man on the shoulder, and the old man looked at it, bewildered, and Kei leaned over the parapet and shouted, he didn't know what, *hold on, hold on to it*; and the old man's hands closed on the rope.

And the heartbeat ended.

***

The call came back.

Not angry. Hisui had been right; it didn't get angry. It came back the way the tide comes back after a wave has gone out: all at once, and heavier, and without any hurry at all. Kei felt it come back through Hisui, through the stone; *come*. And at the foot of the wall, all along it, hundreds of hands came back onto the stone, flat. And leaned.

The old man on the rope turned his face back down toward the valley, and smiled, and let go.

Kei watched him do it. Watched his hands open on the rope, one finger at a time, the way your hands open when you're falling asleep. And the rope came up loose, and the old man put his hands back on the wall with the others, and leaned, and was gone.

Xav hauled the rope in. He didn't say anything. He coiled it, very neatly, the way Pruett coiled it, and put it back where it lived.

Kei stood at the parapet and looked down.

He'd seen three. A boy, at the gate. A woman, at the grating. An old man, on a rope, for a heartbeat, before it took him back. Three faces come up out of the smile, in a heartbeat, at the foot of the wall. He looked along the crowd for more, the whole length of it, in the lamplight, and there were none. All the others had their hands back on the stone. All leaning. All smiling.

The girl with the plait, by the gate, in her nan's shawl. Smiling. With her hands flat on the stone by the arch, an arm from where Rhys Hywel had been, leaning.

She hadn't come up. Not for a heartbeat. Not a hair.

Neither had the big woman with the flour on her hands.

Then he saw the others.

Not standing. That was what he saw. All along the foot of the wall, in the crowd, between the feet of the ones who were leaning, there were people on the ground.

He hadn't seen them fall. Nobody had. Everybody had been looking at the faces. But when the call flinched and the hands came off the wall, and the whole crowd stood there swaying for a heartbeat with nothing holding it up, some of them had stopped swaying. And gone down. The way a coat goes down when you take it off its hook. All at once, all of them together, in a heap, in the frost, without a sound.

They didn't get up when the call came back.

The ones around them leaned on the wall, with their hands flat, and stood on them. Not cruelly. The way you'd stand on a coat on the floor, without noticing it was there.

"Hyvred," said Kei. He didn't know who he was saying it to. She was on the tower; she couldn't hear him. "Hyvred, what are they—"

*They've gone out*, said Hisui.

Very quietly. Round his neck. In a voice Kei had heard him use once, in three years, on the sheep path above Coldwater, at the little wooden horse with a leg missing.

*When it let go. For the heartbeat. They went out.* His tongue went in and out, slowly, tasting the frost, the lamplight, the crowd. *There was nobody under it. It was holding them up. It had been holding them up for a long time. When it let go, there was nothing left to stand.*

Kei stood on the wall.

*Seven*, said Hisui.

***

They brought them in at dawn.

When the neighbours went back across the fields at first light, at a walk, in their rings, with the thing in front of them, the way they always went; seven people stayed in the frost at the foot of the north wall, where they'd fallen. Brannoch's men went out of the postern and carried them in, one at a time, on the shields. Grey-black shields with the hammer-marks still bright, made for holding.

They laid them in the guardhouse yard, by the well, in a row, with their coats over their faces.

Kei went down and looked at them. He made himself. He lifted each coat and looked at each face and put the coat back. Old, most of them. Thin, the way you'd be thin if you'd stood in a valley for two years. A woman in an apron. A man with a white beard who'd had frost in it. A shepherd, by his crook-callused hands, from the hill farms above Coldwater. The miller from Coldwater, Corvin said, from his book; Corvin had bought flour from him in the first year. All of them from the inner rings. The first and the second and the third. The ones who'd walked out first. The ones who'd been standing longest.

None of them had been in the long room. None of them had drunk anything. None of them had said yes.

He'd said it in the spring. To Corporal Dunn. *I don't test on people who can't say no.*

He hadn't. He'd made forty doses for forty people who'd said yes, and tested every one, forty out of forty, ten out of ten. And the dose had done exactly what he'd asked: closed forty doors at the edge when the call pressed. Nothing else.

And the call had flinched. Everywhere. Because it was one call. And in the heartbeat it flinched it had let go of everyone it was holding, the ones at the wall and the ones in the rings and the ones in the curve and seven old people from Coldwater and the hill farms who'd been standing in a valley so long that there was nothing left in them to stand up on their own.

Seven, who couldn't say no.

Three, who'd come up. One who'd stayed. Rhys Hywel was in the Bell, in a bed, with Sister Agathe and two blankets and Corporal Dunn sitting beside him, holding his hand, saying *you're all right, lad, you're all right, you're on the wall*; and he wasn't all right, he couldn't stop shaking and he didn't know what month it was, but he was there. The woman from the grating was in the next bed, asking for her boy. Her boy was in the second ring. She'd been called first, in the first spring, and he'd gone up after her a week later to fetch her home.

The old man on the rope had gone back.

Three, for a heartbeat. One, to stay; two, really, the woman and Rhys. And seven, who'd gone out.

He sat down on the rim of the well beside the row of coats, with the snake round his neck, and opened his window.

He looked at it for a long time.

*Flinch.* It was the right word. He'd have called it that himself, if anybody had asked him. It was what he'd done. He'd made a dose so exact that forty people drank it and felt nothing, and then the thing that had been eating the edge of them for a year put its nose to the edge and flinched.

Forty out of forty. Ten out of ten. Candle. He'd been at nine-something since the summer. It had moved, last night, at full dark, on the wall, while he was watching an old man's hands open on a rope.

He didn't feel anything about it at all. He thought he probably would, later.

*Post it*, said Hisui.

"No."

*It's the rule. Higu's rule. Rule three.*

"Seven people are dead."

*Yes*, said Hisui. *And one boy is in a bed in the Bell asking what month it is. And a woman is asking for her son. And forty people in the long room stood up last night for the first time in six nights and didn't go anywhere, and slept, and the boy with the hands didn't break a third rib.* His tongue went in and out. *You did a dose. A dose is both. It's always both. You've always known that. You just never had to count it before.*

Kei sat on the well with the window open.

*Post it*, said Hisui, more quietly. *And then post the seven. And then post the three. All of it. That's what you'd do if it was somebody else's dose. You'd want to know.*

He posted it.

***

He didn't go to bed.

Hild found him on the well at noon, still there, with the coats gone into the guardhouse and the yard empty and the frost going off the stones. She sat down beside him without asking, the way she'd sat beside Hyvred in the first week, with her staff across her knees and her head on one side.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"I lost a whole section at the Brenn crossing," said Hild. "Twelve. On the bank, the night before. My order. I put them where they were." She turned the staff over. "The next morning there was a thing with too many arms and I lost an eye. Everybody remembers the eye. Nobody remembers the twelve but me." She was quiet. "I say them sometimes. In the yard. When I'm hitting the young man with the staff. He doesn't know. He thinks I'm counting."

Kei looked at her.

"It wasn't your dose," said Hild. "It was two years in a valley. Your dose found out how long it had been. That's all a dose ever does. It finds out." She looked north with her one eye. "The girl in Kingsferry's right. You made it flinch. The rest wants a healer. You're not one. You're the man who knows what's in a jar." She stood up. "And it knows now that you know. It'll be ready for you tonight. So don't do it again till you've worked out what it'll do about it."

"It'll find another edge," said Kei.

"Probably," said Hild. "Go to sleep. Make your root smaller in the morning." She stopped at the long room door. "Or don't. Sit on the well. I'll listen."

She went in.

He sat on the well.

*Smaller*, said Hisui, after a long time, round his neck. *That's what she always says. Smaller. She's right. She's annoying, but she's right.*

"I know."

*You'll do it again.*

"I know."

*Not tonight.*

"No."

*Good*, said Hisui. And then, after a while, very quietly, against his jaw, in the voice from the sheep path: *The girl with the plait. By the gate. When it flinched. She didn't come up. But her hands came off the wall with all the others.* His tongue went in and out. *And they didn't go back on it straight away. Not with the others. A hair after.*

Kei sat very still.

*A hair*, said Hisui. *I tasted it. She's in there. Not far down. Further than the boy. Not as far as the seven.* He settled. *Tell the little one with the scarf. Not today. When you've got something to tell him with it.*

Kei looked at the long room door, where a boy with a red scarf was asleep against a tiger's arm on the floor inside, and had been told nothing yet, by anyone, and was going to wake up and ask.

"A hair," he said.

*A hair*, said Hisui. *That's a dose.*
