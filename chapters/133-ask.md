Severin's father had said that the iron tells you when it's ready.

He'd said it about a thousand times, in the forge at Brakewater, with the river going past the open door and the bellows breathing and a small boy at the anvil with a hammer too big for him, wanting to hit the thing *now*. *Not yet*, his father would say. *Look at it. It'll tell you.* And the boy would look at the iron in the fire, cherry and then orange and then the pale straw-yellow you could only see if you'd been looking at it long enough, and his father would say *now*, and he'd hit it, and it would move under the hammer like butter.

*You can't make it ready*, his father had said. *You can only wait till it is. And then you can't wait any longer, or it's gone.*

He'd waited twelve years to ask this question.

He'd thought of it the first day. A parade ground at Cinderhold, in the dust, a commoner's son of twenty-two with a black sword on his back that no one could lift, standing in the line of new Honed swordsmen waiting to be looked at by the general who'd been sent to hold the east. Severin had come out to Cinderhold to look at the new swords himself, the way he did in those years: a smith's son from the south who'd gone to war at twenty and come up through it one tier at a time, Gleaming, Shaped, Dominion, Ascendant, and still walked down the line on his own two feet. He'd walked down it looking at feet, the way he looked at everyone's. And he'd stopped in front of the boy with the black sword, and looked at the sword, and looked at the boy, and felt it: a banked fire, very deep, that had no business being in a boy of twenty-two.

*Where did you come from?* he'd wanted to say.

He hadn't. The iron hadn't been ready. He'd asked about the sword instead, and the boy had said *I found it*, and that had been true, and not the answer to anything.

He'd wanted to ask it in a tent, five years later, the night before the Redmarch, when they'd argued until the candles went out. He'd wanted to ask it the morning after, sitting beside a grave in the red for an hour with a man who'd gone in at Shaped and come out at Dominion and wasn't saying anything at all. He'd wanted to ask it every year for eight years, reading reports about a fisherman by a lake. He'd wanted to ask it on a jetty in the autumn with a half-cut sword between them and the lake ringing like a bell.

*Ask me in the spring*, he'd said, then. And then the spring, and a single word on cream paper. *Ask.*

He was sitting on the bottom tier of the Lists at midnight on midsummer night, in the dark, on his own, and he could feel that the iron was ready.

***

The Lists were empty.

They'd emptied slowly. After the Banner, after the Kettle's four glasses and the Committee's one and a quarter and forty thousand people laughing so hard the bell-ringer had missed his cue, the crowd had gone out into the city to eat and drink and shout about it, and the sand had been raked, and the marshals had pulled the banner off the mound and gone home. The stands were dark. The bracket on the east wall was a long dark shape with one box filled in, gold line and all, *THE KETTLE. IV*. The paper fishing rods along the rail of the royal box moved a little in the warm wind.

Severin had sent everyone away. Kestra was at the side gate, outside it, with her back to it; he could feel her there, a small hard bright point in the dark, Reaching, very still. Nobody would come through that gate tonight who Kestra didn't want to come through it.

Halvard was on the tier above him. He hadn't sat down. He'd been standing for an hour, with his hands behind his back, thin and grey in the moonlight, looking at the sand. Severin could feel him too. A thin careful flame, burning very steadily, as if it were afraid of going out. It had been burning like that for eight years.

"Sit down, Halvard," said Severin.

"I'd rather stand."

"You'll fall down."

"Then I'll fall down," said Halvard, and didn't sit.

Severin let him. You couldn't make a man sit down any more than you could make iron ready. He sat on the stone with his big hands loose between his knees and looked at the sand and waited.

He felt them before he heard them.

Two of them. Coming in by the main gate, the one at the far end under the west stand, which was supposed to be locked and which Drew had a key to. One very bright and quick and hot, like a sword being swung by someone who's just discovered it fits. And one beside it that wasn't a candle at all. A banked fire, deep, the same one he'd felt on a parade ground a dozen years ago and more in a boy of twenty-two, except that it had stopped being banked. It had been coming back up all year. He'd felt it come up in the autumn on the jetty, a crack running through stone like lake ice. He felt it now, walking across the sand of the Lists toward him in the dark, and it was burning.

Not angry. Severin had felt it angry once, on the Redmarch, the night it went from Shaped to Dominion. This wasn't that. This was a fire somebody had finally let breathe.

He stood up.

***

They came across the sand in the moonlight. A girl of seventeen with a sword the size of a door on her back, and a man with a fishing rod on his shoulder. And two small round yellow things on his head, which were apparently bees, and which Severin had decided at the Lance not to ask about.

They stopped at the foot of the bottom tier.

"Severin," said Ars.

"Ars."

"Halvard."

Above him, Halvard made a sound that wasn't a word.

The girl looked up at the king. She'd looked at him the same way in the yard that morning, and at the forge in the autumn, and in the hen-yard the first day with her arms folded: deciding whether he was worth the trouble. Her mother's look. Severin had had it turned on him once, at Cinderhold, over a stitched forearm. *Hold still or I'll stitch your mouth shut too.*

"You came," said Severin.

"He asked if I would," said Alea. "Dad said I would." She paused. "I would've come anyway."

"I know you would," said Severin. "That's why I asked."

***

He'd thought about how to ask it for twelve years, and in the end he didn't think about it at all. He just sat back down on the bottom tier, because standing felt like being a king, and he didn't want to be a king for this. He wanted to be a man on a step. Ars sat down on the sand at his feet, cross-legged, with the rod across his knees, the way he sat on the end of his jetty. Alea sat beside him. Halvard stayed standing, on the tier above, like a post.

The Lists were very quiet. The wind moved the paper rods on the rail.

"I said I'd ask in person," said Severin. "In the spring. Then I said midsummer."

"You did."

"It's midsummer." He looked at the man on the sand, whose face he'd never been able to see in all those years, whose face nobody could see. He'd stopped trying, about twenty years ago. He looked at his hands instead. A swordsman's hands, a fisherman's, a smith's striker's. "I've wanted to ask since a parade ground at Cinderhold. Since you were twenty-two with a sword nobody could lift. I didn't, because you weren't ready, and I wasn't either." He paused. "I think we both are now."

Ars didn't say anything.

"Where did you come from?" said Severin.

***

There was a long silence on the sand.

Severin had been in a great many silences. Council silences, cold and careful, where twelve lords waited to see which way the king would go. Battlefield silences, the terrible quiet just before. The silence of a forge at night with the fire banked. This was none of those. This was the silence of a man deciding to put something down that he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

He felt it go. The banked fire on the sand in front of him didn't flare. It just settled. The way a load settles when it's set on the ground.

"Somewhere else," said Ars.

Severin waited.

"Not Kharvos," said Ars. "Not past the border, or past the mountains, or over the sea. Not anywhere on any map anyone in this world has ever drawn." He turned the rod slowly in his hands. "Another world. Like this one, a bit. People, and cities, and seas. No magic. No windows. No aura. Nobody's ever been past Initiate there, because there's nothing to be past." He was quiet. "I was born there. I grew up there. I had a mother and a father and a name and a life. And when I was twenty-six I went to sleep one night in a room with a fan going, and I didn't wake up."

Above them, on the tier, Halvard had gone absolutely still.

"I died," said Ars, quite simply. "In my sleep. Nobody knows why. I just didn't wake up." He looked at the sand. "And then I woke up here. Not grown. A baby. In a commoner's house in a village in the hills, with a mother and father I'd never met, who called me a name I'd never heard. Ars." He paused. "I remembered all of it. Everything. Twenty-six years of somewhere else, in a body that couldn't hold its own head up."

The girl beside him had put her hand on his knee. She didn't say anything. She'd heard this, Severin understood. Many times. In the dark, at the top of a stair, at bedtime, from when she was small. She'd heard it so often it was just a story to her, the way the Brakewater river song was just a song to him; and she'd put her hand on his knee anyway, because she'd never heard him tell it to anyone else.

***

"Thirty-four years," said Ars. "Here. That's how long. I was born, and I grew up, and I was six when I said a word under a blanket in a language nobody here had ever spoken, and a window opened in front of my face." He looked up. Not at Severin. At Halvard. "The same word I taught you. On the wall. At Cinderhold. When I was fifteen."

Halvard put his hand on the stone of the tier beside him.

"*Status*," said Ars. Quietly. Not in this world's speech. In something else. A short flat strange word like a stone dropped on a step. "It's from there. From the language we spoke. It's a word we used in games, for this." He gestured at the air in front of his face. "For a window that tells you who you are. I said it at six because I was frightened and it was the only thing I could think of that might help. And it opened."

"Every window," said Halvard. His voice was very faint. "Every window in the kingdom."

"Opens on a word from somewhere else," said Ars. "Yes."

Halvard sat down.

Not on the tier. Not carefully. His legs went, and he sat down on the stone step behind him all at once, the way he'd sat down on a wall walk at Cinderhold twenty years ago with a door open in front of his face for the first time, and put both his hands over his mouth.

Severin didn't look at him. You didn't look at a man when that happened. He kept his eyes on the sand.

***

"Go on," said Severin.

Ars went on.

He told it plainly. Severin would remember that for the rest of his life: how plainly. Like a smith telling you how a blade was made, step by step, without once telling you it was difficult. A field behind his father's house, at fourteen, where something in his hands had drawn out of him for the first time and he'd sat down in the furrow and laughed. The border garrison at fifteen. A wall at midnight. A court mage who came up behind him in the dark. A sword on a hill past the border, standing alone in the ground like a marker, that burned the things that touched it. A healer with cold hands who'd stitched him three times in a month and asked him why he kept getting hurt, and been the first person he'd ever told.

"She knew," said Ars. "Diwa. All of it. From the first year. She said she'd always known there was something wrong with me." Something moved in his voice. "She said it was the way I looked at the stars. Like somebody looking for a particular window in a house they'd moved out of."

Alea's hand tightened on his knee.

"I taught her the letters," said Ars. "On a slate. The ones from there. She only ever wrote three words." He didn't say which. Severin knew one of them. He'd stood beside a headstone at a lakeside forge and watched a man carve *TO DIWA, MY LOVE* into black iron in letters nobody in this world could read.

"And then there was Alea," said Ars. "And a lake. And the Redmarch." He stopped there, the way a smith stops at the quench. Severin knew the rest. Everyone did. "And then eight years of fish."

He was quiet for a while.

"I told Alea when she was old enough," he said. "And nobody else. Not you. Not him." He nodded up at Halvard, on the step. "Not anyone. Diwa knew, and she took it with her. Alea knew. That was all. For thirty-four years." He looked at the sand. "I thought that was how it was going to be. I'd made my peace with it. I'd stopped looking for the window."

"And then," said Severin.

"And then two summers ago," said Ars, "a court mage I taught to open a door took my sword off her grave and put it in the middle of a circle in a hall in Solmere, and thirty-five people came through it."

***

Severin had known the next part was coming. He'd known since the jetty, since the autumn, since the man beside him on the end of a jetty in the dark had said *they're my friends* about five girls in a militia hall and then stopped, as if he'd said too much. He'd known since Oswin, in a dusty archive, quoting something old: *a summoning is a net. It catches what is tangled in it.*

He'd known, and he hadn't asked, because the iron hadn't been ready.

"Who are they?" said Severin.

"My friends," said Ars.

He said it simply. The way he'd said *I died*.

"From there," he said. "From the other world. Thirty-five of them. Some of them I'd known since I was thirteen. Two of them were the brothers I never had. Some of them I'd only known a few months. Some of them I'd never met in person at all." He turned the rod in his hands. "We used to play together. Every night. Across the whole world. There was a thing we had there, a kind of window everybody had, a box of light you could talk through, and play through. We played at war in it. Every night. For years. From different countries. Different sides of the world. I was in one place and they were in thirty others, and we sat in the dark every night and played and talked and laughed, and I made jokes, and they laughed at them, and that was my whole life." He paused. "That was who I was. The funny one. In a box of light."

The Lists were very quiet.

"And then I died," said Ars, "and only three years passed for them. Three years. While I lived thirty-four here." He looked at his hands. "Time runs differently. Eleven years here for every one there. I worked it out last year, from a date under my window. Three years. They were still grieving when your circle caught them. Some of them had only just stopped." His voice was very even. "They didn't come because they were strong, or clever, or chosen. They came because the circle had my sword in the middle of it, and they were tangled in me. Every one of them. Somebody who'd brought somebody to somebody, all the way back to me." He looked up. "That's why they knew each other. That's why it was thirty-five, not three. That's why your Advisor sorted them in fives by who'd brought who, and every five made sense." A pause. "It caught what was tangled in it. And what was tangled in it was everyone I ever loved."

***

Halvard made a sound on the step.

Severin turned his head, then. He couldn't help it.

The Advisor of the kingdom, the thin grey man who'd stood beside him for forty years in council and on walls and in tents, who'd pulled thirty-five people out of the sky without asking anyone, was sitting on the stone step of the Lists in the moonlight with his hands over his face. And he was saying something. Very quietly. Over and over. Not to Severin. Not to anyone.

"*They were not strangers*," said Halvard.

He took his hands away from his face.

"I wrote you a letter," he said. To Ars. His voice was barely there. "The night after the summoning. I never sent it. It's in the drawer." He breathed. "*I'm sorry, Ars. I brought you thirty-five strangers.*" He stopped. "That's how it started. That's what I thought. That I'd pulled thirty-five strangers out of the sky on the strength of your sword, and sent them to the edges of the kingdom to die for us, and that you'd see them pass your door and think of the woman whose grave I'd robbed to do it." He put his hand on the stone. "*They were not strangers*. They were yours. I took your sword off her grave and used it to catch everyone you'd ever—"

He couldn't finish.

Ars got up off the sand.

He went up the tier. Two steps. He crouched down on the stone in front of the thin grey man who'd sat down, and looked at him, the way he'd looked at the five girls on the market road and Group 3 on the red and thirty people at a north gate, as if he were reading something written on Halvard's face and wanted to get every word.

"You brought them to me," said Ars.

Halvard looked at him.

"You didn't know," said Ars. "You couldn't have. You took a sword off a grave because it was the strongest thing you could find, and you put it in a circle, and you pulled, and it brought you my friends." He was quiet. "I hadn't seen them in thirty-four years, Halvard. I'd stopped looking for the window. And then five girls blocked my road on the way to market, and I stood there with my fish and looked at every one of them."

He put his hand on Halvard's shoulder.

"You brought me my friends," said Ars. "That's not a thing you need to put in a drawer."

***

Severin looked away.

He looked out at the sand, silver and empty under the moon, at the mound in the middle with its bare pole, at the bracket on the wall. He let the two of them have the step. Some things you didn't watch.

The girl was looking at him.

She was still sitting on the sand where her father had left her, with her knees drawn up and the black sword on her back and her chin on her knees. Looking at the king, with her mother's look. Deciding.

"You knew," said Alea. "Didn't you. Some of it. Before."

Severin considered lying. He'd been king a long time. He was good at it when he had to be.

"I knew he wasn't from here," he said. "I didn't know where from. I knew the first day. On the parade ground." He looked at the black iron on the girl's back, the half of it she called *Astra*. "He had that on his back. Twenty-two, a commoner's son, with a sword nobody could lift, that he'd brought back out of Kharvos on his own. From a hill a long way past the border, where it had been standing in the ground like a marker." He shook his head slowly. "He told me that at the forge, last autumn, when I finally asked who forged it. I'd wondered for twelve years before I did."

"He went into Kharvos," said Alea. "Once. When he was nineteen. On his own." She said it the way you'd say a thing you'd heard at bedtime a hundred times. "He says it was stupid."

"It was very stupid," said Severin. "It was the stupidest thing I've ever heard of a garrison boy doing." He looked at the sword. "And he came back with that." He paused. "I used to think he was like it. A thing from somewhere nobody goes, that turned up standing where it had no business being, and wouldn't let anyone else lift it." He looked at the man on the step above them, crouched in front of Halvard. "Now I know where he turned up from."

Alea looked at the sword over her shoulder. Then at the king.

"You're not angry," she said. "That he never told you."

"No."

"Why not?"

Severin thought about it. Properly. The way his father had taught him to think about a piece of iron before he put it in the fire.

"Because I never asked," he said. "Twelve years. I never once asked him. I told myself it wasn't time. And it wasn't." He looked at the girl. "Your father waits until things are ready. So do I. It's the only thing we've ever really had in common, apart from your mother stitching both of us." He paused. "You don't get angry at iron for not being ready. You just keep it in the fire."

Alea looked at him for a long moment.

"That's a smith thing," she said.

"Everything's a smith thing," said Severin.

***

Ars came back down off the tier after a while, and sat on the sand again, beside his daughter. Halvard stayed on the step. He'd stopped saying anything. He was just sitting, with his hands open on his knees and his face turned up to the moon, the way a man sits after he's been carrying something up a hill for eight years and has just set it down at the top and isn't quite sure yet what to do with his arms.

"Thirty-five," said Severin.

"Thirty-five."

"From another world. Your friends. Brought through a circle in my capital by my Advisor, with my permission, on the strength of your sword." He turned it over, the way you'd turn a blank over in your hands before you decided what it wanted to be. "And you've known since the first week."

"Since the first day," said Ars. "Five girls. On the market road. I knew every one of them."

"And you didn't tell them."

"Not for a year and a half."

"Why not?"

Ars was quiet for a long time.

"Because I was frightened of their faces," he said eventually. "Because thirty-four years is a long time and three years isn't, and I didn't know what they'd see when they looked at me. Because Halvard said they'd be one target if they were together and I thought I was the biggest target of all. Because I was a coward." He turned the rod. "All of those. None of them were true, in the end. They were just reasons. I had a lot of reasons. I laid them all out on a jetty one night and they turned out to be the same fish."

Severin almost laughed. He didn't.

"And now they know."

"All of them," said Ars. "As of the north gate. And Group 3 at sunset yesterday." A pause. "And you. And him." He nodded up at the step. "That's thirty-seven. And Alea. And Diwa."

"Thirty-nine."

"Thirty-nine," Ars agreed. "Out of a whole world. It's more than I ever thought there'd be."

***

Severin stood up.

He didn't decide to. He found he'd done it. He stood on the bottom tier of the Lists in the moonlight, a big grey man of sixty-five with soot still faintly in the creases of his knuckles from a day of shoeing horses at Hesk's, and looked out at the empty sand, and felt something he hadn't felt in a long time. Not since a forge in Brakewater, on leave, twenty-six years ago, on the twelfth night of making plough blades, when the world bent round him and he understood, all at once, what he'd been for.

The iron was ready. He'd waited for it. And now he couldn't wait any longer.

"There's a door," he said.

Ars looked up at him.

"In Solmere," said Severin. "In the archive under the castle. Where Oswin's been reading since the cat girl got the key out of Halvard." He nodded at the step without looking at it. "Behind a shelf, at the back of the summoning vault. A small iron door. Sealed with King Aldemar's seal." He paused. "Aldemar's been dead three hundred and twelve years. Nobody can use his seal. Nobody can break it, except a king."

"And you've never opened it."

"I never had a reason," said Severin. "Halvard told me, in the winter, after Oswin found it. *That one needs the king. And the king needs a reason.*" He looked down at the man on the sand. "I've been king a long time. I've never once broken another king's seal. There's a rule about it, older than the one about who's allowed to speak to me. You don't open a dead king's door unless you've got a reason he'd have accepted. You stand in front of it and you say the reason out loud, and if it's not good enough, you leave it shut and go away and think about what kind of king you are."

He looked out at the sand.

"I didn't have one," he said. "A summoning that caught thirty-five strangers wasn't a reason. A war coming wasn't a reason; there's always a war coming. Wanting to know wasn't a reason. Wanting to know is never a reason." He turned back. "But thirty-five people who were brought here because they loved a man. Who have mothers waiting, some of them. Who were caught in a net that was never meant for them, on the strength of a sword off a grave." He breathed. "Who want to go home, some of them. And whose way home is a thing nobody's seen in four hundred years."

He looked at Ars. At a face he couldn't see.

"Then I have a reason," said Severin.

***

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Halvard had taken his hands off his knees. He was looking at the king from the step above, with something in his thin grey face that Severin hadn't seen there in forty years. Not since they were both young, and the war hadn't happened yet, and neither of them had anything in a drawer.

"After the Lists," said Severin. "When your girl's won or lost her thing on the sand, and I've fed the thirty-five at my table, and the cat's asked me his question." He nodded to himself. "I'll go back to Solmere. I'll go down into the vault with Oswin and his key. I'll stand in front of a dead king's door and I'll say the reason out loud." He paused. "And if it's good enough, I'll open it."

"And if it isn't?" said Alea.

"It is," said Severin. "I knew it was the moment he said *my friends*." He looked at her. "You don't need to hit a piece of iron to know it's ready. You can see it in the colour."

Alea looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly, the way her mother had nodded once over a stitched forearm, satisfied.

"You're worth the trouble," she said.

"You said that this morning."

"I'm saying it again," said Alea, "because I mean it more."

***

They stayed on the sand until the moon was going down.

Not talking, mostly. Severin sat back down on the bottom tier. Halvard came down off his step, eventually, and sat beside him, close, the way he'd sat beside him in tents for forty years, and didn't say anything, and didn't need to. Ars sat on the sand with his rod across his knees, and the girl beside him leaned sideways, slowly, until her head was on his shoulder, between her ears and his coat, and after a while she was asleep. The black sword on her back leaned against him like a second person.

The wind moved the paper rods on the rail of the royal box. Ten thousand more in the windows of the city, all the way out to the gates. *For the Hero. Just in case.*

"They still don't know," said Severin, at some point. Quietly, so as not to wake the girl. "The city. What the rods are for."

"No."

"They'll find out."

"At the end," said Ars. "If she wins. She's going to fill in a box." He looked down at his sleeping daughter. "She's left it blank on Lucan's roll. She wants to win something that's hers first."

"And if she doesn't win?"

"Next year," said Ars. "That's what she told you."

"That's what she told me," Severin agreed.

He looked down at the girl asleep on her father's shoulder with a door-sized sword on her back. At the man, whose face he'd never seen, whose name wasn't Ars, who'd died in his sleep in another world and woken up in this one and carried it all for thirty-four years and put it down tonight on the sand of the Lists in front of a smith and a mage and his daughter.

"What was it?" said Severin. "Your name. Before."

Ars didn't answer for a moment.

"Declan," he said.

Severin tried it. It sat strangely in his mouth, like a word in the language of a country he'd never visit. "Declan."

"Nobody here calls me that. Except the ones who knew me in person. Gabe. Deston. Xav. A few others." He was quiet. "Online, I was Ice. Most of them call me that."

"Which do you want?"

Ars thought about it for a long time.

"Ars," he said. "From you. You've called me Ars since I was twenty-two. I'd miss it."

Severin nodded slowly.

"Ars, then," he said.

And then, because the moon was going down and the girl was asleep and the Advisor of the kingdom was sitting beside him with his hands open on his knees for the first time in eight years, and because there wasn't anything else that needed saying, the king of Aurelan began, very quietly, under his breath, to hum.

An old tune. A river song. The one the bargemen sang on the river at Brakewater, coming down past the forge door on summer evenings, when a small boy stood at an anvil with a hammer too big for him and waited for the iron to tell him it was ready.

After a while, on the sand below him, very quietly, so as not to wake his daughter, the man with the fishing rod hummed it back.
