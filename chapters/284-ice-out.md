The ice went on a Thursday.

Gigichi heard it go. Everybody on the lake heard it; you couldn't not. She was on her bench at first light, the way she was most mornings now as well as most evenings, with her coat buttoned up to her chin and her cup between her hands and the gold watch ticking on its chain at her hip; and out in the grey, a long way off toward the middle of the lake, something went *crack*, very deep, like a tree coming down in a wood a mile away. And then nothing. And then another, nearer. And then a long low groan that went all the way across from the far shore to the near one and seemed to come up through the bench into her, the way a cart going past comes up through your feet.

By noon the whole lake was moving.

Not fast. It never was. It broke up in great grey slabs the size of barn floors that turned slowly in the black water between them and ground against each other with a noise like a mill, and lifted at the edges, and went under, and came up again somewhere else. The fishermen came down from the town to watch, the way they did every spring; Bram stood on the shingle with his hands in his belt and his boy beside him and said *about time*, the way he said it every spring. The children threw stones. Waddles stood on the end of the trough board in the yard and shouted at all of it.

And the man on the end of the jetty fished.

He'd been fishing through a hole in the ice off the end of the jetty since the second week of the freeze. The hole was gone now; the ice it had been in was a slab turning slowly twenty paces out with a stool-mark still on it. He'd moved his stool back to the last plank and dropped his line straight down into the open water between the jetty posts, where the ice had already gone, and sat there with the rod across his knees and his tea going cold beside him on the plank, as if nothing at all were happening to the lake.

Gigichi watched him from the bench.

She'd watched him all winter. She hadn't meant to; it had just happened, the way the bench had happened. She'd sat on a stone by the lake road one evening the autumn he was away west, waiting for nothing, because she didn't want to be in the yard with everybody being nice to each other; and she'd put her hand on the ground without thinking and something had come up under it, and the next evening there'd been more of it, and by the first snow it was a bench. Roots, mostly. Grey-brown in the winter, and smooth, and the shape of somebody sitting. She hadn't grown it on purpose. She'd just kept sitting there.

It had a back now. That had come at midwinter. She'd been sitting on it the night he came home over the last hill, and she'd felt it come up behind her shoulders while she watched him walk down the lake road to the jetty, a curve of root like an arm along the back of a chair; and she'd leaned on it, and not thought about it.

She looked at it now. It had leaves.

Little ones. Pale green, the size of her smallest fingernail, all along the curve of the back where her shoulders went. They hadn't been there yesterday.

"Oh, *shut up*," she said to it. Out loud.

It didn't.

***

The post came over the ridge road in the afternoon.

The roads had been iron all winter, and the riders had kept coming, a few a month, with the snow kicked up behind them; but the road off the ridge was always mud the week the ice went, and the rider came down it with his horse in mud to the hocks and his coat brown to the waist and a face like a man who'd been told the road was fine. He stopped at the militia hall in the town. And a little later, one of the Tallow boys came along the lake road at a run with a letter in his hand and his tongue out, and saw her on the bench, and stopped.

"For him," he said. "From down the river. It says *urgent*. On the outside. In the Hero's letters." He held it out. "Mother says you read them."

Gigichi took it.

It was a single sheet, folded and sealed with a blob of plain candle wax, gone grey with the road. The outside was written in pencil, in a hand she didn't know, big and square. In English.

*Declan.*

And under it, smaller, the same hand: *Not urgent. Nothing's wrong.*

Somebody else had written *urgent* across the top in charcoal, in the kingdom's letters, which she couldn't read either, but which the Tallow boy evidently could. The post-house in Kingsferry, probably. Somebody who'd seen *Declan* in the Hero's letters and decided that anything written in the Hero's letters was urgent.

*Declan*. Not *Ice*. Group 1 said Ice; they always had. It was what they'd called him on stream, and in chat, and in the channel the first time anybody dared to say it out loud. *Declan* was the other lot. The ones who'd sat across a table from him. Group 6. Group 4.

She turned it over. On the back, along the fold, in the same square pencil: *D, G, C, D, R*.

Group 4.

Who had been quiet in the channel for a month about the sword. Gigichi had noticed. She noticed things; it was most of what she did, now. She'd noticed Deston say *rule four* the night Thea read *Stay*, and Calvin say something about an old woman and a nail and Deston say *Cal* and Calvin say *sorry*. And then Group 4 talking about soup and wind and Harl's files, for a month, and not one word about the black sword, from five people who'd never once been quiet about anything.

*Nothing's wrong*, it said, on the outside.

She was a pessimist. She'd been one her whole life. Nobody ever wrote *nothing's wrong* on the outside of a letter if nothing was.

"Thank you," she said to the Tallow boy, and gave him a copper out of her coat, which was too much, and watched him go off along the lake road at a run.

Then she got up off the bench and walked down to the jetty.

***

He heard her coming. He always did; he heard everybody coming. He didn't look round. He sat on his stool on the last plank with the rod across his knees and the grey slabs turning in the black water past the end of the jetty, grinding, and the line going straight down between the posts.

She stopped two planks back.

"Post," she said. In English. "From Group 4."

He looked round then.

She held it out. He looked at it in her hand for a moment. At the pencil on the outside, *Declan*, and *Not urgent. Nothing's wrong*. She saw him read it. She saw his face not do anything, which was what his face did most of the time; and then she saw it do something very small that she didn't have a word for, at the corners of the eyes. Like a man who's been waiting for a knock on the door and has just heard it.

He put the rod down on the plank. He took the letter.

She went back up the jetty and sat on her bench.

She didn't watch him read it. She wasn't going to do that. She sat on the bench with her back against the curve of root with its new leaves and her hands round the cup, which had gone cold, and looked at the lake. At the slabs turning. At Bram on the shingle with his boy. At the yard behind the cabin, where Xola was doing something to the trough with her *Tide* while Waddles shouted at her, and PenguChi was hanging washing in a wind that would have it all in the lake by supper, and Higu was sitting on the step with her notebook, not writing in it.

Higu looked up. At the jetty. Then at Gigichi.

Gigichi shook her head. Very slightly. Higu looked at her a moment longer and went back to her notebook.

The watch ticked.

***

He read it a long time. Longer than one sheet took. She didn't watch; but she could see the end of the jetty out of the corner of her eye without turning her head, the way you could see anything if you'd been watching it all winter, and she saw that he'd stopped reading and was still holding it. Folded again. In both hands. On his knees, where the rod had been.

He sat like that until the light went.

Nobody went down. Nobody in the yard went near the jetty all afternoon. Gigichi didn't know whether that was because they'd all seen her shake her head at Higu, or because they'd all seen his back, or because everybody who'd lived at the lake long enough knew what the end of the jetty looked like when you weren't supposed to go out on it. Alea came out of the cabin once, at the fourth hour, with two cups. She stood at the top of the jetty and looked down it at her father's back. And then she stood there a while longer, and didn't go down either, and put one of the cups on the top post, and went back in.

The cup sat on the post. Steaming. Then not.

At dusk, Gigichi got up.

***

She'd asked him at ice-out last year. On this jetty, on the last plank, at dusk, with her watch in her fist. She'd asked him in the frost before that, on the gap in the hedge, for the first time, *you okay?*, the way he'd asked her a hundred times since the first autumn without anybody else ever asking him; and he'd said *yes*, and she'd said *that's a terrible answer*, and he'd said *ask me again in the spring*.

So she had. And he'd said *no*. In English. Very quietly. And then *no, that's not right either, that's just the other half of yes*, and told her to sit down, and given her the mess: Diwa, getting further off like the hills and never smaller; saying goodbye to all of them a little at a time since the Lists; wanting them to go home and wanting them to stay and being ashamed of the second. He'd talked for an hour. She'd sat beside him on the plank with her boots over the black water and not said a word, and at the end of it he'd laughed, once, and said *thank you, Gigi*, and that had been all, and it had been the best night of the year.

She'd asked him every time she'd seen him since. Not every day. She wasn't a nag. But every few weeks, when he looked like a man who'd been asked *you okay* by everybody in the world except the one person whose job it was. At midwinter, from the bench, when he came home over the hill. He'd said *yes*. She'd said *good*, and *go on, then, she's on the end*.

She walked down the jetty in the dark.

He didn't look round this time. He'd heard her get up; he heard everything. She stopped beside the stool. She didn't sit down. The slabs were still going past the end of the jetty in the last light, grey on black, slower now, and the air coming off the lake was so cold it hurt her teeth.

"You okay?" said Gigichi.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

She'd known he wouldn't. She waited. The watch ticked in her coat. She thought, the way she'd thought last year: *if he says yes I'm going to push him in*. And meant it.

He didn't say yes.

"No," he said.

In English. Very quietly.

She waited for the rest. *That's not right either.* *That's just the other half of yes.* She waited for him to put his hands flat on the plank and say *sit down, Gigi, it's going to be a mess*.

He didn't.

He sat on the stool with the letter folded in both hands on his knees and looked at the lake, and didn't say anything else. And after a while she understood that he wasn't going to. That *no* was the whole of it. Not the other half of anything.

"Is it the other half of yes?" she said. Because she had to.

"No," said the man.

The watch ticked.

***

She sat down.

On the plank beside the stool. With her boots over the water, the way she'd sat last year. The cold came up through the wood into her. She didn't care.

"Okay," she said.

He looked at her then. For the first time. In the last of the light his face was very still and very tired, and his eyes were doing the thing at the corners again.

"I can't give you the mess," he said. "Not this one."

"Okay."

"It isn't finished." He looked down at the letter in his hands. "Somebody's bringing the rest of it. Up the river. When the river opens." He turned the letter over, once, so that she could see the back, and the five letters along the fold. "They say nothing's wrong."

"Is it?"

He didn't answer that.

She looked at the letter. At the pencil, *D, G, C, D, R*. She thought about Group 4 talking about soup for a month. About Calvin and an old woman and a nail. About Thea at the cabin table in the winter, with Higu counting heartbeats, reading one word folded into a black sword thirty-two thousand times, and coming out, at five hundred and nine, and Ice sitting on the step all night afterwards.

She didn't ask. She was a pessimist; she was usually right; she didn't need to ask.

"Okay," she said again. "Then I'll sit here."

"It's a month," said the man. "At least. The Wren's only just going."

"I've got a bench." She pulled her coat tighter round her and looked at the lake. "It's got leaves on it now. It did that this morning. Without asking." She sniffed. "So I'll sit on it. And when they come up the river with whatever it is, I'll be sitting on it. And after. That's all."

He didn't say anything.

"You don't have to say anything," said Gigichi. "That's the point. That's what *no* is. It's not a door shut. It's just a door. I'll sit outside it." She looked at the water. "I'm good at that. I sat outside Thea's for three years."

He made a sound. She didn't know what it was. It might have been a laugh, a very small one, the smallest she'd ever heard out of him; or it might not.

They sat there.

***

Alea came down at full dark.

She came down the jetty with the lamp, the one she'd taken off him at midwinter and said he could have back in the spring; she'd kept it lit every night since on the cabin step, Thea said, and taken it in at dawn. She came down the thirty-four planks with it in one hand and the other cup, the one off the top post, cold, in the other, and stopped behind her father's stool.

She looked at Gigichi on the plank. Gigichi looked back. Neither of them said anything; they'd never needed to much, the two of them, which Gigichi had always found restful.

Alea put the lamp down on the plank on his other side. She sat down beside it, on the edge, with her boots over the water too, so that he was between them. She held the cold cup out to him.

He took it.

He didn't drink it. He held it in his hands on top of the letter, on his knees, and looked at the lake; and Alea put her hand on the leather of Astra's grip where it came over her shoulder, the way she did sometimes without noticing, and looked at the lake too.

Out past the end of the jetty, the last slabs were going by, slow, black now, with only the lamplight on their edges. One of them had a stool-mark on it. It turned over, very slowly, the way the last slab had turned over last year, and went under, and didn't come up.

Gigichi sat on the plank and listened to her watch.

Back at the other end of the jetty, the landward end, where the planks began and the first grey post went down into the shingle, there was a young tree. An ash. Not much more than a stick still; up to her knee, with a tie of Xola's ribbon round it and a stake beside it that Maren had cut. A cutting off the ash on a knoll at the end of the world, that Maigo had grown in a pot all one summer and Alea had carried west across the whole width of the kingdom and Gigichi had found the place for with her hands on the stones, a year ago, at ice-out, the day she'd asked him the first time in the spring.

She'd said *here*, when she found it. And written it, after, with one finger, in the earth at the foot of the stick, where only the ground would read it. He'd told her that night that he hadn't known if he was going to laugh or sit down.

She looked back at it along the planks in the lamplight. It had one leaf. Pale green. Folded still. The size of her smallest fingernail.

She didn't say anything about it. He'd see it in the morning.

***

They sat there till the lamp burned low.

He didn't say anything else. Not about the letter, or the river, or what was coming up it. Alea didn't ask. Gigichi didn't ask. At some point the cold cup went down on the plank beside the stool, still full, and stayed there; and at some point Gigichi realised he'd put his hand down on the plank too, flat, beside the cup, the way you'd put a hand on a table to steady it; and that Alea, on his other side, had put hers down flat beside his, not touching, a finger away.

She didn't put hers down. It wasn't hers to put. She sat with her hands round her knees and her watch ticking and kept the plank warm, that was all.

When the lamp guttered, Alea picked it up.

"Bed," she said. To him. "You'll freeze. And she'll freeze, and then Higu'll be cross with both of us."

He looked at her. Then he got up, slowly, the way he got up when he'd been sitting a long time, and picked up the rod, and the cup. He put the letter inside his coat. Not in a pocket. Flat, against his chest, under the coat, where he'd have to take the coat off to get to it.

At the top of the jetty, he stopped. And turned round. And looked at Gigichi, who was still sitting on the last plank with her boots over the water because she didn't trust her legs yet.

"Gigi," he said.

"What."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"Thank you for asking," he said.

And went up the yard to the cabin, with Alea and the lamp beside him. And the door shut. And after a moment the window lit, the one on the lake side, low and yellow; and stayed lit.

***

She sat on her bench afterwards. She didn't go in for a long time.

She opened her window there, in the dark, because it was the kind of night when you did. She hadn't in a while. It didn't say much, usually, and what it said hadn't changed much since the autumn before last.

Ninety-one. It had been eighty-six at midwinter. Eighty-one in the autumn, when she'd first sat on a stone by the lake road and put her hand on the ground.

She looked at it a long time. She knew what *Briar* was: a hedge. Thorns, round a field, round a gap, round a cabin, saying *not here*. She'd grown one in the frost of the autumn before last and the window had named it, and she'd been proud of it, because it was exactly what she was. A hedge with a gap in it where she let one or two people through.

This wasn't a hedge.

She put her hand flat on the seat of the bench beside her. On the smooth grey-brown root, where it had grown the shape of somebody sitting. It was warm. Warmer than the night. And along the curve of the back, under her shoulders, she could feel the little new leaves, folded, cold, waiting for morning.

It didn't say *not here*. It had never once said *not here*. All winter, every evening, it had just said: *here's somewhere to sit*. For her. For whoever.

She didn't know what that was called yet. She thought it might be the other half of *Briar*. The way *no* had been the other half of *yes*, last year. And then wasn't, this year; was just *no*, on its own, a door, with somebody sitting outside it.

She shut her window.

She sat there till the cold got into her bones, which didn't take long; and then she went in, past the cabin, where the lake-side window was still lit, low and yellow; and she didn't knock. She just looked at it on the way past.

***

The channel had nothing in it that night about any of it. She checked, in her bunk, before she slept. Group 4 had posted about the Wren breaking up at Kingsferry, two days ago: Calvin, *it's going. the river's going*; Gabe, *hook's done. eye's done. point's done. seven letters*; Deston, *first boat up. all of us*. Rachael, *and me*. Dennis, *I've packed. I've packed twice*.

Nothing else. Nobody said what they were bringing.

At the bottom of the channel, an hour after everyone had gone to bed, there was one more line. From Higu.

[ 23:12 · Higu: Ice-out at the lake today. Everybody's fine. ]

Gigichi read it in the dark and thought: *liar*. And then thought: *no. That's not a lie. Everybody is fine. Everybody's just also something else.*

She thought she'd tell Higu that, in the morning. Higu would say *Chi, go to sleep*. Only Higu called her that. Only when it was late, and it was the two of them.

She went to sleep.

In the morning, the ash at the foot of the jetty had two leaves; and the man was out there before anyone, not on the end of the jetty for once but at the foot of it, sitting on the shingle by the first post with the rod across his knees, not fishing, and the young tree a hand from his boot. And on the bench by the lake road, when Gigichi came out with her cup, there were two cups already. One full, still warm. Waiting.
