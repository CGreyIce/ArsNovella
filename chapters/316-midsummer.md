*Session 1,288. The Open, day one. Sixteen in the draw, any path, first to two touches. Five of them are us.*

Chris wrote it on the bench under the west wall at dawn, before anybody else was up, with the lamps still lit above him in their boxes and the sand of the main ring raked smooth and grey in the half light, the way it only was before the first bout.

It was Lucan's idea. Of course it was. Everything in Valcrest that involved a crowd and a risk of disaster was Lucan's idea. For three hundred years the midsummer Lists had been swords: the open sword and the open spear and the Fledgling, and a mage who wanted to fight went to a college and fought in a cellar. And then thirty-five people had fallen through a floor in Solmere, and half of them were mages, and two summers running Lucan had built them a bracket on the walls of the bowl and let them fight in front of forty thousand people, and the forty thousand had come back the next year with their cousins.

So this year there was an Open. Sixteen places, by invitation of the master of the Lists, any path, any guild, any tier up to Gleaming. *Up to Gleaming* because the council had said Reaching was too much for a festival and Ysolde had said that was the first sensible thing the council had said since the black ice. Lucan had written the invitations himself in purple ink. Five had gone to the Broken Lance.

Newish had framed hers.

*Draw*, Chris wrote. *R1: Newish v Osric Fell (Ashby guard). Winter v Aurel Penn (Solmere college, fire). Ina v Bennet Rook (Lord Hallam's man, spear). Hako v Sgt Hew Talbot (cavalry, lance on foot). Me v Vesna Marl (Solmere fencing house, rapier). Garran Stoke in the bottom half. If it goes the way it goes, Winter meets Ina in the quarters and I meet Hako.*

He looked at that for a long time.

*Note*, he wrote under it. *I'd like it not to go the way it goes.*

***

Newish was first on. She made sure of it. She'd asked Lucan, and Lucan had said the draw was the draw, and she'd said *please*, and he'd put her first.

Osric Fell was the size of a door. He was a guardsman from Ashby with a guardsman's sword and a guardsman's shield and a voice that Chris had heard before, two summers ago, from the third tier of the north stand, shouting *demons* at Winter on the first morning. He didn't remember Chris. He remembered Newish. Everybody did. She was the one who'd talked back.

"Morning," said Newish, in the chalk. "You're the shouting one."

"I'm here to fight, girl, not—"

"You shouted *go back where you came from* at Winter," said Newish. "Two summers ago. North stand, third tier, about there." She pointed. The stand looked. "Which, honestly, fair, we'd love to, if you know the way, but nobody does, so here we are. You also had a cabbage. Did you bring the cabbage? I liked the cabbage. The cabbage had more to say for itself."

The near stand laughed. Fell went red from the collar up.

"*Begin*," said the marshal.

Fell came at her. Of course he did. Shield first, sword high, the big guardsman's rush that was meant to put you on your back in the sand before you'd thought, and that would have, if she'd been anybody else.

She hummed.

Chris heard it go out of her. Not loud. A little tune, three notes, the one she hummed on the stairs; and under it, *Tempo*, the thing she did to time. She didn't speed herself up. She didn't need to. She slowed him: not much, a hair, the last hair of the rush, the bit where a big man's weight is all on his front foot and he can't take it back. Fell's sword came down a heartbeat after it should have, into sand where she'd been, and she was a step to the left of it with her little wooden practice sword, the one she'd painted with stars, and she tapped him on the wrist with it. *Tap*. Like knocking on a door.

"*Touch*," said the marshal. "One–nothing."

"Sorry," said Newish. "Was that rude?"

Fell didn't say anything. He went back to the mark and turned round, and Chris saw his face, and logged it: *angry, now, properly. He'll go faster.* He did. The second rush was faster and lower and the shield came round wider, to catch her if she went left again. She didn't go left.

She stopped humming.

It went out of the bowl. Not just out of her. Chris felt it go: the sound of forty thousand people breathing, and fidgeting, and a child crying in the south stand, and the hawkers, and the gulls; all of it, at once, for one long beat, the way it had gone on the south stand stair in the hail. *Rest*. The near stand stopped. The whole bowl went quiet like a room when somebody important comes in.

And Osric Fell, in the middle of his rush, heard his own breathing.

Chris saw it hit him. A big man in a silent bowl, with forty thousand people suddenly not making any sound at all, hearing his own breath going in and out of him very loud, and his own boots in the sand. He faltered. Half a step. Not because she'd done anything to him. Because he was the only thing making a noise in the world and he didn't like it.

She tapped him on the shield arm. *Tap*.

"*Touch*. Two–nothing. Newish."

The bowl came back, all at once, roaring.

Fell stood in the chalk with his sword down and looked at her. He looked for a long time. Then he said, gruff, not quite to her: "Your friend with the horns."

"Winter."

"Tell her I was wrong." He looked at the north stand. "About the cabbage."

"I'll tell her," said Newish. "She'll say *rude*." And she bowed, which she never did, and walked off the sand humming.

***

Winter was third. Aurel Penn was a fire mage from the college at Solmere, twenty-two, a scholar's coat and a Candle window and a very good chant, and he'd come to Valcrest, Chris thought, because he wanted to be the first mage to beat one of the Thirty-Five in front of a crowd.

He chanted well. Chris had to give him that. It came out of him clear and fast, five lines, and on the fifth a ball of fire the size of a cabbage went across the chalk at Winter, and the near stand went *oh*.

Winter didn't move.

She stood in the chalk with her silver hair down her back and her horns catching the sun and her hands out of her sleeves at her sides, and the fire came at her, and a hand's breadth from her chest it went out. Not with a hiss. It just went out, the way a candle goes out when you open a door on a cold night, and there was a little curl of smoke where it had been, and the smoke had frost on it.

"*That's not*—" said Aurel Penn.

"It's midsummer," said Winter. "It's very warm. There's a lot of air moving." She was quiet. "Your fire makes its own wind. All fire does. It pulls the air in at the bottom." She looked at him. "I just stood where the air was coming from."

*Windward*. Chris logged it. *She doesn't throw cold at the fire. She stands upwind of it and the fire pulls her cold into itself.*

Penn chanted again. Faster. And this time Chris saw her do it on purpose: she breathed out, once, slowly, toward him, and the rime came up off the sand at her feet in little white feathers, all leaning one way, toward Penn, toward the fire he was making, the way rime leans into the wind; and it grew along the draught of his own spell, across the chalk, up his boots, to his mouth. To his breath. To the fifth line of his chant.

His breath froze on the fourth line.

Not his mouth. His breath; the little cloud of it, in the hot air, hanging white in front of his face. And the chant stopped, because the shape of it was in the breath, and the breath was ice, and he stood there with his mouth open and his hands up and nothing coming out of them.

Winter walked across the chalk and put her hand on his arm. Her cold hand. The one she'd put on Ina's arm on Lamp Night.

"*Touch*."

The second was the same. He tried to chant with his mouth shut, which nobody can, and she put her hand on his other arm.

"You're very good," she said to him, after, at the edge of the chalk, while he was rubbing his lips. "You're better than me at fire. I can't do fire at all."

"Then *how*—"

"I didn't do anything," said Winter. "You did. I stood where you were pulling from." She looked at him a moment. "Stand somewhere else next time."

***

Ina was fifth, and Chris didn't know until he was walking out whether he'd put the mask on.

He'd had it in his hand all morning. The red one, with the horns and the teeth and the grin, that Wil had held up on Lamp Night in front of forty thousand people. With it on, Ina's fire burned hotter; everybody knew. With it on, he looked exactly like what they'd been afraid of. He'd worn it for every bout of his life in this bowl.

Bennet Rook spat on the sand when Ina came out of the tunnel.

Not at him. Near him. On the sand, at the edge of the chalk, the way you'd spit by a dog you didn't want in your yard; and some of the north stand laughed, and some of it didn't, and Chris, on the bench, put his pen down.

Ina stopped at the edge of the chalk. He looked at the spit. He looked at Bennet Rook, who was a lord's man, a spearman, square and hard and forty, with a lord's colours on his sleeve and a look on his face that Chris had seen on a lot of faces, the first summer, and fewer since.

Then he gave the mask to Wil.

Wil was on the front bench. He always was. He was eleven now and too big for Lucan's cavalry helmet and wore it anyway. He took the mask in both hands and held it against his chest, the way he'd held it on Lamp Night, and looked up at Ina.

"Hold that for me, would you," said Ina. His voice was the other one. The one he used without the mask: low and careful and very English, like somebody reading a poem in a room with a fire. "I'd like to do this as myself."

He walked into the chalk.

It was a hard bout. Chris logged it after and the log went on for two pages. Rook was good: patient, long, a spear like a fence, and he went for Ina's hands, because everybody knew a mage needed his hands. The first touch was Rook's, a hard thrust that Ina didn't get his fire up in time to turn, on the shoulder. The north stand cheered. The rest of the bowl didn't.

Ina went back to the mark. He shut his eyes.

And when he lit, it was pale.

Chris had seen it once before, from across the bowl, the dusk Ina lit the twenty-seventh lamp on his own. White at the heart. Not the red oni-fire, the big roaring thing that came with the mask; a small pale flame, the colour of wisteria at the edges, sitting in his palm like something he was holding carefully so it wouldn't spill. It wasn't hot. It wasn't big. It was *exact*.

Rook came in with the spear.

Ina put the pale fire on the shaft. Not on Rook. On the spear, a hand's breadth above Rook's front hand, where the ash wood was dry with a summer's sun; one small flame, the size of a thumbnail, like a lamp in a window. And the ash caught. A ring of it, round the shaft, pale and quiet, and Rook felt the heat come down the wood toward his hand and did the thing any man does with a stick that's on fire at his fingers.

He let go.

Ina stepped inside the falling spear and touched him on the chest with two fingers.

"*Touch*. One–one."

The third was the same, nearly. Rook held on longer. He let it burn his glove; Chris saw the smoke. But Ina had lit three of them this time, three thumbnail flames in a row up the shaft like watch-fires along a wall, *Kagaribi*, the thing he'd done with the forty lamps, and the last one was at Rook's knuckles, and he let go.

"*Touch*. Two–one. Ina."

Rook stood in the chalk with his glove smoking and looked at Ina for a long time.

Then he looked at the spit, on the sand, at the edge of the chalk. He scuffed it out with his boot. He didn't say anything. He went off the sand with his back straight, and Ina went to the front bench, and Wil gave him the mask back, and Ina didn't put it on. He held it in his lap all afternoon.

***

Hako was fast. Hako was always fast now. Sergeant Hew Talbot had a cavalry lance on foot, twelve feet of ash with a steel point, and he'd won the open lance at the Ashby fair four years running, and he held it out in front of him like the end of the world. Hako looked at the point coming at him, and grinned his grin, the one Ysolde said was very nice, and went inside it.

Not round. Through. He put his shoulder down and went up the shaft of the lance at a run, the way you'd go up a gangplank, *Commit*, with the point going past his ear, and the shaft hit him across the chest, and he didn't stop, and the lance was twelve feet long and useless with a man at the four-foot mark, and Hako's spear butt was on the sergeant's belt buckle before the sergeant had worked out what had happened.

"*Touch*."

The second time Talbot shortened his grip, which was the right thing to do, and Hako did it again anyway, and took the shaft across the ribs this time, hard, *crack*, and grinned through it, and touched.

"Two–nothing."

"Are you all right?" said Talbot, after. "I heard that go."

"Yeah," said Hako. "That's fine." He rubbed his ribs. "That's really fine."

Chris logged it. *He takes it now. Since Stoke. He used to go sideways. He goes in and it hits him and he's glad.*

He didn't log his own till that night, because he was shaking.

Vesna Marl was from the fencing house in Solmere, where the nobles' children learned the rapier, and she was the best they'd ever had, and she was beautiful at it, and she had a hundred lines.

That was the trouble with *Trace*. He saw the plan. The silver line of the next blow, before it came, the way he'd seen it the first time with Niko's sword coming in under his arm; the thing in his logs, the pattern under the pattern. Most people had one line at a time. Big men had one line and a lot of weight behind it. Vesna Marl had a hundred. Every feint was a line; every flick of the point, every shift of her back foot, a line that might go true and might not, and his eyes filled with silver the way they had in the Street of Coopers market when he'd seen everybody's next step at once and sat down in the road.

She took the first touch. A flick to the inside of his wrist that he saw coming, along with ninety-nine other things he saw coming, and couldn't tell from them.

He went back to the mark and stood there with the silver going in his eyes.

*Look at one*, Ysolde had said, in the street, with him sitting in the gutter. *Let the others be furniture.*

He looked at her front foot. Only that. Not the point, not her hand, not her face; the front foot, in its soft fencing shoe, on the sand. And the hundred lines were still there, all the silver, but they were furniture. They were chairs in a room. He let them be chairs. And he waited, with his sword low, *Release*, for the one line that came from the foot.

It came. A lunge, long, beautiful, the real one, from the front foot pushing. He saw it go true.

He let go of his own feet.

Not a step. Less than a step. The thing he'd learned from Hako's spear last autumn, at the foot of the wall: you don't move till the line goes true, and then you release the whole of yourself into the gap the line leaves. Her point went past his ribs into the place he'd been. His sword was on her shoulder.

"*Touch*. One–one."

The third was the same. She'd seen it now; she gave him nothing but feints for a long time, a whole minute, the near stand getting restless. He sat in the furniture and looked at her foot. When it pushed, he went.

"*Touch*. Two–one."

She saluted him with the rapier, very properly, the way they did in Solmere, and then, less properly, laughed. "You weren't watching my blade at all," she said.

"No," said Chris. "There were too many of it."

*Session 1,289. The Open, quarters.*

*Newish v Marra Hesk. Winter v Ina. Me v Hako. Stoke v the other one.*

*It went the way it goes.*

***

Marra Hesk was sixty-one. She was a drover's widow from the downs with a quarterstaff she'd cut herself forty years ago, and a face like a walnut, and she'd beaten a Gleaming caravan guard in the first round in eleven seconds, and Lucan had invited her because she'd written to him and said she'd like to have a go before she died.

She had no beat.

Newish found that out in the first exchange. Chris watched her face do it. Newish heard the beat in everybody. It was her whole thing; she'd heard it in Ice at the north gate, two summers ago, *his whole beat was waiting*, and she'd heard it in every person she'd ever fought, the little rhythm under the way they moved, that told her when they'd move next. And Marra Hesk didn't have one. She moved the way weather moves. The staff came when it came. Sometimes twice fast and then nothing for ten heartbeats and then once, slow, from somewhere else.

Newish hummed. *Tempo*. And there was nothing to slow, because there was nothing in a rhythm; you can't slow down a thing that hasn't got a speed.

The staff came round and tapped her on the hip.

"*Touch*."

She tried *Rest*. Chris saw her try it: the bowl went quiet, the whole of it, the long held silence. And Marra Hesk stood in the middle of forty thousand silent people and didn't notice at all. She'd stood on the downs on her own with a thousand sheep and no other sound for forty years. She didn't need to hear herself. She'd never once listened.

The staff tapped Newish on the other hip.

"*Touch*. Two–nothing. Hesk."

Newish stood in the chalk with her starry practice sword and her mouth open. Then she laughed. Properly; all of her; the laugh she did when something was so annoying it went out the other side and became wonderful.

"You don't have a *beat*," she said.

"Don't know what that is, girl," said Marra Hesk. "You talk too much."

"*Rude*," said Newish, delighted, and bowed again, and went and sat on the bench next to Chris with her face shining and said, "I want to be her when I'm old. I want to be her *now*."

***

Winter and Ina met in the chalk the way Chris had known they would, since the draw, and neither of them looked at him.

They'd been the two everybody stared at. Since the floor in Solmere. The girl with the demon's horns and the oni with the demon's mask, in a kingdom that had spent ten years expecting demons. They'd walked through the streets of Valcrest together that first summer with people crossing to the other side, and Winter didn't like men, and Ina was the man who understood her best, and they'd never once fought each other. Not in practice. Ysolde had asked, twice. They'd both said no.

They stood in the chalk now and looked at each other.

"Mask?" said Winter.

"No," said Ina. He'd given it to Wil again. "Not for you."

"*Begin*."

Neither of them moved for a long time. The near stand got restless, and then it stopped being restless, because it understood, the way a crowd sometimes understands a thing all at once: that it was watching two people who knew exactly what the other one would do.

Ina lit first. Pale. A row of thumbnail flames, *Kagaribi*, not at her; round her, on the sand, a ring of little fires a pace out, the way you'd set watch-fires round a camp. Warm. Not hot. Just there.

And Winter couldn't stand upwind.

Chris saw it at once. Every fire pulls the air in at the bottom. A ring of fires round you pulls from every side. There was no *upwind*; the air was moving toward the flames from her, in every direction, outward, like a breath going out of her. Her rime came up off the sand at her feet and leaned. All ways at once. Toward the fires. It didn't know which way to grow. It grew a hand's breadth in a circle round her heels and stopped, confused, like a cat in a room with too many doors.

Ina walked through the ring and touched her arm.

"*Touch*. One–nothing. Ina."

Winter went back to the mark.

She didn't look at him. She looked at the sand. And when he lit the ring again, she shut her eyes, and did something Chris had never seen her do: she *pulled*.

Not hard. She'd never pull hard; Ysolde had said no, and Winter had learned in the spring what pulling cost, the blisters on three fingers, the cold that burned. But a little. She took a breath in instead of out. And for one heartbeat, *she* was the thing pulling the air. Not the fires. Her. All the little flames in the ring round her leaned toward her, inward, like heads turning; and the rime came up off the sand at her feet and grew outward, now, all ways at once, every feather pointing at a fire, toward the draught that was coming to her; and reached them, and went over them, and the fires went out one after another round the ring like lamps on a street at dawn.

She opened her eyes. Ina was standing in the middle of a ring of little frosted wicks with his hand still up.

She walked over and put her cold hand on his arm.

"*Touch*. One–one."

The third took a long time. Chris didn't log all of it. He couldn't; he forgot to. They went round each other in the chalk for most of a minute, fire and frost, Ina lighting and Winter breathing, the flames leaning toward her and the rime leaning toward them, and between them on the sand a line where the two met, white on one side and black scorch on the other, as straight as if somebody had drawn it with a rule.

And then Ina put the fire out himself.

He shut his hand on it. The pale flame in his palm. He closed his fingers and it went, and he stood there with nothing, and Winter's rime came across the line on the sand to his boots and stopped, because there was nothing past him to grow toward.

"What are you doing," said Winter.

"I'd rather lose to you," said Ina, "than anybody." He was quiet. "And I'd rather you knew I chose to."

Winter stood there a long time.

Then she walked across the white line on the sand and put her hand on his arm. Not quickly. She left it there.

"*Touch*. Two–one. Winter."

The bowl didn't cheer for a moment. It didn't seem to know how. And then the north stand started it, the stand that had shouted *demons* two summers ago and thrown a cabbage; and it went round the bowl and came back louder, and it wasn't for either of them. It was for both.

***

Hako beat him.

Chris had known he would. He'd beaten Hako once, last autumn, by waiting for *Commit* to go true and releasing inside the spear; and Hako had spent the year since learning the one thing Chris couldn't trace, which was getting hit.

He saw the line. Of course he saw it. Hako only ever had one: *in*. The silver came off Hako's front foot straight at Chris's chest like a road. And Chris waited for it to go true, *Release*, and it went true, and he released into the gap, and put his sword on Hako's shoulder exactly where he'd meant to, hard, a good touch, the best he'd made all day.

And Hako kept coming.

That was the thing you couldn't trace. Chris's sword on his shoulder, and Hako not stopping, not going sideways, not flinching; going *in*, *Inside*, through the blow, the way he'd gone inside Garran Stoke's hammer in the spring, with Chris's steel ringing on his collarbone and his grin a foot away; and his spear butt on Chris's breastbone, at the same moment, the same heartbeat, so that the marshal looked from one to the other and said, "Both," and gave neither.

"I hit you," said Chris.

"Yeah," said Hako. "It's fine." He rubbed his shoulder. "It's really fine. I knew you would. You always do the thing. You do it perfectly." He grinned. "So I just let you."

The next two touches were Hako's. Chris saw both of them coming down the road at him. He released into the gap both times, perfectly, and Hako came through his perfect blow both times, and touched.

"*Two–nothing. Hako*."

He sat on the bench afterwards with his log on his knee and didn't write anything for a long time.

Then he wrote: *I can see the line. He doesn't need me not to. Note: find out what I'm keeping at the end of my stick.*

He looked at his window that night, on the roof of the Lance, with the forty lamps lit on the wall across the bowl.

Garran Stoke went through Winter in the semi like weather through a hedge.

She tried everything. She stood upwind, and he wasn't making any wind; he wasn't a mage; he was a quarryman's son with a leather-capped hammer on a man-length shaft and an iron-rimmed shield the size of a cartwheel, and he walked. He walked in, the way he'd walked in at the spring final, one step at a time, and forty thousand people stamped it for him: *one, two*. She froze the sand under his boots. He walked on the ice. She breathed the rime up his shaft to his gloves, and it froze his hands to the leather, white to the wrist, and he didn't need them to let go, because he didn't let go; he came on with his hands frozen to his hammer and put the leather cap on her horn, very gently, as if he were knocking on a door he didn't want to frighten.

"*Touch*."

She took one off him. Chris logged it carefully. She did it the way she'd done the ring of fires: she pulled, a little, one breath in, and his breath came to her across the chalk, the hot breath of a big man walking, and froze in his beard, and for a heartbeat he couldn't see past his own face, and she put her hand on his shield arm.

"*Touch*. One–one."

But he didn't need to see. He'd walked in blind before, in a quarry, in dust. He walked in. *One, two*. The leather cap on her shoulder.

"*Touch*. Two–one. Stoke."

He took his frozen gloves off at the edge of the chalk, peeling them off his hands like bark, and looked at his white fingers, and then at her.

"I'd not want to do that again," he said. "In the winter."

"It's easier in the winter," said Winter.

"Aye," said Garran Stoke. "That's what I'm saying."

Hako beat Marra Hesk in the other semi, two–one, because Marra Hesk had no beat and Hako had never once needed one. He just went in. She hit him twice on the way, on the forearm, hard, the staff ringing off the bone, and he grinned and let her, and the second time he was close enough to touch, and touched.

"You're a strange boy," said Marra Hesk, on the sand.

"Yeah," said Hako. "That's fine."

The final was the next afternoon. Garran Stoke and Hako, first to two, on the main sand. The spring again, in midsummer. Forty thousand in the bowl.

And on the fourth tier of the east stand, in the morning, Chris saw her.

He didn't know when she'd come in. She hadn't come to the Lance. He looked up from the bench under the west wall at noon, the way he looked at the stands every morning to count them, and there she was: a fox girl in a travelling coat with a black sword over her shoulder too big for her, sitting on the fourth tier of the east stand on her own, where her father had sat two summers ago with a paper rod in his lap. Not in Lucan's box. Nobody near her. Just sitting.

Lucan saw her too. Chris saw him see her, from the master's box, and stand up, and sit down again, and say something to Wil.

Wil went up the east stand stairs ten minutes later with a jug.

***

Stoke didn't walk in.

That was the first thing. Chris saw Hako see it. In the spring Garran Stoke had walked in from the first touch, *one, two*, because that was what he did, what he'd always done, three springs champion of the open spear, and Hako had learned to go inside the walk. This time Stoke stood on the mark with his hammer on his shoulder and his great iron-rimmed shield on his arm and didn't move.

He waited.

The bowl didn't stamp. It didn't know what to stamp. Forty thousand people who'd come to shout *one, two* sat on their hands, and Garran Stoke stood on his mark like a quarry wall, and Hako stood on his with his spear low and his grin going a bit uncertain at the corners.

"*Begin*."

Nothing happened for a long time.

Then Hako went. He had to. Somebody had to, and it was never going to be Stoke. He went in fast, *Commit*, low, at the shield, to go round it to the hammer side the way he'd gone in the spring. And Stoke turned the shield. Just turned it, a quarter, the way you'd turn a door on its hinge; and the iron rim came round into Hako's path at the height of his chest, and Hako went into it.

It rang. The whole bowl heard it. Iron on a man's chest; a sound like somebody dropping a bell. Hako went back two paces on his heels and sat down in the sand.

And the leather cap of the hammer came down, very gently, on top of his head.

"*Touch*. One–nothing. Stoke."

Hako sat in the sand and looked up at the hammer on his head. Then he laughed.

"You waited," he said.

"I learned it off a lass in the Fledgling," said Garran Stoke. "Last summer. Fox girl, big black sword. She sat on her heels for a long while and let a boy come to her." He took the hammer off Hako's head. "Didn't think I'd ever use it. Then I lost to a grin."

Chris looked at the fourth tier of the east stand. Alea was sitting very straight.

Hako got up.

He didn't go in the second time. Chris logged it: *he's waiting. Hako's waiting. He's never waited in his life.* They stood on their marks, the two of them, the quarryman and the boy with the grin, for most of a minute, and the bowl was very quiet, and Chris thought it might go on all afternoon.

And then Hako did the thing nobody would ever have taught him.

He put his spear down.

On the sand. Laid it down, at his feet, carefully, the way you'd put down a thing you meant to come back for. And walked toward Garran Stoke with his hands empty.

The bowl made a sound Chris had never heard it make. Forty thousand people drawing breath at once.

Stoke didn't know what to do. Chris saw it go through him: the hammer coming up, and stopping, because you don't hit a man with no weapon, not in the Lists, not anywhere a quarryman's son was raised; and the shield coming round, and stopping, because there was nothing to block. He stood there with his hammer half up and his shield half round and a grinning boy walking at him with open hands, and for one heartbeat he was a man who didn't know what was coming.

And in that heartbeat Hako went *in*.

Not at Stoke. At the shield. He put both empty hands flat on the face of the great iron-rimmed cartwheel and *pushed*, the way you'd push a cart out of a rut, the way he'd pushed the carrier's cart off his back on the river road; and Garran Stoke, who weighed half as much again as Hako, who'd never been moved in the chalk in three springs, went back one step.

One step. Off his mark.

Hako let go of the shield, and dropped, and his hand found the spear on the sand behind him where he'd put it, without looking, because he'd put it exactly where his hand would be; and came up with it, the short end, and touched Stoke on the thigh plate under the rim.

"*Touch*. One–one."

The bowl went mad.

Stoke stood there, one step back from his mark, and looked at his own feet. Then at Hako. Then he started to laugh. It came up out of him like a rockfall, slow and then all at once, and he put his head back and laughed at the sky, and forty thousand people laughed with him without knowing why.

"*You put it down*," he said.

"Yeah," said Hako. "Sorry. I didn't know if it'd work."

"Nor did I," said Garran Stoke. "That's why it did."

The third touch was Stoke's.

Chris had known it would be. Everybody had, really, underneath; you don't beat a man like that with the same thing twice. Hako went in, the way Hako went in, *Commit*, and Stoke did the thing Hako had done to him in the spring. He went in too. A hammer man, going inside a spear. He shortened his grip on the hammer to a hand's breadth under the head and walked straight up the shaft of Hako's spear, *one, two*, and took the spear across his own chest, and didn't stop, and his face was a foot from Hako's grin; and he put the leather cap of the hammer on Hako's breastbone. Gently. The way you'd put your hand on somebody's chest to tell them to stay.

"*Touch*. Two–one. Garran Stoke."

They stood like that for a moment, chest to chest, the spear across one and the hammer on the other.

"That's yours," said Stoke. "I learned it off you."

Hako looked down at the hammer on his chest. He was grinning so hard Chris thought his face might come off.

"That's fine," he said. "That's really, really fine."

*Session 1,290. Fledgling, day four. The last afternoon of midsummer. New moon tonight.*

*The holder's bench is empty. Lucan's put the cushion on it anyway. The one with the fish.*

Chris went up the east stand at noon.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to sit on the bench under the west wall with the others, Newish with her starry sword in her lap and Winter beside Ina with her cold hand not quite on his arm and Hako with three bruised ribs and a grin, and log the final the way he'd logged everything. But he looked up at the fourth tier at noon, to count it, and she was still on her own. Four days. She'd sat there four days, Wil said, from the first bout to the last, with the jug Lucan sent and a pack at her feet and the black sword across her knees, and hadn't moved from the spot, and hadn't come down.

So he went up.

She looked round when he got to the fourth tier. Her ears went up and then down. She moved her pack, to make room.

"You don't have to sit with me," said Alea.

"I know," said Chris. "I've been logging things from the bench for four years. I've never logged one from up here." He sat. "Your father stood here."

"Two summers ago," said Alea. "And last summer for the final." She looked down at the sand. "He's at the lake. With Gigi."

Chris didn't ask. He'd read the channel. He knew where Ice was, and why, and that he wouldn't go down the river, and that a girl from the lake had come south on her own to keep a promise to a boy on a sand.

"Who are you for?" he said instead.

Alea was quiet a long time.

"Both," she said.

***

Tamlin Brand came out of the north tunnel and Sorrel of Gullhaven came out of the south one, and the bowl stood up.

Chris knew them both, a bit. Everybody in Valcrest did. Tamlin was the cadet from the king's guard school in Solmere, twenty since the spring and in only because the book counted your age on the first day of spring, with beautiful feet and a straight back and a plain good sword; semi two summers ago, final last summer, and both times beaten by the fox girl who was sitting on the fourth tier of the east stand with her hands in her lap. Sorrel was a fisherman's daughter from the Gullhaven quay, eighteen, salt-burned and barefoot, with a sailcloth band round her head and her dead father's boat-hooks cut down to fit her when she was ten. Two summers ago she'd hooked a nobleman's ankle and the far stand had chanted *HOOKS* until Lucan sent somebody to ask them to stop and then joined in.

They'd met before. Last summer, the semi. Sorrel had driven her left hook into the sand and swung round it like a boat round an anchor, and Tamlin had let his sword fall on the shaft and stood on it, and she'd sat down.

"*Begin*," said the marshal.

One touch. The old way, for the Fledgling final. Lucan had wanted two and the book said one and the book won.

Sorrel went first. She always did. She came in with both hooks swinging, the way she'd come at Alea the first summer, one high and one low, never deciding, a lure and a line; and Tamlin didn't chase the lure. He'd learned that. *Look at the whole thing*. He stood with his sword low and watched all of her at once, her feet and her shoulders and both hooks, and when the low hook came for his ankle he wasn't there. He was a pace to the left, with his weight on his toes, and his sword came round at her hook arm, and she pulled the arm in, and the sword went past.

Nobody touched.

"He's looking at all of her," said Alea, very quietly. "He's really doing it."

"You told him to."

"Two summers ago," said Alea. "In the semi. *Look at the whole thing. Next year*." Her hands were tight in her lap. "He's been doing it for two years."

The second exchange was Sorrel's. She did the anchor. Chris saw Tamlin see it coming: the left hook going down into the sand, the shaft bending, Sorrel's whole weight swinging round it. The thing he'd beaten last year by standing on the shaft. And he went for the shaft again, sword coming down; and it wasn't there. She'd let go. She'd driven the hook into the sand and *let go of it*, and swung round nothing, round the air where it had been, with her other hook out at the end of her arm like a gaff; and Tamlin's sword went into the sand where the shaft should have been, and Sorrel's right hook came round behind him and caught him by the back of the knee.

She pulled.

He went down. On one knee, in the chalk, with his sword in the sand and a boat-hook round his leg; and the bowl went *up*, the whole south stand on its feet; and Sorrel was over him with the hook and her grin like a gap in a fence, and her free hand coming in for the touch.

And Tamlin sat on his heels.

Chris saw it. He was looking right at it and he'd still have missed it if Alea hadn't gone very still beside him. Tamlin didn't fight the hook. He didn't pull his knee out of it. He let it have him. He let his weight go down off his toes and back on to his heels, in the sand, all the way, the way a fisherman's daughter on the fourth tier had sat on her heels in this chalk last summer, with forty thousand people sure she was losing, and let a boy come to her.

And Sorrel came to him.

She had to. She had the hook round his knee and her hand coming in; she was committed; she was all the way forward over a boy who'd stopped fighting. And the weight that had gone back on to his heels came up again. All at once. Off the heels and through the knee she was holding and into the sword, which he'd never let go of, which was lying in the sand at exactly the angle a sword lies when somebody means to pick it up, and came up out of the sand with the grit going off it in a fan, and touched her on the ribs. Very lightly.

"*Touch*," said the marshal.

It went quiet.

Sorrel knelt in the sand with her hook still round his knee and his sword on her ribs, and looked at him. Then she looked up. At the east stand. The fourth tier.

"*You taught him that*," she shouted, up at Alea, across the whole of the bowl, outraged and delighted. "That's *yours*. That's the heels thing!"

Alea put both hands over her face.

"I didn't," she said into them. "I didn't teach him anything. He *watched*."

Down in the chalk, Tamlin Brand got up. He took Sorrel's hook off his knee and gave it back to her, handle first, the way you'd give a man back his knife. He helped her up. And then he stood in the middle of the main sand with forty thousand people on their feet round him and the empty holder's bench at the edge of the chalk, the oak and the awning and the cushion with the fish on it that he'd sit on for four days next year, twenty or not, because the bench was the one thing in the Fledgling the book drew no line under; and he didn't look at any of it.

He looked at the east stand. The fourth tier. He'd known where she was the whole time. Of course he had.

Alea took her hands off her face.

She bent down to the pack at her feet and took something out of the top of it: a paper rod, soft at the ends, with a little paper fish on its thread.

Chris knew what it was. Everybody who'd been in this bowl two summers ago knew. He'd seen forty thousand of them go up.

She stood up on the fourth tier of the east stand and held it up over her head. Just the one. In both hands.

And Lucan, in the master's box, stood up, and lifted his arm.

They'd been under the benches all week. Chris found out after. Eleven carts of them, from the store under the north stand, that Lucan had had brought round in the night and handed out at every gate that morning without telling anybody what they were for, forty thousand new paper fishing rods with forty thousand new paper fish. *For the holder who gave the bench back*, he'd said, at the gates. *You'll know when*.

They went up.

All of them. All round. Every stand, every tier, the whole bowl at once, forty thousand paper rods with forty thousand paper fish on the ends of their lines, up over every head in the Lists in the gold light of the last afternoon of midsummer, the way they'd gone up two summers ago for a fox girl with a black sword who'd gone with a hook instead of against it.

And one old one, on the fourth tier of the east stand, in the middle of them, that had been to a final before.

Down on the main sand, Tamlin Brand put his sword down, point first, in the sand at his feet, and saluted the east stand. Fist to the chest.

Alea lowered the rod. She sat down. She was crying; she wasn't doing anything about it.

"He's going to have to sit on that bench next year," she said. "For four days. It's horrible."

"Did you tell him?"

"Last year." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "Nobody listens."

Chris sat on the fourth tier of the east stand with his log shut on his knee and a fox girl beside him with an old paper rod across her lap, and the bowl emptying round them, slow, the way it did, forty thousand people going home with paper fish.

He didn't write anything for a long time.

Then he opened the log, and turned to the back, where nobody looked. To the page that said *Line 36*, in his own hand, that he'd written last summer and never shown anyone. *Line 36: here.*

He wrote under it, small:

*Not here this year. At the lake. On the jetty, with somebody on the shingle. Still here.*

Down on the main sand the marshals were raking it smooth for the morning, the way they always did, though there wouldn't be a morning bout now till next midsummer. The holder's bench stood at the edge of the chalk in the last of the sun with its awning and the cushion with the fish, empty, waiting for a boy who'd sit on it next year for four horrible days.

Up on the great west wall, Ina's forty lamps came on, one after another, from the north end; the bye's first, as always, violet, and then yellow.
