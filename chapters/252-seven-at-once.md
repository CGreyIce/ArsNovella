On Earth, she had always been the one in the middle.

Hana had never decided to be. It had just happened, the way water finds the low place in a field. She'd met Jared, and Jared had met Cursiffe, and she'd met Milli and married him, and Aleaka had wandered into her chat one night and never wandered out, and somewhere in there a funny boy half a world away who watched her streams from when she was nineteen and did stupid things in her chat to make her laugh; and all of them had known each other through her. She was the one who said *you two should talk*. She was the one who noticed when somebody hadn't been on for a while. She was the one who sent the message, and arranged the thing, and made sure.

She'd sent a great many messages. Every one, herself. She'd read every reply before she passed it on. Because if you were the one in the middle and something got dropped, it was yours; and she was methodical and responsible and self-disciplined, all the things her friends said about her when they were being kind; and she'd never once in her life dropped anything.

Except one.

She'd never met him. That was the thing. Eight years of him in her chat and she'd never once met him. She'd arranged everything for everybody, and she'd always meant to arrange that, *one day*, and left it to sort itself out, because there'd be time. And then there wasn't.

She'd been carrying everybody's messages herself ever since.

***

Seven halves on a purple string round her left wrist. And a gold half-heart.

She'd carried them for more than a year. Every night, in the room in the east wing of the castle at Solmere with Milli asleep beside her and his tail across her ankle, she lay in the dark and held the string, and one by one the cords went warm, and one by one she read them and answered them and sent them on. Maren's blue, from Wrenhaven. Corvin's green with the grey bead, from Thornwall. Aldous's plain brown knot, from Cinderhold. Emrys at Kingsferry, Garrick at Duskhollow, Lucan at Valcrest. Oswin's, which was silly, because Oswin was down the corridor and could have just knocked, but which he used anyway, because he liked to.

Seven guides. Seven halves. One of her.

That was what a cord was. She'd made them that way. A pair: one half with a guide, one half with her. What went in at one end came out at the other, in the holder's own hand, carrying what you'd meant. A line from a guide came to her, and she read it, and if it was for somebody else she sent it on. Maren to Corvin was Maren to Hana to Corvin. It always had been. There was no other way for it to go.

And then on the night of Thea's reading, she'd fallen asleep with all seven open.

***

"You snored," said Oswin, at breakfast.

He said it with enormous pleasure. Oswin said most things with enormous pleasure; it was one of the reasons she'd liked him from the first day, when he'd asked if he could measure Milli's tail and Milli had said *no* and Oswin had written down *no* in his notebook as if it were data.

"I didn't."

"Into my cord," said Oswin. "Very gently. Like a kettle. For about an hour after midnight." He buttered his bread. "I heard it in my head. In my own hand. *Snore. Snore.* It was very distinct."

Milli, across the table, put his face in his hands.

"I'm sorry," said Hana. "I must have fallen asleep with them all open. After Thea. I was so tired I didn't close them." She rubbed her wrist. "I've never done that. I always close them."

"I know you do," said Oswin. "Every night. One at a time. I hear you do it." He put the butter knife down. "That's not the interesting part."

She waited.

"The interesting part," said Oswin, "is that about a quarter of an hour after you started snoring, I heard Corvin."

The table went quiet. Jared stopped with his spoon halfway. Aleaka, at the end, with Brisk on the back of her chair glaring at everybody, looked up.

"Corvin always comes through me," said Hana slowly.

"I know. That's what I'm telling you. He didn't." Oswin leaned forward. "I heard him in my head. Corvin. Not you carrying Corvin. Not *Hana says Corvin says*. Corvin. I know the difference; I've had more than a year of the difference. When you carry a line it comes with you in it. You're very—" he hunted for the word— "*present*. In a nice way. Like somebody handing you a cup. This came without you in it. It came like Corvin." He paused. "He said: *Is anyone up?*"

Nobody said anything.

"And I said *yes*," said Oswin. "Into my cord. Without thinking. And Maren said *yes* as well. And then Lucan. And for about an hour, while you snored, the four of us talked. To each other." He picked up his bread again. "Through you. I suppose. I don't know how else. But not *by* you." He took a bite. "Corvin says the king is eating. Maren says Thea's nose stopped bleeding at eleven. Lucan says the Ina boy hasn't come down off the wall since Lamp Night and has a cold. I thought you'd want to know."

Hana looked at the purple string on her wrist.

Seven halves. Pairs. Each one to her. There was no line between Oswin's and Corvin's; there couldn't be; she hadn't made one. She'd have known.

"That's not how they work," she said.

"No," said Oswin happily. "I know. Isn't it wonderful?"

***

She tried it that night.

She lay down beside Milli in the dark with the string on her wrist and opened all seven. One at a time, the way she always did. Maren. Corvin. Aldous. Emrys. Garrick. Lucan. Oswin. Seven small warmths round her wrist, like seven fingers resting on it.

And then she tried not to carry.

She'd thought it would be easy. You just didn't. A line came in, and you didn't pick it up; you let it go wherever it was going. That was all.

It wasn't easy. It wasn't anything. She couldn't do it.

Maren's cord went warm. *Is she there? Is anyone there?*

And Hana had already read it. Before she'd decided anything. She'd read it the way you read a word on a sign; you can't not. And having read it she knew who it was for, *anyone*, which meant everyone, which meant she should send it on; and she felt herself start to, the way your hand goes out to catch a cup somebody's knocked off a table, and stopped herself; and the line sat there, in her, warm, read, not going anywhere. Waiting for her to carry it.

She lay there holding it. She didn't want to carry it. She didn't know how not to.

*It's me*, she sent back to Maren, at last, because she couldn't bear to leave her waiting. *Just me. Sorry.*

And the cord went cool.

She tried for three hours. Every line that came, she read before she could stop herself, and once she'd read it she was holding it, and once she was holding it she had to put it down somewhere, and the only place to put it was where it was going, by her. Seven cords, all open, all night, and every word that came through them came through her hands.

At three in the morning she closed them. One at a time, the way she always did.

Milli was awake. She hadn't known. He was lying on his side with his eyes open in the dark, watching her, with his tail across her ankle.

"Honey," said Milli.

"I can't do it."

"I know. I watched." He moved his tail a little, the way he did. "You checked every one."

"I always check every one."

"I know," said Milli.

***

She tried four more nights. It was the same every time. She opened the seven and lay in the dark and read every line before she could not read it; and carried it; and couldn't stop.

Her bar sat at ninety-eight.

It had sat at ninety-eight since One Pour. It had jumped there in a single night, carrying all seven at once, two hundred and nine times *home* and forty-seven times the other thing, and stopped, and not moved since. She looked at it every morning. *Progress to Lantern: 98%*. She'd started to hate the shape of the nine.

On the fifth morning she went to find Oswin.

***

He was in the long room. He was always in the long room. The table was still pushed against the wall where it had been since the summer, and the ten-foot linen copy of the circle was still on the floor with its weights round the edge, the sleeping-dog doorstop at the north; and Oswin was at the table, under the window, with his brass half-wheel and his dividers and the old instrument case open beside him, copying.

Not the register any more. He'd finished the register in the spring; fourteen leaves, both sides, every line twice. These were the leaves after.

"The method," said Oswin, without looking up. "Fifteen onward. The corner mark changes. They stop recording and start explaining." He touched the leaf in front of him with the end of his pen, very lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "How the circle was drawn. How it was meant to be run. The summoners wrote it for whoever came after. Nobody's read it in three hundred years. The Hall kept it shut."

Edwy was at the glass. On his stool, at the end of the table, in his grey Hall robe with his shaved head and ink on both hands, ruling margins for the copy with a straightedge, left-handed, the way Cursiffe had taught him. He looked up when she came in and smiled, the shy enormous smile he'd had since the spring.

"Good morning, Hanarisu."

"Good morning, Edwy."

And on the floor, at the far side of the linen circle, by the blank half of the fourteenth ring, the Warden.

He was there most days now. Since the summer. From the second bell to dusk, Aubrecht of the Summoners' Hall, very old, very thin, in his black robe, sitting on the floorboards beside the empty arc with his knees drawn up like a boy's and a pink bead in his closed hand. He'd stood on the third ring at the Great Summoning, on a summer morning three years ago, and helped to call thirty-five people out of their rooms. He'd been afraid ever since that somebody would read the circle wrong and it would stop, and everybody it held with it.

He wasn't afraid of that any more. Hana could feel it; she couldn't help feeling things about people. He was afraid of something else now. She didn't know what.

"Good morning, Hanarisu," said the Warden.

"Good morning, Warden."

She sat down on the end of the table, by Oswin's elbow, with her tail round her feet.

"I can't do it," she said.

Oswin put his pen down.

"I lie there with the seven open and I try not to carry," said Hana. "And I can't. Every line, I read before I can stop myself. And then I've got it, and I have to put it somewhere." She looked at her wrist. "It only happened when I was asleep. When I couldn't read anything."

"Then go to sleep," said Edwy.

Everybody looked at him. He went red to the top of his shaved head and looked at his straightedge.

"Sorry," said Edwy. "I just. That's what you said happened. You were asleep. You weren't reading them. So they went where they were going." He ruled a line, very carefully. "You check them because you're awake. I check every line twice because I set fire to them once." He didn't look up. "I don't think either of us is checking because it's needed."

The room was very quiet.

"Edwy," said the Warden, from the floor. Gently.

"Sorry," said Edwy, again.

"No," said the Warden. "Don't apologise." He turned the pink bead over in his fingers. "He's right. I've watched him check every line twice since the fire. Eleven thousand lines and more. He's caught three errors. Three. Every other line he checked because he's afraid of what his hands did once." He looked at Hana. "It isn't checking. It's penance. It's very beautiful. It isn't checking."

Hana didn't say anything.

She thought about eight years of *one day*. One day we'll meet. One day I'll sort it out. She'd sorted out everybody else, and left the one thread she'd always meant to tie to tie itself; and it hadn't. She'd been the one in the middle, and she'd left it.

She'd carried every message herself ever since. Not because they needed carrying.

Penance.

***

"There's a line," said Oswin, after a long time. "In the method. I found it yesterday. I wasn't going to show anyone till I'd checked it." He glanced at Edwy. "Twice."

Edwy almost smiled.

Oswin turned the leaf toward her. She couldn't read it. Nobody summoned could read the old court hand, small and close and slanting, with its strange little hooks; she could barely read the kingdom's ordinary one. But Oswin could, and he put his finger under a line near the bottom of the leaf, beside a drawing she did recognise. Half a circle. The right half. The blank arc of the fourteenth ring, drawn small in the margin, two lines with nothing between.

"*Of the right hand*," read Oswin. Slowly. Translating as he went. "*The right hand is drawn and left. It is not ours to write. We have tried, and it closes. It cannot be written from the edge, as the call is written, by a hand that reaches. It must be held open from the middle, by a hand that holds and does not close; and the lines will find their own way through it, each by the way it came.*" He stopped. "And then: *None of us could*."

The Warden had gone very still on the floor.

"*None of us could*," said Oswin again, quietly. "Twelve summoners. The best in three hundred years. They drew the way home and couldn't open it. Because they kept closing their hands."

Hana looked at the line she couldn't read. At the little drawing of half a circle in the margin.

*A hand that holds and does not close.*

She looked at her own wrist. The purple string. Seven halves.

"The lines will find their own way through it," she said.

"Each by the way it came," said Oswin. "Yes."

On the floor, the Warden opened his hand and looked at the pink bead in his palm, the one she'd given him in the spring rain, that carried *I'm not frightened of this*. He'd held it shut for five months. He held it open now. Flat, on his palm, in the light from the window.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She felt it come off him, the thing he'd been afraid of since the summer, since Edwy had sat on the floor and said *they're people going home*.

Not that the circle would stop.

That nobody would ever be able to open the other half.

***

She didn't try again that night. She lay beside Milli with the seven closed and her hand on the string and thought.

*A hand that holds and does not close.*

She knew how to make a charm. Her Keepsake, her Token. You took a thing, a thread, a bead, and you put a feeling into it, and the feeling stayed there and went with it; and you gave it away. That was the part she'd always been good at. The giving away. You made a charm and you handed it over and you didn't hold on to it. A charm you held on to wasn't a charm. It was just a thing in your hand.

She'd never once thought of the cords like that. The cords she held on to. All seven, every night. She'd made them, and given seven halves away, and kept seven, and the seven she kept she'd been holding every night since, closing her hand on each line as it came, checking, carrying, *mine, mine, mine*. Penance.

She'd made the cords. She'd never let them be charms.

"Milli," she said, in the dark.

"Mm."

"When you learned to wait. At the Lists. When Kestra said *again* every morning for two years." She turned on her side to face him. "How did you stop going first?"

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she thought he'd gone back to sleep.

"I didn't stop wanting to," said Milli. "I still want to. Every time." His tail moved on her ankle. "I just stopped thinking it was my job to make the fight happen. It happens anyway. Somebody else moves. You go when it's time." He yawned. "You don't have to hold everybody's hand at once, Honey. They can hold each other's. You just have to be in the room."

She lay there.

"That's very wise," she said.

"I'm very wise," said Milli. "Nobody believes me." And went to sleep.

***

On the eleventh night she did it on purpose.

She'd told them. Not the channel; the guides. One at a time, the way she always did, before she began: down each cord, *tonight, after the bell. I'm going to open all seven and go to sleep. If you want to talk to each other, talk. I won't be carrying.* And then, because she was Hana and couldn't help it: *Please don't say anything you wouldn't want me to hear. I'll probably hear it anyway.*

Lucan had sent back *I SHALL BE DISCRETION ITSELF*, which meant he wouldn't.

They were all in the room. She hadn't asked them; they came anyway, the way the summoned always came to the bad nights and the strange ones. Jared on the floor by the door with his back to it, because Jared always sat with his back to the door. Aleaka in the window seat with Brisk asleep on her shoulder and her vixen asleep on her feet. Cursiffe at the little table with a pen and paper, because if anything happened he wanted to write it down, in case it needed keeping. Oswin in the doorway, in his nightshirt, with his notebook. Edwy behind him, on the stairs, not quite in. And the Warden, which she hadn't expected, on a chair in the corner, with the pink bead open on his palm.

And Milli, in the bed beside her, with his tail across her ankle.

She lay down on her back. She put her left hand on her chest, over her heart, with the purple string round the wrist and the seven halves resting on her collarbone and the gold half-heart under them.

She opened them. One at a time. The way she always did. Maren. Corvin. Aldous. Emrys. Garrick. Lucan. Oswin, in the doorway, smiling.

Seven warmths.

And then she did the thing she'd never done to anybody she cared about. She'd done it to clerks, and guards, and a merchant in the Lantern Quarter who'd wanted too much for a bolt of pink silk; she'd done it to a lord once at a dinner, on purpose, because he'd been rude to Jared. She'd never once done it to a friend. She did it to herself.

She charmed herself to sleep.

Not much. Not hard. Just the little warm push she gave a crying child in the market, or a frightened horse; the one that said *it's all right, you can put it down now*. She turned it round and put it on herself, very gently, the way you'd put a blanket on somebody already asleep in a chair. *It's all right. You can put it down. They'll find their own way.*

She felt herself go.

***

She didn't sleep. Not quite. She'd thought she would. She went down into something warm and slow and very deep, like sinking into a bath, and stayed just under the top of it, the way you stay just under the water in a bath with your ears below the surface and hear the house going on round you.

And she heard the house.

That was what it was. She'd felt it once before, at One Pour, holding the seven for Thea, *like voices in other rooms of a house*. But then she'd been carrying them, all seven, the whole house on her back.

Now she wasn't carrying anything. She was just the house.

A big house. Seven rooms, round a hall. She was the hall. She could feel each room, warm, lit, somebody in it; Maren's, small and neat and very quiet, with a lamp burning steady; Corvin's, high and cold and full of maps, at the top of a lot of stairs; Aldous's, white, with a stone in it; Emrys's, full of singing he wasn't doing; Garrick's, loud, with boots by the door; Lucan's, gold and theatrical with a smell of wine; Oswin's, which was the long room, with the circle on the floor. Seven rooms. All their doors open onto the hall.

And she didn't walk from room to room with a tray. She lay in the hall, in the warm, and let the doors be open.

Corvin spoke first. Of course he did. He always did; he was the one who was frightened enough to.

*Is anyone up?*

And it went past her. Through her; she felt it go. But not into her hands. It went out of Corvin's room into the hall and across it, the way a voice goes across a hall when somebody calls up a stairwell, and into every other room at once. She didn't pick it up. She didn't read it first. She heard it, the way you hear somebody call in your own house, and didn't get up.

*Here*, said Maren.

*Here*, said Garrick. *Course I'm here. I'm always here. Nobody tells me anything.*

*I AM HERE*, said Lucan, *AND I AM BEING DISCREET*.

And then they were all there. Seven of them. The Advisor's seven, that he'd trained together in Solmere before the summoning, before any of them had met any of the summoned; who'd been friends, Oswin said, some of them, and rivals, some of them, and all of them scattered across the kingdom three years ago to seven postings with five strangers each; and who'd never once since then been in the same room.

They were in the same room now.

She lay in the hall, under the warm water, and listened to them.

***

They didn't talk about much. That was the thing she'd remember. She'd thought, if it ever happened, they'd talk about the war, about the west, about the circle; and they did, a little. Corvin said the fourteenth place was seventeen now, and the king had stopped sleeping, and Kestra had started sitting outside his door. Garrick said Petra had gone west with the boys, and left the stick with Dunstan, and the whole town was on the bar every morning now, children and all, in coats. Oswin told them about the line in the method, *none of us could*, and there was a long silence in all seven rooms at once.

But mostly they didn't.

Mostly they talked about their students.

*Thea's up*, said Maren. *She ate the whole bowl. She says don't follow the other end.*

*Ina's still got a cold*, said Lucan. *He won't wear a scarf. He says it ruins the line of the coat. I have explained to him that the line of the coat is not more important than his lungs. He disagrees.*

*Dennis rings now*, said Emrys, in his hoarse old singer's voice. *Did you hear? A whole bell. He sang me the note. It was a true A.*

*Yan sits at the grave every evening now*, said Aldous. *Not to watch it. Just to sit. I sit with him.*

*Calvin fell in the river again.*

*Malcolm wrote me a list before he left. Of everything that could go wrong on the road. It was four pages. Then he wrote a second list of what he'd do about each one. It was eight.*

*Is the king eating?*

*No.*

*Make him.*

*You make him.*

*I would if I were there.*

*Maren, you've never made anyone do anything in your life except read slowly.*

*That's not true. I made a girl from another world eat porridge every morning for a year.*

And then they were laughing. Seven of them. In seven rooms, across the whole kingdom, at midnight; Garrick's big boom and Lucan's theatrical peal and Maren's little dry one she hardly ever let out, and Corvin, at the top of his stairs, laughing like a man who'd forgotten how and been reminded. And it went across the hall, back and forth, room to room, and none of it went through her hands. It went through *her*; she was the hall; she could feel every word of it pass. But she didn't carry any of it. She didn't read any of it first. She didn't check.

She just let them be in the room.

And nothing got dropped.

That was the thing. She lay there under the warm water, waiting for it, the way you wait for a cup to fall. Waiting for something to go wrong because she wasn't holding it. A line lost. A word gone to the wrong room. Somebody calling up the stairwell and nobody answering.

Nothing.

Every word went where it was going. Each by the way it came. Maren's to Corvin. Garrick's to Lucan. Oswin's to all of them. They found their own way through her, the way water finds the low place in a field; and she didn't have to do anything at all except lie in the middle with her hand open, and not close it.

*A hand that holds and does not close.*

She felt something turn.

Not in the cords. In her. In the middle of her, where the warm was; something turning, slowly, the way a wheel turns on its hub. Not because anybody was pushing it. Because seven people were talking, and their words went round her, from room to room, and the going round was the turning. She was the middle. She didn't push the wheel. The wheel went round on her, and she held still, and that was the whole of it.

She'd been pushing it for two years. One spoke at a time. By hand.

*Oh*, she thought.

And somewhere, a long way under all of it, under seven voices laughing, so faint she almost didn't feel it, the gold half-heart on her collarbone went warm. For a count of three.

Then cool.

She didn't wake up. She didn't carry it anywhere. She let it be what it was, which was a man on the end of a jetty in the dark, a long way north, with his hand on his chest, listening to seven people laughing in a house they didn't know they were in.

She slept.

***

She woke at dawn.

The room was grey. Milli was asleep beside her with his tail across her ankle. Jared was asleep against the door. Aleaka was asleep in the window seat with the vixen on her feet and Brisk awake on her shoulder, glaring at the morning. Cursiffe was asleep at the little table with his cheek on a sheet of paper that said, in his small careful English, *nothing to keep. all fine.* Oswin was gone; Edwy was gone. The Warden was still in his chair in the corner, awake, with the pink bead open on his palm, looking at her.

She lifted her wrist.

Seven halves on a purple string. All seven warm.

Still open.

She'd slept the whole night. Hours. And they'd stayed open, all seven, the whole time, without her holding a single one. She could feel them now, the rooms, quieter, most of them asleep too; Lucan's dark and snoring; Garrick's with the boots gone from the door, out on the bar already; Maren's lamp still burning, the way Maren's lamp always burned; Corvin's cold, high, awake.

*Good morning*, said Corvin. Across the hall. To everybody.

*Good morning*, said Maren.

They'd been doing it without her. For hours. While she slept.

She opened her window.

She read it. And then she lay there on her back in the grey and laughed, quietly, so as not to wake Milli, with her hand over her mouth and her tail going; and woke Milli anyway, because his tail was across her ankle and he felt hers go.

He opened one eye. He looked at her face. Then at the air in front of it, where he couldn't see anything, because nobody could see anybody else's window; and he'd never once in three years needed to, with her.

"Lantern," said Milli.

"*Wheel*," said Hana.

He thought about it.

"Because it goes round," he said. "On you. And you don't push it." He shut his eye again. "Told you. Very wise."

"Very wise," said Hana; and put her hand on his tail, which was a thing you were not supposed to do to a cat demihuman, and which he had let her do and nobody else since the first week they met.

The Warden got up out of his chair in the corner. It took him a long time; he was very old, and he'd sat all night. He came across the room to the bed, slowly, with the pink bead open on his palm, and stood looking down at her.

"*A hand that holds and does not close*," said the Warden.

She sat up.

"I don't know if that's what it means," said Hana. "In the method. I don't know if this is the same thing. It's seven cords. It's not a circle. It's not thirty-five people. It's not the way home." She looked at her wrist. "I don't want you to think—"

"I don't think anything," said the Warden. "I'm too old to think things at dawn." He looked at the bead in his palm. "I stood on the third ring, Hanarisu. Three years ago. I helped to close a great many hands round thirty-five people. I have been very frightened, since, that nobody would ever be able to open them again." He closed his fingers over the bead. Then, slowly, deliberately, he opened them. "I'm a little less frightened this morning. That's all."

He went to the door. Jared woke up and moved out of his way, and he went out, and down the stairs, slowly, one step at a time.

On the landing below, Hana heard Edwy's voice. Waiting, on the stairs, where he'd been all night.

"Good morning, Warden."

And the Warden's, old and thin and very clear.

"Good morning, Edwy."

***

That night, the first night of her Lantern, she didn't open the seven at bedtime.

She didn't need to. They were already open. They'd been open all day; she'd felt them all day, like the rooms of a house you're living in, doors open onto the hall, people moving about in them; and she'd gone about her day in the middle of it, and eaten, and read with Cursiffe, and walked on the walls with Milli, and none of it had been in her hands.

She lay down beside Milli in the dark with the string on her wrist.

Corvin's room was lit. High, cold, at the top of a lot of stairs. He was sending something across the hall, to anyone awake, the way he did now: two lines he'd written down as they were said. *Hyvred: seventeen.* And under it: *The king: I know.*

She didn't carry it anywhere. She didn't need to. Everyone who needed to hear it had their doors open.

She lay in the middle of the house, with her hand open, and let it go round.
