Alea packed the good knife in front of him.

She did it on the second morning out of Valcrest, in the grey before the sun came up, at the tail of Group 3's cart on the edge of the downs. The fire was down to ash. Rhea was already arguing with the horse. Her father had left the knife on the tailboard the night before, after he'd used it to cut the bread, the way he always left it somewhere, and his pack was open beside it with the yoke for the water buckets lying across the top.

She picked the knife up. She wiped it on her sleeve. Plain horn handle, a blade worn thin with twelve years of sharpening, the knife he cleaned fish with and cut rope with and had once taken a splinter out of the king's thumb with. She opened the side pocket of his pack, the small one on the left, and put it in, and did up the buckle.

That was where her mother had always put it. Rowan had told her. And it was where Alea had put it every year since she was twelve, in the dark, on the last night of summer, while he was out on the end of the jetty, so that he'd find it on the road and think he'd remembered.

He was watching.

He was standing at the end of the cart with a bucket in each hand, back from the stream, and he'd stopped, and he'd watched her do the whole thing. He didn't say anything.

She looked at him.

"I wasn't going to say anything," he said.

"You were going to say something."

"I was going to say thank you."

"That's something," said Alea.

He thought about it. Then he put the buckets down and came and looked at the pocket, the small one on the left, with the buckle done up, as if he'd never seen it before in his life. Maybe he never had. Not like this. Not with somebody standing there.

"Five years," he said.

"Five years."

"I thought I was getting better at remembering it."

"You weren't," said Alea, and went to help Wendy with the blankets.

***

She'd always been the one at the top of the road.

That was what she kept thinking, that second morning, walking behind the cart up the first long slope of the downs with Astra on her back and the sky going from grey to white to blue. Every year of her life since she was nine. The last week of summer. The dew still on the pumpkins. Her father coming out of the cabin with the rod over his shoulder and his pack on his back, giving Rowan the hens, giving Odette the key. Walking up the lake road in the grey without looking back. And her at the top of it, by Rowan's gate, with Rowan's hand on her shoulder, watching him go, until the road went round the hill and he wasn't there.

A month there. A few days at the grave. A month back. He'd come down the lake road again in the first frost, and the hens would hate him, and she'd be at the cabin door with her arms folded, and they wouldn't talk about it.

Eight times.

She'd watched him go eight times, and she'd never once seen what was on the other side of the hill.

She was on the other side of the hill now. She'd been on it for two days. And it looked, so far, exactly like everywhere else: a long white chalk track going up and over a long green hill, with sheep on it, and a wind, and larks going up out of the grass on either side of the cart as it went by, so high and so loud you couldn't see them, only hear them, like somebody ringing a very small bell a very long way up.

"Is it like this?" she said. "The whole way?"

Her father was walking beside her with the yoke across his shoulders and a bucket swinging at each end. He looked at the downs.

"No," he said. "I've never come this way. I've always come from the north."

She hadn't thought of that.

"The north road comes down to the river at Ashby," he said. "This one meets it there. After that it's the same road." He shifted the yoke. "After Ashby I'll know where we are."

"And before Ashby?"

He looked at the sheep. "Before Ashby," he said, "I'm just carrying the water."

He sounded very pleased about it.

***

He carried the water every day.

Aldous decided. That was how Group 3 worked, Alea found out, the first morning: every morning, at the tail of the cart, before anyone had eaten, Aldous stood with his book under his arm and his old chaplain's coat buttoned to the neck and told everybody what they were carrying that day. Not what they were doing. What they were *carrying*. As if a day were a thing you carried from one end to the other, and you'd better know before you picked it up.

"Rhea," said Aldous. "The cart."

"I *always* carry the cart."

"You always push the cart. Today, carry it. The horse would like to be consulted."

Rhea looked at the horse. The horse looked at Rhea.

"Salty. Your ribs. Walk, don't ride. Wendy, supper. Maigo, the horse. Niko, your breath. Yan, the front."

And then he got to her father. Every morning. And her father would stand there at the end of the line with the empty buckets and the yoke, very seriously, waiting to be told, the way he must have stood on a parade ground somewhere when he was fifteen. And Aldous would look at him for a long moment with his grave old face.

"Water," Aldous would say.

On the third morning, her father asked if there was anything else.

Aldous considered it. He looked at the cart. He looked at the downs. He looked at the man in front of him, who had killed a general and dyed a plain red and come back across it alone in the morning with a sword on his shoulder, and who was standing in front of him now with two buckets and an expression of great hope.

"Water," said Aldous.

"Right," said her father, delighted, and went to find the stream.

***

She'd heard about Group 3 all her life.

Not as Group 3. As the squad. The four he'd played with every night, on Earth, in the hours after work, in the dark, with voices in his ears. She'd had them as bedtime stories from the age of seven, the way other girls had giants and princesses. Yan, who was calm, and called the plays in the other game, the one where Yan carried him. Maigo, who never stopped talking. Mashi, who was quiet and chill and watched everything, and had lost to her father four hundred times in the game at night when they were on opposite sides, which her father said was an exaggeration, and was probably more. Salty, who her father owed something to that she'd never understood. And Wendy, who wasn't one of the four but was always in the call, who said what she thought, and was usually right, and had once made her father laugh so hard he'd fallen off his chair.

She'd thought she knew them.

She'd been wrong about everything but the talking.

Maigo talked to the horse. Not like you'd talk to a horse, the way Rowan did, *good boy, steady, whoa there*. He talked to it like a friend he'd been travelling with for two years who'd had a hard week. All day. Sitting up on the baggage with his antlers full of leaves and his pixel glasses pushed up on his hood, leaning forward to tell the horse about the downs, and the sheep, and the larks, and Valcrest, and the Lists, and his tree. The horse put one ear back toward him and kept it there, all day, the whole length of the downs.

"What's his name?" said Alea, on the second afternoon.

Maigo looked at her, surprised. Then at the horse.

"He hasn't told me yet," he said.

"How long have you had him?"

"Two years," said Maigo. He leaned forward. "It's okay," he told the horse. "Take your time."

The horse flicked the ear.

***

Mashi sat on the tailboard and breathed.

That was what Aldous meant by *your breath*. Alea worked it out on the second day. He sat on the end of the cart with his legs hanging over the edge, facing backwards, toward her and her father and the road they'd come, with his hands loose on his knees and his eyes half closed and a handkerchief folded beside him on the boards. Very still. Breathing in. Breathing out. Without stopping at the top or the bottom, so the in-breath turned into the out-breath with no seam between them, the way a rope runs through a pulley.

Every so often something happened.

She couldn't see it. She was Tempered; she could feel aura, a little, the way you can feel heat off a stove with your back turned. She could feel his, coming up in him from underneath, the way hers did. And she could feel something else, at the same time, coming *in*, from outside, from the air, the way her father said mana came. The two of them going toward each other in the middle of him like two hands.

And touching.

And Wendy, walking beside the cart, would count. Out loud. Flatly, without looking up from the arrow she was fletching. "One. Two. Three." Mashi's heartbeats. Alea had worked that out too. "Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven—"

And then his nose would bleed, and he'd pick up the handkerchief without opening his eyes and hold it under his nose, and the two hands in the middle of him would come apart, and Wendy would say "Eleven," and go back to the arrow.

"*Again*," Mashi would say, through the handkerchief.

He did it all day. Over and over. Eleven. Eleven. Ten, once, and he swore at it, very quietly, in English. Eleven. Always eleven. As if there were a wall at eleven, in the middle of him, and he kept walking into it with his face, and getting up, and walking into it again.

"Eleven," said Wendy.

"*Again*."

Her father walked behind the cart with the buckets and watched all of it. Every time. And didn't say anything, and didn't help.

On the fourth day Mashi opened his eyes, mid-breath, and looked straight at him over the handkerchief.

"Don't help," said Mashi.

"I'm not."

"You're thinking about it."

Her father was quiet for a moment.

"I'm thinking about it," he admitted.

"Well, don't." Mashi closed his eyes again. "I lost to you four hundred times. I'm going to do this one on my own."

Her father didn't say anything. But Alea saw his mouth, at the corner. And she saw that he stopped watching Mashi's hands after that, and watched the road instead, and only looked up when Wendy said a number.

***

The callouts were the thing she hadn't expected.

She'd heard about them. *Callouts*. Her father had tried to explain them once, when she was eight, and failed. In the game, he'd said, you couldn't see everything, so everyone told everyone else what they could see, very fast, in as few words as possible, and if everyone did it right it was like having one pair of eyes with five heads. She hadn't understood. She'd asked him why they didn't just look.

She understood now.

"Rabbit," said Wendy, on the third afternoon, without raising her voice. "Left. Long."

"Mine?" said Salty.

"Mine."

The bow came off her shoulder and up and the string went back and the arrow went, all in one movement, from the moving cart, across sixty paces of downland, and a rabbit in the grass by a thorn bush went over and didn't get up.

"Nice," said Salty.

"Ugh," said Wendy, with satisfaction.

It went like that all day. Not just rabbits. Everything. A cart coming the other way: *wagon, front, two horses, slow*. A dog at a farm gate: *dog, right, loud, chained*. The posting house on the third evening, coming up over the brow of a hill: *roof, front, chimney going, beds*. Yan at the front with the scythe calling most of it, in his deep slow voice, and the others answering him in single words, *yep*, *mine*, *seen*, *nice*, the way you'd answer somebody you'd been answering for years in the dark.

And her father.

She saw it on the fourth day. She'd been watching for it without knowing she was watching for it. Wendy said *rabbit, right*, and her father's head turned. A fraction before hers. A fraction before Salty's. As if he'd seen it first and was waiting for somebody to say so.

On the fifth day, near noon, on the top of a long hill with the wind going over it, Wendy said, "Two. Right. Behind the—"

"One's a hare," said her father. Under his breath. To nobody.

Wendy's bow stopped.

She looked. Alea looked. Everybody on the right side of the cart looked. Behind the barrow on the right, a long way off, two brown shapes in the grass. One of them sat up.

It was a hare.

Wendy turned round on the cart and stared at him.

"Did you just *call* that?"

"No."

"You called that."

"It was a hare," said her father, reasonably.

"*HE CALLED IT*," said Maigo, to the horse, at the top of his voice.

"He always called it," said Salty.

"He was always early," said Mashi, from the tailboard, without opening his eyes. "That's how you knew it was him. He'd call it before it happened and then be smug for a whole round."

"I wasn't smug."

"You were *so* smug," said Wendy. She was still staring at him. Something had happened to her face, Alea saw, that she was trying to stop happening. "You were insufferable. Every round. *One's a hare*." She turned back round, fast, and put an arrow on the string, and didn't shoot the hare. She shot the rabbit. Clean. From the moving cart. "Well, it's *dead* now," said Wendy, to the downs, a bit thickly. "So."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Yan, at the front, with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu wound round the shaft, said, without turning his head, in his deep calm voice, the way he must have said it a thousand times at three in the morning: "Nice call, Ice."

Her father didn't answer. He walked on behind the cart with the buckets swinging. But the water in them, Alea noticed, wasn't quite steady for a while.

***

Salty walked.

His ribs didn't like the cart. They'd knitted crooked in the autumn, from carrying her mother's headstone across the red, and they ached when the cart went over stones, which on the downs was always. So he walked beside it, short and square and very broad, with the king's father's hammer in his belt and his hands in his pockets, and didn't say anything for hours at a time. Wendy walked beside him when she wasn't on the cart. They didn't talk much either. They didn't need to. Alea had never seen two people who needed to talk less.

He fell into step beside her father on the fifth afternoon. Not beside her; beside him, on the other side, so that her father was in the middle with the buckets.

"Fort," said Salty.

"Fort," her father agreed.

"Eleven."

"I know."

"You bought pies." Salty sounded aggrieved. "In Valcrest. Eleven pies. The king bought eleven more. I ate twenty-two pies."

"You ate nineteen. Wendy ate three."

"I ate *nineteen pies*," said Salty. "That's eleven dinners. Paid. Done." He looked at the downs. "You don't owe me anything."

Her father walked for a while with the buckets.

"Pies aren't dinners," he said.

Salty looked at him sideways.

"A dinner's something somebody makes," said her father. "For you. In a kitchen. I said *when you come out, dinner's on me*. I didn't say *I'll buy you a pie*." He shifted the yoke. "The pies were because you'd come twenty days and I didn't know what else to do with my hands. They don't count."

"They count."

"They don't count," said her father. "At the fort. Eleven nights. I'll cook."

Salty walked on for a long time without saying anything. Alea, on the other side, didn't look at him. She'd learned by now that with Salty you didn't look.

"You can't cook," said Salty, eventually.

"I've been cooking for thirty-four years."

"You couldn't cook on *Earth*. You put a pizza in the oven with the cardboard on. You told us. On stream. The whole chat saw the smoke."

"I've had a long time to practise."

"He's very good," said Alea. She couldn't help it. "He does a fish thing with onions."

Salty looked at her, across her father. Then at her father. His blunt square face was doing the thing it did, Alea was beginning to recognise, when he'd decided something and wasn't going to discuss it.

"Eleven nights," said Salty.

"Eleven nights."

"At the fort. Wendy too."

"Wendy too."

"I'm going to eat every one," said Salty, and walked on, and Alea saw Wendy, on the cart, put her face down on her knees very suddenly, for no reason at all, and stay like that for a while.

***

They came down off the last of the downs on the eighth day and saw the river.

It lay across the bottom of a long valley like a piece of grey ribbon somebody had dropped, wide and slow, with willows along it, and fields on both sides, and a town on the near bank where the road went down to it. Stone houses, with slate roofs. A market square. A long stone bridge with five arches going over the water to the far side. And on a hill behind the town, a long low grey building with a yard in front of it, and in the yard, very small from up here, rows of little grey figures moving all at once, like a field of barley in the wind.

"Ashby," said Aldous, from the box.

Alea knew the name. She'd fought it.

"The military school," she said. "Garth. Round two."

"The cadet," said her father. "With the textbook feet."

"He saluted me."

"He did," said her father. "He was right to."

And then, as they came down the hill toward the town, he went quiet.

She felt it before she saw it. The way you feel the weather change. He was walking beside her the same as he had for eight days, with the buckets and the yoke, at the same pace. But something in him had gone still. And when she looked at him, he was looking past the town. Past the river and the bridge and the fields. At a road.

It came down out of the north on the far side of the valley. A long pale line over the hills, very straight, the way the old roads were. Down through the fields to the far end of the bridge. Where it met theirs.

The north road. His road.

Eight times he'd come down that hill on the other side of the valley. On his own. With the rod over his shoulder and the good knife in his pack, where somebody had put it. At the end of summer. To cross that bridge and go east.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

From here on, it was the same road.

***

There were paper fishing rods in Ashby.

Alea saw the first one in a window on the street that came down the hill into the market square. A thin stick with a string tied to the end and a bit of folded paper for a float, tacked to the inside of a baker's window, among the loaves. Then another, in the window of a chandler's. Then three, in a row, across the front of an inn, with the paint on them still bright.

"*Lucan*," said Maigo, with deep satisfaction.

It wasn't Lucan. Not just Lucan. The riders had come through, Aldous said. The king's riders, and the guild riders, and the posting riders, who went faster than a cart, with the news of the Lists. *A girl from the lake. A black sword. Forty thousand fishing rods held up in the Lists at sunset*. And Ashby had heard. And Ashby, which had sent a cadet to the Fledgling Lists and watched him lose in round two to a fox girl with a sword like a door, had made its own.

Alea walked through the market square under them with her ears going hot.

Nobody looked at her twice. That was the strange thing. They looked at the cart, and the tree boy on the baggage, and the white snake on the scythe, and the short broad man with the hammer, because Group 3 was the summoned and you looked at the summoned. They looked at her sword, because you couldn't not look at a sword like that. But they didn't put it together. A fox girl with a black sword, walking at the back of a cart through their market under their own paper fishing rods, and nobody put it together.

And the man beside her with the buckets. They didn't look at him at all.

"It's very strange," she said, under her breath.

"It's very restful," said her father.

***

Garth put it together.

He came down the hill from the school at a run, in his grey cadet's coat, with his cropped hair and his textbook feet, and stopped in the road in front of the cart, breathing hard. Somebody must have seen the sword from the school yard. He looked at the cart, and the summoned on it, and then at her. At Astra, and her ears, and her face.

"It's you," said Garth of Ashby.

"Hello," said Alea.

He drew himself up. Then he saluted her, properly, the way he had on the sand in round two: fist to the chest, back very straight. A cadet's salute. In the middle of his own market square, with half of Ashby watching.

"The lake girl," he said. "I *told* them. At the school. I told them nobody gets near her, she waits and then she goes first and you're on your back before you've finished your second step. They didn't believe me." He looked at her with something that might have been resentment, or might have been the opposite. "Then the riders came. *She won*. And now they believe me."

"I nearly didn't," said Alea. "The final. I nearly lost."

"Three times," said Garth. "The riders said. The coast girl with the hooks." He shook his head. "Everybody in the school wants to know how you did it."

"I gave her the sword."

Garth stared at her.

"It's a long story," said Alea.

He seemed to accept that. Then he looked round, quickly, at the cart and the road behind it, and lowered his voice.

"Is he with you?" said Garth. "Your father? Everybody at the school wants to know what he looks like. The riders said *Alea, of Wrenhaven, daughter of Diwa, and of Ars*." He swallowed. "Ars. *The* Ars. The old drill-master went white when he heard. He was at the Redmarch." He looked at her. "Is he here?"

Alea thought about it.

"He's carrying the water," she said.

Garth looked past her. At the back of the cart, where a man was standing in the road with a yoke across his shoulders and a bucket at each end. Garth's eyes went to the man's face, and slid off. He frowned. He tried again. They slid off again, and landed on the buckets.

"The water," said Garth.

"Yes."

Garth looked at the buckets for a long moment.

Then, very slowly, as if he weren't sure what he was doing or why, he saluted them. Fist to the chest. Back straight.

"Right," said Garth of Ashby. "Thank you." And turned round and walked back up the hill to the school with his back very straight, and didn't look round once.

Behind her, her father made a small sound.

She didn't turn round. If she turned round, she'd laugh, and so would he, and Rhea would ask why, and they'd have to explain. So she walked on through Ashby under the paper fishing rods with her face very straight and her ears shaking, and behind her, very quietly, the water in two buckets slopped over the edge.

***

The posting house was on the far side of the bridge.

It was the last building in Ashby, where the east road went out of the town and the north road came in. Long and low, with a yard and a well and a stable, and a sign over the door with a horn painted on it, faded. Her father stopped in the yard, and put the buckets down by the well, and stood there for a moment. Then he went in.

Alea went in after him.

The woman in the kitchen was big and grey-haired, with flour to the elbows. She looked up from a table full of dough as he came in. And stopped.

"End of summer," she said. "Same as ever."

"Same as ever," said her father.

The woman looked at him for a long moment. At the rod. At the pack. Then past him, at the window, where somebody had tacked a paper fishing rod to the frame, with its float hanging down. Then back at the real rod over his shoulder.

Her mouth went thin. She didn't say anything about it.

"Back room," she said. "Same as always. I'll put a pan on." And then her eyes moved, and found Alea, standing in the doorway behind him with a black sword on her back.

The woman's hands stopped in the dough.

"Brought someone this year," she said.

"My daughter," said her father.

The woman looked at Alea for a long time. At her ears. At her sword. At her face, which was her mother's face, which this woman had never seen. Then back at him.

"About time," she said, and went to get another blanket.

***

Her name was Annis. She'd had the posting house at Ashby for twenty-two years. She told Alea that night, in the kitchen, while Group 3 ate in the long room and Rhea arm-wrestled two carters and lost to neither of them.

"Eight years," said Annis. She was kneading. She didn't look up. "End of summer. Always the same week. I'd see him come down the north road on the other side of the river in the evening, with that rod. Never a horse. Never a cart. On his own. He'd come over the bridge and in that door and stand there, like he did tonight, and I'd say *back room*, and he'd nod, and that was all. Paid in silver. Never said a word he didn't need. Gone at first light, east." She turned the dough. "And a month later back the other way. Same. Only quieter."

Alea didn't say anything.

"I never asked," said Annis. "You don't, in this trade. People on the east road at the end of summer, going toward the red." She folded the dough over. "You get a lot of those. Widows. Mothers. Old soldiers. They come through every year, the same week, the week of the battle, and go out to the markers, and come back. I don't ask them either." She was quiet. "But he was the youngest. And he came furthest. And he never once had anyone with him." She looked up, at last. "Eight years. I used to think, *somebody ought to be with that man*."

"I wasn't allowed," said Alea.

"I can see that," said Annis. "You're allowed now."

"Yes."

Annis looked at her a while longer. Then at the window, where the paper rod hung against the dark glass.

"I won't say anything," she said. "If anyone asks. Who was in my back room." She went back to the dough. "I never have."

Alea understood, then, that she knew. That she'd known since the riders came through a week ago with the news. That she'd stood in her kitchen and heard *a fisherman*, and *the Black Blade*, and *a girl from the lake*, and thought of a young man with a rod coming down the north road every year at the end of summer on his own, for eight years. And tacked the paper rod in her window anyway. And said nothing.

"Thank you," said Alea.

"Don't thank me," said Annis. "Eat something. You're too thin for that sword."

***

She wrote to Sorrel at the kitchen table, after.

They'd promised. On the morning after the king's table, at the west gate of Valcrest, with the Gullhaven fishers' cart waiting and the guild men shouting for Sorrel to hurry up. *Write*, Sorrel had said, with her hooks on her shoulders. *Gullhaven. The quay. Ask for the boat-hook girl, anybody'll know. I'll write back. I write terribly*. And Alea had said *so do I*, and Sorrel had hugged her again, hard, round the neck, the way she had in the ring, and gone.

The posting house took letters. Annis said a rider went west along the coast road every fourth day, and it'd get there in a month, give or take, if the rider didn't fall in a river.

Alea borrowed a pen and a sheet of the posting house's paper, which was brown and soft and took the ink like blotting paper, and sat at the end of the kitchen table under the lamp, and thought for a long time. Then she wrote.

*Sorrel,*

*I'm writing this in a posting house in Ashby, which is where Garth is from. He saluted me in the street. Then he saluted a bucket. It's a long story.*

*We're going east. Me and my dad and Group 3: the five who came with the scythe, and their guide, who's a chaplain and tells everybody what to carry every morning, and their guard, who pushes the cart because she says the horse is slow. My dad carries the water. Nobody will let him carry anything else. I don't think he's ever been happier in his life.*

*There are paper fishing rods in every window here. I nearly died.*

*We're going to my mother's grave. I've never been. He's gone every year since I was nine and I stayed at home. This year he asked me.*

She stopped.

She sat with the pen over the paper and looked at what she'd written. *My mother's grave*. And she thought of Sorrel on the edge of the well behind the Lists, with her feet in a bucket, saying *mine was*. *Best on the Gullhaven quay*. And not saying anything else, because there wasn't anything else to say about a boat that went out one morning and didn't come back.

Sorrel's father didn't have a grave. There was nowhere to go. There was just the sea.

She nearly crossed it out. She had the pen on the line.

She didn't. She sat for a long time. Then she wrote under it.

*I know yours hasn't got one. I thought about crossing that out. I didn't, because you'd know I had.*

*I'll tell her about you. That you nearly had me three times. She'd have liked you. My dad says she liked anyone who fought with whatever they had.*

*Write back to Wrenhaven. Anybody there will know.*

*Next year.*

*Alea (the lake girl)*

She folded it, and wrote on the outside, *Sorrel. The quay, Gullhaven. The boat-hook girl*, and gave it to Annis with a copper, and Annis put it in a box on the wall with the others without looking at it, the way you'd put away something that wasn't yours to read.

***

After Ashby, he knew everything.

She hadn't understood what he meant, on the downs. *After Ashby I'll know where we are*. She'd thought he meant the road. He did mean the road. But it was more than that.

He knew the milestone a mile out of the town, with a chip out of the top of it like a bite out of an apple, and he touched it as he went by, without looking, the way you'd touch a doorframe in your own house. He knew the well at the crossroads in the afternoon where the water was cold and tasted of iron, and filled the buckets there, though they didn't need filling. He knew the farm where the dog would come out barking, and it came out barking, and he said *hello, dog*, and it stopped barking and looked at him, puzzled, the way the hens at home looked at him in the mornings when he'd been away. He knew which way the road would bend before it bent. He knew where it would go up and where it would go down. He knew a thorn tree.

It was a big one, alone in a field on the north side of the road, very old, bent all one way by a wind that must have blown from the west for a hundred years. He looked at it as they passed. Not for long.

"I slept under that," he said. "The first year."

Alea looked at it.

"It was raining," he said. "I didn't want a posting house. I didn't want anybody to—" He stopped. "I didn't want a roof. So I slept under that."

"Every year?"

"Just the first." He walked on. "After that I let Annis give me the back room. She never asked. That was why."

Alea looked back at the thorn tree until the road went round a hill and it wasn't there.

He went quieter. That was the other thing. He'd been quiet all the way from Valcrest, the way he was always quiet, but it had been the good quiet, the quiet of a man carrying the water and liking it. After Ashby it changed. It wasn't sad. She didn't think it was sad. It was more as if he were listening to something. As if the road had a sound she couldn't hear, and he'd been hearing it eight times a year for eight years, and he was hearing it again.

He still called the hares. But he called them later.

***

She asked him on the ninth night.

They'd stopped at the side of the road, past a village, because the posting house there had fleas. He'd known it would, and said so, and Aldous had looked at him and said *then we won't*, and they hadn't. The fire was going. Group 3 was being loud on the far side of it. Yan was writing in the channel, she could tell; he always sat a little way off with his eyes half closed and his face very still when he wrote, and Suzu would lift her head and watch the air in front of him as if she could see the words. The moon was coming up late over the road behind them, past full, lopsided, like an apple somebody had taken a bite out of.

Her father was sitting on the grass at the edge of the firelight with the rod across his knees, looking east. The way he had every night. The way, she supposed, he'd done every night of every one of the eight times.

She sat down beside him.

"What did you do?" she said. "On the road. All those years. A month there and a month back. On your own." She looked at the dark. "Annis said you never said a word you didn't need."

He was quiet for a while.

"I talked to her," he said.

Alea turned her head.

"In my head. Not out loud. I'm not Maigo." His mouth did something at the corner. "The whole way. Every year. From the top of the lake road to the cairn." He turned the rod over in his hands. "I told her the year."

"The year?"

"What had happened. Since the last time. A month's walking is a long time. You can tell somebody a whole year in a month, if you go slowly." He looked at the road. "I told her about you."

Alea didn't say anything.

"Every year," he said. "The whole year. All of it. In order. What you'd done. What you'd said. What you'd learned. What you'd broken." He was quiet. "The first year I told her you'd stopped talking for a month and then started again on the jetty one morning as if nothing had happened, to tell me there was a heron. The second year I told her you'd learned to read my letters. The English ones. And that you'd written *HELLO* on the cabin wall in charcoal, very large, and I'd left it."

"It's still there."

"It's still there," he agreed. "I told her that the year after." He turned the rod. "The year you were twelve, I told her I'd remembered the good knife." He looked at Alea. "For the first time in three years. I found it in the pocket on the second day and I was very proud of myself. I told her about it for a week." He breathed out, very slowly. "She'd have known. She'd have laughed at me the whole month."

Alea couldn't say anything.

"The year you were thirteen, I told her you'd asked me to teach you the sword. Every day for a month. And that I'd said no every day for a month, and then yes." He turned the rod. "The year you were fourteen, I told her you'd stood on the ice every morning for a year and breathed and felt nothing, and never once complained. And that you'd gone through the ice in the spring, up to your neck, and climbed out on your own, and gone back to the same spot and stood on it again." He looked down. "And that I'd nearly died."

He was quiet for a while.

"The year you were fifteen," he said, "your first tier. Drawn. On the end of the jetty, at dawn, nearly three years after the yes." He looked at the road. "I told her about it from the top of the lake road to the first posting house, and from there to Ashby, and from Ashby to the thorn tree. Six days. Just that."

The fire cracked behind them. Somebody laughed. Rhea, probably.

"And last year," said Alea. Her voice had gone very small. "What did you tell her last year?"

"Last year," said her father, "I told her you'd reached the second tier the week before I left. Tempered. In the militia hall yard, in front of five girls from another world, and that you'd opened your window and shown them." He was quiet. "And about thirty-five people. And a girl who'd known they were coming, and gone up to the top of the road every day for a year to meet them, without telling me. And a sword I'd taken out of a short man's hands on the red. And a boy with antlers who'd planted a tree." He was quiet for a long time. "I told her I didn't know what to do. I told her she'd have known."

Alea sat beside him on the grass and looked at the dark.

She'd thought she'd been left at the top of the road. Eight times. Standing at Rowan's gate with Rowan's hand on her shoulder, watching him go round the hill. She'd thought the road east was the one place in his life she'd never been. The one place that was only his.

She'd been on it every year. The whole way. From the top of the lake road to the cairn. In order. All of it.

"What are you going to tell her this year?" she said, when she could.

He looked east. At the road going on into the dark, toward a fort and a red plain and a grave.

"I hadn't started," he said. "I've been carrying the water." He turned his head and looked at her, and his face was doing something she'd seen it do perhaps three times in her life. "I thought this year you could tell her yourself."

***

She didn't sleep for a long time.

She lay by the fire in her blanket with Astra beside her in the grass and her father's coat over her feet, because he'd put it there when he thought she was asleep, and watched the moon go up over the road. Lopsided. Past full. Going down to nothing, a little more each night, the way Higu's chart said it would. Dark in a week. And then coming back. And then full again, and then full once more, on the night they'd stand at the grave.

*Three full moons*, Higu had said. *Look up*.

She thought about what she'd tell her mother.

She didn't know where to start. That was the problem. Her father had had eight years and a month each way and he'd told it in order, a year at a time, slowly, from the top of the lake road. She had one evening. Or a few days. He'd said a few days. And nine years to tell.

She thought she might start with the box. She'd said so, on the riverbank. *I'm going to tell her about the box*. But lying there she thought that wasn't the start. The box was the end. The start was further back.

The start was a knife, in a pocket, in the dark, when she was twelve.

*I've been packing it*, she thought. *Like you did. Every year. He never noticed. He thought he'd remembered.*

*He told you anyway. Every year. He told you he'd remembered the good knife.*

*You'd have known.*

On the other side of the fire, her father was sitting up with the first watch, the rod across his knees, looking east. Not moving. Listening to whatever the road said, the sound she couldn't hear.

*Slower*, she thought. To him. To both of them.

And then, because she was seventeen and it had been a long day and the downs were long and the larks were loud, she fell asleep. On the road east. The far side of the hill. The road he'd walked eight times on his own, with her on it every time, in his head, all the way.

She was on it now with her feet.
