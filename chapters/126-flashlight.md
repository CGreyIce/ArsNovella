On Earth, Hyvred had seen the world through a hole.

That was the easiest way to explain it, and she'd explained it a lot. Not blind. People always said *blind* and then got embarrassed. It was more like this: hold your hands up in front of your face and make a circle with your fingers, and look through it. That's what you get. A circle. Everything inside it sharp and bright and fine, and everything outside it just gone. Not dark, even. Just not there. You learned to move your head a lot. You learned to listen. You learned that the edges of rooms were where things lived that would hurt you, and that people came at you out of nowhere, all the time, and that most of them didn't mean to.

She'd made a clip about it once. A little video, for her stream, of what it looked like from the inside. A black screen with a round bright hole in the middle, moving around a room. She'd thought it might help people understand.

And a guy in chat had said it looked exactly like osu!'s Flashlight mod.

He'd meant it as a joke. Flashlight was a thing you turned on in a rhythm game to make it harder: the screen went dark except for a circle round your cursor, and you played the whole map through a hole. Good players did it to show off. And this guy had seen her clip and gone *oh, that's just Flashlight*, the way you would, and then somebody else in chat had said *dude she actually has that*, and he had apologised.

He'd apologised *so much*. Eleven messages. She'd counted. *oh no*. *oh no im so sorry*. *i didnt know*. *im an idiot*. *im so sorry*. On and on, scrolling up her chat, until she'd been laughing too hard to read them. It had been the funniest thing that had happened on her stream that month. She'd told him so. He'd sent one more anyway. *still sorry*.

She'd known him four months, all told. Then he'd died.

And then she'd opened her eyes in a hall in Solmere and seen the ceiling.

The whole ceiling. All of it, at once, edges and corners and the painted beams and the dust in the sunlight in the high windows, and thirty-four other people panicking about being in another world all round her, and she'd been standing in the middle of the hall crying because she could see a *ceiling*. She'd never seen a whole one before. She hadn't known they went all the way to the walls.

That was nearly two years ago. She still looked up, sometimes, in rooms, just to check.

***

Group 5 got him on the second night.

Nobody had arranged that either. It was just that Group 2 had walked out of the west gate with him at dawn and not come back until dark, and everybody at the long table in the Broken Lance had known, without anybody saying, that today was theirs. Tiger had nearly gone after them three times. Drew had stopped him three times, with a hand on his chest, quite gently, the way he'd stopped him once at the top of a mountain pass and once at a postern gate.

"It's their turn," Drew had said.

"I know it's their *turn*."

"Then sit down."

Tiger had sat down. For about ten minutes. Then he'd got up again.

When the water-carrier finally came in from the river at dusk, with the five of them behind him all looking like they'd been crying in a pleasant sort of way and Oswin clutching a book to his chest, Tiger had been on his feet before the door finished opening. And he hadn't hugged him. That was how Hyvred knew it mattered. Tiger stood in front of him in the doorway with his ears forward and his hands at his sides, and said, in a voice that was trying very hard to be casual:

"Can I show you something?"

"Now?"

"It's in the Lists. It's kind of far. It's kind of late." Tiger's tail was going. "You don't have to."

The water-carrier looked at him for a moment. Then at the rest of them, behind Tiger. At Drew, who was leaning on the end of the long table with his arms folded and his face very calm. At Bee, with her chalk in her hand and her antennae flat. At Kei, with Hisui round his neck saying something fast. At Hyvred.

"Show me," he said.

***

The Lists were empty at night. Hyvred had never seen them empty.

She'd come in two days ago with Group 5, from the west, after three weeks on the road from Thornwall, and the first thing she'd done after they'd found the Broken Lance was walk up the street to the great pale bowl in the middle of the city and stand at one of the gates and look in. It had been full then. Carpenters building stands. Rakers raking the sand. A man painting a forty-foot bracket on the inside of the east wall, with Drew standing beside him telling him where to put the lines. Lucan in the middle of the sand in his red cloak, with his arms out, turning slowly, weeping.

Now it was dark, and quiet, and huge.

They went in by a side gate that Lucan had given Drew a key to. Down a stone passage that smelled of sand and old oil and horses. Out onto the sand itself, which was raked smooth and silver in the moonlight and so wide that the far side of it was just a pale line. And all round them the stands went up, tier on tier on tier, pale stone going up into the dark like the inside of a shell, so high that the top of them cut a great oval out of the stars.

Hyvred stood on the sand and looked at all of it at once.

She could. That was still the thing. Every tier. Every bench. The forty-foot bracket on the east wall, which was mostly blank boxes, with the spider still in the top corner, sitting in the middle of its web. The paper fishing rods somebody had tied along the rail of the royal box. The moon, and all the stars round it, through the oval at the top. Not through a hole. All of it, edges and all, as far as there was.

And with the other eye, the scope eye, the one with the little red mark in it like a crosshair: she could see the light in things. She'd been able to since the first week. The faint warm glow in the sand where ten thousand people had sat and shouted and been happy. The cold old light in the stone. The light in her friends, moving, the way mana moves in anyone who has any. Tiger's, quick and hot and all in his legs. Drew's, steady, slow, a banked fire. Bee's, sharp and stubborn and fizzing at the fingertips where the chalk was. Kei's, calm, cool, with a little bright knot of something else wound round his neck that was Hisui.

And the water-carrier's.

She didn't look at that. Not with the scope eye. Not again. She'd looked once, at the gate, properly, the way she'd promised. She'd seen what she'd seen. And she'd sat down in the road, and afterwards Deston had asked her what it was, on the high street, very gently, and she'd said *it's his*, and that was the end of it.

It was. It was his. Some things you see, you don't get to keep.

She looked at him with the other eye. Just a man, walking on the sand in the moonlight, with a fishing rod over his shoulder and his face sliding off her sight like everyone else's. She found she preferred it.

***

Tiger's gate was in the yard at the north end of the sand, through an arch, where the carpenters had been working all week.

It was a gate. That was the first thing you could say about it. It was a real gate: two heavy leaves of oak planking on iron hinges, in a frame of square-cut posts as thick as a man's body, with a short stretch of timber wall going off on either side of it and a fighting step behind, so that seven people could stand on the step and hold it. Drew had wanted a gate for the second trial. Lucan had wanted it to look like a castle. Tiger had wanted to build it.

"I built it," said Tiger.

He stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets and his tail very still. "Me and the carpenters. Two weeks. Since we got here. Before we got here, actually, I drew it on the road, Bee helped." He looked at it. "It's for the Gate. The second trial. Seven of us hold it. The champions try to take it." He was quiet. "It's the biggest thing I've ever built."

The water-carrier looked at it for a long time.

Then he went up to the left-hand post and put his hand flat on it. Not pushing. Just resting it there, the way you'd rest your hand on a horse's neck. He stood like that for a moment. Then he knocked on it, once, with his knuckle, and listened.

"It's going to fall down," said Tiger. "Isn't it."

"Why would it fall down?"

"Everything I build falls down." Tiger's voice was very light. Much too light. "That's my thing. It's the joke. Everyone knows. The Minecraft house blew up and since then nothing I build stands. Here. Back home. Doesn't matter. Huts. Sheds. A wall at Thornwall that a cart backed into. A tower that blew over before anyone climbed it." He laughed. It wasn't a very good laugh. "Except one. One thing. A shed, at Thornwall, against the north wall. I built it three times. The third time it got a roof." He looked at the gate. "It stood. All summer, in the storms. All winter, under a foot of snow. It's standing right now. The only thing I ever built that stood."

"Whose shed?"

Tiger didn't say anything for a moment.

Hyvred looked at Drew. Drew was leaning on the arch, very still, watching Tiger the way he'd watched him at the top of a pass with both hands on his chest.

"Ossie's," said Tiger.

***

He told it all. Hyvred had heard him tell it before; she'd been there for most of it. But she'd never heard him tell it like this. Standing in front of a gate in the moonlight with his hands in his pockets, to a man who didn't interrupt.

Ossie. A freckled soldier in the Western Watch at Thornwall, Drawn, nineteen, who'd raced Tiger every single morning along the top of the wall for three months and lost every single time and never once stopped trying. Who'd ridden in with the company from the capital on the day one of its horses kicked Tiger's first shed flat, and apologised for that horse for a week as if it were his fault. Who'd gone up into the passes with Sergeant Dunmore's ten in the first spring, and not come back. Not as himself.

And then, at midsummer, on the deep patrol, at the top of the pass, at midnight. A town with no smoke. Rings of people standing in the dark round a circle burned into a valley floor. And in the third ring, turned up toward the pass, toward them, a freckled face. Calling. *Tiger! Come down! It's warm down here!*

"I'd have gone," said Tiger. "I was going to go. Drew stopped me. He put his hands on my chest and said *not here*. And then at the postern in the autumn he said *not on your own*. *Not ever*." Tiger looked at the gate. "His dad came. In the summer. All the way from Harrowford. A carpenter. Aldo. Kei told him. *It had his face and it wasn't him*. And his dad just nodded, and the next day he put a roof on a shed for somebody he'd never met, because I was his son's friend. He left a shirt on a nail inside. Mended at the elbow." He was quiet. "So it's got a roof. And it stood. The one thing I ever built that stood, and it's *his*. I sit on it every day. And I hate that it stood. Because standing means it's finished. And then it's just a shed. And he's just—"

He stopped.

The water-carrier hadn't moved. He'd stood beside the gatepost the whole time, with his hand still resting on it, listening. He hadn't said anything. He hadn't said *I'm sorry*, or *it wasn't your fault*, or any of the things people said. He'd just listened, the way you listen to a report.

"Tell me about him," he said. "Not the pass. Before."

Tiger looked at him.

"He was really slow," said Tiger, after a moment. His voice wobbled. "Really, really slow. For a Drawn. He'd come up the wall stairs every morning and say *today's the day* and then I'd beat him by about a hundred yards and he'd lie down on the wall and laugh. Every day. Three months." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "He was going to buy his dad a goat. For the yard in Harrowford. He talked about the goat all the time. He'd named it. He hadn't even got it yet and he'd *named* it."

"What was it called?"

"*Cabbage*," said Tiger, and laughed, and then put both his hands over his face and cried, standing in front of the biggest thing he'd ever built, in the moonlight, in the yard of the Lists.

The water-carrier took his hand off the gatepost. He didn't hug him. Tiger had hugged him four times, five, Dennis was keeping a ledger. He didn't need another one. He just stood beside him, close, shoulder to shoulder, facing the gate, the way two people stand looking at the same thing.

"It'll stand," he said, after a while.

Tiger took his hands down. "You don't know that."

"It'll stand long enough," said the water-carrier. "That's all a gate's for. You've built one thing that stood. Now you'll have built two." He looked at it. "I built a lot of things that fell down too. A wall at Cinderhold, when I was sixteen. They made me rebuild it three times. A jetty, the first year at the lake. The old one was half rotten, and I rebuilt it, and I put the posts in wrong and it went sideways into the water in a storm and Diwa laughed so hard she sat down on the shore." He paused. "The second one stood. It's still standing. I fish off the end of it every night."

Tiger stared at him.

"You built the jetty," he said. "In the stories. The one you sit on."

"The second one."

"*What happened to the first one?*"

"It's at the bottom of the lake," said the water-carrier. "With a lot of pike in it. They love it."

And Tiger laughed. Properly, this time, wet and enormous, the way he laughed at everything, and Hyvred saw his light do the thing it did when he was about to run: all of it going down into his legs at once, hot and quick and ready. But he didn't run. He stood there next to the water-carrier in front of his gate, laughing, with his hands in his pockets.

Drew, leaning on the arch, let out a long, slow breath, the way a man does when he's been holding something steady for a long time and has just been told he can put it down for a minute.

***

Bee drew him a dog.

She did it on the inside wall of the Lists, under the forty-foot bracket, while the others were still in the yard. Hyvred heard the chalk going and came back through the arch and found her standing at the foot of the wall with her chalk in her hand and her antennae flat back and her wings very still, drawing. One line. One unbroken line, the way she'd learned in the winter at Thornwall, on the shortest day: a running dog, ears back, tongue out, all four legs off the ground, in one stroke of chalk from nose to tail without the chalk once leaving the stone.

Under it, small, she drew a heart.

Then she stepped back.

The dog came off the wall.

Hyvred had seen it a hundred times now and it still made her chest go tight. It came off the wall the way a reflection comes off water when you lift something out of it: a white chalk-line dog, flat and then not flat, landing on the sand on all four feet. It shook itself. Then it ran. Round the sand in a great wide circle in the moonlight, kicking up silver, leaving paw prints, all the way round the Lists and back, and sat down at the water-carrier's feet as he came through the arch, and looked up at him with its chalk tongue out.

He looked down at it.

"That's a wolf," he said.

"It's a *dog*," said Bee.

"I know," he said. "I saw the bedsheet."

He crouched down, and put his hand on the chalk dog's head. It let him. It leaned into his hand, the way a dog does, and its tail went, and the faint white line of it shimmered a little round the edges where his hand was.

Bee was looking at the heart she'd drawn on the wall. Not at him.

"You did that," she said. "Once. On my art." Her voice was flat and furious and confident, the way it always was, the way it was when she was saying something she couldn't believe she was saying. "I posted the Puffs. The first drawing of them. Two puffballs with blue wings. And somebody put a little heart under it. Just a heart. No comment. I didn't know who. I didn't know who you *were*. I looked at your name and thought *who's that*." She glared at the wall. "I found out after. Hyvred told me. After. When you were dead."

The water-carrier didn't say anything.

"Why?" said Bee.

"It was a good drawing," he said.

Bee's wings buzzed. One short sharp sound. Her antennae went straight up and then flat again.

"That's not a *reason*."

"It's the only reason there is."

Bee looked at him for a long, hard moment, crouched on the sand with his hand on her chalk dog's head. Then she looked at the heart on the wall. Then she said, "*fine*," exactly the way she'd said it at the gate, and the two Pollen Puffs came out of her collar in a yellow cloud and flew straight across the sand, past her, and landed on him. One on each shoulder. Round and fluffy and buzzing, with their little blue wings going and their cheeks blushing, settling in against his collar as if they'd been doing it all their lives.

"*Traitors*," said Bee.

The Puffs didn't care.

***

Kei had been sitting on the bottom tier of the stands the whole time, with his elbows on his knees, listening to Hisui.

"He wants to know," said Kei, when the water-carrier came and sat down on the tier beside him with two Puffs on his shoulders and a chalk dog lying across his feet, "what she's like."

"Who?"

"The other snake."

The water-carrier considered.

"White," he said. "Small. She sits in Yan's collar. She hisses at people Yan doesn't like and people who say his name wrong. She eats things that have souls in them; he has to feed her." He paused. "I've only seen her once. On a road. In the red. She looked at me for a long time and then went to sleep."

Kei listened to Hisui. His face did something.

"He wants to know if she's pretty."

"I don't know if snakes are pretty."

"He says that's a stupid answer." Kei listened again. "He says he's never met another snake who talks. He says nobody's ever been able to hear him except me, in two years, and he's tired of only having me." Kei's calm trustworthy face did something unsteady again. "He says he's been waiting for her since the spring. Since Yan wrote about her in the channel. He says he's going to be very polite and not hiss at anyone and he's practising."

Hisui, round Kei's neck, lifted his head and looked at the water-carrier with small bright eyes and flicked his tongue.

"She doesn't talk," said the water-carrier gently. "Yan says. Nobody's ever heard her."

"He says she'll talk to him," said Kei. "He says she's been waiting too." He listened. "He says two days is too long."

"Everybody says that."

"He says everybody's right," said Kei, and Hisui put his head back down on Kei's collarbone with an air of having made his point.

Kei didn't say anything else for a while. Hyvred watched him. He'd spent two years at Thornwall being the person everybody brought things to. Soldiers who couldn't sleep. Tiger, every night, for months, about Ossie. Bee, once, about her wings. Hyvred herself, about the absence. Kei sat on a step somewhere with Hisui round his neck and listened, calm and easy and trustworthy, and people came to him with whatever they were carrying the way they'd come to a fire.

He'd told her once why. *Somebody did it for me once. Stayed on a call till four in the morning. I never said thank you properly. So I'm just. Doing it forward, I guess*.

He was sitting next to the somebody now. On the bottom tier of the Lists, in the dark, with a snake round his neck. And for once in two years, Hyvred saw, Kei wasn't listening to anyone. He was just sitting there. Being sat next to.

***

Hyvred took him up to the top.

She hadn't planned to. She'd planned to tell him at the long table, or on the road, or some other time when it wasn't midnight and the others weren't all there. But Tiger had told him about Ossie in front of the gate, and Bee had told him about the heart, and Kei was sitting on the bottom step being sat next to, and Drew was watching all of it from the arch with his steady slow banked-fire light, and Hyvred found that she'd stood up and said, "Can I show you something too?"

And he'd said, "Show me."

They went up the stands. All the way. Tier after tier, up the steep stone steps between the benches, until the sand was a silver oval far below with three small figures and a chalk dog on it, and the top of the Lists was a narrow walk round the rim with a parapet, and the whole of Valcrest lay round them in the dark, lamps and roofs and the long pale road going out north toward the Last Look, and the plain going away on every side, silver, to the edge of the world.

Hyvred could see all of it. To the edges. As far as there was.

"West," she said. She pointed. "That way. Twenty days on foot. Thornwall, and then the Greyspine, and then the pass." She took a breath. "I'm going to tell you what's there. Is that okay?"

"Tell me."

She told him.

She'd told it before. To Corvin, who'd written it down. To Hild, who'd listened with her one eye shut. In the channel, in the summer, after the pass, at three in the morning, when she couldn't sleep: *not really. but I'm home*. She'd told it so many times she'd got it down to a shape, the way you get a story down to a shape. She told it the same way now. The old paved road at the top of the pass, wide enough for armies, that nobody had built. The town at the bottom of the valley with no smoke. The lights in it that weren't lamps. The rings. Hundreds of people, standing in rings, in the dark, round a circle burned into the valley floor the size of a fort. Not moving. Faces turned up. Ossie's, in the third ring.

And at the far end of the valley, behind the rings, something that wasn't there.

That was the only way she'd ever been able to say it. Not a dark thing. Not a shape. An absence. A place where there was no light at all, in eyes that saw light in sheep and grass and mountains. The size of a hill. As if somebody had cut a hole in the world in the shape of something very large and left it standing at the end of a valley.

"It turned," said Hyvred. "That night. When I Scoped. It turned round and looked at us. Not with eyes. It doesn't have eyes. It just—faced us. And then it turned back, and we went home." She swallowed. "In the autumn it moved. Down to the near end of the valley. Right at the foot of the pass. The rings doubled. Eleven, twelve of them. And one night every chalk dog Bee had ever drawn at Thornwall started barking at once, and I Scoped, and it had turned to face the town. Thornwall. Us. It looked for about fifty heartbeats. Drew made me stop looking." She stopped. "Then it turned back. Like it had seen what it wanted."

"And since?"

"Snow. All winter. I could feel it under the snow. Waiting." She looked west, into the dark, past the edge of the plain. "When we left in the spring it hadn't moved. It's still there. At the foot of the pass. Corvin and Hild are holding the pass with the king's soldiers. Two hundred and something. And a Bonfire." She was quiet. "It's not in a hurry. That's the thing. That's what Hisui says, every time. *It's not in a hurry*. It's just waiting until it's ready."

***

He'd listened the whole time.

Not the way Kei listened, easy and warm. Not the way the others listened to her in the channel, scared, scrolling. He listened the way Hild listened to a scout's report: very still, with his whole attention, and his face, if she could have seen it, giving nothing back. She had the strangest feeling, standing beside him on the top of the Lists, that she was standing next to a soldier. An old one. An officer, being briefed, at a wall, at midnight.

Then he started asking questions.

How far apart were the rings? Evenly spaced, or closer at the middle? Which way did the faces turn, all toward the pass, or some toward the absence? Did they move in daylight? Did they move at all? The circle burned into the valley floor: was it a ring, or a ring with lines in it? How wide was the paved road at the top of the pass? Was it worn? Which way were the ruts? Had the absence ever moved while she was Scoping, or only between times? When it turned to face Thornwall, had the rings turned with it?

She answered every one. Some of them she'd never been asked. Some of them she'd never asked herself, and found, answering, that she knew. The ruts went *down*. Into the valley. Old ones. The rings hadn't turned with it. The faces had stayed turned up toward the pass the whole time, toward Thornwall, toward where people were, like flowers turned to a window. Only the absence had turned.

The circle was a ring with lines in it.

When she said that, he went very still.

She saw it. Not with the scope eye. She didn't look at him with the scope eye. With the other one. Just a man on the top of the Lists in the dark, standing very still. But something in the stillness changed. The way a lake changes when the wind drops. And for a moment she felt it come off him, the way she'd felt the cold come off the ridge that first night at Thornwall: something very old and very cold and very, very angry, standing up inside a man who'd spent eight years on a jetty teaching it to sit down.

Then it sat down again. She watched it. The way you'd watch somebody put a knife back in a drawer and close the drawer and keep their hand on it for a moment, just to be sure.

"A ring with lines in it," he said. Very quietly.

"Like a wheel," said Hyvred. "Spokes. I drew it for Corvin." She swallowed. "Have you seen one?"

He didn't answer for a long time.

"Once," he said. "Smaller. On a rise, an hour's walk from a wall. With fires round it so I could see."

Hyvred didn't say anything.

She knew. Everybody knew, now. Cursiffe had *Diwa. A healer.* in his ledger from a Group 3 post a year ago. Aleaka had read the books. The whole channel had worked out, piece by piece, over a year, what had happened on the Redmarch to the woman with cold hands, and what the man who loved her had done afterwards. Nobody had ever written it all down in one place. Nobody needed to.

A ring with lines in it, on a rise. With fires round it so he could see.

"I'm sorry," said Hyvred. "I didn't—I'm sorry."

"Don't be." He looked west, into the dark, the way she'd been looking. "That's the best report I've had in eight years. Better than the scouts'. Better than the garrison's. You saw the ruts." He was quiet. "Nobody else would have looked at the ruts."

"I see everything," said Hyvred, a bit helplessly. "That's the—I can't *not*."

"I know," he said. "That's why it's the best report I've had in eight years."

And then, after a while, still looking west:

"Don't go back down there on your own. Any of you."

"Drew won't let us."

"Good," he said. "Drew's right." A pause. "Drew's always right. It's very annoying."

Hyvred laughed. She hadn't meant to. It came out of her on the top of the Lists in the dark, wet and loud and hyper, the way she always laughed, and the stillness beside her changed again, and she felt him smile. She couldn't see it. She felt it. The way you feel the sun come out on the back of your neck.

***

They stayed up there until it got light.

Not talking much. She sat on the parapet with her legs over the drop, the way a boy had sat on a wall at Cinderhold once in a story she'd never heard, and he sat on the walk beside her with his back against the stone and the two Pollen Puffs asleep on his shoulders, and the whole of Valcrest lay under them, going grey, and then pale, and then gold. Far below on the sand the chalk dog had curled up in the middle of the oval and gone to sleep, a white line in the silver. Tiger and Bee had gone back to the inn. Kei was asleep on the bottom tier with Hisui round his neck. Drew was a small figure in the arch, not asleep, never asleep, watching.

"You called it Flashlight mod," said Hyvred, when the sun came up.

He went quiet beside her.

"In my chat," said Hyvred. "My clip. You said *that's just Flashlight*."

"I'm sorry."

"You said sorry *eleven times*."

"Twelve," he said. "There was one after."

"*Still sorry*," said Hyvred. "I know. I remember." She laughed. "I counted. It was the funniest thing that happened all month. I told you that. You said sorry *again*."

"I was very sorry."

"You were an *idiot*." She swung her legs over the drop. She looked down. All the way down. The whole of the Lists, every tier, every bench, the sand and the dog and the arch and the man in it, the city round it, the plain round that, the edge of the world. To the edges. All of it at once. "It's not a flashlight any more," she said.

"No?"

"No." She turned her head and looked at him. With the scope eye. Not *properly*, not the way she'd looked at the gate. Just looking. The gold of it gone sharp, and the little red mark in it, locking on. "It's a *scope*."

There was a pause.

And then he laughed. Out loud. On the top of the Lists at sunrise, with two Pollen Puffs falling off his shoulders in a startled yellow cloud. Not in his rests, the way Newish said. Properly. The way she'd heard him laugh in voice chat a hundred times, at two in the morning, at somebody's terrible play.

"Of *course* it is," he said.

"I know!"

"You've got a *scope*. In your *eye*."

"I *know*. With a *crosshair*. Red." She was laughing too. She couldn't stop. "You were an AWPer. Maigo told us. You were always on the AWP. And I've got a *crosshair*."

"Crosshair placement?"

"*Head height*," said Hyvred. "Hako told me. He says you told him that first. Every time."

"Head height," he agreed.

She sat on the parapet in the sunrise with her legs over the drop and the whole world spread out under her, all of it, edges and all, and laughed until she had to hold on to the stone. And he sat beside her and laughed too. And for a while neither of them said anything at all, because there wasn't anything that needed saying, and the sun came up gold over a city in another world, and she could see every bit of it.

The dawn class was on the practice ground behind the Lists when they came down. Six of them in a ring in the mist, very still. Petra first, the way she always was. Hako with his spear. Xavier. PenguChi with her penguin. Alea with the enormous sword.

And Tiger. Standing on the end of the ring with his hands in his pockets and his hair on end, as if he'd got up at dawn after two hours' sleep and come straight here without thinking about it, which he had.

"You want to learn?" said the water-carrier, at the fence.

"I want to be faster," said Tiger.

"You're the fastest of the thirty-five."

"I want to be faster at the *right time*," said Tiger. "Not just fast. *Earlier*. You said it to Hako. I heard. *Move while it's still deciding to*." His ears went back. "I wasn't fast enough. In the pass. I was fast and I was in the wrong place." He swallowed. "I want to be in the right place."

The water-carrier looked at him for a long moment.

"Stand next to Petra," he said. "She'll tell you when you're wrong."

"I'll tell him every time," said Petra.

"I know," said Tiger. "Drew already does."

Hyvred leaned on the fence and watched them. Seven of them on the wet sand in the mist, in a ring, waiting. And the man in the middle with a rod over his shoulder and two Pollen Puffs that had refused to go home with Bee, sitting on top of his head like a hat.

She looked at the whole thing. All of it. To the edges.

She didn't need to look at anything properly any more. She'd done that.
