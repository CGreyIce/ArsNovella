Lucan of the Lists wept easily, and he was not ashamed of it.

He'd been told to be. Often. By his father, who'd been a magistrate and wept at nothing. By the Council, in his first year as master of the Lists, when he'd announced the winner of the spring tournament with tears running into his beard. By Ysolde, who'd been a three-time champion of the Lists before she was a guard, and who'd stood in the centre of the sand with the crown of laurels on her horns while he wept over her, and said, quite clearly, in front of eight thousand people, *Lucan, you're dripping on me.*

He'd wept anyway. He'd wept for fifteen years. At every final, every upset, every underdog who stood up off the sand when everybody thought they'd stay down. He wept at weddings. He wept at bad plays. He'd wept, last midsummer, so hard that he'd had to sit down in the royal box, when ten thousand people in paper horns had stood up and chanted the names of an oni boy and a horned girl who'd been called demons in the street a year before.

A crowd was the most beautiful thing in the world. He'd always thought so. Ten thousand people deciding, all at once, to feel the same thing. And if you knew how, if you'd spent your life learning how, you could stand in the middle of the sand with your arms out and your red cloak spread and *tell* them what to feel. And they would.

He'd told the crowd to hate them, a year ago, when they arrived. *They are going to hate you*, he'd said to the five of them, at the gate, the first day. *And then they're going to love you.*

He'd been right. He usually was, about crowds.

He'd been wrong about almost everything else.

***

The Lists were empty in the late autumn.

They always were. The tournament season ended at the harvest, and the great oval of the arena stood silent through the winter, its tiers of stone seating going up and up on every side, empty, with the wind blowing old paper horns across the benches. The sand was raked flat and left. In the mornings, now, it was frozen. White with frost, crisp underfoot, so that the five of them left footprints across it when they came in at dawn to train.

Lucan came to watch most mornings. He always had. He'd sit in the royal box, which nobody used in the winter, with his cloak round him and a flask of something hot, and watch Ysolde drill them on the frozen sand.

Chris, with his notebooks. Hako, with his spear, always drifting to the far angle, the empty spot on the left side of any formation, the one Chris said had belonged to someone else once. Ina, with his mask pushed up on his head, the violet fire coming and going at his fingertips now, a small wisteria-coloured flame he could call up on a breath. Newish, who heard everyone's beat, and danced when she thought nobody was looking. Winter.

Winter, with her silver hair and her curling horns, who'd arrived a year ago so guarded that she wouldn't stand within three paces of Lucan, and who still wouldn't, most days, but who'd started saying *good morning* to him in the autumn without being asked. He'd wept about that, too. Privately. In the royal box. Into his flask.

***

She went up on a morning late in the autumn.

Lucan saw it happen. He'd remember that for the rest of his life: that he'd been there, in the royal box, by chance, with his flask, and seen it.

There was a water trough by the east gate of the arena, for the horses on tournament days. A long stone thing, full of rainwater. It was frozen over at the edges, that morning, with a skin of ice round the rim and black water in the middle. Winter had gone over to it between drills to break the ice for a drink, the way she did. She stood there with her hand on the edge of the trough, looking down at the water.

And didn't break it.

She just stood there. Lucan watched her stand there. Ysolde, in the middle of the sand, had stopped drilling Hako and was watching too. So, he saw, was Chris. Chris always watched Winter. Not the way men watched women, Lucan had noticed in the first month; the way a man watches a friend who once told him something hard, to make sure she's all right.

Winter put her other hand on the edge of the trough. She looked down at the black water in the middle, ringed with ice.

Lucan couldn't see her face. He could see her shoulders. They'd gone very still, the way Ysolde's went still on the sand before a bout.

She said something. One word. He didn't hear it from the box. He'd ask her later, and she'd tell him, after a long time, as if it were something private.

*Still.*

***

The water in the trough froze.

Not the way water freezes in the night, from the edges in, slowly, dull and grey. All at once. From the middle *out*. From a single point in the centre of the black water, a white star of frost bloomed and spread, and as it spread it *grew*: up, out of the surface of the water, into the air, in thin white crystal petals, layer on layer of them, curling outward like the petals of a flower opening in fast time. In a heartbeat. In two. Until there was a flower of ice standing up out of the trough, as wide as a dinner plate, perfect, every petal edged with rime so fine that it caught the low winter sun and broke it into colours.

Winter stood there with her hands on the edge of the trough and looked at it.

Lucan was on his feet in the royal box. He didn't remember standing.

On the sand, Ysolde had come over. She stood at Winter's shoulder, a careful three paces away, the way she always did, and looked down at the flower of ice in the trough, and her goat's face did something Lucan had seen it do only once before, on the sand at midsummer, with her arm in a sling.

"There," said Ysolde.

***

Winter opened her window. Lucan couldn't see it, of course. None of them could, none of the natives except the guides. But he saw her look at it, and he saw her face, and he saw Chris come across the frozen sand at a walk that turned into a run halfway, with his notebook already open.

"Twelfth," said Chris. His voice cracked. "Twelve. You're the twelfth."

"I know how to count," said Winter.

"What's it called? The skill. What's it—"

Winter turned her window round and showed him, and Chris read it and laughed, a short wet laugh, and wrote it down.

"*Rime*," said Chris. To the rest of them, coming over. "Spark. Skill: Rime."

Ina had put his mask down. He didn't say anything. He went and stood beside Winter at the trough, on the other side from Ysolde, and looked at the flower of ice, and then held out his fist. Winter looked at it. Then she bumped it with her own. Across the corner of the trough, the way they'd bumped fists on the roof of the Broken Lance at midsummer, across the chimney.

Newish was crying. Hako had his spear in both hands and was grinning at the ice like a boy.

And Lucan, in the royal box, was weeping into his flask so hard that he'd spilled half of it down the front of his good red cloak.

***

Winter found him there, later. When the others had gone back to the inn. She came up the steps to the royal box on her own, which she'd never done, and stood at the top of them, three paces away, and looked at him.

"You were crying," she said.

"I was."

"You always cry."

"I always do," said Lucan. "I'm sorry. It's a terrible habit. My father thought it would ruin my career." He wiped his beard. "It made it, in the end."

Winter looked at him for a long time. She had a way of looking at men, Lucan had noticed, that made them feel weighed. As if she were deciding whether they were safe to stand near. Most men, he thought, didn't pass.

"Don't," she said. "Cry. About me."

"I can't help it."

"I know." She looked out at the arena, at the empty tiers, at the trough by the east gate with the flower of ice still standing in it, catching the sun. "In the summer," she said, "everybody stood in front of me. Ysolde. Hako. Ina. Chris. Newish. The whole arena, with the paper horns. Everybody stood in front of me." She was quiet. "I didn't want to be stood in front of anymore. I wanted to be the one who could stand in front of someone."

Lucan didn't say anything.

"I was in Vietnam," said Winter. "When he died. Ice. Two months, mostly offline. I didn't find out for two months. Everybody else had already cried by the time I knew." She looked at the trough. "I wrote *still*, because that's what I wanted. For everything to stop moving for a second. Long enough to catch up."

She turned and went back down the steps without waiting for him to answer.

Lucan sat in the royal box for a long time after that. He didn't cry. He found he couldn't. It was too big for crying.

***

Drew's letter came that afternoon.

Lucan read it at the long table in the common room of the Broken Lance, with the five of them round him, and Ysolde at the end, and a fire going. He read it aloud, because he always read everything aloud. He'd learned that at the Lists: a letter read aloud to a room became a speech, and a speech could change a room.

He got halfway through.

*We've never once been in one place. Thirty-five of us. Not since the hall.*

He stopped.

He couldn't help it. He'd known Drew would make him cry; Corvin had added a line at the bottom, in his tiny hand, *he'll cry*, and Lucan had been determined, for once, not to. He made it as far as *thirty-five people who've stood next to each other* and had to put the letter down on the table and press the heels of his hands against his eyes.

"Give it here," said Ysolde, and took it, and read the rest herself, flatly, the way she read everything, which made it worse.

*I'll smile the whole way through. That's the job.*

Nobody said anything when she'd finished.

"He's right," said Chris quietly.

They all looked at him.

He had his notebook open on the table in front of him. The big one. ALL GROUPS. He'd been keeping it since the first winter, and it had grown into something Lucan couldn't read but could see was extraordinary: thirty-five names down the side, and columns across, a year of them, numbers in Chris's small square hand, and lines, and notes, and little marks that meant things only Chris knew.

"Look," said Chris. He turned it round so they could see. Lucan couldn't read it. His eyes slid off the English like water off oil. But he could see the shape. "Twelve, now. At the first tier. Out of thirty-five." He put his finger on the names one at a time, too fast for Lucan to follow. "We've each got a little bit of something. Thea can read the windows. Hyvred can see the turned. Dennis can make things see what isn't there. RX can disappear. Winter can freeze a trough into a flower. Ina can set a hunter's face on fire. And none of us have ever stood in a room with more than four of the others." He looked up. "Drew's right. It's not enough to talk every night. We need to *be* there. Together. All of us. Before whatever's at the bottom of that pass is ready."

Lucan looked at the notebook.

And noticed, because he noticed things about rooms, that there was a thirty-sixth column.

At the very edge of the page. Ruled, carefully, in the same neat hand as the rest. With no name at the top, and nothing written in it at all.

He didn't ask. He'd learned, in a year with these five, not to ask about some things. But he saw Chris see him looking at it, and close the notebook, very gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

***

Lucan wrote to the king.

It took him eleven drafts. He'd written to the Crown a hundred times in fifteen years, as master of the Lists, petitions and proclamations and seating plans for royal visits, and he'd never once needed more than two. This one took eleven. The first was nine pages. Ysolde read it and handed it back without a word and he went away and cut it to six. She read the six-page one and said *Lucan*, in a particular voice, and he cut it to four.

The eleventh was one page.

*Majesty.*

*The tournament of the summoned was postponed until the borders were secure.*

*They will not be secure. Not this year, not next. The Advisor's own reports say so. Kharvos is hunting them at every posting; hunters have come for them at Wrenhaven, at Valcrest, on the Redmarch and now at Kingsferry, and something waits at the foot of the western pass that has stopped facing its valley and turned to face us.*

*They are thirty-five, and they have never once been in one place.*

*The boy Drew, of Thornwall, whom I asked to run the tournament, has written to me. I enclose his letter. I would ask Your Majesty to read it twice.*

*I have run the Lists for fifteen years. I know what a crowd can do. I have seen it stand up and chant the names of people it called demons a year before. I ask Your Majesty to let me give the summoned a crowd, and each other, before they need both.*

*Lucan, of the Lists.*

Ysolde read it. She read it twice. Then she handed it back.

"Send it," she said.

***

The answer came twenty days later.

Not by the ordinary courier. A rider in the king's colours, on a horse that had been ridden hard, who wouldn't give the packet to anyone but Lucan, and waited at the door of the Broken Lance until Lucan came down in his dressing gown and signed for it.

It was sealed with the king's own seal. Not the Council's. Not the Advisor's. The small plain one, the one Severin used for his own letters. Lucan had seen it perhaps three times in fifteen years.

He opened it at the long table. All five of them were there. Ysolde was there. Half the common room was there, pretending not to be.

It was in the king's own hand. Lucan knew it. Big square letters, pressed hard into the paper, like a man who'd learned to write late and with a hammer in his other hand.

He read it. Then he read it again, because the king had once told him that the important letters should always be read twice.

Then he read it aloud.

***

*Lucan.*

*I have read the boy's letter twice. I read yours twice too, though it was shorter, which I suspect is Ysolde's doing. Thank her for me.*

*The tournament of the thirty-five will be held in Valcrest, at the Lists, at midsummer next. All of them. Every posting, with their guides and their guards and as many of my soldiers as it takes to make the road safe. You will host it. The boy Drew will run it. Tell him he has my leave to do whatever he likes with the bracket, as long as it ends with all thirty-five standing on the sand at the same time.*

*The Advisor objects. He says one target. He is not wrong. I have overruled him anyway. I told him: by midsummer they will be harder to kill. I have recently seen something that makes me believe it.*

*Make it worth watching.*

*Severin.*

***

Nobody said anything.

Then Newish said, very quietly, "Your beat changed."

Lucan looked at her. She was sitting at the end of the bench with her knees drawn up, looking at him with her head on one side, the way she did when she was listening to something nobody else could hear.

"Your beat," said Newish. "Everyone's got one. Yours has always been a dance tune. Like a fiddle at a wedding. All the time. Even when you're sad." She tilted her head the other way. "It changed. Just now. When you read *midsummer next*." She thought about it. "It's a march now."

Lucan looked down at the king's letter in his hands.

"A march," he said.

"A good one," said Newish. "The kind you'd walk out onto the sand to."

***

He didn't cry.

That was what everybody talked about afterwards, at the Broken Lance. That Lucan of the Lists, who wept at weddings and bad plays and the price of turnips, had stood at the long table with the king's own letter in his hand, and not cried. He'd folded it, very carefully. Put it in the inside pocket of his good red cloak, the one with the stain down the front from Winter's flower. And looked at the five of them, one after another, the way he'd looked at them at the gate on the first day, when he'd told them the crowd would hate them.

"Midsummer," he said.

His voice was perfectly steady. It was the voice he used on the sand, in the middle of the arena, with ten thousand people waiting for him to tell them what to feel.

"Midsummer," said Chris.

"Ten thousand people," said Lucan. "No. More. Twenty. I'll open the north stands, the ones that haven't been used since the old king's jubilee. I'll have every paper-maker in Valcrest working from the new year. Horns. And masks. And snakes, for Group 3, the white snake, and little paper antlers with leaves on, for the tree boy, and..." He stopped. He looked at Hako. "What does the tree boy's group call him?"

"Maigo," said Hako.

"Paper antlers for Maigo," said Lucan. "Ten thousand of them." He took a breath. "And a cat crown, for the cat princess in the capital. And penguins. Small yellow penguins. Somebody in Wrenhaven has a penguin." He looked at Chris. "Do I need to know anything else? About the others? Anything a crowd would want to wear?"

Chris looked at him for a long moment.

Then he opened the notebook, and turned to a clean page at the back, and started writing. Fast. Thirty-five names. And beside each one, in his small square hand, the thing a crowd would want to wear.

***

That night, very late, Lucan went down to the Lists on his own.

He did that sometimes, in the off-season. He'd go in through the east gate with a lantern and stand in the middle of the empty sand, under the stars, with the tiers going up and up on every side into the dark, and listen to the silence, and imagine it full.

The flower of ice was still in the trough by the gate. It hadn't melted. It wouldn't, Winter said, until she let it. It stood there in the lantern light, perfect, every petal edged with rime.

He walked out to the middle of the sand and stood there.

He imagined it. Midsummer. The stands full to the top, twenty thousand people in paper horns and paper masks and paper antlers with leaves on. The smell of hot sand and roasting nuts. The noise. And on the sand, coming out through the gates one group at a time, thirty-five people from another world who'd been scattered across the kingdom for nearly two years and never once stood together. Coming out into the light. Seeing each other's faces.

He'd stand here, in the middle. He'd spread his cloak. He'd tell the crowd what to feel.

He thought he wouldn't need to.

On the way out, by the gate, he passed the three of them. Chris, and Ina, and Winter. Sitting on the low wall by the trough with the ice flower, in the dark, in their coats, where they must have been for some time. Not talking. Just sitting there, the three of them, looking north.

They looked north a lot, those three. He'd noticed it in the autumn. Ever since the night they'd all sat up late at the Broken Lance after something happened in their windows that he couldn't see, and the next morning Chris had ruled a new column in his notebook and left it empty. The other two looked everywhere. These three looked north. Toward the capital, and past it. Toward the lake country.

Like people waiting for a letter, Lucan thought. From somebody who hasn't written yet.

He didn't ask. He nodded to them, and they nodded back, and he went on out through the gate into the street.

And on the way home, through the empty winter streets of Valcrest, with his lantern swinging and the king's letter against his chest, Lucan of the Lists found that he was humming. A march. A good one. The kind you'd walk out onto the sand to.

He didn't cry once, all the way home.

He cried a great deal when he got there. But that was different.
