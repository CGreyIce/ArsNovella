The lake froze the way it always froze: from the edges in, and slowly, and then all at once.

He'd watched it do it twelve times. He knew the order. The reeds first, at the near end, where the water was shallow and still and the cold came down off the hills and lay on it. A lip of grey ice round the stems, a finger wide, that was gone by noon and back by dusk a little wider. Then the shallows along the shingle, thin and clear as a window, so you could see the stones on the bottom through it and the small fish going under it like birds under glass. Then the bay below the field, where Gigichi's hedge stood in its ring with snow on the thorns. And the black water in the middle going on being black, and open, and steaming in the mornings, for a week, two weeks, as if it had decided it wasn't going to.

And then one night with no wind and no cloud and every star out, it did.

He'd know the night when it came. He always did. He'd wake in the dark, in the cabin, and lie there, and listen, and hear nothing. Not the water on the posts. Not the small sounds the lake made all night against the shingle. Nothing. And he'd know, the way you know somebody in the next room has stopped breathing and started again, slower, asleep.

It hadn't come yet. It was coming. He could smell it in the mornings.

***

He fished the open water while it lasted.

Off the end of the jetty, in the dark before dawn, with the cold so hard now that the line froze stiff between casts and he had to run it through his fingers to soften it. The lake didn't care that it was nearly winter. It went on giving him fish. Perch, mostly, slow and heavy with the cold, and once a bream. He put five in the basket every morning.

*Five every morning till the snow. Two in the ice*. He'd meant *till the ice*, really. At the lake they were mostly the same week. This year they weren't, and he'd decided that while there was open water there were five fish, and nobody had argued.

He'd never said why two. Nobody had asked. Higu had probably worked it out. It was because through a hole in the ice you got two, most mornings, if you were patient, and three if you were lucky, and he'd never in nine years put a fish in that basket he'd had to lie about.

He walked them up the lake road at sunrise, the way he'd walked them up every morning since he came home. The basket on the step of the militia hall, with straw in the bottom. Five fish on the straw. And his hand lifted to the window on the second floor, where there was always, at that hour, a small pale shape behind the glass that might have been a moth, and then went back down.

On the way down, every morning, he passed the list.

Not passed it. He didn't take it up the road with him. But it was in his head. Over the table in the cabin, on the same nail as the drawing, a little to the right, with its corner touching the corner of a blue crayon lake. In Higu's small neat pencil, on a page she'd torn out of her book.

*Fish. A tree. Twelve dinners. Gabe. East. Answer them.*

*Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)*

He'd been looking at the sixth one for a week.

***

*Answer them*.

He knew who. He'd read it in the channel, on the end of the jetty, on the night of the first snow. Azac, the biggest of the thirty-five after Gabe, in capitals. *Group 6 is still asking. For the tenth. We know it's his. We know it's his and the three of them. We're asking anyway. WE ASK*.

He knew what the answer was. He'd known before he finished reading it. That wasn't the problem.

The problem was that he'd never once in two years written anything in the channel. Not a line. Not a word. He'd read every one of them, at night, on this jetty, the way you'd listen to people talking in the next room through a wall. He'd answered Dennis once at the very bottom of the first window, in the place under everything, where nobody's name was. That wasn't the same. That was a thing he'd done to the window. Not a thing he'd said.

He could have started. Higu had told him on this jetty that he was in it. *You're in it. You've always been in it*. He could open his window tonight and find the place at the bottom where his name would go, if he had one there, and write *yes*.

He sat on the end of the jetty in the cold with the line freezing in his fingers and thought about that, for a long time, honestly, the way he'd been told to.

And found he didn't want to. Not because he was afraid of it. He'd looked for the fear, the way he'd look for a stone in his boot, and it wasn't there. He just didn't want to. It wasn't how he said things. It had never been how he said things. Thirty-four years in this world and twenty-six in the other and he'd never once in either of them said the thing that mattered by writing it in a box.

He'd said it with fish.

He looked down at the basket on the boards beside him. Five perch, slow and cold and heavy, on the straw. And thought about a long table in a kitchen in Duskhollow, with a woman with her sleeves rolled up and her face red from the oven, and five people round it who'd come up the river last spring to find him, and found him, and gone home again to hold a hole in the ground, and never asked him for anything since.

He went up to the cabin and got another basket.

***

Haskett's barge was at the quay below the mill.

He'd known it would be. Alea had told him, at supper: the *Patient Widow* in from Kingsferry on the last of the open water, loading wool and the miller's flour for the towns downriver, going down at noon. Last run of the year. The Wren froze above Duskhollow every winter by midwinter, and Haskett, who'd been working the river for forty years, said he could feel it in his knees.

He went down at mid-morning with the second basket on his arm.

The quay was busy. It always was, the day of the last barge. Half the town down there seeing the wool off, and the millers' boys rolling flour sacks down the plank, and Haskett on the deck of the long black barge in a coat like a tent, shouting at everyone, with his cap pushed back. The man stopped at the bottom of the plank and waited, the way he waited for anything, and after a while Haskett saw him.

Haskett stopped shouting.

He took his cap off. He always did. He'd done it on this quay last spring, the first time, when he'd brought ten of them up the river against the wind with a sail that should have been empty, and found out at the top of the lake road who it was they'd come for. He'd done it every time since. The man had asked him not to, once. Haskett had said *I'll do what I like with my own cap*, and done it anyway.

He came down the plank with it in his hand.

"Morning," said the man.

"Morning." Haskett looked at the basket. "That fish?"

"Six."

"In snow?"

"In snow," the man agreed. "They'll keep. You're at Duskhollow by morning."

Haskett looked at the basket a moment longer. A plain willow basket with a lid, borrowed from the egg-woman, with snow packed round six perch and a cloth over the top and the lid tied down with string.

"Duskhollow's on a river," said Haskett, slowly. "It's got fish."

"Not these," said the man.

Haskett looked at him. He couldn't see his face. Nobody on this quay could. But Haskett had been on a river forty years, and a man on a river learns to read weather off things that aren't faces.

"Who for?" he said.

"Mother Annick's. The long table. The five who live there." He paused. "Six, at the table, most nights. Give it to whichever of them's on the quay. One of them will be."

Haskett took the basket. He held it in both his big hands, the way you'd hold something that might wake up.

"There's a paper in it," he said. "Under the cloth."

"There's a paper in it."

Haskett nodded slowly. He didn't ask what the paper said. Haskett never asked what anything said; it was why every town on the Wren gave him their letters. He put his cap back on, with one hand, and looked at the basket, and then at the man, and then over the man's shoulder up the hill at the lake road going down to the water, white, between its walls.

"They asked you something," said Haskett. "Didn't they."

"They asked me something."

"And this is the answer."

"This is the answer."

Haskett looked at the basket of fish in his arms for a long moment. Then he laughed. Short and surprised, the way big men laugh on decks.

"Six fish," he said. "In snow. Down forty miles of river. On the last barge of the year." He shook his head. "You couldn't have just written it?"

The man thought about that.

"No," he said.

***

He watched the barge go from the end of the quay.

Noon, with the sun low and white over the hills and no warmth in it. The *Patient Widow* casting off, and the long black hull sliding out from the quay into the current, heavy with wool, with Haskett at the steering oar and his boys at the poles and a willow basket lashed to the rail by the stern, where it would stay cold. The town on the quay waving, because the town on the quay always waved the last barge off. The barge going down the Wren between the white banks, slowly, past the mill and the willows and the last of the houses, toward the bend.

At the bend Haskett lifted his cap. Not to the town. To the end of the quay.

The man lifted his hand.

Then the barge went round the bend, and there was only the river, black and fast between its banks of snow, going away south with a basket of fish on it.

He stood there a while. The town went back up the hill. The millers' boys went back to the mill. He stood at the end of the quay in the cold and watched the place where the barge had been.

*Answer them*.

It was the first thing on the list he'd ever done.

He read it on the end of the jetty with the line frozen in his fingers.

He'd thought Malcolm would count. Malcolm counted everything. He'd counted things in a classroom in Singapore, sitting in the back row with his notebook, the boy who wrote down every way a group project could go wrong and was right about half of them. He'd count six fish, and then he'd count the people at the table, and then he'd count again.

And then, at some point, tonight or tomorrow, he'd look at the woman who sat at the end of that table with her sword on the back of her chair and her arms folded, and had been a girl of twenty-three in a ditch once, on the south line, with her arm broken, nine years ago. And he'd work it out.

He crossed it off in the morning.

With a pencil, at the table, before the fish. He stood in front of the list on its nail beside the drawing, in the grey light, with Alea asleep in the loft over his head and the kettle starting on the stove behind him, two notes, terribly. He looked at the list for a while. Then he drew a line through *Answer them*. One line. Not hard. You could still read it under the line, the way you could still read *We wait for him* on Higu's page of rules.

He looked at it.

Six things on a page. One of them crossed out. He thought about Severin's father's order on the wall of a dead man's forge in Brakewater, in a crooked hand. *Twelve plough blades. Ashdown farm. By the thaw*. And a man of thirty-nine coming in the morning after the burial and making the first one.

It didn't feel like anything. It didn't move his bar; he'd have felt the quiet come if it had. It felt like a man putting a pencil down on a table in the morning and going out to fish.

That was the point, he thought. That was what the king had meant. It wasn't supposed to feel like anything.

He went out to fish.

***

The lake froze on the twelfth night after the snow.

He woke in the dark and knew. He lay in the dark in the cabin, and listened, and heard nothing. Not the water on the posts. Not the reeds. Not the small lap of the lake against the shingle that had been under every night of the last twelve years of his life, so steady he only heard it when it stopped.

It had stopped.

He got up and went out.

The cold was the kind that hurt your teeth. No wind. Every star out, the whole sky of them, white and hard and close, with the frost coming down out of it so fine you could see it in the lamplight like dust. And the lake, from the shingle to the far shore, flat and grey and still, under the stars, with nothing moving on it at all. Not black any more. Not open. Grey, all the way across, the colour of a blade that's been left out in the rain, with the stars lying on it dim and blurred, as if somebody had breathed on them.

He walked out to the end of the jetty and looked down.

Ice. Right up to the posts. Clear, at the edge, so he could see the black water under it, very still, and a bubble of air caught in it like a coin. He put one foot down off the end of the jetty onto it and leaned, slowly, and listened. It made a sound under his boot like a long low note on a string, going away under the whole lake in every direction at once. And held.

Not enough. Not yet. Three more nights, for the ice to be thick enough to walk on properly. Five for a girl with a black sword on her back to drill on, which she would, whatever he said.

He stood on the end of the jetty with one foot on the ice and listened to the lake sing under it, the long low note going out and out and coming back off the far shore. He'd heard it twelve times. He'd never once got tired of it.

*Two in the ice*.

***

He cut the hole on the fourth morning.

The way he always did. At the end of the jetty, a pace out from the last post, where the water was deepest and the fish lay in the winter, slow and heavy and half asleep in the dark under the ice. An auger he'd made himself the first winter, a long iron screw with a cross-handle, that took a quarter of an hour to go through and made a sound like a man grinding his teeth. Alea came down to watch, in her blanket, with her ears flat against the cold. She'd watched it every year since she was five. She'd never once said why.

He broke through. The black water came up into the hole, very slowly, and lay there, steaming.

"Two," said Alea.

"Two," he agreed.

"For the hall."

"For the hall." He sat down on the end of the jetty with a short line and a hook. "Till the thaw."

Alea stood on the ice beside the hole in her blanket and looked down into the black water for a while.

"Why two?" she said.

He looked up at her. Eighteen years old. She'd never asked. He'd thought she never would.

"Because two's what there is," he said. "Most mornings. Through a hole in the ice. If you're patient." He let the line down into the black. "I've never put a fish in that basket that I had to pretend about."

Alea thought about that.

"Mum would have said three," she said. "And said she caught the third one. And bought it off the egg-woman."

He laughed. Out loud, on the end of the jetty, in the cold, so that it went out over the ice and came back off the far shore.

"She would," he said. "She did. Every winter. She said the egg-woman's were better."

"I know," said Alea. "You told me. When I was six." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I'm going to practise on the ice."

"Not yet. It's not thick enough."

"Tomorrow."

"Day after."

"Tomorrow," said Alea, and went back up the shingle before he could argue, the way her mother had gone up the shingle before he could argue, a thousand times, in exactly that blanket.

***

She practised on the ice the day after tomorrow.

Not because he'd said. Because she'd gone out on it the next morning at first light, tested it, felt it hum under her boots the way he'd taught her, and come back in and said *you were right* in a voice of deep resentment, and waited.

He watched her from the window. He always did. He'd watched her practise on the shingle every morning of her life since she could hold a stick, from this window, with a cup of tea, and pretended he wasn't watching. She knew. She'd always known. It was one of the things they didn't talk about.

She stood on the grey ice off the end of the jetty with Astra in her hands, in the snow-light, with her breath going up, and did the forms. The cast. The draw. The third one, the one that sang. Saying something under her breath before each one. He couldn't hear it from the window. He didn't need to.

*Again*.

At the end of the third form, she stopped. As she always did. The long low note going up off the flat of the blade and hanging in the cold over the ice.

And her feet, on the ice, at the end of the third form, pointing at the end of the jetty.

He'd never noticed. Twelve years of watching her from this window and he'd never once noticed where her feet went at the end of the third form. A guard on the king's staff had to tell her, on a shingle in the snow, a fortnight ago. And now he couldn't stop seeing it.

She looked down at them, on the ice. Then she looked up at the end of the jetty. At the post, and the hole beside it in the ice, and the short line going down into the black, and the stool he'd left there.

Then she looked at the window.

He didn't move. He didn't pretend not to be watching. There didn't seem much point any more.

Alea lifted Astra in one hand. Not a salute. Just lifted it, a little, the way you'd lift a hand to somebody across a room.

Then she turned round, set her feet, and did it again.

The letter came on the twentieth day after the snow.

By rider. Not Tamsin; a young one, a boy of nineteen or so on a grey, very red in the face from the cold and very solemn about it, who'd been told *into his hand and no other* and had plainly been told it more than once. He found the man on the end of the jetty with the short line in the hole in the ice and stood on the shingle at the other end of the jetty for a long time, working up to it, while the man fished.

In the end the man went to him.

The letter was very short. He'd known it would be. Halvard had never in his life written a long letter he'd sent; all the long ones had stayed in a drawer.

*Ars.*

*How are the hens.*

*The Summoners' Hall has petitioned the Council for the leaves. They would like to burn them, on the grounds that a thing which cannot be read was not meant to be. Severin answered in two words. I will not write them down. I am told I smiled in council. The Lord Treasurer has asked my secretary whether I am unwell.*

*The drawer is still open. It has nothing in it. I find I look at it every night, and then go to bed.*

*H.*

He read it twice, on the shingle, with the boy waiting.

Then he read the second paragraph again. *A thing which cannot be read was not meant to be*. He knew that sentence. He'd read it in the channel, in Cursiffe's English, at the end of a long night in a vault three hundred years deep: a dead king's letter, written in a hand that slid off everyone but Oswin. *My summoners wished them burned. They said that a thing which cannot be read is a thing which was not meant to be*.

Three hundred years. And somebody in Solmere had kept the sentence, word for word, the way you'd keep a knife.

He filed it. The way he filed things. He didn't like how many things there were in the file now.

Then he went up to the cabin, with the boy following at a respectful distance, and sat down at the table under the list and the drawing, and got out the good paper, the thick cream kind the king had left him a year ago *for when you've got something to say*.

He wrote back.

*Halvard.*

*They hate me.*

*The big red one sat on the end of the jetty with me for an hour on the day you left. I'm not telling anybody. Don't tell Severin.*

*Tell Severin two words was one too many. One would have done.*

*Watch the Hall. Somebody there kept that sentence three hundred years. People don't keep sentences that long for nothing.*

*Leave the drawer open.*

*A.*

He read it over. Then, after a moment, under the *A*, smaller, he added one more line, because the boy was standing in the doorway with snow on his boots looking at the hens through the window with an expression of deep alarm, and it seemed a shame not to.

*Your rider is frightened of my hens. Good lad.*

He folded it, and sealed it with a drop of wax off the candle and his thumb, and gave it to the boy, and gave the boy a cup of tea, which the boy drank standing up, very fast, without taking his eyes off the window. And then the boy went back up the lake road on his grey, past the hen-yard, at a pace that was not quite a canter.

The big red hen watched him all the way to the gate. With deep and patient hatred.

Group 1 came down that evening.

They came down most evenings now. Not all five every night; it depended on Maren, and the militia hall, and the weather, and whether Xola had been crying about something, which she mostly had, and whether Gigichi had decided she was speaking to him, which she mostly had. They came down the lake road in the dark with lanterns and sat round his table and ate whatever he'd made, which was mostly terrible, and talked. In English. All evening. About everything.

Gigichi went to the window first. She always did.

The pot was there. On the sill, under the window, where it had been since the day they came home: a cracked clay pot from the fort kitchens at Cinderhold, with earth in it, and a cutting of ash in the earth about as long as a forearm, with no leaves on it. Bare. Grey. A stick in a pot. Beside it, on the floor, a child's bucket, painted blue.

Gigichi put one finger on the bare stem, very lightly, the way you'd touch the forehead of somebody sleeping.

"She's asleep," she said.

"I know."

"Don't water her."

"I haven't."

"You were going to." Gigichi took her finger away. "I can feel it. You've been looking at her every morning with the bucket and thinking *she looks dry*." She glared at him. "She's not dry. She's asleep. Trees sleep in the winter. You don't wake somebody up by pouring water on them."

"Alea did," said Thea, from the table. "Once. When she was eight. Ice told us."

"*Dad*," said Alea.

"She's asleep," said Gigichi, to the room, firmly. "She'll wake up in the spring. When the ground's soft. Then we put her in, by the water." She looked at the man. "Then. Not before. I'll tell you when."

"You'll tell me when," he agreed.

Gigichi looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked past him at the wall. At the drawing on its nail, and the list beside it, with its corner touching. She read it. He watched her read it, down to the bottom. *Ask again in the spring. (Gigichi.)*

She went red. All at once, all the way to her ears.

"Higu," she said.

"Mm?" said Higu, from the window seat, with her book.

"You *wrote that down*."

"I write everything down," said Higu calmly. "That's what I'm for."

"You put it on his *wall*."

"He put it on his wall," said Higu. "I just tore it out."

Gigichi looked at the list. Then at him. Then at the list again, at the line through *Answer them*.

"You answered them," she said.

"I answered them."

"With *fish*," said Gigichi. It wasn't a question. Everybody at the table had read the channel last night. "You sent fish. Down a *river*." She shook her head. "You're the most ridiculous man in two worlds."

"I know," he said.

Gigichi looked at him for a long moment. Then, quite suddenly, she smiled. Not the smug one. The other one, that she didn't use very often, and he'd only seen perhaps three times, and never at him.

"It was a good answer," she said. "I'd have liked fish."

And went and sat down at the table, very fast, next to Xola, and asked loudly what was for supper, and when he told her said *bones*, before she'd even seen it.

He went out on the ice after they'd gone.

The lake was frozen all the way across now. Thick, grey, singing faintly under his boots when he walked, the long low notes going out into the dark. He walked out past the end of the jetty, past the hole with the lid of ice already forming on it, a long way out. Further than he usually went. Until the cabin was a small gold window behind him and the hall was a smaller one at the top of the hill and the lake road was a white line between them, and there was nothing round him but grey ice and white stars and the far shore, dark, very far.

He stopped there. In the middle of the lake.

It was very quiet. The quiet of a lake under ice, which was the deepest quiet he knew. The whole of the water under him asleep. Fish, and weed, and the cold black stones on the bottom under the place where Diwa had stood once, on the end of the jetty, when half the planks were gone, and said *here*. All of it asleep, under a lid of ice, waiting for the spring.

He opened his window.

Out of habit. He didn't expect anything. He'd have felt it.

Sixty-two. It hadn't moved. It didn't need to.

He looked at it for a while in the starlight, in the middle of the frozen lake, and then closed it, and stood there with his hands in his pockets, listening to the ice.

Six fish. Down forty miles of river. On the last barge of the year.

He thought about a kitchen in Duskhollow. A long table. A woman with her sleeves rolled up saying *if he doesn't stop looking at them I'm going to cook them round him*. A boy from the back row of a classroom in Singapore, counting fish, three times.

And a woman at the end of the table, with her arms folded and her sword on the back of her chair, who'd been in a ditch once, on the south line, on the second night. Who didn't know why the line had held so easily. Who didn't know she'd been the point.

*It isn't mine to tell*, he'd said to Alea, in the autumn, at the ditch.

No. But a fish wasn't telling. A fish was just a fish. You put six on the straw, and people counted, and some of them would work out that one of them was for somebody who hadn't asked.

He turned round and walked back across the ice toward the small gold window, slowly, with the lake singing under him the whole way. Not hurrying. The way he walked everywhere.

The big red hen was on the woodpile when he got there. Waiting.

He went in the back way.
