Gigichi could feel the pumpkins dying from the top of the lake road, and it was making her furious.

She'd felt it the first morning. Standing on the step of the militia hall with a jack pike in a basket and her face wet, which she was not going to discuss with anyone, ever. Under the bones and the salt and the being-very-fine-actually, she'd felt it come up the hill at her through the soles of her boots the way plants always came at her: a field, at the bottom of the lake road, by the water. Late pumpkins. Hundreds of them. On long vines, gone grey-green and limp, with the leaves black at the edges where the frost had been. Half the field already gone. The other half holding on. Tired. The way a plant gets tired, all the way down, in the root, when it's been waiting too long for somebody to come and pick it.

*He'll complain*, Odette had said, about the frost having half of them.

Gigichi didn't care if he complained. She cared about the other half.

She went down the lake road after breakfast with her sleeves rolled up. She didn't tell anyone where she was going. Thea saw her go and didn't ask. Higu saw her go and wrote something in her book, probably *Gigichi went down the lake road angry*, because Higu wrote everything down and was usually right.

***

He was in the field.

Of course he was. On his knees, between the rows, in the frost-white mud, with his sleeves rolled up too and a knife in his hand. Not the good knife; Alea had that, on her hip, where she'd been wearing it since they came home and would apparently be wearing it until she died. A plain field knife. Cutting the stalks of the pumpkins that were ready, one at a time, and turning each one over to look at the underside before he set it on the barrow.

The hens were sitting on the field wall watching him with deep and patient hatred.

He looked up when she came through the gate. She felt him look. She couldn't see his face. Nobody could. She'd shoved a lamp at him once, in the snow, at a door, so she wouldn't have to try.

"Morning, Gigi," he said.

*Gigi*. The name she'd taken in the first week, in the castle, because she and PenguChi both kept answering to *Chi*, and PenguChi had got there first. Everybody used it now. Thea, Higu, Tiger in the channel. Only Higu still said *Chi*, and only when it was the two of them. But he'd said it out loud for the first time at a cabin door in the snow, in English, the way you'd say a name you'd read a thousand times and never once heard yourself say, and she'd shoved a lamp at him.

He said it now as if he'd been saying it for years. She hadn't decided yet whether she minded.

"They're dying," said Gigichi.

He looked at the field.

"Half of them," he agreed. "The frost had them. On the road. Odette says."

"Odette's right. Half of them." She came down between the rows and stood over him with her arms folded. "The other half aren't dying. They're *tired*. There's a difference."

He looked up at her.

"They've been waiting," said Gigichi. "Since the end of summer. For somebody to come and pick them. The mayor's been feeding the hens and watering the hens and doing nothing whatsoever about the pumpkins because he doesn't know anything about pumpkins and he was too frightened to touch them in case he did it wrong and you came home and minded." She glared at the field. "So they waited. And they're tired. And there's a hard frost coming, and if it comes before you've got them in, they won't be tired, they'll be dead."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

"How do you know a hard frost's coming?"

"Higu," said Gigichi. "The moon's three nights from full and the sky's been clear for a week and Higu says the moths came back cold last night." She unfolded her arms. "And I can feel it. In them. They can feel it coming."

He looked at the field again. Then at her.

"Then we'd better get them in," he said.

***

It wasn't a two-person job.

Gigichi knew that by noon. There were hundreds of them. Late pumpkins, the big orange kind that grew slow and went on growing into the autumn and had to be picked at exactly the right moment, cut with a hand of stalk left on, turned, checked, lifted, barrowed up the slope to the cabin and stacked in the lean-to. One at a time. On their knees in the frozen mud between the rows.

She'd never picked a pumpkin in her life. On Earth she'd bought them in a shop, once a year, for a stream. She'd put a face on one, badly, and Ice had been in the chat, and he'd typed *that pumpkin has seen things*, and she'd timed him out for ten minutes and he'd come back and typed *it's still seen things*.

She could feel them, though. That was the thing. She'd always been able to feel plants. That was her magic, the first week, the only thing that had come: she put her hand on a thing that grew and it told her, in a way that wasn't words, how it was. A hedge, thirsty. A tree, tired of the wind. A pumpkin, ready.

She knelt in the mud beside him and put her hand on the first pumpkin of her life and felt it say *now*.

"This one," said Gigichi.

He cut it. He didn't ask how she knew. He never asked.

"This one. Not that one. That one's got another week if the frost holds off, which it won't, but leave it till last. This one. This one." She went down the row on her knees, hand to hand, pumpkin to pumpkin. "Now. Now. Not yet. Now."

He followed her with the knife.

They didn't talk much. They didn't need to. It was like the calls with Higu, on Earth, at two in the morning, where nobody said anything for an hour and nobody had to. Her hand, and his knife, and the cold, and the hens on the wall.

At some point Alea came down from the cabin with a second barrow and started taking them up. At some point Higu came down the lake road with her sleeves rolled up too and her counting book left at the top for once, and knelt at the end of the next row, and said *I can't feel them like you can, just tell me which*. At some point Thea came, and Xola, crying a bit at the pumpkins for no reason she could explain, and PenguChi with Waddles, who ate a worm.

And at some point, about mid-afternoon, Gigichi looked up from the mud and the field was full of people.

***

She didn't know where they'd come from.

Wrenhaven. All of it, nearly. Down the lake road in ones and twos all afternoon without anyone asking them. The egg-woman, with her apron. The miller's boys, who'd carried stones down this road once in the autumn. Edric the baker, with flour still on his arms. The Hollin brothers. Children, a great many of them, running about between the rows getting under everyone's feet and being told off and doing it again. Maren and Bram from the militia hall, Bram crying a little, quietly, into a pumpkin, the way Bram cried at most things. The mayor, in his chain, with his sleeves rolled up, kneeling in the mud next to Old Mabry from the council, who was explaining to him at length the correct way to cut a pumpkin stalk and getting it wrong.

And Odette, at the field gate, in her good coat, with a basket of bread on her arm and her face perfectly composed, saying to everyone who went past her: "*Half*. I *said*. Didn't I say? The frost had *half*."

Nobody had asked them. Gigichi was sure of that. She'd been in the field all day. Nobody had gone up the lake road and asked anybody for anything.

They'd just come.

She knelt in the mud between the rows with her hand on a pumpkin and watched the town of Wrenhaven pick a fisherman's late pumpkins in the cold, all of it, laughing and arguing and getting in each other's way. And thought about a year and a half of baskets on a step. Two fish, and then five. Every morning. And a town that had walked round an empty basket for sixty days without being told.

*They're not doing it for the pumpkins*, she thought.

She looked down the row. He was kneeling a few pumpkins off, with his knife, very still, looking at the field full of people. At all of them. The way he'd looked at a wall full of soldiers with their hands on their chests, Alea said. Like being looked at by weather.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Gigichi could see his shoulders.

"Next one," she said, loudly, to nobody, because somebody had to say something. "*Now*. This one. Come on."

He came on.

***

By dusk they'd got two hundred in.

Not all of them. Gigichi could feel the rest, still on the vines, in the far half of the field down by the water: the ones that had needed another week. Sixty or seventy. Tired. Holding on. And the light going, and the sky over the lake very clear and very pale and very cold, and her breath going up white.

The town went home. The way a town goes home: slowly, in ones and twos, with the children carried. Odette made everyone take bread. The mayor stood at the field gate and said *thank you* to every one of them, which they all pretended not to need.

And the cold came down.

She felt it come. Not the ordinary cold of a clear autumn evening. The other kind. The kind that came down off the hills on the far side of the lake all at once, the way a hand comes down on a table, flat and hard, and lay on everything. She felt the pumpkins feel it. The sixty or seventy in the far half of the field, still on the vine. Going still. The way a thing goes still when it knows.

"It's coming," she said.

He was beside her at the field gate. He'd been beside her all day, she realised, a pumpkin's length off, without her noticing. Alea was at the cabin door with the lamp. Higu and the others were at the top of the slope, by the lean-to, stacking.

"Tonight," he said.

"Tonight. Hard. All the way down." She looked at the far half of the field. "They won't last till morning."

"Then we lose them," he said.

He said it quite calmly. The way you'd say it about a fish that had got off the hook. He'd lost things before. A great many things. Sixty pumpkins wasn't anything.

"No," said Gigichi.

She didn't know she was going to say it. She said it the way she'd said things all her life: before she'd thought about it, too loudly, at the wrong moment. Blunt and bratty and furious.

"No," she said again. "Not these. Not *tonight*. They've waited since the summer. They waited the whole time you were gone. Nobody came. Nobody picked them. They just held on." Her voice had gone wrong. She didn't care. "And you came home and the whole town came down and picked the other two hundred and they're *still* waiting, they're right *there*, and it's not *fair* that the frost gets them on the last night just because they needed one more week."

He looked at her.

She couldn't see his face. She didn't need to.

"No," he said. Very quietly. "It isn't fair."

***

She went down the field in the dark.

On her own. She didn't ask him to come and he didn't. She went down between the rows, past the cut stalks of two hundred pumpkins, to the far half of the field by the water, where the last sixty or seventy were still on the vine in the dark, with the frost already starting on the leaves. White at the edges. Feathery. Creeping in.

She stood in the middle of them.

She'd done this before. Not this. Something like it. In the first year, at the militia hall, at the hawthorn hedge along the back wall of the yard, every night all summer, asking it to grow thicker. And one night, in the first spring, with Chris's list of crossing lines in her head, *they're all asking something to do what it already wants to*, she'd written two words into the hedge with her finger. And it had drawn together. For three heartbeats. And let go.

*Not here*.

She'd written it again that night. Five heartbeats. And again, every night she was home, for a year and a half, at the hawthorn, at the militia hall, along the back wall. Not here. Not here. Not here. Until the hedge was so thick you couldn't see through it, and her bar had crept, and crept, and stuck.

Ninety-eight. Since the summer. Since before the Lists.

She'd thought about why. She thought about everything, at night, in the dark; it was what she did instead of sleeping. She'd decided it was because *not here* was the wrong thing. It was a wall. It was a thing you said to keep something out. And she'd spent her whole life keeping things out, on Earth and here, with her arms folded and a lamp shoved at people's faces and *I'm fine, I'm FINE*, and it had never once made anything grow.

She stood in the middle of sixty tired pumpkins in the dark with the frost coming down and her breath going up white and her hands shaking with cold, and thought about that.

And then she thought: *no. It's not a wall*.

*It's a hedge*.

***

A wall keeps things out. A hedge keeps things *in*.

That was the difference. She'd never thought of it like that. She'd been asking the hawthorn for a year and a half to keep something out, the dark, the cold, whatever was coming up the lake road at night. And it had tried. And it couldn't, quite, because that wasn't what hedges were for. Hedges were for fields. For sheep. For keeping the wind off a garden. For holding the warm in, round something that was growing, so it could finish.

She knelt down in the frozen mud in the middle of the pumpkins. She put both her hands flat on the ground.

And felt them.

Not just the pumpkins. Everything. The vines going out under the leaves. The roots going down into the cold earth. The grass at the edges of the field, and the reeds by the water, and the old hawthorn stumps along the bottom of the field wall, cut back years ago by somebody who'd wanted to see the lake. Dead, she'd thought. Not dead. Sleeping. With roots that went down and down and out under the whole field like fingers.

They wanted to grow. She felt it. They'd always wanted to grow. Somebody had cut them back and they'd waited.

*They're all asking something to do what it already wants to*.

She felt for the gap. The way she'd felt for it every night for a year and a half at the hawthorn: the small place under everything, under the bark, under the cold earth, where you could write. She found it. Under her right hand, in the ground, among the roots.

She didn't write *not here*.

She wrote *stay*.

No. That was Maigo's. That wasn't hers.

She stopped, with her hand on the gap, in the dark, in the frost, with sixty pumpkins going still round her. And thought about what was hers. The thing she said. The thing she'd said to Tiger in the channel, late, in the first spring, when nobody else had. The thing a man in a chat had said to her, once, a long time ago, on a night when she wasn't okay, and she'd never answered.

She wrote it. Into the gap. In English. Two words, lowercase, the way you'd type them.

*you okay?*

***

The ground answered.

She felt it before she saw it. Under her hands, in the earth, everything that had roots, all at once, *turning*. Toward her. Toward the question. The way a field turns its face to the sun. The hawthorn stumps along the bottom wall, and the grass, and the old roots under the whole field that somebody had cut back years ago to see the lake. All of them. Answering.

And coming up.

Not fast. Not like a wall. Like something that had been waiting to be asked, and had been asked, and was saying *no, actually, I'm not okay, I'm cold, and I'm tired, and I've been cut back for years, and nobody's ever asked*. Coming up out of the frozen ground round the edges of the far half of the field. Thorn. Hawthorn and blackthorn and briar, out of the old stumps and the old roots, thick and dark and tangled, a hand high, a knee high, a waist high, going round the sixty tired pumpkins in a ring the way your arms go round somebody who's said they're not okay.

And holding.

Gigichi knelt in the middle of it with her hands on the earth and felt it close round her. A hedge. Thick. Taller than she was now, with the frost on the outside of it, white, feathery, creeping up the thorns. And stopping. At the thorns. Not coming in. The cold lying on the outside of the hedge like a hand on a door, and the inside of the ring still, and dark, and not warm, but *not that*. Not the hard white cold that killed things. The ordinary cold of a field on an autumn night, with the wind kept off, and the ground's own warmth coming up out of it and not going anywhere because something was holding it in.

The pumpkins round her didn't go still.

She felt them. Sixty-odd. Tired. Holding on. And then, very slowly, not holding on. Just *being*. The way you stop holding on when somebody's got you.

***

Her window opened.

In the dark, inside the hedge, on her knees in the mud. Plain and white, in front of her face, lighting the thorns.

She looked at it.

She looked at it for a long time. *Briar*. A plant that grew where it wasn't wanted and wouldn't go away and had thorns on it so you'd think twice before you tried to get past. A plant you planted round things you didn't want to lose.

She laughed.

It came out of her short and cracked and furious. Kneeling in a pumpkin field in the dark inside a hedge she'd grown out of a question, with her window open and her hands in the frozen mud and her face doing something she was not going to discuss with anyone. *Of course* it was briar. Of course. It had been *not here* for a year and a half and it had never once been about keeping anything out.

It had been about holding on.

"Twenty-nine," she said, out loud, to the pumpkins. "I'm twenty-*nine*. Out of thirty-five. Do you know how long I've been at ninety-eight? Three *months*. Since before the *Lists*." She wiped her face with the back of a muddy hand and made it worse. "And it was a *question*. The whole time. I just had to *ask*."

The pumpkins didn't say anything. Pumpkins didn't. But she felt them. Through the ground. Not tired. Not dying. Just there, in the dark, inside the thorns, being held.

***

He was at the gap.

She didn't see him come. She never did. The hedge had grown up round the far half of the field in a ring, but at the top, where the rows ran up toward the cabin, there was a gap. A space between two thick tangles of thorn about the width of a man. She'd left it without meaning to. You always left a gap in a hedge. That was the other thing hedges were for. So people could come in.

He was standing in it. With a lamp. Not coming through.

She looked up at him from the mud.

He looked at the hedge. All the way round. At the thorns, dark, thick, taller than a man, with the frost white on the outside and stopping. At the pumpkins inside. At her.

"Gigi," he said.

"*Don't*," said Gigichi. "Don't you dare. I've got mud on my face and I'm crying and I'm not discussing it."

He didn't say anything for a moment.

Then he said it. In English. The way he'd said it at a cabin door in the snow once, and on a call a long time ago, on a night when she wasn't okay, and she'd never answered. The way she'd just written it into the ground.

"You okay?"

***

She'd answered him once.

At supper, in the snow, the first night, at a long table in the cabin, with Xola crying and a stew that was supposed to be assam pedas and wasn't. He'd said it in English and she'd given him the mess. *I'm not okay. Not all the way. I'm mostly okay. Sometimes I'm fine. Right now I'm sitting at your table eating your terrible stew and you're alive and you asked*. All of it, all at once, too loud, the way she said everything. And he'd said *that's the best answer anyone's ever given me*.

She'd thought about it ever since. Whether it was true. Whether he'd meant it, or been kind.

She looked up at him now from the mud inside a ring of thorn, with her window still open in front of her face. *Briar*. And her hands in the cold earth. And sixty pumpkins round her that had been asked and had answered and were being held.

And found that the mess wasn't there.

She looked for it. It had always been there. Under everything, all her life, in two worlds: the *mostly*, the *some days*, the *I don't know what to do with that*. The thing she'd been keeping out with her arms folded. She looked for it, kneeling in the field, the way you'd feel for a stone in your pocket that you've carried so long you forget it's there.

It wasn't there.

Not gone forever. She knew that. It'd be back; it always came back. But not tonight. Not here. Tonight there was a hedge, and a window, and a man in a gap with a lamp, and sixty pumpkins that weren't going to die, and a whole town that had come down a road without being asked.

"Yes," said Gigichi.

That was all. One word. Not the mess. Not *mostly*. Just yes.

He was quiet for a long time, in the gap, with the lamp.

"That's the second-best answer anyone's ever given me," he said.

She glared at him.

"What's the *first*?"

"Yours," he said. "At supper. The mess." He looked at the hedge. "This one's better for you. That one was better for me."

She didn't know what to say to that. She knelt in the mud and couldn't say anything at all, which had happened to her perhaps twice in her life, both times because of him.

Then he came through the gap.

Not far. He set the lamp down on the cold ground just inside the hedge, between two rows. And held out his hand. To pull her up out of the mud.

She looked at it.

Then she took it. And he pulled her up, out of the mud, onto her feet, inside a hedge of briar in the dark, with her knees soaked and her face filthy and her window still open. And let go.

"Come up to the cabin," he said. "Alea's got the kettle on. The old one. The terrible one." He picked up the lamp. "Higu wants to write it down."

"Higu wants to write *everything* down."

"I know," he said. "That's why she's always right."

***

She went up.

Out through the gap in the hedge, with the lamp, up between the cut rows toward the cabin and the light in the window and a red curtain with a gold fringe hanging crookedly under the eaves. And at the top of the slope, by the lean-to with two hundred pumpkins stacked in it, the four of them were standing. Thea and Higu and Xola and PenguChi, in a row, with Alea in the cabin door behind them with the lamp. Waiting.

They'd seen it. Of course they had. You could see the hedge from the top of the slope: a dark ring round the bottom of the field, thick, taller than a man, with the frost white on the outside and stopping, very clean, at the thorns.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Xola burst into tears and threw her arms round her, mud and all. And Gigichi let her. And PenguChi said *oh, well done* in her calm voice and put her hand on Gigichi's back. And Higu had her book open already, writing. And Thea stood a little apart, with her hands in her sleeves, and looked at Gigichi over Xola's shoulder.

Thea. Who'd been her best friend once, a long time ago, on Earth, before they drifted. Who she'd said *I wasn't a good friend* to, on the stairs, in the first spring. Who'd said *I'm still a bit angry. A lot less*.

"Briar," said Thea. Gigichi's window was still open in front of her face; Thea had read it upside down, the way she read everything. "It's a good word."

"It's a stupid word."

"It's a good word," said Thea. "You hold things in. You always did." She was quiet. "I used to think you were keeping me out. On Earth. At the end." She looked at the hedge at the bottom of the field. "You weren't. Were you."

Gigichi looked at her for a long time over Xola's shoulder.

"No," she said.

Thea nodded. Slowly. Then she took her hands out of her sleeves, and came over, and put one hand on Gigichi's muddy arm. Just for a moment. And took it away.

"*A lot* less," said Thea. "Now."

***

In the morning, the whole town came down to look.

Of course it did. The egg-woman's boy saw it first, at dawn, going down the lake road with eggs, and ran back up, and by the time the sun was over the hill there were fifty people at the field wall and more coming. Standing in the white frost, every blade of grass on the slope gone stiff and glittering, every stalk of every cut pumpkin white, every leaf on every vine in the near half of the field black.

And at the bottom of the field, by the water, a hedge. Thick and dark and tangled, briar and hawthorn and blackthorn, in a ring, taller than a man. White on the outside. Frost all over it, feathery, glittering in the sun.

And inside, green.

The leaves on sixty pumpkin vines, green. The pumpkins under them, orange. Not frosted. Not black. Waiting, still, a little tired, for their last week. With the morning sun coming over the top of the hedge onto them, and a thin warm mist going up off the ground inside the ring where the earth's own heat had been held all night and was letting go.

Odette stood at the field gate in her good coat and looked at it for a long time with her face perfectly composed.

"Well," said Odette. "*Half*." She sniffed. "I said half. I didn't say which half."

***

Garrow came down at noon.

Gigichi saw him from the field, where she was standing inside the hedge with her hands on a pumpkin that wanted two more days. A big soot-faced man in a leather apron, coming down the lake road with his hands in his pockets, the way he walked everywhere, as if he were sorry for being in the way. The smith from the forge in the square. Who'd driven a black stone east on a brewer's dray all the way to a fort on the edge of the red, because he'd wanted to see where it went, and wintered there, and taught a short square man to strike, and come home in the spring with nothing in his belt.

He stopped at the field gate.

The man was at the cabin door. Gigichi watched him see Garrow. She watched him go into the cabin, and come out again with something in his hand, and walk down the slope to the gate.

She couldn't hear them. She didn't need to. She saw the man hold it out.

A hammer. A small old cross-peen, with the handle dark and smooth, and a burn on the haft near the head.

Garrow looked at it. He didn't take it at once.

"He finished it?" said Garrow. Loud enough. A smith's voice, used to being heard over a forge. "The short one? The—" He made a long curved shape in the air with his big hand. "The *scythe*? For the snake lad?"

"He finished it," said the man. "The king held it up to the light. He said to tell you his father would have liked the edge."

Garrow went red to the roots of his hair.

"He said bring it back to Wrenhaven when he was done," said the man. "He's done. He said I'm going home past your forge." He held it out a little further. "He says it's a good hammer. He said to tell you anyway."

Garrow took it.

He held it the way a smith holds a hammer. Loose, at the end of the handle, with the weight hanging. He looked at the face of it. At the nick in the face, the shape of a fingernail, that left a little crescent on everything it struck.

"Tell him I know," said Garrow.

"He said you'd say that."

Garrow looked up at him. Then he laughed. Short and surprised, the way big shy men laugh. And put the hammer in his belt. Where it belonged.

"A good hammer ought to be working," said Garrow. "Not riding about in somebody's coat." He patted it. "The king said that. To me."

"His father said it to him," said the man. "In Brakewater. When he was ten, and wanted to hang it on the wall."

Garrow looked at the hammer in his belt for a long moment.

"Huh," he said. "So it's been going round."

"It's been going round," the man agreed.

***

Gigichi went up to the top of the field at dusk, when the town had gone, and stood at the gap in the hedge, and looked in.

Sixty-odd pumpkins, inside the briar, in the last of the light. Green leaves. Orange skins. Two more days for most of them. A week for a few. She'd come down every morning and put her hand on them and tell him which, and he'd cut them, and they'd go up to the lean-to with the others.

And then the hedge could go.

She thought about that. It didn't have to. She could leave it. It would stand all winter, a ring of briar round the bottom of a fisherman's field by a lake, with the snow on it. People would wonder about it for years. *That's where the girl from the militia hall grew a hedge in one night, the autumn he came back*. It would keep the wind off the field in the spring, when he planted again.

She put her hand on the nearest thorn. Carefully.

*Stay*, she thought. Not wrote. Thought. *If you want to*.

She felt it think about it. Briar thought slowly. Then she felt it decide.

It wanted to.

She took her hand away and turned round. And he was there. Of course he was. Standing a little way up the slope, at the edge of the cut rows, with his hands in his pockets, looking at the hedge.

"It's staying," said Gigichi.

"Good," he said.

"Round your field. All winter. People are going to ask."

"Let them," he said.

She looked at him. A plain man on a slope in the dusk, with a face she couldn't see, who'd said *let them* to an Advisor once about thirty-five people from another world, on a scrap of market paper, and meant it.

"You okay?" said Gigichi.

He went very still.

She'd never asked him. Not once. Not on Earth, where he'd been a name in her chat that asked everybody and that nobody ever asked. Not here. Not at the door in the snow, or at supper, or in a year and a half of baskets on a step. She'd asked Tiger, in the channel, in the first spring. She'd asked Thea, at a door, with a lamp. She'd never once asked him.

He didn't say anything for a long time. The lake went dark behind him. The frost came down. The hedge stood at the bottom of the field with sixty pumpkins inside it, holding on.

"Yes," he said.

She looked at him.

"That's a terrible answer," said Gigichi.

"I know."

"You're supposed to give me the mess."

"I know," he said. And then, after a long time, very quietly, in English: "Ask me again in the spring."

Gigichi looked at him a moment longer.

Then she nodded. Once. And went up the slope past him to the cabin, where the kettle was singing on its two terrible notes and the curtain was crooked and her friends were waiting. And didn't look back.

But she wrote it down, that night, in the militia hall, on a scrap of paper she kept under her pillow with the gold pocket watch that was the only thing she'd brought from Earth. In the dark. In English.

*Asked him. He said yes. Ask again in the spring.*

She'd ask. She always kept her word. It was the one thing about her nobody had ever had to wonder about.
