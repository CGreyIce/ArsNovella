Hyvred's letter came on a grey morning in the ninth week, at the bottom of the courier's satchel, under a bundle of quartermaster's orders and a letter from Rhea's mother that Rhea pretended not to want.

Yan read it standing in the gate, with the wind off the Redmarch pulling at his robes.

He read it twice. The second time, he read it slowly.

The man at the well in Coldwater, whose light was the wrong shape. *Someone wearing someone.* The figure on the ridge at night that had no light at all, like a hole cut out of the dark. Corvin's two years of reports that nobody had believed. Hild, putting herself between Hyvred and the ridge without needing to be told.

And at the bottom, underlined twice, so hard the pen had gone through the paper:

*Tell Aldous they're not only coming from the east.*

Around his neck, Suzu had gone very still.

"Yan?" said Maigo, coming across the yard with an armful of firewood. "You look like someone died."

"Nobody died," said Yan. He folded the letter. "I need to find Aldous."

***

The chaplain read it in the little chapel with no priest, sitting on the front bench, holding the paper close to a candle. He read it three times. Yan watched his face, and watched it change, slowly, the way the sky changes before rain.

"Someone wearing someone," Aldous said at last, very quietly.

"You know what it means."

"No." Aldous set the letter down on the bench beside him. "I know what it *sounds* like. Which is not the same thing." He was silent for a while. "What did you feel? At the grave. You never told me, and I never asked. I'm asking now."

Yan thought about lying. He'd been keeping it for a month, turning it over at night, the way he turned over everything that mattered, alone. But Hyvred had written it down and sent it across the whole kingdom. *Tell Aldous.* She hadn't kept it.

"Grief," he said. "Old. Worn into the ground, like a path. Someone stood there for years." He took a breath. "And under it, something cold. Something that was *done* to someone who didn't want it done. Most of it's gone. But it's still there, at the bottom. Suzu felt it too."

Aldous closed his eyes.

"I've felt that for seven years," he said. "Every time I stood there. I thought I was imagining it. I thought it was an old man's superstition." He opened his eyes again. "The general took her alive, you know. The healer. Before the end. The soldiers who were close enough say he did something to her where everyone could see. Nobody knew what. Nobody had ever seen demons do anything like it, and nobody has since." He looked at the letter. "Unless they have. In the west. Where nobody's looking."

"Someone wearing someone," said Yan.

"I'll write to the Advisor tonight," said Aldous. "Not in the monthly report. Tonight, by the fast courier." He stood, slowly, the way he did everything. "And Yan."

"Yes?"

"Thank her for me. The girl who sees. Tell her she's right. Tell her someone out here believes her."

***

They compared windows that night like always, in the corner of the mess hall, with the score of Rhea and Salty's arm-wrestling chalked on the wall behind them (twenty-one to nineteen) and the soldiers at the next table pretending not to listen.

"Fourteen," said Maigo. "The ground's getting louder. I can feel the whole fort now if I stand barefoot in the yard. Like a heartbeat. The red ground's still screaming, though." He pushed his pixel sunglasses up. "It doesn't scream at night as much. I don't know why."

"Ten," said Salty. Then, unable to help himself: "It moved. Properly. Today. I was doing forms with the practice sword and halfway through it just... got lighter. For about three swings. Then it was heavy again." He looked at his hands. "That was it, wasn't it. That was the aura."

"That was it," said Rhea from the next table, not pretending at all. "Told you it'd come up like a bruise."

"You said a lot of things."

"And I was right about all of them."

"Nine," said Wendy. She had her headphones on, the silent ones, which she'd started wearing almost all the time. "It's still the bow. Always the bow. The second before I let go." She frowned at the table. "Sometimes I can feel where the arrow wants to go before I've aimed it. That's weird, right? That's weird."

"Nine," said Yan.

Mashi was last, as always. "Three," he said. "And three."

"You went up," said Maigo.

"I did." Mashi looked almost pleased, which on Mashi was a small earthquake. "Both of them, same day. Within an hour of each other." He turned his window toward Yan, as if Yan could see it, which he couldn't. "Hyvred said my light was two colours that don't mix. I've been thinking about that. I think they *want* to. I think they just don't know how yet."

"Neither do you," said Wendy.

"Neither do I," Mashi agreed cheerfully.

Yan told them about Hyvred's letter then. All of it. The man at the well, the hole on the ridge, *not only from the east*. And then, because he'd decided in the chapel that he wasn't going to keep it any longer, he told them about the cold thing under the grave.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"So whatever's in the west," said Salty slowly, "it's the same thing. As whatever happened at the grave."

"Maybe," said Yan. "I don't know."

"You think it's the same," said Wendy. It wasn't a question.

Yan looked at her. She was the one who never believed anything. She'd said so herself, the first day: *I don't trust anything*. And she was looking at him now as if she already knew the answer and was only waiting for him to say it.

"Yes," he said. "I think it's the same."

***

The scouts came four nights later.

Yan was on the east wall, because he couldn't sleep, because he rarely could anymore. Rhea was there too, on watch, leaning on the battlements with a cloak around her shoulders, unusually quiet. Below them the Redmarch ran away into the dark, rust-black under a thin moon, and the wind came off it smelling of nothing at all.

"You feel that?" said Rhea suddenly.

Yan didn't feel anything. Then Suzu tightened around his neck so hard he choked.

"There," said Rhea, very softly. She'd gone still, the way a cat goes still. "Out past the third row of markers. Don't point."

He didn't point. He looked.

At first there was nothing. Just the red ground, and the grey grass, and Aldous's wooden markers going away in their long rows. Then something moved between the markers, and stopped. And then another. And then he could see them: shapes, tall and thin and bent forward, moving across the red in short, quick bursts, like hounds following a scent, and then stopping dead, perfectly still, as if listening. When they stopped, he lost them against the ground. When they moved, the moonlight caught something in their faces that might have been eyes.

Six. Then eight. He stopped counting at eleven.

"Demons," said Rhea. Her voice had gone very flat and very professional. "Scouts. Out of bowshot. Don't move." She raised one hand, very slowly, and somewhere along the wall a horn sounded once, low.

The fort woke up around them. Not loudly; a garrison on a frontier knows how to wake quietly. Footsteps on the walls. The creak of bows being strung. Within a minute there were soldiers at every crenel, and Aldous was beside Yan with his staff in his hand, and Salty and Wendy and Maigo and Mashi were coming up the stair behind him, half dressed, Wendy with her bow already in her hand.

"Can I shoot?" she said.

"No," said Aldous.

"They're right there."

"They're out of range, and they know it. And they're not here to fight." Aldous was watching the dark with an expression Yan had never seen on him before: grim, and old, and very tired. "Watch."

They watched.

The shapes had stopped moving. All of them, at once, the way a flock of birds turns in the air. They were standing in a loose line just past the third row of markers, perfectly still. And one of them, in the middle, had lifted one long arm.

It was pointing. At the north tower.

Then, slowly, at the gatehouse. Then at the east tower, where they were standing. Then the south. One by one, it pointed at every tower on the wall, and every gate, and the place where the wall had been patched after a winter flood, and the well-head you could just see over the battlements, and the chapel roof.

"It's counting," said Mashi, very quietly. "It's counting us."

"It's counting the *fort,*" said Salty.

"Same thing."

Maigo had taken his boots off. Yan hadn't noticed him do it. He was standing barefoot on the cold stone of the wall-walk with his eyes shut and his wooden toes spread wide, and his face had gone very strange.

"The ground's flinching," he whispered. "Where they're standing. It's flinching away from them. Like it doesn't want to be touched."

Wendy had an arrow on the string. She hadn't drawn it. Her headphones were down around her neck, and her face was white and set, and her eyes were fixed on the pointing figure in the middle of the line.

"I could make that," she said. "I could. It's far, but I could."

"Don't," said Aldous.

"Why *not?*"

"Because that's what it wants." His hand was on her shoulder, very gently. "It wants to see what we'll do. How far our arrows reach. How fast we wake. Who comes up on the wall first." He looked down at her. "Give it nothing."

Wendy stood there with her arrow on the string for a long, long moment.

Then she lowered the bow.

Yan closed his eyes.

He didn't decide to. He did it the way he'd done it at the grave, the way he'd been doing it every night since on the red ground past the wall: breathe, and wait, and let the world wait back. He reached out past the wall, past the markers, toward the line of still figures in the dark.

Most of them felt like nothing. Like holes. Like cold water, empty. Suzu, who would lift her head at a hen dying on the far side of the fort, didn't so much as taste the air at them. Whatever was in them, it wasn't anything she could eat.

But the one in the middle, the one that was pointing, felt like something else.

It felt like the grave.

Not the grief. The thing *under* the grief. The cold thing, the thing that had been done to someone who didn't want it done. Faint, very faint, the same as it had been in the ground under the cairn. But here it wasn't old. Here it wasn't worn away. Here it was moving, and breathing, and pointing at the towers of Cinderhold one by one.

Yan opened his eyes. He was shaking. Suzu was wound so tight around his throat that his vision had started to go grey at the edges.

"Yan," said Aldous sharply. "*Yan.*"

"The one in the middle," Yan managed. "The one pointing." He swallowed. "It's like the grave. It's the same cold."

Aldous didn't say anything. But Yan saw his hand tighten on his staff until the knuckles went white.

Out on the Redmarch, the pointing arm came down.

And all eleven shapes turned, together, as smoothly as if they'd been one creature, and went back the way they'd come, across the red, in their short quick bursts, toward the dark line of the mountains. Within a minute they were gone.

Nobody on the wall moved for a long time.

***

It was nearly dawn before anyone slept, and most of them didn't.

Yan sat at the little table in the barracks with a candle and a fresh sheet of paper and Hyvred's letter open beside it, and wrote.

He told her about the scouts. Eleven of them, out of bowshot, standing in a line. The one in the middle, pointing at every tower, every gate, the patched place in the wall, the well, the chapel roof. Counting. Aldous saying *give it nothing*. Wendy lowering her bow.

He told her about Maigo, standing barefoot on the wall, saying that the ground was flinching.

And then he told her the rest, because she'd told him, because they'd all promised.

*I felt them. Most of them were nothing. Holes. But the one that was pointing felt the same as the cold thing under the healer's grave. The thing that was done to her, that I never told anyone about until this week. The same cold.*

*You saw someone wearing someone in the west. I think I felt one in the east.*

*Aldous says to tell you he believes you. He says to tell you someone out here believes you. So do I.*

*Write back. Tell me what Corvin says. Tell Kei to tell Hisui that Suzu has been wound around my neck so tight all night that I think she's frightened, and that if Hisui says anything rude about it I'll write to Bee and have her draw him a mongoose.*

He signed it, and sealed it, and sat for a while with his hands flat on the table, waiting for them to stop shaking.

Across the room, Wendy was sitting on the edge of her bunk with her bow across her knees. She hadn't put it down since the wall. Every so often she'd run her thumb along the string, as if checking it was still there.

"I could have made that shot," she said, to nobody in particular.

"I know," said Yan.

"Aldous was right not to let me."

"I know that too."

Wendy was quiet for a moment.

"I don't like it," she said. "Being right about things. I always thought I'd like it." She looked down at the bow. "I thought when it turned out everything was as bad as I said, I'd at least get to feel clever."

"And?"

"I just feel cold," said Wendy.

Yan didn't answer that. There wasn't anything to say. He blew out the candle instead, and lay back on his bunk, and looked up at the dark, and thought about a line of shapes standing perfectly still on the red ground, and an arm coming up, and a finger pointing, one tower at a time.

*They're counting.*

He wondered how long it took a demon to count a fort. He wondered what it did when it was finished.

And somewhere very far away, at the bottom of everything, under the grief and the fear and the long dark of the Redmarch, he thought about a small kitchen, and a woman standing in it, and how many towers there were between him and her.

He didn't put that thought away. Not tonight. He let it stay until morning.
