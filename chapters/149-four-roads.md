Deston woke before it was light on the seventieth day and lay on his bench in the common room of the Broken Lance with his eyes shut, and listened to the threads.

He'd done it every morning since the downs. Since an old stone on a hill, three weeks ago, when he'd stood beside it in the dusk and seen every thread in the kingdom at once. He'd found he couldn't stop. It was like learning to hear a particular instrument in an orchestra: once you'd heard it, you always heard it, under everything, whatever else was playing.

He listened now.

Thirty-five threads in one building. Close. Close enough that he could have reached out and touched any of them. Gold, every one, both ends, the way his own had been gold both ends since a jetty in the spring. Gabe's on the bench beside him, low and steady. Xav's across the room, very quiet, the note of a man who was awake and pretending not to be. Rachael's, upstairs, in the end room with the window open; bright, a healer's thread, and a little tight this morning, the way it had been tight since an apple on a sill. Hana's, up the hill in the king's house, which had stopped being one note and become eight, braided, a cord with seven others threaded on it.

And the loudest one.

At the far end of the common room, on a bench by the window. Gold all the way through. The note that never once raised its voice and was louder than all the rest of them together.

And beside it, very close, a quick bright one, like a sword being swung by somebody who's just found out it fits.

He'd heard those two together every night for two months. On the jetty, and on the road south, and here. He wouldn't hear them together again for three months. Maybe four.

He lay on the bench with his eyes shut and listened to them for a long time, so he'd remember.

***

They were going out on four roads.

He'd heard it at the long table for three days, people working it out. Drew had drawn it on the back of a bracket, the way Drew drew everything. Four gates. Four roads. Four times, because the king's household and Group 3 wanted to be clear of the city before the heat, and Groups 1 and 4 and 6 had the carts and needed the full day, and Group 5 had twenty days to Thornwall and were in no hurry at all.

East first. At first light. Group 3, with Aldous and Rhea and the cart and the horseshoe nailed to the tailboard. And a man with a rod. And his daughter.

North-west at the second hour. The king's household, four hundred people and two hundred horses and eleven wagons, back to Solmere, to a door in a vault. Group 2 with them. Halvard.

North at the third hour. Groups 1 and 4 and 6, and Maren and Bram and Emrys and Sabine and Petra, with the brewer's dray and the miller's cart. Back up the long road to Pellow's Cross and the Sallow and the river, where they'd split: Group 1 to Wrenhaven, Group 4 to Kingsferry, Group 6 to Duskhollow.

West at noon. Group 5. Back to Thornwall, and the pass, and a shed with a roof.

And Group 7 staying. At the gates. All four of them, one after another, to see everyone off, because Chris had said *that's what you do when you live somewhere and people visit*.

***

Gabe was already up when Deston opened his eyes. Sitting on the edge of the bench in the grey, with his big forge-black hands between his knees, looking at the far end of the room.

"East first," said Gabe.

"East first."

"We're going."

"We're going," agreed Deston.

He didn't need to ask where. Xav was already sitting up across the room, with his boots on. They hadn't talked about it. They hadn't needed to. Three of them had stood in front of a choir at thirteen and fifteen and come in on the same beat for four years without anyone counting them in. They knew where they were going.

The east gate.

***

The east gate of Valcrest was the one Group 3 had come in by, ten days ago, at sunset, with a man standing on the road outside it who'd been there all day. It was smaller than the north gate. An old square gatehouse with the road going into it and paper fishing rods still tied along the top of the wall, limp in the dew, that nobody had taken down because nobody wanted to.

Group 3's cart was in the gateway when they got there. Aldous on the box with his book. Rhea checking the harness, very loudly, with the horse looking at her in deep offence. Maigo on top of the baggage with his antlers full of leaves and his hood down. Niko sitting on the tailboard with his hands in his pockets, chill, beside the new horseshoe the king had made, which somebody had nailed to the tailboard upside down. Wendy beside the cart with her headphones round her neck. Salty with the king's father's hammer in his belt.

Yan with the scythe on his shoulder and a white snake round his neck.

And at the back of the cart, by the tailboard, a fox girl with a sword the size of a door on her back, and her red-gold hair tied up, and her ears very straight. Standing next to a man with a rod over his shoulder.

He was carrying the water.

Deston saw it and stopped walking.

Two buckets. On a yoke. Across his shoulders. The same yoke, the same buckets, that had come south on the brewer's dray from Wrenhaven and been carried all the way down the long road by a man who'd asked Maren on the first morning, *give me something to carry*.

"You're carrying the water," said Deston.

"Somebody has to."

"There's a *cart*. You could put it on the *cart*."

"Aldous said I could carry it," said the man with the rod, very seriously. "Aldous is in charge. I asked him what I should carry. He thought about it for a long time and said *water*." He shifted the yoke on his shoulders. "I like being told what to do. I never get to."

Alea, beside him, put her hand over her face.

***

They didn't say much at the gate. They didn't need to. Deston had been afraid they'd have to, all night: that there'd be things to say, and they'd get them wrong, standing in a gateway at dawn with a cart waiting. He'd lain awake working out what he'd say and every version of it had been terrible.

In the end Gabe just walked up to him and stood in front of him, the way Gabe had stood in front of him on a jetty in the spring, and looked at his face, which none of them could see.

"Next year," said Gabe.

"Next year."

"The tenth. You said. All three of us."

"I said."

"You'd better," said Gabe. And then his voice did something, and he stopped, and started again, and said the thing he always said. The last thing he'd ever said to him on Earth, on a call, three years ago, before a room in Malacca with a fan going. "*Sleep*, Declan. *Seriously*."

"I listened to you once," said Declan.

"I know," said Gabe. "Listen again." He put his arm round his neck from the side, like a falling wall, yoke and buckets and all. "Every night. On the road. Sleep. I'm going to ask Yan if you do."

"Yan won't tell you."

"Suzu will," said Gabe, "and Kei can hear her," and Yan, by the cart, made a sound that was very nearly a laugh.

***

Xav didn't hug him. Xav never did.

He stood in front of him in the gateway with his hands at his sides and his face doing nothing at all, the way it had done nothing in a polytechnic lab in Singapore at seventeen when a boy leaned over his shoulder and said *you'd be good at CS, you wait, nobody waits*.

"Okay," said Xav.

"Okay."

"Next year," said Xav. "The grave. And then the lake, after." He paused. "I'm going to make you move three times."

"Nobody's ever made me move three times."

"I know," said Xav. "That's why I'm going to." He held out his fist, low. Declan bumped it with his. "Okay," said Xav. "Again."

***

Deston was last.

He'd known he would be. He was always the middle. In a choir, in a group of friends, in a room: the one who'd introduced half of them to each other, the one everyone came through. The one who stood between the top part and the low part and held the two of them together. He was weak on his own and strong with people round him, Ysolde had said once, in a letter, and she'd been right.

He stood in front of the man with the buckets.

"I can hear your thread," said Deston.

"I know."

"It's the loudest one in the kingdom. It always was. Even when you were dead." He swallowed. "On the roof in Kingsferry. For three years. Faded at my end. Gold at yours. The whole time." He looked at a face he couldn't see. "I'm going to hear it all the way north. Going east. Getting further. I'm going to lie awake at night on the road and listen to it go."

"I know you are."

"I'll know when you get there. To the grave." Deston's voice wasn't working very well. "I'll know when she gets there. I'll hear it."

"Tell them," said Declan. "When you hear it. Tell the channel."

"I will."

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

And then Deston did the only thing he'd ever really been able to do, the thing he'd been doing since he was thirteen in a choir room, the thing that had found a lake at the end of a thread.

He hummed.

The first bar of the middle part. Quietly. In the east gate of Valcrest, at dawn, with a cart waiting.

And stopped.

***

He always stopped. He'd stopped on the roof in Kingsferry for three years, every night, after the first bar, because the top part never came in. He'd stopped on the Last Look, a week ago, because they all knew the rest. He'd stopped here, now, because that was where he always stopped.

Gabe came in. Low. Under him. The way he always had, steady, the floor of the song.

And then the top part.

Too loud. Of course it was too loud. A stranger's voice, lower and rougher and older than the one that used to sing it, and dead on the beat, where Declan always used to come in a fraction behind on purpose. Coming in on the third bar, the way he always had. In English. In a gateway in another world, with two buckets on a yoke across his shoulders.

Three parts. The whole thing.

Deston didn't stop this time.

They sang it all. The three of them, in the east gate of Valcrest at dawn, a song they'd sung in a secondary-school choir room in Singapore when they were thirteen and fifteen, about how you measure a year. Deston would never say the words afterwards, to anyone; they weren't his to give. But he'd say this: that the three of them sang it right through, every verse, all three parts, the way they'd sung it on a jetty in the spring and in a choir room before that, and that nobody in the gateway said anything.

Aldous closed his book on the box of the cart.

Maigo, on top of the baggage, didn't say a single word. For once in his life. The leaves on his antlers turned, all of them, toward the gate.

Rhea let go of the harness.

Xav stood very still at the edge of the gateway with his hands at his sides and his face doing nothing at all, the way it did nothing when it was doing the most.

And Alea, by the tailboard, with her terrible sword on her back and her ears straight up, came in on the last verse. Very quietly. In English. On the top part, beside her father. Because she'd known the song all her life. He'd sung it to her at the foot of the stairs every night when she was small. It was the only song from that world in this one.

Four voices. In a gateway. At dawn.

When it ended, nobody moved for a long time.

***

"You sang it too loud," said Deston.

"I always do," said Declan.

"The horse looked round."

"Horses hate me," said Declan. "Hens hate me. The king's horse is the only one that doesn't, and that's because it's the king's." He shifted the yoke on his shoulders. "Go north, Dest. Hum it on the road. I'll hear it."

"You can't hear threads."

"I can hear *you*," said Declan. "I always could. Even when you were dead."

Deston stood in the gateway for a long moment. Then he stepped forward and put his arms round him, buckets and yoke and all, and the water slopped, the way it had slopped over Tiger at the north gate ten days ago. And held on.

"*Next year*," said Deston, into his shoulder.

"Next year," said Declan.

***

The cart went out at sunrise.

Deston stood in the east gate with Gabe and Xav and watched it go. Down the east road, through the market gardens, toward the low long hill with the shrine at the top where travellers left a coin. Aldous on the box. Rhea walking at the back, pushing it, because the horse was slow. Maigo talking to the horse. Niko on the tailboard. Wendy and Salty side by side. Yan with the scythe.

And at the back, behind the cart, a fox girl with a sword like a door, and beside her a man carrying the water. Not looking back.

At the top of the hill, by the shrine, the man stopped.

He turned round. Just for a moment. With the buckets still on his shoulders. And lifted the rod, a little, off the yoke. Not high. The way he'd lifted it at the first bend of a road out of Wrenhaven, to a man standing in a square who'd said *nobody follows*.

Deston lifted his hand.

So did Gabe. So did Xav.

Then the cart went over the top of the hill, and the man with it, and the girl, and they were gone.

***

Deston listened to the thread go.

He stood in the east gate for a long time after Gabe and Xav had gone back into the city to finish not packing, and listened. The loudest thread in the kingdom, going east. Stretching. Not thinning; a thread didn't thin with distance, it just got longer, like a line paying out from a reel. He could hear it going over the hill, and down the far side, and out along the east road toward the downs and the plain and, a long way off, the red.

And beside it, very close, the quick bright one.

He'd hear them both all the way north. He'd lie on the road at night and listen to them get further.

He went back into the city.

***

The king's household went out at the second hour, by the north-west gate, and it took most of the morning.

Deston didn't go and watch all of it. Nobody could have. Four hundred people and two hundred horses and eleven wagons, and Lucan at the gate weeping at every one of them individually. But he went up to the wall by the north-west gate with Groups 1 and 4 and 6, because they were leaving next and wanted to see the king go, and stood on the wall among the paper fishing rods and watched.

Group 2 rode out together, near the front. Hana on a grey mare with her purple tail over its flank and a cord on her wrist with eight things on it, seven halves and a half-heart. Milli beside her, very still in the saddle. Aleaka with a hawk on her shoulder, glaring at everything. Cursiffe reading on horseback, which nobody had thought was possible. Jared a little ahead of them, half a pace in front and a little to the left, the way he'd ridden all the way from Solmere.

Oswin in a wagon with a ten-foot roll of linen paper held against his chest in both arms.

And the king. In the middle of it all. In his plain coat, on a big plain horse, with soot in the creases of his knuckles, riding home to a door in a vault that he'd say a reason in front of.

And beside him, on a grey horse, thin and upright, the Advisor.

***

Deston listened to the Advisor's thread go out of the north-west gate.

Silver. The first of all of them. The one every native window in the kingdom was copied from, a thread that ran back through twenty years to a boy on a wall at Cinderhold who'd said *you've got one too, you just can't see it*. It was going north-west now, away from the east road, away from the loudest thread in the kingdom, getting longer.

And round it, wound tight, the grey strand.

Frayed. Still. At Halvard's end. Deston could hear it from the wall: a worn, tight, catching note, like a rope that's been held too hard for too long. It had been frayed at both ends on the downs, three weeks ago. Then on the high street of Valcrest it had gone quiet at the other end, and in a small room facing north at dusk it had mended, at the other end, completely.

It hadn't mended at Halvard's.

Deston had known it wouldn't, yet. *Yours is yours*, the man with the rod had said to him, in that room, Deston had heard later. *Nobody can do your end for you. You'll do it when you open the drawer*.

The Advisor rode out of the north-west gate of Valcrest on a grey horse with a key in his coat and a folded report beside it, toward a tower in Solmere with a drawer at the bottom of it that stuck in wet weather.

The frayed end went with him.

Deston watched it go until he couldn't hear it any more.

***

They went out by the north gate at the third hour.

The same gate. The north gate. Where twenty of them had stood on the cobbles at dawn ten days ago, waiting, with a bedsheet that said WELCOME GROUPS 1 4 AND 6!!!!, and a water-carrier had put down his buckets and said *hi, Leaka*.

The bedsheet was still on the gatehouse. Somebody had nailed it up there, at the top of the wall, among the paper rods. The wolf was still on it. Somebody had written *IT'S A DOG* under the wolf, in Bee's furious capitals.

Maren went first, with her list. She'd read it out at the cart in the Lance's yard before they left, in her small exact voice, the way she'd read it at the start of the road south: who was carrying what, who was on which watch, who was with who. At the end she'd stopped, and looked at the line at the bottom, which wasn't a name.

*Water*, it said.

She'd read it out. And then, beside it, in pencil, the new name.

*Gigichi*.

"*No*," Gigichi had said.

"Yes," Maren had said.

"I'm not carrying the *water*."

"He said you'd say that," Maren had said, "and do it anyway."

And Gigichi had stood in the yard of the Broken Lance with her gold watch closed in her fist and her face doing the thing it did, furious and wet and refusing to admit to either. And then she'd gone and got the yoke.

She was carrying it now. At the back of the dray. Two buckets. Scowling.

***

The road went out through the market gardens toward the Last Look. The same road they'd come in by, ten days ago, the other way. Deston walked between Gabe and Xav, the way he'd walked south, with his hands in his pockets, and listened.

At the top of the Last Look, three hours out, he stopped.

He didn't decide to. He just stopped, the way he'd stopped on the downs three weeks ago beside an old stone. Where the road crested the hill and you could see Valcrest one last time before the farms and the downs closed round you. The others went on a little way and then stopped too, when they saw him, and waited. Gabe came back and stood beside him. Xav came back and stood on his other side.

Deston turned round and looked at the city.

***

It lay on the plain in the noon light the way it had lain in the dusk ten days ago, when a man stood a little way down this hill with buckets at his feet and looked at it for the first time in fourteen years. Spilled out. Walls and roofs and roads going in from every side like the spokes of a wheel. The dome of the chapel. The long pale bowl of the Lists. Paper fishing rods in every window, still; he couldn't see them from here, but he knew they were there.

He closed his eyes and listened.

Four roads.

He could hear all of them. That was the thing. He'd stood on the downs three weeks ago and heard twenty threads coming in on four roads to one city, and five of them gold and three warm and twelve faded like ash, and he'd said a number to Xav in the dark: *twelve*. And then he'd stood in a gate square and heard the twelve go gold on one downbeat and his knees had gone.

He heard them going out now.

North-west: five threads. Group 2. With the king's gold one, beaten and square, and Halvard's silver one with its frayed grey strand. Going away along the road to Solmere. Gold, all five, both ends.

West: five more. Group 5, just leaving the west gate at noon. He could feel them start out. Tiger's quick hot one. Drew's steady one. Hyvred's, which saw. Bee's, buzzing out of spite. Kei's, with a little cold green coil wound round it that was talking. Gold. Both ends.

East: five, and two more. Group 3, half a day out already, past the shrine and down onto the plain. Gold, both ends. And beside them, the quick bright one that had been beside the loudest one all its life.

And the loudest one.

Going east. Stretching. Gold all the way through, the way it had been gold all the way through for three years while Deston's end was faded on a roof in Kingsferry. The note that never once raised its voice. Getting longer with every step a man took east along a road with two buckets on a yoke, toward a red plain and a grave with a tree beside it.

And north: here. Fifteen threads. Round him. On the Last Look. Gold. Close enough to touch.

And Group 7's five, in the city, at the gates, staying. Gold.

Thirty threads, going out on four roads from one city, and five staying.

Every one of them gold. Both ends.

***

He opened his eyes.

"Dest," said Gabe, beside him. Quietly. "You okay?"

"They're all gold," said Deston.

Gabe didn't say anything. He couldn't hear threads. He'd never been able to. But he'd stood next to Deston for fourteen years and he could hear Deston, and whatever Deston's face was doing, Gabe heard it.

"On the downs," said Deston. "Three weeks ago. Coming in. Twelve of them were faded. Like ash. Because twelve people thought he was dead." He looked at the city. "Going out, there isn't one. Not one faded thread in the whole kingdom. Not among us." He breathed. "Every one of us knows. Every one of us believes it. All thirty-five. Gold both ends. Going out on four roads to the edges of a kingdom to hold them." He swallowed. "Like a choir. When every voice is in. When nobody's missing their bar."

"Even his?" said Xav, on his other side. "His father's? The grey one?"

Deston went still.

He hadn't been listening for it. He listened now.

There. Out past all the others. In the direction that wasn't a direction. Thirty-six grey threads, going out of the world, toward people who'd stood at a funeral three years ago in another country. Thirty-five of them were theirs: the summoned's, to their own families, to mothers and fathers and sisters, waiting, two months, wherever they were. And one more. Thin and grey and thirty years old. Going out of the world toward a car-park wall. Not broken.

"Still grey," said Deston. "All of them. The ones out of the world. Ours and his."

He was quiet.

"Thea said something," he said. "In the archive. Yesterday. About the blank half of the circle. The way back." He looked at the grey threads in the direction that wasn't a direction. "She said it pushes back *along the threads*. The same way we came. Along whatever the net followed to catch us." He was very quiet. "I thought she meant the gold ones. The ones to him."

"And?" said Gabe.

"It's these," said Deston. "The grey ones. They're the way home. Thirty-five threads going out of the world to the people waiting for us." He swallowed. "They've been there the whole time. Since the hall. I've been looking at them for two years and thinking they were just *sad*." He breathed out. "They're not sad. They're a road."

Nobody said anything on the top of the Last Look for a long time.

"And his?" said Xav, eventually. Very quietly. "The thirty-sixth?"

Deston listened to it. Thin and grey and thirty years old.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Nobody brought him. He just woke up." He was quiet. "I don't know what it's a road to."

***

They went on, after a while. Down the north side of the Last Look, into the farms and the market gardens, where you couldn't see the city any more. Gigichi at the back of the dray with the water, scowling. Higu with three moths over her head in the noon light. Xola's squid waving at a farmer. PenguChi's penguin walking at her heels. Calvin with the kettle tied to the outside of his pack, where it could see. Rachael between Gabe and Sabine. Dennis with a ledger. Malcolm with a notebook. Thea at the front, beside Maren, already Parsing something in the air that nobody else could see.

Deston walked between Gabe and Xav.

He listened to the loudest thread in the kingdom going east, behind him, getting longer. He'd lie on the road tonight and listen to it. And tomorrow. And every night for three weeks, all the way to the river, and then on, up the river road, to a lake, where he'd stop for a night and knock on a door and tell a man called Rowan that somebody was coming back for the late pumpkins.

He'd hear it get to the grave. In a month. More. He'd hear it stop.

He'd tell the channel.

After a while, without meaning to, he started humming. The middle part. Quietly. Under his breath. And at the end of the first bar he didn't stop.

Gabe came in under him. Low.

And on the third bar, very faintly, so faintly that Deston knew he was imagining it, a long way behind them, over the Last Look and the farms and half a day of the east road, somebody came in on the top part, too loud.

He was imagining it. He knew he was.

He kept humming anyway, all the way down the hill.
