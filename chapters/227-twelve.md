Gigichi started the hedge the morning after they got home.

Nobody asked her to. That was the point. If somebody had asked her she'd have said no, on principle, and then done it anyway a week later and pretended it had been her idea; and this way she could skip the first part and just do it, which saved everybody a week.

She'd grown the first of it in the autumn. A briar, round the last of the pumpkins on the slope above the cabin, because the deer were getting them and Ice wouldn't do anything about the deer except watch them with deep personal respect. A ring of it, waist-high, thick as a wall, with thorns like cat's claws and small white flowers that came out in the spring and smelled of nothing. She'd sat in it all winter. On the bad nights. It was the one place on the whole lake shore that was hers, that she'd made, that nobody else could get into without her say-so; and from inside it, if you sat on the upturned bucket she kept there, you could see the end of the jetty and the lamp on the last plank, and nobody could see you.

It wasn't big enough for fifteen.

So on the first morning home, before anybody else was up, she went down the slope with her hands in her cardigan sleeves and stood in the old ring among the pumpkin vines, and put her hands on the ground, and asked it to go further.

It went.

Not fast. She was Spark; she'd been Spark for a long time, longer than she liked to think about, and *Briar* had never once in her life done anything fast. It went the way roots go: slowly, and then you look away, and when you look back it's further than you thought. She pushed it out of the old ring and down the slope, a stem at a time, a yard a day, then two; round the flat green shelf above the shingle where the grass was short and the ground was dry and you could put a table. Round, and round, and back to meet itself, with a gap on the lake side so you could see the water and a gap on the slope side so you could get in.

By the third evening it was a room.

A green room with no roof, forty feet across, walled in briar shoulder-high, with the white flowers coming out along the top of it in the dusk. Room for a long table, if anybody had one. Room for fifteen bedrolls round the edge, if anybody was sleeping out, which in high summer on Lake Ilse everybody would. The lake on one side through the gap, and the jetty, and the forge on the shingle under its sailcloth roof, with the king's stones round the hearth.

She stood in the middle of it on the third evening with her hands in her sleeves and looked at it.

"It's a hedge," she said, to nobody. "It's just a hedge."

Behind her, at the slope-side gap, somebody laughed.

She didn't turn round. She knew the laugh. She'd heard it through her headphones for years, late at night, when somebody in a call said something stupid and he couldn't help it; she'd heard it from the end of the jetty in the spring, sitting in her hedge, and written *he laughed out loud* in the channel with her hands shaking. It was a short laugh. It went up at the end.

"It's a room," said Ice.

"It's a hedge."

"It's a room with a hedge for walls."

She turned round then. He was standing in the gap with the rod on his shoulder and two fish on a string, looking at it. At the whole thing. The briar, the flowers, the gap to the water, the flat green floor. His face did whatever his face did. She couldn't see it. She never had.

"Nobody asked you," he said.

"No."

"Good," said Ice. He looked at the room for a long time. "I'll need a table."

"Garrow's doing it," said Gigichi. "Trestles. And Edric's lending the long board out of the bakehouse. Rowan said." She folded her arms. "Odette's sending cloths. Robin's mother's sending eggs. The whole town knows. You can't stop it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to *try*."

He didn't answer that. He stood in the gap with the fish and looked at the room she'd made, and then at her, and then he went on down the slope to the cabin without saying anything else; and she stood in the middle of her hedge and found that her face had gone hot, which was stupid, and that she was pleased, which was worse.

***

They came in ones and twos. The way people do when nobody's organised it, and somebody should have.

Petra came first. At dawn the next morning, alone, on foot, with the driftwood stick under her arm and her boots grey with the river road, having walked up from Duskhollow ahead of the others without telling anybody, Gigichi found out later, because she'd woken in the night and couldn't stand it. Gigichi saw her from the hedge. A straight-backed woman coming down the last of the lake road in the grey light and stopping at the top of the shingle, and standing there.

Ice was on the end of the jetty with the rod.

He didn't turn round. He didn't need to. Gigichi watched him not turn round for as long as it took Petra to walk down the shingle and out along the jetty, thirty-four planks, and sit down on the last one beside him with her legs over the edge and her stick across her knees.

They didn't say anything.

Gigichi sat in her hedge and watched them not say anything for an hour. Two people who'd been in a ditch together on the Redmarch ten years ago, when one of them was a man holding a line and the other was a girl in it with forty recruits; who'd never, as far as Gigichi knew, talked about it once. They sat on the end of the jetty with the mist going off the water and the hens coming down the planks one at a time to sit beside them, and the big red hen sat down against Petra's hip, and Petra let her.

At the end of the hour he gave her the rod.

She held it. She didn't cast. She just held it, across her knees, on top of the stick, with both hands, and looked at the lake; and he sat beside her with his empty hands in his lap.

Gigichi didn't write it in the channel. Some things weren't the channel's.

***

RX and John came at noon, arguing.

RX was invisible for the last half-mile, which he'd done on purpose, and which John had said was childish, and which RX had done anyway; so what came down the lake road was John, alone, with his light floating at his shoulder, filming the road and talking to nobody. *This is the lake. That's the jetty. That's the hedge, which I've been told about. That's the hens, which I've been warned about*. And then, from about a yard to John's left, out of thin air, a voice saying *you're narrating to nobody, bro, nobody's watching*, and John saying *everyone's going to watch, that's the point*, and Gigichi, at the slope-side gap of the hedge with a basket of eggs, nearly dropping the basket.

RX appeared. Right beside her. He grinned.

"Sorry," he said, not sorry.

"I hate you," said Gigichi.

"Everyone says that." He looked past her at the room. At the briar, shoulder-high, the white flowers, the flat green floor. He stopped grinning. "Oh. Oh, that's sick."

"It's a hedge."

"It's a *base*," said RX, with tremendous respect. "You built a base." He went in. He walked all the way round the inside of it, touching the briar very carefully between the thorns, the way you'd touch a wall in a game to see if it was real. "Declan's going to cook in here?"

"On the shingle. Down there. Then carry it up."

"Fifteen people. In a hedge. Eating his food." RX stood in the middle of it. "Bro."

That was all. *Bro*. But Gigichi heard the thing in it, under the word; the thing in Xav's voice sometimes, and Malcolm's, and RX's when he wasn't being stupid. They'd known him in Malacca. Not online; there. In a polytechnic, at seventeen, in a computer lab, in the canteen, at the place behind the famous place. They'd eaten what he was going to cook. Not on a list in somebody's spreadsheet. In their own mouths, at a plastic table, in the heat, ten years ago, in a world that had stopped.

John was filming her hedge. Gigichi let him.

***

Malcolm and Azac came in the afternoon, slowly, with a handcart. Malcolm had a book under his arm and Azac had the handcart. Neither of them explained the handcart. It had a sack in it and a box and a small cask, and Azac pushed it all the way down the shingle to the forge and left it there under the sailcloth without a word, and came back up.

"What's in it?" said Gigichi.

"Things," said Azac.

"What things?"

Azac considered her. He was the one of Group 6 she knew least: quiet, square, with a *Ward* on him, and a habit of standing between people and doors without seeming to notice he was doing it.

"Peppers," he said. "From Duskhollow market. The right ones. Salty told Petra at the Lists which ones, and Petra told the market. And coconut. From a ship at Kingsferry. Dennis sent it up the river. He said put it on the treasurer's account." He looked at the forge. "And rice."

"That's why you came," said Gigichi. "You said you didn't say why."

"I didn't," said Azac. "Now I have." And went to find a place in the hedge for his bedroll, right by the slope-side gap, between the room and the road, without seeming to notice he was doing it.

Xav came last. At dusk, alone, along the shore path instead of the road, with his practice sword on his back and his chin down. He didn't come to the hedge. He went straight down the shingle and out along the jetty and sat down on the last plank beside Ice, where Petra had sat in the morning, and they didn't say anything either.

Gigichi watched them from the hedge.

"Is he going to do it?" said Thea, beside her. Thea had come out of the dusk the way she did now, without a sound, reading something nobody else could see, with her eyes half shut. "The third move. Is he going to do it tonight?"

"No," said Gigichi.

"How do you know?"

"Because he's sitting down," said Gigichi. "You don't sit down next to somebody you're about to make move."

Thea looked at her.

It was a look Gigichi knew. It was the look Thea used to give her across a table in a café in another life, when Gigichi said something true without trying and Thea hadn't expected her to. They'd been best friends once, on Earth, years ago. Then they'd drifted. Nobody had done anything. It had just happened, the way a boat drifts if nobody's holding the rope. And then a summoning had put them in the same long room for two years and a half, and they'd been polite.

"No," said Thea, slowly. "You don't."

They stood at the gap together and watched two men sit on the end of a jetty in the dusk and not say anything, and didn't say anything either. It was the longest they'd stood next to each other in a year.

***

Gabe and Deston came up the river the day after, on the *Patient Widow*, with Haskett roaring at the steering oar and his two boys on the poles and the whole of Wrenhaven down on the mill quay to see who'd come.

Gigichi heard Haskett from the hedge. Everybody did. A mile and a bit across the bottom of the lake, with the wind from the south, *HOLD HER, YOU USELESS* coming over the water like a dog barking in another valley; and then, a long time later, Gabe and Deston walking down the last of the lake road in the afternoon heat, Gabe with his pack on one shoulder and a hammer on the other.

It was a big hammer. It was the biggest hammer Gigichi had ever seen a person carry like it was nothing. A sledge, the head the size of a loaf, on a handle as long as her arm, black from a forge and worn pale where his hands went.

"It's from Harl's," said Gabe, when he saw her looking. He went red. "For the fire."

"You brought a sledgehammer," said Gigichi, "for a cooking fire."

"It's a *forge*," said Gabe. "You've got to keep a forge right or it eats the coal." He looked down the shingle at the hearth under its sailcloth roof. His whole face changed. "Oh," he said, quietly. "It's happy. It's still happy." And he went down the shingle to it with the hammer on his shoulder and didn't come back for an hour.

Deston stopped at the gap in the hedge.

He stood there with his pack still on and his eyes shut, and his head a little on one side, the way he stood now when he was listening; and Gigichi watched his face go slowly, from the top down, into something she didn't have a word for.

"What," she said.

"Fifteen," said Deston. He didn't open his eyes. "All in one place. All gold. And the lake." He was smiling. "The lake's got a note. Did you know that? It's very low. Under everything. I can hear it from here." He opened his eyes and looked at the hedge, all the way round. "And this. This has got one too. It's yours. It's—" He stopped. "It's like somebody humming with their mouth shut. So nobody'll hear."

Gigichi looked at him for a long time.

"Get inside," she said. "You're letting the flies in."

***

The first dinner was that night.

He wouldn't let anybody help. That was the first thing. Gigichi had known he wouldn't, and had organised it anyway, and been told no by the forge, very kindly, five times, by five different people who'd gone down to offer. Alea was allowed, because Alea had always been allowed. And Gabe, because Gabe had to keep the fire and nobody was going to tell Gabe how to keep a fire. Everybody else was sent back up the shingle to the hedge to sit at the table and wait.

The table was Edric's long board from the bakehouse on Garrow's trestles, with Odette's cloths on it, three of them, end to end, white, with lace at the edges that had come off a bridal veil once, Gigichi had been told, and Gigichi had not been told whose. There were benches from the militia hall. There were fifteen places. Higu had counted them twice.

They sat at it in the last of the light, in the green room with no roof, with the briar flowers coming out white along the top of the walls and the lake through the gap going silver, and waited. Group 1 along one side. Group 6 along the other, and Petra at the end, and Deston, and two places left for Gabe and Alea, and one at the head that nobody would sit in.

The smell came up the shingle first.

Gigichi didn't know what it was. She'd never been to Malacca. She'd never been to Malaysia; she'd never been further east on Earth than a convention in a city she didn't remember the name of. She knew the list. Everybody knew the list now; Wendy had said it out loud by a hearth in a fort on the edge of the Redmarch, and it had gone round the channel in about a minute and a half, and Higu had copied it into the book. *One. Chicken rice balls. Not the famous place. The one behind it. Trust*.

She didn't know what that smelled like. Now she did.

Ginger. Something like ginger. And chicken, and a rich steaming smell of rice cooked in something that wasn't water, and garlic, and a sharp green smell she couldn't name, and under all of it, faint, something hot and red and brown that made the back of her nose prickle.

Across the table, RX had stopped talking.

He'd been talking all afternoon. He'd talked through the hedge and through supper-time and through Gabe coming back up the shingle black to the elbows, and through Higu counting the places. And the smell came up the shingle in the dusk, and RX stopped. Mid-word. With his mouth a little open.

Beside him, Malcolm had put his book down.

Xav hadn't moved. He was sitting very still with his chin on his fist, looking at the gap in the hedge where the smell was coming from. His face didn't do anything. That was how Gigichi knew.

Then Alea came up the shingle with the first of it on a board. And Ice behind her with the rest.

They were balls. Small, round, white. Rice, pressed into balls the size of a walnut, glistening, piled up on Odette's plates in little pyramids. And chicken beside them, pale and soft and shining, cut in slices across the bone, with something green scattered over it. And three small bowls, of three different things: one red, one brown, one yellow-green.

He put them down. All of them. Down the middle of the table, on the white cloths, one plate at a time, without saying anything. He didn't sit down. He stood at the end of the table by the place nobody would sit in, with his hands at his sides.

Nobody moved.

"It's the one behind the famous place," said RX. His voice had gone strange. "Isn't it. It's the one behind."

"It's as near as I could get," said Ice. "The chicken's wrong. They don't have the right chicken here. And the rice is wrong. And I had to guess the ginger." He was quiet. "The chilli's right. Salty's paste. That's right."

RX picked up a rice ball.

He held it for a long time. Turning it in his fingers. Then he put it in his mouth, the whole thing, and chewed; and Gigichi watched a man she'd known for two years and a half as the loudest person on any road, the one who went invisible to scare people and laughed when they jumped, sit at a long table in a hedge on a lake shore in another world and put his hand over his eyes.

He didn't say anything. He sat there with his hand over his eyes and his jaw working and his shoulders very still.

Malcolm picked one up. Then Xav.

Xav ate his slowly. He didn't put his hand over his eyes. He looked at the man standing at the end of the table, the whole time, while he ate it, with his chin on his fist; and when he'd finished he said, quietly, in a voice Gigichi had never heard him use:

"*Ten ringgit*."

Ice laughed.

The short one. The one that went up at the end.

"Twelve now," he said. "Probably. It's been ten years."

"*Twelve*," said Malcolm. "Daylight robbery."

"Daylight *robbery*," said RX, from behind his hand, thickly; and then all three of them were laughing, Group 6, the three of them who'd known him at seventeen, laughing at a long table in a hedge with their mouths full, at a joke about a price that nobody else understood and that didn't matter; and the man at the end of the table stood there with his hands at his sides and his face doing whatever it did, and Gigichi, who couldn't see it, could see his shoulders.

That was the first dinner.

***

The second was asam pedas, and everybody cried.

Not because of anything. Because of the asam pedas. *It's sour and it's spicy and you will cry*. It was a fish stew, red, thin, sour as unripe plums and hot as a slap, with a whole fish from the lake in it cut in pieces and something like okra, and it came up the shingle in Garrow's biggest pot carried between Gabe and Alea, steaming, and fifteen people ate it and fifteen people cried.

Gabe said he didn't. Gabe was red to the hair and had tears running down both sides of his face into his collar.

"I'm not *crying*," said Gabe. "It's the steam."

"*Wendy will not cry*," read Higu, out of the book, calmly, with her eyes streaming. "*Salty will cry*."

Petra ate three bowls and didn't cry at all.

Everybody looked at her.

"I was on the border," said Petra. "We ate worse than this to stay warm." She held out her bowl. "More."

***

Gigichi watched them eat.

She always did. She'd done it on Earth, at conventions, at the long tables after, in other people's kitchens; she'd done it in the militia hall every night for two years and a half without anybody noticing, or if they noticed, without anybody saying, which was the same thing. Who ate. Who didn't. Who pushed it round the plate and said they weren't hungry, which meant something; who ate too fast, which meant something else. Who forgot.

PenguChi forgot. Thea forgot when she was reading something. Xola never forgot but sometimes ate only the bits she liked and had to be watched about the rest. Higu ate exactly the same amount every night, which was its own kind of thing.

She watched the new ones too, now. She couldn't help it. Deston ate slowly and listened to everybody while he did it. Gabe ate like a forge. RX ate fast and talked through it, the way people do who grew up at a table where you had to talk fast to be heard. Malcolm ate one thing at a time, all of it, before he started the next. Azac ate with his back to the slope-side gap and his face to the room, and Gigichi didn't think he knew he was doing it. John forgot, because he was filming. Xav ate everything and looked at the man at the end of the table the whole time.

Petra ate like a soldier. Quick and complete and without looking at it.

Alea ate like her father. Gigichi had noticed that in the first winter. The same small careful way of holding the bowl, close, in both hands, as if somebody might take it.

And Ice didn't eat at all.

He cooked. He stood at the end of the table and watched them eat. And then he went back down the shingle to the forge, and Gigichi, who watched everybody eat, watched him in the firelight under the sailcloth roof, after, when the table had gone loud again: sitting on Garrow's old bucket by the hearth with the dregs of the pot and a spoon, eating what was left, alone, slowly, out of the pot, with his back to them.

She'd seen him eat at his own table with Alea, and with the five of them, a handful of times. Never with more than that. Never when it was a dinner.

She didn't say anything. She didn't put it in the channel. She went back to her own bowl. But she wrote it on the inside of her wrist that night, in the hedge, by the light of nothing, with a stub of pencil she kept there, the way she wrote the things she couldn't put anywhere else: *he eats last. by himself. out of the pot*.

***

The third was satay celup, and it nearly started a war.

*You cook it yourself in a pot of peanut sauce. Salty, don't be scared of the pot*.

The pot was the problem.

It was one pot. In the middle of the table, on a ring of hot stones Gabe had carried up from the forge in a bucket, bubbling, thick and brown and sweet and hot, with a smell like every good thing at once. And round it, in heaps on Odette's plates, little skewers: fish and chicken and mushroom and something Ice wouldn't name that turned out to be eel from the deep end of the lake, and quail eggs from Robin's mother's quails, and cubes of something pressed and white, and greens. You took a skewer and you put it in the pot and you held it there until it was done and you ate it.

Fifteen people. One pot.

It started with Malcolm.

Malcolm had rules. Malcolm always had rules. He'd written *IF HE COMES BACK FOR THE TENTH, THEN* in a notebook in Duskhollow, and he had rules for the pot too, and he explained them, very reasonably, in order: no double-dipping, a maximum of two skewers per person in the pot at once, and if your skewer fell off its stick into the sauce you weren't allowed to go fishing for it with someone else's.

RX said that was the stupidest set of rules he'd ever heard and that in Malacca nobody had rules for the pot, the pot was a free country.

Malcolm said that was because in Malacca there were four of them at a table and not fifteen.

Xola said she'd lost a mushroom in the pot already and wanted it back and that it was a *rescue*, not fishing.

Gabe said if everyone would stop stirring it with their skewers the stones would hold the heat better.

Thea said, reasonably, that what they needed was a queue system, and started to describe one.

Higu began writing down everybody's position.

Deston said everyone was talking at the same time and it sounded like a choir with no conductor, and he meant it nicely, and nobody took it nicely.

Alea said that her father had cooked this for four people at Cinderhold and there'd been no rules and Salty had dropped a whole skewer in on the first night and *nobody had died*.

John was filming.

Petra was eating.

Azac said nothing and put his arm round the pot, quietly, on the side away from RX.

And Ice stood at the end of the table and said nothing at all, and Gigichi, who was watching him, saw his shoulders, and saw that he was trying very hard not to laugh.

And then the big red hen got on the table.

Nobody saw her come in. She must have come through the lake-side gap in the hedge, from the jetty, in the dusk, the way the hens went everywhere now as if they owned it. She was on the bench. Then she was on the table, between Higu's book and Gabe's elbow, enormous, red, with her head going from side to side; and she looked at the pot, and the skewers in their heaps, and fifteen people arguing at the tops of their voices; and she put her head down and went for the quail eggs.

The table exploded.

Xola shrieked. Gabe said *NO* and reached for the hen and the hen went for his hand. RX laughed so hard he fell sideways off the bench into the briar and said a great many things in Malay that Gigichi was glad she didn't understand. Malcolm stood up to restore order and knocked over the rice. Thea was still describing the queue system. Higu was writing *hen* in the book very fast. Somebody's skewer fell in the pot. The hen got an egg and ran down the middle of the table with it, on Odette's white cloths, scattering skewers, with Gabe and Alea and Xola all reaching for her from different sides and none of them getting her, and Deston was laughing with his hands over his ears, and Petra had picked up her plate and was holding it up out of the way and going on eating.

Gigichi sat very still at her end of the table, in the middle of all of it, and watched.

And PenguChi said:

"*Eat*."

***

She didn't say it loudly.

Gigichi would remember that, after. She'd remember it for the rest of her life. PenguChi didn't raise her voice. She'd never raised her voice in two years and a half, not once, not at a demon, not at a guard, not at a snowstorm. She was sitting where she'd been sitting all evening, at the middle of the Group 1 side, with Waddles asleep in her lap; and she put her skewer down on her plate, and looked up, at all of it, the hen and the pot and the fifteen people and the rice on the cloth, and said one word. In her ordinary voice. The slow, calm, steady one, the one that had always made you feel, if you were frightened, that somebody had put a hand on your back.

*Eat*.

And the table sat down.

All of it. All at once. Not because she'd told them to; she hadn't told them anything. Because something went out from her with the word. Gigichi felt it go past. Not magic; not the way her own *Briar* felt, green and slow and coming up through the ground. This was the other thing, the aura thing, the thing Alea had taught PenguChi in the first week: low and warm and from the very bottom of you, the way you'd breathe out. It went out from PenguChi across the table like a breath going out across water, and where it went, people sat down.

Malcolm sat down. Thea stopped describing the queue. RX climbed back out of the briar and sat on the bench and picked up a skewer. Gabe sat down with his bitten hand in his lap. Xola sat down. Alea sat down. Deston took his hands off his ears.

The hen stopped in the middle of the table.

She stood there on Odette's cloth with a quail egg in her beak and her head on one side, looking at PenguChi. And then she walked. Down the table, between the plates, unhurried, stepping over a skewer, past Higu's book; and stepped off the edge of the table into PenguChi's lap, beside Waddles, and turned round twice, and sat. With the egg. And closed her eyes.

Nobody said anything.

Fifteen people sat at a long table in a hedge in the dusk, with the pot bubbling in the middle and the lake through the gap going dark, and ate. Quietly. Each with a skewer in the pot, or two. Nobody counted. Nobody fished for anything. Nobody needed a queue. You put your skewer in the pot and held it there and it was done and you ate it, and somebody else put theirs in, and it was fine. It had always been fine.

Gigichi ate.

She looked down the table at PenguChi, with a hen and a penguin asleep in her lap and her face quite calm and a little puzzled, picking up her skewer again as if nothing had happened.

Then she looked at the end of the table.

Ice wasn't trying not to laugh any more. He was standing very still by the place nobody sat in, with his hands at his sides, looking at PenguChi. Gigichi couldn't see his face. She could see the rest of him. And the rest of him was doing what it did when he felt somebody's candle go up a step, a thing Gigichi had seen before, at the Lists and the long table at the Lance: going still. Completely still. The way a man goes still at the end of a jetty when the line goes taut.

Gigichi put her skewer down.

"Pengu," she said. "Your window."

PenguChi looked at her. Puzzled. Then she put her hand into the air beside her, the way you did, and opened it.

PenguChi looked at it for a long time.

"Oh," she said. In the same voice. Very softly. "Oh. It went."

***

Nobody exploded this time.

That was the strange thing, Gigichi thought, after. When PenguChi had gone Drawn in the first year, in the militia hall yard, the whole yard had gone up. Xola shrieking, Bram knocking his bucket over, Gigichi herself shouting *finally, FINALLY*. They'd been so loud they'd frightened the horses.

Nobody shouted now. They couldn't. *Settle* was still in the air, round the table, low and warm, the way the heat stays in a room after the fire's gone down; and fifteen people sat in it and looked at PenguChi with their skewers in their hands and smiled at her, all of them, in a kind of soft astonished silence, and PenguChi looked back at them with the hen in her lap and went pink to the ears.

"Sorry," she said. "I didn't mean to. You were all so loud."

"Don't apologise," said Thea. Her eyes were wet. "Don't you dare."

Gigichi didn't say anything. She reached across the table, past Higu's book and a plate of eel, and put her hand on PenguChi's wrist, flat, for as long as it takes to count to three; and took it back, and picked up her skewer, and put it in the pot.

At the end of the table, Ice went back down the shingle to the forge without a word. Gigichi watched him go. He sat down on Garrow's bucket by the hearth with his back to them, in the firelight under the sailcloth, and didn't move for a long time.

She didn't think he was eating.

Gigichi sat in her hedge that night after everybody else had gone to sleep.

Not in the big room. In the old ring, at the top of the slope, among the pumpkins, on the upturned bucket, where she'd always sat. She could hear them below her, through the briar: fifteen people asleep on bedrolls round the edge of a green room under the stars. Somebody snoring; Gabe, probably. Somebody talking in their sleep, in Malay. The lake, very faintly, against the shingle.

PenguChi came up the slope in the dark and sat down on the ground beside the bucket, with Waddles under one arm.

They sat there.

"I'm still on Spark," said Gigichi, after a while.

"I know."

"I've been on Spark for ever. Eighty-six percent. Eighty-six for a month." She looked at the lake. "Higu's Candle. Xola's going to be next; she's close and pretending she isn't. Thea's practically Torch. Now you." She folded her arms. "I'm going to be last. Out of the five. I know I am."

PenguChi didn't say anything.

"Somebody has to be," said Gigichi. "It's fine. I'm not saying it's not fine. Somebody has to be last, and it's going to be me, because I'm the one who watches. You can't watch and go up at the same time. You have to stop watching." She was quiet. "I don't know how to stop watching."

PenguChi was quiet for a long time. She had her hand on Waddles's head. Below them, in the room with no roof, the briar flowers had closed for the night, white along the top of the walls in the starlight like a line of small sleeping birds.

"Deston said it hums," said PenguChi.

"What does?"

"Your hedge." PenguChi looked down at it. "He said it hums with its mouth shut. So nobody'll hear." She looked at Gigichi. "That's not somebody who's last. That's somebody who's been going up the whole time with her mouth shut."

Gigichi didn't answer.

"You made a room," said PenguChi, "for fifteen people to eat in. Nobody asked you. You just made it." She stood up, slowly, with Waddles. "That's not watching, Gigi. That's the other thing."

She went back down the slope in the dark.

Gigichi sat on her bucket in her hedge and looked at the lake for a long time. At the end of the jetty, the lamp was lit on the last plank. Somebody was sitting beside it. She couldn't see who. She didn't need to.

She took out her stub of pencil and wrote on the inside of her wrist, under *he eats last*, by the light of nothing:

*pengu says it's the other thing*.

And then, after a while, because nobody could see:

*87*.

The fourth dinner was nyonya laksa, with no note, and nobody argued about anything.

The fifth was popiah, and it fell apart, all of it, every one, exactly as promised, in fifteen people's hands at once; and Xav laughed so hard at RX's going down his front that he had to get up from the table and walk round the outside of the hedge twice before he could come back.

The morning after the fifth dinner, Gigichi went down to the cabin with a basket of eggs from Robin's mother and found Ice's list on the wall over the table, on its nail beside the drawing. Higu's pencil, most of it crossed through now in his: *Fish*, *A tree*, *Answer them*, *Ask again in the spring*. Three words left standing. *Twelve dinners. Gabe. East*. And at the bottom, in his own hand, *Fish* again.

Somebody had made five small marks beside *Twelve dinners*. In pencil. Neat. Ticks, like the ones on Thea's frames, like a builder's marks on a beam.

Higu, obviously.

Gigichi looked at the five ticks for a while. Then at the empty space after them. Seven more. Six of them were on Wendy's list, in Higu's book; everybody knew what they were.

The twelfth wasn't.

She'd asked him. Everybody had asked him. Xola had asked him every night. Alea had asked him in a voice that meant she already knew he wouldn't say. RX had tried to bribe him with the last of the satay. And every time he'd said the same thing, standing at the end of the table with his hands at his sides, in the voice he used for things that weren't jokes:

"Owed."

Gigichi put the eggs on the table. She looked at the list again.

Then she took the stub of pencil out of her cardigan sleeve and, very small, beside *Twelve dinners*, after the five ticks and the seven spaces, where nobody would notice it unless they knew to look, she drew a little briar. Three lines and some thorns. Just to say she'd been there.

It was a hedge. It was just a hedge.

She went back up the slope to see about the table.
