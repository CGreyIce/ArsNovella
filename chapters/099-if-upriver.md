Malcolm had kept saying *next year*.

It had been a joke, at first. On Earth, after Declan moved to Malacca. *We should visit.* *Next year.* And then it had stopped being a joke and become a habit, and then a sort of promise he made to himself every January and broke every December, and then Declan had died in his sleep and there wasn't a next year anymore, and Malcolm had stood at the back of a room full of people in black and thought: *I said next year three times*.

He'd told the others about it on the river road, the first week, round a fire. *I kept saying next year.* They'd all gone quiet. Rachael, who didn't touch people, had moved along the log until she was sitting right beside him, and stayed there.

He hadn't said *next year* about anything since. Not once. Not in a year and a half.

He wrote things down instead.

***

The book had started in the first winter, in Duskhollow, with Azac in bed and a crack in the ceiling. A cheap notebook from the guild store, and a pencil, and one line at the top of the first page: *IF Azac steps in front when he shouldn't.* And under it: *THEN Malcolm puts the backup on him first.*

It had grown. It always grew. Every possibility he could think of, every way a fight could go wrong, every way a plan could fail, written down on the left side of the page with *IF* in front of it, and on the right side, the answer. *THEN.* What to do. Who goes where. What backup goes on whom. He'd filled one notebook by the summer and started another.

And at the back, on a page on its own: *WORST CASE*.

He'd written it the night before Orla Fane's expedition went down through the oak-and-iron door below the first ring. *IF the circle under the Hollow is the same kind of circle as the one that brought us here, THEN somebody is summoning. And they didn't ask either.* He'd been right. Eleven people had walked into that circle and not come out.

He'd written a THEN page after that. *THEN John sees further. THEN Azac's shield holds. THEN RX learns to not be there. THEN Xav waits longer.* And at the bottom, the only one that mattered: *THEN nobody goes anywhere alone.*

Azac had read it and called it the most depressing thing he'd ever read, and then asked for a copy.

Malcolm had eleven pages for upriver. He'd written them all winter. He read them every night, on the deck of the *Patient Widow*, by Emrys's lantern.

They didn't help much. They never did. But they were on paper, and not in his head, and Pryce had been right about that.

***

The country changed above Duskhollow.

Malcolm noticed it on the second day. Below the town, the Wren ran wide and slow and brown between green fields, with villages every few miles and ferries and fishing boats and children on the banks waving at the barge going past upstream under a sail that should have been empty. Above it, the fields stopped.

The river narrowed. The banks went up. Forest came down to the water on both sides, dark, close, pine and birch, still bare from the winter, with old snow lying in the hollows under the trees where the sun didn't reach. The villages got fewer. Smaller. A cluster of roofs at a ford. A mill. A jetty with one boat tied to it.

Haskett had stopped talking.

He'd talked the whole way up from Kingsferry. About the river, about grain, about the price of salt, about his sons, who were idiots, and his late wife, who hadn't been. He stood at the tiller of the *Patient Widow* now with his pipe unlit in his teeth and his eyes on the banks and didn't say anything at all.

"How far does the grain trade go?" Malcolm asked him, on the third morning.

"Used to go to Mallow's Ford," said Haskett. He didn't take his eyes off the bank. "Three days north of here. I took grain up every autumn for thirty years." He was quiet. "Not last autumn. Nor the one before. I came up two summers ago and the jetty at Mallow's Ford was empty and the whole village was standing on the bank watching me come in, and nobody said a word." He shifted the pipe in his teeth. "I didn't tie up. I turned round and went home and I've not been north of Duskhollow since."

Malcolm looked at him.

"Visiting," said Haskett. "That's what they say, downriver. *Gone visiting.*" He spat over the side. "Mallow's Ford's the next village after the Narrows."

***

Malcolm wrote it down. *IF Mallow's Ford, THEN we don't stop.*

It was the first thing he'd written in the book that he was absolutely sure of.

***

They had rules. Malcolm had written most of them, in the winter, and Petra and Sabine had read them on the first night out of Duskhollow and crossed out three and added two of their own, and now they were the rules.

Nobody went anywhere alone. Not to wash, not to piss, not to look at the stars. Not ever.

Rachael was never more than an arm's length from two people. One of them was always Gabe or Azac. At night she slept in the middle of the deck with everyone round her, and Petra or Sabine awake at the bow.

Deston looked at every face on every bank they passed, for threads.

Dennis listened. For bells. For anyone on the banks who said anything at all. Mostly, nobody did.

John watched the light. He'd been watching it since the morning they left Duskhollow, when the Hollow flared white behind them and he'd gone the colour of paper. He stood at the stern every evening as the sun went down and watched the banks, the way you'd watch for the first star. Not for stars. For the other thing. The wrong light Hyvred wrote about from Thornwall, in the people who'd been turned. *A lamp with a hand inside it.*

And Malcolm wrote it all down. Every bank. Every village. Every face. Every night.

***

They passed the first one on the fourth day.

A village on the east bank, at a bend. Twenty roofs. A jetty. A church with a little square tower. Smoke from three of the chimneys, thin, grey, going straight up into the still afternoon.

No dogs.

Malcolm noticed that before anything else, and was angry with himself for noticing it second-hand, because it was the thing Hyvred had written in the first winter, and Kei, and Haskett: *no dogs*. Every village on the Wren had dogs. They'd been barking at the *Patient Widow* all the way up from Kingsferry, from every jetty and every bank, furious at a barge going the wrong way. This village was silent.

And there were people on the bank.

Standing. At the water's edge, below the jetty, in a row. Men and women in plain farm clothes, and a few children, and an old man with a stick. Twenty of them, perhaps. Standing perfectly still, side by side, with their hands at their sides, facing the river.

Watching the barge come round the bend.

***

Nobody on the *Patient Widow* said anything.

Calvin, on the crosstree, had stopped laughing. The tailwind went on carrying them. The sail stayed full. But Calvin had gone very still up there with his arms round the mast, and Malcolm could see his face from the deck, and it was white.

Petra stood up at the bow. Very slowly. Sabine beside her. Neither of them drew. They just stood, with their swords in their hands and the points down, the way you stand when you're not threatening anything and are ready to do something about it.

"Keep moving," said Petra, very quietly. "Nobody look at them too long. Keep moving."

Malcolm looked anyway. He couldn't help it.

They were watching. All of them. Their heads turned, very slowly, together, the way a field of sunflowers turns, as the barge went past. Following it. Their faces were ordinary. Farmers' faces. A woman with a basket on her hip. A boy of about ten with a cap pulled down over his ears. The old man with the stick. Ordinary.

Except that they didn't blink. And they didn't move anything but their heads. And they didn't breathe, Malcolm thought, watching the old man's chest. Or not often. Or only when they remembered.

***

"No threads," said Deston. Barely a breath. "None of them. Not one."

"Their light's wrong," said John, at the stern. His voice was very flat. "All of it. Every one. Like Hyvred said. Like a lamp with a hand inside it." He swallowed. "The boy. With the cap. His is the worst."

Dennis didn't say anything. He was standing at the rail very still, with his ledger shut against his chest, listening. Nobody on the bank said a word. There was nothing to listen to.

And Rachael was in the middle of the deck, between Gabe and Azac, with Maud's needle case in both hands, held against her chest.

They were looking at her.

Malcolm saw it. He'd been watching their heads turn, all together, following the barge, and he saw it happen: the moment their eyes stopped following the barge, and started following *her*. Twenty pairs of eyes on the bank that didn't blink, all of them, at once, settling on a woman in the middle of the deck with a needle case in her hands.

The boy with the cap smiled.

***

Gabe moved.

He didn't do anything. He just stepped sideways, one step, so that he was standing between Rachael and the bank. Very large. Very still. With his blade across his back and his arms at his sides. The way he'd stood in front of the ward door in Kingsferry on the night the hunter came. *Not past me.*

Azac stepped the other way, so that Rachael was between them, and swung his shield off his back and set it on the deck in front of her, upright, without a word.

The heads on the bank followed the barge round the bend. Slowly. All together. Until the trees came down to the water and hid them.

Nobody spoke for a long time after that.

***

Malcolm wrote it down that night.

He sat on a coil of rope by Emrys's lantern, with the *Patient Widow* tied up to a big pine on the west bank, as far from any village as Haskett could find, and Petra awake at the bow and Sabine awake at the stern and everyone else lying on the deck in a heap round Rachael, not sleeping. He wrote it all. The village. The twenty on the bank. No dogs, no threads, wrong light. The heads turning. The eyes settling on Rachael. The boy with the cap, smiling.

*IF the turned are on the upper Wren*, he wrote, *THEN the west reaches further than anybody thought.*

He looked at that for a long time.

Hyvred had written it from Thornwall in the first winter: *they're not only coming from the east*. Corvin had pinned it on a map for two years: villages going quiet in the western passes. Haskett had seen it on the western tributaries, two summers ago. The external power, the thing beyond the Greyspines, the absence at the foot of the pass. Everyone had been looking west.

And here they were, a hundred miles east of the mountains, on the bank of the river that ran straight down the middle of the kingdom, standing in rows, watching the boats go by.

*THEN*, he wrote, and stopped.

He didn't know what came after *THEN*. For the first time in a year and a half, he didn't know.

***

"Mal."

It was Deston. He'd come and sat down on the rope beside him, in the lantern light, very quietly, the way Deston came and sat down beside people.

"You've stopped writing," said Deston.

"I don't know what the THEN is."

Deston looked at the page. He couldn't read Malcolm's writing; nobody could, it was a pessimist's handwriting, small and cramped and tilted, as if it were trying to get off the page before something bad happened. But he looked at it anyway.

"Can I tell you something?" said Deston.

Malcolm waited.

"The thread," said Deston. He put his hand out, flat, palm down, over the side of the barge, pointing north. Into the dark, up the river, past the bend, past the trees. "It's so warm, Mal. I can't tell you how warm. It's warmer than it's ever been. Every mile. It's like holding your hand over a fire." He was quiet. "I don't know what's up there. I don't know how far. I don't know what's between here and there, apart from a gorge Haskett won't talk about and a village nobody's come back from." He looked at Malcolm. "But I know who's at the end of it."

Malcolm didn't say anything.

"You kept saying *next year*," said Deston.

Malcolm looked away.

"You told us," said Deston gently. "On the river road. The first week. Round the fire. *I kept saying next year.*" He was quiet a moment. "I said it too, you know. Not *next year*. I said *soon*. *We'll call soon.* I said it to him the week before. On the phone. *Let's call properly soon.*" His hand, over the side of the barge, closed slowly. "He said *trust*."

Malcolm looked at him.

"So you don't have to know the THEN," said Deston. "Not tonight. Not for this one." He nodded at the page. "Just write the IF. And the IF is: *IF he's up there*." He let his hand drop. "And we both already know the THEN for that one."

***

Malcolm sat there for a long time after Deston went to lie down.

The lantern hissed. The river went past in the dark, quiet, black, going south. Petra at the bow, a shape against the stars, not moving. Sabine at the stern, the same. Calvin asleep at the foot of the mast with his arms still wrapped round it, the way he'd slept every night since Ferrow's Bend. Gabe and Azac on either side of Rachael. Xav lying on his back with his eyes open, looking at the sky, waiting. RX somewhere; Malcolm couldn't see him, which meant he was there. John at the stern, beside Sabine, watching the banks for wrong light.

Ten of the thirty-five. On one deck. In the dark, on a river, going north.

He turned to a clean page in the book.

He wrote *IF he's up there* at the top, in his small cramped tilted handwriting.

And under it, on the right, where the THEN went, he wrote something he'd never once written in either notebook. Not a plan. Not a backup. Not a position or an enchantment or a contingency.

*THEN no more next year.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he went back to the other page. The one he'd stopped on. *IF the turned are on the upper Wren, THEN the west reaches further than anybody thought.* And under it, where he'd stopped, where he hadn't known what came after *THEN*, he wrote the only thing he'd ever really known, the line from the bottom of the WORST CASE page that Azac had asked for a copy of.

*THEN nobody goes anywhere alone.*

***

Haskett told them about the Narrows the next morning.

He'd been putting it off. Malcolm could tell. He stood at the tiller in the grey dawn with his pipe unlit and his eyes on the river ahead, where the banks were getting higher and steeper and the forest was giving way to rock, and the water was running faster and darker between them.

"Gallow Rock," said Haskett. "That's what they call it. The Narrows at Gallow Rock." He took the pipe out of his teeth. "River goes into the hills there. Through a gorge. Half a mile long, maybe more. Cliffs both sides, sixty feet, a hundred. River's half the width it is here and twice as fast." He looked up at Calvin, on the crosstree. "And no wind. Not in the gorge. Cliffs are too high. Wind goes over the top and never comes down."

Calvin went pale.

"So we pole it," said Haskett. "And the towpath. There's a ledge along the west cliff, cut into the rock, for the towing horses. Narrow. Wet. We put the ropes on the ledge and the boys and anyone strong on the ropes, and we pull her through, a foot at a time." He looked at the cliffs coming up ahead, grey in the dawn. "Half a day. If nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?" said Sabine.

Haskett put the pipe back in his teeth.

"Then you'd better have a plan," he said, "because I haven't."

***

Everybody looked at Malcolm.

He didn't know why. He supposed it was because he was the one with the book. He was sitting on his coil of rope with the notebook open on his knees, at the first of the eleven pages he'd written all winter for upriver. *IF the river narrows.*

He looked at it. Then at the cliffs, coming up ahead in the grey light. Then at the nine others on the deck, and Emrys and Sabine and Petra, and Haskett and his boys. And Rachael, in the middle, with Maud's needle case in her hands.

"I have a plan," said Malcolm.

It was eleven pages long. Most of it was wrong. He knew that. Plans always were.

But it was on paper, and not in his head. And at the bottom of every page, in his small cramped tilted handwriting, he'd written the same thing.

*THEN nobody goes anywhere alone.*

He started to read it out.
