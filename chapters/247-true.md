Dennis heard lies.

He'd heard them all his life, a little. Everybody did; you learned, if you grew up the way he had, which aunties meant it and which teachers didn't and which boys at the back of the bus were going to be kind and which were going to be something else. On Earth it had been a knack. Here it was a sound. The first week in the castle, a stipend clerk had looked up from a ledger and said *that's the full amount*, and Dennis had heard it ring: a flat struck note with a buzz under it, a rattle, the way a bell sounds when there's a crack in the bronze. He'd smiled at the clerk until the clerk found the rest.

He'd heard ten thousand since. Little white lies rang thin and high. Kind lies rang soft. Lies you told yourself rang so quietly you had to stand very still to hear them, which was why most people never did. True things didn't ring at all. That was how you knew them: by the quiet.

One man had never rung at all. In two worlds. Not once. Not even when he said *trust*, which was the most untrustworthy word anybody had ever said in Dennis's hearing. *Trust* had rung like a bell made of something that wasn't metal. A joke the size of a lie, wearing a lie's coat, and underneath it, perfectly true.

Dennis had gone up to Malacca once, the year before Declan died, with the others: Gabe and Deston and Rachael and Calvin, on the coach from Singapore. Declan had walked Calvin into the kitchen an hour after they met and shown him the worst kettle in Malaysia like it was a trophy. He'd fed them at a hawker centre at two in the morning. He'd talked the whole time, and Dennis had listened to every word of it, out of habit, for the itch. On Earth there hadn't been bells, only an itch, the way you'd know a coin was bad by the weight.

Nothing. A whole weekend. Not one itch.

He'd thought, on the coach home, that he'd never met anybody that true. He'd been right. He didn't again, until a jetty on Lake Ilse, and a man on the end of it with a stranger's face who said *I've got a kettle*.

***

His own rang.

That was the thing he hadn't told anybody. Not Rachael. Not Sabine, who he told most things, because Sabine had been a mercenary for fifteen years and couldn't be shocked by anything and didn't try to be kind about it. Not Emrys. Not the channel.

His glamours rang.

He'd noticed in the summer. At the Lists, on the main sand, in his first bout, when he'd made three of himself and Milli had gone through the left one like breath on a window and opened his forehead above the eyebrow. He'd lain on his back on the sand with blood running into his ear and the other two going out round him, and he'd heard them go. Three faint flat notes, each with a buzz under it. The rattle. The crack.

His own glamours were lies. Of course they were. That was what a glamour was. You made a thing look like something it wasn't: a wall where the road was, a girl at a window who wasn't there, three Dennises where there was one. He'd been doing it for three years, very well, with a kind of joy; it was the most fun he'd ever had. *Glamour*, his window said. *Spark*. And every one of them, he'd found out at the Lists, had been ringing in his own ears like a cracked bell the whole time, so quietly he'd never stood still enough to hear it.

Once he'd heard it he couldn't stop.

All summer. All the way home up the river from Valcrest. Every glamour he made, a little flat ring at the back of his skull, buzzing. Small ones he could bear. Big ones left his ears ringing for an hour. He'd made a glamour on the barge home to stop a drunk riverman picking a fight with Calvin, a wall of shadow where Calvin was standing, and had to go and lie down in the hold afterwards with his hands over his ears.

He was ninety-four percent to Candle. He'd been ninety-something since the spring, and Spark so long he'd stopped saying it out loud. He was the treasurer. He kept count. He knew exactly how long ninety-four had been ninety-four.

He thought, privately, that it was the ringing. That you couldn't climb on a thing that sounded broken in your own ears every time you used it.

He didn't know what to do about it.

***

"Make one that doesn't ring, then," said Sabine.

He'd told her in the end. Of course he had. He told Sabine everything, eventually; it was a relief, like putting down something heavy on a table that wouldn't break. They were on the river wall at the end of the day, where the guard walked, with the river going past brown and high with the autumn rains and the far quay across the water, and the red-sailed grain barge that had been tied up there as long as Group 4 had been in Kingsferry and never seemed to go anywhere.

"I can't," said Dennis. "It's a glamour. That's what it is. A glamour's a lie."

"Is it?"

"You make a thing look like something it isn't. That's a lie. That's the definition."

Sabine leaned on the wall. She was forty-one and she'd guarded merchant caravans for fifteen years before the crown hired her for the pay, and she'd told Group 4 on the first day that she was here for the pay, and Dennis had heard it ring perfectly true and liked her immediately.

"I've told a lot of lies," said Sabine. "For money. On roads. You'd tell a bandit there were forty of you in the carts when there were six." She looked at the river. "And I've told a few true things that worked like lies. *The bridge is out*, when it was. People didn't believe it. They thought I wanted the toll." She shrugged. "Same words. One rings, one doesn't. The difference isn't the words."

"So what is it?"

"Whether it's so," said Sabine.

He thought about that.

"So make one of something that's so," he said slowly.

"I don't know," said Sabine. "I'm a sword. You're the clever one. Rachael says." And she pushed herself off the wall and went on along it, walking the guard, the way she did every evening at this hour whether there was anything to guard or not.

***

He tried it that night.

In the room he shared with Calvin over the chandler's, by the light of one candle, with Calvin asleep on the other bed with his arm over his face. He sat on the edge of his own bed and held his hand out, palm up, and made a glamour.

Of his own hand.

Exactly as it was. Not three of it. Not bigger. Not a different hand. His own hand, the one he was holding out, in the candlelight, with the ink on the second finger from the ledger and the little white scar on the knuckle from a door at school. He made a glamour of it, laid over it, a hair above it, so that if anyone had been looking they'd have seen his hand and, over it, exactly the same, his hand.

He listened.

Nothing. No ring. No buzz. No crack.

He sat there in the candlelight with a glamour of his own hand over his own hand and felt something go through him like cold water. It didn't ring. He'd made a glamour that didn't ring. For the first time in three years, a glamour that sounded, in his own ears, like nothing at all. Like a true thing.

Then he realised what he'd made.

A mirror. That was all. A glamour of the thing that was there, over the thing that was there, showing exactly what was there. It didn't ring because it didn't do anything. It was a hand, on top of a hand. You couldn't fool a bandit with it. You couldn't make a scout see a wall. You couldn't make anybody see anything they couldn't already see.

He let it go. It went out without a sound.

He lay back on the bed and looked at the ceiling and laughed, quietly, so as not to wake Calvin, because it was very funny and he didn't want to cry about it.

*A true glamour*, he thought, *is just a hand*.

***

He tried it every night for a week.

Different things. A glamour of the candle, over the candle. A glamour of the door, over the door. A glamour of Calvin, asleep, over Calvin, asleep, which was the strangest thing he'd ever done and which Calvin woke up halfway through and said *why are there two of me*, and went back to sleep before Dennis could answer, and didn't remember in the morning.

None of them rang. None of them did anything.

He tried making a true one *bigger*. A candle flame laid over the candle flame, but twice the size; and it rang, at once, cracked, because the candle wasn't twice the size. He tried making it brighter. It rang. He tried making the door look a little further away than it was. It rang. Everything that was even a hair more or less than the thing itself rang; and everything that was exactly the thing itself did nothing.

On the sixth night he sat on the edge of the bed with a ringing headache and his ledger on his knee, not looking at it, and opened his window.

He thought about a man who'd never once rung in two worlds. Who said *trust* in a voice that sounded like a lie and wasn't. Who'd sat on a terrace above a jetty for a whole afternoon with a stranger's face on, and hadn't once pretended to be anything other than what he was. He'd just *been* it. So completely that four people who'd buried him believed it by supper.

He thought: *he never glamoured anybody in his life. He didn't need to. He just made what was there easier to see*.

He sat with that for a long time.

***

The barge came on the eighth day, in fog.

It had been coming for four days. Dennis knew because Deston had written in the channel the morning they left, *on the Widow. eight of us. Haskett says four days down*, and Gabe had written under it *tell Harl to light both hearths*; and Dennis had walked round to the forge and told him, and Harl, who had no eyebrows and hands like spades, had stood in his doorway for a moment saying nothing at all, and gone in and lit both hearths.

So Dennis knew they were coming. He'd been on the river wall at dawn on the eighth day watching for them, because he was the treasurer and it was his barge and he liked to see it come in.

And the fog came up off the river.

It came the way it came at Kingsferry in the autumn, sometimes, when the river was high and warm and the air came down cold off the hills in the night: up off the water in a single grey sheet, all at once, as if somebody had lifted a lid. By the time the sun was up it was thick enough that Dennis couldn't see the far quay from the river wall. By mid-morning he couldn't see the river. He could hear it, going past under the wall, brown and fast and high. He could hear gulls somewhere. He could hear, very faint, from upriver, round the long bend above the town where the Wren came out of the hills, a man calling.

*Haskett*.

He knew the voice. Everybody on the river did. Old Haskett of Fallow's Reach, calling the depth to his boys on the poles, the way bargemen called it, a long rising sing-song that carried. And under it, faint, the knock of poles on a hull.

The *Patient Widow*. In the fog. Coming down.

Dennis stood on the river wall and felt his stomach go.

He knew this river. He'd been the treasurer of a barge on it for two years and he'd had every bend and bar and rock of it explained to him, at length, by Haskett, over a great many cups of something Haskett called tea. The Wren came round the bend above Kingsferry fast and high in the autumn, and the channel ran close under the river wall on the town side, and if you missed the channel you went onto the stones below the old watergate, where the river wall had gone in a flood when Haskett was a boy and never been mended right. The stones were still there. Haskett said a barge that went onto them in the autumn flood went over.

In clear air you steered by the quay lamps. Four of them, on iron posts along the quay, lit every dusk and every fog by the harbourmaster's boy. Haskett said you lined up the second and the third and kept them lined up and you were in the channel all the way.

The harbourmaster's boy had lit them. Dennis could see them, just, from the river wall: four faint yellow smudges in the grey, a little way along, where the quay was.

From the river, from round the bend, in this, Haskett wouldn't see them at all.

***

Calvin came running.

He'd heard it too. He came along the river wall at a sprint with his coat open, the way Calvin went everywhere, and stopped beside Dennis and looked out into the grey where the river was, and listened.

"That's Haskett," said Calvin.

"I know."

"In *this*."

"I know."

"I can blow it," said Calvin. He was already turning his face to the wind, the way he did; he asked it where it wanted to go, before he asked it anything else, because asking against the grain cost him. Dennis watched his face go still, listening. "It's not going anywhere. There isn't any. It's dead calm." He breathed. "I can push it. A bit. Against the grain." His hands were already shaking at the thought. "Not the whole river. A hole. For a heartbeat."

"Try."

Calvin tried.

He put his hands out over the wall and asked the air to move, against itself, against the grain, and Dennis felt it go: a push, a gust, cold, out over the water, and the fog in front of them tore, a little, a hole the size of a cart, and for a heartbeat Dennis saw the brown river going past under the wall and the stones below the old watergate with the water piling white over them, and then the grey came back in and closed, and Calvin sat down on the walk with his hands shaking so hard he put them under his arms.

"Sorry," said Calvin. "Sorry. It's too big. It's the whole river."

Upriver, round the bend, closer now, Haskett called the depth. The poles knocked.

Dennis looked at the lamps.

***

Four faint yellow smudges in the grey. Real. Lit. On their iron posts, along the quay, where they'd always been. The second and the third, in line, marking the channel. Exactly where Haskett needed them to be. Exactly what Haskett needed to see.

They were *there*. That was the thing. They weren't a lie. They didn't need to be anything other than what they were. They just needed to be *seen*.

He'd made a glamour of his own hand over his own hand and it hadn't rung, and it hadn't done anything, because his hand was already as easy to see as it was ever going to be. You could see a hand. You didn't need help.

You couldn't see a lamp in a fog.

He put his hands on the river wall.

He didn't make a lamp. That was the thing he understood, standing there, with Calvin on the walk beside him shaking and Haskett's voice coming down the river out of the grey. If he made a lamp, a new one, a bright one, a glamour of a light where the light wasn't, it would ring. It would be a lie. Haskett would steer by it and it would be a hair in the wrong place, because Dennis wasn't a bargeman and didn't know the channel to a hair, and the barge would go onto the stones.

He didn't make anything.

He took the four lamps that were there, the real ones, the second and the third in line, exactly where they were, exactly as bright as they were; and he made a glamour of them. Over them. The way he'd made a glamour of his own hand over his own hand. Exactly the same. Not brighter. Not bigger. Not a hair to one side. Them.

And then he did the thing he'd never done, that he didn't have a word for until afterwards. He took the fog out of the glamour.

Not out of the air. Out of the *picture*. He couldn't move the fog; Calvin couldn't move the fog. But a glamour was a picture laid over the world, and in the picture he was making, of four lamps on four iron posts on a quay, there was no fog. There was just the lamps. As they were. As you'd have seen them on a clear night, from a barge, round the bend. He laid the picture over the fog, a long way out, all the way up the river to the bend and round it, where a man would be standing at a steering oar looking for them.

He listened.

It rang.

Not cracked. He'd been ready for the crack, the buzz, the flat broken rattle; he'd been braced for it since the summer. It didn't come.

It rang *whole*.

A single note, clear, struck, going out from somewhere at the back of his skull down his arms into the stone of the river wall and out over the water, the way a bell rings when there's nothing wrong with it. He'd never heard that sound. Not once, in two worlds. True things didn't ring; they were just quiet, and the quiet was how you knew them. Nobody rang a bell to say a true thing. They just said it.

He'd rung one. A true thing, made, and held up where it could be seen.

***

Out on the river, round the bend, Haskett stopped calling.

Dennis heard him stop. The sing-song broke off in the middle, and there was a silence, and then Haskett's voice again, not singing, sharp and loud and astonished, the voice of an old man who's been on a river fifty years and has just seen something he's never seen on it:

"*LAMPS. I've got the LAMPS. Second and third. Lined up. Boys, the LAMPS—*"

And the poles knocked, steady now, not feeling for the bottom any more. And out of the grey, a long way off and then not so far, the low dark shape of a barge, coming down the channel on the current, straight, close under the river wall, exactly where the channel was, the way you'd come down it on a clear night lined up on the second and third lamp.

Past the stones below the old watergate. Past them by twenty feet, with the water piling white over them on the far side and the barge sliding by on the brown.

To the quay.

Dennis held it. He didn't know how long. Calvin said, after, a count of three hundred; Calvin had stopped shaking and stood up and counted, because somebody had to. Dennis held four real lamps in a picture with no fog in it, laid over a mile of river, and it rang whole in his ears the whole time, one clear note, not getting quieter, not cracking. And the *Patient Widow* came down the channel and in under the quay wall and bumped, very gently, against the fenders, and Haskett's elder boy jumped onto the stones with a rope.

Dennis let go.

The note faded. Slowly. The way a good bell fades.

And he sat down on the river wall, quite suddenly, beside Calvin, with his hands flat on the cold wet stone, and found he was crying, and couldn't have said why.

***

Gabe was the first off.

Of course he was. He came up the quay steps two at a time with his big sledge over his shoulder in its sling and his face doing something it did when it had been very frightened and was trying not to show it; and he came along the river wall to where they were sitting and stood over them.

"That was you," said Gabe. Not a question.

"That was me."

"We were going onto the stones. Haskett said. He'd lost the channel at the bend. He said *we're going on the watergate stones* to his boys in a very calm voice and his boys went white." Gabe crouched down in front of him. "And then the lamps were there. Right there. Clear as a summer night. Second and third, lined up. Haskett said he'd never seen them so clear in fifty years, and he looked up and there wasn't any fog round them at all, and there was fog everywhere else." He looked at Dennis. "Haskett's boy hung a lantern on the bow and the fog ate it a yard out. And then there were the lamps. Like somebody'd cut them out of the fog and held them up."

"I didn't make them," said Dennis. "They were there. I just—" He didn't have the word yet. "Made them easier to see."

Deston came up the steps behind Gabe, tall and slow, with his eyes half shut, the way he walked when he was listening. He stopped. He looked at Dennis for a long time.

"You rang," said Deston.

Dennis looked at him.

"Down your thread. From round the bend, before the lamps." He sat down on the wall on Dennis's other side. "Your glamours have always rung. Like glass. I heard it at the Lists, the two that weren't you. I never said. I thought it was a thing you'd know about." He was quiet. "That one didn't ring like glass. That one rang like a bell. A whole one. All the way up the river."

Dennis didn't say anything.

He opened his window.

He read it four times. Then he laughed. Out loud, on the river wall, in the fog, so that the harbourmaster's boy on the quay below looked up.

*True*.

Of course it was. Of course it was *True*. The window picked the words out of you, the way it always did, from somewhere you didn't know you kept things. And somewhere at the bottom of him, under three years of making three Dennises and walls where the road was and girls at windows who weren't there, under ten thousand cracked bells, under a weekend in Malacca listening to a man who never once rang, he'd kept that.

Not *lie*. Not *trick*. *True*.

A glamour of what was there. Made easier to see.

"It's a good name," said Calvin, beside him, leaning right in to read it over his shoulder. "It's a very you name. You're going to be insufferable."

"I'm already insufferable."

"You're going to be *more*," said Calvin, and put his still-shaking hands back under his arms, and grinned.

Rachael found him in the evening, on her way back from Maud's bench with her apron still on.

On the river wall, still. He hadn't gone in. He'd sat there all afternoon, watching the river, listening to nothing in particular: the gulls, the water, the harbourmaster's boy whistling, all of it ringing whatever it rang, mostly true, the way most things were if you stood still long enough to hear them.

A king's rider had gone through at noon, in grey, mud to the thigh, asking the harbourmaster's boy for the first boat up to Duskhollow on the king's business, and Dennis had listened, and it hadn't rung.

She sat down beside him. Not close. She never sat close. She sat a little way off along the wall, with her arms folded, the way she sat beside people she cared about, near enough to be there and not near enough to be touched.

"You didn't tell me," said Rachael. "About the ringing. Your own."

"I told Sabine."

"Sabine told me. This morning. When the fog came." She looked at the river. "She said, *he'll do something stupid on the wall, go and watch*. And then you did something clever on the wall, and Sabine said *that's the same thing with Dennis*."

Dennis laughed.

"It's been ringing since the Lists," he said. "Every glamour. Cracked. In my own ears. I didn't know what to do with it. I thought if I told anyone they'd tell me to stop." He looked at his hands. "I didn't want to stop. I love it. It's the most fun I've ever had. Making things look like things."

"I know you do."

"I just didn't know it could be true," said Dennis. "I thought it had to be a lie to be a glamour. And then I made a glamour of my hand over my hand, and it didn't ring, and it didn't do anything, and I thought, *well, that's that*. A true glamour's just a hand." He watched the water. "And then there was fog, and lamps, and Haskett round the bend. And they were *there*. They didn't need lying about. They needed seeing."

Rachael didn't say anything for a while.

"That's what Maud says," she said. "About healing. You don't make anything. You can't make a person. You just help what's already there do what it's already trying to do." "*You make it easier for it*, she says. *That's all a healer is.*"

"That's what I did."

"I know," said Rachael. "I heard Deston say you rang." She was quiet. "I've never heard of a glamour that heals. But I think that's what that was. A barge was going onto the stones and you made it easier for the river to be the river."

Dennis looked at her.

She didn't look back. She looked at the water. But in the last of the light she unfolded one arm and put her hand flat on the stone between them; not touching; a hand's width from his, the way she did.

He put his own beside it. A hand's width away. Not touching either.

They sat there like that until it was dark and the harbourmaster's boy came along the quay below to light the four lamps on their iron posts, the second and third lined up, for the night.

Dennis watched him do it.

He didn't make anything. He didn't need to. There wasn't any fog. They were just lamps, real ones, exactly as bright as they were, exactly where they'd always been, and anybody coming down the river could see them.

He listened to them anyway, out of habit.

They didn't ring at all. They were just true.

***

Gabe didn't come out of Harl's forge until midnight.

Dennis knew, because he walked past it on the way back to the chandler's, and both hearths were lit, and through the open door he could see the two of them: Harl at the bellows, very slowly, the way you'd feed a fire for something small and important; and Gabe at the near anvil, bent over, with a very fine file in one hand and a pair of small tongs in the other, and something in the tongs so small Dennis couldn't see what it was. Grey. Catching the firelight. With something in it, very fine, like frost.

Gabe didn't look up. He didn't say anything. He was looking at the work, the way he always looked at the work.

Dennis stood in the door for a moment.

Then he didn't make a glamour of anything, and didn't say anything, and went home to bed; and on the way, in the dark, on the river street, he heard a cracked bell from a tavern window, a man telling his wife he'd only had the one, and laughed out loud, and found that it didn't bother him at all.
