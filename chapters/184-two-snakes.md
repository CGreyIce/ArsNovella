For eleven days, Hisui talked about nothing but the cord.

Kei had thought, before, that he'd heard Hisui talk. He'd lived with it for two years and a half. A green tree snake round his neck or his wrist or the back of his chair, who had never once since the first morning in the castle stopped talking, in Kei's head, in a small grumpy voice like a kettle that had opinions, every waking minute. About the air, mostly. What it tasted of. What was in it. Who'd been in the room before them, and what they'd had for breakfast, and whether they'd been frightened. Kei had stopped hearing it after the first winter, the way you stop hearing a river if you live next to one. It was just there, under everything. He only noticed it when it stopped.

It had stopped twice. Once when he wrote a word into a cup of something bitter and the window opened in front of his face. Once on the night the wind first changed, coming up off the valley, and Hisui had lifted his head and tasted it and gone completely silent and then said *calling*.

He hadn't known there was a third kind. Where it didn't stop, and didn't stay under everything either, but came up over the top of everything like a river in flood, and stayed there, for eleven days, about one thing.

*When*, said Hisui. *When does it come. The rider. When. How far is Solmere. How fast is the rider. Is it the fast one, the woman, Tamsin, or a slow one. If it's a slow one I'll bite him*.

"You won't bite anyone."

*I'll think about biting him*, said Hisui. *Very hard. He'll feel it*.

***

It came on the eleventh day, in the snow, by a slow one.

A boy of about twenty on a mule, so cold he couldn't feel his hands, who'd come over the last of the open road from Solmere before the drifts shut it, and who'd been told *into Kei's hand* and had asked at the gate for *the one with the green snake*, and been pointed, by every soldier he asked, to the little room behind the guardhouse kitchen where Kei kept his jars.

He stood in the doorway with snow on his shoulders and looked at the jars. Two walls of them. Roots and leaves and bark and powders, and small dark bottles stoppered with wax, and on the top shelf, by themselves, twelve flasks in a row, each one tied at the neck with a green thread.

"What are those?" said the boy.

"Medicine," said Kei.

*Poison*, said Hisui.

"Both," said Kei, and held out his hand.

The boy put the packet in it. Small. Oilcloth, folded, sealed with a drop of purple wax with a cat's paw pressed into it. Kei broke it with his thumbnail.

A cord. Short, knotted, the way Hana knotted all of them, in the patient careful way she'd learned on Earth making charms for a stream and learned again here making them for a kingdom. Green and white, this one. Not her purple and gold. Green twisted round white, tightly, the whole length, so that you couldn't tell which was holding which. And on the end of it, half a bead. Jade, or something like jade, cut clean through the middle, so that the flat face of it caught the lamp.

Round the knot, once, a strip of oiled paper with Cursiffe's blue ink on it. Blank, for now.

And under the cord, folded small, a note. In Hana's hand, which Kei knew from the channel, carefully learned English letters with a little curl on the *y*.

*Kei. For Hisui. The other half goes to the white one; the rider for Cinderhold left an hour after this one, on the fast horse, so it'll be ten days behind you, or a little more if the downs are bad. Green and white because I didn't know which colours snakes like and I thought theirs would do. Tell him to mind his manners. She won't mind hers. H.*

Kei read it out.

Hisui, round his wrist, was silent.

Not stopped. Kei could feel him, against the inside of his wrist, very slightly, the way you feel somebody holding their breath. Then Hisui unwound from Kei's wrist, slowly, and went down his hand, and over his knuckles, and onto the cord. And put his head down on the half-bead. Flat. The way he put his head down on a warm stone in the summer.

And tasted it.

*Cat*, said Hisui, at last. *And ink. And her*. A pause. *The cat one. She held it a long time. She was thinking about us when she made it*. Another pause. *She was thinking about the white one more*.

"That's fair," said Kei.

*It's not fair. It's entirely accurate*, said Hisui. *That's different*.

The boy in the doorway was staring at them. A man in a green coat with a snake lying on a bit of string, talking to it.

"Is it all right?" said the boy. "The—whatever it is? I was told it mattered."

Kei looked down at Hisui on the cord. At his small green head on the half of a bead, very still, with his tongue going in and out, slowly, tasting a cat girl's thoughts.

"It matters," said Kei. "Go and get warm. Tell the kitchen I said you're to have the soup that isn't Bee's."

***

Ten days.

He'd thought eleven had been bad. Ten were worse. Because before there'd been nothing to hold, and now there was a cord on the shelf above his bed with half a bead on it, and a snake who slept on it every night and woke up every hour to taste it and see if it had gone warm.

It hadn't. It wouldn't, not till the other half was in somebody's hand. Kei had explained this. Hisui knew. Hisui tasted it anyway.

*Nothing*, he'd say, at two in the morning, in the dark. *Still nothing*.

"I know."

*How far is Cinderhold*.

"Further than Thornwall is from Solmere. And the downs. And snow on the downs."

*I hate the downs*, said Hisui, who had never been to the downs.

Kei lay in the dark and listened to him hate the downs, and found that he was smiling.

He'd thought about it a good deal, those ten days. He thought about most things a good deal; it was what he did with his evenings, in the little room behind the kitchen, with the jars. People brought him things. They always had, on Earth and here. Not jars; grief. They came and sat on the stool by the door and told him about it, whatever it was, and he listened, and didn't fix anything, and was there the next day too. That was all he'd ever known how to do. He'd learned it from a call at two in the morning, a long time ago, in another world, when a man he didn't know very well had stayed on the line till five while Kei sat on the floor of his room with his back against the bed and didn't say anything at all.

*I'm here tomorrow too*, the man had said, at five, before he hung up. That was all.

Kei had been here tomorrow too, ever since. For everyone. For Tiger, on a shed roof. For Ossie's father, with his hat in his hands. For Drew, three months ago, standing at the shutters at supper with his face lifted to the pass and his eyes gone to somewhere else, while the wind off the valley called him.

And for Yan.

Not here. A kingdom away, at the other end of everything, on a wall above a red plain. But Kei had known Yan longer than he'd known almost anyone. They'd met through Yan, he and Ice, on Earth; it was Yan's call he'd first heard that laugh on. And Kei knew what Yan did with things, because it was what Kei did too. You carried them. Quietly. On your own. Until you'd worked out what they were.

He'd read the channel the night of the song. *Hana's cords carry what you mean. Suzu means things*. And under it, in Yan's careful lines, something Kei had heard before, from people on the stool by his door, in a voice that was steadier than the words.

*I don't know what she's saying*.

***

Thornwall in the winter was very quiet.

It had been loud in the autumn. The wind off the valley, and the dogs barking all night, and Bee's chalk dogs on every wall in the fort barking back. Then the snow had come, and closed the pass, and the wind had gone down under it, and the dogs had stopped, and the whole of the western edge of the kingdom had gone still under a blanket three feet deep.

Hyvred said the absence hadn't moved.

She went up the north tower every morning to look. Kei went with her, most mornings, because Hisui liked the air up there and because somebody ought to. They'd stand at the top of the tower in the cold with the whole valley white below them, all the way to the foot of the pass, and Hyvred would let her sight go out, the gold eye, and look.

The rings were still there. Thirteen of them. Under the snow, so you couldn't see the ground they stood on, but you could see them, the people, standing in the snow to their knees and then to their waists as it got deeper. Not moving. Faces turned to the middle. The green coat in the outermost ring, and the hat man, and a boy in a cap, and an old woman in a widow's shawl with a stick. Thirty-nine who'd come down the pass one at a time in the autumn and filled the last gaps. And in the third ring, freckled, very young, Ossie.

Tiger didn't come up the tower. Tiger had never once come up the tower.

And in the middle of the rings, where they all faced, the absence. Hyvred said it was facing the pass still. Facing out. *Ready*. She said the snow fell through it. Not on it. Through. She'd watched a flake go down through the middle of it and come out the other side and land in the snow below, as if there'd been nothing there at all, and she'd stood on the tower afterwards with her hand over her gold eye for a long time.

*It tastes of nothing*, Hisui said, every morning, at the top of the tower. *Still. It's the only thing in the world that tastes of nothing. Even the snow tastes of something. Iron, here. The snow tastes of iron*.

"I know."

*I don't like it*.

"Nobody likes it."

*The tiger one doesn't come up*, said Hisui. *He's right not to*.

***

The twelve flasks were for that.

Twelve doses. In the little room behind the kitchen, on the top shelf, with a green thread round each neck. *Enough*, Kei had written into the first one, three months ago, at a supper table, with his finger in the air and Drew's face lifted to the shutters and the frost of wrong light going round Drew's head that only Hyvred could see. And the dose had changed in the cup. And Drew had come back.

He'd made eleven more in the three weeks after, one every other day. Each one took him most of a day and most of what he had, and left him lying on his bed afterwards with his eyes shut and Hisui on his chest saying *you look terrible*. Twelve. Enough for twelve people, if the wind came back up off the valley in the spring and called twelve people at once.

It wouldn't be twelve. He knew that. He'd done the sum. He'd watched the wind change in the autumn and call one sentry, and then a groom, and then a woman at the well, and then every dog in Thornwall, and then Drew. A few more every week. And then the snow. If the snow went and the wind came back the way it had been going, it wouldn't be twelve.

He didn't say that to anyone. He made the doses.

Drew came in, the eighth evening, with snow on his shoulders, and sat on the stool by the door.

He did that, now. Not every night. Some nights. He'd come in and sit on the stool with his big bare hands on his knees, the hands he was going to fight with one day when he was Unbound and didn't need the bladed knuckles any more, and not say anything. And Kei would go on doing whatever he was doing with the jars, and not say anything either. And after a while Drew would say *I'm fine* and go out again.

He sat there a long time, the eighth night.

"I'm fine," said Drew.

"I know," said Kei.

Drew looked at the twelve flasks on the top shelf.

"It was like being asked," he said. Quietly. Out of nowhere. He'd never said anything about it, in three months. Not a word. "That's the thing nobody understands. Tiger thinks it was like being pulled. It wasn't. It was like somebody very kind standing in a doorway and asking me to come in out of the cold." He looked at his hands. "And I wanted to. I wanted to so much, Kei. I was standing at the shutters and I thought, *oh, there you are, I've been waiting for you*." He was quiet. "I don't even know who I meant."

Kei put down the jar he was holding.

"And then the dose," said Drew. "Your dose. And it was like somebody shut the door. Very gently." He breathed out. "And I was standing at the shutters in the guardhouse with everybody looking at me and Tiger holding on to my arm so hard it bruised for a week, and I was so *angry*. For about a minute. At you. For shutting it." He looked up. "I never told you that."

"No," said Kei.

"I'm not angry now."

"I know."

Drew looked at him for a long moment.

"Make another one," he said. "Please." And got up, and went out into the snow, and Kei heard him go across the yard toward the ladder at the side of a shed that had stood for two years with nobody under its roof, where Tiger would be sitting in the snow on the ridge-pole the way he sat there every night, looking at nothing, waiting for him.

Kei made another one.

*Thirteen*, said Hisui, on his chest, afterwards, in the dark.

"Thirteen."

*You look terrible*.

"I know."

*The cord's still cold*, said Hisui. And then, after a long time, very quietly, in a voice Kei had never heard him use: *I hope she's all right*.

***

It went warm on the tenth night, a little after nine.

Kei was in the little room with the jars, grinding something. Hisui was on the shelf above the bed, on the cord, where he'd been every evening for ten days. And Kei heard him stop.

Not stop talking. Hisui had been talking. About the root Kei was grinding, which he said tasted of a cellar, and about Bee's soup, which he said tasted of regret. And then, in the middle of a sentence about Bee's soup, Hisui stopped. All at once. The way he'd stopped at the supper table when the wind changed.

Kei put the pestle down.

He went to the shelf. Hisui was lying on the cord with his head on the half-bead, very flat, very still. Kei put out his hand and touched the cord beside him, the green-and-white twist.

It was warm.

The warmth of a hand. Somebody else's. As if somebody a long way off had reached across a table and laid two fingers on Kei's palm. He'd felt it once before, at a table in Valcrest, when Hana handed round the first pair. He hadn't expected it to feel like this. Like somebody he knew.

*He's holding it*, said Hisui. Very quietly. *Yan. He's holding the other half*.

And then his window came up in front of his face. Not his window. The channel. Small and white.

[ 21:07 · Suzuyun: It's here.
21:07 · Suzuyun: The rider came in at dusk. The downs held.
21:08 · Suzuyun: I'm holding it. Kei, is it warm? ]

Kei looked at it.

He picked the cord up off the shelf, with Hisui still on it, very carefully, and sat down on the edge of the bed with the cord across his palm and the snake lying along it.

He didn't write in the channel. He looked at the blank strip of oiled paper wound round the knot, with Cursiffe's ink on it, and thought about it, and then took the little pencil out of his coat and wrote on it, small, in English. Not the channel. This. A very small channel, for two people.

*Warm. He's on it. Put her on yours.*

And held the cord.

He watched the letters on the strip go faint. And come back. And not be his any more.

*Putting her on.*

***

Nothing, for a long time.

Kei sat on the edge of the bed in the little room behind the guardhouse kitchen with a cord across his palm, warm, and a green snake lying on it with his head on half a bead, perfectly still. The lamp ticked. Somewhere out in the yard a door banged and somebody laughed and was told to be quiet. The snow came down past the window.

Hisui didn't say anything.

Kei waited. He was good at waiting. People on the stool by the door took a long time to start, sometimes, and you waited, and didn't fill the silence, because the silence was theirs.

Then the strip changed.

He saw it. The blue ink on the paper round the knot, where *Putting her on* had been. It went faint. And came back.

And wasn't letters.

Not English. Not the kingdom's script, either, the one Kei still couldn't write after two years and a half. Not anything. Marks. Small curling lines, very fine, in Cursiffe's blue, going round the strip in a long unbroken loop and back on themselves and round again, the way a snake's track goes in sand. Not one mark and then another. One long mark, all the way round, that never lifted.

Kei looked at it.

*They carry what you mean*, Thea had said. *And write it in the holder's script*.

The holder.

He looked down at Hisui, lying on the cord with his head on the bead. At the holder.

Hisui's tongue went out. Once. Slowly. Over the strip of paper with the long curling mark on it. And came back.

*Oh*, said Hisui.

***

"What does it say?"

Hisui didn't answer for a moment. He was reading. Kei could feel him reading, the way he felt him taste the air: a very small concentrated stillness, against his palm.

*She says*, said Hisui, *you took your time*.

Kei laughed. He couldn't help it. He sat on the edge of his bed with his hand over his mouth and laughed, and Hisui, on the cord, said *it's not funny, it's rude*, and Kei laughed harder.

*She says it took ten days*, said Hisui, with dignity. *She says she's been lying on a cold bit of string on the end of his bed in a fort for an hour waiting for me to notice. She says I'm slow*. A pause. *I am not slow. I noticed instantly*.

"Tell her you're sorry."

*I'm not telling her that*.

"Hisui."

A long, put-upon pause.

*I've told her*, said Hisui. *She says good*.

Kei wiped his eyes. He picked up the pencil.

"What else?"

*She says*—Hisui stopped. Started again. His voice had gone different. Smaller. Not grumpy. *She says tell Yun I'm here*.

Kei wrote it on the strip. In English, small, careful. *She says: tell Yun I'm here.* And held the cord.

The letters went.

On the other side of the kingdom, a man on a wall above a red plain in the snow would be holding the other half, and looking at a strip of blue ink round a knot, and reading it. Kei didn't need to see his face. He'd read it on enough faces, on the stool by his door.

The strip came back. Yan's hand. Shaky. Three words.

*I know. Thank you.*

***

They talked till midnight.

Not fast. The strip only held a few words at a time, and each one had to be written, and held, and wait to go, and wait to come back. And on Kei's side, every line from Suzu came as a long curling mark that only Hisui could read, and that Hisui read, and told Kei, and Kei wrote down in English, and sent. And on Yan's side, Yan spoke to Suzu out loud, Kei supposed, because she was round his neck and she'd understood every word he'd ever said to her, and Suzu answered by meaning it into the cord. And it came out on Kei's strip as a snake's track. And Hisui read it.

A very small channel. For two snakes. And two men holding the string.

She was cold.

Hisui had said so in Valcrest, on the roof. *Coldly. She says she doesn't like anyone*. Kei had thought, then, that it was a snake thing, or a Suzu thing, the way Hisui was grumpy. It wasn't. It was older than that. She answered everything Yan asked, through Hisui, through the cord, patiently, briefly, without warmth, the way you'd answer a stranger at a door. *Yes. No. That is not important. That is not yours to know*.

Except anything about Yun.

Anything about Yun, and she was warm. Hisui didn't have to say so. Kei could feel it through the cord in his palm, the way you can feel a stove through a wall. Whenever Yan's name came into it, whenever Yan asked about himself, or she answered about him, the warmth in the cord went up. Not a lot. Like someone turning toward a fire.

*She says*, said Hisui, at about ten, *that she doesn't like the treant either*.

"Maigo?"

*She says he's loud and he stands too close to Yun and he smells of leaves. She says she tolerates him because Yun likes him*. A pause. *She says she tolerates me more*.

"That's high praise."

*I know*, said Hisui, with enormous satisfaction. *I know it is*.

***

Yan asked at a little after eleven.

Kei had known he would. He'd been waiting for it all evening, the way he'd have waited for it from somebody on the stool: the real question, under all the others. The one they'd come to ask and had been circling for an hour, getting closer, like a dog lying down.

The strip came back in Yan's hand. Steadier, now.

*Ask her why she looks south.*

Kei read it out.

Hisui didn't say anything for a moment. Then he put his head down on the bead, and Kei felt him wait, and felt, a long way off, through the cord, the warmth on the other side shift, the way a fire shifts when somebody opens a door.

The strip went faint. Came back. A long curling mark. Longer than any of the others. All the way round the knot, twice, and back on itself, without lifting.

Hisui read it.

And didn't say anything.

Kei waited. He waited a long time. The lamp ticked. Hisui lay on the cord with his head on the bead and his tongue very still and didn't say anything at all.

"Hisui," said Kei.

*No*.

"What does it say?"

*I'm not telling him*, said Hisui. *It'll frighten him*.

Kei looked down at him.

Two years and a half. He'd never once heard Hisui refuse to pass something on. Hisui passed on everything. Who'd been in a room. What they'd been afraid of. Whether the soup was regret. He'd never kept a thing back from Kei in his life, because he didn't know how, because he was a snake, and snakes, he'd told Kei once, didn't have insides you could put things in, only outsides you could taste.

He was keeping this back.

"Hisui," said Kei. Gently. The voice he used on the stool. "It isn't yours."

*I know it isn't mine*.

"Then it isn't yours to keep."

Hisui lay there.

*He'll be frightened*, he said, at last. Very small. *He carries everything. You said so. He carries everything on his own and he never puts it down. And this is a heavy one, Kei. I can taste how heavy it is. I don't want to give him a heavy one*.

"It's hers," said Kei. "She gave it to you to give to him. That's the only reason she said it." He was quiet. "If you keep it, you're carrying it for him. And he didn't ask you to."

A long silence.

Then Hisui said it. Quietly. Word for word, the way he passed on everything, as if he were reading it off the inside of his own eyelids.

*She says: they are coming. Out of the south. The old ones. The ones who remember the white*.

Kei wrote it down.

*She says: they heard the song. Everyone has heard the song. But the song is wrong. They are not coming for an elf. They are not coming for a tree*.

Kei wrote it down.

*She says*, said Hisui, and stopped, and made himself go on. *She says: they are coming for me. Not for him. Tell him*.

***

Kei sat with the pencil over the strip for a long time.

He thought about Yan on a wall in the snow, holding a cord. Yan, who'd carried his mother every day for two years and a half and never once said a word, until a night on a forge roof in Valcrest. Yan, who'd asked a white snake on stream what she was saying and answered for her in her voice for years, and found out here that he'd been right all along. Yan, who'd gone green at a grave in the autumn, Wendy said, and been called by a name out of the oldest rites in the kingdom, and come down off the knoll in the morning and slept an hour longer every night since.

*They are coming for me. Not for him*.

He wrote it. All of it. Small and careful, the way Hisui had said it, without changing a word, because it wasn't his to change. And held the cord.

The letters went.

He waited.

Nothing came back for a long time. Long enough that he thought the cord had gone cold, and put his fingers to it, and it hadn't. It was warm. Somebody on the other side of the kingdom was holding it very hard.

Then the strip came back.

Not Yan's hand. A snake's track. Long, curling, without lifting. Suzu, again.

Hisui read it. And this time he didn't hesitate. He passed it on at once, quietly, the way he'd passed on the first one, as if he'd decided that what came after a heavy thing was never his to hold either.

*She says: tell him not to be afraid. Tell him I will not let them take me from him. Tell him nobody takes me from him. Nobody has ever taken me from him*.

*She says: they will call me by an old name. Tell him it is not my name*.

*She says: my name is the one he gave me*.

Kei wrote it down. All of it. He found, when he'd finished, that his hand wasn't steady.

He held the cord.

***

It was a long time before Yan answered. Kei didn't mind. He'd learned a long time ago, on a floor, against a bed, at two in the morning, that the silence after a heavy thing belonged to the person carrying it.

When it came, it was very short. In Yan's careful hand.

*Thank you, Hisui.*

Kei read it out.

Hisui didn't say anything for a moment. Then he lifted his head off the bead, very slightly, and Kei felt him go warm against his palm. Not the cord. Hisui.

*Tell him he's welcome*, said Hisui. *Tell him*—he stopped. *Tell him she isn't cold. Not really. Tell him I didn't understand, in Valcrest. I was too busy being pleased. Tell him she's not cold. She's just old*.

Kei looked down at him.

"Old?"

*Old*, said Hisui. *I can taste it. Through the string. I couldn't on the roof; there was too much else. The city. The goose. Everyone*. He put his head back down. *She tastes old, Kei. Older than the castle. Older than the circle in the floor. Older than the pass*. He was very quiet. *I've never tasted anything older than the pass*.

Kei didn't say anything.

He wrote it down. *Hisui says: she isn't cold. She's old.* And held the cord, and watched it go, and didn't know, at all, what Yan would make of it, on a wall above a red plain with a white snake round his neck who'd looked at a place for the first time in her life three weeks ago and been looking at it ever since.

***

The last thing came at a quarter to midnight.

Not from Yan. From Suzu. A short mark, this time. Very short. A snake's track that went round the knot once and stopped.

Hisui read it.

And laughed. Kei had never heard Hisui laugh. He hadn't known snakes could. A small dry sound in his head, like a leaf blowing across a stone.

"What?"

*She says goodnight, little green*, said Hisui. *She says I may come again tomorrow*.

Kei smiled.

*And*, said Hisui. *She says tell the leaf-man*—a pause—*she means you, Kei, she calls you the leaf-man, because of your coat*—*she says tell the leaf-man that Yun is tired, and he has been tired since the night he went green, and she knows why*.

Kei stopped smiling.

*She says it is hers to tell him*, said Hisui. *Not mine. Not the leaf-man's. When it is time*. A pause. *She says the leaf-man is not to worry*.

Kei sat on the edge of his bed with the cord going slowly cold across his palm, as somebody a long way off put the other half down.

*She says*, said Hisui, *the leaf-man is going to worry anyway, because that is what leaf-men are for*.

***

He didn't sleep.

He sat in the little room behind the guardhouse kitchen with the lamp turned down and Hisui asleep on the cord on the shelf above the bed, finally, after eleven days and ten nights, curled round the half-bead like a green ring. Not talking. Breathing. Kei had never once heard him asleep. He sat and listened to it for a long time.

On the top shelf, the thirteen flasks with green thread round their necks.

He looked at them.

*You don't make medicine for a sickness*, he'd said, three months ago, to a supper table, with his finger still in the air. *You make it for a person*.

He thought about a person on a wall at Cinderhold. Sleeping an hour longer every night. Since the night his hands went green and an old chaplain called him by a name out of the rites. *Soul Eater. Who carries*. And a white snake round his neck who was older than the pass, and knew why he was tired, and said it was hers to tell.

*The leaf-man is going to worry anyway*.

He got up. He took down a clean jar from the shelf, and a slate, and a stub of chalk. And wrote at the top of the slate, small, in English, the way he started everything he didn't know how to make yet.

*Yan.*

And under it, after a long time:

*Tired. Since the grave. One hour more a night. She knows why.*

He looked at it. He didn't know what came next. He didn't know what it was a dose of, or for, or whether there was a dose at all. He didn't know what a man paid, to carry the dead, or in what coin, or whether anything Kei could put in a flask would make it cheaper.

He'd find out. That was all. That was what he did with things. You sat on the floor with your back against the bed and you didn't fix anything, and you were there the next day too.

*I'm here tomorrow too*.

He put the slate up on the shelf beside the thirteen flasks, where he'd see it every morning. And turned the lamp out. And lay down on the bed in the dark under a sleeping snake and a cord with half a bead on it, and didn't sleep, and listened to the snow come down on Thornwall, very quietly, with the iron taste of it in the air that only Hisui could name.

Somewhere south, a very long way off, past the end of every map in the kingdom, something old was walking north.

He didn't know that yet. Neither did Hisui. Only a white snake on a wall at the other end of everything, looking south through the snow, with her tail curled round two of a man's fingers. Holding on.
