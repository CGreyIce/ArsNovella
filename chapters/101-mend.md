Rachael had spent twenty-six years being the patient.

She didn't think about it much, anymore. That was the strange part. For twenty-six years she'd thought about it every day: every cold that might turn into something else, every crowded room, every friend's party she left early because somebody was coughing. Hospitals. Waiting rooms. Doctors who talked about her in front of her, as if she were a chart. Friends who were careful with her, as if she might break. She'd been the fragile one her whole life. She'd been so tired of it.

And then she'd woken up on a stone floor in a castle, with thirty-four other people crying round her, and a soft-spoken old man had knelt beside her and put two fingers on her wrist and said one word in a language she'd never heard and understood perfectly.

*That will hold.*

It had held. A year and a half. She hadn't been ill once. Not a cold. Not a cough. Nothing. Sister Maud said she had the constitution of a cart horse. Rachael had laughed until she cried, the first time Maud said that, and Maud had looked at her as if she were mad.

Everyone else in the hall had cursed the Advisor, at least a little, at least at first. *You took us. You didn't ask.* Rachael never had. She was the only one of the thirty-five, she thought, who was grateful to be here. Who'd woken up in another world and found it was the first one in her life where she wasn't the patient.

She was the healer now.

She'd never once thought about what that would make her, to the things on the other side.

***

She healed everyone at the stone landing above the gorge.

It was the first thing she did. Before she sat down, before she let herself think, before she even put Gabe's knife back in its sheath. Haskett brought the *Patient Widow* in against the little landing where the towpath ended, and the others came down off the ledge and climbed aboard, wet and grey and shaking, and Rachael got Maud's needle case out of her coat and said, very loudly, in the voice she'd learned at the infirmary, "*Right.* Everybody sit down. Wounds first. Nobody touch anything."

They sat down. Even Sabine.

Haskett's younger boy first. His wrist. He'd broken it on the towpath when the rope went slack and jerked him into the rock, and he'd held on anyway, with a broken wrist, because Malcolm's plan said hold the rope. He was fifteen. He was trying not to cry. Rachael knelt on the deck in front of him and took his wrist in both hands, very gently, and felt where it was wrong. She could always feel where it was wrong; she'd been able to since the first week, warmth in her hands near an injury. Two bones. Clean breaks. She'd set worse at the river gate.

She set it. He screamed. She held it, and wrote the line she'd written the day Gabe's arm caught fire at the forge, the day she went up a tier.

*Mend.*

The gold came up in her hands. Faint, in the grey light. And under her fingers she felt the bone remember what it had been, and go back to it.

The boy stared at his wrist. Then at her.

"Don't make it weird," said Rachael. "Next."

***

Gabe's hands. The rope had taken the skin off both palms, holding the barge against the current with things coming down the cliff at him, and he hadn't let go until the wind came. He held them out to her without a word. She closed them. *Close*, she said under her breath, the word she'd been saying a finger's width at a time for a year before *Mend* came, and the burns closed.

Calvin's hands, which were blue again from the crosstree.

Azac's arm. His shield had taken the hat man's blow, with Malcolm's *Hold* in it, and held. Azac's arm behind it hadn't, quite. A bone in his forearm cracked. Not broken. He'd been holding the shield up with it for an hour without saying anything.

"*Azac*," said Rachael.

"It's fine."

"It's *cracked*."

"It's fine *now*," said Azac, and grinned at her, with his scar, the way he grinned at everything. "You're here."

She mended it. She told him off while she did it. He let her.

Sabine's cheek last. A long cut, from one of the things coming down the towpath, from the corner of her eye nearly to her jaw. Sabine had refused to let anyone look at it all the way out of the gorge. She sat on a coil of rope at the stern with her sword across her knees and her face turned away and said *it's nothing*.

"It's going to scar," said Rachael.

"Good."

"It's going to scar *badly*. Right across your face. You'll look like a pirate."

Sabine considered this.

"Fine," she said, and turned her face, and let Rachael close it.

***

Deston was the last.

He hadn't been hurt. Nothing had touched him; Petra had seen to that. But he was sitting at the bow with his back against the rail and his face grey and blood running out of his nose, and his hands shaking in his lap. Rachael knelt down in front of him and felt for where it was wrong.

Everywhere. That was what it felt like. Not a wound. As if something had gone through every part of him at once, very fast, and left him hollow.

"You overdid it," said Rachael.

"I didn't know how to stop."

"You never do." She put her hands on either side of his face, the way Maud had shown her for people who'd poured too much out at once. Not *mend*. There wasn't anything broken. She just let the warmth go into him, slowly, the way you'd hold a cold child. "Breathe."

He breathed.

"I felt it," said Rachael quietly. "In the chorus. All of it." She didn't take her hands away. "Everybody. Gabe on the rope. Xav waiting. John seeing round corners." She swallowed. "And the other one. At the end. North."

Deston looked at her.

"It's warm," said Rachael. "I didn't believe you. Not really. Not all the way. I believed you believed it." Her voice went, slightly. "It's *warm*, Deston."

"I know."

"I'm sorry I didn't believe you."

"You did," said Deston. "You came."

***

Petra came and crouched down beside her while she was putting the needles back in Maud's case.

She didn't say anything at first. Petra rarely did. She just crouched there on the wet deck with her sword across her knees and watched Rachael's hands, the way she watched Xav's feet in the yard at Mother Annick's. Then she said, quite quietly:

"There was a healer on the Redmarch."

Rachael looked up.

"In the war," said Petra. "Eight years ago. Before the end. I was a recruit. Twenty-three, and green as grass. I got a spear through the shoulder in a skirmish the month before the ditch, and they carried me back to Cinderhold, and a healer stitched me up." She was looking at Rachael's hands, not her face. "She had cold hands. That's what I remember. And she told me off. The whole time she was doing it. For needing it. *Next time get out of the way of the pointy end.*" Something moved at the corner of Petra's mouth. "I was so frightened of her I forgot to be frightened of the needle."

Rachael didn't say anything.

"You do it the same way," said Petra. "*Don't make it weird. Next.*" She was quiet a moment. "I heard you, with the boy's wrist. I nearly laughed."

"What happened to her?" said Rachael.

Petra didn't answer for a long time.

"They said she was his," she said at last. "The Black Blade's. The soldiers said it. Nobody knew for certain." She looked north, up the river, past the top of the gorge. "She was taken. In the last week. And then the battle. And then the morning after, a grave out on the red with a black sword over it."

*Her name was Diwa*, Rachael thought. *She was a healer.*

Petra stood up.

"I never thanked her," she said. "For the shoulder." She looked down at Rachael, at the needle case, at her hands. "So. Thank you. For the boy's wrist. For all of them." And then, very slightly gruffly, for Petra: "Next time, get out of the way of the pointy end."

She went back to the bow.

***

She sat down after that. For the first time since the cliffs moved.

She sat on the deck in the middle of the barge, where she'd been standing when the things came down, with her back against the mast and Maud's needle case in her lap. She took Gabe's knife out of its sheath and looked at it.

The blade was grey.

Not stained. Not rusted. Grey, the colour of ash, from the point back a hand's width. Where it had gone into the hat man's hand. The same hand, the same place, as the night market in the first winter, when she'd cut him and black stuff had come out and his skin had moved like water. That had left a shadow on the blade. A dark stain that never quite washed off.

This was different. The black was gone. The blade was grey where it had been black.

She thought about the hat man's hand. The way it had moved like water, round the knife, the way his skin always moved. And then stopped. And gone grey. And the way he'd looked down at it. And then at her.

He hadn't smiled.

He'd smiled every time. On the waterfront, offering her a position in his master's household. At the night market, on the customs-house steps, turning his hat. On the far quay, the night of the hunter, looking up at Dennis. He'd smiled every time.

He hadn't smiled today.

***

Gabe sat down beside her. He didn't say anything. He just sat, very large, with his healed hands loose on his knees, the way he'd sat beside her in the infirmary corridor every night since the first winter.

"Rae," he said, after a while.

"I know why," said Rachael.

He looked at her.

"Why it's me," she said. "Why it's always me." She turned the knife over in her hands. "Maud told me. When I went up the tier. *Healers are one in a few hundred. Every order will want you. And worse.* She said a healer's mana *takes things in. Holds them.*" She looked at the grey on the blade. "She said that's why they took the healer on the Redmarch. The one the general did something to, where everyone could see."

Gabe didn't say anything.

"Yan wrote about her," said Rachael. "In the first winter. The cold under her grave. *Something that was done to someone.*" She swallowed. "And Group 3, in the autumn. When he took the sword out of Salty's hands. *Who was she?* And he said her name. *Diwa. She was a healer.*"

The barge rocked gently against the landing. Somewhere at the stern, Emrys was still humming, very quietly, the song with the gap in it.

"They want to put something in me," said Rachael. "That's what it is. That's what the hat man's *master* wants. That's what *a position in the household in the hills, west* means." She was very calm. She was surprised how calm she was. "They want to empty me out and put something in. The way they did to her. Because a healer holds things. And whatever they put in would hold."

***

Gabe was quiet for a long time.

"Not past me," he said.

"I know."

"Rae. Not past me. Not ever. Not past any of us." He looked down the deck. At Xav, standing at the bow beside Deston, looking north. At Azac, flexing his mended arm and grinning at nothing. At Petra and Sabine at the stern, sharpening their swords and not talking. At Malcolm writing in his book. "You felt it. In the chorus. All of us. You felt what it was like."

"I felt it," said Rachael.

"Then you know," said Gabe.

She did. That was the thing. She'd felt it, for one long minute in the gorge, through every gold thread at once: every person on that barge, and what they were doing, and what they'd do. Gabe holding the rope with his skin coming off. Azac jumping off a ledge with a shield. Xav waiting. RX in the dark. Dennis moving her a pace at a time so fast that nothing could catch her. Every one of them, all at once, pointing at her the way Deston's hand pointed north.

Not past me.

She'd been the fragile one for twenty-six years. People had been careful with her her whole life. She'd hated it. She'd hated being something to be protected.

This wasn't that. She didn't know what this was. It wasn't careful at all.

"I'm not frightened of them," said Rachael. "I want you to know that. I'm not frightened of the hat man. Or the boy with the cap. Or any of them." She looked at the grey on the knife. "I'm frightened of what I'd be. After. If they got me. Because I'd still be a healer. I'd still hold things." She was quiet. "I'd hold whatever they put in me. And I'd hold it *well*."

Gabe didn't say anything. He sat down beside her on the wet deck, close, not touching, the way he'd sat in the infirmary corridor on bad nights: near enough that she knew he was there, not so near that she had to do anything about it.

***

She looked at the knife for a long time after that.

At the grey.

She'd cut the hat man's hand twice. Once at the night market, at Tier 0, when she could barely make her hands warm near an injury. And once today. At the first tier. With *Mend* in her, and *close*, and a year and a half of putting her hands on broken things and asking them to remember what they'd been.

The first time, he'd bled black, and his skin had moved like water, and he'd walked into the river and come back.

The second time, his hand had stopped moving. And gone grey. And he hadn't smiled.

She didn't know what that meant.

She thought about it. She thought about it the way she'd learned to think about a wound at the river gate: not what it looked like, but what was wrong underneath. *Mend* didn't make things. It didn't add anything. It made things remember what they'd been. It closed what had been opened. That was all healing was. She'd been doing it to cuts and burns and broken bones for a year and a half.

The hat man wasn't a person. Deston said so; no threads. John said so; wrong light. He was something wearing a person. Something that had been put in.

She'd cut him with a knife she'd held in a healer's hand, with the first tier in it.

*Close*, she'd been thinking. When the knife went in. She hadn't meant to. It was what she always thought, when something was open that shouldn't be. *Close. Close.*

And whatever was in him, where the knife went, had stopped moving.

***

She didn't tell anyone. Not yet. She didn't know enough. It might be nothing. It might be the knife, or the cold, or something Gabe had done to the steel at the forge. She'd tell Deston, maybe, and Maud, in a letter, when she knew what to say.

But she sat against the mast of the *Patient Widow* at the stone landing above the Narrows at Gallow Rock with Gabe's knife in her hands, and looked at the grey on the blade, and thought:

*Maybe that's why they want healers.*

Not because a healer holds things.

Because a healer closes them.

Because the one thing in the whole kingdom that could take what they put into people and make it *remember what it had been* was somebody with warm hands, saying *close*, a finger's width at a time.

And if you couldn't kill all of those, you could turn them. And then they'd hold.

She put the knife back in its sheath. Very carefully.

***

They didn't stop at Mallow's Ford.

Haskett had said they wouldn't, and they didn't. It came up on the east bank the next afternoon, past the top of the gorge, a village of thirty roofs round a jetty, with a mill and a church and no smoke at all. And people on the bank. Standing in a row, at the water's edge, facing the river, the way they'd stood at the first village.

More of them, this time. Fifty. A hundred.

They watched the *Patient Widow* come up the river under her tailwind with her sail full the wrong way. Their heads turned, all together, slowly, following her.

Rachael stood in the middle of the deck between Gabe and Azac, with Maud's needle case in her coat and Gabe's knife at her belt, and looked back at them.

She didn't look away.

At the end of the row, at the very edge of the jetty, there was a boy with a cap pulled down over his ears. Standing a little apart from the others. Watching her.

He didn't smile either.

***

She wrote to Maud that night. On paper, by Emrys's lantern, the old way, in the careful hand she'd been practising all winter in the native script, because Maud couldn't read English.

It wasn't long. She didn't say where they were. She didn't say why. She said that everyone was well, and that she'd set a broken wrist on a moving barge and Maud would have been proud of her, and that she'd let herself be watched every hour, the way she'd sworn.

And at the bottom, after a long time, she wrote:

*Sister. I cut one of them. With Gabe's knife, and my hand on it, and close in my head. It stopped moving where I cut it. I don't know why. Do you?*

She folded it and put it in her coat, to send from the next town with a rider, wherever the next town was.

Then she lay down on the deck in the middle of everyone, the way she did every night now, between Gabe and Azac, with Petra awake at the bow and Sabine at the stern. And looked up at the stars over the river, which were coming out one by one over the hills to the north.

She thought about a man she'd asked for advice once, years ago, about something so embarrassing she'd never told anyone what it was. And how he'd answered her completely seriously, without laughing once, and then laughed for about ten minutes afterwards.

She thought she might ask him something else, when she saw him.

She thought he'd answer that one seriously too.
