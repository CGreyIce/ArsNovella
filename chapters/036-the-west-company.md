Tiger built a shed in the second week of spring, and on the third day a horse kicked it down.

It wasn't a big shed. It wasn't even a good one. He'd built it against the inside of the north wall of Thornwall, out of offcuts from the carpenter and a door he'd found in a ditch, so that the Pollen Puffs would have somewhere dry to sleep that wasn't Bee's hair. He'd been very proud of it. He'd shown everyone. He'd made Hyvred look at it with her special eyes and tell him whether the light in it was happy.

"It's a shed," Hyvred had said. "It doesn't have light. Sheds don't."

"But if it *did*."

"Then it'd be happy," Hyvred had said, kindly. "Very happy. The happiest shed."

And then on the third day the company from the capital rode in through the gate, a hundred and twenty soldiers in grey and gold with their horses and their wagons and their noise. One of the horses took exception to a flapping bit of sacking on the shed's roof, reared, and kicked the whole thing flat in a single blow.

Tiger stood in the courtyard looking at the wreckage.

"It's fine," he said. "It's fine. It's totally fine."

"It's the Minecraft house," said Drew, beside him, very gently.

"It's *not* the Minecraft house."

"It's a little bit the Minecraft house."

Tiger stared at the splintered boards in the mud for a long moment. Then he started to laugh, helplessly, the way you laugh when the alternative is crying. Somewhere a long time ago, in another world, he'd spent three weeks building a house in a block game, and a friend had laughed until he cried when a super-modded wither blew up the entire spawn.

"Everything I build," he said, wiping his eyes. "*Everything.*"

"You'll build another one," said Drew.

"It'll get destroyed."

"Probably," Drew agreed. "And then you'll build another one."

***

The company was called the Western Watch, which was a new name, and it was led by a captain called Brannoch, who was not. He was a broad, slow, grey-moustached man of about fifty who'd fought on the Redmarch seven years ago and had the look of someone who'd seen too many things he didn't want to talk about. He brought a hundred and twenty soldiers, four wagons of supplies, a forge, a field surgeon, and a court mage.

The mage was called Magister Fenwick. He was thin and nervous and very young for a Torch-tier, maybe thirty, with ink on his cuffs and spectacles that kept slipping down his nose. And he was, Tiger realised within an hour, the first person from the capital who'd ever looked at Corvin's wall of maps without laughing.

"Two years," Fenwick kept saying, standing in front of the wall in the long room at the top of the north tower, turning in a slow circle. "*Two years* of records. Tracks. Sightings. Dates. Patterns." He pushed his spectacles up. "This is extraordinary. Why didn't anyone *read* these?"

Corvin, standing beside him, made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite a sob.

"Nobody believed me," he said.

"Well," said Fenwick, turning to look at him with enormous seriousness, "I do."

Corvin had to go and sit down after that.

***

Tiger liked the soldiers. That surprised him a bit. He'd expected them to be like the guards in the summoning hall, stiff and suspicious, hands on their swords whenever someone with horns or ears walked past. But the Western Watch had been picked, Brannoch said, by the Advisor himself, and most of them were young and bored and very interested in the summoned. Especially the one with the tiger ears.

There was one in particular. A lanky lad of about twenty called Ossie, with freckles and big feet and an aura that had come up at sixteen and never really gone anywhere since. He'd seen Tiger sprinting the walls on the first morning, the way he did every morning, round and round the battlements until his legs gave out. And he'd shouted up from the yard: "*Bet you can't beat me to the gate!*"

Tiger had beaten him to the gate. Easily. And then Ossie had beaten him back. And then they'd raced again, and again, until both of them were lying in the mud in the middle of the courtyard wheezing and laughing, and Hild had come out and stood over them and said, "If you're done," in a voice that made it very clear they were.

After that they raced every morning.

Ossie was a Drawn: first tier, aura out and moving, a little faster and a little stronger than he'd have been without it. He won most of the races. Tiger didn't mind. Tiger was at twenty percent, still an Initiate, still waiting for the thing Drew called *the start of it* to come back. It had come once, in the autumn, sparring with Hild: his legs moving before he'd decided to move. Once, and never again.

Then Thea's letter had come. *Write it. One small line.* And Tiger had read it and thought: *I don't know how to write anything. I'm not a writer. I'm fast. That's all I am.*

He'd thought about it for weeks.

***

It happened on the ninth morning, sparring with Ossie in the yard.

Not racing. Sparring. Wooden swords, the way Hild had taught them, round and round on the frozen cobbles while half the Western Watch stood at the edges and shouted. Ossie was better than him. Ossie had four years of aura and a real sword arm, and he was faster, and he knew it, and he was grinning the whole time.

And he came in fast, high, a big overhead swing, and Tiger saw it coming and knew he couldn't get out of the way in time.

And he *asked*.

He didn't decide to. It just happened, the way the first time had. Except this time he felt it: a gap. In himself, somewhere low down, in his legs. A place where something should be and wasn't. And he wrote into it, without words, the only word he had.

*Go.*

He was three paces to the left.

He didn't remember moving. Nobody saw him move. One moment he was standing in front of Ossie's swing, and the next he was three paces to the side, and Ossie's practice sword was coming down on empty cobbles, and Ossie was staggering, off balance, staring at the place where Tiger had been.

The yard went completely silent.

"How," said Ossie, "did you do that?"

Tiger didn't know. He stood there with his wooden sword hanging from his hand and his legs shaking and his heart going like a drum. He couldn't feel the gap anymore. It had closed. Whatever he'd written had been written, and was gone.

At the edge of the yard, Hild had pushed up her blindfold. She was looking at him with her one good eye.

"Again," she said.

He tried. He couldn't. Not that day, or the next. But his window said twenty-four that night, up four, and every time he closed his eyes he could feel where the gap had been. Like the place a tooth used to be.

"Once is the hardest one," said Drew, at supper.

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true."

***

The patrols started in the second week.

Brannoch sent them out in tens, up into the hills to the west and north of Thornwall, with Fenwick's copies of Corvin's maps and orders to look and not to fight. To find the villages that had emptied. To follow the tracks. To come back and report.

The first patrol found the hut.

The shepherd's hut above Coldwater, where Kei and Hyvred and Hild had found the shoes lined up against the wall and the circle burned into the floor. They found it burned. Not the circle; the whole hut. Roof fallen in, walls blackened, still smoking. Somebody had come back and set fire to it, and taken the shoes and the coats and the little bag of coins with them, and left nothing but ash and the black glassy ring in the floor. The fire hadn't touched that. It was still there, under the ash, perfect and dark.

The second patrol found more tracks. Up past the hut, along the old shepherds' track that ran north across the shoulder of the hills. Boots, mostly, going one way. And among them the other ones: the long narrow prints with too many joints and no heel. They ran north, up toward the high passes of the Greyspine, where the snow was still lying deep and nobody went until summer.

Fenwick spread the map out on the table in the long room and traced them with his finger, and his face went very pale.

"North," he said. "Up into the passes. Toward..." He stopped.

"Toward what?" said Tiger.

Fenwick looked at Corvin. Corvin looked at the map.

"There's nothing up there," said Corvin quietly. "That's the thing. That's what I've been saying for two years. There's *nothing* up there. The passes go over the mountains, and on the other side there's... nobody knows. Nobody's crossed in a hundred years. It's not ours. It's not Kharvos." He looked up. "It's just *out there*."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"The external power," said Hyvred, very quietly. She was standing at the window, looking north, where the mountains were. "The Advisor said. In the hall. *If they come from two sides.*" She turned round. Her left eye, the one with the crosshair, was very bright. "It's not on our side of the mountains. It's on the other side. And it's been coming over."

***

The third patrol didn't come back.

They'd gone out on the fourteenth morning, ten men under a sergeant called Dunmore, north along the shepherds' track toward the passes. They were supposed to be back by dusk. By dusk there was no sign of them. By full dark, Brannoch had every torch in Thornwall lit along the north wall and men standing on the battlements calling into the hills.

Ossie was one of the ten.

Tiger stood on the wall all night. He didn't decide to. He just went up after supper and didn't come down. Hild came and stood beside him for a while, with her head tilted, listening. Then Hyvred, wrapped in a blanket, her glasses pushed up into her hair, looking north with her strange new eyes. Then Drew, who didn't say anything at all, just leaned on the battlements beside him.

"Can you see them?" Tiger asked Hyvred, around midnight. "With your... can you see their light?"

Hyvred didn't answer for a long time. She was looking north, up toward the black line of the hills against the stars. Her face was very still.

"I can see the shepherds," she said finally. "Down in the valley. In their huts. Little lights." She swallowed. "And the soldiers, on the wall. All of you." She paused. "I can't see them, Tiger. Up there. On the track."

"Maybe they're just too far."

"Maybe," said Hyvred.

But she kept looking. And a little while later, she made a sound, very small, and put her hand over her left eye.

"There," she whispered. "On the ridge. Far up. Where the track goes over." She was shaking. "Ten of them. Walking. Coming back." She swallowed again. "Except they're not... their light's not..."

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. They'd all read the letters. Tiger had read Kei's letter about the hut so many times he knew it by heart.

*Someone wearing someone.*

"Are they coming here?" said Drew quietly.

Hyvred watched for a long time.

"No," she said at last. "They're turning. North. Over the ridge. Toward the passes." Her hand was still over her eye. "They're going *away.* All ten of them. Walking very carefully. Like they're remembering how."

Tiger stood on the wall and stared at the black ridge where she was looking. He couldn't see anything. Just hills, and stars, and dark.

Somewhere up there, Ossie was walking north, very carefully, like he was remembering how.

***

He didn't sleep. Nobody did. At dawn Brannoch sent out two more patrols, twenty men each, with Hild and Fenwick and orders to find Sergeant Dunmore's ten and bring them home. They came back at dusk with nothing. Tracks going north into the snow of the passes. Ten pairs of boots. And, among them, walking beside them, the long narrow prints with too many joints.

Tiger went and stood in the courtyard afterwards, in the cold, by the wreckage of his shed. Nobody had cleared it away. The splintered boards were still lying in the mud where the horse had kicked them.

He looked at them for a long time.

Then he picked up a board, and another, and started stacking them against the north wall, where the shed had been.

"What are you doing?" said Bee, from the doorway. She'd come out with the Pollen Puffs on her shoulders, both of them very quiet.

"Building," said Tiger.

"It'll get knocked down."

"Probably." He picked up another board. His hands were shaking. He kept going. "Ossie said he'd help me make it better. Said he knew how to do a proper roof. His dad was a carpenter." He set the board in place. "So I'm going to build the walls. And when he comes back, he can do the roof."

Bee looked at him for a long moment, from the doorway, with the light behind her.

Then she came out into the cold, and picked up a board, and handed it to him.

They worked until it was too dark to see. Neither of them said anything. When they finished, there were three walls standing against the north wall of Thornwall, crooked and rough and roofless, with a gap where the door should go.

Tiger stood back and looked at it.

"It's not finished," he said.

"No," said Bee.

"It's waiting for the roof."

"Yes," said Bee. "It is."

She didn't say *he isn't coming back*. Tiger was grateful for that. He stood in the dark in front of three crooked walls with the Pollen Puffs buzzing very softly on Bee's shoulders, and looked north, at the black line of the hills where Hyvred had seen ten lights walking away very carefully.

Then he went inside, and wrote to everyone, the way they'd promised. He wrote it all down. The company. Fenwick. The burned hut. The tracks going north into the passes. Sergeant Dunmore's ten, walking over the ridge in the dark like they were remembering how.

At the end, he added one line.

*If anyone sees a soldier called Ossie, freckles, big feet, tell him the shed's still waiting for its roof.*
