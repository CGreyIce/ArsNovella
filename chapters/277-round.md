Four.

Malcolm had it in the back of his book, on a page by itself, in his small square hand. Thirty-one lines, one for every time something had hit the *Ward* since the downs. What; where; how hard. And down the right-hand side, a column of ticks and crosses, for whether it had still been there afterwards.

Four ticks.

"An hour if nobody hits it," said Malcolm, at the long-room table, with the book open between the porridge bowls. "A minute if somebody does. That's not me. That's you, on the downs, in the rain. You said it." He turned the book round so Azac could read it. "I just wrote it down for a year."

Azac read it.

He knew it already. He'd known it on the downs, and in the Hollow before that, and on a gravel bar on the Wren before that, where it had been a post and held for one blow. It came up every time. It was round now; it had been round since Petra told him to stand in the middle and stop reaching. It hummed like a glass with a wet finger going round the rim. And then something leaned on it, and it bowed, and he held it with his whole body and his arms shaking and talked and talked, and it bowed more, and then it wasn't there.

*Candle*, the guides said, and the books Fenwick had lent him in the autumn. *It works every time. Ten out of ten.*

Four out of thirty-one.

"You don't have to," said Malcolm.

"I know."

"IF you don't try," said Malcolm, "THEN you don't try. That's allowed." He picked up his spoon. "There's no backup for that one. It's just allowed."

Azac looked at the long-room door.

He'd stood in it every night since the count. Forty-odd nights. He stood in it from dusk to dawn with his shield against the frame and the pale blue showing at the edges of him, and talked, low, to nobody, so that anybody in Group 5 who went out of that door in the dark went out past him and he saw them go. He'd stood in it the fifteen nights before that, too, for different reasons. For something coming in.

On the first of those nights, Fenna Rees had walked across the long room through the middle of forty people holding hands and leaned on his ward with her flat hands, smiling, and it had bowed.

He'd said *sorry*. He remembered that. He remembered saying it over and over, with his arms out and his voice going, and the blue going in round her hands like a sheet with somebody behind it; and Malcolm coming across the floor and pulling her off it from behind; and her pushing Malcolm once, not hard, and going out of the side door into the yard.

The side door. Not his door. He'd had the front.

She'd done the Watch's shirts. In the autumn, when they'd first come, she'd let out the shoulders of two of his, because nobody in Thornwall was made his size and she'd said so, laughing, with pins in her mouth. She was in Corvin's book now, in the column that didn't get crossed out because it didn't need to be. Fenna Rees, the fifth hour of the first night. He'd stood in a doorway and held it, and she'd gone out of a different one.

He didn't put the *Ward* up for anything but the door now. Malcolm's thirty-one lines stopped at the first night. There was nothing after it.

"I know," he said again.

Across the table, John was looking at him the way John looked at everything, as if it was a picture he was going to have to send somebody later and wanted to get right. Rui Xiang wasn't there; or he was, and wasn't letting anybody know. Xav was eating. Xav always ate first and talked after.

"The king's making shields," said Xav, after.

Azac looked at him.

"For the Watch. They lost eleven at the gate on the last night. Agnes said he's doing the bosses himself." Xav put his spoon in his bowl. "He's been making round things all winter, Az."

***

He went that evening.

Down the long street in the early dark, with the snow packed hard underfoot and grey with ash where the Watch had thrown it, and the lamps lit in the windows the way they still lit them, every window, even with nothing coming. Past the Bell, with the healers' room at the back, where Sister Agathe kept one lamp burning all night by the north window and a kettle on the hob for anybody who woke up hearing their name. Past the guardhouse. Round to the long low stone building behind it, with the side door open a hand's width and a red light coming out of it on to the snow.

Kestra was inside the door. She looked at him and his shield, and didn't say anything, and moved half a pace so he could get past, which with Kestra was the same as a speech.

The king was in Agnes's big chair in front of the banked hearth, with his sleeves pushed up and a bar in the fire. Hyvred was on the bench by the quench trough with her knees up, the way she sat most evenings now. She looked at Azac and lifted her hand an inch off her knee and put it down again.

"Azac," said the king.

"Majesty."

"Sit if you like." The king nodded at the other end of the bench. "Don't if you don't. There's a stool." He looked at the shield on Azac's back. "Who made that?"

"An outfitter. The first week. It split in the Hollow and they made another one." Azac took it off his back and held it out, because the king's hand was out. "Malcolm wrote on the rim."

The king took it. He turned it over on his knees: the big round guild shield, the leather facing scuffed white, the boss dented from the Narrows, and round the rim Malcolm's small square letters, *Hold*, over and over, gone silver where the wear had polished them. The king ran his thumb along them. Then he turned it back face-up and laid his hand flat on the boss.

"It's good," he said. "Too heavy at the rim for most. Not for you." He gave it back. "What did you come for?"

Azac stood holding the shield.

He'd meant to say it properly. He'd thought about how to say it all the way down the long street. He was good at telling things; he'd always told things; he'd told the story of every fight he'd ever been in while it was still going on. But he stood in Agnes's forge with the shield in his hands and the king looking at him, and what came out was:

"It bows."

The king waited.

"My ward. It's round. It's been round since the downs. But when somebody leans on it, it bows. And then it goes." He looked at the fire. "Fenna leaned on it."

He hadn't meant to say that either.

The king didn't say anything for a while. He reached into the fire with the tongs and took the bar out and looked at the end of it, which was orange, and put it back.

"Pass me that," he said. "On the bench. By your hand."

It was a boss. Half-made: a disc of iron the size of two hands, dished, rough, with the hammer-marks still sharp on it and the edge not trimmed. Azac passed it. The king turned it in his fingers.

"Do you know why it's round?" he said.

"To fit your hand behind."

"No. You could fit your hand behind a square." He turned the boss so the firelight went over it. "It's round so nothing lands square. Anything that hits a boss hits a slope. It goes off it. Round to the rim." He drew his thumb down the curve from the middle to the edge. "The rim's in the shield. The shield's on your arm. Your arm's on your shoulder, your shoulder's on your back, your back's on your legs. Your legs are on the ground." He put the boss down on his knee. "A shield's held up by the ground, son. You're only the bit in between."

Azac stood very still.

*Don't be a wall*, Declan had said. On a shingle beach, two years ago, with the lake behind him and his open hand flat against this shield. *Walls break. Be a jetty post*. And he'd hit it, not hard, and it had gone through Azac like water through a pipe and into the stones under his feet and left a ring of pebbles round his boots, and Azac hadn't gone back an inch.

"He said be a post," said Azac. "Declan. Into the ground."

"He would," said the king. He didn't look surprised. He didn't look anything. "Every sergeant on the Redmarch says it. I had it off mine, and he'll have had it off his. It's not clever." He picked the tongs up again. "What's yours held up by?"

Azac opened his mouth.

He thought about the *Ward*. About how it felt, from inside, when it came up. He stood in the middle and opened his hand and it came up out of him and round, over, a dome the size of a tent; and he held it. From the middle. Up. With his arms a little out from his sides, the way you'd hold up the middle of a sheet in the wind with nobody at the corners.

"Me," he said. "I hold it up from the middle. Like a tent."

"Tents blow away," said the king.

On the bench, Hyvred laughed. Very short, through her nose, the way she did when something surprised her; and then she put her hand over her mouth, as if she hadn't meant to.

The king looked at her. Then he looked at Azac, and for the first time something moved in the big grey face.

"Go and put the rim in the ground," he said. "And come back and tell me. I like hearing you tell things. Hyvred says you tell the whole town every night and none of it's lies."

***

It was harder than it sounded.

Everything was. That was the first thing Azac had learned in this world, before the *Ward*, before Petra, before any of it: everything that sounded easy was a year.

He tried it the next morning in the guardhouse yard, in the cold, with the sky the colour of pewter and the snow swept into banks against the walls. He stood in the middle. He opened his hand. The *Ward* came up, round, pale blue, humming. And then, instead of holding it, he tried to put it down. To push the edge of it, all the way round, into the cobbles. Not hold it up. Let the ground hold it.

It went.

Not bowed. Just went, the whole thing at once, like a soap bubble, and his ears popped.

"One," said Malcolm, from the mounting block, with his book on his knee. "Cross."

He put it up again. He put the edge down, slower. He could feel it this time: the rim touching the cobbles in front of him, a cold hard line, like putting your foot on a step in the dark. And then, as he felt for it on the left, it lifted on the right, and the whole thing tipped like a lid and slid off the air.

"Two. Cross."

It was the size of it. That was the trouble. When he held it from the middle he only had to feel one place: the middle, himself, his own two arms. When he put the rim down, the rim was everywhere. Twenty paces of it, all the way round, touching the ground in a circle he couldn't see the back of. He could feel the front. He could feel the sides if he thought about them. He couldn't feel behind him at all.

"Three. Cross. Az, it's going at the back every time."

"I know."

"Then—"

"I *know*, Mal."

He did it for an hour. Xav came out at the end of it with a practice sword, and stood outside the ward, and hit it, front-on, quite hard, the way you'd test a door. The ward hummed. The blow went down the slope of it to the rim, and into the cobbles, and the cobbles took it, and Azac felt it go through the ground under his boots like a cart going past in the next street.

It held.

Xav looked at it. Then at Azac. "Okay," he said.

"Four," said Malcolm. "Tick." He looked at the page. "That's the first tick since the first night."

Then something hit it from behind. Very small and very soft and very cold, on the back of the ward behind Azac's left shoulder, where he couldn't see; and the back of the ward went up like a sheet in a draught, and the whole thing went.

"Five," said Malcolm. "Cross. Snowball."

"Sorry," said Rui Xiang, from somewhere behind the well. He didn't sound sorry. He sounded like somebody who'd been waiting an hour to do that. "You weren't looking. You're never looking behind you. You *never* look behind you, Az, I've been behind you for three years, I've stood behind you in the Hollow and on the red and in that door, and you never once look, you just—"

"Talk," said John.

Everybody looked at John. He was sitting on the guardhouse step with his elbows on his knees, watching, the way he did.

"He doesn't look," said John. "He talks. In the Hollow, when Rui Xiang's behind him, he says *Rui Xiang's behind me somewhere, he's doing his thing*. He doesn't know where. But he says it." John looked at Azac. "You talk about the people you can't see. That's what it's for. It's not for you. It's how you know where the back is."

Azac stood in the yard with the ward gone and his arms at his sides.

He'd always thought he knew why he talked. Malcolm had said it once, in the first year, at a fire on the river road, and it had sounded right, so he'd kept it. *If you say it out loud, it's happening to somebody in a story, and not to you*. He'd thought that was it. That was why it worked. He told the fight like he was watching it from somewhere else, a long way off, and it didn't get in.

But it had never been about the fight. He thought about it now. On the downs, in the rain, he'd said *the left side's going a bit*. He'd said *you're watching, John's watching*. In the long-room door every night for forty nights, he didn't say *it's dark* or *it's snowing*. He said *Bee's on the step. Kei's on the bench. Drew's on the roof. Hyvred's awake*. He said where everybody was.

He'd been counting them. All this time. All the way round.

"Again," he said.

He put it up. He put the rim down in front. And then, out loud, in the voice from the Hollow, the one he used when it was going badly: "Okay. Xav's in front of me with a stick. Mal's on the block, on the left, writing. John's on the step, on the right. Rui Xiang's behind me. I don't know where. He's behind me somewhere with a snowball, the little—"

The rim went down. All the way round. He felt it, not as a line he was holding but as a ring of places, the way you know where the chairs are in your own room in the dark. Xav in front. Malcolm on the left. John on the right. And behind, where he couldn't see, a place he'd said, with Rui Xiang in it.

Something hit the back. Small and soft and cold.

It went down the slope of the ward and into the cobbles behind his left heel, and was gone.

Nobody said anything. Rui Xiang came out from behind the well with another snowball in his hand and his mouth open.

"Six," said Malcolm, quite quietly. "Tick."

***

Seven was a tick. Eight was a tick: Xav and Rui Xiang at once, front and back, a sword and a snowball, and the ward hummed and took them both down into the ground and Azac said *and that's both of them, folks, that's both*, and Rui Xiang threw his hands up and said *how*. Nine was a tick. He'd stopped feeling the cold.

Ten was Xav.

Xav put the stick down. Azac saw him do it; he was in front. Xav looked at him for a moment through the pale blue. Then he walked up to the ward and put his two hands on it, flat. Palms out. Fingers spread. Not hitting. Leaning.

The way they'd leaned on the north wall, every night, all autumn. The way Fenna had leaned.

It bowed.

Azac felt it go in round Xav's hands like a sheet with somebody behind it, and he felt his arms come up on their own to hold it, from the middle, the old way, and the rim lift off the cobbles all the way round, and his voice went, and the whole thing went with it.

He stood in the yard breathing hard.

"Sorry," said Xav. Quiet. "I had to know."

"Yeah." Azac's voice wasn't right. "Yeah. Okay."

"Ten," said Malcolm. He didn't write it straight away. He sat on the mounting block with the pen over the page. Then he wrote it. "Cross."

Nine out of ten. Azac stood in the middle of the yard with his hands open and looked at the place where the ward had been. He knew what it was. He didn't have to have anybody tell him. A blow went off it, a stick or a snowball or the weather, because a blow wanted to go somewhere and the ward showed it where. Down. Into the ground. But a person leaning didn't want to go anywhere. A person leaning just *stayed*. Smiling. With their hands flat. And he couldn't push it down into the ground, because it was a person, and he didn't want to.

So he held it up instead. The old way. And it bowed.

He opened his window that night, on his pallet in the long room, for the first time in a while.

It had been eighty-nine before the yard. He looked at it a long time. Then he shut it and got up and went and stood in the door.

***

The storm came three days later.

Hild heard it first. She came in from the north gate at noon, with snow on her shoulders, and stood in the long-room door beside Azac, and said, "The mountains are getting up." And that was all she said, but Brannoch heard her across the room and put his cup down, and within an hour the Watch were going down the long street shutter by shutter with hammers and nails, and Agnes had the big hearth up for the first time in a month, and the king was standing in her side door looking north.

It came at dusk. Down off the Greyspine like a wall falling, over the white valley and the rings and the shut pass, eleven miles in what Hyvred said was the time it took her to count to forty, and into the town all at once. The lamps in the windows went out down the long street one after another, not like the absence, not a curve, just the wind finding every gap in every shutter. Snow sideways. Snow upward, round the corners of the houses, in a roar that never stopped long enough to be a sound; it was just there, all the time, the way the dark was.

John's picture of the valley sat in Azac's window, small, in the corner, where it had sat since Hyvred asked. Twenty-three holes in the snow. The rings, still. The round place at the top. It stayed lit. Snow didn't count. Only them.

At the second hour, Tiger came in out of it.

He came through the long-room door past Azac's shoulder so fast that the snow came in with him like a sack being emptied, with his ears flat to his skull and blood running down from one of them; and he said, "The Bell. The back. The roof's going."

Azac was already moving.

***

It was the old wing.

The Bell was two buildings that had grown together: the inn on the street, with its good thick walls and its sign, and behind it across a little yard the old long room that had been the first inn, a hundred years before, low and stone, with a slate roof on rafters gone black with smoke. That was the healers' room now. That was where Sister Agathe kept her lamp and her kettle.

The roof was going.

Azac saw it as he came round the corner of the yard with his shield up against the wind and Tiger at his elbow. The whole north side of it, the side the wind came at, was lifting. Not blowing in. *Lifting*. He saw it rise a hand's width off the wall-tops in a gust, the whole length of the roof at once, like a lid on a pot, with the slates rattling all over it like teeth; and come down; and rise again, more. Slates were coming off the top in ones and twos and going away into the dark like birds. One went past his head. That was what had had Tiger's ear.

The door was open. He went in.

Sister Agathe was in the middle of the room with her arms over somebody in a bed, and snow coming down on her through a long split along the ridge where the slates had gone. The lamp by the north window was out. There were people on the pallets and the benches: he counted without meaning to, the way he counted the long room. Olwen of Coldwater in the north window seat with a blanket round her, as if she hadn't moved since the autumn. Rhys Hywel on the end bed, sitting up, white, with Corporal Dunn beside him. Mother Ivey from the laundry, on the bench by the hearth. And in the bed under Agathe's arms, a little old woman with a terrible cough, who Azac knew was Old Linnet because on the first night, when Fenna's hand went loose in hers, she'd reached for Perrin's and closed the gap.

Six. And Agathe. And Tiger. And him.

The roof went up again. The whole room heard it go: a long groan from the rafters, a crack somewhere at the far end, and the split along the ridge opened a hand wider and the wind came straight down through it on to the beds like water out of a bucket.

He planted his feet in the middle of the floor.

He didn't think about it. He didn't have time to think about the yard or the king or the ten. He opened his hand and the *Ward* came up out of him, round, pale blue, humming like a glass, over the beds and the benches and the window seat and Agathe with her arms out; and the snow coming down through the split hit the top of it and slid off it, down the round sides, and he put the rim down.

Into the floor. Into the old flagstones, all the way round, a ring of places. He felt it touch.

The roof came down on the wall-tops again with a bang that shook dust out of the rafters, and lay there, groaning.

"Okay," said Azac. His voice came out of him in the Hollow voice, too loud, because it was the only one he had. "Okay. Okay. Agathe's in the middle with Linnet. Mother Ivey's by the hearth. Rhys is on the end. Dunn's with him. Olwen's in the window. Tiger's at the door. I've got the roof."

And then the wind hit it.

***

He'd never been hit like that.

Not by Declan's hand. Not by the thing in the Hollow that had split his first shield. Not by Fenna. The wind came down out of the dark through the split in the roof and struck the top of the ward like a hammer on an anvil, and kept striking, and didn't stop; and every few breaths there was a bigger one, a gust, that came in through the gap in a lump, solid as a door, and hit the dome and *leaned*.

The first big one, it bowed.

He felt it go in at the top. He felt his arms come up on their own to hold it, from the middle, the old way. And the rim lifted off the floor on the north side, toward the window, where he couldn't see; and the snow came in under it on the north side in a long white tongue across the flags toward Olwen's feet.

"No," he said. "No, no—"

It went.

The whole thing. Like a soap bubble. And the snow came down on everybody at once through the split, and Old Linnet in the bed coughed and coughed under Agathe's arms, and Rhys Hywel made a sound.

Azac put it back up.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. Okay. It went. It's back. It's back up, it's—"

It went again. Not at the top this time. At the north side. Behind him. He hadn't said the window. He'd said everybody and then he'd stopped saying them, because the wind was so loud, and he'd started saying *the roof, the roof, it's holding, it's holding* like a man on a rope saying *rope*, and the north side, the back, where Olwen was, had gone thin and lifted and gone.

He put it back up. His arms were shaking.

"Two," said a voice in the door.

Malcolm. With his book inside his coat and snow in his hair, and behind him Xav, and John with a mote of light going in front of him like a lantern, and somewhere in the dark Rui Xiang, probably. Malcolm stood in the doorway with his back against the frame, out of the way, and took the book out, and opened it, and took the pen out of his mouth.

"Two crosses," he said. "Go on. I've got you."

***

They couldn't move them.

Xav tried. He went out into the little yard with Tiger to see if they could get people across it to the inn, thirty paces, and came back in a minute later with Tiger's arm over his shoulder and his own face cut along the cheekbone. "No," he said. "It's full of slate. It's coming off the inn roof too. You can't see your hand." He looked at Agathe. "Can she go?"

"Linnet? In that?" Sister Agathe didn't take her arms off the old woman. "She'd be dead by the door. It's in her chest. She needs to be warm and still and not breathing snow, and that's all she needs, and that's all I've got." She looked up at the pale blue over her head. Then she looked at Azac. "Can you?"

"How long?"

"Till it drops."

"When does it drop?"

Nobody knew. John said Hild said the storms off the Greyspine went *till they'd done*. Corporal Dunn said the one the winter he joined had gone two days. Mother Ivey, by the hearth, said three, and then said she might have been thinking of a different one.

And Olwen of Coldwater, in the north window seat, with her blanket round her, said: "I'm not going anywhere." Not loud. She didn't look round. She was looking at the window, at the shutter, which was nailed shut and banging and had snow coming in round its edges. "He's out there. In this. I'm not going anywhere."

Her son. In the second ring. Eleven miles off, standing in a field in the dark with the snow coming down on him and his hands open at his sides, the way they'd all stood since the autumn.

Azac looked at her. At the back of her head, at her grey hair cut short.

"Okay," he said. "Nobody's going anywhere."

And he put the rim down.

***

He talked.

He talked all night. He didn't decide to. It was the only way he had, and it was the only thing that worked, and some time in the first hour he understood that it was also the only thing in that room that wasn't the wind.

Because the wind was talking too. That was the worst of it. It came down through the split in the roof and across the top of the ward and round the room, and it wasn't a sound so much as a voice, high, long, saying the same thing over and over; and to anybody who'd stood in a field all autumn being called, it sounded like a name. Rhys Hywel had his hands over his ears. Corporal Dunn had his arm round Rhys and was saying *it's the wind, lad, it's the wind, it's only the wind*, and his own voice wasn't steady. Mother Ivey by the hearth was crying, without any noise, with her apron over her face.

So Azac talked over it.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, here's where we are. It's about the third hour. The wind's in the north. It's coming in through the roof. It's not getting in. Agathe's in the middle with Linnet; Linnet's coughing; that's all right, Agathe says coughing's all right, coughing's the good bit. Mother Ivey's by the hearth. The hearth's lit. Somebody put more wood on it; that was Tiger. Tiger's at the door. His ear's stopped bleeding. Mostly." A gust came in, a big one, and hit the top of the dome, and he felt it go down the slope of it, round, to the rim, and into the flags under his boots, through the old stone, gone. "And that's a big one, and it's gone. It's gone into the floor. The floor's a hundred years old. It's not going anywhere."

He was talking to them. Not to somebody a long way off, watching. To them. To Rhys with his hands over his ears. To Dunn. To the people under the roof.

"Rhys is on the end bed," said Azac. "Dunn's got him. Dunn's been on the wall twenty years. Dunn's not going anywhere either. Mal's in the door with his book. Xav's on the bench with a cut on his face, he's fine, he's eating something, of course he is. John's got a light up. Rui Xiang's somewhere. Probably behind me. He's always behind me." Something small and soft hit the back of the ward and slid down it. "That's him. That's Rui Xiang, ladies and gentlemen. He's fine."

On the end bed, Rhys Hywel took one hand off his ear.

"Olwen's in the window," said Azac. And this time he didn't stop. He'd stopped there before; the first time, and the second, and both times the back had gone. He said it, and kept saying it, the place he couldn't see. "Olwen's behind me. In the window seat. The shutter's banging. It's nailed; Brannoch's lads nailed it this afternoon; it's not going to come in. Olwen's looking at it."

The rim went down all the way round. He felt it. A ring of places, the way you know where the chairs are in your own room.

"Five," said Malcolm, from the door, a long time later. "Tick. Six. Tick." And later: "Eleven. Tick."

***

Some time after the middle of the night, Olwen spoke.

"Who are you talking to?"

She hadn't turned round. Azac could see the back of her head past his shoulder if he turned his own, a little, and he didn't; he'd learned that, in the last few hours. If he turned to look at the back, the front lifted.

"You," he said.

She was quiet for a while. The wind came down through the roof and leaned on the ward and went round it and into the floor.

"You say where everybody is," said Olwen.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

He thought about it. He had time; it was a long night. He thought about the yard and the snowball and John on the step. About forty nights in a doorway saying *Bee's on the step, Kei's on the bench*. About a gravel bar on the Wren, and a line in the air going toward a man with his eyes shut, and Azac twenty paces off with no time, putting his finger to the air.

"So I know where the edge is," he said. "If I say you, you're under it. If I forget you, I've left you out, and that's where it goes thin. That's where it always went." He breathed. "I didn't know that till the yard."

Olwen didn't say anything for a long time.

Then she said: "Say Dafydd."

Azac didn't understand.

"My boy," said Olwen. "Dafydd. He's out there. In the second ring. In this." She was still looking at the shutter. "You say where everybody is. Say where he is."

The ward hummed. The wind leaned on it.

Azac knew what would happen. He'd been afraid of it all night; he'd been afraid of it for forty nights. Somebody would ask him to hold something he couldn't hold. Somebody would lean on it with their flat hands and want to go somewhere it didn't reach, and he'd hold it up from the middle with his arms shaking, the old way, and say *sorry*, *sorry*, and it would bow. It bowed when he was sorry. It always had. It had bowed round Fenna's hands with him saying it.

He couldn't put a ward over a field eleven miles off. He couldn't put one over a boy he'd never seen.

He said it anyway.

"Dafydd's in the valley," he said. "In the second ring. Eleven miles north. It's snowing on him. He's standing still, the way they stand. I can't reach him." His voice went, a little, and he let it. "I'm sorry, Olwen. I can't reach him. He's not under it. I'm sorry."

And the ward didn't bow.

He felt it not bow. He felt *sorry* go into it and down the round sides of it and into the rim and into the floor like a blow, like anything else, and the flags take it, a hundred years of stone, and the roof stay where it was, round, humming, with the snow sliding down off the top of it.

Because it wasn't a door. That was the thing. He understood it standing there, with the wind on the roof and Olwen's back to him, so suddenly that he nearly laughed. Fenna's had been a door. He'd stood across a doorway and held a woman in who wanted to go out, and every bit of him had been sorry to be doing it, and a door that's sorry it's shut opens. That was why it had bowed. Not because he'd been weak. Because she'd been leaning on it from inside, and inside was where his ward was *for*, and it hadn't known which way to face.

This wasn't a door. It was a roof. It wasn't holding anybody in. It was over them. And you could be as sorry as you liked under a roof; you could cry under one, and shout, and lie awake all night hearing your name in the wind; and it didn't matter to the roof. It didn't need you to be anything. It just stayed where it was. That was all a roof did.

*Stay*, Thea had said, in the channel, five nights ago, about a black sword. *It's not waiting. It's holding*.

"I'm sorry," said Azac again, to Olwen's back, and to Dafydd, and the ward took that too.

Olwen didn't answer. But after a while she drew her feet up into the window seat, under the blanket, out of the draught from the shutter, the way you do when you've decided to stay somewhere for the night. And put her head against the wall.

"Twenty," said Malcolm, from the door. "Tick."

***

He talked till dawn.

He said Linnet coughing and then Linnet not coughing, and Agathe sitting down for the first time on the end of her bed. He said Mother Ivey asleep by the hearth with her apron still over her face. He said Rhys Hywel asking Corporal Dunn if he could have some of Agathe's tea and Dunn getting up to get it, *Dunn's up, Dunn's at the kettle, that's a first, that's never happened before, ladies and gentlemen*, and Rhys laughing, once, a boy's laugh, startled out of him. He said Tiger asleep against the door frame with his ear tied up in Xav's scarf. He said John's light going out because John had fallen asleep on the bench, and John waking up and putting it back. He said Rui Xiang, behind him, somewhere, every time he went round; and every so often a pinch of snow off the floor hit the back of the ward, and slid off, and went into the flags.

He said Olwen. Every time he went round. Olwen's in the window. Olwen's asleep. Olwen's in the window. And Dafydd, after her, every time, without her asking again: Dafydd's in the valley. In the second ring. I can't reach him.

His arms stopped shaking somewhere in the fifth hour. He noticed because they weren't up. He'd let them down to his sides some time and not known it. He wasn't holding anything up. He was standing in the middle of a room with his hands open and a ring of places round him in the floor, and the ground was holding it, a hundred years of it, and he was the bit in between.

The wind dropped before first light.

It didn't stop. It went from a wall to a wind, the way the call used to go from a sea to a tide at the end of the night, and the roar went back into being a sound, and then a long way off, up the valley, it was a sound going away. Through the split in the roof the dark had gone grey.

Hild came in at dawn with snow to her knees. She looked at the room from the door, past Malcolm, the way she looked at things, with her one eye and the white scar. At the beds. At the people in them. At the pale blue over all of it, round, humming, with snow lying on the top of it in a drift a hand deep, like snow on a hill.

"The mountains have lain down," she said.

Azac didn't let it go straight away. He didn't want to drop a hand of snow on Old Linnet. He narrowed it, slowly, from the top, and let the drift slide off the side of it on to the flags by the hearth in a heap, *and that's the snow going off, folks, that's on the floor now, somebody's going to have to sweep that*; and then he let the rim come up out of the floor, gently, all the way round, the way you'd take your hand off a sleeping cat. And it was gone.

He sat down on the flags where he was.

"Forty-one," said Malcolm, in the door. He'd counted every gust big enough to feel. All night. In the book, in the small square hand, page after page. He looked at the page for a long time. "Forty-one ticks. After the two crosses." He shut the book. "That's ten out of ten, Az. Four times. And a bit."

Azac sat on the floor.

He knew. He'd known some time in the night, round the fifth hour, when his arms came down. He'd known the way you know you can ride a bicycle, not because somebody tells you but because you're already halfway down the road. If he stood up now and opened his hand, it would come up, and the rim would go down, and it would hold. And the next time. And the time after that. It wasn't a thing he did any more. It was a thing that was there.

He opened his window anyway. Sitting on the floor of the healers' room in the grey, with a drift of snow by the hearth and Olwen of Coldwater asleep in the window seat with her head against the wall.
