*Keep your thumb out of the way.*

He hit it anyway.

Not hard. A tap, the hammer coming down on the end of the bar a finger's width wide of where he'd meant, on the side of his left thumb where it held the tongs; and the pain went up his arm in a bright line and he said a word his father would have taken a strap to him for, and put the hammer down on the anvil and stood looking at his hand.

Fifty years. Fifty years since he'd last done that. He'd been sixteen, in Brakewater, with his father at the bellows and the iron going past ready while he stared at a girl in the yard, and his father had said *thumb* and nothing else, and he'd never done it again. Not on the Redmarch, not at the castle forge, not in all the years since he'd stopped being allowed to call himself a smith and kept on being one anyway at night. Never.

He looked at his thumb. It was going red already, and then white at the edge, where the nail would go black in the morning.

By the door, Kestra didn't say anything. She'd seen. Kestra saw everything; it was most of what she was for. She stood with her back to the frame and her arms folded and looked at him, and he looked at his thumb, and neither of them said anything at all, which with Kestra was a conversation.

"Sixty-six," he said, at last.

"I know how old you are."

"My hand moved." He turned it over. "It didn't use to move."

"It's moved since the gatehouse," said Kestra. "You've been holding the tongs in the other one since the snow. I thought you'd noticed."

He hadn't.

He put his left hand flat on the edge of the anvil, the way you'd put it on a table to stop it shaking, and looked at it there. A big plain hand with soot in every crease and the old scars white across the knuckles, and a thumb going black. It had held tongs every night for fifty years. It had held a gate shut for fifteen nights in the autumn, with something in the fields leaning on it, until the blood ran out of his nose into his beard; and it had held a sword on the Redmarch before that, and a bar on Garrow's anvil by a lake while a man he'd known for fifteen years put his hand on the other end of it and made it orange.

It moved now. A little. When he wasn't looking.

He picked the tongs up in his right.

"Pump the bellows," he said. "Slowly. I want to finish these tonight."

***

They were tongs. Agnes's old pair had worn through at the rivet over the winter, the way tongs did, the two halves going slack on each other until they couldn't hold a bar without dropping it; and she'd said nothing about it, because she said nothing about anything, and gone on using them; and he'd watched her drop a bar twice in a week and gone and got a length of good iron from the back of her store.

Two halves. You forged them separately, the left and the right, the same and opposite, each one a long handle and a short jaw with a boss in the middle where they'd cross. You punched a hole through each boss. You laid one over the other with the holes together. And then you put a rivet through, hot, and closed it over, and the two halves were one thing.

He had the halves done. They lay on the bench by the quench trough, side by side, grey-black, with the hammer-marks still bright. He was doing the rivet. A little stub of round bar in the tongs, in the fire, going from red to orange.

Hyvred came in at the side door late in the evening, a little after the bell for the tenth hour.

She came in fast. Not running; she didn't run any more, not since the snow; but fast, with her tail out straight behind her the way it went when she'd been holding something on the tower stair all the way down, and she stopped inside the door with her hand on the frame, and Kestra moved half a pace, and the king looked up from the fire.

He knew her face by now. He'd sat across a forge from it most evenings for two months. He knew what it did when the valley moved and what it did when it didn't, and when she was frightened and when she was angry and when she'd seen something she didn't know what to do with yet. This was the last one.

"It's not here," she said, before he could ask. "The valley's the same. Twenty-three. It didn't move. It's Cinderhold."

He put the rivet back in the fire.

"Tell me."

She told him. She stood in the door with her hand on the frame and told him the way she'd learned to tell things that winter, from Corvin and from Azac, start to end, with the numbers in: fog, in the dead of winter, on the Redmarch, where there'd never been winter fog. Kharvos on the east wall in it, eight of the big ones and forty scouts. A soldier dead on the north stretch. Six hurt. The wall held.

And at dawn, an hour east of the fort, round the grave on the knoll, people.

"Forty," said Hyvred. "Standing in a ring. Round the black stone. Facing in. Yan says there's nobody in them. Holes. Like the curve here." She swallowed. "And the big ones lying among them. Like dogs. Like the dog that came down the pass in the autumn and lay down beside the thing in the valley."

The king didn't say anything.

"They came as far as a line," said Hyvred. "A hundred and forty paces out from the stone. All the way round. Niko's line, where the frost stops. And they couldn't come in. They stood at the line all night facing the grave and at the second bell they all turned round and walked east. Together." She stopped. "Both of them. The hunters and the people. Walking together. On the red. Wendy says the soldiers on the wall all saw it."

The rivet was orange in the fire. He took it out. It was the right colour; it had been ready a while. He held it in the tongs and didn't do anything with it, and it went past orange into red, and past red, and he let it.

"And Rhea," said Hyvred, after a moment, in a different voice. "Their guard. She cut one of the big ones' arm off from three paces. Past her edge. In the fog, on the old wall. Petra says it's Reaching. Petra's a bit cross about it."

"Is she," said the king.

"She says she isn't."

He put the rivet down on the anvil. It had gone dull. It would have to go back in.

"Sit down," he said. "On the bench. You've come down a hundred and twelve steps in the cold with that in your mouth." He nodded at the bench by the quench trough. "Sit down and say it again. Slowly. The bit about the line."

***

He didn't sleep. He didn't try. He sat in Agnes's big chair in front of the banked hearth with the fire breathing under its ash and his black thumb in his lap, and Kestra on her stool by the door with her chin on her chest, asleep, the way she slept, sitting up, so lightly that if he'd stood up she'd have been on her feet before he was; and he thought about it all night.

He'd been on the Redmarch for twenty years, one way and another, from the war at twenty to the crown and after. He'd stood on the east wall at Cinderhold with a spear, and with a captain's sash, and with Halvard beside him in a plain coat, looking out at the black. He knew Kharvos. Not the way the songs knew it. The way you know a neighbour you've fought with across a fence for forty years: what it did in spring, and what it did in summer, and what it never did.

It never came in winter.

That was the first thing. Everybody on the Redmarch knew it the way they knew that water was wet. The big ones hated the cold. They went east at the first frost the way geese go south, and the Redmarch had its winters to itself, every winter of his life, to mend the walls and bury the dead and count what was left. He'd built his whole long war round it. *Hold till the frost*.

And it never walked with anybody.

That was the second thing, and it was the one that had kept him in the chair. Kharvos came at you on its own. Always. It came over the red in the spring like weather, and it hit the wall, and it went home. It didn't have friends. It had never once in his life had anybody standing beside it; nobody would. Even the people in the villages at its edge looked at the floor until it had gone.

He thought about the autumn. The dog that had come down the pass from the west at three in the morning and lain down beside the thing in the valley, *like a dog by its master's chair*. He'd said it himself, on the tower, to the girl with the gold eye, and she'd written it to Solmere through Hana: *they've met. One of them's sent the other a dog.* He'd thought that was the strange thing. One dog. One night.

He'd been wrong about which was the strange thing.

***

He went to the well at first light.

Hild was there. She was always there at first light, on the rim of the well in the guardhouse yard with her back to the north gate and her one eye on the mountains, listening; she'd sat there every dawn since the snow, the way the girl on the tower counted. Petra came across the yard a little after him with her sword on her back and her breath smoking, and sat on the other side of the rim, the way they'd sat in the autumn after they'd measured each other's walls, the pale one and the grey; and the three of them sat on the edge of the well in the cold and didn't say anything for a while.

"Has it ever," said the king. "In your time. On the Redmarch."

"No," said Hild.

She didn't ask what. She'd heard it from Hyvred too, at midnight, on the guardhouse step. She'd lost her eye on the Redmarch at twenty-nine, the morning after the Brenn crossing, to a thing on the bank with too many arms; she'd been on it ten years before that and ten after. She sat with her hands flat on the rim of the well, the way she sat, and looked at the mountains.

"Not in winter," she said. "Never in winter. And never with anybody. I've seen them come over the wall at Cinderhold in their hundreds, and over the river at Brenn, and in the night at the old west fort. Always on their own. Like a flood's on its own." She was quiet. "Their arms go *hush*, *hush*. On the red, in the dark. I'd know it anywhere. I heard it in my sleep for ten years. Nobody ever walked with that sound, sir. You'd have to be dead already."

"They are," said Petra.

Both of them looked at her.

"The ones with nobody in them," said Petra. Flat, the way she said everything. "Yan says. Holes. You'd have to be dead already to walk with that. So they are." She looked at her hands. "Or something like dead. I don't know the word. The boy with the lights would."

The king sat on the rim of the well with his black thumb in his lap.

"It's both hands," he said.

They waited.

He held his hands up. Both of them, in the grey light, over the well: the right one steady, the left one with its black thumb, not quite. "My father used to say a smith with one hand's a man with a stick. You can't forge with one. You hold the iron in the left and you hit it with the right, and if you've only got the right you're hitting something that moves." He turned the left one over. "Kharvos has always been a hammer. It hits. It hits the Redmarch every spring and goes home, and we hold till the frost, and it's been that way since before my grandfather. Forty years of my life. A hammer, on its own, swinging."

"And now something's holding the iron for it," said Hild, slowly.

"Since the autumn." He put his hands down. "The valley. The call, the rings, the people with nobody in them. That's not Kharvos. That's never been Kharvos. It came from the other way, from the west, over the pass, where there's nothing on any map. It holds. It doesn't hit; I stood on that gatehouse fifteen nights and it never once *hit*. It leaned. It held people still in a field and called the rest out of their houses to come and stand with them." He looked north, at the mountains, at the white notch of the pass. "And two nights ago, in the fog, on the other side of the kingdom, the hammer came over the wall at Cinderhold in the dead of winter, which it never does. And while it was hitting, the holding hand walked round the back of the fort to the one place in the east we didn't know we needed to hold, and stood there all night."

Nobody said anything.

"Both hands," said the king. "At once. East and west. One holding and one hitting." He looked at the well. "And us in between, on the anvil."

***

Corvin's cord warmed at noon.

He brought it across the yard himself, at a run, which Corvin didn't do, with his board under one arm and the cord held out in front of him like something that might go off: a hand's length of knotted grey and blue, with a strip of blank linen bound into the middle of it. One of Hana's. She'd made one for every guide in the kingdom, so that the seven could write to each other faster than a rider; and she'd made them so that they carried what you meant and wrote it on the strip in the hand of whoever held the other end. The king didn't understand it. He didn't need to. He understood a horse that didn't have to stop.

The strip had writing on it. In Corvin's hand, which wasn't Corvin's.

"Aldous," said Corvin, out of breath. "From Cinderhold. He sent the letter by the commander's rider last night, sir, it'll be a fortnight on the road. But he's sent it down his cord to Hana as well, and Hana's sent it on to every guide." He held it out. "She says she thought you'd want it before the fortnight."

The king took it.

He read it standing up, in the guardhouse yard, in the cold. It wasn't long. Aldous had never written long; he wrote like a man who'd filled in the fort's book of the dead for twenty years and knew what a line cost. The fog. The east wall. *Eight of the big ones and some forty scouts. The wall held. One dead of ours, Trooper Ennis on the north stretch; six hurt.* The people round the grave. The line. *Both together, on the red, in the open.*

And at the end:

*I do not write that we think so. Rhea saw it, from the east wall. So did I.*

He stood and read it twice.

Then he laughed. Out loud, in the yard, with Corvin standing there with his board and Petra and Hild on the well, a short bark of a laugh that he hadn't known was coming. He hadn't laughed since the count.

"*Don't write we think*," he said. "He's had that off the girl. Off the channel. Off me." He gave the cord back to Corvin. "Every report that man's sent me in three years has said *we believe* and *it is thought* and *the commander is of the opinion*. And now he's had it off a girl on a tower, and he writes *Rhea saw it*." He shook his head. "Good."

"Sir," said Corvin.

"Write back to Hana for me. Now. On that." The king looked north, at the pass. "Three things. Tell her to send Aldous's letter on to the Council as it stands, every word, in his hand, before anybody in Solmere has a chance to tell them what it means. Tell her I've read it. And tell her I'll be home at the thaw, to say the rest to their faces."

Corvin was already writing.

***

He knew what the Council would want.

He'd known since the well. He sat in Agnes's chair in the afternoon with Hyvred on the bench and the tongs' two halves across his knees and thought about it the way you think about a piece of iron before you put it in the fire: where it'd crack, and where it'd hold.

They'd want one target.

They always did. A Council was a room full of frightened men who'd been told the kingdom was on an anvil, and a frightened man wanted something to hit. They'd read Aldous's letter, and they'd read *Kharvos*, and they'd stop reading. Kharvos was a word they knew. They'd had forty years of it. They'd want an army raised in the spring and marched east over the red into the black, to hit the hammer, because the hammer was the thing they could see.

And while the army was in the black, the other hand would hold whatever it liked.

"They'll want the summoned, too," he said, out loud.

Hyvred looked up.

"All of you. In Solmere. Under the Crown, where they can count you." He turned one half of the tongs over on his knee. "Thirty-five people who can see things nobody born here can see. Who write to each other across the whole kingdom in a heartbeat, in a language nobody in the Council can read. And the woman in Solmere in the middle of it all, with her cords to every guide." He looked at the fire. "They'll be frightened of her by the spring. They're half frightened of her now. A frightened Council wants what frightens it where it can see it."

"Hana won't go," said Hyvred.

"No." He almost smiled. "No, I don't suppose she will. And somebody'll stand in front of her, if I know that lot." He put the half of the tongs down. "But I'd rather be in the room."

"Is that why you're going home?"

"I'm going home to say no." He looked at her. "Not by cord. Not by letter. They've had my letters all winter; a letter's a thing you can read in a corridor and put in a drawer. You can't put a king in a drawer." He picked up the rivet in the tongs, cold now, dull. "No army into the black. Not this spring. No summoned brought to Solmere like hens to a coop. Hold both walls. Watch both hands. And find out what's holding them together." He turned the rivet in the tongs. "That's all. It's not clever. It'll take me a month to say it to them and they'll hate every word."

"Why at the thaw?"

He didn't answer straight away. He looked north. Through the forge's side door, up the long street, past the lamps still lit in every window, to the white wall of the Greyspine and the notch of the pass, shut under four feet of snow.

"Because that opens at the thaw," he said. "And I'm not leaving this town on its own the week it does."

***

At dusk, Kestra said it.

She didn't usually. She'd said it once in the autumn, on the gatehouse, and once on the step the night before the man from the lake went home; and both times he'd said *no*, and she'd let it go. She said it now from the door, with her arms folded, while he put the rivet back in the fire.

"You could write to him."

"No."

"He'd want to know the grave held."

"He knows." The king didn't look round. "It's on every one of their windows. They'll have put it there last night, the girl at Cinderhold and the boy with the snake. He's read it. He'll have read it on his step with his tea going cold, and his girl beside him with her hand on that sword." He watched the rivet. "He knows the grave held. He doesn't need a king telling him what he's already read."

"That's not why."

"No," said the king.

Kestra waited.

He thought about a cabin on a lake. About a bar on an anvil, and a man's hand on the other end of it, making it orange. About a step outside a gatehouse in the snow, four nights, the two of them sitting there not saying anything, and a man standing up before the fifth dawn and going home on foot, and the king not writing because a letter would be read and not answered and that was worse.

"Because he'd read it," he said, "and not answer. And then I'd have to sit here knowing he'd read it and not answered. And I'm sixty-six, and my thumb's black, and I haven't the patience for it any more." He took the rivet out. It was ready. "He'll write when he writes. He never has. He answers. He's answered everybody who ever wrote to him for eighteen years, Halvard says, and never once written first." He laid the two halves of the tongs together on the anvil, boss over boss, the holes lined up. "If he ever does, I'll know something's changed."

Kestra didn't say anything else.

He put the rivet through.

***

It went in hot. Through both holes, the stub of it standing up a finger's length above the top boss; and he took up the hammer in his right hand and the tongs in his left, the old tongs, Agnes's, slack at the rivet, and held the new pair down flat on the anvil, and hit.

Three blows. Four. Not hard. Closing the head of the rivet over, round and down, the way you'd close a hand. His left hand moved, once, on the old tongs. He let it. He didn't hit his thumb.

Five. Six. The head of the rivet spread and flattened and closed down over the boss, and he took the hammer off it, and picked up the new tongs, and worked them open and shut a few times while the rivet was still hot, so it wouldn't set stiff. They moved. Smooth, on the one pin, the two halves crossing and uncrossing.

Hyvred had come off the bench to watch. She stood at his elbow with her hands in her sleeves and her gold eye on the tongs.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it."

"It's just a nail."

"A rivet." He held the tongs up in the firelight, open, closed. "Two halves of nothing. Two bits of bent iron you couldn't pick up a coal with. Put one pin through the middle of them, in the right place, and they're a hand." He opened and shut them again. "That's what's holding them together. The hammer and the other thing. Something's put a pin through them, somewhere, and I don't know what, and I don't know where." He put the tongs down on the anvil. "That's what I'm going home to say. Never mind the hammer. Never mind the hand. Find the pin."

Hyvred looked at the tongs for a long time.

"And then what?"

"Then you knock it out," said the king. "And you've got two bits of iron that can't hold anything."

He put the tongs in the quench. They went in with a hiss and a gout of steam that came up round both of them and filled the forge for a moment, white, like the fog must have been on the east wall at Cinderhold; and cleared. He took them out and dried them on his apron and hung them on Agnes's nail by the door, beside her old pair.

On the bench by the quench trough, Hyvred sat down again with her chin on her knees. In a moment she'd go back up the hundred and twelve steps and count, the way she counted at dusk; twenty-three, the same, and Corvin would write it on his board. In a moment Azac would be in the long-room door saying where everybody was. Up the long street the lamps were lit in every window. North of all of it, the pass was shut.

The king sat down in Agnes's chair with his hands on his knees.

He looked at them. The right one steady. The left one, with its black thumb, moving, a little, on its own. Both of them his.

"Both hands," he said, to nobody. And put another bar in the fire.
