Kestra was on her fourteenth book.

She'd started it in Valcrest, on the last night of the Lists, when the thirteenth ran out halfway down a page about a man paying a ballad girl silver to go on singing a song about somebody else. Plain, black, the size of her hand. The same pocket. The king still read them. Not every page. Some.

She wrote what people did. Not what they said. Mouths lied. Feet didn't.

And in thirteen years on the king's guard she had written down the Advisor's feet perhaps a hundred times, and they had always said the same thing. They pointed at the work. Whatever was on the table in front of him: a map, a report, a letter, a list of the dead. In council, with the lords shouting round him, his feet were flat on the floor and square to the table and had not moved. On the wall at Valcrest, with forty thousand people in the stands, they had pointed at the sand. In his study at midnight, she supposed, they pointed at the desk. She had never been in his study at midnight. She'd never needed to be.

*The Advisor's feet point at the work*, she had written, in the third book, eleven years ago. *They always have. I do not think he knows he has feet*.

She'd never had to write it again. It had stayed true.

Until the afternoon of the rider.

***

She'd been at the forge door.

That was where she went when the king went to the castle forge, which was most mornings he hadn't slept and some afternoons he couldn't think. She stood in the door with her back to the frame and her book in her coat and watched the yard, and the king stood at the anvil in his shirtsleeves and made things that didn't need making, and neither of them said anything, and it was the best hour of her day. She'd never told him that. She suspected he knew.

That afternoon he hadn't been at the anvil. He'd been on the step. And the Advisor had come across the yard from the gatehouse, in his shirtsleeves too, which she had never seen in thirteen years, with a letter in his hand, walking the way a man walks when he's been standing still too long and has forgotten how. And the king had looked up from the step, and moved over.

The Advisor had sat down on the step beside him.

They'd sat there all afternoon. They hadn't hit anything. Not once. Kestra had stood in the door behind them with her back to the frame for four hours and watched the backs of two old men's heads, and the light go round the yard, and the Advisor's hand on his knee with the letter in it, not reading it, just holding it. And at some point in the third hour the king had put one big hand on the Advisor's shoulder and left it there, and the Advisor had let him.

She hadn't written any of that down. She'd told the cat girl, after, at supper, when she asked: *they didn't hit anything*. That was all. The cat girl had nodded, slowly, and said *thank you, Kestra*, and gone away and written something in the channel. Kestra didn't know what. She didn't need to.

What she wrote down was the Advisor's feet.

Because when he'd got up off the step, at dusk, stiffly, the way you get up at his age after four hours on stone, and stood there in the yard with the letter, they hadn't pointed at the work. There wasn't any work. There was a yard, and a forge, and a king on a step.

They'd pointed north.

***

*The Advisor stood at the north window of the west tower for an hour after supper*, she wrote, the second night. *He did not read anything. He did not have anything in his hands*.

And the third: *In the long gallery, on the way to council, he turned right at the north stair and stopped and came back. Twice*.

And the fifth: *The bottom drawer on the left in his desk is open. There are letters in it. A great many. Unsealed, in rows. It has stayed open all week*.

She'd seen that one by accident. She'd gone up with a report from the gate guard, in the morning, and he'd said *come in* without looking up, and she'd stood by the desk while he read it. And seen the drawer. Pulled out. Full of letters, laid in rows, in order, every one of them opened, every one with a broken grey seal, and at the bottom a few loose sheets with a black ribbon round them. She had known the Advisor thirteen years. She had stood by that desk a thousand times. She had never once seen any drawer in it open that he wasn't putting something into.

He'd seen her see it. He hadn't shut it.

*It is still open*, she wrote, on the eighth day. And on the ninth. And then she stopped writing it, because it was going to stay open, and she only wrote down things that changed.

It changed on the morning they left. The king sent her up to say the horses were ready, and the study door was open, and the drawer was open, and there was nothing in it at all. Just the dark square on the wood at the bottom where something had lain for nine years and kept the light off. The Advisor was standing by the desk in his riding coat with his saddlebags over his arm. He wouldn't let the groom carry them. He carried them down the hundred and four stairs himself.

*The drawer is empty*, she wrote. *He is carrying it*.

***

He asked on the twelfth day.

At the forge. In the morning, in the grey, with the king at the anvil this time, drawing out a bar for nothing, and Kestra in the door. The Advisor came across the yard in his grey coat with his hands behind his back, and stopped at the edge of the heat. He didn't come in. He'd never come in. In thirteen years she'd never seen him set foot inside the forge; he stood at the edge of it the way people stand at the edge of a river, and talked to the king over the noise.

He didn't talk. He waited. For the bar to go dull, and the king to turn and put it back in the coals.

"Severin," he said.

"Halvard."

"When you go north."

The king didn't look round. He worked the bellows, twice, slowly, and watched the coals.

"In the frost," he said. "I've told the stables. Six horses. Kestra. No carriage." He worked the bellows again. "I wrote *we*. In your letter. I read it over your shoulder on the step." He glanced back, once, from under his eyebrows. "You were coming. I'd have put you on the horse myself."

"I know," said the Advisor.

The king turned the bar in the coals.

"I would like to have asked," said the Advisor.

The king stopped.

Kestra saw him stop. The whole of him. The ninth tier, that he held in all day for the sake of other people, going perfectly still over the anvil, the way it had gone still once in a dead man's forge when he'd put down a hammer he'd been holding for fifty years. He took his hands off the bellows. He turned round.

He looked at the Advisor for a long time across the heat.

"Then ask," said the king.

The Advisor stood at the edge of the forge with his hands behind his back. And Kestra saw his feet. Square, together, the way he stood in council. And then, while she watched, the left one moved. Half a step. Forward. Into the heat. Over the edge of the river.

"May I come with you," said the Advisor. "To the lake."

"Yes," said the king.

That was all. The king turned back to the coals. The Advisor stood a moment longer, half a step inside the forge, and then he turned and went back across the yard to the gatehouse with his hands behind his back, not quite as straight as he'd come.

The king worked the bellows.

"Write it down," he said. Without turning round.

"I have."

"Underline it."

Kestra looked at the page. *He asked*, she'd written. *Nobody has seen him ask for anything in thirteen years. He stepped into the forge to do it*.

She had never underlined anything. Not in fourteen books. A thing was true or it wasn't; you didn't make it more true by drawing a line under it.

She drew a line under *He asked*.

***

They left on the morning of the first frost in Solmere, which was late that year.

Six horses. The king on his big plain bay, in a plain coat, with the crown on a cushion in his rooms and a leather roll behind the saddle that clinked when the horse trotted. Kestra on her grey. The Advisor on a tall quiet gelding from the royal stables that the stable-master had chosen because it would not do anything at all without being asked three times. Three packhorses on a string behind. Nobody else.

The Council had objected. Kestra had stood at the back of the council chamber and watched them object. The Lord Treasurer had used the word *irregular* four times. The king had sat at the head of the table with his chin on his fist and let them go on for nearly an hour, and then said, mildly, *I went last year with one guard and four horses and nobody noticed for a month*, and the Lord Treasurer had said *that is exactly my point, Majesty*, and the king had said *yes, and nothing happened, which is mine*, and got up.

And then the Lord Chancellor had said, carefully, *and the Advisor? Both of you? At once?*, and the whole table had looked down it at the thin grey man at the far end who had not left Solmere, except for Valcrest and once to the coast, in nine years.

The Advisor had looked back at them.

"I've left instructions," he'd said. "For everything. They're in the third drawer on the right. It isn't locked."

Kestra had written down the faces round the table. They'd been very good faces.

***

The north road out of Solmere goes up through the vineyards for a day and then the country flattens and turns to grain, and then to grazing, and then, after Hethe, to the long green hills and the becks and the lake country. Nine days, at a king's pace, which was not a fast pace, because the king liked to stop.

Kestra had ridden it once before. Last autumn. She'd learned on it that nobody in the villages recognised the king, because nobody had ever imagined a king could be a big grey-haired man in a plain coat who stopped at every forge on the road to look at what the smith was making. She learned on it again. The king stopped at every forge. He looked at the hinges and the ploughshares and the pot-hooks and said *that's a good weld*, or, once, *you'll want to fold that again*, to a boy who went scarlet and then went and folded it again while they watered the horses.

The Advisor did not stop at forges. He sat his horse at the edge of each village with the reins in one hand and waited, the way he waited at the edge of the castle forge, at the edge of the heat. He didn't seem to mind. He looked at things. Kestra watched him look at things the way she'd never once seen him look at anything in thirteen years: a dog asleep in a doorway; a woman hanging washing; a field of stubble with a hare sitting up in it. Not reading them. Not working out what they meant. Just looking. As if he'd forgotten the world had things in it that weren't on a desk and had been surprised, on the first morning, to find it full of them.

He rode badly. Not badly. Like a man who'd learned on a farm horse sixty years ago and hadn't been asked to enjoy it since. Upright and careful, with his heels down and his elbows in, as if somebody were going to mark him on it. By the third day he could hardly get off at night. He didn't complain. He got off, and stood holding the saddle for a moment with his eyes shut, and then let go of it and walked into the inn as if nothing were wrong. The king watched him do it every night and said nothing.

On the fourth night the king said, at supper, at a scrubbed table in an inn at Ambry, without looking up from his plate: "There's a liniment. In the roll. Kestra'll fetch it."

"I'm quite well, thank you."

"You walked in here like a man who's been folded," said the king. "Twice. The wrong way." He ate a potato. "Kestra."

Kestra fetched it.

The Advisor looked at the little jar on the table for a long moment. Then he put it in his pocket. In the morning, he walked to his horse a great deal better, and nobody said anything about it, and Kestra wrote down *liniment* and nothing else, because that was the kind of thing the king liked to find in the margins.

***

He asked her about the lake on the fifth night.

They were at Hethe. The crossroads, where the east road goes off over the hill to the Redmarch and the north road goes on. There was an inn at the crossroads with a long low common room and a fire, and the king had gone to bed early, because he'd been at the inn's forge with the smith since they arrived and had burned his thumb and was in a temper about it, which meant he was happy. Kestra sat by the fire with her book. The Advisor sat across from her with a cup of something he wasn't drinking.

She felt him look at her. She went on writing.

"You stayed eleven nights," he said. "Last autumn. At the lake."

"Eleven."

"On the step, the king says."

"On the step."

He turned the cup round on the table. Once. She wrote that down without looking at it. She'd learned to write without looking a long time ago; it was the only way to write down what people did with their hands.

"What does he do?" said the Advisor. "At night."

Kestra looked up.

She'd asked the same thing. Once. On the first night, on the step, with the sword across her knees. Not to anyone. To the dark. *I'd like to know what you do at night*. And then she'd sat there eleven nights and found out.

"He fishes," she said. "Before light. He goes out to the end of the jetty and sits there and fishes, and mostly catches nothing, and doesn't seem to mind." She thought about it. "Some nights he doesn't come in at all. He sits out there with his window open. He reads. I don't know what. I couldn't see it."

"The channel," said the Advisor.

"I know that now. I didn't then." She looked at the fire. "At dawn he comes in. Past me, on the step. He says *Morning*." She paused. "Eleven times. I counted."

The Advisor didn't say anything.

"The hens hate him," said Kestra. "Three of them. They chase him up the woodpile. He minds." She thought about what else. "The girl practises on the shingle with her sword before he's up. He watches from the window and pretends he isn't. He makes a stew that tastes of burnt marsh and he knows it does and he makes it anyway, every week, because it's supposed to taste of somewhere else."

She stopped. That was more than she'd said aloud in a week.

The Advisor was looking at the cup.

"May I read it?" he said.

Kestra didn't pretend not to know what he meant. She'd never let anyone read her books but the king. One page, once, to the archivist, because he'd asked so badly he'd have died of it otherwise. Nobody else. The king had asked her once, in the first year, whether she ever would, and she'd said *no*, and he'd said *good*, and that had been the whole conversation.

She looked at the Advisor across the table. At the cup he wasn't drinking. At his hand on it, and the other hand, on his knee, and his feet under the table. Which were pointing, she saw, up the north road. Through the wall of the inn. At a lake four days away.

She took out the twelfth book. She'd brought it. She hadn't known why. She found the page and turned the book round and put it on the table in front of him, open, and took her hand off it.

*The Hero of the Redmarch*, it said, at the top. In her small square hand, last autumn, on a step, by lamplight.

He read it.

He read it slowly. The way he read reports. All of it, every line, from the top. *He fishes. He says Morning*. The pumpkins. The hens. *He minds*. The stones, every child in Wrenhaven with a stone. The cut. The letters on the headstone that her eyes slid off. *He laughed twice. Both times at the king*. The five girls on the hill, waiting. *I felt nothing from him. Not once. Eleven days*. The king's pressure going quiet beside him, *as if out of courtesy*. And at the bottom, the last line, that she'd sat looking at for an hour before she wrote it.

*He is a fisherman*.

The Advisor sat looking at it for a long time.

"He laughed twice," he said, at last.

"Both times at the king."

"He used to laugh at me." The Advisor didn't look up from the page. "On a wall. At Cinderhold. When I got my own window wrong. I was the youngest court mage the crown had ever sent to the border, with a commission and a good coat, and a boy of fifteen with a borrowed sword sat on the parapet and laughed at me because I'd opened my inventory instead of my skills." His mouth did something. "He said I had it upside down. He was right. I'd been holding it upside down for a week."

He closed the book. Carefully. And turned it round and pushed it back across the table to her with two fingers.

"Thank you," he said.

"He asked about you," said Kestra.

She hadn't known she was going to say it. The Advisor's hand stopped on the table.

"Last autumn," said Kestra. "On the eighth night. He came in off the jetty at dawn and said *Morning*, the way he always did. And then he stopped in the door. With his back to me. And said *How is he*." She looked at the fire. "He didn't say who. He didn't have to."

The Advisor didn't move.

"I said *thin*," said Kestra. "And he said *he always was*. And went in." She put the book back in her coat. "I didn't write it down."

"Why not?"

Kestra thought about that honestly, because he'd asked her honestly.

"Because it was the only thing he said all eleven days that he didn't mean me to hear," she said. "And I thought it belonged to you. Not the king." She stood up. "Goodnight, my lord."

She went up the stairs to bed. She didn't look back. She didn't need to; she knew what she'd see. A thin grey man at a scrubbed table, by a fire, at the crossroads where the road goes east to the grave, not drinking his cup. With his feet pointed north.

***

The frost came properly on the seventh morning.

They woke to it at an inn in the hills above the Wren, and the whole valley below them white, every blade of grass, every wall-top, every bare twig on every thorn in every hedge, and the river black in the bottom of it with a mist lying on it. Their breath went up. The horses' breath went up. The king stood in the inn yard in his coat with his hands round a cup of something hot and looked at the white valley for a long time.

"In the frost," he said. To nobody.

The Advisor was beside him. Kestra hadn't seen him come. He stood in the yard with his hands in his coat and looked at the frost, and Kestra saw his right hand move, inside the coat, at the chest. Over the inside pocket. Touching something there, through the cloth. The way you touch a thing to make sure it's still there.

He'd done it every morning. She'd written it down every morning. *He touches his coat. The inside pocket, left*. She didn't know what was in it. She thought she probably did.

The king began to hum.

The old river song. From Brakewater. Under his breath, into the steam off his cup, the way he'd hummed it all the way north a year ago, and every night on a shingle by a lake with a fisherman humming it back. Kestra had heard it a thousand times. She knew it the way you know the sound of a door in your own house.

And beside him, very quietly, in a thin dry voice that had not been used for anything but council in thirteen years, the Advisor came in on the second line.

Not loudly. Barely at all. A little flat. But the words. All of them. An old river song from a town in the south that nobody had ever heard of, that a smith's son from Brakewater had hummed at every anvil he'd stood at for fifty years, and that a court mage had evidently stood at the edge of the forge and listened to, for forty, without ever once letting anyone know he'd learned it.

The king didn't stop. He didn't look round. But his shoulders did something.

Kestra stood in the inn door behind them with her book in her coat and listened to two old men sing a river song to a frozen valley, badly, in the steam off their breath. And didn't write it down.

She'd remember.

***

Wrenhaven, on the ninth day, at the end of the afternoon.

They came in by the north road, the way she'd come in a year ago, over the last long hill and down past the mill. The town was the same. Grey stone and red roofs and the church tower and the smoke going straight up in the still cold. The square with the well. The forge on the far side of it, with the doors open and a red glow inside and a big soot-faced man at the anvil with a small old hammer in his hand, who looked up at the sound of hooves, and saw who it was, and went red to the roots of his hair, and dropped the hammer, and picked it up again, and held it up. Just held it up, in the forge door, so the king could see it.

The king reined in. He looked at the hammer for a long moment across the square.

Then he lifted his hand to the smith. Once. And rode on. And Kestra, behind him, saw his face, which she'd been seeing every day for thirteen years and still sometimes didn't recognise.

She wrote it down that night. *Garrow held up the hammer. The king could not speak*.

***

The mayor met them at the top of the lake road.

Not in the square. Kestra noticed that. Not at the town hall, with his chain on and the council behind him, which was where a mayor met a king. At the top of the lake road, where it left the corner of the square and went down between the field walls to the water. On his own. With his chain on, because he couldn't help that, and his big grey head bare in the cold, and his hands at his sides.

He'd been waiting. She could see it in the way he stood. A man who'd been standing in one place long enough to have stopped noticing he was cold.

He bowed to the king. A good bow, a soldier's bow, short. The king nodded to him, and said *Hale*, and the mayor said *Majesty*, and that was that between them. They'd done all the rest a year ago, on a shingle, with stones.

Then the mayor looked past the king. At the thin grey man on the tall quiet gelding.

Kestra watched them look at each other.

She knew about the letters. Everybody knew about the letters, by now, at court; the cat girl had been careful, and the cat girl was always careful, but a court was a court. Once a year, for eight years, a letter had gone north from the Advisor's desk to the mayor of Wrenhaven. Three words. *Is he well*. And once a year, for eight years, a letter had come south again, in a big square soldier's hand, with two.

*He's well*.

The mayor had never met him. Kestra was sure of it, watching. Eight years of letters, and they had never once stood in the same place.

The Advisor got down off his horse.

Carefully, the way he got down off it every night. And stood in the road at the top of the lake road holding his reins, in his grey coat, in the frost, a head shorter than the mayor and half as broad. And looked up at him.

"Mayor Hale," he said.

"My lord Advisor."

They stood there.

"Is he well?" said the Advisor.

Kestra saw the mayor's face do a thing. Just for a moment. The thing big men's faces do when they've been holding something a very long time and somebody has finally asked them to put it down.

"Come and see," said the mayor of Wrenhaven.

***

He didn't go down.

That was the next thing Kestra wrote, that night, on the step. *He did not go down*. Because the mayor stepped aside, out of the road, onto the grass at the edge of it, the way you step out of a doorway to let somebody through. And the king rode on past him, onto the lake road, and down. And Kestra went to follow the king, because that was what she did. And the Advisor stood at the top of the lake road with his reins in his hand and did not move.

The king stopped. A little way down. And looked back.

The Advisor was looking down the lake road. Between the field walls, white with frost. Past the fields. To the bottom, where it came out on the shore. The water, flat and grey and still, with the cold lying on it, and the far shore gone blue in the late light. A cabin by the water with smoke going up straight from the chimney. A woodpile. A hen-yard. A jetty, going out into the lake, long and grey and plain, with nobody on the end of it.

And at the bottom of the field below the cabin, by the water, a ring of something dark and tangled, taller than a man, white all over with frost. A hedge. In a ring. Round nothing Kestra could see, at the bottom of a pumpkin field.

There hadn't been a hedge there last autumn. Kestra had sat on that step for eleven nights and looked at that field every one of them. She'd have written it down.

"Who grew that?" said the king, from down the road.

"The silver-haired girl from the militia hall," said the mayor, behind them. "Last week. In one night. The night the hard frost came. To keep it off the last of his pumpkins." His voice did something proud and baffled at once. "The whole town's been down to look at it. Twice."

Kestra looked at the ring of thorn at the bottom of the field.

*The silver-haired one stepped in front of the small one with the creature on her head*, she'd written, a year ago, in the militia hall yard, the first day. *Before she knew she was doing it*.

She took out her book, there on the road, on her horse, and wrote under it, a year later: *She grew a hedge round his pumpkins*. And put it away.

The Advisor still hadn't moved.

The king looked up the road at him for a long moment. Then at Kestra.

"Stay with him," said the king.

And turned his horse, and went on down the lake road on his own, at a walk, toward the cabin, and the hens, and the smoke.

***

She had never once in thirteen years been told to stay with anyone but the king.

She got down off her grey. She stood at the top of the lake road with her reins in her hand, beside the Advisor, and the mayor of Wrenhaven a little behind them on the grass with his hands at his sides, and the cold coming down. And didn't say anything. Kestra was good at not saying anything. It was most of the job.

The Advisor looked down the lake road.

"I find," he said, after a while, quite calmly, "that I can't go down."

Kestra didn't say anything.

"I stood on a wall once," said the Advisor, "for an hour. And then I said a word, and somebody went down off it. And I have stood on walls since, a great many of them. And I find I can't go down off any of them." He looked at the road. "I couldn't come down off the gatehouse. For an hour. With his letter. The king had to come up and fetch me."

"I know," said Kestra. "I was at the bottom of the stairs."

The Advisor turned his head and looked at her.

"Your feet," said Kestra.

He looked down at them.

"They've been pointing at this since the gatehouse wall," she said. "Every corridor. Every window. Every inn. They pointed through the wall at Hethe." She looked at his boots, in the frost, at the top of the lake road. Square together. Pointing down it. "They've never once pointed anywhere but the work. Not in thirteen years. I've got it written down."

The Advisor looked at his boots for a long time.

"And yours?" he said.

Kestra opened her mouth to say something. She didn't know what.

Then she looked down.

She'd known, she thought afterwards. Somewhere. She'd known on the road, the way you know a thing you're not looking at. All last year, after the lake, her feet had turned north, every morning, in the yard at Solmere, at the gate, on the wall. The cat girl had noticed before she did. *You keep looking north, Kestra*. And then in Valcrest, on the wall, at dusk, she'd taken a half of a heart out of her book and put it in a man's hand, and her feet had stopped. Just stopped. Like a compass when you take the iron away.

They hadn't turned north once since. Not on the whole road. She'd been watching for it. They'd pointed where she was going and nowhere else, and she'd thought: *good. That's done*.

They were pointing at the lake.

Not north. Not up the road, the way you'd point if you were only going somewhere. *Down*. At the water. At the bottom of the lake road where it came out by the cabin. At a step, outside a door, where she'd sat eleven nights with a blanket round her shoulders and a sword across her knees and found out, a night at a time, what a man did in the dark.

Kestra looked at her boots.

She hadn't known. Mouths lied. Feet didn't. She'd been saying it for thirteen years and she hadn't once thought to look at her own.

"Oh," she said.

The Advisor didn't say anything. He was too well-mannered. But she felt him not say it, beside her, in the frost, the way she'd have felt the king not say it, and she found that her face had gone hot, which it had not done since she was twenty-one and an old sergeant had caught her asleep on a wall.

***

Somebody was coming up the lake road.

Kestra saw him first. That was her job. A long way down, at the bottom, past the cabin gate, where the king had just reached the hen-yard, and a big red shape had come out of it at a dead run, and stopped dead in the road in front of the king's horse, and then gone, Kestra saw, very slowly, to sit on the king's boot in the stirrup. Which shouldn't have been possible. Which it had apparently found a way to do anyway.

And past the king, out of the cabin gate, a man. Walking. Not fast. Up the lake road, between the white field walls, in the last of the light, with his hands at his sides.

She knew the walk. She'd watched it twelve days. Unhurried. Flat-footed. Like a man walking down to the water to see if anything was biting. The way he'd walked up out of the dark onto the cabin step every morning with his rod, and said *Morning*.

The Advisor saw him.

She felt him see. Not in his face; she didn't look at his face. In his feet. Which had been pointing down the lake road square together for a quarter of an hour, and which, when the man came round the bend by the long field wall into plain sight, went perfectly still. Not moving. Not able to.

The man came up the lake road.

Past the fields. Past the long wall. Past the place where the road steepened and the frost lay thickest in the ruts. Past a line of children's faces at the top of the field wall that Kestra hadn't seen arrive, because the whole of Wrenhaven had apparently known before she had, and was standing very quietly along the edge of the square behind them pretending to do something else. And the mayor stepped back, a little further, onto the grass.

He stopped in front of them. In the road. A few paces off.

A plain man. In a plain coat. Not tall. With his hands at his sides. Kestra looked at him and couldn't see his face. She'd never been able to. Eleven days at the lake and a summer in Valcrest and she'd never once been able to hold it; her eyes went to it and slid, the way they slid off the letters on a black headstone, the way everyone's did. She'd written that down too, the first night. *I cannot see his face. Nobody here can. The girl cannot, and she has lived with him all her life*.

The king could. The king had told her, on the road north a year ago, in the dark. *I've been able to since I was Shaped. Most of the top tiers can. Nobody else*.

The Advisor was Comet.

So Kestra didn't look at the man's face. She couldn't. She looked at the Advisor's.

And saw him see it.

***

She didn't write down what his face did.

She'd thought, on the road, that she would. She'd thought she'd sit on the step that night and write *the Advisor saw his face* and what happened to the Advisor's own, line by line, the way she wrote everything, because it would be the kind of thing the king would want to find in the margins. She'd had her pencil ready in her head for nine days.

She didn't. She found, standing in the road, that she'd turned her head away. That she was looking at the lake. On purpose. The way you'd look away from a window when somebody inside has started to undress.

Behind her, nobody said anything for a long time.

Then the man said, in an ordinary voice, the voice he'd said *Morning* in eleven times on a step:

"Evening."

And the Advisor said:

"Ars."

Just that. Kestra heard the shape of it. A thin dry voice, used for nothing but council for thirteen years, saying one short word in the road at the top of a lake road in the frost. And something under it that she didn't have a word for. That she'd never heard in that voice. That she'd heard once, in a different voice, at the end of a long hour on a wall at Valcrest, in the dusk, when she'd put half a heart into a man's hand and he'd looked at it.

"You're thinner," said the man.

"You said I always was."

There was a pause.

"Kestra," said the man. Not looking at her. She felt him not look at her. "You told him."

Kestra looked at the lake.

"I didn't write it down," she said.

"I know," said the man. "That's why I said it in the door." And then, after a moment, quite gently: "Thank you."

Kestra didn't turn round. She found that she couldn't. She stood in the road with her reins in her hand and her face hot, looking very hard at a jetty with nobody on it.

***

They went down.

Not straight away. The man stood in the road a while longer, and the Advisor stood in front of him, and neither of them moved, and the frost came down, and the town along the edge of the square went on pretending to do something else. And then the man stepped aside. Not far. Half a pace. Onto the frozen verge, the way the mayor had, out of the road.

Leaving the road clear. Down. All the way to the water.

The Advisor looked at it.

And Kestra watched his feet. Square together at the top of the lake road. Pointing down it, where they'd been pointing for nine days and a gatehouse wall and thirteen years she hadn't known about. And the left one moved. Half a step. Forward. Off the top of the lake road, onto the slope, into the frost.

And then the right.

The man fell in beside him. Not in front. Not behind. Beside. And they went down the lake road together in the last of the light, the thin grey man and the plain one, not fast, not talking, between the white field walls, toward the cabin and the smoke and a king sitting on his horse in the road at the bottom with a hen on his boot, waiting.

Kestra led the horses down behind them.

She watched their feet the whole way. She couldn't help it. It was what she did.

The same way. Both of them. Every step.

***

She sat on the step that night.

Nobody told her to. Nobody told her not to. The king was inside, on the rug by the fire, because he'd flatly refused the bed, and the big red hen was asleep on his chest, and he was pretending not to be delighted about it and failing. The girl was in the loft. The Advisor had the bed, under protest; the king had said *you were folded, Halvard, the wrong way, twice* and that had been the end of it. A red curtain with a gold fringe hung crookedly in the loft window over her head, which she would have written down if she'd been able to think how.

And the man and the Advisor were on the end of the jetty.

She could see them from the step. Two shapes, at the far end, against the water, very small, with a lamp between them on the boards. Sitting. They'd been there since supper. She couldn't hear them. She didn't try to.

She sat on the step with a blanket round her shoulders and her sword across her knees, the way she'd sat eleven nights a year ago, and took out her book, and found a clean page, and sat looking at it for a long time with the pencil in her hand.

There was a great deal she could have written. She'd been writing it in her head for nine days.

*The Advisor rode north. He sang on the second line. He saw his face. His feet*.

She wrote, in the end, only one thing. At the top of the page. In her small square hand.

*The Advisor came down the lake road*.

And under it, after a while, smaller:

*So did I*.

And didn't underline either of them. She didn't need to. It was the honest book. A thing was true or it wasn't.

She closed it, and put it back in her coat, and sat on the step with her sword across her knees and her feet on the frozen boards in front of her, pointing down at the water, where they'd apparently been wanting to point for a year. And watched the lamp at the end of the jetty, where two men who'd missed each other for nine years were sitting very close together and saying, as far as she could tell, almost nothing.

It was the best hour of her day. She'd tell the king, perhaps. In the morning.

He probably knew.
