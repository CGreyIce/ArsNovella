Twenty-three.

Hyvred counted them at dawn with her eyes shut and her back to the north, the way she'd counted them every dawn and every dusk since the spring; and then she opened her eyes, and they were still there.

That was new.

She'd always been able to put it down. That was the whole trick of *Range* on its short lead, the thing Hild had taught her on the well in the summer: you looked, and then you stopped looking, and the valley went back to being eleven miles away behind a mountain. You opened your eyes and there was the town. Roofs. Smoke. Bee.

There was the town now. Roofs going down the hill under the new snow, white on white, with the smoke from the bakehouse going straight up into a sky the colour of a pewter plate. The long street, white, with one dark line trodden down the middle of it from the north gate to the square. Bee on the parapet beside her, in two coats and a blanket, with her knees up and a stick of chalk in her fist.

And over all of it, the way a reflection lies over a window, the valley.

Thirteen rings, back where they'd stood for two years, at the top of the valley under the pass. Hundreds of candles in the frost; small, dim, leaning. A freckled one in the third ring, leaning a hair toward the town. A girl at the edge of the outermost one with a blue edge to her light. Curving round outside them on the left, the holes: twenty-three of them, where there'd been thirty-one. And at the foot of the pass, at the top of everything, a round place the size of a hill where the snow didn't lie.

It was snowing in the valley. She could see that, the way she could see everything now. Snow on the rings, on the heads and shoulders of four hundred and some people standing in a field, settling, not melting, because the turned didn't feel the cold. Snow on the road, which nobody swept any more. Snow coming down soft and thick and grey over the whole of the valley except one place, at the top, where it fell and stopped falling a few feet from the ground, as if it had remembered something, and went somewhere else.

The absence was facing the town. It had faced the town since the thirtieth walked into the curve, and it had faced the town for fifteen nights in the long street, and then it had turned on the ridge road and faced a man with a fishing rod for longer than it had ever faced anything; and then it had turned back. And gone home. And sat down in the snow, at the foot of the pass, facing the town.

She shut her eyes. It was there. She opened them. It was there.

"Twenty-three," she said.

Corvin wrote it down. He had the board on his knee and the stub of pencil, and his coat buttoned to the chin, and snow in his eyebrows. Twenty-three, under thirty-one, under thirty-one, under thirty, in a column that went back to the first seven. He didn't say anything about the eight. He'd written them down the first morning, the morning after, with a line under them; and then he'd sat and looked at the line for a long time, and then he'd gone down to see the king.

"Same," said Bee.

"Same."

Bee didn't say anything else. She turned the chalk over in her fist. She hadn't put it on anything in two days, and she wasn't going to now, and they both knew it.

Hyvred leaned over, a little, until her shoulder touched Bee's. Bee let it. Down in the yard, the long room door was open, and somebody very big was standing in it, the way somebody had stood in it every night for fifteen nights; and at the north gate, in the arch, at the bottom of the long street, two people were sitting on the step in the snow, not close, looking north.

She didn't look at them with the red mark. She didn't need to. She could feel them from here, the way you can feel a stove in a room without looking round. The king, like a forge the morning after a long job, the doors pulled to and the fire banked down to a red eye in the ash, still hot right through. And the other one. Who she'd never once been able to look at.

***

She couldn't put it down.

She tried, that first day. She sat on the tower with her back to the north and her eyes shut and did everything Hild had taught her, the lead, the short lead, *you look and then you stop*; and the valley sat in the back of her head like a tune you can't stop humming. She went down the hundred and twelve steps and sat in the long room with the shutters closed and the stove lit and a blanket over her head, and the valley was there, in the dark under the blanket, every candle in it. Bee put both hands over her eyes, which was a thing Bee had done once in the first spring as a joke, and it wasn't a joke now; and the valley was there, through Bee's hands, through the backs of Bee's fingers, through the back of her own head.

"It's the eye," said Kei, at noon, on the well. He had the snake round his neck and his notebook on his knee and he didn't look up from it. "Hisui says it's stuck open. Like a door that's swelled in the wet."

*It will go down*, said Hisui. To Kei, who said it out loud for Hyvred. *When the wood dries. It always does.*

"When?"

*When it dries*, said Hisui, and put his head back down.

So she counted the town instead. If she couldn't stop seeing, she'd see something else. She let the red mark go down off the tower into the streets the way she'd let it go into the yard on the morning of the two walls, and the town lit up.

Everybody had a light. Hundreds of them, in the houses going down the hill under the snow: red and gold and copper and dull iron, small and warm and tired at the edges, the way people are after fifteen nights of lamps in every window and the doors barred. A baby on Tanners' Row, very small and very bright, like a struck match. The farrier, steady. Agnes at the forge, a coal. Old Pruett, an orange stub. You got used to it. She'd stopped looking at people in the summer because there'd been too much in the north, and she'd forgotten what it was like; how many of them there were, and how they all leaned.

That was the thing. They leaned.

She'd known souls leaned. Yan had told them that, in the first year, in the channel: *they lean toward what they love, like a plant on a windowsill.* She'd seen it. Bee leaned at her. Tiger had leaned at the post for two years. But she'd never seen a whole town of them leaning at once, and she saw it now, and she couldn't stop seeing it, so she looked.

They leaned at places.

A house by the laundry, with the shutters closed and the step not swept, and nobody in it. All the lights in the houses either side of it leaned a little toward it; not much. The way you'd lean toward a door you'd heard knocked.

A cot in the dye-yard, in the back room of the dyer's house, with a woman sitting beside it. Her light was bent over it so far it was nearly lying down.

A bunk in the guardhouse barracks, the end one by the stove. She'd sat on it once, in the summer, when its owner had been out carrying shields. All the soldiers' lights in that room leaned toward it in their sleep.

A house on the street up to the west wall, with three small lights in one bed, side by side, very close, leaning at a coat on a nail.

A room above the laundry with shirts in it.

A bunk at the far end of the west wall barracks, under a window, with a pack on it nobody had moved.

She went round the town and counted them. She couldn't stop; she was who she was, and if you were going to look at a thing you might as well look at all of it, and she'd learned that from Bee. Six places in Thornwall that the lights leaned at. Seven people; the Callows had been in one house. Seven, out of nine hundred and thirty-one.

That's what it looks like, she thought. Grief. That's what it looks like from up here. Everybody leaning at a place where somebody was.

She hadn't known it had a shape. It did. It was the same shape as love. It was only pointing at something that wasn't there.

***

The king did the count at noon on the second day, in the square.

He did it sitting down. He couldn't stand long yet; he'd stood on the gatehouse roof for fifteen nights with his door open, and gone greyer every night, and on the last night he'd gone down on the leads and Kestra had carried him down the stair, and he hadn't stood properly since. So Agnes brought a chair out of the Bell, the big one by the fire with the arms, and he sat in it at the top of the square in his smith's apron with a blanket over his knees and snow on his shoulders, and Kestra behind him with her hand on the back of it. And the town came.

All of it. Hyvred watched them come from the steps of the Bell, where Group 5 stood in a row with their backs to the wall, because the king had asked them to be there and they hadn't known how to say no. Down the long street and up from the south gate and out of the lanes. Soldiers, in their grey, with their shields on their backs. Women with children on their hips. The farrier in his apron. The schoolmaster with a hundred and six children behind him in their coats, in a crocodile, holding hands. They filled the square the way they'd filled the granary, packed in, quiet, with their breath going up white over their heads. Nobody pushed.

Brannoch stood beside the king's chair with his hat in his hands. He'd told them, in the long room, the night before, what it was. On the border you read the count in the square on the first day after. All of it. The dead and the gone and the come-back. Out loud, where everybody could hear, so that the town knew what it had paid, and nobody had to find out afterwards in a doorway.

"The old way," Brannoch had said. "From before the Watch. You say it once and you say it all and you don't say it again. And then you take the bars off the doors."

Corvin had the book. Not his board; the other book. The big one with the surveyor's neat columns, *street, house, name, a word*, that Drew had learned nine hundred and thirty-one names out of by candlelight. He stood in front of the king's chair with it open on his arm and his spectacles on, and the square went quiet, the way a field goes quiet when the wind drops.

"The count," said Corvin. "Of the gate. Fifteen nights." His voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. "The dead of Thornwall."

He read them.

"Trooper Huw Bevan. Nineteen. The Watch. At the millstream grating, the first night." A man in the crowd near the front put his hand flat on his chest. Then another. "Thomas Callow. Bethan Callow. Laundry Lane. The first night." More hands. "David Pask. Twelve. The dye-yard. The first night." The woman from the dyer's house, at the front, didn't put her hand on her chest; she couldn't, because both her arms were round herself. Hyvred saw her light bend so far it nearly touched the cobbles. "Fenna Rees. Laundry Lane. The first night."

Somewhere down the square, in the soldiers' ranks, a tall trooper with a new man's face stood very straight with his hand flat on his chest and his eyes shut. Wyn Alder. Eight weeks in Thornwall, and the one who'd cut her down. His light was a thin dull grey, like a blade somebody had put away wet.

"Trooper Ifan Penn. The Watch. On the west wall, the twelfth night." Hands. Half the square now. "Sergeant Owen Rudd. Forty. The Watch. At the north gate, the last night."

At the side of the square, three girls in a row, the smallest one perhaps six, holding each other's hands in a line with their mother at the end. All four put their hands flat on their chests at once, like they'd practised it. Hyvred thought they probably had.

"Seven of Thornwall," said Corvin. He turned a page. "And seven of the valley."

This was the part nobody had known how to do.

They weren't Thornwall's. They'd come down the fields in the rings with the rest, and leaned on the wall, and been the neighbours; and then Kei's dose had flinched the call, and for one heartbeat it had let go of everyone it was holding, and seven of them had had nothing left underneath to stand up on. They'd fallen in the frost at the foot of the north wall. Brannoch's men had carried them in on the shields. They were in the cold room under the guardhouse now, under white cloths, seven of them in a row, old; and nobody in Thornwall knew who they were.

Except one.

She stood up from the front of the crowd, where she'd been sitting on the edge of the well with a borrowed shawl round her: a thin woman of fifty with grey hair cut short and a farmer's hands. She'd come up out of the valley on the night of the dose and lain in the bed next to Rhys Hywel in the Bell for four days, and sat in the north window every day since, watching the fields for her son in the second ring. Olwen, of Coldwater. She'd told Corvin that on the fifth morning, the first thing she'd said that wasn't a question.

She came to the front. Corvin held the book out. She didn't look at it; she couldn't read, she'd told him, and anyway she didn't need to.

"Old Gwil Harries," she said. "From the top farm at Coldwater. He had the white beard. Mair Jenkin, that kept the bees. Her husband was Tomos; he went first, the first year, visiting, before any of us. That was Mair in the apron." She stopped. Her mouth worked. "Dilys Pugh. From the mill. Her grandson's in the fourth ring, Corvin, you've got him, he's called Ianto." Corvin nodded. "Seren Bowen, from the chapel house. Her and her sister went the same night and I don't know where the sister is." She looked down at her hands. "Glyn Lloyd the shepherd. He used to bring his ewes down to us in the snow." She stopped.

"That's five," said Corvin, gently.

"The other two are hill people," said Olwen. "From over the ridge. I never knew them. I'm sorry. I'd tell you if I knew." She looked up, at the square, at all of it. "They were somebody's."

Nobody said anything.

Then, from the steps of the Bell, Drew said them.

He was standing at the end of Group 5's row with his ribs bound up under his shirt so tight he couldn't bend, and his face still a colour Hyvred didn't like, and his hands, which wouldn't quite close any more, hanging at his sides. He'd said every name in Thornwall down the chain in the long room for fifteen nights. He said these the same way. Not loud. Each one as if it was the only one.

"Gwil Harries. Mair Jenkin. Dilys Pugh. Seren Bowen. Glyn Lloyd."

And then he stopped. Because there was nothing to say for the last two, and he was the one who said names, and he didn't have them.

"The man with the grey coat," said Drew, at last. "With the frost in his eyebrows. The woman with the red scarf." His voice went, and came back. "That's all I've got. I'm sorry. That's all."

Olwen of Coldwater looked at him across the square for a long time. Then she put her hand flat on her chest, and kept it there.

Kei was standing at the other end of the row. He had the snake round his neck and his notebook shut in his fist. He'd written all seven down, the five names and the two coats, on the page after the doses, in his small tidy hand. Hyvred had watched him do it. She'd watched him do it again that morning, on the well; and again, on a new page, at noon. As if, if he wrote them out enough times, the sum would come out different.

"The gone," said Corvin. He turned another page. "The book doesn't cross them out. It never has. They're in the valley. They stay in the book." He looked up over his spectacles. "Four hundred and some. The same as the autumn, less nine. Oswald Pell, of the Watch, in the third ring. Elin, from the dyer's, in the thirteenth." He didn't read the rest; he didn't need to. Everybody in the square had somebody on those pages. "They're in the book," said Corvin again. "They stay in the book."

At the end of the row, Tiger didn't move at all.

"And the come-back," said Corvin.

He closed the book.

"Trooper Rhys Hywel, of the Watch," he said. "Sixteen. Gone at the balance. Come back the night of the dose." Down in the soldiers' ranks, a freckled boy with his grey coat buttoned wrong stood up very straight and went red to the ears. "Olwen of Coldwater. Gone the first year. Come back the night of the dose." Olwen didn't move.

"Two," said Corvin.

And the square, which had stood with its hands flat on its chest for the dead, did something Hyvred hadn't seen it do before. Nobody started it. It went through them the way the call had gone through the long room, only the other way round: a sound, very low, from the back first, like the noise a crowd makes when a rider they'd given up on comes over the last hill. Not a cheer. It wasn't loud enough for a cheer. Just everybody in Thornwall letting out a breath they'd been holding since the spring.

Rhys Hywel put both hands over his face.

The king, in the big chair with the blanket on his knees, hadn't said a word. He looked at the square for a long time. Then he said, in his ordinary voice, the one he used for the price of charcoal:

"Take the bars off."

Brannoch's men went down the long street knocking, and all the way down Thornwall, one after another, house after house, the bars came off the doors.

***

That was the count. You said it once and you said it all and you didn't say it again.

It didn't help. Hyvred hadn't thought it would. She'd thought, maybe, that it would be like counting the valley: that if you said the number out loud it got smaller, or at least stayed still. It didn't. It was just said. The lights in the town still leaned at their six places. The valley still sat over the top of everything like a reflection in a window. And Group 5 went back to the guardhouse and weren't all right, one at a time, each in their own way, the way people aren't.

Bee couldn't draw.

Hyvred watched her try. On the afternoon of the count, at the house by the laundry with the step not swept, because there'd always been a dog on that step. Bee had drawn it the first winter: a fat sleeping one, curled up, for Bet Callow, who'd given her a mug of something hot every time she came past with chalk on her hands. It was gone now. The neighbours had leaned on that door on the first night, and the snow had come, and there was nothing on the step but white.

Bee stood in front of the door with the chalk in her hand for a long time.

Her light was tight. That was the only way Hyvred could put it. Bee's light was usually a bright soft yellow, a lamp in a kitchen window, never quite still; it went where her eyes went. Now it was pulled in hard round the middle of her, like a fist round something it wasn't going to let go of. Her hand came up with the chalk in it and stopped a finger's width from the wood.

"I can't find the start," said Bee.

She stood there with her hand up. Then she put it down.

"I can't find where it starts," she said again. "There's always a start. You put it on the nose and you go. I can't find the nose." She looked at her hand. "I did one line. One. The whole town. And I didn't lift it. And now I can't put it *down*."

Hyvred didn't say anything. There wasn't anything. She put her arm through Bee's and they stood on the swept bit of Laundry Lane in the snow and looked at the door with nothing on it, until somebody's boots came crunching up behind them and stopped.

"Supper," said Malcolm.

He had two bowls. He held them out, one each, and stood there until they'd taken them, and then he stood there some more, watching, with his book under his arm, until they'd each eaten at least half. He'd done that for every meal since Group 6 came in at the south gate in the sleet. He went round the whole of Group 5 with bowls like a man going round a field with a bucket. The tower, the well, the long room, the post; he'd climbed the hundred and twelve steps at every meal for fifteen days with stew going cold in his hands and his breath going, and stood on the leads and watched her eat it.

He wrote it down afterwards. She'd seen the page, once, when he left the book on the long room table. *Hyvred: half (dawn). Half (noon). None (dusk), IF she won't THEN sit there till she does. Backup: Bee.* And under it, in the same careful hand, a column for each of them, every meal, every day, going back to the sleet.

"Thank you," said Bee, to the bowl.

"Eat the rest," said Malcolm.

Tiger wouldn't talk.

He'd said one word out loud since the snow. He said it every morning at first light at the oak post inside the arch: walked out of the long room and down the long street and laid his hand flat on the wood, the way Ossie's rule said, and said *tomorrow* to the valley. He meant it now. She could see that. His light, which had always been quick and hot and orange and all over the place, went out from his hand on the post along the road and up the valley, eleven miles, a thin bright thread, and touched a freckled candle in the third ring, and the freckled candle leaned. Every morning. And then Tiger came back up the long street with his ears flat and his tail wrapped round his leg and sat down on the floor of the long room against the wall, and didn't say anything else to anyone for the rest of the day.

Rui Xiang sat beside him.

Not close. A hand away. On the floor, with his back to the wall, with his knees up, the way he'd sat on the mounting block for four days when he was learning to be furniture. He didn't talk either. He didn't look at Tiger. He just sat there, being exactly what he was, which was a boy from Group 6 sitting on a floor; and Tiger, who couldn't stand anybody looking at him, didn't mind, because there was nothing to mind. When Tiger got up, Rui Xiang got up. When Tiger went to the post at dawn, Rui Xiang walked down the long street a little behind him and stood at the corner of the guardhouse until he came back.

Hyvred looked at them once, with the red mark, from the tower. Tiger's thin bright orange, pulled in. And beside it, a hand away, the cup. The hard quiet edge that was Rui Xiang now, with something lit under it, very steady. Tiger's light leaned at it. Not much. A hair.

Drew sat on Ossie's roof.

All night. Every night since the snow. The Pells' house was at the top of the town on the street up to the north wall, with a roof you could get onto from the wall-walk if you didn't mind a drop; Tiger had sat up there for two years. Now Drew did. He went up at dusk with his ribs bound and a blanket and sat on the ridge in the snow facing north, and stayed till dawn. He didn't say the names. He didn't do anything. He was listening. Hyvred knew that without being told, because it was the same thing she was doing. The call had stopped the morning the snow came, and he'd held it off nine hundred and thirty-one people for fifteen nights, and now it had stopped, and he couldn't stop listening for it.

Xav sat beside him.

He'd been there the first night. Hyvred had watched him from the tower: a dark shape going up the ladder from the wall-walk with a second blanket, and sitting down on the ridge a little way along from Drew, not close, and not saying anything. He was there again the second night. And the third. All night, in the snow, the two of them on the roof of a house with a boy's coat still on the nail inside the door, looking north. At dawn he'd stand up and hold out his hand, and Drew would take it, and they'd climb down.

Kei counted his doses.

He sat on the well every afternoon with the notebook on his knee and went through it. Every grain. Forty doses, forty names, forty people who'd said yes; knitwort at the edge, the size of each one. He'd gone through it on the first day and the second and the third, and every time it came out the same: forty out of forty, the call flinched, three came up, two stayed. And seven, who'd never said anything, because nobody had asked them. Because you couldn't ask them. Because they'd been standing in a valley for two years with nothing left underneath.

He wrote the seven out again, on a new page. Five names and two coats. Then he went back to the front and started the doses again.

*It's not the dose*, said Hisui, on the third afternoon. Kei said it out loud, for Hyvred; he always did, even when it was about him. Especially then. *It was the right dose. It was exactly the right dose. You can count it a hundred times. It's still the right dose.*

"I know," said Kei.

*Then what are you counting?*

Kei didn't say anything for a long time. He turned the page back to the seven.

"Whether it was the right thing," he said.

Hisui didn't answer that. He just went round Kei's neck one more time, the long way, and put his head down on Kei's collarbone, and stayed.

And the others held them.

Azac stood in the long room door. He'd stood in it every night of the fifteen, filling it, so that nothing came in that wasn't let; and he stood in it now that there was nothing to come in, every night from dusk to dawn, with his big round shield leaning against the frame and his hand open and the pale blue just showing at the edges of him. Not for what came in. For who went out. So that nobody in Group 5 went out of that door alone in the dark without him seeing them go. And he talked. He'd always talked; he'd told the story of every fight while it was still happening since the first year, Malcolm said, because if he said it out loud it was happening to somebody in a story and not to him. He did it now, very low, to nobody, all night, so that anybody awake in the long room could hear him if they wanted.

"Bee's on the step," Hyvred heard him say, on the second night, at two in the morning, when she couldn't sleep and had given up trying. "Bee's going in. Good. Kei's on the bench. He's writing. Okay." A pause. "Drew's on the roof. Xav's with him. Snowing." Another pause. "Tiger's asleep. Rui Xiang isn't. That's fine. Hyvred's awake." He didn't look round. "Hi, Hyvred."

"Hi, Azac."

"You want some tea?"

"No."

"Okay," said Azac. "Hyvred doesn't want tea." And went on.

John didn't show them anything.

He'd shown them everything for fifteen nights. Every picture of the rings coming across the fields at dusk, every picture of the long street with the lamps going out; he'd put them into every window in the kingdom, one heartbeat at a time, so that everybody knew. He'd stopped on the morning of the snow. He hadn't sent a picture since. People asked, in the channel. Gabe asked; Gabe asked twice a day. *can you show us. just once. is everyone okay.* And John read it, and answered in words, and didn't send anything.

"When they ask," he said, when Hyvred asked him why. In the long room, at the table, with his hands round a cup. His light was very dim. It usually wasn't; John's light was usually all motes, a dozen small gold sparks drifting round him like midges, curious, going to look at things. They were all put away now. He'd folded them in, somewhere, the way you'd fold your hands.

"Gabe's asking."

"Not Gabe," said John. "You. All of you." He turned the cup round. "I've shown everybody everything for fifteen nights. It's not my town. It's yours. I'm not showing anybody anything else about it till you lot say I can."

Petra sat by Hild.

Every dusk. Hild had gone back to the arch on the first evening after the snow, as if it was still her post; she sat on the gate step inside the arch with her sword across her knees and her one eye on the valley, and her light, Hyvred saw, was a hand narrower than it had been at the start of the fifteen nights. Pale silver-white, like water in a bucket in the moonlight; darker where the eye was. Narrower now. As if somebody had poured some of it out.

And on the other side of the arch, on the other end of the step, Petra. With her sword across her knees too. Grey, like slate, all one colour; gone down into the ground, a little, the way it had gone down into the stones of the square every night in front of the granary.

They didn't talk. Hyvred watched them from the tower every dusk and they never once talked. Two Shaped on a step, one at each end of an arch, facing the same way. Like two posts of a gate.

And between them, on the middle of the step, at dusk and at dawn, an old man in a smith's apron with a blanket over his knees, and a man with a fishing rod across his.

***

He didn't fix anybody.

Hyvred noticed that on the second day, and then she couldn't stop noticing it, the way she couldn't stop noticing anything. He'd come down the ridge road at the last dawn with his rod and his line out over the roofs and cut the hunter in two and eight of the holes with it, and the absence had looked at him and turned round and gone home; and since then he'd done nothing at all. He sat on the gate step with the king. He ate what Agnes brought out. He walked once round the walls at noon, slowly, the way you'd walk round a field you'd been told about, and looked at the grating with its six bent bars, and at the oak gatepost with the dent in it, and at the place in the square where Petra's wall had gone down into the stones; and then he came back and sat down again.

People came to look at him. The whole town did, one at a time, over the two days, the way you'd go and look at a fire somebody had lit in a square. They stood at the top of the long street and looked down it at the arch, at the man on the step. You couldn't see his face. Nobody could; the baker's wife came and stood there with her basket for half an hour and her eyes kept sliding off him like water off a waxed coat. They looked anyway. And he let them. He didn't look back, and he didn't look away. He sat there and let them look, the way you'd let the weather be the weather.

Tiger went past him every dawn, to the post. He didn't say anything to Tiger. Drew came down off the roof every morning with Xav, and walked past him to the long room, and he didn't say anything to Drew. Kei sat on the well with his seven; Bee stood in Laundry Lane with her chalk; and he didn't go to either of them, or ask, or say anything at all about doses or lines or names.

He knew. Hyvred was sure he knew. He'd felt every one of them from the ridge road; you didn't cut eight holes out of a curve a mile away without knowing where everybody was. He knew Drew had broken ribs and Kei had seven names and Tiger had one word. He knew she couldn't shut her eyes.

He just didn't try to fix any of it. He sat on the step.

She went down on the third evening.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to count at dusk, the way she always did, and she had; *twenty-three*, the same, and Corvin wrote it down. And then she'd sat on the parapet with the valley over everything like a reflection, and the town's lights leaning at their six places under the snow, and her eyes so tired that they felt like they'd been sanded; and she'd stood up without deciding to, and gone down the hundred and twelve steps, and out of the tower door, and down the long street in the dark, past the guardhouse with Azac in the door, *Hyvred's going down the street, okay*, past the farrier's, to the arch.

Hild looked up. Petra looked up. Neither of them said anything.

The king moved over. Not much. Enough. He did it the way you'd move over on a bench at a long table, without looking round, without stopping whatever he was looking at; the old man's big body shifting along the cold stone a hand's width, toward Hild, leaving a gap between him and the man with the rod.

Hyvred sat down in it.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The step was cold through her coat. The arch was cold. The black oak post at the side of it, the one Tiger touched every morning, had snow on top of it like a cap. In front of them, through the open gate, the fields went away north under the snow, white and grey and blue in the last of the light, flat and soft and empty, with no tracks on them at all; and the swept road went up the valley, not swept any more, white like everything else; and the mountains went up behind it into the dark.

And over the top of all of it, because she couldn't stop, the valley. The rings. The holes. The round place at the top where the snow didn't lie. Facing them.

"I can't stop seeing it," she said.

Out loud. To the fields. It was the first time she'd said it to anyone but Bee.

"No," said the king. "You won't, for a while."

She waited for the rest of it. The *but*. There wasn't one. He was looking north with his big hands in his lap and his beard dark down the front, the way he sat at the end of a long day at the forge, and he'd said a true thing and he wasn't going to say anything else after it to make it better, because it wasn't better.

She turned her head. The other way. Not with the red mark. Just with her eyes.

She couldn't see his face. She'd known she wouldn't. She could see his shoulders, and his coat, which was an old brown coat with the elbows gone; and his hands on the rod across his knees, brown and scarred and still; and the line, wound back onto the reel, and the hook, bent and dull, hooked into the cork of the handle. She could see that he was looking north too. Not at the fields. Further. At the same place she was looking.

"Can you see it?" she said. "From here?"

"Yes," he said.

"All of it?"

"Yes."

She looked north. The rings. Four hundred and some candles in a field under the snow. A freckled one, leaning a hair toward the post six feet from her shoulder. A girl with a blue edge, at the end. Twenty-three holes. And the round place, at the top, facing her; facing all three of them, and the two women at either end of the arch, and the whole town behind them with its lamps lit. Facing them the way it had faced them for two years.

She'd been the only one who could see it for two years. Corvin had the board, and Hild had a sense of it, and the king could feel the pull; but she'd been the one who could *see* it. Every candle. Every hole. Every dawn and every dusk with her eyes shut and her back to the north. She'd seen it for everybody, because somebody had to, and she was the one who could.

And there was somebody beside her, on a cold step, looking at it.

All of it. Every candle. Not because he had to, or because she'd asked him to, or because anybody had. Just looking at it, the way you'd look at the lake.

He didn't say *I'll watch it*. He didn't say *you can stop*. He didn't say anything. He didn't move. He sat there with the rod across his knees and looked at it.

"Okay," said Hyvred. Very quietly. To nobody.

She didn't know when she fell asleep. She remembered the king's arm, warm and solid and smelling of iron and smoke, under her cheek; and the snow starting again, very fine, in the dark beyond the arch. She remembered the valley, still there, over everything, through her eyelids; and not minding it. Not minding it, for the first time in two years, because she wasn't the only one looking.

She remembered somebody putting a coat over her. A big one. Smelling of the forge.

And nothing else, for a long time.

***

She woke at first light with snow on the coat and the king's arm still under her head, and the valley still there.

It was still there. She'd known it would be. Thirteen rings, and the holes, and the round place where the snow didn't lie. It hadn't gone anywhere. The wood hadn't dried. She opened her eyes and it was over everything, the step and the arch and the fields, like a reflection on a window.

But she'd slept.

She sat up. The king's coat slid off her shoulders into her lap. He was awake; she didn't think he'd been asleep. He was looking north. On the other side of her, the step was empty; just a long dent in the snow where somebody had been sitting all night, and the snow not yet filling it.

"He went to get the kettle," said the king. "From Agnes." He didn't look round. "He was here."

"All night?"

"All night."

She looked at the dent in the snow. Then she looked down at the step below her, because somebody was sitting on it.

Bee. In two coats and a blanket, on the bottom step of the arch, with her back against Hyvred's knees and Corvin's board on her lap and the stub of pencil in her fist, and her wings folded flat; asleep, with her mouth open, the way she always slept. There was a number on the board. Under twenty-three, under twenty-three, in Bee's square handwriting, not Corvin's. She'd come down at dawn, then, before Hyvred woke, and asked Corvin, and he'd let her; and she'd climbed the tower and counted, the only way she could, without the eye: by looking at a valley she couldn't see and writing down the same number as yesterday, because it was the same as yesterday, because somebody had to write it down.

*23*, said the board. And under it, very small, in the corner, where nobody would notice unless they were looking at all of it:

A dog. Sitting. One line, the nose and the ears and the back and round, and back up to the nose. The size of a thumbnail. Not lifted.

Hyvred looked at it for a long time.

Behind her, down the long street, the long room door opened. Azac stood aside. Tiger came out at a walk, in the grey, with his ears flat and his tail wrapped round his leg, and Rui Xiang a little behind him; and down the street, and past the farrier's, and into the arch, past Bee asleep on the bottom step and Hyvred awake on the top one, and laid his hand flat on the oak post with the snow on it.

"Tomorrow," said Tiger.

In the third ring, eleven miles away, a freckled candle leaned.

Tiger stood there with his hand on the post. Then he turned round, and looked at Hyvred, and at Bee, and at the board in Bee's lap with the dog in the corner of it; and he sat down on the step beside Bee, with his back against the post, in the snow.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Rui Xiang sat down a hand away.

Up the street, Drew and Xav were coming down from the roof. Kei was coming out of the Bell with the snake round his neck and his notebook shut, for once, in his coat. Malcolm was coming behind them all with a tray. And from the side door of the Bell, carrying a kettle in one hand and a handful of cups hooked on the fingers of the other, not hurrying, a man in an old brown coat with the elbows gone was walking back up the long street toward the arch, the way you'd walk back to your own step.

Hyvred didn't look at him with the red mark. She looked at the board. At the number, and the dog.

She'd count again at dusk. And tomorrow, at dawn. And the day after, and every day till the pass opened, because she was the one who could; and the valley would sit over everything like a reflection in a window, and she'd see it with her eyes shut, and her eyes open, and asleep.

But not alone.
