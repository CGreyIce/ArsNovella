Every tier he had was named for a thing that burned.

Ina had found that very funny at the start. He'd stood in the hall in Solmere on the first morning with a white box in front of his face and read *Tier 0: Initiate*, and Thea had explained, in the first week, what came after it, *Spark, Candle, Lantern, Torch*, and he'd laughed so loudly that a guard had put his hand on his sword. Of course they were. Of all thirty-five of them, he was the one who did the thing the ladder was named after. He was the one climbing it literally. If there was a god in this world, it had a sense of humour, and he'd liked it for that.

He'd stopped finding it funny at ninety-nine.

He'd been ninety-nine since midsummer. Since the night before the Swiss, when he'd put on the mask at the end of the west wall of the Lists and lifted his hand and lit twenty-six of Lucan's forty lamps in a line, one catching off the next like a rumour going down a street, and gone down on one knee in the sand with the twenty-seventh guttering out. A grey line under his window had said *Kagaribi · locked* ever since. Watch-fires. The ones you light along a wall so the people coming home can see the way in.

Ninety-nine. Every morning. All summer. Chris logged it at the top of every count, *Ina 99*, the way he logged everybody, and had stopped saying *still not funny* after the first month because Ina had asked him, very politely, to stop.

***

He'd been methodical about it. He was proud of that. He'd been a great many things on Earth, few of them methodical, but here he had Chris, and you couldn't live in a house with Chris for three years without catching it.

Every night, at dusk, after Lucan's painters had gone home, he went up the west stair to the top of the wall and stood at the north end by the four end lamps with his notebook, and tried.

With the mask: twenty-six, twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-five, twenty-six. Never more. Twenty-six, and then the twenty-seventh flickering violet on the wick and going out, and his legs going.

Without the mask: eleven. Nine. Twelve, once, on a still night.

He wrote them down in columns. He'd learned that from Chris too. And the columns said what he'd always known: the mask made the fire hotter, and the hotter fire went further. Twenty-six against eleven. A child could read it.

So in high summer he'd tried hotter.

That had been a mistake, and he'd known it at the time, and done it anyway, because he was a man who'd never once in his life been able to resist an audience, even when the audience was himself. He'd stood at the north end of the wall in the mask with violet fire on both hands and pushed. Not lifted his hand, not flicked his fingers; pushed, the way you'd push a door that was stuck. And the lamps had gone, one after another down the wall, faster than they'd ever gone, too bright, violet all the way and not settling to yellow, ten, twenty, twenty-six—

Twenty-seven. It caught. He saw it catch. Violet, on the wick, over the twenty-seventh box. For one breath.

Then the skin came off the ends of two of his fingers, and he smelled it before he felt it, and the twenty-seventh lamp went out, and the whole line of twenty-six went out behind it, all at once, like a row of candles in a door left open; and he found himself sitting on the top of the wall with his back to a lamp-bracket and his hand held out in front of him, smoking slightly, and Ysolde coming up the west stair three steps at a time.

She hadn't said anything. She'd taken his wrist and looked at his fingers and put them in the water bucket the painters kept at the top of the stair, and held them there, and looked at him over her ram's horns with a face like a closed door.

"Again," Ina had said, after a while, through his teeth. "Tomorrow. I nearly had it."

"No," Ysolde had said.

That was all. Just *no*. And because Ysolde was a three-time champion of the Lists and a Reaching swordswoman and the only person in Valcrest who'd ever said *no* to Lucan and made it stick, he hadn't.

***

"You're measuring the wrong thing," said Chris.

It was the morning after the burn. They were on the roof of the Broken Lance, where Chris kept his notebook, with the city going about its business below them, carts in the Street of Armourers and somebody's washing on the next roof. Ina had his fingers in a bowl of cold water and Chris had both of their notebooks open on his knees, side by side.

"I'm measuring how many lamps I light."

"You're measuring how hot it is," said Chris. "And then how many. And you're assuming one makes the other." He tapped the columns. "Twenty-six. Twenty-six. Twenty-six. Twenty-five. Twenty-six. Twenty-seven, once, and you lost a fingertip. That's not a curve. That's a wall." He looked up. "When you've got a plateau that flat, it means you've hit a limit that isn't the thing you're changing. You can turn the heat up all you want. The thing that's stopping you at twenty-six isn't heat."

Ina looked at him.

"What is it, then?"

"I don't know," said Chris cheerfully. "That's your job. I just log it." He closed the notebooks. "But I'd look at the twenty-seventh lamp. That's where it stops. Every time. Whatever it is, it's there."

***

He didn't look at the twenty-seventh lamp. Not for a long time. He'd meant to. And then the summer went, and the four roads, and the news coming in down the channel every night from everywhere at once; and then, on the ninth morning after the equinox, he woke before dawn in the room he shared with Hako over the Lance and opened his window to see the time, and the channel was full.

He read it lying on his back in the dark, with Hako asleep on the other bed with his spear propped in the corner.

*Three gone. Not dead.* Drew. At twenty past three in the morning. *Elin, sixteen, Joss's sister. Trooper Rhys Hywel, sixteen. The Widow Cadogan from the north mill. They walked out at dusk. None of them had a window.*

*We held thirty-three. The king leaned.*

And Higu, *Rule four*. And Gigichi, *you okay?*, and Drew, *no. but I'm here*.

He read it four times. Then he got up very quietly, so as not to wake Hako, and sat on the end of his bed with the window still open in front of him in the dark, and took a scrap of paper from his coat and a stub of pencil and wrote the three names down on it. He didn't know why. He didn't know any of them. He'd never been to Thornwall. He'd never met a dyer's girl or a miller's widow or a boy of sixteen with a new shield.

He wrote them anyway. And then, after a moment, a fourth, from the channel, from months of Tiger and Hyvred: *Trooper Oswald Pell*. Ossie. The freckled one, in the third ring, who'd gone in the first spring and stood with his face turned a hair toward the town ever since.

Four names. He folded the paper and put it in the breast pocket of his indigo coat, behind the wisteria corsage, and sat there until it was light.

***

Lucan came to breakfast in his second-best coat, which meant he wanted something.

He didn't eat. He stood at the end of the long table in the Lance with his hands behind his back while Group 7 ate porridge in silence, and waited until even Newish had stopped pretending to talk, and then said: "Tomorrow is Lamp Night."

Nobody said anything.

"You've only ever seen it through the Lance's shutters," said Lucan. "The first year I kept you indoors, because of the horns. Last year you kept yourselves in." He let that sit. "It's the first dark of the moon after the autumn balance." He looked down the table. "Every house in Valcrest puts a lamp in the window. One for each of theirs who went to the war and didn't come home. Every house. Every window. The whole city, dark, and then the lamps." He was quiet for a moment, which for Lucan was a very long time. "So they can see the way in."

Ina put his spoon down.

"The Lists lights the great wall," said Lucan. "Every year. It always has. Before there were brackets on it, before there was a bracket, before you. We hang lamps along the top of the west wall and light them at dark, one by one, so that anybody coming home across the plain at night can see the Lists from ten miles off and know where they are." He looked at Ina. "I'd like you to light it."

"I can light twenty-six," said Ina.

"I know how many you can light. I've watched you every night since midsummer from my window with a glass of wine." Lucan smiled, slightly. "I'd like you to light it anyway. The painters can do the rest. Nobody'll mind." He hesitated. "It would mean something. The city's oni, lighting the war lamps. Three years ago they'd have stoned you for standing on the wall."

"They booed," said Ina.

"They booed," Lucan agreed. "And then a boy stood up on a bench."

Ina looked down at his porridge.

"I'll do it," he said.

"Mask off," said Ysolde.

She hadn't looked up from her bowl. She was at the far end of the table where she always sat, with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, the way a guard sits, with her curled horns catching the light from the window.

"It doesn't burn as well," said Ina.

"Mask off," said Ysolde again. And got up, and took her bowl to the kitchen, and went out.

***

He found her on the sand that afternoon. She was alone at the foot of the west wall, under the bracket, with the forty feet of whitewash going up above her and the thirty-six gold boxes and the forty empty lamps on their iron arms, and she was trimming a wick.

Just one. A little clay lamp, the kind they sold in the Street of Coopers for a copper, sitting on a stone beside her, and she was trimming its wick with a knife, very carefully, the way she did everything.

He sat down on the sand a little way off. She didn't look at him.

"I came home from the war," said Ysolde, after a while, "on Lamp Night."

He waited.

"The last year. My company came back across the plain from the east at dusk, what was left of it. Nineteen out of sixty." She turned the lamp a little on the stone. "And people stood in the street to watch us come in. And when they saw the horns they spat." She said it the way she'd have said it was raining. "Not all of them. Enough. A woman threw a cabbage. I'd been carrying a boy called Piers on my back for three days with his leg off at the knee, and she threw a cabbage at us, because I had horns and the thing that had taken his leg had horns." She trimmed. "I don't blame her. I did, then. I don't now."

Ina didn't say anything.

"The whole street was dark," said Ysolde. "Lamp Night. Every window with a lamp in it. Hundreds. Thousands. And I came up the Street of Coopers with Piers on my back and cabbage in my hair, looking for my mother's house, and I couldn't tell which it was. They all looked the same. Every window, a lamp." She stopped trimming. "And then I saw one that was smoking."

She held up the little clay lamp.

"She could never trim a wick. My mother. Forty years, and she never once trimmed a wick right. It always smoked. There was always a black mark on the ceiling over the table." She put it down. "I saw a lamp in a window, smoking, with a black mark going up the shutter over it; and I knew it was hers. Not because it was bright. It was the worst lamp in the street. I knew it because I knew the hand that lit it."

She looked at him then. For the first time.

"A watch-fire's not for seeing by," said Ysolde. "Anybody can see a light. You can see the moon. A watch-fire's for *knowing*. So the one coming home can look up and say, *that's ours*." She looked at his shoulder, where the mask hung on its red cords. "You put that on and the fire goes hotter. I've seen it. Everyone's seen it. And then you stand on the wall and light lamps for people coming home, in the face of the thing they're afraid of."

"The fire goes further," said Ina. Quietly.

"Does it?"

He didn't answer.

"Twenty-six," said Ysolde. "That's what Chris says. Every night. Twenty-six with that on, and then you fall over." She picked up the lamp and the knife and stood up. "I wonder who's in the twenty-seventh box."

And she went away across the sand.

***

He went up the wall at dusk.

Not to light anything. He went up the west stair with his notebook and the mask on his shoulder and walked along the top of the wall in the last of the light, past the four end lamps, past the lamp over the thirty-sixth box, the bye, the box with no name in it, and on, counting, along the iron brackets, one, two, three; and he stopped at the twenty-seventh, and leaned over the parapet, and looked down at the box under it in the whitewash, forty feet of the Lists' bracket, with the name painted in gold.

*Ina*.

He stood there for a long time.

Of course it was. The bracket ran the way it ran, the way Lucan had painted it in the spring, and the lamps lit the way they lit, one catching off the next down the wall from the north end; and every night all summer, with the mask on, the line of fire had come along the wall through Hyvred and Tiger and Hana and Gigichi and Thea, twenty-six of them, every one of them catching, and stopped dead at his own.

He'd never once lit his own lamp.

He thought about that, standing on top of the wall in the dusk. He thought about Ysolde's mother's smoking lamp, and a woman with a cabbage, and a watch-fire being for *knowing*. And about the mask: grey, horned, red-eyed, grinning, with the red cords hanging from the mouth; worn smooth on the inside by three years of his face. He'd brought it through from Earth because he'd been wearing it. It had been a joke, once. A thing for a stream. Then it had been a thing he wore in front of ten thousand people for one small boy, and took off for everybody else.

A watch-fire lit in that face was a stranger's fire. A light in the window of a house nobody lived in. It would go a long way. It was hot. It would light twenty-six people's lamps for them, people he loved, so that if they ever came home across the plain at night they'd see the way in. And it couldn't light his. Because the thing in the mask didn't have a home to come back to. It was the thing you were afraid of, standing on the wall.

He took the mask off his shoulder. He held it in both hands and looked at it.

Then he put it down, very carefully, face up, on top of the parapet. And lifted his hand, the way you'd lift your hand to somebody at the other end of a street, and flicked his fingers, once, at the lamp over his own name.

It lit.

Small. Pale. Not violet; or not the deep violet of the mask; something paler, almost white at the heart, the colour of the wisteria on his coat. It sat on the wick for a breath, and then settled, and went the ordinary yellow of a lamp, and burned.

It didn't go out.

He stood and watched it for a long time, his own lamp, alone on the wall over his own name, with thirty-nine dark lamps on either side of it, burning, and found that he was crying, and didn't mind at all.

He didn't light any more. He didn't try. He picked up the mask and went down the west stair in the dark and back to the Lance, and wrote one line in his notebook under the columns, under all the twenty-sixes, and went to bed.

*27th lamp: mine. Never lit it. Mask off: lit. Didn't go out.*

***

Lamp Night came down over Valcrest like a held breath.

He'd never seen a city do it. At dusk, the whole of it went dark. Every lamp in every street, every brazier at every gate, every cookshop fire in the Street of Armourers banked to coals; the watch walked the walls with their torches out. For the length of a long breath after sunset, the biggest city in the south of the kingdom was as dark as the plain round it.

And then, one window at a time, the lamps.

He watched it from the sand of the Lists, with the bowl filling up round him in the dark. They came on all across the city, house by house, window by window, small and yellow, not all at once: here, then there, then a street of them, then a hillside; the way stars come out, the way a rumour goes round a market. One lamp for each of theirs who'd gone to the war and not come home. Some windows had one. Some had three. Some had a whole row along the sill, and he looked at those for a long time.

The bowl of the Lists was full. Forty thousand, Lucan said; it was always full on Lamp Night, every family that had nobody to put a lamp in a window for coming here instead, to watch the wall. They sat in the dark in the stands, very quietly, the quietest he'd ever heard the Lists, with little clay lamps unlit in their laps.

Group 7 was on the sand at the foot of the west wall. Chris with his notebook. Hako with his spear grounded. Newish with her scarf, not humming, for once. Winter beside him in her silver hair with her hands in her sleeves, close enough that he could feel the cold coming off her, the faint frost-breath she couldn't help, that he'd learned over three years to stand next to the way you'd stand next to a window in summer.

And at the rail of the front tier of the east stand, in a patched shirt, with a battered old cavalry helmet in his lap and a paper crown of horns on his head with a little white mask stitched on the front, Wil.

Ten, now. Taller. He'd made a new crown every summer; this one was the best yet, with real black paint on the horns. He was looking at Ina across the sand.

Ina walked over to the rail.

"Hello, Wil."

"Hello," said Wil. He looked at the mask on Ina's shoulder. "Are you putting it on?"

"No," said Ina. "Not tonight." He lifted it off his shoulder by its red cords and held it out over the rail. "Will you hold it for me? It's important. I'll need it back."

Wil looked at the mask for a long moment. Then he put the cavalry helmet down on the bench very carefully, and took the oni mask in both hands, and held it against his chest, with the grinning face looking out.

"I'll hold it," said Wil.

***

He didn't go up the wall. He'd decided that in the night. He stood on the sand at the north end of the west wall, at the foot of it, where he'd stood at midsummer with the mask on, and looked up the forty feet of whitewash at the bracket in the dark, the thirty-six boxes he couldn't see and the forty lamps he couldn't see and the gold names he knew by heart.

He didn't push. He'd learned that.

He lifted his hand.

And this time, before he flicked his fingers, he said the name.

"The bye," said Ina. Quietly. To the dark. "For whoever isn't on the wall."

The lamp over the thirty-sixth box lit.

Pale. White at the heart. A breath of wisteria, and then yellow, and steady.

He'd meant to do one. He'd meant to do one at a time, by name, all the way down, the way Drew would have done it, the way you ticked in a green room. He found he didn't need to choose. He said the next name, and the next lamp lit; and the next, and it lit; he didn't flick his fingers again. He just said them. Into the dark, on the sand, in his own voice, the warm British voice that he'd used on Earth for a thousand streams and here for three years at a thousand tables, and the lamps came on along the top of the wall one after another, one catching off the next, as each name went up.

"Hyvred. Tiger. Bee. Kei. Drew."

Group 5. Their five lamps, in a row, over the first five boxes, catching. He thought of a tower and a valley and a man with two cracked ribs.

"Hana. Milli. Aleaka. Cursiffe. Jared."

"Thea. Higu. Xola. Gigi. Chi."

"Yan. Maigo. Salty. Wendy. Niko."

He said them in the wall's order. He knew it by heart; he'd walked it every night since midsummer. The lamps came on.

"Deston. Gabe. Rachael. Dennis."

Twenty. Twenty-five. He heard the stands behind him change. Not a sound; the opposite of a sound. Forty thousand people very slowly stopping breathing. He felt something go out of him into each lamp as it lit, a little warm thread of whatever he was, not heat, not much heat at all, and not come back; and he felt it not matter.

"Chris."

Twenty-six. The lamp over Chris's box caught, white and then yellow. Twenty-six, again. Exactly where he'd always stopped. And he stood on the sand with his hand up and the next lamp dark over the next box, and felt his knees start to go, the way they always went; felt the fire run out of him at the end of his own arm, the way it always ran out.

And he said: "Ina."

He said it the way Ysolde's mother would have said her daughter's name in a dark street. Not loud. Not to the crowd. To the lamp. *That's ours*.

It lit.

He felt it light. He felt it go up out of him into the twenty-seventh lamp, his own, over his own box in gold, and settle there, and burn; and he felt his knees stop going. Just stop. As if the thing that had been running out of him at the end of every night all summer had been running out *toward* that lamp, and had got there, and could stop running.

He stood up straight.

There were still lamps dark on the wall. The nine past his own; and the four at the north end, beside him, the end lamps, over no box, that the painters lit every night and nobody had ever lit for anybody.

"Calvin," said Ina. "Winter. Newish. Hako."

They came as easily as breathing. He didn't feel them go out of him at all.

"Xavier. Malcolm. Rui Xiang. John. Azac."

The whole bracket. Thirty-six lamps, burning, over thirty-five names in gold and one box with no name in it at all.

He turned to the end lamps.

He took the scrap of paper out of the breast pocket of his indigo coat, behind the wisteria, and unfolded it, and couldn't read it in the dark. He didn't need to. He'd read it a hundred times since yesterday.

"Trooper Oswald Pell," said Ina.

The first end lamp lit.

"Elin," said Ina. "The dyer's girl. Sixteen."

The second.

"Trooper Rhys Hywel. Sixteen."

The third.

"The Widow Cadogan," said Ina. "Of the north mill."

And the fourth end lamp caught, white at the heart, and settled, and went yellow; and there were forty lamps burning along the top of the west wall of the Lists of Valcrest in a row, on Lamp Night, against the dark; and four of them were for people he'd never met, standing in a ring in a valley a long way north and west, smiling, with their faces turned the wrong way.

*So they can see the way in.*

He put his hand down.

***

The stands didn't cheer. That was the thing he'd remember. Lucan said afterwards, wiping his eyes with a lace handkerchief the size of a pillowcase, that in thirty years of Lamp Nights the Lists had never once cheered, and Ina said he hadn't expected them to, and Lucan said *no, of course not, but they'd never been quiet like that either*.

They lit their lamps.

All round the bowl, forty thousand people in the dark, with little clay lamps in their laps; and as he stood there with his hand down and his knees perfectly steady, they lit them, one after another, round the stands, off each other, a flame passed from wick to wick along the rows, the way it had gone along the wall. Until the whole bowl of the Lists was a bowl of small yellow lights, rising up from the sand in tiers to the dark, and the forty along the top of the west wall above them all.

At the rail of the east stand, Wil stood up on his bench.

He didn't take his horns off. He held up the mask instead. In both hands. Over his head. As high as he could reach. Grey and horned and grinning, with its red cords swinging, in the light of forty thousand lamps.

Nobody laughed. Nobody spat. A few people near him, on the front tiers, took their own paper horns off and held them up too. Not many. It wasn't that kind of night.

Ina bowed to him. Low, from the waist, the way he'd bowed to the men in grey and blue two summers ago.

Then he opened his window.

He read it. Then he laughed, out loud, the big surprised breathless laugh, alone on the sand of the Lists in the middle of forty thousand people who were all very carefully not making a sound; and the laugh went up out of the bowl into the dark over the city, over all the windows and all the lamps, and somewhere in the east stand somebody laughed back.

Winter put her hand on his arm. Briefly. Cold as a window. Then she took it away again and put it back in her sleeve and looked at the wall and didn't say anything, which was the most she'd ever said.

He stayed till dawn.

He sat on the sand at the foot of the wall with his back against the whitewash, under the bracket, under the forty lamps, while the stands emptied very slowly round him and the clay lamps went home in people's hands through the streets, small lights going out through the gates of the Lists and away across the city, back to the windows they'd come from.

Hako brought him a blanket. Newish brought him a pie, and sat with him while he ate it, and then went to bed without making a single joke, which was so unlike her that he worried about it for an hour. Chris sat on the step of the west stair with his notebook and logged the lamps every hour, *40, 40, 40*, as if they might change, and they didn't.

Winter stayed. She sat on the sand a little way off, in her silver hair, with her knees up and her hands in her sleeves and the cold coming off her, faintly, into the night. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Fire and frost, at the foot of a wall. Two people everybody stared at. He'd stopped minding being stared at, a long time ago. He'd never stopped minding being stared at by her, a little, in case she decided something.

Toward midnight, Wil came down from the east stand.

He'd stayed, too. His mother had come for him twice and he'd said *I've got to give it back* both times and she'd gone away. He came across the sand with his paper horns on and the cavalry helmet under one arm and the mask in both hands, and stopped in front of Ina and held it out.

"You said you'd need it back," said Wil.

"I did." Ina took it. The inside was warm from the boy's chest. "Thank you, Wil."

"Why didn't you wear it?"

Ina thought about that.

"Because they're coming home," he said. "The ones the lamps are for. And when you come home you want to see somebody you know at the window. Not a monster." He turned the mask over in his hands. "I'm not a monster. I know that. You know that. But in the dark, from ten miles off, it's hard to tell."

Wil thought about it.

"I'd know," he said.

"I know you would," said Ina. "You always did. You were the first."

Wil nodded, as if that settled it, and went back across the sand to his mother, who was waiting at the gate with a clay lamp of her own, one, in her hand.

Ina hung the mask on his shoulder by its red cords.

He'd wear it again. He knew that. There'd be a night when a hotter fire mattered more than a known one, when there was something on the far side of a wall that needed to be frightened. He'd put it on then, for that, and not mind.

But not for the watch-fires. Never for those.

He leaned his head back against the whitewash and looked up. Forty lamps, in a row, white at the heart and then yellow, burning steady over thirty-six boxes and four more that weren't boxes at all; and at the north end, nearest him, four lamps over nothing, for a trooper with freckles and a dyer's girl with blue hands and a boy with a new shield and a big woman with flour to the elbow, a long way away, in the dark, who couldn't see them.

*Not yet*, he thought. *But they're lit. When you turn round, they'll be there.*

At dawn, when the sky over the east stand went grey, the forty lamps went out on their own, one after another, back along the wall, the way they'd come; the end lamps last.
