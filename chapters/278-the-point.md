He read it in the barracks, on his bunk, with the stove ticking and Suzu on his collarbone and the snow going past the window in the lamplight like something being poured. Thea's six lines, and then Higu's, and then Hyvred's. *Come and stay. The valley says one and the sword says the other.*

He read it three times. Then he put his hand up to his neck.

*Yes*, said Suzu.

"It's the same sword. The point's in the grave. If the whole sword's saying it—"

*Then the point's saying it*, said Suzu. *It always was. You've heard it every night for two years, Yun. You just didn't have the word.*

He wrote it before he could think about it, which was not the way he did things.

They went out at the eighth hour, in the snow, five of them.

Rhea was at the east gate before he was, with her coat buttoned to the chin and her sword on her back and snow already on her shoulders, as if she'd been standing there since the summer. Salty came down the stair behind him, and Niko after Salty, pulling his gloves on with his teeth. Maigo came last, at a run, with his hood up and his sunglasses on in the dark, which he said was a matter of principle.

"I want to see my tree," he said. "It's my tree. Nobody asked me."

"Nobody's asking anybody," said Wendy, from the top of the gate, where she'd stay till they came back, counting, the way she always stayed. "Go on. Take a lamp. Don't fall in anything."

The commander opened the postern himself. He didn't ask where they were going. He'd stopped asking in the autumn.

It was an hour, in the snow. Down the burial track between the stones in their long rows, white on top and black on the sides where the wind had cleaned them, most with no names, the ones with names filled up with snow so you couldn't read them anyway. Then past the end of the stones and on to the knoll. The ash at the top of it, bare, black against the white, with its branches holding the snow along their tops like arms held out with a tray. Liraun's seedling a hand from its roots, a stick in the snow with one leaf on it, still, in the middle of winter, which nobody could explain and Maigo said didn't need explaining. The cairn.

And at the head of the cairn, the black stone.

He'd seen it a hundred times. He'd sat with his back against the cairn every night of the fifteen and most nights since. He knew it better than the inside of the barracks. A shield of black iron about as high as his chest, with a rounded top, standing on a spike in the old hole, facing west. *TO DIWA, MY LOVE* across the face of it in the letters Ice had cut, in English, that nobody in the fort could read except the five of them. The snow didn't lie on it. It never had. It lay round the foot of it in a smooth bank, and stopped a finger from the iron, the way water stops a finger from a hot stone.

He'd always thought it was because the iron was warm. It wasn't warm. He'd touched it.

He went round to the front of it. To the west side, where the letters were. And knelt in the snow, and took his glove off, and put his hand flat on the face of it, low down, below the letters, where the spike went into the ground.

*Gently*, said Suzu.

He'd felt it before. That was the thing. He'd felt it the first winter the stone stood, two winters ago, and every time since: a note. Very low. Lower than hearing. Coming up out of the iron and down into the knoll, not stopping. Like the hum you feel in a bell a long time after anybody's rung it. He'd thought it was just what the iron did. What iron that burned demons felt like, from inside. He'd filed it.

He felt it now. And this time he had the word.

It wasn't a sound. Thea had said that; she could read the grammar of it and not the sound. He couldn't read anything. But he could feel which way it went. It went *down*. That was what he'd been feeling for two winters and not known: not a hum, not a note, but a direction. Something pressing down into the knoll through the spike, all the time, every heartbeat, the way a hand presses flat on the lid of a pot that's trying to boil. Not hard. Not hitting. Just there. Just not letting it come up.

*Stay*.

He felt it go down through the snow and the green under the snow and the old earth under the green, into the dark, to the cold.

The cold was where it always was. Asleep, a long way down, small and dense, like a coal made of ice. The spike went straight through the middle of it. He'd known that too. He'd felt it the first winter, a nail through a moth. He'd never once thought about what the nail was *doing*. It was saying *stay* to it. Every heartbeat. And the cold, a long way down in the dark, with the spike through it and the ash's roots round it like fingers, was staying.

And there, behind the iron, where it always was, the other thing. Warm. Held. Kept. Out of reach, the way it had been since the first snow, the way it would always be; not his, not anybody's to reach for. He didn't. He never had. He knew it by the shape the way you know a lit window in a house you're not going into, and he let it be.

That wasn't what was wrong.

He didn't know at first what was wrong. He knelt there with his hand on the iron and felt the word go down, and down, and something about it was the wrong shape. Not weak. He couldn't have called it weak. It went all the way down to the cold and pressed, and the cold stayed. But it was like hearing somebody say a thing they've said so many times that the words have gone smooth. Like a man saying *I'm fine*.

He took his hand off.

"Suzu."

She'd come down off his neck. She was in the snow beside his knee, the whole length of her, white on white, with her head up near the foot of the stone and her tongue going. Tasting it. She did that sometimes, with things she didn't trust. She'd never done it with the stone.

*It's been saying it ten years*, she said.

"Eight. Then the year it was gone. Then two."

*Ten*, said Suzu. *It doesn't count the year it was gone. It was still saying it then. From the cabin wall. Across the whole kingdom. To a hole with nothing in it.* She put her head down, very close to the iron, not touching. *And now it's half. Half a sword. The other half's on a girl's back at a lake. It used to be the whole of it saying it, and now it's the point on its own.*

Yan knelt in the snow.

"And?"

*It's tired, Yun*.

She said it very quietly. The way she said things she'd known for a while and decided he ought to hear.

*It's not going to stop*, she said. *Not tonight. Not this winter. It's iron. It doesn't know how to stop. But it rang in the autumn, by itself, when he cast at Thornwall and everything that shape sat up and listened. It rang on its own to put the cold back down. Further than it had ever put it.* She lifted her head. *That cost it. You heard it. You felt it in your teeth.*

He had. He'd felt it in his back through the stones.

*It's been saying one word with nobody saying it back for ten years*, said Suzu. *That's all. That's what tired is.*

"What's she saying?" said Salty.

He was standing at the back of the cairn with his arms folded and snow on his eyebrows. Rhea was at the edge of the knoll, facing east, the way she always faced. Maigo was sitting at the foot of the ash with his bare wooden foot dug into the snow, right in, up to the ankle, and his eyes shut behind the sunglasses.

Yan told him.

He told it straight, the way you told Salty things, because Salty didn't do anything with a thing told any other way except ask again. *It's saying stay. It's been saying it ten years. It's tired.*

Salty looked at the stone for a long time.

"Is it going to stop?"

"Suzu says no. Not this winter."

"Then it's tired," said Salty. "Lots of things are tired." He unfolded his arms. "Is the cold going to know?"

Yan didn't answer that. He didn't know.

"Maigo," said Salty. "What's the tree say?"

Maigo didn't open his eyes. "It says it's winter," he said. "It's asleep. It says ask in spring." He pulled his foot up out of the snow a little way and put it back. "It's still got hold. That's the main thing. It's got its roots round it, round the cold, the way it did when the swords rang. Two hands. It says three with the iron. It says it's not letting go of anything till spring, and then it'll think about it, and also could I stop putting my foot in it, it's cold."

"That's a lot for a tree," said Salty.

"It's a very old tree," said Maigo. "Well. It's three. But its mum's the big one in the fort yard, and she's old." He opened his eyes. "It's fine, Yan. It's holding. It's just a tree. It doesn't know what tired is."

Niko hadn't said anything.

Niko was standing where the knoll went down toward the red, on the east side, beyond Rhea, with his back to all of them, looking at the ground. Not at the snow. At the top of the snow. It had stopped snowing while they talked, without any of them noticing, and the sky had come clear in the east over the red, black and hard with stars, and the cold had come down out of it all at once the way it did on the red on clear nights, so fast you could feel your breath freeze in your nose. And on the top of the snow, the crust was growing frost. Little feathers of it. You could see them coming up in the lamplight if you looked.

"Niko," said Yan.

"They're back," said Niko.

He didn't turn round.

"The letters," he said. "On the frost. They stopped in the autumn, the night he cast. They stopped all at once and there's been snow on everything since, and I thought—" He stopped. "They're back. Out there. On the crust. Not many. They're slow."

Nobody else could see them. Nobody ever had. Niko had seen them on his window, on the third line, since the first winter, little shapes like frost on the inside of a glass that guessed at words and changed their minds; and then, the last year, out on the red at night where the frost grew, the same shapes, coming up on the ground the same way, almost making words and going. Like something trying things. He'd said that, once. *Like something trying things*. Yan had filed it with the rest.

"What are they saying?"

"Nothing," said Niko. "They never say anything. They almost." He was quiet. "I thought they were English. Before. On the third line they were English, they were guessing my name. But out there they've never been English. I just thought they were, because I wanted them to be." He looked round at last. His face was pale in the lamplight, and his nose was red with cold, and there was something in his face Yan hadn't seen since the knoll in the autumn. "Thea's word. The circle's language. The left arc. She said they're like a hand writing. She's described them, in the channel. Some of them." He swallowed. "I think it's that. I think it's always been that. Out on the red. Something writing it in the frost and rubbing it out."

"Writing what?"

"I don't know." Niko turned round again, to the red. "But it doesn't come here."

"What?"

"Come and look."

They went and looked. Yan and Salty, and Rhea, who didn't need to be asked, who'd been listening from three paces off with her hand on her hilt. They stood beside Niko on the east edge of the knoll and looked down where he was looking, at the snow, at the clean white crust going away down the slope toward the red with the frost coming up on it in the lamplight.

"There," said Niko, and pointed.

None of them could see anything. Snow. Frost.

"There's a line," said Niko. "Out there, past the bottom of the knoll. They come up on the crust everywhere out on the red, slow, the way they always did. And they come up to that line." He drew it in the air with his glove, a long curve, going away to the left and the right round the bottom of the knoll. "And they stop. Like frost stops at a wall where the sun's on it. Inside it, there's nothing. Just frost. Ordinary frost. Feathers."

He turned round and looked back up the knoll, past the cairn, past the ash, at the black stone with the snow stopping a finger from it.

"It's round," said Niko. "It goes all the way round. And that's the middle of it."

They measured it the next night.

That was Wendy. She came out herself, the second night, because she said she wasn't having Niko tell her a thing was a circle and nobody counting it; and she brought a coil of rope from the quartermaster with knots in it every pace, and a bundle of the little stakes the fort used for marking graves before the stones went in, and a lamp.

It was clear again. The frost came up on the crust. Niko walked out from the foot of the black stone, east, down the knoll and off the bottom of it on to the red, with the rope paying out behind him and Wendy counting the knots, and Rhea walking beside him with her sword drawn, because it was the red and it was dark and she was a sword. And at a hundred and forty paces Niko stopped and said "here," and put a stake in.

Then they did it west. A hundred and forty. North, a hundred and forty, out across the burial track between the stones. South, a hundred and forty, where the knoll went down into a little dip full of snow up to Maigo's waist and Maigo had to be pulled out of it by Salty by the hood.

A circle. With the black stone in the middle.

"A hundred and forty," said Wendy, on the way back, writing it on a slate with her glove off and her fingers going white. "All the way round. Not a hundred and thirty-nine. Not a hundred and forty-one." She looked at Yan. "Things don't do that."

"Swords do," said Salty.

Wendy looked at him. Then at the slate. "I'm going to do it every night," she said. "If it's the same, it's the same. If it isn't—" She stopped. "I'm going to do it every night."

She did it every night.

A hundred and forty. A hundred and forty. A hundred and forty, and then cloud, and snow, and no frost, and she went out anyway and stood at the stake on the red in the snow and said *I can't see anything*, and Niko said *nor can I*, and they came back. A hundred and thirty-nine, on the fifth night. East. Only east. Niko walked it three times and said the frost came up to a pace inside the stake on the east side, and stopped there, and on the other three sides it came up to the stake and stopped, the same as the first night.

"East," said Rhea.

She hadn't said anything all week. She stood at the east stake on the red in the dark with her sword drawn and her face to the red, the way she stood on the knoll, the way she stood on the east wall now every night for a count of ten on the old cracked stones; and she said it again. "East. Not the other sides."

"It's one pace," said Wendy. "It's frost. It could be wind. It could be Niko."

"It could be," said Niko.

"It's one pace," said Wendy again, and wrote it down.

He told Aldous on the sixth day.

In the chapel, in the afternoon, with the light coming in grey through the high window and the brazier going and the old man at the long table with the fort's book of the dead open in front of him, the way he always was in the afternoons. Yan sat on the bench across from him with Suzu inside his coat and told him all of it. Thea's word. The note going down. Suzu tasting the iron. *Ten years. Tired*. The circle on the frost. A hundred and forty paces, and a hundred and thirty-nine on the east side.

Aldous listened with his hands flat on the book.

He'd seen it. That was the thing about Aldous; he'd been there. He'd been a chaplain on the Redmarch for twenty years before the Advisor sent for him, and he'd buried the dead of the short war on the red and said the words over them, and he'd walked out to that knoll a hundred times in those years with a cart and a spade and seen the black sword standing over a grave with no name on it, point down, in the middle of nothing. He'd noticed when it went. He'd led the escort that took it back.

"The snow," he said, when Yan had finished.

"What about it?"

"It never lay near it." Aldous was looking at the brazier. "Not in any winter. Eight winters. I'd come out in the snow with the cart for the ones who'd died in the infirmary, and the whole red would be white to the edge of the world, and there'd be the sword, standing up black out of it, with a ring round its foot where the snow didn't lie. A pace across." He held his hands apart. "I thought it was the iron being warm. I thought it was the Hero's. That's what we all thought. *Of course the snow doesn't lie on it. It's his*." He looked at Yan. "It was never warm, was it."

"No."

"No." Aldous turned a page of the book, not looking at it. "Things that hold get tired," he said. "Men do. Walls do. A rope does, if you leave it on a mooring long enough, even if nothing ever pulls on it hard. It's not the pulling. It's the always." He put his hand flat on the page again. "I buried a sergeant here once who'd stood a gate at the old west fort for nine years. Nothing ever came through it. Not once. Nine years. And one morning he sat down on the step and didn't get up, and the surgeon said there was nothing wrong with him at all except that he was tired."

Yan didn't say anything.

"What do you do," he said, after a while. "For a thing like that. For a tired wall."

Aldous looked at him for a long time across the table with the book between them.

"You know the answer," he said. "You wrote it. In the autumn. I had Wendy read it to me." He didn't smile. Aldous didn't smile much; he'd buried too many people for it to come easily. But something in the old grave face went a little softer. "*Somebody sits with you and says yes*."

***

So he sat with it.

He'd been sitting with it already. Every dusk, with his back against the cairn, since the autumn; it wasn't new. What was new was knowing what he was sitting beside. He went out at dusk on the seventh day with Rhea and Salty, the way he always did, and sat down with his back against the cold stones of the cairn, and this time he didn't feel for the cold, or for the circle, or for anything. He just sat. With his shoulder against the back of the black stone, the plain side, where Salty had held it in his arms once and laughed. And felt the word go down beside him into the dark.

*Stay*. *Stay*. Every heartbeat. A hand flat on a lid.

He couldn't carry it. He knew that. He'd tried to carry a girl's weight across the whole width of the kingdom in the autumn and nothing had come. *Carry · locked*. He couldn't take any of this off the iron. He couldn't say it for it. He didn't have the word; he only had the direction. He couldn't do anything at all except sit there in the snow with his shoulder against it.

That was what he did.

He didn't know if it made any difference. Iron didn't know who was sitting next to it. Probably. He thought about Ice, before any of them came, sitting just here with the whole sword across his knees and his palm on the earth, and the cold going down; and about the king and Ice on Garrow's anvil, splitting it; and Ice cutting the letters. And about a man who'd carried it on his back for eight years, through a war, every night, not knowing it was saying anything at all.

Not knowing. And then a year on a cabin wall, saying it anyway, across the kingdom, to a hole.

He thought about his mother, then. He didn't mean to. The kitchen table. The post under the salt so it wouldn't blow away. Her hand flat on the top of the pile, feeling the edges of the envelopes, knowing most of them by the shape, waiting for him to come home and read her the rest. Every day. Not knowing when he'd come. Her hand flat on the post. *Stay*.

He put his own hand flat on the snow beside him. Not on the iron. Beside it. And left it there.

*Yun*, said Suzu, after a long time.

"I know."

*I wasn't going to say anything.*

"I know."

She went round his neck, the long way, and put her head down on his collarbone, and didn't say anything, the way she hadn't been going to.

Rhea stood at the edge of the knoll facing east with her hand on her hilt. Salty sat beside him with his knees up and his arms round them, and after a while he put his own big hand down flat on the snow on the other side of the stone, without saying why, and left it there too.

They came back at full dark. At the gate, Yan opened the channel. It was the hour.

He read that one twice.

Nobody wrote from the lake. Nobody ever did, from the cabin. On the eighth night it was a hundred and forty on the east side again.

Wendy walked it twice to be sure. Niko walked it three times. A hundred and forty. The frost came up to the stake on the east, and stopped there, and on the other side of the stake, out on the red, it came up thick and slow in the lamplight and almost made words and went, and Niko stood looking at it for a long time and didn't say what it almost said.

"Could've been wind," said Wendy, writing it down.

"Could've been," said Niko.

"Could've been you," said Salty, to Yan, quite quietly, at the back of the cairn, where nobody else could hear.

Yan didn't answer that. He didn't know. He thought it probably wasn't; iron didn't know who was sitting next to it. Probably. He sat down with his back against the cairn in the dark and his shoulder against the black stone and put his hand flat on the snow beside it, and Salty put his on the other side, and they didn't say anything else.

He opened his window on the way back. He didn't usually, not out on the burial track in the dark. He did tonight.

Eighty-six. It had been eighty-four since the autumn. One of it, he knew, was the hare: Suzu had taken one on the burial track on the fourth night, a big brown one that a hawk had left in the snow with its back broken and its eyes still open, and she'd struck the light off it the way she did, and he'd felt it come down the cord into him warm and small and frightened, and kept a little of it, the way he always kept a little. The snow on its fur. The sky.

The other one he didn't know about. A week of sitting in the snow with his shoulder against a stone. A ring with a middle that kept saying no. A hundred and thirty-nine, and then a hundred and forty again.

It wasn't enough. It wasn't nothing.

*Locked*, said the window. Grey. Under the line. The way it had said since the summer.

*Not yet*, said Suzu.

"I know."

*You sat with it*, she said. *You didn't try to carry it. You didn't try to fix it. You didn't go in after anything. You sat down beside it and put your hand on the ground.* She went round his neck, the long way. *That's the first half of carrying, Yun. Nobody ever does the first half. They go straight for the second and wonder why it's heavy.*

He walked back to the fort in the dark with the stones in their rows on either side of him, white on top, and the one lamp burning on the east gate, and Rhea in front with her sword on her back and Salty behind with his hands in his pockets.

Behind them, on the knoll, the black stone stood at the head of the cairn with the snow stopping a finger from it all the way round. It faced west. It always had. And under it, down through the spike, into the dark, to a small cold thing asleep at the bottom of the world with a nail through its middle and an ash tree's roots round it like two hands, it said the one word it knew. And had said for ten years. And would say tomorrow. Tired, and not stopping.

And a hundred and forty paces out, all the way round, on the crust of the snow on the red, where nobody could see it but a thin boy from another world, something wrote in the frost, and rubbed it out, and wrote again; and came up to the edge of the ring; and stopped.
