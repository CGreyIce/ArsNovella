The pumpkins came in late that year, which Ars took personally.

He'd planted them in the spring in the long bed by the south fence, where the sun lay longest, and he'd watered them and weeded them and moved the hens off them twice a day all summer, and in return they'd sulked through the warm months and only now, with the first frosts coming down off the hills, decided to swell up fat and orange all at once. He spent three mornings carrying them in, one under each arm, and stacking them in the lean-to by the woodpile, and the hens followed him the whole time, complaining.

The hens had always hated him. He'd never understood why. He fed them. He built them a better house than most people in Wrenhaven lived in. He'd once, at some personal risk, fought off a fox for them in the middle of the night in nothing but his boots. They still looked at him every morning as if he owed them money.

"They know," Alea had said once, when she was eleven, with total seriousness.

"Know what?"

"What you are." She'd looked at the hens, and then back at him. "Animals can tell."

He'd laughed at the time. He didn't laugh about it now. He just carried the pumpkins, and let the hens shout at him, and felt the cold coming down off the hills, and was, in a quiet way he didn't often stop to notice, happy.

***

Rowan came on the fourth morning of the harvest, with frost on his horse's mane and a face that said he'd been rehearsing something on the ride out.

They stood at the foot of the jetty, as always. The lake was smoking with mist in the cold, and somewhere out on the water a heron was standing on one leg, pretending to be a post.

"News from the capital," said Rowan. "You won't like it."

"I never do."

"Scouts. In the east." Rowan pulled his cloak tighter. "On the Redmarch. A dozen of them, near Cinderhold. They came out of the dark and stood out of bowshot and *counted* the fort, Ars. Every tower. Every gate. Then they went back. And they've been back since."

Ars didn't say anything. He was looking at the lake.

"And in the west," Rowan went on, "villages emptying. Families going off into the hills at night and not coming back. Some of the summoned saw it. One of them, a girl in Thornwall, says she can see what's wrong with the people who stay behind." He shook his head. "I don't understand half of what Halvard's letters say anymore. But I understand that much."

"Two sides," said Ars.

Rowan looked at him sharply. "That's what Halvard said. Two sides."

"It's what anyone would say."

"And there's the Council," said Rowan, with the face of a man getting to the part he'd rehearsed. "Lord Vane wants the summoned split up among the great houses. *Fostered*, he calls it. Says the guides can't protect them. Says the king's too soft." He paused. "Says there's no hero left to send if it comes to war."

The heron, out on the lake, finally moved. It put its other foot down, very slowly, and went on standing.

"Ars," said Rowan.

"Mm."

"Will you come back?"

He'd known Rowan would ask. He'd known it since the first morning, when Rowan had ridden out with the news of the summoning and Halvard's sealed note. He'd known it the way you know a storm is coming by the ache in an old wound.

"No," he said.

Rowan didn't argue. He'd known the answer too. He just stood there in the cold, with his big shoulders hunched and his mayor's chain glinting, and looked at the lake, and after a while he let out a long breath.

"Didn't think so," he said.

***

Ars went into the cabin.

He didn't explain. He just turned and walked up the path from the jetty and went inside, and Rowan, after a moment's surprise, followed him as far as the door and waited there, the way he'd waited at that door seven years ago with a sword in his hand.

There was a chest under the floor, beneath the rug by the hearth. Ars hadn't opened it in a long time. He pulled the rug back and lifted the boards and lifted the chest out. It was heavier than he remembered, or he was older. He set it down on the hearth and opened the lid.

Inside, wrapped in sacking and an old oilcloth, there was a long, curved shape.

He took it out and unwrapped it on the table, and the light from the window fell across it, and Rowan, in the doorway, made a sound like he'd been punched.

Horns.

A pair of them, black and ridged and curving back on themselves like a ram's, each one longer than a man's forearm. Old now. Dull. Cracked a little at the bases, where they'd been cut. But there was no mistaking them, or what they'd been, or the head they'd come from.

"Gods," said Rowan. "Is that—"

"The general's," said Ars.

He didn't look at them for long. He'd seen them enough, seven years ago, on the Redmarch, in the smoke, on the morning after. He'd stood over what was left of the general and done this without thinking, without deciding, the way you do things in a dream: taken the horns off, carried them back across the red ground, put them in a chest and put the chest under the floor and never once taken them out until today.

He'd never known why he kept them. He thought, standing here now, that maybe he'd always known he'd need them for something.

"If they're counting," he said, "let them count these."

Rowan stared at him.

"Send them to Halvard," said Ars. "Tell him to hang them where the scouts are looking. Over a gate. Somewhere they'll see." He began wrapping the horns again, carefully, in the oilcloth. "They'll know what they are. Every demon on the Redmarch knows what these are."

"Ars." Rowan's voice was very quiet. "That'll tell them you're alive."

"That's the point."

"It'll tell *everyone* you're alive."

"Everyone already knows I'm alive, Rowan." He tied the last cord. "They just don't know where." He held the bundle out. "It's not me coming back. It's just a message. *I'm still here. Think about it before you come.*"

Rowan took the bundle in both arms. It was long and awkward, and he held it like something that might go off.

"And if they come anyway?" he said.

Ars looked out of the window, past him, at the lake.

"Then they come anyway," he said. "And we'll see."

***

He didn't tell Alea about the horns. She was at the guild, she'd said, which meant she was at the militia hall in Wrenhaven sparring with PenguChi and eating supper with five girls from another life. She'd taken the basket with her. Two fish, as always, wrapped in wet leaves.

When she came home that night, the basket was empty, and her face had that look on it again.

He'd been seeing it for weeks. Since the end of summer, maybe. A sort of careful heaviness, as if she were carrying something very full and very fragile and was afraid of spilling it. She'd sit by the fire mending her tunic and stop halfway through a stitch and stare at nothing. She'd start to say something at supper and then not. Twice now she'd said *Dad* in that particular voice, the one that meant something important was coming, and twice she'd looked at his face and said something else instead.

He didn't ask. He'd decided that weeks ago, and he hadn't changed his mind.

She was sixteen. She was clever, and kind, and stubborn, and she'd made five friends from a world she'd only ever heard about in bedtime stories. Whatever she was carrying, she'd picked it up herself, and she'd set it down herself when she was ready. If he reached out and took it from her, she'd never learn how.

So he just said, "Good day?"

And she said, "Mm. Fine. Somebody hit me in sparring. Properly. First time."

"Good for them," said Ars.

Alea glanced at him, quickly, the way she did when she thought she might have said too much. Then she turned away and hung up her coat. "It was," she said. "It was really good."

He didn't ask who. He didn't need to. He'd felt it that afternoon, from the garden, with his hands in the soil: one of the five small candles up on the hill, the slow steady one on the hard path, flaring for a moment, the way aura flares the first time it lands. And beside it another, brighter than all the rest, burning so close to the edge of something that he'd stopped digging to feel it.

Alea looked at him for a moment, with that heaviness in her face, and he waited, and she didn't say it.

"Night, Dad," she said instead, and went to bed.

He sat by the fire for a while after she'd gone, not reading the book on his knee. Then he got up, and took the net that needed mending, and went down to the jetty in the dark.

***

He'd been doing it most nights now. He couldn't have said why. The nets didn't need mending that often. But there was something about the end of the jetty at night, with the cold coming off the water and the stars coming out above the black line of the hills, and his hands working by feel, knot after knot.

Something in it settled him. Something he hadn't known needed settling until it did.

Tonight was very still. No wind. The lake lay flat and black as glass, and the stars were doubled in it, so that he seemed to be sitting at the edge of a hole in the world, with sky above and sky below. His hands moved in the dark. Pull, loop, through, tighten. Pull, loop, through, tighten.

And the quiet came.

He'd felt it before. A few times, this autumn, out here with the nets. The world going still around him. Not silent; the water still lapped at the posts, and somewhere back in the trees an owl was hunting. But *quiet*, in a way that had nothing to do with sound. As if the lake and the trees and the air and the stars, all of it, had paused for a moment to listen to him tie a knot.

Tonight it didn't pass.

He kept working, and it stayed. It settled over the jetty like snow. It went out across the water and up into the hills and down into the dark under the lake, and everything it touched went still and listened, and he was at the centre of it, tying knots in a fishing net with cold hands.

He wasn't doing anything. That was the strange part. He wasn't pushing his aura out, the way he did in a fight. He wasn't reaching for anything. He was just here, completely, on the end of a jetty in the middle of the night, doing one small ordinary thing as well as he could. And the world had noticed.

He finished the knot.

Then, for the first time in a very long time, he opened his window.

He didn't look at it often anymore. There'd been a time, years ago, when he'd checked it every day, every hour, the way he'd checked his phone in a life he barely remembered: watching the numbers climb through the tiers, Drawn and Tempered and Honed and on and on, hungry for the next one. Then the Redmarch, and the night he'd gone in at the sixth tier and come out at the eighth. And then nothing. For seven years, the number had sat where it was. He'd stopped looking. He'd assumed it was finished.

He looked now.

Forty-one.

He stared at it. It had been forty for six years. He was sure of it. Forty, the morning after the battle, when he'd sat on the red ground beside a grave with his sword planted over it and looked at the number for the last time and thought: *that's it, then. That's as far as I go.* Forty, every time he'd glanced at it since, less and less often, until he'd stopped glancing at all.

Forty-one.

He didn't know when it had changed. This autumn, probably. Some night out here, with the nets.

He sat very still on the end of the jetty, with the quiet all around him and the stars above and below, and understood something he'd heard once, years ago, from an old swordmaster at the garrison, who'd said it the way people say things they don't expect you to understand until you're older.

*Nobody climbs to the ninth tier by fighting. Everybody tries. Nobody does it. You have to find your own way up. And it's never the way you expect.*

He'd thought, at the time, that it meant some secret technique. A trick. A way of holding the sword.

It meant this. Pumpkins, and hens, and two fish in a basket every morning, and a daughter he didn't ask questions of, and the end of a jetty in the dark. A life lived slowly, one knot at a time, as well as he could live it.

He thought, distantly, about what the ninth tier meant. About King Severin, who'd reached it before he was forty and would likely live to see a hundred and twenty. About doubled years. About Alea, who was sixteen, and who would, in the ordinary way of things, outlive him by decades.

Unless she didn't have to.

He closed the window. He didn't want to look at it anymore. If he looked at it, he'd start wanting it, the way he'd wanted every tier before, and the wanting would ruin it. He was fairly sure of that.

He picked up the net instead, and found the next tear by feel, and started on the next knot.

The quiet stayed with him until dawn.

***

In the morning, when he came back from the water, he took the two best fish out of his catch and wrapped them in wet leaves and put them in the basket by the door.

Then, after a moment's thought, he took out a third.

A big one. A lake trout, silver and heavy. He wrapped it with the others.

Five girls, and his daughter, and a winter coming. They'd need it.

Alea came down the stairs as he was straightening up, yawning, her ears flat with sleep, and stopped when she saw the basket. He watched her count the fish. He watched her look up at him.

He didn't say anything. He just went to put the kettle on.

Behind him, after a long moment, he heard her pick up the basket. He heard her stand there with it. He heard her take a breath, as if she were about to say something, and then let it out again.

"Bye, Dad," she said.

"Bye."

The door closed. He stood at the stove with the kettle, listening to her footsteps going away down the lake road toward Wrenhaven, and the hens shouting at her as she went, and the quiet still lingering at the edges of everything, like the last of the mist on the water.

He was still smiling when the kettle boiled.
