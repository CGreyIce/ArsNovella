Higu counted every morning.

She'd always counted. On Earth it had been viewers, and the hours she'd slept, and how many days since she'd last spoken to someone she was worried about. Here, for twenty months, it had been five. Five cots in the upper room of the militia hall. Five bowls at Maren's table. Five people on the market road, the first day, and a fisherman looking at each of them as if he were counting too.

Five, for twenty months. She'd got used to five.

Now she stood on the flat bit of roof above the militia hall kitchen, in the early light, with the whole of Wrenhaven below her and the lake beyond it going from grey to blue, and counted fifteen.

Five, behind her, in the upper room. Thea asleep on her face with a pencil still in her hand. PenguChi already up and gone, down to the shore, with a penguin under one arm. Xola asleep with Squiddles asleep on her. Gigichi asleep and frowning, the way she frowned at everything, even dreams.

Five more in the town. Rachael and Dennis in Rowan's spare rooms at the top of the square, because Rowan had looked at Rachael on the first day and said *the room with the lock* without anybody asking him. Calvin above the bakery with Malcolm and John, because the baker had said *beds* and meant it, and because Calvin had worked out on the second morning that if you slept above a bakery you woke up to bread. RX at the inn, or possibly not; nobody was ever sure where RX had slept. Azac at the inn too, in a bed that was too short for him, with his feet out of the end.

And the rest down at the lake. Gabe and Deston and Xav on the floor of the cabin, still, every night, under a growing heap of blankets that half the town had quietly left on the step. Nobody had suggested they move. Nobody would. Higu had watched Odette carry up a third mattress on the fourth day, look at the three of them asleep in a heap on the floor beside it, and carry it back down again without a word.

Fifteen.

Fifteen of thirty-five, in one small town on one lake at the top of the world. More than a third of them. She'd never once, in twenty months, seen more than five in one place. Nobody had. *We've never once been in one place. Not since the hall.* Drew had written that last autumn, in a letter that went all the way to the king, and it had been true.

It wasn't true anymore.

She wrote it down. She had a little book for this, now, the way she'd had a moon chart last spring. One line every morning.

*Day ten. Fifteen.*

She looked at it. Then, because she was Higu, and Higu was honest with her own books even when it was silly, she added a line under it.

*Sixteen. Seventeen.* And then, after a moment: *Twenty, if you count everyone.*

Alea. And the man on the end of the jetty, who was already there, she could see from here: a dot at the end of a line on the blue water, with a rod. And Emrys and Sabine and Petra, who weren't from Earth but who'd come up a river with them and stood in front of them in a gorge, and who counted.

She didn't know how to count it anymore. She decided she didn't mind.

***

It was a strange town now.

Higu had lived in it for twenty months and thought she knew it. She did know it. She knew which fishwife cheated on the scales and which one cheated the other way, out of kindness, for the widows. She knew where the mill-leat froze first, and which of the Tallow boys was courting the brewer's daughter, and that the baker, Edric, sang hymns to his ovens before dawn and denied it. She knew the town the way you know a room you've lived in: by feel, in the dark.

It was the same town. It had just got bigger round the edges, the way a room does when you open a door into another one you didn't know was there.

Gabe was down at the forge on the shingle every afternoon, the king's hearth, with half the children of Wrenhaven sitting on the stones round the edge of it in a row, watching him the way they'd watched the king. He let them. He let them work the bellows, one at a time, and told them when the heat stopped being angry, and they nodded as if they understood. Rachael had gone up to the old herb-woman at the top of the town on the third day and come back with a basket of dried yarrow and a standing invitation, and now there was a queue outside Rowan's side door in the mornings, people with cuts and coughs and bad backs, which Rowan pretended not to notice and Dennis kept in order with a ledger. Calvin had climbed the mill and the chapel tower and the tallest tree on the lake road, in that order, and had been told off by three separate people, and had started telling the fishermen what the wind would do tomorrow. He was always right. The fishermen had stopped laughing at him by the second week.

And Emrys sang.

That was the strangest thing of all. Every morning, at the chapel at the bottom of the square, the old chanter from the capital who'd come up the river with a broken voice stood in the doorway in his blanket and sang the morning office. Out loud. Clear as anything. And the town, which had had no chanter for eleven years, since the last one died, came and stood in the square to listen. Higu had gone once. She'd stood at the back with Xola and heard him lift the last note up over the roofs like a bird let out of a hand, and Xola had cried, and pretended she hadn't, and Higu had let her.

Every evening they went down to the lake.

All of them. Fifteen, and Alea, and the three who weren't from Earth. Down the lake road at dusk with bread and whatever the town had pressed on them, to the shingle in front of the cabin, where there would be a fire, and the fisherman cooking something in a pot that was too big for one man, and a basket by the step.

On the first evening, Higu had counted the fish in the basket.

Fifteen.

She'd looked at it for a long time. Then she'd looked at him, at the fire, with his back to her, stirring the pot, and said nothing. And he'd said, without turning round, the way he always knew where she was:

"The basket stays fifteen."

"You said five."

"That was then." He'd tasted the pot. "Pass me the salt, Higu."

***

The channel was harder.

It had always been her job. Not officially; nobody had ever asked her. But she'd written the rules, on a scrap of paper on the kitchen table at the militia hall a year ago, and Thea had copied them in and pinned them at the top where everyone saw them first, the way rules had always sat at the top of her old chat on Earth. And ever since, without anybody saying so, she'd been the one who watched it. Who read every line. Who noticed when someone had gone quiet for too long, or was being too loud to hide something, and wrote to them, privately, on paper, the old way, so nobody else had to see.

*Be kind. Everyone here is scared.*

*Bad news: say it plainly. Don't make anyone guess.*

*Good news: say it loudly.*

*Nobody is alone.*

Four rules, on the top of the channel, for thirty-five people. And then the two that weren't written anywhere, that only five people had known.

*Five: don't write her name. Not her dad. Not the fish.*

*Six: we wait for him.*

Five people, keeping two rules. That had been hard enough. Twenty months of it. Of watching thirty other people they loved guess and wonder and grieve in the channel every night, and saying nothing.

Now it was fifteen people keeping rule five. Twenty, with Group 3, who'd been keeping it since the autumn without ever being told it had a number. Twenty people, in a channel of thirty-five, all carefully not writing the same name.

It was like holding a door shut with twenty people leaning on it. It held. But you could hear it creak.

***

The first creak had been Calvin.

The morning after the kettle, before anyone was properly awake, Higu had opened her window out of habit and found, at the very bottom of the channel, one line.

[ 05:52 · Calvin: kettle. ]

That was all. One word. With a full stop.

She'd lain on her cot in the grey light and looked at it. And watched, over the next hour, as the rest of the channel woke up and found it too.

Higu had gone down to the bakery before breakfast and found Calvin at the table under the window with a slice of bread in one hand and the dented kettle in his lap, holding it against him like a cat.

"Calvin," she'd said.

"I know."

"Rule five."

"I know. I *know*." He'd looked up at her with his whole face apologising. "I just woke up and it was there. By the bed. I'd slept with it. And I had to tell *someone*, Higu. I'd had it in my head for three years and then it was *real* and I had to put it somewhere." He'd held the kettle tighter. "I didn't write where. I didn't write who. It's just a kettle."

Higu had looked at him for a long moment.

Then she'd sat down across from him and taken a slice of his bread, and he'd let her.

"It's just a kettle," she'd agreed. "Next time, write it on paper. And give it to me. I'll keep it."

He had. She had a little stack of them now, in the back of the counting book. Calvin's, mostly. One from RX, which was just *HIDDEN* in capitals, three times. One from Rachael, which said *he let me look and it's healing* and nothing else. One from Malcolm, in his small careful hand, which was a single IF and a single THEN, and which Higu had read once and then put away and not read again, because it had made her cry in the militia hall kitchen in front of Maren.

*IF he's up there*

*THEN no more next year.*

And underneath, in different ink:

*He was.*

***

The rest of the channel had noticed.

Of course it had. You couldn't take ten people out of a channel of thirty-five, not really, not even when they kept writing, and not have the other twenty notice. Groups 4 and 6 still wrote. Every evening, when Chris put up his numbers, they were there in the column, all ten, the same as always. Rachael's bar. Calvin's Spark. Deston's. Xav's, Azac's, RX's, John's ninety-nine. They wrote goodnight. They answered when someone asked them something.

They just didn't say anything else. No stories. No places. No *the river was brown today* or *Gabe burned his eyebrows off again*. Not since a line from Xav in the early spring, *see you soon*, and then a long silence, and then, five nights ago, two words from Deston, late, that half the channel hadn't understood and the other half had cried at.

*three parts.*

And Groups 1 and 3, who'd been noisy all winter, had gone strangely, carefully quiet with them.

Higu watched the others work it out. It took about a week.

***

[ 21:00 · Chris: Logging.
21:00 · Chris: Thirty-five. All present.
21:01 · Chris: And the blank one at the end. Still blank. Still there. ]

That was every night. Chris's numbers, in a long column, every one of the thirty-five, Tier and percentage, and then, at the very bottom, a line with no name and no number. He'd put it there in the winter. Nobody had asked him why. Everybody knew.

And then, on the eighth night, under the numbers, Tiger.

Tiger was in Thornwall, in the west, at the foot of the pass, where the absence was. Tiger didn't do subtle. He never had, in his life. He had never once asked a question sideways when he could ask it straight to your face.

The channel went still.

Higu, on her cot in the upper room, with the lamp lit, sat up.

She could feel it. Not with any skill. Just the way you feel a room go quiet. Thirty-four people reading the same four lines, in thirty-four places across a kingdom, and twenty of them going very still, and fourteen of them waiting.

[ 21:09 · Kei: Is everyone okay. That's all I want to know.
21:10 · Jared: They're with him. Aren't they.
21:10 · Jared: It's fine if they are. I'd just like someone to say it.
21:12 · Drew: I need numbers for the bracket by the end of the month. That's my only question. Will everyone be at the Lists at midsummer, yes or no. ]

Across the room, Gigichi had sat up too. Higu felt her look over. She didn't look back. She was reading the channel. She was waiting to see who'd answer, and what, and whether the door would hold.

Nobody from Groups 4 and 6 answered.

That was the thing that made her chest hurt. She could see them not answering. Ten people in a small town at the top of the world, with their windows open, reading Tiger's four lines and Kei's one and Jared's two, and not writing anything, because they'd promised. Because they were holding the door too. Because Rachael and Calvin and Deston and RX, who'd never in their lives kept anything from anyone, had learned in a week how to sit very still in a channel and not say the one thing everyone wanted them to say.

Higu put her hand on the window.

It was her job. It had always been her job.

***

She wrote it slowly. She thought about every line before she put it down, the way she always did. Rule two was hers. She'd written it. *Say it plainly. Don't make anyone guess.* She wasn't going to break her own rule to keep another one.

She just had to find the plain thing that was also true and didn't open the door.

[ 21:18 · Higu: Plainly, then.
21:18 · Higu: Groups 4 and 6 are safe. All ten. Together. Nobody is hurt.
21:19 · Higu: They're nowhere near a pass, a border, or a gorge.
21:19 · Higu: Where they are isn't mine to say. It isn't theirs either. It's someone else's, and they've asked us to let them say it themselves.
21:20 · Higu: Drew: yes. Everyone in Groups 1, 4 and 6 will be at the Lists at midsummer. You can put all fifteen in the bracket.
21:20 · Higu: Tiger: I know. I'm sorry. Rule four. You're not alone in not knowing. It won't be for long. ]

She sat back.

She'd written *fifteen*. She hadn't meant to. She looked at it. It was too late to take it back; you couldn't take anything back in the channel. It was there, in her calm careful hand, at the bottom of the window of every one of the thirty-five.

*All fifteen.*

Not *ten and five*. Not *4 and 6, and us*. Fifteen. As if it were one number. As if it had always been.

She waited.

21:23 · Tiger: ok

Higu read that last line three times. *Everyone*, in capitals. From Tiger, who didn't do subtle. Who, Higu suddenly understood, had just done subtle for the first time in his life, very badly, on purpose.

21:33 · Maigo: THIS IS ME NOT SAYING

Gigichi laughed. Out loud, in the dark, in the upper room. Higu looked over at her, and Gigichi was lying on her back on her cot with her arm over her eyes and her shoulders shaking.

"Your face," said Gigichi. "When you wrote *fifteen*. You went white."

"I didn't mean to."

"I know you didn't." Gigichi took her arm off her eyes. "It's fine, Gu. It's true. That's why it came out." She looked at the ceiling. "Six years I've known you. You've never once written something that wasn't true. Not even by accident."

Higu didn't say anything. She closed her window. She lay back down.

After a while, from across the dark room, Gigichi said, in the voice she used in the channel at two in the morning, when everyone should have been asleep:

"Go to sleep, Higu."

"You go to sleep."

"I'm going. I'm telling you first. Rule one."

"That's not what rule one is."

"It is tonight," said Gigichi.

***

The other letter came two days later.

Not through the channel. On paper, the old way, in a leather case with the Advisor's seal on it, brought up the north road by a royal rider who looked as though he hadn't slept in four days. It was addressed to Maren. Maren read it at the kitchen table in the militia hall, standing up, with her coat still on, and then she put it down on the table, face up, and went and stood at the window and looked down the hill at the lake for a long time.

Higu read it. Maren had left it face up. Maren never left anything face up by accident.

It was short. The Advisor's letters were always short.

*Maren,*

*The infirmary at Kingsferry reports that its summoned healer left at the thaw with her group and their guide and guard, on a salt barge, upriver. The guild at Duskhollow reports that its group went with them, with one of its own swordswomen, against advice. Neither has reported a destination. Emrys has not written to me in a month.*

*I believe I know where they have gone. I would be grateful if you would tell me whether I am right.*

*If I am, you will understand why I am writing. Fifteen of them in one place is what I asked the king for a year to prevent. One target. I have been told, plainly, by someone I do not argue with, that this is no longer my concern. I am writing anyway.*

*Is he awake?*

*H.*

Higu read the last line twice.

*Is he awake.*

She looked up. Maren was still at the window.

"What will you tell him?" said Higu.

Maren didn't turn round.

"The truth," she said. "I always have. It's the only thing I've ever told him." She was quiet a moment. "He asked me, twenty months ago, the first week, what the fisherman was. And I wrote back *a fisherman*. And it was true." She turned. Her face was the face she'd worn for twenty months, the plain hard face of a guide with an order, and under it, for the first time since Higu had known her, something else. "I'll tell him yes. All fifteen. And I'll tell him they're safer here than anywhere in the kingdom."

"Is that true?"

Maren looked at her. Then she looked past her, out of the window, down the hill. At the lake, and the jetty, and the dot at the end of it.

"Ask Kharvos," said Maren. "They tried it last summer. Nine nights before midsummer." She picked up her pen. "One of them. Twice the size of a man. It came over the ridge and sat down in the old orchard on the hill behind this hall, where you can see into the upper windows. Your windows. It watched you for an hour."

Higu went cold.

"It never left the orchard," said Maren. "Rowan burned it, in two pieces, in the morning. And sawed off an apple bough that had been cut through in the same stroke, so that nobody would look at it twice." She looked down the hill. "The lake is more than a mile from that orchard."

Higu hadn't known. None of them had. She thought about last summer: the five of them asleep in the upper room, nine nights before midsummer, and something sitting in the dark on the hill watching their windows, and a man more than a mile away on the end of a jetty who had never once said a word about it.

"And the last line?" said Higu. "*Is he awake?*"

Maren dipped her pen.

"He means the other thing," she said. "Not asleep. *Awake.*" She started to write. "He means: is the man I gave a ditch and a war and a dead woman to, eight years ago, back in the world. Is he going to stand up."

"What will you tell him?"

Maren wrote for a while without answering.

"That he's fishing," she said at last. "And that I've never seen him look less asleep in my life."

***

Higu didn't sleep that night.

Not because she was worried. Because she wanted to see.

She went up on the roof above the kitchen, the flat bit behind the chimney, the way she had on a full-moon night last spring, a year and a lifetime ago, when she'd written one line into the world and a moth had come and sat on her palm. The moon was a little past half tonight. Not full. It didn't matter, now; the moth came anyway, these days, whenever she asked, just smaller and fainter when the moon was thin.

She cupped her hands. She thought of the moon, the way you think of a friend's face. And it came: small, white, soft, a moth of light with broad dusty wings and feathery antennae, sitting weightless on her palm.

"Go and see," said Higu.

It went.

She closed her eyes and went with it. Not her body. Just her sight, silver and black and soft-edged, like an old photograph. Out over the roofs of the town. Over the square, where the forge was cold and the chapel was dark and one window above the bakery still had a candle in it, which would be Malcolm, writing. Over the mill, and the mill-leat, and the north wall. Over the old orchard on the hill behind the hall, where one apple tree stood with a bough gone. Over the fields beyond, the hedges, the north road going away pale into the dark hills.

Nothing on it. Nothing in the fields. No shapes standing too still at the edge of the trees. No threadless faces. She didn't know how she'd know a threadless face from a moth's height, but Deston said you could feel it, the wrongness, and she thought she would.

She sent it round. All the way round the town, slowly, the way she'd have walked a wall. East, over the lake road and the reeds. South, along the shore, past the outflow and the weir. West, up into the hills where the pass road went. North again.

Nothing. Spring. Frogs in the reeds, very loud. An owl. A fox, an ordinary one, trotting along a hedge with something in its mouth, that stopped and looked up at the moth for a moment and then went on.

She brought it back down over the lake.

The water was black and very still. The moon lay on it, half a moon, with a long broken path of light running across the water from the far shore to the near one. She let the moth drift down that path, slowly, low over the water, toward the end of the jetty.

He was there.

Of course he was there. Sitting at the very end, where he always sat. Legs over the water. Rod across his knees. Not fishing. Just sitting, facing out, toward the north end of the lake and the dark hills beyond it, where the north road went, and past the north road, a month's walk east, the Redmarch.

He wasn't asleep.

He was more awake than anything she'd ever seen. That was the only way she could put it, afterwards, in her book. Not tense. Not watchful, the way a guard is watchful, with his shoulders up. Just *awake*, all the way through, the way the lake was awake, the way the night was. As if the whole dark country round the lake were a room, and he was sitting in the middle of it with all the doors open, listening to every one.

The moth came down the path of moonlight toward him.

He looked up.

Not startled. Not surprised. He looked up at the moth the way you'd look up at someone coming in late to a room where you'd been waiting for them, and he smiled, a little, and he nodded. Once.

*Go to bed, Higu*, the nod said. *I've got it.*

She called the moth back.

***

She sat on the roof for a long time afterwards, in the cold, with her blanket round her and her empty palms in her lap.

Twenty months. Twenty months of five cots, and a channel, and a door held shut. Twenty months of looking down the hill at one lamp on the end of a jetty, and not going down the road.

She thought about rule six. *We wait for him.* She'd said it at the kitchen table in the summer, the night of the sum, when the five of them had sat round the lamp and decided not to write what they knew. She'd said it because someone had to, and because it was the only plain true thing there was to say that night. *We wait.*

They didn't have to wait anymore.

He'd come up the road to their door with four fish and a fifth for supper. He'd stood on the end of a jetty and sung a part too loud across the water. He was sitting there now, in the dark, awake, with all the doors open, so that fifteen people from another world could sleep in a small town at the top of the world without anyone keeping watch.

She opened her counting book on her knee, and found the page in the back where she'd written the rules out, once, for herself. All six. The four that everyone had, and the two that were only theirs.

She looked at the sixth for a long time.

Then she took out her pencil, and, very neatly, the way she did everything, she wrote one word beside it.

*Done.*

She looked at it. It was true. It was the plainest true thing she'd written in twenty months.

She closed the book, and went down the ladder, and got into her cot in the upper room of the militia hall, among four people who'd been her whole world for twenty months, in a town that now held fifteen, and twenty, and more than she knew how to count.

For the second time since the summoning, Higu slept before the lamp went out.
