Gabe had the box of hooks under his arm when he went to Harl's.

It was the second morning. He hadn't slept. Nobody had, much; Deston had sat up on the infirmary roof both nights with his eyes loose, looking at threads, and Dennis had sat in the kitchen of the rooms above the chandler's doing his ledger over and over and not writing anything in it, and Calvin had gone out on the river at first light both days, low over the water on his own wind, up and down from the customs house to the river mouth, looking. He'd found Dob the second morning, at the boom by the salt wharf, where the river gave things back. He'd come home and sat on the stairs and not come up them.

They'd burned the hat man on the quay the first morning, on a pyre of rope-walk timber, with the watch standing round. Emrys sang the fire. The body went up like paper, with no smoke, and left a good coat's worth of nothing and a hat. The six from the steps were in the back ward at the infirmary, under the watch, sitting on the benches with their hands in their laps. They ate when they were fed. They didn't speak. Haskett had come down off the *Widow* to look at them, and stood in the door a long time, and said the ferrywoman was Bet Ferrin from Mallow's Ford, who'd taken him across the river when he was a boy; and the boy in the fisher's smock had been on the bank at Mallow's Ford two springs ago, with the hundred, watching the *Widow* go by.

Gabe hadn't slept because when he shut his eyes he was on the waterfront with a beam on his chest and the net sheds behind him and forty families coming out of the smoke, and the steps empty at the bottom of the hill. With nobody on them. Because he'd gone.

So he went to Harl's.

***

Harl was at the forge before him. Harl always was. He'd been at the forge before anybody since Gabe's first winter in Kingsferry, when a man from another world with no idea how to hold a hammer had come in out of the rain and asked if he could watch, and Harl had looked at him with no eyebrows and said *you can pump*. Harl had burned his eyebrows off forty years ago and they'd never come back, and he looked permanently surprised, and he never was.

He looked at the box under Gabe's arm.

"Those the hooks," he said.

"Yeah."

"All of them?"

"Thirty-six." Gabe put the box down on the anvil's edge. A plain wooden box, the kind you'd keep nails in, that he'd kept under his bed for five weeks and opened every morning to put another one in. "One every morning. On the bottom step. Since I started standing there." He looked at it. "And the one in his hand. Dennis brought it up off the stone."

Harl looked in the box.

They were very ordinary. That was the thing about them. Thirty-seven fishing hooks, for pike, bright, new, with barbs like cat's claws, lying in a heap in a nail box. You'd pass them on a stall in the fish market and not look twice. Dennis had looked at every one of them with *True* and said the same thing every morning: the most nothing thing he'd ever seen. Rachael had put them under Maud's lens and found no rust, no maker's mark, no grey. There was nothing wrong with them at all except that every one of them had been left on a step for somebody to pick up, and some of them had been, and Maud had.

"What d'you want to do with them," said Harl.

"Melt them," said Gabe.

Harl looked at him for a long time with his permanently surprised face.

"All right," he said, and went to the bellows.

***

They went into the crucible like any iron.

Gabe had half thought they wouldn't. He'd half thought they'd lie in the white heat and stay bright and hooked and wrong, the way the black sword had folded a chisel over at the lake. They didn't. They went soft at the barbs first, the way small things do, and then the bends, and then they slumped together into a little heap that glowed, and then it wasn't hooks. It was iron. A thumb's worth of it, maybe two, white and running at the bottom of the crucible like any iron Gabe had ever melted.

"Huh," said Harl.

"It's just iron," said Gabe.

"It was always just iron," said Harl. "Whoever made them didn't put anything in the iron. They put it in the shape." He worked the bellows. "Take away the shape and there's nothing there."

Gabe stood with the tongs in his hand and looked at it for a long time.

He didn't know what he was going to make. He'd thought he'd just melt them and pour it on the floor and stand on it. That had seemed like enough, at three in the morning. But it was running in the crucible now, white, ordinary, two thumbs of good iron with nothing in it; and he was a smith. You didn't pour good iron on the floor.

"A bell," he said.

Harl looked at him.

"For the ward door." Gabe didn't know where it had come from. "She always said. Rae says it to all of them. *If it goes funny, come at night. Bang on the door. Sister Maud doesn't sleep anyway*." He swallowed. "She doesn't now. Sleep. Anyway. So somebody'll have to hear the door. And Tamsin sleeps like a dead man." He looked at the iron. "A bell. On the door. So anybody can ring it."

Harl didn't say anything for a while.

Then he went to the back, where the moulds were, and came out with a small one, sand and clay, the size of a fist, for a ship's bell he'd never been paid for; and set it in the floor, and held out his hand for the tongs.

"Your pour," said Harl. "Your bell."

***

Maud was buried on the third day.

In the healers' ground on the hill behind the infirmary, where the river-gate sisters had been buried since before the customs house, under a yew. It wasn't a big place. There were forty-one stones in it, small ones, grey, with names. Sister Merewen's was near the top, by the yew's trunk, old and green and hard to read.

Half of Kingsferry came.

Gabe hadn't expected that. He'd thought it would be the ward and the order and them. But they came up Tanner's Row from eight in the morning, in their good coats and their working ones: the rope-walk families with the soot still in the cracks of their hands; the fish market, the gutting girls and the porters and the women with the baskets; the lightermen off the north quay; Ned Webb's mother with Ned, his new arm in a sling he kept taking out to show people; the river-gate watch, the ones who were left, and Sergeant Coyle in front of them with his head bandaged and his face the colour of putty, standing because he'd been told not to. The harbourmaster. A man from the council. Haskett, down off the *Widow*, with his boys. And Yeva, from the north quay, on a stick, on her own, who stood at the back by the wall and didn't come any nearer.

Everybody Maud had ever told off.

The sisters of the order buried her, the way they always did, with the order's words. Gabe didn't understand most of them; they were old, older than the kingdom's ordinary talk, the way the guides' chants were. And then they stood back and waited, because there was a thing that was done, at the river-gate ground, when a healer was buried; and somebody from the ward had to do it.

Rachael went forward.

She was in a black dress that wasn't hers, Sister Wenna's old one, that had stayed in the press when Wenna went west, and it was too long and she'd pinned it up. She was very pale. She hadn't been warm since the steps; she'd been wrapped in Gabe's coat for three days over everything else she wore and her lips were still a little blue at the edges. Her hands were in the sleeves. She hadn't been able to mend anything. She'd tried, every morning, on Tamsin's burned thumb or a cut on Ned's mother's hand, and every morning there'd been nothing, and she'd taken her hands away and looked at them and said *right* and gone to do something else.

She knelt by the grave.

She took the needle case out of the coat. The black horn one, forty years old, older, that had come out of Gabe's pocket on the landing and that Emrys had picked up off the stone and held in both hands all the way up Tanner's Row. She opened it with its little dry click. Eleven needles on the red felt.

She took out one of them. A plain steel one, a middle size, worn thin and a little bent, darker than the others with years of handling. Maud's. The one she'd used most; you could see it.

She put it in Maud's hands. Under the linen, where they were folded on her chest, between the fingers. Carefully. The way you'd give somebody back a thing they'd lent you.

Then she shut the case with the ten that were left, and put it back in Gabe's coat, and stood up.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to; that was the thing that was done. Gabe found out later from Tamsin that every healer at the river gate for as long as anybody remembered had gone into the ground with one needle from the ward, and the rest stayed. *So the ward keeps sewing*, Tamsin said. *And so they've got something to do with their hands*.

Emrys sang.

He hadn't been asked. He stood at the foot of the grave in his old court coat with his ruined voice, three days after the steps, and sang the tune. The one from the ring. No words. Slow, steady, like a heart at rest, the kind of tune you'd hum to a child who couldn't sleep. He'd sung it for three hours on the stone with the moon going down so that a girl could keep her hands in time, and he sang it now at the edge of a grave on a hill with half of Kingsferry standing in the grass, and his voice cracked on every other note, and he didn't stop.

At the back, by the wall, Yeva took her hand off her stick and put it over her mouth.

***

He didn't know where to stand, after.

That was the thing. He'd known where to stand for four years. In front of her, mostly. On the *Widow*, at the Narrows, with a slab of ice the size of a cart coming at the stern. On the waterfront at the night market the first winter, between her and a man turning his hat. On the steps, five weeks, every night, with his back to her window. He'd always known exactly where to put himself. It was the one thing he'd ever been sure he was for.

And he'd left the steps.

She found him on the infirmary roof at dusk. He'd gone up because Deston had been up there both nights and it seemed like a place you went. He sat on the ridge with his burned arm on his knee in Tamsin's dressing, which was a proper one, linen and salve and a lot of tutting; the arm would scar, Tamsin said, all down the outside from the wrist to the elbow, because there was nobody to mend it and wouldn't be for weeks. He didn't mind. He'd never minded scars. He was looking down the hill at the steps.

She came up the ladder slowly. He heard her stop at the top. He didn't turn round.

"Three," said Rachael.

He knew what she meant. He moved along the ridge till he was three paces off. She came and sat on the slates, with the coat round her, and her knees up, and her hands in the sleeves.

They looked at the steps together for a long time.

"I left them," said Gabe.

She didn't say anything.

"Every night. Five weeks. You know why I was there. I was there so that if they came, they'd come through me. I said that. To Deston. *They'll have to come through me*." His voice was doing the thing. He let it. "And the rope walk went up and I went. I didn't even think. I just went. Because there were people in the sheds, and I can't—I've never been able to stand still while a house is—" He stopped. "And they knew. They knew I would. That's why they lit it. It's the rise again. Somebody lights a fire where you can see and you go. That's me. That's what I am."

"Forty families," said Rachael.

He looked at her.

"In the net sheds," said Rachael. "Forty families. Ned's mother's sister's in the third one. With four children. The beam you took on your chest was the door beam of the third shed." She didn't look at him. She looked at the steps. "Tamsin did the burns at the bottom of the Row after. Fifty-two. Nobody died at the fire. Not one. Calvin turned the smoke and you held the beam and they all came out." She was quiet. "If you'd stayed on the steps, they'd still have taken Maud. Off the step. On Thursday. She had it in her palm four days before you left anything." Her voice did something and she put it back. "And forty families would have been in the sheds when the roof came in."

"It was a trap."

"Yes," said Rachael. "And you went into it and got fifty-two people out. That's both." She looked at her hands in the coat sleeves. "It's always both. Maud said. A hand that closes and a hand that opens. You were both. You did the right thing and they used it. That doesn't make it the wrong thing." She was quiet. "It makes them very good at this. That's all."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"Declan," he said. "On the rise. He went. They lit the fires and he went."

"Yes."

"And it was a trap. And he went anyway. And he got there." Gabe looked at his burned arm. "Half."

"Half," said Rachael.

The sun went down behind the customs house. The steps went grey, then dark. Somebody had washed them; the whole fish market had come with buckets the morning after and scrubbed the landing on their knees until there wasn't a mark of the ring left, and then gone over it again with sand. Nobody had put a hook on the bottom step since. Nobody had put anything.

"Two," said Rachael.

He looked at her.

"Two paces," she said. Not looking at him. "You can sit at two."

He moved along the ridge. One pace. He sat at two.

They sat there till it was dark. And at some point, he didn't know when, she put one hand out of the coat sleeve, cold, white, the fingers that couldn't mend anything, and took hold of his sleeve. The sleeve of his good arm, at the wrist. Not his hand. His sleeve. The way she'd held Calvin's in the spring for a breath on the stairs and said *don't make it a thing*.

She held on.

He didn't make it a thing. He sat on the infirmary roof with a woman holding his sleeve in the dark, two paces off, and looked at the steps, and let her.

***

The letter came the fourth morning.

By hand. A boy on a horse that had been ridden into the ground, a big bay from the post-house at Wrenhaven, lathered to the knees; the boy had come down the post road in two days and a night and asked at the river gate for the healer, and been sent up the hill, and stood in the infirmary yard swaying and holding it out. *Kingsferry, the river-gate infirmary, by hand.* And under that, in a hand Gabe knew better than his own, the other word.

*Rae*.

She read it in the yard, standing up, with the boy sitting on the mounting block drinking water and Tamsin seeing to the horse. Gabe watched her read it. He couldn't see it. He didn't need to see it; he knew the hand from the letter in the spring: the English, every letter a little careful, like a man writing his own language for the first time in thirty-eight years. He watched her read it twice. Then she held it out to him.

He looked at her.

"He'd want you to," she said. "It's not all to me."

He took it.

*Rae,*

*I read it all. Every line, as it came. I sat at the near end of the jetty with Gigi and I counted. I didn't come.*

*Nobody has ever done what you did. I want you to know I know that. On the rise there was nobody in the world who could. There is now. It's you.*

*She was herself. That's all of it. That's the whole thing. I'd have given anything I had for that once, and there wasn't anything to give.*

*You'll be cold a long time. Let them be warm near you. You don't have to do anything about it.*

*Tell Dennis he made it true. Tell Emrys I'd like to hear the tune one day. Tell Dest thank you for writing it down as it came. He knew who was reading.*

*Tell Gabe he went to the fire. Tell him I know why. They counted on him going. They didn't count on him coming back.*

*He came back.*

*D.*

Gabe read it three times.

Then he gave it back to her, and went and stood at the far side of the yard with his back to everybody and his face to the infirmary wall, and stood there for a long time, wet and loud and too much, and didn't make a sound.

***

She wrote it that night. All of it. For the channel.

He sat with her while she did it, at Maud's table by the window at the end of the long ward, with the lamp turned low, because that was where she wanted to sit. Two paces. She couldn't do anything with her hands but this. She'd said so, at supper: *I can't mend. I can write. Somebody else is going to have to do it next time, and they're going to need to know how*. So she wrote it. Not in the channel straight off. On paper, first, the infirmary's rough grey dose paper, in her small exact hand, the one she kept her anatomy in; the way she'd have written up a case for Maud. Step by step. Every stroke.

It took her four hours.

He watched her do it. She didn't hurry. She wrote what the grey was, the word, as Thea had read it, and where it started: always in the palm, always from a hook, always waiting. She wrote how you could tell. She wrote the method, the burning, Maren's and Emrys's, for when it was only in the skin. She wrote what you couldn't do, which was close over it; she wrote *everything you close over you close in* and underlined it twice, and wrote *Ned* beside it in the margin, small.

And then she wrote the steps.

What a turning felt like under your hands. The word and the pour, both at once. That you had to open where it was poured and close behind, a stroke at a time, both hands, and that you couldn't do it fast and you couldn't do it alone. That you needed somebody who could hear where the next stroke was; she wrote *Dennis* and then crossed it out and wrote *somebody who can hear lies, or read them*, and then wrote *Thea?* in the margin. That you needed somebody to keep the time, so your hands didn't have to think. That you needed somebody to bring you warmth, along a thread, or you'd run out halfway, and halfway was worse than not starting. That you needed somebody between you and whatever was pouring, who would hold its hand off you if it let go.

That it cost everything you had. That it would be weeks before you could mend a cut finger.

That the one in the middle could come back to herself.

That she might not stay.

She stopped there for a long time. The pen sat on the paper.

Then she wrote: *It works. It's the only thing anybody's ever found that does. It doesn't always save them. It always gives them back. Do it anyway.*

And she copied it into the channel. All of it. Line by line, at two in the morning, from the grey paper, at the window, with the lamp turned low and the steps dark at the bottom of the hill.

He took the bell down to the ward door in the morning.

It was a small thing. Iron, plain, the size of a fist, a bit rough at the lip where the mould had been cheap. Harl had done the clapper. Gabe had done the rest, and filed it, and hung it on a bracket he'd bent himself, and he put it up on the doorpost of the river-gate infirmary at seven in the morning with four nails and a short length of rope off the north quay for a pull, low down, where a child could reach it.

Dennis came out and looked at it.

"Is that them," he said. "The hooks."

"It's iron," said Gabe. "Harl says it was always just iron. They put it in the shape."

Dennis looked at the bell for a long time with his eyes doing the thing. Then he reached out and took the rope, and pulled it. Once.

It rang. Not loud. A plain small note, a bit flat, that went down Tanner's Row in the morning and came back off the net sheds.

Dennis stood there with the rope in his hand and listened to it.

"Huh," he said.

"What?"

"It doesn't ring," said Dennis. He looked at Gabe. "In here." He touched his own ear. "*True*. Every bell I've ever heard since the Lists has rung twice. Once out loud and once in here, cracked or not cracked. This one just rings. Out loud. That's all." He let go of the rope. "It's the first thing I've heard in two years that's just a bell."

Gabe looked at it.

Up at the window at the end of the long ward, Rachael was at Maud's table with the dose book open in front of her and a pen in her hand she couldn't feel very well, writing tomorrow's doses in her small exact hand under the last line in Maud's. She'd heard the bell. She looked down at the door, and at them. She didn't wave.

She held up one hand. Two fingers.

*Two*.

He went up and sat at two.
