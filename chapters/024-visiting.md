"This," said Hisui, from somewhere around Kei's left wrist, "is a terrible idea."

"I know," said Kei.

"You're going up a mountain. After the people who went up a mountain and never came down. With an old woman with one eye and a man who draws maps."

"And Hyvred."

"And a girl with horns who can see things that aren't there. Wonderful. Very reassuring." The green snake shifted his coils, tightening very slightly. "If I die up there, I'm haunting you."

"You can't haunt me. You're already in my head."

"Then I'll be *louder*."

Kei sighed and kept climbing.

***

Nobody else could hear Hisui. Three months in, Kei had stopped trying to explain it.

On the first day, in the summoning hall, he'd thought he was going mad. He'd woken up on cold stone with a small green snake wrapped around his wrist and a voice in his head saying *well, this is a disaster*, and he'd spent the whole of the Advisor's briefing trying to work out whether he'd had a stroke. By the end of the first week, he'd accepted that he hadn't. By the end of the first month, he'd accepted that he was going to spend the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be, sharing his head with the most opinionated reptile in two worlds.

He'd never have admitted it out loud, but he'd have been lost without him.

Hisui could taste the air. That was his gift, and he was unbearably smug about it. He'd flick his tongue out and *know*: what had passed by, what was growing, what was rotting, what was safe and what would kill you. He'd tasted his way round every corner of Thornwall in the first month, and every plant in the valley in the second, and by now Kei could name every herb and fungus within a day's walk of the fortress, which ones would close a wound and which ones would stop a heart, and which ones, alarmingly often, would do both depending on how much you used.

"Poison and medicine," Hisui had told him, very early on, "are the same thing. The difference is the dose. And the intent."

It was the most useful thing anyone had told Kei since he'd arrived. He'd built his whole path on it. Fifteen percent, now. Slow, but steady.

***

There were five of them on the sheep path above Coldwater: Hild in front, with her sword drawn and her head tilted, listening; then Corvin, pale and tight-lipped, with his satchel of maps clutched under his arm; then Hyvred; then Kei; and at the back, because Hild had insisted, a pair of soldiers from the Thornwall garrison, who did not look happy to be there.

The others had stayed behind. Bee had argued furiously to come, and lost. Tiger had argued harder, and lost faster. Drew had been the one to tell them both no, gently, in his voice that made people listen. "Somebody has to be there when they come back," he'd said. "And somebody has to write the letters if they don't." Bee had stopped arguing after that.

It was a cold, clear afternoon in the fifth month. The path went up through scrub and bracken and grey rock, steep and narrow, worn by sheep and, lately, by other things. Every so often Hild would stop and crouch and look at the ground, and Kei would see what she was looking at. Boot-prints, pressed into the soft earth, all going up. A child's among them.

And the other prints. Long, narrow, too many joints. No heel.

"Nothing's come down," said Hild quietly. "Not on this path. Not in weeks."

"How do you know?" said Corvin.

"I listen," said Hild. "Paths have a sound. This one's only been going one way."

***

The hut was an hour above the village, in a fold of the hill, where an old shepherd's track crossed the path. Stone walls, a turf roof, a door hanging open on one hinge.

Hild stopped thirty paces short of it and didn't move for a long time.

"Hyvred," she said.

Hyvred had already stopped. She was standing very still on the path with her hand on Kei's sleeve. Her left eye, the one with the red crosshair in it, had gone very bright.

"There's nobody in it," she said. "No light. Nobody alive." She swallowed. "But it's... smeared. Inside. The wrong light. Like the doorframes in Coldwater, but more. Lots more."

Hisui had gone rigid around Kei's wrist. His tongue was flickering in and out, very fast.

"What?" said Kei, under his breath.

"I don't know," said Hisui. And it was the first time, in five months, that Kei had ever heard him not know something. "There's something in the air. It's not alive. And it isn't dead." A pause. "I don't like it. I don't have a word for it. Everything has a word for it."

Hild went in first, sword out. Then she called them in.

***

It was full of things.

That was the first thing Kei saw, and the thing he would remember longest. Not the burned floor, not the smell. The *things*. Shoes, lined up neatly against the wall: men's boots, women's shoes, a pair of small ones that could only have belonged to a child. Coats folded on a bench. A shawl. A woman's comb. A belt with a knife still in its sheath. A wooden spoon. A little bag of coins, untouched.

As if a dozen people had come in here, and taken off everything they owned, and folded it, and set it down, and then walked out into the hills with nothing.

"Visiting," said Corvin, very quietly. His voice cracked. "Visiting family."

In the middle of the floor there was a circle.

It had been burned into the packed earth: a ring as wide as Kei was tall, black and glassy, with lines running across it in a pattern that made his eyes hurt. Not a pattern he knew. Not the summoning circle in the hall at Solmere, not anything he'd seen in Corvin's books. Something else. Something that seemed to go *down*, the longer you looked at it, as if the floor had a hole in it that wasn't there.

Hyvred made a small sound and turned away.

"It's full," she whispered. "The circle. It's full of the wrong light. Like someone poured it in." She had her hand over her left eye. "I can't look at it. It's like looking at the sun, except it's dark."

"Don't look, then," said Hild.

Kei didn't look either. He looked at the shoes instead. The small ones. And next to them, on the floor where it had been dropped, a little wooden horse, painted brown, with one leg missing.

He'd seen it before. In Coldwater, in the doorway of the empty cottage, on their last visit. Someone had brought it up here.

He bent and picked it up. He didn't decide to. It just seemed wrong to leave it.

"Kei," said Hisui, very quietly. "Kei. Outside."

***

There was a woman on the hillside.

She was walking along the shepherd's track, a quarter of a mile off, across the slope above the hut. A village woman in a brown dress and an apron, with her hair tied back, walking at an easy, ordinary pace, the way you'd walk to market. She wasn't hurrying. She wasn't looking at them.

"That's the miller's wife," whispered Corvin. "From Coldwater. I've bought flour from her. That's—"

"No," said Hyvred.

She was standing in the hut doorway with her hand pressed hard against her left eye, as if it hurt to keep it open. But she kept it open.

"That's not her," she said. "That's someone wearing her."

Kei looked at the woman on the hill. She looked like a woman. She walked like a woman, almost. But there was something about the way her feet came down, very slightly too carefully, as if she were remembering how. And as he watched, she turned her head, slowly, without stopping walking, and looked straight at the hut.

At Hyvred.

"*Back,*" said Hild. "Inside. All of you. *Now.*"

The thing came out of the bracken on the left.

Kei never really saw it. None of them did, except Hild. It was low and long and fast, the colour of the hillside, and it came at the doorway in a rush, and Hild was already moving to meet it.

And Kei saw, for the first time, what the sixth tier meant.

The light came off Hild's sword the way Group 6 had described Petra's, in the letter they'd sent round to every group after Azac was hurt: running a hand's width past the edge. But it didn't stop there. It *grew*. It came off the blade in a sheet, like water off a weir, and spread, and hardened, and became a wall: a curved shield of pale shimmering light, taller than she was, standing in the air between the doorway and the thing. The thing hit it and stopped as if it had run into stone.

Then the light changed shape again. It drew back into the blade and *out* along it, longer and longer, thinning to an edge that stretched three paces beyond the steel, and Hild swung it once, low and flat, without any hurry at all.

The thing screamed.

It was the worst sound Kei had ever heard. Not an animal sound. Something underneath an animal sound, the way the circle in the hut was something underneath a floor. Black stuff sprayed across the bracken, thick and steaming. The thing went over, and rolled, and was up again, and *running*, away up the hill, dragging one side of itself, faster than anything that hurt so much should have been able to move.

Hild let the light go. It fell off her blade like rain and was gone.

On the hillside above them, the miller's wife had stopped walking. She was standing perfectly still on the shepherd's track, watching.

Then she turned, and went on along the track, at the same easy, ordinary pace, and walked over the brow of the hill, and was gone.

***

Nobody spoke for the whole of the climb down.

Hild walked at the back this time, with her sword drawn and her head tilted, listening to everything behind them. Corvin walked with his satchel clutched to his chest and tears running silently down his face, and didn't seem to notice them. Hyvred walked with her hand still pressed over her left eye. Every so often she stumbled, and Kei caught her elbow.

He had the little wooden horse in his other hand. He hadn't put it down.

"That was Shaped," Hisui said, very quietly, somewhere around the bottom of the path. "What she did. The wall. The edge. That's the sixth tier."

"I know."

"That's what it looks like. At the sixth tier. And it ran away."

Kei didn't answer.

"Kei," said Hisui. "I'm frightened."

It was the first time the snake had ever said that, either.

"Me too," said Kei.

***

The old woman was still in her garden in Coldwater, shelling peas into a bowl, as if she hadn't moved since their last visit. Maybe she hadn't. She looked up when Kei came to the gate, and her face was calm and tired and not at all surprised.

"Afternoon," she said.

"Afternoon." He held out the little wooden horse. "I found this. Up on the hill. I think it belongs to someone from the cottage at the end of the lane."

The old woman looked at the horse for a long moment. Then she put down her bowl, and took it from him in both hands, and turned it over, and touched the place where the leg was missing.

"Little Tam's," she said. "He was always losing it." She was quiet for a while. "Did you see them? Up there? Visiting?"

Kei thought about the shoes, lined up neatly against the wall. The coats. The circle. The miller's wife walking over the brow of the hill.

"No," he said gently. "I didn't see them."

The old woman nodded, slowly, as if she'd known that too. She held the horse in her lap and didn't pick up her peas again.

"I'll keep it for him," she said. "For when he comes back."

***

The Advisor's answer was waiting for them at Thornwall.

It had come by the fast courier while they were gone, and the garrison commander met them at the gate with it, and Corvin broke the seal with hands that wouldn't stop shaking and read it standing in the courtyard in the cold.

He read it twice. Then he sat down on the edge of the well, quite suddenly, as if his legs had stopped working.

"Soldiers," he said. "He's sending soldiers. A full company. West, to Thornwall, by the end of the month. And a mage from the court, Torch-tier, to study the villages." He looked up at them, and his thin earnest face did something complicated. "He believes me. He believes all of it. He says *forgive me for not acting sooner*."

He started to cry again. Openly, this time, with his hands over his face. Two years of reports nobody read, and a hut full of folded shoes, and a letter at last that said *I believe you*.

Hild sat down on the well beside him and put her hand on his back, and didn't say anything at all.

***

That night, Kei wrote to Yan.

He wrote it all down: the path, the hut, the shoes, the circle, the miller's wife walking over the hill, the thing in the bracken, Hild's wall of light. He wrote it plainly, the way it had happened, because that was what they'd promised each other the last night in the castle, and because if he didn't write it down he wasn't sure he'd ever stop seeing it.

At the end, he wrote one more thing.

*Hisui says to tell Suzu he's sorry for everything rude he's ever said about her, and that he's frightened, and that if she's frightened too it's all right.*

*He made me write that. I didn't edit it.*

*He's never asked me to write anything nice before.*

He sealed it, and put it with the others for the morning, and sat by the fire in the long room at the top of the north tower with the two walls of maps: Corvin's on the east wall, with all its pins, and Drew's bracket on the west, half finished. Hisui was a cool weight around his wrist, very quiet. Hyvred was asleep in the big chair with her glasses pushed up into her hair and Bee asleep on the floor beside her, holding her hand. Tiger was awake, staring at the fire. Drew was at the table, writing something, slowly, with his free hand pressed flat against the wood as if to keep it still.

"You all right?" Kei asked him.

Drew looked up. His face was calm. It was always calm. But his eyes weren't, quite.

"Something always goes wrong," he said quietly. "I always say that. I always know it." He looked down at the letter in front of him. "I just never thought it would be this."

Kei didn't have an answer for that.

He sat with them by the fire until dawn, with the snake around his wrist, thinking about a little boy called Tam who was always losing his horse, and an old woman shelling peas in a garden, keeping it for him.
