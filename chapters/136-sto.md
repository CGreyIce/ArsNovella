Gabe had made exactly one blade in his life, and it was a wall.

He hadn't meant it to be. He'd meant it to be a sword. He'd stood at an anvil at the Kingsferry forges all through a winter, under a smith with no eyebrows called Harl, and drawn it and folded it and drawn it again, and quenched it too cold the first time so it cracked, and quenched it again in oil with his hands shaking, and ground it, and fitted it, and at the end of all that he'd held it up in the light of the forge door and looked at it and thought: *that's not a sword*.

Short. Heavy. Single-edged, and broad at the end, so that it looked less like a blade than a slab with ideas. Deston had looked at it and said *it's a butcher's cleaver married a gate*, and everyone at the table had laughed, and Gabe had laughed too, because it was true.

He'd carried it upriver to a lake in the spring and put it in the hands of the one person in two worlds he'd wanted to show it to, and said *name it*.

And Declan had looked at it for a long time, and turned it over, and run his thumb down the spine the way he'd once run his thumb down the spine of a book he liked, and said one word.

*Sto*.

*I stand*.

He'd cut it into the flat by the hilt himself, that evening, by the fire on the shingle, with a knife-point, the letters going over twice where the steel was hard. And under it, smaller, round the curve: *AUDENTES FORTUNA JUVAT*. *Fortune favours the bold*. It had been a thing Declan used to say, back home, in his Discord status for about a year, in the Latin, so that nobody understood it except the four of them who knew about the Latin. Gabe had asked him once what it meant and he'd said *it means do it anyway*.

A king had read the blade with his eyes shut, three days ago, at a forge at the bottom of the Street of Armourers, and said *this is a wall*.

Gabe had gone red to the ears. He was still a bit red, thinking about it.

***

"It's *moved*," said Tiger.

"It hasn't moved."

"It's moved. It was there. Now it's *there*."

"They moved it," said Gabe. "In the night. On rollers. Forty men. You helped."

Tiger looked at him. "I know I helped. I'm saying it's *moved*."

It had moved. It had been in the yard at the north end of the Lists, behind an arch, for two weeks, while Tiger and the Lists' carpenters built it. Last night, after the Banner, after the crowd went home and the sand was raked, forty men with ropes and rollers and Tiger directing them like an anxious mother had hauled it out through the arch onto the great sand and stood it up in the middle, where the mound had been. The mound was gone. They'd carted it away in the dark. Now there was a gate.

A real gate. Gabe had to give Tiger that. Two heavy leaves of oak planking on iron hinges, in a frame of square-cut posts as thick as a man's body, with a short stretch of timber wall going off on either side of it and a fighting step behind, so that seven people could stand on the step and look over the top. Tiger had put an iron cap along the whole crest of it, a strip of black iron nailed down along the top edge, because he'd said *it'll stand longer with iron on it*, and nobody had argued. And across the inside of the two leaves, in four great iron brackets, a bar. Oak. As thick as Gabe's arm.

It stood on the sand in the middle of the Lists like something out of a castle, with nothing on either side of it but air.

"You could just walk round it," said RX, looking at it. RX talked fast; he'd been talking fast since the hall. "Like, you could literally just walk round it. It's a gate in the middle of nowhere. There's no wall. You could walk *round*."

"The champions aren't *allowed* to walk round," said Tiger, with great dignity. "It's the *rules*. They have to come *through*."

"That's a very specific rule."

"*Drew* made it."

"Drew makes very specific rules," agreed RX.

***

The Gate went in the afternoon, like the Banner had. Five squads, one at a time, in the order Wil had pulled out of the helmet at breakfast. Six glasses was the most anyone could hold: an hour and a half. If the champions got the gate open before the sand ran out, the hold was over. Most glasses wins.

And the champions were champions.

Lucan had found them. Six of them. Old champions of the Lists, from the last twenty years, who'd won on that sand at midsummer and been crowned in front of forty thousand people and gone home to wherever champions go. Lucan had written to every one he could find. Six had come. They were standing at the far end of the sand now, under the west stand, in a loose row, the way the guards had stood for the Banner, and the crowd was roaring at them by name.

Ser Garret Holm, three times champion, the broad grey man who'd bowed to Alea after round one. A tall woman called Dagny Rook who'd won with an axe and still looked like she could. A white-haired man called Marten Cray who was nearly seventy and had won the lances before anyone in the stands was born. A big cheerful man called Osric with a quarterstaff. Two brothers, Bel and Tobin Harrow, who'd won the pairs three years running a decade ago and who apparently hadn't stopped finishing each other's sentences since.

Wood. No aura. Drew's rules again. But champions didn't need aura to take a gate.

They had a ram.

It was a log. A short thick pine log, slung on ropes, with handles cut into it, that the six of them could carry between them at a run. Lucan had had it made. *It's a gate*, he'd said, at breakfast, delighted, when Tiger went white. *You can't take a gate without a ram. It's tradition*.

"There's no tradition," Tiger had said. "There's never been a gate before."

"There's a tradition *now*," Lucan had said.

***

The Committee went first.

They'd learned. Gabe had to give them that too. They'd sat at the long table at the Broken Lance last night with the Banner's one glass and a quarter still ringing in their ears and argued until midnight about who'd call, and at about a quarter to midnight Thea had said *Yan*, and everybody had looked at Yan, and Yan had said *okay*, and that had been that.

Yan called. Slowly. Deeply. About half a second late every time, so that the six champions came over the gate faster than he could say where they were. But everybody listened to him. That was the difference. The Committee held two glasses. Hyvred saw the ram coming before anyone else did and screamed about it, and Thea took one of the champions' wooden swords apart in the air with something she wrote, which Lucan said afterwards he wasn't sure was legal, and Chris logged it all out loud, and in the end Osric got over the left-hand wall and lifted the bar from inside while Yan was saying *left*.

The crowd laughed again. But it was a fonder laugh, this time.

Wolf held two and a half. Except Newish held three, with Newish slowing the ram so much that it hit the gate like a cushion and the Harrow brothers dropped it on their own feet. The Kettle held three and a half, Calvin's dented kettle tied to the gate as a mascot, Azac narrating the whole thing from the fighting step, Jared standing in front of the bar.

Then the Stronghold.

***

They came out of the tunnel under the east stand at the end of the afternoon, with the sun going gold over the west stand and the whole bowl of the Lists full and roaring.

Gabe went out last but one. He didn't know why. They'd just come out that way. PenguChi first, small and calm, with her penguin walking at her heels like a dog, the only one of them who'd been Drawn for more than a week, apart from Salty and RX. Then Salty, short and broad, with the king's father's hammer in his belt and a bag of oak wedges over his shoulder, because he'd said *if there's a bar, there'll be wedges, and if there's wedges somebody needs to hit them*. Then Tiger, at a run, who turned a full circle on the sand with his arms up. Then RX. Then Milli, with a light curved sword on his hip, Drawn since the middle of the morning, walking like somebody who'd just found out he had a different body and hadn't got used to it yet.

Then Gabe. With *Sto* on his back.

And Ina.

Ina came out last, a long way behind the rest of them, and slowly. On purpose. Gabe saw the crowd see him. The oni in the long indigo coat with the wisteria on the hem and the gold vines and the torn red half-cape on one shoulder and the purple stole on the other, walking out onto the great sand of the Lists at the pace of a man taking the air in a park. With his hands behind his back. And the grey fanged mask hanging at his shoulder by its red cords, grinning at the sand.

He didn't go to the gate. He walked out into the middle of the sand, on his own, and stopped.

And turned toward the north stand.

***

Gabe knew the story. He'd been there for it, two days ago, at the opening. A knot of men in grey and blue livery, high on the north side, where the visitors sat, shouting *demon*. And a boy with a helmet on the front tier of the east stand holding up his horns until the whole city stood up and the shouting stopped.

The men in grey and blue were still there. Gabe could see them. A little apart from everyone else, now, in a section that had somehow got a bit emptier round them since the first day. One of them was still wearing the paper crown of horns a farmer had put on his head. He didn't seem to have noticed.

Ina stood in the middle of the sand and looked up at them.

Then he bowed.

A gentleman's bow. A deep, slow, graceful bow, from the waist, with one hand on his chest and the other out to the side, the long indigo coat swinging, the way you'd bow at the end of a dance to someone you'd enjoyed very much. To the north stand. To the men in grey and blue. As if they'd stood up and cheered him.

The north stand didn't know what to do.

Gabe watched it not know. The whole section went very still. The men in grey and blue looked at each other. The man in the paper crown put his hand up to his head, as if he'd only just realised it was there.

Ina straightened up.

He reached up, with one gloved hand, and took the mask off his shoulder. And put it on. Slowly. Tying the red cords behind his head. The grey oni face, with its red-rimmed eyes and its wide fanged grin, settling over his own face. The demon's face. The one everybody in the city looked away from.

He looked up at the north stand through it.

And the north stand went quiet.

Not booing. Not cheering. Just quiet. The way a room goes quiet when somebody walks into it who knows exactly how they look and has decided to look like it on purpose.

Then Ina turned round, with his hands behind his back again, and walked, at the pace of a man taking the air in a park, to the gate, and up onto the fighting step, and stood beside Gabe.

"Hello, Gabe," he said, from behind the mask. In his warm British garden-party voice. "Lovely day for it."

Gabe stared at him.

"Is he always like this?" he said, to nobody.

"Yes," said PenguChi, beside him. Very calmly.

***

"*Turn the glass*," said Drew, from the master's chair.

The six champions came across the sand.

Not at a walk, this time. Not at a jog. At a run, from the start, with the ram slung between them on its ropes, the way they'd learned to come at it over four squads and four holds and four rounds of the crowd laughing at them when they dropped it. They'd had all afternoon to learn the gate. They knew it now. Gabe could see it in the way they came. They'd learned exactly where the ram had to hit to make the bar jump in its brackets.

"*Steady*," said PenguChi.

She didn't shout it. She said it, the way she said everything, very calm, standing on the end of the fighting step with her penguin at her feet. And Gabe felt it go into him. He'd heard about it in the channel: PenguChi's voice, the thing she could do that wasn't magic, that steadied people. He'd never felt it. It went into him like a hand on the back of his neck, and his heart, which had been going much too fast, slowed down.

The ram hit the gate.

The whole thing shook. The oak leaves bowed in toward them on their hinges, and the bar jumped in its brackets with a crack like a whip, and the fighting step shook under Gabe's boots, and dust came off every plank. In the stands forty thousand people screamed.

The gate held.

"*It held*," said Tiger. "*It HELD*—"

"*Wedges*," said Salty.

He was already down off the step, behind the gate, at the bar, with his bag of oak wedges and the king's father's hammer. He took a wedge out of the bag and set it in the gap between the bar and the left-hand bracket, where the ram had made it jump, and hit it. Once. With the old small cross-peen hammer with the burn on its haft.

It went in like butter.

"*Good hammer*," said Salty, to nobody, and set the next one.

***

They came over the top in the second glass.

The champions had learned that too. You didn't just ram a gate. You rammed it, and while everyone on the step was holding on, you sent somebody over the top. Osric and the younger Harrow, Tobin, came at the right-hand wall with Bel boosting them, and Tobin was over the iron crest and half onto the fighting step before Tiger saw him.

Tiger didn't see him. Tiger was somewhere else. Tiger was always somewhere else; that was the point of Tiger. He was the fastest of the thirty-five, and he'd been running up and down the fighting step since the first glass turned so fast that the champions couldn't tell how many of him there were.

RX saw him.

RX had been on the step, at the right-hand end. And then he hadn't. Gabe had looked away for one breath, at the ram, and when he looked back RX simply wasn't there. He'd pulled his aura in. That was his thing. Not out, like everyone else's, but in, all of it, until he was nearly impossible to sense, until your eyes went to the place he was standing and found nothing there and moved on. *Unseen*. It was his skill. He'd had it since the autumn.

Tobin Harrow came over the top of the right-hand wall onto the fighting step and found himself standing next to RX, who hadn't been there a moment ago, and who tapped him very lightly on the back of the neck with the flat of a blunted dagger.

"*Out*," said the marshal.

Tobin looked at RX. RX looked back, already talking. "Sorry. Sorry. I was there the whole time. I'm always there. People just don't look. It's a whole thing. It's my thing." He was already gone again. "Back to the line, they'll let you back in, it's fine, it's legal, Lucan said—"

Tobin went back down the wall, looking dazed.

"Did you see him?" said Gabe, to Milli.

"No," said Milli, who'd been Drawn for six hours and could see things he'd never seen before. "And I was *looking*."

***

The third glass, they came over the top all at once.

All six of them. Leaving the ram on the sand. Four at the left-hand wall and two at the gate itself, climbing the planks of the leaves, champions of the Lists going up a gate like boys up an orchard wall, with wooden swords in their teeth. Gabe saw Ser Garret Holm's face come over the iron crest, very close, grey and broad and grinning.

And Ina wrote a word.

Gabe saw him do it. Ina was standing on the fighting step at the middle of the gate, with his mask on and his hands behind his back, and he hadn't moved once in three glasses. He hadn't needed to. Now he took one hand from behind his back. Gloved. Black. And put out one finger, and wrote in the air in front of him, very small, very neat, in English, one word.

*Hot*.

It hung there for a moment in violet fire.

Then it ran.

It went off the end of his finger and along the air and onto the iron cap Tiger had nailed along the top of the gate, and it ran along the iron. Both ways. Left and right. The whole length of the crest of the gate, and along both walls, all the way to the ends, a line of violet fire, wisteria-coloured, rippling, so bright that the champions coming over the top of it stopped dead with their hands on the iron.

Not burned. Gabe saw that. They'd stopped before it could. Their hands were on the iron and the fire was a finger's width from their fingers, rippling, and every one of them had gone absolutely still, the way you go still when you put your hand on a stove and realise, just in time, that it's lit.

Three champions. Ser Garret, and Dagny Rook, and the white-haired Marten Cray. Hanging off the top of Tiger's gate with their wooden swords in their teeth and their fingers a hair from violet oni-fire, not moving.

Ina didn't look at them.

He looked down at his own glove. At the cuff of it, at his wrist, where it had come a little loose when he lifted his hand. And very carefully, unhurriedly, with the other hand, he fixed it. Pulled it straight. Smoothed it. Like a man at a garden party adjusting his gloves before shaking hands with a duchess.

Then he looked up, at last, at Ser Garret Holm, three times champion of the Lists, hanging off the gate a foot from his face.

"I should probably mention," said Ina, from behind the oni mask, in his best and warmest and most British voice, the voice of a man pointing out to a guest that the soup is very slightly too warm, "that it's hot."

There was a pause.

Ser Garret Holm took the wooden sword out of his teeth. He looked at the violet fire on the iron. He looked at Ina.

Then he laughed. A big, delighted, champion's laugh. And let go of the gate, and dropped back down onto the sand, and the other two dropped with him, and the forty thousand people in the Lists made a sound Gabe had never heard a crowd make, a kind of enormous, collective, astonished *oh*, and then the roar.

The violet fire on the crest of the gate went out. Not all at once. Slowly, from the ends inward, the way a line of lamps goes out along a street at dawn.

Ina put his hand behind his back again.

"*Is he always like this?*" said Gabe again. He couldn't help it.

"*Yes*," said PenguChi.

***

The fourth glass, the bar cracked.

The champions had gone back to the ram. They'd decided, Gabe guessed, that if they couldn't go over the gate, they'd go through it, and they came at it together with the pine log on its ropes, running, six of them, the old and the young, the axe-woman and the staff-man and the twins, with everything they had, and hit it.

The whole gate jumped.

Gabe heard the bar go. A sound like a branch breaking in frost, very loud, right behind him, and he turned and saw it: the oak bar across the leaves, as thick as his arm, split. Down the middle. Lengthwise, along the grain, from the left-hand bracket nearly to the right. Salty's wedges had held it in the brackets. Nothing was holding it together.

"*Salty*—"

"I see it," said Salty. He was at the bar with the hammer already, but there was nothing to hit. You couldn't wedge a split. "It'll go on the next one."

The champions were backing off across the sand with the ram, for the run-up. Gabe could see them. They'd heard it too. They knew.

"*It'll go*," said Tiger. He'd stopped running for the first time in four glasses. He was standing on the fighting step looking down at the split bar, and his face had gone the colour of the sand. "Gabe. It'll go. The bar'll go and they'll push the gate and it'll—"

He didn't finish.

Gabe knew how it finished. Everybody did. Tiger's houses didn't stand. The Minecraft house, and a hut, and a shed, and a tower, and a wall a cart backed into. Everything he built fell down. It was the joke of the kingdom.

Except one. A shed at Thornwall with a roof that a carpenter from Harrowford had put on, with a shirt on a nail inside, that had stood all summer and all winter and was standing now. The one thing Tiger had ever built that stood. And he hated that it had.

Gabe looked at Tiger's face.

Then he got down off the fighting step.

***

He didn't think about it. That was the thing he'd remember. He was a hands-on sort of person, Deston always said; he did things with his hands before his head had finished deciding about them. He got down off the step, behind the gate, and reached over his shoulder, and drew *Sto*.

It came off his back heavy and short and broad, a slab with ideas, a butcher's cleaver married a gate. He looked at it for half a breath in the gold light.

Then he put it through the brackets.

The bar was split along its length, from the left-hand bracket nearly to the right. The brackets were iron, four of them, two on each leaf, the bar running through all four. Gabe set *Sto*'s point to the inner left-hand bracket, under the split bar, and pushed, and it went through. Through the left-hand pair. Across the gap between the leaves. Into the right-hand pair. A second bar, under the first. Steel, not oak. Short, for a bar; it only just reached. The hilt at the left. The broad end at the right, jammed hard against the last bracket.

He put both hands on the hilt and leaned on it.

"*Gabe*," said Tiger, from the step above him. "*What are you*—"

"It's a wall," said Gabe. "The king said."

The champions came across the sand with the ram.

***

It hit like the end of the world.

Gabe had been hit before. By Harl's hammer, once, by accident, at the Kingsferry forge. By a bronze crucible cracking down his arm. By Declan, once, on a lake shore, at dawn, very lightly, in a lesson, which had been the worst of all. He'd never been hit like this.

The ram hit the gate and the gate hit the bar and the split oak bar went, all at once, into two long halves that clattered down off the brackets onto the sand at his feet. And everything that had been on the bar was on *Sto*.

On him.

The blade bent.

He felt it bend. The whole short heavy slab of it, under his hands, bending in the brackets, the way a branch bends, the way steel bends when everything in the world is pushing on it, and he thought: *it'll go. It's a first blade. Most first blades crack*. And he leaned on the hilt with both hands and all of his weight, everything he had, his whole big body that had been slightly overweight and very strong all his life, and his feet dug into the sand behind the gate, and he didn't move.

It didn't crack.

It bent, and it held, and it came back. He felt it come back. Under his hands. The way a good blade does. The way the king had said it would. *You were right to be frightened. Most first blades crack. This isn't rubbish. This is a wall*.

The champions backed off for another run.

Gabe stood behind the gate with both hands on *Sto*'s hilt and his feet in the sand and his whole body shaking, and looked at the letters on the flat by the hilt, by his left hand. *STO*. In a plain narrow hand, gone over twice where the steel was hard.

*I stand*.

***

They hit it again.

And something happened.

It started in his hands. On the hilt. A warmth, down in the bones of his wrists, where the shock of the ram had gone in, coming up. He'd heard everyone describe it. Twenty-two of them, in the channel, over two years. A door. A post. A held breath. Letting go. He'd listened to every one and thought *that'll never be me, I'm the slow one, I'm the one at the forge*.

It came up out of his hands and into his arms and his chest and his back, and down into his legs, and into his feet in the sand, and he felt his body *harden*. Not much. Just a little. The way iron hardens in the quench. As if every part of him that had been holding on had suddenly, all at once, decided to hold on properly.

And for one heartbeat, just one, with his hands on the hilt, he felt the blade.

Not the weight of it. Not the bend. Something inside it. In the steel. A faint warmth moving through the metal, under his palms, from his hands into the blade and along it, through the brackets, the way heat moves along a bar in the fire. He felt it go along *Sto* from hilt to point, and he felt the steel *answer*. As if the blade had been waiting, the whole time, since a cold quench in a Kingsferry forge, for somebody to put something into it.

It was there for one heartbeat. Then it was gone, and it was just a sword again, under his hands, holding a gate.

*At Honed*, the king had said. *You'll put your hand on a blade and feel what the aura's doing in it*.

He wasn't at Honed. He wasn't anywhere near. But he'd felt it. Just once. For one heartbeat.

His window opened.

[ Gabe · Tier 1: Drawn ]

Gabe looked at it.

He was standing behind a gate in the middle of the Lists, with forty thousand people screaming, and his sword holding the gate shut, and the champions of the Lists backing off across the sand for another run, and a tiger boy above him on the fighting step staring down at him with his mouth open. And he looked at his window, and read the line, and thought, very clearly, the way he thought about everything:

*Oh. Okay.*

***

"*TWENTY-THREE*," screamed somebody, very far away, in the stands. Chris. Gabe would know that voice anywhere now. "*TWENTY-THREE, THAT'S TWENTY-THREE*—"

"*GABE*," said Tiger, above him. "*GABE. YOUR WINDOW. GABE*—"

"I know," said Gabe.

"*YOUR SWORD IS HOLDING UP MY GATE*—"

"I know."

"*THEY'RE COMING BACK*—"

"I know," said Gabe, and put his weight on the hilt again, and the ram hit, and *Sto* bent, and held, and came back.

They didn't get through.

They hit it five more times. In the fifth glass and the sixth. They came over the top again, and RX was there and then wasn't, and Milli was there, waiting, the way he'd learned that morning, and put his curved sword on Bel Harrow's wrist exactly as Bel let go of the crest, and the marshal said *out*. They came at the left-hand wall and Salty met them with his bare hands and lifted Osric off it like a sack of grain. They brought the ram and Gabe leaned on *Sto* and felt his body hold, harder now, the way it hadn't held before.

And PenguChi, on the end of the step, kept saying *steady*. Very calm. Over and over. So that every time the ram hit, Gabe's heart slowed down instead of speeding up.

Tiger ran. Up and down the fighting step, faster than Gabe had ever seen him run, so fast the champions couldn't tell where he'd be. Not toward anything. Not away. Just there. Everywhere. Every time a champion's hand came over the iron crest, there was a tiger boy already standing above it, grinning, with his short blades crossed.

*Earlier*, Gabe heard him say to himself, once, very quietly, as he went past. *Earlier. Not faster. The right place*.

***

"*TIME*," roared the marshal in the royal box.

Six glasses.

The sand had run out of the last one. Gabe heard it. The marshal, and then Lucan, from the master's platform, in a voice that had stopped being a voice somewhere in the third glass and was now just noise: *SIX GLASSES, SIX, THE STRONGHOLD HOLDS, THE GATE STANDS, THE GATE STANDS*—

And the champions, on the sand outside the gate, dropped the ram.

They were laughing. All six of them. Ser Garret Holm was sitting on the sand with his back against the ram, laughing so hard he couldn't get up. Dagny Rook had her axe across her knees and her head back. Old Marten Cray was shaking his white head slowly, the way a man does when he's seen something in his seventieth year that he didn't expect to see. The Harrow twins were saying something to each other that they both finished at the same time.

Behind the gate, Gabe let go of *Sto*'s hilt.

His hands wouldn't open properly. He looked at them. They'd gone stiff, and the knuckles were white, and there were marks across both palms from the grip. He made them open, one finger at a time.

His window was still open. *Tier 1: Drawn*.

He looked up.

***

On the fighting step, Ina reached up and untied the mask.

He did it slowly. The way he'd put it on. Pulling the red cords loose behind his head and lifting the grey oni face off his own and hanging it back at his shoulder, where it grinned at the sky. Underneath, his own face. Teal eyes. One purple horn. White hair going lavender at the ends, a bit damp now from an hour and a half under a mask in the sun.

And his own grin. Every fang. All the way across his face. The grin of a man who knows exactly how cool he's just been and has decided, for once, not to pretend he doesn't.

He looked out at the east stand. At the front tier. At the rail.

There was a small boy there. In a patched shirt, with a battered old cavalry helmet in his lap that he'd very clearly been told he couldn't keep and had kept anyway. On his head a paper crown of horns, old and soft, with a little white mask on the front.

Wil stood up on the bench.

He took the horns off his head and held them up. Over his head. In both hands. As high as he could reach. Looking at Ina.

And then the east stand did.

And then the south. And the west. And then, slowly, raggedly, last of all, the north, until the knot of men in grey and blue were the only people in their whole section still sitting down, and then one of them, the one who'd been wearing a paper crown since the first day without noticing, took it off his head, and looked at it, and stood up, and held it up too.

Paper horns. Forty thousand of them. Held up over the Lists in the gold evening light, toward a gate in the middle of the sand with an oni standing on it, grinning.

Ina put his hand on his chest and bowed to them. Again. The gentleman's bow. To all four stands this time, one after another, slowly, the long indigo coat swinging, while forty thousand people held up paper horns in silence and a small boy on the front tier of the east stand cried.

Gabe found that he was crying too. He didn't mind.

***

It was afterwards that the gate fell over.

They'd come down off the fighting step. All seven of them, onto the sand, in front of the gate, with the champions coming across to shake their hands and the crowd still roaring and the paper horns still up all round the bowl. Tiger had gone to the left-hand gatepost and put both his arms round it, the way you'd put your arms round a horse that had just won a race, and was standing there with his face pressed against the oak saying *it stood, it stood, it stood* over and over into the wood.

"It stood," said Tiger, to Gabe. His face was wet. "*Gabe*. It *stood*. The whole time. Six glasses. A *ram*. It *stood*."

"It stood," said Gabe.

"My gate *stood*."

"It did."

"I've never," said Tiger, and stopped, and tried again. "Nothing I build. Ever. Except the shed. And the shed's—the shed's *his*." He let go of the post and looked up at the gate. All of it. The oak leaves and the posts and the walls and the iron crest. "This one's mine. And it *stood*."

Gabe looked at it too.

Then he remembered *Sto*.

His sword was still in the brackets. Behind the gate. Holding it shut. He'd let go of the hilt when the marshal called time and he'd come down off the step without it, because his hands wouldn't work and Tiger was crying and forty thousand people were holding up horns. It was still there, jammed through the four brackets, a short heavy slab of steel with *STO* cut by the hilt.

"I need my sword," said Gabe.

He went round behind the gate. He put his hand on the hilt. He pulled.

It came out of the brackets with a long scraping ring. Gabe held it up. Not bent. Not cracked. Straight as the day Harl had looked down it at the Kingsferry forge and said *huh*. He ran his thumb down the spine.

Behind him, there was a creak.

Gabe turned round.

The gate was leaning. Very slightly. Both leaves, toward the sand, toward the front, where the ram had been hitting it for an hour and a half. The posts had been driven into the sand of the Lists by forty men in the dark, and they'd held, and held, and held, and the bar had held them together, and then the bar had split and *Sto* had held them together instead, for two glasses, against a ram.

And Gabe had just taken *Sto* out.

"*Oh no*," said RX.

The gate leaned a little further.

There was a moment, Gabe would think afterwards, when everybody in the Lists saw it at once. The champions. The Stronghold. Lucan on the master's platform with his mouth open. Drew in the master's chair, very still. Forty thousand people holding up paper horns. All of them watching the gate lean.

And Tiger, standing in front of it, looking up.

It went over slowly. That was the thing everyone would talk about, in the city, for years. Not with a crash. Slowly. Majestically. Like a very old tree deciding it had stood long enough. The two oak leaves and the posts and the short walls on either side and the fighting step and the iron crest, all of it, tipping forward off its feet in one piece, very gently, through the gold evening air, toward the sand in front of it, where the ram was, and a tiger boy, who stepped smartly out of the way at the last moment, and three champions sitting on a log.

It landed flat on the sand of the Lists with a sound like a door the size of a house being shut.

*BOOM*.

Dust went up. All round it. A great slow golden cloud in the evening light.

Then silence.

***

Forty thousand people with paper horns over their heads didn't make a sound.

Tiger stood beside the gate, flat on the sand at his feet, and looked at it. For a long time. His ears had gone all the way back. His tail had stopped.

Then he turned his head, very slowly, and looked at Gabe. At *Sto*, in Gabe's hand.

"*Your sword*," said Tiger, "*was holding up my gate*."

"Yes," said Gabe.

"*The whole time*."

"For the last two glasses," said Gabe. "The bar split."

"*It was holding up the whole gate*."

"It's a wall," said Gabe. "The king said." He looked down at the blade in his hand. At the three letters by the hilt. "*Sto*. It means *I stand*."

There was a long pause.

"*It stood*," said Tiger. And his voice cracked. And he started laughing, wet and enormous, standing over his flattened gate in a cloud of golden dust. "*It STOOD. It stood LONG ENOUGH*. That's what he *said*. In the yard. In the *moonlight*. *It'll stand long enough. That's all a gate's for*." He was laughing so hard he had to sit down on the fallen gate. "*It stood long enough!*"

And the Lists went up.

Gabe had heard the Lists roar four times now. For Alea's one stroke, and the Kettle's four glasses, and the paper horns, and six glasses at the Gate. He'd never heard it do this. Forty thousand people laughing. All at once. The whole bowl of pale stone shaking with it, people falling off benches, people with their paper horns still in their hands holding onto each other, Lucan on the master's platform sitting down very suddenly in Drew's rocking chair and laughing until the chair tipped back on its short leg and Drew had to catch him.

And then, from somewhere, a chant.

Gabe didn't know who started it. Somewhere in the east stand. A few voices, then more, then the whole stand, then the whole bowl.

*TI-GER. TI-GER. TI-GER.*

Tiger, sitting on his fallen gate, looked up.

*TI-GER. TI-GER.*

And then other names. Gabe heard them come in. *GA-BE*, from the south stand, badly, because they didn't quite know how to say it. *I-NA*, from the east, very loud. *SAL-TY*. *PEN-GU*. *MIL-LI*. Someone shouting *RX*, which didn't chant well and which the crowd kept trying anyway. And under all of it, from somewhere high in the west stand where Calvin was apparently sitting, a separate and entirely unrelated chant of *KET-TLE, KET-TLE*, which nobody could stop.

Gabe stood on the sand in front of a fallen gate with his sword in his hand and his window open in front of his face and forty thousand people chanting his name wrong, and didn't know what to do with his hands.

So he put *Sto* back on his back, and sat down on the gate next to Tiger, and put his arm round his neck from the side, like a falling wall.

"It stood long enough," said Gabe.

"*It stood long enough*," said Tiger, and leaned on him, and laughed.

***

He found Declan at the rail afterwards.

He'd known he would. It was the same place it had always been. On Earth, after every game, every match, every terrible night when they'd lost thirteen to nine, there'd been a moment where everyone else had gone and it was just the two of them, or the three of them, in the call, not talking. Gabe had always known where to find him after. He still did.

Declan was at the rail of the east stand, at the bottom, where the stand met the sand. Not on the fourth tier. He'd come down. He was leaning on the rail with the rod on his shoulder and the two bees on his head, which had woken up in the noise and were buzzing.

Gabe went over and leaned on the rail beside him.

They didn't say anything for a while. Out on the sand, the carpenters were coming out with ropes to see about the gate. Tiger was still sitting on it, refusing to get off. Ina was being mobbed by a crowd of children in paper horns who'd climbed over the rail. Salty was showing the king's father's hammer to old Marten Cray, who'd asked about it.

"Drawn," said Declan.

"Drawn," said Gabe. He held his hands up. The marks across both palms. "I felt it, Declan. In the blade. For a second. Like heat going along a bar."

Declan didn't say anything for a moment.

"That's Honed," he said. "What you felt. That's what Honed feels like."

"I'm not Honed."

"No. You're Drawn. Half an hour ago you were Initiate." He looked at Gabe's hands. "You felt the far end of it. Just once. That means it's there. In you. Waiting." A pause. "The king's going to want you at his anvil."

"He said."

"He means it. He doesn't say things he doesn't mean."

Gabe looked at his hands. Then at the sand, at the fallen gate, at Tiger sitting on it with his arms round his knees, laughing at nothing.

"I took it out," he said. "*Sto*. I didn't think. I took it out and the whole gate fell over."

"I saw."

"Is that—is that bad? Tiger's thing. His houses. It fell down. Again."

Declan was quiet for a long time.

"It stood long enough," he said. "That's all a gate's for." He turned the rod on his shoulder. "And it was standing on your sword. For the last two glasses. Tiger built it, and it stood on your sword." He paused. "That's not his houses falling down. That's two people building one thing."

Gabe didn't say anything.

***

"Was he always like that?" said Gabe, eventually. "Ina."

He nodded out at the sand. At the oni in the long indigo coat, with his mask on his shoulder, crouched down in the middle of a ring of small children in paper horns, writing words in violet fire in the air for them, one after another, small and neat, so that they shrieked and grabbed at them and the words went out in their hands like fireflies. Wil was at the front. Wil had the helmet on.

Declan looked at Ina for a long time.

"He was always cool," he said. "He knew it, too."

Gabe laughed.

"I used to tell him," said Declan. "On calls. Every time. *Ina, that's so cool*. About anything. He'd tell me he'd made toast and I'd say it. He thought I was taking the piss." He watched Ina write a word in fire for Wil, slowly, letter by letter, so the boy could see how. "He told me, the first night. At the Lance. He thought it was a joke. He worked it out on the sand last summer that it wasn't." A pause. "Look at him."

Gabe looked.

Ina, on the sand, crouched in a ring of children, writing *Wil* in violet fire in the air in front of a small boy in a cavalry helmet, letter by letter. And the boy reaching out, very carefully, and touching the fire with one finger. And it not burning him.

"Yeah," said Gabe. "He's cool."

"He's very cool," said Declan.

They leaned on the rail together and watched him, and didn't say anything else, the way they'd sat in a call at two in the morning after a game, not talking. Gabe had his hands on the rail. The marks of *Sto*'s grip were still across both palms. He didn't think they'd go for a while.

He didn't want them to.

"Sleep, Declan," he said, eventually. The thing he'd always said. The last thing he'd ever said to him, on a call, three years ago, before a room in Malacca with a fan going. "Seriously."

Declan laughed. Quietly. The way he had on the Last Look.

"I listened to you once," he said.

"I know," said Gabe. "Look where it got you." He looked out at the Lists. At the fallen gate, and the forty thousand, and the paper horns, and a girl with a sword the size of a door coming across the sand toward them from the tunnel with her ears up, and Tiger on his gate, and Ina in a ring of children writing fire. "Look where it got all of us."

And he put his arm round Declan's neck from the side, like a falling wall, and didn't take it away again.
