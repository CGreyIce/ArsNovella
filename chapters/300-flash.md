The commander sent them out to look at the ground on the sixteenth day, because Aldous asked him to, and because nobody at Cinderhold had ever seen Kharvos stand still before and he wanted to know what it had been standing on.

Ten of them. Rhea at the front, because she was Reaching now and the commander didn't send anybody out on the red without the best sword in the fort in front of them. Wendy with her bow and her knotted rope. Tobin Ferris and five of the autumn draft, with shields. Salty at the back with his arm out of the sling, because nobody could stop him. And Niko, in the middle, with his ribs mostly knitted and his right hand unwrapped for the first time in a fortnight and two shiny pink bars across the palm where the grip had burned him, one on top of the other.

Forty-two times. He'd counted.

They went out of the west gate at first light, which nobody ever did, because there was nothing west of Cinderhold but more red and the old west fort that hadn't been manned in twenty years; and across the open ground toward where the line had stood.

Niko walked with his sword in his hand. Not drawn. Just held, in the sheath, the way you'd carry a stick.

There was frost.

The last of the spring; Maigo said so at the gate, with the new leaves on his antlers curling in the cold, *last one, it says, the ground says it's tired of being cold*. A thin grey skin of it over the red, over every stone and every dead stalk, crunching under their boots. And Niko, who'd seen the letters in the frost for two winters now, the circle's letters, the ones nobody else could see, looked down at it as they walked and saw nothing.

Plain frost. Not written. Just cold.

He'd learned to be glad of that.

***

The line was a mile out.

You could see it before you got there. Not the people; they'd gone east at noon on the second day, a hundred and more of them, with nine hunters at their feet. But the ground they'd stood on. A long curved band across the red where the frost was different: thicker, whiter, crusted up in ridges, the way frost goes on ground that's been walked on and then left. Prints. Hundreds of them, in a line, all facing the same way.

East.

They stood at the end of it in the cold and looked.

"It curves," said Wendy.

She'd seen it from the gate; everybody had. But from here, standing at the end of it, you could see what the gate couldn't. The line wasn't straight. It bent. Very slightly, all the way along, a long shallow curve like the rim of a plate seen from the side, with the inside of the curve facing east.

"Toward Kharvos," said Tobin Ferris.

"Toward something," said Wendy.

She took out her rope. The knotted one she'd measured the ring round the knoll with all winter, a hundred and forty paces, a hundred and thirty-nine on the east. She gave one end to Salty and walked out along the line with the other, counting, and pegged it, and walked on, and pegged it again. Niko watched her do it. She did it the way she did everything: slow and plain and exact, with her lips moving.

It took her an hour. When she came back her face was the colour of the frost.

"It's a ring," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"Part of one. A bit of the edge." She held up the rope. "If you keep it going the way it's bending, it goes round. All the way round. Like the one at the knoll." She looked east, past the line, across the red, toward the black at the far edge of the sky. "Only the one at the knoll's a hundred and forty paces. I can't work out how big this one is. It bends so little." She swallowed. "Miles. A lot of miles. I don't know how many."

"Round what," said Rhea.

Wendy looked east.

"I don't know," she said. "Something a long way off."

***

Niko looked at the prints.

He was a reader, in his way. Not like Thea; he couldn't read the circle's language, only see it, the letters in the frost that came and went. But he'd looked at the prints at the knoll after the fog, in the winter, and seen the letters in them: a pace in from the stakes and not a hand over. He looked at these.

Nothing. Plain frost, crusted on trodden ground. No letters.

He walked along the line. Past the end of it, where Wendy's rope lay pegged in the cold, and on, along the band of trodden white, looking down. The others stayed at the end. He heard Salty say something, and Rhea answer, and Tobin laugh, short, the way boys laugh when they're frightened and pretending not to be.

He was thinking about Flash.

He'd been thinking about it for sixteen days. Since the wall. Since the fourth hunter and the fire going off the end of his blade into its face and the steel coming after, late, into the place where it had been. *Forty-two*. He'd sat on the knoll with Yan every dusk since and not tried it once. He'd sat with his hand on his ribs and his other hand in his lap with the burns on it, and thought.

*It's not faster*.

He'd known that on the knoll. He'd said it to Yan. He'd been doing it faster and faster since the autumn, the fire and the steel, closer and closer together, trying to make two things so fast they looked like one. And they never were. They were always two. The fire first and the steel late, or the steel first and his sleeve on fire. A hair apart. Never the same.

*Struck* had been easy. Not easy. But one thing. A match is struck; a blade is struck; a bell is struck, and the hit and the ring aren't two things, they're the same thing, the ring is just what the hit sounds like. He'd found that. He'd stood on the knoll after the long night with his two candles side by side, aura and mana, and hit something, and it had rung. One sound.

A flash was two. That was what he'd thought, for a year. A flash of fire. A flash of steel. Two flashes. And he'd been trying to make them happen at once.

He stopped walking.

He was half a mile along the line, on his own, with the others small behind him at the end. The sun was coming up over the black in the east, straight in his face, low and red and huge, the way it came up on the Redmarch in spring. And the light came across the red, across the frost, flat, so that every crusted print in the long trodden curve threw a shadow, and the whole line of them lit up at once, white, all the way along.

And on his left, in the middle of the line, something flashed.

Just once. A point of light, sharp, there and gone. The way light flashes off water, off glass. Off steel.

He looked.

***

It was a sword.

Somebody's. A soldier's sword, old, rusted, lying flat in the frost in the middle of the line of prints, half under the crust. It must have been there for years; it must have been there since the long war, out here on the red where nobody came, under snow and mud and frost. One of the dead. One of Aldous's dead, maybe. Somebody who'd fallen on the Redmarch and whose sword had stayed where it fell.

And the sun had come up and caught the one bit of the blade that wasn't rust, and it had flashed.

Niko stood looking at it.

The sword hadn't done anything. That was the thing he couldn't stop looking at. It was lying in the frost. It hadn't moved; it hadn't been swung; nobody had made it flash. The sun had come up, and the light had come across, and the steel had been lying at the right angle, and it had flashed. That was all. The light did it. The steel only had to be turned the right way.

And fire was light.

He stood very still.

A flash of steel wasn't the steel. It was the *light*, off the steel. A flash of fire wasn't the fire. It was the light of it catching. The same thing. The same light. He'd been trying for a year to make fire and steel happen together, two things, and they weren't two things. They were one thing, light, and one of them was where it came from and the other was what it came off.

He'd been pushing the fire down the blade. Making it. And then swinging the steel after it, a heartbeat late. Two things. Two hands. And in the middle of his hot candle, the hard small knot that Yan said was there and never said what it was, and that Niko had known was there for a year: *hurry*. Because he'd always been chasing the second thing with the first.

There wasn't a second thing.

You didn't make the flash. You turned the blade, and the light came off it. All at once. Because it was already there.

He looked down at his sword. In its sheath. In his left hand, the way you'd carry a stick.

"Oh," he said, out loud, to nobody, half a mile out on the Redmarch.

And the frost behind him, on his right, between him and the others, cracked.

***

He heard Wendy before he saw it.

"*Niko*."

Very clear and very calm and very loud, the way she called the count from the gate. And under it, a sound he knew. *Hush, hush*. Long arms in the cold.

He turned round.

It had been lying in the line. In the middle of the prints, a hundred paces back toward the others, in a low place where the frost was thick; grey as the frost and as still as the ground, with its long arms folded under it and its jointed fingers flat, the way they lay at the feet of the threadless. Like a dog. He'd walked past it. They'd all walked past it. He hadn't seen it and Rhea hadn't and Wendy hadn't, and Yan was a mile back on the knoll, too far to feel anything this small and this cold and this still.

One of the nine. It hadn't gone east with the others.

It had stayed.

It was up. Between him and the others. Horned and faceless and grey with beetle-plate down its back, taller than two men, the smell coming off it, hot iron in rain, so strong in the cold air he could taste it. And it wasn't coming at him.

It was going for Tobin.

Tobin Ferris was at the near end of the line with his shield and his father's sword, closest, a pace in front of the other five, the way boys stand when they've been told they're brave; and the hunter went at him across fifty paces of frost the way they went at walls, low and fast, the arms swinging, *hush, hush*.

Rhea was moving. Niko saw her go. Her light going down, he knew, into the ground, the way it did now; she'd cut it from five paces, she'd cut two on the wall. But she was at the far end with Wendy and the rope, sixty paces off, and the hunter was closer to Tobin than she was.

Salty was moving. Enormous, at the back, with the big shield up and the arm that wasn't healed. Too slow. He'd always been too slow. That was what made him Salty.

Niko was a hundred paces behind it.

He didn't think. He ran.

***

He drew as he ran. The sword came out of the sheath in his left hand and he caught it in his right, over the burns, and both his candles came up at once, the way they always came: aura and mana, side by side, the sword and the spell. The bell-note, faint, a struck blade in the cold.

And the old thing came. The push. *Flash*. Make the fire. Put it on the blade. Swing.

He didn't.

He let it come and didn't do it. It was the hardest thing he'd ever done with a sword; it was like holding your hand back from a pan that's falling. Every bit of him wanted to make the fire. He'd made it forty-two times. He knew exactly how.

*There isn't a second thing*.

He ran with the sword in his hand and both his candles up and didn't make anything. He just let them be there. Side by side. Hot and steady. The fire not made yet, the steel not swung yet. Both of them in him at once, the way the light is in the sun before it hits anything.

Tobin had his shield up. The hunter hit it.

Niko heard it from fifty paces: a sound like a door coming off its hinges, and Tobin going down on one knee under it on the frost, and the shield bent, and the hunter's other arm going back for the second blow, the one that would come round the side of the shield where Tobin wasn't looking, the way the one on the wall had come round for Niko.

Thirty paces.

The arm came round.

Ten.

He didn't swing. That was what he remembered afterwards; what he told Yan, on the knoll, that night, with his hands shaking. He didn't swing at it. He'd swung at everything for a year, as hard as he could, as fast as he could, trying to make the steel catch the fire. He got there, and he was inside the arm, between it and Tobin, and the hunter was right there, grey, horned, faceless, the smell, and he didn't swing.

He turned the blade.

Just turned it. A twist of the wrist, the edge coming round to face the thing in front of him, the way the old sword had been lying in the frost at the right angle when the sun came up. Both his candles in it. Not pushed. Not made. Just there, all at once, side by side, the aura and the mana, the steel and the fire.

And it flashed.

***

Everybody saw it. That was the thing he hadn't expected.

He'd thought it would be like the fire, the forty-two times: a sheet of orange going down the blade and off the end. It wasn't. It was white. A point of light, sharp, at the edge of the blade where it turned, the way light flashes off water, off glass. Off steel in the frost when the sun comes up. There and gone. So bright that Wendy, sixty paces off, saw it on the inside of her eyelids for an hour after. So bright that Yan, a mile back on the knoll with his back to the headstone, felt it, the way you'd feel a door open on a lit room, and stood up.

And the hunter came apart.

Not cut. Niko had cut things. He'd cut a scout in half in the autumn with *Struck* and felt the steel go through, and the ring. This wasn't that. There was no going through. There was the flash, at the edge, as the blade turned; and the edge was in the hunter, and through it, and out the other side, all in the same instant as the light. Not one thing and then the other. The light and the cut were the same thing. The fire and the steel were the same thing. The way the hit and the ring had been the same thing, once, at the knoll.

The hunter fell in two halves on the frost on either side of him, the long arm still going round, slowly, empty, and landing a pace from Tobin's bent shield with a sound like a sack of wet sand.

Niko stood between them with the sword in his hand.

It wasn't hot. He looked at it. The blade. It wasn't burning; there was no fire on it; there never had been. Just steel, plain, a little frost on the flat of it from the cold air. And his hand round the grip, on top of the two burns, and it didn't hurt.

It didn't hurt at all.

***

"*Niko*."

Tobin, on his knee, with his shield bent. Staring up at him. The other five of the draft behind him, staring. Salty coming up at last, enormous, out of breath, with the big shield, and stopping, and looking at the two halves of the hunter on the frost, and looking at Niko.

"What," said Salty, "was *that*."

Niko didn't know how to say it.

"I didn't do it," he said. Which was true, and wasn't. "I mean. I didn't make anything. I've been making it. Forty-two times. Making the fire and then swinging." He looked at the sword. "This time I just turned it. And it was already there."

Rhea came up the line from the far end, at a walk, not a run, because it was over. She stopped beside the halves of the hunter and looked at them. At the edges. Clean, both of them, like a thing cut with a blade so sharp it hadn't noticed.

She looked at Niko.

"That's not an edge," she said. "That's not Reaching. I know Reaching." She looked at the sword in his hand, plain steel with frost on it. "That's something else."

"It's the next one," said Niko.

He opened his window. His hand was shaking. He didn't decide to.

He looked at it for a long time, standing on the Redmarch half a mile out, in the frost, with the sun up in his face and the halves of a hunter on either side of him.

*Flash*. No skill under it. There never was, for him; for a spellblade the tier word was the skill, he'd decided that himself, on the red, at the knoll, last year, when the third line in his window had lit at last and he'd had to call it something. *A word that's true of a flame and a blade at once*. *Struck*, and now this.

A flash of fire. A flash of steel. The same light.

He'd been chasing it for a year with both hands.

You didn't chase light. You turned the right way, and it came off you.

***

Wendy pegged the rope out to the end before they went back. She said she wasn't leaving it half done. So they stood in the cold for another hour while she walked the rest of the line with Salty holding the other end, counting, and Niko sat on the frost beside the old soldier's sword with the rust on it and the one bright place on the blade, and didn't touch it.

When she came back she had a number.

"If it's a ring," she said, "and it is, and it keeps bending like that all the way round." She stopped. She'd done the sums on her fingers; she'd done them twice. "It's about forty miles across."

Nobody said anything.

"Forty miles," said Wendy. "And this is the west edge of it. Here. A mile from our wall." She looked east. "The middle's twenty miles that way. In the black."

"What's twenty miles that way," said Tobin.

Nobody knew.

"The knoll's a ring," said Wendy. "A hundred and forty paces. With the spike in the middle." She rolled up the rope, slowly. "The valley at Thornwall's a ring. Thea read it. With a hole in the middle." She looked at the long white curve of trodden frost going away from them in both directions, bending so slowly you could only see it if you knew. "And this one's forty miles. And they stood on the edge of it all night facing in."

"Facing the middle," said Rhea.

"Facing the middle," said Wendy.

Niko looked east. Into the sun, low and red, coming up over the black at the far edge of the sky. Twenty miles. He couldn't see anything. Nobody could. There wasn't anything to see; it was just the black, the way it had always been, mile after mile of it, where nothing had ever been there to die.

Somewhere past it, much further, weeks, a hill. He knew that much now. Everybody at Cinderhold knew it: the Hero had gone east once, at nineteen, and come back with a sword. Nobody knew where.

He didn't know if it was twenty miles or two hundred. He didn't know if it was the same thing.

He looked down at the old soldier's sword in the frost.

Then he picked it up, very carefully, by the hilt, and brought it back with him.

***

He gave it to Aldous at the gate.

He didn't know why. It had seemed like the thing to do, out there, with the rust on it. One of the dead. Aldous's dead, the ones under the red he'd buried and said the words over. Aldous took it in both hands and looked at it for a long time, at the one bright place on the blade, and didn't say anything.

"It flashed," said Niko. "When the sun came up. That's how I found it." He looked at it. "That's how I found mine."

Aldous turned the old sword over.

"Corporal Brenna Tarrow's," he said at last. Very quietly. "The long war, near the end of it. She went out to the old west fort with a message and didn't come back." He ran his thumb along the bright place. "We never found her. Twenty-two years." He looked up. "Where was it?"

"In the middle of the line," said Niko. "Where they stood."

Aldous didn't say anything for a while.

"Then they stood on her," he said. "All night. Facing in." He held the sword against his chest, flat, the way you'd hold a child. "I'll bury it with the others. Behind the chapel." He looked at Niko. "Thank you."

He went in through the gate with it, slowly, an old man carrying a rusty sword like it was the most important thing in the fort.

Rhea, beside Niko, watched him go.

"Tell Petra," she said.

"What?"

"Tell her you can cut without an edge now." Rhea almost smiled. "She'll be furious."

Niko read it on the wall in the sun.

Thirty of thirty-five. He remembered when it was one. Thea, in the first winter, at a bonfire in Wrenhaven; and the rest of them at nothing for months, at *Initiate*, a word that meant you were standing at a door. He remembered when it was thirty-five of thirty-five at Tier 1, the day he'd found *Struck*, last of all of them, and Chris had posted *35 of 35* and nothing else for a whole minute.

Thirty, now, at two. And five at the door, every one of them over ninety, every one of them with something they were keeping.

He didn't post anything. He'd said what he had.

He sat with his back to the merlon and his sword across his knees and the burns on his palm, two shiny pink bars, healing. He'd keep them, he thought. Forty-two times. You should keep the things that taught you.

***

He went out to the knoll at dusk.

Yan was there. He always was, at dusk, with his back to the black headstone and his hand flat on the earth and Suzu round his throat. Salty on one side of him, eating. Maigo sitting cross-legged at the foot of the ash, which had leaves now, a great many, and talking to it under his breath. Wendy on the stakes, counting, not because she needed to.

Niko sat down on Yan's other side.

They didn't say anything for a long time. The red went dark round them. The sun went down behind the old west fort, in the west, behind where the line had stood, behind the place where a corporal called Brenna Tarrow had gone out with a message twenty-two years ago and been stood on all night by a hundred people with nothing inside them facing the middle of a ring forty miles across.

"I felt it," said Yan. "From here."

"I know. You said."

"It was like a lit room." Yan's hand was flat on the earth. "And the iron rang. When you did it. Once. Very small." He was quiet. "Suzu says it was saying something."

Niko looked at the headstone. The black stone, the old point going down through it into the grave, into the dark, into the small dense cold with the ash's roots round it.

"What was it saying?"

Yan listened. With his hand. With Suzu.

"Same as always," he said. "*Stay*." He was quiet a long time. "But Suzu says it said it to you. This time. Not down. Out." He turned his head. "She says it sounded like *yes*."

Niko didn't say anything.

He put his own hand flat on the earth, beside Yan's. Cold. The grave of a woman he'd never met, who'd been the Hero's healer and Alea's mother and was warm somewhere behind the iron, kept, where nobody could reach. He didn't reach. Nobody did. He just put his hand there, the way Yan did every dusk, and sat.

And thought about the man on the jetty. Who'd pulled a sword out of a hill once, at nineteen, Niko knew that much now, everybody at Cinderhold did; and carried it into a war, and put it on her grave; and had sat on the end of a jetty at a lake for ten years with his tea going cold, reading every line of a channel he never wrote in.

He wondered what he was doing tonight.

Fishing, probably.

***

It was the end of the spring.

At Thornwall, a girl on a tower counted twenty-seven holes in a curve at the top of a valley, and four on the far side of a pass, and a boy with freckles in the third ring with his face turned toward a gate; and put the sight down, and went to sleep under a friend's wing, because somebody else was on.

At Kingsferry, five people sat round a kitchen table with a list on the back of a soup bill that said *Names?*; and on the south wharf an old woman, mending, sang to her knots.

At Valcrest, a girl on the roof of the Broken Lance hummed a tune with long gaps in it, and on either side of her a man who'd gone inside a hammer and a girl with frost in her hair sat shoulder to shoulder and didn't fill them.

At Solmere, at the top of the west tower, a king in a travelling coat sat in a chair by a desk with a drawer in it that had been open and empty for years, across from a man who'd been the Advisor for ten years and a court mage before that, and neither of them said anything, because the man had his hands round a letter that hadn't come yet. It was on a big bay horse on the post road, four days out of Wrenhaven, by hand.

And at a lake, at the end of a jetty, in the dark, a man sat on the last plank with his legs over the edge and a hook on an old rod with a word on it, and looked back up the shingle at a lamp in a window that he'd lit himself.

He'd let his tea go cold. Somebody would tell him about it in the morning.

He didn't mind.
