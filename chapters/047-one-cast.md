He'd been sleeping badly since Yan's message.

Not much. Not so that Alea would notice. He went to bed at the usual time and lay in the dark with the window open, listening to the lake, the way he always had. But some part of him that had been asleep for seven years had woken up when he read those lines, and it didn't go back down. *Something crossed the Redmarch tonight. Cold. Heading north-west.*

North-west of the Redmarch was a great many places. Most of them weren't here.

He'd lain awake anyway. And on the third night he'd given up, and got up, and taken his rod and a blanket and gone out to the end of the jetty, and sat there until dawn with the water lapping at the posts and the town dark on the hill behind him. And the night after that. And the night after that.

He told himself it was the fishing. Night was good for pike. It wasn't untrue.

***

On the seventh night, a boy in Valcrest asked what he'd been like.

Ars had his window open on his knees. He did that now, at night, out here, where nobody could see. Only the bottom of it, where the channel was. He kept his eyes off the top, where the line he didn't want to want was. He'd got good at that: opening a window and looking at only the part of it he needed, the way you could walk through a room you'd grown up in without looking at the photographs on the wall.

He'd been reading the goodnights. Maigo saying goodnight to Yan, and Yan saying goodnight to Maigo, and Maigo saying it again. He'd been about to close it.

Then:

He sat very still on the end of the jetty.

Hako. Three nights in an empty server, at one in the morning, the same angles over and over. The boy from Marvel Rivals who'd walked round every corner into every enemy in the first week and apologised every time, *sorry, sorry, sorry*, until Ice had told him to stop apologising and start pre-aiming. He'd been very patient with Hako. He remembered that. He remembered thinking that the boy would be good one day, if anyone bothered to show him how.

***

He couldn't have stopped reading if he'd tried.

Yan's tournament. *And Ice is down. Tragic. The crowd is devastated.* He'd forgotten he'd done that. He hadn't forgotten the tournament, but he'd forgotten that: calling his own deaths, in the final circle, in a sports commentator's voice, because it was the only thing he could do to help by then and Yan needed to laugh. Drew, muting the casters. *I was the reason we won. Trust.* Four kills. Yan had eleven. He'd forgotten the numbers.

Maigo. *THE MACHINE.* He'd been so loud. He'd been twenty-four and so loud.

Gabe and Deston, the choir. The back row. The quiet part. *He never once got caught.* That one was true, and he was absurdly proud of it, thirty-four years later, sitting on a jetty in another world.

Xav. *Okay. Again.*

Malcolm. *I kept saying I'd visit next year.*

He read that one twice, and had to look out at the water for a while before he could go on.

The lines came in and slid up and vanished off the top of the channel, forty at a time, and he read every one before it went. The banana. The cat. John's own shoe; he'd forgotten the shoe. RX and osu!, and the birthday that hadn't happened. Azac: *a hole at the front of the party*.

Then Calvin. The kettle.

*He showed me his kettle like it was a trophy.*

Ars laughed out loud, alone, in the dark, at the end of the jetty. It came out of him before he could stop it, a short helpless sound that startled a heron off the reeds. *It was a terrible kettle.* It had been. It had leaked from the base and taken six minutes to boil and made a noise like a dying goose. He'd bought it for two ringgit at a night market in his first week in Malacca and loved it more than anything he'd owned in his whole adult life.

*He knew it was terrible. That's why he loved it.*

Rachael. Of course it was Rachael.

He was still smiling when he felt it.

***

It came into the edge of him the way a stone comes into a shoe.

He'd been feeling the town all night without thinking about it. That was what Dominion was. Not something you did. Something you were. His aura had stopped being a blade years ago and become something more like weather: a quiet pressure that filled the space around him for a long way in every direction, and in which everything that moved made a small disturbance, the way a fish makes a small disturbance in still water. He felt the herons in the reeds. The hens asleep in their house. A fox, two fields over, going about its business. The town on the hill: hundreds of small warm sleepers, and a few wakeful ones, and the night watch on the gate, dozing.

And the militia hall. Five particular sleepers he'd have known anywhere. Not many of them sleeping, tonight.

And now, on the hill above the town, something else.

Something cold.

He didn't move. He didn't look up from the window. He let it settle into his awareness, slowly, the way you let your eyes adjust to the dark, until he could feel its shape.

It was in the old orchard, on the brow of the hill behind the town, where the apple trees had gone wild. Crouched. Very still. It had come over the ridge from the east, perhaps an hour ago; he could feel the faint cold track of it behind it in the grass, like the wake behind a boat. It had come carefully. It had come round the long way, through the high fields, keeping the hill between itself and the town until the very last. It had found the one place on the brow where you could see straight down the slope into the upper windows of the militia hall.

And it had stopped there, and settled into the dark under the apple trees, and begun to watch.

Fourth tier, he thought. Maybe fifth. Not a scout; nothing like the thin, bent, counting things that came to the edge of the Redmarch. This was older, and heavier, and much more patient. The kind Kharvos kept back for the things that mattered. He'd killed a few of them, a long time ago. They didn't come in packs. They came alone, and they came a very long way, and they waited a very long time, and when they moved, they didn't miss.

*Cold. Colder than anything I've felt out here. Heading north-west.*

So this was where it had been going.

He looked down at his window. At Calvin's kettle, sliding slowly up toward the top of the channel. At the line that had just come in beneath it: Rachael, *I asked him for advice once, about something so embarrassing I'm not telling any of you*.

Five people in the militia hall. Most of them awake, reading this.

He closed the window.

***

He stood up.

The rod was lying beside him on the planks, where it always was. He picked it up. It was a good rod. He'd made it himself, the winter after he came to the lake, from a length of ash he'd seasoned for a year: plain and long and supple, with a cork grip worn smooth by his hand and a simple line of braided horsehair. Anyone in Wrenhaven could have told you it was a good rod. Nobody in Wrenhaven, until a girl with a basket of eggs had walked into him in the market in the winter and looked at it a moment too long, had ever noticed that there was something else folded into it.

He hadn't unfolded it in seven years. He'd cut fish with it, and line, and once a branch that had come down across the lake road in a storm. Nothing else.

He didn't think about it much. That was the thing. People imagined it must be something you had to think about, at his tier: some vast gathering-up, some terrible decision. It wasn't. It was like casting. You'd done it ten thousand times. You stood with your weight on the back foot, the way he'd taught Alea on the ice, and you let it go down through you into the planks so it had somewhere to stand. You felt for the gap. You breathed out, long and slow, until everything was loose and empty and still.

And you cast.

Not out over the water. Up. Behind him, over his shoulder, up the long dark slope of the fields toward the town and the hill and the orchard on its brow. The rod came over in a long smooth arc, the way it had a thousand times on a thousand mornings, and the line went singing out into the night air, and the little lead weight and the hook dropped harmlessly into the wet grass of the lower meadow forty paces behind the cabin.

The edge went on.

He felt it go. Up the slope, over the dark hedges and the sleeping fields, over the roofs of Wrenhaven and the leaded roof of the militia hall and the girl awake behind its window, up the hill, a mile and more, quick and quiet and straight as a thrown thought. Not a blade. Not a light. Nothing anyone could have seen. Just a line, drawn once, through the dark, the way you'd draw a line through a word on a page.

In the orchard on the brow of the hill, under the wild apple trees, the cold thing lifted its head, as if it had heard something.

Then there were two of it.

***

He reeled in.

It took a while. He'd cast a long way. The hook came back through the wet grass of the meadow and across the little beach and up onto the end of the jetty, dripping dew, with a stem of meadowsweet caught on it. He picked the stem off and dropped it into the water and watched it float away.

The edge folded back into the rod. He felt it settle, the way a cat settles. He laid the rod down on the planks beside him, where it always was.

Up on the hill, the cold thing was cooling. He could feel it. Not dead yet, quite; things like that took a while. But it would never get up, and it would never go home, and whatever it had come to see, it would never tell anyone.

He sat down again on the end of the jetty, and pulled the blanket round his shoulders, and opened his window.

Dennis: He was the only person who never lied to me. I'd have known. ]

He read it. Then he kept reading.

***

It went on for hours. He read every line.

Higu's ban poem. He'd forgotten the poem; he'd forgotten he'd been the kind of person who'd write a man an apology poem in a ban reason. *And then "trust."* Of course he had. Xola, gifting subs. Gigichi, *I'm answering now. I'm okay. Mostly.* He had to stop again, after that one, for a while.

Thea. *I thought there'd be time.*

PenguChi. Words on Stream. He'd cheated, a little. Not at the game. He'd just been doing word puzzles since he was a boy, every day, the way other people drank coffee, and he'd never told her. The capital. Jared's postcards. Milli: *the Minecraft bingo guy. I'm sorry. I wish it was more.* Cursiffe: *Faster than anyone I've met. Here or there.*

***

Near dawn, when the channel had gone quiet at last, he did something he hadn't meant to.

He scrolled up. All the way. To the top of his window, past the channel, past the skills and the inventory, to the line he spent most of his life not looking at.

[ Ars · Tier 8: Dominion · Progress to Ascendant: 46% ]

Forty-five, at dusk.

He looked at it for a long time, with the sky going grey behind the far trees and the first birds starting up in the reeds.

He'd cut a fifth-tier demon in half tonight, from a mile away, without getting up off a jetty. It hadn't moved the number at all. He hadn't expected it to. Fighting had never been the way up; not for a long time. He'd known that for seven years.

But thirty-four people had told a boy in Valcrest what he'd been like. And somewhere in the middle of it, while he wasn't looking, it had gone up by one.

He closed the window, and sat and watched the sun come up over Lake Ilse, and didn't think about anything at all.

***

Higu had felt it at a little after one.

She'd been awake. Of course she had; everybody had, in Wrenhaven, the five of them, lying in their beds in the dark above the militia hall with their windows open, reading. She was on her bed under the window with the waning moon coming in, thin and late, across the blanket. She'd just read Calvin's line about the kettle and was smiling at it when the moonlight flinched.

That was the only word she had for it. She'd been feeling the moon all year. It was her path; it pulled at her the way it pulled the tide, and on nights like this, with it up and shining, she could feel it on everything. The roofs. The fields. The lake. A thin pale weight, very gentle, lying over the whole town like a sheet.

And for one heartbeat, the sheet moved.

Like a stone dropped into still water. A ripple, running out across the whole of the moonlight at once, so fast she couldn't tell where it had started. Through the room. Through her. Out over the town.

And under it, for that same heartbeat, a *weight*.

She knew that weight. She'd felt it once before, in the summoning hall, the day the king walked in: as if the whole room had sunk a few fathoms underwater. As if something enormous had stood up, somewhere close, and the air had had to make room for it.

It lasted one heartbeat. Then it was gone, and the moonlight lay still on the blanket again, and the town was quiet, and her own heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

She sat up.

She listened. Nothing. No shout from the gate. No dogs. The lake road was empty, pale under the moon. The fields were still. Nothing had happened. Nothing at all.

She didn't believe it for a second.

***

Thea's door was open a crack, with light coming out of it. Higu pushed it the rest of the way without knocking.

Thea was sitting bolt upright on her bed, with her window open in front of her and her hand pressed flat against her chest. She'd been crying; Higu could see the marks on her face, silver in the window-light. The stories, probably. They'd all been crying, on and off, all night.

But that wasn't why she was sitting like that.

"Did you feel that?" said Higu.

Thea stared at her. "Did *you*?"

They looked at each other.

"The moonlight," said Higu. She came in and shut the door behind her and sat down on the end of the bed, carefully, because her legs weren't quite working. "It moved. All of it. At once. Like a stone in a pond. And then there was..." She stopped. "Do you remember the hall? When the king came in? That weight?"

Thea nodded slowly.

"That," said Higu. "For one heartbeat. Then nothing."

Thea didn't say anything for a while. Then she reached up, very carefully, and touched her window. Not the channel. Higher up. Higu couldn't see it from where she sat, but she knew what Thea was touching. She'd seen it once, in the winter, when Thea had shown her. The line nobody else could see. *Medium: one blade.* And next to it, the sealed value: a little locked box with nothing written in it, that had rattled in the market when the fisherman walked past, and in the yard whenever Alea came near.

"It slammed," said Thea. "Just now. The sealed thing." Her voice was very quiet. "Not rattled. *Slammed.* Like a door in a gale. Right when you said. I thought it was my heart. I thought I was..." She laughed shakily. "I thought it was the stories. I was reading Gigichi's, and I thought I was just..."

"It wasn't the stories," said Higu.

"No."

They sat there on Thea's bed in the dark, with the moonlight on the floor, and looked at each other.

"What was it?" said Thea.

"I don't know."

"Gu. You *always* know."

"I don't know," said Higu again. And it was true. She'd been turning it over since it happened and she had nothing. A ripple. A weight. A sealed value slamming. Nothing she could put a shape to. "Something happened. Somewhere close. Something..." She searched for the word. "Something *big* moved. Once. And then stopped."

"Yan said," said Thea, very quietly. "A week ago. Something cold. Heading north-west."

Higu had been thinking about that too.

"Nobody be alone at night," she said.

***

So Higu stayed.

They sat at Thea's window, the two of them, wrapped in Thea's blanket, and looked out. Past the dark roofs of Wrenhaven, past the hedges and the fields, down the long slope to the lake. The moon was low over it now, and the water was a pale smudge, and at the edge of it, very small and very far away, there was a lamp lit in the window of a cabin.

Higu looked at it for a long time.

She didn't say anything about the jetty. About the moth, and the man reading something in the air, and the girl with the tea, and the two lines at the back of her notebook. She thought about it. She thought about it very hard, sitting there with Thea's shoulder against hers.

But she didn't know what the ripple had been. And she didn't know what the lamp meant. And she'd promised herself, on the roof, under the full moon, that she wasn't going to make anyone guess until she knew.

"We shouldn't put this in the channel," said Thea, after a long while. "Should we."

"No," said Higu. "Not until we know what it was."

"Everyone's scared enough."

"Yes."

Thea nodded. She opened her window again, and the stories were still coming in, slower now, from the capital and from Thornwall: Kei's long phone call, Bee's single heart. They read them together, in the dark, one after another, as the moon went down over the lake. Higu could feel Thea's sealed value still, somehow, even though she couldn't see it: a tight, held stillness in the girl beside her, like someone listening for a door to slam again.

It didn't.

Toward dawn, the lamp in the cabin by the lake went out.

***

The egg-woman's boy brought the message to the town hall at a quarter past six, while Odette was still lighting the fires.

He was a gap-toothed lad of eleven, out of breath, with a basket of eggs on one arm, and he'd been told to say it to the mayor and nobody else. Odette fetched the mayor. The mayor came down the stairs in his shirtsleeves, with his chain of office forgotten on the bedpost and shaving soap on one ear.

"Fisherman says," said the boy, gasping, very proud of himself, "the pike are running."

Rowan Hale went still on the bottom step.

He hadn't heard those words in seven years. He'd heard them a lot, once, during the war: a fisherman's message, passed by children and carters and anyone who happened to be going that way, that meant *come now, come quietly, and don't bring anyone*. He'd been the one who made it up. He'd thought it was funny, at the time.

He gave the boy a copper. Then another. Then he went upstairs and put his coat on over his shirtsleeves and wiped the soap off his ear and walked out of the town hall and down the lake road in the grey morning light, alone, without his chain.

***

He found Ars at the cabin door.

Alea had already gone up the hill with the basket. Rowan had passed her on the road, and she'd waved, and he'd waved back, and he'd seen her glance back at the cabin once with a small frown, the way you glance back at a door you're not sure you locked. Ars was standing on the step with his rod in one hand, looking tired. Not tired the way he usually looked, the old slow ache of a man who'd been tired for seven years and made his peace with it. Tired the way he'd looked during the war. Like a man who'd been up all night.

"Walk with me," he said.

They went up through the back fields, not the road. Ars knew every gate and stile and gap in every hedge between the lake and the hill; he'd walked them all, in the years after the war, at night, when he couldn't sleep. They skirted the edge of the town, keeping to the hedges, and climbed the long slope of the hill behind it in silence, through the wet grass, with the sun coming up over the far trees on their right.

The old orchard was at the top. Wild apple trees, gone to ruin, with grass up to your knees between them. From the brow you could see the whole of Wrenhaven spread out below: the market square, the church, the town hall. And just there, close enough to throw a stone at, the back of the militia hall. Its upper windows. Five of them, in a row.

Ars stopped under the trees. He didn't say anything. He just looked down.

Rowan looked down too.

***

It had been big. That was the first thing. Rowan had seen the scouts, on the Redmarch, in the war: the thin bent things that moved in bursts. This wasn't one of those. It had been twice the size of a man, at least, and built long, with too many joints in its limbs, all folded up beneath it the way a spider folds. It had been wrapped in something like a cloak: a shepherd's coat, stolen, too small, split at the seams. Its skin was black, and it was going blacker where the morning light touched it, crumbling at the edges into something like soot.

And it was in two pieces.

The cut ran from the top of its head to the ground, clean and straight, as if someone had laid a ruler on it. Not hacked. Not torn. *Cut.* The two halves had fallen apart from each other like the halves of an apple. The grass beneath them had been cut too, in a perfect line. So had an apple bough that had been hanging low over the thing's head. The cut end of it was white and clean and smooth as glass.

Rowan stood very still.

He'd seen cuts like that before. Not many. On the Redmarch. In the morning, after.

"It came over the ridge last night," said Ars quietly. "From the east. Round the long way, through the high fields. It found this spot. It sat down here." He nodded at the militia hall below them, at the five windows in a row. "And it watched."

"The girls," said Rowan.

"Yes."

"It came here for *them*?"

"It came a very long way for them." Ars was looking down at the five windows. His face gave away nothing at all. "Kharvos doesn't send these for scouting. They send them for things that matter. Somebody over there has worked out that the kingdom summoned thirty-five people who'll be dangerous one day. And they've decided that the time to deal with them is now, while they're not dangerous yet." He paused. "I'd have done the same, in their place."

Rowan's mouth had gone dry.

"And you," he said.

Ars was quiet for a moment.

"It came for them," he said. "But if it had gone home, it would have told them where I am too."

***

They stood under the apple trees for a while, with the sun coming up.

"What do you need?" said Rowan at last.

"Double the night watch on the hall," said Ars. "Quietly. Good men. Ones who'll stay awake. Tell them it's for thieves, or Vane's people. Anything." He turned from the windows at last and looked at Rowan. "Don't frighten the girls. They've got enough to be frightened of. They don't need this as well."

"And Halvard?"

"Tell him. Today. Quietly, by your own rider, not the post." Ars's voice was very even. "Tell him every posting is being hunted. All seven. Not just this one. Thornwall, Cinderhold, the capital, all of them. Tell him what this was, and how far it came, and what it was looking at." He glanced down at the two halves of the thing in the grass. "He'll want to know how it died."

"What do I tell him?"

"That it met a fisherman."

Rowan almost laughed. It came out as a sort of cough.

"And this?" He nodded at the remains, crumbling quietly in the morning sun.

"Burn it. Before the children find it. Bring a cart and some oil and say you're clearing the orchard." Ars looked at the cut apple bough, at the white smooth end of it. "And cut that branch off properly, with a saw. So nobody looks at it twice."

***

They walked back down together, through the wet fields, as the town woke up below them. Smoke from chimneys. A cart on the lake road. The church bell for the early service. Up at the militia hall, a window opened, and Rowan saw a flash of lavender-pink hair, and heard, very faint, Xola laughing at something.

At the gate of the bottom field, where the path split, one way to the town and the other down to the cabin, Rowan stopped.

He'd asked it in the autumn. He'd known the answer before he asked, and he'd asked anyway, because he'd been asking for seven years and he'd never stopped. He didn't know why he asked it now. Maybe it was the cut bough. Maybe it was the way Ars had stood under the apple trees looking down at five windows in a row, with his face giving nothing away and his rod in his hand.

"You could still come back, you know," said Rowan.

Ars was looking down the path toward the lake.

"Not yet," he said.

Rowan opened his mouth to say *I know*, the way he always did, and stopped.

He looked at Ars. Ars didn't look back. He was just standing at the gate in the early sun, with his rod over his shoulder, looking down the path toward the jetty and the water.

"Not yet," Rowan repeated slowly.

Ars didn't say anything.

"In the autumn," said Rowan, "it was *no*."

"I know what it was in the autumn."

Rowan stood there for a moment longer. He didn't know what to do with his face. He'd heard *no* so many times, in so many ways, over seven years, that he'd stopped hearing it. He'd stopped expecting anything else.

*Not yet.*

It wasn't a yes. It wasn't anything like a yes. But it was the first time in seven years it hadn't been a door shutting.

"Well," said Rowan at last, a little roughly. "I'll take *not yet*."

Ars nodded, once. Then he went through the gate and down the path toward the cabin, without looking back. Rowan stood at the gate and watched him go, all the way down to the water, until he reached the end of the jetty and sat down there in the sun with his rod beside him, a small figure on the edge of the lake, the way he'd sat every morning for seven years.

Then the mayor of Wrenhaven turned round and walked up the hill to his town, to find a cart, and some oil, and a saw, and a rider he trusted. He found he was walking faster than he had in years.
