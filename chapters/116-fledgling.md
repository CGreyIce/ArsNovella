Alea's hands were tired every night.

She'd never told anyone. Not her father. Not the king, who'd measured them with his own big scarred hands in the autumn and said *too big for you now, you'll be cursing it for a year*, and been right. Not Maren, or the girls, or Petra, who'd watched her swing Astra on the shingle every morning for five weeks with her pale soldier's eyes and said nothing, because Petra never said anything she didn't have to.

She'd told Gabe. On the third morning. Under the sailcloth, with her fingers not quite meeting round the grip.

*My hands are tired every night. And it slips in the rain.*

And Gabe had looked at the old leather, wrapped round and round for a hand much bigger than hers, and said *I can fix that*, and then *seriously*, and she'd laughed, because her father had told her years ago that Gabe said *seriously* when he meant it.

She'd waited five weeks. She hadn't asked again. She'd learned that from her father too: you said a thing once, and then you let the other person decide when.

On the thirty-fourth morning, the morning after the king's letter, Gabe came up from the forge before the class, and found her on the cabin step with her porridge, and said:

"Today. If you want."

***

He'd been getting ready. She saw that as soon as they came under the sailcloth.

On the bench at the back, laid out in a row, the way her father laid out hooks: a roll of leather, new, pale, thin, the colour of milky tea. A strip of something darker, finer, that she didn't recognise. A little pot of glue that smelled of fish and horses. Two knives, one of them very small. A rasp. And a block of wood the length of her forearm, with a shape drawn on it in charcoal that she looked at for a long time before she understood it was the shape of her own hand, closed.

"When did you draw that?"

"Last week," said Gabe. "At breakfast. You were holding your cup." He picked up the block and turned it so she could see the lines. "You hold a cup the same way you hold a grip. Everybody does. You just don't notice."

She stared at him.

"Dad said you were good with your hands," she said.

"Your dad," said Gabe, "once watched me fix his chair with a coat hanger and told everyone for four years that I was a genius." He put the block down. "I'm not a genius. I just look at things for a long time." He held out his hand. "Can I have her?"

Alea unbuckled Astra from her back.

***

It was harder than she'd thought, letting him take it.

She'd carried Astra every day since the autumn. Every day. On her back down the lake road, on her back in the market, on her back at the fire. She slept with it leaning against the wall at the foot of the stairs where she could see it from the loft. She'd named it. She'd watched a king and her father cut it out of the sword that had stood over her mother's grave for eight years, and fold it, and fold it, and draw it out on an anvil on this shingle while she worked the bellows until her arms shook. She had never once handed it to anybody.

She held it out to Gabe across the bench, flat, on both hands.

Gabe took it the way he'd taken it the first time, five weeks ago: with his feet set, and his shoulders ready. It still pulled at him. He was the strongest of the fifteen, and he still had to let his arms take it. He laid it on the anvil, carefully, the black blade across the little hook anvil with both ends hanging over, and stood looking at it.

"I'm not going to change her," he said. "I want you to know that first. The blade's perfect. I couldn't do anything to the blade if I tried. I'm not going to touch it." He put one finger on the grip. "Just this. The bit that touches you."

"The leather's from the old sword," said Alea.

She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, fast, before she could stop it.

Gabe looked at her.

"The king put it back on," said Alea. "In the autumn. The same leather. He could have used new. He had new. He took the old leather off the old grip, very carefully, in one piece, and soaked it, and wrapped it back on mine." She swallowed. "Dad wrapped that leather. When he found the sword. In Kharvos, at nineteen. He told me. He cut it off his own saddle because there wasn't anything else, and wrapped it round the grip so he could hold it, and it's been on there ever since. Through everything. Through..." She stopped. "It was on there the day he carried it home. It was on there over Mum."

Gabe didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then he nodded, slowly.

"Okay," he said. "Then we keep it."

"But it's too big. You said. It's wrapped for him."

"It's wrapped for him *now*," said Gabe. "Because it's wrapped round a grip that was made for him." He tapped the grip, through the leather. "There's wood under there. Two pieces of wood, either side of the tang. That's what's too big. Not the leather. The leather just goes where the wood tells it." He looked at her. "So we change the wood. We make it fit your hand. And then we put his leather back on, round *your* wood. Narrower. Wrapped tighter. More times round."

Alea looked at him.

"His leather," she said. "On my grip."

"Your mum's hands," said Gabe, "were cold, weren't they? Like yours. He said."

Alea nodded. She couldn't say anything.

"Cold hands slip," said Gabe gently. "On leather, in the rain. That's half of it. That's why it slips." He picked up the strip of darker stuff from the bench, the fine one she hadn't recognised. "This is sharkskin. Rough side out. It's what the good swordsmiths in Kingsferry put under the wrap for people with cold hands. It grips. Harl gave me a piece when I left, for luck." He held it up to the light. "It goes under. Nobody sees it. Just your hands."

Alea looked at the sharkskin. At the old leather on the grip. At Gabe.

"Okay," she said.

***

It took all day.

He did it the way her father had cut the letters on the stone: slowly, without hurrying, without stopping. She'd thought it would be quick. A grip. A bit of wood and a bit of leather. She'd thought he'd be done by the time the class finished on the shingle below.

It took all day, and she stayed for all of it.

First the leather. He unwrapped it the way the king had, in one long piece, turn by turn, so slowly that it took most of an hour. It came off dark and supple and shaped, still curled into the spiral it had held for sixteen years, and he laid it in a shallow pan of water on the bench to soak, and put a stone on it so it wouldn't float, and didn't touch it again.

Then the wood.

It was old. Black with oil and handling, and she'd never seen it before. Two slabs of something hard and close-grained, riveted either side of the black tang with pins that had gone dull and smooth. Gabe looked at them for a long time. Then he took the pins out. It took him an hour. He wouldn't use a hammer on them; he said the blade didn't like being hit anymore, now that it was finished, and he could feel it, and so could she, if she put her hand on it.

She put her hand on it.

He was right. She'd never noticed. But under her palm, through the black iron, there was something. Not angry. Not asleep. *Waiting*, he'd called it, the first morning. She'd thought he meant it poetically. He hadn't. She could feel it now, now that she was looking for it. A stillness in the blade like the stillness on the lake before dawn. As if it were holding its breath.

"It knows," said Gabe, without looking up. "That something's happening. That's all. It's fine."

The old wood came off. He didn't throw it away. He wrapped it in a cloth and put it on the bench and said *you'll want that*, and she knew she would.

Then he took the new block, the one with her hand drawn on it in charcoal, and began to cut.

***

He talked while he worked.

Not much. Not about anything. Gabe didn't fill silences the way Calvin did, or tell stories while things were happening the way Azac did. He just said things, now and then, when they came into his head, the way you'd drop a stone into water to see the rings.

"He used to sit on the risers like this," he said, at some point in the morning, with a knife in one hand and the block in the other and a long pale curl of wood coming off it. "Your dad. At fifteen. With his knees up. Making faces at us when the choirmaster wasn't looking." He turned the block. "He was always a little bit behind the beat. On purpose. So he could see if we'd notice."

"He told me," said Alea.

"I know he did. You told me which story was your favourite." Gabe blew the dust off. "I'm just telling you the bit he left out."

"Which bit?"

"That the choirmaster never once caught him," said Gabe. "Not once. Every other boy in the back row got sent to the front for laughing. Me about forty times. Deston, maybe ten." He picked up the rasp. "Never your dad. And he was the worst. He was the one making us laugh. Every time. The day all three of us got sent up together, the day in your story, that was only because the choirmaster knew it was him and couldn't prove it, and gave up, and took the whole back row."

"Why not?"

"Because," said Gabe, "he was always looking somewhere else by the time the choirmaster turned round. Every time. Like he knew. Like he could feel the turn coming before it started." He rasped, slowly. "We thought it was luck. For three years. We thought he was just lucky."

Alea sat on the stone lip of the hearth with her knees up and watched the pale wood come away from the block in the shape of her hand.

*The breath before*, she thought. *He was doing it at fifteen. In a choir. In another world.*

She didn't say it. Gabe didn't need her to.

***

In the afternoon, he made her hold it.

Not the sword. Just the two new slabs of wood, held together round nothing, round a stick the width of the tang. He'd cut them and rasped them and cut them again, all morning, and now he put them in her hand and closed her fingers round them and said, "Shut your eyes."

She shut her eyes.

"Now hold it. Not hard. The way you'd hold it in the last minute of a long fight, when your hands are tired. When it's raining."

She held it.

"Where does it hurt?"

She frowned. "It doesn't."

"Where does it nearly hurt?"

She thought about it. Held it. Moved her fingers. "There. Under the first knuckle. On the little finger side."

He took it back. Rasped. Gave it back. "Now?"

"Better."

"Where does it nearly hurt now?"

They did that for an hour. Him rasping, her holding, her eyes shut. Until she held it and there was nothing. Not hurt, not nearly hurt. Just the feeling of something that was exactly the shape of the inside of her hand, the way an old glove is, or a cup you've drunk from every morning for years.

She opened her eyes. Gabe was watching her face.

"That's it," he said.

"How did you know?"

"You stopped frowning," said Gabe.

***

The leather went on last. In the late afternoon, with the sun going down toward the western hills and the class long over and half the fifteen sitting on the stones round the edge of the forge, the way the town's children had sat round the king. Watching. Not saying anything.

Gabe fitted the new slabs to the tang, and pinned them, with new pins, by hand, with the smallest punch from the king's leather roll, tapping so softly that Alea could hardly hear it. He laid the sharkskin over them, rough side out, and glued it with the stuff that smelled of fish and horses. And then he took the old leather out of its pan of water, dark and soft and heavy, still curled into its spiral, and began to wrap.

Round and round. Over the sharkskin. Tighter than it had ever been wrapped. More times round. Each turn laid over the last at exactly the same angle, a finger's width further down, pulled snug, smoothed flat with his thumb. Slowly. Without talking. The whole forge silent but for the lake, and the hens, and somewhere up the road somebody's dog.

Her father came down from the cabin at some point and stood at the edge of the sailcloth, behind the others. Alea saw him. She didn't look round. She watched Gabe's hands, and the old leather going round and round, a strip of a saddle cut off by a boy of nineteen in a place past where the maps stop, going round a grip cut in the shape of his daughter's hand.

It came to the end. There was a little left over. More than there'd been on her father's grip, Gabe said, because the grip was narrower, so the leather went further. He didn't cut it off. He tucked the end under the last turn, and bound it with a thin cord, and tied the cord in a knot she'd seen before. A fisherman's knot. One her father tied on every line.

She looked up at Gabe.

"He taught me that one," said Gabe. "Years ago. On a beach. He said it was the only knot he knew that didn't slip." He didn't look at her father. "I always thought it was a stupid thing to know. I've used it every day for a year."

He held the sword out to her, across the anvil.

Flat. On both hands. The way she'd given it to him.

***

She took it.

And knew.

It wasn't lighter. Nothing about it was lighter. It was the same black slab, the same weight that had pulled at Gabe's arms and made the strongest of the fifteen set his feet. But her hand went round the grip, and her fingers met. All the way round. The first time since the autumn. The old leather, tight and dark, over the rough sharkskin, over the pale wood in the shape of her own closed hand. It didn't move in her grip. It didn't slip. It sat there in her hand as if it had grown there.

And the blade felt it.

She felt the blade feel it. Through her hand, through the leather, through the wood, through the tang, into the long black folded iron that had stood over her mother's grave for eight years. The stillness in it, the waiting, the held breath that Gabe had felt that morning, and that she'd felt under her palm.

It let go.

Not much. Not so that anyone watching would see. Just a breath. Like the lake at dawn when the first wind comes and the surface goes from glass to water. Something in the black blade that had been waiting since the autumn, since a forge and a king and a fold, settled into her hand, and stopped waiting.

"Oh," said Alea.

She didn't decide to move. She stepped out from under the sailcloth onto the open shingle, and the fifteen on the stones scrambled out of her way, and she swung.

The first form. The one her father had taught her at thirteen with a stick of willow. The one she'd done ten thousand times with a practice sword and every morning for five weeks with Astra, too slow, too heavy, her hands burning, her grip sliding at the end of every stroke.

It went round.

It went round like the stroke of an oar. Like the arc of a cast line. Black, and heavy, and exactly where she'd sent it, and back to her hand at the end without a slip, without a jolt, the way a thing comes back that wants to. The air hummed behind it. The pebbles at her feet jumped.

She stood there on the shingle with Astra in her hands, breathing.

Behind her, nobody said anything.

Then Calvin said, "*What*," the way he'd said it when Azac made a ring in the stones. And Xola started clapping. And Gabe, under the sailcloth, sat down very suddenly on the stone lip of the hearth with his elbows on his knees, the way he did when his legs decided for him.

Alea opened her window. She didn't mean to. Her hand just did it, the way everyone's did now.

It had said twelve that morning.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she closed it and turned round, Astra in her hands, and looked for her father at the edge of the sailcloth.

He wasn't looking at the sword.

He was looking at Gabe. Sitting on the hearth with his face in his big hands.

***

"Thank you," said Alea.

She said it to Gabe, later, when the others had gone down to the fire and it was just the two of them under the sailcloth, putting the tools away. The king's leather roll. The two knives. The rasp. The cloth with her father's old wood in it, which Gabe put into her hands without a word.

"Don't," said Gabe.

"I'm going to."

"I know." He tied the thong on the leather roll. "Your dad's going to try too. I'm going to tell him the same thing. *Don't make it weird.*" He smiled, a little. "Rachael's line. She'll want it back."

"Why did you sit down?"

Gabe didn't answer straight away. He put the roll on the bench, and stood there for a moment with his hand on it.

"When you swung it," he said. "The first time. It went round the way he swings things. That's all." He was quiet. "I've watched him do it on the shingle every morning for five weeks. That stroke. And it isn't the same stroke at all, yours. You're smaller and your hands are different and you stand differently and it's *yours*. But it went round the same." He looked at the lake. "Like a song you know, sung by somebody new. The same notes. A different voice."

Alea held the cloth with the old wood in it against her chest.

"He sings it to me," she said. "Your song. When I was little. All three parts."

"I know," said Gabe. "You told us."

"He always sang your part properly," said Alea. "The low one. Every time. He said if he didn't, you'd know somehow, from the other side of the world, and you'd be cross."

Gabe laughed. It came out wet.

"I would've been," he said.

***

Maren came down the lake road at dusk, with a paper.

Alea saw her from the fire. A guide in a dark coat coming down the road with something in her hand and a face Alea knew very well: the face Maren wore when she had something to say and had decided to say it plainly. Rule two. Maren had never read the rules, couldn't read the English, didn't know they existed. She'd been following rule two her whole life anyway.

She came across the shingle and stopped in front of Alea, and held out the paper.

It was a notice. Printed, the way they printed things in the south, with a woodcut at the top of a man on a horse with a lance, and a lot of words underneath in very black ink. Alea had seen ones like it on the guild board in the market, every spring, faded, from years before. She'd never once looked at one properly.

*THE LISTS AT VALCREST. MIDSUMMER.*

*By leave of His Majesty: the Tournament of the Thirty-Five.*

*And, as at every midsummer since the Lists were founded: the FLEDGLING LISTS, open to any adventurer of copper or iron rank under twenty years, of any tier and any path, from any guild in the kingdom, upon the mark of their guild.*

*Entries to the Master of the Lists by the eve of midsummer.*

And at the bottom, in different ink, in a big looping hand that leaned so far to the right it was nearly lying down:

*Every guild in the kingdom has had one of these. I sent them myself. I want them ALL. — L.*

Alea read it twice.

She looked up at Maren.

"Kestra brought it," said Maren. "In her saddlebag. With the king's letter. She said the Master of the Lists had pressed a stack of them on every rider leaving Valcrest for a month, and she'd forgotten she had it until she unpacked." She paused. "I don't think she forgot."

"Copper," said Alea.

"You're copper," said Maren. "You've been copper for years. You never once asked to be tested for iron." She looked at Alea steadily. "You're seventeen. Any tier. Any path. Any guild in the kingdom, upon the mark of their guild." She took a stub of pencil out of her coat. "Every guide the Advisor sent out is sworn to the guild. I can make the mark."

Alea looked down at the paper.

At the woodcut of the man on the horse. At *any tier and any path*. At the loop of the Master's hand. She thought about the sand at Valcrest, which she'd never seen. About thirty-five people standing on it. About a king in the stands who'd measured her hands and said *you'll be cursing it for a year*.

About her father, in the stands, watching.

"You'd have to put a name," said Maren quietly. "On the entry. And a guild. And a sponsor."

"Alea," said Alea. "Wrenhaven. You."

"And a father," said Maren. "Fledgling entries ask for one. In case."

The fire had gone quiet behind her. Alea could feel them listening. All of them. She didn't look round.

She thought about it for a long time. Long enough that Maren shifted her weight. Long enough that somebody at the fire, Calvin probably, started to say something and was shushed.

She thought about being his. The Black Blade's daughter. The Hero of the Redmarch's girl. She thought about every person on the sand at Valcrest, every cadet and every noble's son and every fledgling from every guild in the kingdom, looking at her across the Lists and knowing whose she was, and fighting that instead of her. Losing to that instead of her. Or beating it, and going home and telling everyone they'd beaten the Black Blade's daughter.

She thought about her mother, who'd never told anyone anything until she was ready, and then told everyone everything all at once, at the worst possible moment.

"No father," said Alea.

Maren looked at her.

"Put *Alea, of Wrenhaven, copper*," said Alea. "Sponsored by Maren, guild-sworn guide." She took a breath. "And under *father*, put nothing. Leave it blank."

"It'll look strange."

"Lots of fledglings haven't got one."

"You have."

"I know," said Alea. "I'm going to tell them myself. If I win. At the end." She looked down at Astra in her hands, at the old leather and the fisherman's knot. "Not before. I want to win something that's mine first. Not his. Not Mum's. Mine."

***

Maren wrote it.

She sat down on a log by the fire with the notice on her knee and the stub of pencil, and wrote in the boxes at the bottom in her square careful hand. *Alea. Wrenhaven. Copper. Aura. Tempered.* She paused at *Tempered*, and looked up at Alea, and Alea nodded, and she wrote it. *Sponsor: Maren, guild-sworn guide.* And then she came to *father*, and stopped, and looked across the fire.

At the man sitting on a log on the other side of it, with his daughter's porridge bowl still in his hand from that morning because nobody had taken it off him. Who had been sitting there the whole time. Who hadn't said one word.

Alea looked at him too.

He looked back at her across the fire for a long moment. His face did something. She couldn't hold on to it; nobody could. But she'd been reading him for seventeen years, the shoulders, the breath, the stillness, and she knew what it was doing.

Then he nodded. Once.

*Let her.*

It was what he'd written to the Advisor, in the first weeks after the summoning, when the Advisor wrote and asked about a fox girl bringing fish to a militia hall. Two words. Alea had found the copy in his desk that winter, and asked, and he'd told her. *He asked if he should stop you. I said let her.*

Maren left the box blank.

She folded the notice and put it in her coat, and stood up, and looked at Alea.

"Kestra rides south at dawn," she said. "She'll take it. It'll be on the Master's table in five days." She paused. "And then there's no taking it back."

"I know," said Alea.

***

That night she sat at the top of the stairs in the dark, the way she'd sat when she was small and couldn't sleep, with Astra across her knees.

Below her, on the floor of the cabin, Gabe and Deston and Xav were asleep in their heap. Kestra was in her father's bed, over her objections, snoring very faintly, which Alea suspected she would deny. Her father wasn't anywhere. He'd be on the jetty. He was always on the jetty.

She ran her thumb along the grip. Over the old leather. Over the fisherman's knot at the end. Under it, very faintly, she could feel the roughness of the sharkskin, and under that the shape of the wood. The shape of her own hand.

She thought about the notice in Maren's coat, going south at dawn in a swordswoman's saddlebag. *Alea. Wrenhaven. Copper.* And a blank box.

She thought about the stands at Valcrest. About her father sitting in them, with his rod across his knees probably, because he'd bring it, he'd bring it everywhere, and nobody would know. About the king beside him. About thirty-five people from another world, most of whom didn't know yet that a girl called Alea had grown up in a cabin on a lake listening to stories about them every night. About Leaka, who'd teased him about a name.

About a sandy floor, and a black sword, and a box with nothing in it.

She thought: *Mum would have hated this. She'd have hated not telling.*

And then: *No, she wouldn't. She'd have done exactly this. She'd have waited till the very end, and then told everyone everything all at once, in one breath, at the worst possible moment.*

Alea smiled in the dark.

She leaned Astra against the wall at the foot of the stairs, where she could see it from the loft. And went up the ladder. And got into bed. And lay there for a long time with her cold hands under her cheek, the way her mother used to sleep, feeling the shape of the grip still in her palm, the way you feel a cup after you've put it down.

She was asleep before her father came in from the jetty.

She didn't hear him stop at the foot of the stairs, and stand there in the dark for a long time, looking at the black sword leaning against the wall. At the old leather. At the knot.

She didn't hear him say, very softly, to nobody, in English:

"Look at her, Diwa. Look at our girl."
