The other half took three days.

Ars had thought it would be quicker. It was only going to be a stone. A broad flat face, to stand at the head of a grave. You heated it, you flattened it, you were done. That was what he'd thought.

Severin had looked at him as if he'd suggested shoeing a horse with a rock.

"It's going to stand on the Redmarch," he said, "in the wind and the rain and the frost, for longer than either of us is alive. Longer than the girl. Longer than the kingdom, maybe." He laid the broad half of the sword on the anvil and looked at it. "It's going to stand over her. It isn't a *stone*. It's the last thing anyone will ever make for her." He picked up the small hammer. "We're going to make it properly."

So they made it properly.

***

The point went into the ground. That was the king's idea, and the moment he said it, Ars couldn't think how it could ever have been anything else.

The point half was the broad end of the old blade, where it had widened before it narrowed, with the old point at the bottom, the one that had gone into the red earth over her eight years ago and stayed there. Severin didn't cut it off. He kept it. He drew it out longer and narrower, a long black spike, so it would go deep into the hole where the sword had stood, the same hole, and hold, the way the sword had held.

And above the spike, he made the face.

He spread it. Day after day, a finger's width at a time, *ting, ting, ting*, with Ars striking for the big blows and Alea on the bellows and Garrow standing at the king's elbow with his mouth open. He spread the broad end of the blade out under the hammer until it was nearly as wide as it was tall, and flat, and smooth, a long gentle shield-shape with a rounded top, like the headstones in the churchyard in Wrenhaven, only black. Only iron. Only that iron, folded a thousand times by somebody nobody knew, a long way east, a very long time ago.

On the second evening he quenched it in the lake, the way they'd quenched Alea's, and the lake screamed again, and the town on the field wall flinched again, and cheered.

On the third morning he ground it smooth on Garrow's grindstone, which Garrow had brought down the lake road himself on a handcart, and polished the face with sand and oil until it didn't shine, because it never shone, but until the light lay on it differently. Softer. Like light on still water.

Then he put the hammer down, and looked at it for a long time, and stepped back from the anvil.

"There," he said. "Now it's hers."

And he looked at Ars.

***

Ars had known since the first morning what he'd write on it.

He'd known since before that, probably. Since the grave. Since he'd sat with his back against the cairn on the anniversary with the sword across his knees and told her everything, the summoned and the tree and the chat and the year he'd spent being a coward about it, and had come, at the end, to the hole at the head of the cairn where the sword had stood for eight years, and thought: *she should have a stone. A proper one. With her name on it.*

He'd never put her name on anything. Not on the sword. Not on the cairn. He'd scratched four words into the iron the morning after, kneeling in the red, and none of them had been her name. He'd told Alea, years later, that it was the only thing he could think of that wasn't *sorry*.

He thought of something else, now.

"How do you cut letters in iron?" he said.

Severin looked at him. Then he went to the roll of tools on the shingle, and knelt, and took out something Ars had never seen: a small chisel, very fine, no longer than a finger, with a point like a needle and a handle worn smooth by fifty years of a smith's hand.

"Properly?" said the king.

"Properly."

"Then you'll need this," said Severin, and held it out. "And a small hammer. And more patience than you've got." He sat down on the anvil stump beside the hen. "I'll show you how to hold it. And then I'll go away, and you'll do it on your own. That's how it's done."

***

He showed him how to hold it.

Not like the hot-cut, with the whole of the sea pushed into the edge. Lightly. Between the thumb and two fingers, the way you'd hold a pen. The point set at an angle to the iron, not straight down. The little hammer tapping, not striking. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* Moving the point a hair's breadth at a time along the line, so that the iron came away in a fine curl, like wood from a plane.

"Don't put anything into it," said Severin. "Not aura. Not anger. Not anything. Just your hands." He watched Ars try it on a scrap. "Slower. You're always too fast. You were too fast at twenty-two and you're too fast now." He watched a little longer. "Better. That's a line. Now do it a thousand times, the same, and that's a letter."

Then he got up off the stump, and picked up the hen, and walked away up the shingle toward the shed, and didn't look back.

Ars sat down on the shingle in front of the headstone, where it leaned against the anvil stump, with the fine chisel in one hand and the little hammer in the other.

He didn't write in the language of this world.

He thought about it. He'd thought about it all three days. He'd written in it for thirty years, now. His books, his letters, *let them* on market paper. Anybody in Wrenhaven could read it. Anybody on the Redmarch who came past the grave, a soldier, a chaplain, a trader, could stop and read her name and know who she'd been.

But she'd never been anybody's but his. Not really. Not that part of her.

***

He'd taught her the letters.

The first winter. Before Alea, before the war, before any of it, when she was a healer at the garrison with cold hands who'd stitched him up three times in one month and finally asked him, on the third, *why do you keep doing this to yourself*. And he'd told her. Not all of it. Not then. But enough. And she'd sat on the end of his cot in the infirmary with her cold hands in her lap and listened, and hadn't laughed, and at the end she'd said, *Show me. How you write, where you're from.*

So he'd shown her. The letters. Twenty-six of them, scratched on a slate with a nail, in the infirmary at night, by lamplight, while the garrison slept. She'd learned them in a week. She'd been cleverer than him, always.

She'd only ever really used them for three words.

She wrote them in the flour on the cabin table, after. When she'd been baking and he'd been out on the lake, and she wanted him to know something when he came in. In the frost on the window, in winter. On the back of his hand once, in ink, when he was asleep, so that he'd find it in the morning. Three words. Her name, and his, and one more.

He set the point of the fine chisel against the black face of the stone, at the top, on the left.

And began.

***

It took him all day.

He didn't hurry. He couldn't. Every time he tried, the point skidded, or went too deep, or the line wandered, and he had to stop, and breathe, and start again. Severin had been right. He had no patience for this. He'd never had any patience for anything that wasn't fishing.

So he fished.

That was how he did it, in the end. He sat on the shingle in front of the black stone and did it the way he waited for a pike on a still evening: not thinking about the fish. Thinking about nothing. Letting the tap of the little hammer be the only thing in the world. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* The fine curl of iron coming away. The line going on, a hair at a time, the way a line goes out across water. And when it was done, the next. And the next.

Alea came down at noon with bread, and saw what he was doing, and put the bread down on the anvil stump without a word, and went away again.

The town on the field wall watched him all afternoon. He could feel them. Two hundred people, not making a sound. Rowan at the front. Nobody came down.

On the jetty, Kestra sat with her book closed on her knee, and didn't open it once.

***

When he finished, the sun was going down over the lake.

He sat back on his heels on the shingle, with the chisel loose in his fingers, and looked at it.

Black iron. A long gentle shield-shape with a rounded top, and the light lying on it softly, like light on still water. And across the top of the face, in letters almost nobody born in this world could read, cut clean and even and deep, each one the same as the last, the way Severin said a letter should be:

*TO DIWA, MY LOVE*

He looked at it for a long time.

All the letters were the same depth. All the lines were clean. He'd done it properly, the way a smith would have. Not the way he'd done it eight years ago, kneeling in the red with the point of a knife, crooked, some deeper than others.

He picked up the fine chisel again. And set it, very carefully, in the first stroke of the *L*. And went over it once more. A little deeper. The way he'd gone over one letter twice eight years ago, because the first time it hadn't been deep enough.

Then he put the chisel down.

***

"What does it say?"

Severin was standing behind him on the shingle. Ars didn't know how long he'd been there. Long enough, by his face. He was looking at the black stone, and his eyes were sliding off the letters the way everyone's did, and coming back, and sliding off again.

"I can't read it," said the king. "My eyes won't stay on it."

"Nobody can," said Ars. "Nobody here."

He heard Alea come down the shingle behind the king. She stopped beside him. She looked at the stone, and her eyes didn't slide off it. They went along the line of letters, one by one, slowly, the way he'd taught her to read them, on the slate, the winter she was thirteen. And her lips moved.

"To Diwa," said Alea. Very quietly. "My love."

Severin didn't say anything for a long time.

"She could read it," he said at last. "Couldn't she. Your healer. She could read those."

"She could."

"And the girl."

"And the girl."

The king nodded slowly. He looked at the stone a moment longer, with his eyes sliding off it and coming back. Then he put his big scarred hand on Ars's shoulder, once, heavily, the way he had on the red ground eight years ago, and took it away again.

"That's the right language," he said, and went to bank the fire.

***

That night they sat on the end of the jetty, the two of them.

It was the king's last night. He'd said so at supper. *Tomorrow I'll go back and be a king again. The Council will have noticed by now that I don't hunt.* He'd eaten Alea's fish stew and told Kestra to sit, three times, and given the hen a piece of bread, and then he'd got up and walked out along the jetty in the dark and sat down on the end of it with his boots over the water. And Ars had gone and sat beside him.

It was cold. Properly cold, now. The first real cold of the autumn, coming down off the hills in the dark, with the smell of snow behind it, somewhere a long way north. The stars were out over the lake. The wrong stars, the way they always were, doubled in the black water. Behind them on the shingle, the forge was banked low and red under its sailcloth roof, and beside it, leaning against the anvil stump, two shapes. One long and black, with a grip. One broad and black, with a spike.

Up on the hill, above the town, five candles were awake. They always were, lately.

The king didn't say anything for a long time. Neither did Ars. They'd sat like this before, once. On the red ground. They'd been good at it then, too.

***

"Kharvos," said Severin at last.

"Yes."

"They're coming. Not this year. Next, maybe. The year after." The king looked out at the dark water. "They've been counting Cinderhold for a year. They've been hunting the summoned all summer. They sent fifty scouts and three hunters across the red for your sword." He was quiet. "That's not a probe. That's a war that hasn't started yet."

"I know."

"And the west." Severin's voice went flatter. "Fenwick's eleven pages. A town over the Greyspines with no smoke, and the turned standing in rings round a circle the size of a fort. And something at the far end that doesn't have any light at all." He shook his head slowly. "Two fronts. Halvard says the demon king isn't coming alone this time. I didn't believe him. I believe him now."

Ars didn't say anything.

"I've got the thirty-five," said the king. "Halvard's net. Nine at the first tier, a year in, and one of them can make a window talk across the kingdom, and one of them can see the turned, and one of them's a tree." Something that might have been a laugh. "They're good. They're better than I thought. They're not ready. They won't be ready for years." He turned his head and looked at Ars in the dark. "I've got one Ascendant. Me. Sixty-four. I've got the Advisor, Comet, who poured half his light into a floor. And I've got you."

"You've got a fisherman."

"I've got a fisherman," said the king, "who cut three hunters in half on the Redmarch last month without looking round. Halvard's chaplain wrote it all down. Three pages." He looked back at the water. "You could come back."

"Not yet," said Ars.

"It was *no*, once."

"It was."

The king was quiet for a while.

"What changed?" he said.

***

Ars opened his window.

He didn't decide to. He just found it open, on his knees, in the dark, the way he'd found it open on the end of this jetty most nights for a year. The channel scrolling, faint and quiet. Mashi, on his wall: *three heartbeats. still no faint.* Maigo: *MASHI THAT'S FOUR TONIGHT.* Mashi: *three.* Maigo: *I COUNTED FOUR.*

He scrolled past it. Up. To the top of the window, past everything, to the one line he hadn't shown anyone. Not Alea. Not Rowan. Not anyone.

He'd looked at it on the night of the cut. After the hour on the jetty, after he'd gone back and taken the bellows and worked them slowly, the way the king said, until the iron was ready. He'd looked at it in bed that night, in the dark, and lain there for a long time, and not slept.

He turned the window round, in his hands, so that Severin could see.

***

The king didn't say anything.

He looked at it. Ars watched him look at it: the way his eyes went to the number and stopped, and stayed, and didn't slide off, because it was in the window's language and not Ars's, and anyone connected to the first window could read it. He watched the king read it once, and then again, and then a third time, as if it might change.

"Halfway," said Ars.

His own voice sounded strange to him. He'd never said it out loud.

"I'm halfway," he said. "To the ninth." He looked at the number. "And I can feel it growing, Severin. Not just the number. Me. I can feel it. Every week. Like a fire somebody's bringing up a little at a time." He was quiet for a moment. "I haven't felt anything grow in me since the Redmarch. I didn't think I would again. I thought I'd stopped."

The king still didn't say anything.

"It was forty," said Ars. "The morning after. When you came and sat with me. I looked at it then, for the last time, I thought, and it said forty, and I thought: *that's as far as I go*." He looked out at the lake. "It said forty for six years. I stopped looking. Then last autumn it moved. One. And then again. And again." He closed the window. "Fifty, since you brought down the hammer."

***

"How?" said Severin.

His voice was rough. Ars had never heard it like that.

"Fishing," said Ars.

The king looked at him.

And then laughed. Out loud, on the end of the jetty, in the dark, a great startled bark of a laugh that went out over the lake and came back off the far hills. He put his face in his big scarred hands and laughed until his shoulders shook.

"*Fishing*," he said, through his fingers.

"Fishing," said Ars. "And pumpkins. And hens that hate me." He looked down at his own hands. "And not doing anything. For a long time. And then doing small things, where nobody could see." He was quiet. "Fish in a basket every morning. Some coats. A few lessons, sent up a hill in somebody else's mouth." He thought about a ring of flowers, and a young tree, and a small cracked voice saying *I hope that's okay*. "Letting people help me. That's a new one. That's the stones. That moved it too." He looked at the black shapes on the shingle behind them. "Putting things down."

The king took his hands away from his face.

"Twelve plough blades," he said.

Ars looked at him.

***

Severin told him.

About Brakewater. The forge where he'd been born, on a pile of sacking in the corner. His father, honest and slow. The long war at twenty, and the climb, tier after tier, and everyone saying *before he's fifty*. And the winter of his thirty-ninth year, on leave, the month his father died, and an order on the forge wall in his father's crooked hand. Twelve plough blades for the Ashdown farm. Due by the thaw. Half paid.

"I'd never told anyone," said the king. "Twenty-five years. The songs say it was a battle. I let them." He looked at the water. "It was twelve plough blades for a farmer called Ashdown. One a day. In a dead man's forge, in the middle of winter. Not thinking about the war. Not thinking about anything. Just the iron." He was quiet a long time. "On the twelfth night, the world went quiet. And then it bent."

Ars sat very still on the end of the jetty.

"Plough blades," he said.

"Plough blades."

"For a farmer called Ashdown."

"He's dead now. His grandson still uses them." The king's mouth twitched. "I checked. Once. Years later. In disguise. They're very good plough blades."

And Ars laughed. And the king laughed. And the two strongest men in Aurelan sat on the end of a fisherman's jetty in the dark, laughing at plough blades and fishing rods until they had to wipe their eyes, while behind them on the shingle the hen woke up and complained.

***

"There's never been two of us," said Severin, when they'd stopped. "At once. Halvard's looked. Four hundred years of records. One at a time, if that. Most generations, none." He looked at Ars. "Do you understand? If you go up, there'll be two of us. For the first time. With a war coming on two sides."

"I understand."

"Then hear me." The king turned on the end of the jetty, so that he was facing Ars properly, and his voice changed. Ars knew that voice. He'd heard it in a tent, the night before the Redmarch. The voice of a man who didn't give orders often, and meant every one.

"Don't come back to court," said Severin. "Don't come back to the capital. Don't come to Cinderhold. Don't come anywhere." He held Ars's eyes. "Whatever you're doing here, keep doing it. Fish. Grow your pumpkins. Feed your hens that hate you. Put your fish in your basket. Let people carry you stones." He was quiet a moment. "That's an order. It's the only one I'll ever give you. I'll never give you another."

Ars looked at him for a long time.

"I'll obey it," he said.

"You've never obeyed anything in your life."

"I'm very good at doing what I'm told," said Ars. "Ask your striker."

The king laughed again, shortly, and turned back to the water.

***

They sat for a while longer.

Then Severin said, without looking round, "They're up there."

Ars didn't need to ask who. He could feel them. Five candles on the hill above the town, awake, close together, at an upper window. They'd been there every night since the king came. Watching the forge. Watching the jetty. Watching the two of them sitting on the end of it in the dark.

"I know," said Ars.

"All five. Every night. Kestra says they look at the lake road like a door they've decided not to knock on yet." The king was quiet. "The little one on the hard path told her that. *We just wait.*"

Ars didn't say anything.

"Halvard thinks they're yours," said Severin.

The jetty was very quiet. The water lapped at the posts below them.

"He's thought so since the summoning," said the king. "He's never said it to me in those words. He doesn't have to. Thirty-five people who all know each other, and a net that caught everything tangled in one sword." He looked at the black water. "And a fisherman who looked at each of them on a market road, the first day, as if he were counting them. And sends them fish. And didn't ask Halvard who they were. Not once."

Ars didn't answer.

"Halvard never asked where you came from," said Severin. "In twenty years. A garrison boy with a window, and words nobody had ever heard, and a sword from a place past where the maps stop." He turned his head and looked at Ars in the dark. "I've wondered for twelve. I've never asked either." He was quiet a moment. "I'm not going to be the first."

Ars looked at him for a long time.

"Ask me in the spring," he said.

The king held his eyes. Then he nodded, once, slowly, the way he'd nodded on the red ground eight years ago, and in the hen-yard on the first evening, and on the floor beside the sword.

"In the spring, then," he said.

***

He wrote the letter to Aldous before he went to bed.

At the table, with the split-nibbed pen he'd been meaning to fix for three years, while Kestra sat on the step and Alea dried the bowls and pretended not to read over his shoulder, and read over his shoulder.

*Father Aldous.*

*This comes with her stone. Half of the old blade, made properly, by someone who knows how. The point goes into the hole where the sword stood. It's the same point. It knows the way.*

*I'd like the five who carried the sword to carry this. The last stretch, by hand, across the red. They started it. They should finish.*

*There are stones with it. A great many small ones, in a sack. Children carried them down to the lake for the forge, one each, every morning, and I said thank you for every one and couldn't think what to do with them. I've thought. Set them round the foot of hers. In a ring, inside the flowers, by the tree. So she knows a whole town carried her stone.*

*Thank you for the markers. All of them.*

*Ars.*

He read it over twice. Then he folded it, and sealed it, and wrote *Aldous, Cinderhold* on the outside, and sat there for a moment longer with his hand on it.

Alea put the last bowl away.

"Dad," she said.

"Mm."

"What did you show him? On the jetty. I saw you turn your window round."

He looked up at her.

She was standing by the hearth with the drying cloth in her hands, sixteen, Tempered, with her mother's eyes and his stubbornness and a black sword called Astra leaning against the wall at the foot of the stairs. Looking at him the way Diwa used to. Deciding whether he was worth the trouble.

He opened his window again, and turned it round.

She read it. He watched her read it. He watched her read it twice, the way the king had, and then a third time. He watched her face.

"Halfway," said Alea.

"Halfway."

She put the drying cloth down very carefully on the table. Then she came round the table, and put her arms round his neck from behind, the way she had when she was small, and held on, and put her face in his hair. And didn't say anything at all for a long time.

"You're going to be insufferable," she said eventually, muffled.

"Probably."

"*Fishing*."

"You were listening."

"Everyone was listening. The whole *lake* was listening. You were both *laughing*." She held on tighter. "Dad."

"Mm."

"I'm so glad," said Alea.

He put his hand up over hers, on his shoulder, and held it there. And outside, on the step, very softly, a pencil moved across a page for the first time in three days, and stopped, and moved again.
