The decree came on a Tuesday, nailed to the door of the Lists by a royal herald in a blue cloak, and by noon the whole of Valcrest was in mourning.

Chris read it in the crowd outside the gate, standing on tiptoe to see over a forest of heads. It was short. Royal decrees, he'd learned, were always short; the longer the news, the shorter the paper.

*By order of His Majesty King Severin. The tournament of the summoned, announced for the turning of the year, is postponed until such time as the borders of the kingdom are secure. The summoned will remain at their postings and continue their training. Long live the king.*

That was it. Four lines. Somewhere behind him a woman was actually crying. A man near the front had torn the paper horns off his little son's head and thrown them in the gutter, and the boy was howling.

Chris read the decree three times. Then he went back to the Broken Lance, where he found Lucan sitting alone at the end of the bar with his face in his hands and his loud red cloak pooled round his ankles like a dropped flag.

"It's fine," said Lucan, without looking up. "I'm fine. Everything's fine."

"You don't look fine."

"I'm *devastated,*" said Lucan into his palms. "I'm completely devastated. I had a bracket. I had a mixed round. I had *Drew.*" He lifted his head. His eyes were red. "Do you know how long I've waited for a tournament like this? Fifteen years. Fifteen years of farm boys and guardsmen and nobles' second sons who couldn't hit a barn with a lance. And then you lot arrive, and I think, *finally*. *Finally*, something worth watching." He put his face back in his hands. "And now *borders.*"

Chris sat down on the stool next to him.

"The borders are pretty bad," he said gently.

"I *know* the borders are bad. I read the letters too. Everyone reads your letters. Nobody can understand a word of them, but they read them anyway." Lucan sniffed. "That's not the point. The point is it's not *fair.*"

"No," Chris agreed. "It isn't."

They sat there for a while.

***

The decree wasn't a surprise. Not really. Chris had seen it coming for weeks, the way you see a crash coming in a program when the memory starts climbing and nobody's freeing anything.

It was the letters. They'd been coming in from every corner of the kingdom since the autumn, and every one was worse than the one before. Coldwater. The scouts. The hut on the hill, with the shoes lined up against the wall and the circle burned into the floor. Hild's wall of light. And then, last week, from Kingsferry: the midwinter market. Three people with no threads, walking through a crowd of thousands toward Rachael. A man whose hand bled black when she cut it. An empty coat on the edge of the quay, with no lining, shaped like someone's memory of a coat.

Chris had read that one out loud at the table in the Broken Lance, the way he read all of them now. When he'd finished, nobody had said anything for a long time. Then Newish had said, quite quietly, "She *cut* him, though," and something in her voice had made Chris look up. Newish, who ragebaited crowds, who'd caught a cabbage and held it over her head like a trophy, had tears on her face.

So no. The decree wasn't a surprise. You didn't hold a tournament in an arena with eight thousand people in it when there were things walking around in crowds wearing other people's faces.

He still understood how Lucan felt.

***

He had the notebook, still. The one he'd started in the autumn, the night Winter asked for everyone's numbers. *ALL GROUPS*, it said across the top, in his small square hand. Every letter that came in, he'd go through it with a pencil and copy out every number he could find, and put it in a column under the right name, with the date.

It had turned into something he hadn't expected. He'd thought it would be a tally. A way of keeping track.

It had turned into a map.

You could see it, if you laid the pages side by side. Thirty-five columns of numbers creeping upward, week by week, slowly, unevenly. Some of them in steady lines: PenguChi, who climbed exactly one percent a week as if she'd decided to. Some of them in jumps: Rachael, who'd gone up four the night she held the dockworker together and three more after the night market. Some of them stuck for a month and then lurching forward: Tiger, whose number had sat on eight for five weeks and then, in one afternoon with Hild, jumped to twelve.

And one column, on its own, way out at the top of the page.

[ Thea · 96% ]

Ninety-six. As of the last letter. Ninety-six percent of the way to the first tier, the first of the thirty-five to get anywhere close.

He'd written to her, after that one. A long letter, the kind he wrote her most weeks now: half code, half nonsense, the two of them trading guesses about the lattice under the windows like a pair of engineers trying to reverse-engineer a machine they'd found in a field. *Every window inherits from a first window,* she'd written to him, months ago. He thought about that every day. Thirty-five instances of one class. And somewhere, somebody had written the original.

*When you get there,* he'd written to her, at the end of the last letter, *look at the root. See if it changes. I want to know what the first window looks like from one step up.*

She hadn't answered yet. He hoped that meant she was busy.

***

Group 7's own numbers, that night, were slow and steady and nothing to write home about. He wrote them home anyway.

"Twelve," said Hako. "The spear's getting lighter. Ysolde says by spring I'll be able to hold a stance for an hour."

"Twelve," said Newish, and then, with a strange little frown: "and the beat's getting louder. In the bouts. I can hear it before people move now, every time. It's like..." She tapped the table, twice, in a quick uneven rhythm. "Like that. Everybody's got one. A rhythm. And if you hear it, you know when they'll go." She looked at the others. "Is that tempo? Is that what Lucan meant, when he said my path was going to be *tempo?*"

"Probably," said Chris, writing.

"Thirteen," said Ina. "The heat's steadier. I can keep it lit for a minute now, under the mask. Before it was seconds."

"Twelve," said Chris, of himself. "Still twelve. My logs say I'm overtraining. I'm ignoring my logs."

They all looked at Winter.

She'd been quiet all evening. She'd been quiet all week, since the night-market letter. She sat at the end of the table with her silver hair falling over her face and her hands flat on the wood, and she didn't look up.

"Thirteen," she said. And then, very quietly: "I'm going out early tomorrow. To the yard."

Nobody asked why. She went out early every morning. But Chris heard something in her voice he hadn't heard before, and he wrote it down in the margin of his notebook, in his small square hand, without quite knowing why.

*Winter: something's about to happen.*

***

Drew's letter came two days later, from Thornwall.

It was addressed to Lucan, but Lucan brought it to the table at the Broken Lance and made Chris read it out, because, he said, "If I read it myself I'll cry again, and I've cried enough this week to float a barge."

*Lucan,*

*I heard. I'm sorry.*

*I've run eleven tournaments. Something went wrong at every one of them. A server crashed. A team didn't show. Someone's mother called in the middle of a final. Once a whole building lost power.*

*You know what I learned? Something always goes wrong. The job isn't stopping it. The job is still being there, with the bracket in your hand, when it's over.*

*Keep the bracket. I'm keeping mine. On the wall, next to Corvin's maps, where I can see it every day. Every time I look at it, I think: one day all thirty-five of us will be in the same place again. And I'll be running it. And you'll be standing next to me, wearing that cloak, making a speech that's far too long.*

*It's postponed. It isn't cancelled. Those aren't the same thing.*

*Drew.*

Chris finished reading. Lucan was sitting very still, with his red cloak round his shoulders again and his face doing something complicated.

"*Far too long,*" he said finally, with enormous dignity. "My speeches are *exactly* the right length."

Then he took the letter from Chris and folded it very carefully and put it inside his cloak, against his chest. And he got up, and straightened his shoulders, and went out of the Broken Lance with his head up.

"Where's he going?" said Hako.

"To the Lists," said Ina, watching him go. "I think he's going to start planning."

***

That night, after Lucan had gone and the Broken Lance had emptied, Chris found Hako out on the sand.

The Lists were dark and silent, the great tiers of empty seats going up into the night on every side like the inside of a bowl. Somebody had left a single lantern burning by the master's platform. Hako was standing in the middle of the sand with his spear, not training, just standing there, looking at the far end of the arena.

The far angle. The spot where, in the first bout, he'd stepped into the gap.

Chris walked out and stood beside him. For a while neither of them said anything.

"I keep thinking about it," said Hako eventually. "That bout. The far side. How it felt like somebody should be standing there." He shifted the spear on his shoulder. "You said that's exactly where someone should be. And then you went all quiet. And Newish went all quiet. And nobody's ever told me why."

Chris looked at the empty sand.

"That was his spot," he said. "Ice's. In the trios. Me and Winter and him, or me and Newish and him. He always took the far angle. He'd sit on it for a minute without moving, and talk the whole time, making terrible jokes, and then take one shot and win the round." He was quiet a moment. "After he died, we stopped playing trios. Me and Newish tried once. We kept leaving the gap. It felt like walking with a limp."

Hako didn't say anything for a long moment.

"He taught me," he said quietly. "CS. Apex. I only ever played Marvel Rivals before. He just... took me into calls and showed me. Never got annoyed. Never once made me feel stupid." He looked down at his spear. "Eight months. That's all I had. I always thought there'd be more."

"Yeah," said Chris. "Everybody thought that."

"So when I stepped into that spot." Hako's voice wobbled very slightly. "I was standing where he'd stand. Doing what he taught me. Without even knowing."

"You were," said Chris. "You filled it. First time anybody has in three years."

Hako stood there in the middle of the dark arena for a long time, holding his spear, looking at the far end of the sand. Then he wiped his face with the back of his hand, quickly, the way you do when you don't want anyone to see.

"Okay," he said. "Then I'll keep it warm. For when it matters."

Chris didn't ask him what he meant. He wasn't sure Hako knew either. But they stood there together on the empty sand until the lantern burned down, and when they walked back to the inn, Hako was humming, very quietly, something that Chris was almost certain had been in a stream once, a long time ago, with a third voice laughing over the top of it.

***

Chris didn't sleep much that night. He rarely did anymore. He lay on his bed in the little room above the common room and listened to the city outside: the watch calling the hours, a dog barking somewhere, the wind off the plain rattling the shutters. And a little before dawn, he heard Winter's door open, and her footsteps on the stairs, going down.

He got up and went to the window.

The practice yard behind the Lists was just visible from up here: a grey square in the dark, frosted at the edges. As he watched, Winter came out of the side door and walked into the middle of it and stopped, in her coat, with her breath smoking.

And on the fence at the edge of the yard, already sitting there, as if she'd been there all night: Ysolde.

Neither of them said anything. Chris could see that even from up here. Winter just stood in the middle of the empty yard, and Ysolde sat on the fence, and the sky over the plain got slowly paler, grey and then pearl and then the faintest rose.

There was a puddle in the yard, near Winter's feet. Half-frozen already, from the night. A skin of ice round the edges, and black water in the middle.

Winter looked down at it.

She held her hand out over it. Not angry. Chris could tell, somehow, even from the window. Not angry, not frightened, not anything. Just standing there in the cold with her hand out, perfectly still, the way Ysolde had taught her.

For a long time, nothing happened.

Then the black water in the middle of the puddle went white.

Not all at once, the way the stallholder's cup had. Slowly. From the edges inward, a thin feathered skin of frost creeping across the water toward the centre, in fern-shapes and fan-shapes, like breath on a winter window. It met in the middle, and closed, and the puddle was white from edge to edge.

Winter didn't move. She stood there with her hand out over the frozen puddle and her silver hair falling round her face, looking down at what she'd done.

On the fence, Ysolde said one word. Chris couldn't hear it from the window, but he could see her lips. Every teacher in every letter seemed to have a word like it: Kestra's *good*, Petra's *better*. Ysolde's was shorter.

*There.*

Winter looked up at her.

And then, for the first time Chris had ever seen, for the first time since the summoning hall and the soldiers and the swords, Winter smiled. Properly. A real smile, small and startled and enormous, the kind you can't stop once it's started.

Chris stepped back from the window before either of them could look up and see him. It wasn't his to watch, really. It was theirs.

He went back to his bed and sat on the edge of it, and opened his notebook to the *ALL GROUPS* page, and found Winter's column, and next to the thirteen he'd written two nights ago, he added a note in the margin. Not a number. Her number wouldn't change for a while yet. Ysolde had said months. Maybe a year. Maybe longer.

Just a note.

*Did it on purpose.*

Then he looked at the column at the top of the page, the one way out on its own, and the number in it.

Ninety-six.

*Come on, Thea,* he thought. *Come on. Somebody has to go first.*

Outside the window, the sun came up over the plain, and the frost in the yard began to shine.
