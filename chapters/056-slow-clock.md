Thea had been reading the inside of the channel every night since she built it.

Not the messages. Everyone read those. The *inside*. The lines underneath, the ones nobody else could see: the structure she'd written, the shape it had hooked into, the long nested branches of English that ran down and down under every window to the place where they all met. She'd built the channel in an afternoon, in a rush, with Higu beside her, and it had worked, and she'd been so happy that she hadn't looked too closely at *how*.

Now she looked. Every night. Sitting on her bed with her window open and her knees pulled up, reading, the way she'd read code at three in the morning back home: line by line, looking for the thing that didn't make sense.

She'd found a lot of things that didn't make sense. She'd expected that. The windows were old, and strange, and had been written by someone who thought like a gamer but had clearly never written a line of real code in his life; it was all patches on patches, like a house where every owner had added a room. She loved it. She'd have loved it even if it hadn't been magic.

But since the night the moonlight flinched, she'd been looking for one thing in particular.

She didn't know what it was. That was the trouble. She'd sat at the window with Higu that night, with the sealed value in her own window slammed shut like a door in a gale, and looked out at the lamp in the cabin by the lake, and felt something enormous move once and then go still. And she'd thought: *the windows know. Whatever that was, the windows know.* Because the sealed value was in the windows. And it had slammed.

So she read. Every night. Looking for whatever it was the windows knew.

She found it on a hot night at the end of summer. And it wasn't what she was looking for at all.

***

It was in the messages.

Every message in the channel had a little tail on it. She'd known that from the start; she'd written half of them herself. A bit of structure hanging off the end of each line, invisible, telling the channel who'd sent it and in what order. Housekeeping. She'd never looked at it closely. Why would you?

Tonight, bored, tired, at two in the morning, she looked.

There were two numbers in every tail.

The first one she understood at once. It counted. One for every day since... something. She did the sum in her head, and it came out at three hundred and some, and she thought: *since the summoning. It's counting days since we came through.* That made sense. Every message, stamped with the day it was sent. Fine.

The second one didn't count like that.

It was much smaller. And it barely moved. She scrolled back through what was left in the channel, forty lines, a day and a half, and the second number was the same on every line. She went to the very first message she'd ever sent. She couldn't see it anymore; it had scrolled away in the first hour. But she'd kept a copy of the channel's insides, in her head, from the day she built it. She knew what the tails had said that day.

The second number had gone up by thirty-two.

Three hundred and some days, by the first clock. Thirty-two by the second.

She stared at it. She did the sum again. She did it a third time, on her fingers, like a child.

About eleven to one.

*A slow clock*, she thought. *Something in the windows is keeping a slow clock. Eleven of our days to one of its.*

And then, because she was who she was, and it was two in the morning, and she couldn't leave a thing alone, she went down into the second number, under it, to see what it was made of.

It wasn't a number.

It was a *date*.

***

It was written in English. Of course it was. Everything was. A month, spelled out in full, in plain capital letters. A day. A year.

She knew the month. She knew the day. She knew the year. She'd know them anywhere. She'd lived them.

It was the day they'd left.

Not the day they'd *arrived*: that was the first clock, counting from the summoning. This was the other side. This was the day on the other side of the floor. The day she'd sat down to stream, in her room, in another world, and done her usual opener and waved at chat and then the screen had gone white and the floor had gone soft and she'd woken up on cold stone under a painted ceiling.

The window remembered it. It had written it down, the moment they came through. And under it, on the second line, it had been counting forward from it ever since. Slowly. One day for every eleven of theirs.

She looked at today's date. The slow one.

It was the same month. Thirty-two days later.

***

She sat very still on her bed in the dark.

*A month*, she thought.

*At home, it's been a month.*

She'd been here nearly a year. She'd lived a whole year in this world: a summer and an autumn and a winter and a spring and most of another summer. She'd reached the first tier. She'd made friends with a fox girl and a grumpy tree and a man who sold eggs. She'd built a chat in a magic window. She'd cried herself to sleep for her mum maybe a hundred times, imagining her mum doing the same, a year of it, a year of a phone ringing out, a year of police and posters and not knowing.

And at home it had been a month.

She didn't know whether to laugh or be sick. She thought she might do both.

Her mum had been looking for a *month*. Not a year. A month. Thirty-two days. Her mum was still in the first terrible weeks of it, still ringing hospitals, still thinking it was a mistake, still expecting a call. If Thea went home tomorrow, if somebody opened a door tomorrow and she stepped through it, she'd be gone a month. People came back from a month. People came back from a month all the time.

If it took ten years here to reach the way home, it would be less than one there.

She put her hands over her face.

And then, very slowly, from somewhere much further down, another thought came up. Not about her mum. Not about her.

It came up the way the slow clock had: quietly, a little at a time, from underneath. She didn't want it. She pushed it back down. It came up again.

*Eleven to one.*

*Ice died three years ago.*

She took her hands off her face.

***

She didn't go to Higu. She went to all of them.

She knocked on every door on the corridor at half past two in the morning, one after another, until there were five of them in the common room in their nightclothes with one candle lit, and Squiddles asleep on Xola's head, and Waddles asleep on PenguChi's feet, and Gigichi looking at her as if she'd lost her mind.

"I found something," said Thea. "In the windows. I need to tell you. And then I need you to tell me if I'm wrong."

She told them. The tails. The two clocks. The date.

At home it's been a month.

There was a long silence.

Then Xola made a sound, a small high sound like something breaking, and put both hands over her mouth. Squiddles woke up on her head and went a frightened shade of pale lilac and wrapped all his little arms round her ears.

"A *month*," Xola whispered. "Mum's only been... a *month*. She's only been... oh. *Oh.*" Her violet eyes were swimming. "She'll still be... she'll still think I'm... she'll still be *ringing* me, Thea. She'll be ringing my phone. Every night. She'll be..."

She couldn't finish. She started to cry, properly, with her whole body, the way she'd cried on the first day in the hall. Gigichi was beside her before anyone else had moved, with an arm round her, holding on.

Gigichi's own face had gone white. "A month," she said, very flat. "My mum. A month." She didn't cry. She just held on to Xola, and stared at the candle.

Higu hadn't said anything at all.

And PenguChi, sitting cross-legged on the rug with Waddles on her feet, took a long slow breath, and let it out, and began to talk.

She didn't say anything important. Thea couldn't have told you afterwards what she said. Something about how a month wasn't long. How their families were strong. How a month meant there was still time, so much time, more than they'd thought. It didn't matter. It was the voice. PenguChi's voice, slow and soft and steady, the voice that had steadied Xola on the first day in the hall and Thea in the snow, that Maren said was a gift and Bram said was witchcraft. It went into the room like warm water into cold hands. It didn't take anything away. It just held it.

Xola's crying slowed. Gigichi's grip on her loosened, a little. The candle stopped shaking in Thea's hand.

"There's still time," said PenguChi softly, at the end. "That's what it means. It means there's still time to go home. More than anyone thought." She smiled her slow enormous smile. "That's good news. Rule three. It's just very loud good news, and it's two in the morning."

Xola laughed, wetly, into Gigichi's shoulder.

And Higu, sitting very still in the corner with her notebook on her knee, said: "Thea. That isn't all of it. Is it."

***

Thea looked at her.

"No," she said.

Higu nodded slowly. She opened her notebook. Not at the back, where her lists were. At a clean page in the middle. She took out her pencil.

"Ice died three years ago," she said. Calmly. The way she said everything. She wrote it down. *3.* "Three years. At home."

Nobody spoke.

"If it's eleven to one," said Higu, "then three years at home is..." She wrote. Thea watched the pencil move. *3 × 11.* And then, more carefully, because Higu was careful: *closer to 11 and a bit.* "About thirty-four," said Higu. "Here."

She put the pencil down.

"Thirty-four years," she said. "Here. Since he died."

The candle flame stood very straight in the still air.

***

"No," said Gigichi.

They all looked at her.

"No," she said again. "I know what you're doing. I know where you're going. Stop." Her voice was very hard. "He *died*. In Malacca. In his sleep. He didn't come *here*. He couldn't have. He'd be..." She stopped.

"He'd be thirty-four," said Higu quietly. "If he came as a baby. And he'd have a sixteen-year-old daughter if he'd had her at eighteen."

Gigichi's mouth closed.

"I'm not saying it's true," said Higu. "I'm saying it *fits*. For the first time. Everyone's been saying it can't be him because of the timeline. Milli said it. Hana said it. I said it to myself. Every time I got close. *It can't be him. He'd have to have been here for thirty years.*" She looked down at the page. "And he has. That's all this says. It's been thirty-four years here. Somebody from our world could have been here that long. Somebody who died three years ago could have been here that long."

She turned the notebook round, and turned back the pages, to the back. To her lists.

"So I want to do something I should have done months ago," she said. "I want to put everything on the table. Everything. All of us. Everything we've seen and not said. And then we look at it together." She looked round at them, one after another. "And if it's nothing, it's nothing. And if it isn't..."

She didn't finish.

***

They put it all on the table.

It took until dawn.

Higu went first. She read them her lists. *Things Alea says that aren't answers.* Two pages of them, in her small careful handwriting, every one with a date. *My dad says a road's only blocked if you stop on it.* *The fisherman says it'll freeze tonight.* *Dad says.* And then the lines at the very back, under the lists, that she'd never shown anyone.

*The books come from the lake.*

*Alea is his.*

*He has a window.*

"On the full moon," said Higu. "When I reached Spark. My moth went down to the lake on its own. I didn't send it. I saw the jetty. He was sitting at the end of it, at midnight, reading something in the air. The way we read our windows. Scrolling." She swallowed. "And Alea brought him tea, and he closed it. Like this." She flicked two fingers sideways. The gesture they all made, a hundred times a day. "I'm sorry. I should have told you. I wasn't sure."

Thea went next. She told them about the market in the winter, all of it, the bits she'd told and the bits she hadn't. The sealed value beside *Medium: one blade* rattling so hard it hurt, the whole time she stood in front of him. The rod in his hand with something *folded* into it, an edge, she'd Parsed it before she could help it. *I've never been anywhere but here*, and how it had felt like a door being closed very gently by someone who'd done it many times.

*Trust.* And *there's plenty of fish*.

"And the night the moonlight flinched," she said. "My sealed value slammed. Not rattled. *Slammed*. I've never felt it do that. Not for Alea. Not for him in the market." She looked at Higu. "Something happened that night. Something big moved, once, and stopped. And the next morning, Yan said the cold thing he'd been following for a week had gone out. One stroke. North-west." She swallowed. "And the morning after that, there were twice as many militia on the night watch round this hall, and nobody would say why."

Gigichi, still holding Xola, said very quietly: "Maren and Bram bowed."

They all looked at her.

"On the road," said Gigichi. "The first week. When Thea and I were fighting. He came down the road with his rod and his basket and Maren and Bram *threw themselves* on the ground. Both of them. Maren doesn't bow to the *king*, I'd put money on it. And then she said *a fisherman*, and wouldn't say anything else, ever." Her jaw was tight. "And Bram tells everyone the Hero's eight feet tall and lives in a fortress in the east. Bram, who'll tell you anything. Bram, who's never once in a year told us a single true thing about the Hero."

"Because he's not allowed to," said Higu.

"Because he's not allowed to," said Gigichi.

Xola had stopped crying. She'd sat up, in the circle of Gigichi's arm, with Squiddles still wrapped round her ears, and she was staring at the candle.

"The coats," she said slowly. "In the winter. Rowan brought them up, the mayor. Five coats and the firewood. *A gift from the town.* And he said..." She closed her eyes, remembering. "*I told him—*, and then he stopped, and said *I told them*. Like he'd nearly said something he wasn't supposed to." She opened her eyes. "Somebody measured us. With their eyes. Gu said that. Somebody who'd looked at every one of us on the road."

And PenguChi, last, very softly, from the rug.

"The lessons," she said. "On the ice. In the winter. Alea came up the hill and taught me three things. Don't push it. The back foot. The out-breath." She looked down at Waddles, asleep on her feet. "And I asked her where she learned them. And she said *my dad*." Her voice wobbled, just slightly. "Alea never had any trouble with those three things. She told me so, once. She never needed them. Those were *my* three things. The exact three things I was stuck on." She looked up. "Someone who knew exactly where I was stuck, taught them to Alea, so she'd bring them to me."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

***

Then Thea added the rest. The things that weren't theirs, but that they'd all read, in the channel, in the last month.

The books. Group 2's books, that came down from the north, the books Higu had written about in the spring. *On Waiting*, with Alea's exact words to PenguChi in the yard. The bait. *Trust.* And the chapter about nets, that Aleaka had put in the channel herself, shaking. *The small person who runs this house inspected my nets and found them wanting.*

"Alea," said Xola, in a very small voice. "Alea *complains* about his nets. All the time. She says he leaves them in a heap on the jetty and she's always telling him off."

The Latin on the sword. *Ad astra per aspera.* And Gabe and Deston and Xav and John: *Declan loved Latin. Every status. Every Steam group.*

The windows. Written in English, all the way down, from the very first one. A first window that was still running. That everyone's had been copied from. That the channel was hooked into.

The Hero of the Redmarch, who'd had a window before anyone. Who'd taught it to the Advisor. Who'd given the kingdom its words for it. *Status. Inventory. Skills.*

It lay there on the table between them, all of it, in the candlelight, like the pieces of a puzzle they'd been carrying around in separate pockets for a year.

***

Gigichi said it in the end. Gigichi, who said everything flat and blunt and true, and always had.

"The fisherman's the Hero," she said.

Nobody argued.

Thea heard her own voice say the next part. It didn't sound like her. It sounded very far away.

"And the Hero's Ice."

The candle burned. Outside the window, the sky over the lake had started, very faintly, to go grey.

"And Alea," said Xola. Her voice cracked. "Alea's *his*. Alea's Ice's *daughter*."

***

Nobody moved for a long time.

Thea didn't know what she felt. She'd imagined this, sometimes, in the dark, without ever letting herself imagine it properly. She'd thought, if it ever happened, she'd scream. Or laugh. Or cry. Or run down the hill in her nightdress and bang on the cabin door.

She didn't do any of those things. She just sat there on the floor of the common room with the candle in her hands, feeling something so big and so strange that it didn't have a shape yet.

*He's been here*, she thought. *The whole time. A mile away. The whole year.*

*He brought us fish.*

*He brought us coats.*

*He stood in front of me in the market with the snow coming down and I said* trust *to his face, and he looked at me, and said* there's plenty of fish, *and walked away.*

"He gave me nothing," she said out loud. Her voice shook. "In the market. I said *trust*. To *him*. And he gave me *nothing*."

"He gave you coats," said Gigichi quietly. "Two weeks later. He gave all of us coats."

Thea looked at her.

"He gave PenguChi three lessons," said Gigichi. "He gave us four fish a day for a year. He gave us his *daughter*, Thea. Every single day. He sent her up the hill with a basket." She was holding Xola very tightly. "He didn't give us nothing. He gave us everything except his name."

***

And then Thea understood the other thing.

It came all at once, the way the first thought had come: up from underneath, quietly, and then suddenly there. She felt the blood go out of her face.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, no."

"What?" said Higu.

"The channel." Thea's hands were shaking so badly she had to put the candle down. "It's hooked into the first window. I told everyone. The first night. *Whoever that was, wherever they are, technically they can read this too.*" She looked at Higu. "Gu. It's *his* window. The first one. The one everything's copied from."

Higu had gone very still.

"He can read it," said Thea. "He can read *all of it*. He's been reading it since the first night. Every line. I built a chat for thirty-five people, and there were thirty-six." She laughed, a horrible shaky laugh. "*Hi, whoever you are. Thanks for the windows.* I said *hi* to him. I said *thanks*."

"The night Hako asked," said Higu, very quietly. "What he was like."

They all went still.

Thirty-four people, telling a boy in Valcrest what their dead friend had been like. The kettle. The choir. The ban poem. *I'm answering now.* *It was the right answer.* *You're cute when you're bossy.* All of it, in the channel, one line after another, all night.

And at the end of a jetty on a lake, a mile below them, a man reading every word.

"He read all of it," whispered Xola. "He *read* it. All of us. Saying all those things." She put her face in her hands. "Oh, he read all of it."

PenguChi was crying now. Quietly. The first time Thea had seen her cry all year.

"I hope he did," PenguChi said softly. "I hope he read every single one."

***

It was Higu who saw the last thing. Of course it was.

"Thea," she said. "The slow clock."

Thea looked at her.

"Does he know?" said Higu. "That it's only been three years? At home?"

Thea opened her mouth, and closed it.

"He's been here thirty-four years," said Higu slowly. "He doesn't have a slow clock. Or if he does, it's behind the sealed value, where you can't read it, and maybe he can't either. He's never seen our dates. He's never..." She stopped. "If he thought about it at all, he'd think it was the same. Thirty-four years there, too. Everyone he knew, grown old. His father..."

"His father," said Gigichi. Very quietly.

They all knew. Not much. Ice hadn't talked about his family much on stream. But they knew his mum had died, years before. And they knew, from the way he'd said it once, that his dad was all he had left over there.

*Three years.* His dad would be three years older. Not thirty-four. Three.

"Alea knows," said PenguChi.

They looked at her.

"The first week," said PenguChi. "At the fire. When we told her about Ice. Higu said he died in his sleep, three years ago." She wiped her eyes. "And Alea went so still. I remember. I thought it was because it was sad. It was sad. But she went *so* still." She looked at Higu. "She's known. Since the first week. She's known it's been three years for us, and thirty-four for him. And she's never..."

"She's never told him," said Higu.

"That's why," said Thea slowly. "Rule five. *Don't write my name in the channel.* That's why she went pale. It wasn't about her name. Or not only." She looked at the window. At the lake, grey now, under the coming dawn. "She knew he'd be reading. And she knew that if any of us ever put the three years in the channel, he'd find out. From a list of forty lines. From strangers. At two in the morning."

"Like Leaka nearly did," said Higu softly.

The room was very quiet.

"If I post the slow clock," said Thea, "he reads it."

Nobody answered.

"Everyone needs to know," said Thea. "Everyone's families. Everyone's mums. A month, not a year. They *need* to know." Her voice cracked. "And the second I write it, he finds out that his dad's alive. From *me*. In a chat. And Alea doesn't get to be the one who tells him."

***

They decided as the sun came up.

Not quickly. Not easily. They went round and round it in the grey light, the five of them, with the candle burned down to nothing and the animals awake and fretful. But in the end it came down to three things, and they all agreed on all three.

They wouldn't tell anyone. Not the channel. Not Group 2, not Aleaka, not Gabe, not anyone. Not who he was. Not where. It was his to tell. He'd made that very clear, for a year, in every way a man could make something clear without saying it. *I've never been anywhere but here. There's plenty of fish.* If he wanted to be found, he'd let himself be found. If he didn't, they'd let him not be.

They wouldn't tell Alea that they knew. Not yet. She'd asked for rule five. She'd been carrying her own thing, alone, for a year. They weren't going to drop theirs on top of it.

And Thea wouldn't post the slow clock. Not yet. She'd write to Chris, in a sealed letter, on one of Cursiffe's strips, and ask him to check his own window and tell her if she was wrong. She'd tell him about the clock. Only the clock. Nothing else.

And then they'd see.

"Rule six," said Higu, when they'd agreed. "We wait for him."

"There are only five rules," said Xola, half laughing, half crying.

"There are six now," said Higu. "We just don't write that one down either."

***

Thea wrote the letter that morning, at the table in the common room, while the others slept.

*Chris. Number four. Please check your window. Go under the channel. Every message has a tail; every tail has two numbers. The second one is a date, in English. Tell me what it says. Tell me what it said the day the channel opened. Tell me if I'm wrong.*

*Please don't put this in the channel. Not yet. I'll explain when I can.*

*If I'm right, it's the best news any of us have had all year. I just need to be sure before I tell everyone their mums have only been looking for a month.*

She sealed it with a blue strip, and gave it to Bram to take down to the king's rider.

She didn't write anything else. She didn't write *I think I know where he is*. She didn't write *he's been reading every word we wrote*. She didn't write *his dad*.

She just sat there, afterwards, at the window, and looked down the long slope of the fields to the lake.

***

Alea came at the usual time, with four fish in the basket, and her ears up, and her tail swinging.

They were all in the yard. They'd agreed on that too, at dawn. Be in the yard. Be normal. Be exactly the way they'd been yesterday and every day for a year.

They weren't very good at it.

Xola's eyes were red. Gigichi's jaw was tight. PenguChi was standing in her wide stance in the far corner with her eyes closed, the way she always did, but her hands were trembling. And Higu was sitting on the wall with her notebook closed in her lap, not writing in it, looking at Alea with an expression Thea couldn't read.

Alea stopped in the gateway.

She looked at them. All of them. One after another. And Thea saw her see it. Saw her ears go back, very slightly, and her face go still, the way a pond goes still when something moves underneath.

"What?" said Alea. "What is it? What happened?"

"Nothing," said Thea.

Alea looked at her.

"Nothing," said Thea again. And then, because she couldn't help it, because her voice was going and her eyes were going and she had to say *something* or she'd say everything: "Thank you. For the fish."

Alea stood very still in the gateway with the basket on her arm.

"They're the lake's fish," she said slowly. "We just ask them politely."

Thea had to turn away. She looked at the hedge. At the hawthorn, along the back wall of the yard, which was thick and dense and green now, with hardly a gap in it, because Gigichi had been asking it every night all summer to keep things out.

"Tell your dad," said Thea, to the hedge, very carefully, "thank you. From me."

There was a long silence behind her.

"I will," said Alea.

Thea didn't turn round. She didn't trust her face. She heard Alea put the basket down on the bench by the door. She heard her cross the yard to PenguChi, and say *hi, Penguin*, and PenguChi say *PenguChi*, and Alea laugh. A little too brightly. The way you laugh when you've seen something and decided, for now, not to ask.

Thea stood by the hedge and looked over it, down the slope, to the lake. To the end of the jetty, where a small figure was sitting in the morning sun with a rod across his knees, the way he'd sat every morning for a year. Every morning for seven years. Every morning, maybe, for thirty-four.

*Not yet*, she thought. *Not yet. We'll wait.*

*But we know.*
