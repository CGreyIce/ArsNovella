Gabe woke against the ward door with his blade across his knees and a crick in his neck that felt like someone had hammered a nail into it.

He'd slept there every night for a month. He'd sleep there every night for another month, if that was what it took. He was a big man, and a big man in front of a door was a door that didn't open, and that was the one thing he knew for certain he was good for. He'd known it on Earth, in a different way: at parties, in crowds, on packed buses, wherever somebody small needed somebody large to stand next to them. He knew it here. Sabine had drilled it into him for a year. *A man your size should be a wall.*

Last night he'd been a wall. The thing had looked at him, and at the door, and gone round.

He didn't feel like he'd won anything.

He sat there in the grey morning light with the rain stopped and the river quieter and the hole in the courtyard wall full of pale sky, and opened his window. Habit. Everybody did it first thing, now, the way you used to reach for your phone. Check the channel. See who was alive.

He stopped.

***

The channel had changed.

He didn't understand it at first. He just knew it looked wrong, the way a room looks wrong when somebody's moved the furniture in the night. The lines were the same lines, the same names, the same plain English. But there were more of them. Many more. He scrolled up, out of habit, expecting to hit the top in a few flicks, the way you always hit the top, forty lines and gone.

He didn't hit the top.

He kept scrolling. Past Dennis's post from two in the morning. Past Dennis's *second* post, and his third. Past Rachael's *I'm fine, really*, which had gone up and away in a minute last night and was still here, sitting in the channel, as if it had never left. Past Xav's *what happened? where's dennis's post?* Past Niko's heartbeats from yesterday evening, and Tiger's goat, and Kei's mushroom. Back, and back. Yesterday afternoon. Yesterday *morning*.

And at the front of every line, small and grey, where nothing had ever been before, there was a time.

***

He stared at it.

[ 02:14 · Dennis: Thea we need more than forty lines.
02:14 · Dennis: I've posted this three times and people still haven't seen it.
02:16 · Althea: I know. I'm sorry. I've tried.
02:16 · Althea: It's the one part I can't change. It's written too deep. It won't let me. ]

And then, further down, this morning, the channel waking up and finding it the way he had, one person at a time:

[ 06:02 · Chris: Something's changed. Logging.
06:03 · Chris: Lines retained: I count 200. Not 40. 200.
06:03 · Chris: And every line has a time on it. When it was sent.
06:05 · Maigo: WHAT
06:05 · Maigo: I CAN SCROLL UP
06:05 · Maigo: I CAN SCROLL UP AND IT'S STILL THERE
06:06 · Maigo: I'VE WANTED THIS FOR A YEAR

06:09 · Tiger: drew it says you went to bed at 11:40
06:10 · Drew: I'd like that removed.

06:12 · Rachael: and mine. and gabe's. and everyone's from last night.
06:14 · Suzuyun: Thea. Thank you.
06:15 · Hanarisu: Thea, this is wonderful. Thank you. ]

And then, a little after that, on its own:

[ 06:21 · Althea: It wasn't me. ]

***

The channel went quiet for a moment.

Then it went the way it always did, everyone at once. *What do you mean it wasn't you. Who else can even do that. Thea are you being modest. THEA. Is this a Spark thing, did somebody go up a tier.* Malcolm, inevitably: *IF it wasn't Thea, THEN who.* Chris: *Logging that too.*

Gabe watched it all go past. All of it staying, now, line after line, not vanishing off the top, so that the whole conversation stacked up in front of him like a pile of letters.

And then, a few minutes later:

[ 06:29 · Althea: I don't know how. I'm sorry. I looked last night after Dennis asked. The forty was written into the bottom of the channel, down where I couldn't reach it. Somebody's rewritten it.
06:29 · Althea: It's in the same writing as the rest of the window. Not mine. ]

And that was all she said. She didn't answer anything after that. Somebody asked her *whose then?* and she didn't reply. Somebody else said *does it matter?? it's amazing*. Maigo said *IT DOESN'T MATTER, I CAN SCROLL UP*.

Gabe noticed, without quite knowing why he noticed, that Higu hadn't said anything at all.

Higu, who said *rule three* to every bit of good news, loudly, first. Higu, who'd written the rules at the top of the channel. Nothing. Not a word. The whole of Wrenhaven, in fact, had gone quiet after Thea's two lines. Gabe didn't think much of it. They were probably asleep. It was very early.

He closed the window. He had a door to sit in front of.

*In Wrenhaven, at an upper window, two people who'd been awake since five sat side by side and looked at the times on the channel and didn't say anything to each other for a long time. They each had an idea about whose writing it was. Neither of them said it. Rule six.*

***

He didn't get up on the roof until that evening.

The rooms above the chandler's on Rope Street had a flat bit of roof behind the chimney stack, leaded, with a low parapet, that you could get to through the window at the end of the passage if you didn't mind climbing over a rain barrel. They'd found it in the first week. It had been theirs ever since. His and Deston's.

They'd sat there the first night in Kingsferry and hummed the song. *Seasons of Love.* The way they'd hummed it a hundred times, in a hundred places, since they were thirteen and fifteen in a secondary school choir in Singapore, with Declan standing between them doing his part. They'd hummed it without the words, and left the gap where his part went, and Emrys, coming up through the window, had heard the gap. *I could hear exactly where it should go.*

They'd hummed it most nights since. Leaving the gap a little longer, every time.

Deston was already there when Gabe climbed out over the rain barrel. Sitting with his back against the chimney and his knees drawn up and his hands loose in his lap, looking north. Up the river. The way he'd been looking north, Gabe realised, sitting down beside him, every single night for months. He'd just never let himself notice.

They didn't say anything for a while.

Down below, the river ran black and quick in the dusk. Lamps were coming on along the quays. Somebody was hammering at the hole in the infirmary wall, a storey above the river-gate, fitting new stones.

"Is it the thread?" said Gabe.

He'd asked it once before. In the summer, on this roof, the night after Rachael went up a tier. Deston had said *not yet*. Gabe had said *when you're ready*, and meant it, and not asked again.

Deston was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," he said.

***

He told him all of it.

He did it the way Deston did everything, carefully, in order, with his hands open in his lap as if he were holding the thing he was describing and wanted Gabe to see it properly. Gabe had known him since he was thirteen. He'd never once heard him tell something out of order.

He told him about the spring. About lying on this roof the night Xav's thread went quiet, following it every evening, feeling it tug back. About the night he'd felt Xav standing still at the far end of it, in Duskhollow, waiting, and squeezed. And then turned his head, without meaning to, and seen another one.

"From here," said Deston. He put his hand flat on his own chest. "Same as yours. Same place. As thick as yours." He looked at Gabe. "You know what yours looks like? To me? Gold. A rope. It's the thickest thread I've got. It's been the thickest since I was fifteen."

"I know," said Gabe. He didn't, really. But he knew.

"This one was as thick," said Deston. "But there was no colour in it. Nothing. Grey. Faded. Like a rope that's hung in a window for thirty summers and had all the colour bleached out of it." He swallowed. "*Old*. That's what I thought. Distance doesn't fade them. I've told you that. Xav's is three hundred miles away and it's as bright as yours. This one wasn't far. It was *old*. It had been there a very, very long time, on its own, with nobody pulling on the other end."

He turned his head and looked north. Up the river.

"It goes that way," he said. "North. Up the Wren. Past Xav. Past Duskhollow, past everything I've got a thread to. Toward the lake country." He was quiet. "You asked me, in the spring, the night I saw it. You said *who do you know up north?* And I said *nobody*."

"I remember."

"I lied."

"I know," said Gabe. "I've known since you said *nobody*."

Deston looked at him.

"You've never once in your life said *nobody* like that," said Gabe. "You said it like a man putting something down very carefully so it wouldn't break." He looked at his own big hands. "I just thought you'd tell me when you could hold it."

***

"It moved," said Deston.

Gabe waited.

"That night. On this roof. When we hummed it." Deston's voice had gone very quiet. "We got to the gap, where his part goes. And we left it. And the thread trembled. Once. Like someone a long way off had turned their head." He shut his eyes. "I didn't let myself think it. I didn't let myself finish the thought. Not all summer. I'd done it once already, Gabe. I'd lost him once already. In a phone call, at two in the morning, from you. I wasn't going to lose him twice because I'd let myself hope about a grey line nobody else could see."

Gabe didn't say anything. He remembered the phone call. He'd made it.

"And then Thea posted the clock," said Deston. "And everyone did the sum. And you wrote *declan, if you can somehow read this*. And I wrote *me too*." He opened his eyes. "That night. After. I came up here on my own, and I looked at it."

"And?"

Deston held his hand out flat in the dusk, palm down, over the parapet, pointing north up the river.

"It's warmer," he said. "Since that night. It's not grey anymore. Not quite. There's a colour coming into it. The faintest. Like a rope somebody's taken in out of the sun and started using again." He breathed out. "I think it's because he knows. That we know. I think when the other end of a thread finds out somebody's pulling, it starts pulling back."

He lowered his hand.

"It's him," said Deston. "It's Declan. Up the river. It's been him the whole time."

***

Gabe sat on the roof in the dark for a long time.

He thought about the funeral. He'd been at it. Him and Deston, in their black shirts, in the heat, with Declan's dad shaking every hand and not crying. He'd thought, standing there, *this is it, then. This is the last thing.* He'd thought it for three years. A month, now. He kept having to do the sum again.

He thought about a book about nets, and a line in it about a small person who ran the house, and *yes, ma'am*. About a phrase on a sword in Latin that Declan would have put in his status and refused to translate. About Yan, on the Redmarch, on the anniversary, writing *he didn't say*. About Hana's lines from the Advisor, which he'd read so many times he knew them by heart: *the Hero didn't ask who we were. Not one question.*

He thought about the last thing he'd ever said to him. On a call, at two in the morning, the last night. Declan promising to be on early tomorrow, and Gabe telling him to go to bed. *Sleep, Declan. Seriously.*

And Declan had. And not woken up.

"Upriver," said Gabe.

His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat.

"Upriver," said Deston.

***

"Not now," said Deston. "It's nearly winter. The upper Wren freezes from the lakes down, Emrys says. The roads close. And the hunters..." He looked at the hole in the infirmary wall, down by the river, with the lamplight on the new stones. "We're not leaving Rachael. Not this winter. Not after last night."

"No."

"Spring," said Deston. "When the river opens. All of us. Rachael comes, or nobody goes." He was quiet a moment. "And Xav. He's on the way. Duskhollow's on the river; we'd go right past him. I can feel his thread from here the whole way up." Something like a smile. "He'd never forgive us."

"He'd kill us," said Gabe. "Slowly. While waiting."

"He would."

"Not the channel," said Gabe.

Deston looked at him.

"Not the channel," said Gabe again. He'd been thinking about it since the morning. Since the times on every line, and two hundred lines that didn't go away anymore, and a whole kingdom of people reading every word. "Group 3 asked him straight out. *Are you him.* And he didn't answer. He walked away west with his sword. Yan said that was an answer. *Not yet*." He looked at his hands. "If we put *the thread goes up the Wren to the lake country* in the channel, every one of the thirty-five reads it by breakfast. And half of them are on the road by the end of the week. Tiger would be on the road by lunch." He shook his head. "Thirty-five of us in one place, with hunters coming over walls. The Advisor said it in the summer. One target."

"And he didn't answer," said Deston quietly.

"And he didn't answer," said Gabe. "He's got his reasons. He always had his reasons." He breathed out. "He'll tell us why when we get there. He'll tell us to our faces. And then I'm going to hit him."

Deston laughed. It came out wet.

"Then I'll hit him," said Gabe. "Very gently. And then I'm going to sit down somewhere and not get up for a week."

***

They wrote to Xav that night. A real letter, on paper, sealed with one of Cursiffe's blue ink strips, numbered, the way they'd been doing since the summer of the missing letters. Deston wrote it. Gabe sat beside him at the table above the chandler's and read every line over his shoulder, the way he'd read Deston's homework over his shoulder at thirteen.

It wasn't long. Deston had never needed long.

*Xav,*

*Spring. When the river opens. Groups 4 and 6, together, upriver, past you and on. You know why. I'll tell you everything when we see you, all of it, from the beginning; I should have told you in the spring. I'm sorry I didn't.*

*It's warm now. It wasn't, before. Since the night of the sum.*

*Not the channel. Not yet. You know why that too.*

*Gabe says he's going to hit him. I've said he can't. You can be the tiebreaker.*

*D.*

***

They told Emrys the next morning.

It had to be Emrys. He was their guide; he'd answer for them to the Advisor; and the Advisor had said, in the summer, in every posting's letter, *no gathering of the summoned. One target.* Groups 4 and 6 going upriver together was a gathering, any way you looked at it.

Emrys listened to the whole thing sitting on the edge of his bed with a hot drink for his throat, in his nightshirt, which was how he received most things now. He didn't interrupt. He didn't ask why. Gabe watched him not ask. He'd been watching Emrys not ask things for a year.

When they'd finished, Emrys looked at them for a long time.

"Upriver," he said. His voice was still hoarse. Worse, this morning, after last night's note. "Past Duskhollow."

"Yes."

"Into the lake country."

"Yes."

Emrys looked down at his drink.

"I heard you hum it," he said. "The first night. On the roof. I came up through the window and you were humming it and you left a gap, and I could hear exactly where it should go." He was quiet a moment. "I've been a musician all my life. I've heard every song in this kingdom. I'd never heard that one." He looked up at them. "I've wondered every night since who used to sing the third part."

Neither of them said anything.

"I'll write to the Advisor," said Emrys. "Today. I'll ask leave. I'll tell him it's my idea, if you like, so it's on my head and not yours." He set the drink down. "He may say no. You know that."

"We know," said Deston.

"He may say no," said Emrys again, more quietly. "But I don't think he will."

***

The answer came eight days later, by the fast rider, in a sealed packet with the Advisor's own mark on it. Emrys opened it at the table above the chandler's, with all six of them there, and Sabine in the doorway. Gabe watched him unfold it.

It was a single sheet. There was a single line on it, in a small careful hand, in the middle of the page.

Emrys read it. His eyebrows went up. He read it again.

Then he turned it round and laid it on the table so they could all see.

They couldn't read the script. None of them could; handwriting slid off their eyes the way English slid off the natives'. Emrys read it out for them, in his hoarse cracked voice, sounding puzzled.

"*Let them.*"

***

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Gabe looked at Deston, and Deston looked at Gabe, and something passed between them that had been passing between them since Gabe was thirteen, across a choir room, during a bad rehearsal, when somebody else in the room had done something so completely ridiculous that you couldn't look at each other or you'd laugh.

*Let them.* Hana had written it in the channel, a month ago. What the Advisor had told her. *When he asked the Hero what to do about us, the Hero wrote back two words.*

The Advisor knew where they were going. He knew exactly where. And he'd answered them in the Hero's words.

"That's all?" said Calvin, leaning over the page. "Two words? No conditions? No *one target*?"

"That's all," said Emrys.

"What does it *mean*?"

"I think," said Emrys slowly, looking at Gabe and Deston, and then away again, out of the window, at the river running north, "it means somebody else has already decided. And he's just passing it on."

Dennis, at the end of the table, put his hand flat on the page beside the two words. He didn't say anything. But Gabe saw him go very still, the way he went when something rang.

"It's true," said Dennis quietly. "Whoever wrote that meant it."

***

Gabe went to the forge after that.

Harl's forge on the south wharf, where he'd worked every afternoon for a year, where Harl had taught him that the heat stops being angry and burned off his own eyebrows proving it. He didn't work. He just went in and sat down on the bench by the cold quench trough and took his blade off his back and laid it across his knees and looked at it.

He'd made it in the summer. The first real blade he'd ever made. Short and heavy and single-edged, with a broad spine and a plain hilt and a guard he'd fitted himself, the day Rachael closed the burn on his arm. *A butcher's cleaver married a gate.* It had held a door last night against a thing that had thrown Sabine into a well.

He'd never named it.

He'd thought about it. He'd thought about it a lot, in the summer, sitting on this bench. You were supposed to name a blade, here; every swordsman in Kingsferry had a name for his, usually something terrible. *Widowmaker. Bonecutter.* Gabe had sat on this bench and thought: *Declan would have named it in Latin.* He'd have looked at it for about four seconds and said something in Latin with a completely straight face and then refused to tell you what it meant, for a month, until you'd looked it up yourself. And it would have been perfect.

Gabe didn't know any Latin.

He looked at the blade on his knees for a long time.

"I'll ask him," he said, to nobody, in the empty forge. "In the spring. I'll ask him to name it."

Harl, coming in from the yard with a bucket, stopped in the doorway and looked at him.

"Ask who?" said Harl.

"My brother," said Gabe.

Harl didn't know he had a brother. Harl didn't ask. He put the bucket down, and went and stoked the fire, and left him alone.

***

That night, on the roof, they hummed it.

The way they always did. *Seasons of Love*, without the words, the two parts, Gabe's low and Deston's in the middle, in the dark, behind the chimney stack, over Kingsferry, with the river running north below them toward the lakes. They hummed it all the way through, the way they'd sung it at thirteen and fifteen, in a choir room in Singapore, and then on a stage, and then in a car park after a concert with Declan doing his part too loud and getting told off by a teacher.

They got to the gap. Where his part went.

They left it.

They left it longer than they ever had. A long time. The two of them sitting there in the dark on the leaded roof, not humming, holding the place open, the way you'd hold a door.

Deston had his hand out over the parapet again, flat, palm down, pointing north.

Gabe watched his face. He watched it change.

"Well?" said Gabe, very quietly.

Deston didn't answer straight away. He was looking north, up the river, at nothing Gabe could see. His eyes were wet.

"It's warm," he said. "It's *warm*, Gabe. It went warm, just now. In the gap." He lowered his hand slowly. "Like somebody on the other end heard the space where he's supposed to be."

Gabe looked north, up the black river, toward a country of lakes he'd never seen, and a town he didn't know the name of, and a man he'd buried.

"Spring," he said.

"Spring," said Deston.

They finished the song. And this time, for the first time, in the gap, very quietly, so quietly that it might have been the wind, Gabe sang the third part himself. Badly. It had never been his part. He didn't know all the notes.

He thought Declan would have laughed at him.

He thought that was fine.
