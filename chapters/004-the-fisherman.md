The fish weren't biting, which suited Ars fine.

Lake Ilse lay flat and grey under the morning mist, so still that the end of the jetty seemed to float. He sat with his legs over the edge and his line in the water and his mind pleasantly empty, which had taken him most of a lifetime to learn how to do.

The rod across his knees was old and plain and slightly too long. Anybody in Wrenhaven would have told you it was a fishing rod, because that was what it looked like and that was what he did with it. Only a handful of people alive knew that its shaft would take an edge, or what it had done in his hands on the days when he hadn't been fishing. He preferred it that way. It was, by a long way, the best fishing rod he had ever owned.

Behind him, on the boards, Alea was working through her forms.

He didn't need to look to know how she was doing. He could hear it: the shuffle of her feet, the hiss of the practice blade, the small impatient huff she gave every time she finished a sequence and didn't like it. Three years of this, every morning since she was thirteen. It had taken her all of those three years to reach Drawn, the first tier of aura, and she was prouder of it than of anything in her life, and she hid that badly.

"Your weight's on your heels," he said.

"It is not."

He waited.

The next sequence was better. She didn't say anything about it, and neither did he, and her tail gave one small satisfied flick that she would have denied if he'd mentioned it.

This was the life now. The cabin behind them, with its crooked chimney and the herbs drying in the window. The vegetable rows he'd dug himself. The hens, who hated him. The stack of paper on the table by the fire where, some evenings, he wrote things down: stories, mostly, and notes about fish, and the occasional joke he couldn't resist. A trader took the finished pages south a few times a year and sold them under no name at all. Ars had no idea who bought them and liked it that way.

He was thirty-four. He felt sixty, most days, because in every way but the mirror he was. He'd been someone else once, somewhere else, and he didn't think about it much any more. That had taken a long time to learn as well.

A horse was coming along the lake road.

He knew who it was before it came out of the mist, from the weight of it and the rhythm, and he reeled in his line without hurrying.

"Ars," said Rowan Hale, swinging down from the saddle with the stiffness of a big man who'd spent too long on a horse and too long behind a desk. He was going grey all over now, and the mayor's chain sat oddly on him, like a collar on a bear. Seven years ago he'd stood at this cabin's door with a sword in his hand while a war happened somewhere else, keeping a nine-year-old girl alive. He'd never quite stopped looking like a man standing at a door.

"Rowan."

"Odette sent bread." Rowan held up a cloth bundle. Alea had already abandoned her forms and was coming down the jetty at a speed that had nothing to do with aura. "And I've got news, and you won't like it."

Ars took the bread and handed it to his daughter without looking. "Then tell me inside."

They didn't go inside. They never did. They stood at the foot of the jetty with the mist burning off the water, and Rowan told him.

"The capital did a summoning."

Ars didn't say anything.

"Thirty-five people," Rowan went on. "Brought in from somewhere else. Not demons, they're saying. Not from Kharvos, not from anywhere on any map. *People.* The Advisor's put them in groups of five and scattered them across the kingdom to train." He hesitated. "One group's coming here. Five girls, a guide and a guard. They'll be lodging at the old militia hall. Halvard's orders."

"Halvard," said Ars.

"There's a note with it. Sealed, for my eyes." Rowan's mouth twisted. "It says they're to be given whatever they need, and that the fisherman on Lake Ilse is not to be bothered by any of them. Which I assume means you, unless you've been taking lessons from the Brennick boy."

Ars looked out at the lake.

*From somewhere else.*

A summoning needed something to pull through. Every scholar who'd ever written about one agreed on that much: you couldn't reach into nothing and grasp nothing. You needed a medium, something with a hook in it, something that belonged to the place you were reaching toward. He'd read that once, years ago, in a book he'd had no reason to read except that he'd wanted to know whether the door that had brought him here could open the other way. It couldn't. He'd stopped reading after that.

For a moment, without meaning to, he thought of red ground, and a black shape standing in it over a grave.

He put the thought away.

"Tell Odette thank you for the bread," he said.

Rowan stared at him. "That's it?"

"It's a time of peace."

"Is it?"

Ars was quiet for a while. Behind him, the bread had been unwrapped, and Alea was standing very still with half a loaf in her hand, her ears turned toward them like a pair of sails.

"No," he said at last. "It isn't."

By the end of their first week in Wrenhaven, Thea had decided the town hated their clothes.

It wasn't hostile, exactly. It was just that every single person they passed turned to look, and then kept looking, and then nudged whoever was next to them so they could look too. Five girls in clothes from another world, walking down a cobbled high street lined with timber houses: a jersey worn as a dress, a jellyfish skirt, a cardigan falling off one shoulder, a mustard jacket covered in pink cross-stitched X's. Xola's gills got stares. Squiddles, perched on Xola's head, got screams.

"I think they like us," Xola said, on the third day, and a woman crossed the street to avoid her.

The days had a shape now. Up before dawn in the long room above the old militia hall. Bread and porridge downstairs. Then the yard, where Maren made them sit in the cold and *sense*.

That was the whole of it, at Initiate. You couldn't cast. You couldn't do anything. You sat with your eyes closed and tried to feel the mana moving in you and around you, and most of the time you felt nothing at all except your own legs going numb.

"Everyone feels nothing at first," Maren said, every morning, with exactly the same expression, "and then one day you don't."

PenguChi trained apart from the rest of them, in the far corner of the yard with Bram, because she'd chosen aura. Aura was slower, Bram told her cheerfully, and harder, and most people gave up on it, and he'd never once seen a mage hold a bridge. PenguChi just nodded, slow and steady, and kept breathing.

At breakfast on the seventh day, they compared windows. They did it most mornings. It had become a kind of ritual, half encouragement and half confession.

"Three," said Xola. "But I think the washbasin likes me. When I'm upset, the water goes still."

"Four," said Higu. "More at night. It's always more at night."

"One," said PenguChi, without any sign of distress at all.

"Two," said Gigichi, and then, defensively, "and I can feel the roots under the floor. Don't look at me like that. I can."

"Fifty," said Thea.

She hadn't meant it to sound like anything. It came out sounding like something anyway.

"Of course you're ahead," said Gigichi.

"I'm not *ahead.* I just see it differently. I told you that the first night. There's structure under the—"

"Under the window, yeah. You told everyone. Twice."

"Gigi," said Higu, very quietly.

Thea put her spoon down. She was aware of the old, familiar heat starting at the back of her neck, the same heat she'd felt for months on Earth every time she'd looked at her phone and seen Gigichi had gone quiet again, and then had to be the one who reached out. Again. Always.

She didn't say anything. She just picked her spoon back up.

It held until the afternoon.

Maren sent them to market with a list: rope, lamp oil, soap, a whetstone for Bram. The road from the militia hall ran down through the edge of town and then out along the lake shore toward the market square, and somewhere along it, Thea finally noticed that Gigichi wasn't carrying anything.

She'd been carrying Gigichi's half of the morning chores for a week. She'd fetched water for both of them, because Gigichi had slept in. She'd swept both their ends of the long room. She'd done it without saying a word, because she had promised herself that this time, in this world, she wouldn't be the one who started it.

"You could take the oil," she said.

"I've got the list."

"The list weighs nothing."

"Then you take the list." Gigichi held it out. "Go on. Since you're so good at doing everything."

And that was it. Thea stopped in the middle of the road and turned around.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that. You know exactly what you're doing. You did it for months on Earth. I did *everything*. I planned everything, I showed up for everything, I checked on you when you went quiet, and you let me, and then you acted like I was the problem for noticing."

"Maybe I was tired of being *managed* by you."

"Managed? I was holding the whole thing together!"

"Nobody asked you to!"

"Okay," said Xola, stepping between them with both hands up and Squiddles clinging to her hair in alarm. "Okay, okay, okay, can we not do this in the middle of a road in a medieval town—"

"We're doing it *now*," said Gigichi. "She wants to do it now, let's do it now."

"Gigi, breathe," said PenguChi.

It didn't work. It was the first time Thea had ever seen PenguChi's voice not work. Higu had taken Gigichi by the arm and was saying something low and urgent. Behind them, Maren had gone stiff, and Bram had shifted his weight forward onto his toes, his hand loose at his side, ready to step in the moment either of them did something stupid.

Thea opened her mouth to say the worst thing she could think of.

"You're blocking the road."

The voice came from behind her. It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be.

She turned.

There was a man standing on the road from the lake, with a basket on one arm and a long fishing rod across his shoulder. He wasn't tall, or broad, or anything she would have noticed in a crowd. He was just a man in plain clothes, somewhere in his thirties, with the patient, slightly tired look of someone who'd been waiting for them to finish and had now stopped waiting.

Beside him stood a girl of about sixteen, with fox ears and a fox's tail and an expression of open, delighted curiosity.

"Then walk around," snapped Gigichi.

Maren made a sound Thea had never heard a person make.

She was across the road before anybody could move, her hand clamped on Gigichi's elbow so hard that Gigichi yelped. Bram was right behind her. He didn't grab anyone. He just put himself in front of the rest of them like a wall and walked, and the four of them found themselves herded to the verge without quite knowing how. And then both of them, Maren and Bram, turned toward the man on the road and bowed.

Not a nod. A real bow, low and long, the kind Thea had seen the soldiers give the king. Maren went down so far that her neat pinned hair nearly touched her knees.

The man looked at them.

He looked at all five of them, one at a time: Xola, Higu, PenguChi, Gigichi, Thea. It took no more than a second for each. But when it was her turn, Thea had the strangest feeling, as if she'd been recognised by someone she'd never met. Then he walked on past them, unhurried, with his basket and his rod, and the fox girl followed him, turning back twice to look at them over her shoulder with bright, unembarrassed interest.

And under Thea's window, which she hadn't even opened, something moved.

She felt it rather than saw it: the lattice of light she'd spent a week learning to read. Deep in it, the line she'd found on the first night, *Medium: one blade*, and the second value beside it, the one that was sealed like a door with no handle.

As he passed, the door rattled in its frame.

Then he was gone around the bend toward the market, and the road was just a road again.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Who," said Higu, carefully, "was that?"

Maren straightened. Her face had gone from white back to its usual composed nothing, but her hands, Thea noticed, were not quite steady. "A fisherman," she said.

"You *bowed* to a fisherman," said PenguChi.

"Yes."

"Like, a lot."

"Yes." Maren looked down at the shopping list, which had somehow ended up in her hand, and smoothed it flat with great care. "Rope, lamp oil, soap and a whetstone. We'll go to the market now. Gigichi, you'll carry the oil."

Gigichi opened her mouth, looked at Maren's face, and closed it again.

They walked to market in silence. Thea didn't look at Gigichi, and Gigichi didn't look at Thea, but somewhere in the last few minutes the fight had gone out of both of them, leaving only a raw, embarrassed space where it had been.

At the back of the group, Bram was still looking down the road where the fisherman had gone, with an expression Thea couldn't read at all.

"Dad," said Alea.

"Mm."

"That was Xola."

He kept walking.

"The axolotl one. You said she laughed louder than anyone you'd ever met. And the one in the blue jersey with the ponytail was Thea, you said she was always the one fixing everybody's problems, and the one with the heart on her head was PenguChi, and—"

"Eggs, Alea."

"*Dad.*"

"We came for eggs."

She made a frustrated noise and stomped along beside him, and her tail lashed, and he didn't look at her, because if he looked at her she'd read him, the way she always could, and he wasn't sure yet what his face was doing.

He'd known them before he reached them. That was the thing. He'd known them from fifty paces down the road, the way you know a song from the first two notes. Xola's hair, lavender melting into pink, impossible in any world. The pink and violet streaks in Thea's ponytail. PenguChi's little yellow heart. Higu's white bow. Gigichi's silver hair and the set of her shoulders. He had watched all five of them on a glowing screen in a room in Malacca, a lifetime ago. He'd sat in Higu's chat with a moderator's sword next to his name. PenguChi had only ever known him as the guy in her chat who was suspiciously good at word games.

He'd liked two of them, once, the way you like people when you're twenty-five and they make you laugh. It had passed long before he died, and they'd both known it, and it hadn't mattered.

They looked exactly the same.

He was thirty-four years older, and they looked exactly the same.

At the market he bought eggs and bread and a small bag of the honey sweets Alea liked and would never ask for. He paid, and thanked the egg-woman, and asked after her son's leg. He did not look around for five girls in clothes from another world, and he didn't need to. He could feel them somewhere at the other end of the square, five faint candles of mana barely lit, the way you feel the sun on the side of your face.

"You're not going to say anything," said Alea, as they started back toward the lake. "Are you."

"To who?"

"To *them.*"

"They were blocking the road," Ars said. "Now they aren't. They're fine."

She was quiet for a long time after that. When he finally glanced at her, she was looking at him with her mother's eyes, very sharp and very kind, and it was a near thing.

That night, after she'd gone to bed, he went down to the end of the jetty and stood there in the dark. The lake was black and still. Up in Wrenhaven, the lamps were going out one by one.

Thirty-five people, Rowan had said.

He didn't need to ask who the other thirty were. Some part of him already knew.

"What did you do, Halvard," he said quietly, to the water.

It was the first English he'd spoken aloud in years. The lake took it, and gave nothing back.
