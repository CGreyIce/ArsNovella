Thea felt them come over the ridge at a quarter past three in the morning, the way you'd feel a phone buzz in a coat on the back of a chair in another room.

Seven of them. Six together and one apart, a little heavier, a little warmer, as if whoever was carrying it had it against their chest. Coming down the south road out of the dark at a horse's walk, a mile off, half a mile, the circle's hand written small and close on oiled paper in a wooden case, and every line of it humming very faintly in the back of her head the way the six had hummed in the winter. Not loud. Not a voice. Just *there*, and *roughly where*, and the third thing, the one she'd only learned to feel since Lantern, which was whether they were well.

They were well. All seven. Not a fault on them. She lay in her cot in the upper room of the militia hall with her eyes open in the dark and listened to them come down the road, and felt something in her chest go loose that she hadn't known was tight.

*Edwy checked every line twice*, Hana had written, three days ago. *He signed the note. First time*.

She'd believed it. She'd have believed it anyway. But it was different to feel it, the whole length of the south road, coming toward her in the dark, exact.

Across the room, Higu sat up.

"They're here," said Higu. She couldn't feel them. She didn't need to. She'd been watching Thea's face in the dark for two years and a half by the light of a moth. "Aren't they."

"A mile," said Thea.

Higu lay down again. Then sat up again. Then reached under her cot for her book, and her pencil, and the stub of a candle, and lit it, and wrote something, and Thea didn't ask what, because she knew. *04:15 the leaves. Thea felt them a mile off*. Higu had written down every single thing that had happened to the five of them since a market road on the first day, and she wasn't going to stop for this.

***

Kit came in at first light.

Solemn, nineteen, in the king's grey, mud to the knee, with his horse steaming in the yard and the oak case in his saddle-bag and the seventh leaf, the heavy one, in his coat. He gave the case to Maren on the step, with both hands, the way you'd give somebody a baby you'd been told not to drop, and then took the seventh out of his coat and gave her that too, and stood back.

"Eight to fourteen, miss," said Kit. To Maren. Then, looking past her, at Thea in the doorway in her socks with her hair still in its night plait: "He said to tell you there's no hurry. Master Oswin. He said he's stopped guessing too."

"Did he."

"Yes, miss. And the cat girl said to tell you the same." Kit hesitated. "And the Warden's holding something and won't put it down. The cook says. I don't know what that means. They said you'd know."

"I know," said Thea.

PenguChi came out behind her with Waddles under one arm and looked at Kit, mud to the knee, steaming, as solemn as a heron.

"You came last time," said PenguChi. "In the winter. What's your name? I asked then and you said *Kit* and went red."

"Kit," said Kit. And went red.

"Kit," said PenguChi, in the voice, the low warm one that steadied people, that had steadied a whole table of them the night the stone rang. "Come in. There's porridge. Gigichi made it, so it's terrible, but it's hot."

"I heard that," said Gigichi, from inside.

"You were meant to," said PenguChi. And Kit went in, red to the ears, with his hat in his hands, and Thea stood on the step with the case in her arms and the seventh leaf on top of it and felt them hum.

***

She opened it on the long table, where she'd opened the first six in the winter, with all of them round her.

That was a rule now. Nobody had made it. In the winter she'd opened the case on her own at four in the morning by candlelight because she couldn't wait, and they'd come down one by one and found her there with six oiled sheets laid out in a row and her hands flat on the table either side of them and her face doing something none of them had ever seen it do, and Xola had said *you could have woken us*, very hurt, and Thea had said *I know*, and they hadn't talked about it again. This time she waited. She sat at the end of the long table with the case shut in front of her and the seventh leaf on top of it, and ate Gigichi's porridge, which was terrible, and waited until all four of them were round her and Maren was in the doorway with her list and Bram behind her eating an apple.

Then she opened it.

Felt. Tissue. Six oiled sheets, each one folded once along a line Oswin had ruled for the purpose, so that the fold went between frames and not through them. A note on top in Oswin's hand, which Maren read out, because Thea couldn't read it and never would: *Eight to fourteen. Both sides. Edwy checked every line twice. That is the end of the first part. I do not know what it is a list of. I have stopped guessing too. O.* And under it, in a different hand, smaller, a little uneven: *Every line. Twice. E.*

"He's never signed one," said Maren. "Hana says."

Thea looked at the four letters. *E*, and the full stop, and the words before it that she couldn't read. A boy of twenty who'd come up through the cellars with a jar of oil in the autumn to burn all of this, weeping. She touched the *E* with one finger.

Then she unfolded the first sheet, and put her hand flat on it, and Parsed.

***

She'd tried to explain Parse to them once, in the first winter, the week she went Spark, and failed. She'd said *it's like when you look at a web page and you can see the code under it*, and Xola had said *I can't see the code under a web page*, and Thea had said *no, I know, that's the point*, and they'd all looked at her, and she'd given up and said *it's like reading*, which wasn't true either.

It was like this. You put your hand on a thing that was written in the circle's hand, the oldest one, the one that wasn't writing the way music isn't writing, and you went *in*. Not through your eyes. Through the place under your eyes where windows were. And the lines on the paper stopped being lines and became structure: this inside that, this calling that, this ring holding these things and passing them to that ring, the way a function holds its arguments and passes them on. You couldn't read what it *said*. Not yet. Not at Lantern. It was written, she'd worked out in the winter, by a hand more than three tiers above hers, and when she tried to read the actual words of it they swam, the way a page swims when you've been awake too long. But you could see the *shape*. You could see what was inside what. You could see whether it was well.

It was well.

Leaf eight. Both sides. Exact. Not a fault. Not one place, anywhere, where the lines ran close and Oswin's hand or his pen had slipped and the structure caught on itself like a thread on a nail. She'd found three faults on the first leaf, in the autumn, before the dividers; she'd found none on the six. She found none now.

Leaf nine. Exact. She felt, very faintly, at the bottom of the second side, the place where Edwy had found the two errors in the winter and Oswin had done it again: a seam, like a mended sock, perfectly mended. Leaf ten. Exact. Eleven. Twelve.

She went on. She didn't look up. She knew they were watching her face; she could feel Higu's book open, and Xola holding her breath, and Gigichi not holding hers on purpose, loudly, to show she wasn't. Thirteen.

Fourteen. The seventh one. The heavy one, the one Kit had carried against his chest. She unfolded it and put her hand on it and went in, and it was exact too, and at the bottom of the second side the frame stopped, halfway down the page, and there was nothing under it. Blank. Ruled margins, Edwy's, and Oswin's corner mark, and nothing.

The end of the first part.

She took her hand off it. She sat back.

"Exact," said Thea.

Xola let her breath out. Squiddles, on her head, went from grey to a cautious sort of pink.

"All of them?" said Gigichi.

"All of them," said Thea. "Seven leaves. Both sides. Not a fault anywhere." She looked at Maren. "Tell Oswin. Tell Edwy. Tell them exact. Use that word. Don't let Hana put it in nice words."

"I'll tell them," said Maren. And for the first time since Thea had known her, in two years and a half, Maren didn't write it down first. She just went out to the step, where she kept the blue cord with the white bead, and Thea heard her say it out loud to the cord, in her small precise voice, the way you'd say it to a person. *Exact*.

***

She chalked them up after breakfast.

The wall was the plastered one at the end of the long room, by the stair, where Higu had drawn the map of the lake road in the first winter, the week they found out about the jetty, and nobody had ever washed it off. The map was still there at the top, faded, in Higu's careful chalk: the market road, the lake road, the stone wall, the little square at the bottom that was the cabin, and a cross on the end of a line that was the jetty, and beside it, in Higu's writing, *here*. Under it, since the winter, Thea's frames. Leaves one to seven. Twenty-eight of them, in rows of four, chalked as exactly as she could chalk them, which wasn't very, because chalk wasn't a pen and plaster wasn't paper.

She added twenty-eight more.

It took her all morning. They helped. Not with the chalking; nobody else could chalk them, because nobody else could see what Thea was copying when she put her hand on the oiled paper and went in. But Xola held the sheets flat against the wall for her, and Gigichi brought more chalk from the box in the hall when she ran out, twice, and PenguChi stood behind her the whole time saying nothing in the steadying voice, which was a thing PenguChi could do now, since the winter: say nothing in a way that steadied you. And Higu sat on the bottom stair with her book on her knees and copied the wall as it went up, frame by frame, the way she'd copied the first twenty-eight in the winter, because chalk came off and pencil didn't.

A frame was this.

A ring. Small, a hand across. Not the circle; Thea had thought so at first, in the winter, and then seen it wasn't. The circle had fourteen rings. This had one. It was the *idea* of the circle, the way a little drawing of a house on a map is the idea of a house. And in the middle of the ring, a mark, a different mark in every frame, small and dense and complicated, the kind of mark that in code would have been a reference to something defined somewhere else. And out of the middle, through the ring and past it, lines.

Lines out. Some frames had one. Some had two, or three, or six. One, in the winter, had had eleven. They went out of the ring in different directions, at different angles, different lengths, and each one ended in another small mark, and every one of the end marks was different from every other one.

And on the right side of the ring, an arc.

Just a short one. Drawn outside the ring, close against it, on the right, the way the fourteenth ring of the circle in the summoning hall had its right half drawn and left blank. The same shape. Thea had known it at once, in the winter, the way you'd know a function you'd written yourself. *The way home*, Cursiffe had written on the oiled sheet over Oswin's big copy of the circle, in Valcrest, last summer, in blue English capitals: *IT'S A FORM WITH A BLANK BOX*. The second arc. The return.

In every one of the first twenty-eight frames, there were marks on it.

Small ones. Ticks. Little strokes across the arc, close together, like the marks a builder makes on a beam to show where to cut. Different numbers of them in different frames. She'd chalked them all in the winter, every tick, as exactly as chalk would let her, and she'd stood in front of them every night since and refused to count them.

She didn't count them now. She chalked them. Frame twenty-nine: ticks. Thirty. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. All the way down leaf eight, across leaf nine, onto ten. Ticks. Ticks.

Frame thirty-seven: ticks.

Frame thirty-eight.

She stopped with the chalk in her hand.

No ticks.

The arc was there, close against the right side of the ring, the same shape as every other. The lines were there, going out; four of them, in this one. The mark in the middle was there. The arc was just empty. Blank. Drawn, and left.

She looked at the oiled sheet under her hand for a long time. She went back in, to make sure. She felt the structure of it, the frame and the ring and the four lines and the arc, and the arc was well, it was exact, there was nothing wrong with it. It was just blank. On purpose. The way the fourteenth ring was blank on purpose.

She chalked it. Blank.

She chalked thirty-nine. Blank. Forty. Blank.

Behind her nobody said anything. Xola was still holding the sheet. PenguChi was still standing there. On the bottom stair Higu's pencil had stopped.

She did all of them. Fifty-six. She didn't stop again. She chalked every frame to the end of the fourteenth leaf, the last one halfway down the page with nothing under it, and stepped back from the wall with chalk to the elbows and her plait coming down, and looked at it.

Fourteen rows of four.

The top nine and a bit, ticks. The bottom four and a half, none.

"Right," said Gigichi.

She was standing at the end of the long table with her arms folded. Gruff and small, with her gold pocket watch in her fist, the one with the leaf engraved on the lid, the one she'd had in her hand when she came through the floor of the hall and hadn't let go of since. She'd been quiet all morning. Gigichi was often quiet; it was a different thing from PenguChi's quiet. PenguChi's steadied you. Gigichi's was where she kept the thing she was going to say until she'd decided whether to.

She'd decided.

"I know you're not going to guess," said Gigichi. "I know you're not going to say what it is. You said, in the winter. *If I say it I'll start seeing it*. Fine." She looked at the wall. "Then don't. Count them."

Thea looked at her.

"Count them," said Gigichi again. "Not what they mean. What's there. You can count what's there without guessing. Higu does it every day. That's all Higu does. She doesn't guess what a moth means, she writes down where it went." She unfolded her arms. "So count."

Thea looked at the wall for a long time.

Then she said, "Higu."

"Yes," said Higu.

"Lines out. Every frame. Count them."

Higu turned a page in her book, to the copy, and started.

It took an hour. It shouldn't have; there were fifty-six frames and none of them had more than a dozen lines. But they did it the way Higu did everything, twice, once each, Higu reading from her book and Thea checking against the chalk with her hand on the oiled paper, so that if either of them got one wrong the other would catch it. *Two by two*, Edwy would have said. Red ticks.

They wrote it on the wall. Under each frame, small, in Higu's hand, a number.

*3. 1. 5. 2. 2. 8. 4. 1. 6. 3*. Down the rows. Thea read out the ticks too, by the end, not counting them as anything, just counting them, the way Gigichi had said: what's there. And she found, doing it, the thing she'd been not letting herself find since the winter, and it wasn't a guess, it was arithmetic, which was a different thing entirely.

In every frame with ticks, the number of ticks was the same as the number of lines out.

Frame one. Three lines, three ticks. Frame two. One, one. Frame six, eight and eight. All the way down, frame after frame, to thirty-seven: four lines, four ticks.

One tick for each line.

She didn't say anything. She looked at the wall, and she felt the others look at it, and she felt them find it too, one after another, the way you feel a room go quiet in pieces. Xola first, because Xola always saw things first and said them last. Then PenguChi. Then Gigichi, with a sound in her throat she turned into a cough. Higu had found it before any of them, because Higu was the one writing the numbers down, and Higu hadn't stopped writing. Her pencil was going very steadily, and her face was doing nothing at all, and there were tears going down it onto the page.

Nobody said what it meant.

Thea had told them not to. In the winter. *If I say it I'll start seeing it, and then I'll see it everywhere, and I'll never be able to read what it actually says*. And they'd listened. They were still listening. All four of them, in a row, at the end of the long table, looking at a wall of chalk, and not one of them said it.

"Frame fifty-five," said Higu. Very steadily. "Two lines. No ticks."

Thea looked at it. Second from the end. Two lines out of a small ring, going away in different directions to two different marks. An empty arc.

*In the eleventh year of my reign*, a dead king had written, in a letter in a cupboard behind an iron door, three hundred years ago, *the circle brought two. A man and a woman. They asked me every day for nine years to send them home. Neither of us was answered*.

"Don't," said Thea.

"I'm not," said Higu. "I'm counting." She wrote *2* under fifty-five. Then she looked at the last one.

They all looked at the last one.

Frame fifty-six. Halfway down the second side of the fourteenth leaf. The heavy one, that Kit had carried against his chest. Thea had chalked it last, at the bottom right of the wall, below everything, on its own, because it was on its own: the last frame, with nothing under it but Edwy's ruled margin and blank paper.

A ring. A mark in the middle, dense and complicated, different from every other mark in the middle of every other ring. And lines out. Lots of them. So many that when she'd chalked it her hand had started shaking and she'd had to stop and put the chalk down and start again. They went out of the ring all the way round, in every direction, close together, like the spokes of a wheel, like the rays of a sun a child draws. And each one ended in its own small mark, and every mark was different.

And on the right, close against the ring, the arc. Blank.

Higu counted. Out loud. Slowly. Pointing at each one with the end of her pencil, not touching the chalk.

"Thirty-three," said Higu. "Thirty-four." She stopped. Her pencil moved. "Thirty-five."

Nobody said anything.

Squiddles, on Xola's head, went white.

"That's us," said Xola.

***

It was the only thing anyone said for a long time.

Thea sat down on the bottom stair beside Higu. She didn't decide to; her legs did it. She sat there with chalk to her elbows and her plait come down and looked at the bottom right corner of the wall, at a little ring with thirty-five lines going out of it like a child's drawing of the sun, and felt it, the way she'd felt the leaves on the south road at a quarter past three in the morning.

Not on the wall. The wall was chalk. In the copy, on the oiled paper on the long table. In the original, in a box with *KEEP* on the lid, on a shelf in a cellar in Solmere three hundred miles south. The same frame, in the circle's oldest hand, that hand that was older than the kingdom, that had drawn the middle rings of the circle and the blank right arc of the fourteenth and every one of the fifty-five frames before it.

It was them.

A king had found forty leaves under the first stone of the summoning hall, more than three hundred years ago, and put them in a box, and locked them in a cupboard behind an iron door with his own seal, and they'd lain there in the dark ever since, sealed, and nobody had touched them. And two years and a half ago, in a hall on the other side of the vault wall, a circle had pulled thirty-five people through a floor because they'd loved a man who'd been dead for three years and gone for thirty-four.

And in the box, in the dark, on the fourteenth leaf, halfway down the second side, a frame had written itself.

"It's still running," said Thea. Out loud, quietly. Not a guess. A thing you could see. "The list. It's not a record somebody kept. It keeps itself. Every time the circle runs, it adds a frame." She swallowed. "It wrote us down."

"It wrote us down," said Higu. And wrote that down.

Gigichi had come and sat on the stair above them. She didn't say anything. She put her hand on the top of Thea's head, the way you'd put it on a child's, very briefly, and took it away, which from Gigichi was a speech.

"Can you read the ticks?" said PenguChi. From the table. In the voice.

"No," said Thea.

"When can you?"

"I don't know." She looked at her window. She didn't need to; she knew what it said. *Lantern*. And under it, the bar to Torch, which had sat at eighty-four since the snow and said eighty-five now, because something had moved in her this morning, putting her hand on the fourteenth leaf, and she'd felt it go. "When I'm Torch. Maybe. It's written above Lantern. Torch might be enough. It might not." She was quiet. "It's written above me. That's all I know. I can see it's there. I can see it's exact. I can't see what it says."

"Then that's what we know," said PenguChi. "That's enough for today."

"It isn't," said Thea.

"No," said PenguChi. "It isn't. It's what there is." She said it in the voice, and it worked, the way it always worked, which was infuriating, because Thea knew exactly what she was doing and it still worked. "Write it down, Gu."

Higu wrote it down.

Thea got up. She went to the wall, and took the chalk, and in the empty right arc of frame fifty-six, the last one, the one with thirty-five lines, she didn't write anything. She wrote it under it, small, in the space below where Higu had written *35*.

She went down to the lake at dusk.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to stay in the long room and look at the wall, which was what she'd done every evening since the winter: sit on the bottom stair with Higu's copy on her knees and look at the chalk until it went dark and refuse to see it. But at sunset she found she'd put her boots on, and her coat, and gone out of the door and down the market road and down the lake road, past the stone wall, past Gigichi's hedge with the last of the white still in it, past the tree at the land end of the jetty that was up to Gigichi's knee now and had more leaves than anyone was counting, and out along the planks.

He was at the end. Of course he was.

She hadn't seen him since Alea went. Nobody had, much; he'd been to the hall twice, with the fish, and gone again. Higu said the lamp on the last plank had been lit every night. Higu said he still said *Tobyn* at the first cast, every morning, and lately, at the second, very quietly, *Alea*, as if it were a thing you could say to the water and it'd get there.

He was sitting with his legs over the end and the rod beside him and the three hens in a row behind him, the red one in the middle. The lamp wasn't lit yet. He didn't turn round. He never did. He'd hear her on the planks, the way he heard everyone.

"Exact," he said. Before she'd got there. To the water.

She stopped.

"You read it."

"I read it." He didn't turn round. "Fifty-six."

She sat down. Not beside him. On the plank behind him, between the red hen and the one with the bald patch, with her back against the last post and her knees up, the way Alea sat at night. The red hen looked at her with one eye and decided she was allowed.

They sat there. The sun went down behind the hills on the far side of the lake and the water went gold and then grey.

"It wrote us down," said Thea. "It keeps itself. Every time the circle runs. It added us."

"Yes."

"I'm not guessing what the marks mean."

"I know."

"Don't guess for me."

"I wasn't going to," he said.

She looked at the back of his head. She couldn't see his face; nobody could. She'd stopped minding that a long time ago. She'd learned the rest of him instead, the way he'd taught them all to without meaning to: the shoulders, the hands, the way he held still. He was holding very still now. Not the way he held still for a fish. The other way. The way he'd held still at the edge of the light on the night of the bonfire the winter before last, when she'd gone Candle with her hand up and he'd been there, at the back, and she'd known.

"You're thinking about it," said Thea. "The ticks."

"I'm thinking you counted," he said. "Instead of guessing. On a wall. All five of you." He was quiet. "That's what I'm thinking about."

"Gigichi made me."

"I know. I read that too." He picked up the rod. He didn't cast. He just held it, across his knees, the way he held it when he was going to say a thing and wanted something in his hands while he did. "When I was nineteen," he said, "I found a sword in the ground in Kharvos, on a hill, on its own, like a marker. I didn't know who'd made it. Nobody did. I still don't. I carried it eight years and stood it over her for seven and never once found out. And you know what I did, in the end, after all that time, when I wanted to know?"

"What?"

"Held a chisel on it while a king who used to be a smith brought the hammer down," he said. "And cut it in half." "Because the only way I was going to know anything about it was to see what was inside." He put the rod down. "You've been looking at the outside of a thing for two years. And today you looked inside, and it had your name on it. Not your name. Your *line*." He was quiet. "That's a lot for one day."

Thea didn't say anything for a while.

"I want to go home," she said. It came out very small. She hadn't meant it to come out at all. "I want my mum. I want to finish my degree. I've got a year left and it's been three years on Earth and I don't know if they'll let me back." She swallowed. "And there's a list on a wall that says some people went home and some didn't, and I'm not allowed to say which bit is which until I can read it, because I made the rule, and I'm going to keep it, and I hate it."

"I know," he said.

"You don't want to go home."

He didn't answer that. He never had. She'd asked him, in the winter, on this jetty, and he'd said *mostly*, about something else, and she'd said *me too*, and he'd put his hand on the back of hers for a moment and taken it away.

He didn't put his hand on hers now. He reached up, without turning round, and lit the lamp on the last post. The little flame went up in the dusk, yellow, and steadied, and the jetty came up out of the dark around them, all thirty-four planks, and the three hens, and her own knees.

"Frame thirty-seven," he said. "The last one with marks. You'd know, wouldn't you. If you could read it. How long ago that was."

"Yes. Probably."

"And frame thirty-eight. The first one without." He looked at the lamp. "Something happened between those two. Thirty-seven had a hand on the arc. Thirty-eight didn't." He was quiet. "That's not a guess. That's counting. The same as yours."

Thea looked at him.

"Somebody stopped," she said. Slowly. Not guessing. Counting. "Between thirty-seven and thirty-eight. Whoever made the marks." She swallowed. "And nobody started again."

"Not yet," he said.

She sat there in the lamplight with her back against the last post and the red hen's warm weight against her hip and felt it, from three hundred miles off and from half a mile up the hill, from the box with *KEEP* on it and from the oiled paper on the long table and from a wall of chalk at the end of a room: fifty-six frames, exact, running. The last one with thirty-five lines and an empty arc on the right, drawn, and left, on purpose.

*Not yet*.

He'd said that to Rowan once, on the hill above Wrenhaven, the first year, with a demon in pieces at his feet, when Rowan asked if he'd ever come back. *Not yet*. Not *no*. The whole town knew he'd said it. It was the most hopeful thing anyone had ever heard him say.

"Exact," said Thea. To the water.

"Exact," he said.

She stayed till the lamp needed oil. Then she walked back up the lake road in the dark, past the tree and the hedge, and up the market road, and into the long room, where the others were asleep or pretending to be, and sat down on the bottom stair in front of the wall, and looked at it. At *TODO*, under the last frame, in her own hand.

And under that, she found, when she held the candle up, a line that hadn't been there at noon. In Higu's careful chalk. Very small.

*not yet*.
