Kingsferry rang like a bell tower, and Dennis had never been so glad to hear anything in his life.

He heard it from the river, before the *Patient Widow* even came round the last bend. Faint, at first, and then all at once, the way a town comes up out of the morning mist: the quays, the cranes, the warehouses with their doors open, the boats three deep along the south wharf, the smoke from the forges going up straight in the still air. And the noise. The ordinary roaring noise of a river port at the seventh hour of the morning, carts and gulls and hammers and somebody shouting about fish.

And under the noise, if you were Dennis, the bells.

Hundreds of them. Little cracked bells, high and thin and slightly off, coming off the quay like spray off a wave. *Fresh this morning*. A bell. *Best price on the river*. A bell. *I'll have it for you by noon*. Bell. *My last offer, sir, I swear on my mother*. A whole peal of them, that one, the moneylender at the end of Rope Street, whose mother had been dead for twenty years and who swore on her about forty times a day.

Dennis stood in the bow of his own boat with his hand on the rail and his face up and listened to Kingsferry lie to itself, and grinned.

He'd missed it.

That was the thing nobody understood. Not Sabine, who understood most things. Not Deston, who could hear the threads between people and should have understood this. He'd tried to explain it once, on the road south, and they'd looked at him as if he were mad. Wrenhaven didn't ring. Valcrest, at the end, hadn't rung either: a whole city of forty thousand people keeping one secret so carefully that nobody said a single untrue thing about a water-carrier for a week. It had been beautiful. It had also been, for a man who'd heard every lie in every room since he was seventeen, like going three months without salt.

Kingsferry was salt.

"You're smiling," said Sabine, beside him.

"Listen to it."

Sabine listened. She was a former mercenary captain with fifteen years of caravans behind her and a face like a closed ledger, and she didn't hear bells, and she listened anyway, because she was Sabine and he'd asked.

"I hear a port," said Sabine.

"It's *lying*," said Dennis happily. "All of it. Constantly. To everyone." He spread his hands at the quay. "Isn't it wonderful?"

Sabine looked at him for a long moment.

"You're the only man alive," she said, "who comes home from a war and is glad to be cheated."

"I'm not going to be cheated," said Dennis. "I'm going to do the cheating." He paused. "Ethically."

"You've never once in your life done anything ethically."

"I've done *lots* of things ethically," said Dennis. "I just don't make a fuss about it." And Sabine, who'd been his business partner for two years and had a cut in his boat, laughed, the short dry bark of a woman who'd heard a great many lies and liked his best, and went to see about the ropes.

***

They came in at the south wharf on the ninety-seventh day.

The *Patient Widow*, low and wide and tarred black, with old Haskett at the steering oar saying *easy, easy* to nobody and his two boys on the poles, and Calvin up the mast with the kettle in the crook of his arm, waving at the dock men, who waved back, because everybody on the south wharf knew Calvin. Four days from Wrenhaven. Haskett said nobody had ever come down from the lake country in four days. Calvin said it wasn't him. It was him.

They'd left Group 6 at Duskhollow on the first morning. All of them on the quay there, in the mist, the five of Group 6 and Petra in a row on the landing stage, and Xav at the end, not waving, with his face doing nothing at all, and Garrick's half of Hana's cord taken out of his pack and put into Garrick's hand at last. Malcolm had written something in his book as the barge pulled away. Dennis had seen him. *IF*, and then nothing, and then he'd looked up and seen Dennis looking and shut the book.

And now Kingsferry. And the infirmary.

Rachael was off the barge before it touched.

Dennis watched her go. She didn't wait for the plank. She went over the side onto the wharf in one long step, the way she'd gone over the side of a barge in the Narrows with her bag in her hand, and walked up the wharf toward the river gate without looking back. Small and quick and dark, with her bag over her shoulder and Sister Maud's forty-year needle case in it, and her arms not folded for once, because she was going somewhere.

Gabe went after her. Not close. He'd learned. Five paces behind, with his big hands in his pockets and his sword on his back, the way he'd walked behind her all the way down the long road from Valcrest and back up it, near enough that she knew he was there and not so near that she'd have to do anything about it.

Deston went after Gabe.

Dennis sighed, and went after Deston. Somebody had to. *Rachael always with two*, Sabine had said, in the spring, on the river, and nobody had ever said she could stop.

***

Sister Maud was on the infirmary step.

She was always on the infirmary step at the seventh hour, with a broom, because she said you could tell everything about the day from who came down the river street at the seventh hour and in what state. She was seventy-three, and as small as Rachael, and as brown and creased as an old glove, and she'd run the river-gate infirmary for forty years. She'd given Rachael the needle case in the spring with the words *come back, you've been setting collarbones wrong*, and Rachael had said she had not, and Maud had said she had, and they'd both been right.

She saw Rachael coming up the river street. She stopped sweeping.

Rachael stopped at the bottom of the step.

They looked at each other. Two small women, one old and one not, on either end of four stone steps, with a broom between them.

"You're late," said Maud.

"Three months," said Rachael.

"Four. You said two." Maud leaned on the broom. "There's a boy in the end bed with a collarbone. I've been saving it for you."

Rachael didn't say anything for a moment.

Then she came up the four steps, past Maud, and in at the door, without stopping. And Dennis, at the bottom of the street with Gabe and Deston, heard her voice from inside, already, sharp and dry and perfectly at home: "Who set this? This is *wrong*."

And Maud, on the step, smiled, very slightly, into her broom. And went back to sweeping.

No bells. Not one. Not on the whole of the infirmary step.

***

It was a good week.

Dennis would think that afterwards. That it had been a good week, the first one home, and that it had been good the way the last day of a holiday is good: because you don't know it's the last one.

Gabe went back to Harl's forge on the south wharf on the first morning, and Harl, who was sixty and deaf in one ear and had burned his own eyebrows off three times, looked up from the anvil and said *you're late* and handed him a hammer, and Gabe struck for him all day and came back to Rope Street with soot to the shoulders and a grin like a lantern. Calvin went up the masts. Deston went to talk to everyone, the way he did; the chandler downstairs, the woman at the bakery, the dock men; and came back every evening with half the river's news and none of its lies, because people didn't lie to Deston, somehow. They just didn't.

And every night after supper, Deston went up on the roof.

The same roof. The flat leaded roof above the chandler's on Rope Street, with the chimney stacks and the view down the river, where he'd sat every night for three years with his hand out flat over the parapet, listening to a thread that was faded at his end. Dennis went up with him, the second night, because Deston looked as if he might want somebody, and sat on the leads with his back against a chimney and watched him do it.

"Still there?" said Dennis.

"Still there." Deston had his hand out. Flat. Palm down. Pointing east and a little north, over the warehouses, toward nothing. "Gold. Both ends. All the way." He was quiet. "He's at the fort. He's not moving. He's just there." He tilted his head. "It feels like somebody sitting very still in a room they used to know."

Dennis didn't say anything.

"In five weeks," said Deston, "he'll walk out to the grave. With her. For the first time." He took his hand back. "I'll feel it from here. I'll feel it stop." He looked at Dennis. "I said I'd listen. On the night. I told Gabe. We're all going to be up here."

"Then I'll be up here," said Dennis.

Deston smiled. Then he put his hand out again, flat, over the parapet, toward the east, and Dennis sat against the chimney and listened to Kingsferry ring below them in the dark, the taverns and the quays and the bedrooms, all the little cracked bells of a city lying itself to sleep, and didn't hear a single one from the man beside him.

He never did. Deston was like Declan that way. It was the only thing the two of them had ever had in common, apart from Gabe.

***

On the eighth morning, there was an apple on the step.

Dennis and Deston were walking Rachael to the infirmary. It was the seventh hour, a grey wet morning with rain coming and the river high and brown, and Gabe had gone to Harl's early because Harl had a rush on, so it was the two of them, one on each side, the way Sabine's rule said. Rachael between them, a pace ahead, not touching anyone. Talking about the boy with the collarbone, who was mending, and who'd been set wrong by a bonesetter on the north wharf who Rachael said should be thrown in the river.

They came round the corner into the river street, and Rachael stopped.

Dennis nearly walked into her. He caught himself. And looked up the street, where she was looking, at the infirmary, at the four stone steps with the door at the top.

Maud wasn't on the step. That was the first thing. Maud was always on the step at the seventh hour. But it was raining, a little, and she'd have gone in.

And on the top step, in the very middle of it, exactly in front of the door, there was an apple.

***

It was red.

Dennis would remember that, for some reason, more than anything. Not the shape of it, or the place, or what it meant. The red. It was the reddest thing he'd ever seen. Redder than a cherry. Redder than blood, which he'd seen a good deal of, in the last two years. A red show apple, polished till it shone, the kind the apple-sellers stacked in a pyramid at the front of their baskets because they looked best and tasted of nothing. Sitting on the wet grey stone of the infirmary step in the rain, perfectly still, perfectly round, with a single drop of water running down its side.

Exactly in the middle. Exactly where you'd stand, if you'd come to the door, and were going to knock.

Rachael didn't move.

Dennis stood beside her on the wet cobbles, and *listened*.

He did it the way he always did. Not trying. Just letting the street come in. And the street came in: the river street at the seventh hour in the rain, the chandler two doors down putting out his shutters, a woman at a window shouting at a child, a cart going by at the bottom. And the bells. The ordinary morning bells of Kingsferry. *Be there in a minute*, from the window. Bell. *It's not going to rain much*, from the chandler, to somebody. Bell. Little cracked lies, all down the street, the way there always were.

And round the step, nothing.

Not a bell. Not a voice. Not the ordinary sound of a step in a street in the rain. A silence. A round, close, perfect silence, with the apple in the middle of it, like the silence in the middle of a ring when you drop a stone in a pond and the water hasn't yet decided which way to move.

Dennis had heard that silence before.

Once. In the first autumn, on this same river, in a grain factor's yard. A man in a green coat had said *lovely weather* to him, pleasantly, and Dennis had listened for the bell, the way he listened for it in everything, and there hadn't been one. Not because it was true. Because there'd been nobody inside the words to tell a lie. Nobody inside the man.

He heard that now. Round an apple. On a step.

Nobody inside it.

***

"Dest," said Dennis, very quietly.

"I know." Deston was looking at the apple with his head on one side and his eyes not quite focused, the way he looked at the threads. "There's nothing on it. Not even the nothing the threadless have." He was quiet. "They've got a gap, Den. Where a thread should be. I can feel the gap, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue." He swallowed. "That hasn't even got the gap. It's like it's not there. It's like there's a hole in the step the shape of an apple."

Dennis didn't say anything. He watched Rachael.

She hadn't moved since the corner. She was standing in the river street in the rain with her bag over her shoulder, very straight, looking at the apple on the step. And Dennis watched her face do the thing it did in the infirmary when somebody came in bleeding. Go flat. Completely flat and practical, all at once, as if a door had been shut on everything else.

"Rae," said Deston.

"I know," said Rachael.

"He said tell me. If they ever came again. He said I pull the thread and he'll feel it."

"I know what he said."

"So I'm—"

"No," said Rachael.

Deston stopped.

Rachael didn't look at him. She was still looking at the apple. Her voice was perfectly level.

"He's at a fort on the edge of the red," she said. "With his daughter. In five weeks he's going to walk out to her mother's grave with her for the first time in nine years. On the full moon. Higu worked it out." She was quiet. "He's walked that road on his own eight times. This is the first time he hasn't had to."

"Rae—"

"You're not pulling anything," said Rachael. "Not for an apple." She hitched her bag up on her shoulder. "It's an *apple*, Dest."

***

Then she walked up the steps.

Dennis's heart went into his mouth. He nearly grabbed her. He didn't; you didn't grab Rachael, not ever, not even now. He took a step after her and stopped, and Deston beside him took a step and stopped, and they stood at the bottom of the infirmary step in the rain like two men at the edge of a cold river, and watched her go up.

One step. Two. Three.

She stopped on the third, below the top. And looked at the apple, close. Six inches from the toe of her boot.

Then she bent down, and picked it up.

Not with her hand. Dennis saw that. She pulled the cuff of her sleeve down over her fingers first, quite calmly, the way you'd pull your sleeve down to pick up a pan off a stove, and picked it up through the cloth. She held it up and looked at it. Close. In the grey light, with the rain on it. Turning it over, once, the way she'd turn a wrist over to look at a break.

And Dennis, at the bottom of the steps, listened.

The silence moved. It went with the apple. A round close silence, in Rachael's sleeved hand, moving through the morning noise of the river street like a hole moving through cloth. Nobody inside it.

Rachael came back down the steps with it. Past Dennis. Past Deston. She didn't look at either of them. She walked across the river street, through the rain, to the river wall on the far side, the long low wall of grey stone along the top of the bank where the street ran beside the water, the wall a hunter had come through once, in the first autumn, two buildings along. The river below it was high and brown and fast with the rain.

She stopped at the wall.

She looked at the apple one more time.

Then she drew her arm back, and threw it.

***

It went a long way. Dennis hadn't known she could throw like that. It went up over the wall in a long high arc, red against the grey, the reddest thing in the world, turning over and over in the rain. And came down in the middle of the brown river with a small flat splash that you wouldn't have heard if you weren't listening for it.

And was gone.

The river took it. The current had it under and away before the rings had even spread. Downstream. Toward the sea.

Rachael stood at the wall with the rain on her face and looked at the place where it had gone.

"Terrible apple," she said.

Just that. Not loudly. Not to anyone. The way you'd say it about a bad apple from a market stall that you'd bitten and spat out. With deep contempt. In a perfectly level voice.

Dennis listened to it.

No bell.

She meant it. Every word. She stood at the river wall in the rain and meant, with the whole of herself, without a single crack in it anywhere, that it was a terrible apple. And Dennis, who'd heard every lie in every room since he was seventeen, and had heard Rachael tell perhaps four hundred of them, mostly the word *fine*, stood in the river street and heard her not tell one, and found that his eyes had gone hot.

Then she turned round, and walked back across the street, and up the four steps of the infirmary, past the place where the apple had been. She didn't step round it. She stepped right on it, square, with her whole weight, the way you'd step on a crack in a pavement to show it who was in charge.

And opened the door, and went in.

From inside, a moment later, very sharp: "Who left this window shut? I said *open*. Every morning."

***

"*I'd have thrown it in the river*," said Deston.

They were still standing at the bottom of the steps. In the rain. Neither of them had moved.

Dennis looked at him.

"Valcrest," said Deston. "The morning after the threadless left. The apple on her sill. He took it off and closed his hand and it was gone." He was looking at the infirmary door. "And she said, *you didn't have to do that*. And he said *I know*. And she said *I'd have thrown it in the river*." He swallowed. "And he said *I know you would*."

Dennis didn't say anything for a moment.

"Winter told you that?"

"Winter told everyone that. In the channel. At dawn. Before anybody else could." Deston was quiet. "And then Rachael wrote one line, under it. *It was a terrible apple*."

The rain came down on the river street. The chandler had his shutters out. The woman at the window had stopped shouting at the child. Somewhere down at the south wharf, a hammer was going, very fast, very steady, the way only one striker in Kingsferry struck.

"She threw it in the river," said Dennis.

"She threw it in the river," said Deston. He'd started to smile. Slowly. In the rain. "He knew she would."

***

Sabine posted a watch that night.

She didn't make a fuss about it. She never made a fuss about anything. She came up to the room above the chandler's after supper, where Group 4 sat round the table with the kettle in the middle of it and a jug and the rain on the window, and listened to Dennis tell it, all of it, the step and the silence and the river and *terrible apple*. And then she stood up, and said *right*, and went out. And by midnight there was a man of the river watch at each end of the river street, and one on the roof of the chandler's opposite the infirmary, and Sabine herself in the doorway of the bakery across from the step, in her old mercenary's coat, with her sword across her knees, not sleeping.

"You don't have to," said Rachael, at the window, looking down at her.

"I know," said Sabine.

"It's an *apple*."

"It's an apple that came to your door," said Sabine. "And didn't knock." She settled her back against the bakery doorframe. "I've guarded caravans for fifteen years. I've had a lot of things come to a lot of doors. The ones that knock, I don't worry about." She looked up at the window. "Go to bed, healer. I'm paid for this."

"You're not," said Rachael. "Not for this."

Sabine looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight from the bakery.

"No," said Sabine. "Not for this."

And Rachael, at the window, didn't say anything. Then, after a while, she said *goodnight, Sabine*, quite quietly, and didn't shut the window, and went to bed.

***

Dennis didn't sleep either.

He sat at the table with Emrys, by the lamp, with the jug between them. Emrys had his half of the cord on the table in front of him. Purple and gold, knotted, with its half-bead and its strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot. He'd been looking at it all evening.

"I'll have to tell her," said Emrys.

"Hana?"

"The Advisor said. All of us. When the cords were given out." Emrys turned the half-bead over with one finger. His voice was hoarse; it was always a little hoarse, since it came back. "Anything that bears on the summoned. Anything strange. Tell Hanarisu, and she'll know where it needs to go." He looked at the strip. "Since the girl in Wrenhaven did whatever she did on the jetty, they carry anything. Clean. As much as you like."

"Then tell her."

Emrys picked up a pen. He wrote on the strip, slowly, in the small careful hand of a court musician who'd copied out a thousand songs. Not many words. Dennis watched him write them. He couldn't read them; the native script slid off his eyes like water off oil. But he watched the shape.

*An apple on Maud's step this morning. Placed. Rachael threw it in the river. She is well. We are watching.*

Emrys held it. In his closed hand. In the lamplight.

After a moment, his face changed. Very slightly. And he opened his hand and looked at the strip, and Dennis saw the shape of the letters on it change, the way they did, to something else.

"She's awake," said Emrys. "In Solmere. She says: *Thank you. Tell her it was a terrible apple*."

Dennis laughed. He couldn't help it. In the middle of the night, in the room above the chandler's, with Sabine in a doorway across the street with her sword on her knees and something out there in Kingsferry that had come to a door with nobody inside it.

"She already knows," he said.

***

He tried something, before dawn.

He hadn't meant to. He'd been sitting at the window, watching the street, and Sabine in the bakery doorway, and the infirmary step in the lamp-light from the corner, wet and grey and empty. And he'd thought: *if it came to the door once, it'll want to know what happened*.

People always did. That was the thing about people. You left something for somebody, a gift, a threat, a letter, and you wanted to know. You'd come back and look. Everybody did. Every cheat on every quay in Kingsferry came back to look at the mark, after.

He didn't know if it was people. But it had come to the door.

So he opened his window, and looked at the infirmary step across the street, at the place in the middle of the top step where you'd stand to knock. And he thought about red. The reddest thing he'd ever seen. Polished. Perfect. A single drop of water running down its side.

And he wrote, with one finger, in the air outside the window, very small, the word he always wrote. The word he'd written on the night of the third hunter, when a thing came through the river wall for Rachael and he'd made it see her at a window she wasn't at.

*There*.

And on the top step of the infirmary, in the lamp-light, in the rain, there was an apple.

Not a real one. Dennis knew that; Dennis had made it. Glamour. A picture of a thing, put into the air where the eyes would find it. Red, and round, and polished, exactly in the middle of the step, exactly where the other one had been. Anyone walking down the river street would have seen it. Sabine, in the bakery doorway, saw it, and went very still, and then looked up at Dennis's window, and saw him there, and understood, and didn't move.

And Dennis sat at the window and held it there. It was hard. Glamour wasn't meant to be held; it was meant to be quick, a trick, a moment. He held it anyway, the way Thea had held the channel on a jetty, by all accounts, on the night of the barge, though he'd never be a Lantern and didn't want to be. Holding it. A red apple on a step in the rain that wasn't there.

And waited.

***

She came at the grey hour.

He saw her at the bottom of the river street, by the river wall, where the street came up from the south wharf. He didn't see her arrive. She was just there, the way the threadless had always just been there, on the far quay, in the first autumn. A woman. Not old, not young. In a plain brown dress and a shawl, with a basket on her arm. A basket of apples. Red ones, polished, stacked in a little pyramid at the front.

She stood at the bottom of the street and looked up it.

At the infirmary step. At the apple on it. The one that wasn't there.

Dennis held his breath. He held the Glamour.

She looked at it for a long time. Very still. With her head a little on one side. The way, Dennis thought, with his skin going cold all down his back, the hat man had looked at him across a quay in the first autumn, after the hunter went into the river: *as if it had learned something*.

Then she smiled.

He'd remember that. A small, pleasant, ordinary smile, the kind a woman with a basket of apples gives you at a market stall when you've asked how much. It went across her face and stayed there.

And she turned round, and walked away. Down the river street, toward the wharf, past the corner lamp.

Dennis watched her go under the lamp.

And afterwards, for the rest of his life, he could never remember whether she'd had a shadow. He'd been looking. He'd been looking straight at her, with the lamp-light full on her and the cobbles bright and wet under it, and he'd *looked*, and he couldn't remember. It was the one thing he could never get back. It was as if somebody had reached into his memory of that moment and taken that one thing out, very neatly, the way you'd take a coin out of a purse, and left everything else.

She went round the corner, and was gone.

He let go of the Glamour. On the step, the red apple that wasn't there went out like a lamp. The step was grey and wet and empty.

Across the street, in the bakery doorway, Sabine had stood up. She'd seen the woman. She was looking down the street toward the corner, with her sword in her hand, very still.

She looked up at Dennis's window.

"In Kingsferry," said Sabine. Quite quietly. Not a question.

"Or was," said Dennis.

***

He told Rachael at breakfast. All of it.

He'd thought about not telling her. He'd thought about it for an hour, at the window, after Sabine had gone down the street to the corner and come back and shaken her head. *Gone*. He'd thought about the hundred things you could say instead. *Nothing came*. *Quiet night*. Easy things. He was very good at easy things. He'd been saying them all his life.

He told her anyway. Because she'd stood at a river wall in the rain and meant every word, and he'd heard her mean it. And because he'd once said to a man on a terrace above a lake, *you never rang, not even with trust*, and the man had said *a joke in a lie's coat*, and Dennis had thought about that a great deal since.

Rachael listened. She sat at the table with her cup in both hands and her face perfectly flat and listened to all of it. The Glamour. The woman. The basket. The smile. The shadow he couldn't remember.

When he'd finished, she didn't say anything for a long time.

"She came back to look," said Rachael, eventually.

"She came back to look."

"And she smiled."

"Yes."

Rachael looked at her cup.

"At home," she said, "I used to think bad things happened to me because I was the kind of person bad things happened to." Her voice was very level. "I had the immune system of a wet paper bag. Every winter. Every cold. Every hospital. I used to sit in waiting rooms and think: *well, obviously. It's me*." She turned the cup. "You stop expecting anything else."

Dennis didn't say anything. He listened. No bell.

"Here," said Rachael, "something keeps coming for me. Three hunters. A man in a hat. A woman in the barley. And now apples." She looked up. "And every single time, somebody's standing in the way. Gabe in a doorway. Deston walking me past. Sabine in a bakery with her sword on her knees all night. You at a window making fake fruit." She was quiet. "*Him*, in a yard, closing his hand."

"Rae," said Dennis.

"I'm not the kind of person bad things happen to," said Rachael. Very quietly. "Not here. Here I'm the kind of person people stand in front of." She put the cup down. "I'm still getting used to it."

No bell. Not a whisper of one.

Dennis sat across the table from her with the rain on the window and the kettle in the middle of the table, and found that his eyes had gone hot again, for the second time in two days, which was two more times than in the previous two years put together.

"Don't tell anyone I said that," said Rachael.

"I'll tell everyone you said that."

"I'll throw *you* in the river."

"You'd have to touch me," said Dennis.

Rachael looked at him for a long moment over the cup. Then her mouth did something at the corner. Very small. Very dry. The thing it did, these days, more often than it used to.

"I'd use a boat-hook," said Rachael, and got up, and took her bag, and went down the stairs to go to work. With Gabe five paces behind her, because Gabe had come up from the forge at dawn when he heard, and Deston behind Gabe, and Sabine at the corner, and the whole of the river watch at both ends of the street.

And Dennis, at the window, watching them go down the river street in the rain, toward a step that she'd walk up the middle of without stepping round anything at all.

***

In the evening, he went up on the roof with Deston.

They didn't say much. Deston had his hand out, flat, over the parapet, east. Dennis sat against the chimney. Below them, Kingsferry rang the way it always did, a whole city of little cracked bells, lying itself to sleep.

"Still there?" said Dennis.

"Still there," said Deston. "Gold. Both ends." He was quiet. "He didn't feel anything. I didn't pull." He tilted his head. "He's just sitting. At the fort. Very still."

"Good," said Dennis.

He meant it. He listened to himself say it, and there was no bell, and he was glad.

Somewhere down by the south wharf, in a city that size, a woman with a basket of apples was going about her business, or wasn't, or had never been there at all. And at the top of the river street, four stone steps led up to a door that stood open, with the lamp lit over it, because the healer inside had said *open, every morning*, and meant it.

And in the middle of the top step, where you'd stand if you'd come to knock, there was nothing at all.

Just a step. Wet, and grey, and stood on.
