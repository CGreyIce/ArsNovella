Rowan saw them come over the last hill, and went in, and sat down at the table, and found that his hands were shaking.

He hadn't expected that. He'd expected a great many things, for sixty days. He'd expected to see them come over that hill in the first frost the way he'd seen one man come over it eight times: alone, with the rod on his shoulder, not looking at anything. He'd expected to stand at his gate. He'd expected the girl to be beside him, because of the letter. He'd expected to feel glad, and to not show it, because the mayor didn't.

He hadn't expected to look up the road at the end of the afternoon and see two of them. Coming over the brow of the hill by the crooked hawthorn, in the low gold light, side by side. A man with a rod. A girl with a sword on her back and something held against her chest in both arms. Close. Walking at the same pace. Hers.

And to stand there for a moment at his gate with his hand on the post. And then turn round and go in and shut the door, because he'd known, the moment he saw them, that he couldn't stand there and watch them come down into the town, and through the square, and past his gate, and on down the lake road, and not shout.

So he'd gone in.

And now he was sitting at Odette's kitchen table with his hands flat on the wood either side of two cups, and they were shaking, and he couldn't make them stop.

***

The cups had been on the table for sixty days.

Odette's good one, with the blue rim. And the chipped brown one, with the crack going down from the lip like a river on a map, from a cabin by a lake. Side by side, at his end, facing the door. He'd put them there the afternoon Maren put a folded square of cream paper into his hands at the river gate. *Late pumpkins. A.* *Both of us. A.* He'd washed them every morning since, though they were clean. And put them back. Facing the door.

Odette had never once told him to put them away.

She came in from the back now, from the yard, where she'd been taking in the washing before the frost got it. She stopped in the doorway. She looked at him, at the table, at his hands either side of the cups. She'd seen it too; Rowan knew she had. The kitchen window looked up the north road, and Odette noticed everything that came down it, and had for thirty years.

"He didn't stop," said Rowan. "At the gate. He went past."

"I saw."

"He went on down the lake road. With the girl. In the dark." Rowan looked at the cups. "He didn't even look at the house."

"No," said Odette. She put the basket of washing down on the dresser. "He wouldn't."

Rowan looked at her.

"He said he'd knock," said Odette. "Like a stranger." She began folding a sheet, very fast, very neat, the way she folded everything. "A stranger doesn't come in through the back on his way past with a bucket. A stranger goes home first." She snapped the sheet. "He'll have the hens to see to. And his face to wash. And whatever the girl's carrying to put somewhere. And then he'll come up the hill." She folded the sheet in half. "On purpose."

"How do you know that?"

Odette looked at him over the folded sheet.

"Because that's what I'd do," she said. "If I'd been planning it since a kitchen in the summer." She put the sheet on the pile. "Sit still, Rowan. You'll wear the table out."

He sat still.

His hands went on shaking.

***

The frost came down at dusk.

He watched it from the window. Over the square, white on the cobbles. Over the roofs, white on the slates. Down the hill, white on the stubble and the hedges and the reeds along the lake road. He couldn't see the cabin from Odette's kitchen; the hill was in the way. But he could see the lake beyond it, the far part, going dark, going still. And a thin line of smoke going up from behind the hill, straight, in the cold, the way smoke from a cabin chimney goes up on a still night. He'd lit that fire himself, at noon, when the frost was coming and he'd known, the way you know weather, that they'd be home before dark.

Odette lit the lamp.

She didn't light the good one. She lit the kitchen lamp, the plain one, and put it on the dresser, not the table, so the table was in the half-light and the door was in the light. And then she sat down at the far end of the table, by the window, with her mending, and didn't say anything.

Rowan sat at his end. With the cups.

The church clock struck seven. Then eight.

At half past eight, he heard someone coming up the hill.

***

He knew the step.

He'd known it twelve years. You learned a man's step, in a town this size. You learned it in the market and on the lake road and in the square, and if he'd been a soldier with you once, at a cabin door in a war, you learned it in your bones. It wasn't a heavy step. It was the opposite. A man who walked as if the ground were something he was being careful with. Unhurried. Not slow. The step of somebody who knew exactly how far he was going and had decided he was going to get there.

It came up the hill from the lake road in the dark, on the frost, past the militia hall. Rowan heard it go past the militia hall. He heard it stop there, for a moment, a little longer than you'd stop to catch your breath. And go on.

Up through the top of the town. Past the church. Into the street.

Past the gate.

It didn't come in at the gate. It didn't come round the back, where everybody came. It went on along the front of the house, on the cobbles, the way nobody ever went, to the front door. The one on the street. The one nobody used.

And stopped.

Rowan sat very still at the table.

At the other end, by the window, Odette had stopped sewing.

He heard nothing for a long time. A man standing on a doorstep in the frost, in the dark, not knocking. He heard him breathe. Once. The way you breathe before you go into cold water.

And then he knocked.

Three times. Not loud. Not soft. The knock of a man who'd never knocked on a door in this town in twelve years except once, on a night in the summer before a road south, and who'd thought about how to do it ever since.

***

Rowan didn't remember getting up.

He was in the hall. He was at the door, with the bolt in his hand, which was shaking, and then the bolt was back and the door was open and the cold came in, and the frost-light from the street, white, and there was a man on the step.

A plain man. In a plain coat. Without the rod; he'd left it at the cabin. Without a bucket, or a sword, or a girl. With his hands at his sides and his face doing something that Rowan couldn't see, had never been able to see, not once in twelve years, and didn't need to, because he'd learned the rest of him in a door in a war.

They stood there. Rowan in the hall with the lamp behind him. The man on the step with the frost.

"Evening," said the man.

Rowan looked at him for a long time.

"You'll want a cup," he said.

"I'll want a cup," said the man.

***

He came in.

Through the front door, into the hall, which nobody had come through in twenty years except the man himself, once. And Rowan shut the door behind him and shot the bolt, slowly, and stood with his hand on it for a moment, because he wasn't sure, just then, that his legs were his.

When he turned round, the man was standing in the kitchen doorway, looking at the table.

At the two cups. Side by side. At Rowan's end. Facing the door.

He didn't say anything. He looked at them for a long time. Rowan saw his shoulders go. All the way. The way they'd gone, he'd heard, at a gate in the east under a wall full of old soldiers with their hands on their chests.

"How long?" said the man. Very quietly. "Have they been there."

"Since Maren gave me your letter," said Rowan.

The man turned his head and looked at him.

"Sixty days," said Rowan.

The man didn't say anything at all.

At the far end of the table, by the window, Odette had stood up. She was standing very straight, with her mending in her hands, the way she stood at council when Old Mabry was about to start on the drains. And the man looked at her. And Rowan saw her face do the thing it didn't do in public, ever, and then stop doing it, by force, and be the city secretary's face again.

"I'll put the kettle on," said Odette.

"Odette," said the man.

"Sit down," said Odette. "Both of you. You're letting the cold in just by standing there."

***

They sat down.

Rowan at his end, the man across from him. The two cups between them. Odette poured. Not the brewer's second-best, this time. Tea, hot and strong, in a pot. Rowan didn't know why. He didn't ask. She poured it into the two cups at Rowan's end, the blue-rimmed one and the chipped one, and pushed the chipped one across the table to the man, and took her own cup, a third one, and went back to her end by the window, and sat, and picked up her mending, and didn't look at either of them.

The man looked at the chipped cup in front of him.

He put both his hands round it. Not to warm them. His hands were never cold. Rowan had noticed that the first winter, twelve years ago, when Diwa's always were. He put his hands round it anyway, the way you'd put your hands round a cup somebody else needed to warm theirs on.

"You kept it," said the man.

"You said you'd want it back."

"I did." The man looked at the crack running down from the lip. "I meant it as a joke. Sort of."

"I know you did," said Rowan. "I kept it anyway."

The man was quiet for a long time.

Rowan waited. He'd stood at a cabin door every night for a season, in a war, with a sword in his hand and a little fox girl asleep upstairs, and nothing had come, and he'd stood there anyway. He could wait at a kitchen table.

"I don't know where to start," said the man.

"At the beginning," said Rowan. "You said. In the summer. *From the beginning*." He picked up his own cup. "I've got all winter."

***

The man looked at the cup in his hands.

"It was my name," he said. "Declan."

Rowan didn't move.

He'd heard it before. Once. At this table. On the night before the road south, the man had said it, very quietly, *it was my name, Declan, before, a long way from here*, and Rowan had said *don't*. *Not tonight*. *You'll tell it badly*. And put his hand flat on the table between them, near the man's, not touching it.

He didn't say *don't* now.

"Declan," said Rowan. Trying it. In his own mouth. A strange short name with a hard sound in the middle, like a stone in a stream. "Declan."

"Before," said the man. "A long way from here."

"How long?"

The man looked at him over the chipped cup.

"Thirty-four years," he said. "And another world."

***

He told it.

Rowan would never be able to tell anybody afterwards what it had been like. He tried once, to Odette, who'd been there and didn't need telling. He couldn't. It wasn't a thing that went into words twice. It had gone into words once, at a kitchen table, from the half-light, in a voice Rowan had known for twelve years, and that was all the words it was ever going to go into.

He could tell you the shape of it. That was all.

A room. In a city by the sea, a long way from here, in a world with no magic in it, and no windows, and no aura. Hot. With a fan turning in the ceiling. A man of twenty-six going to bed late after talking to his friends, in their voices, in his ear, the way they talked there, through a thing on your head. Going to sleep.

And not waking up.

And waking up instead in a cottage, on the other side of everything. A baby. With twenty-six years in his head and no way to tell anyone.

Rowan listened to that part with his hands flat on the table. He didn't say anything. He thought about a baby lying in a cradle in a cottage in a village nobody had heard of, looking at the ceiling, knowing everything, and not able to say one word of it. For years. He thought about that for a long time, while the man went on.

Six years of not knowing what was wrong with him. And then one night, under a blanket, frightened, saying a word out loud because it was the only one he had that might mean anything. A word from there. A short flat strange word like a stone dropped on a step.

And a door opening in the dark in front of his face.

The field behind his father's house, at fourteen. The garrison at fifteen, with a borrowed sword too long for him and a temper too short, and a young court mage on a wall at midnight who'd found him sitting in a gap looking at nothing. *You've got one too. You just can't see it*.

A girl in the infirmary at Cinderhold, with cold hands, who'd stitched him up three times in a month and asked him why he kept getting hurt.

***

Rowan had known her.

That was the thing. He'd known her twelve years ago, when they came to the lake. A fox girl with cold hands and her ears flat when she was cross and a laugh that went straight through you, standing at the door of a half-ruined cabin with a small child behind her skirts, looking at Rowan, the new militia captain, come down the lake road to see who'd bought the old Fenwright place. *Well*, she'd said. *You'll be wanting tea*. And he had.

He'd known her. He'd liked her. He'd stood in that door with her daughter asleep upstairs while she went east, because the man asked him to, and because she'd looked at him across that same door the morning they left and said *keep her*, and he'd said *I will*.

He'd never known any of the rest.

He listened to it now. How they'd met. How she'd laughed *at* him, not with him, the first year, until he worked out it was the same thing. How he'd taught her twenty-six letters on a slate with a nail, at night, in the infirmary, while the garrison slept, and she'd learned them in a week and only ever used them for three words. *In the flour on the table. In the frost on the window*. How they'd found the lake, and she'd stood on the end of a jetty that was half gone and said *here*.

How the war came.

***

Rowan knew the war. He'd been in it, at the other end. At a cabin door. He'd known the shape of it from there: everybody called east, the man and the healer going together, the long silence, and then a man coming home across the fields in the last week of summer, alone.

He'd never known the middle.

The man told him the middle.

The second night. The south line. A ditch, and forty soldiers in it, and a girl of twenty-three at the end with her arm broken, who wouldn't put her sword down. Dawn, and the infirmary wall open, and the healers gone. And the afternoon. Fires on a rise. A ring of them. And something in the middle that he'd seen from the wall.

An hour, with his hands in the stone, because a thin grey man beside him had said *that's an order*, and it was right.

And then *go*.

And the whole of the host between the wall and the rise, all afternoon, walking.

And her. On the rise. In the middle of a ring burned into the ground. Half gone. Still knowing him.

Rowan sat very still at the table and didn't look away from the man's face, which he couldn't see, while the man told him what she'd asked. And what she'd given him to carry home. Every word. In order. *Stay healthy. Live well. Listen to your father. I'm sorry, and I love you*.

*And then I did what she asked*.

The man stopped.

The kitchen was very quiet. The lamp on the dresser. The frost on the window. At the far end of the table, Odette had stopped sewing a long time ago and was sitting with her hands in her lap and her face perfectly still and wet.

Rowan didn't say anything.

He'd stood in a door every night for a season. He'd seen a man come home across the fields at dawn in the last week of summer, alone, with nothing on his shoulder and his face like a house with the fire gone out. And he'd moved out of the doorway. And taken the little girl upstairs. And sat on the end of the bed with her.

He'd never known what the man was carrying, coming across the fields.

He knew now.

***

He told the man something then. He hadn't meant to. It came out of him.

"She asked where her mother was," said Rowan. "That morning. When you came home." He was looking at his hands. "I'd taken her upstairs, when I saw you coming across the fields. I knew. From the way you were walking. I didn't know what, but I knew." He breathed. "I sat on the end of the bed. She sat up. She said *is Dad home*, and I said *yes*. And she said *where's Mum*."

The man was very still.

"I didn't answer," said Rowan. "I couldn't. It wasn't mine." He turned his cup. "I said, *your dad'll tell you*. And I waited. On the end of the bed. With her. Till you came up the stairs." He was quiet. "I've never told you that. I've thought about it every year since. Whether I should have said something. Anything. So she didn't have to wait for it."

The man didn't say anything for a long time.

"You were right," he said. Eventually. "It wasn't yours." He looked at the chipped cup in his hands. "And you waited with her. So she wasn't on her own when it came." He was quiet. "That's what you did, Rowan. Your whole life. You stood in a door so somebody wasn't on their own when it came."

Rowan didn't trust himself to say anything.

"I came up the stairs," said the man. "And you got up off the end of the bed. And you put your hand on my shoulder going past. Once. And went down." He breathed. "I've never told you I remember that."

***

He told the rest.

Rowan didn't stop him. Odette didn't. The church clock struck ten, and then eleven, and then midnight, and Odette got up once and made more tea, without a word, and put another log on the fire, and sat down again. And the man told it.

Eight years on a jetty. A girl growing up, with a sword, and a slate, and a basket of fish on a step. A window with a bar on it, stuck at forty for six years, and then moving. And then a hall in Solmere, a long way away, and thirty-five people coming up out of the floor of it.

His friends.

Rowan had known that much. Everyone in Wrenhaven had worked it out, in the end, the way a town works out anything, by watching. Fifteen of them had sat in this town for months. But he hadn't known how. He listened to the man tell him how. A circle on a floor, and a sword in the middle of it, and the sword tangled in the man, and the man tangled in all of them, so the net that came for strangers caught thirty-five people who'd loved him.

And the time.

*Three years*, said the man. *There. Thirty-four here*. And his father, alive, a long way off, in a world with no magic, having probably found somebody new. And the friends, who'd lost him only three years ago and come up out of a floor and found him thirty-four years older, fishing.

Rowan listened to that part with his hand over his mouth.

"Your father," he said. "He's alive."

"I think so. Probably."

"Does he know? That you—"

"No," said the man. "He thinks I died in my sleep. Three years ago. Which I did." He was quiet. "He buried me. The friends said he did it well. Shook every hand. Didn't cry once. And afterwards he sat on a low wall in the car park on his own, and a woman came out and sat beside him and took his hand." He was quiet. "He's with somebody." He turned the cup. "He's all right. I think he's all right."

Rowan didn't say anything. He thought about a man burying his son, a long way off, and keeping it together. And the son, here, at a kitchen table, thirty-four years older, saying *he's all right*.

He thought he'd never in his life heard anything sadder said more kindly.

***

The man told him about the summer.

The road south. The Lists. Forty thousand paper fishing rods. A girl's box on a roll, *daughter of Diwa, a healer, and of Ars*. A king's table. A way home that might exist and that only somebody at the very top of everything could open, and nobody had ever been there, and the king had said *I'll spend the kingdom finding it*.

"Will you go?" said Rowan. "If they find it."

The man looked at him across the table.

"It isn't mine," he said. "The way home. It's theirs. Nobody brought me. I came the other way." He was quiet. "I'd want them to be able to choose. Every one. And I'd want to be standing at the end of whatever it is when they go, so they're not on their own."

"And you?"

"I've got a lake," said the man. "And a girl. And a cabin with three hens that hate me." He looked at the chipped cup. "And a cup I want back."

Rowan nearly laughed. He didn't. His face did something instead.

***

And the east.

The man told him about the east last. Rowan had thought he might not. He'd thought that part might be the man's, and the girl's, and nobody else's, the way the grave had been the man's alone for eight years. He told it anyway.

The road. The water, every day, because a chaplain told him to. Eleven dinners in a forge. A wall full of old soldiers putting their hands on their chests, and him looking up. A gap in a parapet where he'd stood an hour once, nine years ago, with his hands in the stone. A letter from the thin grey man who'd told him to stand there. *I know. I knew on the wall*.

And a knoll, an hour out on the red. A ring of green. A tree a boy with antlers had planted. A cairn of children's stones from this town, with a white one at its head that he'd waded into the lake in the dark to get, and a white one on the top from Mother Breck. And two halves of a black sword standing side by side, ringing.

And a girl at the head of the cairn under a full moon, saying her mother's last words back to her. One at a time. Out loud. For the first time.

"*I love you too*," said the man. Quietly. Repeating it. "That was the last one. She said it last."

At the far end of the table, by the window, Odette made a sound.

Rowan looked at her. She had her hand over her mouth. She'd had her face perfectly composed all night, for six hours, through all of it, the hall in Solmere and the rise and the hour, and she'd kept it. The city secretary's face. And at *I love you too* she'd put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders had gone, and she wasn't stopping them.

The man looked at her too.

"She said you'd make her a curtain," said the man. Gently. "On the riverbank in Valcrest. When she wrote *both of us*. She said *Odette will pretend she isn't crying and make me a curtain*."

Odette took her hand away from her mouth.

"I did," she said. Her voice wasn't steady. She'd stopped trying to make it be. "Out of the council tablecloth."

"I know," said the man. "Rowan wrote."

"He did *not*."

"Maren," said the man. "Through Hana. Through Aldous. To me. At Cinderhold." He nearly smiled; Rowan could hear it. "Everything goes round now. The whole kingdom knows about the tablecloth."

Odette looked at him for a long moment. Then she got up. She went to the dresser, and opened the bottom drawer, and took out something folded. Red. With a gold fringe. And a lining of small blue flowers.

She put it on the table in front of him.

"For the loft," said Odette. "The girl's window. It's got no curtain. It never has. You never thought of it." She sat down again, very straight. "Tell Mabry and I'll deny it."

The man put his hand on it. On the red cloth with the gold fringe. Flat. The way you'd put your hand on something warm.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said Odette. "Just hang it."

***

It was nearly dawn when he stopped.

Not because he'd finished. Rowan didn't think it would ever be finished. Because the window over the dresser had gone grey, and the church clock had struck five, and the man had come, slowly, the way the Wren comes down to the lake, to the end of the part that was today.

*And then I came up the lake road and the hens went for me*.

He stopped. He looked at the cup in his hands. It was empty. It had been empty for an hour.

Rowan looked at him across the table.

He'd sat there all night. Twelve hours, nearly. He'd heard a man tell him thirty-four years, and another world, and a war, and a grave, and a way home, and a father, and thirty-five friends, and a girl saying three words under a full moon. He'd listened to every word, the way he'd said he would. *From the beginning. And I'll listen to every word*.

He found he didn't have anything to say.

He found that that was all right.

"Thank you," said the man. Very quietly. "For listening."

Rowan looked at him.

"Which one do you want me to call you?" he said. "Now."

He'd asked it in the summer. At this table. Before the road. And the man had said *the one you've always used*. Rowan asked it again now, because it was a different question now, and they both knew it.

The man was quiet for a long time.

"I was Declan for twenty-six years," he said. "There. In a world you'll never see. To people who loved me, who are here now, and call me that, some of them, and some of them call me Ice, which is a different story." He looked at the chipped cup. "And I've been Ars for thirty-four. Here. To you. To her mother. To her."

He looked up.

"Ars," he said. "The one you've always used."

"Ars," said Rowan.

"Ars."

Rowan nodded slowly.

"Then that's settled," he said. And picked up his own cup, which was also empty, and drank from it anyway, because that was what you did at the end of a thing, and so did the man, and at the far end of the table Odette laughed, wet, into her hand, at the two of them drinking from empty cups at dawn.

***

He walked the man to the door.

The front one. On the street. The one nobody used. He drew the bolt back and opened it, and the cold came in, and the frost-light, grey now with the dawn, and the street was white, and the roofs, and you could see the smoke from the cabin chimney going up straight over the hill.

The man stood on the step.

He had the red curtain over his arm, folded. He looked out at the white street, and down the hill, toward the lake you couldn't see from here.

"I've got to go," he said. "There's a basket."

Rowan knew.

The whole town knew. The empty basket on the step of the militia hall, with a stone in it, that everybody had walked round for sixty days. The girls had put it there. *And then he'd have to fill it*.

"Go and fill it," said Rowan.

The man turned round on the step. And held out his hand.

Not to shake. Rowan looked at it. The man's hand, palm up. Waiting. And Rowan understood, after a moment, and went back into the kitchen, and picked up the chipped brown cup from the table, the one with the crack running down from the lip like a river on a map, and came back and put it in the man's hand.

*Keep the cup. I'll want it back*.

The man looked at it.

Then he gave it back.

"Keep it," he said. "Here. On your shelf."

Rowan looked at him.

"I'll want to come back for it," said the man. "Often." He was quiet. "I've never once in twelve years come up this hill without a reason. I'd like to have one. A cup's a good reason."

Rowan stood in the doorway with the chipped cup in his hand and the frost coming in round his feet and couldn't say anything at all.

"Thank you, Rowan," said the man. "For the door." And then, after a moment, because he'd always known exactly what Rowan needed to hear and had always, until tonight, been too careful to say it: "For both doors."

He went down the step.

Down the white street in the grey dawn, with the red curtain over his arm, toward the lake road, past the church. Not fast. Not slow. Rowan watched him go. He didn't look back. At the corner he lifted one hand, a little, without turning round. The way he'd lifted the rod at the first bend of the lake road in the summer, to a man in a square who'd said *nobody follows*.

Then he was gone, round the corner, down the hill, toward a basket.

***

Rowan went back into the kitchen.

Odette was at the table, where she'd been all night, with her mending in her lap and her face washed. She'd washed it, at some point, when he was at the door. She looked up at him. At the chipped cup in his hand.

"He gave it back," said Rowan.

"I heard."

"He said he'll want to come back for it." Rowan looked at the cup. "Often."

Odette didn't say anything for a moment. Then she got up, and took the cup out of his hand, very gently, and took it to the basin, and washed it. Though it was clean. And dried it. And reached up, and put it on the shelf over the dresser, beside her own blue-rimmed one.

Side by side. Facing the door.

"There," said Odette.

Rowan sat down at his end of the table. In the grey light. And put his hands flat on the wood.

They weren't shaking.

He sat there a while, with Odette at the window, the two of them, not saying anything, the way they'd sat on the step of an empty cabin in the summer with a hen between them. And at some point the sun came up over the hills on the far side of the lake, and the frost on the window went gold, and then clear.

"Declan," said Rowan. To the kitchen. Very quietly. Trying it once more, in his own mouth, now that he'd been told. A strange short name, with a hard sound in the middle, like a stone in a stream.

Then he put it away. Somewhere safe. Where you'd keep a thing somebody had given you to hold, that you'd never need to use.

"Ars," he said.

"Ars," Odette agreed, and poured them both the last of the tea, cold, and they drank it anyway.
