Hana read the cords every morning at nine, at the reading table at the foot of the Council chamber, in front of twelve chairs and the king's.

That had been the bargain since the spring. Since the morning Jared went down the stair past her and stood between her and the room, and took the butt of Brask's staff in the breastbone without moving, and told twelve frightened men to read every word out loud. They'd done it. Not because Jared asked; because the king, at Thornwall, by Hyvred's hand in the channel, had said *that's the right way round*, and then come home at the thaw and said no to both of Vane's motions in his travelling coat, and then gone up the west tower for days. Every cord that came to Hana's hands came to this table. Every knot was read aloud. Nothing went to the guides or came back from them that the Council didn't hear the same morning, in her voice.

She'd hated it at first. She'd stood at the reading table in the spring with her ears flat and her tail wrapped round her ankle under her skirts and read Aldous's cords from Cinderhold and Corvin's from Thornwall to a room full of men who'd voted to take them off her, and felt every one of them watching her hands. Waiting for the charm. Waiting to see her do it.

She hadn't. Not once. Not in six months.

And somewhere in the second month she'd stopped hating it, and started to notice that the room was quieter when she read. That the Warden leaned forward. That the old Treasurer, who was deaf in one ear, had moved his chair. That Lord Vane, who had wanted the cords in his own clerks' hands, sat every morning with his own clerk beside him writing down every word she said, and that his clerk wrote slowly, and that she'd started, without meaning to, reading at the pace of his pen.

***

She'd read the Kingsferry cord three weeks ago.

It had come in the night. Emrys's, the red-and-white one with the little bone bead, the one she'd tied for him in the first autumn when the Wheel was new; and it had come cold, colder than a cord should, and when she'd put her fingers on the knots in the dark at the window of the east wing she'd known before she read the first one that something had happened that she would have to say out loud to twelve men in the morning.

She had. She'd stood at the reading table at nine with the cord in her hands, and Jared on the stair behind her, and Milli in the gallery with his tail very still, and read it.

*The rope walk burned. Sister Maud taken from the fire. A ring on the customs-house steps, lit for the infirmary window. Six of the turned. A priest of Kharvos, a demon, as strong as a fifth-tier man. Watchman Benno dead. A porter, Dob, drowned. Sabine's ribs. The girl went in. Four with her. She closed it from inside. The priest went into the river. The man in the hat is dead. Sister Maud died herself at dawn. The girl cannot mend. Emrys.*

Her voice had gone on *herself*. She'd stopped, and stood there, and the room had waited. And then she'd read it again from the start, because the Treasurer's good ear was on the other side, and he'd asked.

Nobody had said anything for a long time.

Then Lord Vane had stood up.

***

He stood up again this morning.

She'd known he would. He'd stood up every morning for three weeks, after the reading, in his grey at the temples and his voice like a good cloak. He'd never once said *alliance*. He'd never once said anything she could put her finger on and call a lie. He'd said, the first morning, *a demon in a priest's robe on the steps of the king's own customs house, in a royal port, with the watch dying on the stones*, and let it sit. He'd said, the second, *the only person in the kingdom who can undo this thing is a summoned girl in a river town with four friends and an old guide*. He'd said, the third, *we lost a healer of forty years because she was not protected*.

He'd made a new motion on the fifth day. One. Plain.

*That the healer Rachael be brought to Solmere. Under the Crown. To be kept safe, and to teach the Crown's own physicians, in the Crown's own house.*

It had sat on the table for two weeks and a half, waiting for the king.

And this morning the king was in his chair.

He'd come down from the west tower at last. Not in his travelling coat. In the plain dark coat with the burn on the left cuff that he wore to the forge, which Hana had never once seen him wear in this room. He sat in the thirteenth chair, at the head of the table, with Halvard at his right and Kestra behind him at the wall with her arms folded, and didn't look at anybody while Hana read. She read the day's cords. Corvin's, from Thornwall, about the bridge and Sergeant Dunmore and the healer's white hands. Aldous's, from Cinderhold, about the band: three men's widths of it now, three gaps, warm, and the cold under the grave leaning less every night. A short one from Emrys about the girl: still cold, still can't mend a cut finger, writing everything down.

The king listened to all of it with his big hands on the table, the left one over the right.

Then Vane stood up.

"Majesty," said Vane. He bowed. "The motion."

"I've read the motion," said the king.

"Then Majesty knows I make it with no ill will," said Vane. "The girl is the only one. The cords say so. Every cord this Council has heard since the spring says so. She is the only one in the world who has ever closed one of these things, and she's in a river town on the end of a hundred miles of water with a smith and a wind boy and a man who sells cheese." He spread his hands. "I'm asking for her to be kept safe. That's all. In the safest house in the kingdom. Where the Crown can protect her."

The king didn't say anything.

"With respect, Majesty," said Vane, "she should not be left where she's wanted."

The room was very quiet.

Hana stood at the reading table with her hands on the cords and her ears up, because she couldn't make them go down. She looked at the king. He was looking at his hands.

"Read me Emrys's report," said the king. "From Kingsferry. The first autumn."

Hana didn't understand.

"The guide's," said the king. "To the Advisor. About the man in the hat, the first time he came to the infirmary." He didn't look up. "Halvard. You have it."

Halvard looked at him for a long moment. Then he put his hand inside his coat and took out a folded paper. Old. Soft at the creases. Not a cord; a report, in a court musician's beautiful hand, every letter like a note on a stave. He'd carried it, Hana thought, a long time.

"Read it," said the king. "What he said to her."

Halvard unfolded it. He found the place.

"*The man said*," Halvard read, "*that he was there on behalf of his master, who had a household in the hills, west of the town, a large one. That his master would very much like to offer her a position. A healer's position, in his house. Under his protection. That she would want for nothing.*"

He stopped.

Nobody said anything.

The king looked up, then. At Vane. Not angry. Hana had never once seen him angry. This was a man who'd been sitting in a tower for days and on a forge stool for longer, looking at a thing until he knew its shape.

"A position," said the king. "In a household. Under protection. Wanting for nothing." He turned his hands over on the table. "That's what the man in the hat offered her. The first autumn. On the waterfront. With his hat in his hands." He looked at Vane. "And that's what you're offering her now. In my name."

Vane didn't move. His face didn't change. Hana watched it not change, and thought that it was the bravest thing she'd ever seen him do, and the most frightened.

"I offer it in good faith, Majesty."

"I know you do," said the king. "That's what frightens me." He put his left hand flat on the table. "Sit down, Vane."

Vane sat down.

***

The king stood up.

He didn't do it often, in this room. He'd said once, to Halvard, that a king who stood up in Council was a king who'd run out of patience; he liked to sit, and let them talk, and say *no* at the end. He stood up now. He was sixty-six and broad as a door and his coat had a burn on the cuff, and he stood at the head of the table with his hands at his sides and looked at twelve chairs.

"Here's my answer," he said. "To the cords. To the motion. To all of it. To the thing in the east that's found itself a friend." He didn't say *alliance* either. He didn't need to. "Write it down, Vane's man. Slowly. I'll wait for your pen."

The clerk wrote. The king waited for him.

"One," said the king. "The healer Rachael stays where she is."

Vane's clerk wrote it. Vane didn't look up.

"She stays in Kingsferry, in the river-gate ward, where Sister Maud put her. Where she's got the people who went into the ring with her and came out." He looked down the table. "She's not wanted in Kingsferry because she's in Kingsferry. She's wanted because of what she is. She'd be wanted in this keep. She'd be wanted in my own bedchamber with Kestra across the door. The only difference is that in Kingsferry, when they come for her, she's got four people who'll go in with her. Here she'd have a household." He let that sit. "So she stays."

"Two," said the king. "Every healers' order in the kingdom sends two of their own to Kingsferry. By the river, before the autumn rains. The river-gate sisters first, and the temple sisters, and the college physicians, Vane, yours too, the ones you wanted her to teach. They go to her. Not her to them. She can't mend. She can teach. Emrys says she's written it all down already, every stroke, and she'll write it again for anyone who asks, and she'll sit beside them while they learn it on the grey cases off the docks till their hands know it." He looked at Halvard. "And when they know it, they go west. To Thornwall. And east. To Cinderhold. And to every town on the Wren with a hook in its reeds."

The clerk's pen was going very fast.

"Three," said the king. "The Marshal sends two companies to Cinderhold. Not to march. To *stand*." He looked at the Marshal, who was very old and had fought under his father. "There's a line in the red a mile west of the fort, forty miles round, that something's writing deeper every night. A man of the summoned sat down on it and the writing went round him. They've three gaps in it now. Three men. Every night. I want two hundred. In shifts. All along the west side of it, as far as the old west fort and further, every night from dusk to the first grey, standing on the line." He was quiet. "*A ring's only a ring if it's closed*. The summoned girl at the lake said that. She's right. I want it full of holes."

The Marshal looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once.

"Four," said the king. "A crier in every river town from the lake to the sea. *Don't pick up hooks. If you've a cut that's gone grey and cold, go to a healer. Don't close it. Go to a healer*." He looked at the Treasurer. "The Crown pays. For the criers and the healers' roads and the companies. All of it. Out of my own purse first, Treasurer, before you start."

The Treasurer, who'd been about to start, didn't.

"Five," said the king. "No army into the black. No summoned to Solmere. I said it in the spring. I'm saying it again so Vane's man can write it twice."

The clerk wrote it twice.

The king stood at the head of the table for a long moment.

"Every one of you," he said, "wants me to answer a hammer with a hammer. You did in the spring. You do now. A demon on my customs house; send an army into the black. A ring that turns people; bring the only girl who can undo it into my house and lock the door." He shook his head. "I've been a smith longer than I've been a king. Both hands, I said, in the winter, at Thornwall. A hammer from the east and a holding hand from the west, and us between them on the anvil." He looked at his own two hands. "You don't beat a hammer with a hammer. You'll break the anvil. You find the pin."

"And what's the pin, Majesty?" said Vane. Very quietly.

The king looked at him.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I know what holds against them. Twice now. A man sitting down on a line. And a girl who goes into a ring and asks first." He was quiet. "So I'm sending them more of both. That's my answer. Healers and men to stand." He sat down. "It's not an army. It's what's worked."

***

Nobody voted.

That was the strange thing, after. Hana had thought they'd vote. They'd voted on everything in the spring; she'd stood at the foot of the table and watched the hands go up, seven to five, for taking the cords off her. This morning nobody asked for a vote and nobody offered one. Vane's motion sat on the table where it had sat for two weeks and a half, and the king had answered it, and Vane looked at it for a long time, and then reached out and turned the paper over.

Face down.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Hana saw the Warden see it, and the Treasurer, and the provincial lord from the downs who'd changed his hand in the spring. She saw Halvard see it, at the king's right. She saw Kestra, behind the king at the wall, unfold her arms.

"The cords, my lady," said Vane, without looking at her. "Tomorrow. At nine."

"At nine," said Hana.

"Read slowly," said Vane. "My man's hand is tired."

Hana looked at him for a long time across the chamber.

"I always do," she said.

***

She went up the west tower at noon with the day's cords.

That was the other bargain, her own, that nobody had asked her for. Every noon, after the reading, she took the cords up the long stair of the west tower to the Advisor's room at the top, and laid them on his table, and he read them again himself, slowly, with his spectacles on, the way he read everything. He'd never asked her to. She'd done it the first week, because he'd sat through the reading at the king's right with his face very still and she'd thought he might want them in his hands. He had. He'd said *thank you* and she'd come back the next day.

Aleaka was on the landing, two flights from the top, with Brisk on her wrist. She was always there, more or less. She said the west tower was the best place in Solmere to fly him from; Hana thought it was because the man at the top of it carried something very heavy and Aleaka could feel it from anywhere in the keep, and liked to be near enough to know if it got heavier.

"It's lighter," said Aleaka, as Hana went past. "Since the spring. A lot." She scratched Brisk's head. Brisk tried to bite her. "It went heavier three weeks ago. For a day. The morning you read the river cord." She was quiet. "And then lighter again. Than before."

"Why?"

Aleaka shrugged. "I only feel it. I don't know what it is." She looked up the stair. "He's writing."

***

He was.

At the table by the west window, with the drawer under it shut, and the map on the wall behind him that he'd made smaller every year: six feet by four of oiled linen with the whole kingdom on it and an inch of blank at the bottom where the downs ran out. He had a sheet of cream paper in front of him, and a pen, and he'd written two lines on it, and was looking at them.

He looked up when she came in. He didn't cover the paper. He'd never once covered anything when she came in; she'd noticed that, in the first week, and thought about it a great deal.

"My lady," said Halvard.

"My lord." She put the cords on the corner of the table. "From this morning. Corvin's and Aldous's and Emrys's."

"Thank you."

She should have gone. She always did; she put them down and said the day's word for the weather and went. She didn't. She stood by the table with her ears up and her tail very still, because she'd seen the top of the paper, upside down, and she could read upside down; she'd learned at the reading table.

*How are the hens.*

That was the first line. Of course it was. It was always the first line. Halvard had written it at the top of every letter for years, and Ars had answered it at the top of every one of his.

The second line was blank. Halvard had written something and crossed it out, and written something else and crossed that out too, and there was a lot of ink, and then nothing.

He saw her see it.

"He said *ask me another*," said Halvard.

Hana didn't say anything.

"In the spring. In his answer. *Ask me another*." He looked at the crossed-out lines. "I've known him eighteen years. I asked him one question, on a wall, by not asking it. And in the spring I asked him a second, *why did you go*, and he answered it. He said he was happy, and he didn't know what to do with it." Halvard took his spectacles off and held them. "And then he said *ask me another*. And I've been sitting here three weeks with the drawer shut trying to think what."

He looked at the paper.

"I tried *how is the girl*. The healer. And crossed it out, because I know how she is; I've read every cord. I tried *will you come to Solmere*. And crossed it out, because he won't, and he shouldn't, and it's not a question, it's a summons with a question mark on it." He turned the spectacles over. "I tried *did you know*. About the nail. And crossed it out, because he told me. He didn't." He was quiet. "Every question I can think of is one I already know the answer to. Or one I'm only asking so he'll say what I want."

Hana stood by the table.

She thought about the king, at the head of the Council table that morning, with his hands at his sides. *He's never asked anybody for anything*, he'd said, in the west tower, in the spring, with the first letter on his knee; Halvard had told her that, one noon, when she'd asked him nothing at all. *He's never asked anybody for anything. And he's asked you to tell me.*

She thought about a man on a jetty who'd sat at the near end three weeks ago, Gigi said in the channel, for the first time; who'd let a seat come up out of the roots under him; who'd said *you?* to a girl on a bench and nearly made her fall off it.

"What would you ask him," said Halvard. "My lady. If you could ask him one thing."

Nobody had asked Hana what she'd ask Ars. Not in four years. Not the Council, not the king, not the thirty-five, who'd all decided long ago what they'd ask and mostly never would. She stood by the table in the west tower and thought about it properly, the way she thought about knots.

"What he needs," she said.

Halvard looked at her.

"He's never asked anybody for anything," said Hana. "The king says. And everybody's always asking him things. *Are you coming. Where did you find it. Why did you go*. All of them about what he did, or what he'll do." She looked at the paper. "Nobody's ever asked him what he needs. I don't think anybody's ever thought he did."

Halvard didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he put his spectacles back on, and dipped the pen, and wrote it on the second line, under the hens, slowly, in the kingdom's hand, without crossing anything out.

*What do you need?*

He looked at it.

Then he signed it. The one letter. *H.* And under it, after a moment, carefully, the way a man draws who has practised in secret on the backs of old reports, a fish.

Hana looked at the fish. It had a fin. It had an eye in the right place. It looked, very nearly, like a fish.

"That's better," she said, before she could stop herself.

"I've been practising," said Halvard, with great dignity, "since the spring."

***

He sent it by hand. He always did. A rider from the post-house at the north gate, on Mistress Hennet's standing order, all the way to the lake, nine days on a good horse; unsealed, folded once, with the one word on the outside.

Hana didn't send anything to the lake. She never had. Maren had a cord, on the Wheel, at Wrenhaven; but Maren was busy with her new hands and the grey cases, and the man on the jetty had never in his life wanted a string to anybody.

But she sent one to Kingsferry.

She tied it on the roof of the east wing at dusk, with Milli beside her on the leads with his tail round her ankle and his chin on his knees. A new cord. Not Emrys's; a new one, for the girl. She'd tied hardly any for the thirty-five; the Wheel was for the guides, and the thirty-five had the channel. But the king had stood at the head of the Council table that morning and said a thing, and it needed to go to the girl the way the king said things, not in a line in a window. In a knot. Held.

White and grey, for a healer. A bead of black horn, that she'd bought in the market at noon because it was the colour of a needle case she'd heard about in a cord three weeks ago. And in the knots, the king's words, the ones he'd said to her on the stair after the Council when she asked him what to send:

*Not to come. To teach. Where she is. The Crown asks for her by name.*

She tied the last knot and held it in her hands a long time.

"Honey," said Milli.

"Mm."

"You've been holding that for ten minutes."

"I know." She looked at it. "Emrys said. In one of his. Sister Maud told her once, in the first winter: *when somebody who knows what they're asking asks for you by name, you'll go. And I'll pack your bag myself*." Her ears went down. She let them. "She doesn't have to go anywhere. They're coming to her. All of them. That's what he said." She was quiet. "And Maud's not there to pack it."

Milli didn't say anything. He moved his tail, a little, round her ankle.

"I wanted it to be held," said Hana. "When she gets it. Not read. Held. So she knows somebody asked properly."

"She'll know," said Milli.

She let it go.

The cord went the way cords went, off the edge of the east wing roof into the dusk, along the Wheel, down the threads that only she could feel, west and north and down the river; and she felt it go the whole way, the way she felt every one go, a long thin pull in the middle of her chest like a fishing line running out. All the way to Kingsferry. To Emrys's hands, who'd carry it up the hill. To a window at the end of a long ward where a girl who couldn't mend anything sat every night at an old woman's table with the dose book open and a pen in a hand she couldn't feel very well.

Hana sat on the roof with Milli's tail round her ankle and felt it arrive.

And then, a long time after, very faint, so faint she thought she'd imagined it: the cord going warm. Not answered. You couldn't answer a cord unless you were a guide. Just warm. The way a thing goes warm when somebody's been holding it in both hands for a long time against their chest.

She didn't tell anybody. It wasn't hers to read aloud.

In the morning she went down to the reading table at nine and read Corvin's and Aldous's and Emrys's to twelve chairs and the king's, at the pace of Lord Vane's clerk's pen, slowly, every word. And when she'd finished, the Warden leaned back, and the Treasurer moved his chair, and Vane said *thank you, my lady* without looking up, which he had never once said in six months.

Up in the west tower, the drawer was shut.

On the north road, nine days off on a good horse, a rider with a bay and a leather bag was carrying a sheet of cream paper with the hens at the top, and a question under them that nobody had ever thought to ask, and a fish, very nearly, with a fin.
