The lake had been iced at the edges for seventeen days.

Not much. A rim of it, grey and thin, along the reeds at the north end and round the jetty posts, that went out a little further every night and came back a little every afternoon when the sun got on it. The middle was still open, black and still. The fish were under the ice at the edges, where they liked the roof; Bram had found that out at the first ice, with a hand and a half of steel and a reed falling over, and had come down every morning since with his boy's rod to sit on the stool at the reeds and not talk about it.

Ars was at the end of the jetty with the line out through the gap where the ice didn't reach, because the jetty was where he sat. The lamp was lit beside him. He'd lit it early; he lit it earlier every week, as the dark came in. The hen was in Odette's kitchen, because it was too cold now for a hen on a plank, whatever the hen thought about it.

Alea was on the cabin step behind him, mending a strap on Astra's harness with an awl and a bit of waxed thread, and swearing at it under her breath in the way her mother had sworn at stitches.

It was a quarter of an hour after sunset. The far shore had gone. The town on its slope had its lamps lit, one at a time, up the hill. He was thinking about nothing. He was good at it. He'd had a lot of practice.

His window opened.

He hadn't opened it.

That had happened once before, on the road east, at sunset on the west rise above the red, with the boy who did it standing a few paces off: the red plain going down into the dark, put into every window at once by a boy with lights who'd only just learned how. Two seconds of it, shaking, and gone. He'd stood on the rise afterwards for a long time.

This one didn't shake.

***

A valley. Long and pale and narrow, white with frost under a moon three-quarters full, between dark walls of mountain. He knew it without being told. He'd never been there. He'd read it in Higu's drawings and Corvin's letters and in a picture John had sent of a tower roof at night, and he knew it the way you know a place somebody you love has described to you a hundred times.

People in it. Standing in the frost in rings, hundreds of them, coats and shawls and a soldier's grey, a woman in an apron, a man with a beard with frost in it, a girl with a plait at the edge of the outermost ring. Thirteen rings. He counted, because he was what he was, and it took him less than the heartbeat to do it. Every one of them had their face turned toward him. Toward the town. Toward whoever was holding the picture.

And on the left, outside them, a curve of more that stood the way furniture stands.

And in front of them. On the near side. Between the rings and him.

He didn't have a word for it. Nobody did; Hyvred had said that from the start, and Thea, and Corvin's eleven pages had never once got nearer than *the absence*. In John's picture it was a burn. That was the only way to say it. As if the picture were a page and somebody had held the middle of it too close to a candle: brown at the edges, curling, and in the middle nothing, not black, not dark, just *not picture*. The size of a hill. Sitting on the valley floor in front of the rings, with the frost round it and none on it.

Facing him.

The heartbeat ended. The window closed itself.

He sat on the end of the jetty with the line out and the lamp beside him and his hands on the rod, and he went still.

He felt himself do it. The thing at the bottom of him. Under the deciding, where the red was, where it had always been since a field behind his father's house when he was fourteen: it went quiet. All of it. The whole of it, the eighth tier and everything that had come up in him toward the ninth since the hall, all of it at once, still, the way the lake goes still in the moment before a pike comes up, when every small fish in the shallows has felt it coming and stopped.

Behind him, on the step, the awl stopped.

"Dad?"

He didn't answer for a moment.

"Dad. Your face." She'd stood up. He heard her. "I can't see your face. I never can. But I can see your back. And your back just—" She stopped. "What is it?"

"John," he said. "From the tower." He reeled in. Slowly, the way he always did, so the line came in flat and dripping over the ice. "It's moved."

***

Group 1 came down the lake road at a run eleven minutes later, with two lanterns between five of them, which meant they'd left in a hurry; and Maren behind them with her new pink hands pushed up her sleeves against the cold, and Bram behind her, big and grey, carrying a third lantern and his boy's rod, because he'd been on his way down to the reeds anyway, he said afterwards, he hadn't been coming because of anything.

They all came into the cabin. There wasn't room. There was never room. Higu stood by the stove with her book open against her chest and her pencil going. Thea sat at the table with her face very white and her hands flat on the wood, the way she sat when she was trying not to read anything. Xola stood by the door. PenguChi sat on the floor with her back against the wall. Gigichi came in last, in her cardigan, with the forget-me-nots in her hair gone brown, and sat on the end of his bed without asking, the way she'd sat on the last plank.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Thirty," said Thea at last. "It's thirty now. At the fourteenth place. Hyvred posted it after." She swallowed. "And it's turned round. All of them have. They were all facing the pass and now they're all facing the town. It's—" She stopped. "It's facing her. The tower. She looked at it on purpose. She told the king not to stand in front of her."

He'd read that, after the picture. *on purpose this time*. In Hyvred's small lowercase letters.

"I want to read it," said Thea. "The circle. Through Hyvred. Again. Now, while it's—"

"No," said Higu, without looking up from her book.

"Higu—"

"Three days in bed last time. And it looked at you. Down the line. You said." Higu's pencil didn't stop. "It's looking down the valley now. It'd see you coming before you'd got your shoes on."

Thea didn't argue. She put her hands over her face instead.

He looked at the wall.

He didn't mean to. It was just where his eyes went. Over the table, on Garrow's two iron pegs, a long grey-silver blade lay with the frost in it catching the lamplight; under it a drawing in blue crayon of a jetty with forty-one planks; beside that, on its nail, a list hung back to front, blank side out. He'd looked at that wall every day since he came home over the last hill with his daughter behind him. He'd looked at it every morning and every night and he'd never once taken the sword down.

He could feel it from across the room. He always could. The patience.

Everybody in the cabin saw where he was looking. Nobody said anything about it, in the very particular way that meant everybody was thinking about it.

"What are you going to do?" said Maren.

She was the only one of them who would have asked. She'd been one of Halvard's seven; she'd written everything down for twenty years, and crossed things out, and written them again. She asked things.

"I don't know," he said.

It was true. He heard it ring true in himself, the way Dennis would have heard it. He didn't know. He'd known for two years exactly what he was doing; he was ploughing, the king said, and fishing, and the bar came up a percent at a time, and he'd been east and come home, and he'd known. He sat at his own table now with his own people round him and his hands flat on the wood like Thea's and didn't know.

"Okay," said Maren. And wrote something down, and crossed it out.

***

They went up the lake road at midnight. All but one.

Alea didn't go up. She didn't live up there; she lived here, in the loft, where she'd lived all her life. But she didn't go up the ladder either. She sat on the step outside the door with Astra across her knees and the harness mended and nothing to do with her hands, facing the lake, and he sat beside her. The lamp burned on the end of the jetty. The ice ticked at the posts as it went out a little further.

"You're going," said Alea.

"I don't know."

"You are." She didn't look at him. "You went still. On the jetty. I've seen you do it before. Not many times. I could count them." She turned the black sword over on her knees. "Every time you did it, afterwards, you did something."

He didn't say anything.

"I'm coming," said Alea.

"I know."

"I'm packed. I packed while Thea was talking." She was. He'd seen her do it: the loft ladder, quietly, the good knife on her hip, Oakes's pike hook in her bag, the last four sheets of the king's cream paper in their oilcloth from under the drawing. "I'm Honed. I've been east. I stood on a wall at Cinderhold."

"I know."

"So I'm coming."

He looked at the lake.

He wasn't going to say no. He'd stopped saying no to her about things that mattered a long time ago. And she was right. She was Honed, and she'd been east, and her edge didn't dull while she held it. She'd be good. She'd be better than good.

He didn't say anything. He sat on the step beside her and waited, the way you wait with a line out.

She sat there a long time.

She looked at the lamp on the end of the jetty. She looked up at the town on its slope, at the lights going out one at a time, at the militia hall at the top where five lanterns had just gone in and the windows had lit. She looked at the north end, at the reeds, where a big grey man had cut one down a hand and a half past his edge and said *Reaching* in his own mouth, and Maren, with her burned palms, had said *congratulations, Bram*. She looked at the ice going out round the posts.

She looked for a long time at the lamp.

"Who lights it," said Alea. "If we both go."

He didn't answer.

"You light it every night," she said. "On the last plank. Every night of my life. I used to watch you from the loft. I never asked who it was for." Her voice didn't do anything. Her fingertips did; they went along Astra's edge, tapping, the way they did when she was keeping something down. "And when you went east I lit it. Gigichi lit it some nights. And you came over the hill and it was there."

"Yes."

"Somebody has to keep the lake," said Alea.

He turned his head and looked at her.

She was looking at the lamp. Her ears had come forward. Her face, in the dark, was her mother's face exactly, the way it was more and more now. And it was her own. It had been her own for a long time.

"Mum chose here," she said. "Out of everywhere. She could've gone anywhere with you. She said *here*." She swallowed. "Somebody has to be here. When you come over the hill. Somebody has to be on the end of the jetty with the lamp lit." She stopped tapping. "You've done it every night of my life. I can do it for a winter."

He didn't say anything for a long time.

He'd never have asked her. She knew that. He'd have taken her west and been glad of her every step of it and never once said *I wish somebody had stayed*. He'd have come over the last hill in the spring with her beside him and seen the lamp out, and the jetty dark, and said nothing, and minded, and never told anybody he minded, and she'd have seen him mind from three paces off by his back.

Gabe had asked her that, on the road west. Gabe had told him, later, sitting on a mounting block outside a posting-house: *Alea asked what you need that you'd never ask for.* He'd thought Gabe meant a hook.

"Go," said Alea. "Somebody has to keep the lake. It's me."

***

He didn't sleep. He hadn't expected to.

He sat at the table after she'd gone up the ladder, with the lamp turned low and the sword on the wall, and at a little after five in the morning his window opened again, the ordinary way, because he'd opened it, and he read the channel.

He read all of it. Everything since the picture. Mashi's *it was like being in the room*; Salty's *what do you need* twice; Tiger's *so they all look like him now*, which he read three times. Wendy. Hyvred on the tower, *on purpose this time*. And at the bottom, an hour old, from Solmere:

[ 04:02 · Hanarisu: from Corvin. Hyvred saw something come down the pass in the night. from the other side. from the WEST. ]
[ 04:02 · Hanarisu: a hunter. Kharvos. Hild says by the way it moves. it lay down next to the thing like a dog. ]
[ 04:03 · Hanarisu: I've woken the Advisor. he's reading it now. ]
[ 04:09 · Niko: from the west? ]
[ 04:10 · Niko: they trot. long arms. it's them. we've had four on the red this year. ]
[ 04:11 · Salty: what's it doing THERE ]
[ 04:15 · Hanarisu: the Advisor says: two sides. he says Yan said it first. ]
[ 04:16 · Yan: I said they'd come from two sides. I didn't know they'd be standing next to each other. ]

He read it twice.

Then he closed his window, and got up, and went to the wall, and took the sword down off the pegs.

***

It was heavy.

He'd known it would be. He'd lifted it out of the hearth by the lake with his own hands on the last day of the forging, when it was still too hot for anybody else to stand near, and felt it then: not the weight of steel, though there was plenty of that, nearly a man's length of it, grey-silver, laced all through with frost. The other weight. The weight of something that had fallen out of the sky into a river when a king was eight, and lain under sailcloth in a smith's yard for fifty-eight years, and been made, at last, into the shape it wanted, by a king and a boy from another world with a hammer between them. It was heavy the way a decision is heavy. It sat in his two hands like it had been waiting there.

Gabe's grip. Black bog-oak, from how he held a cup. *In both hands. Close. Like somebody's going to take it.*

He carried it to the door.

He opened the door and stood in it. In the cold, in the dark before dawn, with the sword in both hands, point down, its tip resting on the boards of the step; looking out at the lake.

The lamp was still burning on the end of the jetty. It burned all night, now. The ice had gone out another hand round the posts. Up the slope the town was dark, except for one window, Odette's, where Odette was already up because Odette was always up; and above that, at the top of the hill, the militia hall, dark, with five people in it who'd sat at his table in two worlds.

*For the day you leave the lake*, the king had said. Standing in this doorway, with frost in his beard, the morning the sword went up on the wall. *Not for now. For the day you leave the lake.*

He was leaving the lake.

He stood there and let himself feel it. All of it. What it would be, to walk west with this on his back. He knew exactly what it would be; he'd walked east with a sword on his back before, twenty years ago and more, down a long road to a red plain with a garrison behind him and a temper in front of him, and he'd been very good at it. The Black Blade. He'd been the best there was at it. People still sang about it, badly, in inns. Bram had told it to Thea and the others round the militia-hall fire in the first month, half of it wrong, very pleased with himself, not knowing.

If he took this, that was what would go west. That was what this was for. Not a man with a rod. The other one.

He thought about the valley. About thirteen rings of people standing in the frost with their faces turned toward him. A woman in an apron. A man with a beard. A girl with a plait, at the edge, who'd gone back for her nan's shawl. A freckled boy in the third ring who turned his face a hair every morning when a tiger slapped a gate.

They weren't the war. They were somebody's neighbours, with something wearing them.

He thought about a cold thing curled up asleep in the frost beside a hole, like a dog by a chair.

It was a dog. He'd cut a dog like that in two from the end of this jetty, with the hook, a mile off up the slope, and sat down and kept reading. You didn't take a sword to a dog.

And the thing. The absence. Facing him out of the middle of a burned picture. He didn't know what it was; nobody did. But he knew one thing about it, because Hyvred had told him, and Thea, and Corvin, every letter for two years: it was patient. It wasn't in a hurry. It was *building* something, word by word, eleven miles up a road. It wanted the town. It wanted the eyes on the tower.

You didn't kill a thing like that with a sword on a winter morning. Not this winter. Not with this. Whatever this sword was for, whatever the king had seen in it when he said *the day you leave the lake*, it was for a day when there was something at the end of the road that had to be finished. The day the war came. The big one. The one Halvard lay awake about and Mashi drilled for on the red.

This wasn't that. This was his friends, on a wall, at the far end of the kingdom, holding the line against their own neighbours with shields made for holding. They didn't need the Black Blade. They needed somebody to come and stand behind them.

He stood in the door for a long time.

"Not that day," he said.

He said it to the sword. Out loud. Quietly, the way he'd said it on the morning he walked east and left it here, and on the evening he came home. He said it the way he'd said *not yet* for thirteen years on this step, and the way he'd stopped saying it. It came out exactly as true.

Then he turned round and carried it back across the room and lifted it up onto Garrow's two pegs over the drawing and the blank side of the list, and settled it there, and took his hands off it.

It lay there. Grey-silver, laced with frost, patient. Not cross. Just there. It would wait. It had waited fifty-eight years under sailcloth for a man to come and lift it. It could wait one more winter on a wall.

He went out of the door and down the shingle and out along the jetty, thirty-four planks, to the end, where the lamp was; and picked up the rod.

It was leaning on the last post where it always leaned. Long and plain and grey with use, a fisherman's rod, nothing, a thing a man carried. A cheap reel that turned. And at the end of the line, hooked into the cork of the handle where he hooked it when he was walking, a small dull iron hook, bent a little at the shank, rusted a little at the eye.

He held it for a moment. It didn't weigh anything. It never had.

***

He packed the way he'd packed at nineteen. From the list he didn't need to write down any more, because he'd written it so many times. A coat. A blanket rolled in oilcloth. Bread and cheese and a twist of salt. A flint. The plain fish knife, not the good one; the good one was Alea's. A spare line.

Alea came down the ladder while he was doing it. She'd been awake; of course she had. She was in her coat with Astra on her back and her bag on her shoulder, packed, exactly as she'd been at midnight; and she stood at the bottom of the ladder and looked at the wall, at the sword back on its pegs, and at him, at the rod in his hand; and he saw her see it.

She didn't ask. She went and put the kettle on the stove instead. The terrible two-note kettle, from a tinker, for two coppers and a pike. It began, after a while, to make its two notes, and neither of them was the right one.

They had tea standing up.

Then she took Astra off her back and leaned it against the wall by the door, under the sword. Took her bag off her shoulder and put it on the table. Took her coat off. She did it without saying anything, one thing at a time, the way her mother had unpacked a medicine bag at the end of a day at the garrison, putting each thing back where it lived.

He watched her do it.

"I'll light it at dusk," said Alea. "Every night. Till you come over the hill."

"I know."

"And I'll do the dawn class with Bram. And I'll make Maren eat. And I'll go up to the militia hall and sit with Thea when she's being stupid about reading things." She was looking at the stove. "And I'll fish. Badly. You'll come home and there'll be no pike left in the reeds because I'll have had them all, and you'll have to buy fish at the market like a person."

"Good," he said.

She laughed. A small one, through her nose, the one that was her mother's. Then she put her arms round him.

She hadn't done that since Cinderhold. She did it now, hard, in her shirtsleeves, with her face against his coat; and he put the arm that didn't have the rod in it round her, and stood in his own cabin at dawn with his daughter's ears under his chin, and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything that needed saying that she didn't already know.

"Don't do anything stupid," said Alea, into his coat.

"I never do anything stupid."

"You cut a lake in half."

"That wasn't stupid. That was a pike."

She let go. She stood back and looked at him, at where his face was, the place she'd never been able to hold on to; and she didn't try. She looked at his shoulders and his hands and the rod, the way she'd always looked at him, the way everybody did, and that was enough. It had always been enough.

"Go," she said.

***

Group 1 were on the lake road. He should have known.

All five of them, at the top of the shingle in the grey light, in their coats, with Maren behind them and Bram behind her. They hadn't come down. They'd waited at the top, where the shingle met the road. Higu had her book against her chest.

He stopped in front of them.

"Don't put it in the channel," he said.

Thea's head came up.

"Why?"

"Because I might not be in time." He said it plainly. He'd always said things to Thea plainly; she was the one who'd known first, in two worlds, and she was owed it. "It's a long way. I don't know how long it'll take me. If they think I'm coming, they'll hold on for me." He looked past her, west, over the hills, where it was still dark. "They have to hold on for each other. Not for me. They can. I've watched them."

Thea looked at him for a long time.

"That's not fair," she said.

"No."

"Rule four," said Higu.

"I'm not alone," he said. "I've got a rod."

Higu looked at the rod. Then she opened her book and wrote something in it, and turned it round, and held it up so he could read it in the grey light. Her neat pencil.

*He's going. DON'T TELL. (Rule four doesn't count. He's got a rod.)*

Underlined twice.

"Thank you," he said.

Gigichi didn't say anything. She came past the others and stood in front of him, in her cardigan, with her hands in the sleeves of it and the brown forget-me-nots in her hair, and looked up at him for a moment.

"You okay?" said Gigichi.

He thought about it. Properly. The way she deserved, because she'd asked him a hundred times in a chat on the other side of the world, and he'd never once answered, and then on the last plank, in the autumn, he had.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded. And went back to the others. And that was all.

Maren had her new hands folded in front of her. She looked at the rod, and at him, and at the cabin behind him with its door open and his daughter in the doorway; and she didn't write anything down. Bram, behind her, big and grey, with his boy's rod in one hand and his sword back on the hip where it belonged, looked at him the way he'd looked at him for three years: the way you'd look at a story somebody had told you wrong when you were a boy, now that you'd met the man.

"Go on, then," said Bram.

So he went.

***

He ran.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to walk; it was a long way, longer than any walk he'd done in years, and you didn't start a long way at a run. But he got to the top of the shingle and onto the lake road and his legs did it. The red came up, a little, the way it came up in Tiger, into his legs and nowhere else, without his asking; and he was running.

Up the lake road in the grey. Past the militia hall gate. Past the first houses, where a dog came out to bark at him and didn't, and sat down in the road instead and watched him go. Into the square.

Odette was at her door with a basket of eggs, the way she was every morning. She looked up. She saw a man running across the square in the first light with a fishing rod in one hand and a roll on his back, a man nobody in Wrenhaven had ever seen run, not once, not in thirteen years, not from a fire or a flood; and she dropped an egg.

He heard it go. He didn't stop.

Rowan was in the doorway behind her in his nightshirt with a cup in his hand, because Rowan had heard the dog. He opened his mouth. Then he shut it again, and lifted the cup, a little, the way you'd lift it to somebody across a room; and that was all, and that was enough.

And the hen.

He hadn't thought about the hen. She'd been in Odette's kitchen all night. Odette had opened the door for the eggs, and the hen had come out behind her, and seen him; and she came across the square after him in the lurching waddle that was all a hen had for running, wings half open, making a noise like a door that needed oiling. Out of the square. Up the hill road after him. Faster than he'd have thought a hen could go.

She followed him to the top of the last hill. He let her. He slowed, at the end, so she could. And at the top, where the road went over, where he'd stood every time he came home for thirteen years and looked down at it all, she stopped, and sat down in the road, puffed up and furious and out of breath, and wouldn't come any further.

He stopped too. Just for a moment. He turned round.

There it was. The way it always was from here. Grey in the first light. The lake with its rim of ice along the reeds, and the black open middle. The far shore. The town on its slope with the smoke going up, and Odette in the square picking up an egg, very small. The militia hall. The seat Gigichi had made in the hedge, facing the road, with nobody in it.

And at the bottom of the shingle, a cabin, very small, with a jetty going out from it, thirty-four planks.

And on the end of the jetty, a lamp. Lit. In daylight, where nobody needed it. And beside it, sitting on the last plank with her legs over the water and no sword on her back, a girl, with her ears forward, lifting her fingertips.

He lifted the rod.

She couldn't have seen it. It was too far, and the light was wrong. She lifted her hand anyway, higher, the whole hand; and held it there.

He looked at the hen.

"Go home," he said. "She'll need you."

The hen glared at him.

He turned round and went over the hill. West. The road went down the far side through the bare woods toward the river and on, and on, a long way, longer than he'd let himself think about on the step. He knew he couldn't run all of it. Nobody could run all of it. He'd run as much of it as he could, and walk the rest, and run again; and get there when he got there, and hope it was in time.

He ran.

Behind him, at the top of the last hill, a red hen sat in the road with her feathers puffed up against the cold, facing west, the way she faced the water on the last plank; and didn't go home for a long time.
