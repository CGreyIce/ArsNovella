Alea woke in her mother's infirmary on the morning of the tenth and lay still and listened to it.

She'd slept here last year too. The same bed, the end one by the east wall, under the high window. Hesper had made it up for her without being asked, both years, with a blanket that smelled of the laundry and Nell's lavender and something else underneath, old and clean, that she thought might be the smell of the room itself; and she'd lain in it last year all night without sleeping, with her ears turned toward every sound, trying to hear her mother in it.

She hadn't. You couldn't. Ten years. It was a room: a long low whitewashed room with a row of cots and a table at the far end, and a stove, and shelves of jars with labels in a hand she knew, because it was the hand she'd learned her letters from. Her mother's. *Comfrey. Willow. Yarrow. Feverfew.* Nobody had ever relabelled them. Hesper said nobody would.

She'd thought, last year, that she'd feel her here. She hadn't. She'd felt a room.

This year she didn't try.

She lay in the end bed with her hands on the blanket and listened to the infirmary wake up. The stove ticking as Hesper fed it. A boy two cots down, a recruit with a sprained wrist, snoring. Somebody in the yard outside pumping water. The wind on the east wall, which wasn't the wall it had been. They'd rebuilt it. Ten years ago it had been opened like a door, her father said, in the night, from outside, by something that knew exactly where to put its hands; and in the morning there'd been a hole in it you could walk a cart through, and six dead on the floor, and four healers gone.

She put her hand flat on it. On the new stones, by the bed. They were cold, and very square, and a little paler than the old ones on either side.

"Morning," she said. To the wall.

That was all. She didn't know who she was saying it to. She got up.

***

Rhea had held him three counts.

That was the first thing anyone said to her, in the yard, at breakfast. Pim said it, the stable lad from Ashby, who wasn't a lad any more but still had the blue bucket; he said it to her before she'd even sat down, with his face lit up, as if it were the news of the year. *She held him three. At Oakes's table. Last night. Three counts. I counted. Everybody counted.*

Alea had been there. She'd seen it. She'd been at the end of the forge bench with her chin on her fist, the way she'd sat every night of the eleven dinners last summer, watching her father put his elbow on Oakes's table across from Rhea's with the whole forge round them and half the garrison crowded in the door. And Rhea had put her arm up, the left one, the stiff one, that had been broken in the spring. Not the right. On purpose.

*That's your bad arm*, her father had said.

*It's the one I've been practising with*, Rhea had said.

And Alea had watched her put her elbow down on the scarred oak and her hand in his, and her face go still, and something go down out of her into the table, into the floor, into the forge, the way Yan said it did, like water. And he hadn't moved. And she hadn't moved. And Pim had counted, very loud, with the whole forge counting with him: *one. Two.*

*Three.*

And on four, her father's hand had gone down onto the table, slowly, the way a tree goes over, and taken Rhea's with it; and Rhea had sat back on the bench with her bad arm held against her chest and her face red and wet and absolutely furious with joy, and said *AGAIN*.

*Next year*, her father had said.

*Next year*, said Rhea. *Four.*

He'd laughed. Alea had seen him do it. The real one, the one from the shingle, that came up out of the same place as the other thing; in a forge at the edge of the red, with sixty soldiers watching, on the night before the tenth.

She'd thought, watching him, that last year he wouldn't have.

***

Petra was in the yard.

She'd been in the yard since dawn. Every dawn since the ditch, Pim said; she'd come out before it was light and stood by the old oak post in the corner, an hour, and then brought her sword across. And nothing. Alea watched her do it while she ate. A long plain blade going across the grey air in the corner of the yard, flat, at chest height. And the air staying air.

She didn't seem upset about it. That was the strange thing. Alea had seen her at the Lists, and on the lake, and on the road east, and she'd thought Petra was the kind of woman who'd be upset about a thing like that for the rest of her life. She wasn't. She brought the sword across, and nothing happened, and she looked at the place where nothing had happened with a sort of patient attention, like somebody watching a pot they know isn't going to boil yet.

Xav was on the step, watching her. He'd been on the step every morning too.

Alea went and sat beside him.

"It'll come back," said Xav. Before she'd asked.

"I know. You said. *Now* went for a week."

"*Now* went for a week." He didn't look away from Petra. "She knows it'll come back. That's why she's not upset. She's just—" He thought about it. "She's finding out where the door is. In the dark. So she can find it again."

Alea watched the sword go across.

"She saw his face," said Alea. "At the ditch."

"Yes."

"Nobody under the sixth," said Alea. "The Advisor told me. On the jetty, in the snow. He said nobody's ever been able to tell him why." She looked at Petra. "She's the sixth now."

"Yes."

"She didn't say what he looked like," said Alea. "Afterwards. In the yard. Anybody would have asked. Nobody did. She didn't say."

"No," said Xav. "She wouldn't."

"She said he looked tired."

Xav was quiet for a while.

"He does," said Xav. "Look tired. I can't see it, I just know." He turned his head and looked at Alea, finally. "Not as tired as last year. Deston says. His note."

Alea didn't say anything to that. She looked across the yard at the forge, where her father was sitting on the bench outside it in the early sun, not doing anything, with a cup of Oakes's terrible tea in both hands, close against his chest. Salty beside him, talking. Her father nodding. Not as tired as last year.

She didn't need Deston to tell her. She had ears.

***

They went out in the late afternoon.

The way they'd gone last year: along Aldous's markers, at Salty's pace, an hour. Except that last year there had been eight of them, and the sword on the green waiting, and her not knowing what she'd do when she got there. This year there were twenty.

Aldous at the front, in his chaplain's black, with his book under his arm and the white cloth folded over it. Group 3 behind him, all five, Yan with Suzu on his collarbone and his scythe on his back, Maigo with his hood up, Salty, Wendy, Niko. Then her, and her father. Then Gabe and Deston and Xav, together, the three of them, the way they'd been together at the top of the lake road the first time she ever saw them. Then the four from Duskhollow. And Petra, at the back, with her sword on her hip and the stick under her arm, walking very straight.

Rhea didn't come. She stood on the east wall over the gate with the twenty-five, when they went out, with her hand on her chest; and she lifted it off, once, when Alea looked back, and touched her fingertips together three times, the way Hesper had at the west gate, and Alea didn't know where she'd learned it and didn't ask.

Four hundred and eighty-one markers.

Alea counted them this year. She hadn't last year; she hadn't been able to look at them. She looked at them now. Stone after stone, in their long rows on either side of the burial track, grey, some with names cut in the kingdom's letters and some with numbers and some with nothing, and the green of the knoll coming down to meet them in the riverbeds on either side, so that the last hundred markers stood up out of grass full of small white and blue flowers that the fort had stopped being able to name.

She read the names. Not all of them. Some.

Her mother's wasn't there. Her mother's was on the knoll, under a black stone, in letters Alea couldn't read and didn't need to.

***

The knoll was green to the top.

Last year it had been green too, a little, round the cairn; a ring of soft grass on a grey hill, with flowers in it that her father had been feeding for nine years from a jetty two hundred miles away without knowing. This year the whole hill was green. And the riverbeds below it. And the ash on top, which last year had been a young tree on a grey hill, was standing up, full, every leaf on her turned in. Toward the middle. Toward the cairn. Toward them, as they came up the slope.

And on her east side, a hand from her foot, a small thing a hand high with seven leaves, and the leaves on that side of the ash turned toward it.

Maigo stopped on the slope.

"That's her," he said. To all of them. To nobody. "That's Liraun's. I introduced them." He pushed his sunglasses up on his antlers. "They can hear each other now."

Nobody said anything. Gabe, behind Alea, made a small sound.

Then they came up onto the top of the knoll, and the cairn was there.

***

She'd described it to Sorrel, at Gullhaven, one night on the hard by the boats. Sorrel had asked; Sorrel always asked. *What's it like. Your mum's.* And Alea had tried. A cairn of stones on a green hill. A black stone standing in it. And she'd stopped, because that was all it was, and it wasn't anything like all it was.

It was the same as last year. That was the first thing. Exactly the same. The ring of stones at the foot, the children's stones, that Maigo said the children from the fort had been bringing out one at a time for years, a stone each, from the riverbeds. The scorched one beside them, that had been in the fire the night of the invasion. Mother Breck's, on the top, from a hill above Valcrest. The white one at the head: her father's. A smooth white stone the size of two fists that he'd waded out off the end of the jetty for, at the lake, the autumn after, to the place under the last plank where her mother had stood one evening in the shallows with her skirts held up and said *here*, about nothing, about the evening, about being alive.

And the black stone.

It stood up out of the cairn as tall as Alea's shoulder. Black iron. Half of a sword that had been the length of a man and as broad as a man's back, that a king had split on an anvil by a lake; the plain back of it to the east, and on the west face, the face toward them, toward the fort, toward home, the words.

She couldn't read them. She'd never been able to read them. They were in her father's other language, the one from the world he'd come from, that the summoned spoke and the channel was written in and nobody in this world but him could read; and he'd cut them into the iron himself, on the shingle at the lake, with the king's fine chisel, every letter the same depth the way a smith would; the second time. The first time had been eight years before that, kneeling in the red with the point of a knife on the grip of a sword.

She knew them anyway. By the shape. He'd drawn them for her on a slate the winter she was thirteen, the only words in his language he'd ever written down for her, and she'd known them by the shape since.

*To Diwa, My Love.*

And the L in the last word gone over twice, where his hand had slipped, or hadn't; where he'd gone back and cut it again, deeper. She'd asked him, last year, why. He'd said *because it was the most important one*.

She stood in front of it.

***

She unslung Astra first.

She didn't decide to; it was just what you did. She'd done it last year without knowing why, and it had been right. She lifted the black sword off her back, the smaller half, the one the king had reforged for her out of the other half of this stone, and laid it on the grass beside the cairn. Not against the stone. Beside it. A hand from it. The way you'd put a cup down beside another cup.

And the stone rang.

Low. The way it had last year. A note she felt in her feet before she heard it, coming up out of the black iron and down into the green and out across the whole knoll, very low, very pure. And Astra, on the grass, answered it. Higher. The same note. Not the same. The way two bells from the same tower sound when somebody strikes one and the other answers.

They rang together.

Everybody on the knoll went still. Alea felt it go through them all, behind her, all twenty. Maigo's breath going in. Yan's going out, slowly. Deston making a sound she'd only ever heard him make once before, at the lake, the morning her father showed them what was at the top: a sort of hum in his throat, very quiet, as if he were trying to find the note himself and hold it.

And under them, under the knoll, under the stone and the spike that went down under the stone into the ground like a nail, and the ash's roots wrapped round the spike like fingers, and the new roots, the seedling's, very small, that had reached across a hand of earth to find hers; something cold, a long way down, that had been listening for two days, lay down.

She felt it go. Through her feet. The way you feel a dog in another room lie down when the house goes quiet. Further down than last year. Further than in the spring, Maigo said afterwards, when Niko's sword rang on the knoll and it put its head down. Down. And the roots going down after it, and closing.

The note went on for a long time.

Then it faded. The way a bell fades. Down and down until it was gone and there was only the wind on the knoll, and the ash's leaves moving in it, and somebody behind her crying very quietly, and she didn't turn round to see who.

***

Aldous did the rite.

He'd done it last year with the white cloth and the old words, which weren't words, Yan said, they'd always been a song, and nobody had known until the elves came out of the south in the winter and sang it over fourteen hundred graves. Aldous sang it now. He wasn't good at singing. He'd said so. He had an old man's voice, cracked, a chaplain's voice that had said the same words over the dead of the red for ten years without music in them, and the music had only come back into them this year, and it didn't fit his mouth yet.

He sang it anyway. He knelt at the foot of the cairn with the white cloth spread on the grass in front of the stone and his book shut beside him and his hands flat on the cloth, and sang, very quietly, in a tongue nobody on the knoll knew but him and Yan and a snake; and when he got to the end, to the place where the song had a name in it, he stopped, and looked up at the stone.

"*Diwa*," sang Aldous.

Just that. The name. On the last note.

And Yan, behind Alea, very low, so low she nearly didn't hear it, sang the line after it. The one that wasn't in Aldous's book. The one the elves had sung over every grave in the winter, that Aldous said was theirs, the carrier's line, the one that went at the end.

Yan didn't know how he knew it. He said so after. He just did.

Suzu, on his collarbone, didn't make a sound.

***

Then it was hers.

She stepped up to the stone. Close. Close enough to put her hand on it, which she didn't, yet. She stood there with her ears forward and her hands at her sides and the good knife on her left hip, her mother's knife, that her mother had given her father the summer Alea was five after throwing his old one off the end of the jetty in a temper; and looked at the shape of the letters she couldn't read.

"Mum," said Alea. "It's me. It's the tenth."

She didn't know how loud to say it. Last year she'd whispered most of it and then shouted the end. She said it the way she'd say it across a kitchen table.

"I'm going to tell you the year," she said. "Dad usually does. He tells you on the walk. He told me. Every year, the whole way east, out loud, everything. And then this year he said, at the lake, in the spring: *you tell her*. So I'm telling you."

She breathed.

"I went to the sea."

***

She told her about the sea.

About the *Patient Widow*, loading flour at the mill quay in the spring, with Haskett at the oar and his two boys on the poles, and her father at the quay without the rod, and six sheets of the king's cream paper in oilcloth, *for when you've got something to say*. About the river. The Wren going down and down to the coast, and the bar at Gullhaven, where Sorrel's father had gone over, that her father had told her to watch.

About being sick for two days on a coaster called the *Gannet* with her face in a bucket, and Sorrel on the hard when they came in saying *you look terrible* and *it means you did it properly*.

About Gullhaven. The town that faced out, at the edge of everything, with its back to the kingdom. About a small clinker-built boat, grey over old blue, with *NEXT YEAR* on the stern in letters a sixteen-year-old had painted in a temper, the N gone over twice. About Abner, very old, with three fingers on his left hand, who'd sailed with Sorrel's father twenty years and said *we knew him, that's why we couldn't*. About Sorrel's mother, Ruth, thin and raw-handed at the salt-shed troughs, whose own mother had been a herb-wife, who'd looked at Alea across a trough full of herring and said *you've a healer's face*.

"I haven't," said Alea, to the stone. "I've got Dad's. Everybody says. But she said it. So I'm telling you."

About *Tobyn*. The name nobody on that quay had said for three winters. Her father saying it, at the lake, at his first cast every morning, because she'd asked him to, in a letter. And her saying it at the sea, at hers. And Ruth dropping a basket of herring the first time she heard it, and picking it up, and saying it too.

About the sea not caring. About how it didn't. About how she'd stood on the end of the Gullhaven mole in a gale with Sorrel and the whole of the sea coming at them, grey and enormous, and understood for the first time what her father meant when he sat on the end of the jetty: that there was a thing in the world bigger than you that didn't care about you at all, and you could sit beside it anyway, and it was all right.

"Then I came back," said Alea. "Down the coast. To Valcrest. For the Lists."

***

She told her about the title.

About sitting on the holder's bench for four days under an awning with crossed lances on it and a cushion with a fish embroidered on it that Lucan had had made, while sixty-four cadets climbed a tree in six rounds below her. About not looking at the stands. About Sorrel, hooking the ground in the semi, with her father's boat-hook, and a boy called Tamlin Brand letting his sword fall on it and taking it off her.

About walking out onto the main sand on the last afternoon to meet him.

About being at sixty-one, to Honed, all four days, and not being able to make it move; and about standing on the chalk in front of Tamlin Brand with the whole bowl of the Lists watching, and remembering, for no reason, a thing her mother used to do at the tent flap. In the war. Before she went out. *Count to three.* Her fingertips together. *Well, then.*

"I counted," said Alea. "On the chalk. Like you. One. Two. Three. And I waited. Like for a fish. Dad's thing. He said it the first time he ever showed me, when I was small: *it's more like waiting for a fish*. I didn't know what he meant. I waited on the chalk like for a fish, and the edge went." She swallowed. "Silver. All the way down. Astra. It went silver all the way down, Mum, like frost, and I'm Honed. My window says *Wait*. That's the skill. *Wait*."

She looked at the black sword on the grass beside the stone.

"And I looked up," said Alea. "After. At the stands. And he was there. On the fourth tier. He'd come. He hadn't said. I hadn't asked, and he came anyway, and he'd been there all week behind a pillar in the north stand, and I didn't know." She laughed, a little. It came out strange. "He lifted his rod. At me. And the whole city lifted theirs."

Behind her, somewhere, she heard Gabe sit down on the grass.

***

She told her about the sword on the wall.

About a king walking up the lake road beside eight oxen with his hand on the rail of a dray, and a stone under sailcloth that had fallen out of the sky into a river when the king was eight. About five days at the anvil on the shingle. About her father putting his bare hand on the cold end of it and it turning over under his palm and going red, like it had been waiting. About the lake screaming when he put it in.

About Gabe making the grip. In the night. On his own. From how her father held a cup.

"In both hands," said Alea. "Close. Like somebody might take it. That's what Gabe said. He made Astra's grip from how I hold a cup, and he made Dad's from how Dad does." She looked at the stone. "It's on the wall. Over the table. Over my drawing. On two pegs Garrow put in. The king said *for the day you leave the lake*." She was quiet. "He didn't take it. He left it on the wall. He took the rod."

She thought about that.

"I think that's good," she said. "I think you'd think that's good."

***

She told her about the twelve dinners. About eleven nights in Gigichi's hedge with fifteen people round a table that wasn't a table, and her father cooking every one of them on the forge on the shingle, things from the city by the sea in the other world that he'd promised his friends before he died, and standing at the end of the table watching them eat. Never sitting down. Eating on his own, after, out of the pot.

"And then the last night he sat down," said Alea. "With us. And ate bread. Because the twelfth was owed. It's Salty's. Salty's going to cook it. Tomorrow, he says." She looked over her shoulder for a moment, at Salty, standing at the edge of the green with his big arms folded and his face going red. "And PenguChi told him to sit down and eat every night on the road. Not after. With us. And he has. Every night. Twenty-two nights."

She turned back to the stone.

"He sits down now, Mum," said Alea.

She hadn't known that was going to be the thing that did it. She'd thought it would be the sea, or the title, or Honed. It was that. She stood there in front of the black stone with her hands at her sides and found she couldn't say anything else for a while, and her father behind her didn't move, and nobody on the knoll said anything.

When she could, she went on.

***

She told her about Petra.

About a woman who'd been a girl of twenty-three in a ditch on the south line, with her arm broken, at the end of forty recruits, who wouldn't put her sword down. Who'd come to the lake to say thank you. Who'd asked her father, on the last night, to stand where he stood.

"And he did," said Alea. "Yesterday. On the red. Four paces out. And she climbed out of the ditch and walked past him and stood in front of him." She was quiet. "Nobody's ever done that. Deston says. Nobody's ever stood in front of him. And she made a wall. A hundred paces. And the whole red went quiet behind it." She looked down at the grass. "And she can see his face now. She's the sixth. The Advisor told me, in the snow, nobody under the sixth. She saw it. She said he looked tired."

She didn't say the other thing. The thing about the ditch, that her father had told her at a kitchen table one winter when she was old enough: that the south line was a feint. That he'd held it all night while something came in through the wall of this infirmary, the wall she'd put her hand on this morning, and he'd known by midnight and stayed. She didn't say it because her mother knew. Her mother had been on the other side of that wall.

She didn't say it because Petra was standing at the back of the twenty, with her hand flat on her chest, and didn't know, and wasn't going to.

***

She told her about Rowan. That he kept the step swept. That he'd said to tell her, though he'd never met her: *I keep the step swept, and the girl comes up for her supper more than she lets on, and Odette's still got the bit of your blue cloth in her sewing box that you left behind the time you mended my coat.*

"Dad told me to tell you that," said Alea. "On the walk. He said Rowan said to. And the hens." She stopped. "Rowan said to tell you about the hens. He said women like hearing about hens."

Behind her, somebody laughed. A short helpless sound, quickly stopped. Salty, she thought. Or Gabe.

"There's three," said Alea. "Two brown ones and a big red one. The red one's stupid. She sits on the end of the jetty with him every morning. She walked down the whole table at the third dinner through fifteen people's bowls and sat in PenguChi's lap. She pecked Dad's wrist when we left. Odette's got her in the window that looks down the lake road so she'll see him come home first." She thought about it. "She will, too. She'll see him before me."

She laughed. It came out wet. She didn't care.

"That's the hens," said Alea. "That's the year. That's all of it, I think. I'll have forgotten things. Dad would've remembered everything. He always does." She stopped. "I'm not him. I'm telling you anyway."

She put her hand on the stone.

Flat. On the black iron. Cold. Over the letters she couldn't read, over the L gone over twice. She stood there with her hand on it and her ears flat back against her head and her eyes shut.

"I'm well," said Alea. Very quietly. "I'm trying to live well. I listen to him. Mostly. He's slower now. Not as tired as last year. Don't be sorry." A breath. "I love you."

That was the five. Her mother's five. *Stay healthy. Live well. Listen to your father. I'm sorry. I love you.* The last words; the ones her mother had given her father on a rise an hour from here, ten years ago, for her, at the end, when there was nothing else left to give. She'd answered them out loud last year for the first time, line by line, at this stone. She'd decided, on the walk, that she'd answer them every year.

She took her hand off the stone.

***

Gabe went next.

He hadn't said he would. None of the three had. They'd said, at the Lists last summer, on the step of a painters' yard, *the tenth*, and her father had said *yes*, and that was all anybody had ever said about it. They'd come. That was what they'd promised. They'd come with him to the grave on the tenth.

Gabe got up off the grass where he'd sat down and came up to the stone, slowly, big and careful, and stood in front of it the way he stood in front of an anvil. He didn't say anything for a long time. He looked at the letters. He could read them. He was the first person Alea had ever stood beside who could.

"Hi," said Gabe. To the stone. "I'm Gabe. I'm his friend. From before." He stopped. "I made her grip. Astra's. That's her, on the grass. I made it from how she holds a cup." He swallowed. "And I made his. The new one. From how he does." He looked at the letters a while longer. "You've got good letters. He did them well. The L's gone over twice."

"I know," said Alea.

"It's the most important one," said Gabe.

He stood there a moment longer. Then he put his big hand flat on the top of the stone, once, the way you'd put a hand on somebody's head; and took it off, and went back.

Xav didn't touch it. He came up and stood beside where Gabe had stood and looked at the stone with his chin down, the way he looked at everything, as if he were waiting for it to move first.

"I'm Xav," he said. "I made him move three times. On the shingle. In the summer." He was quiet. "He laughed. Sitting in the lake. I haven't heard him laugh like that since—" He stopped. "Since before. I thought you should know. That he does it again."

And Deston.

He didn't say anything at all. He came up to the stone and stood in front of it, the tallest of them, taller than her father, with his hands at his sides; and shut his eyes. And Alea knew what he was doing, because she'd seen him do it on the jetty. He was listening.

Then he started to hum.

***

She knew the song.

She'd heard it once. At the east gate of Valcrest, last summer, at the end of the Lists, at dawn, when the three of them, Gabe and Deston and Xav, had stood in the gate with the whole of the departing summoned round them and sung it, the three of them, badly and beautifully, a song from a school in the other world where two boys had met in a choir at thirteen. It had a gap in it. A place where a fourth voice should have been. And she'd stood at the back of the crowd and not known the words, and found herself singing in the gap anyway, without words, because there was a gap and somebody had to.

Deston hummed it now. At the stone. With his mouth shut, very low.

Gabe joined him. Then Xav. The three of them, standing in a row in front of her mother's grave, humming, the way they'd sung it in the gate, badly, a little out of tune with each other; and the ash's leaves moving over them in the wind, and the stone and Astra still faintly ringing, under it, if you listened.

And there was the gap.

Alea heard it coming. She'd heard it in Valcrest. The place in the song where the fourth voice went, the low one, under the other three, the one that had been Declan's in a choir room in another world sixteen years ago, before he died.

She opened her mouth to fill it.

And her father filled it.

Not singing. He didn't sing. He was standing behind her, a pace back, where he'd stood the whole time, with his hands at his sides. He hummed. With his mouth shut. The way Deston said he hummed along to the lake every day without knowing. Very low, the lowest note on the knoll, so low it was almost the stone's note, under the other three; the fourth voice, in the gap, where it went.

Deston's voice cracked. He kept going.

Alea shut her mouth. She stood there between her father and her mother's grave and listened to four men hum a song from a school in another world, all four parts, for the first time in sixteen years, and the gap closed.

She didn't sing. She didn't need to. There wasn't a gap any more.

***

The sun went down behind the fort.

They stayed. You did. Last year they'd stayed until the moon came up, full, over Kharvos, and her father had told her mother the rest of the year, the bits Alea didn't know, quietly, on his knees at the stone, while everybody else went a little way off down the slope and didn't listen. He did that again now. He knelt down at the cairn, at the foot of it, by the white stone at the head, with his hands flat on the grass, and talked. Alea couldn't hear what. She didn't try. She went a little way down the slope with everybody else and sat on the grass and looked at the red going dark.

There wasn't a full moon this year. It had been full five nights ago, on the grey grass, while they walked; it would come up late tonight, Higu had written in the channel, an hour before midnight, a little lopsided, over the mountains. So they sat in the dark. Twenty of them, on a green hill, in the starlight, with the fort's lamps a long way off along the markers and the red all round them, singing its frightened song, which she'd never been able to hear and Deston always could.

Except round the knoll. The knoll was quiet. She could hear that. The grass on it didn't whisper.

After a long time her father got up from the cairn and came down the slope and sat down beside her on the grass.

He didn't say anything. She didn't either. She leaned against him, the way she had on the jetty when she was small, with her shoulder against his arm and her ears down; and he let her.

"You told her well," he said, at last.

"I forgot things."

"You told her the hens."

"Rowan said to."

"I know." He was quiet. "I told her the rest."

"What rest?"

He didn't answer for a while.

"That I sat down," he said. "That you went to the sea and came back. That you're Honed and you didn't look at the stands. That Mashi's a spellblade, and it rang, and the thing under her lay down. That Rhea held me three." He breathed out. "That a girl from a ditch stood in front of me."

Alea didn't say anything.

"And that I'm not as tired," said her father. "As last year."

She leaned on him harder.

***

Aldous didn't go down.

He never did, on the night, Yan said. He sat up with the grave. He'd sat up with it every year for ten years, at the foot of the cairn on the white cloth, with his book shut in his lap and a lantern; and he settled there now, on his knees and then on his heels, an old man in black on a green hill in the dark, and opened the book at the back, to the endpapers, where Alea's name was written in his hand from last year, and didn't read it.

Yan didn't go down either. He said he was tired. He sat with his back against the cairn, the way he sat at the knoll every day, with Suzu round his neck and his scythe across his knees, and shut his eyes.

Deston and Xav stayed at the edge of the green. They didn't say why. Alea knew why; they'd promised. They'd said at the Lists *the tenth* and they'd meant all of it, the whole night, and they weren't going to walk back down the markers in the dark while there was still some of it left. They sat on the slope a little way off with their backs to the ash, side by side, and didn't say anything, and Deston's eyes were shut and he was listening.

The rest of them went down.

Twenty, then sixteen. Along the markers in the dark, with Wendy's lantern at the front and Salty's at the back and the fort's lamps getting nearer. Her father at the back, the way he always walked, with the rod on his shoulder; and her beside him, because she'd decided to walk beside him tonight and not in front.

Halfway down, between the two hundred and fortieth marker and the two hundred and forty-first, she stopped.

Her father stopped too.

She stood there in the dark on the burial track with the markers going away on either side of her and the lanterns going on ahead, and put her hand back over her shoulder, the way she'd put it back over her shoulder a thousand times a day for two years, for the grip.

Nothing there.

"I left Astra," said Alea.

"I know," said her father.

She'd known she had. She'd known when she laid it down on the grass beside the stone, a hand from it, and it rang; she'd known when she stood up and walked away from it, and when she sat on the slope, and when she got up and came down the hill with everybody and didn't pick it up. She'd known the whole way down. She'd left it on purpose and not let herself know it was on purpose, the way you leave a thing at somebody's house so you'll have to go back.

"It's with her," said Alea. "I wanted it to be with her for a bit. On its own. The two halves." She swallowed. "I wanted her to have it for an hour without me."

"I know."

"I'm going back for it."

"I know," said her father.

He didn't say *I'll come*. He didn't say *be careful*, or *take a lantern*, or *it's dark*, or any of the things she could see him not saying. He stood on the burial track in the starlight with his rod on his shoulder and his face that she'd never been able to hold, not once, not in eighteen years, looking down at her.

"Count to three," he said. "When you get there. Before you pick it up."

She looked at him.

"She'd have wanted you to," said her father. "Before you went back out."

Alea stood there for a long time.

Then she put her fingertips together. The tips of the fingers of her right hand on the tips of the fingers of her left. And tapped. Three times. Light.

*Well, then.*

And turned round, and went back up the markers toward the knoll in the dark, alone, without a lantern, setting her own pace; and behind her, she knew without looking, he stood on the burial track and watched her go until he couldn't, and then went on down to the fort, slowly, the way you'd go down when you'd decided to trust somebody with the dark.
