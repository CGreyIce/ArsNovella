They came back across the red on the evening of the third day, and Aldous was on the wall to see them come.

He hadn't meant to be. He'd meant to be in the chapel. He'd sat in it all afternoon with his book on his knee and the white cloth folded on the bench beside him, saying nothing, and at some point the light through the window over the altar had gone gold, and he'd found that he was on his feet, and then on the stair, and then on the east wall, at the twelfth merlon north of the gate. Beside the gap. Looking out.

He wasn't the only one. He'd known he wouldn't be.

Wat was in the gap. Wat had been in the gap every evening since he'd walked through it one morning a fortnight ago, as if it had always been his. Teague was on the gatehouse. Hesper had come up from the infirmary, which Aldous had seen him do perhaps three times in nine years, and was standing a little apart, very large and very still, with his broom, because he'd forgotten to put it down. Oakes was at the bottom of the stair with his arms folded, pretending he was only there for the air.

And out on the red, a long way off, two figures. Walking. Toward the fort. Through the markers, in the low gold light, side by side, close, at an even pace. A man with a rod over his shoulder. A girl beside him with a black sword on her back, and something in her arms, held carefully against her chest, the way you'd carry something small and alive.

The grey grass went still round them as they came.

Aldous could see it from the wall. He'd seen it for a year now and stopped pretending he didn't. A ring of quiet going across the red round the man's boots. Only it wasn't round the man's boots now. It was wider. Round both of them. As if the plain had decided, sometime in the last three days, that the girl was part of whatever it went quiet for.

"Look at that," said Wat, very softly, from the gap. "Both of 'em."

Nobody answered him.

They watched the two figures come across the red in the evening, through the rows of posts with names on them and posts with none, toward the east gate, under the horns. The man didn't look up at the wall.

He didn't need to. He knew they were there.

***

Aldous said the office for the dead an hour before dawn on the hundred-and-thirty-first day, the way he had every morning for nine years.

He said it in the chapel, this time, not on the red, on his knees on the cold flags in front of the bare altar, with his book open on the floor in front of him. The words didn't change. That was what they were for. And then he turned the page, and read the names.

Twenty-two. A page of soldiers from the north line. Men from a village called Harrowmere, most of them, who'd come east together with their sergeant and been found together on the second morning in a gully nobody had been able to get to in time. He knew every name. He'd burned them into the posts himself. He read them out, slowly, one at a time, in the dark, in the plain tongue, not the old one.

And when he'd finished the page, he didn't turn to the next.

He turned back. A long way back. Forty pages. To a page with healers on it, and orderlies, and a boy from the ditch nobody could name. And one line at the bottom, on its own, with a name in it in newer ink than the rest, blacker, a little uneven, because his hands weren't what they'd been.

*A healer. Diwa. The rise beyond the east wall; then the grave on the knoll, under the sword.*

He'd written it in on a rise a mile west of the fort, the morning they came. He looked at it now in the candle-light.

Then he took his pen out of his coat, and the little flask of ink, and under the line, very carefully, he wrote three more words. He'd never written anything under a line in this book. There'd never been anything to write. The book was names and places and nothing else. He wrote them anyway.

*The rites said.*

He looked at that for a long time.

Then he closed the book, and got up off his knees, which took longer than it used to, and went out to the courtyard to tell everyone what to carry.

***

They were all there.

He hadn't asked them to be. There was nothing to carry. Group 3 wasn't going anywhere; the fort was home now, had been home for two years, and nobody needed telling what to carry from the barracks to the mess. But they were there anyway, at the grey hour, in the courtyard by the well, in a line, the way they'd stood at the tail of a cart every morning for twenty days. Rhea. Salty. Wendy. Maigo, with his antlers still in full spring leaf from three days ago, which they hadn't stopped being, and which the whole fort had been staring at ever since. Niko. Yan, with Suzu round his neck and the scythe on his shoulder and something different in his eyes when he looked at people, now, that Aldous couldn't name and was a little afraid of and a little in awe of.

And at the end of the line, the man and the girl.

The man with his pack on his back and the rod over his shoulder. Nothing else. The girl with Astra on hers, in its harness of old saddle leather and sharkskin, and a pack, and in her arms, the small clay pot with the stick in it and its three leaves going gold at the edges. She'd carried it back from the grave like that, against her chest. She hadn't put it down since.

They were waiting. To be told.

Aldous stood in front of them with his book under his arm, in the grey, and looked at the line. And found he didn't know what to say.

He'd told them every morning for twenty days. Rhea, the cart. Salty, your ribs. Wendy, supper. Maigo, the horse. Niko, your breath. Yan, the front. And then him, at the end, and the same word every day. *Water*. It had become a thing. The whole fort knew. Pim the stable lad said it to him in the yard now, *water, sir*, very solemnly, every time he passed with the blue bucket.

There was no cart. There was no road. Not for Group 3. And the man and the girl were going west on their own, a month's walk, with nobody to tell them anything.

Aldous looked at Rhea.

"Rhea," he said. "The gate."

Rhea's face did something. She nodded.

"Salty. The forge. Oakes is waiting." He looked along the line. "Wendy. Maigo. Niko. Yan." He paused on each name. "Stay."

They didn't say anything. They didn't need to. He saw each of them hear it.

Then the girl.

He looked at her for a long time. A fox girl of seventeen with her mother's face, holding a tree in a pot.

"Alea," said Aldous. "Your mother's tree."

She looked down at the pot in her arms. Then up at him. And nodded, once, the way she'd nodded on the first morning here, when he'd said *your mother's sword*, and her ears had gone very still.

And then the man.

***

He stood at the end of the line where he'd stood every morning, with nothing in his hands but the rod. Very seriously. Waiting.

Aldous looked at him.

He thought about the morning they came. *Water*, he'd said, and watched the man understand it: nine years ago he'd carried the sword in, and that day his daughter would carry it. He thought about the twenty days on the road before that. The buckets on the yoke. The man's face when Aldous said it on the third morning, after he'd asked if there was anything else, and been told no, and gone off to find the stream *delighted*. He thought about a hand on the green, by a cairn, under the full moon, that a boy with antlers said had been feeding the flowers for nine years without its owner knowing.

"What should I carry?" said the man.

Aldous thought about it. For a long time. The whole courtyard waited.

"Whatever she lets you," said Aldous.

There was a silence.

Then, at the end of the line, the girl laughed. Short and surprised and wet, the way she'd laughed at the grave, Maigo said, when he told the tree she'd cried at paper antlers. She put her free hand over her mouth. And down the line, Wendy laughed, and then Maigo, very loudly, and then Rhea, and Salty's square face did the thing, and even Niko's mouth went at the corner.

The man looked at his daughter.

"What do you let me carry?" he said.

Alea took her hand away from her mouth. She looked at him with her mother's eyes for a long moment, holding the pot.

"The hammer," she said. "Garrow's. And your own pack. And the rod." She thought about it. "And the good knife. I packed it. Left side." She hitched the pot a little higher. "I'm carrying the tree."

"Right," said the man.

Aldous watched him take it. The way he'd taken *water*, three weeks ago, at the tail of a cart on the edge of the downs. Seriously. Very pleased.

"Right," said the man again. Quietly. "The hammer, then."

***

They said goodbye in the courtyard, and it took all morning.

Aldous had known it would. He'd seen soldiers leave Cinderhold for thirty years, and the ones the fort loved never got out of the gate before noon. He stood by the chapel door with his book and watched it happen, and didn't help, and didn't hurry it.

Oakes came out of the forge with something in a cloth.

He didn't give it to the man. He gave it to the girl. Shoved it at her, rather, the way Oakes gave anything to anybody, as if he were annoyed at it for existing. She had to shift the pot to take it. She unwrapped it.

A hook. A small one, a fisherman's hook, the size of her finger, steel, very fine, with a barb you could barely see and an eye at the top a thread could just go through. Bright from the grinding.

"For the lake," said Oakes. "Pike." He sniffed. "Your father's hooks are rubbish. I saw them. He's been using the same three for nine years, he told me, and two of them are bent." He folded his arms. "That one won't bend."

Alea looked at the hook in her palm.

"You made this?"

"I had a morning," said Oakes. "Short lad was hammering something that didn't need it. I thought I'd make something that did." He looked at the man. "She'll want to catch the one that went through your net."

"She will," said the man.

"Then she'll want a hook that won't bend," said Oakes, and went back into his forge, and started hitting something very loudly, and didn't come out again.

***

Hesper came to the infirmary door.

He didn't come across the courtyard. He stood in the doorway, the one with the lamp over it, large and still, with his broom, and looked at them. At the girl. And at the man.

The man went to him.

Aldous watched them. He couldn't hear what they said. He saw the man stop in front of the big old orderly in the doorway, and the two of them stand there a while. And then the man put his hand in his coat, and took something out, and held it up. Something folded in four. Soft, along the folds.

He didn't give it back. He held it up so that Hesper could see it. And Hesper looked at it, and nodded, slowly, and said something, short.

And the man said something back. Aldous heard that, because the man said it a little louder than he said most things, the way you'd say a thing you wanted somebody standing a long way off to hear too.

"They're very good planks," said the man.

Hesper put his big soft hand over his face.

***

Pim gave him a bucket.

The blue one. Off the hook by the well. He came across the courtyard at a run with it, the way stable lads go everywhere, and stopped in front of the man, and held it out, very stern.

"You'll want a bucket," said Pim. "On the road. For the water."

The man looked at the bucket.

"That's the kitchen's," he said.

"Cook says you can have it," said Pim. "Cook says nobody else ever drew water with it right anyway." He pushed it at the man. "Go on. It's the good one. Everybody knows that."

The man took it. He held it in one hand, the blue bucket, with the rod over his other shoulder, and looked at it for a long moment.

"Thank you, Pim," he said.

Pim went red to the ears and ran back to the stables. And the man stood in the middle of the courtyard of Cinderhold holding a blue bucket he'd been told off for not using on his first morning, and Aldous, by the chapel door, saw his shoulders go.

***

Nell from the laundry gave the girl a shirt, folded, clean, without a word. Teague came down off the gatehouse and shook the man's hand, with his bad arm, and said *sir*, and nothing else, and went back up. A cook gave them bread. A groom gave them a twist of salt. Old Wat had been on the night watch and was asleep, and nobody woke him, because the man said not to.

And then Group 3.

Aldous watched it from the chapel door. He'd watched them for two years. He'd watched them come up out of the floor of a hall in Solmere, frightened, and go east with him to the edge of the red, and learn to fight on it, and carry a sword across it and a stone across it, and keep a thing off their channel for a year because the man hadn't said. He'd watched them stand in a road outside Valcrest and hear him say *yes*.

He watched them say goodbye.

Maigo first. Of course. Maigo couldn't not be first. He came up to the girl and put his wooden hand on the rim of the clay pot, very lightly, on the edge of the earth, and talked to the stick in it. Not to her. To the stick. *Be good. Grow. Tell her everything. She likes being told*. And then to the girl: *put it by the water. Ash trees like water*. And then he turned to the man, and opened his mouth, and for the first time in the two years Aldous had known him, closed it again without anything coming out. And put his arms round the man. Very hard. With his antlers in full leaf going everywhere.

The man held on.

Niko. He didn't hug anybody; he never did. He stood in front of the man with his hands in his pockets and his face perfectly calm and his two candles, Yan said, going round and round each other in his chest even now, slowly, on their own.

"Twenty-two," said Niko. "This morning."

The man looked at him.

"Nice one," he said. "Again."

Niko's face did the thing it did very rarely, which was nothing at all, for a long moment, and then something else. He took one hand out of his pocket. And held it out. And the man shook it.

"*Mashi*," said the man. Quietly.

Niko held on to his hand a moment longer than he needed to. Then let go, and put his hand back in his pocket, and turned away very fast, toward the wall, and stood there looking at the stones with his shoulders very straight.

***

Salty didn't say anything for a long time.

He stood in front of the man, short and square, with his crooked ribs, and looked at the hammer in the man's belt. The king's father's hammer. A small old cross-peen with a burn on the haft and a nick in the face like a fingernail, that had come from a forge in Brakewater in the south, and gone north to a lake, and east to a fort, and south to a city, and east again, and was going north-west now to a forge in a town by a lake where a smith called Garrow would be wanting it back.

"Got the hammer," said Salty.

"Got the hammer."

"Tell Garrow."

"That it's a good hammer," said the man. "I know."

"Tell him anyway," said Salty. He was quiet. Then: "Twelve."

"Twelve," said the man. "At the lake. Next summer. With fresh peppers."

"I'm keeping count."

"I know you are," said the man.

Salty looked at him a moment longer. Then he put his big square hand flat on the man's chest. Not a hug. Salty had never been a hugger. Just his hand, flat, over the man's heart, the way the soldiers on the wall put their hands on their own. And left it there. And took it away.

"Go home," said Salty. "Safely." He paused. "Your friends will want to know you're all right."

The man went very still.

Aldous knew the words. He'd heard them a year ago, on a green on a knoll, from a man with a sword across his knees, to five young people who'd just asked him if he was who they thought he was and not been answered. *Go home safely. Your friends will want to know you're all right*.

Salty had kept them a year. And given them back.

The man didn't say anything. He couldn't, Aldous thought. He stood in the courtyard of Cinderhold with a blue bucket in one hand and a short square man's hand still warm on his chest, and his shoulders went, all the way, and stayed gone.

***

Wendy hugged the girl. Hard. Round the neck, the way the coast girl had, in the ring at the Lists. And said something in her ear that made Alea's ears go flat and then straight up. And then turned to the man, and looked at him, and said, quite clearly, the thing everyone was thinking and nobody would say.

"It was the trip," said Wendy. "You know that? It was the trip. We never got the flights. We never went to the place behind the famous place. And it doesn't matter. We came anyway. You came anyway." Her voice wasn't steady. "It was the trip."

"It was the trip," said the man.

"Next year," said Wendy, "you're coming to *ours*. Properly. We'll cook. Salty can't cook either."

"I've heard," said the man.

And then Yan.

***

Yan stood in front of the man with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu round his neck and the new thing in his eyes, the thing that had come up green on the fallen tree four nights ago while a girl said three words to a stone. He looked at the man. Not at his face. At the other thing. The thing only Yan could see.

Aldous watched him look. He'd watched Yan look at people that way for two years, and been frightened of it, the way he was frightened of the white snake and the old rites and everything older than he had words for. He wasn't frightened now. He'd sat at the end of a fallen tree and watched a young man go green and said a word out of the oldest part of the rites, and been answered.

*Who carries*.

"Green," said Yan. Quietly. To the man.

The man waited.

"The spark," said Yan. "In you. The one that caught on the rise." He was quiet. "It's green. I wanted you to know. I saw it on the night." He looked at the girl beside him. "It's the same green as hers."

The man didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he put out his hand. Not to shake. He put it, very gently, on Suzu, where she lay round Yan's neck. On the white coils. The way he'd touched a leaf on a young tree on a knoll a year ago.

Suzu lifted her head. She looked at him with her two green eyes. And her tongue went out, once, and touched the back of his hand.

"Look after him," said the man. To her.

Suzu put her head back down on Yan's collarbone, and closed her eyes. As if that had never been in question.

"Okay," said Yan.

It was the only word he had. It always was.

***

Rhea had the gate.

She'd gone to it when Aldous told her to, at dawn, and she'd stood at it all morning while the courtyard said goodbye. The west gate, the one the road went out of toward the downs and the river and the whole long way north-west to a lake. She stood beside it with her hand on the bar and her sword on her back and her face doing something Aldous had never seen it do in two years, which was nothing at all.

The man came to the gate. The girl beside him.

Rhea looked at him.

She didn't say anything. She took her hand off the bar. She went to the tail of a cart that was standing by the gatehouse wall, an old supply cart with its shafts down, and put her elbow on the tailboard. And held out her hand. And glared.

The man looked at the hand. Then at her. Then he put the blue bucket down, and leaned his rod against the wall, and put his elbow on the tailboard.

Teague, on the gatehouse stair, didn't need asking.

"One," said Teague.

Rhea's hand went over.

It went most of the way. It stopped, an inch from the wood, with her whole arm shaking and her face gone red to the roots of her hair and her teeth bared, and held. For the length of a word.

"*Two*," said Teague.

It held.

Aldous, at the chapel door, found he'd stopped breathing.

"—*Three*," said Teague.

Rhea's hand hit the tailboard.

The courtyard was very quiet.

Rhea stared at her hand on the wood. Then she looked up at the man, across the tailboard, and her face was doing everything at once.

"*Two*," said Rhea. "I held you *two*."

"You held me two," said the man.

"I held the *Black Blade two counts*."

"You did."

Rhea took her hand off the tailboard. She stood up very straight. And then she did what she always did, what she'd done every night for eleven nights in the courtyard and on the first night out of Valcrest and in a market town in the south against two carters, the thing that was Rhea all the way through, the thing Aldous had been exhausted by and loved for two years.

"*AGAIN*," said Rhea.

The man picked up his rod. He picked up the blue bucket.

"Next year," he said.

***

The garrison was on the west wall.

Aldous didn't know when they'd gone up. He'd been watching the courtyard. He looked up as the man and the girl went in under the arch of the west gate, and they were there. All of them. The whole of the west wall and the gatehouse and the towers on either side. Soldiers and cooks and grooms and Nell from the laundry. The young captain from Solmere with his careful moustache. Teague, back up on the gatehouse. Oakes, who'd come out of the forge after all, at the top of the stair, with his arms folded. Hesper with his broom.

The old ones, scattered among the young. Twenty-five of them. Aldous could pick every one.

And in the north-west tower, at the very end of the wall, bent nearly double over his spear, Old Wat. Who'd been asleep. Who somebody had woken after all, or who'd woken on his own because eighty years on a wall gives you a sense for when you're needed on it.

The man and the girl came out of the west gate onto the road.

And Teague, on the gatehouse, put his hand on his chest.

***

It went the way it had gone on the east wall, five weeks ago. The way it had gone nine years ago. Not all at once. Like candles being lit along a dark hall by somebody walking. Teague. And Jory Platt beside him, with his three fingers gone. Bennet and Hob. Marguerite from the stables. Fenner. Ise. And Wat, at the end, in the tower, slowly, because his hands shook.

And then the young ones. Who knew now. Every one of them. Who'd watched the man carry water for five weeks and cook eleven dinners in a forge and sit in the gap with his daughter every evening, and who'd heard, by now, from the old ones, in the barracks at night and on the wall on the long watches, everything. All of them. Hand flat. Over the heart.

Pim, at the stable door, with his hand on his chest, very solemn. *It's so somebody's looking*.

Aldous, at the chapel door, put his own hand on his chest.

The man stopped.

On the road. Twenty paces out from the gate. With his daughter beside him with a sword on her back and a tree in her arms, and a blue bucket in his own hand, and a hammer in his belt, and the rod over his shoulder.

And turned round.

***

He looked up.

Aldous had seen him do it once before. Five weeks ago, outside the east gate, with the sun behind him, with two buckets set down on the red and the yoke laid between them, saying twenty-five names one after another up a wall without missing one. It had been like being looked at by weather.

It wasn't like that now.

He looked up at the west wall of Cinderhold, at all of it, every merlon, every face, the old ones and the young ones and the ones in between. Slowly. All of it at once and then each part of it, the way he looked at everything. He didn't say any names this time. He didn't need to. He'd said them.

He looked at them the way you'd look at a house you'd lived in once, a long time ago, and been unhappy in, and come back to, and found you'd been wrong about. The way you'd look at it from the road, going away, knowing you'd come back.

And then he lifted the rod.

Off his shoulder. Not high. A little way. The way he'd lifted it at the first bend of the lake road out of Wrenhaven, Maren had written, to a man standing in a square who'd said *nobody follows*. The way he'd lifted it at the east gate of Valcrest, with two buckets on his shoulders, to three brothers singing a song in the gateway.

He held it there. Just for a moment. A plain fishing rod, raised a little, in the morning, on the road, toward a wall.

And then he lowered it to his shoulder again, and turned round, and walked away down the road west, with his daughter beside him. Not fast. Not slow. Not looking back.

Nobody on the wall took their hand off their chest until the road went round the first hill and the two of them weren't there.

***

Aldous went to the chapel.

He sat down on the bench at the back, where a short square young man had sat all night once with a black sword on trestles in front of the altar, two years ago, and where a man with a rod had sat at dusk five weeks ago and said *she liked chaplains*. He sat there with his book on his knee and the white cloth beside him, folded in eight.

He took the cord out of his coat.

Purple and gold. Half a bead. A strip of thin blue paper wound round the knot. He'd used it five or six times since the first night. *Arrived. He looked up*. *Rhea held him one count*. *Tell the Advisor: he answered*. He'd never been sure, each time, that it would work. It always had.

He wrote on the strip. In his own small careful hand. Not much.

*Gone west. Both of them. The whole wall. He lifted the rod.*

He held it. In the chapel. In the morning light through the window over the altar.

After a moment, it went warm. And when he opened his hand, the letters on the strip had changed, the way they did, to an answer from a castle on a rock three weeks away.

He read it. It was short.

*Thank you, Father. I'll tell Maren. She'll tell the mayor.*

Aldous sat with that for a long time.

***

Before he went out, he opened his book.

Not at the page with the healers. Not at the page he'd read that morning, the men from Harrowmere. He turned to the back. Past the last page of names. Past the three hundred and nineteen numbers with no names at all. To the very last leaf of the book, the endpaper, blank, that he'd never in nine years written a word on. Because the book was for the dead. It was names and places and rows and posts and nothing else. That was what it was for.

He took out his pen.

He sat on the bench in the chapel with no priest for a long time with the pen over the empty page. Then he wrote, very carefully, in the plain tongue, in his own small hand, the first line in nine years in this book that wasn't somebody he'd buried.

*Alea, of Wrenhaven. Daughter of Diwa, a healer. And of Ars.*

And under it, after a while, because it was a book of places, and every line in it had a place:

*West. Home. Carrying a tree.*

He looked at it.

Then he closed the book, and put it under his arm, and went out into the courtyard, where Group 3 was standing by the well in the morning sun, not doing anything, the way people stand about after somebody they love has gone down a road. Rhea at the gate with her hand still on her chest. Salty at the forge door. Wendy and Maigo and Niko and Yan.

They looked at him. Waiting. The way they'd waited every morning for two years at the tail of a cart or in a courtyard or on the edge of the downs, to be told what to carry.

Aldous looked at them for a long time.

"Each other," he said.
