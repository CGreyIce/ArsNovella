Hana learned names.

She'd started in the first week, in the castle at Solmere, because she'd been frightened and it was the only thing she knew how to do with frightened. The woman who brought the hot water in the mornings was Ama. The boy who swept the long gallery on a broom twice his height was Pellam. The guard on the archive stair, who'd never once looked at her ears, was Hew. A name was a door, she'd decided, somewhere in that first month. Charm went through doors. If she knew your name, she could reach you; and if she could reach you, she had to decide, every single time, whether she was going to.

She'd decided *no* about a thousand times since. She'd kept count, for a while.

She'd only ever had one name for herself that wasn't hers to give. Everyone here called her Hana. Hanarisu, on the channel. *The cat princess*, at Vane's banquet, in a voice like a man pricing a horse. *My lady*, from Ama, who would not be talked out of it. *Honey*, from Milli, and back.

And then there was the other one.

Two people in the world had ever called her Alice. It had started as a joke, so long ago she couldn't remember which of them had begun it, and it had stuck the way jokes stick when the right two people keep telling them. Jared, who'd say it in a drawl like a man calling across a parking lot. And a voice in her chat, years of it, from when she was nineteen: a name in a list that turned up night after night and said something stupid to make her laugh.

He'd made her laugh for eight years. They'd never been in the same room. Not once.

She'd been sad about that, when he died. Not the way Aleaka was sad, or Jared. Sad in a smaller, colder, more particular way, that she'd carried round for three years like a stone in a shoe. *We never met*. She'd always thought they would. She'd thought there'd be time.

Yesterday, in a gate square, a water-carrier had said *Alice*.

She'd lain awake all night in the king's house on the hill with Milli asleep beside her and her tail round her own ankles, and listened to the word go round and round her head like a song you can't stop humming, and not minded at all.

***

They went down to the Broken Lance at first light. All five of them. Nobody had arranged it. Hana had got up at dawn and found Aleaka already dressed on the landing outside her door, with Brisk on her shoulder, and Cursiffe on the stairs with his book, and Jared in the hall below, waiting, by the front door, with his arms folded.

"He's not at the inn," Jared said, before anyone could ask. It was nearly the first thing anyone had heard him say since yesterday morning. "He's on the sand. Behind the Lists. Since before sunrise."

"How do you know?"

Jared just looked at her.

They found him where Jared said. There was a practice ground behind the great pale bowl of the Lists, a long rectangle of raked sand with a fence round it, where the competitors warmed up before their bouts, and at that hour of the morning it was empty except for six people standing on it in a loose ring, very still, with mist coming off the sand round their ankles.

Hako, with his spear. Petra, with her sword on, like a soldier. Xavier. PenguChi, with her penguin standing beside her on the sand looking at nothing with great seriousness. Alea, with the enormous sword.

And the water-carrier in the middle of them, with a fishing rod over his shoulder, saying nothing.

Hana stopped at the fence. They all did. Kestra was already there, she saw; leaning on the fence post at the far end, the way she leaned on walls, watching. She didn't look round.

"What are they doing?" said Milli, after a while.

"Waiting," said Kestra.

They waited too.

It was a long time before anything happened. So long that Hana stopped watching for it, and just watched them: six people in a ring on wet sand in the mist, not moving, not speaking. A heron could have done it. Then, with no signal that she could see at all, the water-carrier said one word, quietly, and Hako moved.

He moved the way Hana had seen him move on the sand in the summer, from the stands at the Lists, the spear coming round in that long, patient, cheerful arc. And the water-carrier was simply somewhere else. He hadn't hurried. He hadn't seemed to move at all. He'd just been standing on one side of the spear, and then he was standing on the other side of it, with the rod still over his shoulder.

"*Again*," said Kestra, very softly, at the far end of the fence, to herself.

"Earlier," said the water-carrier, to Hako. "Not faster. Earlier. Move while it's still deciding to."

Hako's face lit up like a lamp.

"How do I know when it's deciding?"

"Stop looking at me," said the water-carrier. "Look at the whole thing."

Milli made a small noise beside her. Hana looked at him. He was gripping the top rail of the fence with both hands, and his tail had gone very straight, and he was staring at the sand the way he stared at a page of Oswin's books at midnight, when a sentence went into him like a hook.

*It is more like waiting for a fish*, she thought. *Most people miss the first tug, because they are too busy pulling.*

Milli had read that, the first winter, and his aura had come up three points in a night.

The water-carrier looked up, then. Across the sand, at the fence, at five people standing there in the mist. Hana couldn't see his face. Nobody could. But she saw him see them; saw him see Jared, standing a little in front, the way Jared always stood.

"That's the class," he said. "Same time tomorrow. Petra, you're first."

"I'm always first," said Petra.

"I know," he said. "That's why you're first."

***

He gave them the whole day.

Hana didn't know how he'd arranged it. Perhaps he hadn't. Perhaps it was just that every other group had already had him for a night, and knew without being told that today was theirs. Tiger, coming out of the Lance with his hair on end, took one look at the five of them at the gate of the practice ground and said "*oh*," and turned straight round and went back inside. Calvin, on the step, lifted his kettle in a kind of salute. Gigichi passed them in the street with an armful of bread, stopped, looked at Hana for a long moment, and then pushed a loaf into her hands without a word and went on.

"That's Gigichi's," said Hana, looking at the loaf.

"She does that," said the water-carrier. "Don't ask her about it. She'll say it's from Maren."

They walked out of the city.

Not far. Out through the west gate and along the river, where the market gardens gave way to water-meadows and willows and a slow brown river with reeds along its edge, and there was a long low bank under a willow tree with nobody on it. He sat down on it as if he'd been sitting on it all his life. They sat down round him. Hana wasn't sure, afterwards, who'd chosen the place. She thought perhaps he had. She thought perhaps he'd walked out of the gate looking for the nearest thing to a jetty.

Brisk went up off Aleaka's shoulder and sat in the willow and glared at the river.

Nobody said anything for a while.

It should have been strange. It *was* strange. Five people from a castle in Solmere sitting on a riverbank in the south with a man whose face slid off your eyes like rain off glass. But it wasn't strange in the way Hana had expected. She'd expected it to be like meeting someone famous. Like standing in a room with the Hero of the Redmarch, who'd routed an army, who the ballads said was seven feet tall. She'd expected to be afraid of him.

It was like being on a call.

That was the only way she could put it. It was like three in the morning on a call, when the game had finished an hour ago and nobody had hung up, and everybody was just *there*, doing something else, talking about nothing, the way you do with people you've spent a thousand nights with. Cursiffe had his book open on his knee and wasn't reading it. Aleaka was lying on her back in the grass looking at the willow. Milli was sitting very straight with his hands on his knees, being rational. Jared sat at the end of the bank, nearest the path, between the rest of them and anyone who might come along it, and looked at the river.

And the water-carrier sat in the middle, with his rod across his knees, and looked at the river too, and every so often said something, and every time he did, it was exactly the kind of thing he'd have said in chat.

"There's a pike in there," he said, after a while.

"How do you know?" said Milli.

"There's always a pike."

"That's not evidence."

"I know," said the water-carrier, with tremendous contentment. "Trust."

And Jared turned his head.

***

Hana saw it happen. She'd been waiting for it since the gate.

Jared hadn't said anything to him. Not one word. Not at the gate, when he'd turned his face to the wall. Not in the street, carrying the water. Not at the long table at the Lance, all afternoon and all night, sitting beside him, so close their shoulders touched, and saying nothing at all. Not this morning. He'd said *he's on the sand*, and *behind the Lists*, and that was all Hana had heard out of him since a water-carrier said *I owe you a postcard*.

She knew why. She'd felt it. She'd known Jared for seven years, since he was twenty-one and a voice in her chat with a drawl like a man calling across a parking lot. He'd had a thing in him, the whole time she'd known him here, like a coat too heavy for the weather. Three years of being too far away. He'd told her once, the first winter, very late, in the rooms above the archive, the only time he'd ever talked about it. *I got a message. That's it. That's the whole story. There's no part where I did anything. There's nothing I could have done. Half a world*.

They'd never met. Jared and Ice. Not once in all those years of playing every single night. Half a world.

He'd been in the same place as Ice for twenty-four hours now, for the first time in his life. And he hadn't said a word.

He said one now.

He turned his head and looked at the man in the middle of the bank. At the rod across his knees. At the river. At a face he couldn't see. And Hana watched the coat come off him. Not all at once. Slowly, the way you take off a coat that's been soaked through and is heavier than it should be: one sleeve, and then the other.

"Trust," said Jared.

Just that. In a drawl like a man calling across a parking lot.

The water-carrier looked at him.

"*Trust*," said Jared again. And then his voice broke, and he put his hand over his eyes, and laughed, the low cool collected laugh he'd had on every call for years, only cracked straight down the middle. "*Trust*. You've been saying that for *years*. Every time. *I would never betray you in this game. Trust*. And then you'd sell me out in the first five minutes. Every single time. Every *game*."

"Not every game."

"*Every game*."

"There was one in—"

"There was *not*," said Jared. He took his hand away from his eyes. They were wet, and he didn't care. "There was not one single game. Not *one*. I kept count. I've got a *spreadsheet*. Somewhere. On a computer. In a different *universe*." He was laughing properly now, helplessly, the way he'd laughed at three in the morning when they were twenty-one and twenty-two and nothing had happened yet. "I knew. I knew the second I read it. In the book. Cursiffe showed me. A man who lies about the weather and says *trust*. I knew it was you. I knew it in the first *line*."

"I know," said the water-carrier. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," said Jared. "Be *here*."

"I'm here."

"I *know*," said Jared, and put his arm round the water-carrier's neck from the side, the way you put your arm round a friend at the end of a long night when you've won something, or lost something, and either way you're glad they were there, and pulled him in, and held on. "I know. *Trust*."

Nobody on the bank said anything for a long time.

Milli was looking at the river. He was very carefully looking at the river. Hana took his hand. He let her.

***

Cursiffe had the book open on his knee the whole time. She noticed it after a while. He'd been turning the same page back and forth since they sat down, very slowly, the way he did everything.

"There's a thing in here," he said eventually. Calm. Slow. Relaxed. His head tipped back a little, the way it always was, as if he knew something nobody else did. "In the second book. *Second year. Still fishing*." He turned it round, so the water-carrier could see. "The bit about the fox."

"The hens chased it off."

"The hens chased it off," agreed Cursiffe. "And then there's this line about how the fox didn't seem to mind. *It had the air of a fox who'd been beaten by poultry before and had made its peace with it*." He looked up. "I've heard that joke. On a stream. About a year before you died. You said it about Jared in a game, after he got killed by a chicken. You said it on the fly. It was the funniest thing anybody said all night."

"I remember."

"You stole it."

"I didn't steal it," said the water-carrier. "It's mine."

"You stole that joke," said Cursiffe, with deep satisfaction, "*from yourself*. Thirty-odd years later. In a different world. In a book nobody could read the jokes in." He closed the book. "That's how I knew. Not the *trust*. Jared knew from the *trust*. I knew from the fox. I sat in the archive in the first winter reading that line and laughing like an idiot and Oswin asked me what was funny and I couldn't tell him. Nobody in this whole world could have told him. Except you." His sly half-smile came back, slowly. "I've been waiting two years to tell you that's plagiarism."

"Self-plagiarism."

"It's still a crime."

"Turn me in," said the water-carrier, and Cursiffe laughed, the slow relaxed laugh that turned, like it always did, a bit hyped at the end.

"I was *killed by a chicken*," said Jared, from the end of the bank, in a tone of deep wounded dignity. "In *one* game. *One*."

"It was a very strong chicken," said the water-carrier.

***

Oswin found them at noon.

He came along the river path at a half-run, with his writing case under one arm and three books under the other and his robes splashed to the knee, and stopped dead when he saw them, and stood there on the path breathing very hard. Kestra was three paces behind him, walking. She'd clearly been walking behind him the whole way, at exactly the pace that kept her three paces behind, without once speeding up.

"I'm sorry," said Oswin. "I'm *sorry*. I said I wouldn't. I said I'd give you the day. I tried. I sat in the archive room at the king's house for four hours. I read the same page eleven times. I couldn't." He was holding the books against his chest like a shield. "I have *questions*."

"Oswin," said Kestra.

"I have *so many questions*."

"*Oswin*."

"I know. I *know*." Oswin turned to the water-carrier, and his calm scholar's face, the face that was so much sharper than he let on, was doing something it had never done in two years of Hana knowing him: it was shaking. "I'm sorry. I'm not—I've collected every account. Every one. Every ballad, every soldier's letter, every report from the eastern garrison for twelve years. I have a *shelf*. I have the general's horns sketched from life. I know the name of the horse you rode at Cinderhold when you were nineteen, which you had for a month, and which was called—"

"Please don't," said the water-carrier.

"I'm not going to say it. I'm not *going* to." Oswin swallowed. "And I worked it out. You know that? From the *records*. Not from jokes. Not from a voice. From four hundred years of paper. On my own. On a map." He was very close to tears. "Nobody's ever told me if I was right."

The water-carrier looked at him for a long moment.

"You were right," he said.

Oswin sat down on the river path. Just sat down, in the dust, with his books in his lap, the way he'd sat down in a doorway once in Solmere with a fox girl hugging him.

Then, after a while, he opened the top book, which was the first of the anonymous books from the provinces, Oswin's own copy, the one with the cracked spine and the ink stains and four years of his notes in the margins, and held it out, with a pen, and said in a small voice:

"Would you sign it?"

Kestra put her hand over her face.

The water-carrier took the book. He looked at the cover, which had no name on it, because he'd never put one there. He opened it to the first page, which said, in the native script, in a trader's careless hand, *Written down by someone who should be fishing*. And under it, in English, in a plain narrow hand that Hana had never seen and knew instantly, the way you know a voice, he wrote one letter.

*A*.

He gave it back.

Oswin looked at it. At the single letter. He looked at it for a very long time. Then he closed the book very carefully and held it against his chest with both arms and didn't say anything else for nearly an hour, which Hana would have said, that morning, was impossible.

***

The afternoon went the way afternoons go on riverbanks.

Somebody went back to the city for food and came back with too much. The sun moved round the willow. Brisk caught a vole and was furious about it. Kestra sat at the top of the bank with her back against the willow and her eyes shut and her little black book in her coat, and did not appear to be listening, and was. Milli asked a lot of questions, in a calm rational voice, about the status window and the summoning and the tiers, and got answers to some of them and *ask the king* to others.

"Do you know the way home?" said Milli, at one point. Very calmly. As if he were asking about the weather.

Hana went still.

The water-carrier didn't answer straight away. He looked at the river. Then he looked at Milli, properly, the way he'd looked at each of them at the gate.

"No," he said. "Nobody does. Not yet." A pause. "Ask the king. Not me. He knows more than he's said. He's been waiting for one of you to be strong enough to ask him."

"Only the strong are allowed to speak to the king."

"I know," said the water-carrier. "That rule's older than he is. He hates it." He turned the rod in his hands. "Ask him anyway. After the Lists. At his table. He'll answer you."

Milli looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, once, and didn't ask anything else, and sat with his hands on his knees and his tail round Hana's ankle, thinking.

And Aleaka, who'd been lying on her back in the grass all afternoon looking up at the willow and saying almost nothing, said, very slowly, the way she said everything, to the leaves:

"You named her Alea."

The water-carrier didn't say anything for a moment.

"I named everything Alea," he said.

"You named your *daughter*—"

"I named everything Alea."

Aleaka sat up. She had grass in her hair and her round gold glasses were crooked and her split blonde-and-auburn hair was coming out of its buns, and she looked at him with her arms folded and her chin up, in her slow stern British voice, the voice of a headmistress, the voice that had always, always made him laugh:

"I *told* you," she said. "On a call. I said, *you've got one name. You're going to name your children Alea*."

"You did."

"And you said *maybe I will*."

"I did."

Aleaka's chin went higher. "I *win*," she said.

"You win."

"I never win. You always turn it round. Every time. For a year. You'd let me boss you about and then you'd say something and I'd lose." Her voice did the thing it did, stumbling, going round a word twice. "I *win*. This one. I'm having it. You can't turn it round."

The water-carrier was quiet.

"You win, Leaka," he said. "I named my daughter Alea. You were right." He paused. "And you're very cute when you're right."

Aleaka opened her mouth.

Then she shut it again, and her ears went flat, and her face went bright red from her collar to her hairline, and she lay back down in the grass and put both her arms over her face and said, muffled, furious, "*I lose*," and Cursiffe laughed so hard he had to put his book down.

***

Hana didn't ask him anything until the sun was going down.

She'd had a question for two years. She'd had it since the first winter, since a banquet where a lord had called her the cat princess, since the night Jared and Cursiffe had shown her a book about fishing and she'd sat on the end of her bed and worked out, privately, carefully, that someone from Earth had been here first. Long before them. Alone. She'd decided then that it couldn't be him, because the timeline didn't fit; and she'd been wrong, and she'd found out she was wrong, and the question had stayed exactly the same.

She'd said it once, out loud, to the others. *I want to meet him. I want to ask him how he stood it*.

The sun went down over the water-meadows. The river went gold, and then red. The others had gone quiet, the way you go quiet at the end of a long day outside. Jared was asleep on the bank with his arm over his eyes. Aleaka was asleep with her head on Cursiffe's knee, and Cursiffe was reading the second book in the last of the light with a finger keeping his place in it. Milli was beside her, with his tail round her ankle.

"How did you stand it?" said Hana.

He knew what she meant. She saw him know.

"Thirty-four years," said Hana. "On your own. Knowing. Nobody to tell. Nobody who'd understand if you did. You woke up in a field and everybody you'd ever known was—" She stopped. "I've been here two years. I've had Milli the whole time. I've had *all* of them. And some nights I still sat in the window seat in Solmere and thought I'd go mad. How did you *stand* it?"

The water-carrier didn't answer for a long time.

"I didn't," he said. "Not for a while. Not at the start. I was six, and I said a word under a blanket, and a window opened, and I cried, because it meant I wasn't mad. And then I was fourteen, and fifteen, and angry. For years. Really angry. I'd stand on walls at midnight and look at nothing." He turned the rod in his hands. "Then I met a healer with cold hands who stitched me up three times in one month and asked me why I kept getting hurt. And I told her. Everything. She was the first person I ever told." A pause. "Then there was Alea. Then there was a lake. Then there were fish, and books, and pumpkins, and hens that hate me." He looked out at the river, red in the sunset. "You don't stand it, Alice. Not on your own. You find one person, and you tell them. And then it's not *it* any more. It's just your life."

Hana didn't say anything.

"And then," he said, "about two years ago, thirty-five more turned up, and it stopped being my life and started being everybody's." She heard the smile in his voice, even if she couldn't see it. "Which was much worse. And much better."

Hana found that she was crying, a bit. Not much. She didn't mind.

"I always wanted to meet you," she said. "Back home. I thought there'd be time. I thought one day I'd go to Malaysia or you'd come to—somewhere. And we'd get food and I'd say *finally* and you'd say something stupid." She wiped her eyes. "And then there wasn't any time."

"There's time now," he said.

"You've got a different face."

"I know. Sorry about that."

"It's a terrible face. I can't even *see* it."

"Everybody says that."

Hana laughed, wet. Then she leaned sideways, slowly, and put her head on his shoulder, the way she'd never once been able to on a call, and left it there, and watched the sun go down over a river in another world, with Milli's tail round her ankle and the man who'd made her laugh for eight years sitting beside her, finally, in the same place.

"*Finally*," she said.

"Hi, Alice," he said. Something stupid.

***

Kestra came down the bank at dusk.

Hana saw her come. She'd been sitting under the willow all day with her eyes shut and her book in her coat, and now she stood up, and came down the slope of the bank with the long easy dangerous walk of a woman who'd been in the king's guard, and stopped in front of the water-carrier.

Hana had taken her head off his shoulder. She sat very still.

Kestra stood there for a moment. Then she took her little black book out of her coat. The book she wrote everything in, every report, every line the king read twice. She opened it, near the end, between two pages, and took something out of it, and held it out to him on her palm.

Half a purple cord. With half a tiny gold heart on the end.

Hana stopped breathing.

She'd cut it herself. In the winter. On the end of the bed, late, with Jared's knife, when Milli was asleep. The best cord she had, purple, the colour of her hair. A little gold heart she'd bought in the market in the first month and never known what for. She'd cut the heart in half, very carefully. And she'd given one half to Kestra in the courtyard in the rain and said *if you ever stand in front of him again, give him this half*.

*If I'm told to*, Kestra had said.

And then: *Perhaps anyway*.

Kestra had gone north since. Hana knew she had. A month ago, to a lake, with a letter, and come back with a look on her face Hana had never seen there. And Hana's own half had stayed cold. All month. She'd checked it every morning, and every night. Cold.

She'd thought Kestra had forgotten. Or decided against it. Or not been told to.

The water-carrier looked at the half-heart on Kestra's palm.

"I had it at the lake," said Kestra. Her terse dry voice, final, the voice that barely spoke. "In my book. All the way there. All the way back." She didn't look at Hana. She looked at the water-carrier. "I didn't give it to you there."

"Why not?"

Kestra was quiet for a moment.

"Because she wasn't there," she said. "And she's never been in the same place as you. Not once. Not ever." She held her palm out a little further. "It seemed to me she should be. When it went warm."

The water-carrier didn't say anything.

He took the half-cord off her palm. Very carefully, the way you'd take something that might break. He looked at it in his own hand, the half of a little gold heart, the size of a fingernail.

"Nobody told you to," he said.

"No," said Kestra. "Perhaps anyway."

***

It went warm.

Hana felt it go. She'd been wearing her half tied round her wrist for six months, the purple cord and the half of a gold heart lying against the inside of her wrist where the pulse is, and it had been cold the whole time. Cold in the winter in Solmere. Cold on the road south. Cold this morning, at the fence, in the mist.

It went warm the moment he closed his hand.

Not hot. Warm. The exact warmth of somebody else's hand. As if someone had reached out across the bank, a few feet, and laid two fingers very gently on the inside of her wrist, and left them there.

She didn't look at it. She didn't need to. She sat on the bank in the dusk with her hand in her lap and Milli's tail round her ankle and the warmth on her wrist, and looked at the water-carrier holding half a gold heart in his fist, and Kestra standing over him with her empty palm, and her feet, Hana noticed, for the first time since the autumn, pointing nowhere at all.

"It's warm," said Hana. Very quietly. "Kestra. It's warm."

Kestra looked at her then. For the first time. A long, dry, dangerous look, the look she'd given Hana in a courtyard in the rain.

"Good," said Kestra.

Then she turned round and went back up the bank and sat down under the willow again and closed her eyes, as if nothing had happened at all.

***

They walked back to the city in the dark, all of them. Jared woke up and carried Aleaka's boots, because Aleaka had decided to walk barefoot in the grass and then regretted it. Cursiffe read the last page of the second book by the light of the west-gate lamps. Oswin walked with the signed book held against his chest in both arms and didn't say a single word all the way back, which had never happened before and would never happen again.

At the Broken Lance the water-carrier stopped, and the others went in, and Hana stopped too, on the step, with Milli.

"I'll keep it," he said. He held up his fist. "Here. In my coat. With the good knife."

"You'll lose it."

"Alea will make sure I don't," he said. "She makes sure about everything. She packs the knife. She's done it since she was twelve. I never noticed."

Hana laughed.

"Goodnight, Alice," he said.

He went in. She heard the roar from the long table as the door opened, and Tiger's voice, and Dennis saying something about a ledger, and then the door shut and it was just the street, and the lamps, and the paper fishing rods in every window going up the hill to the big house where she slept.

She walked home with Milli. Up the hill, in the dark, slowly, with his hand in hers and his tail round her wrist, the one with the cord on it.

"Honey," said Milli, about halfway up.

"Mm."

"Is it still warm?"

Hana looked down at it. At the half of a gold heart on the inside of her wrist, in the lamplight.

It was. It stayed warm all the way up the hill, and through the door of the king's house, and up the stairs, and into bed, and all night long, while Milli slept and the city went quiet and the lamps went out one by one along the high street below. She lay awake in the dark with her hand on her chest and the warmth on the inside of her wrist, two fingers' worth, like somebody keeping her pulse.

She didn't sleep much. She didn't need to.

It was only the second night in her whole life that she'd slept in the same city as him, and the first one she'd spent warm. She wanted to be awake for as much of it as she could.
