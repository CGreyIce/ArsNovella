They came down the river road on the ninety-second day, in the middle of the afternoon, and Rowan Hale heard them before he saw them.

He was in the council chamber. He was always in the council chamber on the third day of the week, because that was the day the council met, and the council met whether or not anybody had anything to say, which most weeks they didn't. Old Mabry was talking about the drains. Old Mabry had been talking about the drains for eleven years. Rowan had his chin on his fist and his chain round his neck and his eyes half closed, the way he'd sat through eleven years of drains, and he heard it come up the hill through the open window.

Not cheering. Wrenhaven didn't cheer; it wasn't that kind of town. A sound like a long breath let out. Doors. Feet. A child shouting, high and thin, from somewhere near the river gate, one word, over and over.

"*Back. Back. They're back*—"

Old Mabry stopped in the middle of a culvert.

Rowan was already on his feet.

***

He'd been waiting fifty-two days.

He hadn't told anyone that. He didn't need to. The whole town had been waiting, in its way, since the morning in the early summer when fifteen people and a fox girl and a man with two buckets had gone up the lake road with a brewer's dray and a miller's cart and not come back. The town had watched the road. Every day. Not the way it watched for a cart, or for weather. The way you watch a door in a house where someone's ill, without ever quite looking at it.

And then the reports. Maren's, every few days, by rider, folded small, that came to Rowan because Maren had arranged it before she left. *Day nine. Caddow*. *Day nineteen. The Sallow. He carries the water*. And then the riders from the south with the news from the Lists, which nobody in Wrenhaven had believed for a full day and then everybody had believed at once, so that for a week there wasn't a window in the town without a paper fishing rod in it, cut out of anything anyone had. Old Mabry's grandson had made one out of the minutes of a council meeting. Rowan hadn't said anything. They'd been very long minutes.

*A girl from the lake. A black sword. Daughter of Diwa, a healer. And of Ars*.

He'd sat at Odette's kitchen table the night that one came, with the rider's report in his hand, and not said anything for an hour. Odette had sat across from him and not said anything either. And then she'd got up and made a paper fishing rod out of a page of the city accounts, and stood it in the window, and they'd both looked at it.

And then the riders had stopped. Because the Lists were over, and the summoned were coming home, and there was nothing more to report except the road.

Fifty-two days.

He came down the hill from the council house at a pace that wasn't a run, because the mayor didn't run, and through the square, where people were coming out of every door, and down the river street toward the gate. And there they were.

***

It was the dray he saw first. The brewer's dray, with the militia's horses in front of it, that Rowan had watched go up the lake road in the summer with its wheels new-greased and its barrels empty and fifteen packs strapped in the back. Coming down the river road now in the afternoon sun, dusty to the axles, with the miller's cart behind it.

And people. A great many of them. More than had gone.

He'd known there'd be more. Maren's reports had told him. *Groups 4 and 6 north with us as far as the river. They will take the barge home from your quay*. But knowing a thing and watching it come down a road were different. He stood at the river gate of Wrenhaven, which was a gate in name only and had been open for forty years, and watched it come.

Five girls first, at the front, the way they'd gone. The five from the militia hall at the top of the lake road, who'd blocked a fisherman's road on their first day in the world and waited at the top of it for twenty months. The tall pink-and-violet one walking with her hands in her sleeves. The little one with the penguin at her heels. The one with the squid. The one with the moths, who saw everything. And the horned one, Gigichi, walking beside the dray with a yoke across her shoulders and a bucket of water at each end, and a scowl on her face like a thundercloud, and not spilling a drop.

Rowan looked at the buckets for a long moment.

*Gigichi*, Maren had written, from the road north, in the report he'd got four days ago. *He said ask Gigichi. She said no. She has carried it every day*.

And then the rest. The big young man with the forge-black hands and the sword on his back. The quiet one beside him, who listened to things nobody else could hear. The one with the kettle, who was holding it, actually holding it, in the crook of his arm like a cat. The small dark woman walking a little apart from the others with her arms folded, who'd felt something under a fisherman's heart on Rowan's own terrace in the spring and told him it was healing. The rest of them. Maren and Bram, and the musician, and the two hard women with swords, one of whom had been in a ditch once.

And the town coming down the river street behind Rowan. All of it. Not cheering. Just coming, and standing, the way it had stood at the top of the lake road in the summer to watch them go.

Nobody at the back of the dray with two buckets and a rod.

Rowan had known there wouldn't be. Maren's reports had told him that too. *He has gone east, with the five from Cinderhold. The girl with him. For the anniversary*. He'd read that one standing up, in the street, outside the council house, and stood there for a while after.

He'd known. He looked anyway.

***

Maren came straight to him.

She didn't stop for anyone. She came through the gate past the dray and through the crowd that was closing round the fifteen like water round stones, small and neat and dusty, in her riding coat with her list-book under her arm, the way she'd come down the market road two years ago with five frightened girls behind her. She came and stood in front of him, at the gate, and looked up at him.

"Mayor," she said.

"Maren."

She didn't say anything else. She unbuttoned the inside pocket of her coat. She took something out of it. A sheet of paper, folded small. Thick paper. Cream-coloured. Good paper, the kind they made in the capital, the kind Rowan had seen once in his life before, on a letter with a king's seal on it.

She held it out.

"Into your hands," said Maren. "He said."

Rowan looked at it.

He didn't take it at once. He stood at the river gate of Wrenhaven with the whole town round him and the summoned coming home and the militia horses blowing in the sun, and looked at a folded square of cream paper in a small dusty hand, and found that his own hands didn't want to move. The way they hadn't wanted to move eight years ago, at a cabin door, in the last week of summer, when a man had come home across the fields alone with nothing on his shoulder.

He took it.

It weighed nothing. He'd known it would. He stood holding it, and didn't open it.

"Not here," said Maren. Quite gently. She'd seen him not open it. Maren saw everything; it was what made her good. "He said you'd read it at the kitchen table. With the cups on the shelf."

Rowan looked at her.

"He said that?"

"He said that," said Maren. And then, because she was Maren, and couldn't help it: "Then the girl took it off him and wrote on it."

***

He didn't open it in the street.

He didn't open it in the square, where half the town stopped him to shake his hand as if he'd been the one who'd come home, which in a way he supposed he had. He didn't open it at the top of the hill, where he stopped and turned round and looked back down at the river and the crowd round the dray and the long blue shine of the lake beyond the roofs, and the north shore, and the place where you could just see, if you knew, the silver of a jetty against the water.

He opened it at Odette's kitchen table.

She was there. Of course she was there. She'd heard the same shout from the river gate in the records room at the council house, and come home the back way, faster than he had, because she always knew which way was faster. She was standing at the dresser with her back to him, doing something to a jug, when he came in, and she didn't turn round.

"Well?" said Odette.

"He sent a letter."

The jug stopped.

Rowan sat down at the table. Odette's table, where they'd sat with the brewer's second-best and the candle and the window open on the warm dark, the night before the road south, and a man had said *it was my name* and Rowan had said *not tonight*. He put the folded cream square down on the wood in front of him.

He looked up at the shelf over the dresser.

Two cups. Odette's good one, with the blue rim. And a chipped one, plain brown, with a crack going down from the lip like a river on a map, that Rowan had found on the step of an empty cabin on the morning after the road south, with a scrap of paper folded inside it. *Keep the cup. I'll want it back*. He'd put it on the shelf that afternoon. He'd looked at it every day for fifty-two days.

He unfolded the letter.

***

It was very short.

He'd known it would be. The man had never written him anything long in twelve years. *The pike are running*, once, by the egg-woman's boy, in the old war code. *Keep the cup*. That was all, mostly. A man who'd cut four words into a black iron headstone and gone over one letter twice, and called that everything he had to say.

Two lines. Two hands.

The top one in the plain narrow script of Aurelan, the hand Rowan had seen on a thousand market slips and a few dozen notes, firm and level and unhurried, like a man walking.

*Late pumpkins. A.*

And under it, in a different hand. Rounder. More careful. The script Odette had taught a fox girl of six at a cabin table, with her tongue between her teeth, one letter at a time, because her father's hand was too narrow to learn from.

*Both of us. A.*

Rowan read it.

He read it again.

Then he put it down on the table, very carefully, flat, with his big hand on it, and put his other hand over his eyes, and sat there in Odette's kitchen in the afternoon sun with the window open and the town coming up the hill outside, and cried. Not loudly. He'd never done anything loudly. He sat with his hand over his eyes and his shoulders going, a grey old man with a mayor's chain round his neck like a collar on a bear, and couldn't stop.

*Late pumpkins*. He was coming home. After the grave. After the anniversary. When the pumpkins were late in the field and the frost was coming. He'd come down the lake road the way he always did, in the first frost.

And he'd knock.

*Both of us*.

Eight years. Eight times. Rowan had stood at his gate at the top of the town in the last week of summer with his hand on a little girl's shoulder and watched a man walk up the lake road alone, with the rod over his shoulder, and not look back. Eight times he'd felt the girl's shoulder under his hand go stiff and then go still, and stay still, all the way until the road went round the hill. Eight times she'd come home with him and Odette, and slept in the lace room, and not talked about it. And eight times, a month and a half later, they'd all three stood at the top of the lake road in the first frost and watched a man come down it, alone, and nobody had said anything.

She'd gone with him.

She'd gone, this year. To her mother. *Both of us*. And they were coming home together. *Both of us*, in her own round careful hand, under his.

Rowan sat with his hand over his eyes and wept for the girl he'd stood in a door for, and for the man who'd let her go with him at last, and for the woman under the stone who'd stood on the end of a half-ruined jetty once and said *here*. And for himself, a little. Because he'd been holding that door for eight years and he hadn't known, until this minute, at this table, how much it had weighed.

***

Odette hadn't turned round.

She was standing at the dresser with her back to him and the jug in her hands, perfectly straight, the way she'd stood at every council meeting for twenty years. He heard her put the jug down. He heard her breathe, once, in a way that wasn't quite steady.

"Well," said Odette, to the dresser. "Well. That's that, then."

"That's that," said Rowan, from behind his hand.

"Late pumpkins." She opened a drawer. He heard her rummaging in it. "They'll be very late. The frost'll have had half of them. He'll complain."

"He'll complain."

"And the girl won't be in the lace room." The rummaging stopped. "Not this year. She'll be at the cabin."

"No," said Rowan. "She won't be in the lace room."

Odette was quiet for a moment. Then she turned round.

Her face was perfectly composed. Perfectly. The city secretary's face, that had run the stones like a general and the packing like a siege. Her eyes were very bright, and her mouth was set very firm, and if she'd been anybody else Rowan would have said she'd been crying, but she was Odette, so he didn't.

She had a pair of shears in her hand. And something red over her arm.

"What's that?" said Rowan.

"It's the tablecloth from the council chamber," said Odette.

Rowan took his hand away from his eyes.

"The *council* tablecloth?"

"Mabry's been spilling ink on it for eleven years," said Odette. "It's a disgrace. I've been meaning to replace it." She shook it out. It was very red, and very large, and had a gold fringe, and a great dark blot in one corner shaped like a duck where Old Mabry had once knocked over the inkwell talking about the drains. "The loft window at the cabin's got no curtain. It never has. He's never thought of it. She'll be sleeping up there in the frost with the wind off the lake coming straight through the shutters." She held the cloth up against the light. "It'll need lining."

"Odette," said Rowan. "That's the council's."

"Then the council can come and ask for it," said Odette, and sat down at the other end of the table with the shears and the red cloth and the gold fringe, and began to cut, very fast, with her mouth set very firm, and didn't look up once.

Rowan watched her for a while.

Then he got up, and reached down the two cups from the shelf. Her good one with the blue rim. And the chipped brown one. He washed them both at the basin, though they were clean, and dried them, and set them on the table, side by side, at his end. Facing the door.

Odette looked up.

"He won't be here till the frost," she said.

"I know."

She looked at the cups for a long moment. Then down at the red cloth in her lap, and the shears. Then she went back to cutting, a little more slowly.

She didn't tell him to put them away.

***

They knocked at dusk.

Three knocks, on Odette's front door, the one on the street that nobody used because everybody came round the back. Rowan was still at the table. He looked up. So did Odette. Nobody knocked on that door. The council used the back. The egg-woman used the back. The man with the rod had knocked on it exactly once in twelve years, late, on the night before the road south; before that he'd only ever stood at the gate until Rowan noticed him, which was at once.

Rowan got up and went through and opened it.

There were three young men on the step.

The big one, the forge-black one, Gabe, who filled the doorway. The quiet one, Deston, beside him, with his head a little on one side the way he held it when he was listening to something nobody else could hear. And a third, a little behind the other two, a narrow quiet man with a still face, whom Rowan had seen on the terrace in the spring standing next to the fisherman in a way that had made him think, at the time, of a sword standing next to its sheath. Xav.

They stood on Odette's step in the dusk, the three of them, in a row. Rowan saw the big one take a breath.

"Mayor Hale," said Gabe. He said it very carefully, the way a young man says something he's practised. "He asked us to knock."

Rowan looked at the three of them.

"He said go up the lake road," said Gabe. "Just for a night. Knock on Rowan's door. Tell him I'm coming back for the late pumpkins." He swallowed. "Check the jetty's still standing. Check the hens hate somebody."

There was a short silence on the step.

"So," said Gabe. "He's coming back. For the late pumpkins." He looked at Rowan, very earnest, in the dusk, with his big hands at his sides. "That's the message."

Rowan found that his eyes had gone again. He'd thought he'd finished, at the table, an hour ago. Apparently not.

"I know," he said. "He wrote."

"Oh," said Gabe. He looked at Deston. Deston looked at Xav. Xav's face did nothing at all.

"He wrote," said Rowan, "and then he sent three of his brothers three hundred miles out of their way to tell me the same thing in person, and knock on my front door, which only one other man's done in twenty years." He stood back from the doorway. "Come in. Odette's got a jug. And you'll want to see the letter."

***

They saw the letter.

They sat round Odette's kitchen table, the three of them, with the jug and the candle and the red tablecloth in heaps all over the end of it and Odette sewing in the middle of the heaps, and passed the cream square from hand to hand. *Late pumpkins. A.* *Both of us. A.*

Gabe read it, and put it down, and put his big hand flat on the table beside it, and didn't say anything for a long time. Deston read it, and smiled, slowly, and then looked at nothing, the way Rowan had seen him look at nothing on the terrace in the spring; and said, quietly, to nobody, "It's still gold. Both ends. All the way to the fort."

"What is?" said Odette, without looking up.

"His thread," said Deston. "To her. And hers to him." He looked at the cups at Rowan's end of the table, side by side, facing the door. "And yours."

Rowan didn't ask what he meant. He thought he probably knew.

Xav read it last. He held it for a long time. Then he folded it again along its folds, very neatly, and handed it back to Rowan across the table.

"She's never been," said Xav. "Has she."

"Never." Rowan put the letter in his coat, against his chest. "Eight years. She asked at eleven. He said *when you're older*. She never asked again."

Xav nodded slowly. He didn't say anything else. But his eyes, Rowan saw, went to the window, and east, the way the fisherman's eyes went east from the end of his jetty every evening. And stayed there a while.

"He's going to tell you everything," said Gabe, eventually. "When he gets back. He said. All of it."

"He said."

"It's a lot," said Gabe. He sounded almost apologetic. "It's—Mayor, it's a *lot*. It took him all night, with us. On the jetty. And we already knew half."

"I've got all winter," said Rowan.

Gabe looked at him. Then he laughed, short and startled, and wiped his face with the back of a forge-black hand, and Deston laughed too, and even Xav's mouth did something at the corner. And Odette, in the middle of the red cloth, said *mind the fringe* to nobody in particular, and went on sewing.

***

They went down the lake road at first light, the four of them.

Rowan took them. He had the cabin key in his coat, that Odette had given him off her ring, and he had the chipped cup, because he'd found when he got up that he couldn't leave it on the shelf. He carried it in his coat pocket. He didn't know why. It knocked against his hip all the way down the hill.

Past the militia hall at the top of the lake road, with its shutters still closed and its yard empty and the brewer's dray standing in it with its shafts down. Past the place where five girls had blocked a road. Down between the hawthorn hedges and the stone wall and the reeds, the way the road went down, the way he'd walked it every morning for fifty-two days with a bucket of grain for the hens and nobody at the bottom.

The big one went quiet halfway down.

Rowan saw it happen. They'd been talking, the three of them, a little, the way young men talk walking anywhere: the barge at noon, whether Calvin had slept with the kettle again, what the big one's smith would say when he got back to Kingsferry. And then the road came round the last bend, and the lake was there, all of it, at once, very blue and very still in the early light, with the mist lying on it in long white bands. And the cabin. And the hen-yard. And the jetty, going out into the mist.

Gabe stopped talking.

He stood in the road and looked at it.

"The last time I came down here," he said, after a while. "In the spring. Off the barge. He was on the end of it. Fishing." He was quiet. "And Dest started the song, and I came in, and the top part didn't. And then it did."

"I heard about that," said Rowan.

"The whole town heard about that," said Deston.

"The whole town heard about that," Rowan agreed.

They went on down. The cabin was shut. Not locked; it had never been locked, whatever Odette said about keys. The step was swept, because Rowan swept it every morning. And from round the back of the cabin, from the run, as they came into the yard, there came a sound Rowan knew very well. A sort of rising, outraged, many-throated chuckle. The sound of three hens who had been told about breakfast and could see that the people coming into their yard were the wrong people.

The big red one came round the corner of the cabin first.

She stopped. She looked at them. At Rowan, whom she tolerated. At Deston, whom she ignored. At Xav, whom she looked at for a long moment, very still, with her head on one side, and then, astonishingly, ignored as well.

And at Gabe.

Rowan would tell it for years. He'd tell it at council meetings, when Old Mabry got onto the drains, and at the midwinter bonfire, and to the fisherman himself, at that same kitchen table, in the frost, with the cups out, and watch him put his face in his hands and laugh until he couldn't breathe. He'd never once get it right. It wasn't a thing you could tell. You had to have been in the yard.

The big red hen looked at Gabe, the largest and kindest and most gentle young man Rowan had ever met, who'd held a gate shut with a sword against a ram, standing in the yard in the early light with his big hands at his sides and an expression of hopeful goodwill.

And she lowered her head, and spread her wings, and *went for him*.

And the other two came round the corner of the cabin behind her like a cavalry charge.

***

"*Why*," said Gabe, from the top of the woodpile.

He'd gone up it backwards, very fast, without seeming to touch it, the way a cat goes up a tree. He was crouched on the top now, on the neat stacked logs under the eaves, with his knees up round his ears, and below him, in a ring, three hens, looking up. Not making any sound. Just looking. With the steady patient malice of creatures who had all morning.

"I don't know," said Rowan. He was holding onto the fence. He was having some difficulty standing. "I don't know. They've never done that. Not to anybody. Not even to him."

"*Why me*?"

"He said check they hate somebody," said Deston. He was sitting on the cabin step. He'd sat down quite suddenly, a few moments ago, and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders were going. "He said they'd be glad of the variety."

"*He knew*," said Gabe, from the woodpile. "He *knew*. He *planned* this."

"He didn't plan it," said Xav.

They all looked at him. Xav was standing in the middle of the yard, among the hens, perfectly still, with his hands at his sides. None of the hens had gone near him. One of them, a small brown one, was actually standing on his foot.

"He didn't plan it," said Xav. "He just knew." He looked up at Gabe on the woodpile. His face did nothing at all. "He always knew where you'd be."

Gabe looked down at him from the top of the woodpile, with three hens in a ring below him, in the early light.

Then he started laughing. Helplessly. With his knees round his ears. So hard the logs shifted under him and Rowan had to go and hold the pile steady, laughing himself, with tears running into his beard for the second time in a day and the eighth time, he thought, in about twelve years. And the hens watched all of it, very calmly, waiting for somebody to come down.

***

The jetty was still standing.

Rowan had known it would be. He'd checked it every morning for fifty-two days, from the step, without going out on it. Every post. Every plank. He'd watched the ice-breaking floods in the spring go under it and the summer storms go over it, and it had stood, the way it had stood since a man and a woman and a very small girl had rebuilt it out of a half-ruin, plank by plank, more planks than you'd need.

He didn't go out on it now, either. He stood at the land end, by the first post, with the cup in his coat.

Gabe went.

He'd come down off the woodpile eventually, when Rowan had distracted the hens with the grain, and walked out along the jetty, slowly, in the mist, with his hands in his pockets. Deston behind him. Xav last. Out along the silver planks, over the still water, the boards giving a little under Gabe's weight, the way they must have given under the fisherman's every night for eight years. To the end.

And sat down.

The three of them. On the end of the jetty, in a row. Gabe in the middle, where the boards were worn smooth and pale in one place, a hollow the shape of a man who'd sat there every evening for eight years with a rod across his knees. Deston on one side. Xav on the other. With their legs hanging over the water, and the mist lying on the lake in front of them, and the sun just coming up behind the hill.

Rowan stood at the land end and watched them.

He'd never been out there. Twelve years, and he'd never once set foot on the end of it. It was the man's. It had been the man's and the woman's, and then just the man's, and Rowan had never in his life thought of going out to the end of it without being asked, the way you'd never go into somebody's bedroom.

He watched three young men from another world sit on it, in a row, the way brothers sit, where the fisherman sat.

And he found he didn't mind at all.

Out in the middle of the lake, very far off, something turned over in the blue, and the rings went out, slow and wide, all the way across the still water to the jetty, and broke softly against the posts under three pairs of hanging feet.

"Is that the pike?" said Deston.

"That's the pike," said Rowan, from the shore.

Xav leaned forward. Very slightly. On the end of the jetty, with his hands on the edge of the boards, looking at the place where the rings had come from. His face did nothing at all. His eyes did everything.

"Not this year," said Xav, to the lake. Very quietly. "Next year."

***

They left at noon, on the barge.

The whole town came down to the quay to see it go. The *Patient Widow*, which a man called Dennis had bought for eighteen months of a king's stipend and a feeling, low and wide and tarred black, with old Haskett at the steering oar and his two boys on the poles, and the summoned of Groups 4 and 6 crowded into it with all their packs and the musician and the two hard women with swords. The one with the kettle up the mast. The small dark woman with her arms folded on the cargo, who looked at the crowd on the quay with an expression Rowan had come to recognise as *if anyone touches me I will go back to Kingsferry by swimming*. Nobody touched her.

The big one stood in the stern, and waved, all the way down the river until the reeds took the barge round the bend. Rowan waved back. He felt a fool. He didn't stop.

And then they were gone, downriver, toward Duskhollow and Kingsferry and the sea, and the quay emptied, slowly, the way a church empties, and Rowan stood at the edge of it with his hands in his coat, one round the chipped cup.

Five girls were standing beside him.

He hadn't noticed them come. The five from the militia hall, in a row on the quay, watching the bend in the river where the barge had gone. The tall pink-and-violet one with her hands in her sleeves. The little one with the penguin. The one with the squid on her shoulder, crying, quite openly, the way she did everything. Gigichi, with her arms folded and a scowl.

And the one with the moths. Higu. Small and calm, with her hood up and her counting book under her arm, and something in her other hand.

A basket.

A willow basket, a fisherman's basket, with a lid and a strap. Empty. Rowan knew it. He'd seen it every morning for a year and a half on the step of the militia hall, with two fish in it, and then five. He'd seen it come up the lake road on the girl's arm.

"We kept it," said Higu.

Rowan looked at her.

"He sent it with Alea. The last morning in Valcrest. With five fish in it." Higu looked down at the basket. "We said we'd keep it. And when he came back, it'd be on the step of the hall. Empty. And then he'd have to fill it."

She didn't say anything else. She turned, and went up the river street toward the square, and the lake road, and the militia hall at the top of it, with the basket on her arm. The other four went with her. Rowan, after a moment, went too.

He watched her do it.

The militia hall at the top of the lake road. The step, worn hollow in the middle. Higu set the empty basket down on it, in the middle, exactly where it had always been, where it had sat every morning for a year and a half. She looked at it for a moment. Then she took a flat grey stone from the wall beside the step, a lake stone, smooth, the size of her palm, and put it in the bottom of the basket, so the wind wouldn't take it.

And stood back.

The five of them stood in the road and looked at it. An empty basket on a step, with a stone in it. Nobody said anything.

Rowan didn't say anything either.

He thought, standing there in the road at the top of the lake road with the chipped cup in his pocket, that the whole town would walk round that basket for two months. The children on their way to the lake. The egg-woman. Old Mabry. Nobody would touch it. Nobody would need to be told. They'd step round it on their way past the militia hall, every day, the way you'd step round something somebody had left for somebody else. The way you'd step round two cups set out on a table, facing a door.

And then one morning, in the first frost, there'd be fish in it.

***

He went home.

Odette was at the table, where he'd left her, with the red council tablecloth in pieces all round her and the gold fringe in a heap on the floor and a curtain, very nearly finished, over her knee. Lined. With a pattern of small blue flowers on the lining, from somewhere; he didn't ask where.

The two cups were where he'd left them. At his end. Side by side. Facing the door.

He took the chipped one out of his coat, where he'd carried it all morning, down the lake road and onto the quay and back up to the step of the militia hall, and found that he'd been holding it so tight in his pocket that the crack had left a line across his palm, like a river on a map.

He put it back on the table. Beside the other.

"The hens went for the big one," he said.

Odette's needle stopped.

"*Went* for him?"

"All three," said Rowan. "He went up the woodpile like a cat."

Odette looked at him for a long moment over the curtain. Then she put the needle down, and put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders went, the way they'd gone on the cabin step in the summer. And Rowan sat down across from her, at his end of the table, between the two cups, and found that he was laughing too.

Outside the window, the afternoon went on. The town went about its business. Somewhere down the hill, three hens were sitting on a woodpile, very satisfied. And at the top of the lake road, on the step of the militia hall, an empty basket sat in the sun with a stone in it, and everybody who passed it stepped round it, and nobody said why.

*Late pumpkins*, thought Rowan.

*Both of us*.

He'd get the cups out. He'd already got the cups out. He'd leave them there.
