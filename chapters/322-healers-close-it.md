Kei read it aloud at the Bell in the morning, at the long table in the old wing, with the kettle on and the lamp still lit though it was light.

He'd read it four times in the night. Once as it came, at two in the morning, sitting up on his pallet in the long room with Hisui round his neck and Drew asleep on the next one. Once straight after, to check he'd understood the order. Once at four, slowly, the way he'd read a recipe for a dose he'd never made, with a pencil, copying it out into his own book in the kingdom's letters so that he could read it to people without stopping to turn it in his head. And once at six, standing at the window in the yard, because he couldn't not.

*This is how. All of it. For whoever's next.*

Sister Agathe sat across the table from him with her hands folded. Sister Wenna sat beside her. They'd come up from Kingsferry two springs ago, the three of them, when the king asked for healers and Maud said *no* to Rachael and sent them instead; and Brother Ansgar had gone home after the first month because he fainted at blood, and Agathe and Wenna had stayed. Through the call, and the siege, and the count, and the winter. They ran the old wing. It had been the first inn, before the Bell was the Bell, and now it was the healers' room, with a lamp by the north window that was never put out and a kettle that was never off, for anybody in Thornwall who woke in the night hearing their name.

They couldn't read the channel. Natives couldn't. They'd known for two years that it was there, that the thirty-five talked in it across the whole kingdom, the way you'd know there was a river under a town without ever seeing the water.

So Kei read it to them.

He'd told them the other thing first. In the night, at half past five, before any of it. He'd gone across the yard and knocked on the old wing's door and Agathe had opened it with the lamp, in her nightgown, and looked at his face, and said *who*.

*Sister Maud*, he'd said.

Agathe hadn't said anything. She'd stood in the door with the lamp for a long time. Then she'd gone back in and sat down on the edge of Wenna's bed, and woken her, and Kei had stood in the yard outside and heard Wenna make a sound he'd remember.

That had been the night. This was the morning. They sat at the long table with their hands folded and their eyes red and their aprons on, because they were healers and it was morning, and Kei read them how Maud had died.

***

He read it all. Every word. He didn't skip.

What the grey was, and where it started, and how you knew it. That you couldn't close over it; Rachael had underlined that twice, and Kei said so. The burning, for when it was only in the skin, the way Maren and Emrys had learned it; Wenna made a small noise at Emrys's name. Then the turning, a stroke at a time, and who you needed with you, and what it cost. That the one in the middle could come back to herself. That she might not stay.

And the four short sentences at the end, which he read slower than the rest, and which Agathe asked him to read again, and he did.

He put the book down.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The kettle ticked on the hob. In the long room across the yard somebody was laughing, Bee, at something Tiger had said, and then stopped, because Hyvred had come in and told them.

"Four days," said Agathe at last.

Kei looked at her.

"She had it in her palm four days," said Agathe. "Off the step. And didn't tell the girl." She looked at her folded hands. "That's her. That's exactly her. She'd have died of the black rot in that ward sooner than take a healer off a case to look at her."

"She'd have said the girl was no use to anybody till she'd slept," said Wenna. Her voice was thick. "She said it to *me*. My first winter. I'd been up two nights with a lighterman's crushed foot and she took the bowl out of my hands and said *you're no use to anybody till you've slept, girl, go to bed*. And then she sat up with him herself." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "She was seventy if she was a day."

"Seventy-one," said Kei.

"Seventy-one," said Wenna, and put her face in her hands.

***

Hisui hadn't said anything all morning.

That was rare. Hisui said things all the time. Hisui had an opinion about the porridge and the weather and Kei's doses and the state of Drew's feet and whether the third ring tasted fuller today or yesterday, and he said all of it, all day, round Kei's neck, in a voice only Kei could hear. Kei had stopped noticing, mostly, the way you stop noticing a clock.

He noticed now.

*You're going to do it*, said Hisui, at last, when Agathe and Wenna had gone to see to the north ward. *Aren't you. The girl's way.*

Kei didn't answer. He was looking at the book.

*You taste like you did before the antidote*, said Hisui. *When you were going to drink the quietwort. Like you've already decided and you're waiting to be told no.*

"I haven't decided anything."

*You've decided everything except who*, said Hisui. *That's the bit you're stuck on. I can taste it. Who.*

Kei shut the book.

That was it. That was exactly it. He'd read Rachael's eight steps four times in the night and every time he got to the end he'd looked up the valley, out of the long-room window, toward the bridge and the swept road and the pass, where thirty-one people stood in a curve in a valley eleven miles off and forty of them came down at dawn on the mornings Tiger ran and stood at the far end of the millstream bridge with their hands open. Turned. Someone wearing someone. And he'd thought: *it works*. And then: *on who*.

He didn't test on people who couldn't say no.

He'd said that in the first winter, to Corporal Dunn, who'd offered. He'd tested every dose he'd ever made on himself, and been sick for weeks, and not known Hild for half an hour once on the north road; and he'd never once put a drop of anything into anybody who couldn't look at him and say *yes* first. It was the one rule he had that wasn't about measures.

The turned couldn't say anything.

***

Hyvred said Ossie, at noon, on the tower.

He'd known she would. He'd gone up because she was up there, the way she was most of the day now, on the top of the north tower with Bee's coat round her shoulders and her sight held up the valley, eleven miles, to the curve. She was more than halfway to Lantern; she could hold it there for hours now without her eye bleeding, mostly. She didn't look round when he came up.

"Ossie," she said.

"I know."

"He's pulling. From underneath. The seam's loose along the top. It's been loose since the race." She didn't move. "When the boy came to his mother on the bridge, he looked. Him. Not it. I saw it go along the join." She was quiet. "He's the only one of the forty who's awake under there. The others are asleep. Pressed flat, like flowers in a book, the way I said. Ossie's pressed flat and he's *pushing*."

"I know."

"So it should be him."

"Yes," said Kei.

She looked at him then.

"You're not going to," she said.

"Not first," said Kei. "Not on him." He looked up the valley. "If it goes wrong the first time, it goes wrong on whoever's first. Rachael said. Halfway's worse than not starting. And I don't know what halfway does to somebody who's been turned for three years. Nobody does. She did it on a woman who'd been turned for a night." He swallowed. "I'm not finding out what halfway does on Ossie. In front of Tiger."

Hyvred looked at him for a long time.

"Then who," she said.

Kei didn't answer.

***

Nell Dunmore answered.

He hadn't asked her. He'd have said afterwards that he hadn't asked anybody; that he'd sat in the old wing with the book and not asked a living soul, because it wasn't a thing you could ask. But Thornwall was a small town, and Agathe had talked to Corvin, and Corvin had talked to Hild, and Hild had said something at the well to the wife of the sergeant who'd taken ten men north up the shepherds' track three years ago and not come back. And at four in the afternoon Nell Dunmore came across the Bell's yard in her widow's grey, which she'd worn for three years though nobody had ever told her she was a widow, and knocked on the old wing's door, and asked for the snake man.

She was a big plain woman of fifty with forearms like a baker's. She sat down at the long table across from him, where Agathe had sat, and put her hands flat on it.

"You're going to try the girl's way," she said. "On one of the ring."

"I haven't—"

"Hild says you are. Hild says you're stuck on who." She looked at him. "Try it on Wat."

Kei didn't say anything.

"Sergeant Dunmore," said Nell. "Walter. Wat. He's in the front of the third ring with his coat buttoned wrong. He's buttoned it wrong every day of our married life and I've done it up again every morning at the door and he's never once thanked me." She didn't blink. "Try it on him."

"I can't," said Kei. "He can't say yes."

"No," said Nell. "But I can."

"It's not the same."

"No," said Nell Dunmore. "It's not." She looked at her big hands on the table. "Twenty-six years I was married to that man. He was a sergeant in the Western Watch the whole of it. And every time there was a thing to be done that might go wrong, a patrol, a bad crossing, the Brenn in spate, the first one into a burning byre, he went first. He never once asked if he could. He just went, and came back and told me after, and I'd shout at him, and he'd say *somebody had to, Nell, and it might as well be the one who knows how*." She was quiet. "He took ten lads up the shepherds' track because the captain wanted it looked at and he wasn't going to send a corporal. He went first."

Kei didn't say anything.

"If you could ask him," said Nell, "he'd say yes before you'd finished asking. He'd be cross you'd asked. He'd say *it might as well be me*." She looked up. "I know that the way I know he'll have his coat buttoned wrong. I'm not guessing. I'm not hoping. I'm telling you what my husband would say, because he can't." Her voice didn't change at all. "And if it goes wrong, it goes wrong on him, and that's what he'd want. Better him than the Rees boy. Better him than somebody's lad."

Hisui was very still round Kei's neck.

*She tastes true*, said Hisui. Very quietly. *All the way down. Like iron.*

Kei sat at the long table for a long time.

"If it goes wrong," he said, "I don't know what it'll do to him."

"No," said Nell. "Nor did he. Up the shepherds' track." She stood up. She pushed the bench back. "He's been gone three years, snake man. I've buttoned his coat up in my head every morning for three years. Whatever you do to him, he'll still be gone if you don't." She went to the door. She stopped there. "I'll be on the bridge. When you do it. Behind your friend. I'd like to be where he can see me, if he can see anything."

***

They did it on the seventh morning.

It took that long to get it right. Not the method; Rachael had written the method, and Kei had read it so many times he could have recited it. The *who*. Not who to try it on. Who to have there. Rachael's eight steps had eight kinds of people in them and Thornwall had to find every one.

*Somebody who can hear or see where the next stroke is*. Hyvred. She could see the seam. She'd seen it on Ossie from a pace. She'd have to be a pace from Sergeant Dunmore, on the bridge, with forty of the turned at his back; and Hild said no, and Hyvred said *I'll be behind Drew*, and Hild said nothing for a long time, and then *Behind Drew*.

But Hyvred could see it and the healers couldn't, and you couldn't follow somebody else's eyes a stroke at a time with your hands. So Bee.

Bee had chalk. Bee always had chalk. She'd drawn the running dog on every slate in Thornwall and she'd drawn a line across the mouth of the pass in the first snow that had held the absence for a heartbeat. *One Line*. She could draw a line where somebody told her and it would be exactly there. So the plan was: Hyvred would look, and say, and Bee would draw it, a chalk line on Sergeant Dunmore's face exactly where the seam ran, so the healers could see where to put their hands.

*Somebody to keep the time*. Azac, who'd held a ward all night in a winter storm by telling it what everybody was doing. He'd talk. Steady. He was good at steady.

*Somebody between you and whatever's pouring*. Drew, on the bridge, the way he always was. Post. And Malcolm wrote on his boots at the postern without being asked; he'd done it every morning since the boy, and held them up after, nine and ten and twenty out of twenty.

*Somebody to bring you warmth along a thread*. They didn't have one. There was no Deston at Thornwall. Kei had sat with that for two days and found nothing. Kei had asked in the channel, and Deston had said *I can't from here, I've tried, the threads go but nothing goes down them that far*; and Rachael had said *then keep it short. very short. halfway is worse.*

And the heartbeat.

That was his. *Flinch*. The dose the call couldn't get through. He'd made forty of them for the night of the count and given every one, and the call had flinched everywhere at once, for a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat three of the called had woken and two had stayed. A heartbeat. That was all he'd ever been able to give anybody: one heartbeat when the thing holding them let go of the shoulder.

Rachael had done it in three hours.

He had a heartbeat.

"Then it's not to close it," said Agathe, the last night, at the long table, with Wenna beside her and the book open. "Not all the way. We can't. Not in a heartbeat." She put her finger on the page, on Rachael's line. "*Halfway is worse than not starting*."

"Then what's it for?" said Wenna.

Agathe was quiet a long time.

"To find out if he's there," she said. "Under it. That's all. One stroke. Open one stroke at the top, where Hyvred says it's started, and look. And close it again behind." She looked at Kei. "That's what Maud would say. You don't cut a man open to see what's wrong with him if you can't close him again by supper. You open a hair. You look. You close the hair."

Wenna looked at the page for a long time.

"I'll open," she said. "You close. I've got the quicker hands."

"You've got the colder hands," said Agathe.

"That too," said Wenna.

***

The ring came down at the first grey on the seventh morning, the way it always did: *like people coming home from market*. Forty of them, across the road, a pace off the far end of the millstream bridge, facing the town. The miller's widow in her shawl. Ossie at the front. And beside Ossie, square and grey, Sergeant Dunmore, with his ten in a row behind him and his watch coat buttoned wrong.

Kei walked out with them.

Drew first, at his walk, with *Unless* round the welts of his boots, on to the bridge, to the middle, and turned round. Back to the ring. Then Hyvred behind him, barefoot, in Bee's coat, with her eye already going. Bee with her chalk. Azac. The two sisters in their aprons with their sleeves rolled. Kei with his satchel and the one dose in it, in a flask the size of his thumb, that he'd made at midnight and not slept since. And behind them all, last, to the near end of the bridge, where she'd said she'd stand, a big plain woman in widow's grey with her hands folded.

Tiger wasn't running.

He'd asked to. Kei had said no. Not this morning. *If Tiger turns, it lets go of all of them at once, and walks them all back a pace, and we lose him*. Tiger had stood in the long room with his face doing nothing and said *okay*, and gone up on the wall, and he was there now, at the parapet over the postern, with Rui Xiang a hand from him, watching.

They went past Drew.

That was the hardest thing. Drew stood in the middle with his back to the forty and his face to the town and his hands open at his sides, and they walked past him. Seven people. Past the post. To the far end of the bridge, to the last stone, a pace from the ring.

To Sergeant Dunmore.

He didn't look at them. None of them did. They looked at the town. Over Kei's head. With their faces patient and a little puzzled, the faces of people who've walked to the end of the lane and can't remember why. Dunmore's face was square and grey and stubbled and his eyes were on the wall, and his coat was buttoned with the third button in the second hole, all the way up, wrong.

*He tastes of nothing*, said Hisui. Very quietly. *Like the others. Like a cellar. And under it—* Hisui stopped. *Under it, something asleep.*

"Hyvred," said Kei.

"I see it." Her voice was strange, the way it went when the sight was full. She was a pace from Dunmore, behind Drew's back, with her hand on Drew's shoulder. "It goes round the face. Jaw. Ear. Hairline. Chin. It's tight. It's tight all the way round." She was quiet. "Not like Ossie's. Ossie's is loose at the top. This one's—" She stopped. "It's started at the hairline. On the left. Above the eye. There's a stroke there that's the first one. Like a knot at the end of a thread."

"Bee."

Bee stepped up. She didn't look frightened. She'd drawn a line across the mouth of a pass with the absence coming at her. She put her chalk out, a hair from Sergeant Dunmore's forehead, and Hyvred said *there, left, a finger's width up*, and Bee drew.

A white line. A finger long. On a turned man's face, above his left eye, at the hairline, exactly where a seam began.

Dunmore didn't move.

"Azac," said Kei.

Azac started talking.

"Drew's on the bridge," said Azac. Steady. Low. The voice he'd used all night in the winter storm with the slates coming off the roof of the north ward. "Drew's on the bridge and his feet are set. Hyvred's behind him with her hand on his shoulder. Bee's got the chalk. Nell's at the near end. Tiger's on the wall. Rui Xiang's a hand off him. Malcolm's on the gate. Everybody's here." A breath. "Everybody's here. Wenna's hands are up. Agathe's beside her. Kei's got the flask."

Kei put the flask to Sergeant Dunmore's lips.

He'd never done that. Not to anybody who couldn't say yes. He stood there on the last stone of the millstream bridge with a thumb's worth of knitwort and something else he'd spent two years finding, in a flask, at a man's mouth; and the man couldn't say anything; and at the near end of the bridge a woman in grey had said it for him. *It might as well be me*.

"Yes," said Kei, to nobody, and tipped it.

***

The call flinched.

He felt it. Everybody did. It went through the ring the way it had gone through the whole valley the night of the count, all forty at once, a twitch, like a man in his sleep when a door bangs: every head going a hair to one side. And the thing in Sergeant Dunmore, the thing holding him up from inside, that Hyvred said was a hand on a shoulder, let go.

One heartbeat.

Wenna put her hands on the chalk line.

She did it the way Rachael wrote. Kei watched her do it. She didn't push. She put her left hand by it, a hair off, and listened, for a breath he didn't think she had, and found it. The first stroke. The knot at the end of the thread. And she went back along it, with *thank you*, the way you'd unpick a stitch, and it opened.

A finger's length. At the hairline. A line, a hair wide, white chalk on either side of it, opening.

And under it—

Kei saw it. He'd remember it all his life. Under the seam, in the hair-wide gap above Sergeant Dunmore's left eye, not the patient puzzled face. A different face. The same face, but not the same: flatter, paler, pressed, like a flower in a book, the way Hyvred had said. Sergeant Dunmore's own face. Asleep.

And then, in the gap, under the seam, an eye opened.

His own eye. Not the one that looked at the town. The one under it.

It looked at Kei. Then past him. Down the bridge. Past Drew's back. To the near end, where a big plain woman in grey was standing with her hands folded.

The mouth didn't move. The patient puzzled mouth on top didn't move at all. But Kei heard it anyway; everybody on the bridge heard it, and Hyvred said afterwards that she saw it come up from underneath through the gap in the seam like air coming up through a crack in ice:

"*Nell*."

Then the heartbeat was over.

***

The pour came out first.

That was the thing Rachael had written and Kei had read four times and still not understood till he saw it. You open where it's poured. And what's poured comes out. And if there's nobody between you and it, and nobody closing behind, and nobody bringing you warmth, it comes out on to whatever's there.

Wenna's hands were there.

It came out of the gap above Sergeant Dunmore's eye like cold out of a cellar door when you open it in summer. Not smoke. Not dark. Just cold, so cold you could see it, a white breath; and it went over Wenna's fingers where they were on the seam, and her fingers went white. Not pale. White. The white of frost on a window, all at once, to the knuckles, to the wrist.

And the heartbeat ended, and the thing that had let go of the shoulder took hold again.

From inside. Kei felt it. Hyvred said it: *it's pulling it shut*. The seam above Sergeant Dunmore's eye, the finger's length Wenna had opened, closed. Not the way Rachael closed things, with a hand behind. From the inside. Like somebody pulling a door to in a hurry, from the other side, with your fingers still in it.

Wenna's fingers were in it.

She screamed. Agathe had her by the wrists and pulled, and Wenna's hands came off the seam with a sound like ice cracking off a gutter, and the seam was shut, tight, all the way round, and the chalk line was a white line on a patient puzzled face that was looking at the town.

The eye underneath was gone.

The ring stepped back. One pace. All forty. Not toward them; away. The way it had stepped back off the bridge when Tiger turned. As if something in it had been hurt and didn't know where.

And then it stood. And looked at the town. And didn't move again till dusk.

***

Wenna's hands were white to the wrist and wouldn't bend.

Agathe got them into a basin of lake water in the old wing, not warm, cold, the way you did with frost, and held them there, and Wenna sat on the bench with her face grey and her teeth going and didn't make a sound after the first one. Kei knelt by the basin. He couldn't do anything. He'd never been able to do anything for a burn but say what was in it.

"A week," said Agathe. Not to him. To Wenna. "If they come back. Maybe more. You'll lose the nails." She held the white hands in the water. "You'll keep the fingers. I think. I'll know tomorrow."

"I opened it," said Wenna, through her teeth.

"You opened it."

"He was there." Wenna's eyes were wet and very bright. "Under it. I saw him. He *looked*."

"I know," said Agathe. "I saw."

Nell Dunmore was in the doorway.

Kei hadn't heard her come. She stood in the old wing's door with her big hands folded at her waist and her widow's grey with the dust of the swept road on the hem, and looked at the basin, and at Wenna's hands in it, white. Then at Kei.

"He said my name," she said.

"Yes."

"I heard it." She didn't come in. "From the near end. Past your friend's back. Everybody did." She was very still. "He's in there."

"Yes."

"Asleep."

"Yes. The others are asleep. Hyvred says. All but one." He looked at the basin. "He woke up. For a heartbeat. To look at you."

Nell Dunmore stood in the doorway for a long time.

Then she came in. She came across the old wing to the bench, and knelt down by the basin beside Kei, slowly, the way a big woman of fifty kneels, and looked at Wenna's white hands in the water. And she put her own hands in the basin, round Wenna's, the big baker's forearms in the cold lake water to the elbow, and held them.

"Thank you," she said. "For opening it."

Wenna looked at her. She was crying now. She hadn't before.

"It didn't work," said Wenna.

"It did," said Nell Dunmore. "I was told he was gone. Three years. I was told it by the captain and the council and the chaplain and half the town, and I wore the grey because they said, and I did his coat up in my head every morning anyway." Her hands were steady in the water. "He's not gone. He's asleep. That's a different thing. You can wake a man that's asleep." She looked at Kei. "I've done it every morning for twenty-six years."
