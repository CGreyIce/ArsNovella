Salty had never once in his life been able to wait for anything, and on the fourth morning of the Swiss he drew the only person in either world who'd never once asked him to.

"Salty and Wendy," said Chris. At the wall. In the logging voice, which went a bit odd on the second name. "Main sand, first bout. Two–one against two–one. Winner's through at three–one. Loser's two–two and in the deciders tomorrow."

Group 3 was standing under the wall in a row. Nobody said anything.

Then Maigo said, "*Oh no*," very loudly, with his antlers going back, and Niko said, "Huh," and Yan didn't say anything at all, and Suzu, on the scythe, lifted her head and looked at Salty with her green eyes in a way that Salty had learned, over two years and a half, meant she found something very funny and would never say so.

Wendy was standing at the end of the row with her bow over her shoulder and her lavender headphones round her neck. She looked up at the wall. At the two boxes, side by side, in the fourth column, with a line between them in lampblack. SALTY, in gold. WENDY, in gold.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked at Salty.

"Well," said Wendy. "That's stupid."

"Yeah," said Salty.

"I'm not going to go easy."

"Good."

"I mean it," said Wendy. "You'll want me to. Halfway through. You'll get that face. Don't."

"I won't."

"You will," said Wendy. "You'll get the face you got on the knoll when I told you not to let go." She turned round and walked off toward the tunnel with her bow, not looking back. "Don't."

***

He went to the forge.

He always went to the forge before a bout. Last summer it had been Hesk's, on the Street of Armourers, where a king had stood with his hand over his eyes looking at a scythe; this summer Hesk had let him have the back anvil again for the week without being asked, because Hesk had been at the Lists last summer and seen a short broad man from another world carry a black stone across the Redmarch in the ballad they sang about it now in the taverns, and Hesk, like most of Valcrest, didn't charge people out of ballads.

He didn't make anything. He just stood at the anvil with the fire going and a bar of iron in the tongs, and heated it, and took it out, and put it on the horn, and hit it.

Not hard. He'd learned that. From Garrow, in the first snow of the winter before last, at the second anvil at Cinderhold, with the pass shut and the war asleep across the red and a king's father's hammer in his hand with a nick in its face like a fingernail. *Slower*. *Wait for it*. *The heat stops being angry if you wait for it*.

He'd never waited for anything. On Earth, he'd been the one who went in. In every game, every round, every server: the one who heard the call and went, first, all of him, before anyone else had finished deciding. *Salty doesn't wait*, they said. In the channel. On the sand. On the wall at Cinderhold, where Rhea said it with something like love. *Salty goes in and hits things and they fall over*. It was true. It had always been true. He'd lifted a champion of the Lists off a gate last summer like a sack of grain, and lost the Singles final to Xav because Xav had stood still and let him swing at nothing, and laughed so hard he'd dropped the greatsword.

He'd learned to wait at the anvil. He'd had to. Iron didn't care how much you wanted it to be a scythe. You heated it and you watched the colour and you waited, and the colour went from angry red to straw to the deep quiet orange that meant *now*, and if you hit it before *now* it cracked and if you hit it after it was cold and you'd wasted the heat. You couldn't hurry it. You couldn't go in. You had to stand there with the tongs in your hand and your whole body wanting to go and *wait*.

He'd forged a scythe that way. In six months. Thousands of hammer marks, every one of them with a little crescent in it from a king's father's hammer, all down both faces like the scales on a fish. Every one of them a wait.

He'd never learned to wait anywhere else.

He heated the bar. He took it out. Straw. Not yet. He held it. He felt it, the thing that came up in him at the anvil, that he'd never felt anywhere but here: a warmth, from the bottom of him, from the place under his aura where Bear lived, where the weight of things sat when he carried them. It came up slowly when he waited, the way the heat came up in the iron. He'd never had a word for it. Wendy had one. She'd said it on the knoll, on the night of the scouts, with her bow drawn and her one last arrow, looking at him on his knees with his arms round a black stone: *the forge coming up in him*. She'd said it to the channel. She hadn't known he'd read it.

Orange. *Now*.

He hit it. Once.

It rang.

***

He'd met Wendy through Yan. Everybody had met everybody through Yan; Yan was the middle of a wheel the way Hana was, except Yan never noticed. He'd been in a Discord call with Yan and three people he didn't know, playing CS at two in the morning, and somebody new had joined the call, a girl with a quiet voice who said *hi* and then nothing else for an hour, and then, at the end of a round they'd lost badly, said very flatly, *that was the worst rotate I've ever seen*, and Salty had laughed so hard he'd had to mute.

She'd been right. It had been his rotate.

She'd always been right. That was the thing about Wendy. She was a skeptic; she'd been a skeptic on Earth and a skeptic on the floor of the hall in Solmere and a skeptic on the cart going east the first week, saying *there's no way home, there's no Sun mage, this is a lie they tell you so you'll fight*. She didn't believe anything she hadn't counted. And she counted everything. She'd counted the scouts on the red, a hundred and sixty-one and four hunters, from the wall, and the forty who went out, and the forty-eight living at dawn. She counted for the people who couldn't see. That was what she'd said, at the gate, the night of the knoll. *That's what I'm for*.

They'd been going to go to Malacca. To see Ice. That autumn, the autumn before, the one that hadn't come. She'd found the flights. She'd had them in a tab on her laptop for three months, open, not booked, because she was a skeptic and something might come up, and something had: a call at three in the morning that wasn't a game.

She'd never closed the tab. She'd told him that, in the first winter, on the wall at Cinderhold, with the snow coming down and Yan asleep against a merlon. *It's still open. On my laptop. In another world. Three years. Nobody's closed it*.

He thought about that tab sometimes. More than he thought about most things.

The main sand at noon on the fourth day was full.

Not as full as for Xav and RX; nothing would be that full again until the bracket. But full. They'd heard. The whole city had heard: the strong one from the ballad, the one who carried the stone, against the archer who'd counted the scouts, and the boy selling pies at the south gate had told everyone who'd listen that they were *together*, the two of them, like that, and the women at the wells had passed it on. Forty thousand people who'd come out on the fourth morning of a strange tournament to watch a man and a woman who loved each other try very hard to put each other on the sand.

Salty came out of the north tunnel with the greatsword on his shoulder. Not his own; his own was on its peg at the Lance, where it had been all week. The Lists' heaviest practice blade, blunted, nearly as long as he was tall, the one he'd lifted Milli off the sand with last summer and fought Xav with and dropped laughing. It pulled on him slightly to the right. He let it.

Wendy came out of the south tunnel with her bow.

They'd drawn the big ring for this one. The marshals did, for archers: not the eight-pace chalk ring of a sword bout but the old one, thirty paces across, painted on the sand in white, that they used for the Fledgling's open bouts, so that a bow had room to be a bow. Thirty paces. On the knoll she'd put arrows into scouts at sixty in the dark. Thirty was nothing. Thirty was across a room.

They stood at opposite sides of the ring.

She was wearing the headphones. Round her neck, the way she always wore them, silent, lavender, the thing she'd had on her head when she came through the floor of the hall, that had never once played anything here. She'd said once they were the only thing in this world that still smelled of her room.

"*Begin*," said the marshal.

Salty went in.

***

He didn't decide to. He'd never once decided to. He heard *begin* and he was going, all of him, all at once, across the sand, the greatsword coming off his shoulder, the way he'd gone across the red two summers ago with fifty scouts in a ring and a black sword on his back, the way he'd gone at a gate in the Lists, the way he'd gone at everything in two worlds since he was old enough to go.

The first arrow came at him at twenty paces. Padded. It would have hit him in the chest. He took it on the flat of the blade without slowing down, *thock*, and felt it, Bear, the weight of the arrow sitting not on his arms but on his aura, the way the stone had sat on it all night on the knoll, so that it didn't push him back at all.

Second arrow. Fifteen paces. Same. *Thock*.

Third. She'd stepped left. It came at his right side, past the blade. He turned his shoulder into it and took it on the meat of his arm and it bounced off and he didn't stop.

Ten paces.

She didn't back away. That was the thing. Any archer in the Lists would have backed away from a short broad man coming at them with a greatsword at ten paces; that was what thirty paces were *for*. Wendy didn't. She stood exactly where she'd been standing, at the edge of the white line, with her bow drawn and her face doing the thing it did when she was counting, and let him come.

He swung.

Not at her. Never at her; he'd never once in his life swung at Wendy and he wasn't going to start. At the bow. A flat arc, the whole weight of the practice blade, at the place where the bow was, to take it out of her hands, the way you'd take a stick off a dog.

The bow wasn't there.

She'd dropped it. On purpose. A hand's width before the blade got there, she'd let go of it, and it fell, and the greatsword went through the air where it had been with a sound like wind through a door, and Salty, at the end of his swing, all of him gone into it, all at once, the way he always went, was a pace from her with nothing in front of him and the blade out to his right and the whole of his body still going.

And she wasn't holding anything at all.

She'd stepped back. One pace. Out of the ring's edge and back in. And she had her hands up, empty, in front of her, the way you'd put them up to show somebody you weren't carrying anything, and her face was still doing the counting thing.

"Three," said Wendy.

He looked up.

***

There were three arrows in the air.

Above him. Not falling. *Held*. The three she'd shot at him on the way in, the three he'd taken on the blade and the blade and the arm, that had bounced off and gone up and should have come down on the sand behind him. They hadn't come down. They were hanging in the air over the ring, a man's height above his head, three of them, padded, very still, with a faint light round each one like frost on a window, the way the single arrow had hung over the wall at Cinderhold on the night of the first snow, the winter before last, when she'd found *Aloft*.

One arrow. She'd only ever been able to hold one. For a few heartbeats. She'd said so in the channel a hundred times. *One. Four heartbeats. Five if I'm angry*.

She was holding three.

And he understood, standing there at the end of his swing with the greatsword out to the right and his whole body still going, what she'd done. What she'd been doing since *begin*. She hadn't been shooting at him. She'd known he'd take them on the blade. She'd known he wouldn't stop, because he never stopped. She'd shot them *at him* so they'd bounce *off* him, up, into the air, where she could catch them. And she'd counted. One, two, three, as he came, one for every arrow, the way she'd counted the scouts on the red, the way she counted everything, under her breath, to keep the numbers in the place where she could hold them.

She'd counted them into the air.

"Three," said Wendy again. Quieter. Her voice had gone strange. "Salty. I've got *three*."

Her window was open. He could see the edge of the light of it, in front of her face. She wasn't looking at it. She was looking at him.

He knew what was going to happen. He could see it the way you see a round end before the kill feed comes up. She was going to drop them. All three, at once, from three places over his head, onto his back and his shoulders and the top of his head. He couldn't block three. Not with a greatsword at the end of a swing. He'd lose. Three–nought, three touches, in the time it took to breathe out.

And he could stop it.

He could feel how. The swing wasn't finished. He was a pace from her, with the blade out to his right and the whole of his weight still going into it, and if he let it carry on, if he turned with it the way he always turned with it, the way he'd turned with everything all his life, the flat of the practice blade would come round in a circle, all the way round, and touch her on the side before the arrows came down. He'd win. Three–one. Through. It was the one thing he'd ever been good at. Going on. Not stopping.

And the face came.

He felt it come. The face Wendy had told him about at the wall. The face from the knoll. It came up in him the way it had come up on his knees on the green with his arms round a black stone and seven hours of scouts pulling and his ribs gone and Wendy at the edge of the ring with her last arrow saying *don't let go*. Not the face of a man who was going to lose. The face of a man who was going to *stop*. Who was going to hold the swing, at the end, with everything going, a pace from somebody he loved, because he couldn't bear to finish it.

*Don't*, she'd said.

*I won't*, he'd said.

He didn't stop.

***

It wasn't stopping. That was the thing. He'd think about it for a week afterwards, at Hesk's back anvil, with a bar in the tongs. It wasn't stopping, and it wasn't going on. It was the third thing.

It was the anvil.

The swing went on. He let it. The whole weight of the greatsword carrying on round in its arc, the way his weight always carried on, all of him, all at once. But he didn't *follow* it. He'd never once in his life not followed his own swing. He let it go round, and he stood still inside it, the way you stand still at an anvil with the iron in the tongs, and waited, and let the thing come up.

It came up.

From the bottom of him. From the place under Bear where the weight of things sat. The warmth, slowly, the way the heat came up in iron, from angry red through straw to the deep quiet orange that meant *now*. He felt it come up through his chest and down his arms into his hands and into the hilt of the Lists' heaviest practice greatsword, which wasn't his, which he'd never carried across anything; and he felt the blade, at the end of its arc, get heavier.

Not heavier to swing. Heavier to *land*. As if everything in him that had ever waited at an anvil had gone down into the steel at once and was sitting there, at the edge, waiting for the moment.

The flat of the practice blade came round in its circle and stopped, by itself, a finger's width from Wendy's side.

Not because he'd stopped it. Because it had arrived. Because it had come round to exactly the place it was going and it was so heavy now, with everything he'd put in it, that it didn't overshoot by a hair. It just *landed*. In the air. A finger from her side. The way a hammer lands on hot iron when you've waited for *now*: once, all of it, and still.

The sand under his feet jumped.

All of it. The whole ring. Thirty paces of sand jumped, a finger's height, all at once, and came down, the way the sand on the knoll had jumped when the stone rang; and every one of forty thousand people in the stands felt it come up through the benches into their seats and gasped.

And three arrows came down on his back.

*Thock. Thock. Thock*. On his shoulders, and between his shoulder blades, and on the top of his head, padded, very lightly, the way you'd tap somebody on the back to tell them they'd forgotten something.

"*Touch*," said the marshal.

There was a pause.

"Touch," said the marshal again, in a different voice. "To the archer. Three. And—" He stopped. He was looking at the practice greatsword, a finger from Wendy's side, perfectly still, in the air, with nothing holding it there but a short broad man standing very still inside his own swing. He looked at the sand, which had settled. He looked at the bow on the ground. "And the blade's not touched. It's a finger off. So it's to the archer." He sounded unsure. He'd been marshal of the main sand longer than anyone, and he'd never once said *it's a finger off* in a voice like that. "It's to the archer. Three–one. Through."

Wendy didn't move.

She was standing at the edge of the ring with her empty hands still up and her face doing nothing at all, looking at the blade a finger from her ribs. Then at him.

"You didn't stop," said Wendy.

"No."

"You didn't go on either."

"No."

She looked at him for a long time. At his face. At whatever it was doing. She'd always been able to read his face; she was the only one who could, in two worlds, all the way down.

"What was that?" said Wendy.

He didn't know. He opened his window to find out.

He found Lucan that evening, outside the master's house, at the top of the Street of Coopers.

He'd brought the crock. He'd been carrying it all week, in his pack, on his back, under the greatsword, from Cinderhold; a big brown earthenware crock with a cloth tied over the top and a cork under the cloth and wax over the cork, so heavy that anyone else would have put it in the cart, and he hadn't, because Bear meant the weight sat on his aura and not his back, and because he wasn't going to let anyone else carry it.

Pepper paste. The fort's. His. From six plants in pots on the warm stones behind Oakes's hearth at Cinderhold, grown from seeds he'd picked out of a man's leftover dried peppers last summer, the ones from the ship street in Valcrest that a man had bought for eleven dinners on a forge fire. Peppers, garlic, salt, oil, and a spoon of the brown paste from the ship street, which he'd kept a jar of all winter, rationing it, a spoon at a time. Teague put it on his eggs now. Old Ansel said it made him feel his teeth. The elves had tried it and one of them had gone red to the ears.

He held it out to Lucan.

Lucan looked at it. Then at Salty. Lucan knew. Lucan had known since the night the cart came in by the north road and he'd stood in the road and said nothing and given a man a key.

"The twelve," said Lucan.

But he stood at the top of the Street of Coopers in the dusk with his window still faintly glowing at the edge of his eye and his ribs aching the way they did when he'd laughed or cried or lifted something, and he'd done all three, and thought about a tab on a laptop in another world, open three years, with two flights in it to a city by the sea that nobody had booked.

*Don't*, she'd said.

He sat down in it.
