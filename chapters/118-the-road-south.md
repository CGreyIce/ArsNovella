Maren had a list, and there was a blank on it.

She'd made the list the night Kestra came, in the upper room at the militia hall, by one candle, the way she made all her lists. Two wagons. Four horses. Twenty-two people, counted twice. The road south: sixteen days, if the weather held and the Sallow was down, posting houses at Caddow and Pellow's Cross and Fennick, and after that the downs, and after the downs the plain, and after the plain Valcrest. Water. Watches. Who walked and who rode and who drove, and who could not on any account be put next to whom for more than a day at a time.

She was good at lists. She'd been making them for the Advisor since she was twenty-three.

And beside every name on this one there was a job. *Bram: miller's cart, first watch, horses.* *Sabine: dray, second watch.* *Petra: rearguard.* *Emrys: morning office, wherever we are; fires.* *Althea: the water casks, with Gigichi*, because they'd asked to be put together, and Maren had looked at them for a long moment and written it down. *Rachael: never alone. Gabe or Dennis, always.* *Xavier: front, with whoever's in front.* Twenty-one names. Twenty-one jobs.

And one name with nothing beside it at all.

She'd sat with the pen over that line for an hour. She'd tried *rearguard*. She'd crossed it out. She'd tried *at his discretion*, and crossed that out too, because it sounded like the Advisor, and because she wasn't sure the Advisor would have known what to write either. She'd tried leaving it, and found she couldn't bear the look of it: his name, alone, at the bottom of her list, with a space beside it like a question nobody was allowed to ask.

In the end she'd left it blank and gone to bed. It was the first list she'd ever made that wasn't finished.

***

He came and asked her for a job on the first night.

They'd made the posting house at Caddow by dusk, the way she'd planned, and it had been a good first day. A long one. The lake road and the square and the south gate, and then the hedges and the hill, and then the lake country unrolling behind them blue and green in the afternoon until the last of the water had gone behind the hills. Nobody had said much after the bend. Alea had walked at the front beside her father for the first hour, with the clay whistle in her fist, and then dropped back without a word and walked beside Petra for the rest of the day, and Maren had seen her face and looked away.

At Caddow the posting house had stabling for the horses and not much else, so they camped in the paddock behind it, and Maren did what she always did. She walked the camp with the list. Fires. Water. Watches. Bram talking to the horses, as he had all day, about their mothers, which the horses seemed to like. Sabine counting the money they'd spent at the posting house and looking at the posting-house keeper as if he'd personally taken it from her purse.

She'd got all the way round, and was standing by the miller's cart with the list in her hand and the blank at the bottom of it, when a voice behind her said, "What's mine?"

She turned round.

He was standing a pace away, in the dusk, with his rod over his shoulder and his hands empty. Nobody else was near. She had no idea how long he'd been there.

"Your—"

"Job," he said. "Everyone's got one. I watched you do it." He nodded at the list. "What's mine?"

Maren looked at him. At the man the Advisor had described to her on a sheet of paper twenty-one months ago. *He is a fisherman, and nothing more.* At the Hero of the Redmarch, the Black Blade, the man who'd cut a demon in half from the end of a jetty more than a mile away and sat down again and gone on reading. At a plain man in a plain coat, asking her what he should do.

"You don't have one," said Maren.

"Why not?"

"Because you're—" She stopped. She'd been going to say *the Hero*. She'd been going to say *you're the reason any of us are safe on this road*. She'd been going to say a great many things, and every one of them sounded, in her head, like something out of one of the songs.

"I'm the girl's father," he said. "This week. That's all I am. Severin said so." He held out his hand. Not for the list. Just held it out, open, the way you'd hold out a hand for a bucket. "Give me something to carry, Maren."

She looked at the hand.

And then, because she'd been one of the Advisor's seven for nine years and had never once failed to carry out an order to the letter, and because this was, she realised, an order, the first he'd ever given her, she looked down at the list and wrote, at the bottom, beside his name, in her square careful hand:

*Water.*

He read it upside down. And smiled. She couldn't hold on to his face, nobody could, but she saw the smile; it was in the shoulders.

"Thank you," he said, and went to find the buckets.

***

"He likes being told what to do," said Alea, later, at the fire, with her chin on her knees. "He never gets to. Nobody ever tells him anything." She watched him come back up from the posting-house well with two full buckets and set them down by the casks, and go back for two more. "Mum used to. She'd tell him to fetch things just to watch him go and fetch them. He'd go every time."

Maren didn't say anything.

"You've made his week," said Alea. "I mean it." She was quiet for a moment, watching him. "He's going to carry the water all the way to Valcrest now. You know that. You're never going to get the buckets off him."

He carried the water all the way to Valcrest.

***

The lake country gave out on the third day, and after that it was farms.

Maren hadn't been south of the lake in a year and a half. She'd forgotten how big it was down here: the long low valleys opening out one after another, the barley coming green on the slopes, the villages strung along the road every few miles with their wells and their chapels and their dogs. And she'd forgotten, or perhaps she'd never really known, what the summoned were, to the people who lived in them.

They were *famous*.

She'd known it, the way you know a thing from reports. She'd read the Advisor's dispatches. Songs about Group 7 in Valcrest; paper horns; a market in the capital selling little clay squids. But Wrenhaven had kept its head about the five girls, mostly, because Wrenhaven had been keeping its head about something much bigger for eight years and had the practice. The rest of the kingdom had not had the practice.

At the first village they were cheered. At the second, the whole of it came out to the road: farmers in from the fields, women with babies, a priest, the miller, a man with a goose under his arm for no reason anyone could discover, and a crowd of children who ran alongside the wagons shrieking names. *The squid lady!* That was Xola, with Squiddles on her shoulder going pink with alarm. *The moth! Show us the moth!* That was Higu, who went very red and, after a moment, let her moth go up off her finger in a little spiral of silver, and the children screamed as if she'd set off a firework. *The penguin! The penguin!* PenguChi took Waddles out of her pack and held him up, gravely, like a relic, and Waddles regarded the children with deep suspicion and was cheered louder than any of them.

They knew Rachael, too. Somebody's cousin had been in the infirmary at Kingsferry. There was an old man at the third village with a hand that hadn't closed properly in four years, and Rachael stopped the wagons, and knelt in the road with him in front of everyone, and twenty minutes later he closed it, and wept, and the whole village wept with him. They knew Gabe, by his size and his strange square blade. They knew Calvin, because a song had come up the river about a summoned boy who could climb a mast in a gale.

Nobody knew the man carrying the water.

Maren watched it happen in every village. The crowd parting round the wagons, and pressing in round the summoned, and reaching for them, and asking. And in the middle of it, or at the edge of it, or a little ahead on the road with two buckets in his hands and a rod over his shoulder, one plain man in a plain coat, whom nobody looked at twice. Their eyes slid off him. They always had. She'd seen it in Wrenhaven every day for a year and a half: people saying *evening* to him at the market and forgetting his face before they'd finished the word.

Here they didn't even say *evening*. They didn't know there was anything to say it to.

And he loved it.

She hadn't expected that. She'd expected him to find it hard. A man who'd been the most famous person in the kingdom for fifteen years, walking through village after village of people who'd made up songs about him, and not one of them knowing. She'd expected him to be quiet about it. Or sad.

He wasn't quiet about it. He walked through the third village with a child on his shoulders, a small girl who'd lost her mother in the crowd and been crying, and nobody noticed him do it, and he carried her the length of the street and handed her back to her mother at the well, and the mother said *thank you, sir* without looking at him, and turned straight round to stare at Calvin. And he came back to the wagons grinning. Maren saw it on him like sun on water.

"You could tell them," she said, that night.

"Tell them what?"

"Who you are."

He thought about it. Seriously, the way he seemed to think about everything now, as if each question were a fish and he wanted to see what it was before he decided whether to keep it.

"No," he said. "I've been somebody since I was twenty. I haven't been nobody in fifteen years." He looked at the fire. "It's nice, Maren. You've no idea. It's like being on holiday."

***

At Pellow's Cross there was a song.

Maren should have seen it coming. Pellow's Cross was where the road for the capital went off to the west, the biggest posting house between the lake and the downs, with a proper inn and a proper common room and, as it turned out, a proper innkeeper's daughter of about fifteen with a lute and a voice and absolutely no idea who had just walked in out of the rain.

They took the common room for the night, because it was raining. Twenty-two of them, wet through, crammed round three tables by the fire, with the inn's other guests pressed back against the walls staring at the summoned as if a menagerie had come in. Bram ordered for everyone. Sabine argued the price. The innkeeper, a red, round, overjoyed man, refused to take a copper from anyone, and Sabine looked at him with something close to grief.

And then the innkeeper's daughter stood up on the hearth with her lute, pink to the ears, and announced to the room that in honour of their famous guests she would sing *The Ballad of the Black Blade*.

The table went very still.

Maren saw it go. All the way round. Like a wind going over barley. Higu's hand on her counting book. Gigichi, stiff as a post. Xav's face doing nothing at all, the way it did, and his eyes going very slowly, very carefully, to the end of the bench. Deston with his eyes shut. Gabe beginning, very quietly, to stand up.

At the end of the bench, with his back to the wall and a cup of the inn's cider in his hands, the fisherman put out one hand without looking and laid it on Gabe's arm.

Gabe sat down again.

"Let her," said the fisherman, very softly.

So she sang it.

***

It was terrible.

Maren had heard it before. Everyone had. It was the most popular ballad in the kingdom, and there were forty versions of it, and this was, by some distance, the worst. In this one the Black Blade was seven feet tall. His eyes burned like coals. He'd been born in a lightning storm, the seventh son of a seventh son, and had killed a bear with his hands at the age of nine. He rode a black horse called Midnight, which ate meat. He had slain a thousand demons before breakfast on the Redmarch and two thousand after, and the general had begged for mercy on his knees and been given none, and the Blade's great black sword had sung like a bell with every stroke, and the sky had turned red, and the earth had turned red, and the river had run backwards for a day.

Maren didn't dare look at the end of the bench.

She looked at the summoned instead. Which was worse.

Calvin had his face in both hands. His shoulders were going. Malcolm was writing something in his book, very fast, and she suspected it was the words. Azac was staring at the ceiling with the expression of a man being slowly crushed under a weight. Xola had Squiddles held up in front of her face like a shield, and Squiddles had gone a colour Maren had never seen him go. John was leaning very gently against RX, who had put his aura so far inward that he was, for all practical purposes, not there. And Dennis, at the end of the next table, with his chin in his hand, was flinching. Every line. A small, sharp, involuntary wince, as if someone were ringing a cracked bell an inch from his ear, again and again and again.

*He rode a black horse called Midnight.* Wince.

*His eyes were coals.* Wince.

*Seven feet tall.* Dennis closed his eyes.

And then the song went the way all the versions went in the end. It slowed. The girl's voice dropped. And she sang about the maid.

In this one she was a princess, of course. In every one she was something she hadn't been: a princess, a sorceress, a queen of the fair folk. The demons took her, and the Blade rode out alone to save her, and was too late, and held her as she died, and swore a vow upon her body, and the girl on the hearth sang that part with her eyes shut and her whole heart, the way you can only sing something you've never once had happen to you.

Nobody at the three tables breathed.

Maren made herself look.

He was sitting exactly as he had been. Back to the wall, cup in his hands. Listening. She couldn't see his face. She could see Alea, beside him, with her ears flat to her head and her hand on her father's knee. She could see Gabe's big fist, on the table, clenched. She could see Xav looking at nothing.

And then the last verse.

*And every spring upon the red, where she lies under stone, / the flowers come up blue and white, that he planted on his own.*

Dennis's head came up.

He looked across the room at the girl on the hearth, startled. Then at the end of the bench. And Maren, who had heard from Rachael what it meant when Dennis went still like that, understood.

No wince. Not on that line.

The girl finished. The room clapped, loud and long. The innkeeper's daughter went pinker than ever and curtseyed on the hearth and nearly fell off it.

And the fisherman put his cup down, and stood up, and went across the common room through the press of people who didn't look at him, and stopped in front of the girl, and put a coin in her hand. Not a copper. Silver. Maren saw it flash.

"That was lovely," he said. "Would you sing it again?"

The girl stared at the coin. Then at him, the way everyone looked at him, as if trying to remember a word.

"The whole thing, sir?"

"The whole thing," said the fisherman, and went back to his seat at the end of the bench, and sat down, and picked up his cup.

Calvin made a sound into his hands like a kettle coming to the boil.

***

"She got the flowers right," he said, afterwards, outside.

The rain had stopped. Maren had come out to check the horses, which didn't need checking, and found him in the inn yard sitting on the rim of the trough, with his rod across his knees, looking at the clouds going off the moon.

"Dennis said," said Maren. "He said it was the only line that didn't ring."

"Dennis would know." He turned the rod in his hands. "I've heard all forty. Over the years. Bards used to come up to Wrenhaven to sing them at me, before Rowan put a stop to it. One of them sang one under the cabin window for an hour, and I went out in the morning and he'd gone and left his lute." He smiled. "Rowan kept the lute."

"Kestra says the songs are worse than the man."

"Kestra told me." He looked at the yard. "I told her the songs are fine. They're just about somebody else. A big angry man on a black horse who ate demons for breakfast." He was quiet for a moment. "Nobody wants to sing about a healer with cold hands who laughed at him when he got stitches. Nobody knows there's anything to sing."

Maren didn't say anything.

"But somebody got the flowers right," he said. "Somebody, a long time ago. Some soldier who walked past that rise in the spring and saw them, and told a bard, and the bard put it in, and it's been going round the kingdom ever since in the middle of all the rubbish about the horse." He looked up at the moon. "I'll take that. I'll take one line."

"Was there a horse?" said Maren, before she could stop herself.

He laughed. Out loud, in the wet yard, so that one of the horses put its head out of the stable door to look.

"I walked," he said. "Nearly always. I had a horse once, for about a month, when I was nineteen. I ended up cutting its saddle into a sword grip."

"And the horse?"

"Ate grass," he said. "Not demons."

***

The Sallow was up.

Maren had known it might be. Kestra had told them, the night she came: *Your road south is a disgrace*. Twice she'd nearly lost the king's letter in a river, and the second time, she said, had been the Sallow, at the ferry below Fennick, where the bridge had gone out in the spring floods and the ferryman was running a rope across by hand and charging a silver a head for the privilege of very nearly drowning.

The ferryman wasn't running anything now. The rope had gone, three days ago. The Sallow came down out of the hills brown and fast and loud, twice its width, with whole trees turning over in it, and the ferryman sat on the bank by his upturned boat with his pipe and told them it would be a week. Perhaps two. Perhaps, if it rained again in the hills, the rest of the summer.

Maren stood on the bank and looked at the river, and at her list, and at the sixteen days on it, which were now nineteen days or twenty, or never, and felt, for the first time since Wrenhaven, the cold little drop in her stomach that she'd felt on the road to the market twenty-one months ago with her knees going to the dirt.

*Midsummer. Valcrest.* The king's hand. *Bring the girl.*

"We'll go round," said Bram. "Upstream. There's a ford at Hollin, my uncle used to say."

"Your uncle's ford," said the ferryman, "is under eight feet of water, and your uncle is a liar."

"He's dead, actually."

"Then he's a dead liar."

Calvin went up a tree on the bank and felt the wind for a long time and came down and said he could probably get one or two of them across, if they were light, and if they didn't mind landing in a field quite hard. Gabe looked at the river and said he could probably carry the dray across if somebody else carried the horses, and Sabine said she'd like to see that, and Gabe said he'd like to see it too, and nobody laughed.

The fisherman stood on the bank a little way off, with the rod over his shoulder, and looked at the far side. At the trees. There was a stand of big old elms on the opposite bank, a stone's throw downstream from the ferry, with their roots in the water and their tops leaning out over the flood.

He didn't say anything.

"We'll camp," said Maren. "Tonight. We'll see in the morning." She looked at her list. "If it's no lower, we'll go upstream and try your uncle's ford."

"He's dead," said Bram.

"I know, Bram."

***

In the morning there was a tree across the river.

Maren saw it first, because she was always up first. She came out of the tent in the grey light with her list, the way she did, and stopped.

One of the old elms on the far bank was lying across the Sallow. From the far bank to the near one, the whole length of it, its crown tangled in the willows twenty paces from the camp and its great trunk spanning the brown water like a bridge, with the flood going white round it and under it and not moving it an inch. It must have come down in the night. It had come down exactly across, the way you'd lay a plank over a ditch.

She went down to the bank.

The ferryman was already there. He was standing on the near bank with his pipe gone out in his mouth, looking across the river at the stump on the far side.

Maren looked at it too.

It was waist-high. And the top of it was flat. Perfectly flat. As flat as a table, as flat as the top of the black stone at the grave, cut through clean in one line from one side of the trunk to the other without a single splinter, so that in the grey light it shone, pale and wet, like a plate.

Nobody had heard anything. She'd had Sabine on second watch and Bram on first, and Bram never slept on watch, and Bram had heard nothing.

She turned round.

The fisherman was sitting on the near bank a little way upstream, with his line in the water, fishing. He had a perch on the grass beside him.

"Did you—" said Maren.

"It was leaning," he said.

Behind her, from somewhere among the waking camp, Dennis said, without any heat at all, "Cracked bell."

The fisherman didn't turn round.

"A joke," he said, "in a lie's coat."

***

They took the wagons across in pieces, which took all morning: the barrels on Gabe's back, and the horses led one at a time along the trunk by Bram, talking to them about their mothers, and the dray and the cart unloaded and rolled across on their own wheels with everyone pulling on ropes and Sabine shouting. The ferryman watched the whole of it from the bank without saying a word, and at the end of it, when the last of them was across, he walked out onto the trunk to the middle of the river, and stood there with the flood going white under his boots, and looked at the stump on the far bank for a long time.

Then he looked back along the trunk at the camp, and the wagons, and the twenty-two of them. At the summoned. At the plain man coiling his line on the far bank.

He took his pipe out of his mouth.

"Seven feet tall," he said, to nobody. "Eyes like coals."

And put the pipe back in, and walked back to his side of the Sallow, and sat down by his boat.

***

She wrote her report that night, at Fennick, by one candle.

She wrote one every new moon, and the new moon had come on the road, and so she sat at a trestle in the posting-house yard at Fennick with the camp asleep around her and her careful hand going down the page, the way it had for twenty-one months. Distances. Weather. The Sallow. Rachael and the old man's hand. The children and the moth. Calvin and the tree. PenguChi, who was sparring Petra every dawn now, and losing slightly less every morning. Alea, who was sparring everyone, and losing to nobody but her father, and Petra twice, and once, on a wet morning, Xavier, who had stepped sideways in the breath before and then looked as surprised about it as she did.

And at the end, the part she always found hardest. The part the Advisor read first. She'd known that for a year: he read the reports from the back.

*Concerning the fisherman.*

She sat with the pen over the page for a long time. In the stable behind her a horse shifted. Bram was talking to it, very low, about its mother.

What was there to say? *He's on the road.* The Advisor knew that; Rowan had written. *He cut a tree down in the night with a fishing rod from a hundred paces away and put it across a river in flood as neatly as a plank across a ditch, and then sat on the bank and caught a perch.* That was true. It was the kind of thing she'd have written once, in the first months, when she still wrote down everything he did as if it were a sighting of a comet.

She didn't write it.

She looked across the yard instead. At the fire, gone down to embers. At the shapes asleep round it, twenty of them. At the end of the yard, by the gate, a man sitting on a mounting block with a rod across his knees, keeping the third watch, because he'd asked her for it on the fourth night and she'd written it down beside *water* without a word.

He wasn't looking at the road. He was looking at them. At the fire, and the twenty shapes round it. The way he'd looked at the town, the morning they left, standing at the top of the lake road. The way he'd looked at five girls on a market road, a year and a half ago, before her knees went to the dirt.

Maren looked at him for a long time.

Then she bent over the page and wrote, in her square careful hand:

*He carries the water.*

She looked at it. It was the truest thing she'd ever written in a report.

And under it, after a long time, smaller, because the Advisor had asked *How is he* at the bottom of every letter he'd ever sent her, and she had never once known what to answer:

*I think he is happy.*

She sanded it, and folded it, and sealed it, and went to bed. And for the first time in twenty-one months, she didn't lie awake.
