The invitation came on thick cream paper with a gold edge and a seal Hanarisu didn't recognise, and Oswin went white when he saw it.

"Lord Vane," he said. "Oh, no. Oh, that's not good."

"Who's Lord Vane?" asked Milli.

"The Council," said Oswin. "The king's council. Twelve lords who advise the Crown on everything the Advisor doesn't. Vane leads half of them." He turned the invitation over in his inky fingers as if it might bite. "He's been arguing for a month that the summoned are too valuable to be left with guides and guards. He wants you *fostered*. Placed in noble houses, as retainers. Under the protection of the great families." He said *protection* the way most people said *debt*. "And now he's giving a banquet in your honour."

"That's nice of him," said Jared.

"It isn't," said Oswin. "It's really, really not."

***

It was the third month. The capital had stopped staring at them, mostly. The five of them could walk through the lower town without a crowd now, though children still followed Aleaka and her cats, and old women still crossed themselves at Milli's tail for reasons nobody could explain. They trained every morning with Kestra and studied every afternoon with Oswin, and in the evenings they sat by the fire in the prince's sitting room and compared their windows, the way everyone did.

"Ten," said Hanarisu, that night.

"Six," said Milli, and then, with enormous, poorly-hidden pride: "I hit her today. Kestra. On the arm. With the practice sword."

"He did," said Jared. "Clean. She said *good*. Not *better*. *Good.*"

"She's never said good," said Aleaka, delighted.

"She's never said good," Milli agreed, and his tail curled up at the end, and Hanarisu leaned over and kissed his ear because she couldn't help it.

"Nine," said Jared.

"Twelve," said Aleaka, "and a hawk landed on my arm in the mews this morning. A real one. I didn't have a glove. It didn't grip. It just... sat there. And looked at me." Her fox ears twitched. "The falconer said it's never done that for anyone. Not even him."

"Thirteen," said Cursiffe. "And I've been testing something." He held up a sheet of paper covered in his narrow English handwriting. "I gave a page like this to three of the castle scribes. Asked them to copy it. Just copy the shapes. They can copy anything, it's their job." He set it down. "None of them could. They'd start, and lose their place. Every time. It's like their eyes slide off it."

"It's not just that they can't read it," said Hanarisu slowly. "They can't *hold* it."

"Not yet," said Cursiffe. "Maybe not ever."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a bell rang the hour.

"So whatever we write to each other," said Jared, "nobody here can read, and nobody here can copy."

"And nobody here can forge," said Cursiffe quietly.

They were all quiet for a moment, thinking about that.

***

Lord Vane's banquet hall was gold and white and very warm, and it was full of people who wanted something.

Hanarisu could feel it the moment they walked in. Not with any magic, or not only. She'd spent years on a screen in front of thousands of strangers, learning to read a chat as it scrolled past: who was there to enjoy themselves, who was there to be noticed, who was there to take something away with them. A banquet hall was just a slower chat. The lords and ladies turned to watch the five of them come in, smiling, and every smile was a little hook.

Lord Vane himself was tall and silver-haired and handsome in a careful sort of way, and he took Hanarisu's hand when she was presented to him and held it a moment too long.

"The cat princess," he said, smiling. "I've heard so much about you."

"From whom?" said Hanarisu, smiling back.

He laughed, as if she'd made a joke. She hadn't.

The dinner was very long. They were seated apart, one to each table, each next to a lord or lady who spent the meal being fascinated by them. Hanarisu's was Vane himself. He asked about her world, and her training, and her friends. He asked very kindly about whether she felt the guides and guards were *adequate*, and whether a woman of her obvious talents might not be better served with a house behind her, with resources, with *friends at court*. He mentioned, lightly, that the Advisor was getting old, and that the king listened to the Council more than people thought.

And as he talked, Hanarisu felt it.

The warmth. Under her ribs, where it had come from in the archive. It rose up in her the way it had when she'd smiled at the Keeper, and pressed against the inside of her chest, and waited. She knew, quite suddenly and quite clearly, that if she let it out, if she smiled at Lord Vane the way she'd smiled at the Keeper and let that warmth go *out* toward him, he would tell her everything. What he really wanted. Who he'd promised what. What the Council was planning. And then, if she asked nicely, he would probably stand up in front of his whole banquet hall and announce that he'd changed his mind about the summoned altogether.

It would be so easy.

She sat very still, with her hands in her lap and the warmth pressing at her ribs, and looked at Lord Vane's handsome, careful face.

He had no idea. That was the thing. He was a dangerous man, and a greedy one, and she was quite sure he would cheerfully have had all five of them parcelled out to the great houses like prize horses. But he had no idea what was sitting across the table from him, and no way of knowing if she used it, and no way of saying no.

She thought about the Keeper's face in the archive. The puzzled look. *As if he couldn't remember why he'd agreed.*

She let the warmth go back down.

It wasn't easy. It went slowly, reluctantly, like a cat being put out of a warm room. But it went.

"I'm very happy with my guide and guard, my lord," she said, in her own voice, with her own smile, the one she'd used on chat for years and never needed magic for. "But it's so kind of you to worry. Tell me about your house. Do you have animals? My friend Aleaka adores animals."

***

The cats arrived during the fish course.

Hanarisu never found out how. Aleaka swore afterwards that she hadn't called them, that she'd been sitting perfectly still at her table being polite to a deaf old marquess. But somewhere between the soup and the fish, a grey castle cat appeared in the doorway of Lord Vane's banquet hall. Then a black one. Then a tabby, and two more grey ones, and an enormous orange tom with one ear. All six of them walked the length of the gold-and-white hall in a solemn little procession, between the tables and under the chairs, past the lords and ladies and the gaping servants. And they sat down in a ring around Aleaka's chair, facing outward, like guards.

Aleaka had gone the colour of a beetroot.

"I'm so sorry," she said, to the room in general, her stammer completely out of control. "I d-didn't— they just— I'm so sorry—"

There was a terrible silence.

Then Jared, at the next table, started to laugh.

He didn't mean to. Hanarisu could see that he didn't mean to. But it got out of him anyway, low and helpless, and then the deaf marquess at Aleaka's table, who had apparently missed nothing at all, wheezed and slapped the table and said *by the gods, girl, they've come to rescue you*. And then half the room was laughing, and the other half was trying not to, and Lord Vane's carefully planned evening of quiet persuasion fell gently and completely apart.

Hanarisu didn't laugh. She only watched Vane's face as it happened. She watched him decide, very quickly, to laugh too, because he was a man who knew which way a room was going. She watched the calculation behind it.

He'd try again. She was sure of that. But not tonight.

***

Kestra was waiting for her in the corridor when they left.

She'd stood at the back wall of the banquet hall all evening, in her king's colours, saying nothing and eating nothing. Now she fell into step beside Hanarisu as the others went on ahead, and for a while she didn't say anything at all.

"You could have had him," she said at last.

Hanarisu kept walking. "I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." Kestra's voice was flat, the way it always was. "I was watching you. Halfway through the second course, you nearly did it. The same thing you did to the Keeper. I saw your face." She glanced sideways. "And then you didn't."

They walked a few more steps.

"The king has read about you," said Kestra.

Hanarisu stopped.

"About the Keeper," Kestra went on. "I wrote it down. That's my job. I write down what I see and I send it to him, and he reads every word." She turned to face Hanarisu in the corridor. "He sent it to the Advisor with a note. It said *watch her*."

"I didn't *mean* to," said Hanarisu, very quietly. "With the Keeper. I didn't know I could, until I had."

"I know. That's what worries them." Kestra studied her for a long moment. "A charm that works at the bottom of the ladder will be a dangerous thing at the top. That's what I wrote. I meant it."

"Then write this down too," said Hanarisu.

Kestra raised an eyebrow.

"I won't use it on anyone who can't tell it's happening." Hanarisu made herself say it plainly, so there would be no mistaking it. "Not to win an argument. Not to get into an archive. Not on a man like Vane, even when he deserves it. If I ever use it, they'll know. Or I won't use it at all."

The corridor was very quiet.

Then Kestra took out her little book, and her pencil, and wrote something down in it, slowly, without hiding it. And then, for the first time Hanarisu had ever seen, she turned the book around and held it out.

There was one line on the page, in Kestra's blunt square hand.

*She drew her own line tonight.*

"I'll send that to the king," said Kestra. She closed the book. "He'll want to know."

***

Jared and Cursiffe were waiting for her in the sitting room.

She knew at once that something was wrong. They were sitting too far apart, on opposite sides of the fire, the way people sit when they've been arguing about whether to say something. Milli had gone to bed. Aleaka had gone to bed, still mortified, with all six cats. Oswin had gone back to his archive. It was just the three of them, and the fire, and a small brown book on the table between them that she recognised.

"We need to show you something," said Jared.

They showed her.

She read the passage about the bait twice. Then she read the forty pages Cursiffe had marked before it, slowly, the way she read everything, in silence, while the two of them watched her face and she didn't let them see anything at all.

*I have never once, in all my life, lost a fish.*

*Trust.*

"Who else knows?" she said, when she'd finished.

"Nobody," said Cursiffe. "Just us. Since the third week."

"You've been sitting on this for two months."

"We didn't know what it meant," said Jared. "We still don't. He's dead, Alice. We all know he's dead. We were all there when the news—" He stopped. "It's a coincidence. It has to be."

"Does it?" said Hanarisu.

She set the book down on the table and looked at the fire.

And she put it together, piece by piece, the way she'd been putting things together all evening at Vane's table, in silence, behind a pleasant face.

The first window. Thea's letter, last week: *every window inherits from a first window. Somebody had the first one.* The Advisor in the hall: *it started with a single man.* Status, inventory, skills, spoken in an accent, in English, in a kingdom that had never heard English: *the Hero's words.* The man who'd had the first window had named things in English.

Which meant he'd known English.

Which meant he'd come from where they came from.

*Someone from Earth was here before us.*

She held very still with that for a moment, and let it settle.

Then she followed it one step further, the step Jared and Cursiffe hadn't let themselves take, and found the wall at the end of it.

The Hero of the Redmarch had broken an army seven years ago. He'd had the first window before that, long before; the Advisor had been learning from him for years. Whoever he was, he'd been in this world for decades. And the friend who'd finished his jokes with *trust* had died in his sleep in Malaysia three years ago. They'd all been there for that. They'd all been at the edge of it, crying in calls, reading the messages.

It didn't fit. It couldn't. The numbers were wrong.

"It's a coincidence," she said.

Jared let out a breath. Cursiffe didn't move.

"Probably," Hanarisu went on, quietly. "Somebody from our world came here, a long time ago, before any of us. Somebody who spoke English. Somebody who gamed, maybe, or knew people who did. Maybe he picked up the same habits our friend had. Lots of people say *trust*." She looked up at them. "That's what I think. But I don't know. And until we know, we don't tell anyone."

"Not even the others?" said Jared.

"Not the other groups. Not yet." She hesitated. "And not Aleaka."

Jared started to argue. She saw him start. Then she saw him stop, and remember the same thing she was remembering: the months after, when Aleaka hadn't come to calls, and then had, and had been so quiet. The way she'd come back to them slowly, an inch at a time.

"She's only just got her feet under her," said Hanarisu softly. "Here. In all of this. I won't hand her a hope I can't keep."

Jared looked at the fire for a long time.

"Okay," he said finally. "Okay. But if we find out—"

"If we find out, she's the first to know. I promise."

Cursiffe picked up the little brown book, and held it for a moment, and then put it inside his coat, close against his chest, as if it were something fragile.

"I'll keep reading," he said. "If there's anything else in there, I'll find it."

***

She lay awake for a long time that night, with Milli asleep beside her and his tail across her ankle.

*Someone from Earth was here first.*

She hadn't said that part out loud. Not to Jared or Cursiffe, not to anyone. She'd kept it behind her pleasant face, the way she kept most things, until she knew what it was worth.

But she turned it over in the dark, again and again, like a coin. A man from their world, alone in this one, decades ago. Learning the language. Teaching the Advisor to see his window. Naming things in English because there was nobody to tell him the words. Breaking an army. And then going away somewhere, quietly, so completely that the king and the Advisor wouldn't say where.

*He has given enough.*

She thought: *I want to meet him.*

She thought: *I want to ask him how he stood it. Being the only one.*

And then, very quietly, in the part of her that had cried in a call three years ago and never quite stopped, she thought about a voice that had always made her laugh, and how much she would have liked to hear it say *trust* one more time, even if it was lying.

Especially if it was lying.

She closed her eyes, and kept her face pleasant even in the dark, out of habit, and eventually she slept.
