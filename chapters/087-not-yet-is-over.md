He felt it from the edge of the square, the way he'd felt it from the end of his jetty a year ago. Only closer.

A thread. Thin and bright and steady, going out of her open hands into the heart of the fire. Not pushed. *Given*. The way you'd hand someone a cup. And the fire taking it, and turning white, and every candle in Wrenhaven saying it back.

He'd felt Thea reach the first tier from across a frozen lake, last midwinter, mending a pike-torn net. He'd felt her candle catch, and sat there in the snow with the net in his hands, and thought about his own first tier, at fourteen, in a field behind the house he grew up in. He hadn't gone to the fire. He hadn't gone for eight years.

This year he'd gone. He'd stood at the edge of the light with Alea in front of him and watched her do it, three paces from Maren, with her hands up and her lips moving. He'd heard the two words. Everybody near the fire had. Nobody else had understood them.

*Still here.*

He'd felt the lamp in his own window flare with all the rest. All the way down the lake road. He'd felt it go, in his chest, like a hand closing.

***

Alea didn't say anything on the way home.

She held his hand down the lake road in the dark, the way she hadn't since she was very small, and he let her, and neither of them said a word. At the cabin door she stopped, and looked at him in the light from the window, the lamp that had flared, and squeezed his hand once, hard. Then she went in and up the stairs and he heard her door close.

He went down to the jetty.

It was cold. The coldest night of the year, the way midwinter always was. The lake was white from shore to shore, frozen deep, and the snow on the jetty boards was crisp underfoot, and the stars over the hills were very bright and very many and very wrong, doubled in nothing now, because the water was ice. He sat down on the end of the jetty in the snow with his rod beside him and his feet over the frozen lake.

He didn't fish. You couldn't, on ice, not from the jetty. He'd brought the rod anyway. He always did.

Up the hill, above the town, five candles. Awake. Close together, at the upper window. One of them brighter than she'd been an hour ago. Much brighter. A candle, now, in the window's own word. He could feel her from here the way you'd feel a fire across a room.

He opened his window.

***

The channel was going mad.

Of course it was. Rule three. Good news, loudly. Higu had written it, in the end, twenty minutes after the fire, while the town was still roaring in the square:

[ 23:48 · Higu: Thea reached Candle. Tonight. At a bonfire, again, a year to the night.
23:48 · Higu: Tier 2. The first of us.
23:49 · Higu: Rule three. ]

And then everyone. All thirty-four of them, it felt like, at once, the two hundred lines filling up and pushing up and up. Maigo in capitals so large they were nearly illegible. Mashi: *of course it's thea*. Chris, with numbers. Dennis: *second tier before any of us has finished the first one, this is a scandal*. Rachael. Tiger. Hana. Yan, slowly: *Thea. That's wonderful.* Xav: *gg.* Gabe: *THEA*. Kei: *Hisui says congratulations, and that you're showing off.* Wendy, who'd been Drawn for a month: *I'm so happy I could shoot something.* Newish: *what's your skill what's your skill WHAT'S YOUR SKILL*.

And Thea, at last, one line:

[ 23:57 · Althea: Patch. It's called Patch. I can fix small things now. ]

He read it, and something in his chest went tight.

*Patch.* Of course it was. Of course that was what the window would give her, after a year of building a channel into thirty-five windows with her bare hands and Higu holding the lamp. After a year of being the one who fixed things. After three weeks of looking at two hundred lines in a writing that wasn't hers and knowing, he thought, exactly whose it was.

She hadn't written *still here* in the channel. She hadn't written where. She hadn't written who'd been standing at the edge of the light. None of the five had. Rule five.

He sat there and read the channel go past, everyone shouting, and the five of them in Wrenhaven, after Higu's three lines and Thea's one, saying nothing else at all.

Watching it. The way he'd watched them watch it for a year.

***

He went through the reasons.

He did it properly. Slowly. The way he'd go through a net for tears in the spring, a hand's width at a time, not skipping any. He'd been giving himself reasons for sixteen months, and he'd never once laid them all out in a row and looked at them. He did it now, on the end of the jetty, in the snow.

*Kharvos.* They hunted the summoned. They'd sent a hunter to the orchard above this town, and he'd cut it in two before it could go home and tell anyone where he was. They'd sent fifty scouts and three hunters across the red for his sword. If he went up that hill, if anyone knew the Hero of the Redmarch sat down to supper with five of the summoned, they'd be in more danger, not less.

He looked at that one for a long time.

They were already in danger. They'd been in danger since the hall. Kharvos hadn't sent a hunter to Wrenhaven because of him; it had sent one because of *them*, and found him in the way. Nobody needed to know about a supper. Five people walking down a lake road at dusk to a fisherman's cabin weren't a target. They were a town where people walked to supper.

That one wasn't true anymore. He wasn't sure it had ever been.

***

*Halvard. One target.* Thirty-five in one place. He'd agreed, in the summer. He still agreed.

Five people at a table wasn't thirty-five. And the king had overruled Halvard anyway; he'd read it in the channel, in Chris's lines: *the tournament of all thirty-five at midsummer. By midsummer they'll be harder to kill.* Severin had seen something that made him believe it. Ars knew exactly what he'd seen.

That one wasn't true anymore either.

***

*Let them grow.* That had been the real one, at the start. That if he walked up the hill in the first month and said *it's me*, they'd lean on him. Look to him. Wait for him to fix everything, the way they'd have done on a call, when he was the funny one who always knew what to do. That they'd stop climbing on their own. That he'd be the thing standing between them and the world, instead of them learning to stand in front of it themselves.

He thought about Thea, an hour ago, with her hands up in front of a bonfire, giving a thread of herself to the fire and every candle in town answering.

About PenguChi, at Drawn, the ninth, cracking a practice post with a stroke she'd learned to wait for.

About Higu's moth, and Xola's turning water, and Gigichi's hedge that drew itself together on a word.

About Yan, on the Redmarch, calling it like Apex with no weapon at all. About Salty standing in the middle of fifty of them holding a sword he couldn't swing. About Wendy's arrow. About Dennis making a hunter see a girl at a window. About RX, unseen, crying on a wall.

They'd grown. All of them. Without him. Without leaning on anyone. Thirteen past the first tier. One at the second. They'd built a channel across a kingdom and written a book about him and carried a sword across the red and a stone after it, and every single thing they'd done, they'd done standing on their own feet.

They didn't need him to stand up.

That one wasn't true anymore.

***

*Telling them would put a weight on them.* That if they knew it was him, they'd have to carry it. Keep it secret. Lie for him. Not tell the others.

They'd been carrying it for six months. He'd watched them. Rule five, rule six, the night of the sum when they wrote nothing at all. He'd been watching them carry it for him for six months without being asked, and without ever once putting it down.

That one had never been true.

***

He sat on the end of the jetty in the snow and looked at the row of reasons he'd laid out, and found that there was only one left.

The one he'd told Alea three weeks ago, on this jetty, with the snow coming down. The one he'd never said out loud before in his life.

*I'm frightened.*

Of their faces.

Of opening the door, and seeing five people from a stream half a world away, who'd known a funny boy who gifted them subs and modded their chat and narrated his own deaths in Apex. Who'd laughed at everything. And watching them look at a man of sixty in a body of thirty-five, who barely smiled, who'd killed more things than he could count, who'd cut the woman he loved in two on a battlefield and planted a sword over her. Watching their faces try to find the boy in him. And not find him.

He'd been frightened of that for sixteen months.

And tonight, at the edge of the firelight, he'd stood there with Alea in front of him and watched their faces. All five. When they turned and saw him.

He'd seen Xola's face. And Gigichi's, with her shoulders going up. And PenguChi's, slowly. And Higu's, who'd already known. And Thea's, last, at the fire.

None of them had looked for the boy.

They'd just looked at *him*. At what he was now. A plain man in a plain coat at the edge of a bonfire. And their faces had done the thing faces did when somebody you'd been waiting for a very long time walked into the room.

And then Thea had turned round and given the fire a thread of herself and said *still here*.

***

He sat there for a long time.

Then the quiet came.

It came the way it had come on this jetty a hundred times in the last year. Like a cat that couldn't decide whether to stay. Settling, and then lifting, and then settling again. Only this time it didn't lift. It settled over the end of the jetty and the frozen lake and the snow and the man sitting in it, and stayed. Deep. Still. The quiet of a lake on a windless night, when you can't tell where the water starts.

He didn't need to look. He looked anyway.

[ Ars · Tier 8: Dominion · Progress to Ascendant: 52% ]

Fifty-two.

It hadn't moved for the fire. It hadn't moved for the stone, or the arrow, or the snow. It hadn't moved for Thea's candle, or Maren's face when he stopped beside her, or Alea's hand on the way home.

It had moved just now. Sitting here. For nothing. For deciding.

He closed the window and sat in the quiet, and laughed. Very quietly. At himself. Sixteen months. He'd climbed two tiers in one night on the Redmarch, once, with a sword and a rage. And it turned out that the hardest thing he'd ever done in either world was going to be walking a quarter of a mile up a hill to say *hi* to five people who'd been waiting for him at a window.

Or, he thought. Or not walking up the hill at all.

He thought about it. And smiled.

***

Alea came down at dawn with the basket.

He was on the step, waiting for her. He hadn't slept. He'd sat on the jetty until the cold got into his bones, and then come in and sat by the fire, and then come out onto the step when the sky started going grey, and waited.

She stopped in the doorway with the basket on her arm and her coat half on and looked at him. At his face. He didn't know what his face was doing. Something, probably. She'd always been able to read it.

"Dad?" she said.

He'd already put the fish in the basket. He'd done it an hour ago, in the dark, at the ice-hole off the end of the jetty, the way he'd done it every morning for a year. The best ones. Laid in the straw.

Four of them.

Alea looked down at the basket. She counted. He watched her count. Her lips moved.

"There's four," she said.

"Take them four fish," said Ars.

She looked up at him.

"Four?" she said. "It's been five since the autumn. Since PenguChi. It's always five, Dad, it's—"

"The fifth one's for supper," said Ars.

Alea stopped.

"Tonight," said Ars. His voice came out steadier than he'd thought it would. "Here. At the cabin. All five of them." He looked at her. "Tell them. Tell them..." He stopped. He found he didn't know what to tell them. He'd been trying to think of it all night, and he hadn't. "Tell them your dad says come for supper."

***

Alea dropped the basket.

Not on purpose. Her hands just opened. It fell on the step at her feet, and the lid came off, and four lake fish slid out onto the snow, silver in the grey light, and lay there. Neither of them looked at them.

She stood in the doorway with her coat half on and her hands still open in front of her, as if she were still holding the basket, and stared at him. Her ears had gone completely flat. Then completely straight up. Then flat again.

"Tonight," she said.

"Tonight."

"*Here*."

"Here."

"*All five.*"

"All five." He looked at the fish on the snow. "And you. And the hens, probably. I can't stop the hens."

Alea made a sound. He didn't know what kind of sound. It wasn't a word.

And then she was on the step, on her knees, in the snow, with her arms round his neck, the way she'd done the night he showed her *halfway*, and her face in his shoulder, and she was shaking all over, and he couldn't tell if she was laughing or crying and he didn't think she could either.

"*Finally*," she said, into his coat. "Dad. *Finally.*"

He put his arms round her and held on.

***

Then she sat up, and wiped her face, and looked at him, and looked past him into the cabin, and her expression changed completely.

"*Tonight?*" she said, in an entirely different voice.

"Yes."

"Dad. *Dad.* Look at the *house*."

He looked at the house. It was clean. It was always clean. He'd lived alone with a child for eight years and kept it clean the way he'd kept his sword clean, every day, without thinking about it.

"It's clean," he said.

"It's *not* clean, it's *fisherman* clean, there are *nets* on the *wall*, there's a *sword* by the *stairs*, there's *hen* on the *rug*—"

"That's Captain's rug."

"*Captain doesn't get a rug tonight.*" Alea was on her feet. She'd picked the fish up off the snow without looking at them and put them back in the basket and put the lid on. "I'm taking these up. I'm telling them. And then I'm coming straight back and we're cleaning *everything*." She pointed at him. "And you're not cooking that stew."

"I'm cooking that stew."

"Dad, it's *terrible*—"

"It's from home," said Ars.

Alea stopped.

She looked at him for a long moment, in the doorway, in the grey light, with the basket on her arm.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Cook the stew." Her ears went very slightly pink. "But you're doing the pumpkin properly. The way Mum did it. Not the way you do it."

"Yes, ma'am," said Ars.

She stared at him. And then she laughed, a short startled helpless laugh, the way she'd laughed at nine, the first winter after, the first time he'd ever said it to her.

Then she went. Up the lake road, at a run, in the snow, with the basket swinging and her coat flying out behind her and Astra bouncing on her back in Garrow's harness. He watched her go until she turned the corner at the top, past Rowan, who'd been standing there at the top of the road since dawn, the way he always was, and who turned his head to watch her go by at a dead run, and then turned it back, slowly, and looked down the lake road at the cabin.

Ars lifted one hand.

After a moment, at the top of the road, so did Rowan.

***

He spent the day getting ready.

Alea came back within the hour, out of breath, with her face blotched and her eyes enormous and the empty basket, and said only *they're coming*, and *Maren said go*, and *Bram cried into a bucket*, and then wouldn't say anything else because she had to sit down on the step for a while. Then she got up and started cleaning.

They cleaned everything. He'd thought it was clean. It wasn't, apparently. Alea took the nets down off the wall and folded them into the chest and swept behind it and found a hook he'd lost three years ago. She moved Astra from the foot of the stairs to the corner by the hearth, and then back, and then to the wall by the door, and then back to the foot of the stairs, and said *it looks wrong everywhere*, and he said *it looks like it lives here*, and she glared at him and left it where it was. She banished Captain from the rug. Captain went and sat in the hen-yard in the snow and looked at the cabin door with an expression of profound betrayal that Ars, for once, found he sympathised with.

He went to the store under the floor and brought up the best pumpkin. The big one, from the south bed, that he'd been saving. He did it Diwa's way, the way Alea wanted: halved and scooped and roasted in the hearth with honey and the dried red peppers and salt, until the skin went black and the inside went soft and sweet and the whole cabin smelled of it. Diwa had laughed at him the first winter for putting pepper on pumpkin. Then she'd eaten half of it. Then she'd made it every winter after, better than him, and he'd never once got it as good as she did. He didn't, today. It was close.

And the stew.

***

It was supposed to be assam pedas.

It never was. It had been, once, a long time ago, in a different life: a sour, hot, red fish stew from Malacca, that he'd eaten at a stall by the river every week for two years and finally, the year before he died, learned to make in his own kitchen with his own bad kettle on the side. Tamarind and chilli and a dozen things he'd never be able to find in this world. Torch ginger. Laksa leaf. Belacan. Things that didn't exist here.

He'd been trying to make it for thirty years anyway.

Every winter, at least once. With whatever he could find. Sour plums dried in the autumn, for the tamarind. Wild red peppers from the south hedge. A bitter root from the far shore that was almost, if you didn't think about it too hard, like galangal. Lake fish instead of sea fish. Thirty years of getting it wrong, a little less wrong every winter, and eating it alone at the table after Alea had gone to bed, because she said it tasted like a fire in a swamp.

He made it now. In the big pot, with the fifth fish, the best of the morning, cut into pieces. The sour plums. The peppers. The root from the far shore. He stood over it at the hearth for an hour, tasting, and adding, and tasting again, while Alea swept round him and said nothing about the smell.

It was wrong. It was always wrong.

It was the closest he'd ever got.

***

And at some point in the afternoon, while Alea was upstairs and the stew was on the fire and the pumpkin was in the hearth, he went out into the hen-yard.

He didn't decide to. He just found himself there, standing in the snow, in front of three hens, who looked up at him with the cold, total contempt they'd shown him every single day for five years.

He cleared his throat.

"Thea," he said.

In English. Out loud. The first time he'd said her name out loud in this world. He'd spoken English here perhaps a dozen times in thirty years: to Diwa, teaching her the letters; to Alea, at bedtime, the names of his friends; once to the lake, sixteen months ago, the night of the summoning. *What did you do, Halvard.*

The hens stared at him.

"Xola," he said. "Higu." It was coming easier. He could hear his voice doing it. Not his old voice; that was gone, in a body that had been born in a commoner's cottage on the other side of the world from it. But the shape of the names was the same. The shape of his mouth round them. "Chi..." He stopped. "Chi 1. Chi 2."

Captain cocked her head.

"It's me," said Ars, to the hens. "It's..."

He stopped.

He tried again. "It's me. It's..."

It wouldn't come. He stood in the snow in front of three hens and opened his mouth, and the word was right there, the one he'd been for twenty-six years and not been for thirty-four, and it wouldn't come out. It just sat in his throat like a stone.

"Hi," he said instead.

That one came. Easily. As if it had been waiting.

Behind him, from the cabin door, Alea said, "Dad."

He didn't turn round.

"Are you rehearsing?" said Alea.

"No."

"You're rehearsing. To the *hens*."

He looked at the hens. The hens looked at him.

"Yes," said Ars.

***

Alea didn't laugh. He'd thought she would.

She came down off the step instead, and across the snow, and stood beside him in front of the hen-yard, and looked at the hens too. Then she took his hand, the way she had the night before, coming home from the fire.

"You don't need to rehearse," she said. "You just need to open the door."

"I know."

"They'll do the rest."

"I know."

She looked at him sideways.

"Are you frightened?"

He thought about it. Honestly. The way she deserved.

"Yes," he said.

"Good," said Alea. And squeezed his hand. "That means it matters."

***

At the end of the afternoon, when the light was going and the stew was done and the pumpkin was done and the table was set, he lit the lamp in the window.

He did it himself. Every night for eight years, he'd lit that lamp at dusk and put it in the window, the one the five of them watched from the hill. He'd never once thought about it. Tonight he stood in front of it with the taper in his hand for a long time before he touched it to the wick.

The table was set for seven. Alea had counted twice. Seven bowls, seven cups, seven spoons, on a table that groaned. Seven, because Alea had said, very firmly, *and Waddles and Squiddles have to have something, Dad, they're people*.

He sat down on the step, outside, in the cold, in the last of the light, with his hands between his knees, and looked up the lake road.

His hands were shaking.

He looked at them. He'd walked across the Redmarch alone with a sword on his shoulder the morning after the worst night of his life. He'd cut three hunters in half without looking round. He'd held a chisel in his bare hands while a king brought down a hammer with the whole of the ninth tier in it. His hands had never once shaken. Not in thirty-four years.

They were shaking now.

He let them.

At the top of the lake road, far off, in the blue dusk, against the snow, five small lights appeared. Lanterns. Coming round the corner past the place where Rowan always stood.

Rowan wasn't standing there.

He'd stepped aside.
