Alea hated staying at Rowan's.

She'd hated it since she was nine. Every year, a few days before the anniversary, her father packed his bag and picked up his rod and walked east, and every year she came up the lake road with her own bag and knocked on the mayor's door, and Odette opened it and said *there you are, love*, as if Alea had been lost, and took her up to the spare room with the lace on everything. It had a lace bedspread. Lace on the pillows, and the curtains, and a little lace mat under the jug on the washstand. When Alea was nine, she'd lain awake in it every night of the fortnight, staring at the lace, waiting to hear her father's boots on the road.

She was sixteen now. She was *Tempered*. She could pick up the water butt behind the militia hall on her own, full, which Bram said was showing off and Maren said was useful. She could have stayed in the cabin by herself perfectly well, and fed the hens, and not died.

"Your father asked," said Odette, the way she said it every year.

"I know he asked."

"Then that's that." Odette put a hot water bottle in the lace bed, though it was the last week of summer and nobody needed a hot water bottle. "He's never once asked me for anything else, Alea. Not in seven years. Not in eight, now." She smoothed the bedspread. "He can have this."

So Alea stayed. She lay in the lace bed at night with the window open and Rowan snoring through the wall like a sawmill, and stared at the ceiling, and thought about her father walking across the Redmarch toward a grave with a hole in it. And a sword in a chapel, waiting to be carried home.

She thought about it every night. She didn't sleep much.

***

She went up to the militia hall every day anyway. She always did. It helped.

Maren made her carry things. That was the main difference, since she'd gone up a tier: Maren had noticed, about four minutes after Alea opened her window in the yard, that the second tier hardened the body, and had immediately found a great many heavy things around the hall that needed to be somewhere else. Barrels. Sacks of grain for the stables. The old rack of practice spears that nobody had moved in ten years. Alea moved all of it. It was very boring, and it helped too.

Group 1 didn't say anything about her father being away. They hadn't said anything all week.

They knew. Alea knew they knew. They'd stood at the gate of the militia hall a week ago and watched him walk past, and he'd looked at each of them, one after another, the way he had on the very first day on the market road, and Thea had nodded to him, and he'd nodded back. And then Xola had asked *he's coming back, isn't he?*, and Alea had said *he's coming back*, and nobody had said anything else.

Nobody had said anything since. It was the strangest, kindest thing. Five people who knew exactly who her father was, and exactly where he'd gone, and why, and who looked at her every morning across the yard with their faces full of it, and didn't say one word.

She'd never loved anybody so much in her life, and been so annoyed with them at the same time.

***

On the morning after the anniversary, they were all different.

Alea saw it the moment she came in through the gate. They were in the yard already, the five of them, and none of them were training. They were standing together by the water butt in a little knot, with their heads close, the way they stood when something had happened in the channel she couldn't see. Thea's eyes were red. Xola was holding Squiddles very tightly against her chest. Gigichi had her arms folded so hard her knuckles were white. PenguChi was sitting on the edge of the trough with her hands over her face. And Higu was writing something in her notebook, very fast, and didn't look up.

They all went quiet when Alea came in. Then they all looked away from her at once. Very carefully. The way you look away from someone you're trying not to look at.

Alea's heart stopped.

She stood in the gateway with her bag on her shoulder and couldn't move. She thought: *the Redmarch*. She thought: *the scouts. The twenty-five scouts Yan wrote about. Fifty, maybe. A hundred.* She thought: *he's on his own out there, he's always on his own out there*—

"Alea," said Maren, from the door of the hall. "The spears. Please."

She didn't move.

And Higu, at the water butt, without looking up from her notebook, without looking at Alea at all, said to nobody in particular, in her small clear voice:

"Group 3 are all right."

The yard went very still.

"They were attacked on the Redmarch," said Higu. Still writing. Still not looking up. "Carrying a sword. A lot of them. More than they could fight." Her pencil moved across the page. "Somebody came for them. Nobody died." She turned the page over. "He took it home."

That was all. She didn't say anything else. She didn't look up. She went on writing in her notebook as if she'd been talking about the weather, and the other four went on not looking at Alea, very hard, all at once.

Alea stood in the gateway.

She found that she had put her hand over her mouth. She took it away again.

"Thank you," she said. To nobody in particular.

Then she went and moved the spears. All of them, the whole rack, across the yard and back again, twice, because her hands were shaking and she needed something to do with them. And nobody said a word about it. And when she came back past the water butt the second time, PenguChi, still sitting on the edge of the trough, reached out without looking and squeezed her wrist, once, and let go.

***

He came home two days later, at dusk.

Alea was at Rowan's, at supper, picking at Odette's stew. She'd been picking at it for half an hour. Rowan was talking about the harvest, and the price of barley, and the new well in the east quarter, the way he talked at supper, in his big slow voice, filling the room. Alea wasn't listening. She was looking out of the window at the market square, the way she'd looked out of it every evening for a week.

So she saw it. The way the square changed.

It was nothing, at first. Just people stopping. The baker, closing his shutters, stopped with one shutter in his hand. Two women at the well stopped talking. A boy with a hoop stopped running, and the hoop rolled away on its own across the cobbles and fell over. And all of them, one after another, were turning their heads the same way, toward the east road, where it came into the square past the church.

Alea was out of her chair before she knew she'd moved.

"Alea—" said Odette.

She didn't hear the rest. She was through the door and into the square, and there he was.

***

He came into the square from the east road at a walk, in the last of the light, the way he always came home. A plain man in a plain coat, with his pack on his back and his rod in his hand, dusty to the knees. Just as he'd left. Just as he came back every year.

Except that this year he had something on his shoulder.

It was long. That was the first thing. Longer than he was tall. A long bundle wrapped in grey canvas, old sailcloth by the look of it, tied round at intervals with twine. He carried it on his left shoulder, the way you'd carry a plank, or a ladder, or a bundle of sticks for the stove, with his left hand resting easily on the front end of it and the back end going away behind him over the cobbles. It didn't look heavy. It looked as if it weighed nothing at all.

Every single person in the square was staring at it.

He didn't seem to notice. He walked across the square at the same easy pace, nodding to people as he passed, the way he always did. He nodded to the baker. The baker, still holding his shutter, nodded back slowly, staring at the bundle.

"That's a big fish, Ars," said the baker.

"It is," said her father. "Evening, Edric."

He saw Alea standing in front of Rowan's door, and he stopped.

They looked at each other across the square. She didn't run to him. She wanted to. She stood there with her hands in fists at her sides and looked at him. She couldn't hold his face; nobody could. She didn't need to. He was tired, and dusty, and older than he'd been a week ago, and something else that she couldn't name. Lighter, maybe. As if he'd put something down.

He nodded at her. The tiniest nod.

And then he turned and went on, down the lake road, toward the cabin. And she went after him. Not running. Walking. Two paces behind, the way she'd walked behind him when she was small. All the way down the lake road in the dusk, with half of Wrenhaven watching from doorways, and neither of them saying anything at all.

***

The hens went for him at the gate.

They always did. They'd hated him since the day he bought them; Alea had told him once, when she was eleven, that animals could tell, and he'd said *tell what?*, and she'd said *you know what*, and he'd never asked again. Three of them came across the yard at him in a line, with their necks out and their wings half up, and the big red one went for his boot, the way she did every single evening, and he stepped over her, the way he did every single evening, and she looked furious about it.

Then the big red one saw the bundle, and stopped. And looked at it. And backed away, slowly, and went and stood in the corner of the yard, and didn't come out again.

Alea had never seen her do that. Not once, in five years.

He went in. She went in after him and shut the door.

***

He tried to put it on the table first. The table made a noise Alea had never heard a table make, a long low groan from somewhere deep in its joints, and he took it off again at once, very quickly, and laid it on the floor instead, in front of the hearth, on the rag rug. Then he knelt down beside it, and took out his knife, and began to cut the twine.

Alea knelt down on the other side.

He did it slowly. One knot at a time. And then he took hold of the edge of the grey canvas and folded it back, the whole length of it, the way you'd fold back a blanket from someone sleeping.

It was the sword.

***

She'd known it would be. Of course she had. She'd known since the moment she saw the shape of it on his shoulder in the square. She'd known for three years, since she was thirteen and he'd first put a practice sword in her hands on the ice and said *when you reach the second tier*. She'd known all her life, really.

It still took her breath away.

It lay on the rag rug in front of the hearth, black, enormous, as long as her father was tall and as broad across as her own shoulders. The firelight didn't shine on it. It wasn't the kind of iron that shone. It lay there and drank the light, the way it always had, and the edges were notched in a dozen places, deep notches, and the grip was wrapped in old dark leather gone smooth with his hands, and the pommel at the end was a plain iron fist.

She remembered it.

She hadn't thought she would. She'd been so small. But she remembered it now, all at once, looking at it, the way you remember a smell. It had stood by the door. Here, in this cabin, when she was tiny, before the war, before everything. It had leaned against the wall beside the door, the way other families had a broom or an umbrella there, and she'd pulled herself up on it. On the flat of the blade. She'd learned to stand holding on to it, before she could walk.

She remembered her mother shrieking. Actually *shrieking*, from across the room, and running. And her father laughing, the way he'd laughed back then, which wasn't the way he laughed now. *It's the safest thing in the house. Nobody's ever going to steal it. Look at her. She's strong already.*

Alea put her hand on the flat of the blade. It was cold. It was always cold.

She found she couldn't see very well.

***

The letters were still there. She found them with her fingers before she found them with her eyes: up near the grip, just above where the leather started, scratched into the black iron. Rough. Crooked. Some deeper than others. One gone over twice.

*AD ASTRA PER ASPERA.*

She'd never seen them. She'd known they were there; he'd told her, the year she was thirteen, when he told her everything. About Earth, and Ice, and his friends, and the words he used to put on everything. He'd told her he'd scratched them on the sword the morning after, kneeling by the grave, with the point of his knife, before he planted it. *To the stars, through hardships.* She'd asked him why, and he'd said he didn't know. He'd said it was the only thing he could think of that wasn't *sorry*.

She traced them with her fingertip, one letter at a time, the way you'd trace a name on a stone.

"Go on," said her father quietly. "Pick it up."

***

She looked at him.

"Go on," he said again. "You're Tempered. Try."

So she stood up, and bent down, and got both hands round the grip, the way he'd taught her to hold a sword since she was thirteen, right hand high, left hand low. The leather was warm from the fire, and smooth, and much too thick for her hands. She had to stretch her fingers to close them. She let the aura come up the way he'd taught her, the way she'd taught PenguChi. Not pushing. Rising, like water in a jar. Down through her back foot so it had somewhere to stand. Out on the breath.

She felt it go into her bones. That was the second tier. That was what Tempered meant: the aura going in, not out, into the body itself, hardening it, the way a smith quenches iron. She felt her arms go hard, and her back, and her legs. She felt the grip in her hands like part of her.

And she lifted.

The grip end came up off the rug. The pommel. Then a hand's width of the blade. Then more. Up to her knees. Up to her waist, with her teeth clenched and the aura roaring in her and her arms shaking, and the point of the sword still on the floor, scraping on the hearthstones, as if it had no intention of ever leaving them.

And there it stopped.

She couldn't get it higher. She couldn't swing it. She could barely hold it where it was. It was like trying to lift one end of the cabin. She stood there in front of the fire with her father's sword held at her waist by the grip, and the point on the floor, and felt the weight of it pulling at her shoulders, *pulling*, as if it wanted her on the floor too.

She put it down. Carefully. The way he'd taught her to put down anything heavy.

"I can't," she said.

"I know."

"It's too *big*. It's—Dad, it's too big. It's not even close."

"It was made for a grown man," said her father. He was still kneeling on the other side of it, with his forearms on his knees, watching her. "For a grown man at Shaped, in the middle of a war. Angry." He looked down at it. "It was made for the worst night of my life. It was never going to fit you."

Alea stood there breathing hard, looking at it.

Then she remembered what he'd said, a week ago, in the yard. With Group 1 watching. *You'll need a sword. Half of it, maybe.*

"Half of it," she said slowly.

"Half of it."

***

He told her. Sitting on the floor in front of the fire, on either side of the sword, with the lamp unlit and the room going dark around them.

Half for her. Cut and reforged into a sword her own size. Still black. Still that iron. Still a buster sword, a slab of a thing, because that was what the second tier was *for*, and because she was his daughter and she'd never once in her life wanted anything small. Something she could swing on the ice, and grow into, and carry for the rest of her life.

"And the other half?" said Alea.

"Your mother."

She looked at him.

"A stone," said her father. "For her grave. A proper one. Not a hole in the ground where something used to stand." He put his hand on the flat of the blade. "Half of it goes back to her. It was hers for eight years. It should stay hers."

Alea didn't say anything for a while.

"Who's going to cut it?" she said at last. "You?"

"I could cut it." He said it quite simply. He wasn't boasting. He never boasted. He could cut anything; she knew that. "I couldn't make it into anything afterwards. I'd ruin it. It's old iron. It's strange iron. It needs to be cut by a smith who understands it, and heated, and worked, and folded, for days." He looked at the black blade in the firelight. "It needs a smith who knows iron like that."

"Who knows iron like that?"

"The best I know," said her father.

He got up off the floor, and went to the shelf by the window, and got down paper, and the ink, and the pen with the split nib he'd been meaning to fix for three years. And he sat down at the table, which groaned at him again out of habit, and lit the lamp, and began to write.

Alea came and read over his shoulder. She always did. He always let her.

*To Severin*, it said, at the top.

She stared at it.

"Dad," she said. "That's the *king*."

"He was a smith before he was a king."

"He's the *king*."

"He was a smith first. He's never forgiven anybody for it." Her father dipped the pen. "He watched me use this sword once. Through a spyglass, from the wall at Cinderhold, the morning after. Afterwards he told me exactly who should have forged it, and how, and what they'd done wrong." He wrote a line. "Then he told me it was good iron anyway. He'll want to see it again. He'll pretend he doesn't. He'll come."

"The king is going to *come here*?"

"I'm asking him to."

"To *Wrenhaven*?"

"To the cabin," said her father. "The forge in town's too small. We'll have to build one by the water." He wrote another line, and blotted it. "Odette's going to have a great deal to say."

Alea sat down very slowly on the end of the bench.

***

He wrote for a long time. She didn't read the rest. She sat on the end of the bench and looked at the black sword on the rug in front of the fire, and at her father's back, bent over the paper in the lamplight, and listened to the pen scratch.

Then he stopped writing. And sat there with the pen in his hand, not moving.

"Alea," he said.

"Mm."

"Someone planted a tree for your mother."

***

She didn't understand him at first.

"What?"

"At her grave." He put the pen down. He didn't turn round. "Inside the ring. Beside the cairn. A tree. A young one. It's taller than..." He stopped. "It's taller than the person who planted it. It's been there since the spring. The flowers have been out all summer, because of it. They've never been out all summer. Not once, in eight years."

Alea stared at his back.

"Who?"

He turned round on the bench, then. And looked at her. And something happened to his face that she'd only seen happen to it once or twice in her whole life. It went young.

"Maigo," he said.

She knew the name. Of course she knew it. She'd known it since she was thirteen and he'd lain on the end of her bed in the dark and told her stories about his friends, one after another, every night for a whole winter, until she knew all thirty-five of them by heart. The little tree one. The cocky one. The one with the sunglasses he wouldn't take off. The one who'd got four kills in a pistol round once, in a game she'd never seen, and her father had screamed so loudly that—

"Maigotron?" said Alea.

Her father's mouth twitched.

"Maigotron," he said.

***

She cried.

She hadn't meant to. She'd been holding it all week, in the lace bed, in the yard, moving spears back and forth across the yard with her hands shaking. She'd held it in the square, and on the lake road, and in front of the sword. She couldn't hold it anymore. It came out of her all at once, the way it had the first winter after, when she was nine, on the end of the jetty: great ugly gulping sobs that went on and on and didn't stop.

He came and sat beside her on the bench and put his arm round her, and didn't say anything, and let her.

"He planted Mum a *tree*," said Alea, into his coat.

"In the spring."

"*Why?*"

"He said he felt something under the ground. Holding on to her. And he thought it must be lonely, out there, holding on on its own." Her father's voice was very quiet. "So he brought her a tree. So she wouldn't be alone out there." He paused. "He asked me if that was okay."

Alea cried harder.

"What did you say?"

"I said thank you." He was quiet for a moment. "It was all I could say."

***

He told her the rest, after. When she could listen.

About the Redmarch. About fifty scouts and three of the big ones, and the escort that couldn't hold them, and a young soldier called Dace and an old sergeant called Teague and a chaplain who'd burned himself out holding a ring of fire. About a short, square, enormous man with two broken ribs who'd carried the sword half a day and wouldn't let go of it. Who'd stood in the middle of fifty demons holding it, unable to swing it, and said *come on, then*.

"Salty," said Alea, wiping her face.

"Salty."

"He wouldn't let go?"

"I had to ask him." Her father looked at the sword on the rug. "He'd been holding on so long his hands had forgotten how to stop. I had to tell them they could." He was quiet for a moment. "Then he asked me who she was."

Alea went still.

"What did you say?"

"Her name," said her father. "And what she was."

Alea looked at him for a long time. He didn't look away.

"And then," she said slowly, "at the end. Did they ask..."

"Salty asked if I was him."

Her breath stopped. "And?"

"I told them to go home safely," said her father. "I told them their friends would want to know they were all right."

Alea stared at him.

"Dad," she said.

"I know."

"That's the *worst* answer you've ever given anyone. In your *life*."

"I know."

"You told *Salty* to *go home safely*? After he carried Mum's sword half a day with *broken ribs*?"

"It was the only answer I had." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I stood there. And they were all looking at me. All five of them. And Yan..." He stopped. "Yan saw. I felt him see. All the way down. And Maigo had his sunglasses on his head, the same ones, the *exact* same ones. And I opened my mouth, and the only thing in it was *Maigotron*, and if I'd said it, I'd never have got to the end of the sentence." He let his hands drop. "So I told them to go home safely."

Alea didn't say anything.

"And they did," said her father. "And they didn't post it."

She looked at him.

"In the channel," he said. "The next morning. They told everyone. The ambush, and the Hero, and the sword, and her name. All of it. And everyone asked, *was it him*, thirty of them, over and over." He shook his head slowly. "And Group 3 wrote *he didn't say*. Every one of them. Even Maigo. Maigo, in lowercase." He was quiet for a moment. "I've never seen Maigo write anything in lowercase in my life."

Alea wiped her face again with the heel of her hand.

"They're like you," she said.

"No." Her father looked at the fire. "They're better than me. They always were."

***

They sat there for a long time after that, on the bench, with his arm round her, looking at the sword.

"Did you tell Mum?" said Alea at last. Quietly. "At the grave. About all of it. About them."

"I tell her everything."

"What did she say?"

He thought about it, honestly, the way he always did when Alea asked him something that mattered.

"She laughed at me," he said.

"Why?"

"Because I told her I'd been a coward for a year." He smiled, very slightly, at the fire. "She'd have laughed at that. She always did, when I was being stupid about something. So I laughed for her."

Alea leaned her head on his shoulder.

"You're not a coward," she said.

"I'm a bit of a coward."

"You're a *bit* of a coward," Alea agreed.

***

He finished the letter after she'd gone up. She heard him through the floor, the pen scratching, stopping, scratching again. Then the sound of the sealing wax. Then nothing, for a long time.

She couldn't sleep. Of course she couldn't. She lay in her own bed for the first time in a week, with no lace anywhere, and listened to the lake, and stared at the ceiling. And when the moon came round to the window, she got up and went down the stairs in her nightdress, very quietly, the way she had when she was small.

He was asleep in his chair by the hearth.

He hadn't gone to bed. He'd pulled the old armchair round to face the fire, and he was asleep in it, with his chin on his chest and his hands loose in his lap, and the black sword lying on the rag rug at his feet, in the last of the firelight, where he could reach it. The way a dog sleeps across a doorway.

The letter was on the table. Sealed. *Severin*, it said on the outside, in his square careful hand, and nothing else.

Alea stood at the bottom of the stairs and looked at him for a long time.

Then she went and knelt down on the rug beside the sword, and put her hand flat on the cold black iron, near the grip, over the crooked letters.

She didn't say anything out loud. She didn't need to.

Then she opened her window, the way she'd opened it in the yard a week ago in front of everyone. She still wasn't used to doing it where people could see.

[ Alea · Tier 2: Tempered · Progress to Honed: 3% ]

Three percent. It would take years. It had taken three years just to reach the first. She looked at the number for a while, and found she didn't mind at all.

She went back to bed. She slept.

***

In the morning there were five fish in the basket on the step. The best five. And beside the basket, the sealed letter.

"Rowan's own rider," said her father, from the jetty, without turning round. "Not the post. Tell him it's for the capital, and it goes into one pair of hands, and nobody else's."

"Whose hands?"

"He'll know."

Alea picked up the basket, and the letter, and put the letter inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it. And she stood on the step a moment longer and looked at him: a plain man on the end of a jetty, with a fishing rod, in the morning, looking out at the lake. Just as he always was.

Except that he was humming. Very quietly. A tune she didn't know, from somewhere she did.

She'd never heard him hum before.

She went up the lake road, toward the town, and the hill, and five people at a militia hall who'd look at her face and look away and not say anything. And she thought that maybe, today, she'd let them see.
