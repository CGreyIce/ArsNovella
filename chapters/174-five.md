Higu was awake when he knocked.

She was always awake at that hour; she'd kept the third watch since the road north from Valcrest, at the window of the room she shared with Thea in the militia hall, with the shutter open a crack and her counting book on her knee and three moths on the sill. She'd heard him come up the lake road, stop at the step where the basket was a moment longer than you'd stop to catch your breath, and go on, up through the town to Rowan's front door, the one nobody used. She'd sent a moth after him, and then called it back before he knocked. Some things weren't the channel's, and some things weren't hers.

She'd heard the knock anyway, three times, on the frost, and written it down. *Day one hundred and fifty-two. Half past eight. Three knocks*. And nothing else all night. The light in Rowan's kitchen window stayed on, very small at the top of the town, until the moon went down.

***

At the grey hour, she sent the moths out.

Not to Rowan's. She'd never look in there. Down the lake road. Over the hawthorn hedges and the wall and the reeds, white with frost, to the lake. The way she'd sent them every night for a year and a half, in the first winter, to a jetty where a man sat with a window open in front of his face, scrolling, reading the channel that he'd never once written in.

Three of them. One high. One low. One out over the water.

She sat at the window with her eyes shut and let them come back to her in pieces.

The lake. Grey, very still, with the mist lying on it in long white bands, the way it lay on the first mornings of the cold. The far shore just a darker grey. The reeds, stiff with frost.

The cabin. Smoke going up from the chimney, straight, thin. The hen-yard. Three hens on the woodpile in a row, puffed out against the cold, asleep, with an air of having won something.

The jetty.

Somebody was on the end of it.

***

It wasn't him.

Higu knew at once. Her moths knew everybody's shape by now; they'd been carrying shapes to her for two years. It wasn't the shape of a man sitting on the end of a jetty with a rod across his knees and his back very straight. It was smaller. Kneeling. On the boards at the very end, with something long held out over the water.

A rod. *His* rod. Held out over the water by somebody kneeling on the end of a jetty in the mist.

With a black sword lying on the boards behind her.

And on her hip, catching the grey light, the dark handle of a knife.

Higu sat very still at her window with her eyes shut.

She knew the shape. Of course she knew it. She'd watched it come up the lake road with two fish in a basket every morning for a year and a half. She'd watched it at the Lists from the fifth tier of the south stand, coming at a coast girl like a pike. She'd watched it at the long table in the Broken Lance in Valcrest, at the end of the last full day, take a moon chart out of her hands and fold it in four and put it inside its coat against its chest.

Alea. Home. On the end of the jetty, at dawn, on her first morning home, kneeling in the place where a man had sat every evening of her life, fishing with his rod.

Higu sent the low moth closer.

***

Alea wasn't fishing the way her father fished.

Higu had watched him a hundred times, through moths. He sat. He cast once, a long easy line out over the water, and the float sat on the lake, and he waited. For an hour, sometimes. Not moving. And then moved once.

Alea was kneeling. Not sitting. Up on her knees on the end boards, with the rod held out low over the water in both hands and her whole body leaning toward the line, very still, the way a cat goes still at the edge of a pond. Her ears were forward. All the way forward. Her tail was perfectly still behind her.

And the float wasn't on the water by the reeds, where her father always cast. It was out beyond the end of the jetty, in the deep water, under the mist. Where the lake went down to the dark. Where, Higu had heard Alea say once, laughing, at the long table, there was a big old pike that had gone through her father's good net in the spring and that he'd laughed at until he sat down.

*That was your mother*, he'd said. *That's exactly what your mother would have done*.

Alea was fishing for it.

Higu could see the line, through the low moth. Thin, and going down into the grey water off the end. And on the end of it, under the water, where the moth couldn't see, a hook. She knew it was a new hook. She'd read it in the channel, a week ago, in Maigo's capitals, because Maigo read everything in the channel back to the tree and the tree, Maigo said, had wanted everybody to know: *OAKES MADE ALEA A HOOK THAT WON'T BEND. FOR THE PIKE. THE NET ONE*.

The float went under.

***

Higu would remember it for a long time.

She'd never cared much about fishing. She'd never understood it, on Earth; it had seemed like the most boring thing a person could do, sitting by water waiting for nothing. She'd learned to understand it here, a little, watching a man on a jetty through a moth's eyes for a year and a half. But she'd never felt it.

She felt it now.

The float went under, and Alea didn't move. That was the first thing. Didn't strike. Didn't pull. Knelt there on the end of the jetty with the rod in both hands and her ears forward and let the float go down, and down, the line going with it, out into the deep water. Waiting. Higu could see her waiting. Not her father's waiting, the long still fisherman's waiting. Something else. Something with her whole body in it.

And then the line went slack.

All at once. The way it goes slack when whatever's on the end of it stops pulling away and turns, and comes back toward you, fast, faster than you can reel in.

The cow pike. Coming straight at the jetty.

Higu's moth saw it. Under the water, in the mist, a long dark shape the length of a child, coming up out of the deep at the end of the jetty fast and straight, the way it had come at Alea's father in the spring. Straight at the posts. Straight at the net that wasn't there any more. To go under the jetty and out the other side and be gone, the way it had gone before.

Alea didn't pull back.

She came at it.

***

Higu saw her do it. She'd seen her do it once before, in the Lists, in the final, from the fifth tier, with a coast girl's hook on her sword: not pull back. Go *with* it. Faster than the line.

Alea stood up on the end of the jetty in the mist with the rod in both hands and went with the pike. Not into the water; along the boards. Two steps, three, fast, sideways, along the end of the jetty, toward the place where the pike was going to go under, *before* it got there. Keeping the line tight by moving the way the fish moved, faster than the fish, so that when it turned to go under the jetty the line didn't go slack. It went *tight*. All at once. With the rod bent nearly double and the line singing in the cold air and something very big and very angry on the end of it, under the boards, stopped.

Stopped.

By a hook that wouldn't bend.

The pike thrashed. Higu's moth saw the water under the jetty go white. Alea held it. On her knees again now, on the end boards, leaning back, with the rod bent and her heels dug into a gap between the planks and her whole Tempered body going into it, steady, the way you'd hold a boat in on a rope. Not fighting. Holding. Letting it pull, and pull, and pull, and not giving it anything to pull against except a girl who wouldn't let go.

It took a long time.

Higu didn't know how long. She sat at her window with her eyes shut and her hands gone tight on her book and didn't breathe, very nearly, for the whole of it.

And then the pike came up.

Slowly. Out from under the jetty, into the open water at the end, tired, turning on its side. Long and dark and mottled green and gold, with a jaw like a bear-trap and an eye like a coin, the biggest fish Higu had ever seen through any moth's eyes in two years. Alea brought it in. Hand over hand, at the end, the line wrapped round her fist, until it was lying in the water against the end posts of the jetty, beaten, its gills going.

She knelt on the end boards and looked down at it.

***

Higu had thought she'd land it.

That was what you did, with a fish like that. You landed it. You took it home. You put it in a basket and carried it up a road and said *look*. Her father would have. Higu had seen him land pike through a moth's eyes a dozen times, very quietly, and clean them on the end of the jetty, and that was that.

Alea didn't land it.

She knelt on the end of the jetty in the mist, with the rod laid down beside her and the line wrapped round her hand, and leaned over, and looked at the pike in the water against the posts. For a long time. The moth was close enough, now, that Higu could see her face. She'd never been able to see her father's. She could see Alea's.

Alea was crying. And smiling. Both. The way people do when they've done the thing they set out to do and found it was a different thing than they thought.

She reached down into the cold water with her other hand. Very slowly. And took hold of the hook, where it was set in the corner of the pike's great jaw. And worked it free. Carefully. The way you'd take a splinter out of somebody's thumb.

And the pike lay there in the water against the jetty posts, free, with its gills going, and didn't move. For a moment. As if it didn't know it was free. As if it were waiting to be told.

Alea put her hand flat on the water above it.

Higu couldn't hear what she said. The moth didn't carry sound; they never had. She saw Alea's lips move. Two words, Higu thought. Maybe three.

She thought she knew what they were.

The pike turned. Slowly. Under Alea's hand. And went down, into the grey water, into the deep off the end of the jetty, long and dark and unhurried, the way a big fish goes when it's decided it's not frightened any more. And was gone.

Alea knelt on the end of the jetty with her hand still flat on the water where it had been, and didn't move for a long time.

Then she wiped her face on her sleeve. And picked up the rod. And sat back on her heels on the end boards, in the place where the boards were worn pale in a hollow the shape of a man, and looked out at the mist going gold on the lake as the sun came up behind the hill.

***

Higu called the moths back.

She opened her eyes. She was at the window of the room at the top of the militia hall, with the shutter open a crack and the frost on the sill and her counting book on her knee, and her hands were shaking.

Behind her, Thea was awake. Sitting up in the other bed with her knees up, watching her. Thea always woke when Higu came back from a long look; she said she could feel the moths go out and come in, through the channel somehow, the way you feel a door open in another room.

"What?" said Thea.

Higu didn't answer at once. She opened her book. She wrote, under *three knocks*, in her small neat hand, by the grey light from the window:

*Dawn. Alea caught the cow pike on the end of the jetty with Oakes's hook. And let it go.*

She looked at it.

"Gu?" said Thea.

"She caught it," said Higu. "The pike. The one that went through his net. The one he said was her mother." She closed the book. "And let it go."

Thea didn't say anything for a long time.

"Of course she did," said Thea, eventually. Very quietly.

***

He came up the lake road at sunrise.

Higu watched him from the window. She didn't need a moth for this. The window looked straight down the lake road between the hedges, white and gold in the first sun, all the way to where it bent round toward the water. And up it, from the bend, came a man. A plain man in a plain coat, without his rod, because his daughter had it on the end of the jetty. With something over his arm, folded, red, with a gold fringe that caught the sun. And something in his other hand.

Fish. On a string. Through the gills, the way fishermen carry them up from the water. Five.

He came up the lake road between the white hedges, at his pace, not fast, not slow, with the frost going to water in the sun on either side of him. Past the place where the hedges ended and the wall began. Past the last bend. To the top of the lake road. To the militia hall.

To the step.

He stopped.

Higu watched him look at the basket.

It had been there sixty days. She'd put it there herself, on the day they came home from Valcrest, at the top of the lake road, with the whole of Group 1 standing in the road behind her and the mayor beside them. A willow basket. A fisherman's basket, with a lid and a strap, that had come up this road every morning for a year and a half with two fish in it, and then five. Empty. With a flat grey stone from the lake shore in the bottom of it, so the wind wouldn't take it.

And the whole town had walked round it. For sixty days. The children on their way to the lake. The egg-woman. Old Mabry. Nobody had touched it. Nobody had needed to be told.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he put the folded red cloth down on the wall beside the step, carefully. And knelt.

***

He took the stone out first.

Higu watched him do it. He picked it up out of the bottom of the basket, the flat grey lake stone she'd put there sixty days ago, and held it in his palm, and looked at it. Turned it over. Then he put it in his coat. In the inside pocket, on the left. Over his heart.

Higu didn't know why that made her eyes go. It did.

Then the fish.

One at a time. Off the string, into the basket, laid side by side on the willow, the way he'd laid them every morning for a year and a half: carefully, in order, as if the order mattered. Two perch, striped, the size of a hand. A bream, broad and gold. A long silver fish she didn't know the name of, that he always put in, that Xola said tasted of the lake. And a pike. A jack. Small, for a pike. Long and green and mottled, with a jaw like a bear-trap.

For Gigichi. *Because you'll complain the whole time and eat all of it*.

Five.

He put the lid on. He did up the strap. He sat back on his heels on the step of the militia hall at the top of the lake road with the basket in front of him, full, and his hands on his knees, and looked at it.

And then he looked up. At the window. At her.

***

He knew she was there. He always had. A year and a half of moths at the end of his jetty in the dark, and he'd known about every one of them, and never once said, until one night at the long table in Valcrest when he'd said *your moths are very quiet, Higu*, and she'd gone red to her ears.

He lifted his hand. A little. The way you'd lift your hand to somebody at a window you knew was awake.

She lifted hers back.

He didn't knock. He didn't call up. He didn't come in. He stood up off the step, and picked up the folded red cloth off the wall, and put it back over his arm, and turned round, and went back down the lake road between the white hedges, toward the water, at his pace, not looking back.

The basket stayed on the step. Full. In the sun.

Higu sat at the window and watched him go all the way down to the bend. Then she closed her book and stood up.

"Thea," she said.

"I saw," said Thea. She was already out of bed.

***

They went down together. All five of them.

Higu didn't wake the others. She didn't need to. Thea went down the stairs with her boots in her hand, and by the time they got to the door at the bottom, Xola was in the hall in her nightgown with Squiddles on her shoulder, because Xola woke when anybody in the hall woke, and PenguChi was behind her with Waddles at her heels, very calm, already dressed. And Gigichi was coming down the stairs from the top floor in her coat with her long silver hair down and a scowl like a thundercloud, because nobody had woken her and she'd woken anyway.

"What," said Gigichi.

Higu opened the door.

The cold came in. The sun. The white yard. And on the step, in the middle, exactly where it had always been, the basket. Full. With the lid on and the strap done up.

Nobody said anything.

Xola started crying. Of course she did. Openly, with her whole face, the way she did everything, with Squiddles patting her cheek with one small tentacle. PenguChi stood very still with her hand on Waddles's head. Thea had her hand over her mouth.

Gigichi went down the step and knelt and undid the strap.

She lifted the lid. She looked at what was in the basket for a long time. Two perch. A bream. A long silver fish. A jack pike.

"*Bones*," said Gigichi.

Her voice cracked on it.

Then she put her face down on the lid of the basket, on the step of the militia hall at the top of the lake road, in the first sun on the first morning after the frost, and her shoulders went, and she didn't say anything else at all.

Nobody touched her. Nobody needed to. They stood round her on the step in a row, the five of them, the way they'd stood at the top of this road for twenty months, and waited.

***

There was something under the fish.

Higu found it, after, when Gigichi had sat up and wiped her face on her sleeve and said *I'm fine, I'm FINE*, very fiercely, to nobody, and they'd carried the basket into the kitchen and put it on the table. Under the pike, at the bottom of the basket, flat against the willow, where the stone had been. A piece of paper. Folded in four. Soft, along the folds, the way paper goes when it's been carried against somebody's chest for two months.

Higu knew it before she opened it. Her hands knew it. She'd drawn every line on it.

Her moon chart.

The one she'd kept since the first week at Wrenhaven, on the back of a page Maren had given her, in charcoal and then ink. Two years of moons, going round and round. The one she'd folded up at the end of a long table in Valcrest and put into the hands of a fox girl with a black sword, and said *look up*.

She unfolded it on the kitchen table.

Her moons. Every one. Small and careful, in rows. Two years of them. And at the end, the three she'd added in fresh ink in Valcrest: full, and then full, and then full. And under the last one, in her own small English, the words she'd written for Alea.

*The night. An hour after sunset. East, over the red.*

And under that, in a different hand, new.

Higu stared at it.

Not her hand. Rounder. More careful. The round careful script of somebody who'd learned her letters at a cabin table from a city secretary when she was six, and then, at thirteen, twenty-six more, in English, on a slate, with a nail. In English now. In ink. A little blotted, as if it had been written somewhere cold, by firelight, on a knee.

And above the words, a drawing. Small. Not as careful as Higu's. Somebody who'd never drawn a moon before, trying.

A hill, black. A ring on it, darker. Mountains behind it. And over the mountains, coming up, enormous, round, full, so big it filled half the space at the end of the chart, a moon.

And under it, the words.

*I looked up.*

***

Higu sat down.

She didn't decide to. She just found she was sitting at the kitchen table in the militia hall with the moon chart in front of her and five fish in a basket beside it and four people round her, very quiet, reading it over her shoulder.

*I looked up*.

"She drew it," said Thea. Very softly. "The moon. Over the red. She drew it for you."

"She's terrible at drawing," said Xola, teary and delighted. "Look at it. Look at the mountains. They're *triangles*."

"It's perfect," said PenguChi.

Higu didn't say anything. She sat at the table and looked at a black hill with a ring on it and a full moon coming up behind it, drawn by somebody who'd never drawn a moon, and the three words under it. And thought about the end of a long table in Valcrest, two months ago, and a fox girl looking for a long time at a small circle drawn over a line on the back of a chart, and folding it along Higu's folds, and putting it inside her coat, and saying *I'll look*.

*I looked up*.

She'd worked out a lot of things in her life. On Earth, and here. Where the moon would rise and how bright it would be and how far the moths would fly on a clear night. Where a hunter would come from on the road north. Which watch nobody wanted. She'd worked out where a man lived, a year and a half ago, from a moth and a light on a jetty and a thing that wasn't quite a lamp, and written *Alea is his. He has a window* in her notebook, and told nobody, and been right.

She'd never once known, until this morning, whether anybody ever looked where she told them to.

***

She went down the lake road at noon, on her own.

The others had gone down at mid-morning, all four, carrying the fish back down because Gigichi said *we're not eating these on our own, are you mad, he'll have nothing in*. And there'd been a great deal of noise at the bottom of the lake road for about two hours, which Higu had listened to from the window of the militia hall, smiling, and not gone to. Xola's laugh. Gigichi's voice, very loud, about the pike. And once, very clearly, carrying up the hill on the cold still air, a girl's voice, shouting *I CAUGHT IT. AND LET IT GO*. And a man's, under it, not shouting. Laughing.

She went down at noon, when it had gone quiet.

She found him on the end of the jetty.

Of course she did. Sitting where he always sat. In the hollow worn pale in the boards. With the rod across his knees, which his daughter had given back to him, and his back very straight, looking at the lake. Alone. The others were in the cabin; Higu could hear them, through the open door, and smell fish frying, and see a bright red cloth with a gold fringe hanging very crookedly in the little window under the eaves of the cabin roof.

She walked out along the jetty. He heard her. He always did. He didn't turn round.

She sat down on the boards. Not close. At the far end of the jetty from him, a few planks back, with her knees up and her counting book on them. The way she'd sat in the winter, on the ninth evening, copying down his window by lamplight.

They didn't say anything for a while.

"Thank you," she said, eventually. "For the chart."

"It's not from me," he said. "She put it in."

"I know. I mean for carrying it up." She looked at the book on her knees. "You took the stone out."

"I've got it," he said. He touched his coat. The inside pocket, on the left. "It's a good stone."

"It's just a stone off the shore."

"I know," he said. "That's why."

***

She opened her book.

Not at the front, where the moons were, and the watches, and *Alea is his*. At the back. Where she'd copied something, a long time ago, on the ninth evening of the first winter, while he sat on this jetty with his window open for the first time in front of anyone and Thea asked questions and Gigichi said *Maim at seventeen?* and he said *it was a difficult year*.

His window. All of it. Every skill, with the ages she'd guessed beside them in pencil. Eight lines.

And under them, two more. Empty. With a number in front of each.

*9.*

*10.*

*For when you find out*, she'd written beside them, small.

She looked at line nine.

"Still empty," she said.

"Still empty," he agreed.

"What is it now?"

He didn't answer at once. He lifted one hand, and moved it in the air in front of him, the small habitual movement she'd watched through a moth's eyes a hundred times at the end of this jetty, and his window opened. She couldn't read it from where she sat. She didn't need to. She waited.

"Sixty-one," he said.

Higu looked at her book.

"It was sixty," she said. "At the closing. In Valcrest."

"It moved," he said. "At Cinderhold."

"For what?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"A letter," he said.

Higu didn't ask whose. She didn't need to. Hana had put enough in the channel, carefully, round the edges, for anybody who was paying attention to know. A rider out of Solmere at dawn. A rider back, three weeks later, and a man on a gatehouse wall not coming down for an hour.

She took her pencil out. She wrote, very small, in the margin beside line nine, under *for when you find out*:

*61. A letter.*

She looked at it. Then, after a moment, under it:

*He looked up.*

***

"It'll fill," she said. "Line nine."

"Probably," he said.

"When?"

He thought about it. Properly. The way he thought about everything, slowly, from the bottom up.

"I don't know," he said. "It moves when I put something down. Not when I do something. When I stop carrying something I didn't know I was carrying." He was quiet. "I've been putting things down all year. Sixty-one."

Higu looked at the lake.

"What else are you carrying?" she said.

He didn't answer. She hadn't expected him to. She sat on the jetty with her book on her knees and watched him not answer, the way she'd watched him not answer things through moths for a year and a half, and saw, the way she saw most things, that it wasn't because he didn't know.

It was because he did.

"Not yet," said Higu.

He turned his head, then. For the first time. And looked at her down the length of the jetty.

She couldn't see his face. Nobody could. She'd stopped trying on the first day, on the market road, when he'd stood in front of five girls blocking his way and looked at each of them in turn and she'd felt it, the look, without being able to see where it came from, the way you feel the moon on your face with your eyes shut.

She felt it now.

"You'd make a good strategist," he said.

"I am a good strategist."

"I know," he said. "I modded your chat."

Higu went red to her ears.

He looked back at the lake.

***

They sat there a while longer. The noise from the cabin went on behind them: Xola laughing, Gigichi complaining about bones, a kettle starting to sing, two notes, terribly, because somebody had found the cabin's old one in the back of a cupboard, the one that wasn't Calvin's, that had been there all along.

"The basket," said Higu, eventually.

"Tomorrow," he said.

She looked at him.

"Five," he said. "Every morning. Till the snow. And then two, in the snow, because the ice." He turned the rod over on his knees. "The basket stays five."

Higu wrote it down. Under *61* and *he looked up*, very small, in the margin of a copy of a window that had two empty lines at the bottom.

*The basket stays five.*

She closed the book.

She sat on the jetty a while longer, in the cold noon sun, with the lake very still in front of her and the hills on the far side going purple and a thin line of smoke going up behind her from a cabin where four of her friends were eating fish with a man's daughter and arguing about bones. And thought about a moon chart lying on a kitchen table at the top of the hill, with a black hill on it and a ring and a full moon so big it filled half the page.

*I looked up*.

She'd go back up the hill this afternoon. She'd sit at the window tonight, with the shutter open a crack and her book on her knee and three moths on the sill. She'd send them out at the grey hour, the way she always did, down the lake road, to the water.

And at sunrise she'd watch a man come up the lake road between the hedges with five fish on a string. And kneel at the step. And fill the basket. And lift his hand to her window, a little, and go back down.

And she'd lift hers back.

Every morning. Till the snow.

She'd count them. She counted everything.
