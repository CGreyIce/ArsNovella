Cursiffe had been carrying the end of the world in his shirt since the winter, and he'd got used to it, which worried him.

It was one sheet. Good paper, Oswin's, from the archive's own stock, folded in four. On it, in English, in his own small slanting hand with the dark blue ink from the vial on his chain, everything he and Oswin had found in a purse book on shelf sixty-one under the old keep. The two from the eleventh year of the Open-Handed. The east-wing rooms, and the tutor, and the paper the woman covered in circles. The nine years. *Every day*, the old king had written in his own hand in the margin, *they ask me to send them home, and every day I have nothing to tell them*. And the tenth year, when they stopped asking.

He'd written it all out, that night in the winter, so that he'd have it straight. And then he'd folded it and put it inside his shirt against his chest, and it had been there ever since. Four months. He'd slept with it. He'd washed round it. He'd sat in the Council chamber in the spring while Lord Vane talked about the safety of the realm, with the paper warm against his breastbone and the whole of the answer to the only question the thirty-five had ever really asked written on it, and said nothing.

He wasn't allowed to say anything. That was the thing. He'd promised. The readers in Kingsferry had asked, and the man at the lake had asked them, and so it went down the line: off the channel, till he says. And he'd promised something else, besides, in the spring, to a cat who'd stood on a burning roof and been all in one place.

*When I can tell you, you'll be first*.

He'd written that down, too. One word, on a scrap. And folded it, and given it to Milli; and Milli had put it in his shirt, where Cursiffe kept his.

So there were two of them now. Two folded papers in two shirts in the east wing. And one of them had the end of the world on it, and the other had one word, and Cursiffe had started to think, over the last month, that he understood the second one a great deal less than the first.

***

It had started with Vane's clerk.

Lord Vane had lost both his motions in the spring, to the king in his travelling coat, in a voice that didn't need raising. He had not lost his interest. Two weeks after the king went up the west tower, a clerk from the Chancellor's office had come to the east wing with a sheaf of forms about the *custody of documents pertaining to the summoned*, very polite, and been sent away by Jared, very politely, and come back four days later when Jared was in the yard with Brask.

Cursiffe had found him at his desk. Not touching anything. Just looking: at the drawer, at the papers on top of it, at the vial of ink. A thin young man with an ink-stained cuff and a careful face, who'd said *I beg your pardon, I was looking for the master of the wing*, and gone.

That night Cursiffe had written KEEP across the front of the drawer. In English, small, in the blue ink. It was the only thing he could do. He'd written it inside the cover of a three-hundred-year-old book in the winter and watched the pages lie down flat under it and stop crumbling; he'd written it on the cords Hana made, on the ends where they frayed, and they'd stopped fraying. *Keep*. It was what his ink did. Things he wrote it on lasted.

And the drawer had lasted. When the clerk came back the next week, Cursiffe was in the Council chamber, and Jared was at the forge, and the clerk had opened the drawer, and taken out everything in it, and copied it. Letter by letter. English he couldn't read, onto Chancellery paper, very carefully, the way a clerk copies a contract in a language he doesn't know.

Cursiffe had found the copies a week later, on Vane's own desk, when Halvard sent him to the Chancellery on an errand that Cursiffe suspected afterwards had not been an errand at all.

Nothing important. His notes on the cords. A list of Hana's pairs. A recipe for ink. But the drawer had kept them, perfectly; not a crease; not a blot. It had kept them so well that every letter was there to be copied.

*Keep* kept things from time. It didn't keep them from people.

***

So he'd tried the other thing.

He'd never had a word for it. Hana called it his seals; everybody did. Since the first year, since the hall, since he'd found that his ink worked in English and nobody in this world could read English but thirty-five people and a fisherman, his writing had been a kind of lock just by being what it was. No native could read it. No native could forge it. Halvard used him for that sometimes, when something had to go to the guides and couldn't be opened on the road.

But that wasn't a seal. That was just a language nobody spoke. Vane's clerk had copied it anyway.

He wanted a real one. A word that shut.

He'd tried it on a letter to Hana. One evening in the spring, at his desk, with Jared asleep: a note, nothing in it, *this is a test, ignore it*, folded, and across the fold, in the blue ink, one word. SHUT. He'd thought about it very hard. He'd thought about Hana opening it and nobody else, the way you'd think about a key fitting one lock.

The ink had gone into the paper wrong.

He'd felt it. Like a pen nib catching. The word went down across the fold and didn't sit on the paper the way his ink always sat; it went *into* it. The iron in it, the iron-gall that he'd mixed into the blue to make it last; it ate. He watched the word sink into the fibres and spread, black at the edges, like a burn spreading in cloth. It went through to the other side. It went into his fingers, where he was holding the paper flat, and he felt it there too, a cold bite like frost, and pulled his hand away; and the pads of his first two fingers were black, and stayed black, and hurt.

And his nose had bled. Onto the desk. Which it never did.

The letter was shut. He'd been right about that. He took it to Hana in the morning with his fingers wrapped in a handkerchief, and she took it in her small hands, and turned it over, and tried to open it, and it wouldn't. Not stuck. Shut, the way a stone is shut. She pulled at it. Jared pulled at it. Milli, who was stronger than he looked, pulled at it with his whole body and his tail out straight. It didn't so much as bend at the corners.

It was shut to everybody. Hana too. A dead thing. A lock with no key.

He'd put it in the stove. It hadn't burned, either. In the end Jared had taken it down to the forge in the old keep and the smith there had put it on the anvil and hit it, and it had broken like slate.

***

"You've been funny since the spring," said Aleaka.

She'd come to find him in the archive, where he'd gone because it was quiet and Oswin didn't ask questions. She stood at the end of the long table under the lamps, between the Wardens' minutes and the carp treatise, with Brisk on her bare arm, the bark-coloured hawk with the bent feather that sat on nobody else. She didn't sit down. Leaka never sat down until she'd decided you wanted her to.

"Funny how?"

"Heavy." She looked at his chest, where the paper was. She always looked there now. She'd reached for it once, in the spring, on the day her window went and she'd sat on the floor of Hana's room testing *Heft* on everybody in turn, and stopped with her hand an inch from his shirt, and taken it back. *It's somebody else's*, she'd said, and he hadn't understood.

"It's the paper," said Cursiffe.

"It's not the paper." Aleaka tipped her head on one side. "The paper's heavy. It's always been heavy. It's got the tenth year in it. But that's not what's changed." She looked at him. "You've been trying to *make* something heavy. Since the spring. And it doesn't want to be made."

He looked at his black fingertips.

"I tried to write a lock," he said.

"I know. Milli told me. The letter Jared broke on an anvil." She stroked the hawk's head with one finger, and the hawk let her. "Milli's got one of yours too. In his shirt. Where you keep that."

"I know. I gave it him."

"Have you asked him about it?"

He looked up.

"He tried to open it," said Aleaka. "Once. He told me. The night of the balance, in the spring, when the call went over Thornwall and Hana had all seven cords open and nobody slept. He couldn't stand it, not knowing, and he took it out on the roof and tried to unfold it." She was quiet. "It wouldn't open."

Cursiffe stared at her.

"It wasn't stuck," said Aleaka. "He said. It wasn't shut like a stone, either. Like the letter. He said it was just *not yet*. Like a door with somebody's hand on the other side." She looked at the paper under his shirt again, and then at him. "So he put it back."

"I didn't do anything to it," said Cursiffe. "I just wrote it. And folded it. I didn't—" He stopped.

He hadn't. He'd written one word on a scrap, at his desk, in the spring, with a cat across from him who'd just stopped holding still by holding on. He'd written it very carefully. He'd meant it more than he'd meant anything he'd written in this world. He hadn't thought about locks at all.

He'd thought about a promise.

***

He didn't sleep that night.

He lay on his cot in the room he shared with Jared, with Jared asleep and the lamp out, and thought about it the way he thought about everything: slowly, with his eyes shut, turning it over, the way you'd turn a pebble in your fingers to find the flat side.

*Keep* kept things from time. That was its grammar, if it had one. It said: *this, and not the years*. The purse book's pages lay down because the years couldn't get at them any more.

And SHUT had been a lock, and a lock says: *this, and not anybody*. Not anybody at all; that was what a lock was. He'd tried to make it say *not anybody but Hana*, and his ink hadn't known how, because a lock doesn't know who anybody is. It only knows *no*. So it had said *no* to everybody, and eaten the paper doing it, and his fingers.

But the scrap in Milli's shirt hadn't said *no*. It had said *not yet*.

It knew who it was for. It knew when.

Because it wasn't a lock. It was the thing he'd written on it. *When I can tell you, you'll be first*. He'd folded a promise, and the paper had kept it. Not from time. Not from everybody. From *before*. It would stay shut until the thing he'd promised was due, for the one person he'd promised it to; and then—

He didn't know what then. He lay in the dark with the end of the world against his chest and thought: *then I'll find out*.

***

The word came down the channel on a warm evening at the start of the summer.

He was at his desk, with Jared on the other side of the room mending a strap and humming, when the channel came up in his window on its own the way it did when somebody wrote something nobody had been waiting for and everybody had.

Cursiffe sat very still.

"What?" said Jared, across the room. He'd seen Cursiffe's face; Jared saw everything, the way Jared saw a fight a step before it happened. "Cur. What."

Cursiffe stood up.

"Where's Milli?" he said.

***

He wasn't in the east wing. Hana said he was on the roof. Hana was in the doorway of their room with the cords on her wrist, seven of them and the purple one with the little gold heart, and she looked at Cursiffe's face, and then at the channel still up in her own window, and her ears went flat and then very slowly up again.

"Go," she said. "He's been on the roof every night for a week. He knows something's coming."

He went up. The narrow stair at the end of the wing, and the trapdoor, and out onto the leads, where the roof of the east wing ran flat for twenty paces between the chimneys before it dropped away to the courtyard. The sun was down. The sky over the old keep was going green at the edges. And Milli was sitting on the parapet at the far end with his legs over the drop, the way he sat now, with his tail hanging down behind him perfectly still.

He looked round when the trapdoor went. He saw who it was. He didn't get up.

"Is it now?" said Milli.

"It's now," said Cursiffe.

He crossed the roof and sat down on the parapet beside him. Not close. A hand's width. His legs over the courtyard, four storeys down, the lamps coming on in the windows of the old keep across the way. He didn't like heights. He didn't say so.

"You said first," said Milli.

"I know. I came straight here. I haven't told anybody. Jared saw my face. That's all."

Milli nodded. He put his hand inside his shirt and took out the scrap of paper. It had gone soft at the edges in four months, the way paper goes when you carry it against yourself, and grey with handling; but it was still folded, very small, the way Cursiffe had folded it. Milli held it in his palm and looked at it.

"I tried to open it," he said. "Once."

"Leaka told me."

"It wouldn't." Milli turned it over with one finger. "It wasn't stuck. It just wasn't *ready*." He looked at Cursiffe sideways. "What did you do to it?"

"I don't know," said Cursiffe, which was true. "Try now."

***

Milli didn't have to try.

He hadn't even put his fingers to the fold. He was holding it in his flat palm, looking at it, and it opened. By itself. Cursiffe watched it happen: the scrap of paper unfolding in the cat's hand in the green dusk, one fold and then the next, slowly, the way a leaf uncurls in the sun, without anybody touching it; until it lay flat on Milli's palm, a small grey square, with one word on it in Cursiffe's slanting hand in the blue ink, as fresh and wet-looking as the night he'd written it.

*First.*

Cursiffe felt it go.

Not out of the paper. Out of him. The thing he'd folded into it four months ago without knowing, the promise, the *not yet*: it went, the way a held breath goes, and he felt it go all the way through him, from the paper to the vial on his chain to his chest where the other paper was, and down, and set. Like ink drying. Like a seal pressed into wax and lifted, and the shape left behind.

In front of him, in the dusk, over the courtyard, where only he could see it:

He looked at it for a long time.

*Seal*. Not a lock. A lock says *no*. A seal says *this was promised, and to whom, and when*; and keeps it, against time and against everybody, until the promise comes due; and then opens, by itself, for the one person it was for.

He'd done it by accident. Four months ago, on a scrap, meaning it. And he'd spent a month trying to do it on purpose and eaten his own fingers, because he'd been trying to make a lock.

"Cur," said Milli.

"Yeah."

"It says *first*."

"Yeah." Cursiffe's voice came out strange. "That's what I promised."

Milli looked at the word on his palm for a long moment. Then he folded it up again, carefully, along the old folds, and put it back in his shirt. And then he looked at Cursiffe, and his face was very calm. All in one place. The way it had been on the burning roof, Hana said, with a boy round his neck and four storeys of nothing under him.

"Okay," said Milli. "Tell me."

***

So Cursiffe told him first.

On the roof of the east wing, in the dusk, with his legs over a four-storey drop, before anybody. He took the other paper out of his shirt, the one with the end of the world on it, and didn't read it. He didn't need to. He'd read it every night for four months.

He told him about the purse book. Shelf sixty-one. The old king's own hand. The two from the eleventh year: frightened, Aldemar had written in the margin, *and so am I*. The rooms in this wing; this wing, three hundred years ago, perhaps under this roof. The tutor. The woman who covered paper in circles. The man who asked for iron.

And the nine years.

*Every day*, Aldemar had written, *they ask me to send them home, and every day I have nothing to tell them*.

Milli didn't move.

"Nine years," said Cursiffe. "Every day. He wrote it down, Mill. Over and over, for nine years. And then in the tenth year it stops."

"Stops."

"They stopped asking. In the tenth year. And asked for a forge instead. Star-iron. A road east." He looked at the paper in his hand without reading it. "They made something. A sword. With a word folded into it, the way smiths fold steel in our world, over and over. *Stay*. Thea read it in Astra in the winter. And they took it east, to a hill, a month into the black, and drove it into the middle of something, to hold it down. And they never came back."

The lamps were coming on all over the old keep.

"They never went home," said Milli.

"No."

"Nobody sent them. Nobody could. In nine years."

"No."

"And they asked every day." Milli's voice didn't change. Cursiffe had been afraid it would. It didn't; it stayed exactly where it was, very quiet, all in one place. "Every day. And then they stopped, and made something, and went and put it in the ground a month from anywhere, and that was the end of them."

"That was the end of the page," said Cursiffe. "Not of them. The page ends."

Milli was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, "Is that what's in your shirt? The answer?"

"That's it."

"Whether anybody's ever gone home."

"Nobody we know of," said Cursiffe. "Nobody in three hundred years of the register. Not them. Not the ones before them, as far as Oswin can find." He folded the paper. "I'm sorry, Mill."

Milli looked out over the courtyard. His tail hung down behind him over the drop, perfectly still. And his hand went up, without him seeming to know it, and rested flat on his shirt, over the place where the scrap of paper was.

"Then somebody's going to have to be first," he said.

***

He went down to the rooms after that. All of them were there. Hana on the bed with the cords, Jared on the floor with his back against the wall and his strap forgotten in his hands, Aleaka by the window with Brisk. Nobody had written anything in the channel since Deston's four lines. Thirty people across the kingdom sitting up in their beds and windows waiting.

Milli came down behind him and went and sat on the bed beside Hana, close, his shoulder against hers, and she looked at his face and her ears went back and she put her hand on his knee and didn't ask.

Cursiffe sat down at his desk.

He took out the paper with the end of the world on it and unfolded it and laid it flat under the lamp. Then he put it in the channel. Not the paper; you couldn't put paper in the channel. The words. All of them, as plainly as he could, in the order they'd happened, the way Oswin would have wanted it. It was the longest thing anybody had ever put in the channel, Chris said afterwards. Chris counted.

He didn't write it for the channel. He wrote it for thirty-four people who'd been asking the same question for four years, and had never once been told the answer, and deserved to be told it straight.

He started with his own window, because that was the rule, and because it seemed only fair to give them something good before the rest.

Cursiffe read it all twice. Then he folded the paper with the end of the world on it, for the last time, and didn't put it back in his shirt. He put it in the drawer, and wrote nothing on the drawer at all.

Across the room, Milli had his face in Hana's shoulder, and Hana had her hand on the back of his neck, and Jared was looking at the floor, and Aleaka was looking at Cursiffe.

"Lighter," said Aleaka.

He didn't ask her what. He was learning.
