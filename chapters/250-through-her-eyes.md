The king's message had come up Maren's blue cord on the morning after he reached Thornwall, from Corvin, by way of Hana in Solmere, and come out in Maren's head in Maren's own small exact hand, the way the cords wrote; and Maren had read it out at breakfast in the militia hall with her spoon in the other hand, the way she read everything, slowly, every word.

*To Thea of Wrenhaven, from the king, by Corvin. We need eyes that can read what Hyvred sees. When she can. Not before.*

*Not before* came through underlined. Maren said so. The cords carried what you meant, and Corvin had meant it twice.

That had been nearly a fortnight ago. Thea had spent it not doing it.

She'd had reasons. She'd written them down, in the notebook she kept for things she was afraid of, under *Can read it. Can't write it.* and *Headache 7 hrs. Don't do that again. Yet.* from the first autumn, three years ago.

*One. I've only done it once. By accident. With seven people pouring.*

*Two. I don't know how to stop.*

*Three. Hyvred looked west after One Pour and something looked back.*

*Four. If it looks at me, it looks down the line. The line has Hana on it. And Deston. And Hyvred.*

Higu had read the list over her shoulder on the hall step, which Higu always did and never apologised for, and had taken the pencil out of Thea's hand, and written under it, in her own small fierce letters:

*Five. Then learn how to stop first.*

So she had.

***

You couldn't practise on the valley. That was the problem with it. You couldn't do a small one first.

So she practised on the cord.

Three nights, in Maren's rooms over the guild office, with the lamp on the table and Maren's blue cord across it, a plain knotted cord of blue wool with one white bead on it that Hana had knotted in Valcrest in the second summer. Hana held the other half, three hundred miles south in Solmere. Deston held her thread, a long way downriver in Kingsferry, from a bench outside the guild house with Gabe asleep against the wall beside him. And Thea sat at the table with her hand over Maren's hand on the cord, and opened the door.

It was easier the second time. That frightened her a little. She'd expected it to be like the first time, seven people pouring into her at once and the binding ringing and the register coming up out of the dark; and instead it was like opening a door she'd opened before. Deston came up her thread, humming, *hi, Thea*. Hana came up the cord, warm and hoarse and very steady. And Thea did what she'd learned at One Pour, which was nothing. She didn't hold them. She was the room they met in. And they met.

And then she read the cord.

Just the cord. Not the binding; not the register; not anything that could look back. A knotted cord of blue wool on a table. She Parsed it with Hana's meaning in it and Deston's note under it and found it was beautiful: a single line of the world's language, very short, very old, wound into thread by a cat-eared girl with clever hands who'd never once in her life been able to read what she was writing. *Carry. Carry. Carry.* Over and over, a hundred times, down the length of the cord. Like a child's chant.

Then she practised leaving.

That was the hard part. At One Pour she hadn't left; it had simply ended, when the register sank and Deston let go and seven people went back to being seven people. She didn't know how to *make* it end. She tried closing her eyes; it didn't. She tried letting go of Maren's hand; it didn't, it held through the air, through the table, through the cord. She tried thinking *stop*, very hard, the way you'd think it at a dog, and the room she was being only got louder, as if *stop* was something else to meet.

On the second night Higu said, from the window seat, with her notebook on her knees: "How did you get out of the chat?"

Thea looked at her.

"On the first night," said Higu. "In the first winter. When you built it. You made a room in the binding for thirty-five people to talk in, and then you went to bed. You didn't stay in it all night. How did you get out?"

"I didn't get out," said Thea slowly. "I just stopped being the one holding it open. It stayed open by itself. I only—" She stopped.

She'd closed the window. That was all. She hadn't closed the channel; the channel was a room, and the room stayed. She'd closed *her* window onto it. Her own door. The room went on without her.

"You don't close the room," said Thea. "You close your door."

Higu wrote it down.

On the third night she tried it. Deston came up her thread, Hana came up the cord, the three of them met in her and read the cord again, *carry, carry, carry*; and then Thea closed her door. Not the room. Her side of it. The place where they met, she stopped being it; she stepped back from it the way you'd step back out of a doorway to let somebody else through.

It cut.

Clean. She felt it go. Deston's note, gone, all at once, the thread still there but nobody on it. Hana gone off the cord, back to Solmere. The cord lying on the table, a cord. And Thea sitting with her hand over Maren's, alone in her own head, with a nosebleed.

Small. A few drops. Maren gave her a handkerchief without being asked.

And then Drew wrote at twenty past three in the morning, *three gone. not dead*.

And she read it in the dark in the room she shared with Higu and Gigichi over the militia hall, with Gigichi breathing in the next bed and Higu not, because Higu was awake too, she could tell; and she lay there and read the three names, and thought about *not before*.

In the morning she wrote to the king.

Not to Corvin. To the king. In English first, in her notebook, because she didn't know how else to write to him in her own words. And then she read it out to Maren slowly, word by word, and Maren sent it down the blue cord, so that it would go to Hana, and from Hana to Corvin, and come out in Corvin's head in Corvin's script, and Corvin could carry it up a hundred and twelve steps and read it to a king.

*The night after tomorrow. Two hours after dark. Hyvred on the tower, looking north, not west. I'll need Hana on the cords and Deston on the threads. I've learned to stop. — Thea.*

The answer came down the cord before noon.

*Yes. — S.*

And, under it, a second line that came through warmer, which Maren said meant Corvin had added it himself: *He said, out loud: she's never once said she'd learned a thing she hadn't. Good girl.*

Maren read that bit out too. Thea went red to the ears and went out into the yard and sat on the trough for a while.

***

They were all there. She hadn't asked them to be. They came anyway, up the outside stair to Maren's rooms at dusk, one by one, the way they'd come to every bad night for three years.

Higu, with the notebook and a fresh pencil, in the window seat, to count. Xola, with a blanket she put round Thea's shoulders without asking and then sat on the floor at her feet, leaning on her shins. PenguChi on the end of Maren's bed with the penguin on her knee, very still, the penguin flat. And Gigichi, who pulled the other chair up close to Thea's, on her left, and sat in it, and put her hand round Thea's wrist. Not her hand. Her wrist. Her thumb on the pulse.

The way it used to on Earth, a long time ago, before they'd drifted, when they used to fall asleep on a call together at four in the morning and wake up with the call still going.

"You okay?" said Gigichi.

"No," said Thea.

"Good," said Gigichi. "Me neither." And held on.

Bram stood outside the door at the top of the stair, big and grey, with his sword on one hip and Higu's clerk's ruler on the other and his bad shoulder against the doorframe. He didn't come in. He'd said, coming up, *I'm not much use for reading*, and Thea had said *you're the door*, and he'd thought about that and nodded and stood in it.

Maren sat across the table. Her hands were bandaged still, both palms, from the night in the summer she'd tried to be more; she laid them on the table either side of the cord, palm down, carefully, the way you'd put down something hot.

"Two hours after dark," said Maren.

Thea looked out of the window. The lamps of Wrenhaven, and the dark beyond them, and the lake road going north into it, toward a cabin with one light.

She put her hand over Maren's bandaged hand on the cord.

"Now," said Thea.

***

Deston first. Up her thread, from the bench outside the guild house in Kingsferry. The note, low and warm and a little rough. *Hi, Thea.* And something else in it tonight that hadn't been in it on the practice nights: he was frightened, and he was letting her hear it, because he'd never once lied to anybody he loved about being frightened, and he wasn't going to start in her head.

Hana next. Up the blue cord, under Maren's hand, under hers, from a room in the castle in Solmere where she sat on the floor with all seven cords on the purple string round her wrist, open, every one; Thea could feel the other six behind her, like voices in other rooms of a house. *Carry*. What Hana carried tonight was very simple. *I'm here. I've got you. I've got all of you.*

And then, through Deston, a long way. Hundreds of miles south and west, from the top of a tower with a hundred and twelve steps, a girl in a blanket with her back to the north wall.

No. Not with her back to it. Facing it. Tonight she was facing it.

Hyvred came in cold and clear and very far-sighted, the way she always came; and Thea's sight went *long*.

***

She saw the valley.

At night. Through Hyvred's eyes, which saw in the dark the way nobody else's did; not light, exactly. The *light in things*. The candle in the middle of a person. The faint grey glow in old stone, where mana had soaked into it for a thousand years and never left. The cold blue glimmer of water in the millstream far below. All of it laid out under her, the long dark valley, eleven miles of it, from the north wall to the shoulders of the mountains where the pass road went in.

And at the far end, at the foot of the pass, the rings.

She'd read about them for a year. Hyvred had written them every night; Higu had drawn them on the *WEST* page; Corvin had chained the distance with Fenwick. She'd thought she knew what they looked like.

People.

Standing. In rings, one inside the next, round an empty middle, out in the open valley at night with the frost on the grass round their feet. Hundreds of them. A ring of twelve at the centre, small, close; then a ring outside it, and another outside that, wider and wider, thirteen of them, each one a little wider than the one inside it. And every one of them a light. A candle in the middle of a person, burning, the way any person's burned. A woman in an apron. A trooper with freckles, in the third ring, turned a hair toward the town. A man in a farmer's coat. Hundreds of candles in the dark, in rings, perfectly still, with their faces all turned the same way, up toward the pass, smiling.

And outside the thirteenth, on the left, a short curved line of holes.

That was the only word she had. Holes. Places in the valley, at the edges of Hyvred's sight, the size and shape of people, where there was no light at all. Not dark; dark had light in it, the grey of stone, the blue of frost. These had nothing. Like somebody had cut people out of the world with very sharp scissors and left the shapes. Fourteen of them, in a curve, outside the thirteenth ring, on its left side, going round.

*Threadless*, said Deston's note, very quietly, under everything. *I can't see them at all. Not even the gap.*

*I can,* said Hyvred. Cold and clear. *Barely. It's like looking at the place where your eyes go when you close them.*

Thea looked at them for a long time.

Then she Parsed.

***

She'd never Parsed anything she couldn't touch. She'd never Parsed anything through somebody else's eyes. She'd never Parsed people.

It came anyway. The way it always came: you looked at a thing, and the thing had a shape, and the shape had parts, and the parts had names. The valley came apart in her sight into structure, the way a page comes apart into letters when you look too long; and the rings—

She knew them.

She knew them before she'd finished reading the first one. She'd seen them every day of her life here. She'd woken up in the middle of them.

*It's the hall*, said Thea. Out loud. She heard herself say it, a long way off, in Maren's room. She heard Higu's pencil start. *It's the circle on the floor of the hall in Solmere. The one we woke up in. Ring for ring.*

Thirteen rings. The same widths. The same spacing. The same small dense knot at the centre where the reference went, the thing like a knot drawn by somebody who'd never seen rope, that she'd read in the first frame of the register at One Pour; except here the knot was twelve people standing very close together in the frost. The same lines, spoking out. She could read it. It was the same language, the world's language, the one she'd spent three years learning to read and could still barely write. The same grammar. The same hand.

But in the hall it was written in silver, in grooves cut in black stone, three hundred years ago.

Here it was written in people.

Every person in every ring was a word. She could read them. That was the worst thing; she'd remember that, after. She could *read* them, the way she read the binding, the way she read the cord, *carry, carry, carry*; each candle in each ring a word in a line in a circle, standing exactly where the word stood in the silver on the floor in Solmere. The woman in the apron was a word. The trooper with freckles was a word. A girl at the edge of the thirteenth ring, with a plait down her back and her hands at her sides, blue to the wrist, was a word, the last word of the last line of the thirteenth ring, still wet.

*Elin*, thought Thea. And didn't say it. Joss wasn't there to hear. It didn't matter.

She said: *They're writing the circle in people. They've written thirteen rings. They're writing the fourteenth.*

***

The fourteenth ring.

She'd stood over it on a floorboard in Valcrest in the second summer with a vial of blue ink and written *TODO* in its empty half. She knew the fourteenth ring better than she knew her own handwriting. It wasn't one ring. It was two arcs, each half a circle, meeting at the top and the bottom. The left arc was the call. Full, dense, written all the way along, reaching out. *The left half reaches out and pulls*. That was what had pulled thirty-five people out of their rooms on a summer morning, from bedrooms and desks, across whatever lay between one world and another.

And the right arc was blank. Drawn, very carefully, all the way round, a mirror of the left; and left empty. On purpose.

She looked at the valley's fourteenth.

The left arc, outside the thirteenth ring, curving round. Fourteen holes in the world in a curved line. And she Parsed them, and they were words too. Of course they were. The same words as the left arc of the fourteenth ring on the floor in Solmere, in the same order, the same grammar, written in people with nothing in them. She could read them. She'd read them before.

*Come*.

That was all the call was, under everything. The oldest verb in the world's language, the one in every summoning in the register, the one that reached out. *Come. Come here. Come to this. There you are. Oh, there you are.* Written once in silver on a floor in Solmere and it had reached across worlds. Being written now, word by word, in threadless bodies, at the foot of a pass. Fourteen words of it. A little under half the arc.

And it already worked. She could feel it working. Half a call. A call that only reached eleven miles. A call that had been faint in the spring and was louder now; loud enough, three nights ago, to reach thirty-three people in a long room and three outside it.

*It gets louder as they write it*, said Thea. *Every word they add, it reaches further. When the arc's finished—*

She didn't finish. She didn't know. The arc in the hall had reached across worlds.

She looked at the right side of the valley's fourteenth ring. Where the other arc would be. Where, in the hall, somebody three hundred years ago had drawn a perfect blank mirror of the call and left it empty on purpose, so that one day somebody might write in it the way home.

Nothing.

Not a blank arc. Not a line. Not a mark. The frosted grass of the valley, empty, all the way round the right side of the thirteenth ring, the way grass is empty. Whoever was writing this circle in people hadn't drawn the second arc at all.

*There's no right arc*, said Thea.

She heard Higu's pencil stop.

*In the hall they drew the way home and left it blank*, said Thea. *Here they haven't even drawn it. Whoever's writing this isn't planning to send anyone back.*

***

She should have stopped there.

She knew that. She'd written it in the notebook: *read it, close your door*. She'd read it. She had the shape of it, the thing the king needed: *it's the hall's circle, in people, and they're writing the call, and there's no way home in it*. That was enough. That was more than anybody had known. She could close her door now and wake up in Maren's room with a nosebleed and Gigichi's thumb on her pulse, and it would be enough.

But the call had a line.

Every line in the world's language went somewhere. That was what a line was; that was what she'd learned at One Pour, reading the ticks, *each one the end of a line going back out along the line its person came in by*. The call went out to the town, to Thornwall, eleven miles; she could see that end of it, a faint thread of meaning going out from the left arc down the valley toward the north wall and the lamps and the tower she was looking from.

And it had another end.

Behind it. Behind the left arc, behind the rings, going the other way. West. Up the pass road, between the shoulders of the mountains, into the dark, past where Hyvred's sight went, past the edge of Corvin's maps.

Of course it did. Somebody was writing this. You couldn't write a call without a caller.

She followed it.

Only a hair. She told herself that after. Only a hair; only to see which way. The way you'd lean round a door to see who'd knocked. She leaned along the line, west, through Hyvred's eyes, up the pass road, a mile, two, the line going on into the dark like a thread pulled out of a coat—

*Thea*, said Hyvred.

Very sharp. Very cold. She'd never heard Hyvred's voice do that. *Thea, don't—*

It turned.

***

She'd read about it. Hyvred had written it, the morning after One Pour, in the channel, in capitals, and then deleted the capitals and written it again small. *Something looked back.*

She'd thought she understood that too.

It wasn't looking. Looking was what eyes did, and this didn't have eyes, or if it had them they were the size of the valley. It was attention. The whole of an attention, vast and slow and patient, that had been facing somewhere else, turning; the way a page turns, in a book the size of a mountain range; toward the place where she'd leaned along its line. Toward Hyvred's eyes. Toward the thing behind Hyvred's eyes that wasn't Hyvred.

Toward her.

She felt it find the line. The line she'd come up. Hyvred's sight, and through it Deston's thread, and down it Hana's cord, and at the end of all of them, a girl in a chair in a room over a guild office in a town by a lake, with her hand over another woman's on a blue plait.

It started to come down the line.

Not fast. It was never fast. *Not in a hurry at all*. It came the way the call came, warm and huge and patient, *oh, there you are*, down Hyvred's sight, and she felt Hyvred go rigid on the tower, all those miles away, and heard her make a small sound; and into Deston's thread, and she felt the note falter, the warm low hum going thin; and toward the cord, toward Hana, sitting on a castle floor with all seven cords open on her wrist, *carrying*. Seven guides on the other ends of them. Seven doors, open.

*Thea*, said Deston. Very calm. The calmest she'd ever heard him. *Now would be good.*

She closed her door.

***

Not softly. She'd been told not to make it soft.

She didn't close the room; she couldn't; she didn't own it. She closed herself. She stopped being the place where they met. All at once, everywhere, the way you'd step back out of a doorway with something coming through it; and she felt the cut go through her like a blade through a rope, *sharp*, exactly where she'd aimed it, at herself, at the one place where all four lines crossed. Not Hyvred's eyes. Not Deston's thread. Not Hana's cord. Hers. The meeting. Gone.

And the attention, coming down the line, found nowhere to go.

She felt that. Just for a heartbeat. Like a hand coming down a banister in the dark and finding the banister ends.

Then everything was gone, and she was in a chair in Maren's room with her face wet and warm and somebody saying her name, and the lamp swinging, though nobody had touched the lamp; and the ceiling, and Gigichi's face very close, upside down, saying *Thea Thea Thea* with her thumb still on Thea's wrist, and Higu at the window with the pencil in her hand saying very clearly to nobody: "Six hundred and twelve."

Thea tried to say *the others*.

It came out as blood. Quite a lot of it. Down her lip and her chin and onto Maren's blanket round her shoulders. She heard Xola make a noise and PenguChi say something sharp, and Maren's bandaged hands came across the table and took her face, both palms, very gently, burns and all, and held it.

"Breathe," said Maren.

She was breathing. She'd learned that at One Pour. She held on to it.

"The others," said Thea, through blood.

"Look," said Maren. And let go of one side of her face, and nodded at the air in front of it, where the channel was.

***

She didn't remember most of the three days.

She remembered pieces. The ceiling of the room over the militia hall, which had a crack in the plaster shaped like the Wren. Gigichi asleep in the chair beside her bed with her hand round Thea's wrist, every time she woke, at every hour, in the dark and in the light; she didn't know when Gigichi slept, or if she did. Maren's voice, reading something slowly aloud, a word at a time, and the lamp beside the bed burning very steadily while she read. Xola making her drink broth. PenguChi's penguin sitting on her chest, very warm, like a hot-water bottle with opinions.

Her head. Mostly her head. It hurt the way it had hurt in the first autumn, the seven-hour headache, *don't do that again, yet*; except this one didn't stop at seven hours. It sat behind her eyes like a stone and went on sitting. When she opened them the light went through her like nails. When she shut them she saw rings.

She saw them every time. Thirteen rings in the frost, round an empty middle, and a short curved line of holes on the left. And on the right side, nothing.

On the second night she woke up and Higu was sitting on the end of the bed in the dark with her notebook open on her knees and a candle on the floor, drawing.

"What are you doing?" said Thea. It came out as a croak.

"*WEST*," said Higu. She didn't look up. "You talked. The whole time. Six hundred and twelve heartbeats and you talked for six hundred of them. I wrote it all down." She turned the notebook round.

It was the page. The *WEST* page, the one Higu had started the morning after One Pour, with one word on it, underlined. It wasn't one page now. It was six. Higu had drawn the valley from what Thea had said: the north wall, the eleven miles, the pass road. The rings, thirteen of them, carefully, with a compass. The knot at the centre, *12 people*. The holes on the left, fourteen of them, drawn as little empty circles, with an arrow and *THREADLESS. "COME." ½ arc (a bit less)*. And on the right, where the second arc would be, nothing. Just paper. And in the middle of the paper, in Higu's small fierce letters, one word, and a box round it.

*NONE*.

And on the last page, on its own, an arrow pointing off the left edge of the paper, with *the other end* written along it; and at the end of the arrow, past the edge of the drawing, where the paper ran out, Higu had drawn nothing at all, and written under the nothing, very small: *don't*.

Thea looked at it for a long time.

"It's good," she said.

"It's yours," said Higu. "I just wrote it down." She turned the notebook back round and looked at it herself. "You said *Elin*. In the middle. You didn't say it out loud. I heard it anyway."

"She's the last word," said Thea. "In the thirteenth ring. The last word of the last line. I could read her." She shut her eyes. "Gu. I could *read* them."

Higu didn't say anything.

Then she put the notebook down on the floor beside the candle, and got up, and lay down on top of the blankets on the far side of the bed, on her back, with her arms folded, not touching, and stayed there till morning. Which was a thing Higu had never once done in three years.

*Rule four*, Thea thought. And slept.

***

On the third day she sat up.

The stone behind her eyes was smaller. She could look at the window. Gigichi was in the chair, awake for once, with her hand round Thea's wrist, reading something in her own window with the other hand.

"Hi," said Gigichi.

"Hi."

"You okay?"

Thea thought about it.

"I will be," she said.

Gigichi looked at her for a long time. Then she nodded, once, and didn't let go of her wrist, and went back to reading.

Thea opened her window.

She didn't look at the channel. She'd read it later. She looked at her own window, the first thing she'd looked at every morning for three years, the white box in plain letters in the air in front of her face.

Three.

It had been nothing since One Pour. Since the night she'd gone Lantern, it had sat at nothing, every morning, a blank where a number should be, the way bars sat when you'd only just arrived at a tier. She'd expected that. Torch was a long way off; it was supposed to be. She hadn't expected it to move for years.

Three percent.

She thought about what had moved it. Not the reading. She was almost sure. The reading had been the biggest thing she'd ever done, the whole valley, the whole circle, thirteen rings of people read like a page; and she'd done it before, at One Pour, more or less, and it hadn't moved anything then. It was something else.

It was the cut.

One small, sharp thing. Not big. Not *more*. She hadn't pushed; she hadn't held; she hadn't been louder. She'd put one cut in exactly one place, the only place it could go, at the one point where four lines crossed; and it had gone exactly where she meant it to, and not a hair to either side. Hyvred's eyes, untouched. Deston's thread, untouched. Hana's seven cords, untouched. Just her. Just the meeting. Gone.

*Aimed*.

She sat in the bed and looked at the three percent for a long time.

***

Maren came in at noon with broth. She put it down on the table and sat on the end of the bed, with her bandaged hands in her lap.

"Corvin wrote," said Maren. "The king says rest." She looked at her hands. "And then he says, *tell her it turned to me, and I stood in front of the boy's tower and looked back at it, and it went back to facing the pass. Tell her it's all right. Tell her she was right to cut.*" She paused. "And then Corvin says the king sat down on the tower floor for an hour and didn't say anything, and Hyvred sat with him."

Thea nodded.

"And Hana slept fourteen hours," said Maren. "And Deston says the thread's fine and Gabe made him eat."

"Good."

Maren was quiet for a while. She looked at the window. The lake road going north.

"I felt it," said Maren. "On the cord. Under my hand. The cut." She turned her bandaged hands over in her lap, palm up, and looked at them. "I thought it would be big. When you said you'd learned to stop. I thought it would be like slamming a door. I held my hand as flat as I could, so it wouldn't burn me." She was very still. "It wasn't big. It was the smallest thing I've ever felt. Like a needle. In exactly one place. And everything stopped."

Thea didn't say anything.

She'd decided, at the lake, on Ars's step, with the hens round her feet and Maren's burned palms in linen at his table, that she wouldn't tell her. That it was Maren's to find. That if she said *it's not more, it's aimed*, and was wrong, she'd have taken it away from her; and if she said it and was right, she'd have taken it away from her too.

She didn't tell her now.

Maren looked at her palms for a long time. At the bandages, and the place under them where the skin was new and pink and shiny from a night in the summer when she'd tried to be louder than she'd ever been, and burned the curtain off the militia-hall window.

"A needle," said Maren. Quietly. To her hands.

Then she got up, and put the broth into Thea's hands, and went out; and Thea heard her on the outside stair, going down, slowly, one step at a time, the way she read.

She drank the broth. She read the channel. Three days of it; she read every line.

In the evening Gigichi helped her down the outside stair, and across the yard, and up onto the trough, where she'd sat after the king's *good girl*, and they sat there together with their backs to the militia-hall wall and looked at the lake road going north in the dusk. Higu came out with two cups of tea and gave them one each and sat on the step. Xola came out with the blanket. PenguChi came out with the penguin, which got onto Thea's lap and stayed.

At the end of the lake road, a long way off, past the last of the town, a single lamp came on in the window of a cabin.

Thea looked at it.

She hadn't written to him. She didn't need to. He'd read every line in the channel for three years from the end of a jetty, at night, not answering. He'd have read all of it. *No right arc*. *Something looked*. *I'm not doing that again.* He'd be sitting on the last plank right now with a rod across his knees, not fishing, mostly, and a red hen against his hip; and he'd have read it, and put it away somewhere, the way he put things away, and gone on not going.

*Not yet*, he'd said, a fortnight ago, at the lake, when she told him about the shape in the west. Not to her. To himself. She'd heard it anyway.

"He knows," said Gigichi, beside her. Not a question.

"He knows," said Thea.

The lamp in the cabin window burned, small and yellow, at the end of the road.

Thea drank her tea. Three percent. A needle. Thirteen rings and a call and no way home, written in people at the foot of a pass. A girl with blue hands, the last word of the last line, still wet.

*Read it*, she thought. *Close your door. And then, one day, write the other arc.*

She didn't know how. She didn't know if anybody could. In the hall they'd drawn it and left it blank for three hundred years.

But she'd written *TODO* in it, once, in the second summer, in blue ink no native could read, on a floorboard in Valcrest.

She hadn't forgotten.
