By the third month, there were children in Valcrest wearing paper horns.

Winter saw the first pair on a boy of about seven in the fish market: two black cones of folded paper tied under his chin with string, sticking up from his head at a jaunty angle. He was running between the stalls, roaring, chasing a smaller girl with a wooden sword. When he saw Winter he skidded to a stop, stared at her with his mouth open, and then roared at her too, delighted, as if she'd understand.

Winter didn't know what to do with her face.

By the end of the week there were a dozen of them. By the end of the month there were sashes in the market in Group 7's colours, which nobody had chosen and which were, as far as Winter could tell, black and lavender and red, with a little white mask stitched on the end. There was a song about Newish and the cabbage. There was a song about Hako's spear, which Hako pretended to hate and hummed constantly. In the Broken Lance, off-duty guards who'd crossed the street to avoid them in the first week now bought Ina drinks and asked him, very politely, whether they could touch the mask.

"Second act," said Lucan, beaming, every time he walked into the inn. "Second act, my friends. Didn't I tell you?"

He had told them. Winter would give him that. He'd said that the crowd would hate them, and then love them, and he'd been right about both.

She trusted the second part much less than the first.

***

"You're thinking it again," said Ysolde.

They were in the practice yard behind the Lists, in the cold early morning, before the others were up. Winter had started coming out alone at dawn in the second month. She told herself it was to train. Mostly it was because the yard was empty, and nobody was looking at her, and she could stand in the middle of it and breathe without wondering what anyone wanted.

Ysolde had started coming out at dawn in the second month too. Winter suspected that was not a coincidence.

"Thinking what," said Winter.

"That they'll turn again." Ysolde was sitting on the fence, sharpening a practice blade that didn't need sharpening. Her ram's horns caught the first light. "That the same people throwing flowers this month will be throwing stones next month. That it's only a matter of time."

Winter didn't answer, which was an answer.

"They might," said Ysolde, after a while. "Crowds do. They threw flowers at me for three years when I was champion, and then I lost one bout to a boy from the coast, and they threw something else." She examined the edge of the blade. "Frightened people are stupid. I told you that."

"You did."

"Adoring people are stupid too. Just in a nicer way." Ysolde set the blade down. "You don't have to trust them. You just have to know the difference between them and the people who stay."

Winter looked at her. At the ram's horns, and the scarred hands, and the woman who'd come out to an empty yard at dawn for a month without ever once saying why.

"And how do you know?" she asked. "Which ones stay?"

Ysolde shrugged. "You wait," she said. "And you see who's still sitting on the fence."

***

The trouble came in the eleventh week, on a bright cold afternoon, in the middle of the Street of Armourers.

The five of them were walking back from the yard. Hako was carrying his spear over his shoulder and accidentally knocking things off stalls with it. Chris was explaining, to nobody in particular, a theory about the way aura progress related to sleep. Newish was eating a pastry she'd been given for free by a baker who'd heard the cabbage song. Ina was beside Winter, a little behind the others, with his mask hanging at his shoulder, and they were talking about nothing much, which was the thing she'd come to like best about him.

He never asked her for anything. That was it, she'd realised, after a month of trying to work out why he didn't make her skin crawl the way most men did. He talked to her the way he talked to everyone: honestly, with his whole attention, and without wanting anything back. He'd never once tried to stand closer than she wanted. He'd never told her to smile. When she didn't feel like talking, he didn't talk, and it was a comfortable silence instead of an expectant one.

They were the two people in Valcrest who got stared at most, and some days that was all they had in common. Some days it was enough.

"*Demon.*"

The voice came from in front of them. A young man had stepped out of an armourer's doorway into the middle of the street: tall, fair, beautifully dressed, with a sword on his hip that had never been used and a little knot of friends behind him who looked as if they'd come to watch something.

He was looking at Ina.

"That's what they're calling you, isn't it?" he said. His voice carried. He meant it to. "Before the songs. Before the cabbage. Demon. And now the whole city's making paper horns for its children." He stepped closer. "My name is Aldric Vane. My father sits on the King's Council. And I don't think a thing that wears a demon's face should be paraded through the Lists while the real demons are counting our forts."

The street had gone quiet. Stallholders had stopped. A woman with a basket of apples had frozen with one in her hand.

"So," said Aldric Vane. "I'm challenging you. Here. Now. Steel, not wood. Let's see what's under the mask."

Winter felt it before she knew what it was.

It started in her hands. The cold. Not the cold of the morning, but the other cold, the one she'd felt when she reached for mana: like putting your hand in snow until it starts to burn. It came up through her wrists and into her chest, fast and sharp, and she realised she was angry. Properly angry, the way she hadn't let herself be in months. Angry at this boy in his beautiful clothes, at the way he'd said *thing*, at the knot of friends behind him smirking, at the street holding its breath.

Next to her, a stallholder's cup of water on the counter went white.

She saw it out of the corner of her eye. Frost. A thin skin of it, spreading across the top of the water in a rush, feathering up the sides of the cup. It lasted two heartbeats. Then it was gone, melted, and the cup was just a cup.

Nobody else had seen. Nobody was looking at the cup.

Except, she realised, Ina. He'd glanced at it, and then at her, and for a fraction of a second something passed across his face. Not surprise. Something closer to *ah*.

Then he turned to Aldric Vane, and smiled.

"No," he said pleasantly.

Aldric blinked. "*No?*"

"No." Ina's voice was exactly the same as it always was: calm, unhurried, British, the kind of voice that made people go quiet to hear it. "I won't fight you. I'm an Initiate. I can't cast a spark. You've got a sword your father bought you and friends to tell you you're wonderful. If I fought you, you'd win, and you'd have proved that a man with a sword can beat a man without one. Which everyone in this street already knew."

Somebody laughed. It was quickly smothered.

"But you asked what's under the mask," said Ina. "That's a fair question. I'll answer it."

He reached up, very slowly, and lifted the grey mask from his shoulder. He held it up so that the whole street could see it: the grinning fanged face, the red-rimmed eyes, the red cords hanging from its mouth. And then he turned it around and held it out to Aldric Vane, empty side first.

"Nothing," said Ina. "It's a mask. I put it on when I need to be braver than I am. That's all it's ever been." He tilted his head. "I'll take mine off for you any time you like. I wonder if you'd take off yours."

There was a long, perfect silence in the Street of Armourers.

Then the apple-woman laughed. Loudly, helplessly, a big honk of a laugh that she tried to stifle and couldn't. And then a stallholder laughed. And then somebody started clapping, and then half the street was clapping, and Aldric Vane's friends were very suddenly finding reasons to be somewhere else.

Aldric stood there for a moment longer, red to the ears. Then he turned and walked away down the street without another word.

Ina put the mask back on his shoulder. His hand, Winter noticed, was trembling very slightly.

"That was terrifying," he said, conversationally, to nobody. "I'd like to sit down now."

***

He sat down on the edge of a fountain at the end of the street, and Winter sat down next to him, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"You saw the cup," she said finally.

"I saw the cup."

"I didn't mean to do it."

"I know." Ina turned his head and looked at her. "It's all right. I feel it too, sometimes. With the mask on. Like a coal in my chest that somebody's breathing on." He smiled faintly. "Mine's heat. Yours is cold. We're very well matched."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little bit funny."

It was, a little bit. Winter didn't smile. But something in her chest loosened, the way it had in the arena when the crowd split. She looked down at her hands. They were ordinary hands again. Not cold. Just hands.

"Why didn't you fight him?" she said.

Ina was quiet for a moment, watching the water in the fountain.

"Because that's what he wanted," he said. "That's what everyone always wants, when they call you a demon. They want you to act like one. If you fight, you've proved them right. If you run, you've proved them right." He shrugged. "So I tried to do something they didn't expect."

"You told him to take his mask off."

"I told him the truth. It tends to surprise people."

Winter looked at him for a long time: at the white hair going lavender at the ends, the single purple horn, the green eyes, the hand that still wasn't quite steady on his knee.

"Thank you," she said. "For not making me fight him either."

Ina's eyebrows went up. "I didn't know you were going to."

"Neither did I," said Winter. "That's why I'm thanking you."

***

That evening, at the Broken Lance, two letters came at once.

The first was for Lucan. He read it at the bar, and then read it again, and then made a noise that Winter could only describe as a strangled wail, and the whole common room turned to look.

"He wants to run it," said Lucan. "*Drew.* Your Drew. He's sent me a bracket." He held it up: a sheet of thin paper covered in charcoal lines and boxes and arrows. "He wants to run *my* tournament. With a *mixed round.* Random teams, one from every group, so they all fight beside people they've never met." He stared at the paper. "That's... that's actually brilliant. That's better than anything I'd have done. How *dare* he."

"So say no," said Newish, grinning.

"Say *no?*" Lucan clutched the bracket to his chest. "I'm going to say yes, and then I'm going to make him tell me how he thought of it, and then I'm going to steal it for every tournament I run for the rest of my life." He sat down heavily on a stool. "Oh, I'm going to *love* this man. I hate him already."

The second letter was for all of them. It was from Thornwall.

Chris read it aloud by the fire, and nobody interrupted him.

Coldwater. Two more houses empty. Tables set, a child's wooden horse dropped by a door. An old woman shelling peas who said everyone went visiting, at night, in the hills. The new ones in the miller's house, who didn't keep dogs, because the dogs didn't like them. Tracks going up the sheep path and none coming down. And Yan's letter from the east, copied out in Hyvred's hand: eleven scouts on the Redmarch, counting the towers of Cinderhold one by one, and one of them cold like the healer's grave.

*Someone wearing someone, in the west. Someone wearing someone, in the east. Two sides.*

When Chris finished, the common room had gone quiet around them. Not just their table: the off-duty guards at the next one, and the armourer at the bar, and the barmaid with a tray of cups in her hands. Nobody in the Broken Lance could read the English on the page. But they'd heard Chris's voice change.

"Two sides," said Hako softly. For once he didn't say it would work out.

Winter took the letter from Chris when he'd finished and read it again herself, slowly, every word. Then she read it a third time.

She'd been in Vietnam when Ice died. Two months, mostly offline, in a town with bad signal and good noodles, and she'd come back to find a stack of messages she couldn't bear to scroll through, and a friend who'd already been gone eight weeks. Everyone else had known for two months. Everyone else had already cried, and been to the calls, and started to get used to it. She'd been the last to know.

She'd promised herself, after that, that she'd never be the last to know anything again.

So now she read every letter. Every one that came to the Broken Lance, from every group, twice, sometimes three times, until she knew them by heart. She knew Thea's numbers and Yan's scouts and Deston's threads and Azac's shield. She knew about Rachael's hands glowing in the infirmary and Bee's sparrow and the old woman's peas.

And she knew, sitting there by the fire with the letter in her lap and the common room silent around her, that something was coming. From the east, and from the west, and from underneath, in the dark, where Group 6's Hollow was spitting up things that shouldn't be there. And that thirty-five people at the bottom of a ladder weren't going to be ready.

"Eight," she said, out of nowhere.

The others looked at her.

"My number," said Winter. "Eight. I didn't say it last night. It went up after the street." She put the letter down. "I want to know everyone's. Every night. Not just ours. Everyone's. I want to know how fast we're going."

Chris looked at her for a moment. Then he took his notebook out of his coat, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote *ALL GROUPS* across the top in his small square hand.

"Seven," he said. "Hako's seven. Newish is seven. Ina?"

"Eight," said Ina, from beside her.

Chris wrote it down. Then he looked up at the rest of the room: at the guards, and the armourer, and the barmaid with her tray, all still watching them.

"It's fine," he told them, cheerfully. "We're just doing maths."

Nobody believed him. But one by one, they went back to their drinks.

***

Ysolde was sitting on the fence when Winter came out to the yard at dawn the next day. She didn't look up. She just moved over, slightly, to make room.

Winter climbed up and sat beside her.

They didn't say anything for a long time. The sun came up over the Lists, and the city woke up below them, and somewhere a child was already roaring in paper horns.

"He didn't fight," said Ysolde eventually. "The oni. I heard."

"No."

"Good." Ysolde turned her blade over in her hands. "Fighting's easy. Anybody can fight. It's knowing when not to that takes training." She glanced sideways at Winter. "And the cup?"

Winter went very still. "How do you know about the cup?"

"The apple-woman told the baker, who told the barmaid, who told me." Ysolde almost smiled. "It's a small city. Everybody's somebody's cousin."

Winter looked down at her hands.

"It was two heartbeats," she said. "That's all. I was angry and it just happened."

"Then don't be angry," said Ysolde, "and make it happen anyway. That's the work." She set the blade down. "It'll take you months. Maybe a year. Maybe longer. And one morning you'll come out here and do it on purpose." She paused. "I'll be on the fence."

Winter didn't say anything. She didn't trust herself to.

But she stayed sitting on the fence beside the goat-horned woman until the sun was all the way up, and when she finally climbed down and went into the middle of the empty yard to breathe, she knew exactly who would still be there, watching, when she turned around.
