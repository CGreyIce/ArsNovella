Azac had been putting it off for two weeks.

He wasn't proud of that. He'd said *later* on the mayor's terrace, the second day, with everybody watching, and Declan had looked at him for a long time and said *later* back, and Azac had felt it settle between them like a promise. And then he'd let it sit there. Every day. Every evening at the fire, with the question in his mouth like a stone, and every evening he'd swallowed it and gone back up the lake road to the inn and lain in a bed that was too short for him with his feet out of the end and stared at the ceiling.

There wasn't a crack in this ceiling. He'd looked. He'd got used to having a crack to look at.

He knew why he was putting it off. He wasn't stupid. He'd just never been any good at the bit before the hit. In a game, in the old days, he'd stand at the front and wait for the boss to wind up and his hands would sweat on the keyboard and his heart would go, and he'd *hate* it, the waiting, every second of it. And then the hit would land and he'd be fine. He was always fine once it landed. It was only ever the waiting.

Asking was the waiting. He'd been waiting two weeks.

On the fifteenth morning he watched Xav make Declan move twice.

He was sitting on the wall at the top of the shingle, with the town, in the mist. He saw the stick go out and find nothing, and the whole beach go silent, and the stick come round again. He saw Declan lower it and say *once* and Xav say *okay, again*, and he saw Declan laugh and pull Xav in by the back of the neck. And something in Azac's chest went tight and warm and stupid, watching his friend standing there in the mist with his face doing nothing and his eyes doing everything.

He'd known Xav for years. Since a Discord call, the first night, when Xav had been the one who ran round corners. He'd never seen him look like that.

That evening, at the fire, Azac didn't swallow it.

***

He found Declan at the nets.

Not on the jetty, for once. Round the side of the cabin, in the last of the light, where the nets were hung on poles between the cabin wall and the fence to dry. Declan was sitting on an upturned crate with a net across his knees and a wooden needle in his hand, mending a hole the size of a dinner plate. Something big had gone through it. Something with teeth, by the look of the edges.

"The pike," said Declan, without looking up. He always knew who was there. "The one Xav nearly had. He came back last night and went straight through my good net." He pulled a knot tight. "I think he's making a point."

Azac stood by the fence.

He was the biggest of the five of them. He'd always been the biggest. On Earth he'd been the one people asked to carry things, and the one bouncers looked at twice. Here he'd got bigger. Twenty months of an outfitter's shield and a guild master's drills had put muscle on him he hadn't known he had room for, even on the mage's path. He stood by the fence in the dusk and felt enormous and clumsy and about twelve years old.

"Sit down," said Declan. "You're blocking my light."

Azac sat down on the grass with his back against the cabin wall. The hens came round the corner, saw him, and settled in a row along the fence, the way they'd started doing whenever any of the fifteen sat still for long enough. They didn't do it for Declan. They never did it for Declan.

"Later," said Declan, still mending. "You said later. On the terrace."

"Yeah."

"It's later."

"Yeah," said Azac. "I know."

He looked at his hands. They were big, and scarred across the knuckles from the shield straps, and they were sweating. The way they'd sweated on the keyboard.

He thought: *it's just the waiting. You're always fine once it lands.*

"Do you want it back?" said Azac.

***

Declan's needle stopped.

He didn't look up. He sat there with the net across his knees and the needle stopped halfway through a knot, very still, the way he went still when he was listening to something a long way off.

"Want what back?" he said. Carefully. As if he already knew.

"The spot," said Azac.

It came out easier than he'd thought. Once it had started.

"The front. In everything. Every game. Every raid. Every party we were ever in, for years, you stood at the front. You took the first hit. You'd say *don't worry, I've got this, trust*, and then something completely untrustworthy would happen, and you'd laugh about it in the call after." He looked at his hands. "And then you didn't wake up. And there was nobody at the front. And we tried to play without anyone in it, for a few weeks, and it was wrong. Like a missing tooth. So I stood in it."

"I know," said Declan quietly.

"Not because I was good at it. I wasn't. I was terrible, the first year. I just couldn't stand looking at the gap anymore." Azac swallowed. "And then I kept standing in it. Three years. Here too. First day in the Hollow, Garrick said *front*, and I was already there before he'd finished the word. And I kept going there. Every time. Even when I shouldn't. Even when Garrick said *back*. Because it was the spot. Because somebody had to be in it, and it had to be me, because it had been you, and you were gone, and I couldn't..."

He stopped.

"I went forward in the Hollow when Garrick said back," he said. "The first autumn. A thing came out of the wall that split shields like paper and I planted my feet in front of it, at Initiate, with a shield I'd bought from an outfitter, because that was where you'd have been. And it opened me up from here to here." He touched his side, through his shirt, where the scar was. "A month in bed. Rachael says I should have died. Mother Annick says I nearly did, twice, in the first night, and Malcolm held it shut with both hands until the healer came."

Declan had put the net down.

"I worked it out," said Azac. "Lying there. I worked out I'd been standing there for the wrong reason. Because the spot had to be full, not because anybody needed me in it. And Petra asked me in the yard, after, would I go in front again. And I said yes. But because I trusted them behind me. Not because the spot had to be full." He looked up. "And that was right. That was better. I've been doing that for a year and a half. In the Hollow. In the Narrows. It's been good."

"I know," said Declan. "I heard. The shield in the Narrows. Malcolm's thing going into it."

"Yeah." Azac took a breath. "And then I got off a barge, and walked down a road, and you were there."

The hens shifted along the fence.

"You're *here*," said Azac. "You're not gone. You're right here, and you're the strongest person in the kingdom, probably, except the king. You make Gabe sit down with a *stick*. And I'm standing at the front, in your spot, at Initiate, with a guild shield. And I keep thinking..." He stopped. Started again. "I keep thinking, it was only ever borrowed. I kept it warm. And now you're back. So I should give it back. That's what you do with a thing you borrowed. You give it back when the person comes home."

He looked at Declan.

"So," said Azac. "Do you want it back?"

***

Declan didn't answer for a long time.

He sat on the upturned crate in the dusk with his hands on his knees and looked at Azac. Azac couldn't hold on to his face. Nobody could. But he was learning, the way all fifteen of them were learning, to read the rest of him. The shoulders. The breathing. The stillness.

The stillness had changed.

"Can I tell you why I stood there?" said Declan.

Azac blinked. "What?"

"At the front. In the games. Can I tell you why I did it?"

"I know why you did it. You were good at it. You were the best of us."

"No," said Declan. "I wasn't. You've got it backwards. You always had it backwards. All of you." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "I stood at the front because I couldn't stand the waiting."

Azac stared at him.

"In CS I waited," said Declan. "Because Xav needed somebody to show him how, and it's a game where waiting wins, and I'd worked that out. But everything else? Every raid, every dungeon, every stupid party with all of you at two in the morning?" He shook his head. "I couldn't bear it. The bit before the pull. Everyone standing there, buffing, checking, Malcolm going *wait, wait, wait, has everyone got their cooldowns*, and the boss just standing there. I'd sit at my desk and my hands would sweat and my heart would go and I'd *hate* it. Every second. So I'd go in. Before anybody was ready. Before Malcolm finished his sentence. Just to make it *start*. Because once the first hit landed, I was fine. I was always fine once it landed."

Azac didn't say anything.

He couldn't. His mouth had gone dry.

"*Trust*," said Declan. "*Don't worry, I've got this, trust.* That's what I said before I went in. Every time. Do you know why?"

Azac shook his head.

"Because I didn't. I didn't have it. I never had it. I was scared every single time and I couldn't stand being scared, so I ran at it." Declan almost laughed. "That's not a tank, Azac. That's a man who can't sit still. You lot thought I was brave. I was just impatient."

***

The light was almost gone. Somewhere down on the shingle, somebody had lit the fire, and Azac could hear Calvin laughing at something and Gabe telling him to shut up. Somewhere further off, the lake, against the posts.

"And then I came here," said Declan.

He looked at the nets.

"And I stood at the front of everything for twelve years. On my own. Garrison walls. The Redmarch. Places past where the maps stop. Every fight on the border for twelve years, I was at the front, because I was the strongest, and because I still couldn't stand the waiting." He was quiet a moment. "And there was nobody behind me. Do you understand? Nobody. There was a whole army behind me, sometimes. But nobody *behind* me. Nobody I'd turn round and look at and know they'd hold if I fell. Nobody who'd say *move* and I'd move." He breathed out. "It's not brave, standing at the front on your own, Azac. It's not. It's just lonely. It's the loneliest place in the world."

Azac looked at him.

"You stood in front of a thing in the Hollow at Initiate," said Declan, "with a guild shield. Knowing it could kill you. Knowing, Azac. Not thinking it was a game. Not thinking there'd be a respawn. Knowing. And you did it anyway, because the people behind you were your people." He leaned forward. "That's braver than anything I ever did in a game. Braver than most of what I did here."

"I did it for the wrong reason," said Azac. "That time."

"And then you worked out the right one. Lying on your back staring at a crack. On your own." Declan's voice had gone very quiet. "Do you know how long it took me to work out the right one? Years. A girl with cold hands telling me off three times in one month. And a ditch." He shook his head. "You did it in five weeks."

***

"So, no," said Declan.

Azac looked up.

"No, I don't want it back." Declan picked up the net again, and the needle, and found the knot he'd been halfway through, and pulled it tight. "It was never borrowed. You didn't keep it warm for me. You *made* it. It's not my spot that you're standing in. It hasn't been for years. It's yours. You stand in it differently than I ever did. You stand in it because you trust the people behind you. I stood in it because I didn't trust myself to wait." He tied off the knot. "Yours is better."

Azac sat against the cabin wall with his big sweating hands in his lap.

"Keep it," said Declan.

"But you're..."

"I'm here. Yes." Declan didn't look up from the net. "And I'm going to tell you something I need you to hear, and I need you to really hear it, because I think it's the thing you actually came round the side of this cabin to ask, under the other thing." He tied another knot. "When you stand in that spot, you are not filling a hole. There isn't a hole. There never was one, not the way you thought. And if there was, it's gone now. I'm right here. I'm sitting on a crate mending a net that a fish went through on purpose."

Azac found that his eyes were stinging.

"You're not standing in for anybody," said Declan. "You're just standing. Like Gabe. It's allowed."

***

Azac didn't say anything for a long time.

He sat in the dark against the cabin wall, with the hens in a row along the fence and the fire going on the shingle below, and Declan mending the net beside him by feel now, because it was too dark to see the knots. And something that had been sitting in Azac's chest for three years, since a phone call on an ordinary morning, since a gap on a team sheet, since a crack in a ceiling, very slowly came loose.

"I thought you'd want it," he said at last. "I thought you'd say yes. I'd planned what to say. I was going to say *okay*, and give it back, and then go and stand somewhere else, and be fine about it."

"Would you have been?"

"No," said Azac. "Not even a little bit."

Declan laughed. Out loud, in the dark.

"I know," he said. "I've known you for years. You'd have been miserable. You'd have stood at the back for a week, sulking, and then the first time anything came at us you'd have been in front of me before I'd stood up, and I'd have had to pretend not to notice."

"Yeah," said Azac. "Probably."

"Definitely." Declan put the net down. "Get up. Bring your shield."

***

They went down to the shingle.

The fire was going, and everybody was round it, and everybody looked up when they came down the side of the cabin, the big one and the fisherman. Azac with his shield on his arm. Declan with nothing in his hands at all.

"Don't mind us," said Declan.

He walked past the fire to the edge of the light, where the shingle went down to the water, and turned round, and looked at Azac.

"Stand there," he said. "Feet like you would. Like something's coming."

Azac stood. He didn't have to think about it. Feet apart, left forward, weight down, the shield up and angled, the way Garrick had drilled him and the Hollow had finished teaching him. He'd stood like this a thousand times.

"Good," said Declan. "Now. In the Narrows. Malcolm put something into your shield. What did it feel like?"

Azac thought about it.

He'd thought about it a lot, actually. He hadn't told anyone. The gorge, and the things coming down the cliffs head first, and the barge swinging toward the rocks. And Malcolm, behind him, white as paper, writing something in his window with a shaking hand, and then, all at once, the shield on his arm going *heavy*. Not heavier. *Heavy*, the way the ground is heavy. As if it had put down roots.

"Like it was holding on to something," said Azac slowly. "Not to me. To the ground. Through me." He frowned. "And then Deston did his thing, and everyone's threads lit up, and it was like... like everyone's stuff was going *through* the shield. Malcolm's. Calvin's wind, a bit. Rachael's, when she was behind me. All going through it. Into the ground. And I was just..." He stopped. "In the middle."

Declan was looking at him very intently.

"That's it," he said. "That's your spot."

Azac didn't understand.

"Not the front," said Declan. "I thought that was the spot too, for years. It isn't. The front's just where you stand. The spot is the place where everybody else's things meet. Malcolm's hold. Deston's threads. Calvin's wind. Rachael's hands. They all went to *you*. Into your shield. Through you, into the ground. You were the place they met." He tilted his head. "I never had that. Nobody ever put anything into me. I stood at the front alone. You stand at the front with nine people's magic going through your arm."

He stepped back.

"I'm going to hit the shield," he said. "Not hard. Don't brace against it. Don't be a wall. Walls break. Be a jetty post." He nodded at the dark water behind him. "The water hits a post all day and the post doesn't fight it. It just stands there and lets the water go round, into the lake bed, and it's still there in the morning. Let it go through you. Into the ground. Like you did in the gorge."

Azac set his feet.

Round the fire, nobody was talking anymore.

Declan hit the shield.

He didn't use anything. No stick. Just the flat of his hand, open, against the face of the shield, the way you'd slap a door. It didn't look like anything.

It went through Azac like a wave through a jetty.

He felt it. All of it. A weight like the lake itself, coming into the shield and up his arm and into his shoulder, enormous, more than he'd ever felt in his life. In the Hollow, the thing that split shields like paper had felt like a hammer. This felt like a *tide*. And every part of him wanted to brace. To lock his arm and plant his feet and fight it, the way he'd fought everything that had ever come at the front.

He didn't.

He let it go. Down. Through his arm, through his chest, through his hips and his legs and his feet, into the shingle, into the lake bed, into the ground under everything. Like water round a post.

And it went.

All of it. It went down through him into the dark ground and spread out and was gone, and he was standing there on the shingle with the shield up, exactly where he'd been, with his feet exactly where they'd been. Not a pace back. Not an inch.

There was a ring in the shingle round his feet. A ring of pebbles pushed outward, a hand's width, perfectly round, as if something had dropped onto the beach from a great height right where he stood.

***

Nobody said anything.

Then Calvin said, "*What.*"

Azac lowered the shield. He looked down at the ring of stones round his feet. He looked at his arm. It didn't hurt. It didn't even ache. It felt the way it felt after a long swim: heavy, and warm, and very, very alive.

He looked at Declan.

Declan was looking at the ring in the shingle. Then at Azac. And something in the way he was standing, Azac thought, was the way Xav had looked that morning in the mist, with his face doing nothing.

"How hard was that?" said Azac. His voice came out strange.

"Hard enough," said Declan. "I'd have knocked Gabe into the lake with that. I'd have put most of the guards in Valcrest through a wall."

Behind them, at the fire, Gabe said, "*Hey*."

"You'd have gone in the lake too," said Declan. "Yesterday. If you'd braced." He looked down at the ring of stones. "You didn't brace."

"You said be a post."

"I did," said Declan. "Most people can't. Most people hear *be a post* and they think *be a wall*, because that's what front means to them. I did, for twelve years." He looked up at Azac. "You heard *post*. You let it through."

Azac stood there.

He opened his window. He didn't decide to. He just did, the way they all did, without thinking, there on the shingle in front of everyone. And looked at the bar. And it had moved. Not to the edge. Nowhere near. But more than it had moved in a month, in one heartbeat, the way Deston's had moved before the Narrows, and Calvin's before the wind. The way, he'd heard, everybody's moved, just before.

He didn't say the number. He didn't need to. He closed the window.

"It's not the front," he said slowly. "The spot."

"No."

"It's where everybody's stuff meets."

"Yes."

"And then it goes into the ground."

"And then it goes into the ground," said Declan. "And you're still standing there in the morning." He picked up a pebble from the ring at Azac's feet and turned it over in his fingers. "It was never mine, Azac. I never once stood in it. I didn't even know it was there. You found it. In a gorge, at Initiate, with a guild shield, with nine of your friends behind you." He put the pebble in Azac's hand and closed Azac's fingers round it. "Keep it."

***

They went back to the fire.

Somebody passed Azac a bowl. Somebody else, Rachael, came round and took his arm without asking and held it in both her warm hands for a long moment, listening, the way she did, and then let go and said *don't make it weird* and went back to her place, wiping her eyes. Malcolm, across the fire, had his IF/THEN book open on his knee and was writing something very fast, and when he saw Azac looking he held it up so Azac could read it in the firelight.

*IF the shield is where everything meets*

*THEN everybody stands behind Azac.*

Azac laughed. It hurt a bit, the good kind of hurt.

He sat by the fire with the pebble in his fist and his shield beside him on the shingle. And Declan sat down next to him, the way he'd sat next to Xav on the jetty that first morning. Shoulder to shoulder. Not saying anything.

After a while, Azac said, "I'm still going to be in front of you. You know that. Whatever happens. At the Lists. On the road. Whatever comes. The first time anything comes at us, I'm going to be in front of you before you've stood up."

"I know," said Declan.

"Even though you don't need it."

"I know."

"Even though you could knock me into the lake with your hand."

"I know, Azac."

Azac looked at him sideways.

"Does that bother you?" he said.

Declan was quiet for a moment. He looked into the fire. Then he looked round it, slowly, at all of them. At the fifteen, and his daughter, and the three who'd come up the river. At the people he'd stood in front of, alone, without knowing it, for twelve years on a border and eight on a jetty.

"No," he said. "It's the best thing anybody's said to me in this world."

He bumped Azac's shoulder with his own.

"Trust," he said.

"Don't," said Azac, immediately. "Don't say that. Every single time you say that, something terrible happens."

Round the fire, everyone who'd ever been in a call with Declan at two in the morning said, at the same time, with total conviction:

"*Every* time."

Declan laughed so hard he nearly fell off the log. And Azac sat beside him, in front of nothing, behind nothing, just next to him, with a pebble in his fist and his shield on the shingle, and laughed too, and found that for the first time in three years there was nothing at all sitting in his chest.

He'd been fine once it landed. He always was.

It was only ever the waiting.
