Alea woke on the day with Higu's moon chart against her chest.

She'd slept with it there for two months. Folded in four, in the inside pocket of her coat, over her heart, since the last full day in Valcrest, when a small calm girl with three moths round her head had put it into her hands at the end of a long table and said *look up*. She'd taken it out every night on the road east and looked at it by the fire. She'd taken it out every night at Cinderhold and looked at it on the wall. Higu's tiny careful drawings, in charcoal and then ink: a moon for every night since the first week at Wrenhaven, two years of them, going round and round. And at the end, in fresh ink, three more. Full, and then full, and then full. And under the last one, in Higu's small neat English, the only words on the page:

*The night. An hour after sunset. East, over the red.*

She lay in her cot in the women's barracks in the grey before dawn with Wendy asleep in the next one and Rhea snoring at the far end like a bellows, and held the chart against her chest through her shirt, and didn't open it. She didn't need to. She knew what it said.

*Today*.

***

She went up on the wall first. She always did.

Her father was already there. Of course he was. In the gap, at the eleventh merlon north of the gate, sitting on the parapet with his legs over the drop and the rod leaning against the stone beside him, the way he'd sat every morning since a rider brought him a letter. She came along the wall walk, straight, through the place where the sentries' trough in the stone used to bend away and didn't any more, because Wat had walked through it and then everybody had, and sat down beside him.

They didn't say anything for a while.

The sun came up over Kharvos. The red went gold, all of it, all the way to the mountains. The markers in their long rows caught the light on their tops, like the teeth of a comb. And a long way out, on a low knoll above a dry river, a small tree stood up very bright beside a dark stone, with its leaves just beginning to turn at the edges.

"Today," said Alea.

"Today," said her father.

She looked at the tree for a long time. She'd looked at it every morning and every evening for five weeks. She'd said *hi, Mum* to it in her head, every morning, and *goodnight* every evening. She'd never once gone out there. She'd said she wouldn't. *On the night*. *With you*. *Not before*.

She'd kept it. Five weeks. It had been the hardest thing she'd ever done, harder than the final, harder than standing in the ring with her heels on the chalk and Sorrel's hook on her sword. Every morning, seeing it there, an hour away, and not going.

"You kept it," said her father. As if he'd heard her.

"I kept it."

"Eight years, I kept you from it." He was looking at the tree too. "You kept yourself from it five weeks." He was quiet. "I don't think I could have."

"You could have," said Alea. "You did it eight years."

"That was different."

"How?"

He thought about it. She felt him think about it. She couldn't see his face; nobody could. But she'd been reading the rest of him all her life, and she felt him think about it, the shoulders and the breath and the hands on the stone, slowly, all the way down, the way he thought about a pike's water.

"I was keeping you from something," he said. "You were keeping something for her."

Alea didn't say anything.

She put her head on his shoulder, in the gap, in the sunrise, and they sat there until the bell rang for breakfast in the courtyard below.

***

The day was very long and very short.

She'd known it would be. She'd stood in a tunnel under the east stand of the Lists at noon on the last day, waiting to walk out, and the hour had been a year and a breath at the same time. This was like that, all day.

She cleaned Astra.

She didn't need to. Oakes had re-stitched the harness in the first week, the left strap that was coming loose, and grumbled the whole time about who'd done the sharkskin, and then said, grudgingly, *well, he's got good hands, whoever he is*, and she'd said *Gabe*, and Oakes had said *hm*. The blade didn't need cleaning; nothing ever marked it. She sat on the step of the forge in the morning with the black sword across her knees and a cloth in her hand anyway, and ran the cloth down the flat, and down, and down, the way her father had run his thumb over the letters on the grip the day the king gave it to her.

*Astra*.

She'd named it for where it was going. *Ad astra*. Her father's words, cut into the headstone's other half nine years ago by the grip in a language nobody in this world could read, and still there, on her half, the letters crooked and scratched with the point of a knife, one gone over twice. The other half had different words. Four, in English, clean and even, cut with a king's chisel. She'd read them aloud on the shingle the day he finished them. She'd never seen them in their place.

"It's clean," said Salty, from the second anvil, where he was hammering something that didn't need hammering.

"I know."

"You've cleaned it six times."

"I know," said Alea.

Salty looked at her for a long moment over the anvil. Then he went back to hammering, and didn't say anything else, which from Salty was very kind.

***

At noon Maigo found her and gave her a pot.

A small clay pot, the kind the fort's kitchen kept herbs in on the windowsill, with earth in it, and in the earth, a stick. A thin green stick about the length of her hand, with three leaves on it, the edges just beginning to go gold.

"It's for you," said Maigo.

He stood in front of her in the courtyard with his hood down and his antlers full of leaves, turning gold at the edges like the leaves in the pot, and his pixel sunglasses pushed up, and his white glowing eyes very serious.

"It's from the tree," he said. "Her tree. I took a cutting in the spring. Before Valcrest. I grew it in the barracks window all summer." He was quiet, for Maigo. "So you can take it home. To the lake. So there's one there too." He looked at the stick in the pot. "So she's got one at both ends."

Alea looked at it.

She didn't say anything. She couldn't. She took the pot in both hands, very carefully, the way you'd hold a bird, and looked at the three leaves on the thin green stick, and her ears went flat and then up and then flat.

"I'm going to tell the tree tonight," said Maigo. "That I gave you one of its children. Trees like being told things." He swallowed. "And I'm going to tell it everything else tomorrow. When you've—when it's morning." He looked at her. "Is that all right? Can I tell it about you?"

"Yes," said Alea. Her voice came out very small. "Tell it everything."

***

They left an hour before sunset.

All of them. Out of the east gate, under the horns, onto the red. Alea and her father first, with Astra on her back and the rod on his shoulder and nothing else, because he said there was nothing else you needed. And behind them, a long way behind, the way her father had asked, Group 3. Aldous with his book and something white folded over his arm. Rhea, very quiet, for Rhea. Salty and Wendy. Niko with his hands in his pockets. Maigo. And Yan, with the scythe on his shoulder and Suzu wound round its shaft.

And the wall.

She looked back once, at the gate, without meaning to. The whole east wall of Cinderhold was full. Every embrasure. Every merlon. Soldiers and cooks and grooms and the old woman from the laundry and old Oakes in his apron and Pim the stable lad and Teague over the gate with his cap off. All of them. Standing along the top of the wall in a long silent row in the gold light, the way Wendy said they'd stood when the black sword went out, two years ago, and when it came back.

Not saying anything. Just watching them go.

Her father didn't look back. But she saw his shoulders go, a little. The way they went.

And at the eleventh merlon north of the gate, in the gap, there was somebody standing. Bent nearly double. Leaning on a spear. Old Wat, who was eighty and still on nights and had walked straight through the gap one morning a fortnight ago as if the path had never bent. Standing in it now. In the place where her father had stood for an hour nine years ago with his hands in the stone.

Wat lifted his shaking hand.

Not to his chest. Not a salute. He just lifted it, a little, the way you'd lift your hand to somebody going down a road you knew they'd come back up.

Alea lifted hers back.

Then she turned round, and walked out onto the red beside her father, toward the place she'd looked at every morning for five weeks.

***

The red went quiet round him.

Wendy had told her about it. She'd seen it herself, once, at the old south line, from the edge. She'd never been inside it before.

She was inside it now. Walking beside him through the markers, close, so close their sleeves touched. And round them both, in a ring a few paces across, the grey grass stood still. Didn't shiver. Didn't make the sound. The long dry *hush*, like somebody in another room telling somebody else to be quiet, that she'd heard every night for five weeks through the walls of the barracks: it stopped. Round them. As they walked. And came back behind them, slowly, the way the sound comes back into a room after somebody's left it.

She'd thought it would feel like something. Like walking in a bubble, or behind glass. It didn't. It felt like the jetty. Like sitting on the end of it at dusk on a still evening, when the lake went flat and you could hear a fish turn over at the far end.

It felt like home.

"Does it always do that?" she said.

"Since the morning after," said her father.

"Why?"

He walked a while before he answered. Through the markers. Past the names burned into the wood, and then the posts with no names.

"I don't know," he said. "I've thought about it for nine years. Aldous has an idea and won't say. Yan says the ground knows me." He was quiet. "I think it's tired. I think it's been hurting for nine years and it's very tired, and when I walk on it, it remembers that I'm the one who made it red. And it stops. For a bit. To see what I'll do."

Alea looked at the grey grass, standing still round their feet.

"What do you do?"

"Walk," said her father. "Slowly. And don't do anything else."

***

She saw the knoll before he said anything.

She'd seen it every day for five weeks from the wall. She'd known what it would look like close. Wendy had told her. Maigo had told her, the first night out of Valcrest, by the fire, all of it, the tree and the stones and the ring. Her father had told her, on a riverbank under a willow, *you can see it from the cairn if you look east*.

It wasn't like any of that.

It was a low round hump in the red above a long dry gully where a river had been once. A line of dead grey trees along the gully's edge, bare and bleached, like old bones. One of them fallen, a long time ago, lying grey and smooth along the ground at the foot of the knoll like a bench. And the knoll going up from it, gently, red, with grey grass on it, the same as everywhere.

And then, at the top, green.

A ring of it. Round the top of the knoll. Green grass, real grass, soft, the colour of the bank of the Wren in the spring, in a perfect ring about twenty paces across, with nothing but red outside it. And in the green, flowers. Small ones. White, and blue. Low to the ground, the way flowers grow on hillsides that get a lot of wind. Still flowering, at the end of summer, when nothing on the whole of the red had flowered in nine years.

And in the middle of the green, a cairn. Stones piled up long and low, the length of a person. Grey field stones, and red stones off the plain, packed close and neat, by somebody who'd known how to build a cairn so it would stand.

And at the head of it, a stone.

Black. Upright. Taller than she was. Rounded at the top, broad and flat in the face, the shape of a shield, or a door. Black iron. The light of the low sun lying across the face of it soft and still, the way light lies on the lake at dusk when there's no wind. Facing west.

And beside it, a tree.

Young. Slender. An ash, taller than Salty, taller than Rhea, with a smooth grey trunk and a crown of leaves just beginning to turn gold at the edges. Growing up out of the green, a pace from the black stone, leaning toward it a little, the way a person leans toward somebody they're talking to.

Alea stopped.

***

She stopped at the foot of the knoll, by the fallen tree, and couldn't move.

Her father stopped beside her. He didn't say anything. He didn't take her hand or put his arm round her or tell her it was all right. He stood beside her in the ring of quiet with the rod on his shoulder and let her stand there, the way he'd let her sit on a riverbank under a willow with her hands over her face. The way you let a fish run.

Behind them, a long way back, she heard Group 3 stop too. At the edge of the markers. Not coming on.

She looked at it.

At the black stone. At the tree. At the green. At the small white and blue flowers moving a little in the wind off the mountains, the only things moving on the whole of the red. At a cairn the length of a person.

Nine years.

She'd been eight. Nearly nine. She'd been at the cabin, with Rowan in the door and Odette in the kitchen, and her mother had gone east with her father because the king called everybody and she was the best healer on the border and she'd never once let him go anywhere on his own. And she'd made a drawing the night before, at the kitchen table, of the three of them on the end of the jetty with too many planks. *Come home soon*.

And her father had come home in the last week of summer, across the fields, alone, with nothing on his shoulder.

And her mother had been here. The whole time. Nine years. An hour from a fort on the edge of the red, under a cairn, in a ring of flowers that came back every spring when nothing else did. While Alea learned to read, and fish, and hold a sword, and grew up, and went to the Lists, and wrote her name on a roll in front of forty thousand people. Here. On her own. With a sword over her and then a stone.

Not on her own. Alea looked at the tree. *So she wouldn't be alone out here*.

Her ears had gone flat. All the way flat.

Her hands came up in front of her chest.

Not palm to palm. The way Hesper had shown her, in the infirmary, standing in the doorway with his back to her. Just the fingertips. Pressed together, very lightly, the way you'd hold something very small between them.

She tapped them.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

"Well, then," said Alea. Very quietly.

And went up.

***

She stopped at the edge of the green.

She didn't know why. It felt like the edge of something. Like a doorstep. Red earth under her boots, and then, a step on, grass. Soft. Real. She looked down at it, at the place where the red stopped and the green began, a line as clean as a line drawn with chalk. And looked up at the black stone, close now, ten paces away, with the low sun on it.

At the letters.

They were near the top. She'd known they would be; she'd watched him cut them, on the shingle, all one day, with the king's chisel, in the autumn of her seventeenth year. Four words in English, in the plain capitals he'd taught her on a slate with a nail the winter she was thirteen. She'd read them aloud on the shingle that evening, with the king standing behind her not able to.

She read them aloud now.

"*To Diwa*," said Alea. "*My love*."

Her voice didn't do anything. She'd thought it would. It came out quite plain and steady, the way it had come out at the Lists, on the first day, when she asked Lucan to leave the box blank.

She looked at the L. The first stroke of it. Gone over twice. Cut a little deeper than all the others, a little wider, where his hand had gone back to it once, at the end of the day, when everything else was done. She'd seen him do it. She'd never asked why.

She stepped onto the green.

***

It was soft under her boots. That was the first thing. After five weeks of stone and red earth and wall walk, soft. She walked across it, slowly, between the small flowers, white and blue, careful not to step on them, to the foot of the cairn.

The children's stones were there.

In a ring. Round the foot of the cairn, inside the green, small stones the size of a child's fist, set close, each one touching the next. Grey field stones. Brown river stones. All different. She knew them. She didn't know them; she'd been at the forge on the lake shore the week they came, with the king at the anvil, but she'd been watching the sword, not the pile. But she knew them anyway. Wrenhaven. Every child in Wrenhaven had brought one, down the lake road, to the forge, in the autumn. The Hollin brothers. The miller's boys. Edric's girl. A whole town, carrying her mother's stone.

And at the foot of the black stone, in the middle, where the ring began and ended, a white one.

Small. Flat. Smooth as an egg. Lake-white, the white of the stones on the shingle below the cabin, that she'd skipped across the water every summer of her life.

And beside it, a dark one, slightly scorched, like a piece of a hearth.

She knelt.

She knelt at the foot of the cairn, in the green, inside the ring of children's stones, with Astra on her back, and looked at the white one.

"Wendy put it there," she said. "At the head. Where I'd see it first."

"She did," said her father.

He'd come up behind her. She hadn't heard him. She never did. He was standing at the edge of the green, where the red stopped, a pace back. Not stepping on. Not yet.

"I asked her who put it on the pile," said Alea. "On the first night out of Valcrest. She didn't know." She didn't look round. "You went still."

Her father didn't say anything for a moment.

"I did," he said.

Alea waited.

"The night before the dray went east," said her father. Very quietly. "With the stone on it, and the sack. Garrow had the pile in the forge, ready, all the children's stones, a heap of them on the hearth-wall." He was quiet. "I went down to the end of the jetty. In the dark. And I took my boots off and went into the water. Off the end. Where it's deep enough to come up to your chest, in the autumn, and cold."

Alea's ears had gone very still.

"Where she stood," said her father. "The day she said *here*. The jetty was half a ruin then. She walked out on it, on the planks that were left, all the way to the end, and stood there, and looked at the lake. And said *here*. And I looked down, past her feet, into the water." He breathed. "There was a white stone on the bottom. Right under the end. I saw it. I remember thinking, *that's a good stone*. I don't know why I thought it." He was quiet. "I never once in twelve years went and got it."

"Until the night before the dray."

"Until the night before the dray," said her father. "I went in up to my chest and felt for it with my feet in the dark. It was still there. Twelve years. Right where she'd stood." He looked at the white stone at the foot of the cairn. "I put it on the top of the pile. And didn't tell anyone. Because it was hers. Not mine."

Alea looked at it.

Small and white and smooth as an egg. From under the end of a jetty. From under the place where her mother had stood the day she chose a lake.

She reached out and touched it. With two fingers. The way you'd touch something to see if it was warm.

It was.

***

He stepped onto the green.

She heard him. A step, onto the grass. Then another. He came and knelt beside her, at the foot of the cairn, inside the ring of children's stones, slowly, one knee and then the other, the rod laid down on the green beside him. And put his hand in his coat.

He took out a stone.

Another white one. Small and flat, from the lake shore. Not as smooth. A little chipped at one edge, the way a stone is chipped when it's been kept in somebody's apron pocket a long time.

"Mother Breck's," said her father.

Alea looked at it.

"In the square," said her father. "The morning we went south. She was on her stool. She held on to my hand and pulled me down and gave me this, and said one thing in my ear that nobody else heard." He turned it over in his fingers. "*She'd have wanted to see this*."

Alea didn't say anything. She couldn't.

"She was the only one in Wrenhaven who ever said her name to me," said her father. "After. Once. In the market, the first winter. Just the name. *Diwa*. And then went on buying her onions." He was quiet. "She brought us a pie the first week. When we came. She stood at the door and looked at your mother's ears, and her hands, and you hiding behind her skirts, and said *well, you'll want feeding*." He looked at the stone. "She's past ninety. She'll never come this far."

He leaned forward, and set it on the top of the cairn. In the middle. Where it would catch the light.

"She'd have wanted to see this," he said. To the cairn. "So I brought her."

***

Then Astra.

She'd thought about it for two months. Every night on the road, every morning on the wall. What she'd do with it. *She'll be there*, she'd said on the riverbank. *At the grave. With the other half. They haven't been in the same place since the cut*. And her father had said *I don't know what it does when it's two things in the same place*, and she'd said *we'll find out*.

She stood up.

She reached back over her shoulder, the way she'd reached back ten thousand times on the ice and the shingle and the sand, and took the grip. Old leather. The strip a nineteen-year-old had cut from his own saddle in Kharvos and wrapped round a sword he found standing in the ground on a hill, a long way past where anybody's maps went. Sharkskin over it now, where Gabe had re-done it. A fisherman's knot at the end. And she drew it, out of the harness, over her shoulder, in one long easy line, the way you'd lift a rod.

Her mother's half of a black sword. In the low gold light. Half a slab of black iron that a king had drawn narrower and given a new point, with four crooked words scratched by the grip with the point of a knife, one letter gone over twice.

She turned to the black stone.

The other half. Taller than she was. Broad and flat in the face, rounded at the top, the old point of the same sword drawn out into a long spike under it and driven down into the same hole it had stood in for seven years. Four words on it. One gone over twice.

They'd lain side by side on Garrow's anvil, the morning of the cut, orange in the noon light, not quite touching.

Alea stepped up to the head of the cairn, beside the stone, on the green. And held Astra out in front of her, point down. Above the grass. A hand's width from the black face of the stone.

And put the point in the ground.

***

It rang.

She felt it before she heard it. Through the grip. Through her hands and her arms and her chest and the soles of her feet on the green. A note. Coming up out of the black iron in her hands and out of the black iron beside her at the same moment, the same note, one note, from two places, as if the two halves had both been holding it in for a year and let it go at once.

High. Clear. Long. Like a bell.

It went out over the red.

She heard it go. Across the knoll, and down off it, and out over the plain in every direction at once, the way a sound goes out over still water. Over the markers. Over the gullies. Over the rise, an hour out, with its empty ring. Out to the mountains, and off them, and back. She'd learn later that they heard it at the fort, an hour away. That every soldier on the wall turned their head at once. That Wat, at the eleventh merlon, put his hand on his chest without knowing he'd done it.

Her father had gone very still beside her.

"That's the note," he said. Very quietly. "That's the one. On the shore. When it parted." He was looking at the two halves. Astra, upright in the green with her hands on its grip; the black stone beside it. A hand's width apart. Not touching. "It made that sound when it came apart. And it hasn't made it since."

The note went on. And on. Ringing in Alea's hands, in the grip, in her chest, in her teeth. Not getting quieter. Holding. One note, the same note, out of both halves, as if they were the same thing in two places.

And under her feet, something went down.

She'd never forget it. She'd never be able to say what it was. Under the green, under the cairn, under the earth, somewhere deep, something that had been there the whole time, very still, went *further*. Deeper. The way a boat settles when you step off it onto the jetty. The way a breath goes out of somebody who's been holding it.

The way a dog lies down.

And the note stopped.

***

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Alea stood at the head of her mother's grave with her hands on the grip of her mother's half of a black sword, point down in the green, a hand's width from the other half, and found she was shaking. All of her. She hadn't noticed when it started.

"What was that?" she said. "Under us. Something—"

"I felt it," said her father.

"What was it?"

He didn't answer at once. She felt him decide. She felt him think about not telling her, the way he'd not told her a hundred things, on a hundred evenings, to keep them off her shoulders. And she felt him not do it.

"There's something under her," he said. "Cold. Yan's felt it since his first week. He showed me, yesterday, from the rise." He was very steady. "It isn't her. Yan was sure. It's under her. Something they put in, that didn't go when she did." He looked at the black stone. "The sword held it down for seven years. Point down, in that hole. And then the spike. Yan says it's like a nail through it."

Alea looked at the two halves. At the ground between them.

"And now?"

"Now it went deeper," said her father. "When they rang. Both of them. In the same place." He was quiet. "I don't know what that means. I don't know what it is. Neither does Yan." He looked at her. "But it went down. Not up."

Alea looked at Astra in her hands. At the five crooked letters by the grip. *Ad astra per aspera*. Her father's words. And beside it, four clean ones. His too.

She didn't take it out of the ground.

"I'm leaving it there," she said. "Tonight. Next to her. While we're here." She swallowed. "They haven't been together since the anvil."

Her father looked at the two halves of his sword, standing side by side in the green, in the last of the light, a hand's width apart. One with his old words on it. One with his newest.

"No," he said. "They haven't."

***

Aldous came at sunset.

Alea heard him on the slope. Slow, the way an old man climbs. With his book under his arm, and over the other arm, folded, the white cloth. He stopped at the edge of the green, where the red ended, and didn't step on. He stood there in his old chaplain's coat with the sun going down red behind him in the west, over the fort, over the downs, over everything, and looked at her.

Not at her father. At her.

"With your leave," said Aldous.

Alea looked at him. A grave old man with a long face, who'd buried eleven hundred and four people on this plain with names and three hundred and nineteen without, and read twenty of them every morning before dawn, and never once known her mother's name until a soldier's story on the red a year ago. Who'd written it into his book on the morning they came, on a rise, in fresh ink.

She nodded.

He stepped onto the green. He came to the foot of the cairn, carefully, between the flowers, and stood there. And unfolded the cloth.

It was very long. Plain white linen, old and soft and very clean, the kind of white Alea had only seen on a few things in her life: snow on the lake in the first week, before anybody walked on it. A white snake. He shook it out, the way you'd shake out a sheet, and it went up in the evening air and down again, slowly, and lay over the cairn. Over the stones. Over the children's ring and the white one at the head and the scorched one beside it and Mother Breck's on the top. All of it. White. From the foot of the cairn to the foot of the black stone, which stood up out of the end of the white like a door standing up out of snow.

Then he opened his book. He didn't look at it. And he began to say the words.

***

Alea didn't understand them.

Nobody did, any more. Her father had told her: the church tongue, older than the kingdom, older than the one before it, that only old chaplains like Aldous still knew. It didn't sound like words. It sounded like the Wren in the spring, under the ice, when the thaw's coming: low and slow and going on and on, the same shapes coming round again and again, rising a little and falling. Aldous said it with his eyes half shut and his book open in his hands and his long grave face perfectly still. Not loudly. The way you'd say something to somebody asleep in the next room, so as not to wake them, and so they'd know you were there.

She stood at the head of the cairn beside the black stone and Astra, and listened.

Her father stood beside her. He didn't say anything. He didn't move. He stood with his hands at his sides and his head a little bowed, looking at the white cloth over the stones, and Alea felt him not move for a whole hour. Felt him listen. To words in a tongue he didn't know either, said over a woman he'd buried here with his own hands nine years ago with nobody to say anything over her at all.

*She liked chaplains*, he'd told Aldous, in the chapel, the first night. Alea had heard about it from Hesper, who'd heard it from Aldous, which was how everything went round in Cinderhold. *She said they were the only men in the fort who'd sit with somebody dying and not try to fix it*.

Aldous didn't try to fix anything. He just said the words, over and over, low and slow, with the sun going down behind him.

At some point Alea's hands came up in front of her chest. The fingertips. She didn't decide to. She tapped them, very lightly, once and twice and three times, in time with something in the old words that came round and round. And again. And again. The whole hour.

She didn't say *well, then*. There wasn't anywhere to go in to. She was already in.

***

The sun went down.

Aldous stopped. Not suddenly. The words came down the way the Wren comes down to the lake: slower, and slower, and then just the sound of the water, and then not even that. He closed his book.

He stood at the foot of the cairn for a moment longer. Then he bent, slowly, and took hold of the end of the white cloth, and began to fold it back. Off the stones. Off the children's ring. Off the white one at the head. The way you'd fold back a blanket in the morning from somebody who's woken.

He folded it in eight. He held it against his chest.

He didn't say anything to her father. He looked at him once, across the cairn, and her father looked back, and Alea felt something go between them, the way it went between her father and the king. Something two old men knew that didn't need saying.

Then Aldous looked at her.

"Your mother had the rites," said Aldous. Very quietly. "Now."

He bowed. To her. Not to her father. A small bow, an old chaplain's bow, the kind you'd give at the door of a house where somebody had died. And then he went back down off the green, carefully, between the flowers, and down the slope to the fallen tree at the foot of the knoll, where Alea could see, in the dusk, the others sitting. In a row. All seven of them, on the long grey trunk, with their backs nearly to the grave. Waiting.

***

Then it was just the two of them.

Her father sat down. On the green, at the foot of the cairn, with his back against nothing and his knees up and his arms loose round them. The way he sat on the end of the jetty. And didn't say anything.

Alea sat down beside him.

The sky went from red to grey to blue over the red plain, slowly, and the first stars came out over Kharvos. The wind dropped. The grey grass on the red below the knoll stopped whispering, all of it, everywhere, not just round them; the whole plain went quiet, the way it went quiet sometimes at dusk, Wendy said, for a quarter of an hour, as if it were listening for something.

Alea looked at the black stone.

"Hi, Mum," she said.

Out loud. For the first time. Not in her head on the wall. Out loud, at the foot of the cairn, in the dusk, in a voice that came out plain and steady and a little bit small.

"It's me," said Alea. "It's Alea." She stopped. "I came."

***

She'd thought she'd start with the box.

She'd said so, on the riverbank. *I'm going to tell her about the box*. She'd thought about it for two months. How she'd say it. In order. The Lists, and the roll, and the box left blank all week, and Lucan holding out the pen. And the sunset on the last day, and forty thousand people, and the words.

She didn't start with the box.

"I've been packing the good knife," she said. "Since I was twelve. In the side pocket. Where you put it." Her voice was very steady. "He never noticed. He thought he remembered. He told you he'd remembered, every year, all the way here." She looked at the stone. "You'd have laughed at him the whole month."

Beside her, her father made a sound. She didn't look at him.

"I pack it in front of him now," said Alea. "He said thank you."

She told her.

Not in order. Nothing in order. Everything. The way it came. The heron that she'd told her father about on the jetty the morning she started talking again, the year after. The first fish she caught on her own, a perch, very small, that she'd made him put in a bucket and keep as a pet for three days until it died and she'd cried for a week. *HELLO* on the cabin wall in charcoal. The slate and the nail and the twenty-six letters, the winter she was thirteen. The sword. The yes, after a month of no. The ice, every morning for a year, and nothing. Going through the ice in the spring. Drawn, on the end of the jetty at dawn. Five girls at the top of the road, and a year of going up there every day and not telling him, and him knowing anyway, and leaving two fish in a basket on the step of the militia hall every morning so she'd have something to take. Tempered, in the militia hall yard, in front of them all.

And a sword coming home in a canvas bundle on his shoulder through the town at dusk. *Half of it. Half for her; half for your mother*. And a king at the door with a roll of hammers. And a shingle, and a forge, and a note that went out over the lake. And four words, cut all day with a chisel. *TO DIWA, MY LOVE*. And the L gone over twice.

She told her about Astra. Standing in the green beside her now.

She told her about the road south, and the Lists, and the box.

"It was blank," said Alea. "All week. *Alea, of Wrenhaven*, and then nothing, where it says father and mother. Everyone else had something in it. Sorrel didn't. Sorrel's dad's at the bottom of the sea." She was quiet. "And on the last day I took the pen and I wrote it. *Daughter of Diwa, a healer*." She swallowed. "And then *and of Ars*. In that order. You first."

She looked at the stone.

"Lucan read it out," she said. "At sunset. In front of everyone. Forty thousand people. *Daughter of Diwa, a healer*." Her voice went, a little, on the last word, and came back. "They all heard your name. All of them. Everyone in Valcrest knows your name now, Mum. It's in a song."

She stopped. She had to.

Beside her, her father had his hand over his eyes.

***

"I nearly lost," said Alea, after a while. "The final."

The stars were out properly now. All of them. Thick and bright over Kharvos, the way they only were out here, with nothing between you and them.

"There was a girl with two boat-hooks. From the coast. Sorrel. She pulled me across the ring by my own sword. I had my heels on the chalk. I was going to go over." She was quiet. "And then I thought about a pike."

She told her about the cow pike. In the reeds, at the north end of the lake, in the spring. That took his line on a misty morning, and instead of running, the way every pike in the world ran, turned. And came straight at him. Straight at the jetty. So fast the line went slack. And went under the jetty and through his good net and out the other side and was gone.

"He laughed till he sat down," said Alea. "And then he said to me, *you know who that was? That was your mother*." She looked at the black stone. "*That's exactly what your mother would have done*."

Beside her, her father took his hand away from his eyes.

"So I did it," said Alea. "In the final. I gave her the sword. Like a fat worm. And when she pulled, I didn't pull back. I came at her. All of me. Faster than the line." She swallowed. "Like a pike. Like you."

She was quiet.

"He says you used to do it to him," she said. "In arguments. He'd have you on the line and he'd pull, and you'd just come at him all at once, and the line would go slack, and he'd lose. Every time. For ten years." Her ears went flat, and up. "He says he never once worked out how you did it."

"I never did," said her father, very quietly. To the stone. "I still haven't."

"I won," said Alea. "By a hair. With your trick." She put her hand flat on the green, beside the children's stones. "I didn't learn it from him. I didn't learn it from a pike." She looked at the stone. "Dad says I learned it from you. The pike just reminded me."

***

The light went.

Not all of it. The stars stayed. But the last of the day went out of the west behind the fort, and the red went dark, all of it, from the knoll to the mountains. Alea sat at the foot of the cairn and looked east.

At the rise.

She could just see it. A long low shape against the stars, an hour out, a little south. Darker than the dark round it. She couldn't see the ring. She knew it was there. Her father had knelt in it yesterday, Yan said, and put his hand on it, and said *that's done*.

She looked at it for a long time.

She'd said she'd look. On the riverbank. *I'm going to stand there, at the grave, with her half on my back and Mum's half in the ground, and look east at the rise*. She'd thought it would be the hard thing. The thing that proved something.

It wasn't. It was just a rise. A hump in the dark, an hour away, where something had been done that the ground hadn't forgiven. Her mother wasn't there. Her mother was here, under the stones, with a tree leaning toward her and a white stone from under the end of a jetty at her head.

"She's not there," said Alea. Quietly. To her father.

"No," he said.

"I looked."

"I know."

She took the moon chart out of her coat.

She didn't open it. She held it, folded, against her chest, the way she'd held it every night for two months. And looked at the rise. And above the rise, at the dark edge of the world, where the mountains of Kharvos went up black against the stars.

*An hour after sunset*. *East, over the red*.

*Look up*.

She looked up.

***

The moon came up over Kharvos.

Very slowly. First a line of light along the top of the mountains, thin and white, like the edge of a coin. Then the edge of it. Then more. Gold, at first, the way it always was low down, and enormous, the way it only ever looked out here with nothing between you and it. Coming up out of the mountains behind the rise like something being lifted very carefully out of water.

Full.

All of it. Round and gold and huge, standing on the black line of the mountains for a moment as if it were resting there, and then going on up. Lighting the red. All of it, at once. The plain from the mountains to the fort silver and rust and grey, and every marker in its row with a thin line of light along its top, and the rise, black, with the moon behind it, and the ring on it suddenly visible, a dark circle on a silver hill.

And the knoll. The green. The flowers, white and blue, gone silver. The tree. The cairn. The white stones, shining.

And the black stone, and Astra beside it. Both of them. The moonlight lying across their faces the way light lies on still water.

Alea sat at the foot of her mother's grave and looked up at the full moon rising over the red, and couldn't breathe.

*Higu*, she thought. *You worked it out. On a chart. Two years of moons, since your first week. For this*.

*I looked up*.

***

She stood up.

She didn't decide to. She just found she was on her feet, at the foot of the cairn, in the moonlight, facing the black stone. With her father still sitting on the green beside her, looking up at her.

There was one more thing. She'd known all along there would be. She'd known since a riverbank under a willow, since a road east, since a well-head in Ashby with a borrowed pen. She'd known since she was nine.

Her mother had given her father words. At the end. On the rise, in the ring, when she was half gone and still knew him. Words for Alea. He'd carried them home across the fields in the last week of summer, alone, with nothing on his shoulder, and sat down at the kitchen table across from a nine-year-old girl, and said them to her. Every one. In order.

She'd never said them back.

She'd never said them at all. Not out loud. Not once in nine years. They were the only words in the world she'd never been able to say, because they weren't hers to say. They were hers to be *given*.

She looked at the stone. At the four words on it. *TO DIWA, MY LOVE*.

"*Stay healthy*," said Alea.

Her voice came out very clear in the moonlight. Not shaking.

"I am," she said. To the stone. "I'm Tempered. Gabe fixed my grip. Oakes says I'm too thin for the sword but I'm not." She breathed. "I'm healthy, Mum."

"*Live well*."

"I'm trying." Her voice went, a little. "I am. I've got a lake. I've got a basket on a step, and five girls at the top of a road, and a coast girl who writes terribly, and a tree in a pot. I won the Lists." She stopped. "I'm living well."

"*Listen to your father*."

Beside her, on the green, she heard him make a sound.

"I do," said Alea. "Mostly." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "He's slower now. I told him to be. He listens to me too, now. Sometimes." She looked down at him, sitting on the grass with the moon on him and his face that nobody could see turned up toward her. "He brought me. This year. He asked."

She looked back at the stone.

"*I'm sorry*," said Alea.

She stood there for a long moment.

"Don't be," she said. Very quietly. "You don't have to be. You did everything. You came east because they needed you and you went into the tent every time and you counted to three and you went in." She tapped her fingertips together, once, twice, three times, without meaning to. "And you asked him. On the rise. You asked him so he wouldn't have to decide. And you gave him words for me so I'd have something." She swallowed. "You don't have to be sorry, Mum. Not for anything."

The moon went up. The red was silver all the way to the mountains.

"*And I love you*," said Alea.

She couldn't say anything for a long time after that.

Then she said it. The last one. Back. Out loud, in the moonlight, at the head of the cairn, to the black stone with four words on it and a white one from under the end of a jetty at its foot. In the plain clear voice of a girl who'd written her mother's name on a roll in front of forty thousand people.

"I love you too."

***

Her father stood up.

She heard him. On the green, behind her. She didn't turn round. She stood at the head of the cairn with her face wet and her ears flat and the moon on her, and felt him come up beside her. And stop. And not touch her. Not yet.

He put his hand in his coat.

He took out something folded in four. Soft, along the folds. Brown at the edges. She knew it before he opened it.

He opened it. Along the old folds. Carefully, in the moonlight, the way you'd open something that might tear. A lake, in blue wax pressed on hard. A jetty, with too many planks. Three people on the end of it, holding hands. A fish with a smile.

*MUM DAD ME*.

*COME HOME SOON*.

He held it up. In front of the black stone. In the moonlight. So that the four words on the stone and the six on the drawing were side by side, the way the two halves of the sword were side by side, a hand's width apart, in the green.

"Hesper kept it," he said. To the stone. Very quietly. "Nine years. In a book. He took it down off the wall the morning after, before anybody came in." He was quiet. "I thought it was gone. With the jars."

He held it there a long time.

"She put in more planks," he said. "Because you told her I was proud of my planks." His voice went, the way it had gone on the wall at Cinderhold with this same drawing in his hands. "They're very good planks, Diwa."

Alea took his hand.

She didn't decide to. She just found her hand had gone into his, the way it had gone into his on the wall at Wrenhaven once, in the dark, when she didn't know she was doing it and he'd frozen. He didn't freeze this time. His hand closed round hers. Warm. It was always warm. That had been him. Hers had always been cold, like her mother's.

They stood there. At the head of the cairn. In the moonlight. Holding a child's drawing up in front of a black iron stone, and each other's hands, and not saying anything.

*COME HOME SOON*.

*We did*, thought Alea. *We came. Both of us*.

***

They stayed all night.

He'd said they would. *A few days. At the grave. The way I always do*. Only this year there were two of them, and the green, and a fire Salty built at the foot of the knoll without being asked and then went away from, back to the fallen tree. And a blanket Wendy brought up and put down on the grass at the edge of the green and went away from too.

Alea lay on the blanket with her head on her father's arm, the way she'd lain on the first night out of Valcrest, and looked up at the moon going over.

She thought she wouldn't sleep. She'd thought that on every important night of her life, and she always had. She lay there and looked at the moon, and at the black stone against the stars, and at Astra standing beside it, and at the tree, which was leaning toward the stone, a little, the way a person leans toward somebody they're talking to.

"Dad," she said.

"Mm."

"Why did you go over the L twice?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"Because it's the first letter of *love*," he said eventually. "And I'd done it too fast the first time. I'd been cutting all day and I was tired and I did the first stroke of it too fast. Not wrong. Just fast." He was quiet. "And I looked at it, at the end, and I thought: that's the one word I'm never going to do too fast again."

Alea didn't say anything.

She turned her face into his arm.

***

Just before she slept, she saw a light.

At the foot of the knoll. At the fallen tree. Where the others were sitting, in a row, in the moonlight, with their backs nearly to the grave. A small light, low down. Green. Not like a lamp. Like a lamp somebody had shaded with a leaf. Very soft, very faint, the colour of new grass, or a leaf held up to the sun, or the eyes of a white snake.

It came and went. Once. Twice.

Alea lifted her head.

Her father had turned his. He was looking down the slope at it too. Very still. She felt him look.

"What's that?" she whispered.

He watched it a moment longer.

"Yan," he said. Very quietly. "Don't look." He put his hand on her hair. "It's his. Let him have it."

She didn't look.

She put her head back down on his arm, and closed her eyes, and the last thing she heard before she slept was the grey grass on the red below the knoll, all of it, everywhere, not whispering. Quiet. The whole plain, as far as you could hear. As if it had been told something, very gently, by somebody it knew, and had decided, for one night, to believe it.
