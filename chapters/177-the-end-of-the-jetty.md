Halvard had built the lake in his head a great many times, and he had got every part of it wrong.

He'd built it from reports. That was how he built everything. Nine years of them, in a drawer that wasn't the bottom drawer: Maren's monthly ones, in her small exact hand, that never said anything they didn't have to; the mayor's yearly two words; a page of a guard's book, read once, last week, at a scrubbed table in an inn at Hethe. Out of those he had built, over nine years, at night, at a desk, a lake. Large and grey and windswept, in summer, always in summer, with a long jetty going out into it and a cabin on the shore, very small, very far off, and a man on the end of the jetty with his back to the land.

He'd never once put himself in it. He'd always been looking at it from somewhere else. From a tower. From a wall.

It was not large. That was the first thing. He'd made it the size of the Redmarch, in his head, because it had swallowed a man the size of the Redmarch; and it was a lake. A long one, with hills on the far side gone blue in the dusk and reeds at the near end and a mist lying on the water. You could have rowed across it in an hour. It was not grey, either. It was black, and still, with the last of the light lying on it like a sheet of something poured.

And it was not summer. It was frost. Every blade of grass on the slope down to it white and stiff, and every stalk in the cut field, and every thorn on a ring of hedge at the bottom of the field that no report had mentioned. And the jetty was shorter than he'd built it. Much shorter. Plain grey planks on grey posts going out thirty paces into the black water, and stopping.

And the man wasn't on the end of it.

He was walking down the lake road beside him. Half a pace off. Not talking.

Halvard had imagined a great many things in nine years. He had never once imagined that.

***

They didn't talk on the way down. He'd thought they would. He'd thought, on the road, at night, in nine different inns, about what they would say, and had composed it, the way he composed things for council, and had arrived at the top of the lake road with nothing at all, and found when he got there that he couldn't move his feet.

The guard had told him about his feet. He'd known her thirteen years and never heard her say so many words together. He was still thinking about it. He found he was looking at them as he walked: his boots, on the frozen road, going down. Left and then right. As if somebody else had charge of them.

Beside him, the man's boots went down too. Unhurried. The same pace exactly. Halvard didn't know whether the man had slowed to his or whether they had always walked at the same pace and he'd forgotten. Twenty years ago, on the walls at Cinderhold, they had walked the circuit together most nights at the change of the watch, a court mage and a garrison boy, and he'd never once thought about whose pace it was.

He thought about it now. All the way down.

***

The hens met them at the gate.

He'd been warned. The guard had written it; the king had told him, on the road, at length, more than once, with an expression Halvard had not seen on the king's face since they were both young. *Three of them. The big red one leads. They hate him. He minds*. He'd thought it was a joke. Severin's jokes were mostly about hens.

It wasn't a joke.

The king was in the yard. Still on his horse, in the middle of the hen-yard, with the reins loose, looking extremely pleased with himself, and on his boot in the stirrup, which should not have been possible for a bird, sat a large red hen with her eyes half closed. The other two were on the gatepost. They watched the man come through the gate with a look of deep and patient hatred that Halvard recognised, after a moment, from the faces of the Lords of the Council when the king came in late.

Then they looked at Halvard.

He stopped in the gateway. He didn't know what else to do. He was a Comet-tier mage; there were perhaps four people alive who could do what he could do with his hands; he had stood in front of a demon general's host on a wall and not stepped back. He stood in the gateway of a hen-yard and did not move at all.

The smaller of the two came down off the gatepost. It walked round him. Slowly. Once, all the way, the way the guard walked round a room before the king went into it. It stopped at his left boot. It looked at the boot for a long time with one eye, and then with the other.

It pecked it. Once. Not hard. Considering.

And then it sat on it.

Halvard looked down at the hen on his boot.

"Severin," he said, without moving.

"She likes you," said the king, from his horse, delighted.

"How can you possibly tell?"

"She hasn't killed you," said the king. "Look at him." He nodded at the man, who had gone round the edge of the yard by the woodpile with his back to the wall and was watching the remaining hen on the gatepost the way you'd watch a man with a crossbow. "Nine years he's fed them."

The man said something under his breath in a language that wasn't any language Halvard knew, and that Halvard nonetheless understood perfectly.

Halvard stood in the gateway with a hen asleep on his boot. He found that something was happening to his face, and he let it.

*He wants to see the hens*, he had written. *So do I*.

He'd seen them.

***

The girl was at the cabin door.

He'd known she would be. He'd known what she would look like. He'd read about her for nine years; he could have drawn her. A fox demihuman, seventeen, tall for her age, with her mother's ears and her mother's tail, and a black sword, half a sword, in a harness on her back, and a knife on her left hip in an old sheath.

He hadn't known she would have her mother's face.

Nobody had told him. Maren's reports said *Alea is well* and *Alea reached the second tier* and *Alea is training with PenguChi every morning*, and they had never once said *she has her mother's face*, because Maren had never seen her mother's face. Halvard had. Twenty years ago, at Cinderhold, for years. A healer with cold hands in the infirmary tent who'd stitched half the garrison and argued with all of it. Who'd stitched a court mage's hand once, when he'd cut it on a map-case, and told him he was holding the knife wrong and so was everybody in this fort and she was sick of it.

The girl stood in the door with the lamp behind her and looked at him with that face.

He stopped at the bottom of the step. The hen got off his boot, offended, and went away.

She looked at him for a long time. He let her. He'd been looked at by a great many people in his life who had cause to look at him like that, and he had never once looked away, and he didn't now.

Then she put her hand on her chest.

Flat. Over her heart. The way the whole garrison had done it on the wall, the morning after, while a man walked in under the gate across the red with a black sword on his shoulder and didn't look up. The way Halvard had done it himself, on the wall, with the king beside him. He hadn't known anybody had told her. He hadn't known it had gone anywhere but that wall, that morning, nine years ago.

*It's so somebody's looking*, somebody had said, he'd heard. A stable boy, at the fort, this autumn. In the channel, by way of the cat girl.

He stood at the bottom of the step of the cabin by the lake and looked at the girl with Diwa's face with her hand on her chest.

And put his own on his.

They stood like that for a moment. Neither of them said anything. Then she took her hand away, and he took his, and she stepped back out of the doorway to let him in.

"You're getting the bed," she said. "Dad says. You don't get a say."

"I was told," said Halvard, "that I was folded. The wrong way. Twice."

Something happened in the corner of her mouth. Her mother's mouth.

"You were," she said. "I saw you get off the horse."

***

He didn't remember much of supper, afterwards. That annoyed him. He remembered everything; it was what he was for. He'd remember the order of business at a council meeting thirty years ago and who had coughed during which item. He sat at a table in a cabin by a lake, at the most important supper of his life, and afterwards he could remember almost none of it.

He remembered pieces.

The table. Plain, scrubbed, too long for the room, with a groan in one leg that made everybody jump when the king leaned on it. A red curtain with a gold fringe hanging crookedly in a window under the eaves, which the girl said was a tablecloth, and which was. A row of pegs by the door with a girl's coat on the third one that wasn't the fox girl's; a small chair by the hearth that nobody sat on. A bucket by the door, a child's bucket, painted blue, with nothing in it.

And over the table, on a pin, a drawing.

He looked at it a great deal. He couldn't help it. A child's drawing, in wax crayon, gone soft and brown at the edges. A big blue lake. A jetty going out into it with too many planks, every one of them drawn separately, very carefully, as if somebody had told the child to put in more. And three people on the end, holding hands: a big one with a fishing rod and a fish on the line bigger than he was, smiling; and two with orange triangles on their heads and tails.

*MUM DAD ME*, it said, over the top.

And under it, crammed in at the bottom because she'd run out of room: *COME HOME SOON*.

He knew where it had been. He'd read it in the infirmary report, nine years ago. *The healers' table overturned. All jars broken. Nothing salvageable*. He'd signed that report. He'd thought there was nothing left.

"Hesper kept it," said the man, beside him. He'd seen Halvard look. "In a book. Nine years."

Halvard didn't say anything.

"Count the planks," said the man.

Halvard counted them. He couldn't help that either.

"Forty-one."

"The real one's got thirty-four," said the man. "She was eight. Diwa told her I was proud of my planks." He looked at the drawing. "I was. I am."

And the stew. He remembered the stew, because it was very bad. A fish stew, red and sour and hot, that the man put in front of him with an expression Halvard could see and nobody else at the table except the king could, which was the expression of a man who knew exactly how bad it was and was going to watch you eat it anyway. *It's supposed to be something else*, the girl said. *From where he's from. It isn't*. The king ate three bowls and said it was the best thing he'd eaten since the last time, and the hen on his knee ate some of his bread.

And the man's face.

That most of all. That was the thing he would remember, afterwards, when he'd forgotten the order the dishes came in and what the king said about the weld on the pot-hook. Sitting across a table in lamplight from a face he'd last seen at the eleventh merlon of the east wall at Cinderhold, the whole of an hour, doing the thing a face does when its owner is holding on to a stone so hard the stone breaks. He'd seen it every night since. In the dark, behind his eyes, before he slept, if he slept. Nine years.

It wasn't doing that.

He watched it. He couldn't stop. Across the table, in the lamplight, the man listening to his daughter tell the king about a pike. The way the face went still and then not still, the corner of the mouth, the eyes on the girl. Older. Lined at the eyes in a way it hadn't been. Thinner. Brown from a summer on a road. A face he had known since it was fifteen, scowling at him over a parapet, doing the most ordinary thing in the world. Listening to somebody it loved talk about a fish.

He was one of two people at the table who could see it. The girl couldn't. She'd lived with it seventeen years and never once been able to hold it; Maren had written that, the first year, and he'd read it a dozen times. *Nobody here can see his face. Not even the girl*. And she sat beside it now, talking, waving her hands, with her father's face turned toward her doing that, and couldn't see it.

He wanted, very much, to tell her.

He didn't. It wasn't his to give. He sat across the table and looked at it for both of them, and ate the terrible stew, and didn't remember any of the rest.

***

"Come and see the end of the jetty," said the man.

It was late. The king was on the rug by the fire, flat on his back, having refused the bed with a great deal of noise, with the red hen asleep on his chest and his hands folded on his stomach under her and his eyes shut, pretending to be asleep so that nobody would make him move. The girl had gone up the ladder to the loft. The guard was on the step outside the door; Halvard had seen her settle there, with a blanket, and a sword across her knees, and her book on the boards beside her, open, with nothing written on the page.

Halvard got up.

His knees told him about the horse. He ignored them. He took his coat off the peg. And then, after a moment, he went to the saddlebags on the floor by the bed, and knelt, which his knees also told him about, and undid the buckle on the left one, and took out the bundle.

The man watched him do it. He didn't say anything.

They went out past the guard on the step. She didn't look up. She moved her boots, very slightly, to let them past, the way you'd move your feet in a narrow corridor, and went on looking at the lake. And they went down the shingle in the dark to the jetty, and out along it, with the man carrying the lamp, and the boards hollow and grey and cold under their boots, thirty-four of them, Halvard counted, and stopped at the end.

There was nothing at the end. No rail. No bench. Just the last plank, and the black water under it, still, with the cold coming up off it, and the far shore gone, and the stars.

The man sat down on the end of the jetty with his legs over the water.

Halvard looked at the drop. Not far. The height of a man, perhaps, to the water. He thought about his knees, and the horse, and his age, and the dignity of the Advisor to the Crown of Aurelan.

He sat down on the end of the jetty with his legs over the water.

***

For a long time, neither of them said anything.

He'd known they wouldn't. He'd known it on the road. They had never said much, the two of them; it had never been how they were. Twenty years ago on the walls at Cinderhold they had walked the circuit at the change of the watch most nights for two years and he could remember perhaps a dozen things either of them had said. The boy had talked to everyone else. To the guards, the cooks, the horses. He had talked a great deal of nonsense to the healer in the infirmary tent and she had talked a great deal of it back. He'd never talked much to Halvard. He'd walked beside him.

Halvard had always thought that was because the boy didn't like him very much.

He thought, sitting on the end of the jetty, that he had perhaps been wrong about that, for twenty years. He had been wrong about a great many things. He was a strategist. He was not, it turned out, a very good one about anything that mattered.

He put the bundle down on the boards between them.

It was quite thick. Thirty-eight letters, folded, their grey seals broken, in order, the oldest at the bottom. Tied with a strip of black ribbon. And under the ribbon, on top, eleven loose sheets, unfolded, that had never been letters at all.

The man looked at it in the lamplight.

Then he put his hand into his coat. The inside pocket. And took out a small square of folded paper, soft along the folds, and put it down on the boards beside the bundle. Halvard knew it. He'd written it on the gatehouse wall, in pencil, against the stone, with the king's hand on his shoulder, and sealed it with nothing because he'd had nothing with him. *We will come. In the frost*.

And Halvard put his hand into his own coat, the inside pocket, left, where it had been for five weeks, and took out the other one. The reply. Sealed once with a thumbprint in infirmary candle-wax. Folded and unfolded so many times that the folds had gone white. And put it down on the boards beside the other two.

Three pieces of paper, on the end of a jetty, in the lamplight. And two old men with their legs over the water, not looking at each other.

"Thirty-eight," said the man.

"Yes."

"Every one of them says the same thing."

"Yes."

"I know what it says." The man looked at the bundle. "You put it at the end. *I have missed you every day*. You said it was the only part you never had to work out." He was quiet. "I've read that one. I know what the other thirty-seven say."

"Then you don't need them," said Halvard.

"No," said the man. "I don't need them."

He picked the bundle up anyway. He weighed it in his hand, the way you'd weigh a fish, the way Halvard had once seen him weigh a sword at fifteen, at the armoury door, before he'd ever been allowed to lift it.

"I'll have them," he said.

Halvard looked at him.

"One a year," said the man. "On the anniversary. When I get back from the grave. I'll open one, and read it, at the table, with a cup of tea." He turned the bundle over. "Thirty-eight years. I'll be seventy-four when I get to the last one."

"You'll be older than I am now."

"Nobody's older than you are now," said the man. "You were born older than you are now."

Halvard made a sound. He didn't know what it was. It came out of him on the end of the jetty, in the cold, and went away over the water. He thought it might have been a laugh. He hadn't heard himself make one in some years and couldn't be sure.

"And the eleven?" said the man.

Halvard looked at the loose sheets under the ribbon.

"They stop," he said. "All of them. At the same place."

"*And then you came up onto the wall*."

Halvard went very still.

"You told me," said the man. "In the letter. That they all stop at the wall." He slid the eleven sheets out from under the ribbon, carefully, and held them out. Not to keep. Back. "These aren't letters. They're the wall. You don't have to keep the wall."

Halvard took them.

He held them for a while. Eleven sheets of the good paper he kept for the king, in his own hand, in nine years of ink, some of it brown and some of it black. Some a page. Some three lines. One a single word and a blot where the pen had stood too long. He didn't need to read them. He knew where each of them stopped.

Then he lifted the glass of the lamp, and held the first one to the flame.

***

It took a long time. He didn't hurry it. He held each sheet by the corner until it caught, and turned it so the flame went up it evenly, the way you'd turn a page, and held it over the water until it was nearly to his fingers, and let it go. And it went down onto the black water, burning, and went out, and lay there, a small grey curl on the dark, and drifted.

The man didn't say anything. He sat beside him and watched them go down. One after another. Eleven of them, burning, going out on the water under the end of the jetty, drifting off in a slow grey line toward the reeds.

The last one was the single word. *Ars*. And the blot.

Halvard held it a long time before he put it to the flame.

When it was gone, he sat with his empty hands on his knees and looked at the eleven grey curls on the water, very small, going away.

And something happened.

He felt it. He was a mage; he'd felt mana move in himself for fifty years and could have told you where it was in his body at any moment, the way you can tell where your hands are with your eyes shut. This wasn't mana. It was further in than that. Under the place mana came from. A place he didn't have a name for, that he'd only ever felt once before, at fifteen. Not his fifteen. The boy's. On a wall, at midnight, when the boy had leaned over and put his finger on the empty air in front of Halvard's face and said *there, that's yours, you've got it upside down*.

Something in that place, that had been pulled tight for nine years, the way a rope is pulled tight on a mooring post in a gale, and frayed where it rubbed, and fraying, every night, a strand at a time.

It wasn't tight.

***

The man's head came round.

Not to Halvard. To the empty air in front of his own face. He did the small habitual thing with his hand that Halvard had watched him do at fifteen, at twenty, at thirty, and never once understood until a wall at midnight, and his window opened. Halvard couldn't see it. He'd never been able to see anyone else's. He saw the man's eyes go to it. And move. Reading.

The man read for a while.

Then he laughed. Not loudly. Short, under his breath, surprised, the way he'd laughed at fifteen when Halvard got his own window wrong.

"What is it?"

"The channel," said the man. "Thirty-five people in five towns who can all write in the same window. I told you about it in Valcrest." He was still reading. "Deston's written. In Kingsferry. Gabe's friend. The tall one, who hears things."

"Hears what?"

The man was quiet a moment.

"Threads," he said. "Between people. He can hear them. He's been able to since the first week. Everybody in the kingdom's got them, he says, running from them to the people they're tied to, and he can hear every one." He looked at the window. "He heard yours, in Valcrest. At the king's house. The night I came up the hill. Mine mended, that night, my end. Yours didn't. He said yours was frayed. At your end. He's been listening to it ever since. All summer. All the way north."

Halvard didn't say anything. He looked at the eleven grey curls going away on the water.

"Listen," said the man.

And he read it out. In the kingdom's tongue, translating as he went, slowly, the way you'd read a letter aloud to somebody whose eyes had gone.

The man stopped reading.

Halvard sat on the end of the jetty with his hands on his knees.

He had read about Deston, the tall one, the one who heard things, in a report from Kingsferry in the first spring, and written in the margin *strings? ask Maud*, and never asked. He had read that the summoned had a way of talking to each other across the kingdom that nobody could see, and he had written *find out* and never found out, because finding out would have meant asking, and asking would have meant—

He had never once imagined that any of them were listening to *him*.

"All summer," he said.

"All summer. All the way north." The man closed his window. "He told the channel when you left Solmere. *Advisor's thread moving north*. Every night since. *Still frayed. Still moving. Ambry. Halvard put his hand over his eyes.

He didn't decide to. He found it there. The way it had gone there once before, in a study in Solmere, with a cat girl standing in front of the desk who'd asked him a plain question. He sat on the end of a jetty in the cold with his hand over his eyes, and his shoulders doing something he couldn't stop, and the man beside him not touching him, not saying anything, just there. Half a pace off. The way he'd always been there, on the walls, at the change of the watch.

***

"I missed you," said Halvard.

He said it behind his hand. To the dark. Out loud.

He'd written it thirty-eight times. He had never said it. He had never said it to anyone, about anyone, in his life. He'd thought, for nine years, that it was the kind of thing that could only be written. That you put it at the bottom of a page, last, after all the work was done, and sealed it, and locked it in a drawer, and that was where it lived.

It didn't live there. He'd carried it up a north road for nine days and it had been in his chest the whole way, not in his saddlebag. It had been in his chest for nine years. He took his hand away from his eyes.

"I know," said the man.

Halvard looked at him.

He could see his face. He was one of two people at this lake who could. He looked at it in the lamplight on the end of the jetty, and it was doing a thing he'd never seen it do. Not at fifteen, not at twenty-two, not on the wall. Not anything he had a report for.

"Me too," said the man.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He'd written it once, in an infirmary, by a candle, *I've missed you too. Every day. Nine years*, and Halvard had read it on a gatehouse wall and not been able to come down for an hour. He didn't say any of that. He said *me too*, the way you'd say it to somebody on a call at two in the morning when they'd said *goodnight* and you meant *goodnight* back and a great deal more and it didn't need saying.

Halvard looked out at the water.

"Twenty years," he said. "This month. The fourth night."

"I know."

"You were sitting on the parapet with your legs over the drop. With a sword that was too long for you. Scowling at the air."

"You were holding yours upside down."

"I was holding mine upside down," Halvard agreed. "For a week. I'd been opening my inventory and thinking it was my skills. I thought I had no skills at all. I thought I'd been given a commission to the border by mistake." He was quiet. "You laughed."

"You looked so offended."

"I was the youngest court mage the crown had ever sent to the border."

"You were holding it upside down," said the man.

Halvard laughed.

He heard it, this time. He was sure of it. It went out of him on the end of the jetty and away over the black water after the eleven grey curls of paper, and the reeds at the near end of the lake rustled, and somewhere behind them on the shore a hen complained about it from inside the cabin.

***

"Do you still?" said the man, after a while.

"Still what?"

"Hold it upside down."

Halvard thought about it.

He hadn't opened his window in front of anybody in twenty years. Nobody did; it wasn't done. Your window was yours, like the inside of your coat. The king had never seen his. He'd never seen the king's. He knew what was in it the way you know what's in a locked room in your own house, and he looked at it alone, at night, at the desk, perhaps once a month, and closed it, and didn't think about it again.

He lifted his hand. He did the thing. The small movement, at the empty air in front of his face, that a scowling boy had taught him on a wall twenty years ago and that he'd never once done since in front of another person.

His window opened.

He turned it. With his fingers, very slightly, the way you'd turn a page round on a desk so the person across from you could read it. He didn't know if it would work. He'd never tried.

The man leaned over and looked at it.

***

They stayed out till the light came.

Halvard hadn't meant to. He had meant to go in at some sensible hour and sleep in the bed he'd been told he didn't get a say about. He found, when he next thought about it, that the stars had gone round, and the lamp had burned down and been trimmed once and burned down again, and the cold had got into him all the way through his coat in a way he would certainly regret, and the far shore was coming back out of the dark, grey, with the hills behind it.

They'd talked, a little. Not much. About the fort. About a cook at Cinderhold called Ambrose who'd hated them both. About the king, at some length, and his hens. About Maren, who had written *he carries the water* and *I think he is happy* at the bottom of a report in the summer, and been right twice. About the girl.

They hadn't talked about the wall. They didn't need to. It was on the boards between them, in three pieces of paper, and it was lying there quite quietly, and neither of them had picked it up again.

The man stood up.

He went back along the jetty to the shore, and came back with a rod. A plain rod, long, with a plain line. Halvard watched him bait it, and cast, the line going out in a long slow curve over the water in the grey light and the float settling on the black. And sit down again, on the end, with the rod across his knees.

Halvard watched him fish.

The guard had written that. *He fishes. Mostly he catches nothing. He doesn't seem to mind*. Halvard had read it at Hethe and thought it was the saddest sentence he had ever read about anybody. He sat beside it now, on the end of the jetty, in the dawn, and watched a man fish and catch nothing and not mind, and found it wasn't sad at all. It was the least sad thing he'd ever seen.

The float went down.

The man didn't hurry. He let it go, and go, and then lifted the rod, quite gently, and the line went tight, and something on the other end of it in the black water turned and pulled and turned again. A good one. The man let it run, and brought it back, and let it run, the way you'd talk to somebody who was frightened. And brought it in. A perch, striped, as long as his forearm, cold and bright in the first light.

He unhooked it. He put a string through its gill and hung it over the side of the jetty in the water, to keep.

He cast again.

By the time the sun came over the hills on the far shore there were five fish on the string. Two perch, a bream, a long silver fish Halvard didn't know the name of, and a pike, small, very angry about it.

The man looked at them. Then he took a flat grey stone out of his coat, a lake stone, the size of his palm, and looked at it, and put it back in his coat. Over his heart.

He turned his head and looked at Halvard. In the sunrise. With his face that Halvard could see.

"Morning," he said.

Halvard looked at him.

The guard had counted it. *Eleven times. I counted*. He'd read that at Hethe too, and not understood why she'd put it down, of all the things she'd seen. A man coming up off a jetty at dawn and saying one word to a stranger on his step.

He understood now.

"Morning," said Halvard.

***

He carried the fish.

He didn't ask to. The man stood up, with the rod, and drew the string up out of the water, five fish on it, dripping, and looked at it, and then at Halvard, and didn't say anything. He hung it on the last post of the jetty and walked off along the boards toward the shore. Leaving it there.

Halvard took it down.

It was heavier than he'd expected. Five fish on a wet string, cold and bright and still twitching, the pike especially. He carried it along the jetty, thirty-four planks, and up the shingle, past the guard on the step, who was awake, with her book open on her knee and nothing written on the page, and who looked at him go past with a string of fish in his hand and did not write it down. He saw her not write it down. She had a very good face for not writing things down.

And up the lake road, beside the man, in the cold bright morning, with the frost going off the fields in the sun and steam coming off the walls. Past the ring of thorn at the bottom of the field, white and glittering, with something green inside it. Past the mayor's house, where a big grey-headed man was standing in an open doorway in his shirt with a cup of tea in his hands and didn't say anything at all, just lifted the cup an inch. Across the corner of the square. To a hall at the top of the lake road, with a step worn hollow in the middle.

There was a basket on the step. Willow, empty, with straw in the bottom, in the middle, where the stone was worn hollow.

The man nodded at it.

Halvard knelt, which his knees had something to say about, and put the fish in the basket. One at a time, on the straw, side by side, the way he'd lay out letters on a desk. Five.

He straightened up. His knees told him about the jetty, and the horse, and everything else, at length. He stood there in the cold in front of the militia hall of Wrenhaven with his hands empty and fish-scale on his coat.

The door opened.

Maren. In her shirtsleeves, with her hair down and a cup in her hand, the way she came down most mornings now, to see the basket filled before the girls were up. She stopped in the doorway. She looked at the basket. She looked at the man, a little way off in the road, with his rod on his shoulder. She looked at Halvard.

She had served him nine years. She'd written him a hundred reports. She had kept an order of his every day for sixteen months that had cost her more than she'd ever once let him see. *He is a fisherman, and nothing more. Tell them nothing*.

She looked at the Advisor to the Crown of Aurelan standing on the step of her hall at sunrise with fish-scale on his coat.

Her face didn't do anything. Maren's face never did. She'd learned that from him.

"My lord," she said.

"Maren," said Halvard.

He looked at the basket. Then at her.

"You wrote that he carries the water," he said.

"He does."

"He let me carry the fish," said Halvard.

Maren looked at him for a long moment.

Then she put her cup down on the step, beside the basket, very carefully. And put her hand on her chest.

Halvard looked at it.

He thought about a wall, nine years ago, and every man on it with his hand on his chest, while somebody walked in under the gate and didn't look up. He thought about a step at the bottom of a cabin, last night, and a girl with her mother's face. He thought about a lake he had built wrong in his head for nine years, every part of it, and the real one, behind him at the bottom of the road, small and black and still, with thirty-four planks.

He put his hand on his.

Behind him, in the road, he heard the man turn round, and start back down the lake road toward the water. Not hurrying. The way he walked everywhere.

Halvard didn't turn round to watch him go. He didn't need to. He knew which way his feet were pointing.

The same way. Both of them.
