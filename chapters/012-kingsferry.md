Kingsferry smelled of tar and fish and river water and money, and Dennis loved it on sight.

"Look at it," he said, on the first morning, standing on the quay with his arms spread wide like a man greeting an old friend. Barges were tied up three deep along the waterfront. Cranes swung bales and barrels onto the wharves. Somewhere a man was shouting prices for salted eel and somewhere else two men were shouting at each other about a debt. "Look at all this *commerce*."

"It's a port," said Rachael.

"It's a *market,*" said Dennis. "The whole city's a market. I've died and gone to heaven." He paused. "Again."

Sabine, leaning on a bollard beside him, looked out at the quay with the satisfied expression of a woman watching someone discover a thing she'd always known. "Supplies for the eastern garrisons go through here," she said. "Grain, steel, salt, rope. Everything the army needs comes down the Wren and gets loaded onto the east road. If there's a war coming, this is where it starts getting paid for."

Deston looked out at the barges and the cranes and the hundreds of people moving along the quay, and for a moment, without meaning to, he let his eyes do the thing they'd started doing.

The threads came up out of the crowd like light through water.

Thousands of them. Thin and grey, mostly, running between strangers who'd brushed shoulders on the quay, or haggled over a barrel, or nodded to each other in passing. Thicker ones between men who worked the same crane. A thick gold one, almost a rope, between an old bargeman and a boy who must have been his grandson, coiling a line together at the end of the jetty. The whole quay was woven through with them, a net of light so dense he had to close his eyes.

"Dest?" said Gabe.

"It's fine." He opened his eyes again, and made himself look at only one thing: the river, which had no threads at all. "Just a lot of people."

***

They lodged above a chandler's on Rope Street, in three rooms with low ceilings and a view of the water, and by the end of the first week they'd each found the place in the city that was theirs.

Gabe found the forges.

There was a street of them down by the south wharf, smoky and loud and ringing, where the smiths made everything from anchor chain to spearheads for the eastern army. Gabe walked down it on the third day, and stopped outside the biggest one, and stood watching the smith work for so long that the man finally came out, wiping his hands, and asked him what he wanted.

"Nothing," said Gabe. "Just watching."

"Watching's free," said the smith. "Bellows aren't. You want to stand there, you work."

So Gabe worked the bellows. Then he swept the floor. By the second week he was holding the tongs, and by the third the smith had let him take a bar of iron from the fire and draw it out on the anvil himself, badly, while the smith told him exactly how badly.

"He says I've got good hands," Gabe reported at supper, sounding faintly stunned. "He says I can feel when the metal's ready. He says most apprentices take a year to feel that."

"Can you?" asked Deston.

Gabe thought about it. "I think so. It's like... it goes quiet. Just before it's ready. The heat stops being angry." He looked at his hands. "That's not aura. Is it? It can't be. I'm at six percent."

"Maybe it's just you," said Deston.

***

On the last night of the second week, Deston found Gabe on the roof.

The chandler's house had a flat bit of roof above the back rooms, between two chimneys, where you could sit with your back to warm brick and look out over the whole waterfront: the barges, the lamps on the quay, the black river going away south toward the sea. Gabe was sitting there in the dark with his knees up and his forge-blackened hands hanging between them, and he was humming.

Deston knew the song before he'd climbed all the way out of the window.

He didn't say anything. He just sat down next to Gabe, against the chimney, and after a moment he came in on the lower line, the way he always had, the way they'd been taught at fourteen in a hot classroom with a piano that was always slightly flat. Gabe didn't look round. He just shifted his part to fit, without thinking, the way you shift your step to walk beside someone.

They sang it through once, quietly, under their breath. Not the words. Just the shape of it.

There should have been a third voice. There always had been: higher than Gabe's, a little behind the beat, because Declan had always been a little behind the beat, on purpose, so that he could make a face at them across the risers when the choirmaster wasn't looking. They both left the space for it. Neither of them said so.

When they finished, there was a long silence.

"What was that?" said a voice from the window.

Emrys was leaning on the sill below them, in his shirtsleeves, a candle in his hand. He'd been there for a while, Deston thought. Long enough.

"Something we sang in school," said Gabe. "Me and him and a friend."

"Just the two parts?"

"Three," said Deston. "Usually."

Emrys looked at them both for a moment, in the candlelight, with an expression Deston couldn't read. Then he nodded slowly.

"I've never heard it," he said. "And I've heard every song in this kingdom." He paused. "You hold the gap for him very well. The third part. I could hear exactly where it should go."

He went back inside without waiting for an answer. Gabe looked at the window for a long time after the light had gone.

"He's good," he said finally.

"Yeah," said Deston. "He is."

They stayed on the roof until the lamps on the quay started going out, not singing anything else, just sitting. Deston didn't look at the threads. He didn't need to. He knew exactly how thick the one between them was. He knew exactly where the third one used to run.

***

Rachael found the infirmary.

It was a long whitewashed hall by the river gate, run by an order of healers who took in sick bargemen and injured dockers and anyone else who couldn't pay, and they were always short of hands. Rachael walked in on the fourth day and asked if they needed help, and the healer at the door looked at her clothes and her face and her obvious lack of any training at all, and said yes, because they always did.

She came back every day after that.

Deston went to find her there, at the end of the second week, and stopped in the doorway, and watched.

She was sitting by a cot near the window. In the cot was a little girl, five or six, flushed and sweating with fever, tossing her head on the pillow. Rachael had one of the girl's hands in both of hers and was talking to her very quietly, the way you talk to someone you want to keep from being frightened. She wasn't doing anything. She couldn't. She was an Initiate, like the rest of them, and none of them could do anything yet.

But her hands, Deston saw, were glowing. Very faintly. A soft gold light, the same colour as the warmth Hyvred had said she carried where the Advisor had touched her wrist.

The girl stopped tossing. Her breathing slowed.

Rachael looked up and saw Deston in the doorway. She didn't let go of the girl's hand.

"I can feel where it hurts," she said quietly, when she came out to him later, in the corridor. Her eyes were very bright. "Not see it. Feel it. Like heat under my fingers. I can't do anything about it yet, I can't heal anything, I just... know." She leaned against the wall. "Do you know how many years I spent in beds like that? Being the one people sat with?" She laughed, a little shakily. "I used to watch the nurses and think, I'll never be on that side of it. Not with my immune system. Not ever."

"And now you are," said Deston.

"And now I am." She pressed the heels of her hands to her eyes. "Eleven percent. Emrys says the healers here would kill for eleven percent in two months."

***

Calvin found the rigging.

There wasn't a mast in Kingsferry he hadn't climbed by the end of the month. He went up them the way other people went up stairs, and sat in the crosstrees, and came down with stories about the weather three days before it happened.

"It's the wind," he said, every time. "I can feel it turning. Before it turns. Like someone's about to open a door in another room."

"Nine," he reported, at their nightly check, "and I'm going to be a wind mage, I'm *calling* it, and you'll all have to admit I said it first."

"Nobody's arguing," said Rachael.

"Somebody might later."

And Dennis and Sabine found each other, which everybody else had known was going to happen since the first day on the road.

Deston never got the full story. He got pieces. A cart of salt that had been bought very cheaply from a man who'd needed to leave town in a hurry and sold rather less cheaply to a garrison quartermaster who'd needed salt in a hurry. A wager on a barge race that Dennis had somehow been on both sides of. A conversation with a moneylender that ended with the moneylender owing *them*.

"Is any of this legal?" Deston asked, at the end of the third week.

"Define legal," said Dennis.

"I don't think I need to."

"Then it's fine." Dennis was counting coins into a little leather bag. "Look, I can tell when people are lying. I always could. Sabine knows every merchant on this river and which ones owe who. Between us we're not *cheating* anybody. We're just..." He searched for the word.

"Informed," said Sabine.

"Informed. Eight percent, by the way. And it goes up every time I do a deal. I don't know what that means."

"It means," said Emrys mildly, from the corner, "that your path cares about how people see things. And how they don't." He looked at Dennis over his book. "Be careful with that. It's the kind of path that can make you very rich or very lonely."

Dennis stopped counting. For a moment he looked almost thoughtful. Then he put the bag away and said, "Why not both," and Sabine laughed so hard she had to sit down.

***

Deston found the threads.

Or rather, he'd already found them. What he found in Kingsferry was how to *read* them.

Emrys helped. Every evening, while the others were out, they sat at the table by the window, and Emrys sang one long low note, and Deston breathed and let his eyes go soft and looked out at the quay below.

"Tell me what you see," Emrys would say.

And Deston would tell him. The grey ones, thin as hair, between strangers. The gold ones, thicker, between friends and families and lovers. The way a thread thickened when two people laughed together, very slightly, like a muscle flexing. The way it frayed, sometimes, when they argued.

He learned that threads didn't care about distance. The bargeman's thread to his grandson didn't get any thinner when the boy ran off down the quay. He learned that they changed slowly, over months and years, and fast only when something enormous happened. He learned that his own thread to Gabe was the thickest thing he had ever seen, and that it had been thick for a very long time. He learned that he was at ten percent, and that ten percent of a thing like this was already more than he knew what to do with.

And he learned, on the last evening of the month, that sometimes, if he concentrated very hard, he could feel the far ends of the threads he couldn't see.

He was sitting at the window with his eyes half closed, following his thread to Xav. It ran out of the room and up the river, north, thinner and thinner with distance, until he lost it against the evening light. But he could still *feel* it. Not see it. Feel it, the way you can feel someone's hand in yours in the dark.

And something was wrong at the other end.

It wasn't anything he could name. It was more like a chord going sour, a note held too long. Somewhere up the river, at the far end of that thread, something had happened. Something bad.

He sat up so fast he knocked over Emrys's cup.

"Deston?"

"Something's happened," said Deston. "To Xav. Or near him. I don't—" He pressed his hand to his chest. "I can feel it. Something's wrong."

Emrys didn't tell him he was imagining it. That was the thing about Emrys. He just looked at Deston for a long moment, and then said, quietly, "Then write to him. Tonight."

***

The letter from Xav came three days later, before Deston's had even had time to reach him.

Four pages, in Xav's small neat handwriting. Deston read them standing at the window, and then sat down and read them again, and then went and found Gabe at the forge and made him read them too, right there among the hammering, with the sparks going up around them.

The tunnel. The thing in the wall. The shield splitting. Azac, in the bed, bandaged from armpit to hip. *A finger's width higher, he'd be dead.*

And Petra, with the light running past the edge of her sword.

*We're not playing anymore,* Xav had written at the end. *I think we all needed to find that out. I'm sorry it was Azac who had to show us.*

Gabe read it twice, in silence. Then he handed it back and stood for a while, looking at the fire.

"He took the hit for them," he said finally. "Azac. He just went in front."

"He's been doing that since Declan," said Deston quietly.

"Yeah." Gabe picked up his tongs, and put them down again. "Yeah. Declan used to do that too. Every game. Didn't matter what it was. He'd just go in front and tell everyone to trust him." He laughed, very briefly. "He'd have been so angry, Dest. If he'd seen it. He'd have been so angry at Azac for copying him."

Deston didn't say anything. He was looking at the thread between himself and Gabe, thick and bright and steady as it had been for years, and thinking about Azac's thread to the rest of Group 6, which he'd seen on the jetty at the ferry town, and which must be stretched very tight right now.

That night he wrote to Xav. Not a long letter, this time. He'd felt it, he said. Three days ago. Something going wrong at the other end of the thread, before he'd known anything.

*I don't know what that means yet. I don't know if I can always feel it, or only when it's bad. But I felt it.*

*Tell Azac Gabe says he's an idiot, and that Declan would say the same, and that we're both glad he's alive.*

*Tell Petra thank you.*

He sealed it, and went to the window, and looked north, up the dark river. The thread to Xav was there, thin as a hair, running away into the night. It didn't feel sour anymore. It felt tired, and sad, and very, very tight, like a rope that has held something heavy and not let go.

It was still holding.

Below him, on the quay, an old bargeman and his grandson were walking home together through the lamplight, and the gold between them was so bright that Deston had to look away.
