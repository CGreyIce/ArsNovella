Deston had always been the one in the middle.

Not the top. The top was Declan's, and Declan had always sung it too loud, and it carried itself. Not the bottom, either; the bottom was Gabe's, a voice like a floor you could stand a house on. The middle was the part nobody hummed on the bus home. It didn't carry anything on its own. If you sang it by itself in an empty room it sounded like somebody waiting for the rest of the song.

But it was the part that knew where everyone else was. That was the thing about the middle. You couldn't sing it without listening to the top and the bottom at the same time, all the way through, every bar. The choirmaster used to say the middle part was the one that held the choir together, and Deston, at thirteen, had thought that was something choirmasters said to make the middle parts feel better.

He knew better now. He'd known since a gorge on the upper Wren, with the turned coming down the cliffs head first and the barge swinging toward the rocks, when he'd stopped trying to be loud and started to listen, and every gold thread on the barge had lit at once.

*Chorus.* It still said it in his window, every morning. He still didn't quite believe it.

And on the twelfth day out of Wrenhaven, on a long chalk ridge in the downs with the whole of the south laid out in front of him, he listened harder than he'd ever listened in his life, and found that the choir was the size of the kingdom.

***

It had been coming on for days.

He'd noticed it first at the second village, the one with the man and the goose. Not the goose; the priest. A little round grey priest in the chapel door, watching the summoned go by with his hands folded, and a thread running from him that Deston had seen before but never this far from the lake: thin, silver, almost too fine to see in daylight. A native thread. A window thread. The kind he and Thea had drawn on the jetty, the copies of copies, going back through a court mage on a garrison wall to a boy who'd said *status* out loud.

It ran from the priest's chest, across the road, through the crowd, and into the plain man carrying two buckets of water past the chapel door.

The priest didn't look at him. The priest was looking at Xola, and Squiddles, who had gone pink. The thread didn't care. It bent toward the water-carrier as he went by the way grass bends behind a boat, and straightened again after he'd passed, and the priest never knew.

After that Deston saw them everywhere.

Not many. Natives with windows were rare; Thea said perhaps one in a few hundred. But there was one in most villages, and two or three in the bigger ones. A posting-house keeper who'd been a soldier on the Redmarch. A guild guide in a market town, with a copper badge and a bored face, who had no idea that the summoned were walking past her, or that anyone was. A woman at a ford with a ferry and a dog and a thread so faint it was barely there, who'd been taught to look at her window by her mother, who'd been taught by somebody, who'd been taught by somebody else. Every one of them ran the same way. Every one of them bent toward the same man, as he went by, and straightened behind him.

He'd told Thea on the fifth night. Thea had got her notebook out by the light of the fire and drawn it as he talked: a road, and a man walking down it, and on either side little points with lines running in. Like the diagram on the jetty, but stretched out long. Like a comb.

"Every one?" said Thea.

"Every one I've seen."

Thea looked at the comb for a long time.

"So the kingdom's windows are his," she said quietly. "We knew that. But it's not just that they started with him. They still *point* at him." She closed the notebook. "Everywhere he goes, every window in the kingdom knows where he is. And nobody who's got one has the faintest idea."

***

On the ninth night the channel filled up.

Deston was on watch, the second one, with Sabine. Sabine liked a quiet watch. She sat on the shaft of the dray with her sword across her knees and looked at the dark like it owed her money, which was how Sabine looked at most things, and didn't speak. So Deston sat on the dray's tailboard and opened his window and read.

It had been busy all week. It was always busy now. Twenty people who'd spent a year and a half writing to each other across a kingdom were suddenly all on the road at once, and it turned out that what twenty people on the road mostly wanted to do in the evenings was tell each other about it.

Deston didn't know what that meant either.

He sat on the tailboard with Sabine silent on the shaft beside him and the camp asleep, and the twenty of them talking across the dark in English, and he looked up from his window, the way you look up from a letter, at the far end of the camp.

At a man on a mounting stone by the road with a rod across his knees, keeping the third watch early because he couldn't sleep, reading the same lines.

He wasn't smiling. Deston could see his shoulders. He was doing the thing he did instead.

***

It wasn't until the twelfth day that Deston really looked.

They'd come up out of the last of the valleys in the afternoon onto the downs: a long bare chalk ridge, white where the road had worn through the turf, with sheep on it and larks over it and nothing to stop the wind. The wagons had gone on along the ridge road at a horse's pace. Deston had dropped back. Then he'd stopped, at the highest point, where an old stone stood up out of the grass on its own, and let them go on ahead.

From up here you could see the south.

He'd never seen it. None of them had. They'd come into this world in the middle of it and been sent out to the edges, and the edges were what they knew: the lake, the river, the Redmarch, the pass. But from the top of the downs the whole of the south lay open below like a map left out on a table. The plain, going away flat and gold and hazy for thirty miles. Farms. Rivers like dropped string. Roads. And at the very edge of seeing, where the haze turned blue, a smudge that might have been smoke and might have been a city.

Valcrest.

He stood by the old stone with the wind in his face, and did what he'd done in the Narrows. Stopped trying to see. Started to listen.

***

The fifteen came first. They always did.

He could feel them without trying now; he could have felt them in his sleep. Gold. All fifteen of them, strung out along the ridge road in front of him, half a mile of gold threads twisting through the wind like kite strings. Gabe's, the thickest, from the front, where he was walking beside the dray. Xav's, quiet and very taut. The five girls', tangled together the way they always were, with one bright stubborn line from Higu to Gigichi that ran outside all the rest. Rachael's, from the cart, steady as a lamp. Calvin's, all over the place.

And from every one of the fifteen, and from Alea, and from Petra and Sabine and Emrys and Maren and Bram, a line to the man walking at the front of the wagons with the buckets. Gold, most of them. Silver from the natives. Thick from Alea, so thick it was hardly a thread at all, more like a rope.

He knew all that. He'd drawn it.

He listened harder.

And then he saw the rest of them.

***

They went out from the man with the buckets in every direction.

He'd known they were there. Of course he had. He'd seen them on the jetty, in the spring, a sheaf of them, running away from the end of the jetty over the lake toward every point of the compass. But at the lake there'd been hills in the way, and a town, and fifteen people all round him who were brighter and closer and louder than anything far off. He'd seen the threads go. He'd never seen where they *went*.

He saw it now.

Twenty of them. Out across the plain, over the farms and the rivers and the roads, so long and so fine they caught the sun like spider silk on a field in the morning. Running south, and east, and west. And as he followed them out with his eyes, with his breath, with whatever it was in him that listened, he saw that they weren't going in twenty directions at all.

They were coming *back*.

They bent. All of them. A long way off, out over the plain, every one of the twenty curved round the way a river curves, and ran in toward the same place. Toward the smudge at the edge of the haze. From the east, five of them, a long way off still, coming in along a road from a fort on a red plain. From the west, five more, down from the mountains. From the north-west, five, travelling slowly in the middle of a great tangle of other threads, hundreds of them, the threads of four hundred people and two hundred horses and eleven wagons of somebody's luggage. And from the south, already there, waiting, five more.

Twenty threads. Four roads. One city.

And the fifteen, here, on a fifth road, coming down off the downs.

And one man, at the front of them, carrying water. Walking down the last road into the middle of all of it.

Deston put his hand on the old stone.

***

He looked at the far ends of the threads. That was the next thing. That was the thing the middle part did: once you knew where everybody was, you listened to *how* they were.

Threads had two ends. He'd learned that slowly, over a year and a half, and then all at once on a lake road in the spring. Each end was the colour of what the person holding it felt, and believed. Most of the time the two ends were the same, because most of the time people felt the same about each other, more or less. Grey for strangers. Gold for love. But not always. His own thread to Declan had been faded at his end for more than three years. Colourless. Old. The colour a thread goes when you think the person on the other end is dead.

Because as far as Deston had known, he was. And the far end, the end in a cabin by a lake, had been gold the whole time.

He looked at the twenty now. At their far ends, a long way out across the plain. And at the near ends, here, running into the man at the front of the wagons.

The near ends were gold. All twenty. Every one. Deston had known they would be. He'd watched the man read the channel every night for six weeks. He'd watched his shoulders.

The far ends weren't.

Five of them were gold too. The five from the east, from the red plain. They'd gone gold, Deston thought, months ago, after a man walked out of the dust on the Redmarch with a fishing rod. Nobody had told them. They'd worked it out, and believed it, and that had been enough.

Three more were warm. Not gold. Not yet. But not faded either. Warm, the way a stone's warm after the sun's been on it. Three of the five already waiting in the city: one steady, one bright, one cold to the touch and warm underneath.

And the other twelve were faded.

Colourless. Old. The far ends of them going out across the plain like threads of ash.

Twelve people on four roads, walking toward a city where they were going to stand in the same place for the first time since a summoning hall. Twelve people who'd known him. Who'd loved him, some of them, for years. Who'd sat up half the night in the channel, the summer before, when a boy in Valcrest asked what he'd been like, and said *he was the funny one* and *he gifted me subs* and *he called his own deaths like a sports commentator*. Who'd heard the stories and the rumours and Group 3's careful report, *he didn't say*. Who'd wondered.

And who still, at the far end, where it counted, thought he was dead.

Deston stood by the stone with his hand on it and found that his eyes had filled up.

Not for them. Not exactly. For the colour. He knew what was going to happen to it. He'd felt it happen to his own, on a lake road, in the spring, when a voice from the end of a jetty came in on the third part too loud and the thread in his chest went gold at both ends at once, so hard and so fast he'd nearly sat down in the road.

That was going to happen twelve times. In four days. In one place.

And he'd be standing next to the man on the other end of all twelve when it did.

***

There was more. There was always more, once you started listening.

The silver threads. He'd seen them on the road, one a village, bending toward the water-carrier as he passed. But from up here he saw them all, and it took his breath away. Not twenty. Not hundreds. *Thousands*. Out across the whole plain, faint as frost, every native window in the south, every guide and every guild clerk and every old soldier who'd once been shown how to look, threads like the ribs of a leaf, running in. They thickened toward the city, the way frost thickens in the low places. Valcrest was white with them. A guild, an arena, a master of the Lists who wept easily. And all of them running back up the long road north, over the plain, up onto the downs, to here. To one man.

*Like every river in the kingdom running into one lake*, Thea had said, on the jetty.

She'd been looking at the wrong lake. The lake was walking.

And two threads he'd never seen before. Coming from the north-west, from inside that great tangle of four hundred people and two hundred horses.

One gold. Thick, old, warm right through, end to end, with the steadiness of a thing that had been beaten flat on an anvil a long time ago and kept its shape ever since. A smith's thread. A king's.

And one beside it that was silver, and something else. The first silver thread. The thickest silver thread Deston had ever seen, because it wasn't a copy of anything; it was the one all the copies had been made from, a court mage on a garrison wall twenty years ago looking at a boy's window. But it wasn't only silver. Under the silver, wound round it, there was another strand, tight, frayed, pulled so hard for so long that it had gone thin and grey in places, like a rope that's held a weight for years and is still holding it, and will go on holding it until somebody comes and takes the other end.

Eight years, Deston thought. Every night in a study. Wondering whether he'd broken him.

He looked at the near end of that one, where it ran into the man at the front of the wagons.

It was frayed there too.

***

And the last ones. He nearly didn't look for them. He knew they'd be there.

Grey. Thin. Not going south or east or west at all, but *out*, in the direction that isn't a direction, the way he'd seen them in the spring and stopped noticing. Thirty-five of them: one from each of the fifteen on the ridge road in front of him, and twenty more from far away across the plain, all going out of the world toward mothers and fathers and sisters and flats and kitchens and a woman waiting by a phone, all thin and grey and not broken.

And one more, from the man at the front.

The thinnest of all. Thirty years old, or near enough. Going out of the world toward a car-park wall and a man sitting on it with his hands between his knees.

Not broken.

Deston took his hand off the stone.

***

He caught up with them at the foot of the downs, at a crossroads with a well and a chapel and a woman selling apples.

He'd come down off the ridge at a run, and he was out of breath, and he'd been going to say it all at once, the twenty and the twelve and the thousands and the two from the north-west, to Gabe, to Thea, to anyone. And then he came round the last bend into the crossroads and stopped, and didn't say anything at all.

Because the woman selling apples didn't have any threads.

None. Not grey. Not silver. Not one. She sat on an upturned basket by the chapel wall with a tray of last autumn's apples on her knees, wrinkled and sweet, a plain middle-aged country woman in a headscarf, and she had no more threads than a chair. Deston had seen it four times before in his life. A stall in a midwinter market. A step by a river. The top of a cliff in a gorge.

She was looking at Rachael.

Rachael didn't know. Rachael was at the back of the miller's cart with Gabe beside her, laughing at something Calvin had said, with the reins of the near horse in her hand. The woman watched her the way you'd watch a dish being carried to a table.

Deston opened his mouth.

He didn't get to shout. Nobody did. Because the man at the front of the wagons, who had been setting his buckets down by the well, had already straightened up.

He hadn't turned toward her. Not yet. He'd just straightened, with the buckets at his feet and the rod over his shoulder, and gone very still, the way the heron goes still on the shingle, the way he'd gone still on the end of the jetty when two people walked down a lake road humming. And the crossroads went quiet. The larks. The wind. The horses stopped shifting in their traces. Deston felt it come down over everything like a held breath. Not all of it. Just the edge. The first inch of a tide.

The woman with the apples stopped looking at Rachael.

She looked at him.

For a long moment, nobody at the crossroads moved, and the woman sat on her upturned basket and looked at the plain man by the well with no threads in her at all and something in her face that wasn't anything Deston had a word for. Not fear. Things without threads didn't seem to have that. Something more like a sum being done.

Then she stood up, without hurry, and put her tray of apples down on the basket, and walked away from the crossroads into the barley.

Deston watched her go. Out into the field, waist-deep in the green, not looking back. He watched her for twenty paces. Thirty.

Then she wasn't there.

The barley didn't move where she'd been. It didn't close behind her, the way barley does. It just stood there, still and green in the sun, as if nobody had walked through it at all.

The tide went out.

The larks came back. A horse shook its head in its traces. At the back of the cart, Rachael was still laughing, and Gabe was looking at the barley, and his hand had gone to the grip of the strange square blade on his back, and stayed there.

The man by the well picked up his buckets.

***

"They know I'm on the road," he said, that night.

They were on the plain. The downs behind them, a long pale wall against the stars. Thirty miles of flat dark ahead, and at the end of it, low on the horizon, a glow. Not a smudge any more. A city's worth of lamps.

Deston had told him all of it. Sitting on the tailboard of the dray, the two of them, with the camp asleep and Sabine on watch pretending not to listen and Gabe and Xav on the ground at their feet in their blankets, pretending to be asleep. The twenty. The four roads. The thousands of silver threads, like a leaf. The king's. Halvard's, frayed. The grey ones, out. He'd told him about the twelve last, because he hadn't known how to say it.

Then he'd told him about the woman with the apples.

"Is that bad?" said Deston.

"It was always going to happen." Declan turned the rod in his hands. "I've been sitting on a jetty for eight years so that they wouldn't know where I was. And the minute I got up, they knew." He was quiet. "That's all right. Better they're looking at me than at her."

"Rachael."

"She's never alone."

"She's never alone," Deston agreed. Gabe, on the ground, made a noise in his sleep that wasn't a sleeping noise.

Declan looked south. At the glow.

"Twelve," he said.

"Twelve faded. Three warm. Five gold."

"Group 3 never asked me." Declan's voice was very quiet. "I never told them. I just walked across the red and took a sword off Salty and walked away again, and they worked it out on their own and believed it." He shook his head slowly. "And three in the city. I can guess which three."

"Chris logs everything," said Deston. "Ina sees everything. And Winter..." He stopped.

"Winter's been angry with me for a year and a half," said Declan, "for dying. You can hear it in every line she writes." He looked at his hands. "That's the cold one, warm underneath."

Deston didn't say anything.

"And twelve," said Declan. "Who still think I'm dead."

"In four days," said Deston. "When they see you. When you tell them." He breathed. "They'll all go at once. I felt mine go. It was like being hit. Twelve of those, all together, and you on the other end of every one."

Declan was quiet for a long time.

"Then I'd better be sitting down," he said.

At their feet, in his blankets, Xav laughed. Once, very quietly, the way he'd laughed on the jetty in the spring. He didn't pretend to be asleep any more.

"Okay," said Xav, to the stars. "Sit down, then. Again."

***

Deston didn't sleep much.

He lay on his back on the tailboard of the dray with his blanket over him and the plain going away flat and dark on every side and the glow of Valcrest low in the south, and listened.

He couldn't help it now. He'd opened something, up on the downs, by the old stone, and it wouldn't quite close. The fifteen round him, gold, asleep. The man on the mounting stone at the end of the camp, keeping the third watch, gold all the way through. And out across the dark, twenty threads coming in on four roads to one city, five of them gold, three of them warm, twelve of them faded like ash. And thousands of silver ones, like frost. And a king's, and a frayed one beside it. And thirty-six grey ones, going out.

He thought: *it's the same shape.*

The diagram on the jetty. Thea's root, upside down. The channel. A star, with one man in the middle. He'd thought, in the spring, that it was the shape of Wrenhaven. Fifteen people round a fire round a jetty round a man.

It wasn't. It was the shape of the kingdom. It always had been. Every window, every thread, every friend, every king, every grief. All of it running in, from every direction, along every road, to one place. It had just taken him a year and a half and a ridge in the downs to see far enough.

Twenty-one months ago, in a summoning hall, the thread in his chest to Declan had been the faintest he owned. Faded. Old. A thread to somebody dead.

He touched it now, in the dark. Without looking. The way you touch a ring.

It was the brightest thing in the world.

He lay there on the dray with his hand on his chest and the city glowing on the edge of the plain, and very softly, under his breath, so that only the man on the mounting stone would hear, he hummed the middle part.

Just the middle. The part that didn't carry anything on its own. The part that sounded, by itself in the dark, like somebody waiting for the rest of the song.

From the end of the camp, after a moment, too loud, somebody came in on the third.
