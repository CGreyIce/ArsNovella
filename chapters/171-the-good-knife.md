Nobody needed him to do anything.

He noticed it on the first afternoon, an hour out of Cinderhold, when the road went round the first hill and the wall was gone and there was nobody behind them. He'd been walking with the rod on his shoulder and the hammer in his belt and the blue bucket swinging from one hand, half a pace behind his daughter, because she'd set off first and he'd let her. And he realised, somewhere on the long slope down off the hill, that he was waiting.

For somebody to tell him what to carry. For somebody to need the water. For a cart to need pushing, or a horse talking to, or a short square man's ribs needing a shoulder under them on a bad stretch. For a fire to need building, or a watch keeping, or a scout on a ridge to need hooking off it before it saw a girl with a black sword on her back.

There wasn't anything. There was a road going west across grey grass, and a girl walking on it with a tree in her arms, and him.

He walked on behind her and waited, for a while, out of habit. For an hour. Nothing happened. Nobody needed anything. The grey grass whispered on either side of the road, the way it always did out here, and stopped round his boots, the way it always did, and round hers.

Somewhere about the second hour, he stopped waiting.

It was the strangest feeling he'd had in thirty-four years.

***

She set the pace.

He'd always set it. On every road he'd ever walked with her, from the market at Caddow when she was ten to the long road south to Valcrest, he'd walked at the front and she'd walked beside him and the pace had been his, without either of them deciding it. A fisherman's pace. Patient. Not slow, not fast, the pace of a man who knows how far he's going and has decided he's going to get there.

She set it now. He didn't know when it had happened. Somewhere between the fort and the first hill. She was a pace ahead, with Astra on her back and the clay pot held against her chest in both arms, and she was walking at her own pace, which was quicker than his, a little. A girl's pace. A Tempered pace, the aura doing what aura did, lending her legs a little more than they had. And he walked half a pace behind and found he was matching it.

He let her.

"You're slow," said Alea, without turning round, after a while.

"I'm carrying a bucket."

"It's empty."

"It's still a bucket."

She made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. She didn't slow down.

***

She let him carry the water.

Not for them. For the tree.

He found that out at dusk on the first evening, when they stopped at the edge of the grey grass where a thin brown stream came down out of the low hills to the south and went into the red and gave up. She put the pot down on the bank, very carefully, on a flat stone, the way you'd put down something asleep. Then she looked at him. At the blue bucket in his hand.

"Water," she said.

He went and filled it.

He brought it back to her and she knelt on the bank beside the pot with the bucket and cupped her hands in it and poured the water onto the earth round the thin green stick, slowly, a handful at a time, the way Maigo had shown her in the courtyard at Cinderhold with his wooden hands. *Not too much. It's little. Little ones drown easy*. The earth went dark. The three leaves on the stick, gold at the edges, moved a little. In no wind.

She looked up at him.

"Every evening," she said. "Maigo said. Till it's in the ground."

"Every evening," he agreed.

So that was the water. He carried it every evening, from whatever stream or well or posting-house pump there was, in a blue bucket a stable lad had given him because the cook said nobody else ever drew water with it right. Not for the kitchen. Not for the forge's quench. For a tree in a pot, the size of a hand, with three leaves on it, that a boy with antlers had grown from a cutting off another tree that was growing at the head of her mother's grave.

He thought it was the most important water he'd ever carried.

***

She took the first watch.

He'd known she would. He'd known on the gate at Cinderhold, when Aldous said *whatever she lets you*. On the first night on the road, by a small fire on the bank of the brown stream with the grey grass round them going still, she sat down on the far side of it with Astra across her knees and the pot beside her and looked at him.

"Sleep," said Alea.

"I'll take first."

"You took first every night for twenty days going east," said Alea. "Yan told me. And every night for twenty days going south. And every night of every road before that, for nine years, on your own." She didn't look away. "Gabe made you promise. On the road. Every night. He was going to ask Yan." She paused. "I'm asking me."

He looked at her across the fire.

She was seventeen. She was Tempered, and fourteen percent to Honed when they left the lake in the spring, and more now, he didn't know how much; she hadn't told him. She'd won the Fledgling Lists by going *with* a hook, like a pike, like her mother. She'd stood at the head of a cairn four nights ago under a full moon and said three words back to a stone that had been nine years coming.

She could keep a watch.

"Wake me at the middle," he said.

"I'll wake you at dawn," said Alea. "And you won't know the difference."

He lay down. On his back, on the bank, with his coat over him and the rod beside him and the hammer in his belt digging into his hip, and looked up at the stars over the red. Thick and bright. The moon not up yet; it came up late now, four nights past full, lopsided, going down.

He didn't think he'd sleep. He never slept on this road. Eight years, going east and coming back, and he'd never once slept more than an hour at a time on it; he'd lain awake and told Diwa the year, in order, from the top of the lake road to the cairn, and then back again.

He slept.

All night. On his back, on the bank of a brown stream, with his daughter sitting up on the far side of a small fire with a black sword across her knees, watching the dark. He didn't wake once.

She woke him at dawn. He didn't know the difference.

***

They crossed the old south line on the second morning.

He'd known they would. You couldn't get west from Cinderhold without crossing it; the road went straight over the band of red that came round the south of the fort like an arm laid across the land. They'd crossed it coming in, five weeks ago, with Group 3 and the cart and the horse that wouldn't step onto it until he walked out first.

There was no horse now. Alea walked onto the red ahead of him without stopping.

He watched the grey grass go still round her boots. Not round his. Round hers. A ring of it, a few paces across, the shivering stopping and the whispering going quiet, the way it went quiet for him. And he was two paces behind, outside it, and the grass round him was still too, in its own ring, and the two rings touched.

He stopped.

She didn't notice. She walked on, with the pot against her chest, toward the long low trench in the middle of the band of red, grassed over grey. And the ring of quiet went with her. Not as wide as his. Narrower. Newer. But there. As if the plain had decided, somewhere in the last week, on a knoll, under a full moon, that she was somebody it went quiet for too.

He thought about a boy with antlers saying *two hands now*.

He caught up with her at the ditch.

She'd stopped at the edge of it. Where he'd stopped, coming in, with Wendy watching. She was looking down at it. A long low trench across the road, half filled in, overgrown. You'd have taken it for an old field drain.

"Petra's," said Alea.

"Petra's."

She looked at it a while longer.

"You came across from the fort," she said. "That way. To get to her." She nodded east, back along the band of red toward the walls you couldn't see from here. "Because he sent you."

"Because he sent me."

"Because he read the reports wrong."

"Because he read them the way anybody would have," said her father. "And they were wrong."

Alea was quiet.

"Does she know?" she said. "Petra. That it was a feint. That you were sent here so they could—" She stopped.

"Xav knows," he said. "He worked it out on the jetty. He said he'd keep it." He looked at the ditch. "Petra doesn't."

"Will you tell her?"

He thought about it. He'd thought about it for nine years, on and off. He thought about a woman of twenty-three in a ditch with her arm broken and her sword in the other hand, who wouldn't put it down. Who'd come up a river to see who'd taught Xav to wait. Who'd stood on his jetty and said *you remember me*, and he'd said *you were the one at the end*.

"No," he said. "It isn't mine to tell. It's hers. And it isn't true, the way she'd hear it." He looked at the trench. "She'd hear *you weren't the point*. She was the point. She was the whole point. I'd have held that ditch if there'd been nothing in the fort to take. I'd hold it again."

Alea looked at him for a long time with her mother's eyes.

"Halvard might tell her," she said. "One day. Now that he's opened the drawer."

"He might," he agreed. "That's his to decide too."

She nodded slowly. Then she stepped over the ditch, very carefully, with the pot held high so it wouldn't tip, and went on across the red to the green on the far side. And the grass went quiet round her as she went. And he stepped over after her, and his went quiet round him, and on the far side, on the green, both rings stopped, and the whispering came back behind them, slowly, the way the sound comes back into a room.

***

The country came back.

Not all at once. He'd known it wouldn't; he'd walked this road east nine times and back eight, and knew it the way you know the stairs in your own house in the dark. The grey grass first, going on for a day past the old line, in tufts, standing in the bare earth. Then the earth not bare. Then the grass not grey: tired green, and then yellow, and then the colour of grass. A farm with its wall rebuilt in two colours of stone, the bottom half black. A farm with somebody in it, an old man at the gate, who looked at them going by and at the sword on the girl's back for a long time, and put his hand flat on his chest.

Alea saw it. She didn't say anything. But at the next farm, when an old woman at a well did the same thing, Alea stopped in the road and put her own free hand flat on her own chest, back. And the old woman's face did something, and she nodded, and went back to her bucket.

They walked on.

"They do that," said Alea. "The old ones."

"The ones who were there."

"Everybody this side of the red was there," said Alea. "Weren't they. Nine years ago."

"Most of them."

She walked a while.

"I used to think," she said, "that it was only you. The Redmarch. That it was a thing that happened to you, and Mum, and nobody else. A thing in a song." She looked at the farm going by, with its wall black at the bottom. "It happened to all of them. Every farm. Every old man at a gate."

"Yes."

"And they put their hands on their chests. For you."

"For her too," he said. "Most of them knew her. Every farm in the east had somebody stitched up by her once." He was quiet. "And for each other, I think. For the ones at the other farms. For the ones in Aldous's book." He looked at the road. "It's not for me. Not really. It's for the thing that happened. I'm just what walks past."

Alea looked at him sideways.

"You don't believe that," she said.

He didn't answer.

"You looked up," said Alea. "At the gate. Both times. You don't believe it's not for you any more." She hitched the pot. "You just don't know what to do about it yet."

***

He read the channel at night.

He always did. Since the first spring, on the end of the jetty, when it had knocked on his window like somebody at a door and he'd opened it and found thirty-five people from another world talking to each other in English for the first time. He'd read every line since. Two hundred at a time, scrolling up and away. He'd never written one.

He read it now, on his back on the bank of a stream on the third night, with his window open above his face and his daughter sitting up on the far side of the fire with the sword across her knees. She knew he was reading it. She'd always known, even before he told her.

He read it twice. *Kei's made enough Dose for twelve people now*. *Still facing out*.

He filed it. The way he'd filed the circle above Coldwater, and the Hollow's light, and a ring with lines in it on a rise. He didn't like how many things there were in the file now. He didn't like the word *ready*, which Hyvred had used, eleven miles from a pass, with her gold eye locked on something that wasn't there.

*In the spring*, the king had told Corvin, through Hana. *I'll come myself*.

He thought about the spring. He thought about the pass, and the rings, and a freckled boy in the third ring who'd been going to buy his father a goat called Cabbage. He thought about it for a long time, on his back, with the stars over him.

Then he closed the window.

Not yet. The spring was the spring. It was autumn, and he was on a road, and nobody needed him to do anything, and his daughter was keeping the watch.

He lay on his back on a hillside on the edge of the green country, with the first real grass in a week under him and his daughter asleep for once on the far side of the fire, because he'd made her let him take the middle, and read that for a long time.

*He didn't come down for an hour*.

He thought about a thin grey man on a gatehouse wall in Solmere, in his shirtsleeves, with a letter. Standing at a parapet. Reading. Not moving. For an hour.

*Then they sat on the step of the forge together all afternoon. They didn't hit anything*.

He put his hand flat on his chest. On his own. In the dark, on a hillside, with nobody to see.

He didn't know why. He just did. The way a man on a wall nine years ago had, without being told, and then the man next to him.

Then he closed the window, and went back to keeping the watch.

***

On the fifth evening, he used the good knife.

They'd come down into proper country by then. Farms with all their walls one colour. Villages with dogs in them, and children, and smoke. A stream that was a real stream, clear and quick over stones, with willows along it, and in a pool under the willows, where the water slowed and went deep, a trout.

He caught it with the rod. He didn't have to think about it. Alea sat on the bank with the pot beside her and her knees up and watched him do it: stand on a stone at the edge of the pool, and cast, once, and wait. And wait. And then move, once.

"That's still boring to watch," said Alea.

"Salty said."

"Salty's right."

He brought the trout up onto the bank, and knelt, and reached into his pack for the knife. The side pocket. The small one, on the left. Where she'd packed it.

He took it out.

Plain horn handle. A blade worn thin with twelve years of sharpening, thinner at the middle than at the ends, the way a blade goes when it's been honed on the same stone by the same hand every week for twelve years. The knife he cleaned fish with and cut rope with and had once taken a splinter out of the king's thumb with. The knife he always forgot.

He cleaned the trout with it. Quickly, on a flat stone at the water's edge, the way he'd cleaned ten thousand fish on the end of a jetty. And washed the blade in the stream. And dried it on his coat.

And then sat, with it in his hand, and didn't put it away.

***

Alea was watching him.

She'd been watching since he took it out. He felt her. On the bank, with the pot beside her and her knees up and her chin on them, looking at the knife in his hand the way she'd looked at the white stone at the foot of the cairn.

"You never told me," she said. "Where it's from."

He turned it over.

"You never asked," he said.

"I'm asking."

He looked at the knife for a long time. The horn of the handle had gone dark and smooth with his hand. There was a small nick in the back of the blade near the hilt where he'd once dropped it on the boards of the jetty, that first winter, and she'd said *well, that's that ruined*, and it hadn't been.

"Your mother bought it," he said.

Alea didn't move.

"The summer you were five. I'd come home from the border for the season." He turned the knife over. "I had a soldier's knife. From the garrison. Long, and heavy, with a guard on it. For cutting things that fought back. I'd had it since I was fifteen." He was quiet. "I used it to clean a fish. The first fish of that summer. Off the end of the jetty. And she watched me do it, and didn't say anything."

He looked at the stream.

"And the next day she went up to the market at Wrenhaven on her own, which she'd never done, and came back with this. From a cutler in the square. Who was very old and is dead now. And she put it in my hand." He was quiet. "And took the soldier's knife off my belt. And walked out to the end of the jetty. And threw it in the lake."

Alea's ears had gone very still.

"*You're not a soldier here*," he said. "That's what she said. When she came back along the planks. *You're a man who cleans fish. Clean them with something that's for fish*."

He held it out to the light. The thin blade. The dark horn.

"Twelve years," he said. "I've never cleaned a fish with anything else. I've never once known where it was." He nearly laughed. "She packed it for me. Every time we went anywhere. In the side pocket. Left. Because she knew I'd forget it, and she wanted me to have the thing that wasn't for fighting." He looked at Alea. "And then you did."

***

Alea didn't say anything for a long time.

The stream went past. The willows moved. On the flat stone beside him, the trout lay cleaned and silver in the evening light. Beside her, the pot with the stick in it and its three leaves, gold at the edges, moving a little in a wind that was really there this time, coming down the stream from the west.

"She threw your soldier's knife in the lake," said Alea.

"Off the end."

"It's still there."

"It's still there. On the bottom. Somewhere under where she stood." He was quiet. "Near where the white stone was."

Alea looked at the knife in his hand.

He held it out to her.

Handle first. The way you hold out a knife to anybody, so they take the safe end. Across the flat stone, across the trout, over the water's edge, toward his daughter sitting on the bank with her knees up.

She looked at it. She didn't take it.

"You've packed it five years," he said. "In secret. And then in front of me. Every time." He held it there. "Carry it."

"Then who'll forget it?" said Alea.

"Nobody," he said. "It'll be on you."

She looked at him for a long time across the knife. With her mother's eyes. Deciding. He saw her decide.

She took it.

She held it in her hand. The dark horn handle. The thin worn blade. She turned it over, slowly, the way he had, and found the nick in the back near the hilt with her thumb, and stopped there.

"What's that?"

"I dropped it on the jetty," he said. "That first winter. She said *well, that's that ruined*."

"It wasn't."

"It wasn't," he agreed.

Alea ran her thumb over the nick. Then she took off her belt, there on the bank, and threaded it through the old leather sheath he handed her, the one the cutler had made to go with it, worn soft and dark and shaped to the knife after twelve years. And put the belt back on. With the knife on her left hip. Where it would sit against her, every step, and she'd know it was there.

She put her hand on it.

"I'm not going to forget it," she said.

"I know you won't."

"Not ever."

"I know," he said.

***

He cooked the trout over the fire. With the last of Oakes's salt, and some wild garlic Alea found under the willows, and nothing else. He did it on a flat stone in the embers, the way he'd done fish on the end of a jetty for twelve years. And they ate it on the bank, with their fingers, in the dusk, with the stream going past and the pot between them.

"Mum used to say you couldn't cook," said Alea, with her mouth full.

"She said that to everyone."

"Rowan said she said you burned water once."

"I did not burn water."

"Rowan said."

"I boiled a pot dry," he said, with dignity, "because I was watching her instead of the pot. That's not burning water. That's a completely different thing."

Alea laughed. With her mouth full, on the bank, with the good knife on her hip. The real laugh. The one that came up from somewhere under everything and didn't care who heard. Her mother's laugh. He'd heard it maybe a dozen times since the Redmarch, and every time it went straight through him like a needle through cloth.

It went through him now.

He didn't mind. He sat on the bank and ate trout with his fingers and let it.

***

She took the first watch that night, too.

He didn't argue. He lay down on the bank with his coat over him and the rod beside him and the hammer in his belt, and the blue bucket, rinsed out, upside down by the fire. And looked at the stars for a while. And at his daughter, on the far side, sitting up with Astra across her knees and the pot beside her, and on her hip, catching the firelight, the dark horn handle of a knife.

He thought about a woman walking out to the end of a jetty with a soldier's knife in her hand. Drawing her arm back. Throwing it. Watching it go into the lake and not come up. And turning round, and walking back along the planks, with her ears up and her cold hands empty, and her face doing the thing it did when she'd decided something and wasn't going to discuss it.

*You're not a soldier here*.

He'd been a soldier since he was fifteen. He'd been a soldier on a wall at Cinderhold with his hands in the stone, and in a ditch, and on a rise, and in a war, and for nine years after the war in his head, every night, on the end of a jetty, going over it. He'd been a soldier on the road east and back eight times. Carrying it.

He wasn't carrying it now.

He was carrying a hammer for a smith in a town by a lake. And a rod. And a blue bucket, for a tree. And nothing else, because his daughter had let him carry nothing else.

He looked at the knife on her hip.

*You're a man who cleans fish*, Diwa had said. *Clean them with something that's for fish*.

He'd thought, for nine years, that he'd been doing that. Living on a lake. Cleaning fish. Letting the world go by. He'd thought that was what she'd meant. *Be the other thing*.

It wasn't. Or it wasn't all of it. He saw that now, lying on a bank in the dark with the stars over him. You couldn't be the other thing on your own. Not really. You could clean fish on the end of a jetty for nine years and still be a soldier inside, on a wall, with your hands in the stone, waiting for an order.

You stopped being a soldier when somebody else took the watch.

***

He slept.

All night. On a bank by a clear stream under willows, in the proper green country, with the grey grass two days behind him and the red three, and the fort and the knoll and the stone and the tree a long way east now, too far to see from any wall. With the hammer digging into his hip. With nobody needing him to do anything at all.

At some point in the night he woke, the way you wake in a strange place, not knowing why. And lay still. And looked across the fire.

She was sitting up. Watching the dark. The way he'd watched it, every night of every road, for twenty years. Very still. With her ears up and Astra across her knees and the pot beside her. Her hand resting on the hilt of the knife on her hip, the way you'd rest your hand on something to make sure it was still there.

She turned her head and looked at him. She'd known he was awake. She always knew.

"Go back to sleep," said Alea. Very quietly. "I've got it."

He looked at her for a long moment, across the fire, in the dark. His daughter. Diwa's daughter. With her mother's face and her mother's laugh and her mother's knife, keeping the watch on a road west, going home.

"I know you have," he said.

And he went back to sleep.
