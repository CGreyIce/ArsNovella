Twenty-nine.

Hyvred counted them at dawn with her eyes shut and her back to the north, the way she'd counted them every dawn and every dusk since the spring. Facing south. Facing the town: the roofs going down the hill in the grey, slate and thatch, white along the ridges with frost; the long street; the south gate with its lamp still lit. And behind her, through the back of her head, through *Range* on its short lead, eleven miles of valley, and the things in it.

Thirteen rings. Candles in the frost, hundreds of them, small and dim and all leaning the same way. A freckled one in the third ring, leaning a hair the other way. A girl at the edge of the outermost one, whose light had a blue edge to it that Hyvred had decided was her hands.

Outside the thirteenth ring, curving round on the left, the holes. You couldn't see a hole. You could only see where the light should have been and wasn't, the way you can see where a tooth's gone. She went along them one at a time, the way Corvin had taught her to count sheep on a hillside, by twos, and then back the other way by ones to be sure.

Twenty-nine. The same as yesterday. The same as the day before.

And at the far end, at the foot of the pass, a hill's worth of nothing, facing the way out. Waiting.

She opened her eyes.

"Twenty-nine," she said.

Corvin wrote it down. He had a board on his knee and a stub of pencil and his coat buttoned to the chin; he'd been writing the number down at dawn and dusk for two years, in a column that went back to the first seven. Beside him, on the parapet, Bee sat with her knees up and her wings folded flat and a stick of chalk in her fist that she wasn't doing anything with.

"Same," said Bee.

"Same."

"Three days."

"Three days," said Hyvred. She knew what Bee meant. Three days was the longest it had stayed the same since the summer. It wasn't good. When the count went up, at least you knew what it was doing.

Bee didn't say anything else. She'd been saying a lot less, since the autumn call. Since a girl with a plait had gone back for her nan's shawl. She still came up the tower every dawn and every dusk, a hundred and twelve steps, and sat on the parapet beside Hyvred with chalk in her hand; and she still slept in Hyvred's bunk with her back to Hyvred's front and her wings squashed between them; and she still drew a dog at every door that needed one. But she drew them quickly now, and didn't look at them afterwards.

Hyvred leaned over sideways, a little, until her shoulder touched Bee's. Bee let it.

"There's something going on in the yard," said Corvin.

***

There was.

Hyvred looked down over the inside of the parapet, south and east, at the guardhouse yard: the well, the posts, the mounting block, the long room's door with Bee's dogs either side of it. There were people in the yard. Not many; it was barely light. A few of Brannoch's soldiers coming off the night watch with their grey shields on their backs. Old Pruett in his leather apron. Malcolm on the mounting block with his book. Azac in the long room doorway, filling it. Some children on the guardhouse step in their coats, with their breath going up.

And two women in the middle of the yard, facing each other, a few paces apart, with their swords still in their sheaths.

"Oh," said Hyvred.

They'd said *later*. At the south gate, the evening Group 6 came in, in the sleet: Hild had come down off the gate and Petra had walked up the road from the back of the six, and they'd stopped in front of each other and looked at each other for a long time, the way Hyvred imagined two big dogs might, and Hild had said *later* and Petra had said *later*, and they'd both gone in to supper. That was five days ago. Hyvred had wondered, on and off, what *later* meant. Everybody had.

It meant now, apparently. At dawn. In the yard. In the cold.

She let her eye go.

She hadn't done it for anything but the north in weeks. She'd stopped looking at people; there'd been too much in the north to look at, and people were tiring. But she let the red mark go down now, into the yard, and the yard lit up.

Everybody had a light. That was what she'd seen on the first morning in the hall, crying at the ceiling: thirty-five people and a room full of court mages, every one of them lit from the inside, a different colour each, like a box of crayons somebody had tipped out. You got used to it. The soldiers in the yard were small warm reddish lights, tired at the edges, the way people are after a night on a wall. Pruett was a stubborn orange coal. Malcolm was a careful neat blue, like a pilot light. Azac, in the doorway, was bigger than he looked, which was saying something, and went pale blue at the edges when he was thinking about holding something up.

And in the middle of the yard, two lights she had to look away from for a moment, because they were so bright.

She'd seen bright before. The king was brighter than anybody; the king, to Hyvred, was a forge with both doors open, and she didn't look straight at him for long. Kestra was a hard narrow white, like a wire in a fire. But these two were something else. These two were *shaped*.

Hild's was pale. Silver-white, the colour of water standing in a bucket in moonlight, and very still on the surface, and very deep. On the left side of it, where the eye had been, there was a place where it went darker; not a hole, not hurt, just a place where it had grown round something a long time ago, the way a tree grows round a nail. Hyvred had looked at Hild's light every day for three years. She knew it the way she knew the sky.

Petra's was grey.

Not dull. Grey the way slate is grey, or the sea under cloud: flat and even and all one colour right the way through, so even that it looked still even when it moved. Hyvred had seen it for the first time at the south gate in the sleet and thought *oh, that's what a wall looks like before it's a wall*. It had a sound to it, almost. Not a sound. A feeling, like a wet finger going round the rim of a glass.

They stood in the yard with their swords in their sheaths and their lights burning, and didn't say anything.

Then Hild drew.

***

She did it the way she did everything, without hurrying, with her head a little on one side, the side with the eye. The sword came out of the sheath and she held it in front of her, point up, in both hands, the way you'd hold a candle in a draught; and the light came off it.

Hyvred had seen her do it at Coldwater, the first year. She'd been too frightened then to watch properly. She watched now. It came off the steel the way water comes off a weir, in a sheet, pale, running past the edge a hand and then two and then not stopping; and spread; and curved; and stood up in the air in front of Hild, taller than she was and twice as wide, a curved shield of pale shimmering light with the yard showing through it, rippled, like the yard seen through the side of a glass.

The children on the step stopped breathing. Hyvred heard them stop.

"Hit it," said Hild.

Petra looked at the wall for a moment. Then she drew too.

Plain steel. A soldier's sword, nothing on it. Hyvred saw the grey come up into it from Petra's hand, along the blade, past the edge, a hand, two, the sheen that everybody in the kingdom who'd heard of Reaching had heard of. Petra went in. Two steps, low, quick, without any noise at all, and cut at the middle of the curve with her whole weight behind it.

*BOOM.*

Hyvred felt it in her teeth, a hundred and twelve steps up. The whole wall bowed in, round the place where Petra's edge had hit it, a dent the size of a cartwheel, pale and rippling; and Petra's sword stopped in the dent as if it had gone into wet clay. Then the wall came back. All at once, like a sail filling. And it threw her.

Not far. Two paces. Petra went back across the frosted yard on her heels with her sword jarred up over her shoulder and her arms ringing, and caught herself, and stood.

On the step, a child said *oh*.

Petra looked at her sword. Then at the wall, which was standing in front of Hild exactly as it had been, curved, pale, untroubled, with the yard rippling through it.

"It gives," said Petra.

"It's meant to," said Hild. "A wall that doesn't give breaks." She let it go. It fell off her blade like rain and was gone, and the yard was just a yard again, with frost on it. "Your turn."

Petra stood in the middle of the yard with her sword up.

Nothing happened.

Hyvred watched her light. It was all there, grey and even and enormous; it went down Petra's arm into the steel, and along it, and off the end into the air, a hand past the edge, a Reaching sheen like any other. And stopped. And stayed there. A hand past the edge. Waiting, the way Petra waited, very straight, with her face giving nothing away.

"There's nobody behind me," said Petra.

"I know," said Hild. "I can hear there's nobody behind you." She'd turned her head; the good eye wasn't on Petra at all. It was on the step, on the children. "Is that how it goes?"

"Yes."

"Always?"

"Since the ditch." Petra didn't lower the sword. "I can't make it in front of me. I can only make it in front of somebody."

Hild thought about that.

She didn't get a chance to say anything. Somebody came off the step. Hyvred saw his light before she saw him: small, quick, hot, with a cold place at the edge of it like a window left open a crack in another room, the cold of a boy who'd been called by name in the spring and stood in the long room every night since. Joss. Elin's brother. He came across the yard at a run in his coat that was too big for him, and stopped behind Petra, a pace behind her, and stood there.

"Joss," said Malcolm, from the mounting block, very carefully.

Joss didn't move.

And the wall came.

Hyvred had read about it. Everybody had. The ditch, the summer before last; the hailstorm on the downs; the gravel bar at Duskhollow at the last dawn class, *the width of the bar, as high as the quay wall*. She'd read it in the channel in Xav's careful sentences and in Petra's own three words, and thought she knew what it would look like.

She didn't. It didn't come off the blade like Hild's. It didn't come off anything. One moment there was a sheen a hand past Petra's edge, and the next there was a wall standing across the yard from the well to the mounting block: flat, grey, the colour of slate, the colour of her light, as tall as the well-house roof and not curved at all, not rippling, not anything. You couldn't see through it. It wasn't light you could see through. It was just *there*, the way a hill is there, and Joss behind it with his mouth open.

Two more children came off the step. Then the rest. They didn't run; they walked, the way you walk up behind somebody's chair at a table. They stood behind Petra with Joss. Six of them. And the wall didn't get any taller. Hyvred saw it get *heavier*. She didn't have another word for it. The grey of it went down, somehow, into the frosted stones of the yard, and stayed.

Hild lifted her sword.

She didn't make a wall. She made the other thing, the thing Hyvred had seen her do in the bracken at Coldwater: the light drew back into the steel and out along it, longer and longer, thinning to an edge that ran three paces past the point, pale and fine and terrible; and she stepped in and swung it once, flat, with her whole body turning behind it, at the middle of Petra's wall.

There wasn't a boom.

There was a sound like a hammer coming down on an anvil with nothing between them. *Tunk.* Flat, short, enormous. The three-pace edge hit the grey and stopped dead. Didn't dent it. Didn't cut. Didn't go in a hair. It stopped the way a blade stops on the side of a mountain, and the whole of Hild's swing went back up the light and into the steel and into her arms, and Hyvred saw her rock on her feet, the first time in three years she'd ever seen Hild rock on her feet, and the pale edge shiver all along its length, like a struck string.

Hild let it go.

The yard was very quiet. The soldiers coming off the watch had stopped where they were with their shields on their backs. Pruett had his mouth open. On the mounting block, Malcolm was writing something very fast.

And Hild laughed.

Hyvred had heard it twice. Once on the tower in the spring, when the king said his father would have clipped his ear. Once, she thought, in the first winter, at something Tiger said that nobody else heard. It was a short dry sound, like a bark, and her whole face changed when she did it.

"It doesn't give," said Hild.

"No," said Petra.

"Then it'll break."

"Not while they're there," said Petra.

She let it go. It didn't fall like rain, the way Hild's did. It just wasn't there any more, the way a hill isn't there when the cloud comes down; and the six children were standing in a frosted yard behind a woman with a plain sword, blinking at the well.

Joss looked up at the back of Petra's head.

"Was that me?" he said.

Petra turned round. She looked at him for a long moment. Something happened at the corner of her mouth.

"Yes," she said. "Go and get your breakfast."

***

They sat on the rim of the well afterwards, the two of them, side by side, not close. Hyvred came down. She told herself it was for breakfast. Bee came with her, and stood by the long room door with her chalk, and Hyvred sat down on the mounting block beside Malcolm, who moved his book over without looking up.

He'd written:

*Hild's: GIVES. Throws you. One person. In front of HER.*
*Petra's: DOESN'T. Six children. In front of THEM.*
*IF Hild THEN a door.*
*IF Petra THEN a street.*

Hyvred read it twice.

"You were at the ditch," Hild was saying. She had her sword across her knees and her head on one side. She wasn't looking at Petra. She was listening to her.

"Yes."

"I was at the Brenn crossing."

"I know," said Petra. "Everybody knows."

"Everybody my age was at the Brenn crossing." Hild turned the sword over on her knees. "I was twenty-nine. I lost the eye the morning after. There was a thing on the bank with too many arms." She said it the way you'd say it had rained. "I didn't look east for a year."

Petra didn't say anything.

"What did you lose," said Hild. "At the ditch."

Petra thought about it. Hyvred watched her light while she did. It didn't move at all.

"Nothing," said Petra. "He stood in front of it."

Hild was quiet for a long time.

"Him," she said.

"Yes."

"They say you can see his face now."

"Since the summer."

"I've never met him," said Hild. "Twenty-six years on one border or another, and I never once stood on the same wall as him." She ran her thumb along the flat of her blade. "If he ever came here, I'd be able to see it. Halvard told me that once. In a letter. As if it were a thing to look forward to."

"He won't come," said Petra.

"No," said Hild.

They both looked north. You couldn't see anything north from the well; the guardhouse was in the way, and the north wall, and the eleven miles. They looked anyway, the way people look at a door.

"Door," said Hild, after a while. "And street."

Petra nodded.

"He's been watching us out of that forge for half an hour," said Hild, without turning her head. "He thinks I can't hear him breathing."

Hyvred turned round. The king was in the forge door across the yard, in Agnes's leather apron, with a pair of tongs in one hand and nothing in them, looking at the two women on the well with an expression Hyvred had only ever seen on him once before, in the spring, on the tower, when he'd opened the door.

He saw Hyvred looking. He lifted the tongs, a little, the way you'd lift a cup to somebody across a room.

Then he went back in, and Hyvred heard him say something to Agnes, and Agnes say *Majesty* in the voice that meant *I'll do it but I think you're mad*, and the hammer start.

***

Two more days. Twenty-nine. Twenty-nine.

On the third evening it was thirty.

She felt it come. That was new; she'd always found them after, the new ones, standing in their places in the curve the way you'd find a new nail in a fence. This one she felt *walking*. A hole, moving, the size of a person, coming down out of the pass at dusk, down the last switchback where the old paved road came off the mountain onto the valley floor; past the absence, close enough to touch it, if a hole could touch anything; along the outside of the thirteenth ring, without hurrying, the way the miller's wife had walked along the shepherd's track at Coldwater in the first year, at a market-day pace; and into the gap at the end of the curve. And stopped.

And stood the way furniture stands.

"Thirty," said Hyvred.

She heard Corvin write it. She heard John, on the other side of her, let his breath out. He'd been coming up every dusk since he arrived. He didn't send anything. He stood at the parapet with his hand in his pocket on his motes, the way Tiger stood at the gate, and listened to her count, and went down again.

"Thirty," said Corvin.

The king was there too. He came up most dusks. He sat against the north parapet with his back to the mountains, the way he always sat, so that if anything in the valley looked at the tower again it would find him first; and he didn't say anything, and Hyvred had stopped noticing him, the way you stop noticing a fire in a room until it goes out.

Behind her, on the stair, somebody coughed.

Kei. Hyvred knew his light without turning: green and quiet and level, a little grey still at the edges from eleven days of drinking his own doses. Hisui was round his neck again. She could see the snake's light too, small and cold and sharp, like a needle in a candle flame.

"Kei?"

He didn't answer for a moment. Then he came up off the last step onto the roof, slowly, and stood there with his hand up at his neck, on the snake.

"It's stopped," said Kei.

"What has?"

"The call." He was listening; not to anything she could hear. To Hisui. "Hisui says it's stopped. It's not saying names." He swallowed. "It's been saying names since the spring. Every hour of every day. In the next room. He says it just—" He stopped. "Stopped. In the middle of one."

Hyvred shut her eyes.

The valley. The rings, the candles, the holes; thirty of them now. The absence at the foot of the pass. And Kei was right. She'd never heard the call herself; that wasn't her lane, that was Kei's and Drew's, and the long room's. But she'd *seen* it, every day, without knowing she was seeing it: a kind of lean in everything up there, toward the pass, toward the way out, the way all the grass on a hillside leans when the wind's been in one quarter for a month. Every candle in thirteen rings had leaned the same way since she'd first seen them.

They weren't leaning.

They were standing straight up. All of them. Hundreds of small dim candles in the frost, perfectly upright, like flames in a room where somebody's just shut the door. The freckled one in the third ring too. All of them, waiting.

The absence turned round.

She felt it. With her eyes shut and her back to it, through the back of her head, eleven miles. It didn't move; it didn't have anything to move with. It just *turned*, the way a compass needle turns when you bring a knife up to the glass. Slowly. A hill's worth of nothing, at the foot of the pass, that had faced the way out for two years with its back to all of them, coming round.

She'd felt it once before. In the spring. It had come round then too, and looked at her, and the king had stepped in front of her and opened his door, and it had turned back. *The way you'd stand with your back to a door you knew somebody was behind*, the king had said.

It came round, and faced down the valley. Toward the town. Toward the tower.

And didn't turn back.

And then it moved.

***

She didn't know how to say it, afterwards. She said it badly to Corvin, and worse to Hild, and in the end she said it best to Bee, at three in the morning, in the bunk, in the dark: *it didn't walk. It was in one place, and then it was a bit nearer, the way a cloud's shadow is in one field and then it's in the next one, and you never see it cross the hedge.*

It came out of the mouth of the pass. Off the last switchback, where it had sat since the first winter, onto the valley floor; and the rings were in its way, thirteen rings of people standing in the frost, and it didn't go round them. It went through. The candles didn't move aside. They didn't need to. It went through them the way a shadow goes through a crowd, and out the other side, the near side, the town side, and stopped there. In front of them. Between the rings and the eleven miles.

And every candle in thirteen rings turned round to face the way it was facing.

All at once. Like a field of grass when the wind changes. Hundreds of them, turning on the spot in the frost, until every one of them was facing down the valley with the absence in front of them, the way a congregation faces the front of a church.

"It's moved," said Hyvred.

She heard the king get up.

She heard him come across the roof. She knew what he was going to do. He'd done it in the spring, and in the spring she'd let him; she'd stood behind him with her hand on his sleeve and her eyes shut while it looked at him instead, while he held it off with a whole ninth tier, *as long as it takes a polished edge to run from straw to blue*. She'd been so grateful she'd cried.

"No," said Hyvred.

The king stopped.

"Don't," she said. "Not this time." She turned round, on the parapet, slowly, until she was facing north. Until the wind off the mountains was in her face. "They need to see it. All of them. You said."

She opened her eyes.

She let the red mark go.

***

It went out over the north wall, over the frosted fields and the millstream and the first milestone, up the long pale valley in the last of the light, two miles, five, nine, the way it went every dawn and every dusk except that she never let it, any more. She let it now. And the valley came up into the scope of it, small and sharp and very far, every stone of the swept road, every frosted tussock; and the rings, thirteen of them, a crowd of people in coats standing in the frost with their faces all turned toward her; and in front of them, on the near side, the thing.

She'd never looked at it straight.

She looked at it now. She didn't know what she'd expected. A shape. A face, somewhere in it, for the red mark to lock on to, the way it locked on to everything. There wasn't one. There was nothing to lock on to. It was a place where her sight went in and didn't come back, the size of a hill, sitting on the valley floor with the frost round it and no frost on it; and it was facing her. Not looking. She'd told Corvin that in the first autumn and it was still true. It didn't look. It *faced*. The way the north faces you, on a compass, whichever way you stand.

It faced her, and she felt it come down the eleven miles toward her, along her own sight, the way the cold comes down a draught; and she held on.

She held on.

"John," she said. Her voice came out very small. "Now. Please."

She heard him take the mote out of his pocket.

He didn't argue. She'd thought he might; he'd stood on this roof seven nights ago with the whole valley in a square in the air and not sent it, *because it's for me*, and the king had said *when it moves*. It was moving. She heard his hand open. She felt the mote go past her, out over the parapet, a small gold spark going north up the valley along the line her sight had gone, faster than she'd ever seen one go; and she felt him shut his eyes beside her.

The square opened in the air.

She didn't look at it. She didn't need to. She knew what was in it: the same thing that was in the red mark, the same valley, the same rings and the same faces, the frost and the moonlight; and the thing in front of them, which to John's motes, she found out later, didn't look like anything at all. *Like somebody held the page too close to a candle*, Bee said, in the bunk, at three in the morning. *Like a burn in the middle of a picture. Brown at the edges. And nothing in the middle.*

"It's for them," said John, beside her. Very quietly. To himself. "It's for them."

She heard him open his window.

And it went.

***

She never saw it go into thirty-four windows. Nobody did, except John, and he said afterwards it felt like letting go of thirty-four strings at once, all in the same breath. But she felt the valley change. She didn't know how else to say it. For one heartbeat, the eleven miles weren't hers.

She'd been looking at them alone for three years.

Corvin had his maps and Fenwick had his numbers and Hild had her ears, and Kei had a snake that could taste it, and Drew had his hands. But the *seeing* had been hers. Every dawn and every dusk, through the red mark or the back of her head, every candle and every hole, every new one at the fourteenth place; and nobody else in the world had ever seen it, not one of them, not even the king. They'd had to take her word for it. She'd been the only window.

For one heartbeat, she wasn't.

She felt them. Not their lights; they were too far. Just the *weight* of them, all of a sudden, looking where she was looking. As if she'd been holding up one end of something heavy on her own for three years and thirty-four people had come up behind her in the dark and taken hold of it.

The thing at the end of the valley faced her, and she faced it back, and she wasn't the only one.

It held. One heartbeat. Steady. Every face.

Then John let go, and the square went out, and the mote nine miles up the valley went out with it, a small gold spark in the frost in front of the rings; and she let the red mark come home, and shut her eyes, and sat down on the roof of the tower where she was, quite suddenly, with her back against the parapet.

Bee was there. Of course she was. Wings, antennae, elbows. She sat down beside Hyvred hard enough to bruise, with her shoulder in Hyvred's arm, and didn't say anything.

The king was standing over them both. Hyvred couldn't see his face; the sky was behind it. She could see his light. The forge with both doors open. He hadn't opened his door. He'd stood there with his hand on it the whole time, she realised; the whole heartbeat; ready.

"Good," said the king.

That was all. He sat down against the north parapet again, with his back to the mountains, on her other side. Not touching. Close.

Her window was going.

It went on for a long time. She didn't read all of it. She read *it was like being in the room* three times, and *on your own any more*, and closed her window, and sat on the roof of the tower with her best friend's wings against her arm, and shook.

***

She didn't go down.

Nobody tried to make her. Hild came up at midnight with a blanket over her arm, and put it over Hyvred and Bee together, and sat down on the far side of the roof with her staff across her knees and her head on one side, listening north. The king slept, or did something like it, sitting up against the parapet with his chin on his chest. Corvin went down at ten and came back up at one with his board and a lantern with the shutter almost closed. John went down to sleep, because Malcolm came up and said *IF John doesn't sleep THEN John's tomorrow is rubbish*, and John said that wasn't even a proper one, and went.

Bee went to sleep against her. Hyvred stayed awake.

She had her back to the north again, and her eyes shut, and *Range* on its lead. She didn't look with the red mark. She didn't need to; she could feel it fine from here. The rings, upright, turned round, facing the town. The holes, thirty, along the left. The absence, on the near side of them now, facing her. Not doing anything. Sitting there in the frost like a dog at a door that's just heard its master's step on the path. Patient.

She thought about the call stopping in the middle of a name. She wondered whose.

And at about three in the morning, something came down the pass.

She nearly missed it. She'd been half asleep, in the way you can be half asleep on watch; she'd been thinking about the hall in Solmere, the first morning, the ceiling. And something at the far end of the valley, at the top of the last switchback, where the old paved road came over the shoulder of the mountain, *lit*.

A light.

Not a hole. A light. That was what woke her up. Nothing had come down that road with a light in it in three years; the threadless came down it, and the called went up it, and the called had lights, but they only went one way. This was coming *down*. A light, small and very bright, coming down the switchbacks in the dark, fast.

She opened her eyes and turned round and let the red mark go, and found it.

It was cold.

She didn't know how else to say it. Every light she'd ever seen was warm; that was what light *was*, to her eye, the warm of a person or a sheep or a hillside in the sun. Even Hisui was warm, in his small sharp way. This was bright the way ice is bright when the sun's on it. White in the middle, and blue at the edges, and none of it warm at all. A candle made out of ice. It looked, she thought, the way a candle would look in a picture of a candle, if the picture was painted by somebody who'd never been warm.

And it moved like a dog.

Low. Fast. Trotting, on all fours, down the last switchback and off it onto the valley floor, with a long easy lope that ate the ground. Its light swung as it went, at the front, on both sides, as if it had something long hanging off its shoulders that swung when it ran. It went down the paved road to the rings and into them, between the candles, between the turned, the way a dog goes between the legs of a crowd; and nobody in thirteen rings so much as turned a hair. It went out the near side of them.

To the absence.

It went right up to it. Closer than she'd ever seen anything go. And it lay down. In the frost, at the edge of the place where her sight went in and didn't come back. It turned round once, the way a dog does, and lay down with its cold light curled up small, and was still. Beside the absence. Like a dog by its master's chair.

"Hild," said Hyvred.

Hild was already awake. Nobody ever knew when Hild was awake. She was sitting exactly as she'd been sitting at midnight, with her staff across her knees; but her head had come round, and her good eye was open.

"Tell me," said Hild.

Hyvred told her. Badly. The cold, the white and the blue; the trot; the swinging. Hild listened to all of it without moving. When Hyvred got to the swinging, she held up her hand.

"Its arms," said Hild.

"What?"

"The thing that swings. At the front. Is it its arms. Long ones. Longer than its legs. Swinging when it trots, like a big dog that's got arms instead of front legs."

Hyvred looked. The red mark went in, eleven miles, as close as it had ever gone; and found it, curled up small and cold at the edge of the nothing, with its long arms folded under it.

"Yes," she said.

Hild didn't say anything for a long moment.

"Kharvos," she said.

Hyvred's tail went still.

"I've never seen one," said Hild. "Not the way you see. I've heard them. All my life. On the Redmarch they trot like that across the grass at night and you can hear their arms swing. *Hush. Hush.* Like a scythe going through long grass." She turned her head, slowly, until the good eye was on the north. "A hunter. Out of Kharvos."

"But that's—" Hyvred stopped. "Kharvos is east."

"Yes."

"It came down the *pass*. From the other side. From the *west*."

"Yes," said Hild.

On the other side of the roof, the king had lifted his chin off his chest.

He didn't say anything at first. He sat there against the parapet in the dark with his big scarred hands on his knees, and Hyvred saw his light do something it had never done in all the time she'd known him: go still. Not smaller. Not dimmer. Still, all the way through, the way a forge goes still in the moment between the hammer coming up and the hammer coming down.

"Corvin," said the king.

Corvin was asleep on his board. He woke up with a jerk.

"What's west of the pass," said the king. "On your maps."

"Nothing, Majesty." Corvin was rubbing his face. "More mountains. Then nothing. Nobody's been. Nobody's maps go past the—" He stopped. He'd heard the king's voice. "Why?"

"Something from Kharvos just came down it," said the king, "and lay down at the feet of the thing in the valley like a dog."

Nobody said anything.

Hyvred thought about Kingsferry. About a letter from Deston, the first autumn, that everybody had read, and Dennis's after it: a hunter coming through a wall in the rain, and three people on the far quay with no threads in them, standing in a row, watching it work. *Like men watching a dog work sheep*, Dennis had said, afterwards. *Two sides*. Yan had said it first, a long time ago, and nobody had known what it would look like. She knew now. It looked like a cold candle curled up asleep beside a hole.

"They've met," said the king. He said it to himself, as if he were reading it off a bar of iron. "Somewhere past the maps. They've met, and they've had a talk, and one of them's sent the other a dog." He was quiet. "Write it to Solmere, Corvin. Tonight. Through the girl. She can walk it up to Halvard in her nightgown."

"Majesty."

"And Corvin." The king looked at the north for a long time. "Don't write *we think*. Write *Hyvred saw it*."

Corvin wrote it.

The king's order went up on the guardhouse door the next day at noon.

He didn't read it himself. He'd written it with Brannoch and Corvin and Hild and Fenwick round the guardhouse table over eleven days, and Drew on the bench along the wall with his hand up, and it was Brannoch's soldiers it was for, mostly, so Brannoch read it. On the guardhouse step, in his grey, with his ploughed-field face, to the whole garrison drawn up in the yard and most of the town behind them up the street to the corner by the Bell, in the thin cold sun. Hyvred stood at the back on the mounting block with Bee. She'd slept three hours. She could see everybody's light from there, all down the street: the town, hundreds of them, small and warm and frightened, a few with the cold at the edge that meant they'd been called and stayed.

Brannoch read it the way he read everything, as if it were the price of oats.

*When it comes, it'll come at dusk.*

*The people walking behind it are ours. You know their names. Corvin has them in his book and Drew has them in his head, all of them, and the ones who walked out in the spring and the ones who walked out at the balance. Ossie Pell. Elin the dyer's girl. Rhys Hywel, who stood on that wall. The Widow Cadogan, who gave us flour. You'll know their faces.*

*Don't kill them if you can help it.*

Brannoch stopped there, and looked up from the paper, at his soldiers in their grey with the king's shields on their arms, grey-black, with the hammer-marks still bright. Nobody moved.

*Push them back. That's what the shields are for. They're for holding, not for hitting. The staves are for holding. You hold the line and you push, and you push, and you don't cut, and if one of them gets past you, you let the next one take him and you don't cut him in the back. If you can't help it, you can't help it, and nobody will say one word to you after, not me and not your sergeant and not his mother. But try.*

*If anything comes over that wall that isn't a neighbour, kill it.*

Then the places. Hyvred knew them already; she'd heard the king say them on the tower at four in the morning, to himself, with his eyes shut, the way Corvin counted sheep. But it was different, hearing them in the sun, read out like the price of oats.

*Hild at the north gate.*

*The girl from the ditch across the square, in front of the granary. The children go into the granary at dusk and are counted by name.*

*Drew in the long room, with everybody who's been called.*

*Kei with him, with his doses.*

*Tiger on his legs, between all of them, carrying.*

*Bee's dogs at every gap a child could fit through.*

*Hyvred on the tower, looking, with John beside her. So that we all see what she sees.*

There was more. Azac with his ward over the long room door; Malcolm with his book, counting everything and everybody and telling Brannoch every hour; Xav on the wall by the postern because Old Pruett asked for him; Rui Xiang where nobody would know he was. Healers in the Bell. Water in every house. Every lamp lit.

*And I'll be at the gate*, Brannoch read, in the king's words, in his own voice. *With the thing that isn't a light.*

He folded the paper and put it in his coat.

Nobody said anything. The town stood in the street in the cold sun with their hands on their chests, the way they'd learned to on this border, and looked at the king, who was standing at the side of the guardhouse step in his smith's apron with his arms folded, not saying anything either.

Then Old Pruett put his hand up.

"Majesty," he said. "What if it's my neighbour. But wearing something."

The king looked at him for a long time.

"Then push, Pruett," he said. "And keep pushing. And if you can hear him in there, tell him his name." He unfolded his arms. "That's all any of us have got. Push, and say their names. Somebody might be listening."

That night Hyvred went up the tower at dusk the way she always did, a hundred and twelve steps, and sat down on the parapet with her back to the north and her eyes shut, and counted.

Thirty. The rings, upright, facing the town. The absence in front of them, on the near side, facing her. And curled up in the frost at the edge of it, small and white and blue and cold, a light like a candle in a picture of a candle, asleep.

Bee sat down beside her. Shoulder to arm.

"Thirty," said Hyvred.

"Thirty," said Bee. She was quiet. Then: "Will you draw me something?"

Hyvred opened her eyes and looked at her.

"What?"

"You heard." Bee didn't look at her. She held out the chalk. "I've drawn forty-one dogs since the balance. I can't think of anything else. My hands just do dogs." She was very blunt about it, the way she was blunt about everything. "You draw something. Anything. So I can look at something that isn't a dog."

Hyvred took the chalk.

She didn't draw. She'd never drawn anything in her life; on Earth, she'd never been able to see the edges of the paper. She held the chalk for a long time. Then she leaned over and drew, on the flagstone of the tower roof between their knees, very badly, a circle. And a smaller one inside it, for a nose. And two dots.

"It's a cow," said Hyvred.

Bee looked at it.

"That's the worst cow I've ever seen," said Bee.

"It's me."

"It's the worst you I've ever seen." But she was looking at it. Her antennae had come up, a little, for the first time in weeks. She took the chalk back and leaned over and gave it two horns, quickly, curved, in one line each, the way she drew everything, and a red mark she didn't have red for, so she just pressed hard; and sat back.

They looked at it together. A bad round cow on a tower roof, with good horns.

Hyvred's window was open. She hadn't opened it. She hadn't noticed it go.

"The bar. Four. It's never moved for looking before." She thought about it. "It moved for looking when somebody was looking with me."

They sat on the tower, the two of them, with a blanket over their knees and a bad cow between them, until the king came up with two bowls and sat down with his back to the mountains; and below them, in the guardhouse yard, in the dark, an old soldier and a younger one sat on the rim of the well with their swords across their knees, not close, not talking, two walls with nothing between them yet, listening north.
