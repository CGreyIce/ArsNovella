She'd drawn it in one line.

That was the thing nobody knew about the running dog on the granary wall, because nobody had watched her do it. The shortest day, the first winter. Tiger on the roof of a shed with no roof, being stupid about a boy who'd gone over the hill. Bee had come out of the guardhouse with a stick of chalk because she couldn't think of anything to say to him, and she was blunt, and when she couldn't think of anything to say she drew; and she'd put the chalk on the granary wall at the dog's nose and gone. Ears. Back. The long stretched-out tail. Down round the back legs, flying, and under the belly, and out along the front legs, reaching, and back up the throat to the nose, where she'd started. And taken the chalk off.

One line. She'd never lifted it.

She'd drawn a thousand dogs since. Forty-one since the balance, at doors, at gaps, sitting up with their chalk tongues out. They took her four strokes, five, a dozen. Quick. She didn't look at them afterwards. They barked when they had to, and held for a while, and faded, and she drew them again.

She'd only ever drawn one dog in one line.

***

"It breaks where you think," said Hyvred.

The tower roof. The thirteenth night. Bee was standing at the north parapet with her right hand up in front of her, the first finger out, and a line of white light coming off the end of it into the dark like a spark off a whetstone, and stopping, a hand long, and falling to bits. Sparks, going down. Out.

Her finger was blistered. It had been blistered for four days.

"I don't think," said Bee.

"You do. Just there." Hyvred had the red eye open. She'd had it open on Bee every night for four nights, between counting the valley; she said it was a rest. "You start it. It comes off your finger. It's lovely, it's mana, I can see it going, white. And then you get to where the ear goes and you stop for half a heartbeat and think *where does the ear go*, and it breaks. Right there. Where you stopped."

Bee looked at her finger.

She'd been trying since the night after Hyvred drew the cow. She didn't know why. She'd never wanted to draw in the air before; she'd been fine with walls. Walls were good. A wall held the chalk for you, and you could go away and the drawing stayed, and did its job, and barked. But she'd sat on the tower roof with Hyvred that night and looked at a bad round cow with good horns between their knees, and she'd thought: *I can't draw on the dark.* And every night since the gate came in, standing up here, she'd looked at the dark over the north wall, at the thing in it she couldn't see, coming at her best friend, and there wasn't a wall there. There was nothing to put the chalk on.

So she'd tried the air.

Corvin had told her, the first year, that it was a thing mages did at the third tier and the fourth. Drew in the air with mana. *It needs a lot of understanding*, he'd said, in his earnest way, *of what you're making it out of*. She didn't understand anything about what she was making it out of. She understood dogs.

"Where does the ear go," said Bee.

"You know where it goes."

"On a wall I know where it goes."

"Then pretend the dark's a wall," said Hyvred. She closed the red eye and turned round, the way she did, until her back was to the north. "You're not looking at the ear. You're looking at the air. You draw the air and the air hasn't got any ears."

Bee didn't say anything.

She put her finger up again. The blister hurt. She made the light come; it came, white, off the end of her finger, the way it came into chalk, the way it had come in the first year into a charcoal sparrow that moved for three heartbeats. And she drew the nose, and the brow, and got to the ear, and the air was in the way. Black and cold and nothing. With the thing in it.

Half a heartbeat.

The line fell to bits.

"There," said Hyvred, with her eyes shut.

***

Fifteen nights.

She'd counted them, the way Hyvred counted the valley. Fifteen nights of it coming at dusk and leaning till dawn and going back across the fields at first light. She'd counted the other things too, because she was who she was, and if you were going to look at a thing you might as well look at all of it.

Huw Bevan, at the grating, the first night. Tom and Bet Callow at the laundry, and Davey Pask in the dye-yard. Fenna Rees in the lane. Seven from the inner rings, the night of Kei's dose, who'd gone out like coats off hooks. Trooper Penn on the west wall, the twelfth night, when the dog from Kharvos came back with one arm and went over the wall at the corner by the mountain and was gone again before anybody got there. Thirteen.

Three back. Rhys Hywel in the Bell, who knew what month it was now and wouldn't stop apologising to Hild. The woman from Coldwater, who sat by the north window of the Bell all day watching for her son in the second ring. The old man with the rope, who'd gone back.

Two, then. Two back.

And the king, on the gatehouse roof, every night, with his door open, going greyer. Hild in the arch with her wall a hand narrower every night, and then two. Drew in the long room on three broken ribs. Petra in the square in front of the granary, every night since the first, with a hundred and six children behind her and the neighbours who'd got in through the grating leaning on her wall till dawn, and her face giving nothing away, and her light, Hyvred said, going down into the stones a little further every night, like a post going into wet ground.

They weren't all right. None of them. Bee knew that. She knew it the way you know your own hands are cold.

Hyvred hadn't slept in a bed in fifteen nights. She slept sitting up against the parapet in the hour after dawn with her head on Bee's shoulder. Tiger touched the post every morning and said *tomorrow* to a freckled face over a wall of light and came back to the long room and sat against Drew's bad side and didn't say anything to anyone. Kei sat on the well every afternoon with the snake round his neck and his notebook and a list of seven names, and made his root smaller, and didn't use it. Drew said the names. All of them. Every night, down the chain. Thirteen more of them now.

And the others held them.

That was the other thing she'd counted. Malcolm on the mounting block at every meal, watching every bowl, writing *IF Hyvred doesn't eat THEN Bee sits on her* and then making sure she did. Azac in the long room door every night with the pale blue round him, round, and in the morning sitting on the step with his head in his hands, and then getting up and carrying Drew to the Bell. John beside Hyvred on the tower every night with his hand on his motes and never once sending anything, because there was nothing they could do with it, until the morning Hyvred asked him to send the town, just the town, the roofs and the smoke, to every window, so that somebody at the lake would know the roofs were still on. Xav on the wall by the postern, and then, at dawn, sitting by Drew's bed in the Bell, saying nothing, all morning. Rui Xiang a hand from Tiger, everywhere Tiger went, plain as a post; nobody noticing him; Tiger noticing.

Petra by Hild. Every dawn, when the walls came down. The two of them on the rim of the well, side by side, not close, not talking, with their swords across their knees.

Bee had drawn none of it. She'd drawn dogs.

***

On the fifteenth night the air tasted of snow.

She didn't taste it; Hisui did. Kei came up the tower at dusk for the first time in a week with the snake round his neck, and stood at the parapet, and Hisui put his tongue out into the wind off the mountains and kept it out for a long time.

*Snow*, said Hisui, through Kei. *Tomorrow. At first light. A lot of it.*

Hild had said so too. At noon, in the yard. *It'll snow tomorrow. I can hear the mountains going quiet.* And the king, on the gatehouse roof, at dusk, with his beard dark down the front: *If it snows the pass shuts. Corvin says. Till the thaw.*

And the thing in the dark knew it too.

Bee felt it know. She didn't have Hyvred's eye or Hisui's tongue or Drew's hands. She just felt it, standing on the tower at dusk with her best friend beside her: the whole of the dark at the foot of the north wall *changing*. For fifteen nights it had come at dusk and leaned and gone back at dawn as if it had all winter. Not in a hurry. Never once in a hurry. Hyvred had said it from the first autumn: *it doesn't seem to be in a hurry.*

It was in a hurry now.

"It's pushing," said Hyvred.

She had her back to it, and her eyes shut. Her voice was very small.

"It's not leaning. It's pushing. All of it. At the king." Her hands closed on the parapet. "It knows. It knows about the snow. It's going to—"

She stopped.

"What?" said Bee.

"It's not looking at the king," said Hyvred. "It's never been looking at the king. It's been looking past him. For fifteen nights. At me."

***

It came in an hour before dawn.

Bee heard the king go down. She didn't see it; the gatehouse roof was a long way off down the long street, a dark square against the darker dark, with the gate lamps under it. But she heard it, from the tower, a hundred and twelve steps up, the way you hear a tree come down in a wood a mile off: not a crash. A long, slow, heavy sound, like a forge door swinging shut in a gale. And then Kestra's voice, once, very high, a sound Bee had never heard Kestra make: *Severin*.

And then the arch.

Hild's wall in the arch had given every night for fifteen nights. Given and come back and thrown them; given and come back. Bee heard it give now, a long deep *boom*, and wait for it to come back.

It didn't.

The boom went on. Longer than a boom. Like a wall of water going over a weir and the weir going with it. And then a sound she'd heard once, on the downs, in the first year, when a wagon wheel went over a dry branch the size of a man's leg. A crack. And then nothing at all, and then shouting.

"*Hild's down*," said Hyvred. Her eyes were open now. The red one. She'd turned round. She was looking down the long street at the arch with the red mark gone tiny and bright. "Hild's down, she's on the cobbles, she's—she's moving, she's all right, she's—" Her voice went. "Somebody's under the gate. A soldier. Against the post. The crowd came through. They pushed him into the post—"

Bee didn't ask who. She found out afterwards. Sergeant Owen Rudd, forty, three daughters, who'd stood in the second rank behind Hild for fifteen nights saying *hold, hold, push*, and been pushed into the oak gatepost by the weight of three hundred people leaning when the wall broke, and held his shield up anyway till his ribs went.

Fourteen.

And the thing came in.

***

She couldn't see it. She'd never been able to see it. Nobody could but Hyvred, and Hyvred said she couldn't really; *it's where my sight goes in and doesn't come back*.

But she could see the lamps.

Every window in Thornwall had a lamp in it. The order. *Every lamp lit.* Fifteen nights of them, all down the long street from the north gate to the square, burning low and yellow behind the glass. And as Bee watched, from the north end, from the arch, they began to go out.

Not blown. Not knocked over. They didn't flicker. They just stopped giving light, one after another, the way your eyes stop seeing a candle when you put your hand over it. The first window by the gate. Then the next. Then both sides of the street at once, in a curve, a curve the size of a hill, coming up the long street toward the square at a walk. At a market-day pace. As if somebody were walking up the street with a great dark cloak held out on both sides and passing it over every window.

Coming toward the tower.

Behind the curve, in the dark, Bee heard doors open. People coming out of their houses into the street. People who'd never been called. Walking. Following.

"Bee," said Hyvred.

"I know."

"It's coming for me."

"I *know*," said Bee.

The curve of dark reached the square.

Petra's wall was there. Across the square from side to side, grey, flat, the colour of slate, in front of the granary with the children in it, with the neighbours from the grating leaning on it. Bee saw the curve of dark reach it. And go over it. Not through. Over; the way a cloud's shadow goes over a wall in a field, without noticing the wall is there. The wall didn't give. The wall didn't break. It didn't need to. The thing in the dark wasn't pushing at the wall. It was going past it. It didn't want the children. It had never wanted the children.

It wanted the eyes.

The lamps in the square went out. All of them. The lamps on the Bell. The lamps on the guardhouse.

The dark came up the last of the long street toward the foot of the tower.

And Hyvred, beside her, turned round to face it.

***

Bee saw her do it. Saw her turn round on the tower roof, slowly, the way she'd turned round on the evening the thirtieth came, until she was facing north, facing down the street, facing the dark coming at her; and open the red eye. And hold it open.

She didn't say anything. She didn't ask anybody to stand in front of her. She just looked at it, the way she'd looked at it every night for fifteen nights with her eyes shut and her back turned, except with her eyes open and her face to it; because somebody had to look at it, and she was the one who could.

And Bee felt it reach her.

Not the dark. The *weight*. The thing that had leaned on the king for fifteen nights with its whole self and a little more. It came up the street and up the tower, a hundred and twelve steps, and leaned on Hyvred. On a cow girl with a gold eye standing on a roof in an oversized shirt and red running shorts and a coat with three blankets over it. All of it, at once, the whole of the dark, leaning on one person.

Hyvred's knees went.

She didn't fall. She went down onto them on the tower roof with her hands on the parapet and her eye still open, still looking, with her face gone white and her mouth open and no sound coming out of it at all.

Bee climbed onto the parapet.

She didn't decide to. She was blunt, and she was assertive, and she'd never once in her life, in two worlds, waited to be told; and her best friend was on her knees on a tower roof with the dark leaning on her. So she climbed up onto the north parapet, a hundred and twelve steps up, in the wind off the mountains, in front of Hyvred, between Hyvred and it; and stood there with her wings out for balance and her antennae flat and the Pollen Puffs clinging to her collar; and put her finger up.

The blister hurt.

She didn't think about the ear.

She didn't think about the air, either, or the dark, or what she was making it out of, or Corvin, or the third tier, or the fourth. She didn't think about anything. She looked at her best friend on her knees behind her, once, over her shoulder. And then she turned round and looked at the dark coming up the long street at the foot of the tower and thought: *you're not having her*.

And drew the dog.

***

Nose.

It came off her finger the way it came off the chalk. White. Not a spark; a line. Out into the dark over the edge of the parapet, over the long street, a hundred and twelve steps down. And she didn't stop.

Brow. Ear. She didn't think *where does the ear go*. She knew where the ear went. She'd drawn it on the granary wall on the shortest day for a tiger being stupid on a roof. It went *there*, laid back, flat, the way a dog's ears go when it's running flat out. Back. The long line of the back, out over the roofs. The tail, stretched out behind, straight, the way it was on the wall. Down round the back legs, flying.

The line was enormous.

She hadn't known it would be. On the wall it had been the length of her arm. In the air, off the end of her finger, it went out over the long street the size of the street. Bigger. Out over the roofs on both sides, white, blazing, a running dog drawn on the dark from one side of the town to the other in one line, and her finger going and going in the cold, and the blister gone, and her arm going, and her whole self going down her arm and out of her finger into the line like water out of a jug.

Under the belly. Out along the front legs. Reaching.

She didn't lift it.

Up the throat. To the nose. Where she'd started.

She closed it.

And the dog barked.

***

It was the loudest sound Bee had ever heard. She'd heard it once before: on the north gate, the first spring, when a chalk dog on the wall barked at something wearing a freckled boy and the lines blazed white and the whole drawing leaned out of the stone. That had been loud. That had been a dog.

This was the running dog from the granary wall the size of a town, barking at the dark.

It didn't come off the air. It was the air. A line of white across the sky over the long street, a dog running flat out with its ears back, between the foot of the tower and the thing coming up the street; and it turned its head on the end of its one long line and *barked*, a huge deep furious bark with breath behind it, the bark of a dog who knows exactly what's standing in front of its door.

And the dark stopped.

Bee felt it stop. Felt it come up against the line, the whole of it, all its weight, the weight that had put a king on his knees and broken a wall in an arch and leaned on Hyvred; felt it lean on the dog. And the dog held.

One line. Not a wall. Not anything. A line of white in the air in the shape of a running dog, that she'd never lifted her finger off; and the dark leaned on it with everything, and it held. She felt it hold the way you feel a rope hold when you're hanging off it. All of her. Down her arm, out of her finger, into the line. Holding.

A heartbeat.

She knew it wouldn't be more. She could feel it going. The line wasn't a wall; it didn't go down into the stones like Petra's or give and come back like Hild's. It was just a line. It was the best thing she'd ever drawn and it was going to hold for exactly as long as she could hold it, which was a heartbeat, and then it was going to fall to bits like sparks off a whetstone and the dark would come up the last of the street and up the tower and take her best friend.

She held it anyway.

And over the top of the dog, past it, east, in the very first grey of the dawn, on the ridge road, where it came over the top of the east ridge into Thornwall from the far side of the kingdom, there was somebody running.

***

She saw him the way you see something out of the corner of your eye when you're looking at something else. A man. Small, from up here, a long way off, on the top of the ridge, against the grey. Running. Down the last of the road toward the top of the hill above the town, at a run; and in one hand, held out to the side the way you'd hold it running so it didn't catch, something long and thin.

She knew what it was. Everybody in the kingdom knew what it was.

He stopped at the top of the ridge.

She saw him stop. Saw him set his feet, wide, on the frozen road, the way a man sets them on the end of a jetty; and bring the long thin thing up and back, over his shoulder, in one hand.

And cast.

***

The line went out over Thornwall.

She saw it. She'd never been able to see it at the Lists; nobody could, it was too fast, you only saw what it did. She saw it now, in the grey, from the top of a tower, because it came straight toward her: a thin bright thread going out off the top of the ridge in a long flat curve, over the roofs, over the square, over the long street and the running dog and the dark, glinting, the way a spider's thread glints when the first sun catches it. A mile of it. More.

And at the end of it, small, dull, bent, a hook.

It came down in the long street.

Right there. At the foot of the tower. In front of her dog. In front of the dark, at the very edge of the curve where the lamps had gone out and the frost on the cobbles stopped; the hook came down out of the air on the end of its line and touched the cobbles, *tink*, so quietly she shouldn't have been able to hear it from a hundred and twelve steps up, and she heard it.

And something went on.

Past the hook. Out of the line. Along it, the way the line had come, and on, past where the line ended; north, down the long street, through the dark, out through the arch, out across the fields, a mile and more. She didn't see it go. Nobody could. She saw what it did.

At the foot of the tower, in the long street, at the edge of the dark, something grey with one arm had been crouched, low, like a dog by its master's chair. It came apart. In two halves. Cleanly. Without a sound. And lay on the cobbles.

And out past the arch, in the frosted fields, in the curve on the left of the crowd where thirty-one people stood the way furniture stands, eight of them at the end of the curve fell down. All at once. In halves. Not like people. Like coats coming off hooks. Like scarecrows when you cut the string. Nothing came out of them. Nothing at all.

And nobody else was touched. Not the people in the long street who'd come out of their houses after the dark; not one neighbour in thirteen rings. Not one. The thing that went out along the line had gone through three hundred people standing in the frost, between them, through the gaps, the way water goes between stones, and touched nobody who breathed.

Bee's line broke.

The running dog fell to bits over the long street like sparks off a whetstone, and went out. She felt it go. She felt her arm go, and her finger, and her knees, and she sat down on the parapet a hundred and twelve steps up with her legs over the town and her wings flat and nothing left in her at all.

And the dark didn't come.

***

It had turned round.

She felt it the way you feel a weight come off a roof. It came off Hyvred. All of it, all at once. It came off the tower and off the long street and off the town, the whole of the leaning, the whole of the dark; and it turned. Not toward the tower. Not toward the king, or the arch, or the square.

East. Toward the ridge.

Toward a man standing on the top of the ridge road in the first grey of the morning with his feet set wide and a fishing rod in one hand and the line still out over the town, a mile of it, glinting, from the tip of his rod to a small bent hook on the cobbles at the foot of the tower.

It faced him.

It didn't look. Hyvred had said that from the start. It didn't have anything to look with. It *faced*. The way the north faces you, whichever way you stand. It faced the man on the ridge with the whole of itself, the way it had faced the king on the tower in the spring, the way it had faced Hyvred tonight; and Bee, sitting on the parapet with nothing left in her, felt it face him, and waited for him to go to his knees the way the king had, and Hyvred.

He didn't.

He stood on the ridge. He didn't move. He didn't do anything at all. He stood there with the line out and let it face him, the way you'd stand on the end of a jetty with a line out and let the lake be the lake.

It faced him for a long time.

Longer than it had faced the king. Bee counted, because she was who she was; she lost count at three hundred. Long enough for the grey to go pale in the east behind him. Long enough for Hyvred to get up off her knees behind Bee and come to the parapet and put both arms round Bee's waist from behind and hold on. Long enough for something white and soft to come down out of the sky past Bee's face, very slowly, and land on the back of her hand on the parapet, and stay there.

Snow.

And then it turned round.

***

It went back the way it had come.

Down the long street. Not hurrying. At a walk, at a market-day pace, the curve of dark going away down the street the way it had come up it, out of the square, past the Bell, past the guardhouse, toward the arch. The lamps didn't come back on behind it; they'd burned out, all of them, every lamp in Thornwall, at the same moment. But the people did. The people who'd come out of their houses and followed it stopped in the street, one at a time, and stood there, and looked round them at the street in the first light with the snow coming down, bewildered, the way you'd look if you'd woken up standing in a field.

Out through the arch. Past Hild, on her feet now, with her sword point-down on the cobbles and her one eye turned east, toward the ridge. Out across the fields.

And the rings turned round, and went after it.

All thirteen. And the curve, twenty-three now, walking on the left, the way furniture would walk. Back across the fields in the falling snow, slowly, in their rings, toward the edge of the valley and the swept road going up it; with their faces turned up the valley toward the pass, the way they'd been turned for two years before it ever came down.

All but one.

In the third ring, one freckled face, turned back over its own shoulder toward the arch, toward the post. The only one.

The snow came down harder.

It came down on the fields and the walls and the roofs and the long street and the two halves of a grey thing with one arm on the cobbles at the foot of the tower. It came down on the swept road. Bee watched it, from the parapet, with Hyvred's arms round her waist; watched the snow fall on the dark straight line of the old paved road going up the valley that had been swept clean of frost and snow every day for two years, as if somebody walked down it ahead of the weather with a broom.

It settled.

The road went white like everything else. And the rings walked up it into the snow, and got smaller, and the snow got thicker, and the valley went white and soft and grey and then you couldn't see the end of it at all.

***

He came down the ridge road at a walk.

She watched him come. Down off the top of the ridge, where the road came over from the far side of the kingdom, with his rod in his hand and the line still out over the roofs, reeling in the slack as he came, and snow on his shoulders, and a roll on his back; down the hill into the top of the town, and along the east lane, and into the square. The children were coming out of the granary. A hundred and six of them, in their coats, counted out by name, into the snow, with Corvin and the schoolmaster and Malcolm at the door. Petra was standing in the middle of the square with her sword at her side and her wall gone and her face turned toward him.

He walked across the square past her and nodded, once. She nodded back.

He came up the long street. Past the Bell, where Agnes was standing in the side door with a kettle. Past the guardhouse, where the long room door was open and Azac was in it and a boy with tiger ears was coming out past him at a run, and then stopping, and then not running.

At the foot of the tower he stopped and bent down and picked up his hook off the cobbles, where it was lying beside two halves of something grey in the snow; and looked at it, and wiped it on his coat, and hooked it into the cork of the handle where it lived.

Then he looked up.

At the tower. A hundred and twelve steps up. At a cow girl in a coat with three blankets over it and her best friend in her arms on the parapet, with snow in their hair.

Bee couldn't see his face. Nobody under the sixth tier could. She'd stood in the stands at the Lists and looked at him all week and never once been able to hold it. She couldn't now. She could see his shoulders, and his hands, and the rod, and the snow on him; and the way he stood at the foot of the tower and looked up.

He lifted the rod. A little. The way you'd lift a cup to somebody across a room.

"Is that him?" said Hyvred, against her back. Very small. "Is that—"

"Yes," said Bee.

"Nobody said."

"No," said Bee. "Nobody said."

Down at the arch, at the north gate, a woman with one eye had turned round from the fields and was looking up the long street at the man at the foot of the tower. She'd been looking for a long time. Bee saw her put her hand flat on her chest, the way people did on that border; and keep it there.

Hild could see his face. She was the sixth tier. She'd said once, on the well, that she'd never met him.

Bee never asked her what it looked like. She thought, afterwards, that it was the only thing in fifteen nights she hadn't counted.

***

Her window was open. She didn't remember opening it.
