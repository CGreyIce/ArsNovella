Higu kept lists.

She'd always kept them. On Earth, in a notes app, in the margins of things: songs she liked, things people said, the order the stars came out in at the window of her old room. Here, in a notebook with a soft leather cover that Maren had given her in the first month, when Maren saw her writing on the backs of old letters. She'd filled most of it.

*Things Alea says that aren't answers.* Two pages. She'd started it in the first autumn and stopped in the spring, when the answers had stopped mattering, because she'd already known.

*Fish in the basket.* A column. Dates down one side, a number down the other. Two, two, two, three, three, four. Five, since the autumn. She'd never missed a day.

*The lamp.* Another column. What time the lamp at the end of the lake road went out each night, as near as she could tell from the bells. Most nights, around midnight. Some nights, much later. One night in the summer, the night of the sum, never.

And on a page on its own, in the middle of the notebook, from the night she'd sent a moth down the hill on the first full moon after the channel and watched a man on the end of a jetty read a window, and close it with two fingers:

*Alea is his. He has a window.*

She'd never shown anyone that page. She'd told them, in the summer, at the table, when it all came out. She'd never shown them the page.

Tonight she'd left the notebook in the upper room, on her cot, closed. For the first time in sixteen months, she didn't want to write anything down.

She wanted to see it.

***

They came round the corner at the bottom of the square, past the church, and stopped.

Higu stopped first. She always noticed things first; it was what the lists were for. She stopped with her lantern in her hand and the snow coming down very lightly round it, and the others stopped behind her, one after another, and nobody said anything.

The top of the lake road was empty.

Not empty. The road was there. The long stone wall going down on the left, and the stubble fields under the snow on the right, and the lake below, white from shore to shore, and the lamp at the far end, in the window, burning. All of it exactly where it had been every night for a year, from the upper window.

But the place at the top of the road where Rowan Hale stood was empty.

He'd stood there every day for sixteen months. Every day she'd been here. Big and grey and immovable, with his chain on or off, at the top of the lake road, in front of it. She'd never once seen anybody go past him. Not a trader, not a pilgrim, not a child. The whole town walked round that place the way you'd walk round a post.

He wasn't there.

He was standing beside it. To the left, against the long stone wall, in the snow, with his chain on, very straight. And Odette beside him, thin and dark, with her keys at her belt and her hands folded in front of her. Both of them out of the way. Both of them looking at the five girls with lanterns who'd stopped at the corner.

Rowan had stepped aside.

***

He didn't say anything. Higu had thought he might. She'd half expected a speech; he gave speeches at everything. He just looked at the five of them, one after another, with his big grey face very still. And then he nodded. Once. And bowed, very slightly, from the neck, the way you'd bow to people passing on their way to something that mattered.

Odette was crying. Without any fuss. With her face perfectly composed and the tears running down it.

Behind the five of them, Higu became aware, the town had come. The whole of Wrenhaven, it felt like. Out of the square and down the street behind them, in the snow, in silence. Following them. The way the town had followed the king.

They came as far as the corner. And stopped.

Higu turned her head and looked back. They were standing in a long ragged crowd across the bottom of the square, two hundred people, more, with lanterns and without, children on shoulders, the baker with flour on his apron, Garrow's apprentice, the egg-woman and her boy. Looking down the lake road. Not coming any further.

Because Rowan had moved.

He'd stepped out from the wall again. Behind the five of them now. Between them and the town. With his back to the lake road and his face to Wrenhaven and his big arms a little out from his sides, the way they'd been for the king. Standing in front of the town, now, instead of in front of the road.

*Nobody follows us down.*

He'd just turned round, Higu thought. That was all. After eight years, he'd turned round.

***

They went down the lake road.

The first of the thirty-five. Higu thought it, walking, and couldn't stop thinking it. A year and a half. Thirty-five people scattered across a kingdom, and every one of them talking about him every night, and wondering where, and not one of them had ever walked down this road. The five of them, at the upper window, had looked at it every night for a year. A quarter of a mile. Ten minutes, at a walk. She'd sent a moth down it once, in the dark. She'd never once put her feet on it.

The snow on it was trodden flat. The town's feet, the day of the stones. A king's, and his guard's. Alea's, every morning and every night. And one other set, in the middle, older, going both ways, every dusk and every dawn for eight years: a plain man walking up to the market for eggs and back.

They walked in a row. Five lanterns. Xola on Higu's left, holding her hand, very tightly, without seeming to know she was doing it. Squiddles on Xola's head, gone a soft anxious lilac. Gigichi on Higu's right, not holding anything, with one hand in her coat pocket where Higu knew the gold watch was, ticking. PenguChi on the end, with Waddles inside the front of her coat with just his head poking out, walking steadily, the way she did everything. Thea on the other end, white, with her lantern held out in front of her as if she were reading by it.

Nobody spoke. The snow came down. The lake lay on their left, beyond the reeds, white and silent and enormous, going away into the dark to the pines on the north shore. The fields lay on their right, under the snow, with the long stone wall between. The road went down.

And the lamp at the end of it got closer.

***

Higu thought about the first day.

She hadn't meant to. It just came up, walking. The road to the market, sixteen months ago, in the late summer, in the dust. Thea and Gigichi in the middle of the road, shouting at each other about something that had been coming for a year. Xola and PenguChi trying to make them stop. Higu with her hand on Gigichi's arm, saying something low and urgent that she couldn't remember now. Maren and Bram watching, ready.

And a plain man coming down the road with a fox girl and a basket, and saying *you're blocking the road*.

Gigichi had said *then walk around*.

And he'd looked at them. Each of them. One after another. The way nobody had ever looked at Higu in her life: as if he were reading something written on her face, and knew the handwriting. And then Maren had gone down on her knees in the dust, and Bram, and the man had walked round them, and gone to the market, and bought eggs.

She'd written it down that night. The first thing in the notebook, before the notebook, on the back of an old letter. *A fisherman. Looked at each of us like he knew us.*

She'd been right. She'd been right the very first night, and it had taken her a year to let herself know it.

***

The hen-yard came up out of the dusk on the right, at the end of the road. A low fence. A shed. A woodpile, neat as a wall, under snow. The cabin beyond, timber gone silver, with smoke coming out of the stone chimney and the lamp in the window, close now, gold, the way it had been from the hill every night, only big. Real. A window. With a sill. And a curtain half drawn.

And the jetty beyond it, running out over the frozen lake on its grey posts, with nets hung along it under the snow, and nobody on the end of it.

For the first time in a year, nobody was on the end of the jetty.

The smell reached them at the gate. Woodsmoke, and roasting pumpkin, and honey, and something else under it, sharp and sour and hot and strange, that made Higu's eyes water a little in the cold. Something she'd never smelled in this world. Or the other one.

*A stew that tastes like a fire in a swamp*, Alea had said.

The hens came for them.

***

Three of them. Out of the dark of the hen-yard, in a line, across the snow, with their necks out and their wings half up, making a noise like a kettle coming to the boil. The one at the front was big and red and the size of a small dog, and she came straight at the gate, and straight at the five of them, with the cold total fury of something that has decided you are the enemy and is going to do something about it.

Xola shrieked. Squiddles went white.

Gigichi said a word Higu had only heard her say twice.

And PenguChi stepped forward.

She didn't do anything. She didn't draw her practice sword, or put out her hand, or kick. She just stepped forward, in front of the four of them, with Waddles' head poking out of the front of her coat, and looked down at the big red hen coming at the gate, and said, in her calm, low, steady voice, the voice that did things to rooms:

"It's all right."

The hen stopped.

It stopped dead, in the snow, a pace from the gate, with one foot up. The other two piled into it from behind and stopped too. All three of them stood there in a heap in the snow, with their heads on one side, looking up at PenguChi.

"It's all right," said PenguChi again. More gently. "We're invited."

The big red hen looked at her for a long moment, with one round orange eye.

Then it sat down. Right there, in the snow, by the gate. Fluffed itself up, and tucked its feet under, and sat, the way a hen sits on a nest. The other two looked at it, and at each other, and sat down too.

Nobody said anything.

"Chi 2," said Gigichi faintly. "What did you *do*."

"Nothing," said PenguChi. She sounded as surprised as anyone. "I just told them."

***

They went through the gate. Past the three hens sitting in the snow like loaves. Up the short path to the step.

And stopped.

There was a step. One stone step, swept clear of snow. And a door. Plain wood, gone grey, with an iron latch. And beside it, the window, with the lamp, and the curtain half drawn. Higu could see in. A table, with a white cloth on it that looked as if it had been borrowed from somewhere much grander, and bowls on it. Seven bowls. Somebody had put a little bundle of dried flowers in a cup in the middle. A fire. A rag rug with nothing on it. A black sword leaning against the foot of a narrow stair. A fox girl's face, appearing at the window, white, and her ears straight up, and then disappearing again very fast.

None of them moved.

Higu stood on the path with her lantern in her hand and Xola's fingers crushing hers and looked at the door. A quarter of a mile. A year and a half. Two pages of non-answers, and a column of fish, and a column of lamplight, and a page on its own that said *he has a window*.

And a door. Right there. That nobody had knocked on.

***

Thea lifted her hand.

She did it slowly. She'd given her lantern to Gigichi without seeming to notice, and her hand came up in front of her, in the cold, the way it had come up in front of the bonfire the night before, shaking. To knock.

She didn't get to.

The latch lifted from inside before her knuckles touched the wood. Higu heard it: the small iron click, very loud in the silence. And the door swung inward, and the warmth came out round them all at once, woodsmoke and pumpkin and honey and the sharp sour fire-in-a-swamp smell, and the gold light from inside spilled out onto the step and the snow and the five of them standing in it.

He was there.

***

He was in the doorway. A plain man. No coat. His sleeves were rolled up to the elbow and there was flour on one forearm, and a cloth over his shoulder, the way you'd have a cloth over your shoulder if you'd been cooking all day and forgotten it was there. Hair she couldn't have told you the colour of. A face she couldn't hold on to. She'd never been able to, any of the times she'd seen it, at the market or the gate or the fire. Her eyes slid off it.

She looked at his hands instead. She'd learned that from Kestra, through PenguChi. *Feet first. Then hands.*

His hands were shaking.

Very slightly. At his sides. The way Thea's had shaken, a moment ago, raised to knock. Higu had seen this man, from a window, with a moth, cut nothing in the dark and sit down and go on reading. She'd seen him stand at the edge of a bonfire with the whole town making room for him. She'd seen the king of Aurelan walk down this road to his door.

His hands were shaking.

She looked up from his hands to his face. And this time, for the first time, it didn't slide.

***

He looked at them.

One after another. The way he had on the first day, on the market road. The way he had at the gate in the autumn, going to the Redmarch. The way he had at the edge of the fire, last night. Each of them, slowly, as if he were reading something written on their faces and wanted to get every word. Thea, white, with her hand still raised. Gigichi, with two lanterns. Xola, crushing Higu's fingers, with Squiddles gone gold on her head. PenguChi, with Waddles. Higu.

He looked at Higu last.

She'd been looked at by him three times before. On the road. At the gate. At the fire. Every time, she'd felt it: as if he knew the handwriting.

This time she knew it too.

He opened his mouth. And shut it. And Higu saw his throat move, the way a throat moves when somebody's trying to say something that's been sitting in it for a very long time and won't come.

Then he said it.

"Hi."

***

In English.

It went through all five of them at once. Higu felt it go. She felt Xola's hand jerk in hers. She heard Gigichi's breath go out of her in a rush. She saw Thea's raised hand drop to her side as if somebody had cut a string.

English. The first English any of them had ever heard him speak. A year and a half of a plain man saying plain things in the language of Aurelan, *you're blocking the road* and *they're the lake's fish* and *mind the ice by the trough*, and the window turning it into English for them on the way, so that it sounded like English and wasn't. And now, at his own door, in the snow, with flour on his arm and his hands shaking, the real thing. His own. Out of his own mouth.

*Hi.*

It wasn't his voice. Not the old one. Higu had heard the old one for years, on streams, in her chat, laughing, in the clips people had posted after. This was a different throat. Older. Rougher. A man's voice from a body born in a cottage on the other side of the world.

But it was the way he'd said it. The shape of it. *Hi*, with nothing after it, a little too quickly, as if it were the beginning of a sentence he hadn't finished deciding. The way he'd typed it in her chat a thousand times, coming into a stream late at night. *hi.* Lowercase. And then nothing, for a minute, while he read the chat. And then something stupid, and *trust*.

She knew it. She knew it the way you know a song from the first two notes.

***

Nobody answered him.

Nobody could. They just stood there on the step in the snow with their lanterns, five of them, staring at him.

He looked at them. And something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Something that was trying to be one and was too frightened to finish.

"Come in," he said. Still in English. A little faster now, as if the first word had broken something loose. "You're letting the cold in."

***

Higu had kept lists for sixteen months.

*Things Alea says that aren't answers.* *Fish in the basket.* *The lamp.* A page on its own, in the middle of the notebook, that she'd never shown anyone.

She'd written down every single thing she'd seen, since the first day, on the road, in the dust. Everything he did. Everything he didn't. Every non-answer, every fish, every night the lamp burned late. She'd thought, some nights, sitting at the window with the notebook on her knees, that if she wrote down enough of it, carefully enough, she'd understand.

She'd left the notebook on her cot tonight. Closed.

She was glad.

There wasn't a list for this. There wasn't a page in the world that would hold it. A plain man in a doorway with flour on his arm and his hands shaking and his face, for the first time, staying still under her eyes. Saying *hi*. In English. Lowercase. The way he always had.

She didn't write anything down.

She let go of Xola's hand. She took one step forward, onto the stone step, into the gold light and the warmth and the smell of a fire in a swamp, and looked up at him, close, closer than she'd ever been.

"Hi, Ice," said Higu.

And went in.
