Hisui tasted it first.

Xav was awake when Kei came in. He mostly was, at that hour. He'd got into the habit in the autumn, when Drew's ribs were bad and somebody had to be awake to hear whether he was breathing right, and the habit had stayed when the ribs had gone; he lay on his pallet by the long-room wall with his eyes shut and listened to forty people sleeping, and somewhere in the fourth hour after midnight he'd hear Azac in the door say *Bee's turned over* very softly to nobody, and know the night was most of the way through.

He heard the door instead. And Kei's feet, bare, on the boards, coming fast and trying not to.

Xav opened his eyes. Kei was standing in the middle of the long room in his shirt with Hisui wound twice round his neck and the snake's white head up past his ear, very still, the way it went when it was tasting. Its tongue went in and out. In and out.

"What," said Xav. Quietly.

"He woke me." Kei's voice was low and flat, the way it always was. "He says the wind's changed." He listened, to something nobody else could hear. "He says it tastes like the other side."

"Of what?"

"The pass." Kei looked at the north wall of the long room, as if he could see through it. "Wet stone. Somewhere warm. Somewhere that isn't here." He paused. "He says he's never tasted it before. Not since we came. He says it's the first time anything's come *over*."

Xav sat up.

He could hear it, now that he was listening. He hadn't noticed. Under the forty people breathing, under Azac's low voice in the door: a sound he'd heard every night for a week without hearing it, the eaves running. But louder. Not a drip. A pour, all along the roof, off every slate at once, the way it ran in the afternoons in the thaw when the sun was on it. At the fourth hour of the night, in the dark, with no sun on anything.

He got up and went to the door. Azac moved his shield to let him past, and looked at him, and didn't say anything, which for Azac was a lot.

Outside, the wind was warm.

That was the wrong thing. That was the thing that made the hair stand up on his arms. A wind off the Greyspine at night in Thornwall was a knife; it came down the valley off the snow and through every coat you owned and you walked bent over into it with your eyes shut. This one came down the long street out of the north like breath. Soft. Wet. Smelling of rock. It moved his hair. Every roof in the town was running with water, and the snow in the lanes, which had been grey and hard at dusk, had gone soft and dark and was sinking as he watched, the way sugar sinks into tea.

Up on the north gate, against the stars, a figure with her hands flat on the stone.

Hild came down off the gate at first light and said it in the guardhouse yard, to whoever was there, which was Xav and Petra with their sticks and Tiger getting his breath at the well and Fenwick with his maps under his arm.

"The mountains are up," she said. "Properly, this time. All the way." She looked north with her one eye, though from the yard there was nothing to see but the back of the north wall. "That's the snow-thief. The shepherds call it that. Comes over the top once a spring, if you're lucky, and eats a month of snow in three days." She took her hands out of her armpits. "We're not lucky."

Fenwick had his maps open against the well-house wall. "Twelve feet in the notch a week ago," he said. "If this holds two days the road's bare to the top. I'd have said the third week of spring. It'll be this week."

Nobody said anything to that.

"Sticks," said Petra.

They'd been doing it every morning since the snow.

Not because it was any use. Xav knew that and Petra knew it. There was nothing in Thornwall for a sword to do this winter; nothing had come down the valley since the last dawn of the autumn, and if it came again it wouldn't be the kind of thing a sword was for. Petra did it because it was what she did in the mornings, the way the king made hinges and Tiger ran. Xav did it because she'd asked, the first morning, holding out the driftwood stick she'd brought all the way from the lake, and he'd never once in three years said no to her.

She stood across the yard from him in the grey with the stick held low in her right hand, and waited.

That was the whole of it. She waited. She'd always waited; it was what had made her dangerous at Duskhollow, a woman past thirty standing on a gravel bar letting fifteen recruits come at her one at a time and not one of them touching her. But she'd waited differently since the summer at the lake. Since she'd put a wall up on the shingle with a man behind her and felt it hold. Before, she'd waited the way you wait for somebody to make a mistake. Now she waited the way the wall did.

Xav waited too.

He knew what she'd feel if he moved first. *Now* was his, and nobody else had it, but Petra had fought him four hundred mornings, and she didn't need a skill to read him. She'd read the lean. There was always a lean: a weight going on to the front foot before a man knew he'd decided, a hand tightening, a breath held. *Now* was the thing in him that felt it in the other man. The heartbeat before. And Petra, who had none of it and all of it, would see his lean coming from across the yard and be somewhere else when it got there.

So he didn't lean. He stood with the stick and let the deciding stay undecided. *Third*: the thing he'd found in the Lists, against Rui Xiang, with Declan behind him in the crowd. Decide late. So late it's not a decision any more, just the move, coming out of you the way a breath does.

The yard dripped. Tiger had gone, back to the well. Fenwick had rolled up his maps and was watching from the well-house door with the face of a man who'd seen a great many things on the Redmarch and didn't think this was one of them.

Petra's eyes went to his left foot.

There. A hair. Not a lean; a look. She was asking it. And *Now* felt her *ask*, the way you'd feel somebody's hand go to a door, and the stick was moving before he knew it was, low, from the left, under her eyes and up, the line he'd never once used on her—

She wasn't there.

She was a hand to the right, which was nowhere, which was where she'd been standing all along with her weight on the other foot, and the driftwood stick was lying flat against his ribs as gently as somebody laying a hand on a horse.

"Again," she said.

"You asked my foot," said Xav. "On purpose."

"Yes."

"So I'd feel it."

"So you'd feel *something*." She stepped back. "You've been standing there all winter not deciding. It's very good. It's better than anybody I've ever seen. And you've been standing there all winter not deciding because there's nothing in the yard worth deciding about." She held the stick low again. "I wanted to see what you'd do if there was. You went for it."

"I went for what you gave me."

"I know," said Petra. "That's what everybody does." She looked at him. "He doesn't."

She didn't say who. She never did.

Xav stood in the wet yard with the warm wind going over the wall and thought about the shingle at the lake. The third move. The thing that had come out of him that neither of them had chosen, and the red coming up under it in the man across from him, huge and cold, and *turning*. Like a fish that won't run. He hadn't gone for it. Everything in him had been going, and he hadn't gone.

"Again," he said.

Thirty-three.

It had said nineteen on the shingle at the lake, the morning of the third move. Twenty-six by the time they got to Thornwall, in the sleet. Thirty-one at midwinter. He'd watched it all winter, every morning, the way he'd watched everything all winter, and he couldn't have told anybody what moved it.

He knew what *Honed* was supposed to be. Everybody did; it was on the first page of every guard's primer at Duskhollow, and Petra had said it to them the first week, standing on the gravel bar with her sword out. *Tier three. Your aura goes into the steel. The steel won't dull and it won't break.* She'd shown them: run her blade along a whetstone the wrong way, hard, edge first, a sound like a cat. And the edge was still an edge. Not a burr on it.

It hadn't moved for any of the things he'd tried. He'd spent the autumn at Duskhollow and the first month in Thornwall trying to push it into the blade, like Malcolm's enchantments, like a coat of something; he'd held a practice sword at arm's length in the guardhouse until his shoulder burned and it had stayed a practice sword. He'd cut. He'd cut a clay dog in half at the end of Tanners' Row on the first night of the battle, with *Now* screaming at him, and it hadn't moved a point.

You watched a thing for long enough and it showed you how it worked, and then you knew what to do. The window had shown him nothing. *Save one for the jetty*.

Fenwick was right. The snow-thief held two days.

By the second noon you could hear the valley from the north wall: a sound like a crowd a long way off, all the water of the winter coming down off the Greyspine at once, the millstream gone over its banks at the bridge and brown across the swept road. By the second dusk the white notch at the top of the valley, which had been white since the last dawn of the autumn, had a dark line down the middle of it.

The road. The switchbacks, coming out of the snow, the way the swept road had come out a fortnight ago. Bare stone, going up in zigzags to the gap in the Greyspine and over.

The king called them to Agnes's forge after supper.

He didn't call it a council. He never did, since the autumn; he said a council was what you had in Solmere with chairs, and he'd had enough of chairs for one life. He just sent Kestra round to say he'd be at the forge, and the people who ought to be there came. Brannoch, the commander, grey and square, with his helmet under his arm the way he always carried it, as if he might need it suddenly. Fenwick with his maps. Corvin with the book. Hild in the side door, where she could see north. Petra beside her, not quite touching shoulders. Agnes at the far hearth pretending she was only there because it was her forge. And on the long bench under the window, the ten of them, Group 5 and Group 6, in a row, the way they'd sat since the first day the king had said *in* and pointed at it.

Hyvred sat in the middle. She was always in the middle now. Bee on one side of her and Drew on the other and the rest of them going out from there, like a thing that had been built round her, which in a way it had.

The king sat in Agnes's big chair with his hands on his knees. He looked at Hyvred.

"Tell me," he said.

She told him. She could do it out loud now, mostly; it had taken her the winter. The notch bare. The switchbacks bare to the bottom. The eight on the far side: standing in a line in the snow at the top of the far road, the way they'd stood for a fortnight, but with their heads turned now toward the gap. Not moving. Not yet.

"Not yet," said the king. He looked at the fire. "When?"

"Tonight," said Hyvred. "Or tomorrow. When it's bare all the way." She stopped. "That's not the sight. I don't know it from the sight. I just know it."

Since we're all here, and since I'll be going home soon and somebody else'll have to ask it after. Do we stop them?"

"That's the other question," said the king. "Fenwick."

The town at the bottom, at the mouth of the valley. The valley going north eleven miles, with the swept road up the middle of it. At the top, the rings, drawn as rings, in Fenwick's careful hand, thirteen of them, and the curve outside them like a fingernail. He said it the way he said everything, fast and very clear, so that you couldn't mistake a word of it. He was sitting at the end of the bench beside Tiger with his hands between his knees. He didn't look at anybody.

Past the rings, past the curve, past it. I'd be at the bottom of the switchbacks by morning." He paused. "They come down one at a time. You could stop one at a time."

"Yeah," said Xav. "We let them come. And we count them. And we stay here." It feels like doing nothing. It's not. It's the move."

Malcolm wrote something in his book.

The king hadn't moved. He sat in Agnes's chair with his hands on his knees and looked at Xav for a long time, the way he'd looked at Azac over a shield boss, the way he looked at iron in the fire when it was nearly ready.

"Aye," he said at last. "That's what I'd have said." He leaned back, and the chair creaked. "I'm glad I didn't have to. It's better coming from one of you." He looked down the bench. "Rui Xiang."

He didn't look at Petra. Xav saw him not look at her. It was very deliberate; it was the way you'd not look at a thing you wanted very much and had decided not to ask for.

Petra saw it too. Xav saw her see it.

The new slates.
