Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 155

The Reckoning

4,402 words · 19 min read

The whole of Tell went up the hill.

He’d never seen it. Not the first time. He’d thought, from what Aldo had said in the evenings at the Climbers’ House, that it would be like a festival, and it wasn’t, and it was. Nobody was dancing. Nobody was selling anything. But from the middle of the morning on the four hundred and seventh day, everybody in Tell was walking the same way.

Up.

Out of the Lantern Market and the Hay Market and the Shambles and the coopers’ lanes. Out of the tall clean houses on the third ring and the fourth, where the shutters had stayed shut for forty days. Out of the farms on the plain, in carts, with their children in the back. Up the Wellgate, in their best clothes, quietly, with their account books under their arms. A whole city, climbing its own hill, the way thirty-six people climbed a stair.

They went up with it.

All thirty-six. Out of the copy-room yard and through the gate and up the Wellgate in the middle of everybody. He walked in the middle of them. Gabe on his right with Hearthwall on his back. Thea on his left with the lantern. The cord on his wrist where anybody could see it. And the thirty-three others round them, in no order, the way they’d walked up every hill and every stair since the white room.

People looked at them. Of course they did. But not the way they had in the market on the first day, when the whole square had stepped back. Most of the faces round them on the Wellgate he knew now. The knife-grinder from the Shambles, who nodded at Rui Xiang. The lamplighter, who walked beside John for a while and said nothing. Nell from the half-door, on the arm of a grandson, who saw him and held up a bun in a cloth, for later. The cooper from Cooper’s Lane, and his brother. Two old sisters arguing about window boxes. Wat in his best grey-blue coat with his moustache waxed. Edith on his arm, looking straight at Thea, nodding once, hard.

And the clerks weren’t on the corners.

He noticed it at the first turn. And the second. Every turn of the Wellgate, every dead lamp, every place where a grey robe had stood for forty-nine days with a book on its arm. Empty.

“They’ve gone up,” said Drew, beside him. Quietly. “Hawise says. They stand at the back of the hall for the Assize. Every year. All of them.”

A hundred and eleven, he thought. He’d counted them going in last night.

The square in front of the Assize was full by noon.

It was a wide flagged space at the very top of the hill, under the great pale wall, with the Assize’s broad steps going up to its open doors at one end and Aldo’s row of narrow houses at the other. And it was packed. Shoulder to shoulder. Thousands. More people than he’d ever seen in one place in either life, standing in the sun at the top of the world with their account books under their arms and their faces turned toward the steps.

The thirty-six stood in the middle of it. People had made room for them without being asked. Not much. A little space, the way you’d make room for somebody carrying something.

Aldo was at the front. Davy and Wat had brought him up in his chair, the twenty paces from his green door to the foot of the Assize steps, and set him down there, in the front row, with his rug and his writing-board on his knees. He’d turned his head when the climbers came into the square, and found Ice in the middle of them, and nodded once.

I’ll sit on your left.

The bell in the square tower struck noon.

And the doors at the top of the Assize steps were already open, and out of them, onto the top step, came a clerk.

It was the tall one. He knew it at once, though he’d never seen it. Thea had described it. Rui Xiang had. John. Taller than the others, in the same long plain grey, with the same plain face. And in its arms, held against its chest the way the clerk at the well had held its book on its arm, the book.

Not as big as Thea had said. Smaller, out here, in the sun. The size of a big book. But grey. All of it. Grey covers, grey edges, the grey of the Countinghouse hall and the Steward’s robe and the Quiet’s face.

The square went silent.

Not all at once. From the front, where people could see it, backwards, like a wind going over a field. Until there was nobody in the whole of the top of Tell making a sound but a baby, somewhere at the back, and then not even that.

The clerk opened the book.

It stood on the top step of the Assize with the book open in its arms and its plain face turned down toward the page, and Ice felt the square hold its breath. Three hundred years, Askew had said. Three hundred years of a clerk standing on these steps at the Reckoning, opening this book, and saying nothing. And coming down. People’s grandmothers had come to watch it. Then people had stopped.

They’d all come today.

The clerk lifted its head.

“The climbers,” it said.

Its voice was flat and ordinary, the voice of the clerk at the well, the voice of the tall thing at the lectern that had said told and where did you come from. Not loud. But it went out over the square and down the Wellgate and, he thought, all the way to the bottom of the hill, the way the evening telling did, because everybody had stopped to let it.

“The climbers stand on the thirteenth.”

And then, after a moment:

“Thirty-six.”

Nobody moved.

For a long moment, nobody in the whole square moved at all. Thousands of people, in the sun, with their account books under their arms, standing absolutely still.

Then, somewhere near the front, an old woman started to cry.

He heard it. Small and cracked and wet, and then louder. And somebody beside her put an arm round her. And then somebody else, at the back, a man’s voice, said something he didn’t catch, and somebody laughed, a short shocked laugh, the way people laugh when they don’t know what else to do. And the square started to breathe again. All at once. A sound like the sea coming in.

And under it, very small, somewhere to his left, in the crowd, at about the height of his waist, a child’s voice began to sing.

Thirty up the well and thirty up the stair—

Somebody shushed it. And then somebody else, a woman, said no, let her. And the child kept going. Thin and high and pleased with itself.

Thirty in the Rolls and thirty in the air—

And another child picked it up. And another. All over the square. Not loudly. Just children, at the height of their parents’ waists, singing a skipping song they’d jumped to all their lives without knowing what it meant.

Thirteen—

They stopped there. All of them. On thirteen. As if they’d all heard it at once. As if, for the first time in three hundred years, the song had come to a number that was now.

Ice stood in the middle of the square with Gabe on his right and Thea on his left and felt his throat close.

On the top step, the clerk shut the book.

And turned. And went back in through the open doors of the Assize.

And the whole of Tell went in after it.

The great hall was full before half of them had got through the doors. They stood at the back. They stood in the aisles. They stood on the window ledges, which was not allowed, and the hall’s ushers in their blue coats told them to get down and they didn’t. A thousand on the tiered benches, and a thousand more standing, and the doors left open so the people in the square could hear.

The thirty-six were put on a bench at the front on the left. Drew had argued with an usher about it and won. Behind them, on the next three benches up, Hawise’s list. Seventy-nine people from the Low Town in their market clothes. Aldo in his chair at the end of the front bench, on the aisle. On Ice’s left, if he’d been standing.

At the back of the hall, against the far wall, under the high windows, in a long grey line, the clerks.

A hundred and eleven. Standing. Books on their arms. Heads very slightly down.

And at the front of the hall, the long table, with its stack of slates and its chalk. And the tall clerk behind it, with the book on the table in front of it, closed. And behind the tall clerk, from floor to ceiling, from wall to wall, the screen.

He’d seen it from the door. He’d thought, from the door, that it looked old.

From here, it looked like the inside of a tree.

It was dark wood, nearly black, carved all over, every hand’s-width of it, in a pattern so close and so deep that his eyes slid off it, the way John said the letters slid in the book. Leaves, he’d thought, from the door. It wasn’t leaves. It was writing. Line after line after line, carved into the wood, from the floor to the ceiling, too small and too close and too old to read. Every line a little different. Every line two hands.

The Rolls. Before the Rolls. The first ones. Cut into a screen, and set up across a hall, a long time before anyone in Tell could remember.

And behind it, nothing moved.

He felt Alea go very still on his shoulder.

“Ice,” she said. Very quietly.

“I know.”

“No. Listen. There’s something behind it.” She was quiet. “I’ve never felt it before. Not on any floor. Not in either—“ She stopped. “It feels like the Steward. And the Gatekeeper. And the thing under the window. All at once.” Another pause. “It feels like the tower, Ice. Sitting down.”

He didn’t say anything.

Beside him, Thea’s hand found his on the bench, under the edge of her coat, where nobody could see. Her fingers were cold.

The voice came from behind the screen.

He’d been waiting for it. Aldo had told him. Calvin had told him. Like a page turning in an empty room. He’d thought he knew what that meant.

He didn’t.

It wasn’t loud. It wasn’t anything. It came from behind the screen and it was everywhere in the hall at once, the way the ticking had been everywhere in the Down. A dry, soft, papery sound, like a great many pages being turned very slowly in a room with nobody in it. And inside the sound, somehow, words.

“The Reckoning,” said the Magistrate.

And the hall, which had been quiet, went quieter.

It heard the small ones first.

He hadn’t expected that. He’d thought it would be him, or Joan, or something big. It wasn’t. It was a boundary between two farms on the plain, where two hands had written two different hedges. And a dowry that one family said was paid and the other said wasn’t. And a cart. And a well that two streets both said was theirs. One after another. Eleven of them, before the sun had moved a hand’s width across the high windows.

And every one went the same way.

The tall clerk opened the book and read both hands. Aloud. Flat and ordinary. The first hand. The second. And then the voice behind the screen, like a page turning, said:

“Who writes it?”

And people stood up.

Not many, for the small ones. Three or four. A neighbour. A teller. A man who’d been at the wedding. They came down out of the benches to the long table and took a slate from the stack and wrote, with the chalk, with the whole hall watching. Their names at the top. And then what they’d seen. And gave it to a teller, Hawise or Orlo or one of the others, who read it out, and laid it on the table, face up.

And when everybody who was going to stand had stood, there was a silence. A long one. The kind of silence a page makes while it’s being read.

And then the voice behind the screen said which hand stood.

The first hand stands. The second hand stands. Told.

That was all. No reasons. No arguing. Two families on the plain looked at each other across the hall, and one of them stood up and walked across and held out a hand, and the other took it. And the next.

Ice watched it eleven times. And he understood something, watching, that he’d never understood in ten years of the tower.

It was fair.

Whatever was behind the screen. Whatever the tower had put there, before the First Tell, and built a city round like a pearl round grit. It read both hands, every time, and asked who’d write them, and listened to every slate. And it was fair.

He didn’t know if that made it better or worse.

Then Wace.

Wace’s book had seventy-three lines in it that the Echo had brought back for one breath. Seventy-three debts he’d marked paid and scratched through, that had come back unpaid. Seventy-three men and women who’d paid him and had it scratched through and then, on the climbers’ three hundred and sixty-third day, seen it come back as if they hadn’t.

The tall clerk read both hands. Wace’s book, as it was now: paid. And the line that had come back, for one breath, under the scratch: unpaid.

“Who writes it?”

Wace stood up himself. He wasn’t supposed to. Nobody stopped him. He was a thin grey man with a thin grey voice, and he said that the climbers had done it, that the lines that came back weren’t told, they weren’t in two hands, they were one breath of nothing, and that if Tell started ruling on things a climber’s scribe had conjured up out of thin air—

“Sit down, Wace,” said the voice behind the screen.

Not loud. Not anything. A page turning.

Wace sat down.

And then people stood up. For the debtors. A great many. Men and women who’d been in the queue in the Lantern Market. Neighbours. A teller from the Cloth Hall who’d written half the lines in the first place and said so, on her slate, in a furious tidy hand: I wrote paid. I saw them pay. The scratch is his.

The silence. The page turning.

“The first hand stands,” said the Magistrate. “Paid. Told.”

The scratch had been the lie. Not the line under it.

Wace sat on his bench with his book on his knees and his thin grey face gone the colour of the clerks. And all over the hall, seventy-three people let their breath out at once.

Ice looked across the aisle.

Hamo Tull was sitting on the far side, on the front bench of the right, in his black with his chain, with his rings laced on his stomach. He’d watched Wace the whole time. His pleasant face hadn’t moved.

But Ice had been watching faces for ten years and four hundred and seven days. He saw Tull’s hands. The rings. The way the fingers, laced together, had gone white at the knuckles.

“Joan Tull, against Hamo Tull,” said the Magistrate.

The hall changed.

He felt it. A thousand people sitting up. The ones at the back craning. The ones on the window ledges leaning in. Every soul in Tell had heard about the page that stood up out of the stitching in the Bench’s crypt. Most of them had heard it four times, from four different people, each version bigger.

Joan stood up. In her brown dress. On her own feet. She came down to the long table with Hawise one step behind her, and stood there, and didn’t look across at the right-hand benches at all.

Hamo Tull didn’t stand. He didn’t need to. He was the Warden of the Bench.

The tall clerk opened the book. Not the grey one. A Roll. Askew had brought it up from the Tellhouse that morning in a locked case, and unlocked it, and laid it on the table, and stood beside it with her stick. The Roll of four winters ago. Fat and cream and brown at the edges. And open at the stitching. And standing up out of the stitching, where a page had been torn out four winters ago, a page.

Whole. In two hands. With a mark at the bottom.

The clerk read it. The first hand: the Roll as it had been, for four years. The page is torn. The will is not in the Rolls. Not told. And the second: the page that stood there now. Piers Tull, fruiterer, of the Wellgate. Being of sound mind. Leaves the shop with the green shutters, with its stock and its trees, to Joan his wife. Told. Hawise Brand. Simon Ashe.

“Who writes it?” said the Magistrate.

Hamo Tull stood up.

He did it slowly, the way a big soft man stands. He smoothed his black coat. He looked round the hall with his pleasant face, at the thousand people, at the climbers, at Joan by the table. And then at the screen.

“It was made,” he said. Warmly. Reasonably. “That page. In front of the Bench. By a climber. Out of a stub of paper and his own ink, while we watched. There’s no Simon Ashe to write it. Simon Ashe is three winters dead.” He spread his ringed hands. “I’m sorry. I’m truly sorry, Joan. But you can’t ask the Magistrate to weigh a thing a climber conjured up out of nothing—“

“Who writes it?” said the Magistrate again.

Not louder. Not anything. A page turning. As if Tull hadn’t spoken at all.

Tull stopped.

And Hawise came round the end of the table, and took a slate, and wrote.

She wrote fast, in her quick round brown hand with a flick on the end of every word, and gave it to Orlo, who read it out, and his voice cracked once in the middle.

“Hawise Brand, teller. I wrote it. Four winters ago. With Simon Ashe, who crossed his Ts twice. I saw it whole for one breath on the climbers’ three hundred and sixty-third day. It’s my hand. I’d know my own hand anywhere.”

He laid it on the table, face up.

Ivo Rook stood up, on the right-hand benches. Two places along from Tull. In his black, without his chain. He came down and took a slate.

Ivo Rook. Piers Tull did my apples for thirty years. He’d never in his life have left that shop to his brother. He told me so. Twice.

And then Nell, from the half-door, on her grandson’s arm. And the cheese woman from the Hay Market. And the man from the Cloth Hall. And Bertha the baker. And the knife-grinder, and the lamplighter, and a whole bench of people from the Lantern Market who’d bought apples off Piers Tull from the shop with the green shutters for twenty years.

Half the Low Town, Joan had said, on the last evening, by the pump.

It was more than half.

And the last one, when the line had nearly finished, when the table was covered in slates face up and Orlo’s voice had gone hoarse reading them, was Cursiffe.

He came down off the climbers’ bench, slowly, the way he did everything. He’d been told by Tell, by the host’s hand. Askew had written him herself. He could stand. He took a slate, and the chalk, and wrote in his small square hand, and gave it to Orlo.

Orlo read it.

“Cursiffe, teller of the Tellhouse, told by the Remembrancer’s hand. I didn’t make it. I told it again. It was already there.”

Orlo laid it on the table.

And the hall was silent.

The silence went on for a long time. Longer than for the boundaries, and the dowry, and the well. Longer than for Wace. Ice sat on the bench with Thea’s cold fingers in his and felt it go on. The page turning, very slowly, behind the screen. Reading. Every slate.

“The second hand stands,” said the Magistrate. “Told. The shop with the green shutters, to Joan.”

The hall went up.

He’d never heard anything like it. Not on floor 7, when the bells rang. Not on floor 10, when the Long Night ended and the light came up over the pass. A thousand people in a stone hall, and a thousand more in the square outside, all making a sound at once, and none of it words.

Joan Tull stood by the table with both hands over her face.

Hawise had her arms round her.

And across the aisle, on the front bench of the right, Hamo Tull stood up.

He didn’t say anything. He stood there in his black, with his chain on, with his rings, and his pleasant face had gone completely still. And he turned round. Not to Joan. Not to the screen. To the back of the hall. To the long grey line of clerks against the far wall, under the high windows.

He looked at them.

Ice watched him do it. He watched Tull look at a hundred and eleven plain grey faces, all in a row, all with their heads very slightly down. Waiting for something. For one of them to look back. To lift a head. To give him something. Anything.

None of them did.

Not one. They stood against the back wall with their books on their arms and their heads down, the way they’d stood on every corner of the Wellgate, and not one of them looked at Hamo Tull.

He’d gone into the Countinghouse at a quarter past one in the morning, on his own, and asked. Three times. And it had taken what he’d asked for, and it hadn’t given him anything back.

Ice watched him understand that.

Tull sat down. Slowly. On the front bench. And put his ringed hands flat on his knees, and looked at them, and didn’t look up again.

The hall took a long time to be quiet.

When it was, the voice behind the screen spoke again. And the hall heard it, and went still, all at once, the way the market had gone still when they came up the well.

“The corded one.”

Ice stood up.

He hadn’t decided to. His legs did it. He stood up off the front bench in the great hall of the Assize, in front of two thousand people, with the cord on his wrist where they could all see it. And Thea’s hand let go of his.

And Gabe stood up beside him. On his right. With Hearthwall.

And Thea stood up on his left. With the lantern.

And behind him, without a word, without anybody telling them, he heard the climbers’ bench stand up. All of it. Thirty-three people getting to their feet at once in a rustle and a creak and somebody’s shield knocking on the bench and somebody saying ow very quietly, which was Calvin.

And behind them, on the next three benches up, he heard Hawise’s list stand up too. Seventy-nine people from the Low Town in their market clothes.

And at the end of the front bench, on the aisle, on his left, an old man in a rush chair put his writing-board down on his knees and said, quite loudly, to nobody, “Well, I can’t stand up, I’m eighty-seven,” and somebody laughed, and the laugh went round the hall and stopped.

He walked forward.

To the long table. With Gabe on his right and Thea on his left, the fire and the lamp. And stopped in front of it, where the disputed one stood, where Joan had stood, where a farmer had stood about a hedge.

In front of him, the tall clerk.

Behind the tall clerk, the screen. Carved from floor to ceiling in lines too small and too old to read.

And behind the screen, something that felt to Alea like the tower, sitting down.

The tall clerk opened the grey book.

He knew where it would fall open. He’d never seen it. He knew. Thea had told him on the Countinghouse step, in the dark, with her arm round him. Two pages, side by side, with the binding between them. Both headed the first year.

The book fell open there.

The tall clerk looked down at the left-hand page. At thirty-four names in an old man’s running hand. And then at Ice.

And then, for the first time, the voice behind the screen spoke to him directly.

“The disputed man’s two hands,” said the Magistrate. “In full.”

A page turned, somewhere very far away, in a room with nobody in it.

“Every line.”
