Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 3

Player Support

3,170 words · 14 min read

Thirty-five voices said it, and then thirty-five voices stopped, all at exactly the same instant. The silence afterwards was the loudest thing Hana had ever heard.

Nobody moved. The little balls of light hung over every shoulder in the room, steady and bright now, the slow pulse gone out of them. Hana’s sat a hand’s width above her left ear. When she turned her head to look at it, it didn’t move, so she found herself looking straight into it, the way you might look into a lamp.

It didn’t have a face. She’d half expected one. It was just light: warm, white and very calm.

“Welcome, Hanarisu,” it said.

Only to her. Its voice had dropped out of the chorus and into something smaller and closer, pitched for one person. All around the room, she heard the same thing happening: a low murmur of names as thirty-five lights greeted thirty-five people separately, each in the same even, pleasant, slightly-too-patient voice. Welcome, Deston. Welcome, Gabe. Welcome, Milli. Milli flinched at his, as if a stranger had tapped him on the shoulder.

“I am your Support,” said the light by Hana’s ear. “I will guide you through the Spire. You may ask me questions. I will answer what I can.”

“What you can,” Hana said.

“Yes.”

She filed that away. She was methodical; it was one of the things she liked best about herself. The rest of the room had started to talk, loud and frightened and all at once, the way people do when the silence breaks. Hana held Milli’s hand and listened, and filed things.

The Supports gave the speech together.

They did it in unison again, all thirty-five of them, like a choir that had rehearsed only this one song. The room went quiet to hear it. Even Calvin.

“You stand in the Threshold,” said the Supports, “at the foot of the Spire. The Spire has one hundred floors. Each floor is a world. Each world has a way up, and each way up is guarded. Climb. At the top of the Spire is the way home.”

A pause, exactly long enough for that to land.

“Your window shows your level, your tier and your class. You have no class yet. You will choose one on the first floor. You will grow as you climb. Your Support will guide you.”

Another pause.

“Death in the Spire is final.”

Nobody said anything to that. Hana felt Milli’s hand go tight round hers, so tight it hurt, and she let it.

“Your world is waiting for you,” said the Supports, more gently, as if they had been designed to say this part gently. “It holds its breath. Not one grain falls until you return.”

And then, before anyone could do anything with that:

“In a short while, seven of you will be chosen to lead. Parties are of five. Until then, you may ask your Support anything.”

The lights dimmed a fraction, back to that patient, attentive glow, and the room exploded.

“Climb what, though?” Calvin was saying to his. “Like, physically? Are there stairs?”

“There’s a way up on each floor,” said his Support. “You will find it.”

“Okay, but is it stairs—“

“What happens if we die?” A girl’s voice, sharp. “Like, what does final mean. Final how.”

“Death in the Spire is final.”

“That’s not an answer, that’s the same sentence.”

“Can I go home now?” Someone very quiet, near the back. “Can I just— is there a way to just go home now?”

“At the top of the Spire is the way home.”

Hana didn’t ask hers anything for a minute. She stood still and listened to the room ask its questions and get the same answers back, word for word, from thirty-five different lights. It was like listening to people phone the same customer service line and all get the same polite woman, reading the same polite script, in thirty-five different rooms.

I will answer what I can.

Then a big voice cut across everything, and the room went quiet again, because it was the kind of voice that does that.

“Where’s the thirty-sixth?”

Gabe. Hana had never met him, but she knew the voice from calls: Ice’s friend from home, the one Ice talked about like a brother, the loud one. He was standing in the middle of the ring with his ball of light at his shoulder and his fists clenched, and he wasn’t asking his Support. He was asking all of them.

“There’s thirty-six circles on this floor. There’s thirty-five of us. Somebody was here. Where’d he go?”

Thirty-five lights answered him at once.

“There are thirty-five Players.”

The words came from everywhere: from the light at Hana’s own ear, from Milli’s, from the light over the girl crying on the floor and the one over the wooden boy in the orange hoodie. All the same, all at once, all perfectly calm. It went through the room like cold water.

“That’s not—“ Gabe started.

“There are thirty-five Players.”

Gabe stopped. Hana watched him stand there in the middle of the room, breathing hard, and she watched other people’s faces as they looked at him. She knew those faces. She’d seen them in her own chat, on bad nights: poor guy. He’s in shock. He was dreaming.

She thought: but I saw him too.

And then, because she was honest with herself, which was tiring, she thought: did I?

She turned to the light by her ear.

“I saw a man by the door,” she said, keeping her voice low. “Black vest. White shirt. Black hair, with a teal streak on the right. He was standing three metres from me when I woke up. Where did he go?”

“There are thirty-five Players, Hanarisu.”

“I didn’t ask how many players there are. I asked where he went.”

“I am not able to answer that.”

Hana tilted her head.

On her streams she’d been a cat princess for years: smug and sweet, with a crown. She knew the voice that went with that face. She’d used it to talk chat out of a hundred bad moods, to get mods to do boring things, and, once or twice, to talk a man at a hotel desk into a room upgrade, which she wasn’t proud of and would do again. It was a voice that leaned toward you a little and asked very nicely, and made you feel that saying yes had been your own idea.

She didn’t know if it would work on a ball of light. She tried anyway.

“Please?” she said softly, leaning in. “Just for me? I won’t tell anyone.”

“I am not able to answer that,” said the light, exactly as pleasantly as before.

“Mm,” said Hana, and leaned back.

It hadn’t worked. That was fine. Most things didn’t, the first time. But the light hadn’t said there was no man. It hadn’t said you were dreaming. It hadn’t said anything about the man at all. Twice now it had stepped smoothly round him, the way you’d step round something on a pavement you’d been told not to look at.

Not able.

“Honey,” said Milli quietly. “Who are you talking to?”

“My light.”

“What’s it saying?”

“Nothing,” said Hana. “Very carefully.”

Milli looked at her. He had the look he always got when he was trying to be rational about something that frightened him: brow down, mouth tight, as if he could reduce the problem to a set of steps. She loved that look. It had got them through a wedding.

“Hana,” he said. “I didn’t see anybody. When I woke up. I’m sorry. I was looking at you.”

“I know.”

“I’m not saying you didn’t.”

“I know you’re not.”

“I’m just saying I didn’t.”

“I know, honey,” she said, and squeezed his hand, and looked at the white wall where the door had been, and was very, very slightly less sure than she had been five minutes ago.

She’d known Ice since she was nineteen. Seven years, give or take, and she’d never once been in the same room as him.

He’d turned up in her chat back when her chat was eleven people and a bot. He was funny before he was anything else: a name in the corner that said exactly the wrong thing at exactly the right moment. When she’d had a bad stream, he’d drawn cats. Not good cats. Increasingly terrible cats, in the chat box, out of keyboard symbols, one after another, each with a worse caption, until she’d laughed so hard she’d had to mute herself. Later he’d joined calls, then games, then the long, comfortable nights where nobody was really playing anything and everybody was just there. He’d called her Alice, one of only two people who ever would, because he’d decided early on that Hana was for the stream and Alice was for her, and then never explained the logic and never stopped.

They’d always said they’d meet one day. Everybody always says that. One day she’d make a trip of it and he’d show her the good food, and they’d laugh about how weird it was to see each other’s real faces.

And then she’d woken up in a white room at the bottom of a tower, three metres from him, both of them wearing the faces they’d only ever known each other by. He’d looked right at her. And he’d gone.

If it had been him.

She found she was holding on to the if the way you hold on to a railing, and she didn’t like it.

“It’s a pause,” someone said.

The voice was a man’s, a little too loud, in the cheerful way of someone thinking aloud and forgetting other people could hear. Hana looked round. A short man in his thirties with a round, friendly face was standing a few circles away, talking to his light with his hands, the way people talk to rubber ducks.

“Sorry. Sorry, everyone. Hi. I’m Chris.” He waved, vaguely, at the whole room. “But that’s a pause. What it said. Not one grain falls. That’s not poetry. Well, it is poetry, but it’s poetry instead of a number.”

“What?” said several people.

“Okay.” Chris turned round to face them properly. He seemed delighted to have an audience and slightly embarrassed about being delighted. “So. If you had a system, and it was only allowed to say certain things, and one of the things it wasn’t allowed to say was a straight answer about time. What would you write? You’d write it holds its breath. Which is: suspended. Not running. And not one grain falls, which is an hourglass, which is the smallest unit of time you can make out of sand. One grain. Zero grains is zero time. And then until you return, which is the condition. The clock starts again when we get back.”

He spread his hands.

“It’s a pause. It’s literally a pause. Home’s paused. Wherever you were when this happened, it’s still that second. That’s what it’s saying.”

There was a long, uncertain silence.

“How do you know that?” said someone.

“I don’t. I’m guessing.” Chris shrugged. “But I write code for a living, and that’s exactly how I’d write it if somebody told me I wasn’t allowed to put the number in. You hide the number in the comment.”

Across the room, a tall man Hana didn’t know said, to his light, clearly and slowly, “Is time passing in our world?”

“Your world holds its breath,” said his light.

“Is it a pause?”

“Your world holds its breath.”

The tall man looked over at Chris and nodded once.

Hana let out a breath she hadn’t noticed holding.

Home was paused. Her apartment, her desk, her streaming set-up with the lights still on. Her phone on the bed. Her mother, somewhere, mid-sentence. Nobody was worrying. Nobody had noticed. Nobody would notice, as long as—

As long as they came back. And came back alive, she supposed. That was the part the light had said in the same calm voice as everything else.

“So nobody knows we’re gone,” said a girl behind her. Very long silver hair, two short black horns. Her voice was flat, as if it didn’t want to have an opinion about this yet.

“Nobody knows we’re gone,” said Chris. “Which is good, right? Nobody’s sad. Nobody’s calling the police.” A beat. “Unless we don’t go back, in which case it’s… not good, exactly. But it’s paused. Paused is something.”

“You’re very calm,” said the silver-haired girl.

“No, I’m extremely not,” said Chris cheerfully. “I’m talking very fast so I don’t have to think about it. It’s a known technique. Hi, by the way. You’re Winter. I know your voice. We’ve played together like four hundred times.”

“Six hundred,” said Winter, and something in her face eased very slightly.

Hana felt someone come and stand beside her, and turned.

A man with short crimson hair swept to one side, red eyes heavy-lidded and amused, a dark red brocade jacket over a black turtleneck. Hana had never seen that face in real life and had seen it a thousand times on stream.

“Alice,” said Jared.

He was one of two people on Earth who called her that. The other one had been standing three metres from her ten minutes ago.

“Jared.” She almost hugged him. Then she did, briefly, one-armed, Milli’s hand still in her other one. “Are you okay?”

“I’m a cartoon,” said Jared. “But yeah. I’m good. Cursiffe’s over there with a glass ink bottle round his neck reading every word his light says out loud, and Leaka’s trying to pet a penguin that isn’t hers. So we’re all here.” He looked at her carefully. “You were saying something to the big guy. About Ice.”

“I saw him.”

Jared went still.

“For real?”

“For real. I think.” She heard herself say it. “I think, Jared. He was by the door. He looked at me. And then he was gone. And now everybody’s light says there are thirty-five of us, and he’s not in any of the circles, and I’m starting to—“

“Nah,” said Jared.

“Nah?”

“Nah. Don’t start.” He said it easily, the way he said everything, cool and unbothered. “If you saw Ice, you saw Ice. I’ll take you over a light bulb any day.” He considered. “Also, if Ice was here, it’d be very Ice to leave before anybody could make him do anything.”

“He wouldn’t just leave.”

“Nah, he wouldn’t,” Jared agreed. “So if he left, he left for something. Man never ran away from anything in his life except a dentist.” A pause. “And that postcard.”

Despite everything, Hana laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

Years ago Jared had sent the group a photo of himself eating fried chicken, deeply satisfied. Ice had turned it into a postcard, with Wish you were here! in looping gold script and Jared’s face in the centre, and posted it to the group chat, and then to her personally, every few weeks, for no reason, ever since. She had something like forty of them. She’d never once asked him to stop.

“He owes me a new one,” said Jared. “I’m counting this as a missed delivery.”

It was Aleaka, in the end, who said the thing Hana needed to hear.

Aleaka found them a few minutes later, with the stranger’s penguin still waddling resentfully away from her in the background. Her hair was split down the middle, golden blonde on one side and auburn on the other, pinned up in two looped buns. She had round gold glasses, soft fox ears, and a fox tail that she clearly hadn’t learned to manage yet, because it kept knocking into things. She’d been one of Ice’s closest friends for less than a year and somehow that had been enough.

She listened to Hana tell it. She didn’t say anything for a while afterwards. She was like that: slow and careful with words, stumbling over them sometimes, as if each one had to be checked before it was allowed out.

“If he’s here,” Aleaka said finally, “he’s okay.”

“How do you know?”

“I don’t.” Aleaka pushed her glasses up her nose. “But he’s always been okay. Even when he wasn’t. He’d make a joke, and you’d laugh, and then you’d think about it later and realise he’d said something true. That’s how you knew how he was. Afterwards.” She looked at the painted-over door. “So if he ran, he’s okay enough to run. And he’ll make a joke about it. Later. And it’ll be true.”

Hana nodded. She didn’t trust her voice.

“Also,” said Aleaka, with great dignity, “when we find him, I’m going to talk down to him for at least an hour, and he’s going to let me.”

That did it. Hana laughed properly this time, and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, and felt her crown slide further sideways. Milli reached up without a word and straightened it.

The last thing she noticed before the lights came down again was the girl in the royal-blue jersey.

She was sitting alone at the edge of her circle, a few places round from the painted-over door, with her window open in front of her. She hadn’t joined any of the groups that had formed while people found each other. A girl with long silver-lilac hair stood not far away with her back very deliberately turned. A girl with lavender hair and gills was hovering between the two of them like someone standing between two cats. The girl in blue didn’t seem to notice any of it. She was frowning at her window and poking at it, one line at a time, the way you’d test a step before putting your weight on it.

Hana was close enough to hear her say, very quietly, to her light, “You said not able.”

“I am not able to answer that, Althea.”

“Right. You keep saying that.” The girl leaned closer to her window. “Not able isn’t no. No would be easier. No is shorter. You’re saying the long one on purpose.”

Her light said nothing.

“Interesting,” said Althea, to no one, and went back to poking.

Hana looked at her for a long moment. She’d never seen her before in her life. She was a stranger: a friend of a friend of someone else’s, probably, from one of the other corners of Ice’s world that Hana had only ever glimpsed in a chat.

But she’d heard not able, too.

Then every light in the room flared at once, brighter than before, and Hana’s Support said in her ear, in the same calm voice as everything else:

“Seven of you will now be chosen to lead.”
