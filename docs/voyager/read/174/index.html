Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 174

Came Down the Hill

6,699 words · 29 min read

Rachael’s hands took longer to come back now.

She’d noticed on floor 14, and said so, because she said things. Kei had measured it. Ten minutes longer each day, at the foot of the straw man, with her hands on the frozen iron and the white going up her arms. Then, on the morning after the bell, at the doors of the Long Barn, with Dec’s twenty fingers one at a time and Hearthwall’s boss under her other hand steaming, it had stopped getting longer and started getting worse. Not longer. Deeper. The white went in further. It came out slower. On the sixth day, when she’d done his last finger and looked at her own hands in the light from the doors, they’d stayed white to the wrist for an hour.

They were pink now. Mostly. On the stair down from 14 she’d looked at them every hundred steps, by the light from the lantern ahead, and they were pink. A bit stiff in the mornings. A bit cold at the ends, all the time, as if she’d been holding something from a cellar.

She didn’t mind. She’d decided that on the first day she had Burden, on floor 4, kneeling by a dead dust-hound with her hands on it for a minute because she couldn’t believe the table wouldn’t do anything. It came out of you. That was what it was for. You didn’t get to take something out of somebody and have it not go anywhere.

She just wanted to know how much. Kei could tell her that.

He’d told her. On the stair. Under his breath, so Dennis wouldn’t hear, which was pointless, because Dennis always heard.

“About a finger,” said Kei. “Each time. Of you. It comes back. But slower than it goes.”

“How many fingers have I got?”

Kei had looked at her. He hadn’t answered. Which was an answer.

The stair came out through the screen.

She’d forgotten. She’d known, the way you know the shape of a thing you’ve only seen once, but she’d forgotten what it was like. Four hundred and twenty-four steps down, in the dark, with Thea singing at the front, still angry, and Newish counting, and then a door. A plain round-topped door, and Tiger opening it, and the light coming in. Pale. Morning. Coming through high windows onto pale stone.

The great hall of the Assize.

She came out into it behind Dennis and stood still.

It was empty. All of it. The tiered benches going up on both sides, a thousand places, with the morning on them and nobody in them. The long table, with no slates. The windows. The beams. And behind her, when she turned round, the screen. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, dark wood carved all over with writing in two hands, the oldest writing on the hill. And in the middle of it, the door they’d come out of. And along the bottom of it, under all the old writing, a line that hadn’t been there when the hill was old. One line. In two hands, side by side, close, as if they’d been carved by two people leaning on each other.

She’d seen it come up. On the day of the Reckoning. With Dec on Hearthwall, grey to the chest, and the grey on the floor, and Thea’s pen in his hand.

She didn’t look at it for long.

Dec was standing in the doorway behind her. In the middle of the door in the screen, with Thea on his left holding his hand and Gabe behind. He’d stopped. He was looking at the hall. At the floor. At the place on the floor, about three metres from the long table, where they’d carried him.

Rachael watched him look. She’d been watching him look at places for four hundred and sixty-eight days. She knew the face. It was the one he had for a place where something had happened that he was putting somewhere.

Then he looked at her. And his face did a different thing.

“Your hands,” said Dec.

“They’re fine.”

He looked at her.

“They’re cold,” said Rachael. Because she’d told him on 14 that if he was going to say every number, so was she. “At the ends. All the time. Kei says a finger a time. It comes back slower than it goes.”

“Rach—“

“Don’t,” said Rachael. “I said it. That’s all. Now you know.”

He shut his mouth. Thea, holding his hand, looked at Rachael for a second, and something went across her face that might have been the beginning of a smile, and then she remembered she was angry and it went away.

Then somebody at the far end of the hall dropped a broom.

It was Orlo.

Orlo Fenn, nineteen, very tall and very thin, with ink on his nose. Standing in the big doors at the far end of the hall, in the light from the square, with a broom on the floor at his feet where he’d dropped it. He’d been sweeping. Rachael didn’t know why a teller was sweeping the great hall of the Assize at seven in the morning. She found out later. He’d been doing it every morning since the Reckoning. Fifty-eight days. Because somebody ought to look at the door. In case.

He looked at them. All thirty-six, standing in front of the screen.

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

Then he turned round and ran.

Out of the doors. Across the square. Down the steps. She heard his feet on the stone, fast, and then his voice, cracking, high, going down the hill, the way sound went down a hill in Tell, carrying:

“CLIMBERS!”

And again, further down, fainter:

“THEY CAME BACK! THE CLIMBERS! THEY CAME BACK DOWN!”

Rachael looked at Dec.

He’d gone white. Not the bad white. Not the white from the Barn Field. The other one. The one she’d seen on him on floor 13, once, on the hill, the first time a teller ran. Except it hadn’t been this teller. It had been an old one, in another—

She stopped that thought where it was. She put it away. She was good at that. Not yet.

“He’s running,” said Dec. Very quietly. To nobody.

“He does that,” said Hawise’s voice, from somewhere in Rachael’s memory. Orlo’s the fastest pen in the Tellhouse. Not his legs. His pen.

His legs were quite fast too, it turned out.

They walked down the Wellgate in the autumn.

That was the thing she noticed first. When they’d gone up the hill, it had been spring going into summer, and the trees on the hill road had been the green of new leaves, and then the dark green of high summer at the Reckoning. Now they were turning. Every tree on every turn of the Wellgate, all the way down the hill, yellow and copper and a red like rust. Leaves on the cobbles. Leaves in the gutters. A smell of woodsmoke and apples.

Dec stopped at the first turn and looked at it.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. Rachael knew what was on Higu’s rock, in the left-hand column, from the day they came up the well: SEVEN WEEKS. AUTUMN. (LEAVES TURNING ON THE HILL ROAD.) She’d read it every evening for fifty days.

The leaves were turning on the hill road.

“It’s just the year,” said Thea. Beside him. Not soft. Not angry either. Just saying it. “It’s not the tower. It’s just autumn. Things go round.”

“I know.”

“You’re making the face.”

“I know.” He looked at the trees a moment longer. “It’s the same,” he said. “The colour. It’s exactly the same.” And then, slower: “Except we’re going down.”

Thea didn’t say anything. But she didn’t let go of his hand, either, and Rachael saw her thumb move once, across the back of his knuckles, and stop.

And then the hill came out.

Orlo had done it. Running down the Wellgate shouting. People came out of their doors. Out of the shops, the half-doors, the taverns, the window boxes, the Cloth Hall at the bend with the tellers leaning out of their booths. They didn’t cheer. Tell didn’t cheer. They stood in their doorways, and on their steps, and at the turns of the hill road, and watched the thirty-six come down. And as they came past, people said things. Quietly. Not to all of them. To one each. The way you’d say good morning to somebody you knew.

Calvin. That was the porter with the squeak, at his door.

Tiger. Joan Tull’s neighbour, from the Wellgate.

The healer. A woman Rachael had done a cut for, at the pump, in the summer. She nodded at Rachael and Rachael nodded back.

The big one who catches things. Nell Fother, at her half-door, two doors up from Aldo’s, with a tray of buns.

Nell gave Dec a bun.

She didn’t say anything about it. She held it out across the half-door as he went past, warm, pork and apple, and he took it with the hand Thea wasn’t holding, and Nell looked at the hand, at the white line round the wrist beside the faint grey one, and her face went still.

“Still free,” said Nell.

“Thank you.”

“I said so.” She looked at Thea. “You’re the one the clerks used to look at.”

“Yes,” said Thea.

“They don’t now.” Nell sniffed. “Stand on the corners still. But they look at their books.” She looked back at Dec. “Eat that. You’re thin.”

And then the green door.

Aldo was sitting outside it.

In his rush chair, on the left of the door, with a rug over his knees and his writing-board in his lap and his grandson Davy leaning on the doorframe behind him, sulky, the way Davy was always sulky, except that this morning his eyes were wet. Aldo’s hands were on the rug. Like knotted string. Ink in the knots.

And in them, a handkerchief.

New. White. Very clean. Folded.

He watched them come down the last of the turn. He didn’t get up. He couldn’t; he was eighty-seven. He sat in his chair with his bright bird’s eyes going over them, one at a time, all thirty-six, the way he’d gone over them on the Reckoning, counting, and when he got to the end he started again, as if he didn’t trust the first count.

Then he looked at Dec.

“You came back,” said Aldo.

Dec stopped in front of his chair.

“Nobody comes back,” said Aldo. “Not in any Roll. Not in sixty years at the door. They go up the hill and they go under the Assize and that’s the last line. That’s where you rule it off.” He looked up at Dec. “I was going to rule you off. I had the pen out. Fifty-eight days ago. And I didn’t. I don’t know why I didn’t. I’ve ruled off every line I ever wrote.” He lifted the handkerchief, an inch, off the rug. “Bought this. In case. Davy said I was being soft.”

“You were being soft, Gramps,” said Davy. His voice was thick.

“I was being right,” said Aldo.

Dec crouched down. In front of the chair. So his face was below Aldo’s. Rachael had seen him do that before, on this same step, fifty-eight days ago, with Drew waiting at the bottom. He crouched down on the cobbles in front of an old man in a rush chair and looked up at him.

“You waved,” said Dec. “Last time. When we went.”

“I did.”

“You were the only one.”

“Well,” said Aldo. “It was early.” He looked at the handkerchief in his hands. “And now it’s the other way round. And I’ve got a handkerchief and nothing to wave it at, because you’re not going, you’re coming.” He was crying. He didn’t seem to mind. “What do you wave at people who come back?”

Nobody knew.

Dec didn’t say anything. He reached out and put his hand on the handkerchief, on top of Aldo’s knotted hands, and left it there.

“Took you fifty-eight days,” said Aldo, after a while. “Coming down.”

“It took us a while.”

“Asked for me first, though. Coming down too.” He sniffed. “That’s two.” He looked at Dec’s wrist, at the two lines, white and grey. He didn’t ask. Tellers didn’t. “You look like a man who’s been carried a long way.”

“I walked,” said Dec. “This time. Most of it.”

Aldo looked at him.

“Most,” said Aldo. “That’s honest.” And he laughed, a high cracked old man’s laugh, delighted, and wiped his eyes with the handkerchief, and said, “There. That’s what it’s for. I knew it’d be for something.”

Rachael had to look away. She looked at Dennis instead. Dennis was looking at her. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. He had the face he’d had at the long table on floor 1, the first night, in the Spoon, when she laughed at Calvin’s boots.

She looked away from that too.

The Tellhouse was open.

Wide steps. The doors. And just inside, at the door-roll, at the long slanted desk where every stranger who came up the well was written down, Hawise Brand. Thin, quick, ink to both wrists, a pen behind each ear. She’d been sitting with her head down over the Roll when they came in. She looked up.

She didn’t say anything for a long moment.

Then she put her pen down, very carefully, and stood up, and came round the desk, and stood in front of them in the doorway of the Tellhouse with her inky hands at her sides.

“You’re told,” said Hawise. “All of you. You don’t need writing in. The host’s hand doesn’t come off.” She swallowed. “You just have to say you’re here.”

“We’re here,” said Higu.

“Thirty-six,” said Newish.

“Then it has to go down,” said Hawise. “In two hands. That’s the law. Anything that happens in Tell.” She went back round the desk. She sat down. She picked up her pen. And then she looked at the place beside her, at the second stool at the door-roll, where the second hand sat.

There was somebody on it.

Rachael hadn’t seen her. She’d been so still. A young woman in a plain brown dress, not grey, with her hair tied back and a pen in her hand and an ink-stone in front of her, sitting very straight on the second stool at the door-roll, looking at them. Looking at all of them. With a face that was ordinary, and forgettable, and not forgettable at all, because it was looking.

Alys.

“She’s on the second hand,” said Hawise. Very level. “Since last week. Askew said when she was ready. She said she was ready.” She looked at her cousin. “This is her first one. On her own. With nobody checking.”

Alys looked at the Roll in front of her.

Rachael watched her hand. She’d seen it on Cooper’s Lane, months ago, when Calvin shouted her name. A pen hand rising a finger’s width off a grey book and shaking. Like a fish going by under ice.

It shook now. A little. Over the page.

Then it steadied.

Hawise wrote. Rachael could see the words from where she stood, upside down, in Hawise’s quick slanted hand.

The three hundred and — no. Hawise stopped, and crossed it out with one stroke, and started again, because the Tell count wasn’t their count, and Hawise had always written them by their own. The four hundred and sixty-eighth day of the climbers. They came down the hill. Thirty-six. They said they were here.

And beside her, on the second stool, Alys wrote it. Word for word. The same. In a hand that had been grey for twelve years and was brown now, and small, and careful, and hers.

Hawise looked at the two lines.

“Told,” said Hawise.

Alys put her pen down. She looked up. Not at Hawise. Past her, at the thirty-six in the doorway. At Calvin.

“You shouted,” said Alys.

Calvin went red to the ears. “I shouted.”

“In the lane. Every week. At noon.” Alys looked at him. “I didn’t know who you were. I didn’t know who I was. And something kept shouting my name.” She was very quiet. “Thank you.”

Calvin opened his mouth.

For the third time in a fortnight, Calvin didn’t say anything at all.

They slept in the shop with the green shutters.

Tiger had the key. He’d had it on a loop of green ribbon round his neck since the day after the Reckoning, under his shirt, and he’d taken it out on the stair down from 14 a hundred times to look at it. He opened the door. Joan was inside. She’d heard. Everybody had heard. She’d come up from the market at a run and was standing in the middle of the shop among the apple barrels with her hands over her mouth, and when Tiger came in she took them away and said “You kept it,” and he said “You said whenever,” and she laughed and cried at once, the way everybody in Tell seemed to do that morning.

The shop was full of apples. Her trees. Piers’s trees, that Hamo had had, that were hers now. The whole of the back room was barrels of them, red and yellow and russet, and the smell of it was like the inside of the Long Barn at harvest-home, and Rachael stood in the door of the back room and had to stop for a moment, because of the smell, and what it reminded her of. A straw man. A doorway. Steam going up off a shield into the stars.

She went back to the front.

Tiger was sitting on an upturned barrel with an apple in his hand. He’d eaten Joan’s first apple at the Reckoning, after fifty days, core and all. This was a new one. He was looking at it.

“You can eat that one straight away,” said Joan.

“I know,” said Tiger. He didn’t. He kept looking at it. “I’ll eat it on floor 1.”

Joan looked at him.

“There’s somebody there,” said Tiger. “Who’d like one. I think.”

He put it in his pack.

Rafe Collier came to the pump on the second day.

Rachael was there because people were. Word had gone round. By the second morning there was a line in the copy-room yard again, the way there’d been in the summer: a cut, a cough, a child with a burn from a kettle, an old man with a knee. She hadn’t meant to. She’d meant to sit down for two days and keep her hands in her armpits. She went down to the yard at first light because Kei was already there, and Malcolm, and then Xola came, and she wasn’t going to sit upstairs and listen.

She did small things. Nothing that would come out of her. A cut, a knee. Kei watched her hands every time and didn’t say anything. She let him.

Rafe was halfway down the line.

She saw the apron first. She always would, she thought. Leather, stained, tied at the back. A big man with forearms like a tanner’s, because he was one. Tanners’ Row. The man who’d thrown mud at Dec on the Wellgate, in the first week, with a stone in it, and hit him on the back of the left shoulder, and Dec hadn’t turned round.

She’d healed him anyway. On the three hundred and sixty-eighth day, a lime-pit burn up the inside of his arm. She’d known who he was. She’d done it, and taken the echo of it into her own palm for ten minutes afterwards, and told nobody. And he’d stood at the Reckoning, after, and put a slate on the table that said I’d have written him first, and held his pink palm up, and Dec had put his hand on it.

He wasn’t hurt. She could see that from where she sat. He got to the front of the line and stood there with something in his hands and didn’t sit down on the stool.

“I’m not hurt,” said Rafe.

“I can see that.”

“I heard you came back.” He held out what was in his hands. “I made these. In the summer. After. I didn’t know if you’d come back. I made them anyway.”

Gloves.

She looked at them. Leather. Soft, the softest she’d ever seen, the colour of a fawn’s back. Lined with something. Fleece. Close-sewn at every seam, with small even stitches, the kind you only did if you were doing it slowly.

“You do the cold,” said Rafe. “Don’t you. I saw. At the pump. In the summer, when you did the old men with the bad hands. And at the Reckoning. When the grey came across the floor and you put your hands on him and they went white to the elbow.” He looked at her hands. In her lap. Pink, mostly. Cold at the ends. “It comes out of you. Doesn’t it. Whatever you take.”

She didn’t say anything.

“So,” said Rafe. “For after.” He put them on the edge of the stool. “When it comes out of you cold. So you’ve got something to put them in.”

Rachael looked at the gloves.

She wasn’t good at this. She’d never been good at this. People gave you things and you were supposed to do something with your face, and she’d never known what. She looked at the gloves for a long time.

“Thank you,” said Rachael.

It came out flat. It always did. Rafe didn’t seem to mind. He nodded, as if that was about what he’d expected, and turned to go.

And Yan was standing behind him.

She hadn’t seen him come.

Nobody ever did. Yan had a way of being somewhere without having arrived. He was standing in the gate of the copy-room yard, with Wendy a pace behind him, and Working On It over his shoulder, the long pale ash haft, the blade folded back along it the way he carried it in towns. He’d been on his way somewhere. He’d stopped.

He was looking at Rafe.

Not at his face. Rachael knew how Yan looked at people. Everybody did, by now; Tiger had told the whole of Yan’s party on the bowsprit on floor 11 and it had gone round from there. He looked at you lower. At your chest. At the place under your breastbone where the light was, the one only he could see. He looked at Rafe Collier’s chest the way you’d look at a door you knew. A door you’d stood outside before. A long time, in the dark, deciding.

I’ll know his light now, Wendy had said he’d said. In the first week, on the Wellgate, when the mud hit. Anywhere. In the dark. I could find him tonight. And then: I’m not going to. Ice didn’t turn round. So I won’t either. And then, after a pause: Today.

Rachael had heard about it from Wendy, on the roof, a long time later. Wendy hadn’t been sure he was joking. She’d decided he probably wasn’t, and that it didn’t matter, because he’d said Ice didn’t turn round, and that was the bit that would hold him.

It was a long time since that day.

Rachael sat on her stool with the gloves on the edge of it and watched Yan look at Rafe Collier, and didn’t move, and didn’t breathe. She didn’t know what she’d do if he did something. She didn’t think he would. She didn’t know.

Yan’s hand was on the haft of Working On It. Just resting there. The way you’d rest your hand on a gate.

Rafe hadn’t noticed anything. He’d turned round, and seen a young man in the gate with a stick over his shoulder, and smiled, a big easy tanner’s smile.

“You’re the one with the stick,” said Rafe. “From the Tellhouse yard. In the summer.”

“Scythe,” said Yan.

“Scythe. Sorry.” Rafe looked at it, interested, the way a man who works with his hands looks at a tool. “That’s a good haft. Ash?”

“Ash.”

“You don’t see a haft like that.” Rafe nodded at it, approving. “Somebody knew what they were doing.”

“Yes,” said Yan.

He didn’t move. His hand was on the haft. His eyes were on the place under Rafe’s breastbone. Wendy, behind him, had gone very still, and put her hand on his back. Flat. Between the shoulder blades. Not pushing. Just there.

And then, across the yard, from the stable step, Dec came out.

He’d been upstairs. In the copy-room, with Thea, who was still holding his hand when she wasn’t holding the lantern. He came down the outside stair into the yard with her behind him, and saw the line, and the stool, and Rafe in his apron, and Yan in the gate.

He stopped.

Rafe saw him.

And Rafe Collier, tanner, of Tanners’ Row, who’d thrown mud at a corded man with a stone in it because he’d been told to by a man with rings on every finger, put his hand up. Palm out. Pink. The way he’d put it up on the long table at the Reckoning in front of the whole of Tell.

“You came back,” said Rafe. Across the yard.

Dec looked at him.

He came across the yard. Slowly. He still walked slowly; Kei said most of it, not all. Thea let go of his hand to let him. He came across the cobbles to where Rafe was standing by Rachael’s stool with his palm up, and stopped in front of him, and put his own hand on it. The left one. With the two lines round the wrist.

“Yes,” said Dec.

That was all. He held it there a moment. Then he let go and looked past Rafe, at the gate. At Yan.

Yan was looking at him.

Rachael didn’t know what went between them. She didn’t have whatever Yan had, or Hyvred, or Deston with his threads. She just had her eyes. And she saw Dec look at Yan, at Yan’s hand on the haft, at Wendy’s hand on Yan’s back, and she saw Dec understand all of it. And not say anything. He just looked at Yan, steadily, the way you’d look at somebody across a table when you’d both seen the same thing and there was no need to say what.

Yan’s hand came off the haft.

He took it away. Slowly. And put it in his pocket.

“See you, then,” said Yan. To nobody in particular. To the yard.

And he walked past. Right past Rafe, close enough to touch, past the stool and the gloves and Rachael, across the yard to the pump, where he worked the handle and drank from his cupped hand and shook the water off and went out of the other gate onto the Wellgate, with Wendy behind him, without looking back.

Rafe watched him go.

“Is he all right?” said Rafe. “Your friend. He looked a bit—“ He didn’t have the word.

“He’s thinking,” said Rachael.

It was true. She didn’t say things that weren’t.

Rafe nodded, satisfied, and touched his forehead to her, and went out of the gate the other way, down toward Tanners’ Row, whistling. He never knew. Rachael was sure of that, then and afterwards. He went home to his pits and his hides and his wife and whatever else a tanner went home to, and never knew that he’d stood in a yard on the morning of the four hundred and sixty-ninth day with a man looking at the light under his breastbone, deciding.

She put the gloves on.

They fitted. Of course they did. He was a man who worked with his hands, and he’d watched hers.

She found Yan on the roof that night.

The flat lead roof over the copy-room, up through the hatch. He was sitting at the edge with his legs over, looking down the hill at the lights of the Low Town, with Working On It across his knees and Wendy asleep against the chimney behind him. Rachael climbed out and sat down a little way off. Not close. She wasn’t a sitter-close.

She had the gloves on.

“You were going to,” said Rachael.

Yan didn’t look round.

“I was thinking about it,” he said. Pleasantly. The way he said everything.

“And?”

“He made you gloves.” Yan looked at her hands. “Good ones. He watched your hands to make them. You can tell.” He shrugged. “That’s not nothing. I don’t know what it is. It’s not nothing.”

“That’s why?”

“No.” He looked back down the hill. “Ice put his hand on his. Again. In front of me. On purpose.” He was quiet. “He knew I was standing there. He knew what I was thinking. And he went and did it anyway, right in front of me, so I’d see.” He almost smiled. “That’s very annoying.”

“It’s very him.”

“It’s the same thing.” Yan turned Working On It a little on his knees. “I said today. On the Wellgate. I said I wouldn’t, today. That was the deal I made.” He looked at her. “It’s not today any more, Rachael. It’s every day now. That’s the annoying thing about today. It keeps being today.”

Rachael didn’t say anything.

“I’ll still know his light,” said Yan. “I’ll always know it. Anywhere. I’m not going to stop knowing it.” He looked down the hill, toward the Tanners’ Row, where there was a light only he could see. “That’s not the same as doing anything.”

“It’s not the same as forgetting, either.”

“No,” said Yan. “I don’t forget.” He said it simply. As a fact. “Ice does. Or he says he does. He doesn’t turn round, and then he forgets which way he’d have been facing. I don’t. I just don’t turn round.” He looked at her. “Does that make sense?”

It did.

Rachael looked at her hands in Rafe’s gloves. She’d healed that man knowing exactly who he was. She’d taken the echo of his burn into her own palm for ten minutes. She’d done it because it was the job, and because the job didn’t ask who you were. And she’d never forgotten the apron. She never would.

“Neither do I,” said Rachael.

Yan looked at her for a long moment.

Then he laughed. Quietly. The way he laughed at things nobody else found funny, and sometimes at things that nobody else would ever know were funny, and once, she’d heard, at a soul.

“Good,” said Yan. “Then there’s two of us.”

The evening telling rang at dusk on the third day.

Three slow strokes, from the Tellhouse. The city hushed, the way it did every evening, the whole hill at once, every voice in every street stopping like a held breath. Rachael was on the Tellhouse steps with the others. She’d been on them for the evening telling forty times in the summer and she still didn’t know what to do with her hands. She put them in Rafe’s gloves.

Hawise read the day’s dead. Four names. An old woman on the Cloth Hall turn. A carter. Two children from a fever in the Low Town, a brother and sister, and Rachael’s hands tightened in the gloves on that one, and Kei, beside her, made a sound under his breath that might have been dose. Their lights went up. She couldn’t see them. Yan could. He was on the bottom step and he lifted his head as each name was said and watched something go straight up into the dark, quick and sure and clean as a thread, and said nothing.

Then it was done. The hush broke. People started to move.

And somewhere in the square at the bottom of the steps, children started singing.

Rachael knew it. Everybody in Tell knew it. The skipping rhyme. Every child on the hill knew it and nobody knew what the numbers meant, and the ones before had come up the well and gone up the stair, and the rhyme had been counting them for three hundred years without anybody knowing.

Thirty up the well and thirty up the stair—

A little girl’s voice. High. Out of tune. Mags. Rachael knew her by now. She’d sung the first line at the well-house on the first day.

—thirty in the Rolls and thirty in the air—

And then Mags stopped. Rachael heard her stop. In the middle of the square, with a skipping rope going round and two other girls turning it and the whole of the Tellhouse steps above her. She stopped and looked up at the steps. At the thirty-six standing on them. And she grinned, a gap-toothed eight-year-old’s grin, and changed the words.

Thirty-six up the well and thirty-six back DOWN—

The other girls shrieked. Somebody’s mother said Mags. And Mags went on, louder, making it up, jumping in:

—thirty-six in the Rolls and thirty-six in TOWN!

It didn’t rhyme very well. Nobody cared. The girls turning the rope took it up, and then the others, and by the third time round the whole square was singing it, the children skipping and the grown-ups laughing and Mags in the middle, jumping, still in at town, which was apparently the new out, because she’d decided it was.

Rachael looked along the steps.

Dec was laughing.

Properly. Out loud, with his hand over his mouth, and his shoulders going, on the Tellhouse steps in the dusk. She hadn’t heard him laugh like that since the Long Barn. Since before the line. And beside him Thea was looking at him laugh, and her face did the thing again, the beginning of a smile, and this time it didn’t go away. Not straight away. It stayed, for a moment, on her mouth. And then she seemed to remember, and set her shoulders, and looked at the square instead.

But she’d let it stay for a moment. Rachael saw that.

Still angry, she thought. Less than yesterday.

She’d tell Dennis. He’d want to do a graph.

The well-house was where they’d left it.

At the bottom of the hill, in the corner of the Lantern Market. A small round stone house with a well in it, a wooden lid, a bucket on a chain. And a door in the back wall. They’d come out of it on the three hundred and fifty-seventh day with the market going silent round them. They went into it on the four hundred and seventy-first, in the early morning, with the market just setting up, and the market didn’t go silent at all. People called out. Joan, from her apple stall. Bertha the baker, with flour on her arms, who’d left bread on their step every morning for a month and wouldn’t let anybody say so: she pushed a loaf at Dennis without a word and he said “I’ll tell everybody,” and she said “You dare.”

Aldo had come down. Rachael didn’t know how. Davy had carried him, she supposed, in his chair, all the way down the Wellgate. He was sitting in the corner of the market by the well-house door in his rush chair, with the rug and the handkerchief.

He didn’t wave it.

He held it up. That was all. Folded. White. Up, in front of him, the way you’d hold up a page to show somebody what was written on it.

Higu stopped at the door to the well-house and counted them in. Not numbers. Names. Thirty-six. Rachael went in behind Dennis. Inside, it was the same: the round stone wall, the well, the lid, the door at the back to the stair. And low on the inside of the doorframe, at a child’s height, thirty short scratches, very old, the ones Yan had counted on the first day.

And beside them, new ones.

Rachael stopped.

Fresh. Pale in the old stone. Scratched with something sharp, a nail, a knife, by somebody small, crouching. Not neat. A row of them, beside the thirty. She counted. She didn’t need to; she knew how many there’d be. She counted anyway.

Thirty-six.

And under them, in the same child’s hand, scratched deeper, one word. Rachael could read it. The plain form. The way every child in Tell was taught to write, from the first day: who came, what they did. No how. No seemed.

DOWN.

She stood there looking at it until Dennis put his hand on her back.

“Mags,” said Dennis.

“Mags,” said Rachael.

She went down the stair.

She thought about it, on the stair. Four hundred and twenty steps down, in the dark, with Thea singing at the front and Newish counting.

Is it safe yet?

She’d asked him that on 13, on this same hill, sitting on the trough in the copy-room yard after the Reckoning, with her hands just pink again. Not about anything. Just: is it safe yet. And he’d said no. Nearly. And she’d said nearly’s good. I’ll ask when it’s not nearly.

She’d been carrying not knowing since floor 1. She’d told him that, on a roof. She was good at carrying. She was going to carry it a while yet. Thirteen was the floor that listened. You didn’t put anything down on thirteen. You didn’t put anything down anywhere the tower could see.

But something had changed, on 14. She’d been at the door. She’d been on her knees at the sill with her hands on Hearthwall and the winter going through her, and she’d heard him. All night. Every number. Sixty. It hurts. Healing. My hands. It’s turned. I can’t come in. I’m telling you. She’d never once in four hundred and fifty-eight days heard him say a number he didn’t have to. And he’d said all of them. Out loud. Up a hill. So they’d know.

That wasn’t safe. That wasn’t anywhere near safe.

But it was a man who’d started telling people things.

She looked at her hands, in Rafe’s gloves, in the dark. Cold at the ends. A finger at a time. It came back slower than it went.

Nearly, she thought. Nearly’s good.

She’d ask when it wasn’t nearly.
