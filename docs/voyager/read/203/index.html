Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 203

The First Furrow

5,960 words · 26 min read

On the Salt you weren’t allowed to talk, and Calvin had been dreading it since the Bastion.

Not allowed wasn’t the right word. Nobody stopped you. The Salt didn’t care what you did, as long as you didn’t make a new sound on it. Sound went down into the crust, a hand’s depth of white over grey brine thick as porridge, and things under the brine heard it, and came up. They came up for new sounds. Not long steady ones; you could saw, and pull a sled, and walk. But a voice. A word. A laugh. Something dropped. One night on twelve, a year and a half ago, Dennis had let a sled stop and a water cask had gone tock on the runner, and six saltmouths had come up out of the crust and swayed there in the dark, listening, and everybody had lain flat for an hour.

So on the Salt you didn’t talk. You signed, the way the rakers signed, or you talked indoors on the rock at Seep, close, with the door shut, or you didn’t.

Calvin had talked his whole life. He’d talked down the stair into the first square of the Red City on the sixty-sixth day and only stopped when he saw what was lying by the fountain. He’d talked through the wisps on three at night for his trial, the whole way, look at my back, so that everybody behind him would have something to follow that wasn’t a light. He talked when he was frightened. He talked when he was happy. He talked when he didn’t know what else to do. He’d made a rule about talking, on four, that you couldn’t whisper there, and he still thought it was the best rule anybody had ever made.

And for nineteen days on the Salt, going west along Gigi’s road to the Mast, he wasn’t going to be able to say a single word out loud.

“You’ll live,” said Rachael, at the top of the stair up from Wenna’s cellar, before they went out. She had her fist in his collar already. “You did it last time.”

“Last time I nearly died.”

“You didn’t, though.”

“I nearly did. Inside. Where it counts.” He looked out of the door at the white. “Nineteen days.”

“Sign,” said Rachael. “You learned. Sign at me.”

He signed at her. It was a sign the rakers didn’t have, and she hit him on the back of the head with her glove, and they went out onto the Salt.

It was green.

That was the first thing. He’d heard about it on the way down, from Dennis, who’d told it at the long table on floor 1 until Mother Crane had thrown a turnip at him for a change. But he hadn’t seen it, not properly, because on the way down they’d come across the Salt at a run, eleven days, and it had been the tail of winter, and the green had been a thin line. Now it wasn’t thin.

Gigi’s road went west from the door across the white like a river. A long green ribbon, two or three metres wide, of grass and low scrub and small grey-green bushes with salt on their leaves, and here and there, every day’s walk or so, a tree. Not big ones. Young. The first ones, Gigi said, the ones she’d planted with Sow on the way up, a year and a half ago, a seed at a time, every evening, where the old forest was buried in the salt rings underneath. They’d come up. All of them. They stood along the green road at every day’s walk like milestones, each with a ring of white stones round its foot, and a name scratched on each stone. Wyn. Henna. Orrin. Tullah. The rakers’ names. So whoever planted it would know who’d looked after it.

And by every tree, a well.

The rakers had dug them. Because the tree was there. Because where a tree came up on the Salt there was sweet water under it, the old water, the last of the sea, that had given up its salt a thousand years ago and was lying under the whole floor moving west a finger’s width a year. The trees had found it. The rakers had followed the trees down. And now there was water on the Salt, every day’s walk, along a green road, all the way west.

Calvin walked along it in the wind with Rachael’s fist in his collar and didn’t say a word, because he couldn’t, and because he wouldn’t have known which one.

Gigi walked at the front. With Xola. Holding hands. She stopped at every tree. She put her palm flat on the bark of each one, a little, the way Maigo did, and stood there a moment with her eyes shut, and then went on. Hyvred, behind her, said every tree had a thread now, going back to her, thin and green. Twenty-three, Hyvred said, by the end, in signs, because Hyvred had learned to sign numbers better than anybody. Twenty-three threads.

At Seep, Tullah came out of the salt-block houses on the long low grey rock and stood at the edge of it with four rakers behind her, cloths down off their mouths, and looked at them coming in along the green.

She didn’t sign.

“You came back,” said Tullah. Out loud. On the Salt.

Calvin flinched. He couldn’t help it. Every one of them flinched. A year and a half of don’t make a sound.

Nothing came up.

Tullah looked at them flinching and something happened at the corner of her deeply lined mouth.

“We talk now,” she said. “Outside. Carefully. Not loud. Not new.” She looked at the green road, going away west. “They don’t come up so much. Not since the trees. Maigo says the roots are in the crust now. It’s not so thin. It doesn’t carry.” She looked at Calvin, specifically, as if she’d been told about him. “You can talk. Quietly. On the road.”

Calvin opened his mouth.

He’d had nineteen days of things saved up. He’d had a whole speech. About the Salt, and the green, and how Gigi had done this, Gigi, with seeds, and how nobody on Earth would ever believe it, and how he’d thought he was going to die of not talking and he hadn’t, and how it was the best road he’d ever seen and he’d seen a lot of roads now. All of it was right there.

What came out was: “Hi.”

Small. Quiet. On the Salt. Out loud.

Nothing came up.

A little girl behind Tullah’s legs, thin and brown with pale squinting eyes and a cloth over her head, looked at Calvin, and put her hand up, and said, “Hi,” back. Out loud. On the Salt.

That was Wyn. Tullah’s daughter. Whose name was pricked into her mother’s palm in blue-black, so that her mother could say it to her on the salt when you couldn’t say anything. Tullah had said it out loud on the Salt for the first time in her life on the way down, a year ago, with Rachael’s hand on Dennis’s arm.

Now Wyn said hi to Calvin on the Salt, and nothing came up, and Tullah looked at her daughter for a long time and then turned round and walked back into Seep without a word, very fast, the way people walk when they don’t want you to see their face.

Rachael’s fist in his collar went tight. Then loose.

“Quietly,” she said, in his ear. “On the road.”

“Quietly,” said Calvin. On the road. And talked the whole nineteen days, quietly, to everybody, about everything, all the way to the Mast, and nothing ever came up once.

The Mast stood up out of the Salt in the far west the way it always had, a ship bigger than the Patience standing upright on her keel in the white, a thousand years old, with her masts gone and her ribs showing through her sides like a dead thing’s, and the stair to thirteen going up out of her hold. They went in through the gap in her side and up the stair, and came out where you always came out on thirteen.

Up the well.

That was how it worked, in Tell. You came up the well. A round stone well-house at the bottom of the hill, with a wide stone ring in the middle and a heavy lid and a bucket on a chain, and the stair came up inside the well itself, round and round the inside of the shaft, and you climbed out over the rim like something the town had drawn up in a bucket. The ones before had come up it, three hundred and twelve years ago, in spring. And the thirty-six, the second winter. And down it again. And now up again.

Calvin climbed out over the rim first. He always did. He’d made himself first on every stair since four. Look at my back.

There was a little girl in the well-house.

Sitting on the rim, with her legs dangling over the shaft, with a stub of chalk. Mags. Who skipped rope on the Wellgate every evening and had changed the rhyme. She was looking at the inside of the well-house door, where there were scratches. Thirty old ones, at a grown man’s height, from three hundred years ago. And thirty-six new ones, at a child’s height, from last winter. And beside them, in big chalk capitals in a child’s hand, DOWN.

Mags looked at Calvin coming up out of the well.

Then she looked at the door. Then at Calvin. Then at the stub of chalk in her hand.

She got down off the rim. She went to the door. And beside DOWN, in exactly the same big wobbling capitals, she wrote:

UP

And under it, after some thought, smaller:

+ GOAT

Then she turned round and ran out of the well-house and up the Wellgate shrieking, and Calvin stood in the well-house with thirty-five people and a goat coming up out of the well behind him and laughed so hard that Rachael had to hold him up by the collar.

Tell came out to look at them.

All of it. The Wellgate, the Low Town, the hill, the tellers in their black coats, the clerks in their grey with their heads up now, looking, not writing. It came out of its doors and down its lanes and stood in the street. And it didn’t shout, the way Tell had shouted on the way down, THEY CAME BACK. It had shouted that already. It knew they came back. Instead, as they went up the Wellgate in a long line, people said their names.

Not all at once. One at a time. As they passed. The way Tell did, the evening telling, the Rolls, the door-roll, every person in the town said and written and kept.

“Calvin,” said a woman at a pie stall, as he passed.

“Rachael,” said a man in a doorway.

“Dennis,” said Bertha, from her bakery step, with a loaf under her arm. “I’m telling everybody.”

“You dare,” said Dennis.

All the way up the hill. Every one of them. Thirty-six names, said by the town as they went past, like a hand put out and taken back, the way Tether had lifted a hand off its ropes. Calvin heard his own name said by people he’d mended hinges for and carried water for and talked at, on the way through, the first time, for twenty days, and he found that his throat had done something and he couldn’t say anything back.

“You’re quiet,” said Rachael, in his ear, surprised.

“Shut up,” said Calvin.

At the top of the hill Aldo was in his chair in his doorway with his grandson Davy beside him and a new handkerchief in his hand. He was older. He’d been old the first time. He lifted the handkerchief when he saw them, the way he had on the way down, up, like a page. And said, in a voice like a door that hadn’t been opened in a while:

“Climbers. In my life.”

And then, because he was Aldo:

“Twice.”

He didn’t see Alys till the Tellhouse.

He’d been looking for her the whole way up the Wellgate. He hadn’t told anybody he was looking. He hadn’t told Rachael, who knew anyway. He’d looked at every grey coat and every black one, every clerk with their head up, every teller on every step. He’d stood in Cooper’s Lane, the second ring, at noon on five separate days, the first time round, and shouted her name across it like across a field, and she’d stopped, and her pen had come up off the page and shaken, and once she’d turned her head. And then she’d dropped her pen in the Countinghouse when Thea went past with a light, and come out, and written on a slate at the Reckoning with a shaking fist Alys Brand. I was in the fourth row, and said Hawise’s name out loud, and put her hand on Hawise’s hand.

On the way down she’d been on the door-roll. In brown, not grey. Writing them in. The four hundred and sixty-eighth day of the climbers. They came down the hill. She’d said You shouted. Thank you. And he’d gone silent, which Rachael said was the third time in a fortnight and a medical emergency.

She was on the steps of the Tellhouse now. With Hawise. With the big book of the door-roll open on a lectern between them and the pen in her hand. Brown coat. Her hair tied back. A small white scar on her lower lip. Ink on her right wrist. She was writing. Hawise said something to her and she looked up.

She saw them come up the last of the hill. Thirty-six and a goat.

She saw Calvin.

And Alys Brand, who had been a clerk in grey for twelve years and never once in all that time made a sound in the street, put down her pen on the lectern. And took a breath. And shouted, across the whole square in front of the Tellhouse, the way you’d shout across a field:

“CALVIN!”

He stopped.

Mid-step. Mid-word; he’d been saying something to Dennis about the pie stall. He stopped dead in the middle of the square with his mouth open and Rachael’s fist in his collar and every person in Tell looking at him.

Alys stood on the Tellhouse steps with her hands at her sides and her face very red.

“You did it to me,” she called. “Five times. In Cooper’s Lane. Like across a field, Hawise said.” She lifted her chin. “So.”

Calvin didn’t say anything.

He couldn’t. He stood in the square and looked at her and his whole face had gone hot and his throat had done the thing again and there was nothing in his mouth at all. Not a word. For maybe the longest time in his life.

“Four,” said Rachael, behind him, in tones of awe. “That’s four. In two years. That’s—Dennis, write it down.”

“I’m writing it down,” said Dennis.

Alys came down the steps. She came across the square to him, not fast, the way she’d walked to Hawise in the Assize with her slate, and stopped in front of him.

“I’m writing you in,” she said. “Coming up. Up. Nobody’s ever been written in coming up the well twice.” She looked at him. “I wanted to say. Before I wrote it.” She swallowed. “Thank you. For shouting. It was the first thing. The first thing anybody said that got in.”

Calvin found his voice. It came out small.

“You’re welcome,” he said.

Alys nodded. Once. Then she turned round and went back up the steps to the lectern, and picked up her pen, and wrote. Calvin saw her do it. Neat, careful letters, in a hand that had been the best in her year, that could write a whole door-roll and never blot. He saw her write a line. Then stop. Then write something else, at the end of it, smaller.

Later, Hawise showed him.

The seven hundred and eleventh day of the climbers. They came up the well. Thirty-six. And a goat. They are going up the hill.

And at the end, small, in the margin, in Alys’s hand:

Calvin shouted first.

That evening at the telling, on the Wellgate, Mags skipped the new rhyme. Everybody on the Wellgate already knew it. They’d been skipping it all day. Calvin thought the whole of Tell probably had it by heart before the thirty-six had even got to the top of the hill.

Thirty-six up the well and thirty-six back DOWN — thirty-six up AGAIN and a GOAT in the TOWN!

“In at TOWN,” shrieked the children, which was the new out, and Mags got a rope round her ankles and fell over, and the goat, who had been standing on the edge of the Wellgate watching the children with great interest, put his head down and very gently butted her back up again.

Calvin laughed till he cried. So did Mags. So, for some reason, did Aldo, from his chair, with his handkerchief over his face.

The stair from thirteen to fourteen was four hundred and twenty-four steps, and at the top of it, it was spring.

He’d known it would be. Dec had said so, on the stair; Dec always knew what the weather would be at the top of a stair, the way other people knew what time it was. He’d still not believed it. The last time Calvin had been on the Long Acre it had been under snow. Snow over nothing; snow over the Grey, the picture of ground that wasn’t ground, that took your leg to the knee if you stepped wrong. Seven hundred people and a dozen horses shut in the Long Barn with the doors closed. A straw man at the end of the barn with frost in his chest and his eyes on Dec. And then a night, and a bell, and Dec walking back in through the door on his own feet, barely, white to the elbows, with the winter gone out of the floor behind him.

There was no snow now.

The valley went down from the door green and brown, in long strips, all the way to the far hills. Field after field after field, hedges between them, the villages small and smoking at the bends of the river. Brightwater. Ashwell. Lammas. Haverley. The fields were brown. Bare, turned earth, wet and dark, with green coming up at the edges and along the hedges, and birds on it. Real birds. Lots of them, going up and coming down. And on the far fields, very small, very slow, people. And horses. And behind the horses, a line of dark earth opening up in the brown, straight as a string.

“They’re ploughing,” said Maigo.

He’d stopped at the top of the stair. He had four leaves now, and two more coming. He took his sunglasses off. He stood at the top of the four hundred and twenty-four steps with his bare eyes on the fields and his face doing something Calvin had never seen it do.

“It’s the first one,” said Maigo. “The first furrow. They’re doing it today.” He swallowed. “We came on the day.”

“Course we did,” said Hako. “Classic.”

They came down into Haverley at noon, across the Barn Field, past the Long Barn. And the whole of the Acre was at the first field.

It was Gorse Piece. Haverley’s first field. Calvin remembered it from the winter: it had been grey then, under the snow, unreachable. Where Gaffer Tobin should have been buried, at the bottom of it, so he’d be there for the next one, and couldn’t be, because the Grey had it. They’d buried him against the Long Barn wall instead, in the snow, with Ina warming the ground.

It wasn’t grey now. It was earth. Brown and wet and turned, the whole long slope of it, all but one strip along the top. And at the head of that strip, the whole Acre. Every village. Seven hundred people, give or take, standing in the mud at the top of Gorse Piece in their spring clothes with their sleeves rolled up, and a team of four big horses in harness, and a plough. And Annet, the Reeve, with her stick.

And beside Annet, in a barrow, the straw man.

Not all of him. He’d been kept. All winter, in the Long Barn, after the bell, with a piece of him in every chimney corner on the Acre and children touching his frozen straw for luck. And this morning, on the day of the first furrow, the old women had gone into the barn and taken him down off his frame, and carried him out here, the way they did every spring, the way their mothers had. He lay in the barrow on a bed of last year’s straw with his plaited face turned up to the sky. His poppy crown. His arms bound with withy. Quiet. Very quiet.

Hild was sitting beside the barrow on a stool. Of course she was. The toothless Lammas woman who’d plaited his face sixty times, and her mother before her, who’d sat at his feet all winter and said you don’t leave it on its own in the dark. She was looking at the straw man the way you’d look at somebody asleep.

She looked up when they came across the Barn Field. She looked at all of them. Then at Dec.

“Did it keep her?” said Hild. “The plait. Your old woman.”

Dec stopped in front of her.

“She’s in the chimney corner,” he said. “She was ill. She nearly went. She’s all right.” He paused. “She said it looked like writing.”

Hild nodded slowly.

“Then she’s kept,” she said. “That’s what it’s for.” She looked at the straw man in the barrow. “Everybody thinks it’s one way round.”

“It’s both,” said Dec.

Hild’s toothless mouth moved, a long way down in her face.

“Now you’ve got it,” she said.

Annet gave Dec the bell.

Not the bell itself. That was the harvest bell, on its post at the edge of the Long Barn, that rang at the end of every day of the harvest and said when the bell goes, the field’s not yours. It had rung for him once already. At first light, after a night out on the Grey, after the last sheaf of the year had stopped trying to kill him and stood still. He’d walked back in at the bell, like a person.

This was a hand-bell. Small and brass and old, with a wooden handle worn black. Annet took it out of her apron and held it out to him across the mud at the top of Gorse Piece.

“For the first furrow,” said Annet. “Somebody rings it in. It’s always been a Reeve. Or a Gaffer.” She held it out. “You came in at the bell. Last time. On your own feet.” Her face was the same flat brown weathered face as always. “You ring it in.”

Dec looked at the bell. Then at Annet. Then, for some reason, at Calvin.

Calvin didn’t know why. He made a face that meant what? Dec looked away.

He took the bell.

The old women cut the straw man.

They did it with sickles, quietly, without talking, the way they’d plaited his face in the autumn. A twist from his chest for Brightwater. A twist from his arm for Ashwell. One for Lammas, from his shoulder, that Hild cut herself with her own hand. One for Haverley, from his heart, where the wedding sheaf had been put in the autumn, right against the neck, so the keeping would have something to hold. Hana and Milli’s sheaf, that they’d bound with one band twisted from either end and one knot tied together. Calvin saw Hana see it, and put her hand over her mouth, and Milli put his arm round her.

The Haverley twist went into the plough. Into the share, the iron blade, tucked into the socket where the beam met it, the way the old women showed. So the harvest goes back in.

Then the horses leaned into their collars.

And Dec rang the bell.

He rang it once. Clear. Small. A brass note, out over the wet brown slope of Gorse Piece and the seven hundred people standing in the mud and the green coming up along the hedges. And the plough went into the earth at the top of the field, and the horses walked, and the first furrow of the year opened down the long brown slope behind them, dark and straight as a string, with a twist of last year’s straw man folded into the bottom of it.

Nobody cheered. You didn’t, at the first furrow. Annet had said. You watched.

They all watched. All seven hundred of the Acre, and all thirty-six, and a goat. They stood at the top of Gorse Piece in the spring mud and watched the first furrow go down the field to the bottom, where Gaffer Tobin should have been buried, and the plough turn, and come back up.

And when it got back to the top, somebody lifted a stick.

Calvin saw it, because he was looking the wrong way. He was always looking the wrong way. He’d been watching the people instead of the plough, because people were more interesting than ploughs, everybody knew that. And at the end of the first furrow, when the plough came back up to the top of Gorse Piece and the horses stopped, steaming, he saw a girl at the edge of the crowd lift a stick into the air.

Not high. An inch. Maybe two. The way you’d lift a stick to say I see you.

Wren.

He knew her. Everybody did. A thin girl of nine or ten, from Haverley, with a bucket. In the harvest, the first time they came up, she’d run up and down the rows all day long with a bucket and a ladle, giving everybody water, without being asked, and when Tiger had offered to carry the bucket she’d said no, it’s my job, and he’d run beside her all day instead, carrying nothing, keeping up.

She was holding a stick. Like Gaffer Tobin’s.

Calvin knew the stick she meant it to be. Everybody on the Acre did. A long old ash stick with notches cut in it, one for every harvest he’d seen in, that he’d lifted an inch at the end of every field, for sixty years, so the people bringing it in would know somebody had seen. Annet had cut the last notch on the morning they buried him, and crossed it, and laid the stick along his side.

It wasn’t Gaffer’s, then. Gaffer’s was in the ground with him. This one was new. Ash, pale, too long for her, with one fresh notch cut near the top. Wren was standing at the edge of the crowd at the top of Gorse Piece with it in both her hands, lifted an inch off the mud. Watching the first furrow come in.

Nobody had told her to. Calvin could see that. Nobody was looking at her but him.

And Ina.

Ina was standing a little way off, with his mask pushed up, and Winter beside him. He was looking at Wren. At the stick. And his face, which never changed, did something very small.

Calvin remembered the words. Everybody did. Ina had said them over Gaffer’s grave against the Long Barn wall, in the snow, the first words anybody had ever said over a grave on the Acre that weren’t Gaffer’s. He watched. That’s all he did, at the end. When a field was in, he lifted his stick, so the people bringing it in would know somebody saw. That’s not a small thing. That’s the thing. And then: Somebody else’ll lift it, next year. Somebody here. You’ll know who. You’ll see them watching.

Ina was seeing her watching.

He didn’t say anything. He looked at Wren with the stick for a long moment. Then he looked across the crowd at Dec. Dec was looking at Wren too, with the brass bell still in his hand, and his lips were moving, very slightly, the way they had in the barn, and on the ledge under the first square on four. Saying something after somebody. Learning it.

Their eyes met. Ina and Dec. Across the top of Gorse Piece. They nodded to each other. Once each.

Calvin didn’t know what it meant. He’d never known what that nod meant. He’d seen them do it twice now, three times, over graves, and once over a girl with a stick, and he’d never asked, because Rachael had told him once that you didn’t ask things on the step, and Calvin was on the step, probably, he thought. He’d always thought he was. He’d never been sure.

He thought he was sure now.

Wren put the stick down.

And Annet, the Reeve, at the head of the furrow, looked across the crowd at the girl with Gaffer’s stick, and nodded. Just once. The way you’d nod at somebody who’d just been given a job, and taken it.

They stayed three days on the Acre. They ploughed. All thirty-six, behind the horses, with the villages; Maigo walked every furrow of Gorse Piece barefoot and said the ground was glad. They ate in the Long Barn, where the straw man’s frame stood empty at the end of the aisle now, and the old bolts lay in a box by the wall, and Bettris said she’d drive new ones in the autumn when he came back in. Dec made soup in the Lammas oven. He looked at every face before he filled the bowl.

On the third evening, Higu went to the line.

Calvin went with her. He didn’t know why; he just did. Everybody did, in the end. The whole thirty-six, in a slow drift down the aisle of the Long Barn in the lamp-light, to the place fifteen paces from the empty frame where, in the winter, Higu had chalked a line across the floor.

It was still there. Faint. Scuffed. A pale line across the boards between the first table and the second.

The line where Dec had sat. Cross-legged, on the near side, for days, with the straw man looking at him, while everybody worked out what to do. That’s not a punishment, Higu had said. That’s just where. The line where a gold thread had come out of the straw man’s heart in the dark and gone across fifteen paces into Dec, asking him something. The line he’d stood up from, on the last night, to tell Higu where he was going, which was out, alone, onto the Grey.

The stair to fifteen was under it.

It had been there all the time. Under the boards, under the line, under where he’d sat. A trapdoor, flush with the floor, that nobody had seen till the snow went and the floor guardian was still and the window came up. The stair to 15 is under the line, where I sat. Higu had written LATER on the rock. Then they’d turned round and gone down.

Higu stood at the line now with her new rock under her arm. Flat and grey, from the river below the mill on floor 1. Blank. She looked at the line for a long time.

“Now,” said Higu.

That was all. She didn’t write it. She just said it.

Dec was standing at the line. On the near side. Where he’d sat. He was looking down at the trapdoor. At the iron ring in it, set flush in the boards. And Calvin, who was always looking the wrong way, who watched people instead of ploughs, was looking at Dec. And he saw something in Dec’s face that he’d never seen there before, in two years, on any floor, at the top of any stair.

He didn’t know what it was. He couldn’t have named it. It wasn’t fear, exactly. Calvin knew Dec’s fear; he’d seen it on fourteen, at this line, and on floor 1 in the chair. It wasn’t that. It was something smaller and stranger and much more ordinary. The face of a man standing at the top of a stair he’d never been up. Like everybody else’s face, at the top of every stair, the first time.

Calvin had never once seen Dec look like everybody else.

He went to the front. Of course he did. He always went to the front. He stepped over the line and stood on the trapdoor, right in front of Dec, with his back to him. Rachael’s fist went into his collar from behind. He didn’t look round.

“Look at my back,” said Calvin. To everybody. To Dec, mostly. “Nobody runs.”

Tiger knelt down and put his hand flat on the trapdoor. He held it there a long time. Then he took the iron ring, and pulled, and the trapdoor came up, and cold air came up out of the dark under the Long Barn floor, smelling of nothing Calvin had ever smelled before in his life.

Newish started counting.

Thea started singing.

And Calvin went down the first step into the dark under the floor, which was the way up, talking.

The Salt: Gigi’s road is green all the way west. A young tree every day’s walk with the rakers’ names on stones round it, and a well by every tree. Hyvred: twenty-three threads.
Tullah, out loud: “You came back. We talk now. Carefully. Not new.” Calvin: “Hi.” Wyn: “Hi.” Nothing came up. Tullah walked away fast.
Calvin talked for nineteen days on the road, quietly. Nothing came up once.
Up the well into Tell. Mags wrote UP beside DOWN on the well-house door. And + GOAT.
Tell said our names as we passed. Bertha: “I’m telling everybody.” Aldo: “Climbers. In my life. Twice.”
Alys, from the Tellhouse steps, across the square: “CALVIN!” He stopped dead. Rachael: “Four. In two years. Dennis, write it down.”
“Thank you. For shouting. It was the first thing that got in.” “You’re welcome.”
The door-roll: Thirty-six. And a goat. In the margin: Calvin shouted first.
The new rhyme: thirty-six up AGAIN and a GOAT in the TOWN. The goat butted Mags back up.
Fourteen: spring. They were ploughing. Maigo: “We came on the day.”
Hild: “Did it keep her?” “She’s in the chimney corner.” “Then she’s kept. It’s both.” “Now you’ve got it.”
Annet gave Dec the hand-bell. “You came in at the bell. You ring it in.” The Haverley twist from the straw man’s heart, where the wedding sheaf was, went into the plough. Dec rang it. The first furrow down Gorse Piece.
Wren lifted a new stick, like Gaffer’s, one notch, an inch, at the end of the furrow. Nobody told her. Ina saw. Dec’s lips moved. They nodded.
Three days ploughing. The frame’s empty. Bettris will drive new bolts in the autumn.
Higu at the line, with a blank rock: “Now.” The stair to fifteen is under where he sat.
Dec looked like everybody else at the top of a stair. First time.
“Look at my back. Nobody runs.”
