Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 131

Where Nothing Grows

6,863 words · 30 min read

Maigo counted his leaves every morning.

He hadn’t told anybody. It wasn’t the sort of thing you told people. He just did it, first thing, before he got up, lying on his back on the salt in his bedroll with the sky going from black to grey to white over him and nobody else awake yet. He’d reach up, under the hood of his orange hoodie, and find the two little branches of his antlers, and go along them with his fingers. Every twig. Every leaf. Counting.

On the first morning on floor 12, the three hundred and twentieth day, there had been two hundred and six.

He knew because he’d counted them on floor 11 as well, on the Patience, out of curiosity, and there had been two hundred and twelve, and he’d lost six to the salt wind on the deck, and that had seemed like a lot. He’d been a bit sad about it. He’d thought, that’s the sea. He’d thought they’d come back on the next floor, the way things came back. They always had. Floor 2’s cold had taken half of them and floor 5’s spring had brought them back in a week.

On the morning of the three hundred and twenty-sixth day, there were a hundred and forty-one.

He lay on his back and counted them twice, to be sure. Then he took his hand down and lay there, and looked at the white sky, and didn’t get up for a while.

Your body keeps the score, Ice had said, once, about something else. Maigo couldn’t remember what. It had seemed like the kind of thing people said.

It wasn’t, here. Here it was just true.

It wasn’t only the leaves.

It was the bark. He’d had bark on his cheeks and the backs of his hands and down his arms since the white room, smooth and brown and faintly glossy, like the bark of a young cherry. It had gone dark and shining on floor 9, in the summer. He’d been so pleased with it. He’d looked at it in the bough-folk’s mirror-shells and thought, oh, that’s nice, the way you’d think it about a new coat.

On the Salt it was cracking.

Fine cracks, first. Like the glaze on an old plate. Then deeper. Along the backs of his fingers, where they bent. At the corners of his mouth. And in the cracks, if he looked, there was salt. White. Fine as flour. Getting in. He could feel it. It didn’t hurt, exactly. It pulled. Like something drinking him very slowly through a very thin straw.

And his root-foot.

He kept it in his shoe now. He’d never kept it in a shoe, not on any floor, not even on floor 2 in the snow. It was his bare foot. It was the one that knew where he was. But on the Salt, the one time he’d tried to put it down, on the first day, at the foot of the grey rock by the door, the toes had gone soft and pale the way they always did, and reached, and touched the crust, and recoiled. Like a hand on a stove. And come up crusted white at the tips, and stung for two days.

So it stayed in a shoe. Wrapped in a strip of Kei’s spare bandage. And every step he took across the white it said no, no, no inside the cloth, very quietly, the way a dog whines behind a door.

He drank his water. His ration. The same as everybody’s: a mug in the morning, a mug at noon, a mug at night, from the casks on the sleds that Chris checked every evening with a stick and a knotted string. It was enough for a person. Kei had worked it out. Enough for a person walking softly in the sun, if they were careful.

Maigo wasn’t sure he was a person. Not the kind Kei had worked it out for.

He drank it, and was thirsty, and didn’t say.

Winter knew.

He should have guessed she would. Winter knew everything about everybody in her party. She didn’t do anything with it, mostly. She just knew, and kept it, the way you’d keep a stone in your pocket. But on the second day out from Seep she fell into step beside him in the line of sleds, softly, with her bow across her back and her silver hair tied up under a cloth against the sun and her black horns sticking out through the top of it, and walked beside him for an hour without saying anything.

Then she reached over and took his hand.

He let her. She turned it over. She looked at the backs of his fingers. At the cracks. At the white in them. She didn’t say anything. She ran her thumb along one of the cracks, very gently, and he felt the salt grate under her skin.

Then she looked up at his antlers. At the gaps.

You couldn’t talk out on the salt. Not really. You could, if you were close enough, in a whisper, but it went down. So Winter didn’t. She just looked at him. With her pale eyes in her sunburned face, very steady.

He shook his head. Don’t.

She kept looking.

He nodded. I know.

She let go of his hand. And reached into her pack, walking, and brought out her water skin, her own, and held it out to him.

He shook his head.

She held it out.

He shook his head again, harder, and pushed it back toward her, and she looked at him for a long moment, and then put it away. And walked beside him, softly, for the rest of the afternoon. And that night, in camp, when Chris was checking the casks with his stick and his knotted string, Maigo saw Winter go and stand next to him and say something, very low, and Chris’s face change, and Chris look over at him.

He looked away.

Ice brought him the pot on the third night.

Maigo didn’t see him coming. That was Ice; you didn’t, mostly. He was sitting on the edge of a sled, on the wood, in the dark, because wood was the only thing on the Salt his root-foot didn’t flinch from. Wood was dead, but it had been a tree once. It remembered. Not much, and not in words. But when he took his shoe off at night and put his bare foot on the planks of the sled, his toes stopped whining, and lay there, and something in him that had been clenched all day let go a little.

He was sitting like that when Ice crouched down in front of him.

Ice had something in his hands. Maigo couldn’t see what. It was dark. Ice held it out.

A pot. A small clay pot, rough, the kind the bough-folk made for seedlings, brown, with a lip, about the size of two cupped hands. Heavy. Maigo took it and it was heavy, and the weight of it in his hands was the weight of something full.

Earth.

He knew before he touched it. He knew through the clay, through his fingers, through the cracked bark on his palms. He knew the way you know a smell before you’ve worked out what it is. Grey. Soft. Fine. A little ash in it. A little rot. The smell of a forest floor after a fire. And in it, faint, very faint, so faint he wasn’t sure he hadn’t imagined it, the memory of a root. A seedling’s root. Red-brown. Reaching.

The Down. The north tree’s foot. The hearth Ina had made, and Winter had ringed with stones.

“Where did you get this,” said Maigo. Not a question. He couldn’t make it into one. It went down into the salt anyway, and he didn’t care.

“Highcrook,” said Ice. Low. Close. “The day after. When we went back down to look at it. I took some. From the edge. Where it wasn’t near the roots.” A pause. “I’ve had it since.”

“Since floor 9.”

“Yeah.”

“That’s a hundred days, Ice.”

“A hundred and three.”

Maigo sat on the sled with the pot in his lap.

He wanted to ask. He wanted to ask so badly that it went all through him, the way sap goes up a tree in spring, from the root to the tips. Why? Why would you carry a pot of earth up three floors in your pack for a hundred and three days? Who does that? Who thinks of that?

He knew who thought of that. Somebody who knew the Salt was coming. Somebody who knew what the Salt would do to a tree.

I remembered, Ice would say. If he asked. That was what Ice always said now, when the floors came. What I remember. Higu’s rule. The left-hand side of the rock.

Maigo didn’t ask.

He didn’t know why not. He just found, sitting there with the pot in his lap and Ice crouched in front of him in the dark, that he didn’t want to make Ice say it. He looked at Ice’s face, as much of it as he could see by starlight, and it looked the way Corran’s had looked on the deck of the Patience with his eyes shut and his fingers on a knot that was worn smoother than the ones round it. Like somebody holding a thing they’d held for a very long time.

He took his shoe off his root-foot. The bandage. And put his bare toes, very slowly, into the pot.

Into the earth.

They went in. Soft and pale. The way they always had. Down into the grey, an inch, two inches, as far as the pot let them, and spread, and found the bottom, and lay there. And the whole of him said oh.

Oh.

He sat on the sled with his foot in a pot of earth from a forest three floors down and the tears came out of his eyes without him meaning them to. They went down the cracks in his cheeks. They stung where the salt was.

“Sorry,” said Ice. Very quietly. “It’s not much.”

“It’s not much,” said Maigo, and laughed, wetly. “It’s a pot.”

“Yeah.”

“You carried a pot.”

“I’m a cook,” said Ice. “I know what happens to a plant if you don’t water it.”

Maigo laughed again, and it went down into the salt, and he didn’t care. He sat there with his foot in the pot, crying and laughing, and Ice crouched in front of him and didn’t say anything else. After a while he got up, and put his hand very briefly on the top of Maigo’s hood, between the antlers, and went back toward the other sleds, where Thea was.

“Maigotron,” he said, going. Barely loud enough. Over his shoulder.

“Hi,” said Maigo.

The pot helped. It didn’t stop it.

He put his foot in it every night. On the sled, in the dark. It was the only time his root-foot stopped whining. But a pot of earth is a pot of earth, and the Salt was the Salt, and every morning when he counted, lying on his back under the white sky, there were fewer.

A hundred and thirty-two.

A hundred and twenty.

On the morning of the three hundred and twenty-eighth day, a hundred and six.

He lay and counted them twice. Then he got up, and put his shoe on, and went and found Chris, who was sitting on the first sled with his notebook, checking the casks with his stick and his knotted string before anybody else was awake.

Chris looked up.

Maigo sat down beside him on the sled. Close. So he could talk.

“I have to do it,” he said.

Chris didn’t ask what. Chris had written it down. Chris had written everything down, on every floor, and on the page in his notebook headed HIDDEN, under Maigo, there were three lines. The Old Wood 1/3: Listen to one that remembers. (Done, d222.) 2/3: Something grows here that was never planted. Find its root. (Done, d236.) And the third, which had been sitting there with nothing after it for ninety-two days.

3/3: Go where nothing grows, and listen.

“Here,” said Maigo. “It’s here. This is where nothing grows. I knew it the first day. I put my foot down and it said no.” He looked at his hands. “I’ve known since floor 9, really. Since it said go where nothing grows. I thought, that’ll be a desert, or a rock, or the top of a mountain. I didn’t think it’d be this.” He looked out at the white. “There’s nothing. Chris. There’s nothing at all.”

“Then what would you listen to?”

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “That’s the bit I don’t know.” He swallowed. “But it says listen. It wouldn’t say listen if there wasn’t anything.”

Chris looked at him for a long time.

“You’ll have to root,” said Chris. “To listen. Won’t you. Properly. Like the north tree.”

“Yes.”

“Into that.” Chris looked at the crust at the foot of the sled.

“Yes.”

“Maigo.” Chris’s voice was very low. “You’ve lost a hundred leaves in eight days just walking on it.”

“A hundred and six,” said Maigo. “I count.”

Chris closed his notebook.

“I can’t do it later,” said Maigo. Before Chris could say it. “Higu says. The lower the floor, the more it can grow. It’s set where you finish it. If I don’t do it here, it’s thirteen, or fourteen, or I don’t know, somewhere else with nothing growing, and there might not be one. This might be the only one.” He looked at Chris. “And Gigi’s is plant something where nothing has grown. Hers is the same place. She can’t plant if nobody knows what’s under it.”

“Higu says,” said Chris, “a lot of things.”

“She’s right about this one.”

“She’s right about most of them,” said Chris. “That’s what’s so annoying.” He looked down at his notebook. At the line. Go where nothing grows, and listen. “How long?”

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “The north tree was three days.”

“We can’t stop for three days. The water.”

“I know.”

Chris sat with that. Maigo watched him sit with it. Chris was the oldest of them. He’d been the head of the party since the white room, since the draft, when he’d picked the four of them. He’d never once asked any of them to do a thing he hadn’t already worked out how to get them back from. Maigo watched him try to work this one out, and not be able to, and know it.

“Okay,” said Chris.

“Okay?”

“Okay,” said Chris. “But you’re not doing it on your own. And we’re asking Higu. And if it starts to go wrong, I’m pulling you out. I don’t care what the window says. I’ll pull you out by the antlers.”

“You can’t pull a tree out by the antlers.”

“Watch me,” said Chris.

Higu said yes.

Not straight away. She sat on the aft sled with the rock in her lap and looked at the right-hand column for a long time, at 10 DAYS’ WATER. MAYBE., which she’d crossed out and rewritten as 6 that morning, and then she looked at Maigo, and at his antlers, and said, “How many?”

“A hundred and six.”

“How many did you have?”

“Two hundred and twelve.”

Higu was quiet.

“Then you’re half gone already,” she said. Very calmly. Not calmly. “And you’ll go all the way if we walk thirty days. Rooting or not.” She looked at the rock. “So it’s not whether. It’s where.” She wrote something. “We stop. Three days. We’ll find the water.”

“How?” said Deston.

“I don’t know yet,” said Higu. “That’s three days to find out.”

And Gigi came.

She came across the camp at dusk, softly, from Hyvred’s party’s sleds, with her crooked green crown on her head, the one she’d braided on floor 6, gone brown and brittle at the edges now like everything, and sat down on the sled next to Maigo without a word, and took his hand. The way Winter had. And looked at the backs of his fingers. And at his antlers.

“You’re doing yours,” she said. Close. Very low.

“Tomorrow.”

“Then I’m watching,” said Gigi. “Mine’s the same place. Plant something where nothing has grown.” She looked out at the white. “I’ve been looking at it for nine days and thinking, plant what? Where? In what? There’s nothing to plant. There’s nothing to plant in.” She looked at him. “You’re going to find out what’s under it. And then you’re going to tell me.”

“If there’s anything.”

“There’s something,” said Gigi. “It says listen. It wouldn’t say listen if there wasn’t anything.”

Maigo looked at her.

“That’s what I said,” he said.

“I know,” said Gigi. “Chris told Higu, and Higu told me. Word for word. You know what Higu’s like.”

He rooted at dawn on the three hundred and twenty-ninth day.

They chose a place. Higu chose it, with Hyvred, who looked at the white with her true sight for an hour and said the crust was thinnest there, in a slight dip, a hundred metres north of the camp, where the salt had a faint grey tinge to it like old ice. Chris’s party walked out to it in the grey light. Softly. Chris and Niko and Winter and Ina, round him. And Gigi, a little behind, with Hyvred.

He took his shoe off.

He took the bandage off. His root-foot looked bad. He hadn’t looked at it properly for days. The toes were pale and shrunken, and the bark on the top of the foot was cracked right across, white in every crack. It didn’t whine any more. It was too tired.

He took the pot out of his pack, and put it down on the salt beside him. He didn’t know why. He wanted it there.

Then he put his foot down on the crust.

It said no.

He didn’t take it away.

Go where nothing grows, he thought. And listen.

He let his toes go soft.

It was the worst thing he had ever felt. It was worse than floor 2, when the cold had come up through his foot and shut him down like a house in winter. That had been the cold saying shh, not now. This was the salt saying nothing at all. His toes went soft and reached, the way they always did, and found the crust, and pushed, and it burned. Not like fire. Like the opposite of water. Like every drop of him being pulled at, all at once, toward the salt, by the salt, into the salt. He felt the roots go out of his toes, fine and white, the way they always did. He felt them touch.

He felt them start to dry.

“Maigo,” said Chris. Somewhere above him. A long way off.

Don’t hurry, he thought. That was what the first one had said. Listen to one that remembers. Do not hurry it.

He didn’t hurry.

He went down.

The crust was thin.

That was the first thing. A hand’s depth. Hard, glittering, grained like old sugar. His roots went through it the way roots go through a crack in a pavement, slowly, finding the weak places, and under it there was something soft. Wet. Grey. A slurry of salt and water, thick as porridge, and it was the first water his roots had touched in ten days, and it was the worst. It was brine. It burned. It pulled at him harder than the dry crust had.

He went down through it.

Under the slurry, salt again. Hard. Layered. He felt the layers. A band, and then a line, and then another band. Over and over. Down and down.

Rings, he thought.

Like a tree’s. Each one a year. A summer when the sea had dried a little, and left a skin of salt, and then a winter when it hadn’t. Band and line, band and line. He’d read the north tree’s rings on floor 9, a thousand of them, lying with his cheek on the bark for three days. He knew how to read rings.

He’d never thought salt would have them.

He went down through them. One at a time. Not hurrying. A year, and a year, and a year. And he listened.

There was nothing.

That was the thing. That was the worst thing. On floor 9 the north tree had been full. Slow, and huge, and full, a thousand summers of fires and leaves and cones and small warm lights, and it had talked. Not in words. In memory. Every ring had something in it. Here there was nothing. Band and line. Salt and salt. Dry. Silent. Like the air above, where your voice fell down in front of you and lay there. He went down through a hundred years of it, and two hundred, and there was nothing in any of it at all.

He was so thirsty.

He didn’t know how long it was. He knew, later, because Chris told him. A day. The first day. The sun came up and went over and went down, and he stood in the dip in the salt with his root-foot gone into it and his eyes shut, and didn’t move, and didn’t hear anything. Chris brought water every four hours. He couldn’t drink it. Chris poured it on his foot instead, where it went into the crust, and Maigo felt it go down and down past him, fresh, a cup of it, gone into the salt like a spark into the sea.

Somewhere in the first night, he started to lose them faster.

He felt them go. Leaves. Not counting. He couldn’t count. He just felt them, one at a time, at the edges of him, letting go. Like lights going out in windows, very far away, in a town he couldn’t see.

Listen.

He listened.

On the second day, he heard the water.

He almost didn’t. It was so slow. It was so slow that he thought, at first, that it was him. His own sap. The very last of it, going round. And then he thought it was nothing, the way you think a sound in the night is nothing. And then he stopped thinking, the way he’d learned to stop on floor 9, slow like a tree, and just listened, and it was there.

Down. Far down. Further than the rings. Under all of it.

Water.

Not brine. Not the slurry under the crust. Something else. Deeper. A great flat dark body of it, lying under the whole of the Salt like a sheet of glass under a tablecloth. Still. Almost still. And moving. So slowly that a year was a finger’s width. West. It was going west. It had been going west for a thousand years, a finger’s width a year, under the salt, in the dark, all the way across.

The last of the sea.

It hadn’t dried. Not all of it. It had gone down. When the sun had taken the top of it and left the salt behind, the last of it had sunk, under its own salt, out of the light, and gone on. Quietly. The way the sea goes. It was still going. It didn’t know it had stopped being a sea. Nobody had told it.

He listened to it for a long time. He didn’t know how long.

And it wasn’t salt.

That was the thing he understood, slowly, the way you understand a thing you’ve been told in a dream. The salt was above it. All the salt. It had come up out of the water, the way cream comes up out of milk, and lain on top, and dried, and kept on drying, a thousand years of rings. And the water underneath, the water that had given it up, was what was left when the salt had gone. Fresh. Or nearly. Old and cold and dark and very nearly fresh.

Oh, he thought. There’s water. There’s all the water in the world. It’s just under the salt.

He wanted to tell Higu. He couldn’t move.

He listened.

On the third day, he heard them.

He’d gone down to the water. Not with his roots; they weren’t long enough; they’d never be long enough. With the listening. With whatever it was that had gone into the north tree’s rings on floor 9 and come out with three hundred years of a cone and a campfire. It went down past his roots, past the rings, down to the dark flat sheet of the last of the sea, and lay on it, and listened along it.

And there was something in it.

Not one thing. Many. Small. Too many to count. Lying in the water, and in the salt just above it, in the last few rings, the oldest ones, the ones from when the sea was still a sea. Tiny. Hard. Still.

Seeds.

He knew them the way he’d known the cones on floor 9. Waiting. He’d felt thousands of cones under the Grow at the north tree’s foot, and he’d said it to nobody but his party: they’re waiting. These were waiting too. A thousand years. Blown out over the sea from some shore nobody remembered, when there was a shore. Fallen in the water. Sunk. And then the sea had dried over them, and the salt had covered them, and kept them. The salt kept everything. Tullah had said so, in Seep. The salt keeps what it covers. You find things out there in the salt that went in before my mother’s mother’s mother, and they’re just the way they went in.

It had kept them.

Not dead. He knew dead. Dead was still something, but it was finished. These weren’t finished. They were the other thing. The thing the north tree’s cones had been, for a thousand years, lying in the dark under the canopy, waiting for a fire that never came.

Asleep, he thought.

There was a forest under the Salt. Asleep. A thousand years of seeds, in the salt, over the last of the sea, all the way across. Every kind. Grass and reed and thorn and things he didn’t have names for. Waiting for something. He didn’t know what. Water, probably. Fresh water. From above. Where they could reach it.

Plant something where nothing has grown.

Oh, Gigi, he thought. Oh, Gigi, there’s so much.

And then, under all of it, from very far off, from the west, from somewhere so deep and so far that he thought at first it was the water itself, he felt something else.

One beat.

Slow. Heavy. Like a door shutting in a house a very long way away.

And then nothing. For a long time. He lay on the dark water with his listening and waited, the way you wait for thunder after lightning, counting. He counted to a number he didn’t have a word for.

And then another.

Something’s alive, he thought. And then: no. Not alive. Not dead. The other thing. The thing the salt did. Kept. Something very big, out in the west, under the salt, kept whole. With a heart in it that beat once in an hour. Or once in a day. Or once in a year. He didn’t know. He couldn’t tell. He only knew it was there, and it was big, and it wasn’t asleep, not the way the seeds were asleep, and he didn’t want to listen to it any more.

He took his listening away from it, very carefully, the way you’d step back from a sleeping dog.

And came back up. Through the water. Through the rings. Through the slurry and the crust. Into himself, standing in the dip in the salt with his root-foot gone into the white and his eyes shut and the sun on his face.

He opened his eyes.

It was dusk. Gold. The white had gone gold to the end of the world.

And the windows were there.

He read them all. Twice.

Then he tried to take his foot out of the salt, and couldn’t, and fell over.

Chris caught him.

Of course Chris did. I’ll pull you out by the antlers. He didn’t. He got there first, out of nowhere, the way Chris always got there, and got his arms round Maigo from behind as he went over, and held him. And Maigo’s root-foot was still in the salt. It was stuck. The roots had gone too deep and too dry and they wouldn’t come back up, and his foot was held in the crust like a post in concrete, and he was hanging off it in Chris’s arms.

“Ina,” said Chris. Very fast. Very low.

Ina knelt. He didn’t need telling. He put his hand flat on the crust round Maigo’s foot, and pulled his mask down, and Maigo felt the fire go into the salt. Not burning. Warming. White-blue, very gentle, round and round the foot, the way you’d warm a frozen lock with your hands. The salt cracked. Softened. Let go, a little.

“Slowly,” whispered Gigi, kneeling on his other side. “Slowly, slowly, let them come, don’t pull them—“

They came. One at a time. The fine white roots, back up out of the crust, dry and brittle and grey, and Gigi held his foot in both her hands and talked to it the whole time, under her breath, the way she talked to her bramble, and the roots came back into his toes, and the toes closed, and the foot came out.

He lay in Chris’s arms on the salt and looked up.

At Niko’s face. At Winter’s. At Ina’s, with the mask pushed up again, very still. At Gigi’s. At Hyvred, behind them, with her hand over her mouth.

They were all looking at his antlers.

He couldn’t feel them. He reached up. It took a long time. His arm didn’t want to. He got his fingers under the hood and found the two little branches and went along them, twig by twig, the way he did every morning. Counting.

He found one.

One leaf. Near the top. On the left. Where the first one had come off, on the first day, at the foot of the grey rock by the door.

He held it between his finger and thumb.

“Hi,” he said.

His voice was a crack. It went down into the salt. Nobody cared.

“Maigo,” said Winter. And her voice wasn’t steady, which it always was. “Maigo, what did you—“

“There’s water,” said Maigo.

They all went still.

“Under it,” said Maigo. “All of it. The whole Salt. It’s not dried. It went down. It’s still there. Under the rings. It’s going west. A finger a year.” He breathed. It hurt. “It’s nearly fresh. It gave all its salt up. It’s just under.”

Nobody said anything.

“And there’s a forest,” said Maigo. He turned his head and looked at Gigi. “Gigi. In the salt. Over the water. Asleep. A thousand years of seeds. Every kind. Waiting.” He was crying. He didn’t have any water to cry with. It came anyway. “There’s so much. There’s so much, Gigi.”

Gigi had both her hands over her mouth.

“And something else,” said Maigo. Quieter. “In the west. Big. Kept. With a heart that goes once in an hour. Or slower.” He shut his eyes. “Tell Higu. I didn’t like it.”

He didn’t remember the next part. They told him after.

They told him that Chris carried him back to the camp, a hundred metres, over the salt, softly, in the gold dusk, with Winter on one side and Niko on the other and Ina walking behind with the pot of earth in both hands. That everybody came. That they laid him on the first sled, on the wood, and he didn’t move.

That Rachael knelt by him and put her hands on him, and Deston said no.

They told him that afterwards. That Deston said no, very quietly, and Rachael looked up at him, and Deston said, not on your own. Not here. Not ever again. That’s the whole point of me.

And put his hands out.

The gold threads. Maigo had seen them on every floor. Deston’s Link. Thin and warm, like sunlight through a crack in a door, going out from Deston’s hands to everybody. What one of you has, all of you may share. He’d seen them on floor 1 in the Fold, and on every floor since, wherever people were fighting. Going out to the people in the fight.

They went out to everybody.

All of them. Thirty-five threads, from Deston’s hands, through the dusk, gold, to every person in the camp. Into Rachael’s hands on Maigo’s chest. Into Kei’s. Into Xola’s, and Newish’s, and Malcolm’s, and Yan’s, all the healers, kneeling round the sled. And into everyone else. Chris. Niko. Winter. Ina, with the pot. Gigi. Hyvred. Gabe. Thea. Ice.

What one of you has.

Rachael healed him.

And it came out of her. Of course it did. On the Salt, it came out of the healer. That was the rule.

But it came out of all of them.

Through the threads. A little. A cup from each. Thirty-five cups. They told him they felt it, every one of them, a sort of tiredness, all at once, like the moment at the end of a long day when you sit down and realise you’ve been standing for hours. Calvin said he yawned. Gabe said it was like giving blood, but somebody else was holding the needle. Ina said it felt like being asked for something, and saying yes before you’d heard what.

Maigo didn’t feel any of it. He was asleep.

But he woke, on the morning of the three hundred and thirty-second day, lying on his back on the sled in the grey light, and reached up under his hood without thinking, the way he did every morning, and found the two little branches, and went along them.

One leaf. Near the top. On the left.

And beside it, so small he almost missed it, so small it was hardly anything, a bud.

Tight. Pale. Curled, like the ferns in the Down.

He lay on his back on the sled and held it very gently between his finger and thumb and didn’t breathe.

Ice was the first person he saw.

He was sitting on the end of the sled with his back to Maigo and his elbows on his knees, the way he’d sat on the deck of the Patience some nights, looking at the sea. Not doing anything. Just there. Thea was asleep on the next sled, curled up, with her lantern by her head.

“Ice,” said Maigo.

Ice turned round.

His face was very tired. More tired than Maigo had ever seen it. Grey round the eyes. He’d been one of the threads, Maigo understood. A cup, the same as everybody’s. It hadn’t been the cup that had made him look like that.

“Maigotron,” said Ice.

“There’s a bud,” said Maigo.

Ice looked at him for a long moment. Then he got up and came round the sled and crouched by his head, and Maigo held up his hand, slowly, and pulled back his hood, so Ice could see.

Ice looked at it.

“Yeah,” said Ice. Very quietly. “There is.”

He didn’t say anything else for a while. He crouched there in the grey light and looked at a bud the size of a grain of rice on the antler of a tree that had nearly come apart in the salt, and his face did the thing Maigo had seen it do on floor 9, at the foot of the north tree, by a seedling in the ash. I never saw one of these. Not once.

He didn’t say that this time.

He said, “You did it. The class.”

“Druid,” said Maigo.

“Druid,” said Ice. And something went over his face, fast, and was gone. “On floor 12.”

“Higu says it’s set where you finish.”

“Higu’s right,” said Ice. He was quiet. “It’s going to be enormous, Maigotron.”

“The class?”

“You,” said Ice.

Maigo didn’t know what to say to that. So he didn’t say anything. He lay on his back on the sled with his hood down and his one leaf and his one bud, and Ice crouched beside him, and the sun came up over the white behind them, the way it did, every morning, whether anybody was ready for it or not.

After a while Ice reached over to the end of the sled and picked up the clay pot. The bough-folk’s seedling pot, with the Highcrook earth in it. Ina had carried it back. Somebody had put it there.

He put it in Maigo’s hands.

“Gigi’s going to want some of that,” said Ice.

“What for?”

Ice looked out at the white.

“To start it,” he said. “She’s going to need something to start it in. You don’t plant in salt.”

Maigo held the pot.

He thought about a forest asleep under the Salt, over the last of the sea, all the way across. A thousand years of seeds. Every kind. Waiting for water, fresh water, from above, where they could reach it.

He thought about Gigi kneeling by his foot, talking to it, the way she talked to her bramble. Slowly, slowly, let them come.

“Ice,” he said.

“Yeah.”

“How do you know she’s going to want it?”

It came out before he could stop it. It went down into the salt. He heard it go.

Ice didn’t answer for a long time.

“I don’t,” said Ice eventually. “I just think she will.”

Maigo looked at him.

He was a tree. Trees listen. Trees remember. He’d spent three days inside the north tree on floor 9 learning how long a thing can be kept without anybody ever saying it out loud.

He looked at Ice’s face, very tired, very still, in the morning light, and he thought: that’s true. You don’t know. Not this time.

And then: this time.

He didn’t know where that came from. It came up through him from somewhere, the way water comes up a root. He didn’t say it. He didn’t even think it again. He put it away somewhere, very carefully, the way Corran had put his hand on a knot, and held it.

“Okay,” he said.

Ice looked at him. And for a second, Maigo thought, he knew. Knew that Maigo had put something away. And didn’t ask.

“Okay,” said Ice.

He got up. He put his hand on the top of Maigo’s head, between the antlers, very lightly, the way he had on the sled with the pot. And went back to Thea, and lay down on the salt beside her sled, and closed his eyes.

Maigo lay with the pot of earth on his chest and his one leaf and his one bud, and held the pot with both hands, and listened.

Under him, through the wood of the sled, through the salt, very faint, so faint he wasn’t sure, the last of the sea went west. A finger’s width a year.

And somewhere in it, in the dark, a thousand years of seeds turned over in their sleep, the way you do when somebody you don’t know yet says your name.
