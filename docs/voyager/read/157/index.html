Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 157

Borne

5,268 words · 23 min read

The screen came apart.

Not broken. Not split. It came apart the way a page comes apart when it’s very old and somebody finally turns it too fast. The carving went first. All the writing, all those lines too small and too close and too old to read, from the floor to the ceiling and from wall to wall. It lifted. Off the dark wood, in thin dark curls, like bark peeling off a tree in the heat. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every line of every Roll that had ever been cut into it, before the First Tell, before anyone in the city could remember. They came off the screen and turned in the air of the great hall like dead leaves in a wind, and the hall screamed.

And behind it, where the screen had been, something stood up.

He’d seen the Steward. Tall, robed, faceless, calm, with a book. He’d seen the Gatekeeper, and the Quiet, and the thing under the window at the top of the tower, the first time, that had had no face at all.

This was all of them.

It was grey. Robed, the way the clerks were robed, in long plain grey, but the robe went up, and up, past the high windows, into the dark under the roof, and its hood brushed the beams. There was no face in the hood. Just a smooth pale nothing, like the Quiet, like the Steward, but so big that looking at it made his eyes water. It had been sitting. For longer than Tell had been a city. On a seat he couldn’t see, behind a screen, reading. And it stood now, slowly, the way a very old man stands, unfolding, and the peeling lines of the screen turned round it in the air.

In one hand it held a pen.

Grey. As long as a spear.

[ The Magistrate · Floor Guardian ]

Alea, on his shoulder, had gone so still he couldn’t feel her.

“Ice,” she said. A whisper. “That’s the tower.”

“Both hands stand,” said the Magistrate.

It said it again. The same words. The ruling. But the voice wasn’t a page turning any more. It was every page in Tell turning at once.

“One record does not match.”

And then, very quietly, under it, the thing Thea had read under a window in the Countinghouse. The thing the tower had been trying to say since the slope in the snow on floor 10, when the Steward closed its book and said the count stands, and didn’t mean it.

“The count will be reconciled.”

He understood. All at once. Standing at the long table with the cord on his wrist and the slates face up in front of him and Thea’s shoulder against his arm.

It had read them. Every slate. A hundred and fifteen hands. And it had done what it had always done, behind its screen, since before the First Tell, for every boundary and dowry and debt and will. It had weighed them, fairly. And they’d come out true to both.

And it couldn’t hold that.

The law of Tell said both hands stood. The tower said one record didn’t match. And the thing behind the screen was both. It was the oldest thing on the hill, and the city had been built round it like a pearl round a bit of grit, and its whole self was two hands that agree, and its whole self was the count will be reconciled. And for the first time in all of time, those two things had come to the same long table and wouldn’t fit.

Something on the hill that doesn’t add up, Aldo’s grandmother had said. Something it couldn’t reckon.

He didn’t add up.

So it would do what the tower did.

It lifted the pen.

“Shields—“ said Drew.

They were already moving. He felt them. Behind him and either side, the way he’d felt them on the Salt under the beak, eight of them, without being told. Gabe first, on his right, Hearthwall up and angled. Azac on the left. Jared, Hako, Chris, Salty, Chi. Drew with nothing in his hands. A wall, between him and the thing with the pen, the way they’d stood on the fifth wall on floor 10 with their shields overlapping like the scales of a fish. Every shield covering the one on its left.

Thea didn’t step in front of him.

He felt her not do it. He felt her whole body want to, at his left shoulder, and not. I can promise there won’t be nobody else there. They go in front. Of both of us. She stayed where she was. At his side. With the lantern up.

Kneel, said everything in him.

He knelt.

Sanctuary went out of him the way it always did now, from his chest, along the threads, warm, into all of them. Where you stand, they are safe. Where they stand, so are you. He felt them stand differently. All thirty-five. And he felt it come back. Rachael’s cool. Xola’s water. Deston’s gold.

And the Magistrate brought down the pen.

It didn’t strike the wall.

He’d braced for it. Everybody had. Eight shields on the slope, Hearthwall in front, ready for a blow like the Preserved’s beak, ready to turn it. Not a wall. A door.

It wasn’t a blow.

The great grey pen came down through the air of the great hall, through the turning dark curls of the screen’s old writing, slowly, the way a pen comes down onto a page. And it drew a line.

Just a line. In the air. Grey. From high up near the roof, down and across, steady and patient, the way you’d strike a line through something in a book. And the line went through the shields as if they weren’t there. Through Hearthwall, and Gabe’s arm behind it, without touching either. Through Azac’s. Through the air between Thea’s shoulder and his.

Through him.

He felt it go in. Not pain. Nothing. A cold, flat, quiet nothing, entering at his left shoulder and going down and across his chest, the way the grey of the Countinghouse hall went on and on without a mark. And then out the other side.

And then the grey.

It came up out of the line. Where the line had gone into him. He saw it on his own hand, on the floor in front of him, where he was kneeling. His fingers going grey. Not pale. Grey. The grey of the clerks’ robes and the Steward’s face and the pages of the Count’s book. Flat. Smooth. Like a picture of a hand. Spreading up his wrist, past the cord, up his arm.

He couldn’t move it.

He tried. He told his hand to move, and it didn’t. He told his legs to stand, and they didn’t. He was kneeling on the floor of the great hall of the Assize in front of the long table, and he couldn’t get up, and the grey was coming up his arms and down his legs and across his chest from the line, and everywhere it went, he went still.

“ICE—“

Alea.

He’d never heard her like that. Not once. Not in ten years and four hundred and seven days. She was screaming. On his shoulder, in his ear, high and thin and terrible.

“It’s striking the first one,” she said. “Ice. It’s striking the first one. It’s striking—” Her voice broke up. Went grey. Came back. “It’s striking ME. I’m the first one. I’m all that’s left of it, I’m the record, I’m—”

He tried to turn his head to look at her. He couldn’t.

He felt her flicker. On his shoulder. The warm small weight of her, the only thing that came back, going light. Going thin. Going grey at the edges.

“Alea—“

“It’s the line,” said Alea. Very far away. “On the page. In the book. It’s drawing it through the left page. Through the first year. Through all of them. Through—”

And she stopped.

He was on the floor.

He didn’t know when he’d gone over. He was lying on his side on the flags in front of the long table, with his cheek on the cold stone and his arms grey to the shoulder and his legs grey to the hip and the grey coming up his throat. He could see the floor. A slate, face down, knocked off the table. His own grey hand, near his face, with the cord round the wrist.

He couldn’t move it.

And in front of his eyes, faint, the way windows always came, a window.

Now.

He looked at it.

He couldn’t do anything else.

He heard them before he saw them.

Thea first. Above him. On her knees. He couldn’t see her face. He could see the lantern, on the floor beside his head, and the light in it lying flat against the glass toward him, so hard it looked like it was trying to get out.

“Ice,” she said. Not screaming. Very steady. Pitched to carry, the way she pitched it for a spell. “Ice. Can you hear me.”

He couldn’t answer.

“He can hear you,” said somebody. Rachael. Close. “He can hear. His eyes are moving. Thea, he can hear—” And then Rachael’s hands. On his back. On the line, where it had gone in. He felt them land, the way he’d felt them land on the heap in the Down. Cool. Steady. Pressing.

And then he felt her pull.

He’d seen her do it once. On floor 10. On the fifth wall, in the snow, with Dennis’s arm going white from wrist to elbow and the cold that couldn’t be healed by anything. What you cannot heal, you may carry. He felt it now. Rachael’s hands on his back, and something going out of him into them. Not much. A finger’s width. The grey. Out of his back, where the line had gone in. Into her.

“Rachael—“ Dennis. Somewhere. “Rach, your hands—”

“I know.” She didn’t stop. “It’s cold. It’s the same as the Rimed. It’s the same kind.” A breath. “It’s not nothing. It’s grey. It’s—it’s being crossed out. Dennis, it feels like being crossed out—”

She pulled again.

He felt a little of the grey go. A little. From his back. And felt it go into her, and climb her fingers, the way the Rimed’s cold had climbed them.

It wasn’t enough. He could feel it wasn’t enough. The grey in him was coming up his throat faster than she could take it, and the line in the air above them, the one the pen had drawn, was still there, hanging, grey, and getting darker. Like ink drying.

“The book,” said Thea.

He heard her stand up.

“The book,” she said again. Louder. To all of them. “It’s not him. It’s the book. The line’s in the book. It’s drawing it through the left page—”

He couldn’t see the table. He could only see the floor. But he heard her. He’d heard her read under windows for four hundred and seven days, and he’d never once heard her sound like this. Like somebody reading the last line of something, out loud, very fast, before the page turned.

“It’s striking the first record. Through him. Because he carries it. If the line goes all the way across the page, it won’t have been. Any of it. And he’s—” She stopped. “He’s in it. He’s the first hand. He won’t have been either.”

Somebody made a sound. He didn’t know who.

“Where’s the book?” said Drew.

“On the table.”

“That’s three metres—”

“Look at the floor,” said Chi. Calm. Very calm. The calmest he’d ever heard her. “Drew. Look at the floor.”

He couldn’t look. He didn’t need to. He could see it, at the edge of his eye, on the flags beside his cheek.

The grey wasn’t only in him.

It was coming out of the place where the screen had been. Across the floor of the great hall. Toward them. Like a tide coming in over the Shoals. Flat and smooth and pale, the grey of a blank page, sliding over the flags. And where it touched a slate that had fallen on the floor, the chalk on the slate went, and the slate was blank. And where it touched the leg of the long table, the wood went grey and smooth and still, and stayed. And it was coming up the legs of the long table, and over its edge, and onto the slates.

A hundred and fifteen slates, face up, in rows. With names on them. With it’s still Dec and he asked and man and he’s in my hand.

He watched the first row go blank.

“On,” said Gabe.

He felt Gabe kneel beside him. Felt the floor shake with it. And felt something big and warm and heavy go down flat on the flags beside him, close against his back.

Hearthwall.

“On,” said Gabe again. “Get him on. Now. Rach. Salty. Azac, the legs.”

They put him on it.

He felt them do it. Hands. A lot of hands. Rachael’s, cold, under his shoulders. Salty’s, big, under his knees. Azac’s. Somebody’s under his head, holding it, the way you’d hold a baby’s: Xola’s. He felt them lift him, and turn him, and lay him down on his back on Gabe’s shield.

It was warm.

He’d known it would be. It does not go cold. He’d stood next to it on the fifth wall and felt it like a stove a metre off. He’d felt Rachael put her white hands on its boss and the cold run out of them like water. He’d never lain on it.

It was like lying on a hearthstone. Warm all the way through, into his back, where the line had gone in. And the grey, where it touched the shield, stopped. He felt it stop. Not go away. Stop. Like ice on a pond that’s found a warm spring and can’t grow over it.

Above him, the roof of the great hall. The beams. The high windows. And turning in the air between him and the roof, the dark curls of the screen’s old writing, and the grey line the pen had drawn, darkening.

And the hood. A long way up. With no face in it. Looking down.

“Lift,” said Gabe.

They lifted.

He couldn’t move. He couldn’t see much. He was lying on his back on a shield, held up at shoulder height by he didn’t know how many hands, and the grey was in his arms and his legs and his throat, and every time he tried to tell his body to do anything, nothing happened.

He couldn’t walk.

Through what you cannot walk.

He couldn’t have walked three metres to save his life. He couldn’t have walked one. Between him and the long table there was a floor turning into a blank page, and he couldn’t walk on it, and nobody could, and they were carrying him onto it anyway.

He felt it when they stepped into the grey.

He felt it through the shield. Through their hands. Through the threads, all thirty-five, behind his breastbone. Gabe’s first. Gabe at the front, under the top edge of the shield, with his arms up and Hearthwall’s weight on his shoulders, stepping off the warm flags onto the grey. And the grey going up his legs. He felt Gabe’s thread go cold from the feet up, the way his own had. Felt Gabe not stop.

And Salty. And Azac. And the others, round the edges of the shield, under it, holding. Stepping into it. Going grey to the ankle. To the knee.

Not stopping.

You will not know if they can.

He didn’t. He lay on his back on Hearthwall and looked up at the roof and the hood with no face in it, and he didn’t know. He didn’t know if they could carry him three metres through a floor that was being crossed out. He didn’t know if they’d make it before the grey got to their hips, their chests, their throats. He didn’t know if Gabe’s legs would hold. He didn’t know if the line would finish across the page first.

He’d known everything. Every floor. Since floor 1. Where the doors were. What was behind them. Which rim. Always.

He didn’t know this.

He lay there and let them.

And the hall started saying names.

He didn’t know who began it. He thought, afterwards, it was Hawise. At the far end of the long table, with her hands on the wood and Alys’s hand on top of them, as the grey came up the table toward them. He heard her voice, a teller’s voice, the voice that read the dead from the Tellhouse steps at dusk, clear and slow and carrying.

“Tiger,” said Hawise.

And then, after a breath:

“Suzuyun. Yan.”

She was reading the right page. From memory. The page she’d written, in the Lantern Market, on the well’s lid, on the first day. In the order they’d come up.

“Salty. Wendy. Cursiffe.”

And the hall heard her.

He heard them hear. He heard the Low Town, on the benches, in their market clothes, with the grey coming across the floor toward their feet, hear a teller saying names in the evening-telling voice. The voice that meant nobody here goes without a name. And he heard them understand.

“Calvin,” said somebody on the benches. A woman. Loud.

“Rachael,” said somebody else.

“Hako.” “Gigi.” “The loud one!” “Calvin!”

And then all of them.

The great hall of the Assize filled up with names. Not in order. Not together. Two thousand people, from the benches and the window ledges and the doors and the square outside, shouting and saying and calling the names of thirty-six climbers they’d known for forty-nine days. The names off the right page. The names on the slates going blank on the long table. Hana. Drew. Higu. Deston. Xola. Tiger, the tiger, the one with the apple—

And where the names landed, the grey slowed.

He felt it. Through the threads. Gabe’s, at the front, cold to the hip, and somebody in the benches shouting GABE, the big one, the one who catches things, and the cold in Gabe’s thread stopping. Not going. Stopping. Holding where it was. Salty’s. Azac’s. And so our hero, somebody shouted, and laughed, and Azac’s thread stopped going grey at the knee.

Nobody here goes without a name.

And under the noise of it, close, at his left side, walking beside the shield with her hand on its rim, he heard Thea.

She wasn’t shouting. She was saying names too. Quietly. Steadily. In order. Not Hawise’s order. Not the order they’d come up the well in the spring.

The other order.

The one on the left page. The one in an old man’s happy shaking hand. In the autumn. Thirty-four names, one after another, under her breath, as she walked beside him through the grey, with her hand on the shield.

The same names. The same people. Nobody in the hall would ever know the difference. Two thousand people saying the names on the right page, and one person, very quietly, beside him, saying the names on the left.

Both records. Being said at once.

He heard her get to his own. Declan. And then hers. Althea.

And then, after a breath, so softly that nobody but him could have heard it, with her mouth almost against the rim of the shield, the thing that wasn’t on either page.

“And the thirty-four who were there before.”

Three metres.

He’d never known three metres could be so long. He’d crossed whole floors. He’d walked a hundred steps up a stair that went four hundred. He’d run across a bowl of gold glass on floor 6 toward one needle gone red.

Three metres, on his back, on a shield, through a floor being crossed out, not knowing.

He felt the shield tilt. Felt somebody’s knees go, at the back, and somebody else take the weight. Calvin’s voice, very close, somewhere under the left edge: I’ve got it, I’ve got it, I’ve GOT it, look at my back. Deston’s gold, coming up all thirty-five threads at once and running along them under the grey like warm water under ice, the way it had on the Long Night when he gave everything he had. Azac’s voice, somewhere: the whole line takes it. Lightly. The whole line—

He felt it happen. The grey in each of them. Thirty-five threads. Thinning. Spreading out. Because it wasn’t in any one of them any more. It was in all of them. A little each. What strikes the line, the whole line takes, and lightly.

The shield stopped.

He was at the table.

They held him up. Level with it. He could see it now, turning his eyes, the only part of him that would turn.

The long table. The slates on it, half of them blank. The grey book, open, in the middle. The left page and the right. Thirty-four names in Aldo’s running hand. Thirty-six in Hawise’s.

And across the left page, slowly, a grey line.

From the top. Through the first year. Through thirty-four. Through Aldo’s names, one by one, and the cries, and one, and Declan. Chose, going down and across the page the way the pen had gone down and across the air. It was two-thirds of the way. It was getting darker. Above it, very high up, the great grey pen was moving in the air in time with it. Patient. Steady.

Striking one.

Thea let go of the shield.

She came round to the head of it. To his hand. His right hand, lying on the warm iron beside him, grey to the shoulder, with the cord round the wrist. She picked it up.

He couldn’t feel it. He saw her do it. He saw her take his grey hand in both of hers and turn it over. And reach into her coat. And take out her own pen.

The one she’d had since floor 1. That wrote in light.

She put it into his hand.

She closed his fingers round it. One at a time. With hers. And kept her hand there, round his, holding it shut.

“You’re the first hand,” she said. Quietly. Only to him. “It’s yours. It’s always been yours. I just signed for it.”

And she moved his hand to the book.

He didn’t write it. He couldn’t. He couldn’t move a finger.

She wrote it. With his hand. Her fingers round his fingers round her pen. On the left page. In the margin. Beside one, beside Declan. Chose, beside her own line in amber: Told. — Althea.

Under it. In a hand that wasn’t hers and wasn’t his. Something in between. Slow and wobbling and pressed hard, the way a child writes when somebody’s guiding their hand.

Told.

And then, because it was the law of Tell, nothing’s told unless two hands write it the same, and because it was his, and because she knew:

— Declan.

The amber lay on the grey page. Under hers. Two lines. Two hands. Hers. His.

The grey line coming down across the page touched it.

And stopped.

The pen stopped.

High up, in the air of the great hall. The great grey pen, as long as a spear, in the hand of the thing with no face. It stopped moving. In the middle of a stroke.

And shook.

He’d seen that. On a slope in the snow on floor 10, at my name’s Drew. At thirty-six, when thirty-five people said his name for him. At a lectern in a hall longer than a hill, over two small lines of amber. A grey hand on a grey page, not knowing what to do.

It shook for a long time.

And the hall, which had been shouting names, went quiet. Two thousand people. All at once. Looking up.

“Three hands,” said the Magistrate.

Not every page in Tell. Just one. Turning. In an empty room.

“Two that were there. And the one it is.”

A long silence.

“Told.”

And then the voice changed. He heard it change. Not louder. Not anything. But for the first time in all the time he’d heard it, it didn’t sound like a page.

It sounded like somebody very old asking a question they’d been waiting a very long time to ask.

“Where did you come from?”

He couldn’t move his mouth. He could feel it, now, a little, the grey going back down his throat, slowly, from the place where the pen had stopped. He couldn’t speak.

Thea answered for him.

She stood at the head of the shield with her hand still round his on the pen, and looked up at the hood with no face in it, and said, quite steadily, in front of the whole of Tell:

“Them.”

And then, after a moment:

“Same as me.”

The Magistrate looked down at them for a long time.

Then it lowered the pen.

Not to strike. It lowered it the way a teller lowers a pen when the door-roll’s done for the day. And it sat down. Slowly. The way a very old man sits. Folding. Down, and down, until its hood was below the high windows again, and below the beams, and gone. And the dark curls of the screen’s old writing, turning in the air of the great hall, came down out of the air like leaves after a wind and settled, all of them, back onto the dark wood. Line by line. Where they’d been.

Not quite where they’d been. He saw it, turning his eyes. Across the bottom of the screen, along the floor, where there’d been nothing before, there was a new line. Carved. Fresh. Pale against the dark.

He couldn’t read it. He didn’t need to.

Two hands.

[ Floor Guardian defeated · thirty-six Players ]

The grey went.

Out of the floor. Out of the table. Off the slates, which came back, one by one, with their chalk, it’s still Dec and he asked and man. Out of Gabe’s legs, and Salty’s, and Azac’s. Out of Rachael’s hands, which were white to the elbow, and which she put flat on the boss of Hearthwall under his back, without asking, and held there while the warmth ran into them.

Out of him.

He felt it go. From the top. His throat. His chest, where the line had gone in. His arms. His hands. His right hand, in Thea’s, round her pen. He felt his fingers come back. Felt them close on the pen on their own.

And on his shoulder, a weight.

Small. Warm. Coming back from very far away, the way the sound had come back to the Salt. Faint at first. Then not.

“Ice,” said Alea.

He couldn’t speak yet. He moved his head. A little. A tip, to the left. Slowly.

“I’m here,” said Alea. Her voice was shaking. He’d never heard it shake. “I’m here. It stopped. It was through the first year and through the names and through the cries and through one and it was at you and it stopped. It stopped on your line.” A sound that might have been a laugh, or something else. “I’m here. I’ve got all of it. Every second. I didn’t let go.”

He tipped his head again.

Heard.

They put him down.

Not on the floor. They didn’t put the shield down. They stood there, all of them, round the long table, in the great hall of the Assize, with two thousand people silent on the benches, and held him up. On Hearthwall. At shoulder height. As if, now they’d done it, they didn’t know how to stop.

He lay on his back on Gabe’s shield and looked up at them.

He could see their faces now. He could turn his head. Gabe at the top, with his arms up and Hearthwall’s edge on his shoulders, grey gone from his legs, crying and not looking down. Salty at his knees. Azac. Calvin, under the left edge, with his face screwed up and his open hand flat against the iron. Rachael with her white hands on the boss. Xola holding his head. Deston with his hand flat on his chest and his gold in all of them. Drew, at the end of the table, with his empty hands at his sides, very still, looking at the screen. Hyvred, and Chris, and Yan with Suzu round his neck. All of them. Thirty-five.

And Thea at the head of the shield, with her hand round his, round her pen.

And behind them, in his rush chair, on his left, facing the hall, an old man with a writing-board on his knees, who’d always said he’d run.

He’d been carried.

Through what he couldn’t walk. Not knowing.

They had.

He read them lying on his back, at shoulder height, in the middle of all of them.

What you cannot carry, they will.

He’d carried everything. Every floor. Every name. Ten years and four hundred and seven days.

He let it go.

Not all of it. He didn’t know how. Not yet. But something. He felt it go out of him, up through the shield, into all their hands at once. Thirty-five threads, and Thea’s, and the old man’s on his left. A little each.

Lightly.

Hawise cut the cord.

She came down the side of the long table with a teller’s little knife, and Alys a step behind her in grey, without a book. And she took his left wrist, where it lay on the warm iron, and slid the knife under the grey cord, and cut it. One stroke.

She held it up. For the hall.

“Told,” said Hawise.

And the whole of Tell said it back.
