Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 115

The High Side

4,125 words · 18 min read

The first time, they’d come up the high side in spring.

He remembered it the way he remembered most things from the first time: in pieces, very clearly, with the gaps between them dark. Meltwater. That was the main thing. The whole throat of the pass running with it, snow going grey and rotten and slumping off the rocks into streams, and the streams going down past their boots toward the Bastion, loud, everywhere, the sound of a mountain thawing. Thirty-four of them, in a line, in the mud. Nobody talking. Somebody’s boot coming off in a drift and Gabe swearing about it for twenty minutes. A bird, once, high up, the first one he’d seen on floor 10.

No Rimed. The Winter had gone back up behind the Gate for the year, the way it always did when the snow went. The throat had been empty except for them.

And no Steward. He’d thought about that every night for fourteen nights. In ten years, he’d never once seen it. Not on this floor, not on any floor. Nothing had ever come down a pass on top of the snow with a book and said the count. The first time, they’d just walked up to the Gate in the spring sun and the thing in front of it had let them come.

It wasn’t spring now.

It was the coldest morning he’d ever walked into.

The throat of the pass started a little way above the fifth wall, where the slope narrowed and steepened and the rock came in on both sides, black and sheer, close enough that you could have thrown a stone from one wall to the other. It went up like that for a long way. A crooked stair of snow and rock between two cliffs, climbing into the mountains, with the sky a thin strip of grey at the top and the wind coming down it from the high side so hard and so cold that it was like walking into a wall of water.

They went up in a line. Thirty-six. Drew at the front, then the tanks. The healers in the middle. Thea with the lantern somewhere just behind him. Gabe on his right, wherever the path was wide enough for two, and right behind him where it wasn’t.

Nobody talked. You couldn’t, in that wind. And there was nothing to say.

And about an hour up, round a bend in the throat where the cliffs drew back a little and the snow lay flat, he saw the first of them.

A Rimed. Standing at the side of the path.

Not coming at them. Standing. Against the cliff on the left, in the snow, very still, the way the hold-folk stood on the wall. Taller than any of them, packed snow and blue ice built round a suit of old grey armour, with its long arms hanging at its sides and its hollow helmet turned toward the path.

Toward them.

Drew stopped. Everyone stopped behind him. Gabe’s shield came up across Ice’s side without either of them thinking about it.

The Rimed didn’t move.

“It’s not coming,” said Drew. Low. Over his shoulder. He had his hands empty at his sides. He’d been carrying them that way since the morning in the snow; Ice hadn’t seen him put the knuckles back on once. “It’s just standing there.”

“Hyv,” said Ice.

Hyvred came up beside him with her glasses off. She looked at it for a long time.

“Nothing in it,” she said. “Same as always. Nothing at all.” She was quiet. “It’s not trying to do anything. It’s just—“ She stopped. “Watching.”

They went on. Past it. Close enough to touch. It didn’t move. Its helmet turned as they went past, slowly, following them, the hollow inside it dark, and then they were round the next bend.

And there were more.

All the way up the throat. On both sides. In the snow against the cliffs, in ranks, one behind the other, standing still with their arms at their sides and their old helmets turned to the path. Ten. Twenty. A hundred. More than they’d ever fought on any night on the wall. More than he’d known there were. Every Rimed that had ever come down the pass to the fifth wall, Ice thought, and every one that never had. Lining the high side of the pass from the fifth wall to wherever the top was, like a crowd lining a road. Watching thirty-six people go past.

“Oh, I hate that,” whispered Newish.

Nobody laughed.

“It’s an audience,” said Azac, very quietly, somewhere behind him. Not narrating. Just saying it. “It’s an audience, Salty.”

“There’s no audience,” said Salty.

“There’s always an audience,” said Azac. “It’s a device.”

Ice walked between them with his lamp shield on his arm and Gabe on his right and didn’t look at any of them. He’d learned that on floor 6, in a bowl of gold glass. You didn’t look. You kept your eyes on the path and the people in front of you and you didn’t give it your face.

But he could feel them looking.

Not the Rimed. They were empty. There was nothing in them to look with. It was something behind them. Something using them the way you’d use a window. A hundred hollow helmets, all turned toward the path, and behind every one of them the same attention. The same weight. The thing he’d felt on the parapet with Deston at dawn after the Long Night, at the back of his neck, like somebody watching you in a crowded room.

It had been looking since floor 8. It was looking now, from a hundred places at once.

“Your foot’s tapping,” said Alea.

“I’m walking.”

“I know. It’s tapping anyway.” She was quiet for a while, on his shoulder, where nobody could see her. “Ice. I’ve counted them.”

“How many?”

“Three hundred and twelve,” said Alea.

He nearly stopped walking.

“I counted twice,” said Alea. “I didn’t believe it either.”

He didn’t say anything. He kept walking. Three hundred and twelve hollow helmets, turned toward the path, on the high side of the pass, in the dark of the moon. He thought about a house on stilts at the edge of a lake on floor 8, kept and swept for three hundred and twelve years. A fire laid on a last morning and never lit.

It’s a number, he told himself. It’s just a number.

He didn’t believe that either.

They came out of the top of the throat a little before dusk.

The cliffs fell back all at once, on both sides, and the path came up over a lip of rock and snow, and they were at the top of the pass. A high flat saddle between two mountains, white and wide and open, with the sky enormous and grey above it and the wind going straight across it from somewhere further up. And across the far side of the saddle, from one mountain to the other, a wall.

Not stone. Ice. Black ice, old and clear and deep, like the ice at the bottom of a glacier that hasn’t seen the sun in a thousand years, going up out of the snow higher than the fifth wall. Higher than any wall. Smooth as glass. You could see down into it, a long way, like looking into deep water, and it went down further than you could see.

And in the middle of it, the Gate.

Two doors. Grey. Iron, or something that looked like iron, and wasn’t, because nothing iron could have been that tall. Thirty metres. More. Set into the black ice side by side, shut, with a line between them so fine you could only just see it. They didn’t have handles. They didn’t have hinges. They had a single great ring each, at about the height of a man’s head if the man was the size of a house, iron, and in front of each ring—

He’d forgotten. He’d seen it the first time, in the spring sun, and he’d forgotten. Or he’d remembered it smaller.

The Gatekeeper was standing in front of the doors with one hand on each ring.

It was armour. That was the first thing anybody would see. A suit of armour, grey, old, plain, without any decoration on it at all, the kind of armour every Rimed on the high side was wearing a piece of. Except whole. All of it. Helm and breastplate and gauntlets and greaves, every plate, standing up, the size of the doors. As if this were the first one. As if every other piece of armour on the high side of the pass were a copy of a piece of this. It stood with its back to the doors and its gauntlets on the two great rings and its helm bowed, and it didn’t move, and there was snow on its shoulders, a lot of it, as if it had been standing there all winter without once lifting its head.

The first time, it had lifted its head when they came over the lip. He remembered that now. It had lifted its head and looked at thirty-four people in the spring sun, and let them come.

It didn’t lift its head this time.

Because something was standing in front of it.

The Steward was at the foot of the Gate, in the snow. Very straight. With its book against its chest. Waiting.

It didn’t come to meet them. It stood there in front of the Gatekeeper’s feet, small beside it, the way a clerk stands small in front of the building he works in, and watched them come across the saddle in the last of the light. Thirty-six people in a line, in the snow, with the wind going across them.

Drew stopped them about forty metres off.

Nobody said anything. They stood there in the snow and looked at it. At the black wall of ice going up, and the two grey doors, and the giant armour with its hands on the rings, and the tall grey figure with no face standing in front of it all with a book.

Behind them, all down the throat of the pass, three hundred and twelve hollow helmets had turned to face the saddle.

“Okay,” said Drew. Very calmly. With his hands open. “Okay. Positions. Like we said.”

They’d said it a hundred times. In the barracks, at the long table, with Higu’s map of the pass and the rock with ALL OR NONE on it. They didn’t need to say it again. They moved. Tanks to the front, in a line, shields up, overlapping, the way the hold-folk did it, every shield covering the man on its left. Gabe on Ice’s right, Hearthwall’s warm edge across him. Azac on Gabe’s right. Chris. Salty. Jared with the thorn still bolted through his boss. Hako. Chi. And Drew at the end of the line with nothing in his hands at all.

The healers behind. The ranged behind them. Thea with the lantern, behind Ice, where he’d asked her to be. Where she’d said she’d be.

And Ice in the line. Not in front of it. In it.

The dark came up from the snow. On this floor it came down from the sky, the way it was supposed to; but up here, on the saddle, with the mountains on both sides, the light went out of the snow first, from the bottom, and climbed. The black ice went blacker. The grey doors went dim. The sky went from grey to the deep blue it went before it went black.

And there was no moon.

He looked up. He couldn’t help it. The sky over the top of the pass was enormous and black and empty, and then, as the last light went, stars. Hundreds. Thousands. More than on any floor. Coming out over the Gate one after another until the whole sky was dusty with them.

No moon at all.

At the foot of the Gate, the Steward opened its book.

“The count,” said the Steward.

He felt it go out across the saddle. At everyone’s shoulder. Calm, level, filling in a form. He felt the fear come up out of thirty-five people in the line behind him and beside him, the way it had come up on the wall every morning for six mornings. The cold, from the middle, going out into their arms and legs, looking for something to grip.

And he felt it not find it.

Not all at once. Not everywhere. But he felt it, through the threads behind his breastbone, the way he’d felt every hit on the Long Night. The fear going into thirty-five people and looking for something to hold. And finding Drew, at the end of the line, with his hands open, breathing. Hold nothing. Then there’s nothing for fear to hold. And finding Chi, behind the tanks, already saying breathe, slowly, before it had even finished landing. And finding thirty-five people who’d stood on a wall six mornings running and said their own names back at it, and knew they could.

They didn’t freeze.

A few of them, for a second. He felt Calvin lock and then let go. He felt Rachael go stiff and then breathe out through her nose, the way she did when somebody was being stupid. He felt Newish freeze, and then, very clearly, through the thread, decide not to.

Nobody stayed frozen.

The Steward’s hand stopped on the page.

It stood there at the foot of the Gate with its white face bent over its book, for a long moment, and didn’t count. And Ice thought, very clearly, the way you think in the half second before something: it noticed that.

Then it counted.

“One,” said the Steward.

“Newish,” said Newish.

It didn’t stop. It went on through the names the way it had gone through them on the wall, steadily, as if it couldn’t hear them. Two, and Calvin. Three, and Hana. All the way along. The number, and the name over it. And he stood in the line and listened to thirty-five people say who they were on the top of a mountain in the dark of the moon, in front of a wall of black ice and a giant with its hands on a gate, and felt something come up in his chest that he didn’t have a word for.

“Thirty-five.”

“Hyvred.”

The pause.

The long one. The one it took every time. The whole saddle went silent. The wind dropped. Behind them, all down the throat, three hundred and twelve helmets waited.

“Thirty-six,” said the Steward.

It landed on him like a hammer.

It always had. On the wall, every morning, he’d felt it. Not fear. He’d never once been afraid of it; he didn’t know why. Something else. A weight, coming down on him, exact and heavy and specific, the way a stamp comes down on a page. This one. Here. He felt it come down now, on the top of the pass, harder than it ever had, so hard his knees nearly went.

He didn’t let them.

It came up in front of his face. And in front of everybody’s, he knew. Thirty-six windows, small and blue in the starlight.

And then everybody else’s went.

All of them. At once. He felt it through the threads. Thirty-five windows closing, the way windows close when you’ve read them. And his didn’t.

His stayed. In front of his face. Irregular. And under it, in grey, small, where he’d never been able to read it and Thea always could, he could see a line.

He could read it.

He’d never been able to read the words under the words. That was Thea’s. That was Source, and he didn’t have it, and he’d never needed it. And he stood on the top of the pass with Gabe’s shield across his side and read the grey line under his own window as clearly as if somebody had written it for him in chalk.

One record does not match.

And under that. Another line. One he knew, without being told, that nobody else had ever seen. Not Thea. Not anybody.

Record found: 1 of 2.

He stood there.

He didn’t move. He couldn’t have. Not fear. Something much colder and much quieter than fear. He stood in the line with the window in front of his face and the grey lines under it and read them, and read them, and read them.

Record found: 1 of 2.

“Ice,” said Alea. Very small. On his shoulder. “Ice. I can see it too.”

“I know.”

“I’ve never seen it. Not once. In ten years and two hundred and eighty-four days. That’s not a window. That’s the thing under the windows.” Her voice had gone very strange. “It’s not hiding it any more.”

At the foot of the Gate, the Steward closed its book.

It didn’t hold it against its chest the way it always had. It held it out. In front of it. Closed, flat, on both its long pale hands, like somebody offering a document across a desk to be signed.

“Thirty-six presented,” said the Steward. “Thirty-six recorded. One record does not match.”

Behind him, he heard somebody breathe in. Thea. He knew her breath. She’d read it too. Of course she had.

“The count will be reconciled,” said the Steward.

And behind it, the Gatekeeper moved.

It didn’t lift its head. It moved its hands. On the rings. The great gauntlets, grey and old and snow-covered, turning, very slowly, on the two iron rings in the doors. And the doors moved.

Ice heard them. A sound like a mountain clearing its throat. Low and enormous and slow, up out of the black ice and the rock under it, through the snow, into his boots. The fine line between the two grey doors widened. A finger. A hand. And through it, out of it, across the saddle, came light.

Gold.

Not the cold blue of the starlight, or the white of the snow. Gold. Warm. The colour of a summer evening, coming out through the gap in the doors in a long thin blade and lying across the snow at their feet. And with it, warmth. He felt it on his face. Warm air, coming out of the Gate, smelling of grass. Of water. Of flowers.

Spring. On the far side. Waiting.

The doors stopped. Open a little way. A gap the width of a person. Gold light pouring through it across the saddle.

“Thirty-five may pass,” said the Steward.

Nobody moved.

He’d known it was coming. He’d known since the first morning on the wall. Thea had said it at the long table, out loud, with all of them listening. It’s going to try to make us leave one. He’d sat there and not said yes and not said no. He’d known exactly what it would sound like.

He hadn’t known it would sound so reasonable.

Thirty-five may pass. In that calm, level voice. At everyone’s shoulder. Like a clerk telling you your form was nearly complete and there was just one small thing. Gold light on the snow and the smell of grass coming through a gate, and on the far side spring, and the stair up, and floor 11, and everything after. Thirty-five people could just walk through. Right now. Into the warm.

And one could stay here, in the dark of the moon, with a window in front of his face that said 1 of 2. And be reconciled.

He didn’t look round. He didn’t need to. He could feel them, all thirty-five, through the threads, behind him and beside him. Every one of them looking at the gold light. Every one of them hearing it.

He waited.

He didn’t know what he was waiting for. Somebody to move, maybe. One of them to take a step toward the gold. He wouldn’t have blamed them. He’d have understood. He’d been on his own on the far side of things before. He’d done it for ten years and a hundred and fifty-three days. He could do it again. He’d stand here in the dark and they’d go through, and they’d be in the warm, and that was the trade, and he’d made worse ones, sitting round a fire on the ninety-ninth floor—

Gabe sat down.

He didn’t say anything. He just sat down. In the snow. Right where he was, in the line, on Ice’s right, with Hearthwall across his knees. He sat down cross-legged on the top of the pass in the dark with the gold light coming through the Gate at him, and folded his arms, and looked at the Steward.

And Azac sat down beside him.

And Chris. And Salty, with a grunt. And Jared. Hako. Chi, slowly, with Waddles. Drew, at the end of the line, sitting down in the snow with his empty hands on his knees.

And behind them, the healers. Rachael first, flat, the way she did everything. Dennis beside her. Deston, with his hand on his shirt pocket. Malcolm, Xola, Kei with Dose up and hissing at the Steward over his shoulder. Newish. Maigo, small and brown, with his root-foot on the snow.

And behind them, all of them. Hana with her crown on her knees. Aleaka. Milli with his tail round his feet. Yan and Salty and Wendy and Tiger and Cursiffe. Hyvred and Gigi and Bee. Winter and Ina and Niko. Xav. Rui Xiang. John. Calvin, last, the way he was always last in a vote, sitting down with a thump in the snow and saying, quite loudly, to nobody:

“None.”

Thirty-five people sitting in the snow on the top of a pass, in front of a gate with spring coming through it, in the dark of the moon. Not moving. Not going anywhere. With their arms folded and their weapons on their knees, looking at a tall grey thing with no face holding out a book.

And Ice, standing in the middle of them. The only one still on his feet.

“Sit down, Dec,” said Gabe. Without looking up. “You’re making the line look untidy.”

He sat down.

He didn’t decide to. His legs did it, the way they’d done it on a root under the seven on floor 9, and in the snow on the third level of the Bastion. He sat down in the snow between Gabe and Deston with the lamp shield across his knees and the window still in front of his face and his eyes so full he couldn’t see the Gate any more. Just gold. Gold light, smeared, and stars.

“None,” said Thea, behind him. Very quietly. Just to him.

The Steward didn’t move.

It stood at the foot of the Gate with the book held out in its two pale hands, offered, and the gold light coming through the gap behind it, and thirty-six people sitting in the snow in front of it. It stood there for a long time.

Then, very slowly, it lowered the book.

It held it against its chest again. The way it always had. And its white face, the face that wasn’t a face, turned. Not toward the line. Not toward the thirty-five.

Toward him.

It had never done that. Not on the wall, not once in six mornings. It had looked at the place where the hammer landed. It had never looked at him.

It looked at him now. Across forty metres of snow, in the starlight, with nothing to look with.

And then it spoke. And it didn’t speak at everyone’s shoulder.

It spoke at his.

Only his. He felt it. Right beside his ear, calm and level and very close, the way Alea spoke. The way nothing else in the tower had ever spoken to him in two lives. Not a window. Not a line of grey text. A voice. For him.

“Where did you come from?”
