Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 53

One Floor Up

4,239 words · 18 min read

“Tell him yourself,” said Nan Gully.

She was sitting on her upturned basket by the winch of the chain-ferry with her pipe, in the morning, with the gull on her hat and the blue island far off across the gap behind her. She didn’t look up from the rope she was splicing.

“I can’t.”

“Can’t,” said Nan Gully, “or won’t?”

He stood by the winch with a basket of fish on his back and Otta’s second barrel lid on his arm, and didn’t answer straight away. The wind came and went. Down on the bridges below Tether, somebody was singing.

“Not yet,” he said.

Nan Gully looked up then. Her sharp old eyes went over him, the way they had on the first day, and stopped at the place beside his head, where Alea was sitting on his shoulder in the shape of nothing anyone could see.

“Hm,” she said. She went back to her rope. “Go on, then. Say it again. Slow. I’m old.”

He said it again, slowly.

“There’ll be thirty-five of them. In two weeks. Maybe less. They’ll come through Tether, and Dennis will try to haggle with you.” He paused. “There’ll be a big one. Broad. Loud. He’ll be wearing a wolf-fur coat, even though it’s too warm for it. His name’s Gabe. Tell him: the bird comes down on whoever stands still. Tell him not to stand still. Tell him—“ He stopped. “Tell him to lean.”

“Lean,” said Nan Gully.

“He’ll know. Or he won’t, and Pell can teach him.” He swallowed. “And tell them all to take fish to the Kitecliff. And to kill the bird often.”

Nan Gully spliced her rope for a while.

“That’s three messages,” she said.

“I know.”

“Triple.” She held out a hand without looking at him. He put the silver in it. She counted it without looking either, and tucked it somewhere under the shawls. “This Gabe. He yours?”

He thought about how to answer that.

“He’s my brother,” he said. “Not by blood. Since I was fifteen.”

Nan Gully nodded slowly, as if that answered everything.

“And you’re not going to tell your brother yourself,” she said, “that the bird’ll take him off the Spindle if he stands like a door.”

“No.”

“Because?”

He looked out across the gap at the blue island, a long way off, with the iron cable sagging toward it over the cloud.

“Because if I say anything to him,” he said, “anything at all, he’ll come up a stair after me before he’s ready. And there’s things up there I haven’t met yet.”

Nan Gully was quiet for a long time. The gull on her hat looked at him. The winch creaked in the wind.

“My husband,” said Nan Gully at last, “went out on the Gullrock span one morning when I was thirty. Fixing a link. Gust took it. I was standing right there.” She pointed with her pipe at the bridges below Tether, without looking. “He didn’t say anything either. Just waved.” She put the pipe back in her mouth. “I’d have liked him to say something.”

He didn’t know what to say to that.

“I’ll tell your Gabe,” said Nan Gully. “Word for word. Lean.” She looked up at him with her sharp old eyes. “And one day you’ll tell him yourself. Or I’ll be cross. Understand?”

“Yes, Nan.”

“Good. Now buy fish.”

He stayed on floor 5 for fifteen days after the Squall.

He hadn’t stayed anywhere that long in this run except the Red City. He found that he didn’t mind. It was a good floor to stay on. The sun came up every morning, a real one. It rained twice, real rain, coming across the Reaches in long grey curtains from island to island. He could watch it coming for an hour before it got to him. Nan Gully’s knee hurt the day before, both times. She said it always did.

He helped at the Linkworks.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d gone back to Otta on the eighty-first day for another shield. She’d taken one look at the split halves of the first one, and then at him, and said everybody loses the second one too, and sold him another for twelve silver. But it was cheaper than that, she said, if he pumped the bellows for a week. So he pumped the bellows for a week. Then another. He stood at the back of the long black shed in the ringing heat, working the great leather lungs of the forge, while Otta and her two apprentices hammered bridge chain. He learned more about chain than he’d ever wanted to know.

“Every spring,” said Otta, on the fourth day, without stopping her hammer, “the wind takes a span.”

“Which one?”

“Never know.” Clang. “That’s the thing about wind. Some years it’s the Gullrock. Some years it’s one of the outer ones, out past the kite-nests, the long thin spans nobody walks but hunters.” Clang. “We check them. We change the links. And then a gust comes nobody’s ever seen, from a way it’s never come, and takes one anyway.” Clang. “Nobody’s fault. It’s wind.”

He worked the bellows and thought about that.

“Alea,” he said that night, in his bedroll behind Nan Gully’s hut. “Did a bridge go, the first time? While we were here?”

The fox thought about it. He could tell she was looking.

“No,” she said slowly. “Not while we were on this floor. Not that I logged.” She paused. “But Ice. I don’t have weather. Not from here up. I never did.” Her voice went careful. “From floor 5, weather stopped being in the logs. It never happened the same way twice. I logged the bridges, and the gale-kites, and the bird, because those were the same every time. But the wind—“ She stopped. “The wind’s real here. I can’t tell you what it’s going to do. Nobody can.”

He lay looking up at the kites humming on Nan Gully’s roof against the real stars.

Ten years of memory. Every boss pattern, every hidden quest, every place on every floor where somebody had died. And from here up, none of it covered the weather.

“Okay,” he said quietly.

“Okay?”

“It’s okay.” He wasn’t sure it was. “It’s the same for everyone. That’s what real means. Nobody knows.”

He read World every night.

They were on floor 4 still. Average twenty-two, then twenty-three. Dennis had found a market on the plateau, and had opinions about it. Bee had asked, in World, whether anybody else was having dreams about dust. Fourteen people had said yes. Hana had posted a picture she’d drawn of the dry fountain. Calvin had posted PSA: THE DEAD STAY ON FOUR. DON’T LOOK AT THEM IF YOU’RE BEE. Bee had posted thank you calvin, and then, a minute later, i looked.

On the eighty-sixth night, Chris posted.

[ World · Chris: kiln down. all 35. azac took the first vent and nearly didn’t come back. gabe took the second. stood in it. xav finished it. malcolm’s healing now, properly. we’re ok. ]

He read it four times.

Gabe took the second. Stood in it.

He’d known Gabe would. He’d thought it on the crown of the Spindle six days ago, sitting against a dead bird. He’d have stood like that in front of the Kiln, if they’d let him. They’d let him. And Gabe had stood in front of a furnace with his feet planted and his shield up and his shoulders forward, braced, a wall. The way Declan had stood. The way Declan had taught him, years ago, in games, on school nights. The way that worked on the Kiln. The way the bird on the Spindle had been made to kill.

“He’s fine,” said Alea quietly. “Ice. We’re ok. Chris said.”

“I know.”

“Nan Gully’s going to tell him.”

“I know.” He kept reading.

He laughed out loud. It came out so suddenly that the gull on Nan Gully’s hat woke up on its peg in the hut and screamed at him.

He lay in his bedroll behind the hut and laughed until he was crying, quietly, with his hands over his face, so as not to wake Nan Gully. Alea sat on his chest and didn’t say anything at all.

Stop standing in fire alone.

Malcolm. Who’d always done two of everything, in case the first one failed. Who’d sat at the back of a classroom in their first week at poly with a face of quiet doom and let a loud boy talk to him until they were friends. Who’d died, the first time, on floor— He stopped himself. He didn’t go there. Not tonight.

Malcolm’s healing now, properly.

“He learned to heal,” he said into his hands. “Alea. Malcolm. He was never a healer. Not the first time. Not once in ten years. He made things hard to break. That was all he ever did.”

“I know,” said Alea. “I logged it.”

“Why’s he healing?”

The fox was quiet for a moment.

“I think,” she said carefully, “it might be for you.”

He didn’t answer that. He lay there with his hands over his face until he fell asleep.

On the ninety-fourth night there was a second whisper.

He was sitting on the rock behind Nan Gully’s hut, cooking. Gull eggs and salt fish, the way he’d cooked every night for two weeks. The window came up on its own, small and quiet, at the top of everything, the way Gabe’s first one had on the second day. And under it, where the first one had sat alone for ninety-two mornings, there was a second.

Alea didn’t say anything. She just let it sit there.

[ Whisper · Gabe: dec. xav told me about the probe. still going huh. ok. we’re coming up tomorrow. all of us. still here. me too ]

He read it.

He put the pan down off the fire, carefully, onto the rock, and read it again.

Xav told me about the probe.

Of course he had. Of course Xav had remembered. A classroom that smelled of whiteboard markers, and a whisper, nine years ago, it’s still going, you know. He’d picked the name on the first night on floor 1, sitting by a fire, and told Alea Xav might think about it for a second, and then he’ll think nah. He’d known it wasn’t true when he said it. Xav never thought nah. Xav waited. And then, when he was sure, he moved once.

Still going huh.

Still here. Me too.

He put his hand over his mouth.

He knew what it meant. Of course he did. What’s it saying? he’d whispered, at seventeen, in the dark of a lecture hall, with a pale blue dot on the screen. And Xav had whispered back: Nothing, really. Just numbers. Just “I’m still here.” And Gabe had heard that story, somewhere on floor 4. He’d understood all of it in one go, the way Gabe understood things, with his hands rather than his head. And he’d sat down and typed the only answer there was.

Me too.

“Ice,” said Alea, very gently.

“I know.”

His fingers had opened the whisper reply. He hadn’t told them to. They were just there, on the window, and the little line was blinking, waiting. He typed:

ok

He looked at it.

Two letters. That was all. ok. He’d typed it on the second day, under a pine on floor 2, and deleted it. He’d typed it in his head every morning since, when Alea pinned the whisper at the top of his window. Ninety-three mornings.

We’re coming up tomorrow. All of us.

If he sent it, Gabe would read it on the stair. On the two hundred and forty-four steps between the furnace hall and floor 5, in the dark, with thirty-four people behind him. He’d come out of the door at the top onto a little island in the sky in the middle of the night, and stand there, and look round. And Declan would be a day’s walk away across a hundred chain bridges, at the bottom of a needle with a bird going round it. And Gabe would cross them. In the dark, if he had to. On floor 5, where the wind came from a different direction every minute and nobody’s logs had the weather.

He deleted it.

One letter, then the other. The line blinked in the empty space.

“Soon,” he said to the window, the way he’d said it on the high terrace on floor 4. His voice wasn’t working. “Not yet. But soon.”

Alea pinned the second whisper underneath the first, at the top of his window, where he’d see them both every morning. She didn’t ask. She just did it.

He picked the pan back up off the rock. The eggs had gone cold. He ate them anyway.

He left Tether on the ninety-fifth morning, before Nan Gully was up.

He left fish on her basket by the winch, wrapped in a cloth, enough for three days. On top of it he left the last of the Kitecliff herb, in a twist of paper, and a note in his own handwriting. He hadn’t written a note since the white room. It said: For the big one in the coat. Put it in his eggs the morning he goes up. Thank you, Nan. He looked at it for a long time, and then didn’t sign it, and went.

He crossed to the Spindle by the long bridges and climbed halfway, to the ledge where the steep bridge met the needle. He made camp there, in a hollow in the rock out of the wind. From the ledge he could see the whole of the Reaches spread out below him going west. And far off on the western edge, so far it was just a speck against the cloud, the little island with the stone hut on it, where the door from floor 4 came out.

He sat on the ledge with his back to the rock and his knees up and the barrel lid beside him, and waited.

They came through at dusk.

He felt the first one before the sun had quite gone. A pull, small and fine, waking up somewhere just behind the ember in his chest and running out across the whole of the Windward Reaches. Across a hundred islands and a hundred chains and miles of evening air, to a speck of rock on the western edge. It swung. It settled. It pointed.

Calvin.

Then the second, a hair to the left. Rachael.

He counted. He didn’t decide to. He sat on the ledge with the dusk coming up gold and red out of the cloud and counted, in a whisper, as they woke one by one, the way he’d counted on the roof across from the high terrace. Dennis. Deston. Azac. Xav. Gabe, and the pull of it harder than the others, the way it had been on floor 4. He’d stopped wondering whether that was the skill or him.

Thirty-four. Thea.

Thirty-five. Drew, last.

Thirty-five threads, running out from his chest across the evening, so long and fine that he could only feel them if he held his breath. All pointing the same way. To a rock the size of a house, at the edge of everything, in the dusk.

None of them was red.

“Thirty-five,” said Alea, beside him.

“Thirty-five.”

He sat on the ledge and held his breath and felt them. He could feel them moving. Small, restless, the way people move on an island the size of a house when there are thirty-five of them on it and nobody will cross a bridge in the dark. Somebody going round and round the edge, fast, and then stopping, and then going round again. Tiger. It had to be Tiger. Somebody moving from person to person, stopping by each one for a moment and moving on. Deston, or Drew, counting heads. And somebody who had gone to the very edge of the island, the edge that faced east, toward the middle of the Reaches and the needle. They had stopped there, and not moved since.

He didn’t know whose thread it was. They were so far away, and so fine, and there were so many of them, all pointing the same way. He couldn’t tell.

He thought he knew.

“It’s going to be a long night for them,” said Alea. “Nobody sleeps the first night on five.”

“Nobody slept the first time.” He looked at the speck in the west, going dark now as the dusk came up out of the cloud. “Calvin said oh in the morning. That’s all. Oh.”

“Calvin’s got two arms this time,” said Alea. “I think he’ll say more than oh.”

He laughed. It came out wet.

He didn’t sleep either. He sat on the ledge all night with his back to the rock and the threads running out of him across the dark. The stars came out above him, real ones, too many, thrown across the sky without any order at all. Far off in the west, the thread on the eastern edge of the little island didn’t move. All night. It stayed there, at the edge, facing the needle. Until the sky behind him began to go grey.

He went up to the crown in the last of the dark.

The Squall was there. A new one, out of wherever they came from, circling the needle in the grey light before dawn. The dead one from the eightieth day still lay on the crown, grey and still, half sunk into itself. Floor 5 didn’t take anything away. The new one saw him come over the edge of the spiral path onto the crown, and he saw its head turn.

He didn’t hurry. He stood in the middle of the crown and let the wind have him.

He knew it now. It took nine minutes.

It circled. It came in low and he cut its legs as it passed, leaning, never still, never going anywhere. It went up above him and beat its wings to pin him down. He let it. He waited, pressed onto one knee, still, until it folded its wings and came. And when the pressure dropped, half a heartbeat before it dropped, he moved once. It landed on nothing. He went through the tendon behind its foot. When it turned to strike him, he went down under the beak and up under the jaw.

The sun came up as it fell.

He stood on the crown of the Spindle beside two dead birds with his sword in his hand and the first edge of the sun coming up gold out of the cloud. The light came across the whole of the Reaches in one long wave, island after island lighting up. And far off on the western edge it reached the little island, the one the size of a house. Thirty-five threads, all at once, stopped moving.

All of them. Every single one. Still. For the first time all night.

He knew why. He’d stood on that island once. He knew what it was like when the sun came up over the cloud and hit your face, and you’d forgotten, you’d forgotten for ninety-five days, what a sun was.

He stood very still too, on the crown, beside the dead bird. And watched it come up with them.

Then, a few seconds later, all at once, the threads started moving again. Wildly. In every direction. One of them went round and round the edge of the island so fast he could barely follow it. Tiger. Definitely Tiger. A cluster went to the edge and stopped, close together, and then a few of them broke away. He could feel them shaking, a fine shiver all along the threads, the way you feel a phone buzz in somebody else’s pocket. Laughing, maybe. Or crying. Or both.

The one on the eastern edge, that hadn’t moved all night, still didn’t move.

“Ice,” said Alea. She was looking north. “Look.”

He looked.

Off the top of the Kitecliff, gold in the new sun, something had gone over the edge. Grey. Wide. Then another. Then another. Seven kites, going off the white cliffs into the morning, finding the wind and rising, higher and higher, out across the Reaches in a long line. They came toward the Spindle the way they had sixteen days ago, toward the needle where there was no bird.

And past it. West. Toward the little island at the edge of everything, where thirty-five people had just seen their first real sun.

He’d killed it at dawn for this. He hadn’t said it to Alea. He hadn’t said it to himself. But he’d known, coming up the spiral path in the dark. On the morning they saw their first sun, he wanted there to be people flying in it.

He watched the seven kites go out across the Reaches in the gold light toward the island. Very small, from here. He couldn’t see the island. He couldn’t see what thirty-five people did when seven winged people came out of the sunrise toward them, gliding. But he felt it. All along the threads, all at once, a shiver. Thirty-five people going still again, and then not.

“Pell,” said Alea softly. “Pell’s taking them to see the new ones.”

“Pell’s showing off.”

“Pell is absolutely showing off,” the fox agreed.

He laughed. He stood on the crown of the Spindle beside two dead birds in the sunrise and laughed, and wiped his face with the back of his wrist, and laughed again.

The arch was at the far side of the crown. A plain stone arch, older than anything on floor 5, with a door in it. He’d looked at it every day for sixteen days. He went and stood in front of it now, with the barrel lid on his arm and the sun warm on the back of his neck.

He didn’t go through it straight away.

He stood there and held his breath and felt for the threads. Thirty-five of them, running out from his chest across the whole of the Windward Reaches to a little island in the west, in the sun. Moving. Laughing. Alive. With seven kites going round over their heads.

“They’ll be on this floor a while,” said Alea. “Two weeks. Maybe three. They’ll come up the Spindle when they’re ready. Nan’s got your message. Pell’s got your fish.”

“I know.”

“And you’ll be on six.”

“I’ll be on six.”

He opened the door.

He counted the steps. Two hundred and eighty. The threads went faint behind him as he climbed, fainter, finer, until at the top he had to hold his breath and stand quite still to feel them at all. Thirty-five. Still there. Still moving. He stood at the top in front of the door and felt them for a long time.

Then he opened it, and they went. One by one, from the edges in, the way they had on floor 4. The last was a thread that pointed to the eastern edge of a little island. He’d never found out whose it was.

The heat hit him in the face like an oven door.

He stepped out into white. White sky, so bright he couldn’t look at it, with a sun in it that hurt. White sand, going out in every direction in great slow dunes. Except it wasn’t sand. It glittered. Everywhere he looked, it threw the light back at him, in flashes, in sheets, in a glare that made his eyes water. Dunes of glass, broken and ground and blown into hills by a wind that came across them hot as breath. On the far horizon, where the white sky met the white ground, something shimmered. A lake, maybe. A city. Trees. He knew it was none of those things.

[ Floor 6 · the Glass Waste ]

He took out the helm.

He looked at it for a moment, white and smooth and faceless in his hands. Then he put it on. The world went narrow and dark: a slit of white sky and white glass. The glare dropped to something he could look at. His own breathing was loud in his ears.

He started walking.
