Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 212

There

6,303 words · 27 min read

He could feel them across the river all night.

Five threads. On the top of the right-hand cliff, across the Narrows, across a quarter-mile of flood going through the gap in the dark with a sound like the end of the world. Five gold threads going out from somewhere under his breastbone across the black water and up the cliff to five people sitting on bare rock in the cold. He’d felt them for three days, from a day’s walk off. He could feel them much better now. Close. Tired. Frightened, a little, all of them, the steady low frightened you got used to and stopped noticing. And one of them cold.

Ina’s.

He sat on the ledge on the left cliff, high above the jam, where Chris’s party had camped for three nights before they went across to the right, and felt Ina’s thread, cold at the end. At the hands. Like a thread that had been dipped in the river and not dried.

He couldn’t get to him.

That was the thing he sat with all night. He’d always been able to get to them. That was the whole of him. On every floor, in two lives, he’d been the one who got there. A floor ahead, first, alone, in the dark, through whatever was in the way. On fourteen he’d walked out onto the Grey. Thirteen days ago, on this river, he’d gone under the ice after Calvin without thinking. He’d never in his life been a quarter-mile from somebody whose hands had gone black with cold and not been able to get there.

The river went through the gap between them. Fast and black and full of ice. There was nothing to stand on. Nothing to wait for two plates to touch on. Brann had said you let the river bring you across, and the river wasn’t going across. It was going down.

He sat on the ledge and felt Ina’s thread, cold, a quarter-mile off, and didn’t go.

Thea sat beside him. She didn’t say anything either. She had the lantern in her lap, turned down low. It was leaning across the river. Toward the right cliff. Toward the five.

You can’t, said Alea. Very quietly, on his shoulder. I know you want to. You can’t.

“I know.”

Nobody can. Not tonight. She was quiet. They’re all right, Ice. They’re tired. Ina’s hands. But they’re sitting together, and they’re not moving, and Winter’s got his wrist. I can feel it. Through you. A pause. She hasn’t let go of it since yesterday.

He didn’t say anything.

That’s somebody else getting there, said Alea. That’s all. Somebody else got there first.

He sat on the ledge until the sky went grey over the black cliffs, and didn’t go, and felt somebody else hold on to Ina’s wrist all night on the far side of a river he couldn’t cross.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done. He thought, at dawn, that it might be harder than the Grey.

Winter shot the line across at first light.

He saw her do it. Small and far off on the top of the right cliff, a figure with her hair blowing everywhere, standing at the very edge with her bow up and drawn and a long pale cord trailing off the back of the arrow down to Niko’s hands behind her. She didn’t call anything. Sound didn’t carry over the Narrows anyway, not over the river. She just drew, and held, and looked across the gap at the left cliff, at the ledge, at all of them standing there in the grey light looking back.

Rui Xiang stepped out to the edge of the ledge and lifted one hand. Palm out. Here.

Winter loosed.

Her last arrow. Ice knew it was. He’d felt it, through her thread, a long time ago, on the second day: four left, a small flat cold in her, the way Winter counted things. Then one. She’d been saving it. One for whatever. She sent it across the Narrows in the grey dawn with a cord on it, high, in a long slow arc over the flood, and it came down on the left cliff a metre from Rui Xiang’s hand and went into a crack in the rock and stuck there, humming.

Rui Xiang had the cord before it stopped humming.

Tiger was beside him already. With Burr’s rope. The old one, dark with years, that Burr had given him on nine, that he’d carried down every floor and back up again, and swum through a flood with only last night to make the Float fast. He tied it to the cord. Burr’s knot. Then Rui Xiang and Tiger and Salty hauled the cord back across, hand over hand, with Burr’s rope going out behind it over the river, and on the far side Niko and Chris and Maigo hauled it in. And made it fast. On both sides. To spurs of rock. With Burr’s knot.

A rope. Across the Narrows. Forty metres above the flood.

“Nobody left down,” said Tiger. Under his breath. To the rope.

They came across one at a time.

Hand over hand, with their legs hooked over the rope, hanging under it, the way you’d cross a gorge on nine. Sabe first, because Sabe said so, and nobody argued with Sabe; an old woman of fifty-eight thaws going hand over hand across a rope over the Narrows with her black pole tied across her back. She didn’t look down once. Then Tuli, with her mother’s pole across hers. Then Maigo, slow, with four leaves on his head and his feet bare. Then Niko. Then Chris, one-handed with his other arm strapped, which made Ice’s chest go tight the whole way across, and Rachael, on the ledge, say something about Chris that Ice had never heard her say.

Then Winter and Ina.

Together. Ina couldn’t hold the rope. He held his hands up when they got to the edge, Ice could see it from across the gap, and looked at them, black to the wrist, and Ice felt it through his thread: not pain. Something worse. A sort of quiet, in the hands, where the fire should have been. And Winter, beside him, without saying anything, tied a loop of Burr’s rope round Ina’s chest under his arms, and clipped it to the main rope, and went across hanging under it herself, hand over hand, pulling him along on the loop beside her. Forty metres. Above the flood. With one arrow’s worth of strength left in her and Ina’s weight on her.

She didn’t look at him once on the way across. She looked at the rope. At her hands.

Ice watched them come. All the way. Every metre.

They got to the ledge. Gabe reached out and took Ina off the rope under the arms like taking a child off a wall. Winter came up after him on her own, onto the rock, onto her knees, and stayed there, with her hands flat on the stone in front of her and her hair over her face, breathing.

Then she reached out without looking up and found Ina’s wrist again. Above the black. And held it.

Ice went to them.

He didn’t decide to. He was across the ledge and on his knees in front of Ina before he’d thought. He looked at Ina’s hands. Black. Not burned. Not frostbitten; he’d seen frostbite on two, in both lives, and this wasn’t it. Black the way the inside of the river was black. Cold the way the bottom of a well was cold. From the inside out.

“Ina,” he said.

Ina looked at him. His mask was pushed up. His face was grey and very calm.

“It’s all right,” said Ina. “It’s coming out. Slow. Maigo says.” He looked at his hands. “It’s not mine. The cold. It’s the jam’s. It went into me when I put the fire in. I took a bit.” He was quiet. “It’ll go.”

Ice looked at the hands.

“You took a bit,” he said.

“Of the cold. Of what it’s holding.” Ina looked at him with his calm grey face. “It’s holding so much. All of it. On its own.” He tilted his head, a fraction. The way he did when he’d decided to say something true. “I thought, if I took a bit, it’d be less. For it.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a long moment.

Then he put his own hands round Ina’s. Both of them. Round the black. He didn’t know why. He couldn’t heal anybody but himself. He’d never once in two lives been able to heal anybody but himself. He just put his hands round Ina’s and held them, and they were so cold it hurt, the way the river had hurt, all the way in.

And he felt it.

Through Bearing. Through the thread. Through his own hands. The cold in Ina’s hands. Not the cold. The weight of it. The thing Ina had taken. A bit. A very small bit of something enormous, carried in a man’s hands because he’d thought it would make it less for somebody else.

What you cannot carry, he thought. He didn’t know where it came from. They will.

He didn’t use Borne. He didn’t know how to use it for this; he didn’t know if it could be used for this. He just held Ina’s hands, and felt the weight, and let it come into his own hands a little, the way you’d take one end of something heavy off somebody’s shoulder without asking. A little. Not all. Just enough that it wasn’t only Ina’s.

Ina’s face changed.

“Oh,” said Ina.

“Is that—“

“Lighter,” said Ina. Surprised. He looked at his hands in Ice’s. “It’s lighter. Just a bit.” He was quiet. “You took a bit.”

“A bit.”

“Of mine.”

“Of yours.” Ice looked at the black hands in his. They were still black. They’d be black for days, Maigo would say later. But Ina’s thread, in his chest, had gone from cold to cool. Like a stone that’s been in the sun for a minute. “You took a bit of its. I took a bit of yours.” He didn’t know what he was saying. “That’s—“

He stopped.

Ina was looking at him. Winter was looking at him, with Ina’s wrist in her hand. Chris was looking at him, from a step away, with his notebook in his good hand and his face doing something very careful.

“That’s how,” said Ina. Very quietly. “That’s how you do it. With a thing that’s holding everything on its own.” He looked down at the jam below the ledge. At the heart in the ice. “Somebody takes a bit. And then somebody takes a bit of theirs.”

Nobody said anything.

Chris opened his notebook, one-handed, against his knee, and wrote something, and didn’t show anybody.

He told Sabe about the house after they’d eaten.

He’d thought about how to do it all night. On the ledge, with Ina’s thread cold across the river. He’d thought about telling her straight away, the moment she came off the rope, and decided she’d want to eat first. Then he’d thought about not telling her at all, and leaving it for Haddo, or for somebody from Keddle, somebody who knew her. And then he’d thought about Mother Crane, on floor 1, who had never in her life let anybody tell her a thing slowly.

So he went and sat down across from Sabe, on the rock of the ledge, by the fire, with the jam groaning below them. Tuli was beside her. They were both eating fish out of their hands.

“Sabe,” he said.

Sabe looked at him with her milky eyes. She put her fish down.

“Out with it,” said Sabe.

He nearly laughed. He didn’t.

“The water came up,” he said. “At Keddle. The day after the first word. Over the porches. The plates hit the legs.” He made himself say it plainly, the way she’d want it said. “Fourteen houses went. Into the river. Yours was one of them.”

Tuli’s hand stopped halfway to her mouth.

Sabe didn’t move.

“Everybody got out,” said Ice. “All eighty-seven. Before they went. They’re on the cliff, at the top of the path, by the stair door. With their poles. Six houses held. Gigi held them.” He nodded across the ledge at Gigi. “With thorns. Round the legs.” He looked at Sabe. “And Hako held the ladder. In the river. While they came across.”

Sabe sat very still for a long time.

Then she put her pole down on the rock. Tock. And tipped her head. And listened. Not to the jam. Somewhere else. Up the river, maybe. A day off. To a place where her house had been.

She lifted the pole.

“Every spring,” said Sabe, “she takes something.”

“I know. They said.”

“Every summer we put it back.” She looked at him. “My mother’s house. My gran’s. Built on the same legs. I was born in it.” She was quiet. “It’s gone, is it.”

“It’s gone.”

Sabe nodded. Slowly. Then she picked up her fish again and ate a bit of it.

“Who held the ladder?” she said.

Ice looked across the ledge. Hako was sitting with Bee, on the far side of the fire, with his borrowed spear across his knees and Bee asleep against his shoulder and a large wet grey dog asleep across his legs, which had followed him from Keddle onto the Float and down the river and up the cliff, and which nobody had asked to.

“Him,” said Ice.

Sabe looked at Hako for a long time. Then she said, loud enough to carry across the fire:

“You. Big one. Did you hold the ladder at Keddle?”

Hako looked up. He looked at Sabe. Then at Ice. Then back at Sabe.

“Yes,” said Hako.

“Till they were all across?”

“Yes.”

“My father did that,” said Sabe. “In my mother’s time. Held the ladder at Keddle. Half a day.” She looked at Hako with her milky eyes. “Then a plate came down.”

Hako didn’t say anything.

“No plate came down on you?” said Sabe.

“Some came down,” said Hako. “I leaned on them.”

Sabe looked at him for a long moment. Then something happened, a long way down in her face, under all those lines.

“Good,” said Sabe. “That’s what he’d have said.”

And went back to her fish.

Tuli hadn’t moved. She was sitting beside her gran with her hand still halfway to her mouth, looking at the fire. Her face was very flat.

Ina got up. He’d been sitting with Winter on the far side of the ledge with his black hands in his lap. He came round the fire without saying anything and sat down on Tuli’s other side. Not close. About the width of his mask.

He didn’t say anything.

After a while Tuli put the fish down, and leaned her head on Ina’s shoulder, and didn’t cry, and didn’t say anything either.

They had a council on the ledge at noon.

All thirty-six, and Sabe and Tuli, crammed onto a shelf of rock meant for seven and a fire, sitting on each other’s legs and against the cliff and, in Calvin’s case, on the very edge with his feet over the drop and Rachael’s fist in the back of his coat. Below them, the jam. A third of a mountain, heaped against the foot of the left cliff, groaning, with the flood roaring through the open gap on the right. And in the middle of it, close under the clear black ice, so close you could see it plainly from the ledge in the noon light, the heart.

Curled. Pale. Bigger than a house. On its side, with four slack broken arms lying against it and one arm going out sideways into the rock of the left cliff, just under the ledge, as thick as a tree, glistening, tight. Holding everything that was left. With all of itself.

Going once, once, once. Fast.

Chris had his notebook open on his knee. One-handed. He’d drawn the whole thing again, on a new page. The jam, smaller now. The one arm. The heart. He’d crossed out 5, 4, 2, 3. He’d left 1. And under the heart, his question mark, and under the question mark, in his square hand:

You don’t fight a thing that’s frightened. You sit with it until it isn’t. (Ina.)

Somebody takes a bit. And then somebody takes a bit of theirs. (Ina, this morning.)

He turned the notebook round so they could all see.

“That’s what we’ve got,” said Chris. “That’s all of it. Everything we know. Sabe’s gran said let it go early, bit at a time. We’ve done four bits. This is the last bit. And it’s not a bit. It’s everything it’s got left.” He put his finger on 1. “Maigo says it’s not a knuckle. It’s holding with the whole arm. You can’t crack it. You can’t cut it. If you take it anyway, it lets go of everything at once and the whole jam goes and the lake with it, and the Float’s moored half a mile up.”

“And the moorings,” said Sabe. “Below. The reed-bays. Where the Float goes in summer. Where Keddle’s people go when the water’s down.” She put her pole down. “All of it.”

“So it has to let go itself,” said Chris. “A bit at a time. Like the others. But it won’t. Because it’s frightened, and it’s holding the last of everything on its own.” He looked at his notebook. “And Ina says somebody has to sit with it. Close. And take a bit.” He looked up. “And then somebody takes a bit of theirs.”

Nobody said anything.

Nobody looked at Ice.

He felt them not look. Thirty-five threads, all at once, carefully. The way they’d carefully not looked at him for two years, every time he said the first time, every time he knew something he couldn’t have known. The way Niko had carefully not looked at him on the crown of the Spindle. They weren’t looking at him now. Not because of what he knew.

Because of what he didn’t.

They didn’t know if he’d go. That was the thing. He felt it in all of them. On every floor for two years he’d have been on his feet before Chris finished the sentence. Me. There. I’ll do it. A floor ahead. First. Alone. They’d have spent the whole council trying to stop him, the way they’d tried on fourteen, at the line, until Higu said that’s just where. And now he was sitting on the ledge with Thea’s hand in his and his back against the cliff, and he hadn’t moved. And they didn’t know what that meant.

He didn’t either, entirely.

Higu was looking at him.

She was the only one who was. She had the rock on her knees. The new one, from floor 1, with A FOREST and A RIVER and HE DOESN’T KNOW and HE ASKED HOW. She was looking at him across the ledge with her chalk in her hand, the way she’d looked at him at the foot of every stair since the Long Acre. Waiting for one word.

“Ice,” said Higu. “Where?”

He looked down at the jam.

At the heart. At the one arm. At the place on the clear black ice, right over the middle of the curled shape, where the ice was thinnest and you could see the darkness going once under it. A place about the size of a man kneeling.

He’d never answered where on fifteen. He’d said I don’t know on the first morning and meant it. He still didn’t know. He didn’t know what was under the ice, or what it would do, or whether it would work. He didn’t know anything about it at all, except what thirty-five people and an old woman and a girl had found out and written down in a notebook with one hand.

But he knew where.

Not because he remembered. Because he’d decided.

“There,” said Ice.

He pointed. At the place on the ice over the heart.

Higu looked at where he was pointing. For a long time. Then she wrote it on the rock. In the right-hand column. Small. THERE. She didn’t underline it.

Then she looked back up at him.

“How?” said Higu.

He didn’t know.

He said so. Out loud, on the ledge, to all of them. He’d thought it would be hard, and it wasn’t, and that surprised him more than anything had on fifteen.

“I don’t know how,” said Ice. “I’ve never done anything like it. Nobody has.” He looked at the heart. “I know what I’ve got. I can kneel. Sanctuary. Where you stand, they are safe. Where they stand, so are you. I know that one. I knelt for it, and you held. And I’ve got Borne. What you cannot carry, they will. I’ve used it once. On fourteen. On the Grey. You held me up.” He looked round at them. “I don’t know if either of them works on a thing like that. On a heart in the ice. I don’t know if it can feel us. I don’t know if it’ll let me near it. I don’t know if, if I kneel there and try to take a bit of what it’s holding, it’ll be a bit or it’ll be all of it. And if it’s all of it, I don’t know what happens.”

He stopped. He’d run out of things he didn’t know. That was new too.

“So tell me,” said Ice. “What you’ve got. All of you. And I’ll write it down.” He looked at Chris. “Or Chris will.”

Chris was looking at him with a face Ice had never seen on him. He didn’t say anything for a moment. Then he turned to a new page in his notebook.

HOW, he wrote at the top, one-handed. (He asked.)

Rachael spoke first.

She was sitting against the cliff behind Calvin, with her fist still in his coat. She had her gloves off. She looked at her own hands for a long time before she said anything. They’d carried an old woman’s fever on floor 1, three hours at a time, for three nights. They’d only just stopped aching.

“Burden,” said Rachael. “What you cannot heal, you may carry. Carry it somewhere it can be put down.” She looked down at the heart. “I can’t heal that. Nobody can. It’s not hurt. It’s just holding.” She was quiet. “But I can carry. I’ve carried a fever. I’ve carried the grey out of you, when the book was crossing you out.” She looked at Ice. “I can put my hands on it. On the ice over it. And take some of what it’s holding. Into me.”

“And then?”

“And then I carry it somewhere it can be put down,” said Rachael. “That’s the second half. Nobody ever reads the second half.” She looked at him. “You. You’re where it can be put down. You kneel. And I bring it to you. A bit at a time. And you put it down.”

“Down where?”

“Into us,” said Deston.

He was beside Rachael, against the cliff, with his hand flat on his chest the way he always held it. He hadn’t said anything all council.

“Link,” said Deston. “What one of you has, all of you may share. I did it on the Grey. Into all of us. Into the threads. Your threads, Dec. They go everywhere. To all thirty-five of us. I just put my gold down them.” He looked at Ice. “You kneel. Rachael carries it from the heart to you. You take it with Borne, and it goes down your threads, and I spread it. All of us. And Azac—“

“Overlap,” said Azac. Quietly. “Spread it thin. Thin enough that it’s light. The whole line takes it. Lightly.” He looked down at the heart. “That’s all a wall is. Nobody holding it on their own.”

Ice looked at them. Deston. Rachael. Azac. Calvin with his feet over the edge. Dennis beside him. Deston’s party. The first people he’d ever watched walk into a fight without him.

“Maigo,” said Ice.

Maigo was sitting barefoot on the edge of the ledge, his feet on the rock, his eyes shut. He opened them.

“I’ll be in the cliff,” said Maigo. “With my feet. Listening to the arm. To where it’s holding. I’ll know when it lets go a bit. Before anybody.” He shut his eyes again. “I’ll tell Sabe.”

“And Sabe and Tuli will say when,” said Chris, writing. “When it’s ready to let a bit go. When it’s not. Like the others.”

“Niko,” said Ice.

Niko looked at him. His thumb was on his sword. On the back of the blade.

“I can’t cut it,” said Niko. “Maigo says. There’s nowhere to cut.” He was quiet. “But I can leave something. Not a cut. I don’t know what. I’ll know when I’m there.” He looked at Ice for a long moment. “I’ll be there. That’s all. Where you can see me.”

“Yan,” said Ice.

Yan was at the very back of the ledge. Against the cliff. As far from the edge as he could get, with the scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck and Wendy beside him. He’d been there since they came up. Ice knew why. Everybody did, now. Wherever I stand, there’s an Edge.

“I’ll stay up here,” said Yan. Cool and level. “Away from it. I’m not putting an edge on that ice.” He looked down at the heart. “It’s got a light. Did you know? It’s big. It’s old. It’s down at the bottom of itself, in the middle, pulled in tight. Like hers was. On floor 1.” He looked at Ice. “I’ll watch it. If it starts to go up, I’ll tell you.”

“And then?”

Yan looked at him a long time.

“And then you’ll know,” said Yan. “Which way it’s going.”

Ice nodded.

He looked round the ledge. At the rest of them. Higu with the rock. Kei with Dose. Hyvred with her glasses on. Bee with two strips of birch-bark in her coat. Hako with the dog. Gigi and Xola. Chi. Drew, with his bare hands. Xav, very still, reading the ledge. Malcolm, with his slate, on which Ice could see A is everybody. John with a mote in his palm. Rui Xiang, Salty, Wendy, Tiger, Cursiffe. Hana and Milli, Jared, Newish, Aleaka with the goat. All of them.

He didn’t ask them anything. He didn’t need to. Where they stand, so are you. They’d be where they were. Holding the threads. Lightly.

And Gabe.

Gabe was at the front of the ledge, by the drop, with Hearthwall across his knees. Warm. He’d been looking down at the heart the whole council. He hadn’t said a word.

He looked up now. At Ice.

“On four,” said Gabe. “In the furnace hall. There was a shape on the floor.”

Ice looked at him.

He knew the shape. A man with a shield, unscorched, in a black glassy fan of burned stone. Traced twice, because he’d stood in exactly the same place both times, on day 44 and on day 66, on his own, in front of a fire that could melt iron. He’d never told anybody. He’d never needed to. They’d come up the stair a long time after and found it on the floor.

“I stood in it,” said Gabe. “When we did ours. It fitted. A bit narrow.” He was quiet. “You stood in a fire on your own. Twice. And I stood in the shape you left, after, with six healers behind me.” He looked down at the ice. “I’ve thought about that a lot.”

“It’s not a fire,” said Ice.

“It’s a thing you’re going to kneel in front of that’s bigger than a house,” said Gabe. “Close enough.” He put his hand flat on Hearthwall. “A shield that covers the one beside you. It does not go cold.” He looked down at the ice. “That’s ice. That’s very cold. And you’re going to be kneeling on it.” He looked back up. “I’m not standing in the shape after. Not this time. I’ll be beside you. With the shield. On your right, where I always am.” His voice went, a little. “At the same time.”

Ice didn’t say anything for a long moment.

Then he nodded.

“Deal,” he said.

Thea hadn’t said anything all council.

She’d sat beside him against the cliff with her hand in his and the lantern on her knee, and listened to all of it, and said nothing. Not when he said there. Not when he said I don’t know how. Not when Gabe said at the same time. He’d felt her not say anything. He’d felt her hand in his go still, and stay still, the whole time.

When the council broke up, and people started moving, getting ropes, going down the cliff path to the foot of the jam, she didn’t move.

He looked at her.

She was looking down at the heart. At the place on the ice where he’d pointed. There. Her face was doing nothing at all.

He knew what she was thinking. He’d always known. On fourteen, at the line, she’d stood in the door of the Long Barn with the lantern while he walked out onto the Grey, and she hadn’t stepped round him. Go. Come back. The tower had been watching her, Alea said, for that. For whether she’d step in front. In the other life, at the very end, she had. She’d stepped round him with a ward up and something had gone through the ward and through her, and she’d said four words he hadn’t heard, then. He knew them now. And in this life, at every edge, she’d stood beside him, or behind him with the light, and not stepped round. Because she’d promised. Because he’d asked.

She was going to have to do it again. Stand at the edge with a light while he knelt in front of something bigger than a house.

“I know,” said Thea. Before he could say anything.

“I didn’t—“

“I know you didn’t. You asked. You said there, and then you asked how, and you let everybody tell you. You let Rachael carry it to you. You let Deston and Azac spread it. You let Gabe kneel next to you.” She was still looking at the ice. “You’re not going out there on your own. Not this time. Not like the Grey.”

“No.”

“So I’m not going to be angry.” Her voice was very steady. “I want you to know that. Before. I’m not going to be angry, after. Because you asked. Every single bit of it. You asked.”

He didn’t know what to say.

“I’m going to stand at the edge of the ice,” said Thea. “With the lantern. Where you can see it. And I’m not going to step round you. And I’m not going to step in front.” She turned her head and looked at him. Her eyes were dry. “I’m going to hold the light. So you can find your way back.”

“I know.”

“Go,” said Thea. “Come back.”

He held her hand.

“I’ll tell you the numbers,” he said. “Every one. Out loud.”

“I know you will.” Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. Not not one. “You always do now.”

They went down the cliff path in the afternoon, all of them, to the foot of the jam.

The jam stood above them. Close now. A wall of heaved and broken ice going up against the foot of the left cliff, higher than a house, higher than three, glittering, groaning. The flood roaring past on the right. The one pale arm going out of the ice into the rock beside them, as thick as a tree, so close you could have touched it. Ice did. He put his hand flat on it as he went past. It was colder than anything.

Tuli and Sabe went first up the face of the jam. With their poles. Tock. Here. Not there. And Maigo after them, barefoot, to a place on the cliff right beside the arm, where he sat down and put his feet on the rock and shut his eyes.

Rachael went up with Ice. And Gabe, with Hearthwall. And Niko.

The rest stayed. At the foot of the jam, on the rock. Thirty of them. Deston with his hand on his chest. Azac beside him. And Thea, at the very edge of the ice, where the jam met the shore, with the lantern held up in front of her, the amber light going up the face of the jam toward him.

Leaning toward him.

At the top of the ledge, very small against the black cliff, Yan sat with his scythe across his knees and his eyes on the ice.

They climbed. Slab by slab. Behind Sabe’s pole. Tock. Tock. The jam moved under them, a little, groaning. The heart went once, under the ice, under their feet. Fast. He felt it come up through his boots into his legs into his chest, once, once, like a drum.

And then they were there.

The place. On the top of the jam, in the middle, where the ice was clear and black and thin, about the size of a man kneeling. And under it, close, so close he could have put his hand through if the ice weren’t there, the heart.

He looked down at it.

It was looking back.

He hadn’t known it had eyes. Nobody had. Bee had drawn it curled on its side with its knees up and its arms out, like something in a bad dream, and she hadn’t drawn eyes, because she drew what was there and from the Float there hadn’t been any. There were now. Just one. Under the ice, in the pale curve of the curled thing, very close, an eye. Not like a person’s. Not like an animal’s. Round and dark and old, the colour of the deep water at the bottom of the river, with something moving in it very far down, like light at the bottom of a well.

It was looking at him.

He didn’t know how he knew. He just knew. The way he’d known on the Long Acre that the straw man was looking at him, long before Chris’s string. It was looking at him. The way something very frightened looks at the first thing that comes close.

Ice, said Alea. Very quietly. On his shoulder.

“I know.”

It knows you’re there.

“I know.”

What are you going to do?

He didn’t know.

He knelt down.

On the ice. Over the heart. On the place he’d said. There. And Gabe knelt beside him, on his right, close, shoulder to shoulder, and put Hearthwall down flat on the ice in front of both of them, warm, and the ice under the shield went tick, softly, warming. And Rachael knelt on his left. With her gloves off. And put her bare hands flat on the clear black ice over the heart’s eye. And Niko stood a little behind, where Ice could see him if he turned his head, with his sword drawn and his thumb on the back of the blade.

Below them, at the foot of the jam, the amber light leaned up the ice.

Ice put his hands flat on the ice beside Rachael’s.

He looked down at the eye.

“Hello,” said Ice.
