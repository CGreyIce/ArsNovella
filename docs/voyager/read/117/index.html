Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 117

Spring

3,930 words · 17 min read

The far side of the Gate was spring.

He’d known it would be. He’d come through here once before, in a line, in the mud, with meltwater running past his boots. He’d forgotten what it was like to come through it out of the dark.

The tunnel through the black ice ended, and the gold that had been pouring through the doors was suddenly all round them, and it wasn’t gold any more. It was morning. Early morning, pale and warm, the sun not up yet but the sky already light. A valley. High and wide and green, cupped between peaks so tall their tops were lost in the brightness. Grass. Real grass, soft and new and wet with dew, going away from the foot of the ice wall in a long gentle slope down to water. And flowers in it. Small ones, white and yellow and a blue so deep it was almost purple, thousands of them, all through the grass, all open.

And below the valley, past its lip, nothing.

Cloud. A whole sea of it. White, and soft, and endless, lying in every direction under the edge of the valley like a floor. The peaks came up out of it like islands. The valley sat on top of it like a bowl on a table.

They were above the weather. Above the snow. Above the Winter, and the pass, and the Bastion, and the whole of floor 10.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

They stood at the foot of the ice wall in the grass, thirty-six of them, still holding hands, the way they’d come through. Nobody had let go. Behind them the two grey doors were shut, and on this side they weren’t doors at all. They were just ice. A black glassy cliff going up, smooth and deep, with no line in it, no ring, nothing to say there’d ever been a way through.

Calvin let go first. He let go of Hana’s hand on one side and Dennis’s on the other and walked out into the grass, three steps, four, and stopped. And knelt down. And put both his hands flat on it.

“It’s warm,” said Calvin. Very quietly. For once.

There was a lake at the bottom of the slope.

A tarn, Higu called it, because Higu knew the words for things. Small and round and so still it was like a mirror lying in the grass, with the sky in it and the peaks in it and the first gold of the sun coming up over the eastern ridge in it. Its edges were full of reeds. Something was singing in the reeds. A bird. A real one, small and brown, sitting on a reed-head and singing as if nobody had ever told it there was a Winter.

And at the far end of the tarn, on the other side of the water, the valley ran up into a cliff of grey rock. And in the cliff, at the bottom, where the grass met the stone, a door.

Plain. Round-topped.

Like all of them.

Ice looked at it for a long time.

“That’s the stair,” he said. Quietly. To Higu, because it was hers to know. “Floor 11.”

“Tomorrow,” said Higu.

“Tomorrow.” He looked at the sky. “We’ve got till tomorrow night. Then it puts the Gatekeeper back.”

“Then tomorrow,” said Higu. She didn’t take the rock out of her pack. She didn’t need to. She looked round at all of them, standing in the grass in the morning, and her calm half-lidded face did something he’d never seen it do.

“Today,” said Higu, “nobody does anything.”

Nobody did anything.

That was the strangest thing about the whole of the tenth floor, he thought afterwards. They’d come up the high side through three hundred and twelve watching helmets. They’d sat down in the snow in front of a gate with spring coming through it and said none. They’d held a ring against the oldest armour in the world while a thing with no face watched. And then they came through, and lay down in the grass, and slept.

Most of them. Right there. Where they’d stopped. Rachael went round with her hands on everybody first, all thirty-five of them, one after another, the way she did after every fight, and when she got to the end she lay down in the grass beside Dennis without a word and was asleep before Dennis had finished asking her if she was all right. Hako lay on his back with his arms out like a starfish and said it’s fine, once, to the sky, and was gone. Calvin talked for about four minutes about the warmth and the flowers and the bird and how there was a bird, an actual bird, did anybody else see the bird, and then stopped in the middle of a sentence.

Gabe didn’t sleep. Gabe sat down in the grass a little way up the slope from the tarn with Hearthwall beside him and his back against a boulder, where he could see everybody, and didn’t say anything. He looked at Ice once. Then away.

Ice didn’t sleep either.

He walked. Slowly. Round the edge of the tarn, in the grass, with the flowers wet against his boots and the sun coming up gold over the ridge and the bird singing in the reeds. He hadn’t walked anywhere slowly in a very long time. He found he didn’t know how. He kept speeding up, and noticing, and making himself stop.

“Your foot’s not tapping,” said Alea. On his shoulder. “Because you’re walking on it. I’m noting it anyway.”

He didn’t answer. He was looking at a rock.

It was on the far side of the tarn. Near the door. A flat grey boulder at the water’s edge, half in the reeds, worn smooth on top, the kind of rock you’d sit on to take your boots off and put your feet in the water.

He’d sat on it.

The first time. He knew it was the same one. He hadn’t thought about it once in ten years, and he knew it the second he saw it, the way you know a face in a crowd. Spring, the first time. The Winter gone. Thirty-four of them, coming through the doors into the valley in the mud, too tired to look at anything. And somebody sitting on this rock with her boots off and her feet in the water, singing, very quietly, to nobody. An old song. One that went round and round, up and down, like somebody rocking a chair.

He’d stood a little way off. Up the slope. Where she couldn’t see him. He’d listened to the whole thing.

He’d nearly gone down. He’d nearly sat on the grass beside the rock and said something. He didn’t know what. Something about calls, and Earth, and a year of nights. Something about how he’d thought it had passed and it hadn’t, quite. He’d stood on the slope with his weight on one foot, for a long time.

And then Gabe had shouted for him from the far side of the tarn about the tents, and he’d gone.

There’ll be time, he’d thought. Going. Once we’re out.

That had been the first year. The tenth floor. There’d been ninety floors after it, and ten years, and a dinner on forty-three that neither of them had called anything, and her hand on his arm on seventy, gone before he could think about it. And a wall of rewritten light. And three words, maybe four. And no time at all.

He stood on the far side of the tarn in the morning and looked at the rock.

“Ice,” said Alea.

“Mm.”

“I need to tell you something.”

He turned his head.

She was sitting on his shoulder, small and pale, with her tail round his neck. Her white pinprick eyes were very wide.

“I can’t open anything,” said Alea.

He didn’t understand.

“Windows,” she said. “Any of them. Your sheet. The rankings. The map. The log.” She was quiet. “I’ve been trying since the doors closed. I can’t. There’s nothing there. It’s like the stair. Like being on the stair between floors, when the threads go faint, except it’s not faint. It’s gone. There’s no window here.” Her ears went flat and then up again. “There’s nothing watching.”

He stood very still.

“Nothing,” he said.

“Nothing. Not the Steward. Not the thing behind the Steward. Not the floor. I can’t feel it at all.” She looked at the sky. “I don’t know why. Maybe it’s because it’s a milestone. Maybe it’s between. Maybe it just doesn’t bother, past the Gate, because nobody’s ever stayed.” She was quiet for a long time. “It’s not looking, Ice. For the first time since floor 4. For the first time since you told the dark everything, in that house. It’s not looking.”

He looked at the rock. At the reeds. At the bird.

At the far side of the tarn, up the slope, sitting on the grass with the lantern beside her and her knees up, Thea. Not asleep. Looking at the water.

“Until tomorrow,” he said.

“Until we go up the stair,” said Alea. “I think. I don’t know. I’m guessing.” She put her nose against his ear. “I’m guessing it’s one night.”

He spent the day not deciding.

He was very good at it. He’d been not deciding things for two hundred and eighty-five days and ten years before that. He cooked, because that was what his hands did when he wasn’t using them for anything else. There was nothing to cook with. There were reeds, and wild onions on the south slope, small and sharp, which he found in about four minutes because he always found the onions, and fish in the tarn, which Aleaka called up for him without being asked, asking them very politely, and three of them came. And Tuppence wasn’t here, and the wrens weren’t here, but there was a bird in the reeds, small and brown, and it came and sat on Aleaka’s shoulder halfway through the afternoon and stayed there.

He made a fire on the shore. Gabe built it, without being asked. They didn’t talk.

He cooked the fish with the onions on flat stones in the fire, and when people woke up, one by one, in the warm afternoon, there was food. They ate it on the grass with their hands. Nobody talked about the Gate. Nobody talked about the Steward or the count or the window or the two grey lines. They talked about the bird, and the flowers, and how warm it was, and Calvin’s theory that the cloud was solid and you could walk on it, which Salty offered to test by throwing him off the edge.

He watched them. All of them. Thirty-five people, sitting in the grass on the top of the tenth floor, eating fish with their hands in the sun.

I came from them, he’d said. That’s where.

It was true. It was the truest thing he’d ever said to anything in that tower. He’d said it to a thing with no face, and it had said that is not a place, and it was wrong. It was the only place he’d ever been from.

And there was one of them who didn’t know.

Not the theory. She’d had the theory since floor 6. She’d had the feeling since the white room. She’d read one record does not match under a window with her body locked and blood on her lip. She’d heard the fourth morning in the snow behind a long hall and put it down. She’d heard I remember all of it on a barracks step at two in the morning and said good.

She’d stepped round him.

Twice now. Once in a throne room on the hundredth floor, and once on the top of a pass in the dark of the moon. The same ward. The same wall of rewritten light. And the second time he’d seen her do it, and knelt there, and let them hold, and she’d lived.

You’ll be the first, he’d said. On a bridge. On a step. On a roof.

You don’t ask me that, and I don’t ask you what the first time was.

He’d asked her. On the roof. Don’t step in front of me. And she’d said no. And then she’d done it. And he’d knelt and watched and not got up.

That deal was finished. They’d both broken it, he thought. Or kept it. He didn’t know any more.

He watched her eat fish with her fingers on the far side of the fire, with Xola leaning on one shoulder and Gigi on the other, laughing at something Calvin had said, with the lantern beside her in the grass. Her ponytail had come loose. The pink and blue streaks were lying across her cheek.

He’d closed her eyes once.

He wasn’t going to do it again. Not without telling her why.

Gabe found him at dusk, on the far shore, by the rock.

He’d gone there without deciding to. He was standing at the water’s edge with his hands in his pockets, looking at the boulder in the reeds, and the sun going down behind the western peaks, and the sky over the sea of cloud going gold, then pink, then a deep slow violet. The bird had stopped singing. Somewhere behind him, at the fire, Newish was trying to teach the hold-folk song with all the verses to Tiger, and Tiger was getting it wrong on purpose.

Gabe came and stood beside him. On his right. He didn’t have his shield. He’d left it at the fire. He just stood there with his hands in his own pockets.

They didn’t say anything for a while.

“You’re going to tell her,” said Gabe.

It wasn’t a question. Gabe didn’t do questions about Declan any more. He’d stopped on floor 8.

“Yeah,” said Ice.

“Tonight.”

“Alea says nothing’s watching. Up here. Tonight.” He looked at the rock. “I don’t know if that’s true. She doesn’t either. But it’s the first time since four that I’ve—“ He stopped. “It might be the only night.”

Gabe nodded slowly.

“Good,” he said.

Ice looked at him.

“I mean it,” said Gabe. “Good. She should be first. You said she would be. On the bridge. Xav told me.” He was quiet. “I don’t mind, Dec. I thought I would. I don’t. I’d have minded if it wasn’t her.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

“I know enough,” said Gabe. “I know the bit that’s mine. I know I made you a fire.” He swallowed. “I know I made you a shield, and it broke. I don’t need you to tell me the rest. Not yet. Maybe not ever.” He looked at the water. “But she needs all of it. Doesn’t she. She’s the one who stepped round you.”

Ice didn’t answer. He looked at the rock in the reeds.

Gabe looked at him looking at it. Ice felt him do it. Felt him follow his eyes to the flat grey boulder at the water’s edge, and back to his face, and see whatever was on it. And go still.

He didn’t ask. Ice watched him not ask. He watched Gabe stand there on the shore of the tarn in the dusk with his hands in his pockets, putting it together the way he’d put everything together for two hundred and eighty-five days, from faces, without a word, and not say anything at all.

“Yeah,” said Gabe, eventually. Very quietly. “Yeah. I thought it might be that.”

He put his hand between Ice’s shoulder blades. Flat. The way he had at the top of the stair on floor 9. The way he had at the broken gap on floor 8.

“Go on, Dec,” said Gabe.

She was at the fire.

He walked back round the tarn in the last of the light, slowly, and the fire got closer, and the voices, and the smell of fish and onions. They were all round it. Thirty-five people, in the grass, on the top of the world, in the warm, with the stars starting to come out over the eastern ridge. Newish was still singing. Tiger was still getting it wrong. Hana had her crown off and was letting Bee draw her. Drew was sitting with his bare feet in the grass looking at his own open hand. Maigo had his root-foot in the earth and his eyes shut and looked happier than Ice had ever seen him.

She looked up when he came into the firelight.

He stopped. On the far side of the fire from her. He didn’t know what to say. He’d had two hundred and eighty-five days, and ten years, and he still didn’t know.

“Thea,” he said.

Everybody went quiet.

Not all at once. One at a time, round the fire, the way the wall had gone quiet every morning between thirty-five and thirty-six. Newish stopped singing. Tiger stopped getting it wrong. Bee’s charcoal stopped. Calvin, in the middle of telling Dennis something about the cloud, stopped with his mouth open, and Rachael said Calvin without looking, and he shut it.

Thea looked at him across the fire.

“Will you walk with me?” said Ice.

He didn’t know what his face was doing. He could feel thirty-four people looking at it. He could feel Gabe, behind him, on the edge of the firelight, not looking at anything. He could feel Xav, on the far side, with his hands on his knees, very still.

Thea looked at him for a long time.

And he watched her understand. He saw it happen. The way he’d seen her understand things on every floor, on a walkway on floor 2 with lines of light across it like code, at the top of a pit on floor 6, on a bridge on floor 7. Her face going still. Her eyes going very wide, and then very steady.

She’d known what you’ll be the first meant. She’d always known.

She stood up.

She picked up the lantern. She didn’t say anything. She came round the fire, past Xola, who let go of her arm, past Gigi, who looked up at her, and past Calvin, whose mouth was open again, and stopped in front of him. With the lantern in her hand. With his light in it.

“Yeah,” said Thea. “Okay.”

They walked away from the fire together. Round the tarn. Toward the far shore, and the rock in the reeds, and the cliff with the door in it, in the last of the dusk.

Behind them, nobody said anything for a long time.

Then, very quietly, from somewhere by the fire, he heard Xola say, in a voice that was trying very hard not to be wet:

“Babe.”

And then he heard Newish start the song again, softly. All the verses. And everybody else joining in, one at a time, round the fire. Badly. Loud enough that nobody on the far shore of the tarn would be able to hear a single word anybody said.

They sat on the rock.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d walked her round the tarn not knowing where he was going, and his feet had gone there, the way they’d gone to the far side of everything for ten years. The flat grey boulder at the water’s edge, half in the reeds. She sat on it. He sat beside her. With the lantern between them on the stone.

The stars were out. All of them. More than the night before; up here there was nothing between them and the sky, not even the thin air of the pass. And the cloud below the lip of the valley was silver in the starlight, endless, like a sea that had stopped moving.

She took her boots off.

He didn’t say anything. He watched her do it. She unlaced them, and pulled them off, and put her bare feet down into the water at the edge of the tarn, the way she had once before, a long time ago, on this rock, in a spring he’d never told anybody about.

“It’s cold,” said Thea.

“It always was.”

She turned her head.

He hadn’t meant to say it. It had just come out. The way always had come out at the Breath, and the fourth morning behind the long hall. He sat there on the rock beside her with his hands locked round his knees and his heart going so hard he could feel it in the scar on his palm.

“Ice,” said Thea.

“I know.”

“You said always.”

“I know.”

She didn’t ask. Even now. She sat there with her feet in the cold water and the lantern beside her and looked at him, and waited, the way she’d been waiting since a white room, and didn’t ask.

And then the sky moved.

He saw it first. Over the eastern ridge, low, above the sea of cloud. A smudge of green. Faint. Like light seen through a curtain. And then it wasn’t a smudge. It was a ribbon. Long and soft and green, unrolling across the sky above the peaks, slowly, the way a fern unrolls. And then another, beside it. Paler. With an edge of violet. And another. Moving. Folding. Rippling, the way a curtain ripples when somebody opens a window in another room. Across the whole sky. Over the valley, over the tarn, over the cliff and the door and the rock and the two of them sitting on it.

Green and violet and a pale cold blue. Silent. Enormous. Moving very slowly over the top of the world.

“Oh,” said Thea.

He’d never seen it. Not the first time. Not on any floor. In ten years he’d never once seen the sky do that.

He didn’t know if it was the tower. He didn’t think it was. Alea said nothing was watching. He thought it might just be the sky, on the top of a mountain, on the night of no moon, doing what skies do when there’s nobody there to see them. And there were two people there to see it.

Thea had her face turned up to it. The green was on her face. On the pink and blue streaks in her hair. On the lantern.

He looked at her for a long time.

On his shoulder, small and pale and faintly glowing, like moonlight through a curtain on a night with no moon, Alea pressed her nose against his ear.

“Nothing’s watching,” she said. “Go on.”

He breathed in.

“Thea,” he said. “The first time.”

She turned her head and looked at him.
