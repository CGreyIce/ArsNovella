Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 196

Midwinter

5,579 words · 24 min read

Six hundred and nine, said Alea.

He knew. He’d known since the Glass. He’d known, really, since a night on the Long Acre with a window in front of his eyes that nobody had written, ten years and a whole life ago. He’d been awake since Aleaka went out of the door. He’d heard her stop in the street. He’d heard the hens, and the dog, and the silence of the goat. He’d sat with his back to the wall by the stove and his hand on the pot and Thea’s head on his shoulder, and waited for the window in the kitchen wall to go grey.

It went grey.

She’s asleep, said Alea. She was on his knee, small, curled, the way she sat when she was trying to be no trouble. I can’t see her. I can’t see anybody on floor 1 who isn’t one of us. But Yan says she’s asleep. He’s been sitting under her floor all night. He says her light’s the same as yesterday. Kitchen.

“I know,” he said. Out loud, very quietly. Thea didn’t wake.

You don’t have to do anything yet.

“I know.”

Ice.

“I know, Alea.”

She was quiet for a while. Then she uncurled and climbed up onto his shoulder, where she’d sat on the first morning, a minute after the white room, when he’d stood in the arch on floor 1 under a sky like a ceiling and she’d said Ten years. Again.

I’m here, she said. I’m right here.

He put his hand up and touched the place on his shoulder where she was, though there was nothing there to touch.

He went out at first light with a cup of something hot. Aleaka was where he’d known she’d be, in the middle of Crooked Street, standing beside the Bridge Goat in the painted snow with her hands in her coat and snow on her shoulders and on the goat’s back, both of them looking up at the small window under the eaves. Somebody had put a coat round her in the night. Gabe’s. The wolf coat, down to her ankles. Gabe was asleep in the Spoon’s doorway in his shirt, with the grey dog lying across his legs.

Aleaka took the cup without looking away from the window.

“She’s asleep,” said Ice.

“I know.” Aleaka drank. “He knows too.” She meant the goat. “He’s not worried yet. He’s just watching.”

“Okay.”

“It’s not now,” said Aleaka. Slowly. “It’s today. But it’s not now.”

He looked at her. At the goat. At the crow on the spoon sign. At the street, empty and white, and the snow coming down on it in its rows from a painted sky, and the smoke from a hundred chimneys going straight up into the grey. Floor 1. Small and the same.

“Thank you,” he said. “For staying up with him.”

“He leaned,” said Aleaka. “In the night. A bit.” She smiled into her cup. “He’s never leaned.”

Mother Crane came down the stair at the usual time with her candle. Eleven breaths. He counted them. Kei, lying on the bench by the window with his eyes shut and his book on his chest, counted them; Ice saw his lips move. Eleven.

She was fine.

She came into the kitchen in her apron and her husband’s boots and looked at the stove, and at the long room full of people pretending to be asleep, and at him by the onion sack with the little knife already in his hand.

“You look like death,” she said. “Again.”

“Morning.”

“It’s barely morning.” She took the big knife off its hook. Then she stopped, and looked at the front door, which somebody had left open, and the street beyond it. “What’s that goat doing on my street?”

“Keeping watch,” said Ice.

“Of what?”

He didn’t answer.

Mother Crane went to the door and looked out. At the goat, and Aleaka in a wolf coat beside it, and the crow on her sign, and the grey dog from the inn asleep across a large man’s legs on her doorstep. She looked for a long time.

“Animals,” she said at last. “Something in the weather.” She sniffed. “My hens didn’t lay. Not one. They always lay.” She came back and sat down at the long table, across from him, with the onion sack between them. “Something in the weather,” she said again, as if she were deciding it.

They peeled.

She talked about her husband. He’d known she would; she always did, in the mornings, at the onions, and he’d been grateful for it every morning for sixty-three days, because it meant he didn’t have to say anything. Today it was the carts. The winter he’d driven the cart up to the high farms in a blizzard because old Brannock’s wife was having a baby and the midwife wouldn’t go, and he’d come back three days later frozen to the seat with the midwife wrapped in every blanket in the cart and the baby named after him. Tam Brannock. Ugliest baby on the floor. Looked just like him. She laughed. She laughed so much she had to put the knife down, and wipe her eyes with her wrist.

He watched her laugh.

He watched her hands on the knife, knotted and floury and steady. He watched her colour, red from the stove, going down when she stepped away from it. He watched her breathe.

She was fine.

It’s not now, Aleaka had said. It’s today. But it’s not now.

“You’re as bad as that doctor,” said Mother Crane, catching him. “Looking. Always looking. What’s wrong with all of you this winter?”

“Nothing,” he said.

“Liar.” She threw an onion skin at him. It came off whole. It always did, for her. “Peel.”

He peeled.

He made the broth at ten.

He used her pot. The good one. He’d carried it six hundred and nine days, up thirteen floors and down thirteen again, with a dent in it from a tree on nine and a scorch on the bottom from a fire on the Salt where there was nothing to burn but what they’d carried. It had been his pot so long that it didn’t feel like a thing any more. It felt like a hand. He took it off his pack, where it had hung by its strap since the first morning, and washed it at her pump in the yard, and brought it in and put it on her stove.

Mother Crane looked at it on her stove.

“That’s my pot,” she said.

“Yes.”

“On my stove.”

“Yes.”

“I thought you weren’t giving it back.”

“I’m not,” he said. “Not yet. I’m making something in it.”

She folded her arms and watched him. He made Plain Broth the way she’d taught him on the first morning, a year and a half ago, with her standing over him saying nothing, which had been worse than shouting. Bones and water and an onion cut in half with the skin on for the colour. Salt. A carrot. Thyme. Let it go slow. Don’t touch it. Skim it. Don’t touch it. He’d made it on every floor. He’d made it on the Salt, where there was no water but what Xola made, and on fourteen, in the Long Barn, for seven hundred people, a hundred pots, and looked at every face before he filled the bowl. He’d never made it in this pot on this stove.

It smelled the way it had always smelled. It smelled like the first morning.

“What’s it for?” said Mother Crane.

“Later.”

“Later for what?”

“Just later.”

She looked at him a long time with her small sharp eyes.

“You’re a strange boy,” she said. “All of you. Strange.” She went back to her bread. “Don’t let it boil.”

“I know.”

“I know you know. I’m saying it anyway.”

They were all in the Spoon by noon.

Nobody had organised it. Drew hadn’t said anything. Higu hadn’t. It had just happened, the way the thirty-six did things now, without anybody deciding; one by one they came in from the mill and the smith’s and the yard and the class hall and the inn, with reasons. Salty had come to look at a hinge. Gabe had come to bring wood. Hyvred had come to sit by the window because the light was better. Calvin hadn’t bothered with a reason. Calvin had just come in and sat down at the long table and put his feet up on it.

“Feet,” said Mother Crane.

He took them down.

“Have none of you got anywhere to be?” She stood in the kitchen with her hands on her hips, looking at her cookhouse, full to the walls at noon on a weekday with thirty-five people doing nothing, very carefully. “It’s the middle of the day. Don’t you lot climb things? Go and climb something.”

“It’s winter,” said Drew.

“It’s always winter, with you lot, apparently.” She looked at the pantry door, half open, where WINTER WATCH was written in chalk on the inside in Drew’s square letters. She looked at it a moment longer than she’d looked at anything else. Then she sniffed. “Well, if you’re staying, you’re peeling.”

So they peeled. All of them. The whole of the Copper Spoon at noon on the six hundred and ninth day, thirty-five people round a long table peeling turnips and onions and carrots for a stew none of them would remember eating, because Mother Crane had decided that if they were going to sit in her cookhouse they were going to be useful, and nobody argued.

Newish made her laugh at one. She did it every day; she’d put herself on the rota for it before Drew had finished drawing the grid. Today it was a story about Mother Tallow on floor 2, who had a mouth on her even worse than Newish’s, and who’d made Ice split wood properly with an axe while he was still kneeling in the snow, and Mother Crane laughed so hard at properly that she had to sit down.

She sat down at the kitchen end of the long table. On the bench. Across from him. The way she’d sat across from him every morning for sixty-three days.

She was still laughing. She put her hand flat on the table, to steady herself, the way you do when you’ve laughed too much.

He looked at her hand.

It was trembling.

It was so small.

That was what he’d think about, afterwards. For the rest of his life. How small it was. A tremor in an old woman’s hand on a table after she’d laughed at a joke. Nobody else saw it. Newish didn’t. Calvin didn’t. Thea, beside him, didn’t, because she was laughing too, at properly, with her head back. It was just a hand on a table, shaking a little, the way hands shake.

Kei saw it.

Kei was on the bench by the window with a turnip in one hand and a knife in the other, and Ice saw him see it, across the room, and stop. Not move. Just stop. With the knife halfway through the turnip. And look at Ice. And Ice looked back at him, and neither of them moved, and the whole Spoon went on laughing round them for a second longer.

“Ooh,” said Mother Crane. She’d stopped laughing. She put her other hand to her face. “It’s warm in here.”

It wasn’t. It was midwinter, and the door was open, and the snow was coming in.

“Is it warm?” said Mother Crane. “I’m warm. Somebody’s built my fire up too high.” She looked at the stove. “Gabe. Have you been at my fire?”

“No,” said Gabe. Very quietly.

“Somebody has.” She went to stand up. She got halfway. She put her hand on the table again, and sat back down, heavily, on the bench, with a small surprised sound. “Ooh,” she said again. “My feet. I’ve been on my feet since five.” She laughed. It didn’t come out right. “I’ll just sit a minute.”

The Spoon had stopped laughing.

All of it. All at once. Thirty-five people at a long table with turnips in their hands, and not one of them moving. Newish had her mouth open in the middle of a word. Calvin’s feet were on the floor. The snow came in at the open door in its rows and lay on the threshold and nobody shut the door.

Kei put the turnip down. And the knife. And got up, and came down the long room, not fast, not slow, the way he’d walk to a bed, and sat down on the bench beside her. On her other side. He didn’t touch her. Not yet.

“Mother Crane,” he said.

“What?” She was looking at the stove. “What is it? Why’s everybody gone quiet? Is it the stew? I haven’t put it on yet. It’s not even—“ She stopped. She put the back of her hand against her own cheek. “I am warm,” she said, in a different voice. “That’s funny. I’m never warm.”

“Can I?” said Kei.

She looked at him. At his hand, held up, a little way from her face. At his face.

“You and your looking,” she said. But she didn’t say no.

Kei put the back of his hand against her forehead.

He held it there for a long time. Ice watched his face. He didn’t need to. He knew. He’d known since the Long Acre, since the Glass, since the white room, since a window in the snow. He knew what Kei was going to say before Kei’s face did anything at all.

Kei took his hand away.

“It’s started,” he said. To Ice. Not to her.

Same day, said Alea, on his shoulder. Very, very quietly. Midwinter. Six hundred and nine. Same day.

He didn’t say anything. He found that he’d stood up. He didn’t remember doing it.

“What’s started?” said Mother Crane. “What are you talking about?” She looked from Kei to him. “Onion boy. What’s he talking about?”

“You’ve got a fever,” said Kei.

“I’ve never had a fever in my life.”

“You’ve got one now.”

“Nonsense.” She put both hands on the table and pushed herself up. “I’ve a stew to put on. I’ve thirty-six people in my cookhouse who’ve done nothing all day but peel. I’ve—“

Her legs went.

Not much. Just at the knee, the way a step goes when you’ve misjudged it in the dark. She swayed. Her hand went out for the table and missed.

He caught her.

He was already there. He’d been there since he stood up; he’d come round the end of the long table without knowing he was doing it, the way he’d come round a corner in Tell into a crypt full of grey, the way he’d come down a lane on the Long Acre into the snow. He caught her under the arms as her knees went, and she was so light. So much lighter than he’d thought. She’d been so big, in his head, for ten years. Short and wide and loud and furious, a woman you couldn’t move. And she weighed nothing. She weighed the same as Mother Tallow had, on the night of the Long Storm, when he’d carried her up her own tower.

“Put me down,” said Mother Crane, into his shoulder. “You great—put me down—I’ve a stew—“

“Gabe’s got the stew.”

“Gabe can’t cook.”

“Thea’s watching him.”

She made a sound against his shoulder that was half a laugh and half something else.

“Upstairs,” said Kei. He’d stood up too. He had his book under his arm and his hand already in his coat, on the jar. “Her bed. Now. Rachael—“

“Here.” Rachael was already at the foot of the stair behind the stove, with her gloves in her hand.

He carried her up.

He’d never been up the stair.

Not in either life. He’d lain at the bottom of it for sixty-three nights with his hand on the pot, and looked at it, and thought about it. Narrow and steep, behind the stove, the wood worn pale in the middle of each step by forty years of her feet and her mother’s and her gran’s. He’d counted her up it every night. Eleven breaths.

It was fourteen steps. He counted them. He carried her up them with her head on his shoulder and her husband’s boots knocking against his knees, and she said put me down on every one of them, more quietly each time, until on the twelfth she stopped saying it.

The room was under the eaves. He’d known it would be. He’d known since a night on the Long Acre, when he’d stood under a bare tree in the snow and thought her bed over the kitchen and never once seen it.

Small. Low, so he had to duck under the beam. One window, a small square one in the slope of the roof, with snow on the outside of it. A narrow bed with a patched quilt. A wooden chair beside it, old, with the seat worn into a shape that wasn’t hers. A chest. A shelf with a candle on it and a comb and a cup. And on the floor at the foot of the bed, side by side, neatly, the way you’d leave them for somebody who was going to come in and put them on in the morning, a second pair of boots. Big ones. Old. Polished.

Not the ones she wore. The ones she didn’t.

He put her down on the bed. Carefully. The way you’d put down something that might break. She lay there on the patched quilt with her face flushed and her eyes open, looking up at the beam over the bed, and the snow on the window, and him.

“Onion boy,” she said.

“I’m here.”

“You’re in my room.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody’s been in my room. Not since him.” She looked at the boots at the foot of the bed. Then back at him. “You’re very rude.”

“I know.”

“Get out.”

“No,” he said.

She looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were very bright. Too bright. He’d seen eyes like that before. More than once. In more than one life. He’d been frightened then. He’d been frightened in a way he’d never let himself be frightened since, because he’d learned that it didn’t help.

He was frightened now. All the way down. He let himself be.

“No,” said Mother Crane, very softly. “I didn’t think you’d go.”

Kei did the first dose at three.

He did it at the chest by the window, with his book open on it at the inside back cover and his little knife and his cloth and a cup, and he didn’t let anybody else near. Willow bark from the river below the mill, steeped. Ma Yarrow’s root, fresh-dried. A spoonful of Lammas honey. And from the jar, with the tip of the knife, a grain of the dark blue flower. One grain. The edge.

That’s the right amount, said Dose, round his neck, loud enough that Ice almost heard it.

Kei held the cup to Mother Crane’s mouth, and she drank it, and made a face, and said it was the worst thing she’d ever tasted and she’d been married to a carter. And Kei wrote the time down, twice, and sat back on the edge of the chest with his hands on his knees, and let out a breath that Ice thought he’d been holding since the Long Barn.

Then there was nothing to do but sit.

The rota said ICE. The first three hours. Kei had written it there on purpose; Kei had told Drew on the stairs, and Drew had told him. Because that’s where he’ll want to be. Because a measure you don’t let somebody keep isn’t a measure, it’s a punishment. So he sat. On the chair. Her husband’s chair, with the seat worn into somebody else’s shape. Beside the bed.

He’d brought the broth up.

She didn’t want it. She said so. She said she wasn’t hungry, and she said it was too hot, and then she said she was cold, and pulled the quilt up, and her teeth chattered, and Rachael came and put a second blanket on her and went back to stand by the door with her gloves off and her hands ready. And then she said what’s that smell, and he told her, and she turned her head on the pillow and looked at the pot in his lap.

“That’s my pot,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You made something in it.”

“You taught me.”

“I did not. I showed you the once.” She looked at the broth. “Plain Broth,” she said. “Bones and an onion with the skin on. For the colour.”

“Yes.”

“Did you skim it?”

“Twice.”

“Hm.” She looked at it a while longer. “Go on, then.”

He fed her. A spoonful at a time. Out of her own pot, with one of her own spoons from the drawer in the kitchen. She drank it. She made a face at the first spoonful and said too much salt and drank the second. And the third. He didn’t say anything. He just fed her, slowly, the way you’d feed somebody who’d once handed you two loaves and a pot on a cold morning at the bottom of a crooked street, when you had nothing, and said you’ll pay me when you come back down.

“You came down alone,” said Mother Crane. Halfway through. Her eyes were half shut. “That first morning. Ahead of the rest.”

“Yes.”

“Gran said that.” She drank another spoonful. “One comes down alone. She never said what happened to them.”

“I know.”

“I’ll find out, maybe.” She smiled, a little, with her eyes shut. “Now.”

He held the spoon very still.

“Drink your broth,” he said.

She drank her broth.

Thea lit the candle at dusk.

He’d asked her to. He couldn’t let go of the spoon, and he couldn’t let go of the chair, and he’d asked her, when she came up the stair at the end of the light with the lantern, to take the thing out of his vest pocket. The left one. Where he kept the things he kept. The blank card from the white room. Bee’s drawing of his face. The little bronze bell from seven. Mother Tallow’s first candle, the best one, the one she’d given him on the night of the Long Storm, for your way up, in case nobody’s keeping one for you, that he’d lit once, in the Fen, and never again.

And beside it, the second one. Plain tallow. Hand-dipped. For her window. So she knows somebody’s keeping one. When she’s poorly. It helps, knowing. It helped me.

Thea took the second one out. She looked at him. He nodded.

She put it in the window. The small square one in the slope of the roof, with the snow on the outside. She stood it on the sill in a dish from the shelf, and lit it from the lantern, and it caught, and the small yellow flame stood up straight in the cold air of the window and didn’t move.

Then she put the lantern down beside it. On the sill. The amber light and the yellow one, side by side, in the window under the eaves of the Copper Spoon at the bottom of Crooked Street, where everybody in the street could see them.

Down in the street, the goat lay down. Ice heard Aleaka say so, later. The old goat had stood all night and all day in the middle of Crooked Street looking up at the dark window. And when the candle went in, and the lantern beside it, he lay down. Right there, on the cobbles, in the snow. With his chin on his knees. Facing the light.

Mother Crane turned her head on the pillow and looked at the window.

“Who’s that for?” she said. “The candle.”

“You,” said Thea.

“I’m not out there. I’m in here.”

“It’s so you know somebody’s keeping one,” said Thea. “A woman on floor 2 sent it. She’s kept a light in her window every night since she was nine. She said it helps, knowing.”

Mother Crane looked at the candle for a long time. The light was in her eyes. Too bright. Too bright.

“Floor 2,” she said.

“Floor 2.”

“Hm.” She shut her eyes. “That’s a long way to send a candle.”

“She’s very old,” said Thea. “She’s allowed.”

And Mother Crane laughed. Just a breath of it. With her eyes shut, flushed and burning, in her own bed under the eaves. And Ice sat on her husband’s chair with her pot in his lap and her spoon in his hand and listened to her laugh, and didn’t let himself think anything at all.

Chi came at six.

He’d known she would. The rota said ICE and then CHI, and she came up the stair at six exactly, with Waddles left at the bottom with Hyvred and a cup of something hot in her hands. She didn’t say anything. She came in and ducked under the beam and stood at the foot of the bed, by the boots, and looked at him.

“Your three hours,” said Chi.

“I know.”

“Kei’s rule.”

“I know.”

He didn’t move.

Chi looked at him a while longer. Then she came and sat down on the end of the chest, by the window, by the candle and the lantern, across the narrow room from him. And put her hands round her cup. And looked at him. Steadily. The way she’d looked at him across the Spoon every night for sixty-three nights. The way she’d looked at him at the door on six, at dawn, and said breathe.

He breathed.

It came off her like warmth off a wall. He could feel it. He’d always been able to feel it, since the cistern, since she’d told him she was terrified and he’d told her it never sounds like it when it works. He sat in the dead man’s chair beside the burning old woman and felt Chi be calm at him from across the room, and his hands, which he hadn’t known were shaking, stopped.

He put the spoon in the pot. He put the lid on the pot. He stood up.

“Six,” he said to Chi.

“Six,” said Chi. “Go and sleep.”

“I won’t sleep.”

“Then go and not sleep somewhere else.” She didn’t look away from him. “Thea’s on the bottom step. She’s been there since dusk. Go and sit with her.”

He went.

He stopped in the door, under the beam, and looked back. At the bed. At the old woman in it with her eyes shut and her face burning and her chest going up and down, too fast, too shallow. At Rachael by the wall with her hands bare and ready. At Kei on the chest by the window with his book. At Chi on the other end of the chest, with her cup, being calm at nobody now, at the room, at the old woman, at the candle. At the candle in the window, and the lantern beside it.

At the boots, at the foot of the bed, side by side, polished. Waiting for somebody to come in and put them on in the morning.

He went down the stair.

Thea was on the bottom step.

She’d been there since dusk, Chi had said. She was sitting on the bottom step of the narrow stair behind the stove, with her back against the wall and her knees up, in the dark of the kitchen, with no light. She’d left the lantern in the window. She looked up when he came down.

He sat down next to her.

The kitchen was dark. The long room beyond it was full of people not sleeping. He could feel them, all thirty-five, all through the Spoon, on benches and floors and against walls, every thread in his chest pulled taut and quiet, the way they’d been on the Long Acre at the line, the way they’d been in the crypt in Tell, the way they’d been at the top of every stair. Nobody was asleep. Nobody was talking. Somebody had shut the front door. Somebody had banked the fire.

Over by the far wall of the kitchen, a long way from the stair, he could see Yan. Sitting on the floor with his back against the pantry door, on the other side of WINTER WATCH, with his scythe across his knees and Suzu round his neck. Looking up. At the ceiling. At the boards above the kitchen, where the room was.

“Yan,” said Ice. Quietly.

Yan didn’t look down.

“Her light,” said Ice.

“It’s there,” said Yan. “Kitchen. It’s gone smaller. Since three.” His voice was cool and level, the way it always was. “It’s not moving. It’s just smaller.”

“You can see it? Through the boards?”

“I can see everybody’s, through anything. You know that.” Yan was quiet a moment. “I’m not going up. Wherever I stand there’s an edge. I’m not putting one by her bed.”

“I know.”

“If it moves,” said Yan. “I’ll tell you.”

Ice looked at him across the dark kitchen. At his face, tipped up toward the ceiling, watching a light nobody else could see. Yan, whose lot he’d gone to on Earth when everything with Thea and Gigi went wrong, and who’d never once asked why. Yan, who walked the dead and wasn’t frightened of anything. Yan, who was only ever good to his own.

“Thank you,” said Ice.

“Don’t,” said Yan. “She makes the porridge.”

But the corner of his mouth went up, in the dark.

Ice sat on the bottom step with Thea. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. After a while she took his hand, and held it on her knee, and they sat there together in the dark at the bottom of the stair.

He’d sat in the dark like this once before. In the other life. On the Long Acre, under the third beam of a barn, a month after this night, with a window in front of his eyes that nobody had written. He’d gone out into the snow and stood under a bare tree, and then come back in and sat down in the dark, and somebody had come and sat down next to him with a lamp, and not asked, and stayed till it got light.

She hadn’t known why, that time.

She knew why now.

“Second winter,” he said. Very quietly. To her. “Find her a doctor.”

Thea’s hand tightened on his.

“I wrote that,” he said. “On the first night. In my diary. On floor 1. I’d been awake a minute in the white room and I’d written the note and I’d run and I’d punched a goat and I’d peeled her onions, and I wrote it down so I wouldn’t forget. Second winter. Find her a doctor.” He looked at the dark. “I thought I’d have to find one. On some floor. Bring them down. I thought it’d be one person.”

“It’s not one person,” said Thea.

“No.” He looked up the stair. At the line of light under the door at the top, yellow and amber. “I found thirty-five.”

Thea didn’t say anything. She put her head on his shoulder.

Above them, through the boards, an old woman coughed in her sleep. And then breathed. And then breathed again.

He counted them.

“You’re very rude.” “Get out.” “No.” “I didn’t think you’d go.”
Kei, three o’clock: willow, root, honey, a grain. “The worst thing I’ve ever tasted and I was married to a carter.”
Broth from her pot. “Too much salt.” “You came down alone. Gran never said what happened to them. I’ll find out, maybe. Now.” “Drink your broth.”
Thea lit Mother Tallow’s candle in her window. The lantern beside it. The goat lay down in the street facing the light.
“That’s a long way to send a candle.” “She’s very old. She’s allowed.”
Chi at six. “Your three hours.” I breathed.
Yan, under her floor: “It’s smaller. Since three. It’s not moving.” “She makes the porridge.”
Second winter. Find her a doctor. I found thirty-five.
