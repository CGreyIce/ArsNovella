Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 66

One Floor Ahead

3,647 words · 16 min read

Hyvred went back to the bowl at dusk on the hundred-and-twenty-fourth day, because she had to see where he’d gone.

Bee came with her. Bee always came. They climbed out of the cistern while the others were getting the litter ready, and walked east across the cooling Glass, the two of them, without saying anything. The sun was going down red behind them and their shadows went out long in front, over the glittering dunes, toward the bowl.

On Earth, Hyvred had seen the world through a tunnel. A circle of it, in front of her, and the dark round the edges, closing a little more every year. She’d made a joke out of it, once, on stream. A clip of what it looked like from inside, a little circle of light moving round a black screen. And somebody in her chat had said it looked like a game mode, the one where you could only see round your cursor. Then he’d found out it was real, and apologised so hard and so long that she’d laughed till she cried.

And then she’d woken up in a white room and could see everything.

Not just everything in front of her. Everything behind things. Where light had been. Where people had stood, and sat, and touched. What they’d felt while they were doing it. The whole world, lit up, every inch of it remembering. She’d spent the first week on floor 1 crying at sheep.

She’d been following one light since floor 1. Through every floor. She knew it better than her own.

And yesterday she’d seen it go up like a bonfire.

The bowl was full of it.

She stopped on the western rim, where they’d come down yesterday, and Bee stopped beside her and waited. And Hyvred looked.

His light was everywhere down there. The old trail, the road made of lamplight, going in from the west and turning round at two hundred metres. Over and over, a dozen times, two dozen, all those days, coming in and turning and going away. And from the north, and the south, and even from the far east, more trails, coming in and turning. A web of them, all over the bowl. All stopping in a ring, about two hundred metres out from where the hole had been.

She could see the hole’s place, too. There was nothing there now but shards of broken glass, lying on the gold glass of the bowl in a ring. But where it had stood, the light was wrong. Not light at all. A sort of absence. The way a room looks after somebody’s taken a picture off the wall that’s been there for years, and the wallpaper’s a different colour underneath.

And then yesterday’s trail.

From the north rim. Coming down the north slope in a straight line. Running, she could see it was running, long strides, far apart. Not stopping. Not turning. Straight down into the middle of the bowl, to the place where Thea had fallen. And there—

She put her hand over her mouth.

“What?” said Bee. Quietly.

“It’s still there,” said Hyvred.

Where he’d stood over Thea, there was a place on the glass that blazed. Even now, a day later, in the dusk, it was the brightest thing in the bowl. Brighter than anything she’d seen of his. It was like looking at the place where a lamp has stood all night on a table, and the table’s still warm. It went up. It went out. In all directions. Like the light from a fire, when somebody throws a whole armful of dry wood on it at once.

And then, beside it, where he’d stood up. Where he’d turned round. The light went small. All at once, the brightest and then the smallest. Like the fire had gone out and somebody was holding a match.

And the trail went north. Back up the slope, running. Small, small, small.

“He was so frightened,” said Hyvred. “Bee. Look at it. Look how small it goes.”

Bee couldn’t see it. Bee had never been able to see any of it. But Bee looked anyway, where Hyvred was looking, at the bare glass of the north slope, for a long time.

“He saw her,” said Bee. “And it went up.”

“It went up.” Hyvred’s voice cracked. “It went up like he’d been holding his breath for a hundred days.”

“And then he ran.”

“And then he ran.”

The trail went up the north slope and over the rim. Then it turned. Not west, back toward the cistern, or Deepwell, or the stair. East. Round the rim of the bowl, high, fast. And then, on the eastern side, it went down again. Down into the bowl from the east, in a long straight line. Toward the middle. Toward the ring of black rocks, and the blue.

Toward the water.

Hyvred followed it with her eyes. Down the eastern slope. Across the floor of the bowl. All the way to the edge of the ring of black rocks. And past them. To the edge of the pool.

It stopped there. On the shore. For a long time. The light lay there in a heap, the way it did where somebody had sat down. It was faint, from here, a kilometre off. But she could see it. Sitting on the shore of the Still Water. Through the night.

“He’s going for the boss,” she said. “Bee. He’s sitting at the water. He’s going for it.”

Bee looked at the blue pool in the middle of the bowl, going violet now in the dusk.

“Can we—“

“No,” said Hyvred. “No. Thea. We’ve got to get Thea back.” She was already turning away. It hurt. It felt like turning away from a door with somebody behind it. “And he doesn’t want us to. He ran, Bee. He ran from us.”

They walked back to the cistern in the dark. Bee put her hand in Hyvred’s halfway, without saying anything, and Hyvred held it all the way.

They walked back to Deepwell through the night, carrying Thea on the litter. She said she could walk. Xola said she could not walk. Xola won.

They came over the lip of the pit as the sun came up on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day.

And Gabe was there.

He was sitting on the stone lip of the pit by the top of the bucket chain, where the path came in from the east. In the wolf coat, in the heat, with both shields across his knees. His own round one, and the other one, with the lamp on it and the dark red rim. He had the look of somebody who’d been sitting in one place for a very long time. Two and a half days, Hyvred found out later. He’d sat there since they left. Ostrel had brought him water.

He stood up when he saw them. Slowly. His eyes went over them, one by one, counting. Ten. All ten. Then the litter. Then Thea on the litter, with her shoulder bound and her face pale.

His face went white.

“She’s okay,” said Xola, very fast, before he could say anything. “She’s okay, she’s okay, I fixed it, Kei fixed the fever, she’s okay—“

Gabe looked at Thea. Thea looked back at him from the litter.

“Hey, Gabe,” she said.

Gabe sat back down on the lip of the pit. Hard.

Then he looked at Hyvred.

She’d known he would. She’d known all the way back across the Glass, through the whole long night, carrying the litter in turns. She’d been practising what to say in her head and none of it was right. She went over and sat down beside him on the hot stone, with the morning sun on both of them.

“Did you find him,” said Gabe.

“Yes.”

He didn’t say anything for a second. His hands had gone white on the rim of the lamp shield.

“Did you tell him,” he said.

“No,” said Hyvred. “Gabe. I’m sorry. I didn’t get to.” Her voice was going. “He didn’t stop. He didn’t say anything to any of us. He came, and he—and then he ran, Gabe. I didn’t get to say anything. I didn’t get to say still here. He was gone before anybody could—“

“Was he okay?”

She stopped.

Gabe was looking at her. Not angry. Not anything. Just looking, with the face he had when somebody he loved was hurt and he couldn’t get to them.

“You can see it,” said Gabe. “His light. You can see how he—you always know. Hyv. Was he okay?”

Hyvred looked at him for a long moment.

She could have lied. It would have been easy. He looked fine. He looked strong. He killed it with one stroke. All true. But she’d been seeing that light for a hundred and twenty-five days, and she’d never once lied to anybody about it, and she wasn’t going to start with Gabe.

“No,” she said. “He wasn’t.”

Gabe didn’t move.

“He’s been so frightened,” said Hyvred. “Gabe. For weeks. There’s a hole in the desert he’s been sleeping in, and it’s soaked with it. With his light gone small. Smaller than I’ve ever seen it on anyone.” She swallowed. “And then he came over the hill and saw Thea on the ground and it went up. Like a bonfire. I’ve never seen anything like it. And then he stood up and ran, and it went small again. Smaller than before. All the way up the hill.”

Gabe looked down at the lamp shield on his knees.

“Okay,” he said.

“Gabe—“

“Okay.” He didn’t look up. “Thank you. For telling me the truth.”

They sat there on the lip of the pit in the morning sun. Down below them the bucket chain clanked and slopped, and somebody on the third ring was singing.

Drew told everyone in the round room on the third ring, before he’d even eaten. All thirty-five. Standing in the middle of the room, with Thea on a pile of cushions by the wall and Xola sitting beside her like a guard. And he wrote it on a slate as he said it, line by line, the way he’d promised.

DAY 123. THE BOWL EAST OF THE CISTERN.

SOMETHING THAT SHOWS YOU WHAT YOU WANT. WE ALL SAW SOMETHING. MOST OF US SAW HIM.

THEA SAW IT THROUGH THE LANTERN. IT CUT HER.

A MAN IN A WHITE HELM CAME DOWN THE NORTH SLOPE. HE DIDN’T LOOK AT IT UNTIL HE WAS IN THE LANTERN’S LIGHT.

HE KILLED IT WITH ONE STROKE.

THERE WAS A FOX MADE OF LIGHT ON HIS SHOULDER. ONE SECOND.

HE SAID THREE WORDS: NOT THIS TIME.

HE RAN NORTH.

HYVRED: IT’S HIM.

He put the chalk down.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Thirty-five people in a round stone room at the bottom of a pit in the desert, reading a slate.

“Not this time,” said Calvin, eventually. Slowly. “What does that mean?”

Nobody answered.

Hyvred, sitting by the wall with Bee, looked round the room. She couldn’t help it. Ten people in that room knew what Kei had said at the bottom of the cistern. Twenty-five didn’t. She watched the twenty-five read the slate, and she could see them not understanding. Calvin frowning. Dennis’s mouth open. Rachael’s frown going deeper and deeper. And she watched the ten not look at each other.

And she watched Xav.

Xav was sitting at the back, where he always sat, with his hands on his knees. He’d read the slate once, all the way down. Then he’d gone back up to NOT THIS TIME and stopped there. And he hadn’t moved since. He sat very still, the way he always sat. Except this was a different kind of still. The kind where you’ve stopped breathing and haven’t noticed.

Then he looked across the room. At Thea.

Thea looked back at him.

Neither of them said anything.

Bee showed them the drawing afterwards. Not the whole room. Just a few of them, by the window, when the room had broken up into knots of people talking low.

She’d drawn it in the cistern, the night after. By lantern light. In her sketchbook, in charcoal, the way she’d drawn everything since floor 1. Not one of her drawings that came off the page. Just a drawing.

A fox.

Small. Sitting, with its tail curled round its feet. Narrow face, pointed ears. Two tiny white dots for eyes, done with the very edge of the charcoal where she’d rubbed it away. She’d done the light round it by smudging the charcoal outward with her thumb, so it glowed off the page, sort of. And underneath, in her handwriting: his shoulder. one second.

Aleaka looked at it for a long time.

Aleaka, who’d been followed by every animal on every floor since the white room. With Sleet pressed against her legs, and forty gulls on the roof of every inn in Tether, and two fox ears on the top of her own head, one gold and one auburn.

“It’s a fox,” she said slowly.

“It was a fox,” said Bee. “Teal and white. Made of light. Like the systems. But not a ball. A fox.”

Aleaka looked at it a while longer.

Then she started to laugh.

It came out of her all at once, in her slow, stumbling, careful way. Laughing and then crying and then laughing again, with her hand over her mouth and her eyes streaming. Everyone looked at her. Sleet whined and put her paws up on Aleaka’s knee.

“Leaka?” said Hana.

“He made it a fox,” said Aleaka. She could barely get it out. “His Alea. His—whatever it is. His little light. Everybody else’s is a ball. He made his a fox.” She wiped her eyes. “I asked him once. On a call. Years ago. If he named them all Alea after me. Every companion. Every pet. And he said obviously. Every single one. Trust.” She laughed again, wetly. “He was lying. He’d been doing it since before he met me. I knew he was lying.”

She looked down at the drawing of a little fox with white pinprick eyes.

“And then he went and made it a fox,” she said. Very softly. “Up here. Where I’d never see it.” She touched the edge of the page. “The idiot. The idiot.”

Nobody said anything.

Hana put her arm round Aleaka’s shoulders. Milli put his hand on her back. Bee tore the page out of the sketchbook, carefully, and put it in Aleaka’s hands. Aleaka held it against her chest and didn’t let go of it for the rest of the day.

At noon on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, every window in Deepwell opened at once.

Hyvred was on the lip of the pit when it happened. She’d gone back up there because she couldn’t stay in the round room. Too many people. Too much light, everybody’s, all mixed up and worried. She was sitting on the hot stone with her feet hanging over the edge, looking east. Gabe was beside her. He hadn’t moved from the lip all morning except to go down for the slate.

Her window came up on its own. Everybody’s did. She heard the sound of it all round the pit, thirty-five windows and a whole town’s worth of people looking up, all at once.

Gabe made a sound.

And out in the east, far away, over the edge of the white horizon, something happened to the sky.

A light went up. White. From a point a long way off, right in the middle of the Glass, where Hyvred knew the bowl was. Where the blue water was. A column of white light, straight up into the white sky, so bright it showed even against the noon. It lasted a second. Two. Then it broke apart into lines. Dozens of thin white lines going out in every direction from the point, like the spokes of a wheel, like a star. And then they went out.

Everybody on the lip of the pit had seen it. People were pointing. Somebody below was shouting.

“Noon,” said Hyvred. “He did it at noon.”

“Did what?” said Gabe.

“The boss. The thing in the water.” She was staring at the place on the horizon where the light had been. “He did it in the middle of the day. In the worst of it. With the sun right overhead.” She didn’t know why that mattered. It just did. She could feel it, somehow, the way she could feel the light. “He could have waited for night. Anybody would have. He went in at noon.”

Gabe was very quiet.

“Of course he did,” he said at last. “He always does it the hard way.”

The next morning, the rankings changed.

Hyvred checked them at dawn. She checked them every dawn, the way Chris did, the way Thea did, the way all of them did now. She sat up on her bedroll in the round room with the grey light coming down the pit, and opened them.

Voyager · Lv 38 · Knight · Floor 7.

She looked at it for a long time.

Floor 7.

He’d gone up. In the night, or the evening. While they were down in the pit sleeping. He’d killed the thing in the water at noon, in the white glare, and then he’d gone up the stair. One floor ahead. The way he’d always been.

She felt two things at once, and she didn’t know what to do with either of them.

He was through. He’d got past the hole in the bowl and the thing in the water and whatever else had stopped him for twenty-five days. He’d got through, and he was alive, and he was on floor 7, and his name was still at the top of the list.

And he was gone again.

She got up, quietly, so as not to wake Bee. She climbed the stairs to the lip of the pit.

Gabe was there. Of course he was. Sitting on the hot stone in the dawn with the two shields across his knees, looking east. He’d seen it. She could tell from his back.

She sat down beside him.

“Floor 7,” said Gabe.

“Floor 7.”

“One floor ahead.” He didn’t look at her. “Again.”

Hyvred looked east, at the place where the white light had gone up, at noon, yesterday. And at the place beyond it where she couldn’t see, where somewhere there was a stair, and a door, and a man in a white helm going up it alone.

“He was there, though,” she said. “Gabe. For a minute. On the same floor as us. He came down the hill. He came to us.” She swallowed. “He didn’t have to. He could have stayed on the rim. He could have let it—“ She stopped. “He came. When she was hurt. He came running with his head down so he wouldn’t have to look at it, and he got there.”

Gabe didn’t say anything.

“And he was frightened,” said Hyvred. “The whole time. So frightened. For weeks. And then he saw her and he wasn’t. Not for a second. It went up like a fire.” She looked at Gabe. “I’ve never seen it do that. Not once. Not on any floor. Not even at the fountain on four.”

Gabe looked down at the lamp shield on his knees. The dark grey Fen iron, the little lamp in the middle, the sun-lines going out from it. The dark red rim.

“Then she’s what he needs,” he said quietly.

Hyvred looked at him.

“The lantern,” said Gabe. “The thing he couldn’t get past, for twenty-five days. He got past it in ten seconds, in her light.” He ran his thumb along the rim of the shield. “Higu said. And his light went up like a fire when he saw her.” He was quiet for a while. “He’s been carrying everything up there on his own. And all this time, it’s been her holding the light.”

Hyvred didn’t say anything to that. She couldn’t.

They sat together on the lip of the pit and watched the sun come up over the Glass. White and hard and real, over the place where a man had gone up a stair alone, again.

Down below them, on the third ring, in the round room, somebody had woken up and was moving about. Hyvred could see the light of it from up here, the way she saw everybody’s. And for the first time she noticed something she’d been too tired to see last night.

Thea’s light, lying on the cushions by the wall, was a colour she’d never seen it before. Its own colour still, underneath. But at the edges, faint, warm, like a lamp seen through a window, it had gone a little bit amber.

Hyvred didn’t say anything about that either. She thought she’d keep it, for now.
