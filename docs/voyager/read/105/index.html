Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 105

Backup

3,940 words · 17 min read

Malcolm packed everything twice. He always had.

Two waterskins. Two flints. Two of every bandage, two of every salve Kei had ever given him, two spare bootlaces wound round the same stick. On Earth it had been two chargers, two pens, two copies of every file on two different drives, and a printout in his bag in case both drives died at once, which they never had, which wasn’t the point. It was a joke in the group. Xav had made it, in their first year at poly, when Malcolm turned up to a presentation with three copies of the slides. Malcolm doesn’t have a plan B. Malcolm has a plan B for his plan B. Everyone had laughed. Malcolm had laughed too.

He did it with blessings now. Two, every time. In case the first one fails. He’d done it on floor 4 for Rui Xiang going up onto the roofs, and on floor 4 for Gabe going into the Kiln’s fire, and on every floor since for everyone who went in front. Two blessings. One to do the job, and one in case.

And he’d learned to heal. As a backup. In case Rachael was busy. In case Declan needed fixing when Gabe punches him, which had been the joke, and then Gabe had punched him, on floor 7, in a bakery, and Malcolm had said I’ll do two and Declan had said leave it, I earned it. Ten seconds a cut, by the end of floor 4. Never fast. You’ll never be fast, Rachael had told him. But you’ll be there.

He’d been there, on the fifth wall.

He’d put his hands on a boy’s chest in the snow, and the healing had gone in, warm, the way it always went in, looking for something to agree with. The way Rachael had taught him on floor 4. Find what it’s trying to do anyway. Agree with it.

And there’d been nothing there. Nothing trying to do anything. He’d agreed with nothing for a long time.

He packed his bag twice on the morning after, and then a third time, and then sat on the end of his bunk in the barracks with it on his knees and couldn’t think what else to put in it.

The hold-folk had a house for the wounded on the third level, against the cliff, where the warmth of the Breath came up through the floor strongest. Malcolm went there on the morning after the first night, because he didn’t know where else to go.

It was full.

Not full of the dying. Full of the hurt. Long rows of low beds in a long stone room with a vaulted roof, and on every bed a hold-folk soldier from the fifth wall with a hand wrapped, or a foot, or an ear, or a face. And the same thing under every wrapping. Frost. The touch of the Rimed. White patches on the skin in the shape of fingers, where the cold had gone in and not come out.

“It doesn’t heal,” said the woman who kept the house.

She was old, and small for a hold-folk woman, and her hands were the gentlest hands he’d ever seen. Her name was Ingrith. She was unwrapping the hand of a soldier about Malcolm’s age, a big man with a black beard who lay with his eyes shut and his jaw clenched. Under the wrapping, the ends of three of his fingers had gone white and hard and waxy, like candle ends.

“Not with herbs,” said Ingrith. “Not with warmth. Not with anything. The cold that comes out of them doesn’t go back where it came from. It just stays. And then the part it’s in dies.” She looked at the white fingertips for a long time. “These’ll come off tonight. Before it spreads.”

The soldier didn’t open his eyes. He didn’t say anything.

“Can I try?” said Malcolm.

Ingrith looked at him. At his hands. She’d heard about the climbers, he could see. About the warm hands.

“Try,” she said.

He put his hands round the soldier’s fingers. Gently. And let it come. Warm. Slow. Looking for what the flesh was trying to do anyway, so he could agree with it.

It was trying to die.

That was what he found. Under the white, under the cold, the fingertips had already decided. They weren’t trying to close, or knit, or warm. They were trying to stop. Quietly. The way a lamp goes out when there’s no oil. And there was nothing for him to agree with except that.

He took his hands away. He didn’t say anything.

“No,” said Ingrith gently. She’d been watching his face. “Not with anything.”

The soldier opened his eyes. He looked at Malcolm. And then, of all things, he smiled, a small tired smile in his black beard.

“Thanks for trying,” he said. “Nobody’s tried with warm hands before.” He looked at his fingers. “I’ve got seven more. That’s plenty. My da had four.”

Malcolm stayed all morning. He couldn’t heal anything that the cold had touched. He found that out on every bed. But there were other things. Cuts. A broken wrist where somebody had fallen on the stair. A shoulder put out. Somebody’s ear that a Rimed’s gauntlet had torn and not frozen. Those he could do. Ten seconds. Twenty. Never fast. Ingrith watched him, and after the third one she stopped watching and just pointed.

And at noon, when he’d done everything that could be done, she put a hand on his arm.

“There’s someone asking for you,” she said. “Out front.”

It was a woman. Tall, grey-eyed, in grey, like all of them. About fifty. With a long grey braid and big work-roughened hands folded in front of her, and a face that had been crying and had stopped.

“You’re the warm-hands,” she said. “The climber. On the fifth wall last night.”

“Yes,” said Malcolm.

“You had your hands on my son,” she said. “When he went.”

Malcolm stood very still.

“Ansa,” said the woman. “I’m Ansa. Tamm was mine.” She looked at him. Her eyes were very steady. “Brenna said you were there. Right there. With your hands on him.” She swallowed. “I wanted to ask you something. I won’t keep you.”

“Please,” said Malcolm. His voice came out wrong. “Keep me. Ask.”

Ansa looked at him for a long moment.

“Was he afraid?” she said.

Malcolm thought about it. He didn’t want to. He made himself. He thought about the snow coming down sideways in John’s white light, and the boy on his back on the stones behind the line, with his shield still on his arm, and the frost on his chest in the shape of a hand. And his face.

“No,” he said. “He was looking at the snow.”

Ansa’s mouth moved. Just a little.

“He liked the snow,” she said. “Always. Since he was small. He used to stand in the yard in it with his mouth open.” She looked away, past Malcolm, at the long room full of beds. “That’s all. That’s all I wanted. Thank you.”

“He had his shield up,” said Malcolm. “When he went down. He was still holding it. Covering the man on his left.”

Ansa’s face did something then. She put her hand over her mouth. She stood there in the doorway of the house for the wounded with her hand over her mouth and her eyes shut, for a long time, not making any sound.

“His father was the same,” she said at last, through her fingers. “He’s on the Long Wall. Twelve years.” She took her hand away. “Thank you. For telling me that. Nobody else had.”

She went. Malcolm stood in the doorway and watched her go, down the steep street in the snow, tall and grey and straight, until she turned a corner.

He’d come down this morning thinking the thing he’d failed at was the healing.

He stood there for a long time and thought that he might have been wrong about that.

Rachael found him there at dusk. On the step. With his bag on his knees.

She sat down next to him without asking. She didn’t say anything for a while. Rachael didn’t, usually. She’d taught him everything he knew about healing on floor 4 and she’d done most of it in about forty words.

“Ingrith says you did eleven,” she said eventually.

“Twelve.”

“She says you tried the frostbite.”

“It didn’t do anything.”

“No,” said Rachael. “It doesn’t. I tried yesterday.” She looked at her own hands. “It’s the same as—“ She stopped. “When there’s nothing there. Healing’s agreeing. You can’t agree with nothing.”

They sat there.

“Tamm,” said Malcolm.

“I know.” Rachael was quiet. “Brenna told me.”

“I put my hands on him and it went in and it just—“ He stopped. “There wasn’t anybody to agree with. He’d already gone. Before I got there. And I kept my hands on him anyway. For ages. Like an idiot.”

“Not like an idiot,” said Rachael.

He looked at her.

“I did that,” said Rachael. “On floor 4.” She didn’t look at him. “A dust-hound. A stupid one. It was already dead. Kei had done it. I kept my hands on it for about a minute anyway, because I’d never put them on anything dead before and I didn’t know what it would feel like.” She was quiet. “It felt like nothing. Like you said. It felt like putting your hands on a table.” She turned her hands over in her lap. “You keep them there because you can’t believe the table won’t do anything.”

Malcolm didn’t say anything.

“His mum came,” he said, after a while.

Rachael turned her head.

“She asked if he was afraid. I said no. He was looking at the snow.” Malcolm swallowed. “And then I told her he had his shield up when he went. Covering the man on his left.” He looked at the bag on his knees. “She said nobody else had told her.”

Rachael looked at him for a long time.

“That,” she said, “is the job.”

“What is?”

“That.” She stood up. She brushed the snow off the back of her coat. “The healing’s not the job, Malcolm. The healing’s the bit you do when you can. The job’s being there.” She looked down at him. “You were there. Twice. You were there with your hands on him, and you were there with his mum.” She paused. “That’s the whole job. The rest is just stitches.”

She went. Malcolm sat on the step with his bag and watched her go up the street.

He thought about I’m not fast. But I’m there. He’d said it on floor 4, about Declan, in a furnace hall full of a man’s shape burned into the stone. Next time, somebody should be behind him.

He’d thought it meant something about healing.

Gabe was at the Breath.

He was always at the Breath now. Malcolm went down every evening, the way he had since the first day, to fix his hands. The long stair, the heat coming up, the roar. And at the bottom, at anvil nine, Gabe. With his sleeves rolled to the shoulder and his arms shining and his face lit orange and white by the crack in the floor, and Salty on the bellows, and Jared holding, and old one-eyed Geir behind them with his arms folded saying no.

The bar was different every evening. It had been a bar of grey iron, the first time Malcolm saw it, with something red glowing at the middle. Now it wasn’t grey. It was dark. Not black. Dark the way coal is dark, with something underneath. And all through it, everywhere, faintly, even when it came out of the fire and cooled on the anvil, there was a glow. Deep down. Like the light inside the Kiln-heart had been folded into every part of it and was looking out.

“Seventy-one,” said Gabe, when Malcolm sat down on the warm stone beside the anvil. He didn’t look round. “Seventy-one folds.”

“Twenty-nine to go.”

“Twenty-nine.” Gabe put the bar back in the crack. “Hands.”

He held out his left one without looking. Malcolm took it. Burns across the palm, across the fingers, old ones on top of new ones. He let it come. Warm. Slow. The burns were trying to close. They always were, with Gabe; he healed like he did everything, all at once and stubbornly. Malcolm agreed with them. Ten seconds. Twenty.

“Mal,” said Gabe.

“Mm.”

“If you knew something about Dec.” Gabe was looking at the Breath, not at him. “Something he hadn’t told you. Something big. Would you tell me?”

Malcolm’s hands stopped.

He made them start again.

“I don’t know anything,” he said. “About Declan. Not anything you don’t.”

“I know you don’t,” said Gabe. “I know. I can tell. You’re like me. You’re on the outside of it.” He was quiet. “I’m asking would you. If you did.”

Malcolm thought about it. Properly. The way he thought about everything, which was twice.

“I don’t know,” he said. “It would depend who’d asked me not to.”

Gabe nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he’d expected.

“Yeah,” he said. “That’s what I thought.”

He took his hand back and held out the other one. Malcolm took it. They didn’t say anything else. The Breath went up and came down, roaring. The bar glowed in the crack. Behind them, Geir unfolded his arms and refolded them and didn’t say no, for once, for quite a long time.

When Malcolm went back up the stair, Gabe was at the anvil with the bar, hammering. Seventy-two. And Malcolm, at the top, where the stair came out under the third wall into the cold, stopped and looked back down it, at the orange light coming up out of the mountain, and the sound of the hammer. And thought: he’s asking everybody except the ones who know.

He didn’t know how he knew that. He just did. He’d been watching people from the second row of things all his life.

He found Xav on the barracks roof that night. Of course he did. Xav went up when he was carrying something. He’d done it at poly. Rui Xiang had told Malcolm that morning, on the stairs, in passing, very fast, the way he said everything: Xav’s on the roof again. He moved early on the wall. Don’t let him do it twice.

Xav was against the chimney, where the warmth came up. His coat was open. He had his hand flat on his chest, over his shirt, over the place where Malcolm had healed the five long marks of the Rimed’s fingers on the first night.

Malcolm sat down next to him. He put his bag down between them.

“Does it hurt?” he said.

“No.”

“Liar. Show me.”

Xav looked at him. Then opened his shirt. The marks were still there. Pink, faint, five long lines across his chest. Not white. They’d never been white, not all the way; Malcolm had got there before the cold had decided anything. They were healing. Slowly. The way things healed here.

“They’ll go,” said Malcolm. “A week.”

“I know.”

Malcolm did the second one anyway. A blessing, warm, over the marks. In case.

Xav let him. He’d always let him. Nine years. Malcolm had done two of everything for Xav for nine years and Xav had never once told him not to bother.

“You moved early,” said Malcolm.

“Everybody keeps telling me that.”

“Because you never do.” Malcolm closed his bag. “I’ve watched you in a hundred fights, Xav. On Earth and here. You’ve never once moved before it was time. You’re the one who taught Declan to wait.”

“Half of it,” said Xav. “Somebody else taught him the rest.”

Malcolm didn’t know what that meant. He didn’t ask. He’d learned that about Xav, too, at poly. Some things Xav said were doors, and some were walls painted to look like doors, and you couldn’t always tell which, and if you pushed on the wrong one you just hurt your hand.

They sat there. The snow had stopped. The sky over the Bastion was black and enormous and full of stars, more than Malcolm had ever seen, so many that the sky looked dusty with them. Up the pass, on the fifth wall, the hold-folk were on watch. You could see their fires.

“Who’s your backup?” said Malcolm.

Xav turned his head.

“For what?”

“For whatever you’re carrying,” said Malcolm. “I don’t know what it is. I’m not asking. Rui Xiang told me not to ask, and I wasn’t going to anyway.” He looked at the stars. “But you’re carrying it on your own. I can see you are. And you’re carrying it so carefully you can’t do anything else. You moved early on the wall because all your waiting’s gone into this.”

Xav didn’t say anything.

“Everybody needs a backup,” said Malcolm. “That’s what I’m for. That’s all I’ve ever been for. Two blessings. In case the first one fails.” He paused. “So whatever it is. Whatever you’re going to do with it. If it goes wrong. I’ll be there.”

“You don’t know what it is.”

“No,” said Malcolm. “I don’t need to. I’m not fast, Xav. I’m never going to be fast. But I’m there.” He picked up his bag. “Rachael says that’s the whole job. The rest is just stitches.”

Xav looked at him for a very long time. In the starlight, against the warm chimney, with his shirt still open over the pink marks.

“Thank you,” he said.

Malcolm nodded. He got up. He went to the edge of the roof, where the ladder went down.

“Malcolm,” said Xav.

He stopped.

“Can you be at the top of the Breath stair tomorrow night?” said Xav. “Late. After everybody’s gone up. Just—at the top. Not down. At the top.”

“Why?”

Xav was quiet.

“In case,” he said.

Malcolm looked at him. Then he nodded.

“Backup,” he said.

“Backup,” said Xav.

He saw Xav go out, the next evening, at dusk.

Not down. Not to the Breath. Up. Out of the barracks and along the street toward the long empty hall over the stables on the fourth level, where the hold-folk stored winter hay and nobody went after dark. Malcolm saw him go from the window. And a few minutes later, he saw others. One at a time. Not together. Thea with her lantern. Chris. Winter. Hyvred. Drew. Hako and Kei, together. Higu. Chi with Waddles under her arm. Bee and Gigi. Xola.

Thirteen of them, counting Xav.

He’d seen them do this before. On floor 6, at the top of the pit, when the moon was up. On floor 8, by the fire, in a ring, with a notebook. On floor 9, in the round house on the far side of town where Higu kept her maps. Thirteen people going somewhere, one at a time, and not looking at each other.

He’d never asked. Nobody on the outside had. Gabe hadn’t. That was the thing. Gabe had watched them do it on every floor since floor 6, and never asked anyone.

Malcolm stood at the window and watched the last of them go into the hay hall, and the door close. And sat down on his bunk with his bag on his knees.

They were in there an hour.

When they came out, they came out the same way. One at a time. Not looking at each other. Except Winter. Winter came out with Xav, together, and stopped in the street outside the hay hall in the snow, and said something to him. Malcolm couldn’t hear it. He saw Xav nod. He saw Winter put her hand on Xav’s arm, briefly and hard, and then fold her arms and walk away.

Xav stood in the street on his own for a while.

Then he came back to the barracks. And came into the room where Xav’s party slept. Where Malcolm was sitting on his bunk with his bag, and John was asleep, and Rui Xiang was lying on his back pretending to be, with a knife going over his knuckles in the dark. And Gabe’s bunk was empty, because Gabe was at the Breath.

Xav stood in the doorway.

“Tomorrow night,” he said. To Malcolm. Quietly. “At the top of the stair.”

“I’ll be there.”

“He’ll finish tomorrow. Geir says. The hundredth fold.” Xav looked at Gabe’s empty bunk. “I want to be there when he does.”

Malcolm looked at him.

“You’re going to tell him,” he said. “Whatever it is.”

He hadn’t meant to say it. It just came out. Xav didn’t answer. He stood in the doorway with the snow melting on his shoulders and looked at the empty bunk.

On his bunk across the room, Rui Xiang’s knife had stopped.

“I swore,” said Xav. “On floor 6. That he’d hear it from me. First. Before anybody else. Before any slate. The day it was a fact.” He was quiet. “It’s not a fact. It might never be. I’ve been waiting a hundred and twenty days for it to be a fact, and it isn’t, and it won’t be, not the way I meant, not until—“ He stopped. “And while I’ve been waiting, he’s been standing on Declan’s right with his shield up, not asking anybody anything. And everybody’s worked out there’s a thing except him. Or he’s worked it out too, and he’s on his own with it. Down there. At an anvil.”

He looked at Malcolm.

“I told them tonight,” said Xav. “In the hay hall. All of them. That I’m going to tell him. Not the fact. What we think. So he’s not last.” He swallowed. “Winter said: about bloody time.”

Malcolm didn’t know what any of it meant. What we think. Not the fact. The day. He didn’t know and he didn’t need to. He knew Xav’s face. He’d seen it before a hundred presentations, on the roof of the engineering block in the rain. He knew what it looked like when Xav had finished waiting.

“Okay,” said Malcolm.

“You don’t know what it is.”

“No,” said Malcolm. “But you’re not carrying it on your own tomorrow. That’s what I know.”

He opened his bag. He took out the spare bootlaces, wound round their stick, and the second flint, and the spare waterskin, and put them on the bunk beside him. And then he packed it again. Slowly. Two of everything.

Across the room, in the dark, Rui Xiang’s knife started going over his knuckles again.
