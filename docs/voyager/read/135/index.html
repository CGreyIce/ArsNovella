Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 135

The Mast

6,027 words · 26 min read

On Earth, Hyvred had seen the world through a hole the size of a coin.

She’d got used to it. You did. You got used to turning your whole head to see a thing other people saw by moving their eyes. You got used to doorframes, and the corners of tables, and people standing just to your left who’d been there the whole time. You made jokes about it, on stream, because if you made the joke first nobody else could. And then you woke up in a white room and you could see everything, and you spent the first week crying at sheep.

She still took her glasses off on purpose.

She didn’t need them, here. Not for seeing. She wore them because they were hers, and because they were a door. Glasses on, and the world was the world. Glasses off, and it was the other thing. Everything behind everything. Where light had been, and people had stood, and what they’d felt while they stood there. Magic, in colours. The whole floor lit up, every inch of it remembering.

She took them off in the dark on the three hundred and forty-fifth night, because Bee had woken her and held up a drawing, and looked across the camp at Thea.

At first it was just the camp.

It always was. You had to let it come. You couldn’t look at one thing straight away; your eyes went everywhere at once, the way they had in the white room, and you had to let them settle. So she let them. She sat in her bedroll with her glasses on the salt beside her and Bee’s hand on her arm and let the camp come up round her in the dark.

The steel tent, first. Ina’s fires had gone out an hour ago, but the heat was still in the pots, and she could see it, a dull red ghost in the iron, fading. And round the tent, on the salt, the marks of where everyone had knelt and dug and carried and held, all night, every night, a trodden ring of a hundred small warm smudges, like fingerprints on glass. Winter’s frost on the shields, gone now but not gone, a pale blue memory on the steel. Ina, asleep sitting up with his mask in his lap, and round him the faint white-blue of his fire, the shape of his hands on the crust.

Deston’s threads. Even asleep. Thin gold lines going out from him to everybody, slack now, like spider silk in the morning.

The bedrolls. All of them. Thirty-five people asleep in the dark, and every one of them a shape of light, the colour of whatever they were. Gabe, big and banked and orange like a forge at night. Rachael, white and cold at the edges where she’d taken something into herself that week and hadn’t let it out. Maigo, green, faint, two small bright points of it up on his antler where his leaves were. Gigi beside her, gone a deeper colour since the Salt, brown-gold, thorn-coloured, with a thin bright thread going out from her across the camp to the west, to the green she’d planted last night, and another one behind it, fainter, east, and another, and another, a line of them going back across the white in the dark like a string of lamps. Her road.

And Ice wasn’t there. He was out. She could see the line of where he’d gone, a long way west, past the place where Ina’s little orange fire had been, a single track on the white, steady, and somewhere out at the end of it, too far even for her, him.

And Thea.

Hyvred let her eyes go to her.

Asleep by the second sled with her hand under her cheek and the lantern by her head. The lantern was a light of its own, amber, steady, his. Lure-glass, out of the Fen on floor 3, and his light in it; it had been his light in it since the day he left it on a bench by a door, and Hyvred had always been able to see that, that it was two things at once, a lantern and him. And Thea beside it. Thea’s own colour was strange. It always had been. Not a colour, exactly. Lines. Fine bright lines of light, all over her, moving, the way the text in a window moves when you scroll. Hyvred had told her once, on floor 6. You look like a page. Thea had laughed and said that’s the nicest thing anyone’s ever said to me and meant it.

She looked like a page.

And round her, on the salt, there was dust.

Grey.

Like dust on a window. Like dust in a shaft of light in an old room, very fine, so fine you only saw it when the light came through at an angle. On floor 9 Hyvred had seen it on the Grow. All of it, every fern, every frond, every briar in the Down, with this grey on it like a film. And all of it flowing. In. Slowly. Toward one place. A day’s walk south-west. Like dust going down a drain. And at the place, a hole. No colour at all. The same kind as the Wanting, on floor 6, that had shown Ice thirty-six people at a long table and kept him there.

The tower’s kind.

It was on the salt.

Not much. Not on everything, the way it had been on the Grow. Faint. Here and there on the crust, all round the camp, a film of grey on the white, so pale you’d think it was the salt itself. And it was moving.

Toward Thea.

Not into her. Hyvred looked very hard, with her bare eyes, and it wasn’t going into her. It was gathering. Round her. On the salt round her bedroll, thicker there than anywhere, a soft grey drift round the edges of the light from the lantern, the way dust drifts against a door. As if something were standing outside a window, very close, with its face to the glass, looking in. And its breath on the glass. And the breath was the dust.

Looking at her.

Hyvred sat in her bedroll and didn’t breathe.

On floor 9, on the last morning, when the Sower was ash, every fern and briar on the whole floor had turned and looked up. At Ice. All at once. On the rope. Hyvred had seen it. She’d seen the grey go off every growing thing in the Down at once like a flock of birds getting up, and point. At him. And Thea had said, that night, not at us, that time. Not at all of us.

This wasn’t like that.

That had been fast and huge and all at once, like a shout. This was slow. Quiet. A drift. This was something that had been looking at Ice for three floors, and had turned its head, a little, just a little, without anybody noticing, and seen somebody standing next to him. And was looking at her now. Learning her. The way you’d learn somebody’s face across a room before you walked over.

Not him.

Past him.

At her.

“Hyv,” whispered Bee, in her ear. Her hand was so tight on Hyvred’s arm that it hurt. “Hyv. Is it there?”

Hyvred didn’t answer for a long time.

Then she picked up her glasses off the salt and put them on, and the dust went, and the page went, and it was just a camp in the dark, and Thea asleep by a lantern.

“Yeah,” said Hyvred. “It’s there.”

She didn’t sleep either.

She lay on her back between Bee and Gigi with her glasses on her chest and thought about it until the sky went grey. And by the time it did, she knew three things.

The first was that she was going to tell Thea. Today. That wasn’t a question. Winter had said it, on floor 9, in Higu’s map-house, with her arms folded: I told you first so nobody in this room finds out after. Nobody in the club found out after. That was the rule. They’d made it in the round house on floor 6, the thirteen of them, and they’d kept it. And this was Thea’s. If it was anybody’s, it was Thea’s.

The second was that she didn’t want to.

The third was the one she kept turning over and over in the grey, on her back, on the salt. That it was the club’s theory, and she was in the club, and the theory said Ice had been here before. All of it. With them. And it had gone wrong. And the theory said Thea was the first to see it, and the one who’d sat in the dark on floor 6 and said the word. And since floor 10, since the Gate, Thea had been carrying something. Gigi had said. Gigi had held the lantern for her two nights ago and said you can put your hands down, and Thea had put her hands down and her face had done a thing Gigi said she’d never seen it do.

It’s not looking at the one who’s been here before, thought Hyvred.

It’s looking at the one who knows.

She didn’t know what Thea knew. Nobody did. Nobody asked. But she lay on her back on the salt in the grey and thought: it knows she knows something. And she didn’t know how. And she didn’t know what it would do about it.

And then she thought about floor 6, and the Wanting, and thirty-six people at a long table, and what the tower did when it found out what you wanted most.

She sat up and put her glasses on.

She told her at the still.

At dusk, when the pots were going and the shields were up and everybody was busy. Hyvred went and found her on the sled where she’d been holding Drew’s buckler steady for Winter, and sat down next to her, close, and Bee sat down on her other side with the sketchbook.

“Thea,” said Hyvred. Close. Low. “Can we show you something.”

Thea looked at the sketchbook. Then at Bee’s face. Then at Hyvred’s.

She didn’t say anything. Hyvred watched her go still, all at once, the way she went still before a spell.

Bee opened the book.

She showed her all of them. The four from the morning of the spoon. The one from the night, by the lantern. And the two from last night, and the one from this morning, because Bee had drawn every one since, and Hyvred had looked at every one with her glasses off. In every one, the salt. The ridges in the crust, six-sided, like a honeycomb, all pulled the same way. Not toward Ice. Past him. Toward the edge of the page, or the middle of it, wherever Thea had been.

And more every time. A little. The way the ferns on floor 9 had leaned a little more every day.

Thea looked at them for a long time.

Then she looked at Hyvred.

“You took your glasses off,” said Thea.

“Yeah.”

“What does it look like?”

Hyvred told her. Quietly. The dust. The grey, like the Grow on floor 9, like the Wanting. Drifting on the salt, thicker round her than anywhere. Not going in. Gathering. Like something outside a window with its face to the glass. She told it the way she’d have told anyone a thing they needed to know about a fight, straight, all of it, because Thea was Thea and you didn’t soften things for her. She’d have known.

Thea listened to all of it without moving.

When Hyvred stopped, she didn’t say anything for a while. Across the camp, Ina’s water fire burned white-blue under the pots, and the drops ran down the inside of the shields into the trough. Tik. Tik.

“Since when?” said Thea.

“I don’t know. Bee saw it on the morning of the spoon. I didn’t look before last night.” Hyvred paused. “I don’t look at you, usually. Not like that. It’s rude.”

Something went across Thea’s face. Almost a smile.

“Since the Teeth,” said Thea. “I think.”

Hyvred looked at her.

“There was a step,” said Thea. Very quietly. Not to them. To the drawings. “On the Teeth. In the painted lighthouse. The fourth step from the door. It rocked.” She stopped. “I stepped over it. And then at the real one, at the Last Light, I stepped on it. On purpose.” She looked at her hands. “I thought that would be enough.”

Hyvred didn’t understand. She didn’t need to. She knew what it sounded like, the way you know what somebody’s saying in another room even if you can’t hear the words.

She’s been hiding from it. On purpose. For two floors.

“Thea,” she said.

“Don’t tell him.”

Hyvred went still.

“Please,” said Thea. She looked up. Her face was very calm and her hands weren’t. “Not yet. Hyv. Bee. Don’t tell him.”

“Thea—“

“He’ll stand in front of me.” Thea’s voice didn’t go up. It went down. Lower and lower, the way it did when she was holding something. “You know he will. He’s spent four floors learning how to stand in the middle. Higu put him there. Everybody put him there. He let us. He held Sanctuary on the Patience and didn’t go to the rail, Tiger said, he took one step and stopped.” She swallowed. “If he knows it’s me, he’ll go back. He’ll stand between me and everything. Every second. For the rest of the floor. For the rest of all of them. And he’ll be alone again. In front. Where it hits.”

Nobody said anything.

“I’ll tell him,” said Thea. “Myself. I promise. I’m not going to hide it from him. I’m not going to hide anything from him.” Her mouth went tight. “But I want to work out how first. So that when I tell him, I can tell him what we’re going to do. Not just it’s looking at me. He can’t hear it’s looking at me without a plan in the same breath. It’d break him.”

Hyvred looked at her for a long time.

She thought about Winter, in Higu’s map-house, with her arms folded. So nobody in this room finds out after. She thought about the round house on floor 6. She thought about Gigi’s twenty-one mornings with her hand under her blanket, and Maigo’s leaves, and Bee at breakfast saying my wings hurt out loud, because the Salt made you hide the thing that was going wrong, and the only way to beat it was to say it.

“I won’t lie to him,” said Hyvred. “If he asks me. I’m not going to lie to him.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I’m not asking you to.”

“And not long.”

“No.”

“Days, Thea. Not floors.”

“Days,” said Thea.

Hyvred looked at Bee. Bee looked at her. Bee’s face was white, and her wings had gone flat and stiff down her back under her cloak, and she was holding the sketchbook against her chest like a shield.

Bee nodded. Once.

“Okay,” said Hyvred.

Thea let out a breath.

And then she did something Hyvred had never seen her do. She leaned over, sideways, on the sled, and put her head on Hyvred’s shoulder. Just for a second. The way Gigi did, or Bee. Not the way Thea did, because Thea didn’t. Hyvred sat very still and let her.

“Thank you,” said Thea.

Then she sat up, and took the sketchbook from Bee, gently, and turned back through it to the morning of the spoon. To the one Bee had kept. Ice on his front in the salt with a helm in the air, and Gigi with the spoon, and at the top, small, a woman with a lantern bent over, laughing.

She looked at herself, laughing, for a long time.

“He’s got one of these,” said Thea. “Hasn’t he. The good one. You gave it him.”

“Inside his vest,” said Bee. Very quietly. “Next to the card.”

Thea shut her eyes.

They walked west.

Seven more days. The way they’d walked all the others. Softly, on the straps, in the sun coming up off the white. Ina’s still at night. Gigi’s green every morning, a new dot on the line going back east. Ice going out after dark past the little orange fire, and Thea after him with the lantern, a hundred metres back, and Xola.

Hyvred watched.

She couldn’t not. Every night she took her glasses off, after the camp had gone quiet, and looked. And every night the dust was there. Grey, on the salt. Drifting. Toward the lantern, wherever it was. When Thea was in camp it gathered round her bedroll. When Thea was out on the white behind Ice, a hundred metres back, Hyvred could see it from the camp, a long faint grey smudge on the dark going out across the salt toward the amber, like smoke being pulled out of a room under a door.

More every night.

She told Higu nothing. She told Chris nothing. She’d said days, and it had been three, and then four. She lay in her bedroll at night with her bow beside her and her glasses on her chest and looked at the lantern across the camp and thought, tomorrow I’m going to say it. If she hasn’t. Tomorrow.

And Thea hadn’t.

Hyvred watched her not. She watched Thea walk beside Ice on the straps by day, close, the way they walked now. She watched her sit with him at the still. She watched her start to say something, once, on the fifth night, at the edge of the light, with her hand on his arm, and stop, and say something else. She watched Ice look at her when she stopped. He knew. Not what. He knew there was something. You could see it, on him, if you looked: he’d gone careful again. The full cup. The shoulders. Bee’s drawing had been the only one where it wasn’t there, and it had been there every day since.

He didn’t ask her either.

Everybody on this floor is waiting on a step, thought Hyvred. And none of us are on the same one.

They came up on the sixth night.

At the still. Of course at the still. Ina had got the pots going and the shields up and Winter on the sled at the top, and it was the moment it always was, the hard one, when the brine was first warming and the fire had to come up slow enough not to bubble. Ina was good at it. He’d done it eighteen nights. He knelt in the middle with his mask down and his hand flat and brought it up so slowly that you could have counted a hundred between each breath of steam.

The brine in the big iron pot, Ice’s, bubbled.

Once. A single fat bubble, at the bottom, breaking at the top with a small soft plop.

Nobody moved.

Ina’s hand went flat and the fire went down to nothing. Everybody round the steel tent stopped where they were. Winter on the sled with her arrowhead up. Chris with his shield. Calvin with a mug of brine halfway to a pot. Thirty-five people, frozen, in the dark.

Hyvred already had her glasses off.

She didn’t remember taking them off. Her hands had done it, the way Bee’s hands drew. She was on one knee on the salt by the fourth sled with her bow in her left hand and an arrow already on the string and her bare eyes going out across the crust, down, into it.

That was the thing about true sight that nobody else understood. It wasn’t seeing through. It was seeing where. Where things had been, and where they were, and the warm track a thing left in the world as it went. On floor 9 she’d tracked the Sower when it sank into the earth, by its absence, like a fish under ice, a moving place where there wasn’t anything. Under the crust, here, in the slurry, the saltmouths were things. Long pale warm things, moving. She could see them the way you’d see fish under thin ice on a pond, if the ice were lit from inside.

They were coming.

Four. Five. Out of the dark, from the north, under the salt, long and quick and straight, toward the plop. The way they always came.

And then they turned.

Hyvred watched them turn. All five. Under the crust, a hundred metres out. As if something had put a hand on each of them and moved it. They didn’t come on toward the iron pot, toward the sound. They bent. Round. All the same way.

To the south side of the camp.

To the second sled.

To Thea, kneeling on the salt by the second sled with her hand on the strap of the lantern, very still, not making a sound, because she hadn’t made a sound, nobody had, there was nothing to come to.

She didn’t make a sound, thought Hyvred. She didn’t make a sound and they’re going to her.

She didn’t think after that.

She drew. All the way back. To the ear. On one knee on the salt. With her bare eyes on a long pale warm thing moving under the crust, fast, twenty metres from Thea and closing, and she didn’t aim at the salt. She aimed at it. At where it was going to be. At the place under the white where the warm track was going, a metre ahead of the head, under a hand’s depth of crust and a hand’s depth of slurry, where nobody else on the floor could see anything at all.

She let go.

The arrow went into the salt.

Into the crust. Through it. Hyvred heard it, a sharp chk, the sound of a nail going into ice. And under the white, twenty metres off, the long pale warm thing jerked, and twisted, and stopped. The crust above it bulged, once, and cracked, and something thrashed under it, and went still.

Nobody else had seen anything. They’d seen Hyvred shoot an arrow into the ground.

She already had the next one on the string.

“South,” she said. Not loud. Not a scream. A word. It went down into the salt and she didn’t care. “South side. Four more. Under. Coming to Thea. I can see them. Don’t move. I can see them.”

The second one was at ten metres. She drew and let go and the arrow went into the crust a metre in front of where Thea was kneeling, chk, and under the white the warm thing stopped, and the crust bulged and cracked and a flat white head broke up through it with an arrow standing in the top of it and fell sideways and didn’t move.

Thea didn’t flinch. Hyvred saw her not. She knelt there on the salt with her hand on the lantern and her face perfectly still and her eyes on the cracked crust in front of her, and didn’t move. Because Hyvred had said don’t move.

Three.

The third was coming in from the left, low, deep. Deeper than the others. Under the slurry. Hyvred couldn’t get a line on it through the crust at that angle, it was too deep, the arrow wouldn’t—

Winter shot it.

Hyvred didn’t see her draw. She just heard it, chk, from the top of the sled, and saw the warm thing under the salt go white. Not dead. Frozen. A long pale shape under the crust going still in a rush of blue, the cold going out of it through the slurry like frost across a window.

“Tell me where,” said Winter. From the sled. Her bow up. “Hyv. Tell me where and I’ll do it.”

“Two. One at eleven o’clock from Thea, five metres, deep. One at two o’clock, eight, shallow, fast—”

Winter shot the deep one. Hyvred shot the fast one. Chk. Chk. Under the salt, two warm shapes went still, one white with frost and one with an arrow through the back of its flat head.

And then there was nothing.

Hyvred knelt on the salt with her bow up and her bare eyes going out across the crust in every direction, and there was nothing. No warm things. Nothing moving. Only the dust. The grey. On the white. All round the camp, faint, the way it always was.

Thicker round the second sled than she’d ever seen it.

Round Thea. On the salt. A soft grey drift up against her knees, round the lantern, so thick now that Hyvred could see the shape of it, like the shape of a hand.

Then it went. Thinned. Spread. Back out across the white, slowly, the way breath goes off glass.

Hyvred lowered her bow.

Nobody moved. Not for a long time. Thirty-five people standing and kneeling round a steel tent in the dark, looking at Hyvred, and at Thea, and at five cracked places in the salt round the second sled with arrows standing in them.

And at Ice.

He’d been at the edge of the camp. He hadn’t gone out yet. He’d been sitting on the end of the last sled doing the edge of his sword with the whetstone, the way he did, and he was standing up now, and the whetstone was on the salt where he’d dropped it, and he was looking at the arrows. At where they were. At the five of them, in a ring, round the second sled, round Thea.

Not round the iron pot. Not round the sound.

Round her.

Hyvred watched his face.

She’d taken her glasses off. She could see his colour. She didn’t usually look at it; it was too much, it always had been, it was like looking straight at the sun at noon. But she saw it now. She couldn’t not. And she saw it change. She saw it go from what it was to something else, all at once, in a breath, the way the Salt’s crust had shivered when the bubble broke. Like a fire with a door suddenly opened on it.

He looked at Thea.

Thea looked back at him.

And then she looked at Hyvred. Across the camp. With her face very calm and her eyes saying, as clearly as if she’d shouted it, not now. Please. Not here. Not like this.

Hyvred didn’t say anything.

She’d said she wouldn’t lie to him. She hadn’t said she’d tell him.

Ice didn’t ask her.

He didn’t ask anybody. He stood at the edge of the camp with his sword in his hand for a long time, looking at the arrows. And then he looked at the dark. At the white. All round. As if he could see the dust too. As if he could feel it on the salt like a draught on the back of his neck.

Then he picked up the whetstone. And sat down. On the salt. Not on the end of the sled any more. On the salt beside the second sled, beside Thea, between her and the dark. With his sword across his knees. And didn’t go out that night.

Hyvred saw Higu, on the aft sled, with the rock in her lap, look at him sitting there. And then look at Thea. And then at Hyvred.

Higu didn’t write anything.

They saw the Mast on the three hundred and fifty-fourth morning.

Kip wasn’t there to climb anything. There wasn’t anything on the Salt to climb. It was Hyvred who saw it, because it was always Hyvred who saw things first, and she was walking at the front of the line with her glasses on and her bow on her back when she saw, at the very edge of the world, in the west, where the white met the sky, a line.

Thin. Dark. Upright.

She stopped. The line of sleds stopped behind her, softly, one at a time, the way they did.

“Ice,” said Hyvred.

He came up from the middle. With Thea. He’d walked beside her for two days now, close, on her left, always on her left, between her and the north, where the saltmouths had come from. He hadn’t said anything about it. Nobody had. He came up and stood beside Hyvred and looked west.

“That’s it,” he said. Very quietly. “That’s the Mast.”

They walked toward it all morning.

It got bigger. Slowly. The way things got bigger on the Salt, where there was nothing to measure them against. A dark line. Then a dark line with something at its foot. Then a mast. A real one. Tall. Taller than any of the Patience’s. Black, or grey, at this distance, with a crosstree near the top and something hanging off it, ragged, that had been a sail once and was stiff now, white, salt-stiff, like a sheet frozen on a washing line. And under it, at the foot, a hull.

A ship. Standing up in the salt. On its keel. As if it were still floating, on a sea that had gone.

“One mast,” said Ice.

He’d stopped. Everybody had. They were standing in a line across the white, thirty-six of them and six sleds, maybe two miles off, in the noon sun.

“It had one mast,” said Ice.

Hyvred looked at the ship.

At the mast.

And at the two more. Beside it.

She hadn’t seen them before. Nobody had. They’d been there the whole time, from the first dark line at the edge of the world, and nobody had seen them because they were white, white on white, salt-white against the white sky, and thin. Thinner than the mast. Two of them. Standing up out of the salt on the south side of the ship, close together, tall, as tall as the mast or taller, and very straight.

Not straight. Hyvred looked harder. They had a bend in them. Halfway up. Both of them. A knob, like a knot in a cane. And the bend went the wrong way. Backwards. The way a bird’s leg bends.

And above them, where they met, at the top, higher than the crosstree of the mast, there was something white and round and very large, like a hill made of salt, and folded into it, tucked down along it, the long white curve of something that was a neck, and at the end of the neck, buried in the hill the way a sleeping bird buries its head under its wing, a shape like a spear.

“Hyv,” said Bee, beside her. Very small.

Hyvred took her glasses off.

The world came up round her. The ship. The mast. The salt, and the things in the salt round the ship, that she hadn’t seen with her glasses on. Kept things. The salt keeps what it covers, Tullah had said. Ribs, sticking up out of the crust. Boats. Old ones, small ones, half-buried. A raker’s sled, on its side, with the strap still on it. Birds. Small ones. Dozens. Lying in the salt with their wings out, white, crusted, kept, the way they’d fallen.

And the two white legs going up out of the salt beside the ship. And the hill of white at the top, that was a body. Salt grown over it in plates, like armour, like scales, a thousand years of it, so thick you couldn’t tell where the salt stopped and the thing started. And inside it, deep, very deep, a light.

Slow. Dull. Dark red, like a coal in the bottom of a grate that everyone had forgotten.

It beat.

Once.

Hyvred felt it through the soles of her boots. Through the salt. Two miles off. A long slow heavy thump, like a door shutting in a house a very long way away. Maigo had said that. In the dip. A heart that goes once in an hour. Or slower.

Beside her, Maigo had gone white.

And on the salt round the thing, round its legs, round the ship, all round, as far as she could see, the dust.

Grey. On everything. Thick, the way it had been on the Grow. Like dust on a window in a room nobody had opened for a thousand years. Flowing. In. Toward the two white legs and the hill on top of them, like dust going down a drain.

And then, as she watched, the dust moved.

Not in. Out.

Toward them. Toward the line of thirty-six people standing on the white two miles off in the noon sun. A long grey ripple going out across the salt from the foot of the white legs, slow, soft, like a breath going out across a field.

To the middle of the line.

To the lantern.

Hyvred said, “Thea—“

At the top of the two white legs, in the hill of salt, the long white curve of the neck moved.

Slowly. The way a thing moves that hasn’t moved in a thousand years. Salt came off it. Plates of salt, the size of shields, cracking away from the curve of the neck and falling, turning, glittering in the sun, all the way down to the crust round the ship’s keel, a long way down, and breaking there, and the sound of it came across the two miles of white a long time after, very faint, like ice going on a lake in spring.

The neck unfolded.

Out of the hill. Out from under the wing that wasn’t a wing, that was a great white crust of salt in the shape of a wing. Up. Longer than the mast. A long white curve against the white sky, and at the end of it, coming up out of the salt where it had been buried, a head.

Long and narrow. White. A beak like a spear, longer than a man, straight, crusted, coming up out of a thousand years of salt and pointing at the sky.

And an eye.

Hyvred could see it from two miles away. With her bare eyes. Because she could see everything. It opened, in the side of the long white head, slowly, a lid of salt cracking and lifting off it. Round. Pale. The colour of the inside of a shell.

Not dead. Not alive.

Kept.

It didn’t look at the ship. It didn’t look at the line of thirty-six people on the white. It didn’t look at Ice, standing beside Hyvred with his sword not drawn and his whole body gone still.

The long white head turned on the end of its long white neck, slowly, all the way, like a heron turning to look at a fish in the shallows. And the pale eye found the line. And went along it. And stopped.

On Thea.
