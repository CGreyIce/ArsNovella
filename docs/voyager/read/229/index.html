Chapter 229

How It Ends

6,106 words · 27 min read

And so our heroes came to the middle of the town that forgot.

Azac said it to the square. Out loud. In the voice.

He’d been sick against the wall of a house with no door twenty minutes before. Quietly. The way he always was, before. He’d wiped his mouth and taken his place in the ring on Deston’s left, and stood there with his shield on his arm and his eye on the square and the edge of it on the man to his left, Hyvred’s Hako, big and steady and not moving, and felt the words coming up in him the way the sick had. You couldn’t stop it. You could only choose when.

He chose when Rachael said call it in.

Saga. Tell them how it ends. They’ll fight to make it true.

He’d found it a long time ago, by accident, telling a story to a frightened party in the dark. He’d been telling stories his whole life. He used to tell stories too, Ansa had said, on the fifth wall, about her husband. To the chickens. And he’d laughed and then not. He’d always told them the same way. Third person. Past tense. And so our hero. Because if you said I, it was just you, frightened, in the dark. If you said our hero, it was a story. And stories ended.

He’d never once told one before it happened.

That was the thing about Saga. You told them how it ended. Not how it had ended. How it would. And if they believed you, they’d fight to make it true. It wasn’t magic. Or it was, but the magic wasn’t in the words. It was in them. Thirty-five people hearing a story with the end already in it, and deciding, every one of them, yes, that one, that’s the one we’re in.

He’d done it on the fifth wall on ten, to strangers. And every shield covered the one on its left. And the line held.

It had held.

“And so our heroes came to the middle of the town that forgot,” said Azac. “And it had all of them. Every one the town had ever let go of. Standing on the flags in the grey, waiting to be looked at.”

Out in the square, the grey shapes stood. Held. Rachael’s bare hand out, shaking. Not yet.

And the tall thing in the middle turned.

He saw it.

Straight. Not sideways. Everybody did. He’d been looking at it at the edge of his eye for a quarter of an hour, a tall thin gap in the shape of a person, and now it turned toward the ring and he could look straight at it and it was there.

It was very big.

Taller than the houses round the square. Taller than Turnstead’s wall. Grey. Not stone, not cloth. It was made of things. He could see them, all over it, the way you can see the pieces in a wall made of old bricks. Doors. Hundreds of doors, grey, shut, set in it like scales. Windows. A street, folded, running up one arm of it like a vein, with tiny grey cobbles and a tiny grey pump. Roofs. A chimney. And faces. Everywhere. In among the doors and the windows and the folded streets, faces, hundreds, small, grey, with no features. Rubbed out. Like faces on old coins where a thumb has gone over them a thousand times.

And in the middle of it, where a chest would be, where a heart would be on anything else, nothing.

Not dark. Not a hole. Nothing. The shape of a light, a long low oval the size of a door, and nothing in it. Where one should be and isn’t. Yan had said. Azac had thought he understood. He hadn’t.

It had no eyes. It didn’t need any. It turned the whole of itself toward the ring, the way the windows round the square had all turned toward Dennis’s Double, and he felt it look. At all of them. At Rachael, holding its forgotten open with one bare hand. At Dennis, with the book.

The houses leaned in.

All round the square. Every doorless house, all at once, leaning toward the ring. Not falling. Leaning. The way the streets of Turnstead leaned when nobody watched, except a hundred times harder, and from every side, and the windows, all those hundreds of windows, filling with grey.

“And the town that forgot,” said Azac, louder, because his voice wanted to go and he wouldn’t let it, “didn’t want to give them back.”

The Unseen lifted one arm.

The arm with the street in it.

It came down on the ring between Hako and Azac.

He had perhaps half a breath. He didn’t use it to think. Thinking was for afterwards, on steps, with Dennis writing it down. He got his shield up and across to his left, over Hako, over the place where Hako’s head was, and planted his feet on the grey flags, and the arm came down.

It was like being hit by a building.

That was what it was. A grey arm made of a folded street, coming down out of the air, and he took it on the face of his shield, and his knees went, and his elbows went, and the flags under his boots cracked in a star. And he heard himself make a sound he’d never made.

Overlap.

It went out of him. The weight. Down the line. To Deston on his right, and past Deston, and to Hako on his left and past Hako, and on, both ways, all the way round, thirty-six people, a ring. What strikes the line, the whole line takes, and lightly. He felt the arm’s weight go out of his shield into all of them, thinner and thinner, a thirty-sixth each. Every one of them sagging, very slightly, at once. And every one of them still standing.

He was still standing.

The arm lifted.

And as it lifted, he felt the other thing. Not the weight. The grey. Where the arm had touched his shield, where the folded street had pressed against the iron. A cold that went out instead of in. Up his arm.

He forgot what he was doing.

Just for a moment. His shield was up and he didn’t know why. He was standing on grey flags in a ring of people and he didn’t know—

THUD.

Salty.

Somewhere along the ring. Not with the anvil; the anvil was back at the top of the alley, with Pask. With his sword. The flat of Salty’s greatsword brought down on the flags at his feet, THUD, so hard Azac felt it in his teeth. And again.

Keep Time. While they can hear you, nothing you watch over is lost.

Azac remembered what he was doing.

He was holding a line. On the fifth wall. In the snow. With strangers.

No. In a square. With his friends. In a town that forgot.

THUD.

“And every shield,” said Azac. His voice was rough. It came out of him anyway. “Covered the one on its left. And every one of them heard the hammer. And nobody forgot what they were for.”

All round the ring, shields came up.

Dennis read the tab.

Azac heard it under everything. Under the hammer and the cracking of the flags and the creak of the leaning houses and his own voice going. Dennis’s voice. Not loud. Steady. The voice he used on a stall, when he’d decided what something was worth and was going to get it.

“Hollis the baker,” said Dennis. “A woman. Grey coat. A limp on the left. A brown loaf and a twist, every seventh day, for twenty years. One month unpaid.” He drew breath. “Owed: her name.”

In front of him, the grey woman with the limp stood on the flags. Held open by Rachael’s hand. Waiting.

The Unseen turned its whole vast self toward Dennis.

“Owed,” said Dennis. To it. To the thing taller than the houses. “Her name. You’ve got it. It’s not yours.” He didn’t look away from it. He looked straight at it. A man who’d once cried in front of a cheesemonger. “Call it in.”

The Unseen didn’t give it back.

It came for him instead.

The second arm. Not the street one. The other. Made of doors. Hundreds of grey doors, shut, set into it like the scales on a fish, and every one of them opening as it came down, a hundred grey doorways opening on nothing, coming down on Dennis out of the air—

And Calvin went first.

Azac saw it from four people away. He’d tell it, afterwards, on the step, and it would be the best thing he ever told. But he saw it first. That was the thing he’d keep. He saw it before he told it.

Calvin, on Rachael’s right, with Rachael’s fist in his collar. Who’d turned his head a quarter of an hour ago to look at a grey girl’s face, and forgotten the woman holding on to him for two years. Who’d shouted her name until the windows shook.

Calvin let go of the ring.

Not out. Forward. One step, onto the flags, into the square, toward the thing coming down on Dennis. His daggers out. Both of them.

And Rachael’s fist went with him. In his collar. She didn’t let go. She’d never once let go. She came out of the ring with him, one step, her other hand still out, palm open, holding all the grey shapes on the square.

“LOOK AT MY BACK,” said Calvin.

He’d said it on every floor. On the trapdoor into fifteen. On the stair at Tell. On the Long Acre with a stook on his shoulders. Look at my back. Nobody runs. It was his. It was the stupidest, best thing anybody had ever said in a fight, and he said it every time, and every time it worked.

Slipstream. Go first. Everyone behind you goes faster. Nobody gets left.

And Azac understood. All at once. In the half breath between the doors coming down and Calvin’s feet hitting the flags.

Watch each other. That was the trick. That was Ruth’s whole trick. Thirty, round a fire, watching each other, so the Loose couldn’t take anybody. And the ring was that. Thirty-six people watching left.

But you couldn’t fight from a ring. You couldn’t do anything from a ring but stand.

Look at my back.

If everybody looked at the same thing—

“Calvin’s back,” said Azac.

He didn’t mean to say it in the voice. It came out in the voice. It went across the square and round the ring and into every one of them, the way the Saga did, the way it had on the fifth wall.

“And so,” said Azac, “our heroes broke the ring. And every one of them looked at the same back. And nobody was left. Because nobody looked away from it.”

He stepped out of the ring.

He’d never seen anything like it.

He’d tell it a hundred times and never get it right. Thirty-six people stepping off the edge of a still square, all at once, every one of them. Not in a ring now. A line. Behind one man. And every one of them looking at the same thing.

Calvin’s back.

Straight. Not sideways. Every eye on the square on one back in a brown coat with a woman’s fist in its collar, going first. And because every one of them was looking at it, every one of them was looking at all the others too, at the edge of their eye, everybody in the line ahead of them, all looking the same way. A line of people watching one man, and so watching each other, all the way down.

Nobody was unwatched.

Not one.

The Unseen’s door-arm came down on Dennis.

Dennis wasn’t there. He was in the line. Behind Calvin. Behind Rachael. Running. With the book open in his hand. Faster than Dennis had ever run in his life. Everyone behind you goes faster.

The doors hit the flags where Dennis had been. A hundred grey doorways slamming down on empty stone, and the flags shattering under them, and grey dust going up. And nothing in the doors. Nobody to take.

The line went past it.

Azac ran. Shield up, on his arm, out to the left, over the man on his left, whoever it was, he didn’t look, he looked at Calvin’s back. Covering. Cover the one on your left. Ahead of him, Rachael, her fist in Calvin’s collar, her other hand still out and open and shaking, holding the forgotten on the square as she ran. Dennis behind her. Reading. Out loud. Running and reading.

“The cobbler. A man who walked over on the outsides of his heels. Six years of boots on tick. Owed: his name.”

And behind Azac, the rest. Thirty-two more. He didn’t need to look. He could hear them. Gabe’s breathing. Hako’s dog, barking. Xav, silent. Thea’s lantern, swinging, the amber light going across the grey flags ahead of them all and leaning, the way it always leaned, toward a man somewhere in the middle of the line with a little lantern on his belt.

Not first.

In the line. Looking at Calvin’s back, like everybody else.

“And the one who’d always gone first,” said Azac. In the voice. He couldn’t help it. “Was in the middle. Watching somebody else’s back. For the first time in two lives.”

He didn’t know why he said two lives. It came out. The Saga did that sometimes; it put words in. He’d think about it, afterwards, on the step, for a long time.

He didn’t take it back.

The Unseen came down to meet them.

All of it. Not an arm. The whole of it. The vast grey shape made of doors and windows and folded streets and rubbed-out faces, bending, coming down over the square the way a wave comes down over a beach, with the light-shaped nothing in the middle of it open like a mouth.

Calvin didn’t stop.

He went straight at it. At the middle. At the long oval of nothing where its heart should be. Straight at it, across the shattered flags, with his daggers out and Rachael’s fist in his collar and thirty-five people behind him looking at his back.

The houses round the square leaned in so far their roofs nearly touched over the line. The windows were full of grey. The flags were coming up under their feet in sheets. A slate-dog came out of the wall of a doorless house, then six, then twenty, pouring across the square toward the line—

And went for nobody.

They ran at the line and there wasn’t anybody to go for. Not one person in it who wasn’t being watched. They went along the side of the line, snapping, turning, looking for the point, for somebody alone, somebody nobody had their eye on.

There wasn’t anybody.

Gabe put Hearthwall’s edge through the first one without breaking stride. Drew turned the second, bare-handed, Hinge, into the third. Hako’s spear. Salty’s sword, flat, THUD. Winter’s arrows. Higu’s, faint as moonlight through cloud. Nobody stopped. Nobody looked at the dogs. Everybody looked at Calvin’s back and hit whatever came at them out of the edge of their eye, the way you swat a fly without looking at it, and kept running.

“And they couldn’t find anybody,” said Azac. “Not one. Because there wasn’t anybody nobody was looking at.”

Calvin reached it.

Azac was six behind him. He saw it over Rachael’s shoulder, over Dennis’s head.

Calvin went into the middle of the Unseen.

Into the light-shaped nothing. Where its heart should be. He didn’t slow down. He went into it the way he’d gone up the Moth’s leg on sixteen, without thinking, daggers first, and Rachael’s fist in his collar went with him, and Rachael went with him, and Dennis behind Rachael, reading.

“Pask the smith,” said Dennis. Running. Into the nothing. “His wife. Forty years married. She laughed at him for leaving the bellows open.” He didn’t stop. “The Smithy went into the Loose at her burying. He calls her her. Owed—”

And from the edge of the square behind them, from the mouth of the Loose, where an old man in a leather apron too big for him had come down through the turning streets on Tiger’s rope, after them, at a walk, with Ressa’s hammer that Salty had left with him at the anvil, on the beat, alone, because he’d heard them say they were going to call it in—

From the edge of the square, Pask shouted.

Azac had never heard anybody in Turnstead shout. Not once. Not in fifteen days. You didn’t take your eyes off your street long enough.

Pask wasn’t watching a street. Pask was standing at the edge of the still square with his back to the whole of the Loose, looking straight across the shattered flags at a grey shape standing there, held, with Rachael’s hand out toward it.

A woman’s shape. Grey. Thin. Laughing. You could see it in the shape of her, even grey, even with no face. Laughing at something. At somebody. At a man, maybe, who’d left the bellows open.

“MERRIN,” shouted Pask.

The Unseen stopped.

Azac felt it stop. Everybody did.

The whole vast grey bending shape of it, with Calvin and Rachael and Dennis already inside the nothing in its middle, stopped. In the air over the square. Like a breath held.

And the grey woman on the flags turned her head.

Toward Pask. Toward the edge. Toward an old man with his back to the Loose and a small hammer in his hand. And she wasn’t grey.

Not all at once. From the face out. Azac watched it happen. A face coming up out of the grey the way a coin’s face comes up when you rub the dirt off it with your thumb. A woman’s face. Old. Round. Laughing. With her hair up and coming down at one side. And then the rest of her. A dress. Blue. An apron. Colour. The first colour Azac had seen on that square.

She looked at Pask across the flags.

Pask stood at the edge of the still square with the hammer hanging from his hand and his mouth open.

“Merrin,” said Pask again. Not shouting now. “Merrin. Merrin.”

She didn’t say anything. She couldn’t, Azac thought. She’d been gone eleven years. She’d been gone longer than that; she’d been buried. She wasn’t coming back. She was just—

Remembered.

She lifted her hand. Toward Pask. The way you’d lift your hand to somebody across a street, to say I see you.

And then she went. Not grey. Not thin. She just went, quietly, the way Edda’s glim had gone over the pool on sixteen, on the count, on the beat. Up, and gone. Seen. Named. Let go.

The Unseen made a sound.

Azac would never be able to say what it was. Dennis tried, that night, and gave up, and wrote — in his book. The sound a page makes when it tears. The sound a door makes when it opens after a long time shut. The sound of a whole town drawing a breath.

And Calvin, in the middle of the nothing, put both his daggers into it.

It wasn’t a blow. Azac would get that wrong for the first ten tellings and Calvin would correct him every time. I didn’t hit anything. There wasn’t anything to hit.

Calvin went into the light-shaped place in the middle of the Unseen, the long oval of nothing where a heart should be, and put his daggers into it, and there wasn’t anything there. They went into nothing up to the hilts.

So he did the only thing he had left.

He turned round.

In the middle of the nothing. With his back to it. With his daggers still in it. He turned round, all the way, and looked back down the line. At Rachael, with her fist in his collar. At Dennis, reading. At Azac, six back. At all of them. Thirty-five faces, every one of them looking at him.

And he looked back.

At every one of them. Full on. The way he’d looked at the grey girl. The way Dec had looked across the square at Rachael. Straight. He stood in the middle of the place where nobody was and looked at thirty-five people, one after another, all the way down the line, and Azac saw his lips move.

He was counting.

One. Two. Three.

“And so,” said Azac. In the voice. He could barely get it out. “The one who’d looked away turned round in the middle of the nothing. And looked at every one of them. And counted them. Out loud. So that whatever was in there would know.”

“Thirty-five,” said Calvin.

Inside the Unseen. Out loud. To the line.

“Thirty-six,” said Calvin. And looked at Rachael. Last. Right in front of him. With her fist in his collar.

“Still thirty-six.”

The Unseen came apart.

Not like the Moth. The Moth had opened. The Unseen unfolded.

That was the only word. From the middle, from the place where Calvin was standing, out. The vast grey shape coming apart into the things it was made of. Doors. Windows. Roofs. Streets, folded up in it like letters in a drawer, unfolding. Going out from the middle of the square in every direction, grey going to stone colour, to slate colour, to the colour of real streets, and laying themselves down. Across the shattered flags. Out past the doorless houses. Out into the Loose.

And the doorless houses round the square had doors.

Azac saw it. He’d swear to it on any step. Every house round the square, every one that had faced in with its hundreds of windows and no way in, had a door. Just there. Where it should always have been. Painted. Blue, green, red, brown. With steps.

And the grey shapes on the flags—

They turned. All of them. All at once. The way Merrin had turned. Toward the edge of the square. Toward the Loose. Toward the town beyond it.

And somewhere out in the town, past the Loose, past the Crook and Ottilie’s step and the tower on the wall, somebody shouted a name.

Then somebody else.

Then everybody.

Azac heard it come in from the edges of Turnstead like rain coming across a roof. Names. Hundreds. Called out from steps and windows and doors, in a town where nobody ever shouted, by people who’d forgotten them for nine years or eleven or forty, all at once, the moment the thing in the middle came apart. A baker’s voice, gravel: LETTICE. Lettice Dunmore. The usual— A boy’s, high: Uncle Tobyn! It’s TOBYN— Two old women arguing, very far off, and then not arguing, saying the same name together.

And the grey shapes on the flags, one after another, as their names came in from the town, took colour. Faces. Clothes. And lifted a hand toward the edge, the way Merrin had. And went. Quietly. Up. On no count at all, because the whole town was counting them.

Rachael’s hand came down.

He saw it. At the front of the line, inside the place where the Unseen had been, which wasn’t anything now but the middle of a square with a well in it, an old well, stone, with a bucket. Rachael, with her bare hand out, holding hundreds open. And as they went, one by one, named, the weight came off her, and her arm came down, slowly, the way you put down something you’ve carried a long way.

She stood there.

Her fist was still in Calvin’s collar.

Azac read Dennis’s over Dennis’s shoulder. Dennis was sitting on the edge of the well with the book on his knees, writing. Fast. Filling in dashes. NAME: Lettice Dunmore. He’d heard it come in from the town. NAME: Tobyn Breck. He was crying onto the page and the ink was running and he kept writing.

“Pask’s,” said Azac. Quietly.

Dennis turned back. To the line. Pask’s line. His wife. Forty years. She laughed at him for leaving the bellows open. OWED: her name.

He crossed out her name.

He wrote, in the OWED column, very carefully, in his best hand:

Merrin. Paid.

And then, under it, smaller:

In full.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he shut the book.

“That’s two,” said Dennis. Not to anybody. “Two tabs I’ve closed. In my whole life.”

“Three,” said Rachael. From beside Calvin. “You put me on it. On the square. Owed: everything.”

Dennis looked at her.

“That one’s not closed,” said Dennis. “That one’s never closing. I told you.”

Rachael didn’t say anything. She looked at him for a long moment, with her fist still in the back of Calvin’s coat.

Then she let go of Calvin’s collar.

On purpose. Azac saw her do it. She looked at her own hand, in the brown cloth, the knuckles white, and opened it, finger by finger, the way you’d open a door you’d been holding shut a long time. And took it away. And let it hang at her side.

Calvin went very still.

“I’m not going anywhere,” said Calvin. Quietly. Not turning round.

“I know,” said Rachael. “I can see you.”

He didn’t get a window for a while. He thought he wouldn’t. He’d been telling the Saga the whole way across the square, and Saga was his, it had been his since floor 1, and he didn’t think the tower had anything to say about it.

It did. On the step of Ottilie’s house, that evening, when they’d all come out of what had been the Loose and wasn’t any more.

Be in it.

That was the hard one. Not the telling. He’d always been able to tell. And so our hero. Third person. Past tense. From outside. Safe. A story about somebody.

He’d told it today from the line. From six behind Calvin. Running. With his shield over a man he didn’t look at. And so our heroes. And somewhere in the middle of it, heroes had stopped being a word for somebody else.

He’d done it already. That was why. He’d done it on the square and not known it was the trial.

He sat on the step and looked at it and thought that was probably how most of the hard ones went.

Turnstead slept that night.

All of it. At once. For the first time in the life of anybody in it. Azac sat on Ottilie’s step with Dec beside him, on watch out of habit, and watched it happen.

The Loose was streets. Real ones. Unfolded out of the Unseen and laid down across the middle of the town, with doors and steps and pumps and, in one of them, two streets from the well, a row of seven forges. Cold. Empty. With an old man standing in the middle of the street outside them in a leather apron too big for him, with a small hammer in his hand, saying a name to himself, over and over, the way you’d hum a song you’d just remembered the words to.

And the streets didn’t move.

Nobody was watching them. Everybody was going to bed. And they didn’t move.

Ottilie came out of her door at about the tenth hour of the night. She looked at her street. Then, slowly, she turned her back on it. All the way round. And looked at Azac and Dec on her step.

“It’s not going anywhere,” said Ottilie.

“No,” said Dec.

“Forty years I’ve sat on this step.” She looked at the step. “My mother before me. Her mother.” She was quiet. “I don’t know what to do.”

“Go to bed,” said Dec.

Ottilie looked at him for a long time. Full on. The way she’d looked at Tiger on the step after the alley.

Then she went in. And shut the door. On her street. With nobody watching it.

It didn’t move.

Azac sat on the step for a long time after that, with Dec beside him, not talking. Across the street, on the step of the yellow house, Salty and Wendy, on watch they didn’t need to keep, and Wendy singing, very quietly, and Salty humming, and getting the end right.

“Tell me how it ended,” said Dec.

Azac looked at him.

“You were there.”

“I was looking at Calvin’s back.” Dec almost smiled. “Tell me anyway.”

So Azac told him.

From the beginning. In the voice. And so our heroes came to the middle of the town that forgot. All of it. The ring and the grey shapes and Rachael’s hand and Dennis’s tab and the street-arm and Salty’s hammer and Calvin going first and everybody looking at his back. Pask at the edge of the square. Merrin. Calvin in the nothing, turning round, counting. Still thirty-six.

He didn’t tell the bit about two lives.

He’d said it, on the square. In the Saga. He’d heard it come out of his mouth. The one who’d always gone first was in the middle, for the first time in two lives. And he’d thought about it all afternoon on the step, and he didn’t know where it had come from, and he didn’t ask.

He left it out. He told it the way it was true for everybody.

When he finished, Dec didn’t say anything for a long time.

“That’s how it ended,” said Dec.

“That’s how it ended.”

Dec nodded. Slowly. He looked at the street. At the unwatched street that didn’t move.

“Ruth would have liked it,” said Dec.

Azac looked at him.

“Still thirty-six,” said Dec. Quietly. To the street. “She’d have liked that.”

The gate was there in the morning.

On the far side of the town from the tower. In the wall. Where, Ottilie said, sideways, out of habit, with her cup, there’d never been a gate in her life or her mother’s. Because you couldn’t keep a gate. A gate was a street that went out of the town, and a street that went out of the town would turn while you were looking the other way, and take you with it.

There was one now.

Big. Old. Oak, bound with iron, standing open. And through it, going away from Turnstead under a pale sky, a road. White. Dusty. Going up a long low hill between hedges, and over the top, and away.

Floor 18, said every window. The Long Road.

They left at noon.

Turnstead came to see them go. Not on its steps. In the street. People who’d never in their lives stood in a street without watching it, standing in the middle of it, with their backs to their houses, looking at the thirty-six. Ottilie, with Lirra on her hip and Jessamy beside her. Hollis the baker, with flour to the elbows and a brown loaf and a twist in a cloth, which he gave to Dennis without a word. The Breck boys, with a whistle. Carved. Not finished. The younger one blew it, badly, and grinned.

And Pask.

He came last. Down the middle of the street from the Smithy, slowly, in his too-big apron, with Ressa’s small old hammer in his hand. He went to Salty and held it out.

“No,” said Salty. “Keep it. You’ve got seven forges.”

“It’s yours.” Pask held it out. “She gave it you to listen to. Where there isn’t a hammer.” He looked back up the street. “There’s a hammer here now.” He was quiet. “I’ve got my own. Hers is in the end forge. Merrin’s. She used to strike for me.” He put Ressa’s hammer in Salty’s hand and closed Salty’s fingers round it with his old thin ones. “Somewhere there won’t be one. Up there. You’ll want it.”

Salty didn’t say anything. He went red, and then his eyes went, and Wendy put her hand flat on his back.

They went out through the gate.

Azac went out near the back. With his shield on his back and the taste of sick still faintly in his mouth, because he’d been sick again at noon, quietly, behind Ottilie’s house, before he said goodbye to a town. He always was.

On the road outside the gate, at the bottom of the long low hill, he stopped and looked back.

Turnstead. Grey, behind its wall, under a pale sky. With its gate open. And not one person on a step.

And so our heroes left the town that remembered, he thought. And nobody watched them go. Because nobody had to.

He didn’t say it out loud.

He turned round and went up the hill after the others, behind Deston, with the edge of his eye on the man on his left out of habit, and Rachael somewhere ahead of him walking beside Calvin with both her hands in her pockets.
