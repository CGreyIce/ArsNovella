Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 80

A Thousand Words

3,225 words · 14 min read

Xav sat at the back, the way he always sat, and waited.

They’d come to the square at dusk. All thirty-five, and one more. The market had packed up round them the way it packed up every evening, the striped awnings coming down, the cheesemonger humming, the dog asleep under the butcher’s table with its stomach full of the same sausage. The townspeople walked past and looked at them, a little puzzled, the way they looked at everything for the first time. Thirty-six strangers sitting on the cobbles round the fountain, as if they were waiting for a play.

Merle had brought bread. Two baskets of rosemary loaves, still warm. She’d put them down in the middle of everybody without saying anything and gone and sat on the church steps on the corner, with her floury arms round her knees, to watch.

Declan stood by the fountain.

He’d washed the flour off. Mostly. There was still some in his hair, a white streak beside the teal. He’d put on the white cloak, the one from the bedroll in the tower. Somebody had gone up and got it for him, and Xav thought he knew who. The shield was on his back. The new one. Dark grey, with the lamp, and the dark red rim. He kept reaching up and touching the edge of it over his shoulder, as if he wanted to check it was still there.

His jaw was purple on the left side. Malcolm had offered to fix it. Twice. Declan had said no. Leave it. I earned it. Malcolm had looked at him for a long moment and then said, I’ll do two later, and gone and sat down.

Xav watched him stand there, by the fountain, in front of thirty-five people.

He watched him open his mouth.

And stop.

It was there again. The thing Gabe had said. The thing Xav had seen himself in the kitchen, from the doorway, before the punch. Declan stood by the fountain with his mouth open and paused. One breath. Two. Like a man at the edge of cold water, deciding.

In nine years, Xav had never once seen him do it. Declan was the one who talked first. In their first week at poly, he’d walked into a room full of strangers and talked to every single one of them until they weren’t strangers. He’d never stopped to decide whether to. He’d just gone. Shield up, through the door.

Wait. Then move once. It had taken Xav a year to get him to wait half a second.

Now he waited two breaths before he said anything to people he’d known half his life.

Xav didn’t know what had taught him that. He thought about a week behind a wall in the Red City, and three weeks on a terrace, and eighteen tries at a bowl from every direction. And he thought he could make a guess.

“Sorry,” said Declan.

That was the first word. He said it to all of them, looking round at all of them, one face at a time, the way he’d looked at them in the kitchen.

“I’m going to say that first,” he said. “Before Gabe makes me.” A few people laughed. Gabe, near the front, did not. “I’m sorry. For the white room. For running. For the arch, and not saying anything, and leaving a note on a card instead of waking anybody up.” He swallowed. “For not answering. Any of it. The whispers. World. Gabe, every morning, for a hundred and fifty days.” He looked at Gabe. “I read every one. Alea pinned them. Every morning, at the top. So I’d see them first thing.” He was quiet. “I’m sorry I didn’t answer. I’m sorry I made you chase. I’m sorry you had to sit on a stair in a furnace hall and on the edge of a pit, waiting, and I wasn’t there.”

Nobody said anything.

“That’s the first thousand,” said Declan. “Roughly. The sorry. Gabe says I owe him one more, separately.” His mouth did something at the corner. “I’ll do that one later. In private. So he can hit me again.”

“Damn right,” said Gabe.

More people laughed this time. Xav didn’t. He was watching Declan’s hands. They were at his sides. They were shaking, very slightly.

He told them where he’d been. Not all of it. Xav could tell, from the back, the way you can tell when somebody’s reading from a page they’ve already cut. He told them the floors. Briefly. One at a time. Things they mostly knew, from the slate, from Hyvred’s trail, from Nan Gully and Otta and Ostrel and Hessa. A chapel at dawn. A bell. A pass in a storm. A drowned tower. A thing on a roof. A furnace. A bird. A hole in the air.

He didn’t make any of it sound like anything. He said it the way you’d say what you’d had for dinner. I killed the thing in the square on the twenty-fifth day. I went in with a bell. I watched the dust. Rui Xiang, sitting near Xav, made a very small noise and looked at the ground.

And then Calvin, at the front, who’d been sitting on his hands for twenty minutes with Rachael’s elbow in his side, couldn’t hold it any longer.

“Floor 4,” said Calvin. “The note. Train longer on the third floor.” He was leaning forward. “You wrote that in the white room. Before you’d seen anything. Before anybody had seen anything.” His voice went up. “And you were right. The thing on the roof. You knew it was there. You sat behind a wall for a week watching that square before any of us got near it.” He stared up at Declan. “How did you know?”

The square went very quiet.

Xav watched it happen. Not the question. He’d known somebody would ask it. He’d have bet on Calvin. He watched the answer happen. Watched Declan’s face go still. Watched him look down at Calvin, and something in his eyes go a very long way away, and come back.

He watched Declan’s eyes go, very briefly, to Calvin’s left arm.

Then back up.

“I can’t tell you that,” said Declan.

Calvin opened his mouth.

“Not yet,” said Declan. Quietly. Steady. “I’m sorry, Cal. I can’t. Not yet.”

“Why—“

“Because—“ Declan stopped. He looked round at all of them. “Because the last time I told anybody everything, it went wrong. Badly. And I’m not doing that again. Not till I know what it does.” He was quiet. “I’m not hiding it to be clever. I’m not hiding it because I don’t trust you. I’m hiding it because I don’t know yet if it’s safe. For you.”

Calvin stared at him.

“Trust,” said Declan.

It was the word. The one he’d always said. On calls, in games, at three in the morning. Trust. Usually just before he did something stupid and it worked. Xav had heard it a thousand times.

He’d never heard it said like that. Like it weighed something.

Calvin looked up at him for a long time. Xav watched his face. Calvin, who asked everything, directly, always. Who’d asked every question that had ever occurred to him on every floor since the white room.

“Okay,” said Calvin. Quietly. For Calvin.

And he sat back. And Rachael took her elbow out of his side, and put her hand on his arm instead.

Xav watched the thirteen.

He couldn’t help it. He was in it now. He’d sworn himself into it on the lip of a pit in the desert, under the moon. He sat at the back of the square and watched the faces of the twelve others while Declan said not yet and trust.

Chris, with his notebook closed on his knee, not writing. Kei, very still, with Dose’s head up beside his ear. Hako, who’d gone completely silent, which for Hako was the loudest thing he could have done. Hyvred, crying, not hiding it. Winter, with her arms folded, looking straight at Declan with her blunt silver-haired stare, as if she was memorising his face for a court case. Drew. Higu. Chi. Xola. Gigi. Bee.

None of them looked at each other. That was the thing. Thirteen people who’d sat round a map on the lip of a pit and said a word out loud. And none of them looked at each other when Declan said not yet. They all just looked at him.

And Thea.

She was sitting at the edge of the crowd with the lantern in her lap. Not looking at Declan. Not looking at anybody. Looking at the lantern. At the amber light in it. With her face very calm, and her hand, the right one, lying flat on her left shoulder.

Xav looked at Gabe.

Gabe was sitting near the front with the Kiln-heart Iron in his lap, both hands round it, like something warm on a cold night. He was looking up at Declan. And Xav saw Gabe’s face when Declan said not yet. He saw it go through the hurt, and out the other side, and land on something else.

“Not yet’s fine,” said Gabe. Loudly. To everybody. “Not yet’s fine. He’ll tell us when he tells us.” He looked round at all of them, daring anybody to argue. “He said trust. He always says trust. It always works.” He swallowed. “Mostly.”

Declan looked at him. His face did something Xav couldn’t watch for long.

Gabe didn’t know. Neither did Deston, or Calvin, or most of the people sitting on the cobbles. They didn’t know about the thirteen, or the word, said on the lip of a pit. But Gabe was the one Xav had sworn about. Gabe had been asleep in a round room at the bottom of a pit in the desert while thirteen people sat round a map.

When it’s a fact, Gabe hears it first. Before the slate. From you.

I swear it.

Xav sat at the back of the square in the dusk and held that, very still, with both hands. The way he’d seen Declan hold a whisper, once, in a window, without sending it.

It wasn’t a fact yet. Not yet.

“There’s one more thing,” said Declan. “Before the bells.”

He’d told them about the bells. About the Hour coming out of the clock at midnight. About thirty-six ropes and a beam with words carved in it, and a baker and a boy and seven bells ringing at once and the Hour stopping for three breaths. About twenty-six nights on a bedroll in the middle of a ring of ropes.

“Things change,” said Declan. “After I meet them.”

The square went quiet again.

“The bird on five,” he said. “It didn’t use to land. It used to dive, and pull out, and climb. That’s how you beat it. You stand still and take the dive and hit its legs on the way out.” He looked at Gabe. “That’s how I’d have done it. Standing still. Like a wall. And when I got to it, it landed. On whoever stood still. On me.” He was quiet. “I think it learned that from the Kiln. Where I stood still in the fire.”

Nobody said anything.

“And the thing on six,” said Declan. “The one that showed you what you wanted. That wasn’t there, before.” He stopped. He looked at the ground. “I mean. I don’t think it was supposed to be there. I think it was new. I think it was made out of—“ He stopped again. “I think the tower watches what I do. And changes what’s next. Not to everybody. To me.”

“Why noon?” said Chris.

It came out of him quietly, from the middle of the crowd. Declan looked at him.

“The Prism,” said Chris. “You fought it at noon. With the sun right down into it. Anybody would have done it at night.” He was holding his notebook very tightly. “Higu said it was the hour nobody would choose. Like you were avoiding the obvious way.”

Declan looked at Higu. Higu looked back at him, calm, with her pale lilac eyes.

“Yeah,” said Declan, slowly. “That’s exactly why.” He looked at her a moment longer. “That’s very good, Higu.”

“I draw maps,” said Higu.

“So this floor,” said Declan. “The bells. Thirty-six.” He looked up at the clocktower, at the dark shapes in the belfry against the evening. “I think the tower built a floor I can’t clear on my own. On purpose. Because it can’t see me. Not properly. It can only see what changes round me. And this is the one thing it could build where I’d have to stand in one room with all of you, at one time, with my hands on a rope.” He looked back down at them. “So it could see who was on the thirty-sixth rope.”

“Is it a trap?” said Drew.

“Maybe,” said Declan.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The bell in the tower rang the half hour. One note, rolling out over the square.

“I thought about it,” said Declan. “For twenty-six nights. Lying up there in the middle of the ropes. Whether it was a trap. Whether I should find another way. Whether I should wait for you on eight.” He looked at Merle, on the church steps, with her arms round her knees. “And then she asked me if it would ever be tomorrow.”

Merle looked back at him across the square.

“So let it look,” said Declan.

He said it to all of them. Quietly. In a voice Xav had never heard him use. Not the funny one, not the loud one, not the trust one. A voice like the end of something.

“Let it see me,” he said. “Let it see all of us. Thirty-six hands on thirty-six ropes. I’m done being a floor away.” He looked round at them. “Tonight. At midnight. I’d like thirty-five people to come up a tower with me and pull a rope.” His mouth did something. “And then tomorrow, there’s going to be a tomorrow.”

Calvin was the first to laugh. Then everybody. Then Merle, on the church steps, with her face in her floury hands.

Afterwards, when people had started drifting off in knots to the inn, to the fountain, to the baskets of bread, Xav found him.

He was sitting on the edge of the fountain on his own, with the shield across his knees. He was running his thumb round the inside of the rim, over the little iron rose. The square was going dark. The lamps were coming on in the windows of the houses round it. Above them, the clocktower’s four faces were lit from inside like moons.

Xav sat down next to him. On the edge of the fountain. With his hands on his knees.

They didn’t say anything for a while. They’d never needed to.

“You picked it for me,” said Xav.

Declan’s thumb stopped on the rose.

“The name,” said Xav. “Voyager. Out of everything. You picked the one thing only I’d understand.”

Declan didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Yeah,” he said at last.

“So I’d know what you were doing.”

“So somebody would.” Declan was looking at the shield. “I thought—I thought if you saw it at the top of the list, you’d think nah. Just for a second. And then you’d think about it. And you’d know.” He swallowed. “Going on ahead. On its own. Sending back. That’s all I could do. I couldn’t send words. I could send things.”

“A coat,” said Xav. “A lantern. A thing on a roof. Water.”

“Yeah.”

“I’m still here,” said Xav.

Declan’s face did something. He looked down at the shield.

“I’m still here,” said Xav again. Quietly. “That’s what Gabe sent you. On five. Wasn’t it. After I told him. Still here. Me too.”

“Yeah.”

“I’m still here too,” said Xav. “In case nobody said.”

Declan didn’t answer. He sat on the edge of the fountain with the shield across his knees and his face turned away, and Xav let him. He waited. He was good at that. He’d always been good at that.

“You paused,” said Xav, after a while. “Before you started talking. In the square. And in the kitchen, Gabe said. Before you said your name.”

Declan went still.

“You never used to,” said Xav. “Not once. In nine years. You’d talk to anybody. You’d go through any door.” He looked out at the square. “It took me a year to teach you to wait half a second.”

“I know.”

“Somebody taught you the rest,” said Xav.

Declan didn’t move at all.

Xav didn’t look at him. He looked at the clocktower, at the four lit faces, and the dark belfry, and the thirty-six shapes in it against the stars. He said it the way he said everything. Calm. As if he’d already thought about it for a long time and decided it was just true.

“A week behind a wall,” said Xav. “Three weeks on a terrace. Eighteen times at a bowl. Twenty-six nights in a tower with two hands and thirty-six ropes.” He was quiet. “I didn’t teach you that. Nobody teaches anybody that in a hundred and fifty days.”

The square was very quiet. Somewhere, the dog was barking.

“You don’t have to say who,” said Xav.

Declan turned his head and looked at him.

Xav met his eyes. Just for a second. And he saw it. In the teal, under the bruise, under the tiredness. He saw Declan understand that Xav knew something. Not what. Not all of it. But something. He saw Declan’s face go through being frightened, and then out the other side, and land on something Xav didn’t have a word for.

“Xav,” said Declan. Very quietly.

“When you’re ready,” said Xav. “To tell the rest. To anybody.” He looked back at the clocktower. “Wait as long as you need. As long as it takes. And then move once.”

Declan didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he laughed. Very softly. A cracked, tired, wet laugh, with his head down over the shield.

“You taught me that,” he said.

“Half of it,” said Xav. “Somebody else taught you the waiting.”

Declan looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded. Once. Slowly.

“Yeah,” he said. “Somebody else did.”

He didn’t say who. Xav didn’t ask. They sat on the edge of the fountain in the dark with the shield between them, until the half-past-ten bell rang, and Drew came across the square to say it was time.
