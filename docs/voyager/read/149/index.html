Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 149

The Map of the Countinghouse

3,398 words · 15 min read

Higu drew the Countinghouse on the back of the rock, and it went off the edge.

She did it properly. The way she’d drawn the Glass on floor 6 by moonlight, and the Down on floor 9 from what everybody told her, and the Salt from Maigo’s rings. She sat cross-legged on the copy-room floor with the rock on her knees, back side up, wiped clean, and a stub of chalk, and Rui Xiang on one side of her and John on the other, and she asked them questions until they ran out of answers.

The outside first. That was easy. She’d paced it herself. Thirty paces long, ten wide, four steps up to a plain door, no windows. She drew it small and neat in the corner of the rock. A box.

Then the passage. Ten paces, Rui Xiang said. Grey stone. Grey light from nowhere. She drew it.

Then the hall.

“How long?” she said.

Rui Xiang thought about it. “Longer than the building.”

“How much longer?”

“Longer than the Tellhouse. Longer than the hill.” He looked at her. “I don’t know, Higu. I stopped being able to tell. It went back until it was too grey to see.”

She looked at John.

“Same,” said John. “The mote went along the pillars for a long way. It didn’t get to the end. There wasn’t an end. Just the lectern, and then more grey behind it.”

Higu looked at her rock.

She’d drawn the box in the corner. Thirty paces by ten. And the passage, going in. And now she drew the hall, going out of the passage, and back, and back, past the edge of the box, past the edge of where the building stopped, across the whole of the back of the rock. Pillars in rows. Desks. A lectern at the far end, where Rui Xiang said it was. And then past the lectern, she kept going, in light lines, fainter and fainter, until the chalk went off the edge of the stone onto her knee.

She sat and looked at it.

“It’s not in Tell,” she said.

Rui Xiang and John looked at each other.

“That’s why it doesn’t fit,” said Higu. She put her finger on the box in the corner. “That’s Tell. That’s the building. Thirty by ten.” She moved her finger along the passage, into the hall, out across the rock. “And that’s not. That’s the tower. Somebody put a door in Tell that opens into the tower.” She looked up. “Like the stair. Every stair we’ve ever climbed is longer than the floor it’s in. We just never measured.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Can you draw it bigger?” said John.

“No,” said Higu. “That’s the point.”

Thea told them on the morning of the three hundred and ninety-first day.

Not all of them. Her party. She came and found them where they were, which was in the copy-room yard after breakfast: Drew on the edge of the trough with his buckler off and his knuckles bare, the way they were all the time now; Chi on the stable step with Waddles in her lap; Xola lying flat on her back on the warm stones with Squiddles on her chest, pretending she wasn’t listening to anything; Higu with the rock.

Thea stood in the middle of them with the lantern.

“I’m going in,” she said. “To the Countinghouse. I’m going to read it. The book.” She looked at each of them, one at a time, the way she looked at windows. “Before the Reckoning. I need to know what it says. I’m asking you to come.”

Nobody moved.

Higu watched them not move. She’d been in this party for three hundred and ninety-one days. She knew how each of them was not moving.

Drew was the first. He always was. He sat on the edge of the trough with his empty hands on his knees, and looked at Thea for a long time, and Higu watched his face do the thing it did under the jokes. Thinking very fast. Deciding not to say most of it.

“Is it bad?” said Drew.

He’d asked it on floor 9. On floor 10. At the slate on this floor, when Cursiffe’s ones before came up to ninety-nine. Nobody had ever answered him.

Thea looked at him.

“I don’t know yet,” she said. “That’s why.”

Drew nodded slowly. As if that was an answer. Higu thought it might be the first real one he’d had.

“Obviously,” said Xola, from the stones, without opening her eyes. “Obviously I’m coming. Don’t be stupid.”

“Yes,” said Chi. Calm. From the step.

Higu looked down at her rock. At the hall going off the edge of it.

“I want to draw it,” she said. “The inside. Properly.” She looked up at Thea. “I want to see where it ends.”

Thea looked at her for a moment. Then, very slightly, she smiled.

“Okay,” said Thea.

“We go together,” said Drew. “All of us. Nobody stops at the door, nobody goes ahead, nobody stays behind to look at anything.” He stood up. “And John’s light, if he’s still coming.”

“He’s coming,” said Thea. “He said.”

“Then we’re six.” Drew looked at the yard gate. At the Wellgate past it. At the plain grey building across the road, with a clerk standing on its top step. “Who’s on the step?”

Thea didn’t answer straight away.

“Ice,” she said.

Higu asked him that afternoon. The way she always did.

She found him on the roof, where he went now in the afternoons when he wasn’t out past the Fell Gate. He was sitting on the parapet with the cord on his wrist, looking down at the Wellgate, at the Countinghouse, the way everybody did now. She came and sat on the lead below him with the rock on her knees and the chalk in her hand, and he looked down at her and knew what she was going to ask before she asked it.

“Go on,” said Higu.

He didn’t need to ask what she meant. It had been on the rock since floor 11. You say where. We find what’s true. Every floor. Every door. He’d say what he remembered, and she’d write it on the left, and then they’d go and look.

He was quiet for a while.

“Nothing,” he said.

Higu waited.

“I never went in,” he said. “Nobody did. I didn’t know there was anything in there. I didn’t know it was anything but a building.” He looked across the road at the plain grey door. “The clerks didn’t stand on the corners. They didn’t look at anybody. They went in at dusk and came out at dawn and stayed up the Wellgate, and we walked past them every day for seven weeks, and I never once thought about them.” He was quiet. “I never wondered what they wrote.”

Higu wrote it. Small capitals, on the left of the line, on the front of the rock.

COUNTINGHOUSE: NEVER WENT IN. NOBODY DID.

CLERKS: DIDN’T LOOK AT ANYONE.

On the right, she didn’t write anything.

“That’s not nothing,” she said.

He looked down at her.

“It’s the most you’ve ever told me,” said Higu. “You’ve never once said I didn’t wonder. You always knew where everything was.” She looked at the left column. “That’s a true thing about the first time too. That there was something right in front of you, and you walked past it for seven weeks, and you didn’t know.”

He didn’t say anything.

But he got down off the parapet and sat on the lead beside her, and looked at the rock with her, for a long time. At the two columns. The left one, with all his memories down it, floor after floor, in her small capitals. The right one, with everything they’d found instead.

“You’ve been keeping two records,” he said. Quietly. “Since floor 11.”

Higu looked at the rock.

She hadn’t thought of it like that. She’d thought of it as a tool. A way to stop him carrying it all on his own. You remember. We find out. A line down the middle of a stone.

But he was right. Two columns. One for what he remembered. One for what was true now. And she’d always thought of the left one as the wrong one. The one that had been, and wasn’t any more. The one the tower had changed.

She looked at it now. At ALDO RAN. At 2 MONTHS. OLD ROAD. NORTH. At THE MAST HAD ONE MAST.

They weren’t wrong. They’d happened. They’d just happened somewhere else.

“Yes,” she said slowly. “I have.”

She didn’t wipe the line. Not then. She sat and looked at it with him until the light started going gold, and Chi came up the ladder to tell them it was supper.

They planned it in the tack room, which had become Thea’s party’s room in the afternoons, when the club wasn’t in it. Six of them on upturned buckets and a bale of old straw, and the rock on the floor in the middle, back side up.

“Dusk,” said Drew. “When they go in.”

“The door opens for each of them,” said John. “On its own. I watched it five nights. It opens when they get to the top step and shuts behind them.”

“It opened for Tull,” said Higu.

“It was left open for Tull. Rui said. And Rui went in through the gap.”

“So we follow one in,” said Drew. “The last one. Before it shuts.”

“No,” said Thea.

They all looked at her.

“We don’t sneak,” said Thea. She was sitting on the bale with the lantern beside her, and her face had the reading look on it, the one Xola called the face. “Rui sneaked, and every head in the hall turned round, and it didn’t matter. John’s mote went in under the door, and they all looked up at it. They always know.” She looked at the rock. At the box in the corner, and the hall going off the edge. “There’s no sneaking into that. It’s the tower. It knows where everything is. It’s been counting us since the white room.” She was quiet. “I’m going to knock.”

“Nobody knocks,” said Higu. “John watched. The clerks don’t knock. Tull didn’t.”

“Then I’ll be the first.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Higu watched Thea’s face. She’d been watching it for three hundred and ninety-one days. Since floor 1, when Thea had read her first window like a page of code and said there’s something under this. Higu had drawn maps of everywhere they’d ever been, and if she’d ever drawn a map of Thea’s face it would have had a lot of rooms in it that she’d never been into. Thea read things. Under things. It was who she was.

And for a hundred and six days, since the morning she came back round the tarn at the top of floor 10 with her hair loose and Ice beside her, there’d been a room in Thea that nobody went into.

Higu didn’t know what was in it. She’d decided, on floor 11, the first time she noticed it, that she didn’t need to. She’d put Thea on the rota with Ice for the Shoals anyway. On purpose. Higu didn’t do accidents.

“Who goes first?” said Chi.

“Me,” said Drew. “Obviously. I’m the general. Somebody thank me.”

“Thank you, Drew,” said four people.

“John’s light ahead of me. Not far. Just enough to see.” Drew put a stone on the rock for himself, at the door. “Then Thea. With the lantern. Then Xola, behind her, close.” Another stone. “Chi. Higu. In that order. Chi watches the windows, Higu watches the walls. Higu draws.”

“I’m drawing,” said Higu.

“I know you’re drawing. I said you’re drawing.” Drew put the last stone down. “And nobody stops. Nobody goes off to look at anything. We walk to the book, Thea reads the book, we walk back out. If every head in that place turns round, we keep walking. If they stand up, we keep walking. If they start writing our names in their books, we keep walking.” He looked round at them. “We don’t run. We don’t fight. There’s a hundred and twelve of them and they haven’t got anything in them to fight. We just keep walking.”

“And if it won’t let us out?” said Xola.

Drew didn’t answer that straight away.

“Then Ice is on the step,” said Thea.

Everybody looked at her.

“He’ll be on the step,” she said again. Quietly. “Outside. On the bottom one. With Gabe. The whole time.” She looked at the lantern. “If it won’t let us out, he’ll know. He’ll know before we do.”

Xola rolled over on the straw and looked at her.

“And then what? He comes in? On his own? Into that?” She sat up. Squiddles slid down onto her shoulder. “Thea. He can’t. He’s got a cord. And he’ll—you know what he’ll do, he’ll go straight through the—“

“He won’t,” said Thea.

“He will, Thea, he always—“

“He’ll wait on the step,” said Thea. “He’s getting good at steps.”

Xola stared at her.

And then something went over Xola’s face that Higu had seen on it only a few times. On floor 9, at the top of the rope, when Thea came up last. On floor 11, on a rock in the sea. A kind of stopping. As if Xola had been about to say something very loud and found, halfway, that she didn’t need to.

“Okay,” said Xola. Quieter. “Okay. He waits on the step.”

Calvin went to Cooper’s Lane on the third day, at noon, for the fourth time. Higu didn’t go. But she was on the roof when he came back up the Wellgate, and she saw his face from a long way off.

He was crying. Not sad crying. The other kind. The kind where your face doesn’t know what it’s doing.

“She said it,” he said, when he got to the top of the ladder. To everybody on the roof. To nobody. “Out loud. Half of it. She stopped, and she turned round, nearly all the way round, and she looked at Hawise. At her. And she said Haw.” He wiped his face with his whole forearm. “And then she stopped. Like she couldn’t remember the rest. And her face went—it went like she’d dropped something and couldn’t see where. And then she walked on.”

Higu looked across the Wellgate. At the clerk on the corner below the Tellhouse, standing under its dead lamp, with its plain face and its book.

“What did Hawise do?” she said.

“She stood up.” Calvin sat down on the lead, heavily. “She’s never stood up. Twelve years. She stood up off her step and she said Alys, out loud. Not shouting. Just. Alys. The way you’d say it to somebody across a table.” He looked at his own open hand. Not the one with the names. The other one. “And the clerk didn’t turn round again. But it stopped. At the corner. For a long time. Before it went.”

Nobody on the roof said anything.

“Next week,” said Calvin. To himself. “Next week.”

Higu went to find Thea on the last night. Before.

Thea was on the roof, where she always was now, at the end of the day. On her own, for once. Ice was down in the yard with Gabe, and Xola had gone to sleep early, which Xola never did, which meant Xola was lying awake somewhere on purpose. Thea was sitting on the parapet with the lantern beside her, looking across the road at the plain grey door.

Higu sat down beside her with the rock. Not the back side. The front. The two columns.

She held it out.

Thea took it. Looked at it. At COUNTINGHOUSE: NEVER WENT IN. NOBODY DID. on the left, in the newest chalk, at the bottom of a long list going all the way back to floor 11. At the empty right.

“He said that?” said Thea.

“Today. I never wondered what they wrote.”

Thea looked at it for a long time.

“I drew the map, too,” said Higu. She turned the rock over in Thea’s hands, to the back. The box in the corner. The passage. The hall going off the edge. “I put the step on it.” She touched it. A small line at the foot of the four steps, outside the box. She’d drawn a little figure on it, sitting. Very small. With a mark on its wrist. “Ice’s step.”

Thea looked at the little figure.

“Higu,” she said.

“I’m not asking,” said Higu. “I’ve never asked. I don’t do asking.” She took the rock back, gently, and held it on her knees. “I do maps. And rotas. I put you on the rota with him on floor 11 on purpose, you know. For the Shoals. It wasn’t an accident.”

“I know,” said Thea. “I always knew.”

“I’m going to keep doing it.” Higu looked across the road. “Tomorrow, in there, I’m going to draw everything. The whole hall. As far as it goes. So afterwards, there’s a map. So whatever happens in there, we’ll know where we were.” She looked down at the two columns on the front of the rock. “That’s what I’m for. You remember. We find out. I write it down.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

“He said I’ve been keeping two records,” said Higu. “Today. Since floor 11. And I have.” She ran her thumb down the chalk line in the middle of the stone. Not wiping it. Just touching it. “I always thought the left one was the wrong one. The one the tower changed. What he remembered, and wasn’t true any more.”

She looked at it.

“It’s not wrong, is it,” said Higu. “It’s just somewhere else.”

Thea turned her head and looked at her.

Higu had seen a lot of Thea’s faces. She’d never seen this one. It was the face of somebody standing at the edge of a room nobody else had been in, deciding whether to let somebody see through the door. Just for a second. Just the door.

“No,” said Thea, very quietly. “It’s not wrong.”

That was all.

Higu nodded. She didn’t ask anything else. She sat beside Thea on the parapet with the rock on her knees, both records on it, the one on the left and the one on the right, and the line between them, and looked at the moon.

It was a real one. Floor 13 had a real moon. White, and very far away, and quiet, coming up over the plain beyond the bottom wall the way it had come up over the Glass on floor 6, when Chi had taken Higu’s hand in the line and walked with her until she could walk on her own.

“It’s a good one tonight,” said Thea.

“It’s a good one,” said Higu.

Across the road, at dusk, the clerks had gone in. A hundred and twelve. The door had opened for each of them, and shut. It was shut now. Plain and grey.

Tomorrow, Higu thought, somebody would knock.
