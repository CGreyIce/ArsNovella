Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 86

Wendmere

3,283 words · 14 min read

Wendmere was built across the last fall, and the first time he’d seen it he hadn’t been able to look at it for long.

He’d been grieving, then. They’d all been grieving, the first time, on eight. They’d come down this same stair in the rain, thirty-four of them, quiet. He’d gone first. Of course he had. He’d gone first all the way down the falls without looking at anything except the next step. And when they’d come round the last bend of the stair and seen the city below, the others had stopped and stared, and he hadn’t. He’d kept going.

This time he stopped.

Everybody did. They came round the last bend on the second evening, in a long line, wet with spray. And there it was, at the bottom of the whole staircase of falls, where the river came down its last great drop into a wide green lake.

A city. Built across the fall.

Not beside it. Not near it. Across it. On arches of grey stone that went from one side of the fall to the other, like a bridge, and on stilts sunk into the lake below. Houses on the arches, and houses on the stilts, and walkways between them, and stairs going up and down. The whole thing was hung across the fall like a net. And the water went through it. Under the arches, between the stilts, past the windows, in white curtains and ribbons and sheets, roaring. The houses were grey stone and dark wet timber, roofed with green slate that shone in the spray. There were lamps in the windows already, in the evening. Hundreds of them. Gold, behind the water, flickering where the falls went past.

“Oh,” said Hana.

“Oh my days,” said Calvin.

Bee didn’t say anything. She’d already taken out her sketchbook.

He stood at the bend of the stair with the lamp shield on his back and looked at Wendmere, properly, for the first time in two lives.

It was beautiful. He’d never noticed.

The river-folk came out to meet them on the last stair.

He’d known they would. They’d done it the first time, too. A little party of them, coming up the wet stone steps from the city slowly, in the evening light, to see who’d come down the falls. He watched the faces of the thirty-five as the river-folk came up the stair toward them, and couldn’t stop smiling.

They were tall. Taller than any of the thirty-six, most of them by a head. And thin, and long in the arm and leg, and they moved slowly, the way people move when they’ve got all the time in the world and know it. Their skin was grey-green, like the slate on their roofs, and had a faint sheen to it, like a fish’s. Their hands, when they lifted them, were webbed between the fingers. Their eyes were large and dark and wet. At the sides of their necks, under the jaw, there were three thin slits on each side that opened and closed, slowly, as they breathed. Their hair was long and dark green and hung down their backs in heavy wet ropes, like weed in a river.

“Gills,” said Kei, very quietly, beside him. With enormous professional interest. “They’ve got gills.”

Rude, said Dose, in Kei’s head. Ice could tell from Kei’s face.

The first of the river-folk stopped on the step below Calvin. An old one. Its face was deeply lined, its green hair streaked with grey, and it looked down at all thirty-six of them strung out on the stair above. Slowly. One after another. With its large wet eyes. As if it had all evening, and was prepared to spend it.

Then it looked at the soft white light by Calvin’s shoulder. The little ball that was Calvin’s system, that sat there when he wasn’t paying attention.

And then, slowly, the old one smiled.

“Lights,” it said. Its voice was very deep and very slow, and had a sound in it like water going over stones. “Lights at your shoulders.” It looked up the stair. “Lights. All of you. It has been a long, long time.”

They gave the thirty-six a house. Nobody asked them to. The river-folk just took them down into the city, over walkways and under curtains of falling water, to a long low hall on stilts at the edge of the lake. And opened the door, and stood back.

It was old. Very old. Dusty, inside, as if nobody had stood in it for a very long time. But clean under the dust. Swept, once, and swept again, and the dust allowed to come back. Long tables. Benches. A great stone hearth at one end with a fire laid ready in it, dry, as if somebody had laid it a hundred years ago and nobody had ever lit it. Windows on three sides, looking out on the falling water.

“The house for lights,” said the old river-folk guide, whose name, it turned out, was Oarin. “We keep it. We have always kept it.” He looked round at the dust. “We have kept it for three hundred and twelve years.”

“Three hundred and—“ said Dennis.

“Twelve,” said Oarin, with great patience. “Since the last ones.” He looked at the hearth. “They laid that fire. The last morning. Before they went up.” He was quiet for a moment. “Nobody has wanted to light it.”

The hall went very still.

“You may light it,” said Oarin. “If you like. It is your house.”

Nobody moved for a long time.

Then Ina went to the hearth. He knelt in front of the old dry fire somebody had laid three hundred and twelve years ago. He held out his hand. And a small flame came up in his palm, orange, ordinary, the one he’d had since floor 1. Not the white one. He put it to the kindling very gently. It caught.

The fire burned up in the old hearth in the house for lights. Thirty-six people stood round it in the dust, and nobody said anything at all.

Elder Sefa sent for them on the third morning.

He’d known she would. He’d known since the stair. Lights. It has been a long, long time. The first time, it had taken her a week to send for them. They’d been so quiet, the first time, so grey, that the river-folk had left them alone to grieve. This time, it took her two days.

She lived behind the middle fall.

That was how you got to her. Through the city, up a stair cut into the rock of the cliff beside the falls, onto a narrow ledge. Then along the ledge, behind the water. Behind the great white curtain of the middle fall, with it roaring past a metre in front of your face, so loud you couldn’t hear anything else, so close the spray soaked you through in seconds. Into a cave in the rock behind it.

The cave was dry. Lit by lamps. And at the back of it, on a chair carved out of the rock itself, sat the oldest person he’d ever seen.

The first time, he’d thought that too. He thought it again now.

Elder Sefa was tall even sitting down. Thin as a reed. Her skin had gone from green to a pale silver-grey with age, and was lined so deeply it looked like the bark of a tree. Her hair was white, and very long, and lay over her shoulders and down onto the floor of the cave in heavy pale ropes. Her gills moved very slowly. Her eyes were large and dark and wet and very, very old, and they went over all thirty-six of them, crowding into her cave behind the fall. Slowly. One after another. Taking all the time in the world.

She looked at the soft white lights at their shoulders. Thirty-five of them. Little balls of light, sitting there.

And then she looked at him.

Not at his face. At his shoulder. At the place beside his head where Alea was sitting, in the shape of nothing anybody could see.

“And yours,” said Sefa, “is a fox.”

He went very still.

He felt it go through the cave. Through all of them, the way it had gone through the market in Tether when Nan Gully said light by his ear. Thirty-five people turning to look at him. At his shoulder. At the empty air.

“Hm,” said Sefa. Her voice was like water a very long way down. “The last ones had lights like these.” She lifted one long webbed hand, slowly, and pointed at Calvin’s shoulder, at Rachael’s, at the soft white balls. “All of them. Balls of light. Thirty of them. I remember.” She turned her ancient head back to Ice. “None of them had a fox.”

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

Alea, on his shoulder, had gone absolutely still.

“She can see me,” said Alea. In his head. Very small. “Ice. She can see me.”

I know.

“The first time, she couldn’t. The first time, she looked straight past me. I logged it. She never once—“

The first time, I wasn’t standing here, he thought. The first time, I was at the back, not looking at anything.

“Tell us,” said Thea.

She’d come to the front. With the lantern. She was standing in front of Sefa’s stone chair with the amber light in her hands, wet through from the fall, with her ponytail stuck to her neck. She was looking up at the elder with her calm, taking-it-apart face.

“The last ones,” said Thea. “With lights. Tell us about them. Please.”

Sefa looked at her for a long time.

Then she looked at the lantern. And her old face did something. Something almost like recognition.

“Thirty,” said Sefa. “Three hundred and twelve years ago. I was young. Not two hundred.” Somebody behind Ice made a small choking noise. Dennis, probably. “They came down the falls in the autumn. Like you. With lights at their shoulders. Asking the way up.” She was quiet. “They stayed a winter. In the house. They laid that fire, on the last morning, and went up in the spring.”

“Went up,” said Thea.

“Up,” said Sefa. “The way you all go.”

“And came down?”

The old elder looked at her for a long, long time.

“No,” she said. “None of them came down. Not one.” Her gills moved, slowly. “We kept the house. We swept it. We waited.” She looked round at all of them, crowded into her cave. “And now you.”

Nobody said anything. The fall roared outside.

“What happened to them?” said Hako. From the back. Quietly, for Hako.

“Up,” said Sefa. “That is all anyone down here knows. They went up.” She looked at Hako with her ancient eyes. “Everyone who goes up, goes up. Nobody comes down to tell.”

He stood at the back of the cave with Alea on his shoulder, very still.

He’d heard this. The first time. In this cave, with thirty-three other people, all of them grey and silent. She’d told them the same thing. Thirty of them. They went up. None came down. And he’d stood at the back and not cared. Because he’d been grieving, and because thirty people who’d vanished three hundred years ago weren’t Rachael and Dennis.

He’d never found out what happened to them. Not on any floor. Not at the top. He’d stood in the room on the hundredth floor at the end of everything, and there’d been nothing there about thirty players from three hundred years before. Nothing. Not a name. Not a bone.

He’d never once thought to wonder.

“Their head,” said Sefa, and he looked up. “Their head was a quiet one. A careful one.” Her old eyes had gone somewhere very far away. “Every morning, on the stair out of the city, he counted them. Thirty. Out loud. Every morning. As if he was afraid one would be gone.”

The cave was very quiet.

Ice felt Chris’s pencil stop. He felt Thea, at the front, go very still. He felt, without looking, twelve other people in that cave not look at each other.

“And one of them,” said Sefa.

She’d turned her head again. Slowly. She was looking past Thea, past all of them, to the back of the cave. To him.

“One of them laughed,” said Sefa. “Like you.”

He didn’t know what his face did. He’d never know. He stood at the back of a cave behind a waterfall with thirty-five people turning to look at him again. An elder four hundred years old was looking at him across the lamplight with her ancient wet eyes.

Like you.

“I heard you,” said Sefa. “Last night. Across the water. From the house for lights. You laughed at something one of the others said. The loud one.” She inclined her head very slightly toward Calvin, who went red. “I was on my balcony. I heard it come across the falls.” She was quiet. “I had not heard that laugh in three hundred and twelve years. I sat up all night.”

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

“She never said that,” said Alea. In his head. Very fast. “Ice. The first time. She never said anything about a laugh. Not once. I’ve got every word she said, I logged it, she said thirty, she said winter, she said up, she said nobody came down—”

I didn’t laugh, he thought.

The fox stopped.

The first time, he thought. By eight. I hadn’t laughed in months. Not since four. Not since the first square. He stood at the back of the cave and felt it go through him, slowly, all the way down. She never heard me laugh. So she never knew.

Alea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Oh,” she said at last. Very softly.

Sefa was still looking at him.

“Who was he?” said Ice. His voice came out hoarse. “The one who laughed. Do you know his name?”

Sefa looked at him for a very long moment.

“I was young,” she said. “Not two hundred. I did not ask their names. I only listened.” She lifted one long hand and let it fall again, slowly, onto the arm of the stone chair. “He laughed at everything. He went up first, always. He talked to everyone.” She looked at him. “And every evening, he sat a little way from the others, on his own, and looked up.”

Nobody said anything.

“Go,” said Sefa. Not unkindly. “I am tired. I am four hundred and six.” She closed her ancient eyes. “Come back when you have a better question. I will still be here. I am always still here.”

They went back along the ledge behind the fall, one by one, soaked again, and down the stair into the city, and nobody said anything.

In the house for lights, by the fire Ina had lit, the club met.

He wasn’t in it. He knew it existed. He’d known since Lantern Street, since the bench in the inn at dawn, since thirteen people not looking at each other. He’d known since Xav said somebody taught you the rest. Nobody had told him. Nobody had needed to. He’d watched them for ten years, the first time. He knew what it looked like when some of them were holding something together.

He sat on the other side of the hall, by the window, with Gabe and Deston and the falls going past outside. And watched them gather round the fire. Chris with his notebook open. Thea with her lantern. Winter, Hyvred, Kei, Hako. Xav, a little apart. Higu, and Drew, and the others.

Chris turned to a clean page. Ice could see it from across the hall. He wrote something at the top in his small square capitals. Two lines. Ice couldn’t read them from there.

He didn’t need to.

THE ONES BEFORE.

“They’re making another list,” said Gabe, beside him. He’d seen them too. He didn’t know what the first list was. He’d never been in that circle. But he’d seen them, the thirteen, the way you see things in a family. “Aren’t they.”

“Yeah,” said Ice.

“About the thirty.”

“Yeah.”

Gabe looked at the club round the fire for a while.

“Good,” he said. “Somebody should.” He looked out at the falls. “Thirty people. Three hundred years. Nobody came down.” He was quiet. “Nobody ever even asked where they went.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

I didn’t, he thought. Not once. In ten years.

“Dec,” said Gabe. “The one who laughed. Like you.”

“I know.”

“Who went up first. Always. Who talked to everyone. Who sat on his own in the evenings and looked up.” Gabe was looking at him now. Not angry. Just looking. “That’s you. That’s exactly you.”

“I know, Gabe.”

“So who was he?”

Ice looked out at the water going past the window. White, endless, falling.

“I don’t know,” he said. And it was the truth. For once, it was completely the truth. “I don’t know who he was.”

Gabe looked at him a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, and looked back out at the falls.

“Then we’ll find out,” said Gabe.

That night, he told Drew.

He found him at the end of the hall, by the rock he’d carried down from the stair door. Drew had actually carried it, a flat grey rock the size of a dinner plate, in his pack, for two days, because the rota was on it. He was rubbing out tomorrow’s watches to rewrite them.

“Tomorrow,” said Ice. “Or the day after. When everybody’s rested.”

Drew looked up.

“My trial,” said Ice. “The padlock. Forty-five.” He swallowed. “I want to do it here. By the water. On the jetty at the end of the city.” He paused. “With everybody there.”

Drew looked at him for a long moment.

“Everybody,” he said.

“Everybody.”

“You’ve never wanted anybody there,” said Drew. “For anything. Not since the white room.” He was quiet. “Hyv said. All your trials. On your own. In a cell on four, in a swamp on three.”

“I know.”

“So why now?”

He thought about it. About a stone cell, and a voice in the dark saying what do you carry. About kneeling in the hollows with his palms open. About a long table in a bowl of glass.

“Because I think it’s going to need you,” he said. “All of you.”

Drew looked at him a while longer. Then he took out his chalk, and at the top of the rock, above the rota, in his big square letters, he wrote:

DAY AFTER TOMORROW. THE JETTY. ALL OF US. ICE’S TRIAL.

He put the chalk away.

“Somebody thank me,” said Drew.

“Thank you, Drew,” said Ice.
