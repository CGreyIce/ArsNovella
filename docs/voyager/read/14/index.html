Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 14

Candles

3,152 words · 14 min read

Yan had started seeing the lights on the first morning, and he hadn’t told anyone.

He’d thought at first it was the body. He’d woken up in somebody else’s eyes, after all: pale cyan ones, set in a face he’d only ever seen on a screen, under long silver-white hair he kept forgetting he had until it fell into his mouth. It seemed reasonable that new eyes might take a day or two to settle. So when he looked at Salty across the white room and saw, somewhere in the middle of his chest, a faint warm glow like a lamp seen through a curtain, he’d blinked and looked away and assumed it would stop.

It hadn’t stopped. By the second morning, it was clearer. By the third, he couldn’t stop seeing them.

Everybody had one. A small light, somewhere under the breastbone, burning. They weren’t all the same. Salty’s was huge, a great unsteady roaring thing like a forge fire with the door open, and it flared every time he laughed. Wendy’s, next to it, was small and steady and very bright at the centre, the kind of flame that doesn’t move even when you breathe on it. Tiger’s flickered so fast it looked like it was vibrating. Cursiffe’s was strange, darker than the others, a deep blue-black glow like light through a bottle of ink, and absolutely still.

He’d started calling them candles in his head. He didn’t know a better word. He didn’t want one.

You’re doing the thing, said Suzu, round his neck.

“I’m not doing anything.”

You’re staring at people’s chests again. It’s rude.

“I’m not staring at their chests. I’m staring at their candles.”

That’s what someone staring at chests would say, said Suzu, and Yan laughed out loud in the middle of the south fields, and Wendy turned round and looked at him oddly, and he waved her on.

The south fields were worse than the north ones, they’d been told at the Copper Spoon, which was why Yan had chosen them.

Not much worse. A bit more gorse, a bit more stone, more walls for the Woolbacks to hide behind. And a few of the big ones with darker fleeces and scarred noses, Woolback Rams, which were level four and had, the whole of Hearthstead knew by now, broken Calvin’s arm the day before. Calvin was fine. He’d come down to breakfast showing everybody how he could wiggle his fingers. He’d also told the whole table, at length, that it had been the most frightening thing that had ever happened to him, and then asked if anyone wanted to see the Fold.

Yan had chosen the south fields because of that.

“If we’re going to be scared,” he’d said to his party that morning, outside the Spoon in the grey light, “I’d rather it was now. On purpose. With all of us together. Than later, by accident, on our own.”

“That’s very wise,” Cursiffe had said, adjusting his round wire glasses. “And slightly ominous.”

“I’m a slightly ominous person.”

“You’ve got a snake,” Tiger had pointed out cheerfully. “You’re very ominous.”

So they’d gone south.

They were getting good at it. That was the strange thing; you didn’t expect to get good at something this frightening so fast. Calvin’s message in World about stabbing under the jaw had saved them hours. They’d worked out the rest themselves.

Salty went first. Salty always went first. He was short and very broad, with arms like a builder’s, and the Knight’s door had given him a greatsword nearly as tall as he was, which he swung like a man swinging an axe at a stump. It didn’t cut the wool any better than anyone’s sword did. But when it hit a Woolback, the Woolback stopped, the way a car stops when it hits a wall. Salty would stand there with his feet planted and his teeth gritted and take the charge on the flat of the blade, and let everybody else do the rest.

The rest was Tiger, mostly. Tiger was a Ranger who’d chosen knives, and he was fast in a way that didn’t look possible: a blur of teal coat and chestnut hair that was suddenly on the other side of the sheep, then under it, then gone. He hadn’t stopped grinning since the white room.

And Wendy, behind them all, on a wall, with a bow.

She’d been the one Yan was least sure about. Not her nerve; Wendy had more nerve than anyone he knew, she just didn’t advertise it. But Wendy was a person who expected things to go wrong, and said so, and Yan hadn’t been sure what that would do to her here. The first morning, at the class hall, she’d come out of the bow door with a plain short bow and a quiver and looked at them with total disgust.

“I’m going to be terrible at this,” she’d said.

She wasn’t. She was very good. She didn’t try for under the jaw; arrows were too small for that, and Woolbacks moved too fast. She went for the eyes. Red eyes, small and bright, the only part of a Woolback that wasn’t padded. By the end of the second day she was getting one in three. Today, one in two. Every time one went in, she muttered, “Lucky,” as if she were apologising for it.

And Cursiffe, who was a Mage, who’d come out of the staff door with a slim black staff and his little glass vial of ink still round his neck, mostly watched. He had a spell: a small dart of force that came off the end of the staff, hit things and made them flinch. But he used it sparingly, and in between he stood very still with his head tipped slightly back and his glasses catching the light. Every so often he wrote something down in a little book with a stub of pencil.

“What are you writing?” Yan asked him once.

“Everything,” said Cursiffe. “I’ll know which bits matter later.”

Yan liked him more every hour.

He’d been watching the Woolbacks’ candles all morning, and that was the other thing he hadn’t told anyone.

They had them too.

Not like people’s. Smaller, duller, more like coals than flames: a low red glow in the middle of the grey wool that pulsed when they ran. When one died, he watched it go out. Not all at once. It went the way a coal goes out when you take it off the fire, the red fading through orange to grey, and then a thin thread of something, too faint to call light, rising up off it and gone.

He’d expected that. Animals were alive. Animals had something.

What he hadn’t expected was Mother Crane.

He’d seen her at breakfast, coming down the long table with the porridge pot, and he’d looked at her the way he’d learned to look at everyone now, a little sideways. And there it was, under her stained apron, under the breastbone. A candle. Warm, yellow, a little worn round the edges, steady. Exactly like a person’s. Exactly like Salty’s or Wendy’s or his own.

He’d sat there with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

Then he’d looked at the girl with the eggs, who’d been at the door. A candle. Small and bright and fast, like Tiger’s. Then the weaver, then the man with the cart, then Quill on his way past the window to the class hall, with ink on his fingers. Candles. All of them. Real ones, as far as Yan could tell. Burning the same way his friends’ burned.

He’d been hoping, a little, he realised, that they wouldn’t.

Malcolm had said it at the arch, the first day: they’re NPCs. It’s a game. And part of Yan had wanted that to be true. It would be easier. If the people here were painted fronts and recorded lines, then this was a game, a very good, very frightening game, and you could play a game. You could be brave in a game. You could leave a game behind.

But Mother Crane had a candle. And when the girl with the eggs had dropped one on the step this morning, Yan had watched her candle jump and flare with dismay, then settle again when Mother Crane told her it didn’t matter, the way any child’s would.

Nobody can tell the difference, Yan thought. Not even me.

And he was the only one who could see them.

The Woolback Ram came over the wall in the middle of the afternoon, and it went for Salty.

It was the big kind, the one with the scarred nose, and it was a full two levels above them, and it came in low and fast from the side where nobody was looking. Salty turned round just in time to get the greatsword up. Not in time to get his feet set.

It hit him like a lorry.

Salty went backwards over the wall he’d been standing in front of and landed on the other side in the gorse, and Yan heard the sound he made, and didn’t like it.

“Salty!” That was Wendy, already off her own wall and running.

Yan was running too. He came over the wall and saw Salty lying on his back in the gorse with the greatsword still in his hand and his face gone grey and tight. He looked at his chest. And Salty’s candle, the huge, roaring, unsteady furnace of it, was guttering.

Not shrinking. Guttering. The way a candle does in a draught: leaning hard to one side, flattening, then flaring back up, then leaning again, harder each time, as if there were a wind blowing through Salty’s chest that only Yan could see. Every time it leaned, the flame got thinner at the base.

[ Salty · HP 14% ]

Yan put his hands out and used Minor Heal.

It went in. He felt it go. A warmth from his palms into Salty’s chest, the way he’d done it all morning for bruises and knocks. And the candle steadied for a heartbeat, a little brighter, and then the draught came again and it leaned right over, flat, almost touching the bottom of whatever it burned in.

[ Salty · HP 9% ]

Behind him, Tiger and Wendy were on the ram, Tiger a blur, Wendy’s arrows going in one after another, Cursiffe’s little darts of force snapping at its face. It wasn’t enough. It was still on its feet. And it had turned round. It was looking at Salty again, lying there in the gorse, and lowering its head.

Yan cast Minor Heal again. His mana bar had almost nothing in it. The candle steadied, and the wind came, and it leaned.

Yun, said Suzu.

“I know.”

Yun. It’s not the size. Look at it.

He looked.

She was right. It wasn’t too small. Salty’s candle had never been small in its life. It was the wind. Something was blowing across it, out of the hurt, out of the fear, out of whatever happened inside a person when they were very close to the edge of something. Yan’s heal was making it bigger, and that wasn’t helping, because a bigger flame in a draught just leaned further.

You couldn’t make it bigger.

He thought of home, years ago, on a night the power went out in a storm. He’d lit a candle in the kitchen and carried it through the flat to his mother’s room, past the window that never quite shut. She couldn’t see it, but she’d asked him if it was lit, and he’d said yes. And he’d carried it the way everyone carries a candle past a draughty window: bent over it, with one hand cupped round the side the wind was coming from.

You couldn’t make it bigger. But you could keep the wind off it.

Yan put both his hands out over Salty’s chest. Not touching him. Cupped, the way you’d cup them round a flame, one on each side, curved in. He didn’t push anything into him. He just put his hands where the wind was coming from, and held them there, and thought, as hard as he’d ever thought anything: not this one.

The wind hit his hands.

He felt it. A cold that went straight through his palms and up his wrists into his arms, as if he’d plunged them into a river in winter. It didn’t stop. It just pushed, against his hands instead of against the flame, and his hands didn’t move.

Salty’s candle stood up straight.

It didn’t get bigger. It just stopped leaning. It burned up straight and tall and roaring, the way it always had, sheltered on two sides by Yan’s cupped hands.

The Woolback Ram came over the wall.

It hit Salty, lying in the gorse. Yan felt the hit come through. Not in Salty: in his own hands, a great cold blow like the slam of a door in a gale, and he gasped. But the bar in the corner of his eye didn’t move. Nine percent. It held. And Salty, who’d been lying on his back in the gorse waiting to die, looked up at the ram standing over him, at the hit that should have finished him and hadn’t, and said, in a voice of pure, outraged disbelief:

“Hey.”

Then he got up, and put the greatsword into its jaw.

Afterwards, Yan sat on the wall with his hands in his armpits and couldn’t feel his fingers.

They’d gone white. Not just cold: white, the blood gone out of them, like hands that had been in snow too long. They hurt in the slow, aching way that cold hands hurt when they start coming back. He sat with them tucked under his arms and let them hurt, and didn’t say anything, and Salty sat next to him, very close, the way Salty sat next to people he was grateful to.

“What did you do?” said Salty.

“I’m not sure.”

“I was at nine percent. It hit me. I stayed at nine.”

“I know.”

“That’s not a heal. Heals make it go up. That’s—“ Salty frowned. Salty frowned at most things he didn’t understand yet, not unkindly, the way he’d frown at a weight he hadn’t lifted. “That’s like a shield. On the inside.”

“Something like that.”

“What’s it cost?”

Yan took one hand out from under his arm and showed him. Salty looked at the white fingers for a while.

“Right,” he said. “Don’t do it again unless you have to.”

“I’ll do it as much as I have to.”

“That’s what I said,” said Salty, and bumped Yan’s shoulder with his own, hard enough to rock him, which was how Salty said thank you.

Wendy came and stood in front of them. She looked at Salty, all of him, carefully, from his boots to his face, the way you look at something you nearly lost. Then she looked at Yan’s white hands.

“That was the scariest thing I’ve ever seen,” she said.

“I’m fine,” said Salty.

“I know you’re fine. I’m talking to Yan.” She looked at him with her mismatched eyes, one amber and one violet, very steady. “Thank you.”

“It was mostly Suzu,” said Yan.

It was mostly you, said Suzu, in his head. I just told you to look.

That evening Yan sat on the steps of the Copper Spoon after supper, with Suzu round his neck and his hands wrapped in a cloth Mother Crane had soaked in warm water, and looked at Hearthstead.

The stars had come on. The windows had too, one after another, as people lit their lamps. And under the windows, if he looked sideways, he could see the other lights. Candles. Dozens of them, scattered through the dark town like sparks: in kitchens, in beds, in the inn across the street, in the bonesetter’s at the top of the hill and the weaver’s at the bottom and the mill by the river. Steady ones and flickering ones, old ones and small fast new ones. Every one of them burned exactly like his friends’.

He didn’t know what that meant yet. He suspected it was going to matter more than almost anything.

You’re thinking about him, said Suzu.

He had been. Under all of it, the whole time. The figure by the arch on the first morning, black and white and a flash of teal, gone in a second behind a snake. Voyager, this morning, at the top of a list, a name that didn’t sound like anyone. Gabe sitting at the long table with his porridge untouched, sending a message nobody answered.

“I said I was less sure,” said Yan. “This morning. At breakfast.”

I heard.

“I lied.” He looked down at the white snake on his collarbone. “I’m not less sure. I’m scared. It’s different.”

Suzu turned her head and looked at him, green eyes in the dark.

It was him, she said. I saw him. The loud one. The one who used to make you laugh on the long nights when you didn’t want to. He went out of the door, and he looked back at you.

“Then why the name?”

Names are given, said Suzu. He gave himself one. Perhaps he needed one we didn’t know. She put her head back down on his collarbone. He’ll tell you why. When he can.

Yan sat with that for a long time.

Then he took his hands out of the warm cloth and flexed them. The colour had come back. They still ached. He looked out at the scattered candles of Hearthstead in the dark, and then up the valley, at the black shape of the hills where the Fold was. Beyond it, somewhere, was a stair, and a second floor none of them had seen. Out there, a long way ahead, a man was walking on his own with a candle in his chest that Yan had never seen. He’d been too slow, that first morning. He hadn’t had the eyes yet.

“When we find him,” Yan said, “I want to see it.”

See what?

“His.” He touched his own chest, over the breastbone. “I want to know if it’s tired.”

Suzu didn’t answer. But she tightened, very slightly, round his neck, the way she did when she agreed with him and didn’t want to say so.
