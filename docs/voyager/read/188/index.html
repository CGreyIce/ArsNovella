Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 188

Wait for Me

8,619 words · 37 min read

It took Niko three goes to light the fire, and it wasn’t the flint’s fault.

His hands weren’t right. They hadn’t been right since the crown of the Spindle, the morning before, and they’d got worse on the second bridge, and for the three minutes he’d stood in this room on his own they’d been the worst they had ever been, worse than the first day on floor 1. He knelt at the hearth now with the old woman’s flint and steel and a basket of gull eggs at his elbow and struck, and missed, and struck, and got a spark that went out, and struck again. On the third go it caught. He bent down and breathed on it, the way Ina had shown him on the Salt, slow and steady, and the dry weed under the peat took, and went orange, and the peat started to smoke.

He stayed there, on his knees, longer than he needed to.

The hearth was swept. He’d seen that, when he first came in. Swept clean, with the ash raked to one side and the peat stacked ready and a small pile of dry weed for kindling, the way you’d leave a fire for somebody you expected back. There was nothing in it. No feathers. No twigs. No mess of white. Nothing that had been brought in through the chimney by something that had found the house empty and decided it was a cliff.

He didn’t know why he’d thought there would be. Nobody had told him. Nobody had said a word. Ice had stood on the crown of the Spindle in the first sun and said I came down once, to here, and I was looking at somebody’s back the whole way down, and in case he—and stopped. That was all. And Niko had stood by the arch with his pack on, and looked south at the crooked white rock, and known whose back it was.

He hadn’t had to be told. He’d known on the Salt, really, when Ice said you always try it the first night. He’d known before that. He’d known on the hundred-and-eleventh day, in this room, when the old woman said he looked at me like a man who’d been to my funeral. He’d decided then that it was all he needed to know. He asked her to wait. For me.

Now he knew one thing more. It was the same thing, really. It just had a shape now. It had the shape of a boy coming down a long way, too late, to an empty room with a cold hearth. And somebody walking behind him all the way, the whole way down and the whole way back up, not looking at anything, watching his back.

In case.

He put another piece of peat on the fire. He didn’t ask. He was never going to ask. He’d found, on the second bridge, that he didn’t need to. He knelt there and watched it catch.

“Are you going to sit there all day?” said Hessa, behind him. “Or are you going to cook those eggs?”

“Sorry.”

“Stop saying sorry. You’ve nothing to be sorry for. You lit a fire.” She sat down in her chair by the hearth, the big one with the worn arms, and looked into it. “Took you three goes. I heard.”

There weren’t enough chairs. There never had been. Chris got the second one again, because he was oldest, and sat in it with his notebook on his knee looking faintly embarrassed about it. Winter and Ina sat on the floor with their backs to the wall. Maigo stood by the window in the sun with his sunglasses pushed up on his head and his eyes half shut, exactly where he’d stood a year and a half ago, like a plant that had been put back in its pot. Aleaka stayed in the door, because the gulls still wouldn’t let her come in without screaming, and the gulls, Niko noticed, now included two that had followed her in from Tether and were standing on her boots.

Ice didn’t come in.

He sat down on the doorstep outside, with his back to the doorframe and his face to the swept ring, and Thea sat down beside him.

Hessa looked at him from her chair, through the open door, for a while.

“In or out,” she said.

“Out,” said Ice. “If that’s all right.”

“It’s my step.” She looked at him a moment longer. “I’ll allow it.”

So Ice cooked the eggs. Niko had been going to, but somehow, between the chair and the step, the pan had gone out of his hands and into Ice’s, and Ice had it on a flat stone at the edge of the fire with salt fish and gull eggs and a pinch of something grey-green out of a twist of cloth, reaching in through the door to do it, sitting on the step. It smelled of rain and pepper. Hessa watched him do it with her one eye and didn’t say anything until he’d served it, onto her four plates and three bowls and a pan lid, and then she ate hers, and said it had too much salt in it, and held out her plate for more.

Niko sat on the floor by the hearth and ate his and watched her.

She was older. You couldn’t not see it, up close. She was eighty-four the first time. She’d be eighty-five now, or six; he didn’t know when her birthday was. The cliff of her face had come down a little, the way cliffs do, and her hands were knottier at the knuckles, and when she’d swung the basket off her back in the ring he’d seen her shoulders take a moment to come back up. But she sat in the chair like a post. And she’d come up a rope this morning, out of the cliff, hand over hand.

“Every morning?” said Winter, from the floor. “The rope?”

“Every morning.”

“How far down does it go?”

“Far enough.” Hessa wiped her plate with a bit of bread. “There’s a ledge twenty yards down where they lay the big ones. Any fool can get the little ones off the top.”

“I want to go,” said Winter.

Hessa looked at her for the first time properly. At the silver hair and the bow and the face that liked cold places. “Tomorrow,” she said. “If you get up.”

“I’ll get up.”

“We’ll see.” Hessa put the plate down. And then she looked at Niko, and something in her face set, the way it had on the first day before she said read. “Last winter,” she said, “I nearly didn’t.”

Nobody said anything. Outside on the step, Ice’s back went very still.

“Sat in that chair a week,” said Hessa. She said it to Niko, not to anybody else, in the voice people use for things they’ve already decided are nobody’s business. “Didn’t light the fire. Didn’t go down the rope. Didn’t sweep. Sat there and thought, well. That’s that, then. He came. The boy came. I did what I said I’d do. I waited, and he came, and he went up, the way they all do, and now there’s nothing to stay alive for, and I’m eighty-five and my knees hurt, and I can stop.” She looked into the fire. “So I sat there and stopped. Near enough. And on the seventh day I was sitting there, stopping, and I thought of what your knight said. At my door. Please stay alive till he comes.”

She sniffed.

“And I thought, he’s been, you old fool. And then I thought—“ Her mouth did something at one corner. “He didn’t say comes up. Did he. He just said comes. Till he comes. He never said which way.”

Outside, on the step, Ice put his face in his hands.

He didn’t make a sound. Thea put her hand on the back of his neck and left it there.

“So I got up,” said Hessa. “Out of spite, mostly. And because I’m a pedant. My mother always said I was a pedant. I lit the fire. I swept the ring. I went down the rope. And every morning since, when I sweep the ring, I say it. Stay alive till he comes. Out loud. To the broom.” She looked at Niko. “And then you came. Down. So that’s done now, properly. I’ve no more excuses.”

“You don’t need excuses,” said Niko. His voice didn’t come out right. “You can just stay.”

“Can I.” Hessa looked at him a long time with her one sharp eye. “We’ll see about that, too.”

After breakfast she took the long sword down off the wall.

She had to stand on the chair to do it. Chris got up to help and she looked at him until he sat down again. She lifted it off its two pegs and came down off the chair with it in both hands, carefully, and laid it across the table in front of Niko, in the light from the window.

He’d seen it on the first day from the door. Lines, he’d thought. Writing, maybe. He hadn’t known anything then.

He knew now.

It was writing. The whole blade. Both faces, from the guard to a hand’s width short of the point, covered in tiny punched letters in lines so close together that from a step away the steel looked grey and furred, like frost on a window. Thousands of letters. Tens of thousands. Small and even and patient, each one struck by hand, one at a time, with a punch and a little hammer, the way he’d struck his first line on the Salt by lamplight with Gabe’s smallest punch over four nights.

“Every spell in those,” said Hessa, and nodded at the three old books on the shelf. “And every spell in every book I ever found, anywhere I ever went. All of it. Took me thirty years.”

Niko put out his thumb. He looked at her. She nodded.

He put it on the steel near the guard, on the first line, and read.

It answered. Not much. Not like his own. A faint warmth under the thumb, a hum, like voices in another room. Fire. He moved his thumb along. Frost. Wind. A light. A ward. Something to make a wound close. Something to make a door stick. Spell after spell after spell, all answering faintly as his thumb went over them, like people in a long hall turning their heads as he went past. Somebody else’s spells. Written by somebody, once, a long time ago, in a book. And copied here by a woman on a rock with a hammer, a letter at a time, for thirty years.

He got to the last hand’s width before the point. And stopped.

It was different. It wasn’t punched. It was flat. Hammered flat, and then flat again, and again, until the steel there had a dull wavy shine to it, like water, like a place on a step worn down by a hundred years of feet. And when he put his thumb on it, he could feel, under the flatness, very faint, the ghosts of letters. Hundreds of them. Lines begun and beaten out and begun again on top. Beginnings. Only beginnings. Never more than a few words before the hammer came down.

“What’s this?” he said.

“Fifty-one years,” said Hessa.

He looked up at her.

“The third one.” She was standing by the table with her arms folded. “I went up. Never mind how. I was stubborn and it was a long time ago. The book told me where, the way it told you. I did the first one here, on my own, with nobody to hit me. I did the second one up there.” She didn’t say where. Niko didn’t ask. “And then the book said write a spell nobody wrote. And I came home. And I sat on this rock, and I tried.” She nodded at the flat place. “Every first line I ever wrote. Of the one nobody wrote. I’d punch it in, and read it, and it wouldn’t answer. So I’d beat it out and try another. Fifty-one years.” She was quiet a moment. “Some of them were very good. I was a clever girl. I wrote things nobody had ever thought of. Spells for the colour of the sea at night. Spells for the noise a gull makes when it lands.” She shrugged. “Nothing. The blade knew. Everything I wrote, it was somebody else’s, or it was nothing.”

Niko looked down at the flat bright place on the steel. Fifty-one years of first lines.

“Open the book,” said Hessa.

He knew which book. He took the red one out of his pack, the thin one with no title on the spine that she’d given him at this door on the hundred-and-eleventh day. He’d carried it up nine floors and down nine. He opened it to the back.

There was the page from the Salt, the one that had warmed near Seep, in her young hand. An upright sword with a line of tiny writing down the whole blade. Here they hear you. So—read where only it can. He’d read it a hundred times.

He turned the page.

The last page was blank. It had always been blank. He’d thought, for a year, that it was waiting to fill, the way the other one had. That one day, near enough to wherever it was, the last page would warm in his hands and her young writing would come up out of it and tell him what to do.

“It never filled for me either,” said Hessa. “That’s the one. That’s where it goes.”

“What goes?”

“The spell.” She looked at him as if he’d asked what colour the sky was. “Write a spell nobody wrote. Nobody wrote it. So it’s blank.” She unfolded her arms and put one finger on the empty page. Her knuckle was swollen and her nail was cracked and there was gull muck under it. “You write it. There. And then on the blade. And then you read it to the blade, and if it’s right, the blade answers.” She took her finger away. “And if it’s not, you beat it out and try again. For fifty-one years. On a rock.”

She went and sat back down in her chair.

“Your friend says you always try things too early,” she said. “Go on, then. Try it too early. Get it over with.”

He tried it too early.

He sat at her table all that afternoon with the red book open at the blank page and a scrap of paper beside it, so he wouldn’t waste the page, and wrote spells. He was good at it. He’d been reading spells for a year and a half; he knew how they went, the shape of them, the way the good ones turned at the end like a key. He wrote a spell for the wind on the bridges, so you’d always know which way it was coming. He wrote a spell for the cloud, to see what was under it. He wrote one for the feeling of the first sun coming up, which was the best thing he’d ever written in his life, and when he read it back to himself the back of his neck went cold.

He punched nothing. He read them to his own sword, the scrap in one hand and the blade in the other, the way she’d taught him. Quietly. To it. Not at it.

Nothing.

Not the faintest warmth. The sword lay in his hand like a sword. He read the first-sun one three times, slower each time, and the steel didn’t so much as hum. It wasn’t that the blade didn’t like them. It was that they weren’t anything. They were words. Good ones. They just weren’t anything.

“That’s what I did,” said Hessa, from her chair, with her eyes shut.

“For fifty-one years.”

“For fifty-one years.” She didn’t open her eyes. “Mine were better than that, mind.”

Winter laughed, on the floor. She’d been trying not to all afternoon.

By dusk he had nine scraps of paper and nothing. He went out and sat on the edge of the swept ring with them in his lap and looked at the cloud going pink, and the gulls coming in to the ledges for the night, thousands of them, settling and screaming and settling again. Behind him, on the step, Ice and Thea had gone. Down to the bridge, he thought. Ice had been sitting on the step all day, and then he’d got up when the sun went low, and taken Thea, and gone. Giving him the island.

Ina came out after a while and sat down beside him.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. Ina never did. He’d pushed his grey mask up onto the top of his head, and he sat with his hands in his lap palm up, the way he did now on cold evenings, and a faint warmth came off them that you could feel from a foot away, like a hearthstone.

“They’re good,” said Ina, eventually. He’d read the scraps. Niko had seen him read them, on the table, upside down, while pretending to look at the fire. “The sun one.”

“It doesn’t do anything.”

“No.”

They sat there.

“At Gaffer’s grave,” said Ina. Slowly. As if he was working out how to say it as he went. “On the Acre. When I said the words.”

Niko looked at him.

“Everybody thought they were mine,” said Ina. “After. People said that. Ina’s words. Ice learned them. He said them after me under his breath, I saw him.” He was quiet. “They weren’t mine. I didn’t make them up. I didn’t sit down and write something. They were Gaffer’s. Things he said. Things everybody in the Acre had heard him say for fifty years. He watched. It’s in. You saw it. I just said them out loud over him, in the right order.” He looked at his palms. “Nobody wrote them. Nobody ever wrote them down. They just—worked. Because they’d been working for fifty years before I ever said them.”

Niko didn’t say anything.

“I don’t know if that helps,” said Ina.

“I don’t know either.”

Ina nodded, as if that was a fair answer. He sat a while longer. Then he got up and pulled his mask down, and went back inside to the fire, and Niko sat on the edge of the swept ring in the dark with nine scraps of paper in his lap and the gulls going quiet all round him, and thought about the straw man.

He’d been thinking about the straw man for a month.

Since the Long Barn. Since the old women in the Acre had sat round the great frame in the last week of the harvest, in silence, and plaited his face out of the last sheaf, the way they did every year. Over, under, a twist, a turn, two over, a knot. He’d watched them do it for a whole afternoon. They didn’t look at what they were doing. They didn’t talk. Their hands just went, the same way, over and over, a pattern their mothers had done and their mothers’ mothers. And the toothless woman from Lammas who’d plaited it sixty times had looked up at him and said, It’s his face. That’s what his face is.

It had looked like writing. That was what he’d thought, at the time, in the barn. It looked like a sentence. Somebody had been plaiting the same sentence into a straw man’s face for a thousand years, and nobody who did it knew they were writing. And it had worked. It had kept him. It had kept the winter. It had been working all along.

He thought about Nan Gully at her winch, singing a song that was five generations old, that she’d never written down because she couldn’t write. Chris had written it down for her once, the first week, and she’d said she didn’t need it written, it was in her, and he could keep his bit of paper.

He thought about the rakers on the Salt, reading their mothers’ words off the handles of their saws with their thumbs. Tullah with her daughter’s name pricked into her palm so she could say it to her out on the crust where you couldn’t make a sound.

He thought about Marrit on the quay at Hythe, saying names over the water every evening, because somebody had to.

He thought about the onion skins tied under the corks of thirty-nine water skins at the last cache on six. Nobody had written anything on them. Everybody who found them would know.

He thought about the thirty-six on every stair down, since the Long Acre. Tiger with his hand flat on the door. Newish counting. Thea singing. Higu at the bottom with the rock: What do you remember? Calvin at the front of the line, every stair up and every stair down, saying Look at my back. Nobody runs. Nobody had ever written any of that down either. They just did it. And it worked. Every time. Thirty-six people had gone up and down twenty-something stairs in the dark and nobody had run.

He sat up.

Nobody wrote it. That was what the page said. Not nobody said it. Not nobody thought of it. Hessa had spent fifty-one years looking for words nobody had ever said, because she was a clever girl, and there weren’t any. Everything had been said. Everything had been thought, by somebody, somewhere. You couldn’t make a spell out of something nobody had ever thought of. That was just words. That was a spell for the colour of the sea at night.

But almost nothing that mattered had ever been written down.

The things that worked were the things people said every day, to each other, to a broom, to a straw man, to the dead, and never thought of writing down because they weren’t spells, they were just what you said. They’d been working the whole time. That was the point. A spell nobody wrote wasn’t a new one. It was one that already worked. On somebody. Every day. And nobody had ever thought to put it on a page.

Please stay alive till he comes.

That had worked. It had kept an old woman on a rock alive for a year and a half, through a winter she’d decided not to live through. Out of spite. Out of pedantry. Said every morning to a broom. Nobody had ever written it down.

But it wasn’t his. It was Ice’s. It was hers.

Niko looked down at his sword, in its scabbard, at his hip, in the dark.

He hadn’t told anybody. Not Chris. Not anybody. He’d said it here, in this doorway, on the hundred-and-eleventh day, very quietly, so nobody would hear, with the blade a hand’s width out of the sheath. And he’d said it again the next night. And the night after that. He hadn’t meant to make it a thing. It had just become what he said, at the end of a day, when he cleaned the blade and slid it home. On floor 6 in the cistern, on 8 by the lake, on 12 on the Salt where you couldn’t make a sound, so he’d said it with his thumb instead. On fourteen in the Long Barn with seven hundred people asleep round him. Most nights. Five hundred nights, near enough. To the sword.

I’m here. I’m getting there. Wait for me.

And the sword had waited. It had lit for him on the Salt with no voice. It had come when he called it in the dark with the saltmouths swaying all round. It had waited, every night, for him to come back and say it again.

And on the Salt he’d signed it to Ice, the night after the saltmouths. Here. And wait. And Ice, across the sleds, had signed something back that nobody had taught him.

And Ice had waited too. On every floor. One floor ahead, the whole way up. Niko had heard about the worn place on the high terrace on four, the bright patch on the stone where Ice had sat every evening for three weeks, waiting for thirty-five people to come up a stair. He’d waited on five, here, fifteen days, until the morning they came up. He’d waited for them at the top of every stair since the white room.

It had worked on everybody. Nobody had ever written it down.

Niko got up off the edge of the swept ring, in the dark, with nine scraps of paper in his hand, and went in to the fire.

He wrote it on the blank page by the light of the peat, with Hessa’s own pen, while she watched him from the chair with her one eye and didn’t say anything.

I’m here. I’m getting there. Wait for me.

The ink went on the page and sat there, black and wet. And then, as he watched, it went darker. Not the way ink dries. The other way. It went down into the page as if the paper had been waiting for exactly those words for a very long time and had been holding a place for them, and now it had them, and it wasn’t going to let them go.

He felt the red book go warm in his hands. The way it had on the Salt.

Hessa leaned forward in her chair.

“Read it to me,” she said.

He read it to her.

She sat back. She didn’t say anything for a long while. She looked at the fire.

“That’s not clever,” she said at last.

“No.”

“That’s not clever at all.” Something happened in her voice. “That’s just what you’d say.”

“Yes.”

She was quiet a long time. Then she got up out of the chair, slowly, the way her knees made her, and went to a chest at the end of the bed, and opened it, and came back with a roll of soft leather tied with string. She put it on the table and untied it, and unrolled it. Punches. Thirty or forty of them, in loops, in rows. Small ones. Much smaller than Gabe’s. The smallest no thicker than a pin, their faces worn bright by fifty years of a thumb.

“Thirty years of copying, and fifty-one of beating out,” she said. “I’ll not need these now.” She pushed them across the table to him. “It’s too long.”

“I know.”

“The blade’s only so long. And you’re going in four days. Your girl with the rock said so.” Hessa sat back down. “Say what matters.”

Niko looked at the page. I’m here. I’m getting there. Wait for me. He’d said it five hundred times. He’d never once thought about which part of it mattered. It had just been the thing he said.

He looked at the middle of it for a long time.

I’m getting there.

He’d said that on the hundred-and-eleventh day, in this doorway, twenty-five, with a padlock on his level and a sword that wouldn’t light without a nosebleed and a book he couldn’t read the end of. He’d said it on the Salt with one line punched. He’d said it in the Long Barn. Getting there. He’d always been getting there.

He was sitting at her table. She was in her chair. The fire was lit.

He picked up the pen and drew one line, neatly, through the middle.

I’m here. And then a thin black line, straight and careful, through the middle of it. And then Wait for me.

Hessa watched him do it.

“Why that bit?” she said.

“Because it’s not true any more,” said Niko. “I got there.”

The old woman looked at him across the table with her one sharp grey eye for a long, long time.

“Huh,” she said.

It took two nights.

He did it on the back face of the blade, the one that had been bare, from the dull place near the guard where his thumb went, down toward the point, in Hessa’s smallest punches. She held the blade for him on a flat stone by the hearth with a folded cloth under it, her swollen hands round the hilt, and didn’t let go, and didn’t talk, except once an hour to say lean on it, don’t hit it in exactly the voice Gabe had used on the Salt. He leaned on it. And tapped. Each letter took the time it took. He didn’t hurry. He’d stopped hurrying somewhere on the way up, without noticing.

I’M HERE. The first night.

WAIT FOR ME. The second.

Chris wrote down every letter, with the time he finished it. Nobody asked him to.

In the mornings Winter went down the rope with Hessa for eggs, to the ledge twenty yards down where the big ones were, and came back up the first morning white and silent and the second morning grinning and covered in gull muck to the shoulder, and Hessa said she’d do. Maigo sat in the window in the sun and talked to the moss on the sill, which was the most he’d said to anything since the Glass. Aleaka sat in the door with her gulls and taught Sleet not to eat them. Ina lit the fire every morning before anybody woke, and every morning Hessa came in from the ring with her broom and found it lit, and looked at him, and didn’t say thank you, and the third morning said huh.

And every morning, before she went down the rope, Hessa swept the ring.

Niko watched her do it the second morning, from the door. She swept from the middle out, in a slow circle, the way you’d stir a pot. Not a feather left. Not a fleck of white. And her lips moved the whole time. He couldn’t hear it over the gulls. He didn’t need to.

Ice came up from the bridge every morning and sat on the step, and cooked the eggs, and went down again at dusk. He didn’t come in. He didn’t ask what Niko was doing. On the second night, late, with the last letter half done and Niko’s hand starting to shake, the tapping stopped for a moment while he flexed his fingers. And in the quiet, from outside the open door, from the dark at the edge of the swept ring, where Niko hadn’t known anybody was, Ice’s voice said:

“Mashi.”

“Yeah.”

A pause.

“Nothing,” said Ice. “Just checking you’re there.”

Niko looked at the half-made E on the steel under his punch.

“I’m here,” he said.

He heard Ice breathe out. Then footsteps, going away across the swept rock, toward the path. Hessa, holding the blade on the stone, didn’t look up. But her mouth did the thing at the corner again.

“Lean on it,” she said. “Don’t hit it.”

He leaned on it.

She tested him in the swept ring at dawn on the fourth day, with the gulls watching from every ledge.

She swept it first. The whole circle, from the middle out, with her lips moving. Then she leaned the broom against the tower wall and reached up over her shoulder and drew her sword. Not the long one. That was back on its pegs on the wall inside, with its fifty-one years of first lines. The one on her back. Old and thin and very bright.

They’d all come up. Chris’s party at the edge of the ring, sitting on the bare rock where they’d sat the first time. Aleaka in the door. And at the top of the path, where it came out of the nests, Ice and Thea, standing. Ice had come up before the sun. He’d stopped there, at the edge, and hadn’t come any further, and Thea had his hand.

“Your sword,” said Hessa. “No book, this time. You’ve no need of one.” She looked at him. “Read me a spell nobody wrote.”

“While—“

“While I hit you.” She almost smiled. “You were always slow.”

She came in from the side.

She was slower than the first time. A year and a half slower; eighty-five; he could see it in the way she set her back foot. And it didn’t matter at all. He’d been a padlocked Lv 25 knight the last time, and now he was sixty-two, with nine floors of fighting in his arms, and she still got her blade round his before he’d decided where it was going. The flat of it cracked across his knuckles. He felt a finger go numb. He got his own sword up into the next one by instinct, and the shock went up his arm to the shoulder, and she was already gone, round the other side.

He put his thumb on the back of the blade. On the first words. And read.

I’m here—

He pushed it.

He knew he’d done it as he did it. The old way. The first-day way. Shoving the words into the steel like shoving something through a stranger’s door. The line on the blade under his thumb went warm and fought him, and nothing came, and Hessa’s sword came down on his shoulder with the flat, hard enough that his knee went.

“You’re pushing,” said Hessa. “Again.”

He got up. She came in. She was going for his face this time, a short straight thrust at the eye, and he slipped it, almost, and the very tip of her blade opened a line across his cheekbone that he felt as cold before he felt it as anything else. Something warm ran down to his jaw.

“Again,” said Hessa.

He stood in the middle of the swept ring with the blood running down his face and the gulls screaming and the old woman circling him with her real sword. And he stopped trying.

He didn’t push. He didn’t read it at the blade. He didn’t read it at all. He just put his thumb where it always went, on the dull place, and then down onto the back face, onto the new letters, and said it. The way he’d said it five hundred times, at the end of the day, cleaning the steel, sliding it home. Not a spell. Not anything. Just the thing he said.

Wait for me.

Hessa came in from the left, low, at his legs.

And he cut across her line. Not at her. In front of her. Across the empty air she was about to come through, a short flat cut at knee height, and pulled the blade back.

The cut stayed.

He saw it as he pulled back. A line of light, hanging in the air where his blade had passed. Orange, from inside, the way the blade was orange when it lit. A hand’s length of it. Thin as a hair. Perfectly still in the wind. Humming. Waiting exactly where he’d left it, at knee height, in the middle of the swept ring, the way a cut would wait if a cut could wait.

Hessa’s sword stopped a finger’s width from it.

She stopped. Bent, low, with her blade out. She looked at the line of light hanging in the air in front of her blade. Her one sharp eye went very wide.

“Huh,” she said.

She came round it. Fast; faster than she’d been all morning. From the right, high, at his shoulder. He cut the air in front of her. It stayed. A second line, hanging, at shoulder height. She stopped short of it and spun away, low, and came in again from behind, and he turned and left a third, and a fourth where she was going to be after that, and a fifth. He wasn’t fighting her. He wasn’t even trying to hit her. He was just leaving them. Each one where he’d been. Each one where she was going. Short flat cuts in the air, glowing and still, humming, in the middle of the wind, waiting.

By the time he stopped, there were eleven of them.

They hung in the swept ring all round the old woman, at every height, in every direction, eleven thin lines of orange light in the grey morning, like the bars of something, like the strings of something, like a sentence written in the air in a hand nobody could read. And Hessa stood in the middle of them with her sword out, and turned slowly round, and looked at them all, one after another. Then she lowered her sword.

The gulls had stopped screaming.

All of them. Thousands of gulls on every ledge of Gull Island, and not one of them was making a sound. They sat on their nests and looked at the lines of light in the ring.

Niko put his thumb on the first two words.

I’m here.

And every line came home.

They didn’t close. They didn’t cut. They came back. All eleven at once, along the way they’d gone, the way gulls come in to a ledge at dusk, from everywhere, all together, into the blade in his hand. The steel took them and went orange from the guard to the point, front face and back, the old fire line and the new one, the whole length, steady, humming against his palm like something alive that was glad to see him. And then it settled. And then it was just lit.

He stood there with it.

He read the windows twice. Then he looked up from them, because somebody on the edge of the ring had made a noise, and it was Chris.

Nobody said anything.

“That’s two,” said Chris. His pencil wasn’t moving. That was how Niko knew. “That’s two levels, nine floors under. You don’t get anything nine floors under. Yan got two on eight. It counts from where it opens.” He looked at Niko. “It opened here. On five.”

“Five,” said Winter, on the rock beside him, very quietly.

“That’s lower than any of us,” said Maigo. He’d taken his sunglasses all the way off. “Any of us. Gigi and me on twelve. Yan on eight. Cursiffe on thirteen.” He looked at Niko with his bare eyes in the grey light, and his whole face was smiling, which it hardly ever did. “Five.”

Ina didn’t say anything. Ina was sitting with his mask pushed up and his palms on his knees, and he was smiling too, the small one, the one Niko had seen on the Salt when the blade first lit by touch.

Niko looked down at the windows. Tier 4. Spellsword. A class that counted from the fifth floor. He didn’t know, yet, what that meant. He didn’t know how high it went. Nobody did. He thought, for a moment, of a boy coming down a long way, too late, to a room with a cold hearth.

He didn’t think about it for long. He let it go past. It wasn’t his. It had been somebody else’s, and he’d never once asked, and he wasn’t going to start now.

Hessa had sat down.

She’d done it slowly. Right where she was, in the middle of the ring, on her own swept rock, with her sword across her knees. Niko had never seen her sit anywhere but her chair. She sat there in the grey morning with her long white braid down her back, and she put one swollen hand flat over her good eye. And left it there.

Niko went across the ring and knelt down in front of her.

“Hessa.”

“Give me a minute.”

He gave her a minute. The gulls started up again, a few at a time, and then all of them.

“Four days,” said Hessa, behind her hand. “Took you four days.”

“You had nobody to hit you,” said Niko.

She took her hand away. Her one good eye was wet and furious.

“Fifty-one years,” she said, “I sat on this rock trying to think of something clever.” She looked at the blade in his hand, still lit, humming faintly. “And it was just what you’d say.”

“Yes.”

“I said mine to a broom every morning for a year and a half.”

“I know,” said Niko. “That’s why it worked.”

Ice was still at the top of the path.

He hadn’t moved. He was standing exactly where he’d stopped before dawn, with Thea’s hand in his, and he was looking at the blade. Not at the windows; Niko didn’t think he’d even seen them. At the blade. At the long orange line of it, front and back, in Niko’s hand.

Niko got up off his knees and went across the swept ring to him.

He didn’t say anything. He turned the sword over in his hand, and held it out flat, back face up, so the new letters caught the light. Tiny and even and new, punched by hand, from the dull place where his thumb went down toward the point.

I’M HERE. WAIT FOR ME.

Ice looked at it for a long time.

His face did the thing Niko had seen it do only once, on the bridge the morning before, when the door had stood open on an empty room. It stopped. Like a clock stopping. And then, as he stood there, it started again. Slowly. Niko watched it start.

“Mashi,” said Ice.

“I’m here,” said Niko.

Ice didn’t say anything for a moment. He reached out and put one finger on the steel, just under the letters, not touching them. As if they were somebody else’s to touch.

“You left out getting there,” he said.

Niko had never once said it where anybody could hear. He’d made sure. He looked at Ice. Ice was still looking at the blade.

“I heard you,” said Ice. “Most nights. When I was near enough. In the barn. By the lake. In the cistern.” He was quiet. “You always said all three.”

“I got there,” said Niko.

Ice nodded slowly.

“Not too early,” he said.

“Not too late,” said Niko.

Before they went, he gave Hessa the red book back.

“It’s for the next one,” said Niko. “Somebody has to be here, when the next one comes up. That’s what you said. They’ll need the book.”

“There won’t be a next one.”

“There might.” He thought of the cache on six, an hour from the door, and thirty-nine water skins with onion skins tied under the corks, for somebody coming up in the day, not knowing. “Somebody always does.”

Hessa took it.

She held it a while in her swollen hands. Then she opened it at the back, at the last page, and looked at what was written there. His writing. One line crossed out. She read it with her one eye. Then she closed it and put it on the shelf by the fire, beside the other three. Four books now. She pushed it in so its spine was straight with the others.

“Your sword,” Niko said. “The long one.”

She looked at him.

“The place at the end,” he said. “The flat place. Can I?”

He didn’t have time for much. Higu had said four days and it was the fourth. So he chose one word. He used her own smallest punch, on her own blade, on the bright flat place at the end where fifty-one years of first lines had been beaten out, and he leaned on it, and tapped, and it took the time it took. Four letters. Not his words. Hers. The ones she’d been saying to a broom.

HERE.

He held the blade out to her, hilt first.

She took it. She held it up close to her face, the way he’d held his own on the first day, so she could see her one good eye in the steel. And she put her cracked old thumb on the word at the end of the blade, and didn’t push, and didn’t read, and didn’t say anything at all.

A finger’s length of the steel, at the very end, where it had been flat for fifty-one years, went orange from inside.

It wasn’t much. It was the last hand’s width of an old sword in a stone tower on a rock. It hummed against her thumb. It stayed.

“Huh,” said Hessa.

She didn’t say anything else. She hung it back up on the wall, lit, and stood looking at it with her arms folded, with her back to all of them. And she was still standing like that when they went out of the door.

She came to the edge of the swept ring to see them off. Not further. She stood on the rim of it, straight as a post, with her broom.

“You’ll come down again,” she said. Not a question.

“Somebody has to sit with an old woman on floor 1,” said Ice, from the top of the path. “This winter. We’re going to sit with her. All of us.”

Hessa looked at him.

“So you don’t go to her funeral either.”

“So I don’t go to her funeral.”

“Good,” said Hessa. “Stop going to people’s funerals. It’s very rude.” She looked at Niko. “I’ll wait. Out of spite.”

“I know,” said Niko.

They went down the path between the nests with the gulls screaming at them. At the bottom, on the first plank of the second bridge, Niko looked back. She was still there, on the rim of the swept ring, a straight dark shape against the morning with a broom, and a thin grey line of smoke going straight up out of the chimney behind her. Then she turned and went in.

They crossed the two bridges. They crossed Tether, where the people lifted a hand off their ropes, and Nan Gully at her winch was singing something with words in it now, that Niko didn’t catch, and Dennis said she won’t tell anybody the last verse till it’s finished, I asked. They went out over three more bridges in the afternoon with the kites going round them, all seven, high, Brin turning loops, to the little island the size of a house with the stone hut round the door, where all thirty-six of them had come up into the dark a year and a half ago and sat crammed together and waited for the sun.

Higu sat down on the step of the hut and got the rock out.

“Nobody asks him on the way out,” she said. “Only at the bottom.” But she wrote in the right-hand column anyway, while they waited for Tiger to find the door. Under THE KITES FLY. EVERY DAY. and THE SONG HAS A NEW VERSE, which Chris had told her to put.

HESSA. ALIVE.

She showed it to Niko. He nodded.

“Eighty,” said Ice, to nobody in particular, at the door. Then he looked round, and found Chi, and Chi looked back at him across the little island and breathed, slowly, at him, as hard as she could. And he breathed.

Tiger put his hand flat on the door. Newish started counting. Thea took Ice’s hand. Calvin went to the front of the line and turned round and looked at all of them.

“Look at my back,” he said. “Nobody runs.”

Niko put his thumb on the blade at his hip, on the dull place, and then on the new letters on the back face. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to any more. It answered anyway, warm through the scabbard, steady, the whole length.

And they went down into the dark, two hundred and forty-four steps, toward the quiet.
