Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 150

The Step

3,395 words · 15 min read

Xola woke up before Thea, which had never happened in the history of the world.

She lay on her blanket in the grey before dawn and didn’t move, with Squiddles asleep on her collarbone like a small damp hat, and looked at the ceiling of the copy-room. And then at Thea, on the next blanket, asleep on her side with one hand flat on the lantern.

Thea always slept with her hand on it. Since floor 3. Since the morning on the green hill when they’d found it on a bench by a door, with a little amber light inside it that leaned, always, one way. Thea had picked it up and it had leaned toward her hand, and she’d held on to it ever since. Every night. Every floor. Even on the Salt, where nothing could be heard and nothing grew, she’d slept with her fingers curled round the handle, and the light inside the glass had leaned toward her in the dark like a plant toward a window.

It wasn’t leaning toward her now. It was leaning toward the end of the room, by the wall, where Ice was asleep.

Xola watched it for a long time.

She’d known Thea for years. Years, and three hundred and ninety-four days, if you counted the tower, and Xola always counted the tower. She’d been the one who’d put Thea in a call with him, years ago: her best friend, who’d liked her once and stayed her friend anyway, and the girl who was going to be her best friend for the rest of her life. Because she liked them both, and she thought they’d be good at being friends.

Best thing she’d ever done. She’d said so. Out loud, to both of them. Second best was you.

And for a year, the three of them. Every night. Thea singing. Him on the other end, laughing at nothing. Xola in the middle, in and out, with her four-in-the-morning face. Her best friend, and her other best friend, and her.

And then it had thinned. The way things did. And none of them had said anything. And Xola had let it, the way you let a tide go out, because you thought it would come back.

It had come back. On floor 6, at the bottom of a pit, in the dark, with a sword through a thing that showed you what you wanted. It had come back in a white helm, saying not this time.

She’d have liked a bit of warning.

She’d known since the tarn.

Not what. She wasn’t Thea. She didn’t read under things. She just read Thea, which was a different thing, and which she’d been doing for years, and was better at than anybody alive. And on the morning they’d come back round the water at the top of floor 10, in the grey, to the fire Gabe had built on the near shore, Thea had walked into the light with her hair loose and Ice beside her and a face Xola had never seen on her. Not in all the years. Not once.

Happy, yes. That had been the first thing, the obvious thing, the thing Xola had squealed into her own palms about. Babe.

But under the happy, there’d been something else. Something that had come back round the tarn with her and had been with her every day since. Something Thea was carrying very carefully, in both hands, up a stair.

Xola had seen Thea carry things before. Gigi, at the end, with the shadows coming up under her eyes. She knew what Thea’s face did when she was carrying something and didn’t want anybody to help, because if she let go of it for a second to let somebody help, she’d drop it.

This was that. But bigger. And Thea had been carrying it for a hundred and nine days, and she hadn’t put it down once. Not even for Xola.

Especially not for Xola, Xola thought. Because Xola would have asked. Xola always asked. It was the main thing about her.

So she hadn’t asked.

But Rui Xiang had said it, on the roof, to Xav. And Xav had said it in the tack room, very quietly, to the club, so they’d know. Rui thinks she was told. Not the theory. The thing.

And Xola had sat in the tack room on her upturned bucket and not said anything at all. Because she’d known since the tarn.

She found Thea at the pump, at sunrise, filling the water skins. All of them. Methodically. Six skins, for a walk across a road.

“Morning, babe.”

“Morning.”

Xola took a skin out of her hand and held it under the spout. They filled them in silence. Squiddles slid down Xola’s arm and put one small curling tentacle in the stream of water and kept it there, blissfully. Across the road, on the Countinghouse step, a clerk stood with its book and watched them. Watched Thea.

“He told you,” said Xola.

Thea’s hands stopped on the pump handle.

“Didn’t he,” said Xola. She kept her eyes on the skin. On the water going in. “Under the aurora. At the top of ten. When you went round the tarn and didn’t come back till morning.” She stoppered the skin. “He told you. All of it. Whatever all of it is.”

Thea didn’t move. The water stopped running.

“Xola,” she said. “I can’t.”

“I know you can’t.” Xola put the full skin down on the edge of the trough. Picked up an empty one. Held it out. “Pump.”

Thea looked at her.

“Pump, babe.”

Thea pumped. The water came. Xola held the skin under it and watched it fill, and didn’t look at Thea’s face, because she knew if she did, one of them would cry, and it was too early in the morning for that and they had a road to cross.

“I’m not asking you to tell me,” said Xola. “I’d never. I’m not going to ask what it is. I’m not going to ask what he said. I’m not going to ask you anything, ever, about it, unless you want me to.” She stoppered the skin. “I’m just telling you I know you can’t. So you don’t have to keep pretending to me that there isn’t anything. That’s all. That’s one less thing to carry.”

Thea let go of the pump handle.

She stood there in the copy-room yard at sunrise with her hands at her sides, and Xola watched her face out of the corner of her eye do the thing it had done on floor 12, by the Salt, when Gigi had taken the lantern off her and said I’ve got it, for a bit, you can put your hands down.

It went soft. All at once. Everywhere.

“How long have you known?” said Thea.

“Since the tarn. Since you came round the water.” Xola picked up the last skin. “You had a face on. I’ve known your faces for years, babe. I’ve got a list. That one wasn’t on it.” She held the skin under the spout. “Pump.”

Thea pumped. She was crying, a little, now. Not much. Xola let her.

“I can’t tell you,” said Thea.

“I know.”

“I want to. I want to so much. It’s—“ Thea stopped. “It’s not mine.”

“I know,” said Xola. “It’s his. And he gave it you, because it’s you. Because it was always going to be you.” She stoppered the skin, and set it with the others, and finally turned and looked at Thea properly. “I put you in that call, remember? I know exactly who he’d tell. I’d have been offended if it was anybody else.”

Thea laughed. Wet. Through her nose.

Xola stepped over the six full water skins and put her arms round her. Hard. With Squiddles squashed between them, complaining.

“Just promise me one thing,” said Xola, into her hair.

“What?”

“In there.” Xola didn’t let go. “Don’t step in front of anything.”

Thea went still.

Xola felt it. All through her. The way you feel somebody’s whole body stop.

I think last time it was me, Thea had said. On floor 6. At the bottom of a pit, in the dark, to Xola, before anybody knew anything. Not knowing what she meant. I think last time I was the one who got in the way. And it went through.

Xola had never forgotten it. She’d never known what it meant. She didn’t want to know. She just knew she’d heard her best friend say it, at three in the morning, with her shoulder open, and that every single time since then that Thea had moved toward Ice in a fight, Xola had felt it in her stomach.

“There won’t be anything to step in front of,” said Thea. “It’s a book.”

“Promise me anyway.”

Thea was quiet for a long time.

“I’ll walk behind Drew,” she said. “I’ll read it. And I’ll walk out.”

“That’s not a promise.”

“It’s what I can promise,” said Thea. “Today.” She pulled back, enough to look at Xola. “It’s the most I can promise anybody. Even him.”

Xola looked at her.

“Okay,” she said. “Then I’m walking right behind you. Close enough to grab.”

“I know.”

“I’ll grab, Thea. I’ll grab you by the hair.”

“I know,” said Thea. And laughed again, properly this time. “I know you will.”

She found Ice in the afternoon.

He was on the roof, of course. Where he always went now, when he wasn’t out past the Fell Gate. He hadn’t gone out today. He’d sat in the copy-room all morning, cleaning his sword, which was already clean, and then he’d gone up the ladder and hadn’t come down.

Xola climbed up after him. He was sitting on the parapet with his back to the town and his face to the Countinghouse. Just looking at it. She sat down next to him. Close. Shoulder against shoulder. The way she sat with Thea.

He didn’t move away. He would have, once. On floor 9. On the root.

“Hi, Icey.”

“Hi.”

They sat there. Across the road, the Countinghouse. Plain. Grey. A clerk on its top step.

“You’re going to sit on the step,” said Xola.

“Yes.”

“The bottom one. Outside. The whole time.” She didn’t look at him. “Even if every head in that place turns round. Even if she’s in there an hour. Even if it’s dark and the door shuts and you can’t see anything and you can’t hear anything—“

“Even if.”

“Because if you come in after her, Icey.” Her voice did something. She let it. “If you get up off that step and come through that door after her. On your own. Into that. With your cord on. I swear to God I’ll—“

“I won’t.”

Xola turned her head and looked at him.

“You don’t get to say won’t,” she said. “Remember? Floor 9. The rope. You don’t get to say won’t, ever, about anything, because you always—“

“Then I’ll try,” said Ice.

Xola stopped.

He was looking at the Countinghouse door. His face was doing nothing, the way it always did. But his hands, on his knees, were holding on to each other.

“I’ll try,” he said again. Quieter. “I’ll sit on the step and I’ll try. That’s all I can say. I’m not going to say won’t. You were right. I’ll try.”

Xola looked at him for a long time.

Malcolm had told her. Everybody knew by now. He held his arm out at the pump and said it hurts. He said, I said I’d try. Heal sooner. Somebody asked me.

She knew who’d asked him.

“Okay,” said Xola. And found she couldn’t say anything else for a moment. She looked at his hands, holding on to each other on his knees, and she thought about a boy on the other end of a call at four in the morning who’d laughed at nothing for a year and then stopped calling, and never once said why, and never once said it hurts. Not to her. Not to Thea. Not to anybody.

“Okay,” she said again. “Try’s good. Try’s—“ Her voice went. She put her forehead against his shoulder, hard, the way she did with Thea. “Okay, ice coob.”

He laughed. Short. Surprised. Into the air over the town.

“Nobody’s called me that in—“

“I know how long,” said Xola, into his shoulder. “Shut up.”

He shut up.

They sat there a while longer. Then, without lifting her head, Xola said, “She’ll be okay.”

“I know.”

“You don’t. You don’t know anything. You’ve never known anything. You just say things.” She sat up and wiped her face with her wrist. “But she’ll be okay. Because I’m going to be right behind her. Close enough to grab. And Drew’s in front, and Chi’s there, and Higu’s drawing, and John’s got a light, and you’re on the step.” She looked at him. “And she’s Thea.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. “She’s Thea.”

He said it the way you’d say the name of a star you steered by.

Xola looked at him. And for the first time in all the years she’d known him, she thought she knew exactly what he’d told her best friend under the aurora, at the top of the tenth floor. Not the words. Not what. The shape of it.

Something he’d carried for a very long time. Further than anybody should have to. And given to the only person he could have given it to.

She didn’t say so. She’d promised Thea. She’d never ask.

“I’ll grab her,” said Xola. “If anything happens. I’ll grab her by the hair.”

“I know you will,” said Ice.

The clerks went in at dusk.

Xola stood with the others at the copy-room gate and counted them. She’d never counted them before. John always did. But she counted them now, every one, coming up the Wellgate from every corner, from every turn of the road, walking up the four plain steps one at a time and through the plain door that opened for each of them and closed behind. Grey robe. Grey book. Plain face. Grey robe. Grey book.

One of them was Alys. She knew it was, because Calvin, beside her, made a small noise and then didn’t. A tall one, with brown hair pulled back. She went up the steps like the rest. She didn’t look round.

A hundred and twelve.

The door shut behind the last.

And then the Wellgate was empty, and the Countinghouse was shut, and the light was going, and the whole of the copy-room was at the gate. All thirty-six of them. Hawise, too, and Orlo, at the Tellhouse steps across the road. Calvin had his fist shut against his chest. Rachael had her arms folded. Gabe stood with Hearthwall on his back and his face set like a wall.

Ice went first.

Not up the steps. To them. He walked across the Wellgate in the last of the light, with the cord on his wrist where anybody could see it, and stopped at the foot of the four plain steps of the Countinghouse. And sat down. On the bottom one. With his back against the rail and his knees up.

Gabe sat down beside him. On his right. With Hearthwall across his knees, glowing very faintly in the dusk, warm.

Higu went over and gave Ice the rock.

Xola didn’t hear what Higu said. Something short. Higu never said much. Ice took the rock and held it on his knees, front side up. Two columns. The left one full, the right one full, and the line down the middle.

“Hold the left,” Xola heard Higu say, as she came back. “You’re the left. We’ll bring you the right.”

Then it was them.

Drew first, with his empty hands. John beside him, a small white mote already floating up off his palm and going on ahead, low, up the steps. Then Thea, with the lantern, and the light inside it leaning so hard toward the bottom step that it lay flat against the glass. Then Xola. Close. Close enough to grab. Then Chi, with Waddles at her feet. Then Higu, last, with a slate and chalk instead of the rock.

They walked past the step.

Ice didn’t get up. He didn’t say anything. He sat on the bottom step with the rock on his knees and Gabe on his right, and as Thea went past him, he looked up at her. Just looked.

And Thea stopped. Just for a second. And put her hand on the top of his head. The way you’d put it on something you were coming back for.

Then she went up the steps.

Xola went up after her. Close enough to grab.

Four steps. Plain. Grey. At the top, the plain grey door, shut, with no handle and no knocker and no keyhole. Just a door. With grey light coming out of the crack at the bottom, onto Xola’s boots.

Drew stood aside. He looked at Thea.

Thea looked at the door.

Then she lifted her hand, and knocked.

Three times. Not loud. Just knuckles on wood, the way you’d knock on a friend’s door at the end of a long day. Knock. Knock. Knock.

Nothing happened.

Xola felt her heart going in her throat, in her gills, everywhere. Behind her, at the bottom of the steps, she could feel Ice. She didn’t look. She didn’t need to. She could feel him the way you feel the sun on the back of your neck. Sitting still. Not getting up.

Trying.

And the door opened.

Not a crack. Not a finger’s width. It swung all the way back, slowly, on silent hinges, as if somebody inside had heard the knock and come to answer it. And beyond it, the passage. Ten paces of grey stone. And grey light, from nowhere. And beyond that, the hall.

And from the hall, very faint, the sound of rain on a roof a long way off.

Then the rain stopped.

All at once. Every pen. The silence came down the passage at them like a cold breath, and Xola felt Squiddles go rigid on her shoulder.

Behind her, at the bottom of the steps, she heard a very small sound. Not a word. Just a breath.

She looked back. She couldn’t help it.

Ice was sitting on the bottom step with the rock on his knees. He hadn’t moved. He hadn’t stood up. His hands were flat on the stone.

But his head had tipped. To the left. Slowly. Very slowly. As if somebody standing very close to him had just said something very low, and it had taken him a long time to hear it all.

“Icey—“ said Xola.

He looked up at her. His face was white.

“Go on,” he said. Very steady. “I’m here.”

Thea went in.

Xola went after her. Close enough to grab.

And behind them, Higu, last, stopped on the threshold and drew a line on her slate where the door was. And then, under it, very small, a step. And a little figure sitting on it.

Then she came in too.
