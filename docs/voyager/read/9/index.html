Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 9

Voyager

3,024 words · 13 min read

The level-ups came while he was still standing over the Bellwether, one on top of the other, chiming like a till.

[ Ice · Lv 5 ]

[ Ice · Lv 6 · Tier 1: Squire · Knight
HP 214 / 240 · MP 48 / 48
STR 11 · AGI 8 · VIT 12 · INT 5 · SPI 8
Free points: 10
]

He didn’t spend them yet. His hands were shaking too much to trust them with anything, even numbers.

The bell was still lying at the bottom of the slope where it had rolled, crack up, green bronze catching the starlight. He went down and picked it up. He’d expected it to be heavy; it had been the size of a bucket round the Bellwether’s neck. As his fingers closed on it, it shrank, the way loot always did, folding down smoothly into something he could hold in one hand: a small bronze bell on a short leather loop, with a hairline crack up one side.

[ Bellwether’s Bell (Rare) · trinket
Taunt reaches further. Rings once for each enemy that turns.
]

He hung it on his belt. It made a tiny cracked note as it settled against his hip, and then went quiet.

“That’s useful,” said Alea.

“It’s very useful.”

“You never got one, the first time. Thirty-six people on the floor, and it went to Gabe, and he melted it down for a sword guard on floor six.”

“I remember.” He’d been furious. He’d also laughed so much he’d fallen off a bench. “Don’t tell him.”

“I can’t tell him. That’s sort of the whole situation.”

“I know. I’m just saying. Don’t.”

He went back up the slope and stood beside the Bellwether for a moment longer. It was already beginning to fade the way things in the Spire faded: from the edges in, gently, like breath on a window. By the morning there’d be nothing in the Fold but cropped grass, seven stones, one of them cracked, and the door in the hill.

And by tomorrow night, it would be back.

“Respawn’s twenty-four hours,” said Alea, reading his face. “They’re all still on the floor. It’ll be lying in the middle of the Fold asleep again tomorrow night, with its bell on, as if none of this happened.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“They need it. They need the experience. They need to stand round it in a crowd and get thrown about and be terrified and kill it anyway.” He looked at the cracked stone. “That’s the tutorial. That’s what it’s for. If I took that off them, they’d go up the stairs having never been scared of anything, and then they’d get to floor four.”

Alea was quiet.

“You could leave them a hint,” she said finally. “Scratch it on the stone. Cut the strap.”

He’d been thinking about that since the strap parted. He’d been standing here thinking about it the whole time.

“No,” he said.

“It would save them a try. Maybe two.”

“It would save them a try,” Ice agreed. “And then Deston would stand here looking at it, and he’d think: whoever wrote cut the strap has fought this thing before. And the only person who’s fought this thing before is the person who killed it last night, alone, on the first day, while the rest of us were choosing doors.” He shook his head. “And then he’d think: how did he know to cut the strap the first time? And Deston doesn’t stop thinking once he starts. He’ll keep pulling that thread until it comes out somewhere I don’t want it to.”

“You already left him a note about the third floor.”

“I know. That’s one thread. One’s a warning. Two’s a pattern.”

The fox didn’t argue. He thought she probably agreed with him and had wanted to hear him say it.

He turned his back on the Bellwether and walked to the door in the hill.

The ascension room was exactly as he remembered it, which was starting to feel like a joke the Spire was telling at his expense.

It was round, and small, and dry. A plain stone room cut into the hill, with a low stone bench round the wall, a fire-pit in the middle with a stack of dry wood beside it that hadn’t been there a minute ago, and at the back, opposite the door, the bottom of a stair. The stair went up into the dark, steep and narrow and straight, cut into the rock of the hill.

The moment he stepped over the threshold, a small window opened.

[ Ascension Room · Safe ]

Safe. Nothing in the Spire came into an ascension room. Nothing ever had. He’d slept in a hundred of them. On the worst floors, they’d been the only places he’d slept at all.

He lit the fire. It took three tries with a flint from the vest pocket. He’d forgotten the vest had a flint in it; it was a tactical vest, after all, and somebody, at some point, had decided that included fire. When it caught, he sat down on the stone bench with his back to the wall and the shield beside him and his legs stretched out toward the flames, and he let himself stop.

The Plain Broth had worn off. The ember in his palm was warm. His whole body hurt in a dull, general way he knew from experience would be much worse in the morning.

“Do you want to see world chat?” said Alea.

“No.”

“It’s—“

“I know what it is. Don’t tell me.”

“I wasn’t going to tell you. I was going to ask if you wanted me to keep a log of it, for later, when you do want to see.”

He thought about that. “Yeah,” he said. “Keep it.”

“Okay.”

He knew what was in it anyway. He knew those thirty-five people better than he knew anything. He could have written it himself, line for line. He didn’t need to read it. Reading it would only mean sitting here with his hands shaking, trying not to answer.

He spent his free points instead, to give his hands something to do. Strength, vitality. Two more into spirit.

“SPI,” said Alea.

“For later.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it keeps being true.”

After a while, when the fire had settled and the shaking had mostly gone, he opened the last window.

[ Wayfarer’s Mark · Unique
A blank seal for one who climbs without a name.
Write the name the Spire will know you by. It takes when you next climb.
]

The item lay in his palm when he called it up. It was a small, round, flat disc of pale stone, the size of a coin, cool and smooth and completely blank on both sides.

“That’s the one,” said Alea quietly. “The one I told you about.”

“I know.”

“In nine years of other Climbers’ logs I found it mentioned twice. Twice, Ice. Both of them had come out of the hill alone. Both of them had cleared the first floor before the second dawn. Both of them had cards that wouldn’t take a name.” She paused. “Neither of them got past the thirtieth floor.”

“Cheerful.”

“I’m logging it.”

“Log it quietly.”

He turned the stone over in his fingers. The card in his vest pocket, the one from the white room, was still blank. The book in Quill’s hall had a line with no name in it. The rankings, which he also hadn’t looked at, would have a blank at the top of them tonight, and thirty-five people looking up at that blank.

A blank was a question. A blank was an empty circle on a white floor. A blank was the exact shape of somebody missing, and the three people who’d seen him would look at it and know, in their stomachs, what fitted it.

A name was different. A name was an answer. Even a wrong one.

“Okay,” he said. “Names.”

“Not Declan,” said Alea.

“Not Declan.”

“Not Ice.”

“Obviously not Ice. Thirty-five people know Ice.”

“Something Latin,” said Alea, in a voice that suggested she already knew how this was going to go.

He’d been about to say it. He’d been sitting there with Viator on the tip of his tongue, traveller, wayfarer, the word on the stone in the Testament. It was so clean and so right that it felt like cheating.

“Viator,” he said anyway, just to hear it.

“Gabe knows you love Latin,” said Alea. “Deston knows. Xav knows. John knows. You put it in every Discord status for ten years. You named every group you ever made something Latin. Four of them would look at the rankings, see Viator at the top, and come up those stairs at a run. Gabe wouldn’t even finish reading it.”

He laughed. It hurt his ribs. “Yeah,” he said. “Yeah. He wouldn’t.”

“So not that.”

“So not that.” He leaned his head back against the stone. “xXSheepSlayerXx.”

“No.”

“BellwetherBane.”

“No.”

“Onion Boy.”

“Ice.”

“Mother Crane already knows me as the onion boy. It’s consistent. It’s a brand.”

“I am going to delete myself,” said Alea.

He laughed again, and then he stopped laughing, and sat for a while looking at the fire.

When he was seventeen, in a classroom in Singapore that smelled of whiteboard markers, a lecturer had put up a slide of a photograph. It was mostly black. Somewhere in the middle of it, so small you had to stand right in front of the screen to see it, there was a single pale blue dot.

That’s us, the lecturer had said. That’s the Earth. That photo was taken from four billion miles away, by a probe that had been travelling for thirteen years. It turned its camera round and took a picture of home, and then it kept going.

Xav had been sitting next to him. Xav, who’d been in his class about three weeks and hadn’t said much, had leaned over and whispered: It’s still going, you know. They launched it in the seventies. It’s past everything now. Outside the whole solar system. It’s still sending messages back. Every day. Really faint. They need dishes the size of stadiums just to hear it.

What’s it saying? Ice had whispered back.

Nothing, really. Just numbers. Just “I’m still here.”

He’d thought about that for the rest of the class, and on the bus home, and a lot of times afterwards. A small thing sent out ahead on its own. Further than anything had ever gone. Past every planet, past the edge of everything anyone knew, into the dark. Still going. Still calling home. I’m still here.

It had a record on it, Xav had told him later. A golden one. Greetings in fifty-five languages, and music, and the sound of rain, and a heartbeat. For whoever found it, out there, in case anybody did.

The probe’s name was Voyager.

“Voyager,” he said.

Alea looked at him.

“Voyager?” she said. “Really?”

“Trust.”

“That’s the probe. The one Xav told you about. In your first month of poly.” She’d read the whole of his life over ten years, in the dark, at the bottom of a hundred floors; she knew every story he’d ever told and most of the ones he hadn’t. “Ice, Xav knows about that.”

“Xav knows about a space probe. Everybody knows about the space probe. Half the internet’s had it as a profile picture at some point.” He turned the blank stone over. “It’s not Latin. It’s not a joke. It’s not something I said every day. It’s just a word. Somebody on the top of the rankings called Voyager could be anyone.” He paused. “Xav might think about it for a second. Then he’ll think, nah. Because why would Declan pick that?”

“Why would Declan pick that?” said Alea.

He didn’t answer straight away.

“Because it went ahead,” he said finally. “On its own. Further than anyone. Past everything. So that whatever was out there, it would get to it first. And it never stopped calling home.” He looked at the fire. “I’m still here. Every day.”

“It’s not coming back, though,” said Alea quietly. “The probe. It’s never coming back.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. “That’s the bit I’m changing.”

The fox didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she came out. She hadn’t been out since the chapel. A small shape of teal and white light took form on the stone bench beside him, cat-sized, one tail curling round her feet. She looked up at him with her two white pinprick eyes.

“Okay,” she said. “Voyager.”

He put his fingertip on the blank stone.

It was like the card in the white room. A thin line of light followed his finger and sank into the stone where it passed, and dried. He wrote it slowly and carefully, the way he’d write his name on something important, like a contract. VOYAGER. Capitals. The B rule didn’t come up.

[ Wayfarer’s Mark · inscribed: Voyager
It takes when you next climb.
]

He closed his hand round it. The stone was warm now, like the ember.

He ate, finally. He took Mother Crane’s cloth out and unwrapped it, the half loaf and the whole. He ate the half, slowly this time, tasting it properly, sitting by the fire in the ascension room at the top of floor 1. It was a little stale already, round the edges. It was still the best bread on the floor.

Then he looked at the whole one.

She always gave you two, Alea had said. Because she knew you’d share.

He broke it in half. It came apart in his hands with a soft sound, the crust cracking, the inside still almost soft. He put one half back in the cloth for tomorrow. He looked at the other for a moment, and then put it down on the stone bench beside the fox.

Alea looked at it. Then at him.

“I’m made of light,” she said.

“I know.”

“I can’t eat bread.”

“I know.”

“So why—“

“Because she gave me two,” said Ice, “and there’s nobody else here.”

The fox looked at the half loaf for a long time. Then she lay down beside it on the stone bench, very carefully, curled round with her tail over her nose, and put her small light chin on the edge of the crust, the way a dog guards something it’s been given.

“Thank you,” she said, muffled.

“Don’t mention it.”

“I’m logging it.”

“I know.”

He slept, eventually, with his back against the wall and the shield across his knees and the little cracked bell on his belt, and the fire burning down. He dreamed about nothing. Nothing in the Spire ever came into an ascension room. It was the first time in ten years, or in a day, he wasn’t sure which, that he’d slept without listening for something.

When he woke, the fire was ash. Through the open door behind him, the cropped grass of the Fold was grey with first light, and the sky over the valley was going pale at the eastern edge, where the sun would come up its rail. Somewhere down in Hearthstead, thirty-five people were waking up for their second day in the Spire. One of them, he’d bet anything, had been awake all night.

He stood up. Everything hurt, exactly as promised. He put the shield on his arm and the sword on his back. He picked up the half loaf from beside the fox, who’d faded out sometime in the night, and wrapped it back in the cloth with the other half, because you didn’t leave Mother Crane’s bread behind.

Then he went to the foot of the stair, and looked up into the dark.

It went straight up, steep and narrow, cut into the hill. A hundred and twelve steps, if it was the same as before. At the top there’d be a door, and on the other side of the door the second floor of the Spire, and the Spire would read the name on the warm stone in his pocket. At the top of every ranking every one of them could see, the blank would fill in.

Voyager.

The first time, they’d climbed it all together. Thirty-six of them in single file, on their third morning, because it had taken them two days to find the Fold and work up the nerve, two hours and three tries to kill the Bellwether, and another half a day to stop shaking. Gabe had gone first, because Gabe always went first. He’d counted the steps out loud the whole way up, one, two, three, in his big voice, partly to keep everyone’s spirits up and partly because he’d honestly wanted to know. Somewhere around fifty, Calvin had asked if there was a lift. Somewhere around eighty, Alice had started counting along with him, and then Jared, and then everyone. By the top there’d been thirty-six people chanting numbers in the dark like a football crowd, and the stairwell had rung with it. A hundred and ten. A hundred and eleven.

A hundred and twelve, Gabe had said, at the top, with his hand on the door. Huh. Weird number. And then he’d opened it.

Ice stood at the bottom with one foot raised and listened to the silence in the stairwell for a moment. There was nobody to count. There wouldn’t be, for a long time. He’d chosen that. He’d go on choosing it, floor after floor, for as long as it took them to be strong enough not to need him in front.

He could count on his own.

He put his foot on the first step.

“One,” he said, under his breath.

“Up,” said Alea’s voice in his head, quietly. She sounded half asleep. “Again.”

“Again,” said Ice, and climbed.
