Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 211

Cut Loose

5,557 words · 24 min read

Bee drew what was there.

That was the nearest thing she had to a rule. She’d found it out on the second floor, in the snow, with a stub of pencil, when a sun she’d drawn on the back of a scrap to calm herself down had gone warm in her lap for about thirty seconds. She’d screamed. Hako had laughed. Then he’d stopped laughing, and looked at the scrap, and said Bee. Do it again.

She’d done it a lot since. Suns, mostly. Small round ones with lines coming off them. They kept people warm, for about thirty seconds each, nobody knew why. Parasols on the Glass, and a cloud, and one bad tree, for shade. A shed door with a knot in the third plank, on nine, in the briar, that Hako had held while everybody went through. Other things sometimes. But it worked best when it was true. When she drew a door she knew, every plank of it. When she drew a sun the shape a real sun made in her head. The more she knew the thing, the longer it stayed, and the more it was what it was. When she drew something she’d made up, it was thin, and flickered, and went.

So she drew what was there.

People, mostly, when she wasn’t drawing suns. Faces. She couldn’t help it. She’d drawn every one of the thirty-six, a hundred times each, on every floor. And she’d drawn Ice more than anybody, because Ice’s face did more than anybody’s and showed less, and she’d wanted for two years to get it right.

She’d got it right once. On twelve. On the Salt, in the gold dusk, sitting across from him at the only fire on the floor. His face with nothing held in it. She’d drawn it and shown it to him and he’d laughed out loud, so loud the crust shivered, and Thea had gone red, and Bee had said that’s the face, that’s just you. He’d folded it small and put it inside his vest. He still had it. She’d seen the corner of it on floor 1, when he took out Mother Tallow’s candle.

She’d never got it right since. His face had gone back to holding things.

She was trying again on the morning of the seven hundred and thirty-third day, sitting on a coil of rope at the edge of the Float with a strip of birch-bark on her knee and her Pollen Puffs asleep in her hood, because his face was doing something she’d never seen it do before, and she wanted to have it.

He was standing at the front of the Float, facing down the river. He’d been standing there since before light. Thea beside him. The lantern in her hand. Neither of them talking.

The Float was moving.

Not moving. Straining. It was the strangest feeling Bee had ever had under her feet. The whole raft-town, seventy rafts lashed into one, houses and lanes and the square with the mast, floating on a river that had risen in two days until it filled the valley from hill to hill and was still rising. Floating, and held. Held by ropes. Thick ones, as thick as Bee’s arm, run out from the Float’s edges to iron rings sunk in the rock of the cliff on the left bank, and to great timber stakes driven into the riverbed upstream, long ago, for exactly this. Twelve ropes. And all twelve of them were tight.

The river wanted the Float. Bee could feel it. Under her, through the planks, through the coil of rope she was sitting on, a long steady pull. Down the valley. Toward the Narrows. The river had been pulling for two days, ever since the first word, harder every hour as the water rose behind the jam, and the ropes had held, and the Float had stayed. And now the ropes were creaking. All twelve. A long low creak, like a ship’s timbers, under everything.

“They’ll go,” said Kei, beside her. Quietly. He was watching the nearest rope where it went over the edge of the Float into the water. “Not yet. But they’ll go. They weren’t made for water this high.” He had Dose round his neck. She had her head up, tasting the air. “Dose says by noon.”

Bee looked at the rope. Then at Ice, at the front of the Float, with his back to them.

“Does he know?” she said.

“He knows,” said Kei. “He’s been listening to them since midnight.”

Bee looked down at her drawing. She’d got the shape of him. The back of his head, his shoulders, Thea beside him. The lantern. She hadn’t got his face, because she couldn’t see it from here; she’d have to go round the front. But she’d got the way he was standing. Very still. With his weight on both feet, a bit forward, the way Tuli stood on the ice when she was listening. Not the way he used to stand.

He used to stand like somebody about to go somewhere. Always. On every floor. Even asleep, Hyvred said, his light was leaning toward a door.

He wasn’t leaning toward a door. He was just standing. Listening.

Bee drew that.

“Five threads,” said Hyvred, on the mast.

She’d been up there since dawn, with her glasses on, looking down the river toward the Narrows. You couldn’t see the Narrows from the Float. It was a day off, round two bends of the valley. But Hyvred could see lights further than anybody, and she’d been up the mast every hour since Chris’s party left, looking for theirs.

“I can see them,” said Hyvred. “Just. Down past the second bend. Five.” She was quiet. “They’re tired. All of them. Really tired.” A pause. “Ina’s gone cold.”

Bee looked up.

“His colour,” said Hyvred. “Ina’s. It’s usually warm. Like a hearth. Even when he’s sad. It’s gone—“ She stopped. “It’s gone like the inside of the river. On the hands. Mostly on the hands.”

At the front of the Float, Ice turned round.

He didn’t say anything. He looked up at Hyvred on the mast. And Bee, who had been drawing his back for an hour, saw his face for the first time that morning, and stopped drawing.

He knew. Bee could see it. Before Hyvred said it. He’d felt it, through the threads, whatever the threads were, the gold lines Deston said went from Ice to all of them; he’d felt Ina’s go cold, somewhere far down the river, an hour ago, maybe more. And he’d stood at the front of the Float facing that way and not said anything. And not gone.

He can’t go, Bee thought. He doesn’t know the way. He doesn’t know the river.

And then: He wouldn’t go anyway. Not now. Not on his own.

She didn’t know which was true. She thought maybe both.

“Ina’s hurt,” said Ice. Quietly. Not to anybody. “His hands. And Chris. His arm. Earlier. And now—“ He stopped. He put his hand flat on his chest. “Now they’re frightened. All five. At once. Something went wrong.”

The whole Float had gone quiet. The people who’d heard him, and the people who hadn’t but had seen his face.

“I don’t know what,” said Ice. “I can’t see it. I don’t know what went wrong.” He looked down the river. “I just know it did.”

Thea’s hand was on his arm. Just above the wrist.

“Then we go,” said Thea.

“The ropes—“

“The ropes are going anyway,” said Thea. “Kei says. By noon.” She looked at him. “We go to them. Not you. We. All of us. On the Float. Down the river. The way the Float goes every spring.” She held up the lantern. “We’ll get there. Then we’ll find out.”

Ice looked at her for a long moment.

Then he turned round, and looked across the Float, at the square, at the foot of the mast, where Haddo was standing with his pole on the planks and his head tipped, listening to the ropes.

“Haddo,” said Ice. “When do you cut loose?”

Haddo didn’t answer straight away.

He stood at the foot of the mast with his big shovel hands on his pole and his head tipped, and listened. To the ropes. To the river under the Float. To something Bee couldn’t hear and didn’t think anybody but the people of fifteen ever could. A long time. Long enough that Calvin, sitting on the steps of the meeting-house with his teeth still chattering from the day before, started to say something and Rachael put her hand over his mouth.

Then Haddo lifted the pole.

“Not yet,” he said.

Ice nodded.

“When?”

“When she tells me.” Haddo looked down the river. “Every spring we cut loose. Every spring it’s different. Gran’s time, they cut at dawn, and it was right. My father’s time, they cut at noon, and it was wrong. Too late. The jam went with them right behind it.” He put the pole down again. Tock. “You don’t cut when you want to. You don’t cut when the ropes go. You cut when she says.”

“How will you know?”

Haddo looked at him.

“I’ll hear it,” he said. “Same as Sabe. Same as Tuli. Same as you’re learning.” He was quiet. “There’s a sound. When the water behind’s ready to go and the water in front’s ready to take you. One sound. You hear it once. If you miss it, it doesn’t come again.” He looked at Ice. “Your people at the Narrows. They’ve been letting her go. I can hear it. Bits. Since yesterday.” Something moved in his white beard. “Never heard that before. Fifty years at the foot of this mast. Never once heard the jam let go a bit at a time.” He put his pole down again. “It’s changed the sound. I don’t know the sound now.”

“So how will you know?”

“I won’t,” said Haddo. “I’ll listen.”

They waited.

All morning. The whole of the Float, everybody on it, three hundred of the Float’s people and thirty-one climbers and a goat, standing by the ropes. The ropes creaking. The river pulling. Haddo at the foot of the mast with his pole down, listening, not moving. Bee sat on her coil of rope and drew.

She drew Haddo. His big hands on the pole. His head tipped.

She drew Calvin on the meeting-house steps with Rachael’s hand over his mouth, which made her laugh, and then not laugh, because Calvin had been under the ice two days ago and was still blue round the lips.

She drew Hako. On the edge of the Float with his spear and his shield, a new spear, borrowed from the Float, his own somewhere at the bottom of the river at Keddle. Leaning on the shield. Facing down the river. It’s always fine. She’d screamed at him yesterday for saying it. She’d slapped forty suns on his back. She drew him now and drew him the way he was, standing in the cold with his face set down the river, and when she looked at the drawing it looked like somebody pointing a spear. This way.

She drew the goat. Who was on the roof of the meeting-house, which nobody had ever been able to work out how he’d got onto, looking down the river with great dignity.

And she drew Ice.

She went round the front, finally. She sat down on a coil of rope a little way off from where he stood with Thea at the front edge of the Float, and looked at his face, and drew it.

It took her a long time. Longer than any face she’d ever drawn. She kept stopping. She kept looking at what she’d drawn and then at his face and then at the drawing, and it wasn’t right, and she didn’t know why, and then she did.

It was a new face.

Not nothing held in it. That had been on twelve, for a minute, by a fire. Not the held face either, the match-in-the-wind face, the one Hyvred talked about, the one he’d had on every floor since the white room except that one minute on the Salt. Something else. Something she’d never seen on him and had seen on everybody else, every day, on every floor, for two years.

The face of somebody standing at the edge of something they didn’t know, waiting to find out.

She drew it. She drew it the way it was. And when she held the bark up and looked at it in the noon light, it came off the bark, very faintly, for about three seconds: a face, hanging in the air above her knee, made of light. Looking down the river.

It was true. That was how she knew.

She put it in her pocket. She’d show him later.

The sound came a little after noon.

Bee heard it. She didn’t know she’d heard it. She’d never heard anything through the ice in her life, she didn’t have a pole, she’d never been taught. But she was sitting on a coil of rope on the Float with her hand flat on the planks, because she’d been drawing and her hand was tired, and something came up through the planks into her palm.

Not a boom. Not a crack. Smaller. A sort of sigh. Long and low and gentle. Like somebody in another room letting a breath out that they’d been holding for a long time.

Haddo lifted his head.

He lifted his pole off the planks. He looked down the river. Bee had never seen his face do anything. It did something now.

“Now,” said Haddo.

And then, in the big slow voice of the Steerer of the Float, so that every person on every raft could hear it, the words Bee had heard him say on the night of the first word:

“Cut loose. Hold on. Listen.”

They cut the ropes.

All twelve. At once. The Float’s people at every one, with axes, on Haddo’s word. Bee saw Salty at one, with his own axe, the one he’d made on floor 10, and Gabe at the next, and Jared, and the axes came down together, and the ropes parted with a sound like a bow-string, twelve times, all at once.

And the Float went.

Bee had thought it would be fast. It wasn’t. It was slow. Enormously slow. The whole raft-town, seventy rafts and every house on them and three hundred and thirty people and a goat, turning, slowly, in the flood, like a great leaf on a pond, and starting to move. Down the valley. With the river. Not fighting it. Going with it.

She’d never felt anything like it under her feet. Like the whole town had decided to stop holding on.

The Float’s people were at the edges with their long poles. Not listening now. Pushing. Fending off. The water was full of ice, slabs and floes and broken plates, all going the same way, and the Float went down the river among them, slowly, turning, and the people at the edges put their poles against the ice that came close and pushed it away, gently, the way you’d push a boat away from a jetty. Not hard. Just enough. All round the Float. Three hundred poles.

And the thirty-one climbers did it too. Somebody put poles in their hands. Bee got one. She’d never held one. She stood at the edge of the Float next to Hyvred and put it against a slab of ice the size of a cart as it turned toward them and pushed, the way the woman next to her was pushing, and the slab turned away.

“Bee,” said Hako. From the next raft. With his borrowed spear in one hand and a pole in the other. “You’re doing it.”

“I’m doing it,” said Bee.

They went down the valley.

Past Keddle. Past the cliff with the stair door at the top of it, and the ledge where eighty-seven people from Keddle stood in the noon sun with their poles, watching the Float go by. They lifted their poles. All of them. Up, and held, and down. The Float’s people lifted theirs back. Bee did too. Past the six stilt-houses at the upstream end of Keddle, standing in the flood, leaning, bound together at their legs with Gigi’s thorns as thick as rope, holding. Gigi stood at the edge of the Float and watched them go by. She didn’t say anything. Xola had her hand.

Past the place where the fourteen other houses had been. There was nothing there now. Just water, and ice, going past.

Ice stood at the front of the Float the whole way, with Thea. He had a pole too. He was using it. Bee watched him. Pushing the ice away, gently, the way the Float’s people did, the way everybody did. Not first. Not alone. One of three hundred and thirty poles.

The hounds came at the first bend.

Bee had been waiting for them. Everybody had. Brann had said it; Haddo had said it. When she goes, the hounds come up. Into the open water. Into the leads. They come for anything on the river. And the river was open now, wide, black water between the floes, and they came up out of it the way they came up out of everything, from underneath, pale and fast and flat.

Onto the Float.

Not many. Not like the Narrows; she heard about the Narrows later, twenty-three of them, from Chris. Six. Eight. Coming up out of the water between the floes and up onto the edges of the rafts, low, with their door-wide mouths open. Going for the people at the edges with the poles.

Going for the woman next to Bee.

Bee saw it come. She didn’t have a sword. She’d never had a sword. She had a pole and a stub of charcoal and a strip of birch-bark in her pocket with a face on it. The hound came up over the edge of the raft and the woman next to Bee screamed and fell back and the hound’s mouth opened, wide as a door.

Bee drew a door.

She didn’t think about it. She had the charcoal out and the back of the bark, the side without his face on it, and she drew a door. A real one. The door of Mother Crane’s cookhouse on floor 1, the Copper Spoon, propped open with a brick, the one she’d walked through a hundred times in the winter, that she knew exactly, every plank. She drew it in about three seconds, closed, and held it out, and it came off the bark.

A door. In the air. Between the hound and the woman. Made of light, but solid; a door was solid, that was what a door was. The hound hit it.

It stood for about thirty seconds. Long enough. Long enough for Hako to get there from the next raft with his borrowed spear and put it into the hound’s open mouth over the top of the door. Long enough for the woman to get back on her feet. Then the door wasn’t there.

“Bee,” said Hako. Out of breath. Looking at the place where the door had been. “That was the Spoon.”

“It was the first door I could think of,” said Bee. Her hands were shaking. “That I knew. All of it.”

Hako looked at her. Then he laughed. Short and breathless.

“Classic,” said Hako.

Across the Float, the other hounds were going down. Winter wasn’t there; Winter was at the Narrows. But Aleaka was there, and Rui Xiang, and Wendy with her bow, and Gabe with Hearthwall putting his shield down on the edge of the raft like a door in a doorway, and the hounds hit him and stopped. And Ice. Bee saw Ice, at the front, with his pole in one hand and his sword in the other. A hound came up out of the water at him and he didn’t watch the sides. He stood still. He listened. And when it came, from underneath, he was already moving, and his sword was already in its open mouth.

Tuli had taught him that. Bee knew. Everybody knew. A girl of fourteen with a pole.

They came round the second bend at dusk.

And there it was.

Bee had seen it in the window, on the night of the first word, from a day off. The Breaker · Floor Guardian. Words in the air. She hadn’t known what it would look like. Nobody had. Chris’s party had sent nothing back up the river; there was nobody to send it, and nothing to send it with.

The Narrows were black. Two cliffs, higher than anything, going up out of the flood on either side, closer and closer, until the river went between them through a door. And in the door, against the left-hand cliff, a mountain of ice. Not as big as it must have been; Bee could see the scars on the cliffs where the rest of it had been, long pale gouges in the black rock on both sides. A third of a mountain. Heaped and broken and groaning, glittering in the last of the light, with one great pale arm going out of it into the left cliff like a tree root into a wall. And on the right of it, where the rest of the jam had been, a river. The whole flood, going through the gap, fast and black and roaring, down into the Narrows and away.

And in the middle of what was left, close under the surface of the ice, so close you could see it from a mile off in the dusk, the heart.

Bee saw it.

Curled. Pale. Bigger than a house. Like something very old lying on its side in a bed of ice with its knees drawn up and its arms out. Four of its arms were slack, now, broken at the knuckles, lying against it like dropped branches. One was still in the cliff. Holding. And in the middle of the curl, through clear black ice, a darkness. Round. Going. She could see it going, from here. Fast.

She had her charcoal out before she knew she did.

She drew it. From the front of the Float, in the dusk, with the lights of the Float coming on behind her and the river roaring ahead and Haddo shouting for the poles to steer the Float out of the current toward the bays on the left bank, the reed-bays above the Narrows, where you could moor short if you were lucky. She drew it fast and she drew it the way it was.

And when she looked at what she’d drawn, she stopped.

It wasn’t a monster. She’d thought it would be. Something sleeps in the jam. It took half the Float in Gran’s time. She’d thought she’d draw teeth, or eyes, or something with claws. She’d drawn what was there. And what was there was something curled up. On its side. With its knees up and its arms out, holding on to the rock as hard as it could with the one arm it had left, the way you’d hold on to the edge of a bed in a nightmare.

Something frightened.

Holding everything it had left. All of it. On its own.

She looked at the drawing for a long time.

Then she looked up, at the front of the Float, at the man standing there with Thea and a pole, looking at the heart in the ice.

And then back down at her drawing.

And then she took the other drawing out of her pocket. The face. The new one. And held them side by side on her knee, the two strips of birch-bark, in the dusk, with the Float turning under her toward the bays.

They weren’t the same. She’d never draw them the same. One was a man and one was a thing in the ice that nobody had a name for.

But she’d drawn Ice’s face on every floor for two years. And she’d drawn it holding things every time but once. And she knew, looking at the curled frightened shape on her knee holding everything it had with all of itself, exactly where she’d seen that before.

The last rope went as they moored.

Haddo got them out of the current. Bee didn’t know how; she’d never know how. Three hundred poles and the sweeps at the back of the Float, the long oars the Float only used once a year, and Haddo at the foot of the mast with his pole down on the planks, listening, saying left, left, now hold, now left, and the Float came out of the main pull of the river toward the dark shape of the left bank, slowly, into a wide shallow bay among drowned reeds a half-mile above the jam. And the Float’s people threw ropes. To the rocks on the shore. To the drowned trees. Men went into the water to their chests, the way Hako had, to make them fast.

Eleven held.

The twelfth, the big one at the front, the one that had to take the weight of the whole Float against the pull of the river, the one Gabe and Salty had made fast to a spur of rock on the shore: it held for a breath. Then it parted. Bee saw it go. A long whip of rope through the dusk, and the Float lurching, turning, starting to swing out of the bay, back toward the current, toward the gap, toward the jam.

Everybody shouted.

Bee drew a rope.

She didn’t think. She had the charcoal and the bark, the back of the strip with the heart on it. She drew a rope. A real one. The one she knew best in the world: Burr’s, on nine, the old one, the left coil, dark with three hundred years, that Burr had given Tiger, that Tiger carried over his shoulder on every floor since. She’d drawn Tiger with it a dozen times. She knew every twist in it. She drew it in a line from the edge of the bark to the edge, fast, the whole length, and held it out over the side of the Float toward the shore, toward the spur of rock where Gabe was standing waist-deep with the broken end of the big rope in his hands.

It came off the bark.

A rope. Made of light. Long. Dark gold. Going from Bee’s hands across the water to Gabe’s.

Gabe caught it.

He looked at it in his hands for a quarter of a second. Then he put it round the spur of rock, twice, and leaned back on it with his whole weight, and Salty beside him, and Jared, and the Float stopped swinging. Held. On a rope made of light, for about thirty seconds, while Tiger came down off the Float into the water at a run with the real rope, Burr’s, off his own shoulder, and swam it to the spur, and made it fast, with Burr’s knot.

The light rope went out of Gabe’s hands.

The real one held.

Bee sat down on the edge of the Float with her charcoal in her hand and the bark in her lap, the back of it blank now where the rope had been, and the front of it still with the heart on it. Her hands were shaking so hard she dropped the charcoal into the water.

Hako came and sat beside her. He didn’t say anything. He put his hand on the back of her head.

“It was Burr’s,” said Bee. “The rope. I drew Burr’s.”

“I know,” said Hako. “I saw.”

“It was the only rope I knew all of.”

“I know.”

They sat there. The Float rocked, gently, in the bay, held by eleven ropes and Burr’s. A half-mile down the river, in the dusk, the jam stood in the Narrows against the left cliff with its one arm in the rock and its heart going fast in the ice. On the top of the right-hand cliff, very small, five lights. Hyvred said so. Five. Still there. Tired.

Ice was already off the Float.

He’d gone over the side the moment the last rope held. Into the water, to his waist, and up onto the shore among the drowned reeds, with Thea behind him with the lantern. He was standing on the shore now, a little way off, looking down the river at the jam. At the heart.

Bee got up.

She went to the edge of the Float. She held out the bark. Not the side with the rope; the rope was gone. The other side. The heart.

“Ice,” she said.

He turned round.

She held the bark out across the water to him. He came back and took it. He looked at it in the light of Thea’s lantern for a long time.

“I drew it,” said Bee. “From the front. On the way down. I drew what was there.” She swallowed. “It’s not a monster.”

“No,” said Ice.

“It’s frightened.”

“I know.” He was still looking at it. “Ina said.”

“It’s holding everything,” said Bee. “On its own. With all of itself. Because it doesn’t know how to put any of it down.”

He didn’t say anything.

Bee took the other bark out of her pocket. The face. She didn’t give it to him. She just held it up, in the lantern-light, so he could see it. His own face, looking down a river, waiting to find out. And beside it, in his hand, the curled thing in the ice.

He looked from one to the other.

Bee didn’t say it. She didn’t have to. She’d drawn what was there.

Ice looked at the two drawings for a long time. Then he looked up at her, and his face did a thing that Bee would spend the rest of the floor trying to draw, and never quite get.

“Yes,” he said. “It does.”

He gave her back the heart. He kept looking at the face. Then he gave that back too.

“Keep them,” he said. “Both. Draw what happens next.”

And he turned round, and went down the shore in the dusk toward the Narrows, with Thea beside him and the lantern leaning ahead of them toward the jam, toward the five lights on the cliff. Toward the heart.

Not first. Bee saw that. Gabe was already wading out of the water to go after him. Hako was standing up beside her. All down the edge of the Float, people were getting up.

Bee picked up a new stub of charcoal from her pocket. She’d always have a new stub of charcoal. She put the two strips of bark in her coat, one on top of the other.

Then she went over the side of the Float into the water, and went with them.
