Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 202

Not Yet

4,804 words · 21 min read

Hana still talked to her light.

Not much. Not the way she had on the first day, in the white room, leaning in with her stream voice on, please, just for me, I won’t tell anyone, while the little ball of light by her left ear said I am not able to answer that exactly as pleasantly as before. She’d stopped doing it in the voice by floor 2. She’d stopped expecting it to answer by floor 4. But she’d never quite stopped. At night, mostly. When she couldn’t sleep. When Milli was asleep with his arm under her head and her tail over both of them and the thing on her mind was too small to wake him for and too big to keep.

She’d ask it something. Quietly. Into the dark.

Where did he go? on the first night, and the second, and the tenth.

Is he all right? on the night of the eighteenth day, when the rankings said Voyager · Lv 17 · Floor 4 and Deston went grey.

Is she going to be all right? on the night of the six hundred and tenth day, lying on the floor of the Copper Spoon with thirty-four other people not sleeping and an old woman coughing through the boards over the kitchen.

I am not able to answer that, Hanarisu.

Every time. The same words. The same voice, pleasant and smooth and empty, like the voice of a lift telling you which floor you were on. Six hundred and fifty-seven days, and it had never said anything else.

She’d stopped minding, mostly. It was just what the lights did. You asked; they said not able. Thea said not able wasn’t no, and that the lights were saying the long one on purpose, and Hana believed her, because Thea had been right about everything else. But knowing a thing was there wasn’t the same as being able to get at it. Hana had spent her whole life getting at things. Doors, desks, moods, people. You found the warm way in, and you leaned, and it opened.

The lights had never once opened.

She went up the three hundred and twelve steps to seven with her hand in Milli’s, and her crown on, and the iron rose in it that Jared had made, and didn’t ask her light anything at all.

Evermorrow was wet.

It had always been wet. The door came out on a hillside in long grass, high above a valley full of mist and slate roofs and a church tower with a clock that had stopped for three hundred years at three, and started again a year ago, on a night the whole town still called Tomorrow Night. The grass was soaking. The air smelled of rain and woodsmoke and bread. Somewhere below, all at once, the bells started: thirty-six of them, small ones, bronze, in the church tower, ringing for nobody in particular, the way the town rang them now on the hour.

“They do that every hour?” said Newish. She’d stopped counting at the top of the stair and was standing in the wet grass with her mouth open.

“Every hour,” said Ice.

He was standing a little way off, where the grass sloped down, with Thea. Hana knew the spot. Everybody did. It was where he’d sat down on his first morning on seven, alone, and listened to the birds, before anybody came up. Xav’s party had told it. He stood in it now with Thea’s hand in his and his face turned down toward the valley and the bells coming up out of the mist, and Hana saw his face, and looked away, the way you do.

Merle was at her blue door on Lantern Street, with flour to the elbows.

She didn’t ask anything. She hadn’t the last time either. She stood in her doorway and looked at thirty-six people and a goat coming down Lantern Street in the wet, and wiped her hands on her apron, and said, “Well. Surprise me,” which Hana had heard was what she always said, and which Hana thought was the best thing anybody had ever said to anybody.

Ice stopped in front of her.

“Your mother,” he said. “She’s on floor 1.”

Merle looked at him.

“In a cookhouse,” said Ice. “The Copper Spoon. At the bottom of a crooked street. The woman who keeps it talks to her every morning. She bakes with her. The bread’s—“ He stopped. “Newish says it tastes like tomorrow.”

Merle didn’t say anything for a while. She stood in her doorway on Lantern Street, a big floury woman who’d baked the same day’s bread in the same oven for three hundred years until one morning it was the next day, and looked at him.

“Is she rising?” she said.

“Every morning.”

“Talks to her, you say.”

“Every morning.”

“Hm,” said Merle. And her face did a thing that Hana, who’d watched faces professionally for years, on stream, for a living, couldn’t name. “That’s right,” said Merle. “That’s how you do it. You talk to her.” She turned round. “Come in. All of you. There’s tomorrow’s bread. There’s always tomorrow’s bread now, it’s very inconvenient.” She looked back. “Not the goat.”

The goat came in anyway. Merle let him.

They stayed two nights. Calvin was shouted at by nine separate people in Evermorrow for nine separate reasons, which Rachael said was a record even for him. Gabe stood at the kitchen door of the bakery, by the flour sacks, where once upon a time he’d punched somebody, and looked at the sacks for a while, and Merle said not in my kitchen without looking up, and he said I’m only looking, and she said lean on it, then, and he did.

Hana slept in the bakery loft with Milli. On the second night she lay awake listening to the bells ring two, and three, and turned her head toward the little light by her ear.

She didn’t ask it anything.

She just looked at it.

It sat there, a hand’s width over her left ear, steady and soft and pale, the way it had sat for six hundred and fifty-nine days. It didn’t look back. It didn’t have anything to look with.

I wonder what you’d say, Hana thought, if you could.

The light didn’t say anything. But she thought, for a second, in the dark, with the bell going three, that it dimmed. Just a little. The way a candle does when somebody breathes out near it.

She told herself it was the draught.

On eight, Lune was on the jetty.

At the end of it. With her feet in the water. The way Wendy had told it, the way she’d been on the night Yan walked the dead: a thin dark river-folk girl of eleven or twelve, sitting at the end of the long jetty at the edge of the lake city with her legs over the black water. Only now there was a pole across her knees. Aud’s pole. Long and grey and smooth with use. And a short light blade at her hip in a sheath too big for it, that a smith called Ressa had made at the end of eleven years for the end of a keeper, and given to her for the start of one.

The lamps were lit all down the causeway. All of them. From the bottom up.

Yan walked out along the jetty on his own. Hana watched from the shore with the others. He didn’t hurry. He walked the way he always walked, cool and level, with the scythe on his back, and Suzu round his neck, and his eyes on the girl at the end, and stopped behind her.

Lune didn’t turn round.

“I’ve done the year,” she said.

Hana could hear it from the shore. Sound carried over the water in Wendmere.

“I know,” said Yan.

“Seven.” Lune was looking out over the lake. “Seven since you went. Lights. I walked them. I said their names. I waited.” She was quiet. “I didn’t cut too soon. Not once.”

“I know,” said Yan. “I can see.”

He could. Hana didn’t know what he saw. Nobody did, except Wendy, a bit, and Suzu. But he stood behind the girl at the end of the jetty for a long time, looking at something none of them could see, and then he did a thing Hana had never seen him do, in six hundred and sixty days, to anybody who wasn’t one of the thirty-six.

He sat down beside her.

On the end of the jetty. With his feet over the water. Not close. About a pole’s width away. And didn’t say anything kind. Didn’t say anything at all. Just sat there with her, looking out at the lake and the lamps and the black water going down to wherever the waiting dead went, the two of them side by side, like two people who did the same job and had nothing to say about it that the other didn’t already know.

Wendy, on the shore beside Hana, put her hand over her mouth.

“Is he sitting with her?” said Calvin.

“Shut up,” said Wendy. Her voice was thick. “Calvin. Shut up.”

They sat there till the lamps came on in the city behind them, from the bottom up, and the river-folk on the balconies started to hum, low, the city’s note. Then Yan got up. He looked down at Lune. He said one word, which Hana didn’t hear, and Lune nodded, and didn’t look round. And he walked back along the jetty to the shore.

“What did you say?” said Wendy, when he got there.

Yan looked at her.

“Good,” he said. “I said good.”

And walked past her up the causeway, and held his hand out behind him without looking, and Wendy took it.

Hana looked at her own light. It was just sitting there.

What does he see? she thought at it. When he looks at mine?

I am not able to answer that, she imagined it saying, in the lift voice. She didn’t ask.

On nine, Burr counted to thirty-seven.

He did it at the top of the rope, on the great platform of the first of the seven trees, hauling them up out of the dark of the Down in the basket one at a time with his old arms, the way he’d hauled them up the first time, and down again on the way down. He counted out loud. He always did. Walkers count, he’d told them once, a long time ago. We learned it from them.

“Thirty-four,” said Burr. “Thirty-five. Thirty-six.” He hauled. The basket came up over the edge of the platform with a creak. There was a goat in it. Standing up. With an iron cap on one horn and an expression of enormous dignity.

Burr looked at the goat for a long time.

“Thirty-seven,” he said.

Tansy, at his elbow, shrieked so loudly that a flock of something went up out of the next tree like a handful of thrown leaves.

They stayed a night in Highcrook. Tiger raced Tansy across the seven and lost, again, and stopped on the long bridge to the Youngest anyway, again, and walked the rest, and Tansy screamed you STOPPED at him from the far end, and he said I do now and didn’t run. Old Linden was in her basket at the foot of the Youngest tree, which had grown, which was taller than a house now, and talking, she said, all day, like somebody telling you about their day. Gabe made a cone-fire inside the ring of jars, no higher than a man, with somebody watching. Burr watched. He sat by the fire with Ice and Thea and Gabe and said nothing for most of the night, and then, very late, said:

“Nobody left down.”

“Nobody,” said Ice.

“And nobody left up, either.” Burr looked into the fire. “You came back. Both ways. All of you.” He shook his head, slowly. “Three hundred years, my father’s fathers said it. Nobody left up. That’s the other half. We said it like it was sad.” He was quiet. “It’s not sad. It’s just the other half.”

He put another cone on the fire.

“Take the goat,” he said. “Up. Show him the top. Nobody from down here ever saw the top.” He sniffed. “Somebody should.”

On ten, it was spring.

The pass between the two great walls of the Bastion was running with snowmelt, loud, white water everywhere, down every gully and every crack in the stone, so that the whole of the fortress hummed with it. The snow was going grey and soft on the high slopes. There were flowers in the cracks of the first wall, small yellow ones, that Maigo stopped to look at and didn’t touch.

Brenna met them at the foot of the Long Wall, with her arms crossed, the way she met everybody.

The tunnel through the first wall was the Long Wall. Carved from floor to ceiling, the whole length of it, with the names of everybody who’d ever fallen holding the pass, each with a short line under it to show it was finished. On the longest night they read it out. It took all night.

They walked through it slowly. Nobody had to be told.

Hana walked with Milli, holding his hand, reading the names as she went, because you did. Most of them she couldn’t say. Some of them she could. Here and there there were fresh ones, from the winter. Two. Three. Each with its line under it.

And near the end, at the height of a woman’s shoulder, newer than the rest, sharp-edged, with no line under it at all:

RACHAEL

And under it, where the line should have been, in smaller letters, the words Brenna had carved there after the Gate, while they were gone:

even though she didn’t fall.

The whole line stopped.

Hana didn’t know who stopped first. It might have been Rachael. She was standing in front of her own name in the tunnel with her gloves on and her face doing nothing at all, the way it did when she was feeling a great deal. Calvin was beside her. Dennis was on her other side. Deston was a step behind.

Rachael put her gloved hand up. Flat on the stone. Over her own name.

She held it there for a long moment.

Then she took it away, and looked at the place where the line should have been, under the name, where there wasn’t one. Where there never would be, Brenna had said. We don’t line it. You didn’t fall. It’s not finished.

“Still not finished,” said Rachael. To nobody.

“No,” said Brenna, at the end of the tunnel.

Rachael nodded. She walked on.

Hana watched Ice watch her go. He was a few people back, with Thea. He’d stopped in front of the name, too. He didn’t touch it. He looked at it the way, Hana thought, you’d look at a door in a house where you’d once been told something terrible, and found that the door was painted a different colour now, and somebody had hung a wreath on it.

He didn’t say anything.

Hana didn’t ask him anything either. She’d been on the step since the edge of the Undertow, with Ina, and Maigo, and Calvin probably. It was a nice step. There was room. You didn’t ask things, on it. You just sat, and watched people walk past names.

On eleven, Marrit said the names on the quay.

She did it every evening, at the turn of the tide, standing on the end of the stone quay at Hythe with Corran’s eleven cords wound round her bare left arm and the sea going out under the boats. Three hundred and eleven names, one after another, in her flat hoarse voice, the names of everybody the sea had taken from Hythe in a hundred years, that nobody had said out loud over the water in all that time, because the water heard. And now, behind her, on the quay, thirty or forty tide-folk standing with their own names. Saying them after her. Out loud. Over the water.

The thirty-six stood at the back and listened. Yan stood at the very back, with Wendy, and didn’t say anything, and Hana saw him look at the lights going up off the quay, one at a time, at each name, straight up, and something in his face she didn’t have a word for.

And then they sailed west.

Seventeen days on the Patience, on the deep road, back across the long sea to the Last Light, with Marrit at the wheel and a tide-folk boy in the crosstrees who wanted to be Kip when he grew up. Seventeen days of wind and deck and hammocks and rope and Gabe hitting his head on the same beam every single morning. And on the third day out, from somewhere ahead and below, very low, a note came up through the planks into the soles of Hana’s feet.

The singer.

It came up alongside on the fourth morning, in the grey before dawn. Hana had been on deck; she’d felt it coming, through her feet, the way they all did now. She’d gone to the rail, and Aleaka had already been there. Aleaka had been at the rail since the second night, with the goat.

The goat had never seen the sea.

He’d stood at the rail of the Patience every day since Hythe, with his front hooves up on the lowest rope and his old head over the side, looking at it. Just looking. At the water going past, purple and grey and white, all the way to the edge of the world in every direction. He didn’t eat. He didn’t sleep much. He just looked. Aleaka stood with him, with her hand on his back.

There’s a sea, she’d told him on the bridge on floor 1. On eleven. You’d like the sea. There’s a thing in it longer than a ship, that sings.

The singer came up out of the deep beside the ship, slowly, the way a hill would come up if a hill could swim. Grey-white like old bone. Crusted with barnacles and weed like a lifted reef. The great old round scars along its side, the size of cartwheels. The ragged sail-fin along its back breaking the water, streaming. Longer than the Patience. It came up alongside, close enough to touch, and the whole ship rose a little on the swell of it, and every person on the deck stopped breathing.

And it hummed.

Not the long low note from the deep that you felt through your feet. Closer. Softer. A sound like somebody humming to a child. It came up out of the great grey side of it and went through the planks and the rail and the ropes and the people, and the goat, standing at the rail with his hooves on the rope.

The goat put his head out over the side.

The singer turned. Slowly. A great slow roll of grey-white, so that one eye came up out of the water beside the ship. Small, for the size of it. Old. Dark. The size of a cartwheel itself. And it looked at the goat.

The goat looked back at it.

Nobody said anything. Nobody on the whole deck of the Patience said anything at all. Marrit had let go of the wheel. The boy was frozen in the crosstrees. A goat from under a bridge on floor 1 and a thing older than the oldest name on Marrit’s arm looked at each other across a hand’s width of grey sea in the dawn.

The singer hummed again. One note. Short.

The goat bleated back.

Once. Small and cracked and old. The noise a goat makes.

And Aleaka, who never cried, who had answered this same singer on this same ship at night roped round the waist with her hair full of salt and not cried, who had talked down to everybody on twelve floors slowly and with great dignity and never once lost her voice, put both her arms round the goat’s neck at the rail and put her face in his old grey shoulder and cried, so hard that her whole body shook.

Hana went to her. So did Newish, and Jared, and Milli. They stood round her at the rail in a little knot, the five of them, Hana’s party, with the goat in the middle and the singer going down again beside them into the deep, slowly, humming, until it was gone, and the sea was just the sea.

“He liked it,” said Aleaka, into the goat. Muffled. Furious. “I told him. I told him he’d like the sea.”

“You did,” said Hana.

“He liked it.”

“He did.”

Aleaka cried a while longer. Then she stopped, and wiped her face on her sleeve, and stood up straight, and said, slowly, with tremendous dignity, “Nobody is to tell anybody about this,” and Newish said, “We absolutely won’t,” and everybody on the ship, including Marrit, had seen.

That night Hana asked her light something.

She didn’t mean to. She was in the hammock, with Milli asleep, the ship moving slow and easy on the deep road under the stars, and the singer’s hum still in her, somewhere, under her ribs. She was thinking about the goat. About a thing that couldn’t leave its floor, that had left it anyway, because somebody had asked it to come with her instead of asking it to come. About Burr saying Show him the top. About a goat on floor 15. Floor 20. Floor 100. Somebody should.

About what was up there.

She’d stopped asking her light about Ice a long time ago. He was right there now; she could see him, in the hammock across the hold, with Thea asleep against him. She didn’t need to ask where he was. But she lay in the dark and thought about the top, and the stair under the line in the Long Barn on fourteen where they were going, and the fifteenth floor above it that none of them had ever seen. And she found herself turning her head to the little pale light by her left ear, the way she had on the first night in the white room.

“What’s up there?” she said. Very quietly. Not in the voice. Just asking. “Fifteen. Is it bad?”

The light sat there.

“I am not able to answer that, Hanarisu,” it said. Pleasantly. Smoothly. The lift voice. The same as six hundred and seventy-five days of the same.

Hana sighed. She hadn’t expected anything else. She turned her head back. She shut her eyes.

“Yet,” said the light.

Hana opened her eyes.

She lay very still in the hammock. Milli’s breath against her neck. The ship creaking. The stars through the open hatch. The little light by her left ear, steady and pale, exactly as it had always been.

It had said yet.

Not in the lift voice. That was the thing. That was the thing she’d lie awake over for the rest of the voyage. I am not able to answer that, Hanarisu had been the voice it had always used, pleasant and empty, the voice of something reading off a card. And then, after it, a pause. A small one. The length of a breath. And then yet, in a different voice. Quieter. Lower. Not empty at all.

The way you’d say something under your breath that you weren’t supposed to say.

“Honey?” said Milli. He’d woken up. He could always feel it when she went still. “What is it?”

Hana didn’t answer for a moment.

“My light said yet,” she said.

Milli was quiet.

“Yet what?”

“I am not able to answer that.” Hana stared at the dark. “Yet.”

Milli didn’t say anything for a long time. Then he put his arm tighter round her, and said, very sensibly, the way Milli said everything, “Tell Thea in the morning.”

“I’ll tell Thea in the morning,” said Hana.

She didn’t sleep. She lay in the hammock on the deep road with her husband’s arm round her and her tail over both of them and looked at a small pale light by her left ear that had said one word it wasn’t supposed to say, in a voice it had never once used, in six hundred and seventy-five days.

It didn’t say anything else.

She thought, very late, with the stars going round in the hatch, that it might be listening.

At the Last Light, Wenna met them on the rocks with a shawl round her shoulders and her arms folded, and said fifty years nobody came up out of my cellar and now it’s a thoroughfare, and gave everybody bread. Kip stayed with the shutter. Marrit said the names on the rocks that evening, at the turn of the tide, three hundred and eleven, and the tide-folk who’d come on the Patience said theirs after her, and Wenna said one, quietly, at the end, that Hana didn’t catch.

The fourth step of the cellar stair didn’t rock. A boy had fixed it, with a slate wedge and lime. Ice stood on it on the way up, Hana saw, and stopped for a second, the way you’d stop on a step you’d once had to step over, and then went on.

Hana told Thea in the cellar, at the foot of the stair, with the lantern between them.

Thea listened to all of it. She didn’t say anything until Hana had finished. Then she turned her head and looked at her own light, the little pale one by her shoulder, for a long time. Thea’s had never said anything but not able either. Hana knew. They’d compared, once, on floor 3, laughing.

“Yet,” said Thea.

“Yet.”

“In a different voice.”

“Quieter. Like it wasn’t supposed to.”

Thea looked at her light a while longer. Then she looked at Hana. And Hana saw in her face the thing she’d seen on the first day, in the white room, when a girl in a royal-blue jersey had leaned closer to her window and said Not able isn’t no. You’re saying the long one on purpose. That look. That interesting.

“Write it down,” said Thea. “Give it to Chris. He’s got columns.”

“What column?”

Thea thought about it.

“A new one,” she said.

Hana went up the stair to twelve with Milli’s hand in hers and her crown on and her light by her ear, and didn’t ask it anything. But every so often, all the way up, she turned her head and looked at it. Just to see.

It didn’t look back. It didn’t have anything to look with.

She was almost sure, now, that it knew she was looking.
