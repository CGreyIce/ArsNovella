Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 82

Every Bell at Once

3,281 words · 14 min read

He’d rung one bell for twenty-six nights. He’d thought he knew what a bell sounded like.

Thirty-six people pulled on the one.

And the tower sang.

It wasn’t a sound. Not the way the great bell striking the hour was a sound, or the two bells he’d rung with Merle, or the seven. It was everything at once. Thirty-six bells, big and small, bronze and iron and one that looked like silver, all swinging at the same instant on the same beat, and all of them struck. A chord so enormous it didn’t come in through his ears. It came up through his feet, through the stone floor of the ringing chamber, through the soles of his boots and his shins and his spine and his teeth. It filled the room, and the stair, and the whole tower, and went out through the arches of the belfry over the dark valley like a wave going out from a stone thrown in a pond.

The rope in his scarred hand came alive. It pulled back up, hard, as the bell above it swung back. He let it go up through his fingers, and caught it, and held.

All round the circle, thirty-five other people were doing the same. He could see their faces in John’s lights and the amber of Thea’s lantern. Calvin with his mouth wide open. Rachael with her frown so deep it had gone right through to wonder. Gabe beside him, enormous, both hands on his rope, laughing out loud and not hearing himself. Newish with her chin up, eyes shut, counting.

And he could feel them.

That was the thing he hadn’t expected. He’d felt them on floor 4, and 5, and 6. Thirty-five threads, running out of his chest across whole floors, to an island, a pit, a hill road. Fine as hair. Far away.

They weren’t far away now.

Thirty-five threads, and every one of them was in this room. Within ten metres. Within arm’s length, some of them. So close and so thick they weren’t threads any more. They were ropes. Thirty-five of them, from his chest to theirs, taut and humming with the bells. He felt every one. Calvin’s, racing. Gabe’s, huge and warm and right beside him. Thea’s, across the circle. Steady. Amber at the edge. Pointing straight back at him, the way his pointed at her.

None of them was red.

Two, said the great bell, below them.

“ONE,” said Newish.

They pulled again.

Three.

He heard it underneath the chord. Down below, in the clock room, the grinding of iron wheels. The Hour, stepping down out of the clock face, the way it did every night.

Except it wasn’t. Not the way it did every night.

The grinding stopped.

He felt it stop through the floor. Through the stone. The great wheels of the mechanism, halfway through their turn, just stopping, as if somebody had put a hand on them. And the ticking stopped. The slow loud tock, tock, tock that had been the heartbeat of this tower every second of every one of the twenty-six nights he’d slept in it. It just stopped. In the middle of a tock.

“ONE,” said Newish.

Four.

A window came up in front of his face.

It came up in front of everybody’s. He saw them. Thirty-six windows, all at once, small and blue, one in front of every face in the circle, lit by John’s lights. For less than a heartbeat. He almost didn’t read it. He’d spent a hundred and fifty-three days learning to read windows in half a heartbeat, so he did.

[ Irregular ]

And then gone. All thirty-six, at once.

Most of them didn’t notice. They were pulling, and counting, and the bells were roaring, and Calvin was yelling something nobody could hear. But he saw Chris’s face, across the circle, go very still for a second. And he saw Thea’s eyes move, very slightly, to the place in the air where her window had been. And then come back to him.

“It looked,” said Alea, on his shoulder. Very small, under the roar.

“I know.”

“Ice. It looked at all of us. At once.”

“Let it,” he said.

And then a second window. Only in front of him.

[ The Hour · may be struck ]

He had to let go of the rope.

He’d known he would. He’d lain in the middle of this circle for twenty-six nights doing the sums. If every bell had to ring at once, then every rope had to have a hand on it. And if somebody had to go down to the clock room and hit the Hour, then that somebody had to let go of their rope. And then it wasn’t every bell any more. It was thirty-five.

He’d thought about it every night. He’d never found the answer. He’d told himself he’d find it on the night.

Five.

He hadn’t found it.

He stood in the circle with his scarred hand on the rope and the tower singing round him, and the window saying may be struck. And he didn’t know how to let go.

“Go on, then.”

A voice behind him. At his shoulder.

He turned his head.

Merle was standing there. Right behind him. She’d come in off the top step of the stair, into the ringing chamber, in her apron, with flour still on her forearms. And she was reaching past him. For his rope.

She put her hands on it. Both of them. Above his, on the soft woollen grip.

“I rang one with you,” said Merle. She had to shout it, right by his ear, over the bells. “On the hundred-and-thirty-first night. I know how. You showed me.” Her floury hands closed on the grip. “Go on. I’ve got it.”

He stared at her.

“It said every bell,” shouted Merle. “It didn’t say every player. It said every bell.” Her eyes were very bright. “Your tower counted thirty-six of you. It didn’t count me.”

Six.

“ONE,” said Newish.

Merle pulled.

The bell above them swung, and rang, and the chord didn’t break. Thirty-six bells. Thirty-five players and a baker.

He let go.

He took the stair down to the clock room four steps at a time, with the lamp shield on his arm and his sword in his hand, and the whole tower singing round him.

Seven.

The clock room was full of moonlight, and stillness, and the Hour.

It was halfway out of the clock face. The way it was every night, at this point. Tall, brass and shadow, long-jointed like a mechanism, with its blank round dial of a face. Its long pointed right arm reaching out toward the great iron wheel in the middle of the floor. But it wasn’t moving.

It was stuck. Halfway down out of the clock face, one leg on the floor and one still in the glass. And its dial wasn’t turned toward the wheel. It was turned up. Toward the ceiling. Toward the bells. As if the sound of thirty-six bells at once was a hand holding it in place.

And it was struggling.

He could see it. The brass of it straining, every joint. The long arm shaking, reaching for the wheel, a few inches at a time. The dial tilting, very slowly, from the ceiling back down toward the wheel. It was fighting the sound. And it was winning, a little at a time.

Eight.

He went between it and the wheel.

He didn’t brace. He didn’t plant his feet. He stood in front of the great iron wheel with his weight forward and his body moving, very slightly, all the time. Leaning. Like a kite on a line. Like Pell on the edge of a cliff.

The Hour’s dial came down. All the way. And looked at him.

For the first time in twenty-six nights, it looked at him.

He felt it. The way you feel somebody look at you across a room. Something behind the blank dial, behind the brass, behind the clock. Something a very long way away, looking out through a hole in the world, at a man in a white cloak with a lamp shield on his arm, standing in front of a wheel.

“Hello,” said Ice.

The Hour swung.

The long arm came at him like a scythe. Pointed and straight, the whole length of the clock room, a minute hand made into a blade. Fast. Faster than anything that slow-looking should be.

He took it on the shield.

He didn’t block. He leaned. He angled the lamp shield into the swing the way he’d angled Otta’s barrel lid into the Wanting’s edges. So that the arm didn’t hit it so much as meet it, and go along it, and off.

The pointed end hit the dark red rim.

He heard it. The sound of brass on Kiln iron. A long high shriek, sparks going off the rim in a shower, orange in the moonlight. And he felt the force of it go up his arm and into his shoulder and through his whole body, enough to throw a man across the room.

The shield held.

It didn’t bend. It didn’t crack. The rim didn’t so much as dent. The long arm slid off it and went past him and hit the stone wall of the clock room, and stone came off the wall in a cloud.

He stood there in the dust with the shield on his arm, not a mark on it, and something went through him that had nothing to do with the fight.

A shield that doesn’t break.

Gabe had said that. The first time. On floor 1, in Hearthstead, at a smith’s forge, making nails, with his hands black to the wrist. He’d said it the way you say a wish. One day I’m going to make a shield that doesn’t break. Ice had been standing right beside him. And he’d thought about it for years afterwards. About the other shield, the first time. The one Gabe had made on floor 61 from a dragon’s shoulder plate. The one that had broken at the end, on the hundredth floor, and left him holding with his arm.

It didn’t break till the end, though. It held the whole way up.

This one hadn’t broken.

“Ice,” said Alea.

“I know.”

Nine.

The Hour came at him again. Both arms, this time.

The long one, sweeping. And the short one. The left arm, half the length of the right, blunt and heavy at the end, like an hour hand. It came in low, jabbing, at his ribs, under the shield.

He went with the long one. Leaned into it, took it on the rim, let it slide. And the short one came in under, and he didn’t have anything for it.

It hit him in the side.

He felt something go. A rib, maybe two. The breath went out of him all at once. He went sideways, across the clock room floor, and hit the iron wheel with his back, hard enough to make it ring.

Mend Self. The ember came, warm, from behind his breastbone. It hurt. It always hurt, the closing. He felt the ribs start to knit and gritted his teeth and let it.

And then something else came.

It came down the threads.

Thirty-five of them, from his chest, up through the stone ceiling of the clock room to the ringing chamber above. To thirty-five people with their hands on ropes. And down them, all at once, came warmth. Gold. Not his own. Deston’s. What one of you has, all of you may share. He felt Deston up there in the circle, with his hands on his rope and the gold coming out of his palms anyway. Down the threads, down through the stone. Into him. And with it, them. All of them. Rachael’s cold clean blue, closing his ribs faster than the ember could. Xola’s water. Malcolm’s two blessings, then two more, then two more, on the lamp shield. Calvin’s speed. Gabe’s weight, steady, behind him like a wall.

He wasn’t holding on his own.

He hadn’t held on his own once since the white room, he realised. He’d just never been close enough to feel it.

He came up off the wheel.

Ten.

The Hour’s dial had changed.

He saw it as he came up. The blank round face wasn’t blank any more. There were numbers on it now. Twelve of them, faint, round the edge, the way there are on a clock. And two hands, in the middle, very thin, very dark. The long one and the short one. Pointing.

At eleven.

And moving.

It was counting down. With the strokes. Every time the great bell struck, the hands on its dial moved. And when they got to twelve—

Twelve, said a voice in the back of his head that sounded like Merle. Twelfth stroke, and you blink, and it’s morning. You don’t get the night. You never get the night.

The Hour went for the wheel.

It didn’t go for him. It went past him. It had stopped bothering with him. It went for the great iron wheel with its long arm out, reaching, the way it did every night. Two strokes left. If it got its arm to the wheel and pushed—

He went between them again. Leaning. The shield up. The long arm came down and he took it on the rim, sparks, the shriek of brass on Kiln iron. And the short arm came in, low, at his ribs. And this time he didn’t go with the long one.

He let it go past.

Not on the shield. Past his head. So close he felt the wind of it in his hair, and the pointed end took a lock of black and teal with it. He’d done that on the crown of the Spindle, with the bird. Wait. Then move once. He’d done it at the bottom of a stoop. And in a bowl of glass, with a hole in the air.

Eleven.

He moved once.

Inside both arms. Past the long one, under the short one, close in to the brass body of the thing, where neither arm could reach him. Right up against its chest, where its dial-face looked down at him with its two thin dark hands pointing at nearly twelve.

The middle of the dial. Where the hands met. Where it had come out of the clock face, every night, for a hundred and fifty-three nights.

He put his sword through it.

The Hour stopped.

All of it. At once. The long arm, mid-swing. The short arm, mid-jab. Every joint. It stood there in the moonlight in the clock room, with his sword through the middle of its dial, and didn’t move.

The two thin hands on its face had stopped too. A hair’s breadth from twelve.

He looked up at it.

The dial looked down at him. And for one second, behind the brass, behind the glass, behind the clock, he felt it again. Something a very long way away, looking at him. Not angry. Not anything. Just looking. The way you’d look at a word you didn’t understand, written in a place you hadn’t expected.

Then the dial cracked.

Right across. From the hilt of his sword to the edge. A long fine line, like a crack across ice. And then another. And then the whole face of it was a web of them, white, spreading. And the Hour came apart.

Not loud. Not like the Wanting, like a window breaking. Quietly. The way a very old clock comes apart when you open the back of it and the springs have been tight too long. Brass falling, piece by piece, joint by joint, to the stone floor of the clock room in a slow heap. Gears. Springs. A long pointed arm. A short blunt one. And last, the dial, cracked through, with its two thin hands still pointing a hair from twelve, falling face up onto the floor at his feet.

Above him, in the belfry, the great bell struck.

Twelve.

He stood in the clock room with his sword in his hand and waited for the world to move.

It didn’t.

The moonlight stayed moonlight.

That was the first thing. He was standing in the clock room, and the light coming in through the great round window of the clock face was silver, and soft, and it stayed. It didn’t go white. It didn’t go grey, or gold. The stars he could see through the old thick glass stayed exactly where they were. Nobody blew them out.

And behind him, very slowly, the great iron wheel began to turn.

Forward.

He heard the ticking start again. Tock. The slow loud heartbeat of the tower. Tock. And through the glass of the clock face, he saw the great iron hands outside, on the face of the tower above the square, move. One minute.

Past twelve.

Tock.

[ The Hour · defeated ]

[ Floor 7 has been cleared · thirty-six Players ]

He read it.

He read it again.

Thirty-six Players.

On floor 2, on the Mere, the first time all of them had raided anything together, the window had said thirty-five Players. He hadn’t been there. He’d read it in World weeks afterwards, lying on the floor of an empty room in the Red City while Alea read him the logs. Floor Guardian defeated · thirty-five Players. And he’d lain there in the dark and looked at the number and not been able to breathe.

Thirty-six.

Above him, the bells were still ringing. Not at once any more. Ragged. Joyful. Thirty-five people and a baker, still pulling, not able to stop, the chord coming apart into a great tumbling clamour of every bell in the tower going any old how. And under it, and over it, voices. Shouting. Laughing. Somebody screaming IT’S STILL DARK, over and over, and he thought it was Calvin and then realised it was Hyvred.

And above all of it, through the stone, one voice he knew better than any of them.

Merle. In the ringing chamber. Not counting. Not shouting. Just saying it, over and over, the way you say a thing to make it real.

“It’s still night. It’s still night. It’s still night.”

He sat down on the floor of the clock room, among the brass, with the cracked dial at his feet and the lamp shield on his arm.

Midnight had come. And after it, for the first time in a hundred and fifty-three days, there was another minute.

“Ice,” said Alea. She was sitting on his knee. She was shaking. He could feel it. “Ice. It’s one minute past.”

“I know.”

“It’s one minute past midnight.”

“I know, Alea.”

He looked up at the clock face, at the moon behind it, at the great iron hands. They had moved one minute, and then, as he watched, another.

Tock.

He started to laugh.
