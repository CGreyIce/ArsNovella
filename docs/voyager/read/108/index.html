Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 108

Forty Mouths

4,316 words · 19 min read

The Bastion didn’t have a market, and it was driving Dennis slowly out of his mind.

He’d looked. On the first day. Of course he had. Every floor, he found the market before he found the bed: the ghost market on floor 4 with the woman who wrote on a slate, the fish stalls on the jetties of Wendmere, the nut-sellers on the bridges of Highcrook who’d take a story for a bag of honey-pears if you told it well. He’d found the cheesemonger on floor 7 in about four minutes. He could smell a market. Coppers changing hands, somebody shouting a price, somebody shouting a lower one.

The Bastion didn’t smell of anything but snow and iron and the Breath coming up through the stones.

There were no stalls. No prices. No coin, even; he’d asked, and Brenna had looked at him as if he’d asked whether they kept their water in a sieve. There were the long halls on the fourth level, where everybody ate together at the same tables off the same pots, soldiers and smiths and children and old women. There were the stores under the long halls, where everything was kept: grain, salt meat, wool, oil, iron. And there was a man called Hesk, who kept the stores, and gave you what you needed.

“Gave,” said Dennis, on the third day, standing in the stores with his hands spread. “He just gave. Calvin needed a new pair of boots and he just gave him boots.”

“That’s nice,” said Rachael, not looking up from the bandage she was rolling.

“It’s not nice, it’s insane. What’s the boot worth? What’s Calvin worth? How does he know Calvin’s not going to walk off with the boots and never come back?”

“Where would Calvin go, Dennis? It’s a pass.”

“That’s not the point.” Dennis sat down on a barrel. “There’s no exchange. You can’t tell what anything’s worth if nobody ever trades it. How do you know what’s fair?”

Rachael finished the bandage and started another. She was sitting on the step outside the house for the wounded, on the third level, with a basket of linen in her lap, in the cold sun. She worked there now. Every day, since the first night. She’d stopped coming to meals with the rest of them. Dennis brought her food.

“It’s a wall,” she said. “You don’t trade with a wall. You just stand in it.”

“Azac says that.”

“Azac’s right.” She looked at him then. Just for a second. “Did you bring the blankets?”

He’d brought the blankets.

That was what he’d ended up doing. On a floor with no market, the treasurer of Deston’s party had turned into a sort of supply sergeant, and he hadn’t decided to, any more than he’d decided to find the cheesemonger.

It had started with the house for the wounded. There weren’t enough blankets. There never were, Ingrith said, in winter; the cold came off the cliff behind the house and through the walls, and the Breath came up through the floor, and between the two of them the beds were always either too hot or too cold. So Dennis had gone to Hesk. And Hesk had looked at him with his grey eyes and said the wool was for the wall, first, and the wounded after, and there wasn’t enough this year for both.

And Dennis, who had haggled a cheesemonger down from eight coppers to six and a half with chutney, had stood in the stores under the long halls and felt, for the first time in his life, completely useless. Because there was nothing to haggle with. Hesk didn’t want anything. Nobody here wanted anything. They just had what they had, and shared it, and it wasn’t enough.

So he’d gone back to the barracks and turned out every pack the thirty-six of them had. Everything they’d carried up from floor 1. Two hundred and fifty days of loot. Sawyer shell from floor 9, thick as plate, that Salty said would make good shield-facing. Eel oil from Wendmere that burned clean and didn’t smoke. A bale of bough-folk rope, the grey bark-fibre that was stronger than anything. Shardback glass from floor 6. A sack of river-folk dried fish that Hako had been saving for some reason nobody understood.

And he’d taken it all to Hesk and put it down on the floor of the stores and said: this is for the wall. And I’d like some wool for the wounded. If that’s all right.

Hesk had looked at it for a long time. Then he’d looked at Dennis.

That’s not trading, Hesk had said. That’s giving.

I know, Dennis had said. I’m learning.

He’d got forty blankets. Hesk had brought them up to the house for the wounded himself, the next morning, on a handcart, and helped carry them in. And Ingrith had stood in the door and watched them come in, one after another, and said nothing, and then put her small gentle hand on Dennis’s arm for a long time.

He’d never been so pleased with anything in his life. He’d told Calvin about it four times. Calvin had told him to stop.

He noticed Rachael’s hands on the two hundred and fifty-fifth day.

He’d brought her lunch. Bread and salt meat and an onion Declan had roasted in the long-hall ovens with something that made it taste of the honey-pears from floor 9. He found her inside, for once, at the far end of the long room, kneeling by a bed with her back to him. A soldier with a bandaged foot. And her hands were on the foot, flat, the way she put them on everything she was going to heal, and she was very still.

He waited. He’d learned to, with Rachael.

When she took her hands away, she sat back on her heels. And then, quickly, before she stood up, she put them in her armpits. Both of them. Tucked in, the way you do when you’ve been out in the snow without gloves.

It was warm in there. The Breath came up through the floor.

“Rach,” said Dennis.

She stood up and turned round and saw him, and her face went flat.

“Lunch,” said Dennis. He held it out.

“Put it on the step.”

“What’s wrong with your hands?”

“Nothing’s wrong with my hands.”

“You’ve got them in your armpits. It’s like a sauna in here.”

Rachael looked at him for a long moment. Then she took her hands out. Slowly. And held them up. Not to show him. Just to look at them herself.

The tips of her fingers were white.

Not frostbite white. Not the hard waxy candle-end white of the soldiers in the beds. Just pale. Paler than the rest of her hand, from the first knuckle up, all ten fingers, as if she’d been holding snow. And as he watched, very slowly, they started to go pink again.

“What did you do?” said Dennis.

“I’m working on something,” said Rachael.

“What?”

“Something.” She put her hands back in her armpits. “Mind your own business, Dennis.”

“You’re my business. You’re literally in my party. You’re the healer. If your hands don’t work—“

“They work.”

“They’re white.”

“They’re going pink,” said Rachael. “Look. Pink. It wears off. I’m fine.” She came past him to the door and took the lunch out of his hands on the way. “I’ll tell you when I’ve got it. Not before. You’d only fuss.”

“I would not fuss—“

She was already on the step, eating the onion, with her back to him.

He stood in the doorway of the house for the wounded and looked at her back for a long time. At the hands, wrapped round the bread now, the fingertips going slowly pink in the cold sun. At the way she sat. Hunched. The way she’d sat on the jetty on floor 8 after the Templar trial, the one time anybody had seen her sit down when she didn’t have to.

Rachael didn’t tell people things. Not until they were done. She’d told the whole group about the echo on floor 5 and she’d been carrying it since floor 1. On her own. Every wound she ever closed, felt again in her own body, in the same place, and never said.

He’d find out. He always did. Eventually.

He went to tell Calvin about it. Calvin told him to stop worrying. He worried.

The Rimed came over the cliffs on the two hundred and fifty-seventh night.

Nobody had known they could.

Afterwards, everybody said they should have. The hold-folk had been fighting them for nine hundred years, and the Rimed had come at the fifth wall every winter for nine hundred years, because the fifth wall was across the throat of the pass and there was nowhere else to come. The cliffs on either side went up a thousand metres into the snow and the black rock. Nothing climbed them. Nothing ever had.

And then thirty-six climbers came up a stair, and stood in the line on the fifth wall, and the line held better than it had held in a hundred years. Ten nights. Fourteen. The Rimed came and couldn’t find a gap. Azac told them stories. Declan stood with Gabe’s warm shield across his side. Nobody died after Tamm.

And on the fourteenth night, the Rimed didn’t come at the fifth wall at all.

Dennis was in the stores under the long halls when the bells went. Talking Hesk out of a sack of salt for the house for the wounded, which wasn’t really talking anybody out of anything, because Hesk had already said yes, but Dennis liked to make a thing of it. And the bells went. Not the fifth-wall bell, the deep one at the top of the pass that they’d heard every night for two weeks. A different bell. Higher. Closer. Right overhead.

Hesk dropped the sack.

His face went a colour Dennis hadn’t seen on any hold-folk face, not even Brenna’s on the night Tamm died.

“That’s the fourth,” said Hesk. “That’s the fourth. That’s never—“

Somebody was screaming in the street outside.

Dennis went up the stair from the stores three at a time and out into the street and stopped.

It was snowing. Hard. Sideways. And through it, up the side of the pass, on the cliff above the fourth level, he could see them. Coming down. Not climbing the wall. Coming down the cliff. Head first. Like spiders. Long grey-white shapes in the old armour, their ice fingers in the cracks of the rock, coming down the face of the mountain out of the dark above the city, where nothing had ever come from.

Twenty. Thirty. More. Dropping off the bottom of the cliff onto the roofs of the fourth level, and the long halls, and the stables, and the street.

Behind the fifth wall. Behind the line. Where there wasn’t a line.

The hold-folk were coming out of the long halls with their shields, half-dressed, shouting. Somebody was ringing the bell overhead over and over. A Rimed came off the roof of the stables right in front of him and landed in the street in a burst of snow, and a hold-folk woman with a spear went straight at it, and Dennis heard the crack of the black stone. Then another one came down beside it.

And he thought, very clearly, the way you do in the half second before something:

The house for the wounded is on the third level. Against the cliff.

Right underneath.

He ran.

He didn’t remember most of it. Afterwards he tried, and couldn’t. The street. The snow. The steep stair down from the fourth level to the third, cut into the rock, with people coming up it the other way. Shouting. The bell. A Rimed on the roof of a house to his left, coming down the wall of it head first, and a man with a shield under it. Somebody grabbing at his arm, climber, the wall, they need—and him pulling free.

He came round the corner of the stair onto the third level, into the street where the house for the wounded was, against the cliff, and saw it.

There were three of them. In the street. Outside the long low building with the light coming out of its windows. And one of them was at the door.

And the door was open. And in the doorway, kneeling, with her back to the street and her hands on somebody on the floor, was Rachael.

She wasn’t running. Of course she wasn’t. She was a healer. Whoever was on the floor in the doorway was hurt and she had her hands on them, and the Rimed was behind her, two metres behind her, coming, its long arm reaching down, the old gauntlet, the ice fingers spread, and she knew it was there. Dennis could see she knew. Her back had gone stiff. And she didn’t take her hands off whoever it was. She didn’t move at all.

Rachael doesn’t run.

Dennis didn’t think. He did what he’d done on floor 8, in a white room that was a trial, with a white thing hunting sound and everybody else gone silent. He didn’t think about it. It came up out of him the way shouting comes up out of you.

Double.

What you show them is somewhere it isn’t. They believe it for as long as you do.

He ran at it. Shouting. And forty of him ran at it with him.

He’d made forty once before. On floor 8, in the white room, and it had nearly put him on the floor. He made forty now. He didn’t count them. He just felt them go out of him, all at once, every one of them him, running, shouting. Down the street from every side. Out of the stair behind him. Off the roof of the house opposite. Round the corner of the cliff. Forty Dennises in the snow, all running at the Rimed in the doorway, all shouting the same thing at the tops of their voices:

“RACHAEL—“

The Rimed turned.

It turned its hollow helmet away from the doorway, toward the sound. Toward forty men running at it out of the snow from every direction. And swung. At the nearest one. Its long arm going through him, and him going to smoke, and another one behind him, and it swung at that one, and the next. Turning. Round and round in the street. Swinging at Dennises. And they kept coming, shouting, because he believed in every single one of them. He believed in them so hard his head hurt. He believed in them the way you believe in the floor under your feet.

For as long as you do.

And the real one ran past them all, through the smoke of his own ghosts, to the door.

The other two Rimed had turned too. One of them was going for the nearest Dennis. The other one was standing in the street with its helmet tipped back, turning slowly, as if it couldn’t work out where all the noise was coming from.

And the one at the door had stopped swinging.

It had worked it out. Dennis saw it work it out. Its hollow helmet turned, slowly, away from the ghosts. Back to the door. Back to the woman kneeling in it with her hands on somebody on the floor. And its arm came down again.

He got there first.

He didn’t have a shield. He didn’t have a sword. He was a mage; he had a staff, and he’d left it in the stores leaning against a barrel. He had his arms. He put his left one up. Between the ice fingers and the back of Rachael’s neck. Like you’d put your arm up to stop a door swinging into somebody’s face.

And it closed on his forearm.

It didn’t hurt.

That was the first thing. He’d been waiting for it to hurt and it didn’t. It was just cold. So cold it wasn’t cold any more. It was something else. A sort of white silence, going into his arm from where the fingers were, wrist to elbow, all the way round, and through, and down to the bone, and in. As if somebody had poured winter into him through a funnel.

He heard himself make a sound.

And Rachael turned round.

She was still kneeling. She turned her head over her shoulder and saw him. Saw his arm. Saw the long ice fingers closed round it, and the white going out from under them up his forearm, and his face. And her face did something Dennis had never seen it do in all the years he’d known her. Not once. Not on Earth, not on any floor.

It broke.

“DENNIS—“

Something hit the Rimed from the side.

Hard. A shield. A big one, grey, Breath iron, coming out of the snow with somebody very large behind it. Azac. Azac, with the shield going into the Rimed’s ribs and the thing staggering, and its fingers coming off Dennis’s arm all at once like a hand letting go of a rope. And behind Azac, Calvin, fast, faster than anything, talking, look at my back, look at my back, and a whole crowd of hold-folk coming down the stair behind him in his wind. And Calvin went under the Rimed’s other arm with both daggers into the hollow breastplate, and there was a crack, deep inside.

It came apart. All over the doorway. Snow and ice and old iron, on Dennis and Rachael and whoever was on the floor between them.

Dennis let the ghosts go.

All forty. All at once. He felt them go out of him like a breath, and the street was suddenly empty of Dennises and full of hold-folk with spears, and the other two Rimed going down under them, cracking.

He sat down in the doorway of the house for the wounded.

He didn’t decide to. His legs did it. He sat down in the snow and the broken ice with his back against the doorframe, and looked at his left arm.

It was white. From the wrist to the elbow. All the way round. Not pale. White. Hard and waxy, like a candle. He tried to move his fingers.

They didn’t move.

Rachael had his arm in her lap before he’d finished looking at it.

She had both her hands on it. Flat. One on the wrist and one on the forearm, on the white. And her face had gone back to what it usually was, flat and hard and furious, except that it hadn’t, quite. There was something under it that hadn’t gone back.

He felt her healing go in. Warm. The way it always went in. Looking for something to agree with.

He felt it not find anything.

“Agree with it,” said Rachael. Under her breath. Not to him. “Agree with it, agree with it—“

Her hands stayed on his arm. For a long time. And her fingertips, where they touched the white, started to go white too.

“Rach,” said Dennis.

“Shut up.”

“Rach. Your hands.”

“I know about my hands.”

Ingrith came out of the house. She knelt beside them in the snow, small and old, and looked at Dennis’s arm for a long time with her gentle face. She didn’t touch it.

“It’ll climb,” she said. Very quietly. To Rachael, not to him. “Like the others. Past the elbow by midnight. The shoulder by morning.” She paused. “If it reaches the heart—“

“I know,” said Rachael.

“It has to come off,” said Ingrith. “At the elbow. Before it climbs. Tonight. I’m sorry.”

“No,” said Rachael.

Ingrith looked at her.

“No,” said Rachael again. “Nobody’s taking anything off. Not tonight. Not ever.”

“There’s nothing else,” said Ingrith gently. “You’ve tried. You’ve tried every day for twelve days. On every bed in there. It doesn’t heal. Not with anything. You know it doesn’t.”

“I know it doesn’t heal,” said Rachael.

She was looking at her own hands. On Dennis’s arm. At the fingertips, gone white to the first knuckle.

“I’m not going to heal it,” she said.

Running feet. Lots of them. Down the stair from the fourth level, through the snow.

Deston first. He came round the corner at a run, with his staff and his face grey, and the gold threads already coming out of him before he’d stopped, going out to the five of them, to Dennis, Rachael, Calvin, Azac. Link. Dennis felt it land, warm, in the middle of his chest. It didn’t do anything to his arm. He hadn’t thought it would. It was just Deston, holding on.

“Dennis—“

“I’m fine,” said Dennis. “I’m completely fine. Look. It’s just white. It’s very on-trend.”

Deston crouched down in front of him and looked at the arm and didn’t say anything. His jaw went tight.

And behind Deston, somebody else came round the corner. Fast. Faster than Dennis had ever seen him move. Faster than in the clock room on floor 7. In the snow, with the lamp shield on his arm and his sword still out and his face—

Declan stopped.

He stopped dead, five metres off, in the middle of the street. And looked at them. At the doorway. At Rachael, kneeling, with Dennis’s arm in her lap. At Dennis, sitting against the doorframe in the broken ice. At the arm. At the white.

Dennis had seen that face once before. On the end of a jetty, on floor 8, after the Templar trial. Looking at Calvin’s arms. You’ve got two good arms, Calvin. As if he’d never seen two of them before.

He was looking at Dennis’s arm like that now. Except it wasn’t a good arm.

“Dec,” said Deston. Quietly.

Declan didn’t answer. He came the rest of the way. Slowly. And crouched down in the snow next to Deston, in front of Dennis, and looked at him. Not at the arm. At him. At his face.

“You ran to her,” said Declan.

His voice was very strange. Dennis had never heard it like that. Like somebody reading something off a wall.

“Course I ran to her,” said Dennis. “She was on her own. She wasn’t going to move. She never moves, she’s Rachael—“

“You ran to her,” said Declan again. “Shouting.”

“Forty of me. Shouting.” Dennis tried to grin. It went a bit wrong. “It worked, though. Mostly. It was very good. You should have seen it. I was everywhere.”

Declan didn’t say anything.

He sat down. In the snow. Just sat down, the way Dennis’s own legs had sat him down a minute before. In the street, in the middle of the third level of the Bastion, with the bells still going overhead and the hold-folk running past and the snow coming down sideways. He put his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands and stayed there.

Dennis looked at Deston. Deston was looking at Declan. And Deston’s face had gone very still, the way it went when he was watching something he didn’t understand and was deciding not to ask about it yet.

Calvin crouched down on Dennis’s other side.

“Oh my days,” said Calvin, very quietly, looking at the arm. And then, for once, nothing else.

Rachael hadn’t looked up. She hadn’t looked at any of them. She was looking at her own hands on Dennis’s arm, at the white creeping up her fingers, and her face was very calm. The calm it went when she’d decided something.

“Give me an hour,” she said.

“Rach—“

“An hour, Dest.” She looked up, at last. At Deston. Not at Dennis. “Nobody takes it off. Nobody does anything. One hour.” She swallowed. “I think I can do something. I think I’ve been working out how for twelve days and I just didn’t have—“ She stopped. “I just didn’t want to have to find out on him.”

Nobody said anything.

Dennis looked at her. At her hands. At the white going up her fingers, slowly, from where they touched his arm.

“Rach,” he said. “What are you going to do?”

Rachael looked at him. For the first time since she’d turned round in the doorway with her face breaking.

“Carry it,” she said.

And then she looked at something none of them could see. A window, Dennis thought. Small and blue, in front of her face, in the snow. He saw her read it. He saw her read it twice. He saw her mouth go tight.

“Okay,” said Rachael. To the window. “Okay. The hard one.”
