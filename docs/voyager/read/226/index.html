Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 226

Still Thirty

5,656 words · 25 min read

Write what you saw. Keep it true.

Cursiffe had the line on the inside cover of the ink book now. He’d copied it there on floor 5 from the first page of a stone book in a stone scribe’s lap, and every time he opened the book it was the first thing he saw, and every time he wrote anything he wrote it under that.

He had a lot of things under it. Two years of things. Monsters and doors and windows and what the ink did. Fourteen names and one line each from a city of stilts on eight. Every number Ice had said on the Barn Field on fourteen. And on a page near the middle, from floor 13, from the city archive in Tell, copied so carefully it had taken him most of a night, one line.

Not his words. Not even his letters. He’d copied the letters exactly, the shapes of them, the way you’d copy a drawing, because the line had been written by somebody else’s hand three hundred and twelve years before, and the hand mattered. The way the Ts were crossed, high and a bit to the right, as if she was always in a hurry. The tail on the R, long, going down under the next letter. The full stop, pressed in so hard it had gone through the page.

Thirty. Nobody left down. — Ruth.

In English. Letters from home. In a book in a city that had never seen them, on a page nobody in that city could read.

He’d copied it, and looked at it for a long time, and written underneath, in his own hand: The ones before. One name. A woman. She wrote like somebody who knew she had to be quick and wanted it to stay anyway.

He’d never seen that hand again.

He sat on the step of the house next to Ottilie’s at the top of the alley, with the ink book open on his knee to that page, and listened to Tiger tell Ice about the wall. Numbers. Ours. Thirty. Over and over. And at the bottom it says watch each other. In English. And it’s signed R.

He didn’t need to look at the page. He knew every letter on it.

He looked anyway.

When Ice said his name, he’d already gone white. He could feel it. He’d felt the blood go out of his face on signed R, like water going out of a basin.

“I know the hand,” said Cursiffe. Before anybody asked. His voice came out very level. “If it’s hers, I’ll know it.”

They went in the next afternoon.

Five of them. Tiger first, because Tiger was always first and because it was Tiger’s rope and Tiger’s line through the Loose, and he said he could feel it still, the way he’d come, even though it wasn’t there any more. Not the streets. The rope. Where it went. It’s like a path you’ve walked in snow. Then Cursiffe. Then Yan, with Working On It over his shoulder, because Yan could see lights, and there’d be one stray in the courtyard and possibly, Yan said with his head on one side, other things. Then Wendy, because wherever Yan went Wendy went with her hand on the back of his coat, and nobody had ever once argued with that.

And Ice held the end of the rope.

Cursiffe had thought he’d want to come in. Cursiffe had watched him for two years, and written it all down, and he’d thought: this is the one. The ones before. He’ll want to see. Everybody had thought it. He’d seen them think it, round the top of the alley, at noon, when Tiger tied the knot round his waist and held out the end.

Ice took the end. He wrapped it round his wrist.

“I’ll hold it,” said Ice.

Nobody said anything. Higu looked at him for a long moment. Thea didn’t look at him at all; she just moved half a step closer, so her shoulder was against his.

“Somebody has to hold this end,” said Ice. Quietly. “I’ve done it once. I’m getting good at it.” He looked at Cursiffe. “Bring it back. Whatever’s there. Write it down.”

“I will.”

“All of it.”

“I always do.”

Ice nodded. Then, after a moment, as if it had only just occurred to him:

“Can you show me? After. With your book. The way you can.”

Living Manuscript. What’s recorded can be read back and shared. Cursiffe had only ever done it a handful of times, and never because anybody asked. He’d never been asked. Nobody asked him things; they asked him to write them down.

“Yes,” said Cursiffe. “If you want.”

“I want,” said Ice.

Salty lifted Ressa’s hammer over Pask’s anvil.

THUD.

They went in.

He’d known it from Tiger’s telling. He hadn’t known it.

The Loose was a sentence somebody kept rewriting while you read it. That was what Cursiffe would put in the ink book, that night, and cross out, and put again. You read a line of it, a wall, a door, a window, and by the time your eye got to the end of the line the beginning had changed. Different words. Same page. You couldn’t hold it in your head. It slid.

Except on the beat.

THUD. And the whole page held still. Just for a breath. Like a printer’s block coming down. And in that breath you could read it. A wall, a door, a corner. And then the gap, and the block lifting, and the words sliding again.

They walked on the beat. Tiger in front with his hand going ahead of him, feeling for the line that wasn’t there. Cursiffe behind with the ink book shut and held against his chest and his free hand on the back of Tiger’s belt.

The slate-dogs came twice. The first time Tiger killed it, standing still, on the THUD, the way he’d told them. The second time there were three, out of three doors at once, and Wendy had one with an arrow through the thin place behind the head before Cursiffe had seen it move, and Yan stepped past Cursiffe without a word and put the inside edge of Working On It through the second, low, the slow sweep he used at the lip on eight, and it cracked in two like a dropped slate.

The third came at Cursiffe.

He didn’t have a blade. He’d never been good with one. He had a pen and a book and a bottle of Mott’s eel-oil ink in his coat, warm against his chest the way he’d kept it warm since floor 2.

He had the pen out before he’d thought.

He wrote on the cobbles. On the wet stone, in front of the slate-dog, in the half breath before the THUD. One word. The one he’d written on floor 1 in the mud in front of a Woolback, and on floor 2 in the snow in front of five hounds. Square capitals. Fast.

STAY.

The slate-dog hit the word and stopped.

Not on the beat. In the gap. With the walls sliding all round them. It stopped on the wet word on the cobbles as if it had hit a wall, and stood there, frozen, its flat grey jaws a hand’s width from Cursiffe’s knee.

Writ. What you write, holds. For as long as the ink is wet.

THUD.

Tiger’s blade went into the back of its neck.

It cracked. Fell. Lay on the cobbles across his word, just stone.

Cursiffe looked down at the word. STAY. Wet, black, shining. Already starting to go dull at the edges as the eel-oil soaked into the stone and dried.

He looked at the window. Then at the word, drying.

He knew, before he opened it, what the trial was going to be about. He didn’t know how he knew. The way you know the end of a sentence from the shape of the beginning.

THUD.

They went on.

The courtyard was exactly as Tiger had said, and nothing at all like it.

Small. Square. Walls worn round at the edges like river stones. Moss on the cobbles. The stray, over it, turned down, keeping it still. A ring of blackened stones in the moss, old, the black gone grey.

And the wall.

He saw it the moment he came through the door, because he couldn’t not, because he’d been looking for it for a long time, since the night in the archive in Tell, more than four hundred days ago. He walked straight across the courtyard to it, past the ring of stones, past Tiger, and stood in front of it, and didn’t say anything for a very long time.

Cut into the grey stone at the height of his chest. Rows of marks. Short straight strokes, in fives, four strokes and one across, the way you’d count anything anywhere at home. Six fives to a row. Thirty strokes. And at the end of every row, a number.

30.

He counted the rows. He couldn’t help it. It was what he did.

Forty-one.

Forty-one rows. Forty-one 30s. Going down the wall in a column, one under another, every one the same, every stroke cut with the same tool by the same hand. He could see it. The strokes leaned a little to the right, all of them, the way the Ts leaned in the archive. Hurried. Every night. As if she’d always been in a hurry and had always wanted it to stay anyway.

At the top, over the first row, two words. Small.

FLOOR 17.

And at the bottom, under the forty-first row, cut deeper than everything else. Careful. Slower.

WATCH EACH OTHER.

And under that, with a dash before it, one letter. With a long tail, going down.

— R.

He opened the ink book. To the page. He held it up beside the wall, the letters he’d copied in Tell next to the letters cut in the stone.

The R.

The same tail. Long, going down, as if she’d meant to keep writing and stopped.

He looked at the W. At the Ts in WATCH, crossed high and to the right. At the full stop after OTHER, pressed so deep into the stone the edges of it had crumbled.

He lowered the book.

“It’s her,” said Cursiffe.

Nobody said anything.

“It’s Ruth,” said Cursiffe. His voice had stopped being level. He could hear it. “It’s the same hand. The same R. She cut this. Here. With—“ He looked at the strokes. “With a knife. Or a chisel. Something with a narrow point. Every night. Forty-one nights. She counted, and she cut it, and it was thirty every time.” He looked at the column of 30s. “Every time.”

“Nobody left down,” said Tiger. Very quietly.

“Nobody left down. On seventeen. For forty-one nights.” Cursiffe’s eyes had gone. He didn’t wipe them. He couldn’t let go of the book. “And then she wrote watch each other. At the end. For whoever came next.”

There was something else.

Under the R. Smaller still. He nearly missed it; it was cut so lightly, a scratch, as if whoever made it had been in more of a hurry than ever. Two words and a number.

GOING IN. 30.

And beside it, scratched into the stone, an arrow. Pointing along the wall. To the right. To the courtyard’s other door, the one Tiger hadn’t come in by.

Toward the middle.

“There’s something in there,” said Yan.

He was standing by the other door. The one the arrow pointed at. He hadn’t gone through it. He was standing a pace back from it, with Working On It upright beside him and Suzu round his neck, looking at it the way he looked at lights. Head on one side.

“Past the door,” said Yan. “A long way. In the middle.” He was quiet. “It’s not a light.”

“What is it?”

Yan didn’t answer for a while.

“It’s where one should be,” said Yan. “And isn’t.” He tipped his head further. “You know when you look at the place on a wall where a picture’s been taken down. And the paint’s different. A square. Where it was.” He was quiet. “It’s that. A light-shaped place. Very big. In the middle of everything.” He looked at the arrow on the wall. “They went in.”

“And came out,” said Wendy. Behind him. Very firmly. “They came out. They got cried on 22. And 31. And 40. They came out, Yan.”

“I know,” said Yan. “I’m just saying what it looks like.”

Wendy’s hand was on the back of his coat. Cursiffe saw it tighten.

He looked at the arrow. GOING IN. 30. Scratched, hurried, light. The last thing she’d cut on this wall. The forty-second night, maybe. Or the morning after the forty-first. Thirty of them, standing up from round a fire in this courtyard, and putting it out, and picking up their packs, and going through that door toward a place where a light should be.

He didn’t write anything about what happened to them after. It wasn’t a thing he’d seen. Nobody had seen it. It had happened three hundred and twelve years ago, and whatever it was, they’d been cried on 22 the year after, the clerk’s voice going up from the steps of the Assize in Tell. Somewhere. Climbers. That was all anybody knew.

He’d keep it true.

He read it standing in front of Ruth’s wall with the ink book in one hand and the pen in the other.

He’d known. From the shape of the beginning.

Write it where it will be found. Here. On this wall. Under hers. Where whoever came next, if anybody ever came next, would find it the way they’d found hers.

Let it dry. That was the thing. That was the whole of Writ, the whole of what he was. What you write, holds. For as long as the ink is wet. Two years of it holding. STOP in the mud on floor 1, for a second and a half. STAY in the snow on two. STAY on the cobbles a few minutes ago, with the slate-dog’s jaws at his knee. And every time, the ink dried, and it stopped holding, and was just a word.

Let it dry.

Do not be there when it is read.

He stood in front of the wall.

He’d spent his whole life being there. That was what a scribe was. You were there. You saw it. You wrote it down. The stone scribe on floor 5, in the lap of whose statue the line had been carved: Write what you saw. Not what you heard about. Not what somebody told you. What you saw. You had to be there. He’d been at the doors of the Long Barn with his hands on Hako’s shoulders for every number. He’d been in every house on the stilts on eight. He’d been in the archive in Tell for a whole night copying a dead woman’s R.

And Ruth had cut forty-one rows of 30 into a wall, and watch each other, and gone, and never been there when anybody read it.

And three hundred and twelve years later a man with a rope round his waist had come into a courtyard after a lost child and read it. And it had held. Not the stone; the words. They’d held, all that time, with nobody there. Tiger had come out of the Loose and told Ice, and Ice had gone still, and Cursiffe had gone white, and five people had come into the Loose on a rope to stand in front of this wall.

Because she’d let it dry.

He opened the ink book to a blank page at the back. He didn’t need a blank page. He wasn’t going to write in the book. He just wanted to see it there, empty, for a moment.

Then he shut the book, and put it in his coat, and took out Mott’s ink.

Eel-oil ink. Black. From a city on stilts on eight, from an eel-man called Mott who’d sung so the eels wouldn’t know, whose great-granddaughter had written his name upside down from the water. Cursiffe had been writing with it since. It smelled of the lake.

He knelt down in the moss in front of Ruth’s wall.

He wasn’t going to cut it. He couldn’t. He’d never cut stone in his life. He was going to write it. In ink. On stone. Under hers. And let it dry.

He dipped the pen.

He wrote it under GOING IN. 30., under the arrow, in square capitals, his own hand, not hurried. He’d never once been in a hurry in his life. He wrote it slowly, every letter, the way the stone scribe’s letters had been cut.

FLOOR 17. THIRTY-SIX.

He stopped. He dipped the pen. He thought about it.

WE FOUND YOUR WALL.

WE ARE WATCHING EACH OTHER.

NOBODY LEFT DOWN.

He stopped again. The pen hovered. He thought about whether to put the next thing. Whether it was true. Whether he’d seen it.

He had.

THANK YOU, RUTH.

And under it, a dash. And one letter. His own. Square.

— C.

He sat back on his heels.

The ink was wet. Black, shining, on the grey stone. He could feel it holding. The way Writ always felt: a sort of hum in the pen, in his fingers, in the words. For as long as the ink is wet. The words standing up off the stone, holding whatever they held.

He watched them dry.

It took a long time. Stone didn’t drink ink the way paper did. It sat on the surface, black and wet, and then slowly, from the edges in, it went dull. Matt. Grey-black. Dry.

He felt the hum go out of it.

He felt Writ stop. The way it always stopped. Like a hand letting go.

Let it dry.

And then—

He didn’t know what happened. He’d try to write it that night and get it wrong three times. It was like the opposite of the hum going out. Something going in. Into the dry words on the wall, into the stone under them, the way water goes into the ground after rain and stays.

The words didn’t stand up off the stone any more. They sat in it. Like Ruth’s. Like they’d always been there.

They held.

[ Remembrancer · complete ]

He didn’t see the rest of it. Not then.

He was looking at something else.

The wall had changed.

Not his words. Hers. Ruth’s. The forty-one rows of 30, and watch each other, and the R with the long tail. They were the same. But the grey light in the courtyard had gone strange round them. Thicker. The way the air goes over a road on a hot day.

Echo of the Past, he thought. Very slowly. What happened, and is kept anywhere, you may tell again.

He hadn’t called it. He’d never once been able to call it on purpose; it came when it came. Once, a long time ago, a tiny ink figure in a cloak walking through the lines of a page for one second. On thirteen, four words, out of a grey record into somewhere they were needed.

It came now.

Not ink. Not on a page. In the courtyard. In the grey light round the wall and the ring of stones. Shapes.

Grey. Faint. The shape of people. Like the figure on the page on floor 6, but full-size, and many, and made of nothing at all, not ink, not light, just the grey of the courtyard gone thicker in the shape of somebody sitting. Round the ring of stones. In the moss. Where the fire was.

Because there was a fire. Grey. Faint. Not real. In the ring of blackened stones, the shape of flames, going up, not giving any light.

And round it, the shapes. Sitting. Some lying down. Some with their knees up. Some leaning on each other.

He counted. He couldn’t help it.

Thirty.

No faces. He’d know later why; a wall doesn’t keep faces. It keeps what was cut into it. And a count is a count of people. So the Echo had made people. Thirty grey shapes round a grey fire, the shape of people and nothing more. You couldn’t tell who was who. You couldn’t tell anything about them except that there were thirty, and they were close, and they were leaning on each other.

And one of them was standing up.

At the wall. In front of him. With something narrow in her hand, a knife, a chisel, he couldn’t tell, just the grey shape of something with a point. Standing in front of the forty-first row, where it wasn’t cut yet, where the stone was blank.

And she was counting.

He heard it. Faint. Not in the courtyard; in his head, the way Writ hummed in his fingers. A woman’s voice. Low. Tired. Not hurried, not now. Counting out loud, slowly, the way you’d count heads on a bus before it pulls away.

Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. Twenty-eight.

At every number, one of the grey shapes round the fire moved. Lifted a hand. Or a head. Here.

Twenty-nine.

Thirty.

And the woman’s shape put the point of the narrow thing to the stone, and cut. Four strokes and one across. Six times. Quick. Leaning to the right. And then the number at the end.

30.

Still thirty, said the voice. Very quietly. Not to anybody. To the wall.

And behind her, round the fire, one of the grey shapes said something Cursiffe couldn’t hear, and two or three of the others moved, the way people move when somebody says a thing that makes them all turn and look at each other.

Watching each other.

The woman’s shape turned round. Toward the fire. Toward the thirty. He saw her look at them. No face. He saw her look anyway. The way you see somebody look at their people across a room.

Watch each other, said the voice. Tired. Fond. That’s all. That’s the whole trick.

And the grey went thin.

The fire went. The shapes went. One at a time, from the edges in, the way the ink had dried. The woman’s shape last. Standing at the wall with the point in her hand, looking at the fire that wasn’t there.

Then just the courtyard. Grey light. Moss. A ring of blackened stones. A stray overhead, turned down. The wall.

And his words, under hers. Dry. Holding.

He realised he was on his knees in the moss with his face wet and the pen still in his hand, and Wendy was kneeling beside him with her hand flat on his back, and Tiger was standing very still by the door with his tail wrapped round his own legs, and Yan was looking at the place where the grey fire had been with his head on one side and an expression Cursiffe had never seen on him.

“Did you see,” said Cursiffe. His voice was gone.

“Yes,” said Wendy.

“Thirty.”

“Thirty,” said Tiger. Very quietly. “All of them. Round the fire.”

“She counted them,” said Cursiffe. “Every night. Out loud. And they all—“ He couldn’t finish.

“Still thirty,” said Yan.

He was still looking at the ring of stones.

“There weren’t any lights,” said Yan. “In the shapes. I looked. There wasn’t anything to see. They were just the shape of where people had been.” He was quiet a long time. “But she looked at them like I look at lights.”

Nobody said anything.

He read it on his knees. Then he read it again.

For as long as somebody reads it.

He looked up at Ruth’s R. At the long tail going down under where the next letter should have been.

Three hundred and twelve years nobody had read it. Then a man on a rope.

Do not be there when it is read.

He got up. He put the ink in his coat. He looked at his words on the wall, dry, under hers, for one last time. THANK YOU, RUTH. — C.

Then he turned his back on them.

And walked to the door, and put his hand on the back of Tiger’s belt, and said, “Home,” and didn’t look round. Not once. Not when Tiger opened the door. Not when they stepped through. Not on any of the beats all the way back through the Loose to the top of the alley.

Somebody else would read it.

He wouldn’t be there.

He found, walking out on the beat with his hand on Tiger’s belt and the Loose sliding past him on every side, that that was the most he’d ever been there for anything.

Ice was at the top of the alley.

Still holding the rope. Feet in the same place. Thea against his shoulder. Salty at the anvil, his arm going. Wendy’s song, which Salty was humming now, under the beat, with the end, Cursiffe noticed, right.

Ice counted them. Tiger, Cursiffe, Yan, Wendy. Cursiffe watched his lips.

“Four,” said Ice.

He didn’t let go of the rope.

They sat on Ottilie’s step that evening, the two of them. Everybody else had gone back to the Crook, or was on watch, six steps, six ways. Ottilie was on her own step, two along, with Lirra asleep against her side and her eyes on her street, and Pask was at his anvil in his own lane, two streets away, and Cursiffe could hear him. Tink. Tink. Not every four seconds. Just working. At night. For the first time in eleven years. Salty had moved the anvil back.

Ice sat on the step beside him with the little lantern on his belt humming and the big amber one in Thea’s lap, two houses along, where she was on watch, facing the other way.

“Show me,” said Ice.

Cursiffe opened the ink book.

Not to the page from Tell. To a new page. Near the back. Where he’d written it that afternoon, on Ottilie’s step, the moment they came out: everything. The wall. The forty-one rows. FLOOR 17. WATCH EACH OTHER. — R. GOING IN. 30. His own words under hers. And the Echo. Thirty shapes round a grey fire. A woman counting. Still thirty. Watch each other. That’s all. That’s the whole trick.

He’d written it true. Every word.

He put his hand flat on the page.

Living Manuscript. What’s recorded can be read back and shared.

The ink moved.

He felt Ice go still beside him. On the page, in the lamplight from Ottilie’s window, the lines of his writing loosened, and slid, and came apart, and came together again. Small. A ring of ink stones. A fire made of strokes. And round it, tiny, no bigger than his fingernail, thirty ink shapes. Sitting. Leaning. And one standing, at a line of ink that was a wall, with a stroke of ink in her hand.

Counting.

Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine. Thirty.

He heard it again. Faint. In the hum of the page. And he knew Ice heard it too, because Ice made a sound, very small, beside him, like a breath let out that had been held a long time.

Still thirty.

The tiny ink woman turned round on the page and looked at the thirty tiny ink shapes round the fire.

Watch each other. That’s all. That’s the whole trick.

And the ink went still, and was writing again.

Ice didn’t say anything for a long time.

Cursiffe didn’t look at him. He’d decided that, before he opened the book. He wouldn’t look. Whatever Ice’s face did, it was Ice’s. He’d write what Ice said. Not what his face did. Not tonight.

“They kept each other,” said Ice. At last. Very quietly.

“Forty-one nights.”

“Thirty. Every night.” Ice’s voice did something. “Nobody left down.”

“On seventeen,” said Cursiffe. Carefully. Because that was all the wall said. “On seventeen, nobody left down.”

Ice was quiet.

Then he reached over and, very lightly, the way you’d touch a sleeping animal, put one finger on the page. On the tiny ink woman, standing at her wall.

“Thank you,” said Ice. “For showing me.”

“You asked.”

“I asked.” Ice took his finger away. “I’m getting better at it.” He looked out at Ottilie’s street, grey in the dusk, kept. “That’s the whole trick.” He almost laughed. “She’s right.”

The little lantern on his belt hummed. Four notes. The middle wrong.

Cursiffe shut the book.

He’d write the rest in the VOYAGER book later. Not the ink book; the other one. The brown one with one word on the front, where he only ever put what he’d seen himself. He’d write: Day 798. He held the rope from the top all afternoon and didn’t come in. He asked me to show him. I showed him the ones before. He touched her with one finger. He said thank you.

He wouldn’t write what he’d worked out. That wasn’t a thing he’d seen.

He’d keep it true.

The middle was a light-shaped place, Yan said. Where one should be, and wasn’t.

They told everybody that night, in the Crook, on the steps, in the six-and-six-ways of the watch. Cursiffe read the wall out from his book. Higu drew the arrow on the rock. GOING IN. Chris wrote it in a new column with no heading, and sat looking at it.

Rachael listened to all of it with her gloves off, on the step of the third house, facing north. She didn’t say anything. When Cursiffe read still thirty, he saw her look down at her own bare hands for a long time.

She’d been at seventy since the Hollow. Calvin had told everybody, loudly, on the stair. She hadn’t opened it. Not the window. She’d looked at it and closed it and not said what it was.

Cursiffe didn’t write that down either. It wasn’t his.

GOING IN. 30.

He lay on the floor of the fourth house in the Crook that night, on his watch’s off-hours, and couldn’t sleep, and listened. To Pask’s hammer, two streets off, tink, working in the dark for the first time in eleven years. To Salty’s, on the step outside, THUD, every four seconds, because Salty had found out the strays liked it and settled over the street when it went, and the street held better, and Salty didn’t need anybody’s leave to keep time.

To Wendy, on the step across, teaching him the end of the song.

Write what you saw. Keep it true.

Somewhere in the Loose, in a courtyard that didn’t move, under a stray that kept it, on a wall under forty-one rows of 30, his words were holding. Dry. In the stone. For whoever read them next.

He wouldn’t be there.

He shut his eyes.

In Mott’s ink, under hers: FLOOR 17. THIRTY-SIX. WE FOUND YOUR WALL. WE ARE WATCHING EACH OTHER. NOBODY LEFT DOWN. THANK YOU, RUTH. — C. He watched it dry. It held.
Echo of the Past: thirty grey shapes round a grey fire. No faces. A woman at the wall counting. Each one moved: here. “Still thirty. Watch each other. That’s all. That’s the whole trick.”
Yan: “There weren’t any lights in them. But she looked at them like I look at lights.”
Set Down: “What you write and let dry, holds. For as long as somebody reads it.” He turned his back on it and didn’t look round.
Living Manuscript for Ice on Ottilie’s step. The ink woman counted. Ice touched her with one finger. “They kept each other. Thank you. For showing me.” “You asked.” “I’m getting better at it.”
Rachael has been at seventy since the Hollow. She hasn’t opened it. She looked at her hands when he read “still thirty.”
Pask works at night now. Salty keeps time on the step; the strays settle over the street when the hammer goes.
