Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 204

I Don't Know

5,586 words · 24 min read

He knew fifteen.

He’d known it since the Long Barn. Since before. He’d known it on the way down, on every stair, the way you know a room in a house you grew up in: not the details, not every crack in the plaster, but the shape of it. Where the door was. Which way the light came in. What was waiting behind the second door on the left.

Fifteen was pines.

Black ones, tall as towers, so close together that at noon the forest floor was dusk. Snow under them, blue in the shade, and the smell of resin so strong it got into your food. A town built round the trunks, high up, on platforms, with rope bridges between the trees and smoke going up through the branches; the first time, they’d come up the stair in autumn at Lv 70-something and the town had taken one look at thirty-four climbers and pulled up its ladders. Wolves on the second night. Big grey ones that hunted in the snow under the pines in a long line, silent, and came at you from the side you weren’t looking at. Gabe had lost two fingers’ worth of skin off his shield hand on the second night, to a wolf that went under his rim. Deston had held the line. Thea had found the wolves’ trail in the snow by the light of her staff and followed it to the den, and they’d burned it out, and the town had let its ladders down the next morning, and an old man with one eye had given them bread.

The guardian had been an owl. A great white one, the size of a cottage, that lived in the oldest tree and came down on you without a sound. Four tries. He’d drawn a map of the branches, after. He’d kept it for years.

He was going to tell them about the wolves. He’d decided on the stair from fourteen, before they went down. Tell them where, the way Higu liked. There’s a forest. There’s a town in the trees; it’ll pull its ladders up, don’t take it personally. Watch your sides on the second night. Higu would write it on the new rock. Drew would put it on a slate. Calvin would say wolves, sides, everybody, and Rachael would hit him. And they’d go in knowing, and nobody would lose two fingers of skin, and he’d find the den himself, first, before anybody had to, and—

Ice, said Alea.

He was on the stair. In the dark, a long way up from the trapdoor under the Long Barn floor, in the middle of the line, with Thea’s hand in his and Newish counting somewhere ahead. Two hundred and ninety. Two hundred and ninety-one. Calvin’s voice ahead of that, talking. Gabe’s tread behind, last-last.

Ice. Something’s wrong with the logs.

“What?”

He said it out loud. He didn’t mean to. Thea’s hand tightened.

The logs, said Alea. She was on his shoulder. Small. Very still. Her voice was wrong. He’d heard her frightened before; on the Grey, on four, in the House of Ash when the voice said and you. This wasn’t that. This was smaller. Further in. Fifteen. The first time. I’ve got all of it. Every day. I’ve had it for ten years. And I went to open it. Just now. To have it ready. The wolves, the town—

“And?”

It’s grey.

He stopped on the stair.

Thea stopped with him. Gabe, behind, walked into his back. “Dec—“

It’s grey, said Alea. All of it. Every page. It’s there. I can see it’s there. I can see there’s a floor fifteen, and days, and us on it. But I can’t read it. It’s like— She stopped. He felt her try to find it. It’s like a page somebody’s left out in the rain. The ink’s there. It’s just—gone soft. Run. I can’t read a word.

“Try sixteen.”

Grey.

“Twenty.”

Grey. All of it, Ice. Everything above fourteen. She was quiet. I went back down to check. One. Two. The Fen. Four. The House of Ash. Everything below is fine. I can read every word. I can read the day you punched the goat. A pause, very small. It stops at fourteen. At the barn. At the bell. After that it’s all just—grey.

He stood on the stair in the dark.

He could feel thirty-five threads going out of him up and down the stair, slack and easy, the way they were when people were walking and not frightened. He could feel Thea’s hand. He could hear Newish, a long way up, counting.

“Why?” he said.

I don’t know.

It was the first time she’d ever said it.

In twelve years. In two lives. In every room and on every floor and at the bottom of every stair. Alea had always known. That was what she was. She was the one who’d been there for all of it, and kept it, and handed it to him when he needed it, a date, a name, a number, the way the ice went on six, the place under the jaw. I’ve got it, she’d say. I’ve got all of it. He’d built the whole of this life on that. Every early step. Every hard trial he’d taken knowing what came after.

I don’t know, said Alea again. Smaller. I’m sorry. I don’t know why. I think— She stopped. On floor 1. The line. In World. With no name on it. It came on time, and it was wrong, and you wrote under it, and so did they. Thirty-six of you. And it didn’t go away. And I said it’s never going to match now.

“I remember.”

I think it heard me. Her voice was very small. I think it heard all of us. I think the tower looked at the first record, and looked at the second one, and couldn’t make them match. Not on one. Not ever. So it stopped. She was quiet. It stopped serving the first one. Above where we’ve been. It’s not going to give it to us any more. It’s not going to give it to anybody.

He stood very still.

Ice. I don’t know what’s at the top of this stair.

Thea’s hand was holding his so hard it hurt.

“Dec,” said Gabe, behind him, low. “What is it?”

He didn’t answer. He didn’t know how. He stood on the stair for another breath, two, with Alea small and cold on his shoulder for the first time in either life. And then he went on up. Because Newish was still counting, and Calvin was still talking, and the line was going up, and you didn’t stop on a stair.

Thea didn’t let go of his hand.

She didn’t ask, either. She just held on, all the way up, harder than she ever had, as if she’d heard every word.

Maybe she had. She’d always been able to hear the things he didn’t say.

“Four hundred and thirty-six,” said Newish. “That’s all of them. That’s fifteen.”

Tiger put his hand flat on the door.

He held it there a long time. Longer than usual. Ice saw him, at the front, in the dark, with his palm on the old wood and his tail very still, as if he were listening to something on the other side. Then he pushed.

The cold came in.

Not the cold of two, painted and polite. Not the Grey, which hadn’t been cold, just nothing. Real cold. Cold with weight to it. It came down the stair at them like water, and hit Calvin at the front so that he said a word Rachael didn’t hit him for, and went past them all down the stair into the dark behind, and smelled of something enormous and clean and wet. Snow. Wind. And under it, something else. Something cold and deep and old, like the inside of a well.

And with it, the sound.

A long, low, groaning boom. From somewhere far below and very far away. It came up through the stone under their feet and into their legs and their teeth and the back of their skulls. It went on, and on, and rolled away into silence, the way thunder does.

Then another one. Further off.

Then nothing.

They went out of the door one by one into the light.

It wasn’t a forest.

He came out of the door behind Thea and stood on a ledge of grey stone high on the side of a cliff, and there were no pines. There was nothing tall at all. There was the sky, huge and pale and real, clear blue at the top going to white at the edges, with a low hard sun coming up over far hills on the right and laying long gold light across everything. And under the sky, a valley.

The biggest valley he’d ever seen.

It went away from the foot of the cliff in both directions as far as he could see, between low brown hills streaked with old snow. And down the middle of it, filling it, a mile wide, maybe more, white and flat and blinding in the low sun, lay a river.

Frozen.

A great white road of ice, winding away north and south out of sight, with low banks of brown reed and black willow scrub along its edges. Not smooth. Ridged. Heaved up in long pressure lines like the backs of buried animals, and cracked, here and there, in long dark lines that ran for hundreds of metres and then stopped. Snow lay on it in drifts, blown into ripples. And along its edges, and here and there out on the ice itself, things that weren’t ice. Buildings. Small wooden ones, grey, on stilts, standing up out of the reed beds along the banks with their legs in the ice. And further out, frozen into the river itself, low flat shapes with roofs on them, like barges, like rafts with houses built on, dozens of them, clustered together, with smoke going up from every one into the still cold air.

A town. On the ice. Waiting.

The sound came again. Boom. From far up the river, out of sight. It rolled down the valley toward them and they felt it in the ledge under their feet. On the ice below, out in the middle, one of the long dark cracks moved. Just a little. Like a mouth deciding whether to open.

“Oh,” said Hyvred, beside him, very softly. “Oh, it’s alive.”

He stood on the ledge in the real cold and looked at it.

He didn’t know it.

Not a single thing. Not the cliff, or the valley, or the river, or the hills, or the stilt-houses or the raft-town or the sound. Nothing. He looked at all of it, the whole enormous white bright freezing sweep of it in the morning sun, and there wasn’t one part of it, one stone, one reed, one crack in the ice, that he’d ever seen before in either life.

It was the first time.

He hadn’t had a first time since the white room.

Higu sat down on the ledge with her back against the cliff and took out the rock.

The new one. The flat grey stone from the river below the mill on floor 1, that she’d picked out of the shallows on the morning of the six hundred and forty-third day while Mother Crane shouted at Calvin from the door. Blank. She’d carried it up fourteen floors and not drawn a single line on it. She’d said, on the stair down to the Long Barn’s trapdoor, that she was saving it.

She wet the chalk. She drew three columns, the way she had on the Long Acre. REMEMBERED. TRUE. NOW.

She drew the first short line in the left-hand column.

Then she looked up at him. She didn’t say where. Not yet. She did it the old way. The way she’d done it at the foot of every stair going down, thirteen times, for something to do with her hands.

“Ice,” said Higu. “What do you remember?”

He looked out at the river.

“A forest,” he said.

Higu’s chalk stopped.

“Pines,” said Ice. He couldn’t stop himself. It came out the way the first time had come out, all of it, in a line, the way it always had. “Black ones. Tall. A town in the trees, on platforms, with rope bridges. Wolves on the second night. An owl in the oldest tree.” He stopped. He looked at the frozen river a mile wide in the sun, and the stilt-houses, and the long dark crack moving out in the middle. “That’s what was here,” he said. “The first time. That’s what fifteen was.”

Higu looked at him for a long time. Then she wrote it. In the left-hand column, beside the first line, small and careful: A FOREST. PINES. WOLVES. AN OWL.

She looked up for the middle.

Nobody said anything. The river boomed, far off. Everybody was looking at the river, and then at Ice, and then at the river again.

Calvin said it. Of course he did. Somebody always said it, and on the stairs it was usually Calvin.

“It’s a river,” said Calvin. Quietly. For Calvin. “Dec. It’s a river.”

“That’s true,” said Higu.

She wrote it in the middle column. A RIVER. She didn’t underline it. She sat and looked at the two columns side by side for a long time. A FOREST. A RIVER. The left one and the right one. The first time and this time. Not wrong. Just somewhere else.

Then she looked up at him again, and said the thing she’d said on the Long Acre. The thing she’d said on the ledge in the furnace hall on four. The only thing she’d ever wanted from him.

“Where?”

He looked out at the river. At the raft-town frozen into the ice with its smoke going up. At the long dark cracks, and the pressure ridges like the backs of buried animals, and the far hills, and the low gold sun. At all of it. He looked for anything. A path. A place. A thing he knew. The way he’d always looked at the top of every stair, for the shape of the room he’d grown up in.

There was nothing.

On nine, at the top of the stair down into the Down, he’d said I don’t know for the first time, and Higu had written it down. But on nine he’d known what. He’d known the great trees, and the walkers, and Burr’s rope, and the thing in the Down. He just hadn’t known where, because nothing on nine stayed where it was. He’d been able to tell them what was there. He’d only lost the map.

He hadn’t lost the map now. There wasn’t one. There’d never been one. Nobody had ever drawn it.

“I don’t know,” said Ice.

It went through them. He felt it go. Thirty-five threads, all at once, in his chest, going taut. Not frightened, exactly. Something else. Something he didn’t have a word for. The way a crowd goes still when somebody who always knows the way stops in the middle of a road and looks round and doesn’t.

Nobody said anything.

Higu sat with the chalk over the rock for a long, long moment. Then she wrote. Not in the left column. Not in the middle. In the right-hand one. The NOW. In her small square capitals, very carefully, the way you’d write a fact that might be the most important one on the rock:

HE DOESN’T KNOW.

She looked at it.

Then she put the chalk down, and looked up at him, and her face did a thing Higu’s face almost never did. It smiled. Small. Crooked. Not happy, exactly. Something like the way you’d smile at somebody who’d just walked out onto the ice beside you.

“Okay,” said Higu. “Neither do we.”

He looked at her.

“Neither do we,” said Higu again. “We never have. Not once. Not on one, not on fourteen. We’ve never known what was at the top of a stair. Not one of us.” She picked up the rock and held it against her chest. “We just had you.” She was quiet a moment. “Now we’ve got each other. Same as always. Just—more.”

Behind her, Gabe made a sound.

Ice turned. Gabe was standing at the back of the ledge by the door, last-last, with Hearthwall on his arm and the wolf coat on, and his face was doing something Ice had only seen it do once before. On floor 7, in the bakery, at dawn, when Ice had taken the helm off and said It’s me. Declan., a second before the punch.

“You don’t know,” said Gabe.

“No.”

“You always know.”

“Not this time.”

Gabe looked at him. Then out at the river. Then back at him. And Ice watched something go across his face that he’d never seen on it in fifteen years. Not fear. Gabe was frightened of all sorts of things and never once showed it. Something under that. Something that Ice thought, after, might have been relief.

“Huh,” said Gabe.

He came across the ledge. He stood next to Ice at the edge, shoulder to shoulder, looking out at the frozen river a mile wide in the morning sun. He didn’t say anything else for a while.

“So we’re all new,” said Gabe at last.

“We’re all new.”

“Same floor. Same time. Nobody first.” Gabe looked down at the raft-town. “You’re not a floor ahead.”

“I’m not a floor ahead.”

Gabe nodded slowly. Then he did something he hadn’t done since the white room. Since before. Since they were boys, in a different life. He put his arm round Ice’s shoulders. Not the hand on the back of the neck, the steadying one, the one he did on stairs. The whole arm. Heavy. Warm. The wolf coat and all.

“Good,” said Gabe.

Drew came and stood on his other side.

“Is it bad?” said Drew.

Ice looked at him.

“I don’t know,” he said.

And Drew laughed. Out loud. On the ledge. The real one, the one that came out of him maybe twice a floor and that nobody ever got to keep. He laughed till he had to put his bare hand on the cliff.

“Welcome,” said Drew. “Welcome. I’ve been asking that for six hundred days and nobody’s ever known. Not once. Except Thea. Once.” He wiped his eyes. “It’s not so bad. You get used to it.”

Chris had his notebook out. The fifteenth. He was writing, very fast, standing at the edge of the ledge. Ice didn’t need to see what. He knew what Chris wrote. Chris wrote everything.

And Thea hadn’t let go of his hand.

She’d stood beside him through all of it. Through a forest, and a river, and I don’t know, and Higu’s NOW, and Gabe’s arm. She hadn’t said anything. She was looking out at the river with the lantern in her other hand, and its amber light lay flat against the glass toward him the way it always did now, and her face was doing nothing at all.

That was how you knew.

She knew what it meant. Of all of them, only she knew all of it. She knew there’d been a first time. She knew it had gone on for ten years and ended at the top with everybody dead but him. She knew he’d come back to the first minute to run, to be a floor ahead, to know. She knew that every hard trial and every early step and every stair he’d gone up first in this life had been built on knowing what came next.

And she knew that, for the first time since the white room, he didn’t.

She turned her head and looked at him. Just for a moment. With the morning on her face and the river below.

“Then we find out,” said Thea.

That was all she said.

The path went down the cliff in a long zigzag to the reed beds. They took it slowly, because it was steep and iced and nobody knew it, and because Calvin kept stopping to look at the river and saying oh at normal volume. The goat came down on Gabe’s shoulders, with great dignity and some complaint.

Halfway down, Alea spoke again. Very quietly, on his shoulder. Only to him.

I’ve never not known before.

“I know.”

Twelve years. Two lives. I’ve always had it. Whatever came. I’ve always had the page. She was quiet. I don’t know what that is. She meant the river. I don’t know what’s under it. I don’t know what makes that sound. I don’t know what the town is called. I don’t know if there’s an owl.

“There’s not an owl.”

You don’t know that.

He stopped on the path. He thought about it.

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

Alea didn’t say anything. He felt her, small and still, the way she’d been on the stair. Frightened. The way he’d been frightened on the first morning of the first time, before there was anything to remember. The way everybody else had been frightened, every day, on every floor, for two years, while he went ahead of them knowing.

He put his hand up to his shoulder. To the place where she was.

“We’ll find out,” he said. “Like them.”

She was quiet a long time.

Like them, said Alea at last. And then, very small, in a voice he’d never heard her use, like somebody trying a word in a language they’d only ever read: Okay.

The stilt-houses at the foot of the cliff had people in them.

They came out onto their porches as the thirty-six came down out of the reeds onto the ice at the edge of the river. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Short, broad people in long coats of grey fur with the hoods thrown back, with flat brown faces burned dark by the snow-glare, and long straight black hair in braids. Every one of them carried a pole. A long pale pole of willow, taller than a man, with an iron point at one end and a knob of something at the other. They stood on their porches and on the ice at the foot of their stilts and looked at thirty-six strangers and a goat coming down off the cliff, and didn’t say anything at all.

Then the river boomed again, far away, and every one of them, at the same moment, without looking, put the iron point of their pole down on the ice.

And listened.

Ice saw it. All of them, at once. The way you’d put your hand on somebody’s chest to feel their heart. The poles went down onto the ice with a small tock, and the people stood with their heads tipped, their eyes half shut, listening to whatever came up the pole into their hands. The boom rolled away down the valley. One by one, the poles came up again.

An old woman came out of the nearest house.

She was the oldest thing Ice had seen on fifteen. Bent, but not much, in a coat of grey fur so old it had gone nearly white, with a face like a walnut left out in the snow for a hundred winters. Her pole was black with handling. She came down the ladder from her porch, slowly, and walked out across the ice toward them on the little crunching steps of somebody who’d walked on ice her whole life and knew exactly where to put her feet. She stopped in front of them. In front of Ice, as it happened, because he was at the front, though he didn’t know why he was at the front.

She looked at him for a long time, with eyes gone milky at the edges.

“Climbers,” she said.

He didn’t know if it was a question. Her voice was low and dry and cracked, like ice settling.

“Yes,” he said.

“Gran’s gran said.” She looked past him at all of them. At the goat on Gabe’s shoulders. “Lights came down the cliff, once. Long time ago. Asked the way. Went up the river.” She looked back at him. “Never came back down.”

“We will,” said Ice. He didn’t know where it came from. “Come back down. Eventually.”

The old woman looked at him with her milky eyes.

“Hm,” she said.

The river boomed. Further off now; further down. She put the point of her black pole down on the ice without looking, tock, and tipped her head, and listened, with her eyes on Ice the whole time. Then she lifted it.

“Ten days,” she said. “Twelve. Then the Mother wakes.”

“The Mother?”

She looked at him as if he’d asked what colour the snow was.

“The river,” she said. “She’s asleep. All winter. Now she’s turning over. You can hear her.” She nodded up the valley. “Ten days. Twelve. She’ll wake. And everything moves. The ice. The houses. The town.” She jerked her chin at the raft-town frozen into the ice out in the middle. “You’ll not want to be on her when she does. Not if you don’t know her.”

“What happens?”

The old woman looked at him.

“You don’t know?” she said.

He thought about lying. He thought, for a moment, the old way, about saying something that sounded like knowing. We’ve seen rivers. We’ve been on ice. On six he’d known the Glass. On two he’d known the snow. On eleven he’d known the sea, and the way the swell turned at the Undertow, and Corran’s names.

“No,” he said. “I don’t know.”

The old woman’s face did something. Far down in all those lines, a very long way down, like a fish turning under ice.

“Good,” she said.

He looked at her.

“Nobody does,” said the old woman. “Not me. Not my gran. Fifty-eight thaws I’ve seen. Fifty-eight. And not two the same.” She put her pole down on the ice again, tock, and listened, and lifted it. “You don’t remember the ice. Climber. You can’t. It’s never the ice you remember. Every thaw’s the first one.” She turned round. “You listen to it. That’s all. Every day. Like it’s the first day. Because it is.”

She started back across the ice toward her ladder.

“What’s your name?” said Ice.

She stopped. She looked back over her shoulder at him. As if nobody had asked her that in a long time, either.

“Sabe,” she said. “Come in out of the wind. All of you. Bring your goat.”

He went out on the ice that night.

He couldn’t sleep. Sabe’s house had taken eight of them, and the houses either side had taken the rest, without being asked, because Sabe had said so, and the stilt-houses were warm and smelled of fish oil and smoke and there were furs on the floors. Thea was asleep in the corner of Sabe’s one room with her head on her pack. Higu was asleep sitting up with the new rock in her lap. The goat was asleep by the stove, where Sabe had said he could go, because she’d never seen a goat and wanted to look at him.

He went down the ladder in the dark and walked out onto the river.

It was very cold. Clear. The stars were out, thick, real, too many, the way they’d been on five. There was no moon. The river went away north and south under the stars, grey-white and flat and enormous, with the long dark cracks across it like lines on a page. And it was talking.

He’d thought it was silent, the first time, from the cliff. Between the booms. It wasn’t. Out here, with his boots on it, in the dark, he could hear it. All the time. Small sounds. A tick, far off. A long low creak, like a door opening slowly in a house where everybody’s asleep. A sharp crack, like a stick snapping, somewhere out in the middle, and a sort of hiss after it, running away into the dark. A deep, slow, under-everything groan, so low he felt it more than heard it, coming up through his boots. Like something very big turning over in its sleep.

She’s turning over, Sabe had said.

He stood on the ice and listened to it.

He didn’t know what any of it meant. Not one sound. He listened to the creak, and the tick, and the crack and the hiss, and the groan, and he had no idea. Whether they meant safe or not safe, here or not here, soon or not yet. He’d never once stood anywhere in the whole of the Spire, in either life, and not known what the sounds meant.

He was frightened.

He let himself be. He’d learned that, a long way down. On fourteen, on the Grey, at fifteen percent. You let yourself be frightened, and you said so, out loud, and somebody heard.

“I’m frightened,” he said. Out loud. To the ice.

Behind him, on the bank, by the foot of Sabe’s ladder, in the dark, somebody said:

“I know.”

He turned round.

Thea was sitting on the bottom rung of the ladder. She had the lantern turned down low in her lap and her coat round her and her knees up. She’d been there, he thought, since he came down. Of course she had. The Ina rule. Nobody went out alone. And after the aurora, after everything, it was always her.

“How long have you been there?”

“Since you came down.” She didn’t get up. “You were listening.”

“I don’t know what it’s saying.”

“No.”

“I’ve never not known. Not once. Not since the white room.” He looked back at the ice. “On nine I didn’t know where. But I knew what. I always knew what.” He swallowed. “I don’t know what that is. Any of it.”

Thea was quiet for a moment.

“Neither do I,” she said. “Neither does anybody. Sabe said.”

“Sabe’s seen fifty-eight.”

“And says every one’s the first.” Thea turned the lantern up a little. The amber light came out across the snow on the bank and lay on the ice toward him, flat, leaning. “So that’s fifty-eight first times. Hers. And this is yours.” She looked at him. “That’s all. It’s just your first time. Everybody’s got one.”

He stood on the ice and looked at her.

She’d sat with him in the dark once before, in the other life, on a barn floor, without knowing why, and not asked. She’d sat with him under an aurora on a rock by cold water and heard all of it and put her hand on his arm. She’d sat on the bottom step of an old woman’s stair on floor 1 for three days. She was sitting on the bottom rung of a stranger’s ladder on a floor neither of them had ever seen, at the edge of a river neither of them understood, with a lantern turned down low, because he’d gone out on the ice alone and you didn’t do that.

He came back across the ice to her.

He sat down on the bank, below the ladder, in the snow, with his back against her knees. She put her hand on his head. Then the back of his neck.

They listened to the river together.

After a while, he found he could hear the difference between the tick and the creak. That was all. Just that. He didn’t know what either of them meant. But he could tell them apart now. That was something. That was the first thing.

He’d find out the rest. Like everybody.

The river groaned, a long way down, turning over in its sleep.

Alea: “I’ve never not known before.” “We’ll find out. Like them.” “Okay.”
The stilt-folk put their poles on the ice when it booms. And listen. Sabe. Fifty-eight thaws. “Ten days. Twelve. Then the Mother wakes.”
“What happens?” “You don’t know?” “No.” “Good. Nobody does. Every thaw’s the first one. You listen.”
On the ice at night. I don’t know what any of it means. “I’m frightened.” Thea, on the bottom rung: “I know.”
“It’s just your first time. Everybody’s got one.”
I can tell the tick from the creak now. That’s the first thing.
