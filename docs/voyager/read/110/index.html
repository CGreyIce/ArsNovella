Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 110

The Longest Night

5,354 words · 23 min read

Deston still had the note.

He kept it in the left pocket of his shirt, buttoned, where it had been since the morning they left floor 3. He didn’t take it out much. He didn’t need to. He knew what it said. be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D. Eight words and a letter. On the back of his own card from the white room, in the grey of a fingertip. In handwriting he’d known since he was thirteen, in the second row of a choir, passing notes in rehearsal.

He touched it at night sometimes. Through the shirt. Just to know it was there.

He’d told Gabe, on floor 7, that it was a place to keep it. It’s a pocket, Dest, Gabe had said. And Deston had said the thing he’d been thinking for a hundred and fifty days without saying. It’s the first thing he said to anybody. After. And he said it to me.

He’d meant after the white room. After the arch. After whatever it was Declan had done, alone, in the first minute, while the rest of them were waking up.

He’d thought that was what he meant, anyway. On floor 7.

On floor 10, he’d started to wonder.

He’d been watching. He was always watching, a bit; it was what you did when you held a party together, and he’d held this one together for two hundred and fifty-eight days. You watched who was tired and who was frightened and who hadn’t eaten. You watched where the threads went.

Link. He’d had it since floor 1. Gold threads, out of the middle of his chest, to everybody in his party, and further if he reached. What one of you has, all of you may share. Mana, mostly. Strength, sometimes. He’d sent one under a door on floor 7, to somebody who wasn’t in his party and wasn’t in anybody’s, and felt it land. He’d held Declan’s hand on the jetty on floor 8 and felt the amber go out of Declan through him to all thirty-five.

The threads told him things. Not words. Weather. Who was warm and who was cold. Who was holding something.

And on floor 10, everybody was holding something.

Gabe was. That was the one he couldn’t get past. Gabe, who he’d known since Gabe was thirteen and the baby of the second row. Gabe, who’d gone purple every week when Declan sang the wrong words. Gabe, who told Deston everything, always had, whether Deston wanted to hear it or not. Gabe had come up from the Breath ten days ago with a shield that didn’t go cold, and stood on the barracks step at dawn with his forehead against Declan’s, and since then he’d been holding something so carefully that Deston could feel it through a thread he hadn’t even sent.

And he hadn’t said a word. Not to Deston. Not one.

Xav was holding something. Xav had been holding something since floor 6, and Deston had always known, the way you know when somebody in the next room is awake. Thea was. Thea had been holding something since the white room.

And Declan.

Deston had watched him sit down in the snow on the third level, nine nights ago, in the middle of the street, with the bells going. With his face in his hands. Because Dennis had run to Rachael. Shouting.

You ran to her, Declan had said. In a voice like reading off a wall. Shouting.

Deston had been there. He’d been crouched beside him in the snow. And he’d looked at Declan’s face, and seen something in it he’d never seen in thirteen years. Not grief. Not fear. Something older than both. The face of somebody looking at a thing that had happened a long time ago, and watching it, for the first time, not happen.

He’d decided, kneeling in the snow, not to ask.

He’d been deciding not to ask, one way or another, since floor 3.

The Bastion changed after the night of the cliffs.

Six hold-folk had died. Not on the wall. In the streets of the fourth level, in their own doorways, half-dressed, coming out with shields to meet something that had never come that way in nine hundred years. They carved them into the tunnel of the fourth wall the next night. Deston went. All of them went. They stood at the back while the carver knelt in the lantern light, six times, and the chisel went tik, tik, tik, and the hold-folk put their fists to their chests.

And the next morning, the Bastion found out about Rachael.

Ingrith told Brenna. Brenna told the wall. By noon it had gone round every level of the city, the way news went in a place where everybody ate at the same tables. The climber healer. The cold. She takes it out. She puts it in the smith’s shield. And by the afternoon there was a line outside the house for the wounded that went down the street and round the corner and halfway up the stair to the fourth level. Hold-folk with frost in their fingers, their toes, their ears. Old ones, who’d had it for years and kept it, wrapped, and waited for the part it was in to die.

Rachael stood in the doorway and looked at the line for a long time.

“A finger at a time,” she said, to nobody. “A finger at a time. That’s all I can carry.”

“Then a finger at a time,” said Gabe, beside her, with the shield on his arm.

They did it for nine days. Every afternoon. Rachael going down the line, a hand on a frozen finger, taking it, the white going into her own fingertips. Then back to Gabe on his stool by the door. Then the three flames on the boss going bright, and settling. Then the next one. Gabe didn’t go on the wall in the day any more. He slept in the morning and sat in the doorway all afternoon and stood in the line at night, with the shield’s edge across Declan, and Deston watched the thread to him go grey with tiredness and then gold again every evening, the way a coal does when you blow on it.

On the third day Brenna came down to the house and stood at the end of the line and watched.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. She watched Rachael take the cold out of an old man’s ear. She watched it go into the shield. She watched the old man touch his ear, pink, and burst into tears.

“Nine hundred years,” said Brenna. To Deston, who was standing beside her, because he was always standing somewhere near Rachael these days, in case. “Nine hundred years of cutting it off.”

“I know,” said Deston.

“We’ll carve her,” said Brenna. “When she goes. On the Long Wall. Even though she didn’t fall.” She looked at him with her grey eyes. “We’ve never carved anybody who didn’t fall.”

Deston didn’t know what to say to that. He told Rachael. Rachael said I’m not going anywhere and don’t you dare, and then went very quiet and didn’t speak to anybody for an hour.

The longest night was the two hundred and sixty-sixth.

Brenna told them a week before. She stood in the barracks with her shield on her back and told them what it was. Not as a story. As orders.

“The Long Night,” she said. “The middle of winter. The sun goes down early and comes up late, and in between, the Winter comes harder than any other night of the year. Always. Nine hundred years.” She looked round at them. “And we read the names.”

“All of them?” said Calvin.

“All of them. From the first stone. Every name on every wall, from the bottom of the pass to the top, out loud. Starting at dusk, in the tunnel of the first wall, with the oldest. Going up. Wall by wall. Ending at dawn, on the fifth, with the newest.” She paused. “This year that’s Tamm. And six more.”

Nobody said anything.

“Why?” said Deston.

Brenna looked at him for a long time.

“Because as long as somebody’s saying their names,” she said, “they’re still holding the pass. That’s what we tell the children. Every name on the walls, holding the line with us, the one night of the year it’s hardest.” She folded her arms. “I don’t know if it’s true. My mother didn’t. Hers didn’t. We read them anyway.”

“Who reads?”

“Everybody. In turns. Whoever’s not on the wall. A page each.” She looked round at the thirty-six of them. “You can read, if you want. We’d be glad of it. Climbers have never read the names.” She paused. “Climbers have never stood on the wall, either. Not in the books. Not like this.”

“I’ll read,” said Deston.

He hadn’t meant to say it. It just came out. He looked at Brenna and she looked back, grey eyes, and nodded.

“Then you’ll be in the tunnel,” she said. “All night. Not on the wall.”

“I know.”

He felt Gabe look at him. And Declan. Through the threads, both of them, at once.

“Somebody has to hold the threads,” said Deston. “From somewhere. It might as well be somewhere useful.”

He worked it out with Drew. Of course he did.

On the Long Night, the thirty-six would be everywhere. The fifth wall, where the Winter would come hardest. The cliffs above the fourth level, where it had come once already, with watch-posts the hold-folk had cut into the rock in the last ten days and nobody had slept in since. The fourth level itself. The house for the wounded, where Rachael and Gabe would be when the cold came down. Thirty-six people spread across the whole height of the Bastion, from the third wall to the top, in the dark, in the snow.

“Seven parties,” said Drew, over the rock. “And nobody can see anybody.”

“I can,” said Deston.

Drew looked at him.

“Link,” said Deston. “I can hold all of you. I’ve done it. On eight, with Declan. If I’m somewhere I can sit still, and nobody’s trying to kill me, I can hold all thirty-five.” He paused. “Thirty-six. Mana goes where it’s needed. And I’ll feel it. Who’s hurt. Who’s cold. Who’s running out.” He looked at the rock. “I can tell Calvin where to go. He can be there in a minute, with Slipstream.”

Drew didn’t say anything for a while.

“In the tunnel,” he said. “Reading names.”

“In the tunnel. Reading names.”

Drew wrote it on the rock. DESTON: THE TUNNEL. THE THREADS. And under it, in smaller chalk: CALVIN: WHEREVER DESTON SAYS.

“Somebody thank me,” said Drew.

“Thank you, Drew,” said Deston.

Dusk came at four in the afternoon on the Long Night. The sun went down behind the mountains at the low end of the pass and the sky went from pale blue to deep blue to black in about the time it took to walk from the barracks to the first wall. And the snow started.

The whole of the Bastion came to the first wall.

Not on it. Into it. Into the long stone tunnel through the thickness of it, the one the thirty-six had walked through on their first day, with the names going away into the dark on both sides, floor to ceiling. They’d hung lanterns all the way along it. Hundreds. Every fifth step, a lantern on a hook in the stone, so that the whole tunnel was lit gold from end to end and every name on both walls stood out in it, square and deep, each with its little line underneath.

And in the middle of the tunnel, a stool. And a book.

Not the names. The names were on the wall. The book was the order. Which page of the wall came next, which line, so that the readers wouldn’t lose their place in the dark. The first reader was the oldest woman in the Bastion, a hundred and four, carried in on a chair by two of her great-grandchildren. She sat on the stool with the book on her knees and a lantern held up beside her, and put her finger on the first name on the wall, at the very beginning of the tunnel, near the floor, so old and worn it was almost smooth.

And read it.

Her voice was very small. It carried down the whole length of the tunnel anyway, in the silence. One name. Then the next. Then the next.

Deston stood at the back and listened, and felt the threads.

Thirty-five of them. Out of the middle of his chest. Going out through the stone of the first wall, up the pass, through the city, to everywhere. He could feel them all, the way you can feel your own hands in the dark. Hyvred, high, on the cliffs, cold. Calvin, at the top of the third-wall stair, waiting. Dennis on the cliffs too, with his staff, nervous. Thea at the fifth wall with the lantern. Gabe and Rachael at the house for the wounded. Azac in the line. Xav. Chris.

And one more. Not a thread. He’d never managed to make it one. Not quite. Something else. Warmer. Amber. At the fifth wall. In the line. With a gold thread from Gabe going across it, the way Gabe’s shield went across it.

Declan.

The oldest woman read for an hour. Then her great-granddaughter read. Then a smith Deston recognised from the Breath. Then Ingrith, in her small gentle voice. Name after name after name, going down the walls of the tunnel in the lantern light, and the hold-folk standing along both sides of it in silence, with their fists on their chests, listening.

At about eight, the deep bell on the fifth wall started to ring.

Nobody in the tunnel moved. Ingrith didn’t stop reading. She didn’t even pause. She read the next name, and the next. And Deston felt the threads go tight.

He’d never felt anything like it.

On floor 8, in the Templar trial, he’d knelt at the edge of Declan’s circle and felt the light go out through him to thirty-five people, and come back. That had been one moment. One great warm flood, out and back.

This was hours.

The Winter came at the fifth wall, and he felt it come. Not the Rimed. He couldn’t feel the Rimed. He felt the line. Azac, and Chris, and Salty, and Jared, and Hako, and Chi, and Drew, and Declan, standing in it with the hold-folk, shields overlapping, and the cold coming down on them. He felt every hit. Through the threads. The way Declan had said, once, about his own threads. It was so much worse than being the wall. He felt Jared’s shield take something and Jared’s arm go numb to the shoulder. He felt Azac start talking, under his breath, and the warmth of it going out along the line. He felt Chris go still for three seconds, Breakpoint, and then move.

And the mana. Going out of them as they fought. Down, and down. Healers pouring it out, Malcolm and Xola and Newish behind the line. Hyvred on the cliffs, every arrow. Ina’s white fire on the parapet, which ate mana like nothing else.

He gave it to them.

That was what Link was. What one of you has, all of you may share. He found where it was low, and he found where there was some to spare, and he moved it. Along the threads. Through himself. From Bee, who wasn’t fighting yet, to Ina, who was burning through it. From Maigo, rooted in the rock of the cliff with his feet down drinking from the mountain, to Malcolm, who’d done twenty heals in an hour. From himself to everyone. Sitting on the stone floor at the back of the tunnel with his back against the names and his eyes shut, and the gold going out of him in every direction like water out of a broken pipe.

“Calvin,” he said. Out loud, in the tunnel, not caring who heard. “Fourth level. The east cliff. Dennis—“

He felt Calvin go. Fast. Up the stair, out along the fourth level, in his wind. He felt Dennis’s thread, on the east cliff, go thin and frightened and then flare, forty times, the way it had on the third level: Double, forty Dennises on a cliff in the snow, shouting. He felt Calvin get there. He felt the thread steady.

Ingrith finished her page. A soldier with a bandaged hand took the stool. Orm. Deston knew the hand. Three fingertips gone and seven more. Orm read in a voice like a cart going over cobbles.

The names went on.

He read his page at midnight.

He hadn’t been sure he’d be able to. He’d been holding the threads for four hours. His head felt like a bell somebody had been ringing. He could feel every one of them, thirty-five and the amber one, all the time, every hit, every heal, every mana-drop. But Brenna came along the tunnel at midnight with snow on her shoulders and the scar white through her lip, and touched his arm, and said you’re next, and he got up.

He sat on the stool. They put the book on his knees. The lantern beside him. The place on the wall. A section about halfway along the tunnel, at head height. Names from four or five hundred years ago, he guessed. Still clear. Still deep.

He put his finger on the first one.

He read it.

He didn’t know any of them. Of course he didn’t. They’d died holding a pass he’d never heard of, on a floor of a tower he’d never imagined, hundreds of years before he was born. He read them anyway. Slowly. Carefully. Each one, and the little line under it, and the next. A name. Somebody’s son, somebody’s daughter. A wall. A night. And the line.

The line under the name was the thing. It got him, about halfway down the page. Every name with the same short line under it, cut deep, the line you put under something to show it’s finished. Thousands of them. Each one somebody who’d stood in a line on a wall in the snow and covered the person on their left, and not come down.

And somewhere up the pass, through a thread from the middle of his chest, he could feel thirty-five people doing exactly that. Right now. While he read.

As long as somebody’s saying their names, Brenna had said, they’re still holding the pass.

He didn’t know if it was true. He found, reading, that he didn’t care whether it was true. He read the names the way you’d hold somebody’s hand.

And at the bottom of the page, his voice went.

He didn’t know why. It was just a name. A woman. Six hundred years ago. Fifth wall. Longest night. And the line. He read it and his voice just stopped, in the middle of the next one, and he sat there on the stool in the lantern light with his finger on the stone, and couldn’t.

Nobody in the tunnel moved.

Then somebody put a hand on his shoulder.

He looked up. It was Ansa. Tamm’s mother. Standing beside the stool, in grey, with her braid down her back and her shield on her back and snow melting on her shoulders, because she’d been on the wall and come down for this, for an hour, to hear the names.

She didn’t say anything. She just stood there with her hand on his shoulder.

He finished the page.

The threads went wrong at about three in the morning.

He was back on the floor at the back of the tunnel. He’d given the stool to a hold-folk boy younger than Tamm, who read in a voice that cracked every third name and didn’t stop. Deston had his eyes shut and his back against the wall and the gold going out of him, the way it had been going for eleven hours, and he was so tired he’d stopped feeling tired.

And the amber thread went thin.

Not the gold one. Not one of his. The other one. The one at the fifth wall. In the line. With Gabe’s thread across it.

He felt it go thin. And then flare. Bright. Brighter than he’d ever felt it, brighter than on the jetty. Going out. Out of the line, out across the parapet, out to—

He opened his eyes.

“Calvin,” he said. “Fifth wall. Now. Declan—“

He felt Calvin go. And then he felt the others. All of them. All at once. The gold threads, every one of them, turning. On the cliffs, on the fourth level, at the house for the wounded, on the wall. Thirty-five threads all turning at once, toward the amber, the way they’d turned in the Down on floor 9 toward a light.

He knew what it was before he understood it.

Declan had knelt.

Sanctuary. In the line, on the fifth wall, in the snow, with the Winter coming down on all of them harder than it had ever come. He’d knelt. And the amber was going out of him, the way it had gone out on the jetty on floor 8, through all of them, through Deston’s threads, to all thirty-five at once. Where you stand, they are safe.

And coming back.

Deston felt it come back. All of them. Their light, coming back down the threads to the amber. Rachael’s blue. Xola’s water. Calvin’s wind. Gabe’s weight. Thea’s ward. And his own gold. His own. Going out of him, not to share mana, not to steady anybody, just going. To Declan. Kneeling. In the snow.

He hadn’t known he could do that.

He sat on the floor of the tunnel with his back against nine hundred years of names and gave Declan everything he had left.

At the stool, the hold-folk boy read the next name. And the next.

The reading reached the fifth wall at a quarter to eight.

Deston went up for it. Everybody did, who wasn’t on a wall. Up through the city, through the tunnels of the second wall and the third and the fourth, every one of them lit with lanterns and full of hold-folk reading, the last pages of each, the names getting newer. Up the long bare climb past the last houses. In the grey. The snow had stopped an hour before.

The fifth wall was quiet.

The line was still on it. Hold-folk and climbers, shoulder to shoulder, shields down now, leaning on them. Grey with cold, white with frost. The slope below the wall was the worst Deston had ever seen it. Armour and ice everywhere, all the way down into the dark. The Winter had come harder than any night of the year, the way Brenna had said. And the line had held.

Declan was sitting on the parapet with his back against it and his lamp shield across his knees and his eyes shut. Gabe beside him. On his right. With Hearthwall’s edge across him. Thea a little way off, with the lantern.

Nobody had died on the fifth wall. Deston had felt every thread all night, and he knew. Nobody. Not a climber. Not a hold-folk soldier. Not one.

The first time in nine hundred years, Brenna said afterwards. On the Long Night. Not one.

They read the last names in the tunnel of the fifth wall.

The tunnel was small. Narrow. Not many names. It was the newest wall; it had only been there four hundred years. They stood packed into it, hold-folk and climbers, in the grey light coming in at both ends, and Brenna sat on the stool with the book. She read the last page herself. The names of this winter.

Six names from the fourth wall, from the night of the cliffs. She read them first. Slowly. Each one, and the line.

And then the last one.

“Tamm,” said Brenna. “Son of Ansa. Fifth wall. First night.”

The tunnel was absolutely silent.

At the back, Ansa stood with her fist on her chest and her eyes shut. Rui Xiang was beside her. He’d come down off the wall to stand there. He had a knife in his hand, and he wasn’t spinning it. He was just holding it.

And then, from the end of the tunnel, where it opened onto the parapet and the morning, Azac spoke.

Not loudly. In his storyteller’s voice. The one he used for the device.

“And so the fourth company,” said Azac, “and the third, and the fifth, and all of them, stood on the wall on the longest night of the year. And the Winter came down harder than it ever had. And every shield in the line covered the one on its left, and nobody covered himself. And the names were read. All of them. All night. From the first stone.”

Nobody moved.

“And the line held,” said Azac. His voice shook. “And nobody fell. Not one.”

He stopped.

“And then,” said Azac, “the light came up over the low end of the pass.”

And it did.

Deston saw it. Through the end of the tunnel, past Azac, past the parapet. Gold. Thin and cold and gold, coming up over the mountains at the low end of the pass, all along the tops of them, and down. Onto the snow. Onto the five walls going down the pass one below the other. Onto the city. Onto the line on the parapet, leaning on its shields.

At the back of the tunnel, Ansa made a sound. Not crying. Something else. She put her hand over her mouth.

“That’s how it ends,” said Azac. “It always ends with the light coming up.”

He found Declan an hour later, still on the parapet.

Everybody else had gone down. To sleep, or eat, or both. The hold-folk to the long halls, the climbers to the barracks. Gabe had gone to take the shield to Rachael at the house for the wounded, where there’d be a line of frozen fingers from the night; he’d looked at Deston, going past him on the stair, and Deston had felt the thread between them pull. Just a bit. Then let go.

Declan was sitting where he’d been sitting. Back to the parapet. Lamp shield across his knees. Eyes open now. Looking up the pass. At the high side, going up into the mountains in the morning light, white and gold and empty.

Deston sat down next to him. On his left. Gabe’s side was his right; Deston had worked that out on the second day.

They didn’t say anything for a long time.

“You knelt,” said Deston, eventually.

“Yeah.”

“I felt it. In the tunnel. All of it.” He paused. “I didn’t know I could give you that.”

“Neither did I,” said Declan.

They sat there.

Deston put his hand in his shirt. Into the left pocket. And unbuttoned it, and took out the note.

It was soft now. Worn at the corners. His own card from the white room, his name on the front, and on the back, in the grey of a fingertip, because for the first minute the cards had taken a fingertip as ink, before anybody else was awake. He laid it on his knee. In the morning light. be careful, train longer on the 3rd floor - D.

Declan looked at it. Deston felt him look. Felt the amber go very still.

“I’ve still got it,” said Deston.

“I know,” said Declan. “Gabe said. On seven.”

“It’s a pocket,” said Deston. “Gabe said that too.”

Declan almost laughed.

Deston looked at the note for a long time.

“Gabe knows something,” he said. Quietly. Not looking at Declan. “Xav does. Thea does. Rachael doesn’t, but she will, I think, soon. She’s started looking at you the way she looks at a wound she can’t find.” He looked at the card on his knee a moment longer. “You looked at Dennis’s arm on the third level like you’d seen it before. Not his arm. That. Him. Running to her.”

Declan didn’t move.

“I’m not asking,” said Deston.

He put the note back in his pocket. He buttoned it.

“I’ve known you since you were thirteen,” he said. “Second row. You sang the wrong words every week, on purpose, and looked like a little angel, and I never once told on you.” He was quiet for a moment. “I’ve never needed you to tell me anything, Dec. Not once. I always just knew you’d be there. In the second row. Next to me.”

He looked up the pass. At the gold on the snow.

“This is the first thing you said to anybody,” he said, and touched his pocket. “After. Whatever after was. You wrote it before any of us woke up. And you said it to me.”

He felt the amber shake.

“That’s enough,” said Deston. “For me. That’s all I need. Whatever it is, the thing. I’ve already got the bit that was mine.”

Declan didn’t say anything. He couldn’t. Deston didn’t need him to.

He did what he’d done on the jetty on floor 8. He held his hand out. On the stone of the parapet, between them. Palm up.

Declan looked at it for a long time.

Then he put his own in it. His left. With the scar across the palm.

They sat there. Two people from the second row, on the top of the highest wall in the world, in the morning. Not saying anything. The light came down the pass onto the snow, gold, and onto them.

And somewhere far up the high side, where the throat of the pass went up into the mountains, where the Rimed came from, where Declan had said on the stair there was a gate, Deston felt something.

Not through a thread. He didn’t have a thread up there. Nobody did. He just felt it. The way you feel somebody looking at the back of your neck in a crowded room.

He turned his head and looked up the pass.

There was nothing there. Snow, and rock, and the morning.

Beside him, Declan had gone very still. He was looking up the pass too.

“Dec,” said Deston.

“I know,” said Declan. “I felt it.” He didn’t let go of Deston’s hand. “It’s been looking since floor 8. It’s just closer now.”

Deston looked at him.

“What is?”

Declan was quiet for a long time.

“Whatever’s at the gate,” he said.
