Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 61

Including You

3,710 words · 16 min read

He made camp in the old cistern, half a night’s walk west of the bowl, and tried to work out how to kill a table.

The cistern had been part of something once. A waystation, maybe, for caravans that didn’t cross the Glass any more. There were four broken walls of fused black glass round a square of sand, and in the middle, a round stone mouth going down into a dry pit. The pit was cool. That was the whole of its charm. Five metres down, out of the sun, the air was almost bearable in the middle of the day. He slept down there in the heat with his water skins round him like sandbags and the helm for a pillow. At dusk he climbed out and went to look at the bowl.

He tried it with his eyes shut on the hundred-and-third day.

It was simple. It showed you things, so don’t look. He came down into the bowl at dusk with the helm on and his eyes shut behind the slit, and Alea on his shoulder, telling him where to put his feet. Left. Straight. Little bit right. Dune, step up. He went down across the glass toward the Still Water blind, one step at a time, with his sword drawn.

He heard them at about a hundred metres.

Laughing. The same as before. Gabe, big and stupid. Azac’s and THEN. Calvin. Bread being passed. The scrape of the benches on the floor of the Spoon, on a floor that wasn’t there. He kept his eyes shut. He kept walking.

“Fifty metres,” said Alea, in his ear. “Keep going. It’s a hole. Keep—”

“Declan.”

He stopped.

Not Gabe. Not the laughing. A voice right in front of him, low and level, close enough to touch. A woman’s voice. A voice he hadn’t heard say his name, the first time, for more than a few seconds, on a stair in the Red City on the thirtieth day.

“I went past you on the stair,” said Rachael. Very gently. “I know. It’s all right. You didn’t see me go.” A pause. “Sit down, Declan. Please. It’s all right now.”

He stood in the dark with his eyes shut and his sword in his hand.

He knew those words. Not hers. His. He’d said them on his knees in a stone cell on floor 4 on the fifty-eighth day, with his palms open. I let Rachael run past me. On the stair. I didn’t even see her go. He’d set them down on the stone in front of the grille, where he could see them.

And here they were. In her voice. Turned round, and handed back to him, with forgiveness on them. The thing he’d never get. The thing she’d never had the chance to say.

“Ice,” said Alea. “Ice, that’s you. That’s your words. In her mouth. It’s—”

“I know.”

“Then walk.”

He didn’t walk. He couldn’t. He stood there with his eyes shut, in the dark, in front of a voice saying it’s all right in Rachael’s mouth. For a long moment he wanted nothing in the world more than to open his eyes and sit down.

He went back. Eyes shut, step by step, with Alea’s voice steering him, until the laughing stopped behind him like a door.

He tried it at night on the hundred-and-fifth. Properly at night, at midnight, with no moon. In case the Wanting was a mirage and mirages needed the sun.

It didn’t need the sun.

At night there were lanterns. Hung from the canopy poles, warm and yellow, the way Mother Crane hung them along the rafters of the Spoon when it got dark. The table glowed in the middle of the black bowl like a lit window seen from a road on a winter night. And they were all there, in the lamplight, warmer and softer than in the day. Somebody was singing. Higu, maybe, low. Chi had Waddles on the bench beside her, asleep. Thea at the far end, with her chin on her hand, looking at the empty seat.

It was worse at night. It was so much worse.

He didn’t get within two hundred metres. He stood on the glass in the dark and looked at the lit window in the middle of the bowl. Then he turned round and walked back to the cistern, and sat at the bottom of the dry pit until dawn with his hands over his face.

He tried going round.

It took him three days. He went north along the rim of the bowl, staying high, and came down from the north. The shimmer was there in front of the water when he came down. He went south, a whole night, and came down from the south. The shimmer was there. He went the long way, east, two nights out round the far side of the bowl through dunes nobody walked, with his water running low. And he came down into the bowl from the east, from the side nobody had ever come, at dawn.

The table was there.

Between him and the Still Water. In front of the blue. Exactly where he’d have to go. Always.

“It moves,” said Alea, on the eastern rim, in the dawn. She sounded hollow. “It doesn’t guard the water. It guards you. Wherever you come from, it’s in front of you.”

He sat down on the rim of the bowl.

“It’s not guarding the Prism,” he said slowly. “Is it.”

“No.”

“The Prism doesn’t care if I get through. The Prism’s just a boss. This—“ He looked down at the gold canopy in the bowl, the long table, the faces. “This is for me. It doesn’t want me to get to the water. It wants me to sit down.”

“It wants you to stop,” said Alea quietly. “That’s all it wants. It’s made of everything you carry, and it’s holding it out to you, and saying stop. Put it down. Sit. You’re home.”

He looked at the empty seat between Gabe and Thea.

“And if I sat down?”

The fox didn’t answer for a long time.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I don’t think you’d get up.”

On the hundred-and-ninth day, he walked in with his sword drawn and his eyes open.

He’d decided. On the walk back from the east, with his water down to a mouthful and his lips cracked, he’d decided. It wasn’t them. He knew it wasn’t. He’d known it the first time, even while he walked. It was a hole shaped like a table, Alea said, and it was eating him. If he could just hit it, if he could make his arm swing once, it would break. Mirages broke.

He came down into the bowl in the late afternoon with the sun behind him and the sword up. He walked straight at the table. He didn’t stop when he heard them. He didn’t stop when Gabe looked up.

“Dec?” said Gabe.

He walked up to the end of the table with his sword raised.

Gabe looked at the sword. Then at him. And his face did something it had done once before, in the first run, on floor 9. Ice had come back into a camp after three days alone on the far side of the floor, half dead, with his shield in pieces, and Gabe had looked up from the fire.

Not afraid. Gabe was never afraid of him. Worried. Dec. What happened to you? What are you doing?

“Dec,” said Gabe, quietly. “Hey. Hey. What are you doing, man?”

His arm didn’t come down.

He stood at the end of the table with the sword up over Gabe’s head and his arm wouldn’t do it. It just wouldn’t. He’d killed a thousand things in ten years. He’d killed things that wore people’s faces, on floor 47, on floor 82. He knew it wasn’t Gabe. He knew. Gabe was on floor 5 on the Kitecliff with Pell, learning to lean.

But it had Gabe’s face. Gabe’s worried face. The one Gabe used when he looked at you across a fire and saw you weren’t okay. And Ice’s arm, which had been trained for ten years to stand between that face and everything in the world, would not swing at it.

“It’s not him,” said Alea.

“I know.”

“Ice—”

“I know, Alea.” His voice cracked. “I know it’s not him. I can’t do it. I can’t make my arm do it. It’s his face.”

He lowered the sword.

Gabe smiled at him. Relieved. There you are. And moved up on the bench to make room.

Ice turned round and walked away. Not running. He couldn’t run on the glass in the heat. Just walking, with the sword hanging from his hand, and Gabe’s voice behind him saying Dec? Dec, come on, where are you going? Until it stopped like a door.

“You can’t hit it,” said Alea, that night, at the bottom of the cistern. “Because you can’t stop seeing them.”

He was lying on his back in the sand with his arm over his eyes. He didn’t answer.

“It doesn’t matter whether you look or don’t look,” said Alea. “Eyes shut, you hear them. Night, it’s worse. Round the side, it’s there. It’s not in your eyes, Ice. It’s not even in the bowl. It’s in you. It’s made of you. You can’t walk past it because it’s built out of the thing that makes you walk.”

“I know.”

“You need something,” said Alea, “that makes a lie look like what it is.”

He took his arm off his eyes and looked up at her. She was sitting on the stone lip of the cistern above him, small and teal and white against the stars.

He knew what she was going to say. He’d known since the table.

“The lantern,” said Alea.

He didn’t say anything.

Lure-glass Lantern. He’d cut it out of the Lanternjaw’s mouth on floor 3, on the seventeenth day, in the black water of the Mere. A little glass lantern with a light in it that made other lights tell the truth. Lights that lied, lights that showed you home, lights that walked you into the water with your face calm. When you held it up, they went pale and thin and looked like what they were. Pictures of lights, painted on the dark.

He’d left it on the bench in the ascension room on floor 3. Lit. For whoever came next.

found the lantern. i’m carrying it. the wisps tell the truth now.

“Thea’s got it,” he said.

“Thea’s got it.”

“I gave it to them. For the Fen. The wisps took her the first time, in the first week. I pulled her out by her jersey.” He looked up at the stars. “They needed it more.”

“They needed it on three,” said Alea. “You need it on six.” She was quiet a moment. “You gave away the one thing on any floor that could get you past this. To the one person who—“ She stopped.

“Don’t.”

“I’m just saying it.”

“I know you are.” He put his arm back over his eyes. “I’m not asking for it.”

“Ice.”

“I’m not asking her for it. I’m not asking any of them for anything. Not yet.” His voice was very flat. “I’ll find another way.”

The fox didn’t argue. She didn’t need to. There wasn’t another way, and they both knew it. And he lay at the bottom of the dry cistern in the dark and didn’t sleep.

He read World every night, at the bottom of the cistern, with Alea reading it to him in her dry voice. It was the only thing that helped.

On the hundred-and-third night, she found the thread from the evening before.

She went very quiet before she read it. He’d learned that meant something.

“Drew,” she said. “Last night. On five. The thirty-five of them are still on five.” She paused. “Read it yourself.”

They went on. And on. Down and down the log. Yan. Salty. Wendy. Tiger. Cursiffe. Hana. Milli: including you. i did it once. don’t. Jared. Aleaka. Hyvred, in capitals. Gigi. Bee. Kei. Xav: just two words, nothing else. Chris. Niko. Maigo. Winter. Ina. Azac. Dennis. John. Higu. Xola. Chi.

The last one was Thea.

[ World · Althea: including you. ]

With a full stop. The only one with a full stop.

He lay in the dark at the bottom of the cistern and looked at it for a very long time.

“Thirty-five,” said Alea quietly. “I counted.”

“I did the maths,” he said. His voice had gone. “Hako. We did the maths. They worked it out. The bonus. Alone, outnumbered.” He put the back of his wrist against his eyes. “They worked out what it costs and they voted. All of them.”

“And they said including you.”

“I can’t, Alea.” It came out of him cracked. “I can’t. I’m on six. There’s a thing between me and the water made out of all of them, and I can’t get past it alone, and I can’t get past it with anyone, because there isn’t anyone. They’re on five.” He swallowed. “If it happens, we come and get you. They can’t come and get me. They don’t know where I am. They don’t know what’s here.”

“No,” said Alea. “Not yet.”

He didn’t sleep that night either.

On the hundred-and-tenth night there was better news. He held on to it.

Gabe leaned.

He laughed. On his back at the bottom of the cistern, in the dark, he laughed until he had to roll over, and then he lay on his side with his face in the sand and his shoulders shaking.

Nan Gully. Triple. Word for word. Lean.

“She told him,” said Alea. She was laughing too, a little, in her dry way. “She told him and he went up to Pell and he learned it. Ice. Gabe leaned.”

“Gabe leaned,” he said into the sand. “Gabe. Who’s stood like a door since he was thirteen. Because I taught him to.”

“And now somebody else taught him different.”

He lay there a long time, with that.

On the hundred-and-eleventh night, it was Niko.

He sat up.

He sat at the bottom of the dry cistern and read it, and read it again. And he found that he’d put his hand over his mouth without meaning to, the scarred one, and that it was shaking.

Hessa’s alive. She waited.

The first time, Niko had found her book on the thirty-first floor.

A red one, in a dead library, with no title on the spine. He’d sat up three nights reading it. Then he’d come to Ice’s fire with it under his arm and his face white, and said, Dec. There’s a class. For me. A knight who reads. And then, It starts on five. On an island with gulls. You have to start it before thirty. Niko had been a hundred and fifty-something by then. And he’d gone back down anyway. Twenty-six floors, on his own, against everyone’s advice, just to see. Ice had gone with him, because you didn’t let anybody go anywhere alone.

The tower on Gull Island had been empty. The door was open. The gulls were nesting in the doorway, on the floor, on the narrow bed, in the cold hearth. There was a sword on the wall above a shelf of three books. Nan Gully, very old by then, had told them at the ferry. Hessa? Died the winter before last. In her chair. Waiting for something, folk said. Never said what.

Niko hadn’t said a word all the way back up. Not on any of the twenty-six floors. He’d carried that red book for the rest of his life, and never opened it again.

Please stay alive till he comes.

He’d stood in her door on the eighty-eighth day and looked at her alive. Eighty-four, straight as a post, with her one sharp eye. And he hadn’t been able to stop seeing her chair, and the gulls in the hearth, and Niko’s white face. And he’d said please. Twice. Like a fool. Like a man who’d been to her funeral, which he had.

She waited.

“Alea,” he said. His voice was barely there. “Log it.”

“Already did.” The fox’s voice was very soft. She’d been there. She’d stood in that empty doorway too, the first time, on his shoulder. “Hessa: alive. With the others. Mother Crane, Mother Tallow. Rachael. Dennis. Calvin’s arm.” A pause. “Hessa.”

“How many’s that?”

“Changed things?” Alea counted. “On the list I keep. Twenty-three, now. Things that went wrong the first time that didn’t, this time.” She was quiet for a moment. “Hessa’s the first one you saved by asking somebody to wait.”

He sat in the dark with that.

He’d spent a hundred and eleven days going first. Standing in front of everything. Killing the thing on the roof before anyone came down the stair. And this one he’d saved with one word, twice, on an old woman’s doorstep. Please. And then he’d walked away and left it to her, and to Niko, and to time. And she’d done it. Out of spite, probably. But she’d done it.

He thought about thirty-five people saying including you.

On the hundred-and-sixteenth night, Drew posted again, and Ice understood that they’d started looking.

“Ice,” said Alea slowly. “The statue under the third bridge. That’s—“

“I know what it is.”

He did. He’d never found it, the first time. Nobody had. He knew about it the way he’d known about the Paladin chain: too late, from a book, on a far floor, years after. An Archivist’s chain. A hidden one. For somebody who wrote things down, and made them hold. Cursiffe.

“They’re going to find things,” said Alea. “Things you never found the first time. Things nobody found.”

“Good,” he said.

He meant it. He did. He lay at the bottom of the cistern and felt it, warm, a thing he hadn’t felt in a long time. Thirty-five people looking. Not waiting for him to find it and leave it on a bench. Looking. Writing it down. Going together.

And under it, cold, the other thing.

It can’t see you. It can only see what changes around you.

Every hidden quest they found was something changing. Something new, something that hadn’t happened the first time. And somewhere, something was watching what changed. It had watched him stand in a fire and changed a bird. It had listened to him say every name in a stone room and built a table.

What would it build out of thirty-five people looking for hidden things?

“Alea,” he said. “The Wanting.”

“What about it?”

“It shows you what you want. More than anything.” He looked up at the stars over the lip of the cistern. “That’s what it does. That’s what it’s for. It was made for me, out of what I told it. But it doesn’t—it’s not only for me. It’s an elite. It’s on the floor. Anybody who walks into that bowl—“

He stopped.

“It’ll show them what they want,” said Alea. Very quietly.

“Yes.”

“Ice.” The fox’s voice had gone very careful. “What do they want? Thirty-five people. More than anything. What would it show them?”

He didn’t answer.

He didn’t have to. He’d read it. Every night, for a hundred and sixteen nights, in World, in whispers, in Gabe’s dec. i know its you. In Chris’s graph and Hyvred’s trail. In Thea carrying the lantern everywhere. In thirty-five people saying including you.

He knew exactly what they wanted.

He lay at the bottom of the dry cistern on floor 6 and stared up at the stars. In two weeks, maybe less, thirty-five people were going to come up the stair from the Spindle into the Glass Waste. And somewhere in the middle of it, between them and the water, there was something waiting. Made out of everything he’d ever wanted. And it was going to look at each one of them in turn and show them him.
