Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 144

And So Our Hero

6,061 words · 26 min read

“And so our hero,” said Azac, to the bucket, “was asked to make a speech.”

He was sick in it.

Not very. Just once, quietly, in the corner of the stable under the copy-room, at dawn, where nobody could see. He’d been feeling it come since Deston said his name four nights ago, and it had come. He wiped his mouth on the back of his hand and sat back on his heels and looked at the bucket.

“And was sick in a bucket,” he said. “Which nobody ever puts in the songs.”

“I’ll put it in,” said Dennis, from the stable door.

Azac didn’t look round. “How long have you been there?”

“Long enough.” Dennis came and crouched beside him and looked into the bucket with professional interest. “That’s mostly ham.”

“Thank you, Dennis.”

“You’ll be fine,” said Dennis. “You tell stories to forty children a day on the well-house steps. I’ve watched you. They’d follow you off a cliff.”

“Children are easy. Children want a story.” Azac stood up. His knees cracked. “The Bench doesn’t want a story. The Bench wants a piece of paper in the plain form with two hands on it. Who came, what they asked, what they were told. No seemed. No perhaps.” He picked up the bucket. “I’ve been to watch. Twice. Do you know what they do in there? They read. Somebody stands up and reads a paper, and somebody else stands up and reads a paper, and twelve old men read the two papers, and then they write something. In four hours, the only thing anybody said that wasn’t written down first was sit down.”

“So write it down.”

“I can’t.” Azac looked at the bucket. “That’s the thing, Dennis. I tried. Three nights. I sat up with Cursiffe’s ink and I tried to put it in the plain form, and every time, it came out—“ He stopped.

“Came out what?”

“And so our hero,” said Azac.

He’d been to watch the Bench twice, and both times he’d come out feeling cold.

It sat in the lower hall of the Assize, which was under the great hall, at the top of the hill, behind the pale wall. You went in through a small door in the side of the wall by Aldo’s row of houses, and down a flight of steps, into a long low stone room with pillars, like the crypt of a church, lit by high narrow windows and a great many candles. At the far end, on a raised step, there was a long curved bench of dark wood, and on it sat the twelve Aldermen of Tell, in black, with chains on.

And in the middle of them, in the tallest chair, sat Hamo Tull.

Azac had wanted him to look like a villain. He didn’t. He was a big, soft, well-kept man of about sixty, with a pleasant face and white hands and a ring on every finger, and a deep, warm, reasonable voice that made everything he said sound like something you’d been about to say yourself. He listened to every matter with his head on one side and his rings laced on his stomach. He was courteous to everybody. He smiled at children.

And behind his chair, every time, standing a little apart against the pillar, in long plain grey, with a book on its arm, there was a clerk of the Count.

It wasn’t always the same one. Azac didn’t think it was. But there was always one. Standing behind the Warden’s chair, looking at nothing, while Hamo Tull read papers and smiled.

“And so our hero,” Azac had said to himself, coming out into the sun the second time, “went to look at the dragon, and found that the dragon was a nice old man with rings on, and that was worse.”

Joan Tull came to the copy-room on the night before the Bench, with Hawise, and asked for Cursiffe.

Azac was there. They were all there. It was the only room they had. Joan stood just inside the door in her brown dress, with her hands folded, and said what she’d come to say, and didn’t say anything else.

“I want to bring my matter to the Bench tomorrow,” she said. “The will. In front of him. With Hawise for my first hand.” She looked at Cursiffe, on his blanket at the end of the room. “And I want you to do it again. What you did. In front of all of them. So they can see it.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“If he does that,” said Deston slowly, from the step, “in front of the Bench. In front of Hamo Tull. Then Hamo Tull—“

“Will hate you for the rest of his life,” said Joan. “Yes.”

“He already does,” said Dennis.

“He’ll hate you more.” Joan didn’t look away from Cursiffe. “I know what I’m asking. I’ve thought about it for four days. I’m asking anyway.”

“Is it ours to do?” said Drew. Not to Joan. To all of them. “That’s what I want to know. It’s her matter. It’s her shop. It’s Tell’s law. If we stand up in the middle of it with—with whatever Cursiffe does—“

“It’s mine,” said Joan. “My matter. I’m bringing it. On my own feet.” She looked at Drew then. “I’m asking for his help. That’s all. The way you’d ask a teller to write for you. He can say no.”

Cursiffe hadn’t moved. He sat on his blanket with the iron key round his neck and his hands on his knees, looking at his hands. The ink on them.

“It costs,” he said.

“I know,” said Joan.

“I don’t know how much. Water Street took everything I had and a dead city’s worth of help.” He looked up. “And it might not work. A will’s a small thing. One page. Four winters. It’s kept, because Hawise remembers it, and because it happened. That should be enough. But I don’t know.”

“Then try,” said Joan.

Cursiffe looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked across the room. Not at Deston, or Drew. At the end wall. At Declan.

Azac saw him do it. He didn’t know why Cursiffe did it. He saw Declan look back, with his face doing nothing at all, and the cord on his wrist. And then, very slightly, Declan nodded.

Cursiffe looked back at Joan.

“Yes,” he said.

Declan found him on the roof, after.

Azac had gone up there to practise, because the copy-room had thirty-five people in it and every one of them had an opinion about how he should start. He was walking up and down the flat lead roof in the dark, saying things to the parapet, and stopping, and saying them again.

Declan came up the ladder and sat down on the edge of the hatch and didn’t say anything. He just watched Azac walk up and down.

“And so our hero,” said Azac, eventually, to the parapet, “was watched by his oldest friend while he made a fool of himself, which is the worst kind of watched.”

“You’re not making a fool of yourself.”

“I’m talking to a wall.”

“Walls are good,” said Declan. “You told me that.”

Azac stopped walking.

“Brenna told me that,” he said. “On floor 10. You’re a good shield. For one man.” He turned round. “I told you it. On the fifth wall. After.”

“I know.”

Azac came and sat on the parapet, across from him. Below them, the Low Town was going to bed, light by light, down the hill. Somewhere a dog was barking. Somewhere further off, somebody was singing.

“Declan,” he said.

“Mm.”

“Tomorrow. I’m going to tell them about us. All of us. The floors. Everything we’ve—“ He stopped. “Do you want me to leave you out?”

Declan didn’t answer for a while.

“Yes,” he said.

“Okay.”

“Not because—“ Declan stopped. Looked at his wrist. At the cord. “It’s for the Magistrate. My bit. Not the Bench. If you tell them about me, it’s the first thing Hamo Tull’s going to pick up and hit you with. The corded one. The one the Count says is already told.” He looked up. “Tell them about you. All of you. That’s the bit that’ll work.”

“I wasn’t going to tell them about you anyway,” said Azac.

Declan looked at him.

“It’s not mine,” said Azac. “I’ve known that since the fifth wall. Whatever the bit about you is. It’s not mine to tell.” He shrugged. “And so our hero left out the most interesting part of the story, and everybody said it was the best one he’d ever told.”

Declan laughed. Short, surprised, into his hands.

“You’ll be good,” he said.

“You don’t know that.”

“I do,” said Declan. And then, very slightly, something went across his face, the way it did sometimes, as if he’d heard himself say something and wished he hadn’t. He looked down at the Low Town. “You were always the one who talked. Back home. I stood in front, and you talked. You were better at it than I ever was.”

“At talking.”

“At the bit where you let them cover you.” Declan didn’t look at him. “I never learned that. You did. On a wall. From a stranger.”

Azac sat very still on the parapet.

He’d thought that. Nearly word for word. On the fifth wall, in his head, after the Long Night. And so our hero learned the thing his friend had never learned. He’d never said it out loud. He’d never said it to anybody.

He looked at Declan for a long time. Declan was looking at the city.

Azac didn’t say anything. Some things you just let your oldest friend get right.

The lower hall of the Assize was full by the time the bell went, and Azac had never seen so many people in a room so quiet.

Word had got round. It always did, in Tell. It went up and down the Wellgate faster than Calvin did. They’d come up from the Low Town, a great many of them, and stood at the back of the crypt between the pillars, packed close, in their market clothes, in silence: the apple-sellers and the fishwives and the coopers from Cooper’s Lane, the watch captain Wat with his moustache, the old woman from the half-door with a basket on her arm. Tellers in brown, along one wall, with Askew in front of them on a stool with her stick across her knees. Hawise, beside Joan, at the front. Orlo, very tall, at the back, craning.

And Aldo. They’d carried him down. Wat and Aldo’s grandson Davy, in his rush chair, all the way down the steps from his green door and in through the little door in the wall and down the crypt stairs, and set him down at the end of the front row, with his rug over his knees and his bright bird’s eyes on the bench.

The climbers stood on the left, in a block, with their grey threads on their wrists. Declan wasn’t with them. He was at the back, by the stairs, against a pillar, with Thea. Where he could see and not be seen. The cord on his wrist was under his sleeve.

On the bench, the twelve Aldermen sat in their black and their chains. Hamo Tull in the middle, smiling, with his rings laced.

Behind his chair, against the pillar, a clerk.

The Warden’s matter was first.

Tull read it himself, in his warm reasonable voice, from a paper in two hands. That the climbers who came up the well, being held to the Rolls by order of the Bench until the Reckoning, should not be written by any citizen of Tell under the old custom called the host’s hand, nor told by any hand but the Count’s, until the Magistrate had heard the dispute concerning one of their company. For the peace of the Rolls. Which had been troubled.

He looked up from the paper, and smiled at the climbers, kindly.

“I think we all know how troubled,” he said.

There was a murmur at the back. Not all of it unfriendly to him. Azac heard it, and felt it, and didn’t like it. A great many people in Tell had seen something in the Echo they’d rather not have. Wace the moneylender was in the second row with his arms folded.

“Does anybody answer?” said Tull.

Deston stepped forward.

He didn’t say much. He never did. He stood in front of the bench with his hand flat on his chest and said, “We answer. We’ve asked one of us to speak for us. Azac.” And stepped back. And as he stepped back, Azac felt it: something warm, landing in his chest, like a hand on his back. Deston’s thread. Gold. Go on.

Azac went forward.

He stood in front of the twelve Aldermen of Tell with nothing in his hands. No paper. No shield. He’d left the shield in the copy-room for the first time since floor 1, and his left arm felt like somebody else’s.

“Read your matter,” said Hamo Tull pleasantly.

“I haven’t written it down,” said Azac.

The crypt went absolutely silent.

He heard it go. Like the market going quiet when they came up the well. Like a held breath. On the bench, two of the Aldermen looked at each other. Hamo Tull’s smile didn’t change at all.

“A matter before the Bench,” he said, gently, as if to a child, “is read, climber. In two hands. That’s how it is in Tell. It’s how it’s always been.”

“There’s no law that a matter be read.”

It was Askew. From her stool by the tellers’ wall, with her stick across her knees. She didn’t stand up. She didn’t raise her voice. She didn’t need to. Every teller in the room straightened like a row of pens.

“There’s a law that a matter be heard,” said the Remembrancer of Tell. “I’ve been reading the Rolls for forty years, Hamo. I’d have noticed.”

Hamo Tull looked at her for a long moment.

Then he smiled, and spread his ringed hands. “Then by all means,” he said. “Let’s hear it.”

Azac opened his mouth.

And nothing came out.

He stood in front of the bench with his mouth open and the whole crypt looking at him and his left arm empty, and every word he’d said to the parapet the night before went out of his head like water out of a sieve. He could feel his face going hot. He could feel Deston’s thread in his chest, warm, steady. He could feel everybody behind him holding their breath.

He thought about the fifth wall. About the tenth night of winter, in a line of strangers, with Orm on his left and Ansa on his right, and the Rimed coming down the pass, and his voice not working either. And Ansa, beside him, saying Breathe.

He breathed.

And then he did what he’d done on the fifth wall, when he couldn’t think of anything else to do. He told it.

“And so,” said Azac.

“And so thirty-five of us woke up in a white room. On a white floor. With thirty-six circles on it. And one of them was empty.

“And we didn’t know where we were. Or why. Or what had happened to the thirty-sixth. And there was a door, and it was open, and we went through it. Because there wasn’t anything else to do. And the first floor was a village with sheep in it, and a sky that was a ceiling, and we were frightened of everything. And nobody in that village ever gave anybody anything. Not because they were mean. Because they didn’t know how.”

He hadn’t known he was going to say that. Dennis had said it, a few nights ago, in the copy-room, about the first market in Hearthstead. It had stuck.

“And we learned to fight. Badly. And we went up. And the higher we went, the more real it got. And on every floor, somebody gave us something.

“On the seventh floor there was a town where the same day had gone round and round for a year, and a baker who got up every morning and baked the same loaf, and asked, every morning, will it ever be tomorrow? And on the last night she climbed a bell tower in the dark and pulled a rope with us, so that it could be.” His voice wobbled. He let it. “And it was.”

“On the eighth floor, a woman walked the river’s dead to the edge of the world, every night, until her arms shook. Because somebody had to.

“On the tenth floor, there was a city on a wall. And they had no market, and no coin, and no prices. And a man called Hesk kept the stores, and gave you what you needed. And one of us”—he didn’t look at Dennis—“couldn’t understand it. He kept asking what anything was worth. And in the end he took everything he had and put it on the floor of the stores, for the wall. And Hesk said: that’s not trading. That’s giving.

“And on that wall, on the longest night of the year, they read the names of everybody who’d ever fallen there. Out loud. Oldest first. From dusk till dawn. Because as long as somebody’s saying their names, they’re still holding the pass.”

He heard it. Behind him. In the crowd at the back. A kind of shiver. A shift of feet.

Out loud. From dusk.

Every evening in Tell, at dusk, a bell rang three times from the Tellhouse, and a teller stood on the steps and read the names of the dead.

He kept going.

“And on the eleventh floor there was a sea. And a shipwright called Corran, eighty-nine years old, who built a ship over eleven years and gave it to his granddaughter. And on the last night of his life he said three hundred and ten names out loud, of everybody the sea had taken, that nobody had said for three hundred years. And one of us wrote them down. And one of us learned them all. And called them back up out of the sea.

“And on the twelfth floor there was nothing. Only salt. And no water. And no sound. And we were thirsty, and one of us made water out of fire and frost, so that nobody went without.

“And then we came up a well.”

He looked at the bench. At twelve old men in black.

“And we came to Tell. And Tell said: you may not trade. And we said, all right. We won’t.” He spread his empty hands. “We gave. Hinges. Water. Window boxes. Forty cuts a day by a pump in a stable yard. A dog that was lost. A husband that was in a tavern.”

A laugh, at the back. Startled out of somebody. Then another.

“And every morning, when we came down the stairs, there was something on the step. Bread. Eggs. A ham.” He paused. “Flour.”

He didn’t look round. He’d heard a sound from somewhere behind him that might have been Bertha the baker.

“Nobody told us who. Nobody wrote it down.”

He stopped.

He didn’t know what came next. He’d had an ending, on the roof. Something about the law. It had gone.

He looked at his left arm. Empty. No shield.

“And so,” he said slowly, “our hero, who has been the tank for three hundred and seventy-six days, stands in front of you without his shield, and he feels very stupid, and very cold.”

Another laugh. Gentler.

“Because that’s what a shield is for,” said Azac. “Not for you. For the one on your left. I learned that on the tenth floor, from a woman whose son had died the first night of winter. She stood on my right in the line, all night, and covered me, and I never once saw her do it. And in the morning, there wasn’t a mark on my right side. And I said thank you. And she said: that’s the wall. You don’t say thank you for the wall.”

He looked at the bench. At Hamo Tull. And then past him, up, at the people at the back of the crypt. At the Low Town.

“In our line,” said Azac, “every shield covers the one on its left. And lets the one on its right cover it. A man with a shield on his own is a good shield. For one man. In a wall there’s no such thing as one man.

“We’ve been standing in Tell for nineteen days. On our own. With our shields up.

“We’re asking Tell to stand on our right.”

He took a breath.

“One each,” he said. “That’s all. The host’s hand. One of you, for one of us.”

And then, because he didn’t know how else to finish it, he said what Ansa had said. On the fifth wall. At dawn.

“Somebody should tell how it ends.”

He stepped back.

Nobody said anything.

The crypt was absolutely silent. Not the silence from before, the held breath. A different one. The kind that comes after the last stroke of a bell, when everybody’s still listening to where it was.

And Azac saw, behind Hamo Tull’s chair, against the pillar, the clerk.

Its book was open on its arm. And its grey pen was moving. Small, even, grey on grey. Writing. He didn’t know how long it had been writing. He thought, looking at it, with his heart going like a drum, that it might have been writing the whole time.

“A fine story,” said Hamo Tull.

He said it warmly. He said it the way you’d say it to a child who’d recited something at a feast. He unlaced his rings and laid his white hands flat on the bench.

“A very fine story. I’m sure we were all moved.” He smiled round at his eleven colleagues. “But the Bench of Tell doesn’t sit on stories, does it. It sits on the Rolls. A story is one voice. One hand, if you like. Not written. Not witnessed. Not told.” He spread his hands. “Tell is two hands. That’s all Tell is. If we start ruling on things because somebody said them beautifully—“

“I’ve a matter.”

Joan Tull had stood up.

She stood at the front, in her brown dress, with Hawise on her right, and her hands at her sides, and looked up at her brother-in-law on the bench.

Hamo Tull’s smile didn’t move. But Azac saw his hands, flat on the bench, go very still.

“Mistress Tull,” he said gently. “We’re hearing the Warden’s matter. Yours is out of order.”

“Every citizen of Tell may bring a matter to the Bench,” said Askew, from her stool, not looking up. “At any sitting. Before a vote. That one is written down, Hamo. I can find you the Roll.”

The Alderman on Tull’s left, a thin old man with a long face, coughed into his hand. Azac thought it might have been a laugh.

Tull looked at Askew. Then at Joan. Then, very briefly, over his shoulder, at the pillar where the clerk stood. The clerk didn’t look back.

“Mistress Tull,” said Tull.

Joan read hers. She had it written. Two hands: hers, and Hawise’s. Piers Tull, fruiterer, of the Wellgate, made his will four winters ago and it was told, in two hands, Teller Hawise Brand and Teller Simon Ashe. It left the shop with the green shutters to his wife. The page was torn from the Roll at the stitching. On the three hundred and sixty-third day of the climbers, at noon, Hawise Brand saw it whole for one breath.

“One breath,” said Tull, when she’d finished. Gently. “One hand. Hawise Brand’s. And Simon Ashe, rest him, is three winters dead and can’t be the second.” He shook his head, kindly. “I’m sorry, Joan. I truly am. But that’s not a will. That’s a wish.”

“Then let him show you,” said Joan.

She turned round, and looked at Cursiffe.

He came forward out of the block of climbers. Slowly, the way he did everything. He had his book under his arm, and a horn of the eel-oil ink, and a pen, and nothing else. Askew got up off her stool and went to a long table under the bench and unrolled a Roll there, a fat one, cream and brown at the edges, and found a place in it, and stepped back. And everybody in the crypt who could see, saw it: a Roll, open, and in the middle of it, at the stitching, a stub. A thin torn edge of paper, a finger’s width, where a page had been.

Cursiffe stood at the table and looked at it.

Then he dipped his pen.

Azac couldn’t see what he wrote. Nobody could, except the Aldermen at the near end of the bench, who’d leaned forward. He wrote on the stub. On the torn edge. Small, and square, and very slowly. A few marks. The way Thea had said he’d done on Water Street: what was there, exactly as it was. Nothing that wasn’t.

And then he stopped, and put his pen down, and put both his hands flat on the table on either side of the Roll, and stood there.

And waited.

Azac would remember the waiting. Longer than anything. Longer than his own silence, standing in front of the bench with his mouth open. The whole crypt holding still. Somebody’s child, at the back, starting to ask something and being shushed. Cursiffe standing at the table with his head bent and his hands flat, not moving at all.

Then the ink moved.

It came up off the stub. Azac saw it. Everybody saw it. A line of brown, standing up off the torn edge like the first frost coming up a window, and then another, and another, and then it wasn’t lines, it was a page. A whole page, standing up out of the stitching where the old one had been torn away, pale and thin as breath at first and then thicker, and thicker, and there. With writing on it. Two hands. Azac could see that from where he stood: two different hands, one under the other, on the same page. One quick and round with a flick on the end of every word.

Hawise made a sound.

“That’s mine,” she said. Her voice broke. “That’s my hand. And that’s—that’s Simon’s. That’s Simon Ashe’s hand, look at his Ts, he always crossed them twice—“

The Aldermen were on their feet. Half of them. Leaning over the end of the bench, over the table, reading. The thin long-faced one beside Tull was reading it aloud, under his breath, his lips moving.

“Piers Tull, fruiterer,” he read, “of the Wellgate. Being of sound mind. Leaves the shop with the green shutters, with its stock and its trees, to Joan his wife. Told. Hawise Brand. Simon Ashe.” He looked up. At Tull. “There’s his mark at the bottom, Hamo. Piers’s mark. I’d know it anywhere. He did my apples for thirty years.”

Hamo Tull hadn’t stood up.

He sat in the tallest chair with his white hands flat on the bench and his rings very still, and looked at the page standing in the Roll, and his pleasant face had gone the colour of tallow.

Cursiffe took his hands off the table.

He swayed. Azac saw it. He swayed, and caught the edge of the table, and stood there with his head down for a long moment, breathing. Then Orlo was there, from nowhere, very tall, with a hand under his elbow. Cursiffe didn’t push it off.

The page stayed.

It didn’t go back down. It stood in the Roll where it had been torn out four winters ago, whole, in two hands, with Piers Tull’s mark at the bottom. Kept.

“Trickery,” said Hamo Tull.

He said it quite quietly. But the warmth had gone out of his voice, all of it at once, like a fire with a bucket thrown on it, and what was left was hard and small and cold.

“Climbers’ trickery. This is exactly—this is exactly what I’ve been saying, from the first. They came up the well and broke the Rolls, and now they choose which bits to mend. They come in here and make pages. Out of nothing. In any hand they like.” He looked round at his colleagues. “And you’ll let them? You’ll let a climber write the Rolls of Tell?”

Nobody answered him.

Behind his chair, against the pillar, the clerk had its book open, and was writing.

They voted on the Warden’s matter first. Because it was first, and because that was how it was done.

Azac didn’t understand all of it. There was a great deal of reading. The Warden’s matter was read again, and Joan’s, and the long-faced Alderman said that Joan’s matter was now a matter of two hands that didn’t agree, the Roll of four winters ago with its torn page and the Roll of today with its page standing in it, and that two hands that didn’t agree were not the Bench’s to settle but the Magistrate’s, at the Reckoning. And Askew said yes. And Hawise said yes. And Hamo Tull didn’t say anything.

So it went to the Assize. Joan Tull’s will. With Declan’s cord. At the Reckoning.

And then they voted on the Warden’s matter. That the climbers held to the Rolls should not be written by any host’s hand until the Magistrate had sat.

It went down the bench one at a time. Each Alderman wrote his vote on a slate, and held it up, and a teller read it out. Azac stood with the others behind him and counted, and couldn’t breathe.

Aye. Aye. No. Aye. No.

Five. Two and three. Then the long-faced one beside Tull.

He sat for a long time with his slate on his knee and his chalk in his hand, not writing. Then he looked up, past the bench, past the climbers, past the Low Town at the back of the crypt. At a long table under the bench, where the Roll of four winters ago lay open with a page standing in it that had been torn out.

“I’m Ivo Rook,” he said. To nobody in particular. In a dry old voice. “I’ve sat on this bench twenty-two years. I was in the Reading Hall at noon on the climbers’ three hundred and sixty-third day, reading my mother’s Roll. Her marriage. And it’s water-stained, the bottom of it, it got wet in a flood when I was a boy, you can’t read her name.” He turned his chalk over in his fingers. “And for one breath, it came back. Her name. In her own hand. She signed it herself. She could write, my mother, not many could then.” He was quiet. “I’d forgotten how she made her R.”

He wrote on his slate. Held it up.

No.

After that it went quickly.

Seven to five. The Warden’s matter fell.

And then, while the crypt was still making a noise like the sea coming in, the long-faced old man who was Ivo Rook stood up, and said, in his dry voice, that he had a matter of his own: that the old custom called the host’s hand was older than the Bench, and that any climber written by a citizen of Tell under it was told by Tell, and was not held by any order, and was free to buy, and sell, and trade, and stand at the Assize, and write whoever they chose. And would anybody second it.

Azac didn’t know who seconded it. He heard Aldo, at the end of the front row, in his rush chair, say “Hah!” very loudly, which was not allowed, and nobody told him to sit down, because he was sitting down.

Seven to five.

They came out of the little door in the side of the Assize wall into the afternoon, all of them, into the sun, and the Low Town came out after them, and there was a crowd on the steps under Aldo’s row of houses that went all the way down the first flight of the Wellgate.

Azac didn’t know what to do with his hands. He’d never stood anywhere for this long without a shield. Calvin was hugging him. Deston had his hand on the back of his neck. Dennis was saying something about ham.

And people were asking things. All round them. On the steps. Not to Azac. To Hawise, who’d come out behind Joan with her satchel over her shoulder and her face still blotched from crying.

“Where do you go?”

“Where do we go to do it? The host’s hand?”

“Can anybody?”

“Does it have to be one you know?”

“The Tellhouse,” said Hawise. She had to raise her voice. She did. “Tomorrow. At the opening bell. The door-roll. Bring yourself. You don’t need anything else.” She looked round at them all, on the steps, in their market clothes. “And make sure you mean it. It’s your name, in the Rolls, next to theirs. For good.”

Azac looked back, once, at the little door in the wall.

Hamo Tull came out of it. Last. Alone. He stood on the top step in his black and his chain, and looked down at the crowd on the Wellgate, and at the climbers in the middle of it, and at Joan Tull, standing with Hawise.

Then he looked at Azac.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. Azac had stood in front of a great many things on a great many floors, and he knew what it looked like when one of them decided you were going to be a problem.

Behind Tull, in the doorway, a clerk stood with its book on its arm. It didn’t look at anybody.

Then Tull turned and walked away along the wall, towards the Assize gate, with the clerk two paces behind him.

“And so our hero,” said Azac quietly, to nobody, “made an enemy.” He watched the black back go. “He’d been expecting to.”

“You were amazing,” said Calvin, into his shoulder.

“I was sick in a bucket.”

“You were sick in a bucket and then you were amazing.”

At the back of the crowd, by the turn of the Wellgate, Azac saw Declan. Standing against the wall of Aldo’s house, with Thea, with the cord under his sleeve, where he could see and not be seen. He wasn’t smiling. He was looking at Azac with a face Azac didn’t have a word for.

Then he lifted one hand. Just a little. Palm out. The way you’d hold up a shield for somebody, from a long way off.

Azac lifted his own back.
