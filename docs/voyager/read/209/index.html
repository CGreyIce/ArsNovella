Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 209

A Bit at a Time

4,722 words · 21 min read

Niko went across the jam three times that night.

Once for four. Once for two. Once for three. Alone, roped to nothing, in the dark, with the river groaning under his boots and the heart going once somewhere far below, and Sabe’s pole going tock on the rock of the ledge behind him so he’d know which way was back. And Tuli’s, a beat later, on the shelf at the foot of the right cliff, so he’d know which way was forward. Two poles. Two old women’s voices, one young and one very old, coming up out of the dark from either side of a quarter-mile of heaved ice. Here. Not there. Wait. Now.

He’d done it the night before, for five, and come back grey, and Chris had looked at him in a way that meant Chris was going to write something in the notebook that Niko would never be allowed to read. He’d said it has to be the night before. It did. That was the whole of it. Wait for Me. Leave a cut where you stood, and it will keep. It had to have somewhere to wait. It had to be left, and then come back for. You couldn’t leave a thing and come back for it in the same breath. That wasn’t waiting. That was just a cut.

So he went in the dark.

Maigo had found the knuckles that afternoon. All three. He’d sat on the rock shelf at the foot of the right cliff with his bare feet on the stone for an hour, with his back burned closed and two leaves gone, and listened to the cliff, and then got up and walked along the shelf and put his hand on four, where it went into the rock. There. And then he’d gone across the jam roped to Chris, behind Sabe, and down onto the left cliff, under the ledge, and put his hand on two. There. And then he’d stood on the top of the jam with his eyes shut for a long time, and pointed at three, high on the right cliff, and said I can’t get to it. But there. Where it bends.

Niko went where Maigo had pointed.

At each one, in the dark, he stood where Maigo had stood. With his feet on the ice or the rock beside the pale arm where it went into the crack. He drew his sword. He put his thumb on the dull place near the guard, and then on the back of the blade, on the letters. I’M HERE. WAIT FOR ME. He didn’t say it. He’d never needed to say it; on the Salt you couldn’t, and he’d learned it there, by thumb, in the dark. He read the back of the blade with his thumb, and cut. Once. Short and flat, at the knuckle, against the arm.

And left it.

The cut stayed. A thin line of orange light in the air against the pale ice, a hand’s length, perfectly still, humming very faintly, the way it had hung in the swept ring on Gull Island in front of an old woman with her sword out. Waiting.

He looked at it each time. The orange line in the dark, against the arm of a thing nobody had ever seen, at the bottom of a frozen river, a long way from anywhere.

Wait for me, he thought at it. Out of habit. The way he’d thought it at his sword every night for five hundred nights.

Then he went back.

“You’ll fall in,” said Tuli, at the foot of the right cliff, when he came down off the jam the third time. She’d been sitting on the shelf for two hours with her pole on the ice going tock every few breaths, so he’d have something to walk toward. Her face was white with cold. She’d refused to go back to the ledge. “You’ll fall in, one night. In the dark. And nobody’ll put a pole down for you.”

“You would,” said Niko.

Tuli looked at him.

“You put one down for Ice,” said Niko. “When he was under. You kept putting it down. You didn’t stop.” He sat down on the rock beside her. He was so cold he couldn’t feel his hands. “So I’m not worried.”

Tuli didn’t say anything for a while. She put her pole down on the ice. Tock. Listened. Lifted it.

“You’re like him,” she said. “The one who said white.”

“Ice.”

“Ice.” She said it like she was trying out a strange word. “You do things in the dark on your own. And come back. And don’t say anything about it.”

Niko thought about that.

“I learned it from him,” he said. “Sort of. A long time ago. Before here. He always did things too early, on his own, in the dark, and came back.” He looked down at his blade. “So did I. I always did things too early.”

“Too early,” said Tuli. “That’s the opposite of the jam.”

Niko looked at her.

“The jam does everything too late,” said Tuli. She was looking at it, in the dark, the great groaning mountain of it between the two cliffs. “It holds on. And on. Past when it should. And then it does everything at once.” She put her pole down. Tock. “You do things too early. On your own. A bit at a time.” She looked at him sideways. “Gran says you’re the right person to teach it.”

Niko didn’t know what to say to that.

“Gran said that?”

“Gran said the small quiet one with the sword that lights up,” said Tuli. “I’m guessing.”

They did four at dawn.

The same as five. Exactly the same. That was the thing Chris kept saying, at the fire, before light, with his notebook open and his arm in a sling Maigo had made from somebody’s shirt. We know how now. It’s the same. Do it the same. Maigo’s hand at the knuckle. Sabe and Tuli on the ice with their poles. Not yet. Not yet. Now. Ina’s hands on the arm, mask down, white-blue going in, the knuckle going clear and steaming. Winter’s arrow. Crack. And Niko’s thumb on the back of his blade.

I’m here.

The cut came home. Along the crack, into the arm, the way a thread goes through a needle. Four let go of the rock.

Niko didn’t see what Chris saw. He never did. He saw what was in front of him: a pale arm as thick as a tree coming slowly out of a crack in the black cliff, split at the knuckle, glistening. And then the jam. The section four had been holding. A bigger bit than the corner, the next bit up the right-hand side, groaning and leaning and sliding, forward, through the gap where the corner had gone yesterday. Through the door. Down. With a sound like a hill coming apart. Spray, and the shelf shaking under him, and Tuli’s hand grabbing his coat.

“Chris,” said Winter.

Chris was standing very still on the shelf with his good hand at his side and blood coming out of his nose, looking at all of it.

“It’s fine,” said Chris. “It’s only four’s bit. It’s going on its own. The rest’s held.” He let his breath out. He sat down. “It’s fine.”

The heart went once, under the ice. Hard. Niko felt it in his boots.

“It’s gripping,” said Ina. Quietly. He had his blistered hands held up in front of him, smoking. “The other three. And the heart. It’s gripping harder. Every time.”

“I know,” said Chris. “I can see it.”

“It’s frightened.”

“I know.”

They did two at noon, from the ledge side, under the left cliff. Maigo found it again in the daylight; it was where he’d said. Niko’s cut was where he’d left it, a thin orange line in the shadow under the ledge, humming. Tuli said not yet for a long time, longer than for four, and then now, and Ina put his hands on it and Niko saw him flinch at the heat in his own palms, and keep them there. Winter’s arrow. Crack. I’m here.

Two let go.

Its bit went. Bigger again. The whole lower left side of the jam, sliding out from under the ledge they’d camped on and grinding through the Narrows, so that the ledge itself shook and stones came down off the cliff above and Sabe, on the ledge with her pole, said a word Niko didn’t know. And then it was gone through the door, and the water came after it, and the gap in the front of the jam was wider now. Much wider. A third of the jam gone. The water going through steady, a river, not a tongue.

Tuli put her pole down on the edge of the ice. Tock.

“Three hands,” she said. Her voice was shaking. “Gran. The lake. It’s down three hands.”

Sabe, on the ledge above, didn’t say anything. She put her own pole down on the rock and leaned on it with both hands and her head bowed, like somebody at a grave, or somebody praying. Niko didn’t know which. He didn’t think it mattered.

Winter had four arrows left.

She told them at the fire, at the noon break, flatly, the way she told everything. She’d shot every arrow she had at the hounds on the first day. She’d gone out on the jam that night, roped, with Ina, and pulled every one she could find out of every dead hound and every slab, and got back fourteen. Four on four. Four on two. Two on the hounds that had come out of the jam after two went; fewer than the day before, six, and they’d gone back in after one of them took Niko’s sleeve and Winter froze it to the ice.

“Four,” said Winter. “Three, and one for whatever.”

“Three’s the high one,” said Chris. “On the right. Above four’s place. Maigo can’t get his feet on the rock near it.”

“I can find it from the top,” said Maigo. “Where it bends. I pointed.”

“Ina can’t get his hands on it from the top,” said Chris. “It’s ten metres down the cliff.”

“I can,” said Ina.

Everybody looked at him.

Ina was sitting by the fire with his blistered hands held out to it. Which was strange, Niko thought, for a man whose hands were burned from the inside. He looked at his palms. The blisters had opened. Maigo had closed them twice. They kept opening.

“I’ll go down the rope,” said Ina. “From the top. Ten metres. Hang off the cliff. Put my hands on it.” He shrugged. “It’s only hanging.”

“Your hands are—“

“I know what my hands are.” Ina didn’t look up. “They’ll do one more. Then I’ll stop.” He was quiet. “Somebody would die, if the Float comes down on the whole jam when it goes. A lot of somebodies. That’s my rule. Not unless somebody would die.” He looked at the fire. “Somebody would.”

Nobody said anything.

Winter looked at him for a long time across the fire. She didn’t say anything either. She got up, and went and got her rope, and started tying it to a spur of rock at the top of the right-hand cliff, very carefully, with a knot Burr had shown them on nine. She tested it four times. She didn’t look at Ina once while she did it.

Three was bigger.

Niko felt it the moment Tuli said now. Before Ina’s fire. Before anything. Through the ice, through his boots, standing at the top of the right cliff with the rope going down over the edge and Ina hanging on the end of it ten metres below with his hands on a pale arm where it bent into the rock, and Winter beside Niko with her bow drawn and an arrow on the string. He felt the jam lean. Toward the gap. All of it. The whole remaining two-thirds of it, every slab, the weight of the lake behind, leaning toward the door the way a crowd leans toward an exit.

It’s not just three’s bit, he thought. It’s—

Ina’s fire went in. White-blue, down there on the end of the rope. The bend in the arm went clear. Steamed.

“Winter—“

Winter loosed.

Crack.

I’m here.

The cut came home. Three let go of the rock.

And Chris, on the jam below, roped to Maigo, with his good hand out and his nose streaming, screamed: “NO—“

Niko saw it go wrong.

He didn’t need Breakpoint. Anybody could have seen it. Three let go, and its bit started to go. The upper right-hand side of the jam, a big section, half a village, leaning and sliding toward the gap. And as it slid, it pulled. On the slabs next to it. On the slabs it was leaning on. On one’s bit. The biggest bit. The top left. The heart of the jam, where most of the lake was pressing. Three’s bit was sliding and it was taking the edge of one’s bit with it, the way a tablecloth pulled off a table takes the plates.

The whole jam groaned. Not the way it had groaned for three days. A different sound. Lower. A sound like something deciding.

All of it, thought Niko. It’s going to go. All of it. Now.

The heart went once.

And once.

And once, once, once, so fast he felt it come up through the cliff into his legs like a drum.

“WINTER,” Chris was screaming. From the jam. “The edge—the edge of one—hold the edge—stop it moving—“

Winter had three arrows.

She didn’t say anything. She put one on the string and drew and looked down the cliff and across the face of the jam, at the place where three’s bit, sliding, was dragging on one’s. A seam. A long crack running between two sections of ice the size of houses, opening, grinding. She loosed.

The arrow went into the seam. Hoarfrost. The frost went out from it white in both directions, along the crack, into both slabs.

What you hit stays where it is.

The seam froze. The edge of one’s bit, the slabs at the very edge of it that three’s bit had been pulling, stopped. Held. Frozen in place against the slide.

Three’s bit went on without them.

It tore free. Niko saw it tear. Three’s bit, the half-village, sliding, came away from one’s bit along the frozen seam with a sound like the whole sky ripping, and slid on, alone, forward, down, through the door, into the Narrows, end over end. Spray went up past the top of the cliff. The rope Ina was hanging on swung out over nothing and slammed back into the rock. Niko threw himself flat on the edge of the cliff and grabbed the rope with both hands and held it.

And one’s bit stayed.

The biggest bit. The top left. The heart’s bit. Most of the jam that was left. It groaned and shook and leaned toward the gap, toward the door, with the whole weight of the lake behind it. Winter’s second arrow went into it. And her third. Frost, and frost. Stay where you are. And it stayed. It stayed. Leaning, groaning, held by one great pale arm in the left-hand cliff and by the heart in the middle of it going once once once once and by three of Winter’s arrows in its frozen seam.

It stayed.

The water roared through the gap where three’s bit had been. Then steadied. Then ran. A river through a door, half the width of the Narrows now. And the jam that was left, a third of what it had been, stood at the top of the door, against the left cliff, with one arm and a heart, and held the rest of the lake.

They pulled Ina up.

Niko and Winter, hand over hand, on the rope, with Niko’s arms shaking so hard he could barely close his fingers. Ina came up over the edge of the cliff on his knees with his mask down and his hands black. Not burned. Black, with frost, from the inside out, where the heat had gone into the ice and the ice had come back into him. He held them up in front of his mask and looked at them.

“Huh,” said Ina.

Winter sat down on the rock very suddenly. She had one arrow left on her knee. Her hands were shaking. Not slightly. All the way.

She didn’t look at Ina. She looked at his hands.

“You said one more,” said Winter.

“That was one more.”

“You said then you’d stop.”

“I’ve stopped.” Ina pushed the mask up. His face under it was grey. He looked at her. “Winter. I’ve stopped.”

Winter looked at him for a long time. Then she put out her hand, the one without the arrow, and took his wrist. Not his hand. His wrist, above the black, where it was still his own colour. And held it. Hard. And didn’t let go. And didn’t say anything.

Niko looked away. You did.

Maigo did what he could for Ina’s hands. It wasn’t enough; he said so. I can grow them back. Not this. This is the cold in him. It’ll come out slow. He gave up two more leaves doing it, and sat back against the cliff with four left on his head, and looked at the jam.

“It won’t let go,” said Maigo.

They were all at the top of the right cliff now, all seven, in the late afternoon, looking down at what was left. A third of the jam. Huge still; a third of a mountain. Packed against the left cliff, the upper left, heaved and groaning, with one great pale arm going into the rock of the left cliff, thicker than any of the others had been, and Winter’s three arrows standing out of the frozen seam like pins in a cushion. And in the middle, through black ice, very close to the surface now, because the ice that had covered it had gone through the door: the heart.

They could see it properly now.

Pale. Curled. Bigger than a house. Like something very old asleep, except it wasn’t asleep. With its one remaining arm going out into the cliff, and the stumps of the other four, split at the knuckles, lying slack against it like broken branches. And in the middle of it, through the clear black ice, a darkness. Round. Going once, once, once. Fast. Frightened.

“I’ve been listening,” said Maigo. “To one. The arm. Where it goes in.” He shook his head slowly. “It’s not a knuckle. It’s not holding with a bit of itself. It’s holding with all of itself. The whole arm. Every bit of it. Right into the rock.” He looked at the heart. “It’s the last one. It knows it’s the last one. It’s holding with everything it’s got.”

“Can we crack it?” said Chris.

“No,” said Maigo. “Not with fire. Not with frost. Not with a cut. There’s nowhere to crack. It’s just—holding.” He looked at Ina’s black hands in Winter’s grip. “And Ina’s got nothing left. And Winter’s got one arrow.”

“And if we take the arm anyway?” said Niko. “Somehow?”

Chris looked at his notebook. At the picture. At the five arms, four of them crossed through. At the heart in the middle, with every line in the jam running into it.

“Then the heart lets go of everything,” said Chris. Quietly. “Because it’s got nothing left to hold with but itself. And it only knows all or nothing.” He looked down at the jam. “And everything that’s left goes through at once. And the lake with it. All of it.”

Sabe, beside him, put her pole down on the rock.

Tock.

She listened for a long time.

“Not yet,” she said. “Not tonight. Tomorrow. Maybe the day after. The lake’s lower. It’s bought us a day. Maybe two.” She lifted the pole. “But it’s still too heavy. If it goes all at once, it’ll take the moorings. And anything on the river behind it.”

Nobody said anything.

Niko looked down at the heart.

He thought about Hessa. On Gull Island, in the swept ring, a long time ago, with her real sword out and her one sharp eye on him, saying You push it like it’s a stranger. Don’t push. Read. To it. He thought about forty strikes on the knuckles, and a nosebleed, and then not pushing. Reading it to the blade the way you’d read to somebody sitting next to you. And the orange line going up the steel.

You couldn’t push a heart.

You couldn’t crack it, or cut it, or freeze it. You couldn’t find its knuckle. It was holding with everything it had because it was frightened and it only knew all or nothing, and every bit of the jam they’d taken away had only made it hold on harder to the bit that was left.

He thought about Ina, on the first night, with his hand on the rock. You don’t fight a thing that’s frightened. You sit with it. Until it isn’t.

“Somebody has to sit with it,” said Niko.

They all looked at him.

“Ina said,” said Niko. “On the first night. You sit with it until it isn’t frightened.” He looked at the heart. “We can’t make it let go. Nobody can. It has to let go itself. A bit at a time. And it won’t. Because it’s frightened. Because it’s holding all of it on its own.” He swallowed. “So somebody has to sit with it. Down there. Close. And—“

He stopped. He didn’t know what came after and.

“And what?” said Chris.

“And tell it they’ll hold some of it,” said Ina.

Everybody looked at Ina.

He was sitting against the cliff with his black hands in Winter’s grip and his mask pushed up and his face grey with cold from the inside. He was looking at the heart.

“That’s what you do,” said Ina. “With somebody who’s holding everything on their own. You don’t tell them to let go. They can’t. They don’t know how.” He was quiet. “You sit with them. And you take a bit of it. And then they can let go of that bit. Because somebody’s got it.” He looked at his hands. “And then the next bit.”

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

Niko didn’t look at anybody. Nobody did. They all looked at the heart in the ice, curled and frightened and holding everything with all of itself, all or nothing. And none of them said the name. Not Chris, with his notebook. Not Winter, with Ina’s wrist. Not Maigo. Not Ina.

Niko didn’t say it either.

But he thought it. The way you think something you’ve known for a long time and never put into words. He thought of a man on a hillside on floor 7 with his helm off and flour on his trousers. A man on a ledge on fifteen saying I don’t know. A man who’d held on to everything, every floor, everything he knew, a floor ahead, on his own, for two years. All or nothing. Who’d only ever learned to put any of it down at the very end, on fourteen, at a line in a barn, by asking. And then on the first morning of fifteen, I don’t know, and then how.

Who’d walked a long way down once, in some other life, in the dark, behind a boy who was too late. The whole way. In case.

He put his thumb on the back of his blade. On the letters. I’M HERE.

At dusk, Tuli saw the lights.

She’d been sitting at the edge of the cliff with her pole, listening to the jam. She stood up so suddenly she nearly went over. Winter caught her coat.

“Gran,” said Tuli.

Up the valley. A long way. Miles. On the lake behind the jam, which wasn’t a lake any more, not the way it had been; it was a river again, almost, wide and dark and full of floating ice, running steady toward what was left of the jam and the door. Far up it, in the blue of the evening, a light. Then another. Then dozens. Small and yellow and low on the water, clustered together. Moving. Coming down the river.

And above them, higher, a single light. On a mast.

“The Float,” said Sabe. Very quietly. “She’s cut loose.”

“Now?” Tuli’s voice went up. “Gran, the jam’s still—they’ll come down on it—if it goes when they’re—“

“They had to,” said Sabe. “The water’s been up round them for three days. Higher every day. Haddo’d have held as long as he could.” She put her pole down on the rock. Tock. Listened. “She’s riding. Slow. They’re steering for the bays. Above the Narrows. They’ll moor short.” She lifted the pole. “If they can. If the jam holds till they get there.”

Niko looked up the valley at the lights.

At the front of them, low on the water, at the very front of the Float, small and steady and different from all the yellow lamps, there was a light that was amber.

He knew it. Everybody on the cliff knew it. They’d been following it up a tower for two years.

It wasn’t leaning. Not toward anybody behind it. Not toward the man who’d left it on a bench on floor 3. It was leaning forward. Down the river. Toward the Narrows. Toward the jam.

Toward them.

Chris had his notebook open. His hand was steady. He wrote one line, under the picture of the jam with its crossed-out arms and its heart and its question mark, and Niko read it over his shoulder, the way he always had.

They’re coming. All of them.

And under it, smaller, after a moment:

Somebody to sit with it.

Niko looked down at the heart in the ice, curled, frightened, going once once once in the blue evening, holding everything that was left with all of itself.

Wait for me, he thought at it. He’s coming.
