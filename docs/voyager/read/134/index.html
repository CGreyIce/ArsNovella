Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 134

The Portrait

5,144 words · 22 min read

Bee drew the green every morning.

She did it the way she’d drawn the young tree on floor 9, every morning for eight days from the same place on the drum platform, so you could see it do it. Except that on the Salt you couldn’t stay in the same place. The sleds went on west at noon, every day, and the green stayed where it was. So every morning, before they loaded the sleds, she went out to the newest one with her sketchbook and sat down on the crust a few metres off, on the east side, always the east side, with the sun coming up behind her, and drew it.

Then she turned round and drew the line.

That was the bit nobody had asked for. She just did it. She sat on the salt with her back to the newest green and looked east, back the way they’d come, along the white. And drew what she could see.

Which was nothing. Mostly. White, flat, to the end of the world. But if you looked, and Bee always looked, you could see them. Dots. Small and dark on the white, a long way apart, in a line. Going back east. One for every night since the three hundred and forty-second.

Gigi’s.

She planted one every night now. It had become a thing, the way the still had become a thing, the way everything on the Salt became a thing because you had to do it every day or you died. The pots went on, and the shields went up, and Ina knelt in the middle, and Winter climbed onto the sled at the top with her arrowhead, and while all that was happening, out at the edge of the camp, on the west side, Maigo sat down with his foot in the pot of Highcrook earth, and shut his eyes, and went down. And after a while he’d point. There. That one’s awake. And Gigi would kneel and put her hand flat on the salt and reach.

It came out of her every time. Less than the first time. She’d got better at it, the way you get better at carrying something heavy by carrying it. But Bee saw her face every night, and every night it went grey, and Kei was there with his fingers on her wrist and Dose round his neck and his mouth in a line.

Then a seed. Then a hollow in the crust. A pinch of grey earth round it, from the pot, which was getting emptier. A cup of Ina’s water. Sow. And in the morning, a white root going down into the salt and a green point coming up, red at the tip, and a hedge round it the height of a thumb, brown-gold and hooked, that grew up out of the salt along the root as if the root were a road.

It holds while you’re gone.

And then they went, and left it.

Four, by the three hundred and forty-fifth morning. Bee had drawn every one. And every morning she’d turned round and drawn the line going back east, one more dot on it every time, and the first one too far away to see now, so that she had to draw it from memory, small, at the very edge of the page, where the white met the sky.

Higu had put it on her map. A dotted line. Across the Salt, from the door, through Seep, west. Higu had looked at Bee’s drawing of it for a long time and then drawn it exactly the same, and under it, in her small square capitals, written: GIGI’S ROAD.

Gigi had seen that and gone red and pretended she hadn’t.

Bee’s wings didn’t like the Salt either.

It wasn’t the heat, the way it had been on floor 6. It was the dry. They’d gone stiff in the first week. Then brittle at the edges. The veins in them, which on floor 1 had been soft and dark like the lead in a window, had gone pale and hard, and when she unfolded them in the mornings they made a sound, very faint, like paper. Kei had looked at them and said salt and rubbed some of the lamp oil they’d brought up from the Patience into the edges, which helped, a bit, and smelled of fish.

She didn’t mind. Not much. She kept them folded flat down her back under her cloak and drew.

And she didn’t do what Gigi had done, or Maigo. She didn’t hide it. When they went stiff she said so. She said so at breakfast on the fourth day, out loud, in a whisper, to her whole party. My wings hurt. Because she’d watched Gigi lie on the salt for twenty-one mornings with her hand under her blanket asking for something that didn’t come, and not say. And she’d watched Maigo lose two hundred leaves and not say. And she thought that if the Salt made you hide the thing that was going wrong, then the only way to beat the Salt was to say it.

So she said it.

Hyvred had looked at her for a long time across the sled. Then she’d reached over and taken Bee’s hand and squeezed it. Hard. And not let go all through breakfast.

She still owed him a portrait.

She hadn’t forgotten. She never forgot things she owed. I’m going to draw you properly, she’d said, on the drum platform on floor 9, with the door she’d drawn to get to his light still on the page in her lap. Awake. Doing something stupid. You owe me. And he’d said I know. I saw.

That had been a hundred and twelve days ago.

She’d tried. She’d watched him on floor 10, on the fifth wall, in the snow. She’d watched him on the Patience. She’d sat with her sketchbook open on her knee on a dozen evenings and waited for him to do something stupid, because everybody said he did, all the time, you always do, apparently.

He didn’t.

That was the thing. He’d stopped. Or not stopped. He was still Ice. He still did things nobody else would, alone, at night, with Thea and Xola a hundred metres behind him. But stupid wasn’t the same as that. Stupid was the thing you did when you weren’t being careful. And he was always careful now. Every minute. You could see it, if you drew people. It was in his shoulders. He held himself the way you’d hold a full cup across a room.

She’d drawn him a lot. In the snow, in the hold, on the deck, at the still. They were good drawings. They weren’t the one.

She didn’t think she’d ever get it.

And then, on the three hundred and forty-fifth morning, she went out to draw the green, and he was lying on his face next to it.

She saw him from the edge of the camp.

It was very early. Grey still. The sun not up yet, just a line of gold along the east. The camp was asleep, mostly, in its bedrolls round the steel tent where the pots had gone cold, and Ina was asleep sitting up with his mask in his lap, and the little orange fire out on the west side had gone out. And out past it, about twenty metres beyond the edge of the camp, where Gigi had planted last night’s seed, there was somebody lying on the salt.

Flat. On his front. With his chin on the crust.

She knew who it was from the shape. You learned people’s shapes if you drew them.

She went closer. Softly. You always went softly, on the Salt. She stopped a few metres off, where she always sat, on the east side, and looked.

He was lying on his front on the white next to the newest green, with his face about a hand’s width from it. He’d taken his helm off. The closed white one, the Galeward Helm, with the slit visor. He was holding it upside down, in both hands, at arm’s length, over the green point, so that it made a shadow. A little round shadow, on the salt, exactly the size of Gigi’s thumb-high hedge, with the green in the middle of it.

Shade.

He’d made it shade.

And he had a spoon.

One of his own spoons, from the cooking things, the small wooden one he used for tasting. He had the helm in his left hand and the spoon in his right, and a mug on the salt by his elbow, Ina’s water, his own ration, Bee could see the mark on the mug. And as she watched, he dipped the spoon in the mug, very carefully, and brought it out with a drop of water on the end. One drop. And held it over the green point. And let it fall.

It landed on the salt beside the green, inside the hedge, and soaked in.

He did it again.

And his mouth was moving.

Bee crouched down very slowly on the crust and opened her sketchbook. She didn’t decide to. Her hands did it. She found a clean page and her charcoal was already moving before she’d worked out what she was looking at.

She could hear him. Just. Because she was close, and because he wasn’t being careful, and you could hear a whisper on the Salt if you were close enough and the person whispering had forgotten anyone might be listening.

“—not much,” he was saying. To the green. In a whisper. “I know it’s not much. It’s what I’ve got. You looked thirsty.” Drip. “Don’t tell Gigi. She’ll go spare.” Drip. “You’re doing really well. You’re doing so well. You’re the first one I’ve seen in—“ He stopped. “You’re doing really well.”

Drip.

Bee drew.

She drew him fast, the way she drew when something was happening and she didn’t know how long it would last. The long line of him on the salt. The arms out. The helm upside down at the end of one, a little dark bowl against the grey sky. The spoon. The mug. The green point in its thumb-high hedge, in the bowl of shade. And his face, a hand’s width from it, sideways, his cheek nearly on the crust, his black hair fallen over his eyes and the teal streak in it gone pale with salt.

His face.

She drew his face, and her charcoal stopped.

He wasn’t careful.

That was it. That was the whole of it. For the first time since floor 9, since floor 6, since the corner of Hyv’s streams, years ago, a name in a chat and a voice in a call, she was looking at his face and it wasn’t holding anything. Not the full cup. Not the shoulders. Nothing. It was a man lying on his front on a salt flat at dawn giving a plant the size of a grain of rice his own water off a spoon and telling it it was doing really well.

It was the stupidest thing she’d ever seen.

She drew it.

“What are you doing,” said Gigi.

She’d come out from the camp. Bee hadn’t heard her either. Nobody heard anybody on the Salt. She was standing over him in her nightshirt with her hair everywhere and no crown, because her crown was on the salt round the first green, three days east, and her face was doing a thing Bee had only ever seen it do once, on floor 9, when the Grow swallowed her Bramble.

Ice froze.

He didn’t move anything. Not the helm. Not the spoon. He lay on his front on the salt with the spoon held over the green and a drop of water on the end of it, and his eyes went up, sideways, without his head moving, to Gigi.

“Nothing,” he said.

“You’re watering it.”

“No.”

“You’ve got a spoon.” Gigi’s voice wasn’t loud. You couldn’t. But every word of it was in capitals. “You’re lying on the floor at five in the morning watering my salt plant with a spoon.”

“It looked thirsty,” said Ice.

“It’s a salt plant!” Gigi knelt down. Fast. On the crust beside him, and looked at the green, and at the wet salt round it, and put both her hands to her head. “It doesn’t want your water! It likes salt! That’s the whole point of it! That’s why it’s the only thing that’ll grow here! It drinks salt, you absolute—“ She looked at the mug. “Is that your ration?”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“Ice. Is that your ration.”

“Some of it.”

“You’re drowning it!”

“It’s a spoon, Gigi. It’s like four drops.”

“Four drops is a flood! For something that size! You’re giving it the sea!” She pulled the mug away from him. “Give me the spoon. Give me the spoon.”

He gave her the spoon.

She held it. She looked at it. She looked at him, lying on his front on the salt with his helm upside down at the end of his arm still making shade, very carefully, because he hadn’t been told to stop doing that bit.

“You can keep the shade,” said Gigi, after a moment. In a different voice.

“Okay.”

“The shade’s actually quite good.”

“Okay,” said Ice.

Gigi sat down on the crust. She looked at the green. She looked at the helm. She put her face in her hands. And Bee, three metres away with her charcoal going as fast as it had ever gone, saw her shoulders start to shake.

“You were talking to it,” said Gigi, into her hands.

“I wasn’t.”

“You were. I heard you. You told it it was doing really well.”

“It is doing really well,” said Ice.

Gigi made a sound into her hands that was nowhere near a laugh and was entirely a laugh.

Behind them, out of the camp, people were coming. Slowly. Woken by nothing, the way you woke on the Salt, by somebody near you sitting up. Hako, rubbing his face. Kei, with Dose. Hyvred, with her glasses on and her hair standing straight up on one side. They came and stood behind Bee and looked.

At Ice on his front with a helm in the air. At Gigi with a spoon. At the green.

“Overwatering,” said Kei, very quietly, “kills more plants than drought does.”

“Thank you, Kei,” said Gigi, through her hands.

“I’m just saying. Statistically.”

“Classic,” said Hako.

And behind all of them, at the edge of the camp, with the lantern still lit in her hand from the night, the way it always was at dawn, Thea.

Bee looked up and saw her.

She was standing at the edge of the camp, on her own, a little way back from everybody. With the lantern. Looking at Ice lying on his front in the salt. And she had her other hand over her mouth, and she was laughing. Silently. All of her. The way Hyvred had screamed at the first green. Her whole face gone, her eyes shut, her shoulders going, standing at the edge of a salt flat at dawn with her hand over her mouth, laughing so hard she had to bend over.

Bee drew her too.

She didn’t decide to. Her hand did it. At the edge of the page, at the top, small. A woman with a lantern, bent over, laughing.

She drew the salt round all of them. The way she always drew the ground. The way she’d drawn the ferns on floor 9 every morning for thirty-one days. On the Salt the crust wasn’t flat, not quite, if you looked. It was ridged. Low ridges, a finger high, in a pattern, all over the white, as far as you could see. Shapes. Six-sided, mostly. Like the cells in a honeycomb. Like scales. The salt did it, Tullah had said, drying. It pushed up into ridges and the ridges met and made shapes.

Bee drew them. All of them. Round Ice, round Gigi, round the green. Out to the edges of the page.

She didn’t look at what she’d drawn. Not then. She was looking at Ice.

Because he’d turned his head. On the salt. Still on his front, still with the helm up. He’d turned his head and seen Thea, at the edge of the camp, bent over with her hand over her mouth. And his face.

Bee drew that last.

Quickly. Before it went.

She gave it to him that evening.

She’d gone over it all day, on the strap of the second sled, walking west, in her head. Whether to. Whether it was right. Whether it was the one. She’d decided by noon, and then undecided it, and then decided it again. It was the one. It was the only one she’d ever drawn of him that was.

She tore it out at the still, at dusk, carefully, along the stitching, the way you’d take a page out of a book you loved. She went and found him.

He was sitting on the end of a sled by the steel tent with his sword across his knees and a whetstone, doing the edge, the way he did every night before he went out. Thea was beside him with the lantern. They weren’t talking.

Bee held out the page.

He looked at it. Then at her.

“You said you’d draw me doing something stupid,” he said.

“You did something stupid.”

He took it.

She watched him look at it. That was the bit she’d been frightened of. Not that he wouldn’t like it. That he’d look at it and his face would go careful again. That he’d see the face she’d drawn, the one with nothing in it, and put it away somewhere and fold it up and be careful about it.

He looked at it for a long time.

At himself, on his front, in the salt, with a helm in the air and a spoon and a mug and a green point. At Gigi, in her nightshirt, with the spoon. At Kei and Hako and Hyvred, standing behind, small, half-drawn. At the woman with the lantern at the top of the page, bent over, laughing.

And at the last thing. His own face, turned sideways on the salt, seeing her.

He didn’t go careful.

He laughed.

Out loud. On the Salt. It went down into the crust, and somewhere out on the white the crust shivered, once, and went still, and Bee didn’t care and neither did he. A real laugh, short, surprised, the kind that came up out of you before you could stop it, and then he put his hand over his mouth, the way Thea had, and kept laughing behind it, silently, with his eyes shut.

Thea leaned over and looked at the drawing.

She saw herself at the top. Bee watched her see it. Bent over, with the lantern. And her face went red, from the neck up, all the way, and she put the lantern down on the sled very carefully, and put her face in her hands.

“Bee,” said Thea, through her hands.

“You were laughing,” said Bee. “I draw what’s there.”

“I was not—“

“You couldn’t stand up,” said Ice. Still laughing. “You couldn’t stand up, Thea. Look at you.”

“I’m going to kill you both.”

“It’s a good drawing,” said Ice.

He stopped laughing. Slowly. He looked at the page again. At his own face, at the bottom, sideways on the salt, the last thing Bee had drawn.

“That’s the face,” said Bee. Quietly. “You asked, on nine. Why I drew you asleep. I said you looked younger.” She looked at the drawing in his hands. “That’s not younger. That’s just you. That’s the one from Hyv’s streams. In the corner. The one everybody said was funny.” She swallowed. “I never saw it. Not once. Not in four months. Not up here. Not till this morning.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

He looked at his own face on the page for a long time. In the lamplight, by the steel tent, with the steam coming up off the pots and Thea beside him with her face still red.

Then he folded it. Carefully. Along the crease Bee hadn’t made, in half, and then in half again, so it was small. And put it inside his vest, on the left side, next to the blank card from the white room that he’d carried since the first day.

“Thanks, Bee,” he said.

“You owe me,” said Bee, “nothing now. We’re even.”

“We’re not even,” said Ice. “Not even close.”

He picked up his sword and the lamp shield, and got up off the sled, and went out past the little orange fire into the dark. Thea picked up the lantern and went after him, a hundred metres back, the way she always did. Xola went after her.

Bee watched them go.

She felt very light. Like when you finish something you’ve been carrying a long time and put it down and your arms don’t know what to do.

She went back to her bedroll and opened her sketchbook to look at the other drawings from the morning. She’d done four. Fast, one after another, the way she did when something was happening. The one she’d given him had been the best. But she wanted to look at the others, while she still felt like this, and remember.

She opened the book.

And looked at the salt.

She hadn’t looked at it, that morning. She’d drawn it without looking, the way she always drew the ground. Her hand did the ground. Her eyes did the people.

The ridges. The six-sided shapes in the crust, round Ice and Gigi and the green, out to the edges of the page. She’d drawn them in every one of the four, because they’d been there in every one.

In every one, they leaned.

Not much. You wouldn’t see it if you didn’t know. If you hadn’t sat in a hanging house on floor 9 at midnight with thirty-one drawings of the Down laid out on the floor round you, and seen, for the first time, that every fern in every one of them leaned the same way. Like hair round the crown of your head. Like iron filings round a magnet. Toward a place a day’s walk south-west, where something with no face knelt with its arms in the ground.

The ridges leaned.

Every one of them. On every page. The six-sided shapes weren’t quite six-sided. They were pulled. Stretched, a little, all in the same direction, the way a honeycomb looks if you press your thumb into one side of it. The lines went out from the middle of each page, round Ice and Gigi and the green, and as they went out they bent, all of them, very slightly, the same way.

Up. To the top of the page.

To the edge of the camp.

To the woman with the lantern, bent over, laughing.

Bee sat on her bedroll with the sketchbook open on her knees and didn’t move.

It’s nothing, she thought. It’s the way you hold the charcoal. It’s because she was at the top of the page, and you drew toward her. It’s nothing.

She turned back to the second drawing. The one before. Before Thea came. Ice on his front, alone, with the helm, before Gigi. No Thea in it. She’d been standing at the edge of the camp then, Bee knew, but out of the frame. Off the top of the page.

The ridges leaned up.

Off the top of the page. Toward where she’d been standing.

Bee shut the book.

She sat there in the dark by the steel tent with the book shut on her knees and her wings gone stiff down her back, and looked out past the little orange fire into the dark where Ice had gone, and Thea after him with the lantern, a hundred metres back. She could see the amber. A long way off, on the white. Steady. Moving.

On floor 9 the ferns had leaned toward the Sower.

And then, on the last morning, when the Sower was ash and the ticking had stopped, every fern and briar in the Down had turned and looked up. At Ice. On Burr’s rope, in the air, with his light. All of them, at once. And Thea had said, that night, so quietly that only the club had heard it: Not at us. That time. Not at all of us.

At him.

It had been looking at him for three floors. Everybody in the club knew that. It was on Chris’s page and Drew’s page and in the face Ice made when the slips came. Where did you come from. It looked at him.

It wasn’t looking at him.

Bee opened the book again. She couldn’t help it. She opened it to the first drawing, the one before the one she’d given him, and held it up to the light from the steel tent and looked, very hard, the way she’d looked at the ferns.

Ice, on his front, in the middle of the page. And the ridges round him going out from him, and every one of them leaning past him. Not toward him. Past.

To the edge of the camp. To the lantern.

Oh, she thought.

Oh, no.

She didn’t sleep.

She lay in her bedroll between Hyvred and Gigi with the sketchbook under her blanket against her chest, the way Gigi had kept her hand under her blanket for twenty-one mornings, and didn’t sleep. She lay there and thought about what she’d said at breakfast on the fourth day, out loud, to her whole party. My wings hurt. Because the Salt made you hide the thing that was going wrong, and the only way to beat it was to say it.

She’d said it about her wings. That was easy. Her wings were hers.

This wasn’t hers.

It was Thea’s. Or Ice’s. Or the club’s. Or nobody’s. She didn’t know whose it was. She only knew she’d drawn it, without meaning to, in four drawings on a salt flat at dawn, and that her hand had never once, on any floor, drawn something that wasn’t there.

At some point in the small hours, the amber came back across the white. Thea, with the lantern. Ice ahead of her, white to the knees. Xola behind. They came in past the little orange fire and Ice went to the still and sat down by Ina and Thea came to her bedroll by the second sled and lay down with the lantern by her head.

Bee watched her from across the camp.

She looked at the salt round Thea’s bedroll. The real salt. The ridges. She looked at them for a long time, in the light from the lantern, and they were just ridges. Six-sided. Like a honeycomb. She couldn’t see anything.

Her eyes did the people. Her hand did the ground.

She got her sketchbook out from under the blanket. Very quietly. And a stub of charcoal. And by the light of Thea’s lantern, across the camp, without looking at the page, she drew what she could see. The sled. The bedroll. The lantern. A woman asleep beside it with her hand under her cheek.

And the salt.

She didn’t look at it until she’d finished. Then she did.

The ridges leaned. Every one. On the whole page. Toward the woman asleep by the lantern. And they leaned more than they had in the morning. Not much. A little. The way the ferns on floor 9 had leaned a little more every day for thirty days, until Bee had laid them all out on the floor and seen it.

She shut the book.

Beside her, Hyvred was asleep on her back with her glasses on her chest and her mouth open. Bee looked at her for a long time.

Then she put her hand out and touched Hyvred’s arm.

Hyvred woke the way she always woke, all at once, with her eyes open and her hand already going for her bow. She saw Bee’s face. She stopped.

“What,” said Hyvred. No sound. Just the shape of it.

Bee held up the sketchbook.

Hyvred looked at it. At the drawing. At the woman asleep by the lantern, and the salt round her. She looked at it for a long time with her face very close to the page, the way she looked at everything, because for most of her life on Earth she’d only been able to see a circle the size of a coin. Bee watched her see it. The lean.

Hyvred’s face went still.

“Is it there?” whispered Bee. Close. In her ear. “Hyv. Is it really there? Or is it me?”

Hyvred didn’t answer.

She sat up, slowly, in her bedroll. She picked her glasses up off her chest. She held them in her hand for a moment, and looked at them. And then she didn’t put them on.

She took them away from her face altogether. And put them down on the salt beside her. And turned her head. And looked, with her bare eyes, the way she only ever looked when she needed to see everything, across the sleeping camp, past the steel tent and the cold pots and Ina asleep with his mask, past the sleds, to the bedroll by the second one, where Thea was asleep with her hand under her cheek and the lantern by her head.

Bee watched her look.

She watched Hyvred’s face. In the dark. In the lantern-light from across the camp.

And she watched it change.
