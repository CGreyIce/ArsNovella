Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 173

The One You Get

7,464 words · 32 min read

Rachael did his hands a finger at a time.

It was the only way, she said. She’d learned it on floor 10, in the house for the wounded at the Bastion, on a line of frostbitten hold-folk that went down the street for nine days. You couldn’t take a whole hand of frost out of somebody at once. It came out wrong. It took the hand with it. You took one finger. You held it. You felt the cold in it, all the way to the bone, and you took that into yourself and put it somewhere, and you waited until the finger was pink, and you did the next one.

She knelt on the stone just inside the doors of the Long Barn, where he’d sat down, with Hearthwall flat on the floor beside her, boss up. She took his left little finger between her two white hands. She shut her eyes.

He felt it go. The cold. Out of the finger. Into her. It hurt more coming out than it had going in. He hadn’t known that. He said so.

“It hurts more coming out.”

“I know,” said Rachael. Without opening her eyes. “It does that.”

She put one hand flat on Hearthwall’s boss. Steam came up off it. Then she took the next finger.

Thea was holding his right hand.

She hadn’t let go. Not when he sat down. Not when Rachael came. Not when Kei knelt down on his other side with Dose round his neck and his mouth in a line and started going over the rest of him, slowly, the way Kei did everything, item by item, saying each thing out loud as he found it so somebody would know. Two ribs on the left, cracked, not through. The left shoulder, out, from pulling the shield arm free; Kei put it back in, and Ice said so, loudly, and Kei said sorry and didn’t sound it. Frostbite in both hands, to the second knuckle, deeper in the left. Both arms white to the shoulder, but that was Rachael’s. The legs.

Kei stopped at the legs.

“What does it feel like,” said Kei.

Ice thought about it. He’d promised. He made himself find it.

“Like they’re nowhere,” he said. “Below the belt. Where I went in. They’re there. I can feel them. I can move them. But under that, like there’s a bit of them that went somewhere and hasn’t come back yet.” He stopped. “It doesn’t hurt. It’s worse than that.”

Kei looked at him for a long time.

“Okay,” said Kei. And wrote it in his book. And under it, Ice saw, upside down, he wrote a question mark, and drew a box round it.

Rachael needed his right hand at about the third finger of the left.

“You’ll have to let go of it,” she said. To Thea. Not unkindly. Rachael was never unkind. She just said things.

“No,” said Thea.

Rachael looked at her.

Thea looked back. She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t explain. She sat on the stone floor with his white right hand in both of hers, and her face was wet, and under the wet it was the thing it had been since she put the lantern down by the doorpost, and Rachael saw it, and Ice watched her see it.

“Fine,” said Rachael.

She did the right hand round Thea’s. A finger at a time. Thea’s hands moving out of the way for each one and closing back over the rest.

Thea didn’t say anything to him. Not all morning.

They walked the straw man home in the afternoon.

He watched it from the floor by the doors. They’d put a blanket under him and another over him and a sack of something behind his back against the doorpost, and nobody had tried to move him. He didn’t think anybody would have got very far. He wasn’t sure he could have been moved. He’d sat down on the stone just inside the sill and the whole of him had decided that was where he was going to be now.

So he watched through the doors.

It was still standing where it had put him down. Two hundred paces down the Barn Field, in the snow, on the real ground, in the thin winter sun. Tall as a house. Bowed a little at the head. The frost gone off it. Gold. Every gold there was.

The Acre went down to it.

All of them. Not the way they’d come up the valley at harvest-home, singing, on carts. Quietly. On foot. Every village. Haverley at the front with Annet and her stick, and Ashwell with Osric and his long one, and Lammas, and Brightwater with their quern on a sledge for no reason Ice could see except that they didn’t want to leave it, and Hollin, and Middle Acre, and the top villages whose names he didn’t know. Seven hundred people walking down a snowy field in a long dark line toward a straw man. With ropes. With poles. With the horses, twelve of them, blankets on their backs, led by children.

They got there. They stood round it. For a long time.

Then they brought it home.

He’d thought they’d drag it. On rollers, or a sledge, the way you’d move something that heavy. They didn’t. They walked it. Two hundred people at a time, on poles under the iron of the frame, the way you’d carry somebody on a door, but upright. Tilted a little, back, toward the hill. Ropes from its shoulders to the horses, to hold it steady. And the rest of the Acre walking beside it, and in front of it, and behind it, with their hands on it. On its legs. On its sides. As high as they could reach. Holding it up.

He watched them walk it up the Barn Field in the snow, a pace at a time. It took two hours.

They brought it in through the doors bent nearly double, the way it had gone out. He watched it come past him. A metre from where he sat. The great iron feet, carried, the straw bound round them gold. The poles. The two hundred. Cobb on one of the poles with his face set. Wren running beside it with her hand flat on its knee. Hild, walking, slowly, with her daughter holding one arm and Hako holding the other, her hand on it too.

As it went past him, Hild looked down.

“He’s kept,” said Hild.

Ice couldn’t say anything for a moment.

“He kept me,” he said.

Hild nodded. As if that was the obvious thing. As if it was what anybody would have said. “That’s what it means,” she said. “Ezra’s verse. Everybody thinks it’s one way round.” She went on past him, into the barn, with her hand on the straw. “It’s both.”

They set it up at the top of the barn.

Where it had been. In the same sockets, the left and the right, which were square holes in the stone again with nothing at the bottom of them but lead and stone. Bettris knelt at each foot with her hammer and new bolts she’d made that morning at the portable forge, while the whole Acre held it upright on its poles and ropes, and drove them in. Four at each foot. He heard every one. Tink, tink, tink. The same as the nails on floor 1.

Then they stood back.

It stood at the top of the Long Barn, facing down the barn at the doors, the way the old women had tied it on the night of harvest-home. Tall as the roof. Gold. Its arms down at its sides. Its head straight. Not turned a finger’s width to anybody. Looking at the doors. Looking at the whole of the Acre, all of it, in one place, the way it was supposed to.

Hild’s daughter brought the stool. Hild sat down on it. At its feet.

Hyvred came and knelt by him at dusk, with her glasses off, and told him what she saw. She was crying a bit. She didn’t seem to mind.

“It’s all back,” said Hyvred. “Next year. In all of him. The legs. Every ear. All of it.” She wiped her face with her wrist. “And in the seed. All fifteen sacks. Nothing went in. The grey went all round the bottom of the recess and never went in.” She looked at him. “And there’s a thread. Still. From his heart. To you.” She swallowed. “Not asking any more. Just. There.”

“I know,” said Ice. “I can feel it.”

Thea’s hand tightened on his.

She hadn’t said a word to him all day.

He woke in the night.

He didn’t know what time. The lamps were down. The barn was quiet, the way it had been the first nights of the snow, before the straw man woke: low voices, somebody mending something, somebody singing very softly somewhere. The horses breathing. The doors shut, now, on the cold. And at the top of the barn, in the light of one low lamp, the straw man, gold, with an old woman asleep on a stool at its feet.

Thea was awake.

She was sitting against the doorpost beside him, where she’d been all day. With his right hand still in both of hers, in her lap. The lantern on the floor on her other side, lit, turned low. She was looking at him.

He didn’t know how long she’d been looking.

“Thea,” said Ice.

“Don’t,” said Thea.

He didn’t.

She looked at him for a long time more. Her face was dry now. It had been wet all day and now it was dry, and that was worse, somehow. It made the other thing easier to see.

“I’m not ready to talk to you yet,” said Thea.

“Okay.”

“I’m not leaving either.”

“Okay.”

She didn’t say anything else. She sat there against the doorpost with his hand in her lap and looked at him until he went back to sleep.

She’s right, said Alea. Very quietly. On the blanket by his ear, hand-sized, pale. He’d thought she was asleep. She didn’t sleep. You know she’s right.

“I know,” he said. Into the dark. Not loud enough to wake anyone.

I’m angry too, said Alea. I’m just smaller.

She talked to him on the second day.

Not in the morning. The morning was Rachael, again, a finger at a time, the right hand this time. Then Kei, with his book, measuring, saying each thing out loud. Then Gabe, who came and crouched in front of him and looked at the lamp shield, which somebody had brought up from the field and leaned against the doorpost, white, with the dent in the rim. Gabe looked at the dent for a long time.

“It held,” said Gabe.

“It held.”

“Course it held.” Gabe picked it up. “I’ll beat that out. Bettris’ll let me. Don’t.” He didn’t say don’t what. He took it away.

Then Calvin. Who came and sat down by his feet and didn’t say anything at all, for about a minute, which was the second time in a week, and then said, very fast, “I said your name. Out loud. A lot. I don’t know if you heard.”

“I heard.”

“Okay.” Calvin looked at his hands. “Okay. Good.” And got up, and went, and Ice saw Rachael meet him by the first table and put her hand on his back, once, and take it away.

And then it was noon, and the barn was quieter, and the people near the doors had found other places to be without anybody asking them to, the way the Acre always did. And Thea, who had been sitting against the doorpost with his hand in her lap for a day and a half, said:

“I know why.”

He looked at her.

“You don’t have to tell me,” said Thea. “I know exactly why.” Her voice was very level. It was the voice from the rock in the sea. The one he’d thought of, afterwards, as her mean voice, and never said so. “It wanted you. It had been looking at you for a week. If it walked, it walked at you, through everybody, and the grey came with it. Onto seven hundred people with nowhere to stand. And the seed. And nobody else could go out there, because there wasn’t anything to stand on. And Borne could hold you and it couldn’t hold anybody else.” She stopped. “I worked it out before you stood up. I worked it out on the second night, watching you not sleep.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“So don’t you dare explain it to me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good.” Her hands tightened on his. “Because I’m not wrong to be angry. And you weren’t wrong to go.” She looked at their hands. “Both. I’ve been sitting here for a day and a half trying to make one of those not true. And I can’t. They’re both true.” Her mouth did something. “Gigi said that to me. On six. In the cistern. I’m angry at you for going. And you were right to go. Both. I didn’t understand it till now.”

He didn’t say anything.

“You told me every number,” said Thea.

“I promised.”

“I know you promised.” It came out of her hard and fast and she stopped it, and let it go, and went on lower. “I know. I was there. I was on the rock. I made you. And you did it. You did exactly what you said.” She looked at him. “I heard every single one. Eighty-eight. Seventy-four, my hand. Sixty, it hurts, healing. My hands hurt.” Her voice didn’t change on any of them. “It’s turned. I can’t come in. I’m telling you.” She stopped. “Thirty. And then you didn’t say anything for a bit. And then you said fifteen. Not loud. You didn’t shout it. You said it like it was just a number.”

“It was just a number.”

“It was fifteen.”

He didn’t say anything.

“Do you know what that’s like?” said Thea. Quietly. “Standing at a door. Holding a lantern up. All night. Hearing the person you love count down to fifteen on the other side of a field in the dark. And not being allowed to move. Because you said you wouldn’t. Because you promised too.” Her hands were shaking now, round his. “Do you know?”

“No,” said Ice.

“No,” said Thea. “You don’t.”

She was quiet for a long time.

“It would have been easier if you’d lied,” she said.

He looked at her.

“If you’d done it the old way.” She wasn’t looking at him now. She was looking at the lantern. “Kept your face still. Said nothing. Come back in at the end and said fine. Then I could have been angry at you for that. I could have shouted at you for lying. I’d have had something.” She swallowed. “But you didn’t lie. You told me the truth the whole way. Every word. You kept it. You kept the whole stupid thing, exactly, every bit of it, and there’s nothing to be angry at. Except that it happened. Except that it was you out there. Except that it was always going to be you.” Her voice went. She let it. “And I’m angry anyway. And I can’t put it anywhere. And that’s worse. Do you understand? It’s worse.”

“I know,” said Ice.

“Stop saying you know.”

“Okay.”

They sat there.

The barn went on round them. Somebody laughed, far down at the Haverley end. A child ran past the first table and was caught by somebody and swung round. At the top of the barn the straw man stood gold and still, looking at the doors, with Hild asleep at its feet.

“I’m not going anywhere,” said Thea.

“I know.” He stopped. “Sorry.”

She almost laughed. It didn’t quite come out.

“You’re going to lie there,” said Thea, “and get better. And I’m going to sit here. And be angry at you. For as long as it takes. And you’re going to let me.”

He looked at her.

“That’s not a fair deal,” said Ice.

“It’s the one you get,” said Thea.

She’d said it on the far side of the Gate, on the morning after the aurora, about the secret. Nobody else is getting it from me. You’ll tell them, one day. That’s the deal. And he’d said that’s not a fair deal, and she’d said it’s the one you get, and he’d laughed.

He didn’t laugh now. He looked at her, sitting against a doorpost in a barn on floor 14 with his white hand in both of hers and her face dry and furious and the lantern beside her turned low, and he thought he had never in two lives loved anybody so much.

“Okay,” said Ice.

“Okay,” said Thea.

She didn’t let go.

Xola came on the third morning.

He heard her before he saw her. He always did. She came up the barn from the Haverley end with Squiddles on her head, and her hair down, and her gills flat against her neck, and she didn’t squeak at anybody on the way, which was how he knew. Squiddles was a colour he’d never seen. A dark, sullen red, like a coal.

She sat down on his other side. On the stone. Without asking. With her back against the doorpost on the right, so the two of them had him between them, the way they’d had him between them on a rock in the sea.

She didn’t look at him.

“I’m doing mornings, babe,” said Xola. To Thea. Across him. “You sleep.”

“I’m not leaving,” said Thea.

“I know you’re not leaving.” Xola still didn’t look at him. “I’m not asking you to leave. I’m asking you to sleep. Sitting up. Right there. And I’ll do mornings.”

“I’m not—“

“Babe.” Xola looked at her, then. Just her. “You’ve been holding it for two days. All of it. On your own. Give me half.”

Thea looked at her.

And something in Thea’s face, that had been held very straight for two days, let go. Not all of it. A bit. At the edges. She made a sound that was nearly a laugh and nearly something else. And Xola reached across him, over his chest, over the blanket, and took Thea’s hand. The one that wasn’t holding his. And held it.

He lay there between them.

They’d done this once before. On a rock in the sea, with the tide coming in, the two of them, chewing him out, and he’d lain on the rock and let them. And before that. A long time before that. On Earth, before he went quiet and went off to Yan’s lot and nobody knew why. The three of them. He’d forgotten what it was like. Being between them. Being the thing they were both angry at, together.

He hadn’t forgotten. He’d just not let himself remember.

“Right,” said Xola. She let go of Thea’s hand. She put both of hers on his chest, over the blanket, over the cracked ribs, flat. “I’m healing you. Properly. Because it’s my job. Not because I’m not angry.”

“Okay,” said Ice.

“Don’t talk.”

He didn’t talk.

The water came. Xola’s heal. He’d felt it on the rock off the Stair, and on the hill on 13 when she said let me do it properly, and it was always the same: like cold clear water going through the wound, washing it, the way a river washes a stone. It went into his ribs. Into his shoulder. Down into his legs, where the nowhere was, and it stopped there for a long time, and he felt her frown.

“What’s that,” said Xola. Not to him. To his legs.

“Where I went in,” said Ice. “The grey.”

She didn’t say anything. She kept her hands there. The water went round and round the nowhere, like a river round a rock it couldn’t move, and then, very slowly, some of it went in.

He felt a bit of his left leg come back. Not much. A finger’s width. From somewhere.

Xola took her hands away. She sat back against the doorpost and wiped her hands on her knees, hard, as if there was something on them.

“Fifteen,” said Xola.

She still hadn’t said his name. He’d noticed that. She’d said babe to Thea twice and right and don’t talk and what’s that, and she hadn’t once said his name. She never did, when she was angry. She hadn’t on the rock in the sea, either.

“Fifteen,” said Xola again. “You said fifteen out loud like you were reading the weather. I was right behind Calvin. At the door. I had both my hands on his shoulders. Do you know that? I could feel it. Every time it hit you. It went up the threads and through all of them and through Calvin and into my hands. Every single time. Eighty-eight. Seventy-four. Sixty. I could feel all of them.” She wasn’t looking at him. “And I couldn’t heal you. Because you were two hundred metres away. On nothing. Being held up by seven hundred people and a deer in a doorstep.” She stopped. “That’s my job. That’s the only thing I’m for. And I couldn’t do it. I had to stand there and feel you get to fifteen through Calvin’s shoulders and not do my job.”

“I’m sorry,” said Ice.

“Don’t.” Squiddles went darker. “Don’t say sorry. You’re not sorry. You’d do it again.”

He didn’t say anything.

“See,” said Xola. To Thea. “He’d do it again.”

“I know,” said Thea.

“I know you know. I’m saying it.”

“Say it then.”

“He’d do it again,” said Xola, louder, to the barn, to the doorpost, to nobody, and somebody at the first table looked round and then very carefully looked away. “He’d go out there again. On nothing. With no healer. At ten above. And say every number. Out loud. So we’d know. And it was right. It was the right thing. It was the only thing. And I’m so angry I could bite something.”

“Me too,” said Thea.

“Good,” said Xola. “Good. Then we’re both angry.”

They sat there on either side of him, angry. He lay between them. After a while, Xola put her hand on the blanket over his chest, not healing, just there. And Thea’s hand was still round his. And neither of them said anything else for a long time.

You’ve got two of them, said Alea, on the blanket. Two. Being angry at you. At the same time. She sounded almost pleased. That’s very efficient.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t trust his voice.

Xola stayed till dark. She healed him twice more, at noon and at dusk, properly, and said don’t talk both times, and he didn’t. She ate beside him. She argued with Kei about his legs. She made Squiddles go a slightly less furious red by the evening, which Ice thought might have been on purpose.

When she got up to go, at the end of the day, she stopped. Standing over him. In the doorway. With the lamps going down behind her and Squiddles on her head.

She looked down at him for a moment.

“Icey,” said Xola.

That was all. Just that. Very quietly. And then she went down the barn to her place, and lay down, and didn’t look back.

She came on the fifth day too. And the seventh. Not every day. She’d said mornings and she meant some mornings. The days she didn’t come, Thea was angry on her own, and the days she did, Thea gave her half, and they were angry together, and the anger seemed to go further, somehow, shared between them. Like the winter had gone further up the threads.

Thea never left.

Not once. Not in seven days. She slept against the doorpost, sitting up, with his hand in her lap. She ate there. She went to the trough by the horses to wash and came straight back, and the first time she did it he counted, without meaning to, and it was four minutes, and when she came back she looked at his face and knew he’d counted and was angrier.

She didn’t talk much. Some days she didn’t talk at all. Some days she talked a lot, in the level voice, about nothing, about the barn and the gleaners and the weather, and then stopped in the middle of a sentence and looked at him and didn’t finish it, and he knew where the sentence had gone. Some days she was kind, a little, at the edges, and then she’d catch herself being kind and stop.

She made the soup.

That was the fourth day. He couldn’t. His hands were coming back a finger a day, Rachael’s way, and on the fourth day he had six, and none of them were good enough for a ladle. And at the hour he’d always made it, Thea got up, without letting go of his hand until the last second, and went to the Lammas oven, and made it.

It was terrible.

She brought him the first bowl. She sat down against the doorpost and held it while he ate, because he couldn’t hold it, and watched him eat it with her face perfectly still.

“It’s terrible,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Thea.

“There’s too much salt.”

“I know.”

“The onions aren’t cooked.”

“I know.”

He ate all of it.

“It’s the best thing I’ve ever eaten,” he said.

“Shut up,” said Thea.

But she gave him the second bowl too. And she didn’t take her hand back for the rest of the day, even when she could have.

The rest of them came and went.

Rachael, twice a day, a finger at a time. By the sixth day she’d done all twenty, his hands and his arms, and the white was gone except for a line round his left wrist like a bracelet, beside the faint grey one he’d had since Tell. Rachael looked at the two lines for a while. She didn’t say anything about them. She put her hand on his shoulder, once, briefly, which was more than she did for most people, and went.

Kei, every day, with his book. Measuring. Saying it out loud. The ribs, knitting. The shoulder, holding. The hands, coming back. The legs. Every day, the legs. A finger’s width back, then another, then two. By the seventh day Kei said most of it and wrote it down and didn’t draw a box round it.

Maigo, who’d come out of the sill grey and shaking and slept for two days against the doorpost on Ice’s other side, with Gigi watering him. On the third morning a new leaf came up on his antler. Small. Bright green. Gigi saw it first and made a sound and pointed and Maigo opened his eyes and looked at it cross-eyed and said “Oh. Hello,” and went back to sleep.

Gabe, with the lamp shield, on the fifth day. The dent beaten out. The rim the colour of old blood again. The lamp in the middle, polished. He leaned it against the doorpost beside Ice and stood looking at it.

“There,” said Gabe.

“Thank you.”

“Don’t.” Gabe crouched down. He looked at Ice for a long time. “You asked,” he said. “On the line. You asked Higu.”

“I asked.”

“First time,” said Gabe. “Ever. In your whole life. I’ve known you since I was thirteen and I’ve never heard you ask anybody for anything.” His jaw was tight. “You asked to go and nearly die. That’s the first thing you ever asked for.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“Next time,” said Gabe, “ask for something else.” He stood up. “Ask for a sandwich. Anything.” He looked at Thea. Thea looked back at him. Something went between them that Ice didn’t catch. Gabe nodded, once, and went.

Hyvred. Higu. Drew, who came and stood at the door and looked down at him and said, “Somebody thank me,” and Ice said, “For what?” and Drew said, “Nothing. I just wanted to hear it,” and Ice said, “Thank you, Drew,” and Drew nodded and went away, apparently satisfied. Hana and Milli, who came together, and stood there, and Hana said “It held,” very quietly, about the knot, and Milli didn’t say anything at all and had his hand on the ring on his finger the whole time. Calvin, every day. Saying something very fast and going.

Nobody asked him anything.

Nobody asked why the straw man had looked at him like that. Nobody asked how he’d known it would come out after him. Nobody asked about the first time. They came and stood by the door and said what they’d come to say and went. It was the thing they were best at. He lay there and let them, and was grateful, and couldn’t find a way to say so that didn’t sound like the start of something he couldn’t finish.

Hild came on the seventh day.

The Acre was going home. They’d decided it the night before, at the first table, Annet and the reeves and the old ones. The snow was real now. You could walk on it. The fields under it were real, all the way down the valley, bare, frozen, waiting; Hyvred had been up on the roof every day and said so. The villages were real. The houses were still there, cold, with the hearths gone out. Somebody had to go and light them.

They’d take a piece of the straw man each. That was the custom. Every village. A twist of him for the chimney corner, through the winter, so the children could touch it for luck. And in the spring they’d come back up the valley and take him down, and plough him into the first furrow of every first field, so the year went back in.

Hild had a twist of him in her hand.

She stood at the doorpost, leaning on her daughter, and looked down at him. Small and old and toothless, in her blanket.

“From his hand,” said Hild. “The right one. That held you.” She held it out. A short plait of straw, three strands, over and under, the ears at one end, tied with a band. Gold. “For your old woman.”

He looked at it.

“On floor 1,” said Hild. “I heard. At the line. Your snake boy said.” She held it out further. “For her chimney corner. Through the winter. So it’s kept.”

He couldn’t take it. His hands were back. He could have. He couldn’t.

Thea took it.

She let go of his hand to do it. The first time in seven days, except for the trough. She took the plait from Hild’s fingers, carefully, both hands, the way you’d take a bird. And she reached over and opened his vest, and put it inside. Into the pocket. Next to Bee’s portrait of him, folded small. Next to a blank white card from a white room.

She did the button up.

Then she took his hand again.

“Thank you,” said Thea. To Hild. Her voice did something on it.

Hild nodded. She looked at Thea for a moment, and then at him.

“She’s angry at you,” said Hild.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“Good,” said Hild. “Somebody should be.” And she went, slowly, on her daughter’s arm, out of the doors into the snow, to walk home.

Annet was last.

She came at the end, when the carts were packed in the lane and most of the Acre had already gone, a long dark line going down the white valley in the sun. She stood in the doorway with her stick and looked at him lying there with his back against the doorpost and Thea beside him.

“Bell went,” said Annet.

He looked up at her.

“First light. I rang it. Off an oven.” She sniffed. “Wren says it’s not a bell. Wren’s ten.” She looked down the valley. “Bell went. And you came in.” She looked back at him. “Took you a while. Two hundred paces, on your knees half of it. But you came in.” She tapped her stick on the sill, once. “Like a person.”

He didn’t say anything.

“Cobb said that about you,” said Annet. “On the east ridge. Like a person.” She almost smiled. “He was right. I’ll not tell him.”

She looked at the stick in her hand. At the notches on it, a hundred years of them, her mother’s and hers, a notch for every harvest day, a cross for every field in. The two rings for the frost, the one low down and the one she’d cut on the day of the swallows, much higher. And above that, new, near the top, cut so fresh the wood was still pale in it, something he’d never seen on the stick before.

A bell. A small rough shape of one. Cut with the point of a knife.

“Never had one of those,” said Annet. “On the stick. In a hundred years.” She tucked the stick under her arm. “There’s a song,” she said. “A bad one. Out of the old barn, and back to the old barn.” She looked at the thirty-six, all of them, in the doorway behind him, packed, waiting. “Passers come back down.”

“Yes,” said Ice.

“Then you’d better go and do it,” said Annet Sallow. And turned, and went down the lane after the carts, with her stick, and didn’t look back.

The stair was under the floor.

It had been there since the morning of the bell. Tiger found it. He’d gone to the line, where the chalk had been, where Ice had sat for three days, to rub out what was left of it, because Higu said to. And under the chalk, where the flagstones met in the middle of the aisle, there was a ring. Iron. Set in the stone. And round it, cut in the floor so fine you’d never have seen it if you weren’t on your knees with your nose to it, the edge of a door.

A plain round-topped door. In the floor. Exactly where he’d sat.

Tiger had opened it a crack. Stone steps, going down into the dark under the Long Barn, and somewhere down there, he said, turning, and going up. And warm air coming out. And a smell he couldn’t name. And a sound, very faint, a long way off, that he couldn’t describe and didn’t try.

He’d shut it. And sat on it. And waited for Higu.

Higu had come and looked at it, and at the rock, and written on the rock, in the third column, small:

FIFTEEN: UNDER THE LINE.

And under that:

LATER.

It was still under the line now. On the eighth morning. They stood round it, all thirty-six of them, with their packs on, in the empty Long Barn, with the straw man at the top of the barn looking at them and nobody else there at all. Ice stood on his own two feet. Kei had said he could. Most of it. He had the lamp shield on his back and his sword, which Wren had brought up from the field on the first afternoon and handed to Gabe very solemnly, as if it might bite, and a twist of straw in his vest.

Tiger had his hand on the ring.

“Open it?” said Tiger.

Higu looked at the rock. At the three columns. REMEMBERED, and TRUE, and NOW. And at the bottom of NOW, under FIFTEEN: UNDER THE LINE, the word LATER, and above everything, from the night of the vote, in the middle, bigger than the rest:

DOWN. ALL OF US.

“No,” said Higu.

Tiger took his hand off the ring.

And they turned round.

All thirty-six of them, at once, in the middle of the Long Barn, with their backs to a door that went up. And walked the other way. Down the aisle, between the empty tables, toward the doors and the snow and the long white valley going away south in the sun to wherever Haverley was, and the old barn at the bottom of it, and a stair, going down.

He looked back once. At the doors.

The straw man stood at the top of the barn. Gold. Kept. Looking at the doors, the way it always would. And from the middle of its chest, from the knot, so thin he could only feel it, a thread. Not asking. Just there.

He touched his vest, where the twist of straw was.

Then he went out into the snow.

It took three days to walk down the valley.

Real snow, on real fields. The gleaners’ tracks going ahead of them, a hundred carts, seven hundred people, all the way down. The barrows on the east ridge, white mounds with their dark doorways, and Hyvred said the bulls were breathing in there, slow, asleep, and would be till the gorse. Gaffer Tobin’s grave against the wall of the Long Barn, under the snow, with his stick laid along his side; they stopped there on the first morning, all of them, and Ina said nothing at all this time, because nothing needed saying, and Ice stood beside him and didn’t either.

Haverley on the third day. The bridge. The green with the great oak, bare now, black against the snow. The tithe barn. Annet in the door of her house with smoke coming out of the chimney, lifting her stick to them as they went past, once, the way Gaffer used to lift his at a field.

The third beam.

He stopped under it. He didn’t mean to. The tithe barn doors were open, and he looked in, at the east wall, at the third beam, where the first time a line had come up in World from nobody, and he’d gone out and stood under the oak in the snow, and come back, and sat down here. And somebody had sat down next to him without asking.

Thea stopped beside him.

She was holding his hand. She’d held it the whole way down the valley. Three days. She was still angry. He could tell. It was in her shoulders and the set of her mouth and the way she’d answered Calvin at breakfast. It hadn’t gone. It was just quieter now, the way a fire’s quieter when it’s been banked for the night.

“This is where we heard,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Thea. She looked at the beam. “This is where we’re going down from.”

They went on.

The old barn was where they’d left it, at the edge of Old Barn Piece, at the very bottom of the Long Acre, where the hills came together.

Real stone. Real snow on its roof. A stair in its end wall, going down, the one they’d come up out of on the four hundred and tenth day into the last of the summer, with the light coming in. Old Barn Piece round it, bare, white, waiting. The first field the Grey had taken. The first one that would be ploughed, in the spring, with a twist of straw man in its furrow.

They stood at the top of the stair.

“Count,” said Newish.

And they counted. Not numbers. Names. The way they always did, at the top of every stair, since floor 1. Newish first, and then round, all of them, the thirty-five, and then him, last, and he said Ice, and then Gabe nudged him, and he said Declan. Thirty-six. No gap.

“Four hundred and twenty-four,” said Newish. “Both ways.” She looked down the stair. “I said I’d count every one.”

They went down.

He was in the middle. Thea a step below him, going down, with her hand up behind her, holding his. Gabe a step above. The way they’d come up, on floor 13, on the stair out of Tell, only the other way round. He counted. Under his breath. Flat on the eights, the way he always had, since the second row of a choir a long time ago, and never once fixed.

Round about the fortieth step, Thea started to sing.

He hadn’t expected it. She’d said it on the rock on the far side of the Gate, under the aurora. I’ll sing on every step. On every floor. And she had. Every stair since. Up. He’d never thought about whether she’d do it going down.

She did it going down.

The second-row song. The one Gabe had hummed on the stair out of Tell. Quietly. To the steps. In the dark. With his hand in hers, and her shoulders still set, and her voice perfectly steady.

She was still angry. He could hear it in her. Under the song.

She sang anyway.

Gabe picked it up, above him, about the sixtieth step. Then Deston. Then more of them, one at a time, going down. Calvin, singing the wrong song, loudly, until Rachael said Calvin. Ice sang it too, flat on the eights, and didn’t fix it. Thea sang it right.

Four hundred and twenty-four steps. Going down. All thirty-six of them.

At the bottom there’d be Tell, and Aldo with his new handkerchief, and Joan’s green shutters. And after that the Salt, and Gigi’s road, and the sea, and Marrit on her quay saying names of an evening. Thirteen floors. A hundred and forty-two days. And at the bottom of all of it, at the bottom of a crooked street, a low room with a spoon painted on its sign and onions in the air, and an old woman who’d put them all on a tab and said they’d come back down.

He had a twist of straw for her chimney corner. In his vest. Next to his own face, laughing, and a blank card.

“Two hundred,” said Newish, from somewhere below. “Halfway.”

“Still angry,” said Thea. Between verses. Not looking back. Holding his hand.

“I know,” said Ice.

“Good,” said Thea. “Count.”

He counted.
