Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Prologue

The Hundredth Floor

2,418 words · 11 min read

The thing at the top of the Spire died the way a screen dies.

It didn’t fall. It didn’t scream. It had been sitting on its black glass throne for the whole fight, and it was still sitting there when Ice’s sword went into its face, because it had never once needed to stand. Its face was a window. It always had been: a pale, square panel where a head should be, the same soft white as every status window the thirty-six of them had opened every morning for ten years. Text crawled across it constantly, too small and too fast to read. When the blade went in, the text slowed.

Ice leaned on the hilt with everything he had left. The panel cracked from the point outward in a fine white web, and the light behind it went thin, and thinner, and the words ran slower and slower until he could have read them one at a time if he’d wanted to.

He didn’t want to. He’d stopped reading anything it said around the time it killed Drew.

The light went out.

The throne room was very quiet after that.

He stood there a while with his weight on the sword, because he wasn’t sure his legs would hold him if he let go. His shield arm hung at his side. The shield itself was somewhere behind him in two pieces. Gabe had made it on the sixty-first floor out of a dragon’s shoulder plate and a stubbornness that had lasted four days, and it had stopped the last blow, the one with all the room’s weight behind it, and then it had stopped existing. Ice’s forearm under it felt like a bag of gravel. He didn’t look at it.

[ Floor 100 · Boss defeated ]

The window opened in front of his face the way they always did, polite and centred and in no hurry. He let it sit there.

His own bar sat in the corner of his eye, red and very short. It was getting shorter. He’d stopped feeling the hole in his side a minute ago, which he knew from ten years of experience was not a good sign.

He pulled the sword out of the dead thing’s face. It came free with a sound like a plate breaking. He took one step back from the throne, and his knees decided for him, and he sat down hard on the floor of the hundredth floor of the Spire.

From there he could see all of them.

Deston was nearest the door. He’d been the one holding all thirty-six of them together for ten years, and he’d died the way he’d done everything else: standing in front of other people, with his hands out, holding them together. The links he’d thrown between the six of them to share their mana had still been glowing when he went down, faint gold threads from his palms to everyone else’s. They’d faded about ten seconds after he did. Ice had felt each of them go.

Yan was against the left wall, his scythe across his knees as if he’d sat down to rest. Suzu was coiled around him, very small and very white, her head on his collarbone. She’d gone a heartbeat after he had. Ice had heard her make one sound, and then nothing.

Alice was in the middle of the floor where the second phase had caught her. Her crown had come off. It lay a little way from her hand, the gold bead chains spread across the black stone like spilled water. She’d charmed the thing for four whole seconds near the end. Nobody had ever charmed it before. Four seconds was how long it had taken Ice to cross the room.

Drew was by the throne steps. His knuckles were still on. He’d fought the last fifteen minutes bare-handed, because his fists had stopped needing a blade somewhere around the ninetieth floor. He’d made the thing stand, once, for half a second. Ice would have liked to tell somebody that.

And Thea.

Thea was right in front of him. Close enough to touch, if he leaned. She’d been behind him for most of the fight, where a mage belonged, and then in the last ten seconds she hadn’t been. She’d seen it coming before he did. She always saw things before he did. She’d stepped around him and thrown up a ward he hadn’t known she could make, a whole wall of rewritten light, and the blow that should have gone through his chest had gone through the ward and then through her instead. It was the only reason he’d been close enough to finish it.

She’d said something as she went down. He’d been too busy killing the thing to hear what it was.

He’d been trying to work out what it was for eleven seconds now.

He looked at her for a long time. Her ponytail had come loose. The pink and blue streaks lay across her cheek. Her eyes were open, and one of the little star-shaped glints in them was still catching the light from the dead throne.

He reached over with the arm that still worked and closed them.

“Sorry,” he said, which was useless, and then, because nobody else could hear him: “I didn’t catch that. What you said. I’m sorry. I was busy.”

The room didn’t answer. Nothing in this room had ever answered a question honestly.

On the other side of the doors, the gate had gone quiet.

The boss-room doors were twenty metres of black iron, and they’d closed behind the six of them with a sound he could still feel in his back teeth. On the other side, the last stretch of corridor had been full of everything the Spire had left to throw at them, and the backline had held it so the six of them could get through.

Gabe, Xav, Gigichi, Azac, Cursiffe and Ina. Six for six. That was the trade they’d agreed on, sitting round a fire on the ninety-ninth floor, and nobody had argued, because they’d done the maths a hundred times and it came out the same every time. Six in front. Six behind. Close the doors.

He’d heard them through the iron for a long time. The first few minutes, the sound had been huge: Ina’s fire roaring, Cursiffe shouting the words of a seal. Then less. Then the gate had just been Gabe’s voice, and Xav’s, and the deep flat boom of Azac’s shield taking hits, the three of them calling to each other the way they always had. Gabe was always loudest. Xav always went quiet right before he did something clever.

Then Xav had gone quiet and not done anything after.

Then the boom of the shield had stopped.

Then, for a long time, it had just been Gabe, shouting at nothing in particular, the way he shouted at a forge when the steel wouldn’t take. Ice hadn’t been able to make out the words, but he’d known the tone. It was the tone of a man telling something it could have him when he was finished and not one second before.

Then nothing.

Ice didn’t go to the doors. He knew what was on the other side. He’d been carrying a list in his head for ten years: who, and where, and which floor, and what they’d said. He’d thought the list would feel heavier at the end. It didn’t. It felt finished. That was worse.

Thirty-six of them had woken up in a white room ten years ago. Thirty-five names were on the list now.

“One out of thirty-six,” he said to the ceiling. “Worst drop rate I’ve ever seen.”

It was a terrible joke. Somewhere under the noise in his head he could hear Gabe’s breath out through his nose, the tired one that meant Declan, no. He held on to it until it faded.

[ The Spire has been cleared ]

The second window opened over the first, then a third over that.

[ Surviving players: 1 of 36 ]

Ice read that one. He couldn’t help it.

“Ice.”

The voice came from his left, at knee height. It was a voice he’d heard every day for ten years, and only for the last two had it sounded like a person.

A fox was sitting on the black stone beside his leg. It was about the size of a cat and made of light: pale teal at the edges and almost white at the centre, one tail curled around its feet, two pinpricks of brighter white for eyes. When it moved, it left a faint shimmer in the air, like heat over a road. It hadn’t always looked like that. For eight years it had been a ball of light that said Welcome, Player and Quest updated and You have leveled up, like everyone else’s.

Then, on the eighty-eighth floor, at the end of a quest chain that had dragged him across fourteen floors and cost him most of a year, there’d been an altar that nobody had built a road to in a very long time. He’d stood in front of it holding something he’d taken from a boss: a soul, still warm, which he had no right to and no use for. And on what he thought later was the stupidest impulse of his life, he’d poured it into the only thing that had been with him every single day since the white room.

It had blinked at him for a while. Then it had said, in a voice that was not quite the old one, That was a terrible idea, and he’d laughed so hard he’d had to sit down.

She’d been a fox ever since. He’d named her Alea, because he named everything Alea. He’d named his first pet Alea, and every companion in every game he’d ever played. Gabe used to call it a disease.

“Your health’s at four percent,” Alea said.

“I know.”

“It’s going down.”

“I know that too.”

“I can’t stop it. I’ve tried everything I have. I’ve tried everything I don’t have.” Her ears went flat. “There’s a fourth window coming. I can see it loading. It isn’t one of mine.”

Ice looked at her. She looked back. Ten years of looking at each other had given them a lot of ways to say things without saying them.

The fourth window opened.

It was plainer than the others. No border, no centring, no polite little chime. Just two lines of text on white, and under them two buttons, side by side.

[ You have reached the top. Choose. ]

[ Go home ] [ Go back ]

He read it twice.

“Go back,” he said. “Back where?”

“I don’t know,” Alea said. “I don’t think it’s a place. I think it’s a when.” Her tail twitched. “Ice. I’ve never seen anything like this. In ten years I have never seen a window I couldn’t read the code under. I can’t read this one. It’s older than me. It’s older than it.” She tipped her head at the dead throne. “I think whatever made the systems left this here. For whoever got this far.”

“Nobody got this far,” he said. “I got this far.”

“Yes.”

He looked at Go home for a long time.

Home was Malacca. Home was a rented room with a fan that clicked on every third turn, and a kettle, and a view of a wall. It was twenty-six-year-old Declan’s phone on the bedside table, with messages from thirty-five people who would never send another one. Time hadn’t moved there, the systems had said on the first day, in their strange careful way. He would go back to the same night, the same minute. He’d probably be healed. It was a reward screen, after all; reward screens didn’t kill you.

He’d wake up, and it would still be that night, and the group chat would still be going. And somewhere in the world Gabe’s phone would be lying face-up on a bed in Singapore with nobody in the room to pick it up, and it would stay like that forever.

“If I go home,” he said, “they don’t.”

“No.”

“And if I go back?”

“Then I think you go back,” Alea said quietly. “To the start. To the white room. I think you’d be twenty-six and level one, and they’d all be there, and none of them would remember any of this. Not the floors. Not the fire on ninety-nine. Not her.” The fox didn’t look at Thea. She’d learned years ago which things not to look at. “You’d remember. That’s what it’s offering. Everything you know. Nothing you’ve got.”

“Would you remember?”

She was quiet for a moment. “I’ve got a soul,” she said. “You gave it to me. I think souls go where they’re carried. If you go, I’m coming. I’m not asking.”

His bar dropped to three percent. The edges of the room had started to grey.

He looked at Deston by the door. At Yan against the wall with Suzu on his chest. At Alice’s crown. At Drew’s knuckles. At the doors, and what he wasn’t going to look at behind them.

He looked at Thea last, and longest. He’d always done that, even back when there’d been nothing to look at her for: back on Earth, years ago, when he’d liked her and she’d known it, and it had passed, the way those things pass. Then ten years in this place, and it had come back so slowly he hadn’t noticed it arrive, the way you don’t notice a tide. A dinner on the forty-third floor that neither of them had called a date. Her hand on his arm on the seventieth, gone before he could think about it. Neither of them had ever said anything. There’d always be time once they got out.

She’d said something as she went down, and he hadn’t heard it, and now he never would.

Unless.

“Ice,” Alea said. “Two percent.”

He reached out and put his finger on Go back.

The window didn’t ask if he was sure. He was grateful for that. If it had asked, he might have laughed, and he didn’t have the breath.

“Okay,” he said, to all of them. “Again.”

The room went white.
