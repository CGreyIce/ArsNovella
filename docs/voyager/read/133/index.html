Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 133

Sow

6,503 words · 28 min read

Every morning for three hundred and forty days, Gigi had opened her watch, and every morning it had said nine minutes past two.

On the Salt, she opened it lying on her back on the crust, in her bedroll, with Bee asleep against one shoulder and Hyvred against the other, the way the three of them had slept every night since the door, for the warmth. You couldn’t hear anybody breathe on the Salt. You had to feel it. So they slept close enough to feel it.

She opened the lid with her thumb. The little gold leaf on it, worn smooth. Nine past two. The second hand stuck halfway between the four and the five.

Fine, she thought. Home, holding its breath.

She shut it.

Then she put her hand out flat on the salt beside her bedroll, under the blanket, where nobody could see, the way she’d done every morning since the three hundred and nineteenth day, and asked.

Nothing came.

It never did, here. That was the thing she hadn’t told anybody. Not Hyvred, not Bee, not Thea. On every floor since the white room, she’d put her hand on the ground and asked, and something had come. Warm. Out of her palm, down, into the earth, pulling. And then up. Thorns. Brown-gold, hooked, holding. Bramble. It was hers. It had always been hers. On floor 9 the Grow had swallowed it, and that had been the worst day of her life on this world, the day it went into the dark and didn’t come back, it’s not mine, it’s his. But even then, it had come. It had gone down into the ground and tried.

On the Salt, it didn’t go down.

It went to the bottom of her palm and stopped. Like a hand reaching into a bag and finding the bag sewn shut. There was no down. There was salt, and under it brine, and under that more salt, a thousand years of it, and nothing in any of it that would let a root in. She asked, and the warmth went to the skin of her palm and pressed there, against the crust, and couldn’t get through.

Twenty-one mornings.

She took her hand back under the blanket and lay there and looked at the white sky.

Plant something where nothing has grown, said the window, which she didn’t need to open, because she knew it by heart. It had sat in the corner of her eye since floor 9. Since the Sower’s ash. Not this floor, she’d said, then. Later.

It was later.

She had no idea how.

Maigo brought her the pot on the three hundred and fortieth morning.

He came across the camp in the grey light, slowly, the way he walked now, with one hand on Niko’s shoulder and the clay pot held against his chest in the other. The bough-folk’s seedling pot, rough and brown. He’d had it every night since Ice gave it to him. He sat with his root-foot in it by Ina’s fire. Gigi had watched him do it, and watched his face go soft every time his toes went in, and had felt something in her chest she didn’t have a name for. Not jealousy. Something next to it.

He sat down on the end of her bedroll. Niko let go of his shoulder and went to see about breakfast.

“Hi,” said Maigo.

“Hi,” said Gigi.

He had a leaf. She saw it before anything else. Up on his antler, on the left, near the top, beside the one old brown-edged one that had survived the dip in the salt: a new one. Small. Pale, almost yellow, uncurled now, the size of her thumbnail. The bud from the morning Ice sat with him.

“It opened,” she said.

“Yesterday.” He touched it. He couldn’t seem to stop touching it. “I’ve got two now. That’s a hundred per cent more than I had.”

Gigi laughed. You couldn’t laugh on the Salt, not properly, but it came out of her anyway, into her sleeve.

He held out the pot.

“Ice said you’d want this,” said Maigo.

She looked at it. At the grey soft earth in it, with the faint smell of ash and old leaves and the Down. Highcrook. The north tree’s foot. The hearth Ina had made, where the stone-cone opened, and the seedling that Gigi had kept alive for eight days with a hedge of thorns, come back every day, until it didn’t need her any more.

She’d been there every morning. She knew that earth. She’d knelt in it.

“For what?” she said.

“To start it,” said Maigo. “He said. She’s going to need something to start it in. You don’t plant in salt.”

Gigi looked at the pot for a long time.

“How did he know?” she said.

Maigo didn’t answer straight away. He sat on the end of her bedroll with his two leaves and his root-foot bare on the wood of the sled-runner beside him, and looked at the pot in her hands. And his face did something. She’d known Maigo a long time. Since before. She’d watched his face on every floor, in the summer and the winter, and she’d never seen it do this. Like somebody holding a thing very carefully in a closed hand, and deciding, again, not to open it.

“I asked him that,” said Maigo. “He said he didn’t. He said he just thought you would.”

Gigi didn’t say anything.

She was in the club. She’d been in it since the round house on floor 6, since Thea said the word in the dark. She knew what the club thought. She knew what was on Drew’s page and Chris’s page and Higu’s ONES BEFORE page and the page with no heading that Thea kept in her head. She knew what I just thought you would might mean, from a man who’d carried a pot of earth up three floors for a hundred and three days.

Maigo wasn’t in the club.

She looked at his face.

Oh, she thought. Oh, Maigo. You’ve found it too.

“Thank you,” she said. And took the pot.

She told them at breakfast.

All of them, her party, round the sled with the nut bread from Seep that tasted of salt and the cups of Ina’s water that didn’t. She told them in a whisper, close, the way you had to. She told them about the twenty-one mornings. About her hand on the crust and the bag sewn shut.

Hyvred put her bread down.

“Gi,” she said. Not loud. You couldn’t. But the shape of it was capitals.

“I know.”

“Twenty-one days—“

“I know. I didn’t want—“ Gigi stopped. “Maigo didn’t say about his leaves either. Till Winter took his hand.” She looked at the pot. “It’s the Salt. It’s what it does. It makes you hide the thing that’s going wrong, because you can’t say it out loud anyway.”

Kei was looking at her very steadily, with Dose round his neck. Dose was looking at her too. Kei’s snake didn’t like the Salt. She’d been round his neck since the door and hadn’t come down once.

“It’s not gone,” said Kei. “Is it. The magic. It’s just got nowhere to go.”

“I think so.”

“Then you need somewhere for it to go.”

“There’s nowhere,” said Gigi. “That’s the whole—“ She held up the pot. “Except this. And this isn’t nowhere, Kei. Things have grown in this. A whole tree grew in this. The window says where nothing has grown.” She looked out at the white. “That’s out there. That’s the Salt. That’s the only place on this floor that’s never had anything grow in it. Ever.”

“So you plant on the salt,” said Hako.

“You can’t plant on the salt. Ice said. You don’t plant in salt.”

“Ice also said,” said Hako, “and I’m quoting, it should burn, I think, it’s a plant, mostly.” He took a bite of nut bread. “Classic. He’s not always right about plants.”

Bee had her sketchbook open on her knee. She hadn’t drawn anything. She was looking at the pot.

“What would grow in salt?” said Bee.

Nobody answered.

And then Gigi looked up, across the camp, to where Maigo was sitting by the cold ring of Ina’s pots with his root-foot in nothing, on the wood, with Niko.

A forest, he’d said. In the dip. With his one leaf, hanging off his own root in Chris’s arms. A thousand years of seeds. Every kind. Asleep. Waiting.

Every kind.

“Maigo,” said Gigi. Not loud. It didn’t carry. She got up and went.

“Some of them grew at the edge,” said Maigo.

He said it slowly. He’d put his foot in the pot again, the Highcrook earth, on the salt by Gigi’s bedroll, because she’d asked him to, and shut his eyes, and gone down. Not with his roots. He didn’t have to, any more. That was the thing his new window had given him. Undergrowth. Root anywhere, however deep. He could put his foot in a pot of earth on the crust and go down through it, through the clay, through the salt, all the way to the dark flat water under everything, as if the pot were a door.

He’d been down there a long time. The camp was packing up round them. Higu had looked at the two of them, and at Gigi’s face, and at the pot, and written something on the rock, and told everybody to unpack again.

“When it was a sea,” said Maigo. Eyes still shut. “There was an edge. A shore. And at the edge there were things growing in the shallows. In the mud. Where the sea came in and went out and left the salt behind on them every day.” He frowned. “They didn’t mind it. The salt. They liked it. They drank it. They grew fat on it.”

Gigi was kneeling in front of him on the crust.

“And their seeds blew out,” said Maigo. “On the wind. Over the sea. And fell in. And sank. And then the sea went. And the salt came over them.” He was quiet. “Most of the seeds down there are forest ones. Grass, and thorn, and things from a long way off, that blew a long way. They’re asleep. Deep asleep. They’ll want rain. Real rain, fresh, for a long time, before they wake up.” He breathed out. “But the edge ones aren’t deep asleep. They’re just waiting. They grew in salt. They’ve been lying in salt for a thousand years thinking, this is fine. This is just a bit much.”

Gigi made a sound.

“One of them knows I’m here,” said Maigo. “Under us. Not far. Twenty rings. Thirty.” His eyes opened. They were very bright. “It turned over when I came down. Like a dog in its sleep when you say its name.”

“Where?”

He pointed. Not at the pot. At the crust, a hand’s width to the left of it. At the plain white salt.

“There,” said Maigo. “Straight down.”

She’d only done it once before.

On floor 9, at the foot of the north tree, in the ash, with the Sower standing up out of the ground over all of them and its knees torn out of the old roots, trailing a thousand threads. She’d let go of its hand and grabbed the roots instead, the last anchor still in the hole, and pulled. Uproot. What was never planted, you can pull. She’d pulled a thing out of the earth that had been in it for a thousand years, and it had come, because it had never belonged there. Because nobody had ever planted it. It had just come down.

These seeds had never been planted either.

They’d fallen. Off a shore, on a wind, into a sea. Nobody had ever put one in the ground and pressed the earth down over it and said grow. They’d just come down. And lain there.

She put her hand flat on the crust. Where Maigo pointed.

They were all round her. She felt them more than saw them. Hyvred on her left, with her glasses pushed up into her hair and her eyes doing the thing, the true sight, looking down into the white. Bee on her right with the sketchbook shut, for once, held against her chest. Hako behind her, kneeling, with his hands on her shoulders, because Hako always put his hands on your shoulders when he thought you were going to need holding up, and was usually right. Kei, a little way off, with his fingers on Maigo’s wrist, counting. Maigo, with his foot in the pot, eyes shut again, down there with it.

And Thea.

She’d come across the camp with the lantern. Gigi hadn’t seen her come. She just looked up, and Thea was there, kneeling on the salt across from her, holding the lantern low over Gigi’s hand on the crust, the way she’d held it every morning for eight days at the foot of the north tree in the dark.

“Coming,” said Thea. Very quietly.

Gigi looked at her.

Then she looked down, and shut her eyes, and asked.

It went to the bottom of her palm and stopped. The bag sewn shut. Like every morning.

Not Bramble, she thought. Not growing. Pulling.

She stopped asking it to go down. She asked it to reach. That was different. Growing went down and came up. Pulling reached, and held, and came back. She’d never thought about it before. On floor 9 she hadn’t had time to think. Now she had all the time in the world, on her knees on the Salt, and she let the warmth go to the bottom of her palm, and press against the crust, and she didn’t push it through. She just reached. Through the skin of her hand. Through the crust. Not as a root. As a hand.

Down.

It was like reaching into cold water for something on the bottom. Further than your arm went. She went down through the crust, and the slurry, and the first ring of salt, and the next. She felt them go past. Band and line. Band and line. Hard and dry and nothing in them, like Maigo said. Ten. Twenty. Her hand on the crust had started to hurt.

“Gi,” said Hyvred, very low. “Your nose.”

She knew. She could taste it.

Thirty rings.

And there it was.

She didn’t see it. She felt it. Small. Hard. A point. Lying in the salt the way a stone lies in a riverbed, like it had always been there and always would be. And it wasn’t a stone. It was the other thing. It was the thing her thorns felt like, when they were coming. It was waiting.

And it knew her.

Not her. Maigo. It knew Maigo was there, because his window said it would, what is waiting under the ground will know you are there. And she was there with him. Her hand next to his foot. So it knew her too, a bit. The way you’d know somebody standing next to a friend.

She closed her hand on it.

And pulled.

It didn’t want to come. Nothing that had been still for a thousand years wanted to come. It held on to the salt round it the way the Sower’s knees had held on to the north tree’s roots. She pulled anyway. Slowly. Not in a hurry. Bee says you never let go of anything. She didn’t let go. She pulled, and it came a ring, and stuck, and she pulled, and it came another, and the salt held on to it all the way up, every band, and every band came out of her.

She felt it. In her arm. In her chest. In the back of her head, behind her eyes. The Salt took everything out of you, and it took this. Each ring was like climbing a stair carrying something heavy. Her nose was bleeding properly now, onto the crust. She felt Hako’s hands tighten on her shoulders. She felt her arms start to shake.

Twenty rings. Fifteen.

“Gi,” said Hyvred. “Gi, it’s coming, I can see it, I can see it coming up, it’s like a spark, it’s—“

Ten.

And then something came down a gold thread into her. From behind. A cup of somebody. She didn’t know who. She didn’t need to look round to know whose threads they were. Deston, at the next sled, with his hands out, and his face set, and the gold going from him into her and into everybody, what one of you has, and from everybody, a little, into her.

Five.

The crust cracked under her palm.

A hair-line. In the white. Under her hand. And through it, into the hollow of her palm, pressed up out of the salt by her own closed fingers, something hard and tiny and cold.

She opened her hand.

A seed.

Black. Smaller than a grain of rice. Crusted white with salt, like a stone that’s been in the sea. Lying in her palm in the lamplight, with her blood on her wrist and Thea’s lantern held low over it.

Nobody said anything. You couldn’t, on the Salt. But Gigi heard Hyvred breathe in, all the way, the long ragged breath Hyvred took before she screamed at things, and then not scream. Hold it. Let it out again, very slowly, with her hand over her mouth.

“Hi,” said Maigo, with his eyes shut. To the seed. “There you are.”

She planted it at noon.

She’d thought about it. She’d sat on the crust for an hour with the seed in her closed hand and Kei cleaning her face and Hako’s hands still on her shoulders and thought about it, because the window said where nothing has grown and she was going to get it right.

Not in the pot. Things had grown in the pot.

On the Salt.

She made a hollow. With the butt of Hako’s spear, in the crust, in the plain white salt a hand’s width to the left of where Maigo’s pot had been, right over the place the seed had come up from. Not deep. A thumb’s depth. A little cup in the salt.

And then she took a pinch of the Highcrook earth. Just a pinch. From the pot, between her finger and thumb. Grey and soft, with ash in it. And she didn’t put it under the seed. She put it round. A thin ring of it, in the cup of salt, like a frame. So the seed lay on the salt itself, on the bottom of the hollow, where nothing had ever grown, with the earth of a forest three floors down round it in a circle.

To start it, Ice had said.

Not to plant it in. To start it. A door.

She put the seed down in the middle. On the salt.

Then Ina’s water. One cup. Clear, from the still, from the steam off the brine under the crust, that had gone up into Winter’s frost and come back down without the salt. She poured it in slowly. Into the ring. It ran over the earth and pooled in the bottom of the hollow, round the seed, and the salt drank some of it, and the earth held the rest.

And then she took off her crown.

She’d braided it on floor 6, at the top of the pit at Deepwell, out of the long dark-green grass that grew round the cistern, in the night before the reading club. It had been green for a long time. It had gone brown on floor 11 in the sea wind and brittle on the Salt. It was crooked. It had always been crooked. Bee said it made her look like a hedge that had been in a fight.

She laid it on the crust round the hollow. A ring round the ring.

It didn’t do anything. It was a dead crown made of dead grass.

But it was hers, and it was round it.

Then she sat back on her heels.

“That’s it,” she said.

“That’s it?” said Hyvred.

“That’s what you do,” said Gigi. “You put it in. You water it. You put something round it. And then you wait.” She wiped her nose with the back of her wrist. It had stopped. “There isn’t anything else. That’s all gardening is. Everybody thinks there’s something else.”

They waited.

Higu let them have a day. She stood on the aft sled with the rock and looked at Gigi’s hollow in the salt for a long time and then wrote 1 DAY and underlined it, and nobody argued, because the still made four casks a night now and one day was only a day.

Gigi lay on her stomach beside it.

All afternoon, all evening, the way she’d lain on her stomach on the floor of the hanging house on floor 9 with her face to the gap in the withies, looking down at a thing she couldn’t see. She could see this. She could see the crown, and the ring of grey earth inside it, and the little cup of salt, and the water going down, slowly, into the salt and the earth. And the seed. Black. Not doing anything.

Maigo sat with her for a while with his foot in the pot. Then Bee, drawing the hollow. The same view, over and over, the way she’d drawn the seedling at the north tree’s foot every morning from the drum platform, so you could see it do it. Then Hyvred, lying beside her, not saying anything, which for Hyvred was the hardest thing in the world.

At dusk, Thea came.

She sat down on the crust on the other side of the hollow with the lantern and put it down by the crown, low, the amber coming warm over the white. And didn’t say anything. The way she hadn’t at the north tree, for eight mornings. Just there.

They sat for a long time.

Across the camp, Ina’s fires came up. The water fire under the pots, white-blue, in the steel tent. And the small orange one, out on the salt, ten metres west, burning on nothing. Gabe’s. Ice hadn’t gone out past it tonight. He’d looked at the hollow at dusk, and at Gigi lying beside it, and at Thea, and put his sword down by the sled, and sat by Ina’s fire instead.

Gigi looked at her across the hollow.

She’d known Thea a long time. Longer than this world. She’d put everything on her, once, on Earth. Every bad night and every good one, until Thea couldn’t carry any more of it and put it down, and walked away, and was right to. And then on floor 9, at the foot of a tree in the dark, Thea had come down a rope with a lantern every morning for eight days and said coming. And Gigi had said, on the sixth morning, on her knees in the ash, the thing she’d never said to anybody: I blamed you for everything. And Thea had said I know. And they’d put their foreheads together, and that had been the start of it.

And Thea had changed since.

Not since floor 9. Since floor 10. Since the Gate, and the spring after, and a night under the aurora that Thea never talked about. Gigi had watched her on every floor since. Thea carried something now. You could see it, if you knew her. In how she sat. Like somebody with a pack on that they’d stopped noticing was there, the way you stop noticing a pack after a week, except that every so often she’d shift it, without thinking, and you’d see how heavy it was.

Gigi was in the club. She thought she could guess what was in it.

She didn’t ask.

“You’re carrying something,” said Gigi instead. Very low. Across the seed.

Thea looked at her.

“Since ten,” said Gigi.

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Yes,” she said.

“Is it heavy?”

“Yes.”

Gigi nodded. She looked down at the hollow. At the seed in the salt in the ring of forest earth in the ring of her dead crown.

“Okay,” she said.

That was all. She didn’t say anything else. She didn’t need to. She sat on the salt with Thea in the lamplight, and after a while she reached out across the hollow and took the lantern. Gently. Out of Thea’s hand. And held it. Low. Over the seed. The way Thea had held it for her.

“I’ve got it,” said Gigi. “For a bit. You can put your hands down.”

Thea looked at her empty hands.

She didn’t say anything. Her face did something that Gigi had never seen it do in all the years she’d known her, on two worlds. It went soft all at once, from the middle, like something giving way.

She put her hands in her lap.

It was the first time in her life, Gigi thought, that she’d ever taken something off Thea instead of putting something on her. It didn’t feel like anything much. It felt like holding a lamp.

They sat there until midnight, Gigi holding the lantern and Thea with her hands in her lap, neither of them saying a word. And when Ice came across from Ina’s fire in the small hours, and stopped at the edge of the light and looked at them, Thea didn’t get up. She just looked at him. And he looked at Gigi holding the lantern, and something happened to his face, and he sat down on the salt where he was, a long way off, and watched them.

He didn’t come any closer. He stayed there until dawn.

On the three hundred and forty-first morning, she opened her watch. Nine past two.

She shut it and rolled onto her stomach and looked.

Nothing.

The seed. Black. On the salt. In the cup. The water had gone down in the night, into the salt and the earth, and the ring of grey Highcrook earth had gone dark with it. The seed hadn’t moved. It was exactly as she’d left it.

She lay there and looked at it until the sun was up.

Then she got up and poured another cup of Ina’s water into the ring, slowly, and sat back on her heels, and waited.

All day. Nothing.

Higu stood on the aft sled at dusk and looked at the hollow and looked at the rock and didn’t write anything. She came across the camp instead, and crouched down by Gigi, and looked at the seed.

“Tomorrow,” said Higu. Very low. “We have to go tomorrow. At noon. The Mast’s thirteen days.” She paused. “I’m sorry, Chi.”

Higu only ever called her that in private. Nobody else ever had.

“I know,” said Gigi.

“You can leave it,” said Higu. “With a hedge. Like the young tree. It holds while you’re gone.”

“My hedge doesn’t work here.”

“I know,” said Higu. “I’m sorry.” She stood up. “Noon.”

Gigi lay down on her stomach beside the hollow, and didn’t sleep.

It came up in the night.

She didn’t see it start. Nobody did. She’d fallen asleep in the end, in the small hours, on her face on the salt beside the crown, with the lantern beside her gone low, because Thea had put it there and gone to sleep herself, on the other side, curled up. Gigi woke because the light had changed. Grey. Then a line of gold in the east.

She didn’t open her watch.

She lifted her head off the salt and looked.

There was a thread.

In the cup. On the salt. Out of the black seed, which had split. A thread, no thicker than a hair, white, going down. Into the salt. Not into the earth. Past it. Straight down into the white, into the crust, the way her roots had gone into the Down on floor 9 when she was asking. It had gone down a finger’s depth. She could see where it went in.

And up.

The other way. Up out of the split seed. A point. A tiny pointed thing, no bigger than a grain of rice standing on its end. Pale at the bottom. And at the top, where the light from the east was just coming over the edge of the world and lying across the salt, gold, touching it.

Green.

Not much. A smear. A point of it. Red at the very tip, like a match-head, and below that, green.

On the Salt.

Gigi didn’t move. She couldn’t. She lay on her stomach on the crust with her chin on her hands and looked at it, and didn’t breathe, and the sun came up behind her over the white, the way it did every morning, whether anybody was ready or not, and lit it.

The windows came up.

She put her face down on her arms on the salt.

She didn’t make a sound. She couldn’t. Nobody could, on the Salt. She lay there with her face in her arms and her shoulders going, and after a while, she felt somebody lie down beside her, on her left, close enough to feel breathe. Hyvred. And somebody on her right. Bee. The way they slept. And Hako’s hand on her back. And Kei, somewhere. And Thea, on the other side of the crown, awake now, with her hand over her mouth.

When Gigi lifted her head, Hyvred was looking at the green point in the salt with her glasses pushed up and her eyes doing the thing.

And Hyvred opened her mouth, all the way, and screamed at it.

Silently. The whole thing. The long ragged breath in and then the scream, every bit of it, her face screwed up and her fists clenched and her whole body in it, the way she’d screamed at the sun on floor 5 and at every good thing on every floor since, in capitals. Except that on the Salt it came out of her as nothing at all. Not a sound. Just Hyvred, on her knees on the crust at dawn, screaming in perfect silence at a thing the size of a grain of rice.

Bee started laughing. Silently. Then Hako. Then Gigi, into her sleeve, crying and laughing at the same time, and Hyvred went on screaming until she ran out of breath and fell over sideways onto the salt and lay there, and Thea had both hands over her face.

“It took,” said Gigi, when she could. Into her sleeve. “It took. Oh, it took.”

Maigo came across the camp on Niko’s arm, as fast as he could, which was not very.

He knelt by the hollow. Slowly. He put his foot in the pot, because he always did now. And then he leaned down, with his two leaves on his antler, the old one and the new one, until his face was very close to the salt. To the green point. And looked at it for a long time.

“Hi,” said Maigo. “I’m Maigo. You’re going to be—“

He stopped. He looked at it.

It was the size of a grain of rice. It was going to be the size of a hand, if it was lucky. It was a salt plant. Fat and jointed and low, like the ones Maigo said grew at the edge. It was never going to be enormous. Not ever.

“—small,” said Maigo. “You’re going to be small.” His face did something wonderful. “That’s fine. That’s fine. Somebody has to be.”

Gigi laughed so hard into her sleeve that she had to lie down again.

Ice came last.

She’d known he would. He’d come across from the fire in the night and sat a long way off at the edge of the light and watched her hold the lantern, and he’d still been there at dawn. She’d seen him when she lifted her head. Sitting on the crust with his knees up and the lamp shield beside him and his helm in his lap, twenty metres away, looking at the hollow. Looking at her.

He came over when the others had gone back to the sleds. When it was just her and the green point. He crouched down on the other side of the crown, where Thea had sat, and looked at it.

He didn’t say anything for a long time.

“It’s green,” he said, eventually.

“It’s green,” said Gigi.

He looked at it. Then he looked up at her. And his face—

She was in the club. She’d sat in the round house on floor 6 with her crooked crown on her head and heard Thea say the word, and she’d sat on every floor since and watched the slips come, one at a time, and kept them. She knew what the club thought. She knew there was a list, somewhere, in somebody’s head, of the ones who hadn’t come back the first time, and she didn’t know who was on it, and she’d never let herself think about whether she was.

She thought about it now.

Because Ice was looking at her across a seed in the salt the way you’d look at somebody you’d lost, who’d come back, and was kneeling on the ground in front of you with blood dried on her wrist, growing something.

“Thank you,” said Ice.

Very quietly.

“For what?” said Gigi.

He didn’t answer. He looked at her a moment longer. Then he looked down at the green point in the cup of salt, and reached out, very slowly, the way you’d reach out to something that might break, and didn’t touch it. Held his finger a hair’s width above it. Just above the red tip. The way you’d hold your hand over a candle.

Then he took it back, and got up, and went. Fast. Toward the sleds. Not looking at anybody.

Thea was already walking to meet him.

Gigi watched them. She watched Thea reach him, out on the white between the hollow and the sleds, and say something, and him stop, and stand there with his head down, and Thea put her hand flat on his back.

She didn’t ask.

She looked down at the green point instead.

Okay, she thought. Okay. Then I’m staying. She didn’t know why she thought it. It was Thea’s word, from the north tree. You said staying. That’s what you wanted most.

She put her hand flat on the salt beside the hollow, and asked.

And it went down.

Not far. Not much. Just a little. The warmth, out of her palm, into the crust, down. Through the place where the thread had gone, the white root, the hair of it, a finger’s depth into the salt. Down along it, the way water goes down a string. And then out, and round, and up.

Thorns.

Tiny ones. Brown-gold. Out of the salt. A ring of them, a hand’s width across, coming up round the hollow inside the dead crown, hooked, holding, the way they always had. Not tall. Not thick. A hedge the height of a thumb. But a hedge.

Hedgerow. Grow it round what you love. It holds while you’re gone.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

It had come up out of the salt. Out of the one place on the floor where something grew. Along the root, the way a path goes along a river.

Sow, said the window. Plant it where nothing grows. It will take.

“Oh,” said Gigi. Out loud. To nobody. It went down into the salt, and she didn’t care. “Oh, I see. I see. You start it. And then there’s somewhere.”

They left at noon.

She left it. That was the hardest thing. Harder than the pulling. She stood at the hollow with her pack on and her party round her and looked at it, the green point and the white root and the ring of thumb-high thorns and the dead crown round all of it, alone on the Salt, with the sleds already moving west behind her, softly.

“It holds while you’re gone,” said Bee, beside her.

“I know.”

“You let go of the young tree.”

“I know.” Gigi didn’t move. “I never let go of anything.”

“You let go of that,” said Bee.

Gigi looked at her.

Then she turned round, and picked up the strap of the fourth sled, and put it across her chest, and walked.

She didn’t look back. She’d decided not to. She managed it for about two hundred metres. Then she looked, and there was nothing to see, just white, and the faint dark spot of the crown on it, very small, getting smaller.

That night, at the camp, when Ina had the pots going and the shields were up and Winter was on the sled at the top with her arrowhead, Gigi went to Maigo with the pot of Highcrook earth and said, “Again?”

Maigo put his foot in it, and shut his eyes, and went down.

“There,” he said, after a while. “That one’s awake.”

She put her hand on the crust.
