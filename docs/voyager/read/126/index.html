Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 126

Knots

8,459 words · 37 min read

Cursiffe kept two books.

The first was the little one he’d had since floor 1. It was nearly full now. Monsters, mostly. How they moved, what they did just before they did the thing that hurt, which side they favoured, what they did when they were frightened. Doors, and where they’d been. And ink. A great deal about ink. Frozen on two. Dry on six. Runs on eight. Keep the ink warm. Honey at noon. Eel oil in the spray. If anybody ever read it after he was gone, they’d think he’d been a man with a serious problem about ink, and they’d be right.

The second was newer. He’d started it on floor 6, at the top of the pit, the night he’d heard Hako say club across the round room and stop, and look at him, and not say anything else. It had one word on the cover, VOYAGER, and nothing inside it that he hadn’t seen or heard himself, or been told by somebody who had. When it was the last kind, he wrote who.

On the night of the three hundred and fifth day, after the four seconds, he sat in the galley of the Patience with that one open on his knee.

Ottery let him. He wasn’t sure why. She let almost nobody into the galley, which was a low hot box under the quarterdeck with a brick stove in it and hooks in the beams and every pan on the hooks in an order that only Ottery knew, and she guarded it the way Salty guarded the anvil and Wendy guarded her arrows. Ice had been in there every day since they sailed and was still, as far as Cursiffe could tell, on probation. But Cursiffe had sat down on the upturned bucket in the corner on the first night out of Hythe, with his book, and not touched anything, and not said anything, and Ottery had looked at him for a long time with her small dark eyes in her vast silent face and gone back to her stove. And every night since, there’d been a mug of something by the bucket when he came in.

He thought it might be because he didn’t talk. Ottery didn’t talk either. It was restful.

He wrote with the eel-oil ink, small and square, by the light of the lamp on its hook.

Day 305. In the edge of the Undertow. Hana: “I don’t know how long I can hold something that big.” Ice: “Four seconds. You held it four seconds.” Hana: “When?” Nobody asked. I didn’t either. (Heard. Everyone did.)

He read it back. Then he turned back a page and read the ones before it.

Day 287. The rota on the rock: SHOALS (S. REEF): ICE. THEA, XOLA WATCHING. Thea at breakfast: “He was head the first—“ She stopped. Nobody asked. (Heard.)

Day 299. The first night over deep water, everybody put wax in. He put his in without being shown. Like he’d done it a hundred times. (Milli. Jared. I was below.)

Day 300. In the galley, to Aleaka: “Two months. Every night. I never once took it out.” (Aleaka. She told me because I asked her what she was so cross about. I am writing it down because she said I could.)

He read them all again.

Then he turned back further. Floor 10. Floor 9. Floor 8. Floor 7, and the blue door, and the face. Floor 6, and the noon trail through the Prism’s beams that bent left and right and stopped, he walked the gaps. Floor 5. He’d gone back and written in the early ones later, from what people remembered, with who after each, and he’d been very careful about it. Page after page. In his own hand, small and square. Nothing on any of them that he hadn’t seen or heard or been told. All of it true.

And all of it went the same way.

That was the thing. That was what he sat and looked at, in the galley, with Ottery’s back to him and the stove ticking and the sea going past the planks a hand’s breadth from his ear. Every entry on its own was a small thing. A man who knew too much about a door. A man who put wax in his ears the right way. A man who said four seconds as if he’d counted them before. On their own, any of them could have been anything. But they didn’t sit on the page on their own. They sat together. And when you put enough small true things together on a page, they started to point. The way iron filings point, if you put a magnet under the paper. You couldn’t see the magnet. You could only see every single filing on the page lying down in the same direction.

He knew which direction. He’d known for a while.

He didn’t write it.

He sat with the pen over the page for a long time, and didn’t. Because it wasn’t something he’d seen. It was something he’d worked out. And those were different things, and the difference was the whole point of the book. A book of true things you’d seen would keep. He’d been told so, on a ledge under a bridge on floor 5, by a stone scribe with a keyhole in its lap. Write what you saw. Keep it true. A book of things you’d worked out was a different book, and other people were already writing that one, in a corner by the fish, and he wasn’t in it.

And it wasn’t his. That was the other thing. Whatever it was, it was Ice’s, and Ice hadn’t said it.

So he put the pen down, and blew on the page, which he didn’t need to do with eel-oil ink but always did anyway, and closed the book.

In his shirt pocket, the leaf was warm.

It had been warm for days. Since the night the singer came. He took it out and held it flat on his palm, the thin stone leaf the statue had given him, the size of a tongue, grey and smooth. Warm, the way a stone is warm when it’s been in the sun. On floor 6 it had been warm toward the Prism’s scars. Toward what remains. He turned slowly on the bucket, holding it, the way he’d learned to, and felt it cool on one side and warm on the other, and stopped.

North-west. Behind them. Back the way they’d come.

Where the Undertow was.

He sat and held it for a while.

What remains, he thought. What remains of what?

Through the galley hatch he could see a slice of the main deck, silver under the moon. The foot of the mainmast. The nest of sailcloth. Corran in it, very small, with his blanket up to his chin. Maigo beside him on a coil of rope with his back to the mast and his eyes open, the way he’d sat every night since the launch, with one hand resting very lightly on the blanket.

Cursiffe watched them. He watched Maigo wait. He watched the blanket, for a long time, until it moved.

Then he went to bed.

In the morning the singer turned west.

It had been running south ahead of them since the edge, a long pale shape under the water, now and then rising enough to show the ragged ridge of its back with the sun coming through it. The hum came up through the planks all night and all day. You stopped noticing it, mostly, the way you stopped noticing the Breath on floor 10 after a while. And then it changed, and you noticed.

It changed at dawn on the three hundred and sixth day. Lower. Longer. And the shape ahead of them in the water leaned, slowly, the whole enormous length of it, and swung round to the west, into the sun’s shadow, and went on.

Marrit stood at the wheel and watched it go.

“That’s not round,” she said.

Nobody answered. She wasn’t talking to anybody. She’d taken her wax out four nights ago with everybody else, and she hadn’t stopped looking at the sea since, not properly, not for one minute that Cursiffe had seen. As if she’d been looking at it with her eyes shut for thirty years and somebody had just told her she could open them.

“The old road goes north,” she said. “Island to island. Water at Grebe, water at the Skerries, water at Long Holm, and then the long run to the Fingers, and then round. Always round. Three hundred years. You go round, because the deep sings.” She watched the singer. “It’s going straight.”

Higu was beside her. It was Higu’s day with the rock, which on the Patience lived on top of the aft hatch, wedged in with a coil of rope so it didn’t slide. A flat grey stone from the Stair, with a chalk line down the middle. On the left side, REMEMBERED. On the right, nothing yet, for this.

“How long’s the old road?” said Higu.

“Two months,” said Marrit. “Bit more.”

Higu looked across the deck. At Ice, who was at the starboard rail with a bucket, doing something to a fish, and who had gone very still.

He didn’t turn round. He went on doing the thing to the fish. After a moment.

Higu wrote, on the left: 2 MONTHS. OLD ROAD. NORTH.

On the right, she wrote: ?

“We’ll find out,” she said, to nobody, and put the chalk in her pocket.

Corran didn’t sit up that day.

He’d sat up every day until then. On his bucket, mostly, by the mainmast, with his mug and his blanket and his opinions, telling Pell the bosun that the main brace wanted easing and telling Jago at the wheel that he was oversteering and telling Ottery, once, through the galley hatch, that her biscuit was a crime against the sea, at which Ottery had come out of the galley, the whole width of her, and stood over him, and he’d laughed until he coughed and she’d gone back in. That had been on the second day out. Cursiffe had written it down. Corran and Ottery. “A crime against the sea.” She stood over him. He laughed. (Saw.)

On the three hundred and sixth day he lay in his nest of sailcloth with his eyes shut and his breath going in and out very slowly, and Maigo sat beside him, and Rachael came twice and knelt and put her hands on him, and took them away again, and didn’t say anything, and went.

The tide-folk went quiet around him.

Cursiffe noticed that first. Not quiet the way you go quiet round someone ill, softer, kinder, coming closer. Quiet the other way. Further off. The crew worked round the mainmast as if there was something on the deck there they had to step round without looking at it. Pell, who was the size of Gabe and Azac together and had a voice like a rope dragged over a wet deck, went past the nest with his eyes on the sails. Jago at the wheel kept his face to the west. Kip, the ship’s boy, who was twelve and the fastest thing in the rigging Cursiffe had ever seen apart from Tiger, didn’t come down the mainmast all morning, and when he did, he came down the foremast instead and went the long way round.

Only Marrit went to him. And when she did, she knelt by the nest and held his hand and didn’t say anything, and her other hand, the whole time, was wound round her tide-cord at her wrist, so tight the knots went white.

Cursiffe watched the cords.

He’d been watching them since Hythe. Everybody on the island wore one. A thin plaited cord, tarred dark, round the left wrist, with knots in it. He’d thought at first they were for counting something. Tides, maybe. Days at sea. He’d written TIDE-CORDS: COUNTING? on the first evening and left it with the question mark, because nobody had told him and he hadn’t seen.

He found Kip at noon, in the bow, on his own, splicing a line with his tongue between his teeth.

Cursiffe sat down on the deck a little way off, the way you’d sit near a bird, and didn’t say anything for a while.

“You’re the one with the book,” said Kip eventually, without looking up.

“Yes.”

“Jago says you write down everything anybody says.”

“Not everything.” Cursiffe thought about it. “Only the true things.”

Kip looked at him sideways. He had a thin brown face and salt-white hair cut short and a tide-cord on his left wrist, newer than most, with two knots in it.

“How do you know which ones are true?”

“I write down what I saw,” said Cursiffe. “Or what I heard. Not what I think about it.” He paused. “That’s usually the bit that isn’t.”

Kip considered that. Then he went back to his splice.

“What are the knots?” said Cursiffe.

Kip’s hands stopped.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. The ship lifted and fell. The hum came up through the deck, low and long.

“One for every one the sea’s got,” said Kip. Very quietly. “You tie it when they go. On the night. And you don’t say them. Not over the water.”

“Why not?”

“Because the water hears.” He said it the way you’d say the sky’s up. “If you say their name over the water, the water learns it. And it calls for them.” His hand had gone to the cord. To the two knots. He held them between his finger and thumb, the way you’d hold a small stone you’d picked up on a beach and liked the shape of. “So you tie a knot instead. And you can touch it. And you don’t say it.”

Cursiffe didn’t write it down. Not then. He sat with it.

“Two,” he said.

“My dad,” said Kip. “Two winters ago. And Dannet.” He stopped. “He taught me to scull. Off the harbour wall. He was old.” He didn’t say any more. He didn’t say which knot was which. Cursiffe didn’t ask.

After a while Kip went back to his splice, and Cursiffe went back down the deck.

He looked at the cords on the way. Jago’s, at the wheel: nine knots. Pell’s, on the great thick wrist on the main brace: fourteen. Ottery’s, when she came out of the galley to throw a bucket over the side: four, very close together, and one by itself at the end.

Marrit’s: twelve.

And Corran’s.

Corran wasn’t wearing a cord. He was wearing eleven. They went round his left forearm from the wrist almost to the elbow, wound one above the other, so close together that Cursiffe had taken it on the first day for a sort of black leather bracer. The ones at the wrist were newer, brown, tarred. They got darker going up. The highest, just under the elbow, was so old it had gone black and hard and shiny, like a piece of wood that’s been handled for a hundred years.

Every one of them was knotted. All the way along. Knot after knot after knot.

Cursiffe stood by the mainmast and looked at the old man’s arm, lying on top of the blanket in the sun, and tried to count, and couldn’t.

Yan found him in the hold that evening.

It was the only place on the Patience you could be alone, more or less, if you didn’t mind the smell of salt fish and tar and the bilge. Cursiffe didn’t. He’d found a corner behind the water casks where a hatch grating let the light down in a square, and he’d been sitting there with his first book, the ink one, writing SALT AIR: INK FINE with some relief.

Yan sat down on a cask beside him. He didn’t say anything for a while. Suzu was round his neck, small and white, with her head up.

That wasn’t like Yan. Yan was the head. Yan was the one who said right at the start of a day and that’s enough at the end of it, and in between talked quite a lot, mostly to Suzu, out loud, so that everybody in the party had long ago got used to hearing half of a conversation with a snake. He wasn’t a man who sat on casks in holds and didn’t say anything.

“Cursiffe,” said Yan, eventually.

“Yes.”

“Can I tell you something? It’s a bit stupid.”

Cursiffe closed his book on his finger. “Is it the thing with people’s chests?”

Yan’s face did something very complicated.

Suzu, round his neck, made a small noise that Cursiffe couldn’t hear and Yan obviously could, because Yan said, out loud, “Don’t,” and then put his hand over his face.

“I’m sorry,” said Cursiffe. “Was that rude? I’ve got it written down. Floor 1.” He opened the ink book, which went back further than the other one, and found the page. It was near the front, in his earliest hand, which was messier, because he hadn’t learned yet how to write small on a moving surface. He held it out.

YAN: LOOKS AT PEOPLE’S CHESTS (NOT LIKE THAT). PUTS HIS HANDS UP ROUND PEOPLE WHEN THEY’RE HURT. LIKE ROUND SOMETHING SMALL. FLAME? (Saw.) Don’t ask. He’ll say.

Yan read it.

He read it twice.

Then he laughed. Properly. With his hand still half over his face. And then he stopped laughing, and took the hand away, and his pale cyan eyes were wet.

“Floor 1,” he said.

“Day 3,” said Cursiffe. “The south fields. You put your hands round Salty when the Woolback hit him and his HP stopped. I wrote it that night.” He took the book back. “I didn’t know what it was. I still don’t, really. And I thought, he’ll say. People usually do, if you don’t ask.” He shrugged. “So I didn’t.”

“For three hundred days.”

“Three hundred and three.”

“Suzu says you’re cleverer than me,” said Yan.

“That isn’t true.”

It is, said something by Yan’s ear, which Cursiffe didn’t hear, but Yan’s mouth twitched exactly as if it had.

So Yan told him.

He told it quietly, sitting on the cask with his hands between his knees, the way you’d tell somebody about a thing in your family that nobody talks about. The lights. Under everybody’s breastbone, from the first morning, in the white room. Candles, he called them in his head, because he didn’t have a better word and didn’t want one. Salty’s like a forge with the door open. Wendy’s small and steady. Tiger’s going so fast it looked like it was shaking, until Neth. Cursiffe’s.

“What’s mine like?”

“Dark,” said Yan. “Like light through a bottle of ink. Blue-black. Very still.” He looked at Cursiffe’s chest for a moment, and Cursiffe found that he didn’t mind. “It’s got more at the edges now. Since the statue.”

“Hm,” said Cursiffe. He thought about that. He decided he liked it. He didn’t write it down. Some things you didn’t need to.

And then Yan told him about floor 8.

He’d heard some of it. Everybody had. The old woman in the net, the boat, the pole, the causeway, the Edge. He’d been there, on the last jetty, at the back. He’d seen Yan walk out along the stones under the fast water with the long pole, step and set, step and set, and stand at the lip. He’d written it. Yan walked Neth to the Edge. Aud gave him the pole. (Saw.) But he hadn’t known what Yan had seen. He hadn’t known the light had stayed in her, after, like a boat with no current. That river-folk burned so low and so long that when they died there was nothing left over to carry it, and it couldn’t rise, and somebody had to walk it to where it was going.

“Corran’s is like that,” said Yan.

Cursiffe went still.

“Low,” said Yan. “Long. Like a lamp turned down for the night. It’s been like that since Hythe. I thought it was because he was old. But it’s not just him. It’s all of them. Marrit. Kip. Pell. Ottery.” He swallowed. “Tide-folk burn like river-folk. Low and long. They’ll do the same thing. When they go. I know they will. They won’t rise.”

“And at sea,” said Cursiffe slowly, “there isn’t a causeway.”

“There isn’t an Edge,” said Yan. “There isn’t anything. There’s just—“ He stopped. He looked down, at the planks under his feet, at the bilge under the planks and the sea under the bilge. “Hyvred said it was full.”

Neither of them said anything for a while.

“Show me,” said Cursiffe. “Your window. If you’ve got one.”

Yan held up his hand and opened it. Cursiffe couldn’t see it. Nobody could see anybody else’s window. But Yan read it out, the way they all did now, in the flat voice you used for windows.

“The Edge, two of three. Learn their names.”

“Whose?”

“I don’t know.” Yan closed his hand. “I’ve had it since floor 8. A hundred and forty days. I thought it was floor 10. The wall. When the Steward counted us, and everybody said their names back over the numbers. I thought, that’s it, that’s names, that’s got to be it. I stood on the wall and listened to everybody’s. I learned them. I knew them already. It didn’t move.” He looked at his hand. “I don’t know whose. I don’t know who they are.”

Cursiffe sat with his book on his knee in the square of evening light coming down through the grating.

He thought about Kip, in the bow, with two knots between his finger and thumb.

Because the water hears.

“They don’t say them,” he said.

Yan looked up.

“The tide-folk,” said Cursiffe. “Their dead. They don’t say their names. Kip told me. Today. When somebody goes to the sea, they tie a knot in the cord, and they never say the name again. Not over the water. Because the water learns it and calls for them.” He stopped. He made himself go slowly, the way he always did, so he’d still be right at the end of it. “So every one of the sea’s dead, for three hundred years. Every one of them went in the water without anybody saying who they were.”

Yan had gone white. Not the white of his hands after Shelter. All of him.

“Corran’s got eleven cords,” said Cursiffe. “Up to his elbow. I tried to count the knots and I couldn’t.”

In the morning, Corran asked for Salty.

Cursiffe saw it because he was on the aft hatch with the rock, copying Higu’s two columns into his ink book the way he did every few days, so there’d be a record of the record. The old man had woken up. He was lying in the nest with his eyes open, looking up the mainmast at the sky, and he moved one hand, and Maigo bent down, and listened, and straightened, and looked round, and called across the deck.

“Salty. He wants you.”

Salty was at the forward rail with Jared, arguing about something to do with rivets. He came over at once. He came the way people came to Corran now, the 36 at least, who hadn’t got the tide-folk’s way of stepping round: straight, and quick, and then slow at the last few steps, as if the deck there was thinner.

He knelt by the nest.

Corran said something. Cursiffe couldn’t hear it. He wasn’t trying to. He was looking at the rock.

And then he wasn’t, because Corran had pointed, with one long finger, at the lashing under the mainmast’s foot, where something had been stowed in oilcloth since Hythe, and Salty had reached under and pulled it out.

It was long. Longer than a man. Wrapped in old oilcloth and tied with marline.

Salty unwrapped one end. Cursiffe saw pale wood. Very pale, close-grained, smooth, the colour of milk with a drop of tea in it.

“Ash,” said Corran. His voice had gone thin over the last two days, but it still carried, the way old sailors’ voices do. “First piece I cut for her. Eleven years ago, near enough. Off the first tree. I kept it back. Seasoned. I was going to make her a tiller out of it, one day, when she’d earned one.” The thin cracked laugh. “She’s got a wheel. Fancy. Doesn’t need a tiller. So.” He let his hand drop. “A haft wants ash.”

Salty didn’t say anything.

Cursiffe had never seen Salty not say anything. Salty always said something. Usually several things, loudly, at least one of which was a joke and at least one of which was about himself.

“How did you—“ said Salty. His voice went. He tried again. “How did you know?”

“Lad,” said Corran. “I’ve lived in Hythe eighty-nine years. It’s got very thin walls.” He shut his eyes. “Every night. In my forge. While the big one was doing my rudder irons with his one good fist, and the little one thought nobody was listening.” His mouth went up at one corner. “Tap. Tap tap. Wait. Tap. I know what folding sounds like. I’m not deaf. I’m old.”

Salty knelt there with the length of pale ash across his knees.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Don’t thank me. Make it right.” Corran’s eyes opened again. Milky at the edges. He looked at Salty for a long moment. “And not in a hurry. You’ve got a hurry in you. I could hear that too.”

“Yeah,” said Salty. “I’ve been told.”

He wrapped the wood up again, very carefully, the oilcloth and the marline exactly the way they’d been. He took it down into the hold himself. He didn’t let anybody help. He didn’t look at Yan, who was in the bow, as he went past.

On the way back up he looked at Cursiffe instead. On the aft hatch. With the book.

“Don’t,” said Salty.

“I wasn’t going to,” said Cursiffe.

He wasn’t. He’d known for a long time what Salty was making. Since floor 8, really. A hammer that never got used, at a man’s belt, every day, and a man who went down to a river-smith’s forge every night and sat on a stool and listened. Working on it, under his breath, at the back of a crowd on a jetty, with Wendy’s elbow in his side. You didn’t need to be clever. You just needed to have been there.

But it wasn’t done. And you didn’t write things down that weren’t done. Not things like that. Not when somebody was still making them, with their whole heart, in the dark, a finger’s width at a time.

So that page stayed empty.

That night Corran asked for Yan, and for the book.

Maigo came to find them. He found Cursiffe in the galley on his bucket and Yan on the foredeck with Wendy, and brought them both back to the mainmast, and then stood a little way off with his arms folded and his face doing something that Cursiffe had never seen Maigo’s face do, which was absolutely nothing at all.

Marrit was already there. Kneeling by the nest with the old man’s hand in hers.

“Grandad,” she was saying. “No.”

“Sit down, love,” said Corran.

“Grandad—“

“Sit down.”

She sat. On the deck, with her knees up, still holding his hand. Her face was wet in the lamplight. She didn’t wipe it.

Corran looked up at Yan, and then at Cursiffe, and then at the book in Cursiffe’s hands. The ink one. He’d brought it without thinking.

“Not that one,” said Corran. “Have you got a new one? A clean one?”

Cursiffe had. He always had. He’d bought three in Hythe, off a net-maker’s wife who kept the accounts for half the harbour, because you never knew. He went and got one. He came back. He sat down on the deck, cross-legged, with the clean book open on his knee and the pot of eel-oil ink wedged in the angle of the mast so it wouldn’t slide, and the lamp on its hook over him, and waited.

“You’re the one who writes things down so they keep,” said Corran. “That’s what they say. The mapmaker girl. That your pages keep.”

“If they’re true,” said Cursiffe.

“They’re true.” Corran moved his left arm. He couldn’t lift it far. He didn’t need to. It lay on top of the blanket, in the lamplight, with the eleven cords wound round it from wrist to elbow, black at the top and brown at the bottom, knot after knot after knot. “My gran said them. Every one. Out loud. On the quay, of an evening, to anybody who was passing, like a song. Every single name on her cord. They called her touched.” The corner of his mouth went up. “She was right about the singer. I’d like to know if she was right about this.”

Marrit made a sound.

“Seventy years,” said Corran. He wasn’t looking at anybody now. He was looking up the mast, at the sky, at the stars. “I’ve tied every one of these myself. Every one the sea’s had out of Hythe, since I was a boy. Somebody has to. And I’ve never said one. Not once. Not over the water.” He breathed in. It took a long time. “I’m going to say them now. All of them. I’m too old to be frightened of the water. And I’d like them said. By me. Before I’m one of them.” He looked at Cursiffe. “And I’d like them kept. After. By somebody who’ll keep them true.”

Cursiffe dipped the pen.

“From the top,” said Corran. “The black one. That’s Gran’s. She gave it me when she went, and I wore it over mine. Hers go first. Then mine.” He shut his eyes. His fingers found the top of the oldest cord, up by his elbow, by feel. The first knot. “You’ll have to be quick, lad. I’m slow, but there’s a lot of them.”

“I’m not quick,” said Cursiffe. “But I don’t stop.”

“Good,” said Corran. “Good lad.”

And then he started.

“Treeve Fulmar,” said Corran. “Gran’s uncle. Went out after crab in a fog and nobody heard him. Before Gran was born. She had it from her mother.”

Cursiffe wrote it. Treeve Fulmar. Small, and square, the first line of the clean page. And beside it, in smaller letters still, because it was what Corran had said and therefore what was true: Gran’s uncle. Crab. Fog.

“Morwen Gull. Bryok Gull. Mother and son. The big storm before the long winter. The Gull went over off the Skerries.”

Morwen Gull. Bryok Gull.

“Old Hedra. No boat. She walked into the sea one morning at the bottom of the tide and kept walking. Gran said she was tired.”

Hedra.

The fingers moved along the cord. One knot. The next.

He was slow. He was very slow. Some knots he held a long time before he said anything, and Cursiffe waited with the pen up, and the ship lifted and fell, and the hum came up through the deck, and nobody spoke. And then he’d say it. A name. And who they were. Sometimes only a word or two. A good sculler. Couldn’t sing. Married late. Sometimes more. Once, for a girl called Elowen Petrel who’d been eleven, a whole long story about a dare and a seal and a rope that wasn’t tied, and when he got to the end of it he stopped for a long time, and then said, “Gran never got over that one,” and moved his fingers on.

By the end of the black cord there were forty-one names on the page.

Then the next cord. Gran’s still, but newer. Names Corran had been alive for. He started to know them himself, not just from her. Used to give me apples. Had a dog that went in the boat with him. Taught me my knots. The page filled. Cursiffe turned it.

At some point Yan sat down.

He’d been standing, at Cursiffe’s shoulder, the whole time, very still, with Suzu round his neck. He sat down on the deck now, slowly, as if his legs had decided it for him, with his back against the mast beside Corran’s head, and his hands in his lap. Cursiffe glanced at him once. Yan was looking at the old man’s chest. Just looking. His face, in the lamplight, was wet.

The third cord was Corran’s own.

His fingers stopped on the first knot of it for a long time.

“Kitto Sanderling,” he said.

Cursiffe wrote it.

“My brother.” The cracked voice didn’t crack. It went very level instead. “Fourteen. I was nineteen. We were on the Shoals, at the bottom of the tide, after the mussels. He was always further out than me. And a wracker came up out of the channel. The old one. The big one. The one your Ice killed, down there, on our second day.” He breathed. “She got him by the leg. And I got him by the hand. And she pulled, and I pulled, and the tide was turning.” A long pause. “I let go of his hand.”

Nobody said anything.

“Seventy years I’ve had that knot,” said Corran. “Right there. Under my thumb. Every day. I’ve never said his name.” He opened his eyes and looked up at the stars. “Kitto. There. Kitto Sanderling. My little brother. He was quick. He was quicker than me. He’d have been a better man than me, and he never got to be.” He closed his eyes again. “There. That’s one.”

Kitto Sanderling. Brother. Fourteen. Old Shears, on the Shoals. Seventy years.

Cursiffe’s pen was steady. He didn’t know how. He thought it might be because if it hadn’t been, he wouldn’t have been able to write, and he had to write. That was what he was there for.

The names went on.

Somewhere in the fourth cord, Kip came down out of the rigging.

He came down the mainmast, not the foremast, for the first time in two days. He dropped the last bit onto the deck, barefoot, and stood there by the nest with his cord in his fingers and his thin brown face working, and didn’t say anything, and Corran opened his eyes and looked at him.

“Kip,” said Corran. “Come here. Sit by me. Listen. Your dad’s coming up. Near the end. And old Dannet.”

Kip sat.

And then Pell.

The bosun came aft from the bow, very slowly, the whole vast bulk of him, and stopped at the edge of the lamplight. He had his fourteen knots in his fist. He stood there.

“Some of mine aren’t on his,” he said, to Cursiffe. A voice like a rope dragged over a wet deck, very quiet. “I’m not from Hythe. I’m off Seal Holm. Two days north. Ours go to the sea there and nobody here would know them.”

“Then say them,” said Corran, with his eyes shut. “When I’ve done. He’ll put them at the end.”

Pell sat down on the deck where he was. It took a while. The ship tilted slightly.

Jago gave the wheel to Marrit’s second, a narrow silent woman called Morva, and came down from the quarterdeck. Then two more of the crew, the Lusk brothers, who were tall and thin and looked exactly alike and never spoke to each other. Then Morva lashed the wheel, and Chris said he’d watch it, and she came down too.

And then the galley hatch opened, and Ottery came out.

She stood at the edge of the light with her four close knots and her one by itself. She didn’t come any closer. She didn’t sit. She stood there with her vast arms folded and her face like a wall, and when Corran’s fingers reached a knot near the bottom of the sixth cord and he said, “Tamsin Gannet,” Ottery’s mouth moved, and nothing came out, and then it moved again.

“Tamsin,” she said. A whisper. The first word Cursiffe had ever heard her say.

“Ottery’s girl,” said Corran, gently. “Eight. Swam like a seal. Better than her mother, and her mother could outswim the harbour bell.” He let the knot go. “There. Tamsin.”

“Tamsin,” whispered Ottery.

Cursiffe wrote it. Tamsin Gannet. And beside it, Ottery’s girl. Eight. Swam like a seal. And then, because it was true, and because Ottery had said it and he’d heard her, he made a small mark next to the name. A tick. For a second person who’d had that knot.

He’d done it before without thinking. For Kitto, when Marrit had touched one of hers, high up on her cord, at the same moment as Corran said it. For a fisherman called Bastian Fulmar, when Pell’s hand had closed. He hadn’t decided to. He’d just done it. A tick, for every cord that held that name. For everybody who’d loved them enough to tie the knot.

He kept doing it.

By the seventh cord some of the names had four ticks. By the ninth, there was one with seven. A net-mender called Ysella, Corran’s wife’s sister, who’d gone out in a squall to bring in somebody else’s nets and hadn’t come back, and every single tide-folk sitting on the deck round the mainmast had her on their cord.

“Loveday’s not on here,” said Corran, somewhere in the ninth. “My wife. She went in her bed. In Hythe. With the window open so she could hear the bell.” His mouth moved. “You don’t knot for that. That’s a good way to go. I’d like it.” He opened his eyes and looked up the mast at the sky. “This’ll do.”

Marrit put her face down on the blanket.

The moon went over. The lamp burned down and Maigo trimmed it. Cursiffe turned the page, and turned it, and turned it. His hand ached and then stopped aching and then went numb, and he kept writing. He didn’t stop. He’d said he wouldn’t.

And the 36 came.

Not all at once. Nobody called them. Cursiffe didn’t see who came first. He just looked up, somewhere in the tenth cord, to dip the pen, and saw that the circle round the mainmast was bigger than it had been. Wendy, sitting very close behind Yan, with her knees up and her arms round them. Salty behind her. Tiger crouched on the rail with his tail round his ankles. Hana and Milli. Rachael, standing, with her arms folded. Gabe. Deston, with his hand on his shirt pocket. Thea, at the back, by the hatch, with her hand in Ice’s.

Ice wasn’t looking at Corran. He was looking at the deck. At nothing. With his whole face very still. He looked, Cursiffe thought, like somebody listening to something that wasn’t the names. Or that was the names, but somebody else’s.

Cursiffe didn’t write that. He hadn’t seen it, exactly. He’d only thought it.

“Jory Kittiwake,” said Corran.

Marrit’s head came up off the blanket.

“My boy,” said Corran.

He was very tired now. His voice had gone to a thread. He’d done ten cords. This was near the wrist end of the tenth, and his fingers had found this knot, and stopped on it, and Cursiffe could see, by the lamp, that it was worn smoother than the ones round it.

“The long calm,” said Corran. “Twenty-four summers ago. On the Kittiwake. His mother’s boat. With Marrit, six years old, asleep in the bow under his coat.” He breathed. “He took his wax out. In the night. To listen. He always wanted to know things, Jory. He always asked. And something called him, and he went.” A long pause. “Folded his shirt on the rail first. He was always tidy. Tidier than me.” The corner of his mouth went up, and down. “Twenty-six.”

Marrit was looking at him.

“I never said it,” said Corran. “Not once. Not even to you, love. Not even ashore. I thought, if I say it, the sea’ll hear, and it’ll come for the rest of you.” He moved his hand in hers. “And it never stopped calling anyway. Did it. Every night. Whether we said them or not. That’s what I’ve been lying here thinking. All this long while. It never stopped calling. We just stopped answering.” He breathed out. “So.”

“Jory,” said Marrit.

Out loud. Over the water. In the middle of the deep, on the deep road, with the hum coming up through the planks and the stars going over and nothing between her and the sea but a hand’s breadth of oak.

Every tide-folk on the deck went still.

Nothing happened.

The sea went past the hull, the way it had all night. The ship lifted, and fell. The hum went on, low and long. Nothing came. Nothing called. Nobody went over the side.

“Jory,” said Marrit again. Louder. Her voice broke on it, and she said it through the break. “Jory Kittiwake. My uncle. He put his coat over me.” She was crying, now, properly, with her face up. “I remember his coat. It smelled of tar. I didn’t know he’d gone till the morning, and Grandad wouldn’t say, and nobody would say, and I thought, I thought for years—“ She stopped. “Jory.”

Cursiffe wrote it. Jory Kittiwake. Corran’s son. Marrit’s uncle. Twenty-six. The long calm. Folded his shirt. He always asked. And two ticks.

And then Kip said, “Sennen Petrel,” very fast, very high, as if it had come out of him before he could stop it. “My dad.”

Corran smiled with his eyes shut. “That’s right. He’s next. Well done, lad. You beat me to him.”

After that it was different.

Cursiffe couldn’t have said how. It just was. Corran still said them, slowly, one by one, knot by knot. But now, when he said one, somebody else on the deck said it too. Not every time. Not loud. Just a voice from the circle, a beat behind him, saying it back. Sennen Petrel. Dannet. Pell’s voice like a rope. Jago’s. Ottery’s whisper. The Lusk brothers, both at once, for a man called Hob who’d been their grandfather, and then looking at each other, startled, as if they hadn’t known the other one was going to. Marrit, for nearly every one.

And the leaf, in Cursiffe’s shirt pocket, against his chest, was hot.

Not warm. Hot. As hot as he’d ever felt it, on floor 6, at noon, on the glass. He didn’t take it out. He didn’t need to. He knew which way it was pointing. He could feel it, without turning, through his shirt. North-west. Behind them. Back across the deep road. Toward the place where the sea went round and round and down.

What remains.

He wrote.

The eleventh cord was the newest. Brown. Tarred. Short. The last few knots on it were from the last few years, and Corran said them quickly, because they were people everybody on the deck had known. A boy the wrackers took last year. A man in the spring storm. And then he got to the end of it, the last knot, right at the wrist, and held it, and said, “That’s Bel Fulmar. Last autumn. Off the Fingers. She’d have been sixty,” and let go, and there weren’t any more.

The circle was very quiet.

“Seal Holm,” said Corran. “Pell.”

Pell said his. Nineteen names nobody on Hythe had known. He said them slowly, in his rope-voice, with who they were, and when he got to the end of them he put his great hand over his face and sat like that.

Cursiffe wrote the last of them. He blew on the page.

He counted. He didn’t need to. He’d been numbering them in the margin, small, the whole night, because a record ought to be able to tell you how much of it there was.

“Three hundred and ten,” he said.

Corran nodded slightly, with his eyes shut.

“And Corran,” he said. “Of the Patience. Put me at the end, lad.” The corner of his mouth went up. “Two Rs. Everybody forgets one.”

Cursiffe looked at the page.

At the last line. Three hundred and ten. At the space below it.

He didn’t dip the pen.

“No,” he said.

Everybody looked at him.

He felt them look. He didn’t mind. He’d been looked at for being slow all his life, and he wasn’t slow, he was careful, and the difference was usually that careful people were right afterwards. He held the pen up, dry, over the empty line, and looked at the old man in the nest.

“It isn’t true yet,” said Cursiffe. “You’re still here. I only write what I saw.” He swallowed. “I’ll write it when it’s true. Not before. I’m sorry.”

There was a long silence round the mainmast.

Then Corran opened his eyes, and looked at him, and laughed.

It was very small, and very thin, and it was the best laugh Cursiffe had ever heard. It went on for a while. It turned into a cough, and Maigo held him, and he finished it. He made sure he finished.

“Fair,” said Corran. “That’s fair. You’re a careful one.” He shut his eyes again. “Gran’d have liked you. She was careful too. Nobody ever noticed. They only noticed the touched bit.”

He didn’t say anything else.

He went at dawn.

Cursiffe was still there. Most of them were. He’d fallen asleep sitting up, against the mast, with the book shut on his knee and the pen still in his fingers, and he woke because the light had changed. Grey. Then a thin line of gold along the east, behind them, over the deep road. The hum under the deck had gone very soft. As if the singer, somewhere ahead in the water, was listening too.

Marrit was kneeling where she’d knelt all night. She had both his hands now.

Maigo was watching his chest.

Cursiffe watched Maigo. That was the clearest way to see it. Maigo didn’t move. Maigo had sat up every night since the launch with his hand on the old man’s blanket, and he knew the shape of every breath by now, the long in and the long out and the long, long gap after. He was waiting for the next one. Cursiffe could see him waiting. All of him.

The gap went on.

And on.

And Maigo, very slowly, took his hand off the blanket and put it in his lap.

Marrit didn’t make a sound. She leaned forward and put her forehead down on her grandfather’s two hands, and stayed like that, and the light came up gold along the deck and over her back, and nobody moved.

Rachael was there. Standing. She’d been there since some time in the night. She knelt now, on the other side of the nest, and put her hands on him, flat, the way she did, and Cursiffe saw the faint warmth of a heal go into him, the way it went into everybody she touched, without her deciding. And then she took her hands away. And held them in front of her, palms up, and looked at them.

“He wasn’t ill,” said Rachael. Very quietly. To nobody. “He was just old.”

She put her hands down. She didn’t touch anyone. She stood up and went and stood at the rail with her back to them all and her arms folded, and Deston went and stood near her, not touching either, just near.

Cursiffe looked at Yan.

Yan was still sitting against the mast, where he’d been all night, by the old man’s head. Suzu was round his neck. He was looking at Corran’s chest. His face had gone the colour of the sky.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. Cursiffe had sat in the hold with him, two nights ago, and been told what to look for, and he knew what Yan was seeing now because he could see Yan’s face seeing it.

It hadn’t risen.

Whatever was in the old man, whatever had burned there low and long for eighty-nine years like a lamp turned down for the night, it hadn’t gone up. It hadn’t gone anywhere. It was still there. In him. Lying in the nest of sailcloth at the foot of the mainmast with nowhere to go, like a boat with no current.

Waiting.

Cursiffe opened the book.

The clean one. He found the page. The last line. Three hundred and ten. The empty space below it. He dipped the pen in the pot of eel-oil ink, which was nearly dry, and wrote, small and square, in his steadiest hand.

Corran Patience. Shipwright. Marrit’s grandad. He built her in eleven years and saw her swim. He said them all. He wasn’t frightened of the water. Eighty-nine.

Two Rs.

Three hundred and eleven.

He looked at it for a long time. He blew on it. It didn’t need it. He did it anyway.

Then he made one tick beside it. And another. And another. He went round the circle in his head, everybody who was there, everybody who’d sat up all night on the deck while the old man said the names, and he made a tick for each of them, because by now every single one of them had that name on them somewhere, whether or not they had a cord to tie it in.

When he’d finished, it went right across the page.
