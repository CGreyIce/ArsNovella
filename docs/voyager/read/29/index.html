Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 29

Measure

3,036 words · 13 min read

Kei had always believed that everything was a matter of dose, and the Spire had not yet given him a reason to change his mind.

Water would drown you. Salt would kill you. Sleep, too much of it, was a kind of death, and too little was another. Love, in the wrong amount, at the wrong time, with the wrong person, had nearly done for him once. He’d sat up until four in the morning on a call with Ice about it, years ago, and Ice had not once told him what to do. Just listened, and asked questions, and let him get to the end of it on his own. Everything was poison. Everything was medicine. The only difference was how much.

So when he woke up in the white room in the green haori from his own model, with a little green snake round his wrist, it hadn’t surprised him at all that the snake was both.

You’re doing the thinking face, said the snake.

“I’m always doing the thinking face.”

You’re doing the one where you think you’re wise.

“I am wise,” said Kei mildly.

You’re twenty-five, said the snake, and you put your boots on the wrong feet this morning.

He looked down. He had.

She’d talked to him from the first minute. Not out loud. In his head, a dry, cool, slightly bored voice, like someone who’d been reading the same book for a thousand years and found you mildly interesting as a footnote. She was very rude. She was rude about Hako’s optimism and Bee’s drawing and Hyvred’s crying at sunsets, and about everybody in Larchwick and everybody at the Copper Spoon. She’d been especially rude about Kei.

She’d never told him her name.

He’d tried. He’d offered her names the way you’d offer a cat food, one after another, waiting to see which it would eat. Jade. (No.) Verdance. (Absolutely not.) Fern. (I’m not a plant.) Hisui, which was the name he’d half-meant to give her on stream, years ago, and never had. (That’s a man’s name, she’d said, which he wasn’t sure was true, and then: That one’s for somebody else. He had no idea what she meant by that.)

You can call me something when you’ve earned it, she’d said finally, on the fourth day.

How do I earn it?

You’ll know.

He’d stopped asking after that. She was right; he’d know. That was the infuriating thing about her. She was almost always right.

The herbwife’s name was Ma Yarrow, and she lived in a crooked house at the back of Larchwick that smelled like the inside of a teapot.

Kei had found her on the second day on floor 2, the way he’d found the bonesetter on floor 1: by asking. The Copper Spoon had taught all of them that much. In every town, there was somebody who knew things. If you asked politely and listened properly, they’d usually tell you. Ma Yarrow was round and red-faced and wore three cardigans, one on top of the other, and had bunches of drying herbs hanging from every beam of her low ceiling. She’d looked at him, at the green haori with the gold serpents, at the little snake on his wrist, and said, “Well, you’re a strange one,” and let him in.

He’d been going back every evening since.

She was teaching him salves. Frostbite first, because it was floor 2, and because Larchwick lost more fingers to the cold than to the wolves. You crushed the leaves of a little grey-green plant that grew under the snow on the south slopes, and mixed them with goose fat and the ash of a particular bark, and warmed it, and it drew the cold out of the skin. Then fever. Then cough. Then something for a burn, and something for a wound that had gone bad.

“Most of it’s the same thing,” said Ma Yarrow, on the third evening, watching him grind. “That’s the secret. Nobody tells you. Half the things in this room will cure you and kill you, depending.” She tapped the side of the mortar. “Depending on how much.”

Kei stopped grinding and looked at her.

“I know,” he said.

Ma Yarrow looked at him a moment longer. Then she smiled, a slow red-faced smile, and went to get him a cup of something that tasted like a hedge.

[ Life skill learned: Apothecary ]

There was one remedy she taught him that he kept coming back to, though he didn’t know why.

It was for a winter fever. A deep one, the kind that sat in the chest and didn’t shift. Ma Yarrow said it took the old ones every year, in the second half of the winter, when they’d been cold too long and their bodies stopped fighting. Willow bark for the heat. A root that grew by the frozen river, dried and powdered, for the chest. Honey. And a little, a very little, of something from a dark blue flower that would stop your heart if you took a pinch too much, and kept it going if you took exactly enough.

“Takes three days to make,” said Ma Yarrow. “And you have to be there. You can’t leave it with somebody and go. You have to sit with them and give it them, a bit at a time. Day and night.” She sighed. “That’s why it doesn’t save many. Not because it doesn’t work. Because nobody’s got three days to sit with an old woman.”

Kei wrote it all down in the back of his book. Carefully, twice.

Why that one? said the snake.

“I don’t know,” said Kei. “It felt important.”

You don’t even know any old women here.

“I know Mother Crane.”

The snake was quiet for a moment, in his head.

Hm, she said.

Hako got bitten on the fourteenth day, on the south slopes, by something none of them had seen before.

They’d gone south for the grey-green frostbite leaf, because Ma Yarrow wanted more, and because Drew’s board said the south slopes were quiet. It was quiet. No wolves, no packs, just long white slopes going down to the frozen river, and pines, and silence. Hako was in front, as always, with his spear over his shoulder, talking about how quiet it was. And then he stepped on a patch of snow that moved.

It came up out of the snow like a whip. Pale blue-white, as long as Kei’s arm, as thick as two fingers, with a flat wedge of a head. It went into Hako’s calf just above the boot, and was gone back into the snow before anyone could move.

[ Frost Adder · Lv 9 ]

Hako looked down at his leg.

“Huh,” he said. “Ow.”

“Hako!”

“I’m fine. It’s fine.” He was still grinning. Hako was always grinning. “It’s a little snake. It’s barely—“

He sat down. Quite suddenly, in the snow, as if somebody had pulled a chair out from under him. The grin was still on his face, but it had gone a bit fixed. His face was going pale. Not white, blue. A faint blue tinge round his lips, spreading.

[ Hako · Poisoned (Frostvenom) · HP 74% ]

[ Hako · HP 61% ]

Kei was already kneeling beside him.

He put his hands on Hako’s leg, on the bite, and used Minor Heal. It went in, the way it always did: the cool green feeling going down out of his palms into the hurt place. And it did nothing. Worse than nothing. Kei felt it hit something in Hako’s blood that was cold, colder than the snow, and slide off it like water off ice.

[ Hako · HP 52% ]

“Kei,” said Bee, very calmly, which was how Bee sounded when she was frightened. “Kei.”

“I know.”

Hako was shivering now. Great shuddering shivers that went right through him. His lips were blue and his hands were going blue at the fingertips. He was still trying to grin.

“S’fine,” he said. “It’s gonna be fine. It’s always fine.”

It’s in his blood, said the snake in Kei’s head. Not bored now. Quick and precise. It’s freezing his blood from the inside. Your heal can’t touch it, because it isn’t a wound. It’s a venom. You can’t heal a venom, Kei. You have to answer it.

“With what?”

With itself.

Kei looked at her. She’d come down off his wrist onto the snow beside Hako’s leg, very small and very green, and she was looking at the bite with her tongue going in and out, fast.

I can taste it, she said. It’s simple. It’s a cold-venom. It wants to make him still. So you give him a little of the thing that wants him still, and his blood learns to fight it. A pause. A little. Not a lot. If you give him a lot, you kill him faster.

“I don’t have any—“

You have the snake that bit him. It’s under the snow. Two metres to your left. It hasn’t gone anywhere. They don’t, after they bite. They wait.

Kei turned his head and looked at the snow two metres to his left. It looked like snow.

“Hyvred,” he said. “Two metres left of me. Under the snow. Is there something there?”

Hyvred looked. He saw the little red mark in her left eye lock on.

“Yes,” she said. “Cold. Small. Coiled.”

“Can you hit it without killing it?”

Hyvred’s eyebrows went up. Then she raised her bow, and the reticle steadied. She put an arrow into the snow two metres to Kei’s left, very carefully, and pinned something to the ground through the tail. It thrashed. The snow round it went everywhere.

Kei got the vial out of his bag, the little glass one Ma Yarrow had given him for tinctures. He did the thing he’d seen people do on the kind of nature programmes he used to fall asleep to. He took the frost adder behind the head, the way you’d hold a pen, and pressed its mouth against the rim of the vial until it bit down. Its fangs went over the glass, and a few drops of something pale and blue and faintly glittering ran down the inside.

Then he let it go, and it went back into the snow.

He had a vial of venom. He had Hako, shivering, blue to the lips, at forty percent.

[ Hako · HP 40% ]

He had, in the bottom of his bag, the things Ma Yarrow had given him: willow bark, the river root, a little twist of honey in waxed paper. And he had the snake on the snow beside him, saying a little, Kei. Not a lot.

How much?

You know how much.

He did. That was the strange thing. He looked at the vial of blue venom in his hand and he knew. The way he’d always known, in the half-joking way he used to say things on stream. The difference between a poison and a cure is the dose. He’d said it a hundred times. He’d never had to mean it.

He tipped the vial. One drop, glittering, into a little of the honey on the back of a spoon. Then half of another. He stopped. He looked at it.

Yes, said the snake. That.

He put the spoon in Hako’s mouth.

For about ten seconds, nothing happened, and Kei thought he’d killed him.

Then Hako’s whole body went rigid, and he gasped, a great dragging breath like somebody coming up out of deep water. The blue round his lips went darker, darker, and Kei’s heart stopped. And then, slowly, from the edges in, it began to fade. The shivering eased. His fingertips went from blue to white to pink. His eyes, which had been going unfocused, came back.

[ Hako · Poisoned (Frostvenom) · fading ]

[ Hako · HP 43% ]

[ Hako · HP 51% ]

Kei put his hands on Hako’s leg again and used Minor Heal, and this time it went in. The cold in Hako’s blood had become something his body was fighting instead of something that was winning. The heal could help it fight.

And then a window, gold at the edges, just for him.

[ Unique skill: Measure
The difference between a poison and a cure is how much. You’ll always know how much.
]

Kei sat back on his heels in the snow and read it. He read it three times. His hands were shaking. He couldn’t remember the last time his hands had shaken.

“Huh,” said Hako, from the snow. His voice was hoarse, but it was back. He was looking up at the grey painted sky with an expression of mild surprise. “See? Told you. Fine.”

“You nearly died,” said Bee.

“Yeah, but I didn’t.”

“You nearly died, Hako.”

“But I didn’t,” said Hako, and grinned, a real one, the whole thing. “Because Kei’s a genius. And Hyvred’s a genius. And the snake’s a genius.” He turned his head and looked at the little green snake on the snow beside his leg, who looked back at him with what Kei could only describe as tolerance. “Thanks, snake.”

Tell him he’s welcome, said the snake, and that he’s an idiot.

“She says you’re welcome,” said Kei. “And that you’re an idiot.”

“Fair,” said Hako.

Bee knelt in the snow beside him and drew, with her finger, a very small sun in the air above his chest. It glowed honey-yellow and gave off a faint warmth, like a candle held close, for about thirty seconds. Then she drew another, and another, one after another, until his lips had stopped being blue at all. She didn’t say anything while she did it. Bee showed she cared with her hands.

That night, in the trapper’s hall, Kei sat by the fire with the snake on his wrist and his book in his lap, and wrote it all down. The adder. The venom. One drop and a half. The look on Hako’s face. Measure.

You earned it, said the snake.

He stopped writing.

“Earned what?”

The name. You can call me something now.

Kei looked down at her. She was lying along the back of his hand, very small and very green, her head raised, her eyes on his.

“What do you want to be called?” he said.

That’s not how it works. You give it. I take it or I don’t.

He thought about it for a long time. He thought about the frost adder’s venom, glittering in the vial. About Ma Yarrow’s crooked house and her three cardigans. About the line in the back of his book, written twice, for a winter fever, which took three days and somebody who’d sit with you. About one drop and a half.

“Dose,” he said.

The snake was silent for a moment.

That’s a terrible name, she said.

“I know.”

It’s a word, not a name.

“I know. I’m sorry. I’ll think of a better one.”

Another silence.

No, said the snake. Keep it. For now. She put her head down on his knuckle. Until you think of something better. You won’t. But you can try.

[ Companion named: Dose (for now) ]

Kei sat by the fire and laughed, very quietly, so as not to wake anybody. It was the first time he’d laughed properly since the white room.

Hyvred found him there later. She came and sat by the fire with her knees pulled up and her tail curled round her feet. She didn’t say anything for a while, and neither did he. Hyvred, who was hyper and loud and talked to everybody about everything, was sometimes very quiet late at night, and he’d learned to let her be.

“It’s fading,” she said finally.

“The trail?”

“His light. I went out to the bottom of the east slope this evening, after the adder. To look.” She was staring at the fire. “I could see it going up, all the way. Up past the hut, and up the slope, and up to the pass, to the tower. And then over it, down the other side, north. To a lake, I think, at the top of a valley. And a door in a cliff.” She swallowed. “That’s where the stair is. The stair to three. I can see where it is.”

Kei looked at her.

“That’s very useful,” he said.

“I know. I’m going to put it on the board tomorrow. So everybody knows where the stair is.” She pulled her knees tighter. “But it’s fading, Kei. Every day it’s fainter. In a few more days I won’t be able to see it at all. It’ll just be snow.”

Kei thought about that.

“Then look at it properly now,” he said. “While you still can. All of it. And remember it.”

Hyvred turned her head and looked at him. Her mismatched golden eyes, the left one with its little red reticle, were very bright in the firelight.

“I’m trying,” she said. “I keep looking at it. Every time I look, I think: he walked here. On his own. In the snow. And nobody saw him do it but me. Ten days late.” She laughed, a bit wetly. “That’s a lot of looking.”

“It’s the right amount,” said Kei.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she leaned her head on his shoulder, briefly, and he let her. And they sat by the fire in the trapper’s hall until it burned down, not saying anything.

Tell her, said Dose, very quietly, in his head, that she’s not the only one who sees him.

“What do you mean?” said Kei, under his breath.

But the snake had gone to sleep, or was pretending to, and didn’t answer.
