Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 161

Somebody on Floor 1

7,045 words · 31 min read

The boy came up the valley at a run, at first light, on the seventh morning, shouting.

Ice heard him from the bridge. He’d gone down to the river to wash, the way he did, in the white dew, with the swallows still sitting on the barn roof behind him in their long dark line. And from somewhere down the valley, south, past the river meadows and the first fields, a voice. High. Thin. A boy’s voice, cracking, going up and down with the running, coming closer.

Not words. Not at first. Just the sound of somebody running and shouting at the same time, the way you’d shout if something was on fire.

Then words.

“Reeve! REEVE! The Old Barn Piece! The Old Barn Piece!”

He came over the last stile and along the river path and onto the bridge, and nearly ran into Ice, and swerved, and went on, across the green, toward the houses. One of the two lads who carried Gaffer Tobin’s chair. The younger. His face was white under the brown and his boots were soaked to the knee from the dew and there was something on his hands, grey, like ash, that he kept wiping on his shirt as he ran, and that didn’t come off.

“Reeve!”

Doors opened round the green.

Annet Sallow came out of the third house along, with her hat in one hand and her stick in the other and her hair still down, and the boy got to her and stopped, and bent double with his hands on his knees, and said something Ice couldn’t hear. And Annet’s face changed.

He’d seen a lot of faces change. In two lives. He’d never seen one change quite like that. It didn’t go frightened, or angry. It went blank. Like a page somebody had wiped.

She put her hat on. She didn’t stop to tie her hair. She walked past the boy, fast, across the green, toward the bridge, toward him, with the stick, and her face still blank.

“Come with me,” she said, as she went past. Not to him. To anybody. To the green.

He went.

It was the field by the old barn.

The one they’d come out into. The one he’d stood at the edge of, six days ago, in the evening, with the light coming in gold behind him and the whisper going on and on. Old Barn Piece, the gleaners called it. It hadn’t been cut yet. It was the last field at the bottom of the valley, the furthest from Haverley, and it ripened last, Annet said, because it was low and the mist sat on it. It was to be cut at the end of the week.

It was grey.

All of it. Every stalk. He came through the gap in the hedge behind Annet and stopped, and the people behind him stopped, and the whole of Haverley, it felt like, came through the gap one by one and stopped behind them, and nobody said anything.

The wheat was still standing. That was the first thing. It hadn’t fallen, or rotted, or been trampled. It stood exactly as it had stood six days ago, chest-high, heavy-headed, every stalk bent the same way under its own weight. But it wasn’t gold. It was the colour of ash. Of the grey in a fire grate in the morning. Of the stone of the barn behind it. A flat, dull, even grey, the same all over, stalks and ears and leaves, with no shine on it anywhere. In the low early sun, with the white dew everywhere else, it didn’t glitter. The dew hadn’t settled on it.

And it didn’t move.

There was a breeze. He could feel it on his face, coming up the valley from the south. The hedge beside him moved in it. The grass at his feet moved. The next field up, Gorse Piece, cut and stooked, the stooks standing in their rows, the loose straw on the stubble between them lifting and settling. Everything moved.

The grey field didn’t move at all.

No whisper. No waves running away over it. It stood there in the breeze like a painting of a field, perfectly still, every grey ear in place.

Annet walked into it.

Nobody stopped her. She went in at the edge, three steps, between the grey stalks, with her stick, and stopped, and put out her hand, and took hold of an ear of wheat, the way she must have taken hold of ten thousand ears in her life, to feel if it was ready.

It came apart in her hand.

Not broke. Came apart. The ear, and the stalk under it, down to the ground, all at once, like ash when you touch it in the grate the next morning and the whole shape of the log falls in on itself. A soft grey dust that ran down through her fingers and fell on her boots. No sound. No smell. Nothing.

She stood looking at her hand.

Behind Ice, somebody in the crowd at the hedge made a sound. A woman. Old. She’d put her apron up over her face.

Annet took another one. The same. She took a third, further in. The same. She stood in the middle of the grey wheat with grey dust on her hands and her sleeves and her boots, and turned round slowly, and looked at it. At all of it. The whole field, standing grey and still in the breeze from the hedge to the old barn wall.

“It was ready,” she said. Her voice was very level. “Yesterday it was ready. I walked it. I was going to cut it on the fifth day.” She looked at her hands. “It was the best it’s been in ten years.”

Nobody said anything.

“Frost doesn’t do this,” said Annet. “Blight doesn’t do this. Rain doesn’t. Bulls don’t.” She looked up, at the crowd at the hedge, at the gleaners and the thirty-six passers. “Seventy years my mother kept the stick. Thirty, I have. And her mother before. And there’s nothing on it for this. There’s no ring for this.” Her voice didn’t change at all. “What is it?”

Nobody answered.

Then the two lads came through the gap in the hedge, both of them, the younger one still white, carrying the chair. They’d gone back for him. Nobody had told them to. They set it down on the headland, at the edge of the grey, and Gaffer Tobin sat in it very upright with his stick across his knees, and looked at the field.

He looked at it for a long time.

“Seventy-four harvests,” he said at last. Very thin. Like paper. “Never.”

That was all he said.

He didn’t lift his stick.

“It’s the tower’s,” said Hyvred.

She’d climbed onto the stone wall of the old barn, the way she always climbed onto things, and she was standing on it with her hand over her eyes, not looking at the field, looking into it. With the eyes. The way she’d looked at the dust on the Salt, and the Grow on floor 9.

“It’s the grey,” she said. “The same. The dust. The kind that came off the Grow when it was wearing a plant, and off the Wanting on six, and the stuff that came down the Salt at Thea.” Her voice had gone small, the way it did when she saw something she didn’t like. “It’s all through it. All of it. It’s not on it. It is it.”

Gigi was on her knees at the edge of the field, beside the hedge, with one hand flat on the grey earth between the stalks. She’d put it there before anybody could stop her. She didn’t look up.

“There’s no down in it,” she said.

Her voice was very quiet. Very flat. Bratty, and cracked down the middle, the way it always was when something was wrong.

“Everything’s got a down,” said Gigi. “Every bit of ground I’ve ever put my hand on. On every floor. Even the Salt. The Salt was no, for a thousand years, but it was there, it had a down, it was just saying no.” She pressed her hand flatter. “This hasn’t got one. There’s nothing for it to say no with. It’s not dead. Dead’s got a down. Dead’s just stopped.” She took her hand away and looked at it. It was grey to the wrist. “It’s like it was never there. Like somebody painted a field and then rubbed it out and left the outline.”

Maigo, beside her, didn’t put his foot in it.

He’d taken his shoe off again, on the walk down. Ice had seen him do it. Every morning now, on the Acre, Maigo walked to the fields barefoot on his root-foot, letting it go a little way into the earth with every step, because the earth here was full, he said, and it was like walking on a warm floor. He’d come through the gap barefoot. He’d stopped at the edge of the grey. And he’d stood there, with his root-foot a hand’s width from the first grey stalk, and not moved.

“The seeds,” said Maigo. Very quietly. “In the ears. In the ground under them, the ones from last year that fell. There’s always seeds. Always. Asleep, or awake, or waiting, there’s always—“ He stopped. “There aren’t any. They’re not asleep. They’re not anything. They’re gone.” He looked at Gigi. “Like on six. When the Wanting went. Like something took them back.”

Yan was looking at the field too.

He was standing a little apart from the others, by the old barn, with Working On It upright beside him and Suzu round his neck, and his head on one side. Not frightened. Ice had never once seen Yan frightened of anything he could see. Interested. The way he’d looked at the Death Rolls, and the evening telling, and the Preserved going up out of the dead heron in the rain.

“There’s nothing in it,” said Yan. “Nothing at all. Not even coals.” He tipped his head the other way. “The Third Tell had nothing in it. But it was kept. You could feel it being kept. This isn’t kept.” He sounded almost pleased, the way he did when he found a thing he hadn’t seen before. “It’s blank.”

Blank, said Alea, in Ice’s ear. Very quietly. Like a page.

He went cold.

Higu sat down on the headland with the rock.

She didn’t say anything. She took it out of her pack, set it on her knees, found the chalk, and wrote, on the right, under everything else:

THE GREY. NEW.

OLD BARN PIECE. (WHERE WE CAME IN.)

STANDING. STILL. NO WIND IN IT.

TOUCH IT: DUST. NO DOWN (GIGI). NO SEEDS (MAIGO). BLANK (YAN).

She stopped. Looked at it.

And Thea, who was standing beside Ice with the lantern in her hand, unlit, in the morning, and who’d been looking at the grey field without saying anything at all, very still, the way she stood when she was reading a window, turned her head and looked at the rock.

Not the right side. The left.

At the column of what he remembered. At the top of it, in Higu’s small neat capitals, from the first evening, from the shade of the barn wall:

WINTER.

FIELDS: BARE. ALL IN.

He watched her read it. He watched it go into her. He watched her face go white from the top down, slowly, the way his had, on the Patience, when he heard himself say four seconds.

“It’s making it match,” said Thea.

Very quietly. Higu looked up.

“What?”

“It’s making it match,” said Thea again. She didn’t look away from the rock. “It couldn’t strike either of them. In Tell. It tried. It drew its line through the first one and the line stopped. And the Magistrate said both hands stand, and it had to sit down.” She was speaking very slowly. Working it out. He could feel her do it. “So it can’t make the first record not have been. It can’t rub out the first time.” She pointed. With one finger. At the left column. “So it’s doing the other thing. It’s making the second time into the first.”

Nobody said anything.

“He said bare,” said Thea. “On the first night. Bare fields. All in. Nothing in them. Winter.” Her finger moved to the right column. To THE GREY. “And it’s making them bare. It started at the door. Where we came in. Where the first time started.” She looked up, at the grey field standing still in the breeze. “It’s making the fields what he remembers. One at a time.”

Higu looked at the rock for a long time.

Then, very carefully, with the chalk, she drew an arrow. From the left column to the right. From FIELDS: BARE to THE GREY. Across the line down the middle, which had been there, untouched, since the morning of the Teeth.

The first thing that had ever crossed it.

“Oh,” said Drew. Very quietly. “Oh, that’s horrible.”

“Where did it start?” said Chris. He was looking at the field. Measuring. “The edge. Which edge.”

“The bottom,” said Hyvred, from the wall. “The south end. By the barn. It’s thickest there. It goes up the field. The top edge is thinnest. It’s still—“ She stopped. “It’s still going. Slowly. Up. Toward the hedge.”

Everybody looked at the hedge. The top hedge of Old Barn Piece. On the other side of it, Gorse Piece, cut and stooked, the stooks standing gold in their rows in the sun, the loose straw lifting in the breeze.

“So tomorrow,” said Chris.

He didn’t finish. He didn’t need to.

It was in Gorse Piece the next morning.

Every stook. Every one, grey, standing in its rows in the grey stubble, perfectly still, with the dew settled on everything round it and nothing on them. When Calvin, who shouldn’t have, touched the nearest one, the whole stook fell in on itself like a sandcastle when the tide comes in, without a sound, and lay on the stubble in a heap of grey dust the shape of nothing.

And the top hedge of Old Barn Piece had gone grey too. The hedge between the two fields. Just that one. The leaves, the twigs, a blackbird’s nest in it from the spring with three old broken eggshells in the bottom, all grey. All perfectly still.

A field a night.

Annet stood at the gate of Gorse Piece with her stick and counted.

Not out loud. With her thumb. Down the notches. Ice watched her do it. He watched her thumb go down past the crosses, and the rings, and down, and down, to the bottom, to the rings for the frost, the low one her mother had cut and the high one she’d cut herself two days ago, under the oak. Then up again. Then she turned and looked up the valley. At all of it. Field after field, going away north between the hills in the morning sun, gold and pale gold and brown and green, to the haze at the top where the Long Barn was, three days’ walk.

“Sixty-one fields,” she said. “This side of the river. To the barn fields. Forty-three the other side.” She looked at the stick. “If it’s one a night. If it only does one a night. If it doesn’t get hungrier.” She looked at the grey stooks. “Two months. About.”

“And the frost?” said Drew.

Annet didn’t look at him.

“The frost,” she said, “is early.”

She put the stick under her arm. She looked at the grey field for one more moment, and then she turned her back on it, the way he’d turned his back on the bull, and looked at the gleaners standing in the lane behind her. All of Haverley. And the thirty-six passers among them, with their weapons and their sunburn and their straw-scratched hands.

“Then we bring it in faster,” said Annet Sallow.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Cobb, at the front, said, “Aye.” And somebody behind him said it. And somebody behind them. A low sound, going back along the lane, the way the bell went up the valley: aye, and aye, and aye.

In his chair at the edge of the lane, where the lads had set him down, Gaffer Tobin lifted his stick off his knees.

An inch.

At Annet. Not at a field.

And put it down again.

They brought it in faster.

He’d never seen anything like it. Not in either life. Not the Bastion, not Tell, not the Patience on the slip. The whole of Haverley went up the valley that morning to the fields, every person who could hold a rake or tie a band or carry a sheaf or bring water to somebody who could, and the thirty-six went with them, and nobody went back down until the bell. They cut Long Meadow, which should have taken three days, in one. The mowers went up it in a line from dawn, swish and step, swish and step, and when one of them flagged another came up out of the binders and took his blade and went on, and the one who’d flagged went back and bound. Nobody stopped for noon. People ate walking. Wren ran up and down the rows with a bucket and a ladle all day, without being asked, giving everybody water, and when Tiger offered to carry the bucket she said no, it’s my job, and he ran beside her all day instead, carrying nothing, keeping up.

The thirty-six were good at it now. Seven days. Not good like the gleaners. But good. Calvin’s bands held. Thea had stopped inventing new ways. Salty and Gabe and Azac and Hako carried, and Jared and Chris and Drew carried, and the stooks went up behind the binders in their rows faster than the old men could point. Maigo walked the field barefoot ahead of the mowers, his root-foot just in the earth, and told them where the ground was soft and where there were stones, and the mowers listened to him the way they’d started listening to Higu, without asking why.

Yan asked the lean mower for a go.

Ice saw it happen, at mid-morning, at the end of a row. Yan walked up to the end of the line with Working On It and said something to the lean man, very short. And the lean man looked at him, and at the scythe, for a long time. And then he took his own blade off his shoulder, the short one, the one for corn, and held it out.

Yan put Working On It down on the stubble very carefully, the way you’d put down something asleep, and took the field scythe, and stepped into the line.

He was bad at it. Of course he was. The first stroke went too high and left a row of stubble a hand taller than everyone else’s. The second went into the ground. But the third. Ice watched the third. Yan swung from the hips, not the arms, the way he’d been watching the lean man do it for a week, and the blade went through the base of the wheat an inch off the earth in one long flat silver stroke, and the wheat leaned, and fell, in a row, behind it.

The lean man nodded. Once.

Yan stepped, and swung again.

He mowed with them for an hour. Wendy stood at the end of the row the whole time, with her arms folded, watching, not binding, with an expression Ice couldn’t read from where he was. When he came back to her at the end of it, sunburnt and sweating and with his hair stuck to his face, he said something to her that made her hit him on the arm, hard, and then laugh.

He said it wants to go lower, said Alea, who’d been listening. The scythe. His. He said this one’s for corn and his wants to go lower.

“Lower than corn?”

He didn’t say.

He didn’t hunt that day. Nobody hunted. Higu took the barrows off the rock in the morning and wrote HARVEST across the whole of the rota, and nobody argued. At dusk the bulls came down off the ridge, after the bell, and went through the east side, and nobody went up to stop them.

But he thought about it. All day. Binding, carrying, walking up and down the rows with sheaves in his arms and straw down the back of his shirt. He thought about the grey.

He thought about it the way you’d think about a stone in your boot. Every step.

It’s making it match.

Thea had been right. He’d known she was right the moment she said it, the way you know a thing is true when somebody says it out loud that you’ve been not saying to yourself. He’d seen her face go white. He’d felt his own do it, inside, where nobody could see.

It wasn’t the fields. Not really. Not only.

It was the bell. The empty chair. The swallows, eight days early. The second ring on Annet’s stick, a long way higher than the first. The tower couldn’t make the first record not have been. So it was pulling the second one toward it, a bit at a time, a field at a time. Toward the winter. Toward bare fields and shut doors and an empty chair by the barn, with snow on the seat.

And somewhere a long way down, on floor 1, in a kitchen behind a long table, an old woman with a stained apron was making porridge for nobody. And in the first record, at midwinter, in her bed above that kitchen, she’d caught a fever and died. On her own. With nobody to sit with her.

It doesn’t need to make anything, he thought. Not there. That’s the worst of it. It doesn’t need to paint a fever. It just needs to let it come.

Or make sure, said Alea.

He stopped walking.

He was in the middle of a row with a sheaf under each arm, in the afternoon sun, on Long Meadow. Thea was ten metres ahead of him, binding. Gabe was behind him, carrying. Wren went past with her bucket and Tiger went past with nothing, keeping up.

You were thinking it, said Alea. I’m just saying it. Out loud. Like Higu. A pause. It’s made things on time before. The Squall. The Wanting. The Quiet. It took what you’d told it and made it and put it where you’d walk into it. It can make a fever, Ice. It can make a fever on time.

“I know.”

I know you know. She was very quiet. I’m saying it because you’ve got to tell them. Tonight. Not because of the grey. Because of the grey, you’ve got two months on this floor before it gets to the barn. And then thirteen floors. And then her. A pause. And you were going to wait. You were going to wait till the harvest was in and see what was true. I know you. You were going to wait till it was safe to say.

He didn’t say anything.

It’s not going to be safe to say, said Alea. It’s going to be less safe every day. Like the fields. She was quiet for a moment. Tell them where. Thea said. Tell them where, and let them decide how. That’s all it is.

He started walking again.

He put the two sheaves where the old man was pointing. He went back down the row for two more.

They ate on the green, the way they always did, under the oak, with the lamps lit in its branches.

It was the Thorne family’s turn. A big family from the other end of the village, red-haired every one of them, the grandmother included, who cooked mutton in a pit with herbs and brought it out in great wooden trenchers, and bread, and a sharp white cheese with blue in it that Bee wouldn’t eat and Hako ate for her, and plums. There were early plums now. Purple ones, with a bloom on them like breath on a window, from the orchards on the western slope. Everybody was too tired to talk much. Wren fell asleep on the bench with her head on Tiger’s arm and her bucket still in her lap, and Tiger sat very still for an hour so as not to wake her and didn’t eat his plum.

It was Calvin who asked. Of course it was. Because nobody else would, and somebody had to.

“What happens,” said Calvin, “when it’s all in?”

He said it to Annet, down the trestle. Loud. Across everybody. With a plum in his hand.

Annet looked at him.

“When it’s in,” she said, “it’s harvest-home.”

“What’s that?”

Annet looked at him the way Cobb had looked at Drew, the way the gleaners looked at any question that was too big to need asking. But she answered. She put her bread down.

“When the last sheaf’s cut,” she said, “in the last field, at the top of the valley, by the Long Barn, the whole Acre goes up. Every village. Everybody. We bring the last sheaf in on a cart, with the horses dressed, and the girls riding on top, and we sing it in. All the way up the valley.” Her voice had changed a little. It had gone lower. “And then we eat. In the Long Barn. All of us at once. It’s the only night of the year the whole Acre’s in one place. There’s long tables the length of the barn and we eat till we can’t and then we dance till we can’t.” She picked up her bread again. “And then it’s winter.”

“And the straw man?” said Ice. Quietly.

Annet looked at him. Sharply. From under the hat.

“We make him,” she said, after a moment. “From the last sheaf. That night. And put him up at the end of the barn, to keep the winter.” She went on looking at him. “Who told you about him?”

“Nobody,” said Ice.

“Hm,” said Annet. “Nobody tells you a lot.” She looked down the trestle again, at Calvin, at all of them. “And anybody who means to wed, weds then.”

There was a small silence.

“Weds?” said Newish.

“At harvest-home,” said Annet. “In the Acre, that’s when you do it. Under the last sheaf, in the Long Barn, in front of everybody. So the whole valley’s there to see it, and there’s bread for the wedding, and nobody’s working the next day.” She shrugged. “That’s how it’s always been. If you mean to, you wait for harvest-home. My mother did. I did. Cobb did, though nobody knows why she had him.”

“Oi,” said Cobb.

Ice wasn’t looking at Annet.

He was looking down the trestle, past Calvin, past Newish with her mouth open, at Hana. Who had gone very still. Who was sitting beside Milli with her crown in her lap and her ears gone straight up, looking at nothing, at the lamps in the oak, at the dark. And Milli, beside her, who’d gone red. Slowly. From the neck up. All the way to the ears. Not looking at Hana. Looking at his plum, with enormous concentration, as if it were a problem he’d been asked to solve with steps.

Jared, on Hana’s other side, put his cup down.

“Alice,” said Jared.

“Don’t,” said Hana. Very quietly. Not looking at him either.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re about to do something.”

“I’m not,” said Jared. “I’m just sitting here. Eating my plum.” He took a bite of his plum. He chewed it. He looked at the oak. “With my eyes,” he added.

Newish had both hands over her mouth.

On the other side of the trestle, Aleaka had put her face down on her arms on the table. Her shoulders were going. Sleet, under the bench, sat up and looked at her, and then at Hana, and then put her head on one side.

Milli was still looking at his plum.

“We were going to,” he said. To the plum. Very quietly. In the rational voice, the one he used when he was trying to make something into steps. “At home. We had a date. It was in the spring.” He turned the plum over. “And then a white room.”

Nobody said anything.

Annet looked at him for a long time. Then at Hana. Then she picked up the jug in front of her and filled Milli’s cup, without asking, to the brim, and put the jug down.

“Well,” said Annet Sallow. “It’s not spring. It’s harvest.” And went back to her bread.

He told them that night.

Not on the green. In the barn. After the lamps in the oak were out and the Thorne family had gone home with their trenchers and the gleaners had gone to bed, and the thirty-six had come in through the great doors into the dark and the smell of straw and lit the two lanterns they always lit, one at each end, and sat down. The way they did every night now, before sleep, in the straw, in a rough ring, with Higu and the rock in the middle, for the evening. What was true. What was next. Who had the watch.

He waited until Higu had done the watch.

Then he stood up.

He hadn’t stood up at an evening before. Not on the Acre. Not in Tell. Not since the long table at the Bastion, when he’d told them about the Gate. Usually he sat, between Gabe and Thea, and said where, when Higu asked, and let them do the rest.

Everybody stopped.

He felt them stop. Thirty-five threads, going still. Not afraid. He wasn’t sure what. Waiting.

He looked at them. At thirty-five faces in the lamplight in a barn on floor 14. Sunburnt. Straw in their hair. Tired. Calvin with his mouth still full of the last plum. Rachael with her knees up. Kei, at the edge of the ring, with Dose round his neck and his book in his lap, the way he always had it in the evenings, writing down what he’d learned that day from the gleaners’ grandmothers about whatever they’d taught him. Hana with her crown still in her lap and her ears still up. Gabe on his right, sitting, looking up at him. Thea on his left. Not looking up. Looking at the lantern.

Where, said Alea. Just where.

“There’s somebody on floor 1,” said Ice. “I want to see her. Before the winter.”

He stood there.

He didn’t say anything else. He’d thought he would. He’d thought, all afternoon, walking up and down Long Meadow with the sheaves, that when he stood up he’d have to say more. Why. Who. How long. What happened at midwinter on floor 1 in the first record. He’d thought they’d ask, and he’d have to find something true to say that wasn’t the thing.

He found that that was all of it. There’s somebody on floor 1 I want to see before the winter. That was true. That was where.

He sat down.

Nobody said anything for a while.

It was a strange silence. Not the silence of people who didn’t know what to say. He knew that silence. He’d had it for ten years, in the first life, every time he came back into a camp and sat down at a fire without a word. This was the other kind. The kind he’d learned on floor 8, at the long table in the house for lights, when Higu first said you tell us where, we decide how. The silence of thirty-five people taking a thing he’d said and putting it somewhere, carefully, where it would keep.

Nobody asked who.

Nobody asked why.

He watched them not ask. He watched it go round the ring like the light going round the Last Light, slow, steady, one face at a time. Deston, at the far side, nodding very slightly, to himself, as if it was a thing he’d been expecting and was glad had come. Rachael looking at him with her chin on her knees, and not saying is it safe. Xola, beside Thea, with her hand on Thea’s arm, very still. Drew, with his arms folded, already thinking: Ice could see him thinking, could see the numbers going behind his eyes. Higu with the rock.

Kei had stopped writing.

He’d stopped with his pen on the page. He was looking down at his book. Not at the page he’d been writing on. He’d turned it, without seeming to know he was doing it, to the back. To the inside of the back cover, where there was something written, small, a long time ago. Twice. And round his neck Dose had lifted her head, slowly, and was looking at the page too.

Kei didn’t say anything. He sat there, looking at it.

“Okay,” said Calvin.

That was all. With his mouth full. Okay.

“Okay, Dec,” said Gabe. Beside him. Quietly. And put his hand on Ice’s knee, once, and took it away.

Higu wrote it on the rock. He watched her do it. Not in either column. Under them both, across the bottom, where she put the things that weren’t remembered or true but just next. In her small neat capitals:

ICE: FLOOR 1. BEFORE THE WINTER. (SOMEBODY.)

And then she drew a box round it. A neat one. All four sides.

“That’s yours,” said Higu. “That doesn’t come off.” She looked up at him. “Anything that’s yours stays yours.”

“When’s winter?” said Drew. “On floor 1.”

“Midwinter,” said Ice. “Floor 1’s second one. About the six hundred and tenth day.”

Drew didn’t need to work it out. Ice watched him not need to. “That’s a hundred and ninety-two days.”

“About.”

“And the grey’s got two months of fields,” said Rachael. From behind her knees. “Before it gets to the stair.”

“About.”

“And then thirteen floors,” said Chris.

“Going down’s quicker,” said Thea. Very quietly. To the lantern. “Nothing’s in the way. We know where every stair is.”

Nobody said anything to that.

Drew opened his mouth.

“Not tonight,” said Higu. Without looking up from the rock. “Tonight it’s on the rock. That’s all. Tomorrow we bring the wheat in.” She put the chalk away. “Tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. Till it’s in. We can do the sums when we’ve got the wheat in.” She looked round the ring, steady, half-lidded. “Everybody’s got the watch they’ve got. Go to sleep.”

“Good,” said Deston.

And that was all anybody said about it.

They went to sleep. All of them. One at a time, in the straw, in the dark, the way they did, lying down where they were. The lanterns turned down. The great dark roof going up over them into nothing, with the beams in it he couldn’t see.

Kei was the last. He sat at the edge of the ring for a long time after everybody else had lain down, in the light of the far lantern, with his book open on his knees at the back page, and Dose round his neck, looking at it.

Then he closed the book. Very carefully. And lay down. And didn’t sleep, either; Ice could see his eyes, open, in the dark.

He had the late watch with Gabe.

Higu had done it on purpose. He knew she had. They sat on the step of the tithe barn in the warm dark, outside, with the doors open behind them and the green empty and the moon, full now, enormous and yellow over the eastern hills, laying everything out in silver. The valley. The fields. The stooks in their rows. The barrows along the ridge, black against the sky, with nothing moving on them now; the bulls had been and gone.

And a long way down the valley, south, at the very bottom, past the river meadows, two fields that didn’t shine in the moonlight at all.

Gabe sat on his right with Hearthwall propped against the doorpost beside him and didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then he said, “The swallows went.”

“I saw.”

“This morning. All of them. I came out to the river and the roof was empty.” Gabe looked up at it. At the long ridge of the barn roof against the moon. “Gaffer said they’re early.”

“Eight days. Maybe ten.”

“Yeah.” Gabe was quiet. “He lifted his stick at her. Annet. At the gate. Not at a field.” He turned the shield a little against the post. “I’ve been watching him all week. He only ever does it at fields.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

They sat there. The moon went up. An owl called, and another answered it, down by the river.

“Floor 1,” said Gabe.

“Yeah.”

“You don’t have to tell me who.”

“I know.”

“I’m not asking.” Gabe looked at his hands. “I’m just saying. In case you were sitting there thinking I was going to. In case you were sitting there working out what you’d say if I did.” He almost smiled. “You’ve got the face.”

“What face?”

“The working-out-what-you’d-say face. You’ve had it since you were thirteen.” Gabe leaned back against the doorpost. “I’m not going to ask. Not who. Not why. I’ve never asked you why about anything. Not on Earth. Not up here. Not once.”

“I know,” said Ice. “It’s the—“

He stopped.

He’d been going to say it’s the best thing about you. And it was. But that wasn’t the thing. The thing was that he’d gone down into the Down on floor 9, in the first life, alone, and come back three days later half dead, and Gabe had looked up from the fire, and never asked. And on floor 1 in this life he’d walked out of the white room again, without a word, and Gabe had stood up and started to say Dec, and the lights had come up between them, and for a hundred and fifty days Gabe had climbed after him and never once, when he caught up, asked him why.

“I know,” he said again.

Gabe nodded.

“So I’m not asking,” he said. “I’m telling.” He turned his head and looked at Ice. In the moonlight his face was the face from the forge on floor 10, the face from the stair on floor 9 with the fire behind it, the face from the white room in both lives. “Whoever it is. Whenever it is. However many floors. I’m coming.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“I know you didn’t.” Gabe looked back at the moon. “That’s why I’m telling you. So you don’t have to.” He was quiet. “You’ve been on your own on a lot of stairs, Dec. Going up. You’re not going down one on your own. Not while I’ve got arms.”

Ice looked at the green. At the moon on it. At the oak, enormous and black, with the lamps in its branches dark.

He’s telling you where, said Alea. Very softly. He learned it from you.

“Okay,” said Ice.

“Okay,” said Gabe.

They didn’t say anything else for the rest of the watch. They didn’t need to. They sat on the step of the tithe barn in Haverley with the doors open behind them on thirty-four people asleep in the straw, and the moon over the barrows, and the swallows gone, and, at the bottom of the valley, two fields that didn’t shine.

At midnight Deston and Thea came out to take the next watch. Deston looked at the two of them on the step for a moment, the way he looked at things through Link, feeling where everybody was. Then he nodded, once, the way he’d nodded in the ring.

“Go to bed, Dec,” he said.

He went.

He went in and along the east wall in the dark to the third beam, and lay down in the straw under it, where he’d sat with Thea on the first night. The wall was cooler than it had been. Not cold. Just cooler. The year was turning, Gaffer Tobin had said. You could feel it in the stone.

He lay there and listened to thirty-four people breathe.

And at the bottom of the valley, under the moon, the grey went quietly up the next hedge.
