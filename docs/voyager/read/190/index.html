Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 190

Nobody Runs

5,847 words · 25 min read

Drew counted them asleep.

He didn’t mean to. It was just what his eyes did now, at the end of a day, the way some people check the door’s locked before bed. He’d started on floor 1 without noticing and he’d never stopped. In doorways. On stairs. In the Long Barn with seven hundred other people asleep round them, where it had taken him the best part of an hour. In the hall in Tell. He counted them in and he counted them out and he counted them asleep, and when the number came out right he could sleep himself, and when it didn’t he got up and went and found out why.

Thirty-six, in the first square of the Red City, round a dry fountain, in the red dusk that never got any darker.

It took a while, because they’d gone to sleep the way they’d been talking: everywhere, all at once, mid-sentence. Calvin had kept his promise. He’d talked until his voice went, and then talked in a hoarse whisper that he insisted wasn’t a whisper because it wasn’t on purpose, and then fallen asleep with his head on Rachael’s knee at the east wall in the middle of a story about a goat. Newish had sung. Not well. On purpose. Hako had told Hyvred something that made her laugh so loud the carrion birds on the north roof all lifted at once and came down again in a different order. Salty and Gabe had argued about hinges for an hour. Somebody had started the hold-folk song from the Bastion, and everybody had joined in, loudly and badly, the way they had the night of the aurora, and it had gone round the square and bounced off four storeys of empty windows and come back to them, and for a while the whole first square of the Red City had been nothing but noise.

Now it was quiet. The ordinary kind. Thirty-six people breathing.

Drew counted them. He got thirty-five, and frowned, and counted again, and found the thirty-sixth sitting on the south edge of the fountain with his back to the rest of them and his hands on his knees, awake, looking at nothing.

Of course. He never counted Ice asleep. Ice was never asleep when Drew got to him.

He left him. Thea was asleep against the fountain basin beside Ice with the lantern in her lap, and that meant Ice wasn’t on his own, whatever it looked like. Drew got up and went the other way instead, across the square, to the east wall, where somebody else was awake too.

Rachael didn’t look up when he sat down. She was sitting with her back against the red stone and her legs out in front of her, with Calvin’s head on her left knee and Dennis asleep against her right shoulder with his mouth open. She had one gloved hand in Calvin’s hair. She wasn’t stroking it. Just resting there, the way you’d rest your hand on something to keep it from blowing away.

Drew sat down against the wall a little way from Dennis, careful not to wake him. He put his bare hands between his knees.

For a while neither of them said anything. The carrion birds shuffled on the roof. Somewhere on the far side of the fountain, Hako was snoring.

“My feet did it,” said Rachael eventually.

“I saw.”

“Twice now.” She was frowning at the fountain. “Here. In this square. The first time, I took his hand.” She didn’t look down at Calvin. She didn’t need to. “Today I came over here. To this wall. I didn’t decide to. My feet just went.” She was quiet. “I don’t know why.”

“No,” said Drew.

“I don’t like not knowing why.”

“I know you don’t.”

Rachael was the most practical person Drew had ever met. She healed the way other people breathed. She’d been a terrible teacher, on four, the first time, because she couldn’t explain what she did, she just did it; she’d frowned at Malcolm and cut her own arm and said agree with it until he did. She didn’t believe in feelings she couldn’t put a bandage on. And she’d walked across a square to a blank wall because her feet told her to, and stood there, and taken Calvin’s hand.

“Is it bad?” said Drew.

He hadn’t meant to ask. It was his question. He’d been asking it for most of a year, about everything, of everybody, and nobody had ever answered it except Thea, once, at the foot of a lectern, and that answer had been yes.

Rachael thought about it. She took her time. She always did.

“In Tell,” she said. “After the Reckoning. When they’d got him off the shield. I asked him if it was safe yet. To ask.” She was looking at the fountain. “He said no. Nearly.”

“I heard.”

“So I said I’d ask when it wasn’t nearly.” She moved her hand a little in Calvin’s hair. “I’m still not asking. I want you to know that. I’m not.”

“I know.”

“He told you something. On thirteen.” She looked at him, for the first time. “Thea said. Something with always in it.”

Drew looked at his hands.

“That I was good at it,” he said. “Making people stand where they needed to stand.” He’d turned it over every night since, the way you turn a stone in your pocket. “You always were.”

“Always,” said Rachael.

“Always.”

They sat with that. Neither of them picked it up. Drew had found, since Tell, that there were a lot of people in the thirty-six who had a word like that in their pocket, one each, from Ice, given by accident, and that none of them ever took it out and showed anybody, and all of them knew the others had one. Always. First time. Once. You always try it the first night. It was like a club nobody had joined.

“Higu walked it,” said Drew. “After. When we were making camp. From the bottom of the high stair to here.” He nodded at the wall behind them. “Twenty paces.”

Rachael didn’t say anything for a long moment.

“Twenty paces,” she said slowly.

She was looking at the foot of the high stair, across the square, on the west side. Where it came down out of the dark from the terrace and the door. She was looking at it the way she looked at a wound, measuring.

“That’s a long way,” said Rachael. “Twenty paces. If somebody’s bleeding.” She frowned. “You can’t get to somebody in twenty paces. Not if it’s bad. Not in time.” And then she stopped, and her hand went still in Calvin’s hair, and she looked at Drew with something in her face he’d never seen there. As if she’d heard herself say something in a language she didn’t speak. “Why did I say that?”

“I don’t know,” said Drew.

He didn’t. He really didn’t. But he sat there against the east wall of the first square in the red half-dark, beside Rachael and Dennis and Calvin, alive, asleep, with their heads on her shoulder and her knee, and he thought about a man standing at the foot of a stair, twenty paces away, and he felt the back of his neck go cold.

He didn’t ask. He put it away. In the pocket, with always.

“Go to sleep, Rach,” he said.

“In a minute.” She settled her back against the wall. She didn’t take her hand off Calvin’s hair. “I’m going to ask him one day,” she said. “When it’s not nearly.”

“I know you are.”

“Not here, though.”

“No,” said Drew. “Not here.”

He went back across the square to the fountain afterwards. Ice was still there. Thea was still asleep against the basin beside him with the lantern in her lap, and Ice had his hand on top of hers on the lantern, and was looking at the east wall, where Rachael was.

Drew sat down on the fountain’s edge on Ice’s other side. Not close. He’d learned that too. You didn’t sit close to Ice when he was like this. You sat where he could see you and didn’t have to look.

“Couldn’t sleep?” said Drew.

“I don’t, much. On four.”

“No.”

They sat. Drew looked at the square. It was strange from here, with everybody in it. He’d only ever seen it full of standing people, silent, in a ring round a dead thing. Now it was full of sleeping ones, lying any old how on the red stones, with their packs under their heads, snoring. Somebody had left a boot in the dry basin. Waddles was asleep on Chi’s stomach. The Quiet lay at the foot of the fountain on the east side, long and grey, with its faceless head turned a little, the way it had lain for a year and a half, and for the first time since it fell there were people asleep within three metres of it and nobody minded.

“I read it,” said Ice.

Drew looked at him.

“Your post. On World.” Ice was still looking at the wall. “The night before you came up. Day sixty-five. I was on the roof across from the door. The tall narrow house.” He was quiet. “Everyone’s twenty. We go up tomorrow. All thirty-five. Together. Calvin first. Rachael on his collar. Me last. Nobody runs.”

Drew didn’t say anything. He remembered writing it. He’d written it sitting on the bottom step of the stair on floor 3, the night before, with Calvin asleep beside him and Rachael doing something to Calvin’s ear with her thumb that made him stop twitching. It had taken him a long time. He’d wanted to get it right. He hadn’t known anybody but the thirty-five would read it.

“You put her on his collar,” said Ice.

“Somebody had to hold on to him.” Drew shrugged. “He runs ahead. When he’s frightened. He runs ahead and he talks, so nobody’ll know he’s frightened. He’d been doing it since floor 1.” He looked across at Calvin’s head on Rachael’s knee. “So I put her fist in his collar. She was the only one he’d stop for.”

“Yes,” said Ice.

He didn’t say anything else for a long time. Drew waited. He was good at waiting now. He’d learned it from Ice, in a way, watching him learn it, on the steps in Tell and the stair in the Countinghouse, getting good at steps.

“It was the right order,” said Ice.

He said it very quietly. To the wall. Not to Drew.

Drew looked at the side of his face in the red light. He thought about a square, and a fountain, and a boy who ran ahead talking when he was frightened. He thought about twenty paces. He thought about the first time, which none of them talked about and all of them had a word from, one each, in their pockets.

So, the first time, thought Drew, very carefully, the way you’d step on a stair you weren’t sure of in the dark, nobody had a fist in his collar.

He didn’t say it. He’d never say it. He put it in the pocket with the rest.

“Okay,” said Drew.

“Thank you,” said Ice.

“Thank Rachael. It’s her fist.”

“I know whose fist it is,” said Ice. And something happened at the corner of his mouth that wasn’t a smile, and wasn’t not one. “I’ve always known whose fist it is.”

Always. Drew put that one away too. His pocket was getting full.

They sat on the edge of the dry fountain together until the rankings said it was morning, which on four was the only way you knew. Ice didn’t sleep. Drew didn’t either. At some point Thea woke up against the basin and looked at the two of them, and at Ice’s hand on hers on the lantern, and didn’t say anything, and didn’t take her hand away, and went back to sleep.

In the morning, they buried the Quiet.

Ice carried it.

Nobody asked him to. Gabe had gone over to it first, with Salty, the way they’d gone to Gaffer Tobin in the Long Barn, two big men who were good at lifting things that needed lifting. They’d stood over it, looking down, working out where to take hold. And Ice had come across the square from the fountain and said, “I’ll carry it.”

Gabe looked at him.

“I killed it,” said Ice. “I’ll carry it.”

Gabe looked at him a moment longer. Then he stepped back.

It was light. That was the first thing Drew saw, watching from the fountain. Ice knelt down on the red stones beside the long grey shape and slid his arms under it, carefully, the way you’d lift somebody asleep, and stood up with it, and it came up off the ground as if it weighed nothing at all. Eighteen months of dry wind had taken everything out of it that could be taken. It was just a shape now. Long folded limbs. A smooth grey head with no face. A hole through the chest. It lay across his arms like a heap of dry sticks wrapped in grey cloth, with its long legs hanging, and its head fell back over the crook of his elbow, the way a crane’s does when you pick it up off a riverbank.

He carried it across the first square. Past the fountain. Past the bell on its post, that had rung once. Past Calvin, standing by the east wall with his hands in his pockets and Rachael beside him, who watched it go past and didn’t stop talking, quite, but went quieter. Down the main stair through the cut in the rock, step by step, careful, with the long grey legs swinging. All thirty-five of them following.

The ledge was where Higu had said. Under the main stair, on the south side, below the square: a shelf of rock running along the face of the terrace, maybe three metres deep, with a low parapet and the whole of the Red City falling away below it, terrace on terrace, to the dry river. The rock of the shelf was redder than the rest. Softer. It crumbled at the edges like stale cake. You could see where somebody, sometime, had dug in it; there were old scrapes in the face.

Ice laid the Quiet down on the ledge. Gently.

Then he stood up, and held out his hand, without looking, and Gabe put a spade in it.

Drew had seen Ice do a lot of things. Fight, and cook, and climb, and stand in fire, and kneel. He’d never seen him dig. He dug the way he did everything when it mattered: without hurrying and without stopping. He didn’t look for the place. Drew noticed that. Salty walked up and down the ledge for a minute, tapping with his boot, testing the rock, the way you would. Ice didn’t. He walked to a place about two-thirds of the way along, near the parapet, and put the spade in, and the rock gave under it like earth.

As if he’d known exactly where it was soft.

Drew looked at Higu. Higu was looking at the spade. Her face was perfectly still. She had the rock under her arm.

And Drew looked at Thea.

Thea was standing at the end of the ledge with the lantern, a little apart from everybody, the way she’d stood behind Ice’s shoulder at the wall. She was watching him dig. And her face—

Drew had known Thea since floor 1. He’d seen her laugh and seen her furious and seen her cry, once, on the step of the Countinghouse. He’d never seen her look like this. She was looking at the place where Ice was digging. And then she looked at the rock beside it. Just beside it. A metre to the left, near the parapet. Where there was nothing. Just the red rock of the shelf, soft and crumbling, with nothing in it.

She looked at that place for a long moment. Then she looked away. Out over the parapet, at the city falling away below in the red dusk. And her free hand came up and pressed flat against her own collarbone and stayed there.

Drew didn’t ask. He didn’t even think it, properly. He just saw it, and knew he’d seen something, and put it in the pocket.

Gabe took the second spade. Then Azac. Then Salty. The four of them dug in turns, the way the three of them had dug for Gaffer, without talking. It didn’t take long. The red rock came up in soft clods and piled on the ledge beside them. When it was deep enough, Ice put his spade down and knelt and lifted the Quiet again, and laid it in.

It fitted. Folded. Long legs drawn up, the way it had lain in the square. Its faceless head turned a little to one side. In the red dusk at the bottom of the hole it looked almost peaceful, which Drew wouldn’t have believed of it a day ago.

Ice stayed kneeling at the edge, looking down at it.

Nobody said anything. Nobody knew what you said. You didn’t say words over the thing that would have killed you. There weren’t words for that. Gaffer had had words, and they’d been his own, and Ina had said them for him. The Quiet hadn’t had any. It had never made a sound in its life but one.

And then Ina stepped forward.

He’d pushed his mask up onto the top of his head. He stood at the head of the grave in the red dusk with his hands at his sides, palms out a little, the way he stood when he was going to warm something. He looked down at the Quiet for a long time.

Drew saw Ice’s head come up.

He saw Ice look at Ina. Not the way you look at somebody who’s about to speak. The way you look at somebody who’s about to do a thing you’ve seen them do before, a long time ago, in a dream you can’t get back to. Ice got up off his knees. He moved. Not far. Just round the end of the grave, two steps, to a place on the other side of it where he could see Ina’s face. Where he could see his mouth.

Ina didn’t notice. He was looking at the grave.

“I don’t know what it was,” said Ina.

His voice was quiet. Not a whisper; Calvin had made a rule. Just quiet, the way Ina was quiet, which was the way a fire is quiet when it’s burning properly.

“I don’t know what it was,” he said again. “I don’t think anybody does. It lived up on the roofs. In the dusk. On its own.” He was quiet a moment. “And it listened. That’s all it did. It sat up there in the dark and listened to everything. Every footstep. Every word. Everything anybody ever said in this square, it heard. And it never once made a sound of its own. Not one. Only its heart.” He looked at the hole in its chest. “That’s a long time to listen. That’s a long time to only ever hear other people.”

The ledge was very still.

“We’re not going to be quiet for you,” said Ina. “We can’t. There’s too many of us. We’re too loud. And Calvin’s made a rule.” Somebody, Drew thought it was Hyvred, made a sound that was nearly a laugh and wasn’t. “So you’re going to have to put it down. The listening. You can stop now. You don’t have to hear anything any more. Not us. Not anybody. It’s done.” He paused. “Somebody else’ll have the roofs. The birds. They don’t say much either. You’d have liked them, maybe.”

He looked down at it a little longer.

“Go on, then,” said Ina. “It’s quiet now. Properly. The good kind. We’ll be loud somewhere else.”

He stepped back.

Drew wasn’t watching Ina any more. He was watching Ice.

Ice was standing on the other side of the grave, where he’d moved to. He was looking at Ina. And his lips were moving. Very slightly. Silently. Going over it after him, word by word, the way he had in the Long Barn over Gaffer, learning it. It listened. That’s all it did. You can stop now. It’s done. Go on, then. We’ll be loud somewhere else. Drew could see him doing it. Getting every word. Putting them away somewhere, carefully, one at a time, like somebody packing something they’d once lost and couldn’t bear to lose again.

His face was wet. He didn’t seem to know.

And Calvin was standing by the parapet with Rachael beside him and his hands in his pockets, both hands, both pockets, perfectly still. Drew looked at Calvin’s hands, because Ice had looked at them, once, quickly, as Ina finished. Calvin’s hands weren’t shaking. Calvin was looking at the grave with his mouth shut, for once, and his face sad and puzzled and kind, the way it went when he didn’t understand something and didn’t mind not understanding it, and his hands were perfectly still in his pockets.

Ice looked at Calvin’s still hands for a long time.

Then Ina looked up and saw him. Saw his mouth. The way he had in the Long Barn. Ina’s face did something small and surprised. And then, the way they had over Gaffer, they nodded to each other across the grave. Once each.

Ice said something then. Drew was close enough to hear it, just. He didn’t think anybody else was. It was the kind of thing you say under your breath.

“I’ll remember them,” said Ice. “These ones.”

Ina looked at him. He tilted his head, a fraction.

These ones.

Drew watched Ina hear it. He watched him take it, and turn it over once, very quickly, and see the shape of it. These ones. As opposed to some other ones. Ones Ice hadn’t remembered. Ina’s face didn’t change. Ina’s face never changed. But something in the way he was standing did, a little, the way it does when somebody hands you something heavy and you shift your feet to take it.

He didn’t ask. Of course he didn’t. Ina had been on the step longer than anybody.

“Okay,” said Ina. Quietly. As if putting it somewhere safe.

And he pulled his mask down.

Rachael put the first handful of red earth in. Nobody told her to. She knelt down at the edge, in her gloves, and scooped up a handful of the soft crumbled rock, and let it go, and it pattered down onto the Quiet’s grey folded shape like rain on a roof. Then she got up and stood back, frowning, as if she’d just done something else she didn’t know the reason for.

Then everybody did. One handful each. Thirty-six. Bee too, last but one, with her face set and her hand shaking a bit, but she did it, and looked, and didn’t look away. Then Gabe and Salty and Azac filled it in with the spades, and Ice knelt and smoothed it with his hands, flat, the way you’d smooth a blanket.

Higu drew it on the rock. A small mark on the map of four, below the first square, on the south side, under the main stair. One mark.

She drew it very carefully. Drew watched her. She drew one small mark, and then her chalk stopped, a little way to the left of it, by the parapet, and hovered there. For a long moment. Over nothing. Then she lifted it, and didn’t draw anything, and put the chalk away.

In the right-hand column she wrote THE QUIET. BURIED. INA SAID THE WORDS.

They went up to the high terrace in a long line, up the high stair out of the first square, the way they’d come down it the first time, talking.

Calvin went first. He hadn’t been told to. He just went, at the front, talking, about the grave and about whether you could have a rule about graves, and Rachael went after him, and about four steps up, without either of them saying anything, her gloved fist went into the back of his collar and stayed there. Calvin didn’t stop talking. He didn’t even seem to notice. He just went up the high stair with Rachael’s fist in his collar the way he’d gone up a hundred stairs since floor 3.

Drew, at the back, saw Ice see it.

Ice was in the middle of the line with Thea. He’d looked up the stair at the front, at Calvin and Rachael and the fist in the collar. And he’d stopped on the step. Just for a second. And then Thea’s hand tightened in his and he went on.

The high terrace was the same too. Everything was, on four. The long flat terrace at the top of the city, under the red sky that wasn’t a sky, with the door at the end of it in its arch, and the canyon falling away on the left, terrace below terrace, all the way down to the dry river, and the plateau above on the right. The bare stone room three steps from the top of the stair, with its one window and no door, where somebody had slept for six weeks, with a black scorch across the ceiling where something had burned once and never been cleaned. The tall narrow house across the gap, four storeys, with a broken parapet on its roof and a crack in the parapet you could see through.

And by the door, the worn place.

Drew had seen it before. Everybody had. Hyvred had knelt at it on the sixty-sixth day with her hand flat on it. Thea had knelt at it. Xav had sat in it, after, for a whole night. It was just a patch of stone at the foot of the wall, just to the left of the door, where the wall met the terrace. Worn bright. Smoother than the stone round it, and paler, the way a step goes pale where a hundred years of feet have gone over it, except it hadn’t been a hundred years. It had been three weeks. One man, every evening, for three weeks, sitting with his back to the wall and his knees up, looking at the door.

Hyvred knelt at it again. She put her hand flat on the stone. She stayed like that a moment.

“Still yours,” she said, without looking round. “All of it. Still bright.” She took her hand away. “On four, nothing goes anywhere.”

Ice was standing a little way off, looking at it.

Drew watched him look. It was strange. He’d thought Ice would look at it the way you look at an old chair you used to sit in. Fondly, or sadly, or both. He didn’t. He looked at it the way you’d look at a chair somebody else had sat in. Somebody you used to know quite well, and had been worried about, and hadn’t seen in a long time.

“Sit down, Dec,” said Gabe. He’d come up beside him, with Hearthwall on his back. “One more time. Go on. For old times.”

Ice looked at the worn place a while longer.

“No,” he said.

Gabe looked at him.

“I’m not waiting for anybody,” said Ice. He said it simply, looking at the bright stone. “Everybody’s here.”

Nobody said anything to that. There wasn’t anything to say. Gabe put his hand on the back of Ice’s neck, briefly, the way he did, and took it away. Hyvred got up off her knees. Thea, beside Ice, had her eyes shut.

Drew wrote it down later, on his own slate, in the square letters he used for facts. He wrote it under the date, on its own line, because it was a fact, and because somebody should.

EVERYBODY’S HERE.

The door was in its arch at the end of the terrace. Plain stone. The same door they’d come out of on the sixty-sixth day, one at a time, into the red dusk, with Ice on a roof across the gap counting them under his breath.

Two hundred and four steps down to the Fen.

Drew didn’t decide to do it. He’d been going to do what they’d done at every stair since the Long Acre: Tiger at the front, hand flat on the door; Newish counting; Thea singing; Gabe last. It worked. It had worked all the way down from fourteen. Nobody had run.

But they were on four. At this door. And he’d written something once, on the bottom step of the stair on floor 3, that a man on a roof had read and said was the right order.

So he stood by the arch, with his back to the door, and looked at all of them on the terrace.

“Calvin first,” said Drew.

Calvin looked round. So did everybody.

“Rachael on his collar,” said Drew. He said it the way he’d written it. Like a fact. “Then Deston’s party. Then Xav’s. Then Yan’s, Hana’s, Hyvred’s, Chris’s. Then us. Ice in the middle of us, with Thea, where we can all see him.” He paused. “Gabe last.”

“You were last,” said Calvin. “Last time. You said me last.”

“Gabe’s last now. On the way down.” Drew looked at Gabe. “I’ll be in front of him. I’ll count you through.”

“Nobody’s behind Gabe,” said Hako. “That’s what last means.”

“Then he’s the last-last,” said Drew. “Somebody has to be.” He looked round at all of them. “Nobody runs.”

And he looked at Ice.

Ice was in the middle of Thea’s party, where Drew had put him, with Thea’s hand in his. He was looking at Drew. And then, for the second time since they’d come down the stair from five, Drew saw his face come open. All of it. Like a shutter in the wind.

“That’s the right order,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Drew. “You said.”

Tiger put his hand flat on the door. He held it there, the way he did. Then he pushed, and the door swung in on the dark, and the air came up the stair to meet them, and it smelled of water. Wet, green, rotten, cold. The Fen. Drew had forgotten it was possible for air to smell of anything but dust.

Calvin went first. Talking. Rachael’s fist went into his collar on the first step.

Then Deston, with his gold. Then Dennis, arguing with Azac about the wall on ten. Then Azac. Xav, small and still, reading the dark below. Gabe stepped out of the line to let them past, and stood by Drew at the arch with Hearthwall on his arm, warm. Rui Xiang. Malcolm, with his backup. John, with a mote going down ahead of him, small and bright. Yan, with the scythe. Salty. Wendy. Tiger, who’d opened the door and now went in behind his party with Burr’s rope over his shoulder. Cursiffe, with his pen. Hana, and Milli, holding hands. Jared. Aleaka and Sleet. Newish, counting, from the first step. Hyvred. Gigi and Bee and Kei and Hako. Chris, with his fifteenth notebook. Niko, with his thumb on his sword. Maigo. Winter. Ina, with his mask down.

Then Thea’s party. Chi, with Waddles. Xola. Higu with the rock.

Then Thea. With the lantern.

And Ice, with her hand in his.

He stopped in the arch. Just for a moment. He looked back along the high terrace, at the worn place by the door, bright, and the bare room with the scorch on the ceiling, and the tall narrow house across the gap with the crack in its parapet. At the whole of the Red City falling away below in the red dusk, terrace on terrace, to the dry river, where nothing went anywhere. At the high stair, going down into the first square. You couldn’t see the square from here. You couldn’t see the east wall, or the fountain, or the ledge under the main stair with one small mark on it.

“Seventy-eight,” he said. To Thea. Only to her. Drew heard it because he was standing right there.

“Seventy-eight,” said Thea.

And he went down.

Drew counted him through. Thirty-four. Then himself, thirty-five, and the big man beside him in the wolf coat.

He looked at Gabe.

“That’s thirty-six,” said Drew. “Gabe. Go.”

“After you,” said Gabe.

“You’re last-last.”

“I know. After you.”

Drew looked at him a moment. Then he went into the arch, onto the first step, into the dark and the smell of the Fen, with Thea’s song coming up the stair from somewhere below and Newish’s count under it and Calvin’s voice under that, still talking, the whole way down. Gabe came after him and pulled the door shut behind them both.

And behind them, on four, where nothing went anywhere, the high terrace was empty. And the first square was empty. And under the main stair, on the soft red ledge, there was one grave.

Only one.

Drew counted thirty-six asleep in the first square. Thirty-five, then the one on the fountain’s edge.
Rachael: “My feet did it. Twice now. Here.” Drew: “Is it bad?” “He said nearly. I’m still not asking.”
“Always.” “Always.” Neither of them picked it up.
Twenty paces. Rachael: “That’s a long way. If somebody’s bleeding. You can’t get to somebody in twenty paces. Not in time.” “Why did I say that?”
Ice read Drew’s post on day 65, from the roof. “You put her on his collar.” “She was the only one he’d stop for.” “It was the right order.”
Morning. Ice carried the Quiet. “I killed it. I’ll carry it.” It weighed nothing.
The soft red ledge under the main stair. He didn’t look for the place.
Thea looked at the rock beside the grave. A metre to the left. Then away.
Ina: “It listened. That’s all it did… You can stop now. You don’t have to hear anything any more… We’ll be loud somewhere else.”
Ice mouthed every word after him. Calvin’s hands were still in his pockets. Not shaking.
“I’ll remember them. These ones.” Ina: “Okay.” Mask down.
Rachael put the first handful in. Thirty-six handfuls. Bee looked.
Higu drew one mark. Her chalk stopped by the parapet. She didn’t draw anything there.
Calvin first. Rachael’s fist went into his collar on the fourth step. Nobody told her.
The worn place. Gabe: “Sit down, Dec. One more time.” “No. I’m not waiting for anybody. Everybody’s here.”
Calvin first. Rachael on his collar. Ice in the middle with Thea. Gabe last-last. Nobody runs.
“That’s the right order.” “I know. You said.”
Seventy-eight.
