Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 140

Two Hands

6,956 words · 30 min read

Tell decided things.

That was the first thing Wendy learned about it, and she learned it on the first morning, at the bottom of the hill road, before she’d even had breakfast.

The hill road was called the Wellgate. It went up from the corner of the Lantern Market, past the well-house, through the first wall by a gate that stood open, and on up, in long slopes and short flights of steps, all the way to the pale hall at the top. Everything that mattered in Tell was on it, Hawise said. Or just off it. The Tellhouse, halfway up. The Countinghouse, across the road from the Tellhouse. The Assize, at the top. And all the way up, on both sides, shops.

The first shop on the Wellgate was a baker’s. Wendy smelt it before she saw it. She’d come down the stable-yard steps with Yan and Tiger and Salty and Cursiffe in the grey early light, and Ice had been just ahead of them with Thea and Xola and Gabe, on his way out to hunt, and the smell of new bread had come round the corner of the market and hit all nine of them at once, and Tiger had made a sound like a man who’s been shot.

The baker was a big woman with a red face and her sleeves rolled up, and she was taking the shutter down off her window as they came round the corner. She saw Ice. She saw the grey cord on his wrist. She looked at it for about a second.

Then she put the shutter back up.

Not angrily. Not quickly. She just lifted it, and hooked it back on its hooks, and went inside, and Wendy heard the bolt go.

Ice didn’t stop walking. He didn’t look at the shutter. He went past it as if it was any other bit of wall, with Gabe on his right, and Thea on his left, and Xola behind singing something under her breath, and they went on up the Wellgate towards the gate in the first wall.

Wendy stood and looked at the shutter.

“Rude,” said Tiger, faintly, with great feeling.

“Over here, my loves,” said somebody.

Two doors up, a thin old woman was leaning out over the half-door of a shop no wider than a cupboard, with a tray of something steaming. Small round buns, brown on top, split and stuffed with something.

“Pork and apple,” she said. “Halfpenny. Don’t mind Bertha. Bertha’s frightened of her own oven.” She looked up the road, after Ice. At the cord. Wendy watched her look at it, and think about it, and decide. “Your corded one can have his for nothing, if he comes back this way. Tell him I said.”

They had six each. Tiger had nine. They took two up the road for Ice and Gabe, and caught them up at the gate in the first wall, and Ice looked at the bun in his hand for a long moment as if he didn’t know what it was.

“The old lady with the half-door,” said Wendy. “She said yours was free.”

Ice looked down the hill. At the little shop. At the shut baker’s next to it.

Something happened to his face that Wendy didn’t have a word for.

“Thank you,” he said. To her, or to the old woman, or to the bun, Wendy didn’t know.

It went on like that.

Not everybody. Not the whole city. Most people, most of the time, just looked, and went on with their business, and gave the climbers a little more room in the street than they gave each other. But every day, in every street, somebody saw the cord, and decided.

A man selling knives in the Lantern Market wouldn’t sell to Salty. A man selling knives at the other end of the same market followed Salty halfway up the Wellgate to ask him what he thought of a blade. A woman crossed the road to get away from Ice and Gabe. A girl of about seven crossed it back to ask Gabe if his shield was heavy, and her mother didn’t stop her. A tavern on the second ring put a board out that said NO CORDS. On the third day, the tavern next to it put out a board that said CORDS WELCOME, and by the evening it was full of climbers, and Calvin had made friends with the landlord, and the landlord of NO CORDS was standing in his own doorway watching with a face like a slapped cod.

“It’s like the whole place is a party,” said Hyvred, that night, on the copy-room floor, with great satisfaction, “and everybody gets to pick.”

“Pick what?” said Calvin.

“Sides, Calvin.”

“I didn’t know there were sides.”

“There are always sides.”

Yan said it was the lights. He said he could see them doing it. Everybody in Tell, all day long, deciding. He said it was like watching a field of grass in the wind, except that the wind was inside every single stalk, and blew whichever way it liked.

Wendy couldn’t see the lights. She’d never been able to. She’d got used to that a long time ago, on floor 6, on the lip of the pit, when Yan had said everything’s got a light, and she’d said I’ll take the shots you can’t, and that had been the deal.

But she didn’t need to see them to see what Tell did. She just watched faces. She’d always been good at faces. You couldn’t shoot well if you weren’t.

On the second morning somebody threw something at Ice.

It was on the Wellgate, below the first wall. Wendy was twenty paces behind, with Yan. Ice was ahead, going out again, with Gabe and Thea and Xola. And a man stepped out of a doorway on the left. A big man, in a leather apron, with a lump of something in his fist. And he shouted “Corded!” and threw it.

It was a clod of dried mud with a stone in it. Wendy saw the stone. She saw it leave his hand and saw where it was going and couldn’t do anything about it, with her bow on her back and twenty paces and a crowd between.

It hit Ice on the back of the left shoulder. Hard. She heard it.

He didn’t stop.

He didn’t turn round. He didn’t flinch, or put a hand up, or look at the man, or say anything. His step didn’t even change. He went on walking up the Wellgate at exactly the same pace, with the dried mud breaking up and falling off the back of his shoulder in pieces, as if it had been a leaf.

Wendy had heard about this. Rachael had said it, at a fire once, on floor 9, after Deston’s party had watched him hunt. I couldn’t tell you were hit. I couldn’t tell. Wendy had thought at the time that Rachael was exaggerating, which Rachael never did, and had put it away.

Now she watched it, and understood that Rachael hadn’t been exaggerating at all.

You couldn’t tell. She’d seen it hit him. And she couldn’t tell.

The man in the apron had another one. He’d bent down and come up with it. He drew his arm back.

Gabe didn’t look round either. He just shifted his shoulders, without turning, so that Hearthwall, on his back, swung about a hand’s width to the left. And the clod came, and hit the rim, and burst into dust. And Gabe went on walking.

Thea had her hand on Ice’s arm. Wendy hadn’t seen her put it there. She hadn’t stepped in front of him. She hadn’t stepped anywhere. She’d just put her hand on his arm and kept walking with it there.

The man in the apron stood in his doorway with mud on his fingers, staring after them.

“He meant it,” said Yan, beside Wendy, very quietly. He was watching the man. “When he threw it. He really meant it.”

“And now?”

“Now he doesn’t.” Yan’s face was strange. “It went out of him. When nobody turned round. He’s standing there and he doesn’t know what he meant any more.”

The man went back inside and shut the door.

Yan was still looking at the door.

“I’ll know his light now,” he said. Quite pleasantly. “Anywhere. In the dark. I could find him tonight.”

Wendy looked at him.

“Yan.”

“I’m not going to.” He turned away from the door and started up the Wellgate again, after Ice. “Ice didn’t turn round. So I won’t either.” A pause. “Today.”

Wendy didn’t know, for a moment, whether he was joking. She’d known him for years and she still didn’t always know. She decided he probably wasn’t, and that it didn’t matter. He’d said Ice didn’t turn round. That was the bit that would hold him. She’d make sure it held.

Ice didn’t say anything about it that night. Nobody asked. Thea sat next to him on the copy-room floor and ate her soup, and once, while he was talking to Drew about something else, she put two fingers on the back of his left shoulder, very lightly, where the stone had hit. He didn’t look round at that either.

But he stopped talking for a second.

The thread started with Chi.

Wendy didn’t see her do it. She just saw it on her wrist on the second evening, a bit of grey wool unpicked from the hem of her own cloak and tied round her right wrist in a plain knot. And then on Gabe’s, the next morning, on the wrist he held Hearthwall with. And then Calvin, who tied his so tight his hand went purple and Rachael had to cut it off and tie it again. And Hyvred, who tied three. And Hana, and Jared, and Chris, and Higu, and Drew, and Deston without saying anything, and Rachael, and by the third evening, when Wendy looked round the copy-room, she couldn’t see a wrist without one.

“It’s a stupid gesture,” she said to Yan.

“Yes,” said Yan.

He held out his wrist. Suzu came down his arm, very slowly, and wrapped herself once round it, small and white, and put her head on the back of his hand, and stayed there. Grey enough, in the lamplight.

Wendy looked at that for a while.

Then she unpicked a bit of the grey binding off the grip of her spare bow, and tied it round her left wrist with her teeth.

Hawise saw them the next day. She came up the stable-yard steps with her satchel to see how they were getting on, and stopped in the copy-room door, and looked at thirty-five grey threads on thirty-five wrists. Her face went very still.

“A thread isn’t a cord,” she said.

“No,” said Gabe. “It’s a thread.”

Hawise looked at him for a long time.

Then she laughed. Once, short, through her nose, as if it had got out without asking. And went away down the steps shaking her head, and Wendy heard her laugh again at the bottom, by the pump.

There was always a clerk.

Wendy started counting them on the second day, because she couldn’t not. It was the archer in her. You counted the things that were watching you.

There was always one. In every square the climbers went into, on every corner they stood on, at the top of every flight of steps. Standing still, in long plain grey, with a book on the left arm, not looking at anything in particular. You never saw one arrive. You looked away, and looked back, and there was one by the water trough that hadn’t been there.

They didn’t follow. That was the strange thing. Wendy tested it. She went down the Wellgate on her own, and round the back of the Lantern Market, and through a little alley full of cats, and up a stair on the far side, into a tiny square she’d never been in with a dry fountain and a lime tree. And there was a clerk under the lime tree. Not following. Already there.

“They give up their names,” said Hawise, when Wendy asked her. They were in the stable yard. Hawise was sharpening a pen with a little knife. “When they go in. You walk in the Countinghouse door, and you’re a clerk of the Count, and you don’t come out with your name. You don’t need it. The Count’s got it.” She tested the point on her thumb. “That’s the price. Nobody makes them. Every one of them walked in the door on their own feet.”

“Why would anybody do that?” said Wendy.

Hawise was quiet for a while.

“My cousin did,” she said. “Alys. Twelve years ago. She said she wanted to know what it was all for. The counting. She said the Count knew, and if you went in, you’d know too.” She put the knife away. “She went in. She walks past my door every week, with her book. She doesn’t know me.”

Yan had been sitting on the edge of the trough, listening. He’d gone white.

“That’s why,” he said.

“That’s why what, love?”

Yan didn’t answer her. He looked at Wendy.

“That’s why they’re all the same,” he said. “Inside. They gave them up. Their names. They gave them up, and they’re all the same now.”

He looked very young, sitting on the trough. Younger than Wendy had seen him look since the night on the Patience when he’d said I can’t walk somebody who doesn’t know I’m there.

“Three hundred and eleven names,” he said. “I had to learn them. One at a time. Because nobody would say them. And these people just—walk in. And give them away.”

Hawise looked from Yan to Wendy and back, with her pen in her hand.

“You see something,” she said slowly. “Don’t you. When you look at people.”

Yan didn’t answer that either.

But Hawise didn’t ask again. She looked at him for a while longer with her sharp eyes, and then she nodded, as if she’d been told something, and went back up the steps.

The Tellhouse was halfway up the Wellgate, on the left, and Cursiffe had been at its door before the bread on the first morning.

Wendy went with him on the second afternoon, because Yan wanted to go, and where Yan went Wendy went, and that was the other half of the deal.

It was the biggest building on the Wellgate apart from the Assize. Long and pale, built of the same grey stone as everything, but older, and set back from the road behind a flight of wide worn steps. Opposite it, across the Wellgate, was the Countinghouse: tall and narrow and grey, with a plain door and no windows on the street side at all, not one. Wendy didn’t like looking at it. It was like a face with its eyes shut.

There was a clerk on the Countinghouse steps. Of course there was.

Inside, the Tellhouse was one great hall. The Reading Hall, Hawise called it. High and long and full of light from tall windows down both sides, and down the middle of it long tables, and at the long tables people sat and read, and all round the walls, from the floor to above the windows, were shelves. Not with books on. With rolls. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Fat rolls of thick cream paper on wooden spindles, each one with a little leather tag hanging off the end of the spindle, in pigeonholes, in rows, in tiers, with ladders on rails to reach the high ones.

“The Rolls,” said Hawise. “This hall’s the last two hundred years. Every birth, every death, every marriage, every house sold and every quarrel settled, in two hands. Anybody in Tell can come in and read their own family for a penny.”

People were. That was what the long tables were for. An old man with a magnifying glass on a stick, going slowly down a roll with his finger. Two young women with their heads together, giggling, over a marriage. A mother with a baby, reading something out loud to it.

At the back of the hall, at the very end, behind a long desk, there was a door. Black wood, very old, banded with iron. Shut.

“And that,” said Hawise, “is the rest.”

The Remembrancer was a woman called Maud Askew, and she said no.

She was about seventy, Wendy thought. Small and very upright, in a long black coat, with a stick she didn’t lean on and a face like the inside of a walnut. She sat behind the long desk at the back of the Reading Hall with the black door behind her, and listened to Cursiffe, all the way through, without interrupting once. Which Wendy thought was probably worse than if she had.

“No,” she said.

“I’m a writer,” said Cursiffe. “I write things down. That’s all I do.”

“I can see that.” Askew looked at the book in his hand. At his inky fingers. “I can see that very well, young man. And I’ve no doubt you’d look after the Rolls better than half my tellers.” She folded her hands on the desk. “But you’re a climber. And your company isn’t told. One of you is under a cord. A company with a corded man in it isn’t a told company, and a company that isn’t told doesn’t go into the Rolls below the hall. That’s the law.”

“He’s not in my party,” said Cursiffe.

“He’s in your company. You came up the well together. Hawise wrote you together.” She glanced at Hawise, who was standing a little to one side, looking at the floor. “Hawise wrote you together, and then Hawise put you in my copy-room, which I’ve only just found out about this morning, and which we’re going to talk about, Hawise.”

“Yes, Remembrancer,” said Hawise.

“And even if you were told,” said Askew, to Cursiffe, “even if you were the best-told man in Tell. No flame goes below the First Wall. Not a candle, not a lamp, not a spark. Not since the Wet Year. And there’s nothing down there you can read without a light.”

“I know,” said Cursiffe.

“Then what do you want down there?”

Cursiffe was quiet for a moment. He put his hand flat against his shirt pocket. Wendy had seen him do that more times in the last two days than in the whole of the year before.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Something that’s been lost.”

Askew looked at him over her folded hands.

“Everything down there’s been lost, young man,” she said. “That’s what down there is.”

“Then I’ll know it when I see it.”

“No,” said Askew. Quite kindly. “You won’t. Because you won’t see it.”

And that was that.

They went out of the Reading Hall and down the wide steps into the Wellgate, and stood at the bottom. Cursiffe didn’t say anything. He just stood with his hand flat on his pocket, looking at the black door through the open front of the hall, a long way off at the back, small and shut.

Wendy thought about Ice. At the market, by the rock, the first day. There’s an archive. They wouldn’t let us in. Somebody tried every morning. For a week.

And the way he’d looked at Cursiffe. Not even a second.

She didn’t know what it meant. She put it with the other things she didn’t know what they meant, which was getting to be quite a big pile.

“We’ll come back tomorrow,” said Cursiffe.

He went back every morning.

Wendy didn’t go every time. But she knew. He went at the opening bell and stood in front of Askew’s desk and said, politely, “Good morning, Remembrancer,” and Askew said, “Good morning. No,” and he went and sat at one of the long tables in the Reading Hall with his book, and read the Rolls, the open ones, the last two hundred years, all day. Every birth and death and quarrel. Writing things down.

Yan went with him most days. He didn’t read the births or the quarrels.

He read the dead.

There was a whole wall of them, at the left-hand end of the hall. The Death Rolls. A tier of pigeonholes from the floor to the ceiling, every roll tagged by the year, and every year a thousand names, more. Yan took them down one at a time, and unrolled them on the long table, and sat with his chin on his hand and read them. Every name. Slowly. With Suzu wrapped round his wrist and her head on the back of his hand, as if she was reading too.

On the third afternoon Wendy sat down opposite him.

“You can’t learn them all,” she said. “Yan. There are millions.”

“I know,” said Yan. He didn’t look up. “I’m not trying to.”

“Then what are you doing?”

“Seeing how it’s done.” He turned the roll a little. Smoothed it flat. There was a name under his finger. Gil Hawker, of the Rope Walk, a carter. Two hands.

“Somebody did,” he said. “Every single one. Somebody sat here and wrote every single one of them down. In two hands. So they’d be said.” He looked up at her then. His eyes were very bright, the way they went over a puzzle. “Three hundred years the tide-folk fed their dead to that thing because nobody would say a word. And here it’s a job, Wen. A teller does it on a step, at dusk. I want to see what that looks like. All of them. Written down. With nobody left out.”

Wendy looked at the wall of the dead, from the floor to the ceiling.

She didn’t say anything. She took the next roll down off the shelf, and unrolled it, and started reading it too.

It was Hawise who told them about the Hand Law.

She came up to the copy-room on the fourth evening with a pot of something, as an excuse, and sat on the end of a bench with it on her knees, and when Cursiffe came in from the Tellhouse with ink up to both elbows she said, without looking at him, “There’s an old law.”

Cursiffe stopped.

“Nobody uses it. It’s from before the Wet Year. Before the new presses. When there weren’t enough tellers.” She was looking at the pot. “Any hand that writes a day the same as a teller’s hand, word for word, is a teller’s hand. For the day after.”

The copy-room had gone quiet.

“A teller can go where tellers go,” said Hawise. “That’s all tellers. Not climbers. Not corded companies. Tellers.”

“Why are you telling me?” said Cursiffe.

Hawise looked up at him at last. At his ink.

“Because you’ve been at her desk every morning for four days,” she said. “And she’s started saying good morning first.”

Askew laughed when he asked her.

It wasn’t a nice laugh, exactly. It was dry and short, a single bark. Every teller in the Reading Hall looked up.

“The Hand Law,” she said. “Who told you about the Hand Law?”

Cursiffe didn’t say.

Askew looked past him at Hawise, who had found something very interesting to do at a shelf at the far end of the hall.

“Nobody,” said Askew, “has matched a teller’s door-roll in three hundred years. Did she tell you that? They tried. When I was a girl, there were boys used to try it for a dare. Clever boys. Boys who’d been reading the Rolls since they could walk.” She leaned back in her chair. “Nobody can do it, young man. People write the way they talk. They can’t help it. They put in what they think happened. What they meant. A word here, a word there. And then it doesn’t match. And then it isn’t told.” She looked at him. “You’d need to write a whole day the way somebody else would write it. Word for word. Without seeing their page.”

“Yes,” said Cursiffe.

“And you think you can.”

“I don’t know,” said Cursiffe. “I’d like to try.”

Askew looked at him for a long time.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “At the door. Beside Orlo Fenn, on the door-roll. From the opening bell to the evening bell. Everybody who comes in that door, what they ask, what they’re told, what they do.” She picked up her pen. “At the evening bell, I’ll read them side by side. If they match, you’re a teller tomorrow.” She didn’t look up. “And a teller can go where tellers go.”

“Thank you, Remembrancer.”

“Don’t thank me. You won’t do it.” She began to write something. “Nobody does.”

Orlo Fenn was nineteen, and very tall, and very thin, and had ink on his nose, and was, according to Orlo Fenn, the fastest pen in the Tellhouse.

He was. Wendy watched him. He sat on a high stool at a slanted desk just inside the great door of the Reading Hall, with the door-roll unrolled in front of him and a pen in his hand, and he wrote down everybody who came in, as they came in, in a small fast slanting hand, and never once looked down at the page. He looked at the people. His pen just went.

Cursiffe sat on a stool beside him, at a second slanted desk somebody had found, with his own pen and a roll of the Tellhouse’s cream paper, and his ink. And for the first hour, Wendy thought he was going to fail.

He was too slow. He wrote the way he always wrote, small and square, every letter properly made, and people came through the door faster than he could write them. He had to stop and ask Orlo things. “How do you put it when they don’t give a name?” “What do you call the man with the cart?” Orlo told him. Quickly, and a bit pleased with himself, out of the corner of his mouth, still writing.

“Who came. What they asked. What they were told. What they did,” said Orlo. “That’s all. That’s the plain form.”

“What about how they—“

“No how. No seemed. No perhaps. No I think. Who, what, said, done.” Orlo’s pen flew. “And you put what they said. Not what they meant. That’s the hard bit. Everybody puts what they meant.”

Cursiffe stopped writing.

He sat very still for a moment, on his stool, with his pen in the air. Then he looked at the page he’d done so far. He read it through. And he took his pen, and in four places, very neatly, he crossed out a word.

After that, he got faster.

Wendy sat on the Tellhouse steps all day with her bow across her knees. Across the Wellgate, on the Countinghouse steps, the clerk stood with its book on its arm, all day, facing the Tellhouse. She watched it. It didn’t move. Once, about noon, she realised it wasn’t the same clerk as the morning. She hadn’t seen them change.

Tiger got bored by the middle of the morning and went round into the Tellhouse yard, where there were children. Wendy could hear him from the steps. They had a long rope, and two of them were turning it, and the rest were jumping in, one at a time, and chanting. They made Tiger jump. He was terrible at it, which was surprising, and very funny, and the children shrieked. They were chanting something with numbers in it. Thirteen, twenty-two, thirty-one, forty. Over and over, faster and faster, until whoever was in the rope tripped and was out. Tiger got out every time on forty.

Salty went round the side of the Tellhouse with his tools at about ten, because the great door squeaked. It had squeaked every time anybody came in or out since the opening bell, a long, thin, miserable, rising squeak, and Wendy had seen Salty’s face go through about four stages of suffering. He took the door half off its pins while the old porter wasn’t looking, and did something to the top hinge with a file and a bit of fat, and put it back. And it swung without a sound.

The porter came out of his cubby by the door and stood and stared at it. He was a little old bent man with no teeth.

“That door,” he said, “has squeaked since I was a boy.”

“Not any more,” said Salty, kindly.

The porter looked at him with an expression of the deepest betrayal Wendy had ever seen on a human face, and went back in his cubby, and didn’t come out for an hour.

At dusk, Ice came back.

Wendy saw them coming up the Wellgate from the gate in the first wall. Ice and Thea and Xola, and Gabe. Ice with his shield on his back, scratched in four long lines from top to bottom that hadn’t been there in the morning, and the cord on his wrist, and his face giving away nothing at all.

He stopped at the bottom of the Tellhouse steps.

He didn’t come up. Wendy didn’t know if it was the cord, or Askew, or something else. He stood at the bottom with his hands at his sides, and looked up past Wendy, through the open door, at the slanted desk inside, and at Cursiffe sitting at it on his stool beside Orlo, in the last of the light, writing.

He looked at that for a long time.

Wendy watched his face. She was good at faces. She’d always been.

She didn’t know what that one was. She’d never seen it on him before. The nearest she could get was something like watching a door you’ve seen stay shut a great many times, and seeing it, for the first time, start to move.

Then Thea said something to him, very quietly, and he nodded, and they went on up the Wellgate towards the stable yard.

They did the compare in the Reading Hall, at the evening bell, and half the tellers in Tell came to watch.

Wendy didn’t know how word had got out. It had. By the time the bell went, the hall was full. Tellers in brown at every long table and standing in the aisles and up the ladders on their rails, in rows along the shelves like birds. Hawise, near the front, with her arms folded. And at the very back, by the door, a grey robe with a book on its arm.

“They come to every compare,” said Hawise, when Wendy looked at it. “Always have.”

Askew sat behind her long desk with the two rolls in front of her, side by side. Orlo’s, long and slanting and fast. Cursiffe’s, small and square, with four neat crossings-out near the top.

She read them aloud. All forty-one lines.

Line by line. Orlo’s first, then Cursiffe’s. And after each pair, if they matched, she said “Told,” and the next.

“At the opening bell. Gil Mercer, of the Tannery, asked for the Roll of his grandmother’s house. Was told it is in the third case of the north wall. Went to it.”

“At the opening bell. Gil Mercer, of the Tannery, asked for the Roll of his grandmother’s house. Was told it is in the third case of the north wall. Went to it.”

“Told.”

It went on. The hall was absolutely silent. Twenty lines. Twenty-five. Wendy found she was holding her bow very tight across her knees. Yan, beside her, was holding his breath.

“Told.”

“Told.”

Thirty lines. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. Wendy heard somebody at the back say it can’t be, under their breath, and somebody else hiss at them.

“Before noon,” Askew read, from Orlo’s roll. “A woman with a hen came to the door. Was told hens are not to come in. She said: I’m not paying for that. Went away.”

She moved her eyes to the other roll.

“Before noon. A woman with a hen came to the door. Was told hens are not to come in. She said: I’m not paying that. Went away.”

She stopped.

The whole hall heard it. For that. That. One word.

“Two hands,” said Askew, quite quietly, “that don’t agree.”

Wendy’s stomach went over. She looked at Cursiffe. He was standing beside Askew’s desk with his hands at his sides, quite still, looking at nothing, the way he always looked. She couldn’t tell anything from his face. She never could. She’d have given anything just then for Yan’s eyes.

“Yan,” she whispered. “What’s he—“

“Still,” said Yan. “He’s gone completely still. Like the ink.”

Askew didn’t say not told. She didn’t say anything. She sat with her two hands flat on the two rolls, and looked at Orlo.

Orlo had gone red. Right up from his collar to the ink on his nose. He was looking at the two rolls on the desk. His. Cursiffe’s. His again. And then he looked up, past all of them, at the great door of the hall. At the place inside it where a woman with a hen had stood, that morning, for about the time it took to say six words.

Wendy watched him remember.

You could see it. You didn’t need Yan’s eyes for this one. She watched a nineteen-year-old who had never once in his life been told he wasn’t the fastest pen in the Tellhouse stand in front of half the tellers in Tell and remember, all the way back, honestly, what a woman with a hen had said. And find out.

“She said that,” said Orlo.

His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat.

“She said I’m not paying that. I put for because—“ He stopped. “Because it’s how you’d say it. It’s how I’d say it. It’s what she meant.” He looked at Askew. “It isn’t what she said. He’s right.”

And he leaned over the desk, and took the pen out of the horn, and in front of the whole Tellhouse, very carefully, he drew one straight line through the word for on his own roll.

The hall didn’t make a sound.

Askew looked at Orlo for a long, long time. Her face didn’t move at all. Then she looked down at his roll, at the one crossed-out word, and touched it with one finger, as if to see if the ink was dry.

“Forty years I’ve been reading compares at this desk,” she said. “And in forty years, Orlo Fenn, I have never seen a teller strike his own line in front of me.” She took her finger away. “Not once.”

Orlo didn’t say anything. He was still red.

Askew looked down again, and read the last eight lines. Orlo’s, then Cursiffe’s. She didn’t stop, and she didn’t say anything between them, and they matched. Every one.

Then she put her two hands flat on the two rolls, and looked up at Cursiffe.

“Told,” she said.

The hall went up.

Wendy had never heard a noise like it. Not cheering, exactly. Half the tellers in Tell, who had spent their whole lives in a room where you weren’t allowed to raise your voice, all saying something at once and then remembering where they were and stopping and then saying it again anyway. Hawise had both hands over her mouth. Somebody up a ladder dropped a roll. Yan had grabbed Wendy’s arm and was holding it so hard it hurt.

Cursiffe didn’t move. He stood beside the desk with his hands at his sides, the way he’d stood all along.

But Wendy saw him look at Orlo. Just once. And Orlo look back.

And then, very slightly, both at once, like two hands writing the same thing, they nodded.

Askew gave him a key.

It was iron, as long as Wendy’s hand, black and very old, on a loop of plain cord. She took it off a hook under her desk and held it out, and Cursiffe took it, and it lay across his inky palm like something that had been waiting there.

“A teller can go where tellers go,” said Askew. “For the day after. That’s tomorrow. Not the day after that.”

“Yes, Remembrancer.”

“And no flame below the First Wall.” She looked at him hard. “I mean that. I don’t care what it costs you. You’ll have to find a light that isn’t fire. And there’s no such thing.”

“There is,” said Cursiffe. “I know where to get one.”

Askew raised her eyebrows. Wendy saw her think about it, and decide not to ask.

“And one more,” said Askew. “One. Not two. I’m not having a procession of climbers through the Third Wall.”

“I’m coming,” said Orlo.

Askew didn’t even look at him. “Tellers don’t count.” She stood up, and took her stick from where it leaned against the desk. “And neither do I. I’m coming because I’ve been Remembrancer forty years, and I’ve never once seen what a leaf wants.”

The hall went quiet again.

Cursiffe looked at her. Then down, at his shirt. At the pocket.

“I didn’t tell you about the leaf,” he said.

“No,” said Askew. “Hawise did.” She glanced at the far end of the hall, where Hawise was very busy with a shelf again. “On the first day. Before she told me about my copy-room.” She came round the end of the desk, small and upright, and stopped beside him and looked up at him. “She said you kept putting your hand on your pocket like a man with a hot coal in it. And that when the evening bell went, you looked at the floor.”

He asked Thea that night.

Wendy was there. Most of them were. The copy-room was only one room. He went across it to where Thea was sitting by the end wall with Ice and Gabe and Higu, and stood in front of her, and said, “I need a light that isn’t fire.”

Thea looked up at him.

Then, without saying anything, she reached behind her, and brought out the lantern.

Wendy had seen it a hundred times. Everybody had. It went everywhere Thea did. It was a strange thing, small and old, with a body of dark bent metal and a globe of thick pale glass like a big drop of water, and it had never in all the time Wendy had known it had a flame in it. It didn’t need one. It just glowed. A deep, warm, amber light, from somewhere inside the glass, that showed things the way they were.

“This one,” said Thea. “It’s never been fire.”

“Yes,” said Cursiffe.

“When?”

“Tomorrow. At the opening bell.” He paused. “She said one more. Not two.”

Thea nodded. She didn’t ask what was down there. She looked at the lantern in her hands for a moment, and then she looked sideways, at Ice.

Ice was sitting with his back against the wall and his knees up and the grey cord on his wrist. He’d been listening. Wendy watched him. She watched him think about it. About Thea going down into a hill under a city where the clerks stood on the corners and one of them had stopped writing to look at her. Without him.

His hands, on his knees, closed. Then opened again.

“I’ll be on the step,” he said.

That was all.

Thea’s face did something Wendy couldn’t read. Something very quick and very soft, there and gone, like the light in the lantern when you moved it. She didn’t say anything. She just reached out with the hand that wasn’t holding the lantern and put it on his knee, for a moment, and took it away.

“Who’s the one?” said Gabe.

Cursiffe turned.

He looked round the copy-room. Wendy watched him look. At all of them, on the floor, on the benches, in their blankets, with their grey threads on their wrists. And then he stopped, and looked at Yan.

“Yan,” he said. “If you want to.”

Yan was sitting on his blanket with Suzu round his neck. He looked up.

“Why me?”

“Because it’s dark down there,” said Cursiffe. “And you can see in the dark. And because you read the dead every day for four days and never once asked me what I wanted them for.”

Yan looked at him for a long moment.

“Yes,” he said.

Wendy looked at Yan. At Thea’s lantern. At the cord on Ice’s wrist.

“Then I’ll be on the step too,” she said.
