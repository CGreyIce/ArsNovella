Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 216

Never Shut a Lantern

7,102 words · 31 min read

Lanthorn hung in the roots.

Jared saw it from the bottom of the path, the second time they went down, and stopped walking, and somebody walked into the back of him. He didn’t notice who. He was looking.

The slope came down through the white trunks and levelled out, and at the bottom of it, where the ground was lowest, there was a pool. Black. Round. Perfectly still. As wide as the green at Haverley, maybe, with no edge to it you could see; the water just went from black to black. And round the pool, the trees were bigger. Much bigger. Seven or eight of them, standing in a ring round the water, with trunks you could have built a house inside, and roots. Great pale roots, coming down out of the trunks and arching out over the ground and back into it, like the legs of something, like flying buttresses on a church, taller than a man, taller than three, a whole tangle of them, arch on arch, all round the pool.

The town was in the roots.

Houses. Not built; grown into. Hollows in the great roots, with doors cut in them and little round windows, and floors of pale planks laid across between one arch and the next, and ladders, and walkways of rope going from root to root. Two hundred people, maybe. He couldn’t tell. It went back into the tangle further than he could see.

And everywhere, lanterns.

That was what stopped him. Hundreds of them. Hanging from every root and arch and walkway, on chains, on hooks, on bits of cord: iron lanterns, all of them open. No glass. No doors. Cages of thin iron, woven in a pattern, round or long or bell-shaped, every size from a fist to a barrel. Most of them were empty. Some had a light inside. A pale one, small, resting in the iron like a bird in a nest, and the light coming out through the weave in a hundred little points, scattered, so that the lantern didn’t look like a lamp so much as a handful of stars somebody had caught and hung up.

“Man,” said Jared.

Beside him, Hana let out a breath. “That’s beautiful.”

“That’s iron.” He couldn’t stop looking. “That’s all iron. That’s wire. Look at the weave. Look how fine. That’s—“ He didn’t have a word. “Somebody did that. By hand. Hundreds of them.”

“Thousands,” said Milli, behind them, who’d obviously been counting.

At the edge of the roots, at the bottom of the path, a girl was waiting. Small, thin, with eyes too big for her face and an empty iron lantern in her hand and a light at her shoulder, humming. Isel. She looked at them coming down out of the trees, thirty-six people and a goat and a dog and a white Rimehound, with their thirty-five lights turned down to sparks.

She looked at the sparks for a long time.

“You made them small,” said Isel.

“We asked,” said Milli.

Isel looked at him. Then she turned round and walked into the roots, and after a moment they followed.

It had gone round the camp before the first drip, Milli’s please.

Jared had woken up to Aleaka telling it, in a whisper, to Newish, and Newish telling it, not in a whisper, to everybody, and then thirty-five people sitting round a small fire in the dark saying could you be less, please to their own shoulders, feeling stupid, and every single light going down to a spark. Every one.

His too. He’d said it, and it had gone, and he’d sat there looking at the spark by his ear and felt something he couldn’t put a name to. Like the first time you say thank you to a man at a counter you’ve walked past every day for a year, and he looks up.

Two years. Seven hundred and forty-odd days of Window and Status and Map. Nobody had ever said please.

Chris had written it down in a column headed THE LIGHTS, under Yet and Don’t. Day 746. Milli asked it nicely. It did.

And Thea had looked at her lantern, the big one, the one she’d carried since the Fen, the amber one that leaned, and she hadn’t asked it anything. She’d just taken off her cloak and wrapped it round the lantern, carefully, the whole thing, and carried it in her arms like that, against her chest, down the path. You could see the light leaking out of the folds. Leaning. Toward Ice.

Isel’s gran was at the pool.

She was sitting on a low root at the very edge of the black water, on a cushion, with a blanket over her knees. Very old. Very small. So thin you could see the bones of her hands. Her eyes were the same as Isel’s, enormous and dark, but clouded at the edges, like the eyes of something that had been looking into the dark for a very long time. Her hair was white and long and plaited round her head like a crown.

Her light was at her shoulder. Very small. Smaller than any of the others he’d seen. It hummed, but faintly, so faintly he had to listen for it, and it didn’t move much. It just sat there, close by her ear, the way an old dog sits by an old man’s chair.

“Gran,” said Isel. “They’re here. The ones from below.”

The old woman looked up. At all of them, slowly, one face at a time, the way Mother Crane had looked at them across her table on the first night on floor 1. Then at their shoulders. At the thirty-five sparks.

“Talking ones,” she said. Her voice was very soft and very dry. “Isel said.”

“Yes,” said Hana. She stepped forward. She’d been in front all the way down. “I’m Hana. These are— This is all of us.” She hesitated. “We came up the stair. From the river.”

“From the river.” The old woman looked at her for a long time. “Nobody comes up from the river. The river’s below. Nothing comes from below.” She looked past Hana. At the back of the crowd. “Where is he? Isel said there was one from further down than that.”

Aleaka came forward with the goat.

The goat walked up to the old woman on her root and looked at her with his flat strange eyes and she looked back at him. Then, slowly, she put out one thin hand. The goat sniffed it. Then put his forehead against it, very gently, the iron cap on his horn clinking against her knuckles.

The old woman made a sound. Not a laugh. Older than a laugh.

“Floor 1,” she said. “Isel said. I didn’t know what that meant. I still don’t.” She kept her hand on the goat’s head. “But I know he’s from somewhere a long way under us. And he came up.” She looked at the strays. They’d followed. They always followed the goat now: a dozen of them, more, drifting in a loose ring above the crowd, keeping their distance from the lanterns. “And they know it. Look at them. They’ve never seen a thing come up.”

“Nothing leaves its floor,” said Aleaka. Quietly. “That’s the rule. I worked it out.”

The old woman looked at her.

“Then somebody broke it,” she said. “Or it bent.” She took her hand off the goat. “I’m Edda. I keep the lamps. Isel’s my granddaughter’s girl. Sit down. All of you. You’re too tall to look at.”

They sat on the roots round the pool, all thirty-six of them, and Edda told them about the lights.

Not all at once. In pieces. With long gaps where she listened to something nobody else could hear, and Isel sat at her feet with her empty lantern in her lap, and the town came out of the roots in ones and twos and stood at a distance, pale and big-eyed and quiet, each of them with a light at their shoulder, humming, and listened too.

Every child that was born in the Hollow, Edda said, a stray came to.

Not straight away. In a day, or three, or a week. It came in through the window, or down the walkway, or just appeared one drip, hanging over the cradle, and stayed. And that was your light. Your glim, the Hollow called it. It went where you went. It hummed when you were well and went quiet when you weren’t. It slept in a lantern at night, if you had a lantern, and if you didn’t it slept on your pillow. It was yours your whole life.

And when you died, it went back out.

“Out there,” said Edda. She lifted her chin at the dark beyond the roots, where the strays drifted between the white trunks. “Back to being a stray. Looking. Till another child comes, and it finds them, or one of the others does.” She was quiet. “That’s what they’re saying. All the time. Whose. They’re looking for whose they’ll be.”

Aleaka’s head came up. She looked at Milli. Milli looked at her.

“Whose,” said Aleaka. “That’s what I heard. With—“ She stopped. “I heard them say it.”

Edda looked at her a long time.

“You hear them,” she said.

“Sometimes. When something calls, I hear what it means.”

“Then you’re like my gran.” Edda’s mouth did something. “She heard them. Nobody believed her about that.” She looked out at the strays. “There are fewer every year. Fewer strays. Fewer glims. In my gran’s time, she said, you couldn’t see the dark for lights. The whole Hollow lit, like a sky. Now look.” She lifted her thin hand at the dark, where the lights were scattered, far apart, small. “Fewer every year. Fewer children, too. Because there aren’t enough to go round. A child born with no glim—“ She didn’t finish.

“What happens to them?” said Hana. “The lights. Why are there fewer?”

Edda didn’t answer for a long moment.

Then she reached up, slowly, with her thin hand, and touched the iron lantern hanging from the root above her head. Empty. Old. Black with years. The weave in it so fine it looked like lace.

“Moths,” said Edda.

The smith was called Corwen.

He was the biggest man in Lanthorn, which made him about Jared’s height, and broad, and pale like all of them, with the big dark eyes and a short black beard and forearms that were all smith. His forge was in the biggest root of the biggest tree, a hollow as big as Bettris’s shed, and his fire was small. Banked. Down to a red glow in the hearth, under a hood. And the hood was iron, woven, the same knot as the lanterns, so the glow of the fire came out through it in a hundred red points instead of one.

Jared stood in the door of the forge and looked at the hood for a long time.

“You’ll be the smith,” said Corwen. Without turning round. He was at his bench, doing something fine with a pair of pliers and a coil of wire. “Isel said there was a big one with a shield who looked at the lanterns like they were food.”

“Yeah,” said Jared. “That’s me.”

Corwen turned round. He looked at Jared. Then at Jared’s hands. Then he picked up a lantern off the bench, a small one, unfinished, half-woven, and held it out.

Jared took it. He turned it over in his hands, carefully, the way you’d hold a bird. Iron wire, thin as a bootlace, drawn so fine and even he couldn’t see a flaw in it. Woven. Not twisted, not braided: woven, over and under and round, in a pattern that went in and out of itself and came back, so that wherever you looked there was a gap and wherever you looked there was iron.

“The knot,” said Corwen.

“Why?”

Corwen took a light out of the air.

Jared didn’t see where it came from. It was just there, at Corwen’s shoulder, his own, humming, and Corwen held his open hand under it and it came down into his palm like a bird coming to a hand, and he held it out over the half-woven lantern and it went in. Through the gaps. Into the middle of the cage. And sat there.

And it changed. The light that had been one pale glow at Corwen’s shoulder came out of the lantern in a hundred little points. Scattered. Everywhere and nowhere. You could see the lantern was lit. You couldn’t see where the light was.

“A moth sees a light,” said Corwen. “One point. In the dark. It goes to the point. That’s all a moth is. A thing that goes to the point.” He held up the lantern. “The knot makes it a hundred points. Every one too small to be worth it. The moth can’t find the middle. It goes past.”

Jared looked at it.

“That’s—“ He stopped. “Man. That’s armour.”

Corwen looked at him for a long time with his big dark eyes.

“Nobody’s ever called it that,” he said. “It’s just lanterns.”

“It’s armour. It’s a shield that doesn’t stop the hit, it just makes there not be anything to hit.” Jared turned it in his hands. “That’s the best shield I’ve ever seen.”

Corwen didn’t say anything. He took the lantern back. He held it up, and his light came out of it, through the gaps, the way it had gone in, and went back to his shoulder and hung there, humming.

“There’s no door,” said Jared.

“Never shut a lantern.”

“Why?”

Corwen looked at him as if he’d asked why you shouldn’t put your hand in the fire.

“You shut a light in,” he said, “it dies. Every child knows that. The lantern’s for it to come to. When it’s tired, or when the moths are up, or when it wants to. Not for you to put it in.” He hung the lantern back on its hook. “A lantern with a door’s not a lantern. It’s a jar.”

He stayed in the forge for three drips.

Corwen didn’t ask him to. He didn’t ask to stay. He just picked up a coil of wire off the bench, and Corwen looked at him doing it, and said nothing, and handed him a pair of pliers.

It was the hardest thing he’d ever done with iron.

His hands were a smith’s hands. Gabe’s were, and Salty’s. Big and hard and blunt. Good for a hammer. Good for holding a hot bar steady on an anvil while somebody else struck. Good for a shield. He’d drawn Salty’s hundredth nail out into two rings with them, on floor 14, and punched a rose inside each so small Hana had to hold it up to the light to see it. He’d thought that was fine work.

This was finer.

Over and under and round. The wire wouldn’t sit. It sprang back. It kinked. He got six rows in and the whole thing went crooked, and Corwen came and looked at it and said nothing and unpicked it, all six rows, in about a minute, and handed it back.

He started again.

“You’ve got smith’s hands,” said Corwen, on the second drip. Not unkindly. “This wants a weaver’s.”

“Yeah. I know.”

“My mother’s were. She made half the lanterns in Lanthorn.” Corwen nodded at the hundreds of them hanging outside the door, out in the roots, lit and unlit. “I make the wire. And the rings, and the hooks. I was never much at the weave.” He looked at Jared’s crooked rows. “You’re worse.”

Jared laughed. He couldn’t help it. “Man. Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.” Corwen went back to his bench. Then, without turning round: “You’re doing it for somebody.”

Jared stopped.

“How’d you know?”

“You keep looking at the door.” Corwen drew a length of wire through a plate, slowly, so it came out thinner. “Out at the pool. The one with the crown. Every time she goes past.” He didn’t look round. “You do the weave better when you look.”

Jared didn’t say anything for a long time.

The first thing he’d thought, in the white room. The very first thing, before where are we and what is this and who’s that by the wall. He’d opened his eyes on a white floor, in a circle, and she was next to him. Hana. Real. Not a voice on a call at four in the morning his time and five in the afternoon hers. Not a face in a little square. Her, there, on a white floor, close enough to touch.

Oh, he’d thought. I can reach her.

Seven hundred and forty-six days and he’d never once stopped thinking it.

“Yeah,” said Jared. “I’m doing it for somebody.”

He picked up the pliers. He looked out of the door, once, at the pool, at the small figure in the crown sitting with Edda on her root. Then back at the wire.

Over. Under. Round.

It sat.

The moths came on the fourth night.

He was asleep. He was asleep in a root-hollow at the edge of the town, with Milli and Hana and Newish and Aleaka and the goat and Sleet, because the town had given them hollows, all thirty-six, scattered through the roots, and he’d been so tired from three drips of wire that he’d gone out like a lamp.

He woke because the humming stopped.

All of it. All at once. The whole town. He hadn’t known he could hear it until it stopped: two hundred glims humming, in their lanterns and on their people’s pillows, all round the pool, a sound under everything like the sea under a harbour. And then it stopped. Like a breath held.

Then he heard the bells.

Little ones. Iron. Somewhere out in the roots, somebody shaking a hand-bell, quickly, tinktinktinktink, and then another, and another, all round the town. Not loud. Even the bells in Lanthorn weren’t loud. And then, on every walkway and in every root, the lights going into the lanterns. He saw them out of the door of the hollow, all at once: two hundred pale glims leaving two hundred shoulders and pillows and going into the iron, quickly, the way birds go into a hedge when a hawk comes over. And going to a hundred points each. Scattered. The whole town going from lit to lace.

And out in the dark beyond the roots, the strays.

They didn’t have lanterns. They didn’t have anybody. They were drifting in a loose ring round the edge of Lanthorn, where they always were now, round the root-hollow where the goat slept. Twenty, thirty of them. Pale, single, bright.

The brightest things for a mile.

He was on his feet with his shield on his arm before he knew he’d moved. Milli was already in the door with his knives out. Hana was sitting up in the blankets with her ears flat, and the spark at her shoulder had gone—

Not out. Small. But it was shaking. The spark was shaking, very fast, the way a candle flame shakes in a draught.

“Something’s coming,” said Aleaka. On her knees by the goat, with her hand on his neck and her face white. “I can’t hear it. I can’t hear it at all. There’s nothing to— Kindred doesn’t— There’s nothing in it to hear—“

Out in the dark, beyond the strays, something moved.

Grey. Soft. Big. He saw it come out of the dark between two white trunks and into the edge of the strays’ light, and for a moment he didn’t understand what he was looking at. A cloak, he thought. Somebody’s cloak, blowing. Then it opened.

Wings.

A moth. The size of a large dog. Its wings were as wide as he was tall, grey-brown and dusty and soft-looking, patterned with darker rings like eyes, and they opened and closed slowly as it came, without a sound, not a whisper, not a flutter. Its body was thick and furred, grey, the size of a child. Its eyes were two dark round shining things, red-black, like coals that have nearly gone out. And under its head, coiled, something long and dark and thin, like a watch-spring.

It came out of the dark toward the nearest stray.

And unrolled.

The coil went out from under its head like a whip, a metre long, two, and touched the stray. Stuck. And the stray—

It didn’t go out. It went into the moth. Down the thin dark tube, in a pale pulse, like something drunk through a straw, and the stray got smaller as it went, and dimmer, and smaller, and the moth’s furred body lit up from inside, faintly, grey going pale, and then the stray wasn’t there.

Just the moth. Glowing faintly. Folding its long tongue back up.

There was no sound. That was the worst of it. Not a sound in the whole of it.

Behind it, out of the dark, there were more.

He’d fought a lot of things in two years.

Wolves, on two. Rimehounds. The Old Stag. Dust-hounds on four. The things in the Down on nine. Bulls on fourteen. The Cataract. He’d stood in front of most of them with a shield. That was what he did. You stood in front of the thing and you didn’t move and it hit the shield and not the person behind it. He’d never found anything simpler.

The moths didn’t hit.

That was the problem. He understood it in the first ten seconds. He stepped out of the root-hollow with his shield up and Milli beside him and Newish behind, and a moth came at them out of the dark, silent, enormous, wings open. And it didn’t come at him. It went over him. Over his shield, over his head, its soft dusty belly brushing his hair, toward the hollow behind him, toward Hana, toward the shaking spark at her shoulder.

He turned and swung the shield. Edge-first. Into its body. It was like hitting a pillow. It folded round the shield and came off it and kept going, and Milli was there, already, a blur, and his knife went through one grey wing from front to back and the wing tore like wet paper. The moth fell sideways onto the walkway. Its tongue lashed out. Missed Hana by an arm’s length. Stuck to the root beside her head.

Jared brought the shield down on it. Flat. Hard. Again. It stopped moving.

“Inside,” said Milli. To Hana. “Get inside. Get back.”

“I’m not going to hide in a—“

“Hana.”

She went back. Into the hollow. Her ears flat. Her spark shaking.

All round the town, now, people were fighting. He could hear it. Not the moths; the moths made no noise. The people. Gabe’s voice, somewhere across the pool, shouting something. Salty. Calvin. Hako’s classic. The thud of something big hitting a root. And the glims in their lanterns, hundreds of them, scattered to lace, and the moths going past them. Past every one. Through the town. Ignoring the lanterns completely, the way Corwen had said. Going to the points.

To the strays. And to the thirty-five sparks.

“Count,” said Jared. Over his shoulder. “Newish. Count.”

“One,” said Newish, behind him. Instantly. Loud. “Two. Three. One. Two. Three—“

Upbeat. He felt it come in under his feet, the floor under everything, the one, and his body knew where the next beat was before it came. A moth came out of the dark on the left. On the one, he turned. On the two, the shield came round. On the three, the edge went into its head between the eyes, and it went down, and Milli was over it before it hit the walkway.

“One. Two. Three. One—“

They held the door of the hollow. Him and Milli. On the count. The moths came over and he took them on the shield’s edge, not the face, because the face was useless, they just folded round it; the edge, into the body, into the head. And Milli took the wings. Fast. Every one. Front to back. A moth with one wing came down like a dropped coat and Milli was on it.

Five. Six.

Aleaka had her bow. She was in the door of the hollow behind them with the goat pressed against her legs and Sleet in front of her, snarling, and she was shooting, steady, into the dark beyond the strays. She was crying. He could hear her. Nothing to hear, she kept saying, under her breath, between shots. Nothing. There’s nothing in them. Nothing to hear.

Eight.

The strays were going.

He saw it out of the corner of his eye, between moths. Out in the dark at the edge of the town, the ring of strays round the goat’s hollow getting smaller. One at a time. A moth would come out of the dark, silent, and unroll, and a stray would pulse down the tongue and be gone, and the moth would glow, and turn, and look for the next. Twenty. Fifteen.

“There’s too many,” said Milli. Breathing hard. “There’s too many out there, they’re eating them, they’re just—“

And the next one went over his shield before he could turn.

It was bigger than the others. Grey-white. It came over the top of the shield and over Milli and through the door of the hollow without touching either of them, and he heard Hana scream.

He turned.

The moth was on her. Inside the hollow. On top of her, its great soft wings spread over her and half the floor, pinning her down in the blankets, and its tongue was out. Short. Not a whip. Just a few inches. Pressed against the side of her head.

Against her light.

And her light was screaming.

Not the voice. Not the flat calm I am not able. A sound. High, and thin, and wordless, like an animal caught in a trap, like a child, like nothing he’d ever heard come out of anything that wasn’t alive. It was going down the moth’s tongue in pale pulses. Getting smaller. He could see it. The spark at Hana’s ear going smaller, and dimmer, and the moth’s grey body going pale from inside.

Hana was screaming too. She had both hands up, trying to push the moth off, trying to get her hands round her light, round the tongue, and she couldn’t, the wings were over her arms.

“Don’t,” she was screaming. “Don’t, don’t, don’t, DON’T—”

He didn’t think.

He’d never thought, at moments like this. That was what a shield was. You didn’t think. You were already there.

He went through the door of the hollow and put the edge of his shield into the moth’s head, behind the eyes, with everything he had. All his weight. Both hands. It felt like hitting a sack of flour. The head came off the tongue. The tongue came off Hana’s light, snap, like a thread breaking.

The moth reared up off her. Wings spreading. Filling the hollow. He got the shield up between it and her, edge to the ceiling, and it came down on the shield and he held it. Held it. The whole soft dusty weight of it on the shield, on his arms, pushing down, its tongue lashing, looking for the light, looking for the point, he could feel it looking.

“I’ve got it,” he said. Through his teeth. To Hana. To nobody. “I’ve got it, I’ve got it, I’ve got it—“

Milli came in under his arm. Low. Fast. Both knives. Up, into the furred body, under the wings. Once. Twice.

The moth went still on the shield.

He threw it off. Out of the door. Onto the walkway. It lay there with its wings spread, grey-white, the size of a door, glowing faintly from inside with what it had drunk, and then, slowly, not glowing.

He turned round.

Hana was on her knees in the blankets. She had her hands cupped together in front of her face. Close. Like somebody holding water.

In her hands, there was an ember.

Barely. The smallest light he’d ever seen. Not even a spark. A grain of light, in the hollow of her two hands, so faint you could only see it because everything else was dark.

It wasn’t screaming any more. It wasn’t making any sound at all.

“Please,” Hana was saying. To it. Into her hands. Very quietly. Over and over. “Please. Please. I’m here. Please. I’m here.”

Outside, the light changed.

He saw it through the door of the hollow. Out over the pool. A light. Big. Gold. Brighter than anything on the floor. A great warm ball of it, the size of a cart, hanging over the black water in the middle of the ring of trees, so bright it lit the white trunks and the roots and the hundreds of lanterns and threw shadows for the first time since the arch.

Every moth in Lanthorn turned toward it.

He watched them go. All at once. From the walkways, from the roots, from the edge of the dark where the last strays were. Every grey-brown silent shape turning, the way iron filings turn when you bring the magnet near, and lifting, and going. Out over the pool. To the point. To the brightest point there had ever been.

It wasn’t real.

He knew it the moment he saw it, because he’d seen it before. On five, on a ledge, a man with a shield standing up out of red stone. On five, too, on the end of a bed in an inn, an onion. Glamour. Hana’s. A thing that wasn’t there, made to look like it was.

She was still on her knees in the blankets with the ember in her hands. Her eyes were shut. Her face was wet. And her lips were moving, and out over the pool the great false light hung in the dark and burned.

Centre Stage, he thought. Put something in the middle of every eye.

Beguile. Make them love the wrong thing.

She’d done both. On her knees. Without looking. With her own light dying in her hands.

The moths went into it. All of them. Twenty, thirty, more, a great grey cloud of them, silent, closing on the false light from every side, tongues out, unrolling, reaching for it—

And there was nothing there.

They went through it. Into each other. Over the middle of the black pool, a great tangle of grey wings and furred bodies and long dark tongues finding nothing, finding each other, wrapping round each other, sticking, and the whole silent mass of them folding in on itself over the water and coming down.

Into the pool.

The black water took them without a splash. They didn’t come up.

The false light went out.

Hana made a sound and folded forward over her hands, and Milli caught her before her forehead hit the floor.

Ice came up the walkway at a run.

Jared saw him come. He had his sword in one hand and Tuli’s pole in the other and he was grey with moth-dust to the shoulders, and behind him came Thea with the cloak-wrapped lantern held tight against her chest, and Xola, and he stopped in the door of the hollow and looked at Hana and her cupped hands, and Jared saw his face.

He’d seen that face before. On fourteen. At the line. When Ice had looked at the Grey and decided.

But Ice didn’t say anything. He didn’t come in. He stood in the door and looked at the ember in Hana’s hands and his face did the thing and then, slowly, very deliberately, he made it stop.

He turned to Corwen.

Corwen was on the walkway behind him, with a hand-bell in one fist and a hammer in the other, breathing hard.

“If something held up a light,” said Ice. His voice was very level. “Here. In the town. A big one. Bigger than that.” He nodded out at the pool where the false light had been. “And everything came to it. All the moths. Instead of to the strays. Instead of to us.” He was quiet. “Could somebody do that. Next time. Would it help.”

Corwen looked at him for a long time.

“It’d help tonight,” said Corwen. “And then the Moth would come up the Hollow for it.”

“The Moth.”

“Not one of those.” Corwen nodded at the dead thing on the walkway. “The one they come from. The big one. It lives at the bottom of the Hollow where no light goes. It comes up when there’s something bright enough to be worth it.” He looked at Ice. “I’ve seen it once. When I was a boy. Somebody’s house caught fire. It came up for the fire.” He was quiet. “There isn’t a house there now. There isn’t a street.”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“You hold up a light like that,” said Corwen, “you’ll have every moth in the Hollow tonight. And the Moth tomorrow.”

Ice looked at him for a long moment more. Then he nodded. Slowly. The way you put something down.

“Okay,” said Ice. “Not that, then.”

And he turned round and went down on one knee in the door of the hollow, not inside, not close, and just stayed there. With the pole across his knee. Where Hana could see him if she looked up. He didn’t say anything to her. He didn’t need to.

Thea stood behind him with the lantern in her cloak in her arms, and Jared saw her look at it. At the light leaking out of the folds. Then at the dead moth on the walkway. Then at the lantern again.

She held it tighter.

Corwen was looking at it too. At the bundle in her arms. At the amber light coming through the cloth.

“That one,” said Corwen. Very quietly. To nobody. “That’ll bring it up from the bottom.”

He didn’t sleep.

He went back to the forge. Corwen didn’t ask. He came in a while after, and lit the lamp over the bench, a lantern, his own glim in it, and the light came out of the weave in a hundred points across the bench and the wire and Jared’s hands.

He had the half-finished one. His. The crooked one. Six rows, unpicked; twelve rows, unpicked; twenty rows, and those had sat. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he put it down and picked up the wire.

Small. Not a lantern for a hook. Not a lantern for a root. A lantern for a shoulder. The size of a walnut. A little round cage of the knot, so fine it’d weigh nothing, with a tiny ring at the top, so you could hang it from something. From a crown. Beside an iron rose.

Over. Under. Round.

He didn’t think about the wire. He thought about her. About the white room, and four in the morning on a call, and oh, I can reach her. About a light screaming in a voice nobody had taught it. About her on her knees with her hands cupped, saying please, saying I’m here.

He did the weave better when he looked. Corwen had said. So he looked. Not at the door. Inside. At the thing he was making it for. At her. He knew her exactly. He’d known her for years.

The wire sat. Every row.

Corwen came and stood at his shoulder at some point and watched him for a long time. He didn’t say anything. Then he went back to his bench and drew more wire.

It took six drips.

When it was done it was the size of a walnut and it was the best thing he’d ever made. Better than the rings. Better than the roses. You couldn’t see where the iron stopped and the gaps started. He held it up and the light from Corwen’s lantern went through it and came out the other side in a hundred little points.

He went to find her.

She was by the pool. Sitting on Edda’s root, beside Edda, with Milli on her other side and his tail round her ankle. She still had her hands cupped. She’d had them cupped for six drips. Milli said she hadn’t put them down once.

The ember was a little bigger. He could see it, now, without looking hard. A spark again. Not humming. Not doing anything. Just there.

He crouched down in front of her. He held out his hand, palm up, with the little lantern in it.

Hana looked at it. Then at him. Her eyes were red. Her ears were flat.

“Jared,” she said.

“Corwen says you don’t put it in,” said Jared. “You never shut a lantern. It’s not for you to put it in. It’s for it to come to. If it wants.” He was quiet. “It’s got no door. It’s just somewhere to go. Where the moths can’t find the middle of it.”

Hana looked at the little lantern for a long time.

Then, slowly, she opened her hands.

The spark sat in her palm. It didn’t move. He thought for a moment it wouldn’t. He thought it couldn’t; it was too small, too tired, too nearly gone.

Then it lifted.

A little. Out of her palm. It hung in the air between her hands and his. And it went, very slowly, the way the goat had gone up the first of a hundred and twelve steps, to the little iron cage on his palm. And in. Through the gaps. Into the middle.

And sat there.

And the walnut-sized lantern on his hand lit up in a hundred tiny points, pale, scattered, like a handful of the smallest stars anybody had ever caught.

Hana made a sound and put both hands over her mouth.

He stood up. Carefully. He hung the little lantern on her crown. On the front. Beside the iron rose, the ugly one, the first one, from floor 1. He did the ring up with his fingers. He was very gentle about it. His hands were a smith’s hands and he was gentler than he’d ever been.

From inside the little lantern, very faintly, so faintly he had to lean in, came a sound.

A hum.

One low note. The same note he’d heard, he realised, from every glim in Lanthorn. The same note Milli said he’d heard from her shoulder on the first night, at the edge of the camp, humming to a stray.

Not the voice. Never again the voice, he thought, maybe. He didn’t know. Nobody knew. But a hum. Warm. Tired. Alive.

“Thank you,” said Hana. Through her hands.

“Alice.” He crouched down again, in front of her. He didn’t know how to say it. He’d been trying to say it for seven hundred and forty-six days. “First thing I thought. In the white room. I opened my eyes and you were right there, next to me, on the floor. And I thought: oh. I can reach her.”

Hana stared at him.

“That was the first thing,” said Jared. “Before where. Before anything.”

“Idiot,” said Hana. Her voice cracked. She laughed. It came out wet. “You can. You did. You always— You reached me.”

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

She took her hands away from her mouth and put them on either side of his face, and put her forehead against his, crown and all, the little lantern humming between them, and the iron rose digging into his eyebrow, and stayed there.

Beside her, Edda sat on her root with her own tiny glim by her ear, and looked at the little lantern on Hana’s crown for a long time.

“Corwen’s mother,” said Edda. Softly. “She made one like that for me. When I was a girl. After the house fire.” She lifted her thin hand and touched the empty old lantern hanging above her head. “That’s it. That one.” She looked at Jared. “I’d forgotten you could make them that small.”

Corwen was standing at the edge of the pool. He’d come out of the forge behind Jared. He didn’t say anything. He was looking at the thirty-four sparks on thirty-four shoulders, scattered round the pool. Gabe’s, Salty’s, Calvin’s, Newish’s. Thea’s.

“Thirty-four more,” said Corwen.

Jared stood up. He looked round the pool at them. At the sparks. At Gabe, grey with moth-dust, already looking at him, already standing up. At Salty beside him, already rolling up his sleeves.

“Yeah,” said Jared. “I know.”

He looked at his hands. Smith’s hands. Big and blunt.

He’d do the weave better if he looked.

He knew all of them exactly.
