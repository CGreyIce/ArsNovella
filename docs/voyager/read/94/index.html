Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 94

Highcrook

6,960 words · 30 min read

They swept the house for lights on the last morning, and laid the fire.

Nobody decided to. Ice was in the kitchen at the back, wrapping the last of the smoked eel in oilcloth for the stair, when he heard brooms. He came out with his hands still greasy and found the long hall full of people sweeping it. End to end. Under the sleeping shelves, along the walls, out of the corners where the river damp came up through the stilts and grew a fine green fur on everything if you let it. Somebody had opened all the shutters. The morning came in grey off the lake and lay along the clean boards.

Ina was on his knees at the hearth.

He was building the fire the way it had been built when they arrived. Kindling crossed in a square. Split logs leaned over it in a cone. A twist of dry reed tucked in underneath, where a spark would find it. He did it slowly and carefully, the way he did very few things, and when it was finished he sat back on his heels and looked at it.

“Not lighting it,” said Ina, to nobody in particular. “It’s not for us.”

Ice stood in the kitchen doorway with the oilcloth in his hands and looked at it too.

Three hundred and twelve years. A fire laid on a last morning by thirty people going up, and nobody had dared to light it until Ina put his hand in it and gave it his ordinary orange flame. And now there’d be another one. Laid by thirty-six. For whoever came down the falls next, with lights at their shoulders, asking the way up.

He didn’t say anything. He went back into the kitchen and finished the eel.

The floor had put the Cataract back.

Floors did that, if you stayed. The day after they’d killed it, the big pool under the Great Fall had been boiling in the middle again, and the wrens had gone back to the cliffs and sat in rows and wouldn’t land on the shelves. They’d known it would. They’d stayed eighteen days anyway, for the levels, because floor 9 wanted forty and most of them had come out of the big pool at thirty-five. And on the last morning they had to kill it again, because the way up was behind it.

It was different the second time. Ice had known it would be. It didn’t come up for a count, or for names, or for anything. It lay at the bottom of the pool under the white and waited, and Newish threw “one” at nobody for a quarter of an hour, and nothing happened at all.

So they used the raft.

The plan it had beaten the first time. Gabe and Salty on the lines, hauling, and the raft going round and round the pool in the white water, splashing, stupid, a thing that moved in the water. The tower had taught the serpent to hunt a beat, and then it had taught it to ignore one. Nobody had taught it to ignore a raft any more. It came up under it on the third circuit. Hana took her crown off. Jared’s thorn held. It took an hour, and a cracked rib for Azac, and nobody cheered, and afterwards Chris wrote something down for a long time without saying what.

Then they went to say goodbye.

Oarin came to the end of the jetty. He’d brought his stool. He set it down and didn’t sit on it, and looked at them, all thirty-six, with their packs on, in the grey morning.

“Lights going up,” said Oarin, in his deep slow voice. “It has been a long, long time.”

“We laid the fire,” said Ice.

Oarin looked past him at the house on its stilts, with its clean boards and its shutters closed again.

“Good,” he said. And then, after a long time, the way river-folk said everything: “Thank you.”

Behind him the city hung across the last fall in the mist, all its arches and stairs and walkways, all its lamps still lit from the night. Under the second arch, the trip hammer came down. Thud. Four seconds. Thud. Salty was standing very still at the back with one hand on his belt, where a small old hammer hung beside his knife, and he didn’t look at the second arch at all.

Aud was in her doorway on the lowest stair. She was sitting in a chair somebody had carried out for her, wrapped to the chin, with her hands in her lap. She didn’t get up. Yan went over to her with the long pole across his back and crouched down in front of her chair, and they said something to each other that nobody else heard. When he came back his hands were white to the wrist and he didn’t seem to have noticed.

And on the way out along the shore, as they came under the cliff, Ice looked up.

Behind the curtain of the middle fall, a lamp was lit in a cave. He couldn’t see it properly, only a warm smudge of gold through the falling water. He looked at it as he walked. And as he looked at it, it went out.

Then came on again.

“She’s saying goodbye,” said Alea.

“She said come back with a better question.”

“That was goodbye. River-folk.” Alea was quiet for a moment. “Have you got one?”

He thought about thirty people in a winter, a long time ago. Their head counting them every morning on the stair. One of them sitting a little way off on his own, every evening, looking up.

“Not yet,” he said.

The way up was behind the Great Fall.

He’d known that for ten years. A door in the black rock behind the curtain, at the bottom, where the falling water came down so hard and so close that there was a gap behind it the width of a road, full of roaring and cold blown spray. You got to it along the lowest shelf, sideways, with your back to the cliff. In the first run they’d gone along it in silence, thirty-four of them, soaked to the skin, and he hadn’t looked at the water once.

This time Calvin went first, shouting. Nobody could hear a word of it, which didn’t stop him. Then everybody, in a long line, with their backs to the stone, under the white. The noise was so big it wasn’t a noise. It was the inside of your head. Ice went along in the middle, the way he went everywhere now, with Gabe in front of him and Thea behind, and the spray in his face so thick he couldn’t see either of them. He could feel them. Both threads, close as ropes.

On the ledges inside the gap, on every wet shelf of rock behind the falling water, there were wrens.

Hundreds. Small and dark, white bibs, sitting in rows. Not bobbing. As Aleaka came past, they turned their heads, all of them, the way the fish turned in the mornings. One of them came off a ledge and landed on her shoulder. Rough feathers. A greyed bib. A white one in its wing.

She stopped on the ledge with the whole Great Fall roaring past a metre from her face, and put her hand up, and Tuppence walked onto her finger.

Ice couldn’t hear what she said to it. He saw her say it. Then she held her hand out, toward the water, and the old wren looked at her for a long moment with its head on one side, and went. Back into the white. Gone.

Aleaka stood there a little longer. Then she turned round and kept going, and when she came past him at the door her face was wet, but everybody’s was.

“She lives here,” she said. He read it off her mouth more than heard it. “I asked her to come. She said this is where she lives.”

“You can ask again,” said Ice. “Later.”

Aleaka looked at him. Then she nodded, once, and went through the door.

Three hundred and sixty-four steps.

They counted them together. He’d thought, on the stair up from floor 7, that it might only happen once. That it was a thing you did the first time, the way you only clapped for the first landing. But Newish started it at the bottom without asking anybody, one, and by three it was everyone, thirty-six voices in a stone stair in the dark, and nobody missed one, and nobody went quiet.

No gap.

At the top there was a door. Plain, round-topped. Like all of them. And there was a landing in front of it, wide enough for everybody if Salty breathed in, and they stopped there in the dark, the way they did now, because of Higu’s rule.

“Go on, then,” said Higu.

Ice looked at the door.

He’d done this on floor 8. At the top of three hundred and forty steps, he’d told them: a city down the falls, a serpent in the big pool, don’t swim near the big one. Where. Not how. He’d liked doing it. He’d liked it more than he’d expected to, being asked, and being able to answer, and then standing back while thirty-five people he loved worked out the rest of it in ways he’d never have thought of. Chris had called it a firewall. Ice is the documentation.

He looked at the door, and found he didn’t want to open his mouth.

“Dec?” said Gabe.

“It’s a forest,” said Ice.

He heard Chris’s pencil start.

“A big one,” said Ice. “Bigger than you think. Bigger than that. The trees are—you’ll see. There’s no sky. There’s a town in the top of the trees and the people up there let ropes down at dusk.” He stopped. “Get up before dark. Whatever happens. Don’t sleep on the ground.”

“Why not?” said Kei.

“Because the ground grows at night.”

There was a short silence on the landing.

“Grows,” said Chris.

“Grows.”

“Everything grows,” said Kei, reasonably.

“Not like this.”

“And the boss?” said Higu. “The way up? Where is it?”

And there it was.

He’d known it was coming since the bottom of the stair. He’d known it for eighteen days. He stood with his hand on the door and thirty-five people waiting behind him in the dark, and for the first time since they’d stood round a long table in Wendmere with Drew’s rock in the middle, he didn’t know.

“I don’t know,” said Ice.

Nobody said anything.

“It’s down there somewhere. It’s a long way. It’s—“ He stopped again. “I can tell you what’s there. I can’t tell you where. Nothing stays where it was. Not on this floor.”

He felt it go through them. Not a sound. A change in the threads, all of them, at once, like the change in a room when somebody you’d been leaning on moves.

“So the documentation’s out of date,” said Chris, after a moment. Quite gently.

“It was never up to date,” said Ice. “Not on this one.”

“Okay,” said Higu.

He turned his head. She was sitting on her pack on the edge of the landing with her map case on her knees, and she didn’t look worried. She looked the way she looked at a blank page.

“Then I’ll draw what doesn’t move,” said Higu. “Something won’t.”

“The trees don’t,” said Ice. “The big ones.”

“Then I’ll draw the trees.” She shut the case. “Open the door, Ice.”

Gabe hadn’t said anything. Ice could feel him, right behind his shoulder, in the dark. Gabe had heard something in his voice. He always did. He’d heard it and he hadn’t asked, and that was worse, because it meant he’d heard all of it.

“You don’t like this one,” said Gabe. Very low. Just for him.

“No.”

Gabe waited. He didn’t ask why. After a moment he put his hand flat between Ice’s shoulder blades, the way he had at the broken gap on floor 8. Just put it there.

“Okay,” said Gabe. “Open the door, Dec.”

Ice opened the door.

It was green.

That was all, for a moment. The door opened onto green. Not a colour, a light. Thick and dim and gold-green, like the light at the bottom of a pond on a summer afternoon, and warm, and wet, and so quiet that after eighteen days of falling water his ears rang with it.

He stepped out.

He was standing in a doorway in a wall. The wall went up. It was brown and grey and ridged in deep folds, ridges as tall as he was, with moss in the bottom of them and ferns growing out of the moss, and it went up, and up, and he tipped his head back and it kept going, until it went into the green and was lost. And it went sideways. Both ways. It curved away from him, very slightly, very slowly, so slowly you’d hardly see it curve. A long way off on each side, it disappeared behind other walls.

It was a tree.

He knew it was. He’d come out of this door before. He still stood there with his head back for a long time.

Behind him the others came out, one at a time, into the green, and stopped. He heard them stop. He heard thirty-five people stop talking. Calvin, who never stopped talking, came out of the door with his mouth open to say something and didn’t say it.

The trees were the size of towers. Not big trees. Not old trees. Towers. Some of them were wider at the foot than the house for lights had been long. They stood far apart, very far apart, and between them the ground went away in every direction under a sea of fern and moss, rising and falling over roots as big as hills. And far, far above, so high that it was like looking up at weather, the branches met. All of them. In a roof of leaves so thick that the light coming down through it came down green, and dim, and in long slanting shafts, gold where they landed, with motes going round and round in them very slowly.

No sky.

On floor 4 there’d been no sky, either. Just red dust, and a dark up there where a sky should have been. This was different. The sky was there. You could feel it. You could see where the light was coming from. There was just a forest in the way.

“Why,” said Calvin, in a whisper, “am I whispering?”

Nobody answered him. Most of them were whispering too.

Bee had her sketchbook out, and her charcoal in her hand, and she wasn’t drawing. She was just standing there holding it. Somewhere behind Ice, John made a mote, and it came up off his palm into the green air, small and white, and he looked at it and then at the tree, and laughed under his breath, because it looked like a grain of salt.

And Maigo had walked off the path.

Not far. Two steps. Into the ferns at the foot of the great root beside the door, which were taller than he was. He was standing in them with his hand on one of the fronds. Just holding it, between finger and thumb, the way you’d hold somebody’s wrist to feel their pulse.

“Maigo,” said Chris.

“Listen,” said Maigo.

They listened.

At first there was nothing. Then there was. A creak. Very soft. Like a house settling at night. And then another, somewhere else, and a tick, like a dry twig, and a long slow creak, and more of them, all round, everywhere, very faint, never stopping.

“It’s growing,” said Maigo.

Kei pushed in beside him and looked at the frond in his fingers. At the tip of it, where it was still curled up tight like the top of a violin, the curl moved. They all saw it. Slowly. Like a hand opening in its sleep. It unrolled a finger’s width while they watched, and stopped, and sat there.

“That’s not possible,” said Kei.

“It’s just fast,” said Maigo.

He let go of the fern. He stood there for a moment longer in the green, with his eyes half shut, the way he’d stood on floor 3 in the first warm rain after a winter of being half asleep. Then he came back to the path.

“It’s awake,” he said, to nobody. And didn’t say anything else for an hour.

They walked all day.

There was a path from the door. It was only a gap in the ferns, where the moss was flat and the roots had been worn smooth, and it went the way Ice wanted to go for nearly an hour. Then it forked. And forked again. And went round a fallen tree so big that it took the whole of the middle of the day to get round the end of it, under its roots, which stood up out of the ground behind it in a great wall of earth and stone like a cliff. And on the other side, the path wasn’t there.

“Which way?” said Drew.

Ice looked up.

That was how you did it. He remembered that. You didn’t look at the ground on floor 9, because the ground would lie to you by tomorrow. You looked up. The big trees didn’t move. And a long way ahead, so far off that it was blue with distance in the green air, there was a group of them standing together. Bigger than the rest. Much bigger. Seven of them, in a ring, leaning very slightly toward each other at the top, as if they’d grown up talking.

“There,” said Ice. “The big ones. In the ring.”

“That’s miles,” said Calvin.

“Yeah.”

“We’ll be there by dark?”

“We’d better be,” said Ice. And then, because Drew was looking at him: “We will.”

Calvin went first. He always did. He went ahead into the ferns with his hands moving and his mouth moving, talking, just loud enough, look at my back, look at my back, and the air went fast behind him the way it did, the way it had in the Fen on floor 3, so that everyone who walked in his wake walked a little lighter and a little quicker without noticing. Slipstream. Nobody gets left. Ice watched his back go away ahead of them into the green and kept his eyes on it.

They didn’t have to cut much. That was lucky. Hako took his short blade to a curtain of hanging fern at a narrow place between two roots, and they went through, all of them, one at a time. And Ice was last but two, with Thea behind him, and when he went through the gap he looked down.

In the stump of the fern Hako had cut, at the bottom, a new frond was coming up. Already. Curled tight. Green. A hand’s height.

“Don’t cut it if you don’t have to,” said Ice. Not loudly.

Nobody asked him how he knew. They hadn’t asked him how he knew things for a long time now. Hako looked back at the fern and then at his blade, and put it away.

He didn’t like that they didn’t ask. He’d thought he would. He’d thought it would be easier. It just meant that every time he said a thing like that, thirty-five people quietly wrote it down somewhere, and believed him, and went on.

There was a place on this floor he didn’t think about.

He’d known that since the stair. Since before the stair. Since a long table in Wendmere, and Drew’s rock, and the word floor 9 in chalk. He’d been not thinking about it so hard and so carefully for eighteen days that it had worn a groove in him, the way a rope wears a groove in a post. He didn’t think about it now.

He thought about the first time instead. The part he could stand.

Thirty-four of them had come out of that door. Not thirty-six. Rachael and Dennis were under the red stone on floor 4, and Calvin had one arm, and walked at the back now, and never went first, and hardly talked. Nobody had said why am I whispering. They’d all just whispered. Ice had walked in front, because he was a head, with Deston and Gabe and Xav behind him, his three, and he hadn’t looked up at the trees once. He’d looked at the ground. At the path. Where to put his feet. He’d been twenty-six and had walked in front of thirty-three people up four floors since the last two graves, and he looked at the ground a lot in those days.

He looked up now. He couldn’t help it.

It was so beautiful.

“Your foot’s not tapping,” said Alea. “Your jaw is, though.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re grinding your teeth. You’ve been grinding them since the landing.” She was quiet for a while. “Is it here?”

He didn’t answer.

“It’s here,” said Alea.

“Later,” said Ice.

“All right,” said Alea. And then, very quietly, the way she said things she meant: “I’m here, too.”

The dark came up from the ground.

That was the strange thing about it. He’d forgotten. On every other floor dark came down. Here, under a roof of leaves that never let the sky through, the light only ever came from high up, so when it went, it went from the bottom first. The ferns went grey. Then the roots. Then the dark filled the low places between them, like water rising, and rose, and the trunks of the great trees went dim from the foot upward, until only their tops were still lit, far above, gold-green, like the tops of mountains at sunset.

They were under the ring of seven by then. Right under it. The trunks stood round them in a wide circle, so huge and so far apart that they were more like weather than trees. And there was nothing here. No ropes. No town. Just a wide flat space of moss in the middle of the seven, under the dark, and the creaking getting louder.

That was the other thing about the dark.

All day, the creaking had been faint. Something you only heard if you stopped and listened. As the light went, it came up. It came up all round them, out of the ferns, out of the moss, out of the roots, until it wasn’t creaking any more. It was ticking. Thousands of small dry sounds, like a house full of clocks. Like rain starting. At the edge of the moss, where the ferns began, the ferns were moving. Not in a wind. There wasn’t any wind. They were lifting. Slowly. Unrolling. Leaning in.

“Ice,” said Drew, very calmly.

“I know.”

“Is this the bit where they let the ropes down?”

“Yeah.”

“They’re not letting the ropes down.”

“I know.”

He stood in the middle of the moss with thirty-five people round him and the dark coming up to his knees and looked up into the seven trees, at the last of the gold going out of the leaves at the top. In the first run, the ropes had come down at dusk. He knew they had. He remembered standing here, in exactly this place, with his three behind him and thirty more behind them, too tired to be frightened, and the ropes had come down out of the dark like rain.

And the tower changed what he’d done the old way. Every floor. He’d thought, on the stair, I didn’t do anything here, the ropes were theirs, it can’t take the ropes. He’d thought it very firmly. He thought it now and it sounded thin.

“Close up,” said Ice. “Everybody. Close together. Backs in.”

They did it fast. They’d got good at it. Tanks out, shields up, healers in, everyone else in the gaps. Thirty-six people in a tight ring in the middle of the moss in the dark, and outside it, all round, at the edge of John’s motes, the ferns leaning in, unrolling, ticking, growing.

A fern touched Salty’s boot. He looked down at it.

“It’s—“ said Salty.

Somewhere far above them, in the dark, someone whistled.

Two notes. High, then low. Like a bird, but not a bird. And from somewhere else up there, another whistle answered it. And another. Three notes, rising. And then a voice, a long way up, small and sharp and quick, shouting down.

“WALKERS!”

And the ropes came down.

They came out of the dark like rain. Dozens of them, falling, uncoiling, slapping down onto the moss all round the ring. And on the end of every one, swinging, there was a light. A jar. A glass jar the size of a fist, tied to the rope’s end, and inside each jar a lump of rotten wood glowing a soft cold blue-green, like moonlight through water. They came down all round the thirty-six in a ring of their own, dozens of small cold lights, and lay on the moss, and glowed.

“Foxfire,” said Alea, in a voice of deep satisfaction. “Finally. A floor with taste.”

Ice sat down on a root.

He didn’t mean to. His legs did it. He sat down on the nearest root in the dark with his shield on his knees and put his face in his hands, and Gabe, who was right beside him, didn’t say a single word. He just stood there with his shield up and his back to him, between him and the ferns, until he’d finished.

They went up in baskets.

Big round woven ones, on the ends of the ropes, that took three at a time if the three weren’t Salty. The ropes went up into the dark and over something high above, and somebody up there hauled, fast, and the baskets went up quickly and swinging. Healers first, then ranged, then everyone else. Tanks last. Then Ice.

He’d meant to go last by himself. Gabe had looked at him.

“With someone,” said Gabe. “It’s on the rock.”

So they went up together, in the last basket, him and Gabe and two shields. And as the basket lifted off the moss and turned slowly on its rope, Ice looked down.

Where they’d all been standing in the ring, twenty minutes ago, the moss was gone.

Ferns. Waist high. Unrolled, still unrolling, leaning in over the place where thirty-six people had stood. The ground they’d stood on was already somewhere else. The ring of foxfire jars had been pulled up with the ropes, and the dark down there was total, and in it, everywhere, all round the feet of the seven great trees, the ticking went on.

“Twenty minutes,” said Gabe. He was looking down too.

“Yeah.”

“You knew they’d come,” said Gabe. “The ropes.”

“Yeah.”

“You weren’t sure, though.”

It wasn’t a question. Gabe didn’t make it one. He said it looking down into the dark, in the basket, with his shield on his arm.

“No,” said Ice.

Gabe nodded slowly. He didn’t ask anything else. He watched the dark go away underneath them, and after a while he put his hand on the rim of the basket next to Ice’s, so their knuckles touched, and left it there all the way up.

Highcrook was in the middle of the seven.

That was where the ropes went. Up through the dark of the lower branches, through a layer of leaves so thick it was like coming up through the floor of a house, and out into the middle of the trees, high up, where the seven great trunks leaned in toward each other and their branches crossed and wove and grew into each other. And in all of it, everywhere, there was a town.

Platforms. Wide ones, built out along the great branches, on beams lashed together, with railings of woven twigs. Rope bridges going from tree to tree, dozens of them, swinging very slightly, crossing over and under each other like the threads of a net. And houses. Round ones, woven out of withies like baskets, hanging from the branches on ropes or sitting in the forks like nests, with round doors and round windows, and the light coming out of them blue-green and cold. Foxfire. In jars. Hanging from every railing and every eave and every bridge, hundreds of them, thousands, so that the whole of the middle of the seven trees glowed soft and blue in the dark, like a town under the sea.

And along the platforms, coming to look, quick and quiet, there were people.

They were small. That was the first thing. The tallest of them came up to about Ice’s shoulder, and most of them not that. They were slight and quick, with long arms and long hands, and long fingers that held on to things, and they went barefoot, and their toes were long too and held on to things as well. Their skin was grey and brown, and faintly patterned, in patches and whorls, like lichen on bark. Their hair was a pale grey-green, wispy, in tufts, like the moss that hangs off old trees. And their eyes were big and dark. In the foxfire, when they turned their heads, their eyes shone green, the way a cat’s do at night.

They moved the way birds move. Quick, and then still. Quick, and then still. They came along the railings and the bridges and the branches and stopped and looked at the thirty-six coming up in baskets, and whistled to each other, high soft whistles, and then went still again, staring.

“Oh,” said Bee, very softly, somewhere ahead. “Oh, look at them.”

The rope over the top of the last basket ran round a great wooden drum on the biggest platform. Six of the small people were on the handles of it, hauling, and a seventh was standing beside it, not hauling, with his arms folded.

He was older than the others. Grey in the face, and the moss on his head gone almost white. He had a coil of rope over each shoulder and his forearms were so knotted with muscle that they looked like the roots down below, and on his left hand, he only had two fingers and a thumb. He watched their basket come up level with the edge of the platform and swing in, and he put out the hand with two fingers and caught the rim and stopped it dead.

He looked at Gabe. He looked at Ice. Then he looked past them at all the others, standing about on his platform in the blue light, and his lips moved.

He was counting.

“Thirty-six,” he said, when he’d finished. His voice was low for someone so small, and rough, and quick. “Thirty-six walkers. Good.” He let go of the basket. “Nobody left down.”

“Nobody left down,” said Ice.

“I’m Burr. I’m the ropes.” He looked at Ice with his big shining eyes, sharp, the way you look at someone you’re deciding about. “You knew to stand in the middle of the seven.”

“Yeah.”

“Walkers don’t know that.”

“I was told,” said Ice.

Burr looked at him a moment longer. Then he grunted, and turned away, and said over his shoulder, to all of them: “Listen. I’ll say it once. I say it to everyone who comes up a rope and I say it once.”

They listened.

“Up before dark,” said Burr. “Always. The Down grows in the dark. You saw it. Don’t cut the Grow. Cut it, it grows back twice. You’ll learn. You’ll cut it anyway. Then you’ll learn.” He walked along the line of them in the blue light, quick and then still. “If you’re lost in the Down, stand still. Don’t walk. Everything that walks in the Down at night walks further. Stand still and make a light. We look down all night. We’ll see it. At first light, the rope comes.” He stopped. He looked round at them, all thirty-six, with his eyes shining. “Stand still and make a light. That’s it. That’s all. Walkers never listen.”

Ice heard it the way you hear a song you used to know.

Not the words. The voice. The quick rough low voice, and the way it stopped on light, and the way it said walkers never listen as if it had said it a thousand times and was going to say it a thousand more. He’d heard it before. Here. On this platform, in this blue light, with his three behind him. He’d stood exactly where he was standing now, and listened to exactly this, from exactly this man, and nodded.

And a week later he’d gone down into the Down on his own.

He didn’t think about it. He stood there on the platform with his hands at his sides and very carefully didn’t think about it, and he felt Gabe’s thread, close beside him, turn and look at him. And behind him, a little way off, he felt another one turn as well.

They stayed five days in Highcrook before anything happened.

It was the strangest town he’d ever lived in, in either life. You couldn’t walk anywhere in a straight line. Every street was a branch, and every branch went up or down or round, and if you wanted to get from one side of the town to the other you went over three bridges and up a ladder and along a branch as wide as a road and down through somebody’s porch, and they didn’t mind. The bough-folk didn’t seem to have a word for mine about anything except rope. They ate nuts, and a sort of flat sweet bread made from them, and fruit that grew in the high crowns and tasted like pears that had been told about honey, and grubs, which Hako tried, and then wouldn’t talk about. They slept in their hanging houses in heaps, the whole family in one round woven room, like puppies. They whistled to each other from tree to tree all day, and Aleaka, on the second morning, worked out three of the whistles and whistled one back, and a whole platform of them went still and stared at her and then burst out laughing.

They called the ground the Down. They called the undergrowth the Grow. They called the thirty-six walkers, because walkers walked, and the bough-folk didn’t. Not if they could help it. They went down by day, a few of them, on ropes, to gather, and never far from the rope, and never after the light at the top of the trees started going gold.

“Because of the Grow,” said Burr.

“Because of the sawyers,” said one of his rope-girls, a small fierce one called Tansy with three bright beads in her moss.

Burr gave her a look. She didn’t care.

“You’ll hear them,” she said, to Calvin, who’d asked. “At night. Like somebody sawing wood. A long way off. And then not a long way off.”

Calvin laughed. Then stopped laughing, because she wasn’t.

They levelled in the Down by day. In parties, the way they always had, out from the foot of the seven in different directions, and back to the rope tree by the time the light at the top started to turn. Ice went with a different party every day, because of the rock. Drew had added a line to it, at the bottom, in the blue light, the first night: UP BEFORE DARK. And under that, after a moment’s thought: ALL OF US.

Every morning the Down was different.

Higu nearly lost her mind. She went down the first morning with her board and her pencils and drew everything: every path, every fallen trunk, every fern-gap and stream and hollow between the foot of the seven and a stand of white-barked trees a mile west. It took her all day. It was beautiful. In the morning, she went down the rope with it, and stood at the bottom, and looked at it, and looked at the Down.

The paths had moved. The fern-gaps had closed and opened somewhere else. A stream that had run east now ran round a root that hadn’t been there and came out north. Half the hollows were full. The fallen trunk was still the fallen trunk, but you couldn’t get to the end of it the same way.

Higu stood at the bottom of the rope for a long time.

“Okay,” she said. And went back up, and turned her board over, and started again. And this time she didn’t draw the ground at all. She drew the trees. Only the big ones. Every great trunk she could see from the platforms of Highcrook, as far as the blue distance went, where it stood and how big it was and which way it leaned, and the branches between them where the bough-folk had built their high ways, the long bridges and branch-roads that went out from Highcrook through the middle of the forest to other towns, far off. She drew a map of the ceiling.

“Because it’s the bit that stays,” she said, when Ice found her at it on the third evening. “You said.”

“I said.”

She looked at him sideways. “You didn’t say anything else about this floor.”

“No.”

“You haven’t said anything about this floor for five days.”

He didn’t answer. She didn’t make him. She went back to her map, and he sat beside her on the edge of the platform with his legs over the dark, and watched her draw the trees.

On the fifth night he heard the sawyers.

It was very late. He was on the rota, the midnight-to-two, with Rui Xiang, on the platform at the top of the rope drum. Rui Xiang had his back to a branch and his knees up and was asleep with his eyes open, the way he did, which meant he’d wake up the moment anything moved. Below them, the dark was total. No moon. No stars. No sky. Just the ticking, all the time, coming up out of the Down the way the sound of the falls had come up out of floor 8, so big and constant that you stopped hearing it.

And under it, very far off, a long way to the north, he heard someone sawing wood.

Rrrrrrr. Stop. Rrrrrrr. Slow. Steady. The sound of a saw going through something thick and green. And then, from somewhere else, another one. And the first one again.

He sat very still.

He’d heard that for three nights once. Without stopping. Getting closer every night. He’d heard it until it was the only sound in the world, until he heard it when it wasn’t there, until he’d—

He didn’t think about it.

He sat on the edge of the platform with his arms round his knees and his hands locked together so tight that the scar on his left palm hurt, and listened to the sawyers a long way off in the dark, and didn’t think about it. He was very good at not thinking about things. He’d had ten years.

Somebody sat down next to him.

He knew who it was before she sat. He’d felt her thread come along the bridge from the hanging houses, slow, steady, pointing at him the way it always pointed now. She had the lantern. She set it down on the boards beside her, and its amber light came up warm through the cold blue of the foxfire, and she didn’t say anything at all.

They sat there. The sawing went on, a long way off. Rrrrrrr. Stop.

“That’s the sound,” said Thea, after a while. “Isn’t it.”

He didn’t ask her how she knew. He’d stopped asking her that, too.

“Yeah,” he said.

She didn’t ask anything else. She didn’t ask what it was, or where, or when. She’d promised on a bridge in Evermorrow. What was the first time? Not yet. Okay. She’d meant it. She sat beside him with her hands round her knees, the same as his, and the lantern between them, and listened to it with him. That was all.

It was a great deal.

“Thea,” he said.

“Mm.”

“If I’m ever—“ He stopped.

She waited.

“Nothing,” he said.

“Okay,” said Thea.

And she stayed. She stayed until Rui Xiang woke up properly at two and looked at them both and said Declan in the tone of somebody who’d been awake the whole time and had opinions about it. And then she picked up the lantern and went back along the bridge to the hanging houses, and the amber went away from him through the blue, steady, and he sat there watching it until it went in through a round door and was gone.

North, in the dark, the sawing stopped.

He sat up for a long time after that, listening for it to start again. It didn’t.
