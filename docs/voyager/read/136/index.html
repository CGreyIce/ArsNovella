Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 136

The One Who Knows

5,001 words · 22 min read

His body moved before he did.

That was the first thing. Later, it was the thing he couldn’t stop thinking about. Not the eye, or the neck coming up out of a thousand years of salt, or the plates of it falling and breaking at the foot of the ship like ice going on a lake. His own body. The way it moved.

The long white head turned on the end of its long white neck, all the way, slowly, like a heron turning to look at a fish in the shallows. And the pale eye went along the line of them standing on the white in the noon sun, thirty-six people and six sleds, two miles off. And stopped.

And he’d moved.

One step. Toward her. To his left, where she was, a sled-length away with the lantern. One step, before he’d decided anything, the way his arm turned the shield before he’d seen the tusk. His body putting itself between her and the thing that was looking at her, because that was what it was for. That was what it had been for, for ten years.

“Ice,” said Alea.

In his ear. Not loud. Not frightened. The voice she used for HP, when the number was one she didn’t like.

He stopped.

He stood on the white with one foot in front of the other and his sword not drawn and his whole body pointed at Thea like a needle at north, and he didn’t take the second step.

Thea hadn’t moved at all.

She was standing where she’d been, by the second sled, with the lantern in her hand, looking at the Mast. At the long white head on the end of the long white neck, two miles off, looking back at her. Her face was perfectly still. Calm. The face she wore when she was reading something very fast that she didn’t want anybody to know she was reading.

She didn’t look at him.

He knew she knew he’d moved.

The window hung over everything. Over the white, over the ship, over the two white legs and the hill of salt on top of them. He’d seen a lot of those windows. Eleven floors’ worth in this life and a hundred in the other. He’d never once seen one where the thing in it was looking at somebody else.

“It’s not moving,” said Calvin. Low. Whispering. On the Salt you whispered or you didn’t speak. “Is it? It’s not moving. Why isn’t it moving?”

“It’s stuck,” said Hyvred.

She had her glasses off. She was standing at the front of the line beside Ice with her bare eyes on the Mast and her bow in her hand, not drawn.

“Its legs,” said Hyvred. “They’re in the salt. Grown in. Like Maigo’s roots were. A thousand years of salt up round them to the knee.” She was very quiet. “It’ll have to pull them out. To come.”

“Will it?”

“Yeah,” said Hyvred. “It’s already started.”

He could see it. They all could, once she’d said. Down at the foot of the white legs, where they went into the crust beside the ship’s keel, the salt was cracking. Slowly. A dark line running out across the white from each foot, and then another, like ice on a pond when somebody’s standing on it who shouldn’t be.

And the heart.

He couldn’t see it. Hyvred could. But he felt it. They all did. Through the soles of their boots, through the crust, through the sleds. A long slow heavy thump, like a door shutting very far away.

Maigo, at the back of the line, with one hand on Niko’s shoulder and his foot in nothing, said, “That’s the second one.”

Everybody looked at him.

“In the dip,” said Maigo. “On the third day. It was once an hour. Or slower. I couldn’t tell.” His face was white under the bark. “That’s the second one since it opened its eye. That was about a hundred breaths.”

Nobody said anything.

The long white head hadn’t moved. It was still looking at the middle of the line. At the second sled. At the lantern.

“Thea,” said Higu. Very low. From the aft sled, with the rock. “Walk left. Ten paces. Slowly.”

Thea looked at her.

Then she walked. Left. Along the line, past the third sled, past Hana and Milli, ten paces, slowly, with the lantern in her hand, the way you’d walk past a dog you weren’t sure of.

Two miles off, the long white head turned. Ten paces’ worth. Slowly. Following her.

She stopped. It stopped.

“Ice,” said Higu. “Walk right.”

He walked right. Ten paces. Twenty. Away from Thea. Along the line past Gabe, past Deston’s party, to the end of it.

The head didn’t move.

It stayed where it was. On Thea. On the lantern. Didn’t even glance at him. He stood at the end of the line, alone, the one it had looked at on floor 9 with every fern and briar on the floor, the one it had asked where did you come from at the Gate, twice, and he stood there in the open on the white with his sword at his hip, and it didn’t look at him at all.

Higu wrote something on the rock.

He didn’t need to see what.

They went back.

Higu said so, and nobody argued. Not far. Another mile east, along Gigi’s road, past the last of her green, to the last place where Hyvred said the salt was quiet. Three miles from the Mast. Higu said three wasn’t enough either, and that nowhere was going to be enough, but that you didn’t camp in front of a thing that was looking at you, you camped where you could see it coming.

The head followed the lantern all the way.

He watched it. He walked at the back, on Thea’s left, between her and the north, the way he’d walked for two days. And every time he looked back over his shoulder, the long white head was turned, on the end of its long white neck, a little more. Following. Three miles off now. A white line against the white sky. And at the end of it, the head. And in the head, the eye.

Thea didn’t look back once.

He knew why. He knew exactly why. He’d seen her not look east all night on floor 11, at a light on the Teeth, because it was asking, and if she looked, she’d be telling it something.

She’d been doing it for two floors. And it had seen her anyway.

They set up the still. Because you set up the still. Because on the Salt, whatever was looking at you from three miles away, you needed four casks of water by morning.

Nobody talked about it.

That was the strangest thing. Thirty-six people. You’d have thought they’d have talked about nothing else. But they dug the trough and set the pots and leaned in the shields and Ina knelt in the middle and Winter climbed onto the sled with her arrowhead, and nobody said anything. They kept looking at Thea. Then at the Mast. Then at Ice. Then at each other. And not saying it.

Gabe said it, in the end.

He came and stood by Ice at the edge of the steel tent, with Hearthwall on his back, the three flames on it glowing gold from inside, and looked west at the white line on the white sky.

“Dec,” said Gabe. Low.

“Yeah.”

“Why her?”

Ice didn’t say anything.

“It looked at you,” said Gabe. “On nine. Every fern. Every bit of it. At you, mate. I was there. And at the Gate. Where did you come from. It asked you.” He was quiet. “It’s never looked at anybody else. Not once. Not on any floor. Why her?”

Ice looked at the still. At Thea, on the far side of it, holding Drew’s buckler for Winter, with her face in the white-blue light very calm.

“I don’t know,” he said.

It was nearly true. He didn’t know. Not the whole of it. He knew what she knew, and he knew what the tower had, and he knew what it had done on a step on the Teeth when she’d followed his feet. He didn’t know how much it had seen. He didn’t know what it wanted.

Gabe looked at him for a long time.

“Okay,” said Gabe.

He didn’t ask again. He stood there with his shoulder very close to Ice’s, not touching, and looked at the white line on the white sky, and after a while he said, “Then we stand in front of her,” as if it were obvious, as if it were the end of a sum, and went back to dig.

She told him at midnight.

Not at the still. She waited until the pots were going and the shields were up and the first casks were filling and everyone was where they’d be till dawn, and then she came and found him where he was sitting on the end of the last sled, not going out. He hadn’t gone out for three nights. He’d sat on the salt between her and the dark.

She sat down beside him. With the lantern.

She didn’t say anything for a long time.

“Hyvred saw it,” she said.

He turned his head.

“On the night of the spoon,” said Thea. Not looking at him. Looking at the lantern in her lap. “Bee drew you, and in all of them, the salt leaned. Past you. At me. She showed Hyvred, and Hyvred took her glasses off.” She swallowed. “Dust. On the salt. Like on nine. On the Grow. Gathering round me. Like something with its face to a window.”

He didn’t say anything.

“They told me the next day,” said Thea. “At the still. Eight days ago.” She paused. “I asked them not to tell you.”

He felt it go through him. Cold, first, the way it always was. And then hot.

“Eight days,” he said.

“I wanted—“

“Eight days, Thea.”

“I wanted to have a plan.” She did look at him then. Her face was very white and very steady. “I wanted to come to you and say it’s looking at me and this is what we’re going to do in the same breath. Because I know you. You can’t hear the first one on its own. You hear it’s looking at her and you stand up. You stand up and you go and stand in front of me, and you don’t sit down again. Not for the rest of the floor. Not for the rest of any of them.”

“That’s not—“

“You moved,” said Thea.

He stopped.

“At noon,” she said. “When it looked at me. You took one step. I didn’t see it. I didn’t have to.” She looked down at the lantern. “On the Patience you took one step and stopped. Tiger said. And today you took one step and stopped. And I’ve been sitting here for eight days thinking, what if he doesn’t stop. What if it’s the second step. What if it’s me that makes him take it.”

He didn’t say anything.

He couldn’t.

He sat on the end of the sled with his hands between his knees and looked at the salt. And he thought about a black stone floor, a long way up, and the last ten seconds of everything. A wall of light, rewritten, with text going across it so fast he couldn’t read it. Her, stepping round him. He’d told her. On a rock by a tarn under the aurora. All of it. She knew exactly what it looked like.

“It’s not looking at me,” he said. Very quietly. “Is it.”

“No.”

“It’s looking at the one who knows.”

Thea didn’t answer.

He put his hand inside his vest.

He didn’t know why. He hadn’t meant to. His hand did it. On the left side, where the bell was, and the candle from a tower on floor 2, and the blank card from the white room. And Bee’s drawing, folded small, in half and in half again. He took it out and unfolded it on his knee, in the lantern light.

Himself, on his front, on the salt, with a helm in the air and a spoon. Gigi with the spoon. Kei and Hako and Hyvred, small, behind. And at the top of the page, a woman with a lantern, bent over, laughing so hard she couldn’t stand up.

He’d looked at it every night for nine days.

He’d never once looked at the ground.

He looked at it now.

The ridges. The six-sided shapes in the crust that Bee drew without looking, that her hand did on its own. Round him, round Gigi, round the green. Out to the edges of the page. And every one of them, all of them, very slightly, pulled. Stretched. The way a honeycomb looks if you press your thumb into one side of it.

Up. Past him. To the top of the page.

To her.

He sat and looked at it for a long time.

He’d carried it inside his vest for nine days. Next to the card. Over his heart, more or less. And it had been in his hand every night by the still, and he’d looked at his own face in it and thought that’s the face, that’s just you, and laughed. And the whole time, the whole time, the ground in it had been pointing at her. And he hadn’t seen.

“Bee draws what’s there,” said Thea. Very softly.

“Yeah,” said Ice.

He folded it up again. Carefully. Along the creases. He put it back inside his vest, next to the card.

She’s right, said Alea.

She was on the sled beside him. He hadn’t seen her come. Small, tonight. The size of a cat. Pale teal at the edges and almost white at the centre, with her tail round her feet. Sitting on the planks between him and Thea, looking up at him. Thea could see her. Thea had seen her for one second on floor 6, and since the tarn on floor 10 whenever Alea wanted. She couldn’t hear her. She looked at her anyway.

You’d have stood up, said Alea. I’d have watched you do it. I’ve watched you do it for ten years. You’d have stood up at noon and you’d have gone and stood between her and that thing, and you’d have stayed there. Until it came. And then you’d have been in front. On your own. Where it was.

“I know,” said Ice.

Thea looked at him. Then at Alea. She didn’t ask. She’d learned not to, when he talked to the air.

And she’d have stepped round you, said Alea.

He went still.

That’s what it’s got, said Alea. Quietly. Her tail moving, very slightly, on the planks. Two records. Of you. And it’s been reading your memory since the Teeth. It painted a lighthouse out of it. You stepped over a step, and she followed your feet. And it saw her follow. She was looking at Thea now, not at him. So now it’s got two of her too. One who never knew. And one who does. And in one of them, at the end, she steps round you.

He didn’t say anything.

It’s not going to hurt her, said Alea. Not first. It’s going to give her the chance. It’s going to put you in front of something. On your own. And see what she does.

“What’s she saying?” said Thea.

He told her.

All of it. Word for word. The way he’d told her everything else, on a rock by a tarn, once he’d started. He sat on the end of the sled at midnight on the Salt and told her what the fox said, and watched her face while he did, and her face didn’t change at all, not once, which was how he knew how much it was costing her.

When he’d finished, she didn’t say anything for a while.

Across the camp, Ina’s fire burned white-blue under the pots. Tik. Tik. Out on the white, ten metres west, Gabe’s little orange fire sat on the salt by itself, burning on nothing, for somebody who wasn’t out tonight.

“Then I won’t,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“Step round you,” said Thea. “Then I won’t.”

“You can’t promise that.”

It came out of him before he could stop it. He heard it. His own voice, saying her words. On a roof on floor 10, with snow coming down, he’d said don’t step in front of me, promise me, and she’d said I can’t promise that. And then she’d stepped round him at the Gate anyway. And been right.

Thea heard it too. He saw her hear it.

She almost smiled.

“No,” she said. “I can’t.” She looked down at the lantern. “I can’t promise I won’t. If you’re on your own in front of something, and I can see it coming, and nobody else is there—“ She stopped. “I can’t promise that. I won’t lie to you.”

“I know.”

“But I can promise something else.”

He waited.

“I can promise that there won’t be nobody else there,” said Thea. She looked up. Across the camp. At the still, and the shields, and the thirty-four people round it in the white-blue light. At Gabe, digging, with Hearthwall on his back. At Drew, holding his buckler steady for Winter. At Hako and Azac and Jared and Chris and Salty and Chi. “That’s the plan,” said Thea. “That was always the plan. That’s what I spent eight days on. Not me. Them.” She looked at him. “You don’t go in front. I don’t go round. They go in front. Of both of us. All of them. And we let them.”

He looked at the still.

“Let them,” said Ice.

“It’s the hardest thing you’ll ever do,” said Thea. “I know. I’ve watched you. On the Patience. On nine. On the Shoals.” Her mouth went tight. “I’ve watched you learn to stand in the middle for four floors, and every single time you do it, it costs you something, and you never say what.” She held his eyes. “And it’s the hardest thing I’ll ever do. Because I’ve done it. The other thing. I know exactly how. I could do it in my sleep. I’ve dreamed it.” Her voice went, and she kept going through it. “Every night since the tarn. A wall of light. Text going across it so fast I can’t read it. And you in front of something. And me stepping round.”

He took her hand.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have anything to say that was big enough. He just took it, the one that wasn’t holding the lantern, and held it, on the sled, between them, with Alea sitting on the planks beside their two hands looking up at both of them.

Good, said Alea. Very quietly. That’s good. That’s the first time either of you has said it out loud.

“She says that’s good,” said Ice.

“Tell her I know,” said Thea.

Alea’s tail moved.

She knows I know, said Alea, and Ice laughed.

They told the others at dawn.

Not all of it. They couldn’t tell all of it. He couldn’t, and Thea wouldn’t, and they’d agreed that by the still at four in the morning with their hands together and the casks filling, and Alea had said good again, and gone to sleep on his knee.

But they told them what it was looking at.

Higu called them round the aft sled, the way she always did. All of them. Thirty-six people on the salt in the grey light with the first gold coming up behind them in the east, and three miles west, against the paling sky, the white line of a neck, and the head at the end of it, still turned. Still on the lantern.

Thea stood up.

“It’s looking at me,” she said.

Nobody said anything. Everybody had seen.

“I don’t know everything about why,” said Thea. Calmly. Clearly. Just loud enough. “But I know some. For nine floors I’ve been reading under its windows. The lines it doesn’t show. The grey ones. Every floor. I read the light on the Teeth. I’ve been reading it the whole way up.” She looked round at them. “I think it’s noticed. I think it’s noticed somebody’s been reading it. And now it’s reading me back.”

It was true. Every word of it was true.

It just wasn’t all of it.

He watched the faces. The club’s, who’d sat in the round house on floor 6 when she said the word, and knew what she’d been reading under the windows for, and looked at her now with something very careful in their eyes. Gabe’s, who’d learned the theory from Xav on floor 10 and had never once been surprised. Rachael’s, with her arms folded. Deston’s, with his hand on his shirt pocket. Calvin’s. Ina’s, with his mask pushed up, waiting, on his step, not picking at it.

Nobody asked her anything else.

“Hyvred’s seen it,” said Thea. “Bee’s drawn it. On the night of the bubble, the saltmouths didn’t go to the sound. They came to me.” She paused. “It’s going to come. Today, or tomorrow. When its legs are out. And when it comes, it’s going to come for me.”

“No, it’s not,” said Calvin.

Everybody looked at him.

He was standing at the back with his arms folded and his chin up, the way he stood when he was frightened, which was the only time Calvin ever stood still. He had his left fist closed. He’d had it closed, Ice realised, more or less since the sixth night of the still, when Ina had written thirty-five names on his palm.

“It’s not coming for you,” said Calvin. “It’s coming for us. It just doesn’t know it yet.”

Nobody said anything for a moment.

“Yeah,” said Gabe. “That.”

“That’s not how it works,” said Thea.

“It is now,” said Calvin.

Higu was writing on the rock. Ice watched her. She wrote for a long time. She’d wiped the right-hand side clean in the night, he saw, all of the floor-12 TRUE column, HEALING COMES OUT OF THE HEALER and NOTHING GROWS and WATER: FIRE AND FROST, all gone. And the left-hand side too. REMEMBERED. Wiped. The rock was blank.

She wrote across the whole of it. Both sides. Over the chalk line.

THE PRESERVED. THE MAST. WHEN ITS LEGS ARE OUT.

ICE AND THEA: THE MIDDLE.

NOBODY IN FRONT ALONE.

EVERYBODY IN FRONT OF BOTH.

She looked at it. Then she looked at Ice. Then, after a moment, she wrote one more line at the bottom, smaller.

LET THEM.

She held it up. So everybody could see.

“It’s on the rock, Dec,” said Deston.

He didn’t sleep that day.

They didn’t move camp. There was no point. The head followed the lantern wherever it went, and three miles was as good as ten, and Higu wanted to be close enough to see the legs. So they stayed. Ina let the still go out at dawn and slept. Winter slept. Most of them slept, in the morning, in the heat, under cloaks, because whatever was coming was coming, and on the Salt you slept when you could.

He sat on the end of the last sled and watched the Mast.

Thea slept beside him on the salt with her head against his leg and the lantern by her hand. She’d fallen asleep in the middle of saying something. He didn’t know what. He’d watched her stop, mid-word, and her eyes close, and her breathing go long, and he’d sat very still so he wouldn’t wake her.

Three miles off, the long white head stayed turned.

He could see the legs now, if he looked. Hyvred had told everybody where to look. At the foot of each one, where it went into the salt beside the ship, the cracks had spread. A web of them. Dark lines running out across the white for a hundred metres, two hundred, like the cracks round a stone thrown through a window. And every time the heart beat, and he felt it through the sled, through the salt, a long slow heavy thump, the cracks went a little further.

Maigo was counting. Out loud, by the still, very quietly, in his tree voice. That’s eleven. That was sixty breaths.

Faster.

She’s asleep, said Alea. On his knee. Not asleep herself.

“Yeah.”

You should be.

“I know.”

You won’t, though.

“No.”

Alea didn’t say anything for a while. She sat on his knee in the sun with her tail round her feet, small and pale and almost white at the centre, and looked at the Mast with him.

Ten years, she said eventually. I watched you stand in front of things for ten years. Every floor. Every fight. I told you your health. That was all I was, then. A voice that told you your health. She was quiet. I never once saw you let anybody stand in front of you.

“I know.”

Not even her.

“I know.”

Especially not her.

He didn’t say anything.

It’s going to put you in front of something, said Alea. Today. Tomorrow. It’s going to find the one moment where you’re on your own in front of it and she can see it coming. It’s going to make that moment. It’s very good at making moments. Her tail moved. And you’re going to want to stand up. Every bit of you. I know what you’re like. I’ve got ten years of what you’re like. And when it comes, you have to sit down.

“I know.”

Kneel, said Alea.

He looked at her.

Sanctuary, said Alea. It’s what it’s for. Kneel. Where you stand, they’re safe. Where they stand, so are you. She looked up at him with her pale eyes. It’s never said where you stand, they’re safe, and they stand behind you. It says where they stand, so are you. It’s not a wall. You’ve always thought it was a wall. It’s a place. It’s the middle. It’s the place where you’re all standing together.

He looked down at Thea asleep against his leg.

You kneel, said Alea. And you let them stand. And you trust that where they stand, so are you. Her voice went very quiet. That’s the only thing on this floor it hasn’t got a record of. Not in either of them. Not once in ten years.

He sat with that for a long time.

The heart beat. Through the sled. Through the salt. Through him.

That’s twelve, said Maigo, by the still. Fifty breaths.

“Alea,” said Ice.

Mm.

“Is it there yet? The words. The first time.”

Alea was very still on his knee.

No, she said. I told you. I wasn’t anyone yet. I was a voice that told you your health. I logged the fight. I didn’t log the words. A pause. I’ve looked. Every night since the tarn. It’s not there.

“She told me what she’d say,” said Ice. “On the rock. I believe her.” He looked down at Thea, asleep against his leg. “I just. Every time I ask. In case.”

I know, said Alea. Ask anyway. I’ll look anyway.

Out on the white, three miles off, at the foot of the left white leg, a long dark crack opened in the salt with a sound that came to them a long time after, faint, like a branch breaking in frost.

Thea woke.

She sat up. Fast. All at once, the way she did. With her hand already on the lantern. She looked west.

The left leg was moving.

Not walking. Pulling. Up. Out of the salt. Slowly. The whole great white length of it, from the knob halfway up to the foot buried in the crust, shuddering, and the salt round the foot heaving up in plates and cracking and falling away, and the foot coming up out of it, a thousand years of salt coming with it, clinging, breaking off in sheets.

The heart beat. Through everything. Faster.

All round the camp, people were waking. Sitting up. Reaching for things. Looking west.

Thea looked at him.

“Middle,” she said.

“Middle,” said Ice.

He stood up off the sled.

He didn’t go anywhere. He stood exactly where Higu had told him to stand, at the end of the last sled, in the middle of the camp, with Thea on his right and the lamp shield on his arm and his sword in his hand, and he waited, and he watched thirty-four people get up off the salt and come and stand round them.
