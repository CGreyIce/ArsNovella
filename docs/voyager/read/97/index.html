Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 97

Heartwood

5,009 words · 22 min read

It was summer, and Maigo had never been so awake in his life.

He’d thought he knew what awake was. On floor 1, in the endless soft spring, he’d felt fine. On floor 2 he’d found out what the opposite was, when the cold came up through his root-foot and his whole body said shh, not now, and he’d gone quiet for two weeks and let Salty carry him about like a bag of kindling. Every floor since, he’d felt the season in him before he felt it on his skin. Floor 5’s wind. Floor 6’s glass heat, which had made him want to close every pore he had. Floor 8’s autumn, going slowly yellow at the edges.

Floor 9 was summer. Deep, wet, green, enormous summer. And he was a tree.

The leaves on his antlers had doubled in four days. He’d noticed on the second morning in Highcrook, in the round mirror-shell one of the bough-folk kept by her door. Two little branches poking out through the hood of his orange hoodie, the way they always had, but the leaves on them were big now, and dark green, and there were new ones coming. Tiny, pale, curled tight like the ferns in the Down. His bark had gone dark and glossy. His root-foot, the bare one, the one with no shoe, wanted to be in the ground all the time. He slept four hours a night and woke up bright. He talked more. Chris had noticed. He’d heard Chris say it to Winter, low: Maigo’s talking. And Winter had said it’s summer, as if that explained everything, which it did.

And he could hear.

That was the part he hadn’t told anybody yet. Not properly. He’d said it’s growing at the door, on the first day, with a fern between his fingers. He’d said it’s awake. He hadn’t said the rest, because he didn’t have words for it yet, and he didn’t like saying things before he had the words.

The rest was this. There were two sounds on floor 9, and only one of them was the Grow.

The Grow was loud.

Everyone could hear it, if they stopped. The ticking, the creaking, the dry snap of a frond unrolling, all day faint and all night like rain. But Maigo didn’t only hear it with his ears. He heard it with his foot. With his bark. With the sap that went up through him from the root-foot to the leaves on his antlers. And with that, the Grow wasn’t ticking. It was shouting.

MORE. UP. MORE. THERE. MORE.

Not words. Nothing that grew had words. But if it had, those would be them. It was fast and greedy and stupid and it never stopped, and it shouted the same thing all day and all night out of every fern and briar and sapling in the Down. More. Up. There. It didn’t care what was in the way. It didn’t care what it grew over. It didn’t remember yesterday, and it didn’t wonder about tomorrow. It only wanted.

And on the afternoon of the split, when the walls had come up between the parties, it had been different. He’d felt that too. He’d had his hand flat on the moss under the end of the log, and he’d felt the Grow go from shouting to something else. Something worse. Something that wasn’t a plant shouting at all.

It had felt, for about ten seconds, like something wearing a plant.

He hadn’t said that either. He didn’t have the words.

The other sound was underneath.

You had to be very still to hear it. Stiller than people ever got. It was so slow and so deep that it was more like a weather than a sound. It came out of the great trees. Out of their roots, which went down under the Down further than anything, and out of their trunks, which went up past everything, and out of the whole slow weight of them, standing.

The Grow shouted. The great trees didn’t. They were saying something else, so slowly that he’d only caught a little of it. A word a day, maybe. Less.

He’d caught enough to know one thing. The Grow didn’t remember anything.

The great trees remembered everything.

The bough-folk thought he was the most wonderful thing that had ever come up a rope.

He hadn’t expected that. On every other floor, people had looked at him and then looked again, and said nothing, very politely, the way you don’t say anything about somebody’s limp. The river-folk had been kind. The kite-folk had touched his antlers, once, with great care, as if checking they were real. On floor 2, a Larchwick farmer had gone for a pitchfork, and Salty had had to stand in front of him and explain, slowly, that he was a person.

Up here, the children followed him.

Everywhere. In a crowd. Small and quick and barefoot, with their big shining eyes and their moss hair, along the branches behind him, and along the bridges, and up the ladders, whistling to each other. Look. Look at it. It’s walking. The grown-ups weren’t much better. Old bough-folk he’d never met stopped him on bridges to lay their long fingers flat on his arm, on the bark, and hold them there with their eyes shut, as if he were a shrine. One very old man had cried. Burr’s rope-girl, Tansy, had stood in front of him on his first morning with her arms folded and her three bright beads swinging, and looked him up and down.

“Are you a walker,” she’d said, “or a tree?”

“Yes,” said Maigo.

She’d thought about that for a long time. Then she’d nodded, very seriously, as if it were the only right answer, and gone and told everybody.

“They think you’re lucky,” said Chris, on the fourth day, watching a woman touch his antler on a bridge and then run off with her hand held up, delighted, to show someone. “A walking tree. I think you might be a religious event.”

“I’m a guy in a hoodie,” said Maigo.

“You’re a guy in a hoodie with leaves on,” said Chris. He wrote something down. “That’s apparently a lot, here.”

Maigo didn’t mind. He found he didn’t mind at all. He’d spent sixteen days on floor 2 being carried about like a log, and every day since being looked at and then looked away from. He let the children follow him. He let the old ones touch his bark. And in the evenings, when the light at the tops of the trees turned gold and Burr’s people pulled the last ropes up and the whole of Highcrook settled into its hanging houses like birds into a hedge, he went and stood on the platform by the rope drum, by himself, and put his root-foot on the bare wood of the branch, and listened.

Because that was where it was loudest. The slow sound.

The north tree.

He’d felt it on the first night. He’d felt it every night since. And on the night of the split, alone in one of the seven rooms of five with Chris and Niko and Winter and Ina, walking at a light a long way off, he’d felt it even down in the Down, through the roots, from miles away. A long slow ache. Like a bruise you keep pressing.

It came from the foot of the north tree. Where the sawyers came.

He’d seen the place from above. You could, from the drum platform, if you lay on your stomach and looked down through a gap in the lower branches. Burr had shown them on the sixth morning. A long way down, at the foot of the great trunk, in the green dusk, there was a dark place. A notch. Cut into the trunk, low, where the roots began. And it wasn’t a notch. When your eyes got used to it, you could see how big it was. It went in, and in. A cave. Big enough to stand up in. Big enough for a house. The sawyers had been at it for so long that they’d gone into the north tree like a mine, and you could see, even from fifty metres up, the pale wood at the back of it, fresh, where they’d been last night.

Every year they get a hand’s width further in.

He’d felt every hand’s width. That was the thing. Standing on the branch with his root-foot on the bark, he could feel the whole of the north tree, the way you feel the whole of your own body without thinking about it. And at the foot of it, at the bottom of everything, there was a wound that had been opened every night for longer than anybody alive could remember. And every night, it was opened again.

He’d tried to help on the first night. He hadn’t been able to stop himself. He’d put his foot down on the branch, and drunk, and given it away, the way Taproot did. Put your feet down. Drink from what’s under you. Give it away. He’d healed Yan’s hands that way on floor 2, with the warmth out of a great pine. He tried to give some of it back.

The north tree hadn’t taken it.

It wasn’t that it couldn’t. It was that it didn’t notice. He was a cup of water poured on a mountain. The north tree was so big and so old and so slow that his whole Taproot went into it and vanished, like a raindrop in a lake, and nothing in it moved.

So he stopped trying to give it anything. He just listened.

On the evening of the two hundred and twelfth day, someone else was on the drum platform.

An old woman. Older than Burr. Older than anyone he’d seen in Highcrook. Her moss had gone completely white, and so thin you could see the grey-brown of her scalp through it, and she was sitting on the bare wood of the branch with her back against the trunk of the north tree and her cheek against the bark. Just lying against it. Like someone with their ear to a wall. Her eyes were open. They were pale and milky all over, both of them, and they didn’t shine in the foxfire the way the others’ did.

She was blind.

He stopped at the edge of the platform. He didn’t want to disturb her. But she’d heard him. She turned her head on the bark, a little, toward him, without lifting it.

“That’s the walking one,” she said. Her voice was very soft, and dry, like leaves. “Isn’t it.”

“Yeah,” said Maigo. “Sorry. I’ll go.”

“Don’t go.” She patted the branch beside her with one long hand. “Sit. You’re a tree. I’ve been waiting four days for you to come and sit.”

He sat. He didn’t quite know why. He sat down on the branch beside her, with his back to the north tree too, and his root-foot on the wood, and the hood of his hoodie up, and his sunglasses on. And she reached over without looking and put her hand flat on his arm, on his bark, the way the others did. But she didn’t hold it there and close her eyes. She just left it.

“Linden,” she said. “They call me Old Linden. I listen.”

“To what?”

“To him.” She moved her cheek on the bark. “The north tree. I’ve listened to him every day since I was younger than Tansy. Seventy years.” A small breath that might have been a laugh. “I’m very old, for us. I’m the last one. There was always one. A listener. My grandmother before me, and hers before her. Nobody wants it now. The young ones think it’s an old woman sitting on a branch.”

“What does he say?” said Maigo.

She was quiet for a long time.

“The same thing,” said Old Linden. “My whole life. My grandmother’s whole life. Her grandmother’s. The same thing, over and over. Very slowly.” She turned her blind face toward him. “And I don’t know what it is. I never have. None of us did. We hear him say it, and we don’t have the ears for it. We’re too quick.” She patted his arm. “Bough-folk are always too quick.”

“What does it sound like?”

“Like asking,” said Old Linden.

A window came up in front of his face. Small and blue, in the dark, in the foxfire.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked at the north tree. At the bark beside his face. Grey, and deep, ridged in folds as tall as he was, going up into the dark.

“Okay,” said Maigo. To the window, and to the tree, and to the old woman with her hand on his arm. “Okay. I won’t.”

He told his party in the morning. Chris wrote it down. Niko read it over Chris’s shoulder and said a druid one, very quietly, in the voice he used for things in his red book. Winter looked at him for a long moment and then said “How long?”

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “It says don’t hurry it.”

“Then don’t,” said Winter.

Ina had his mask down round his neck on its cord, the way he wore it now, and he was eating nut bread. He swallowed.

“What do you need?” said Ina.

Maigo thought about it.

“Somebody to stop the kids climbing on me,” he said.

He rooted that afternoon.

He’d done it before. Not often. On floor 3, in the warm, to drink. On floor 4, once, in the red dust, which had tasted of nothing and made him feel sick. It was what his body did when it wanted to. It wasn’t a skill. It was just him. He sat down on the branch by the trunk of the north tree, where Old Linden sat, and put his bare root-foot flat on the bark. And let go.

It went down. That was the only way he could say it. The wooden toes on his root-foot went soft, and then thin, and then they weren’t toes, they were roots. Fine ones, white at the tips, as thin as hair. They went out from his foot across the bark and into it, through the cracks, the way water goes into dry ground. Into the north tree. He felt them go. It didn’t hurt. It was like putting your hand into warm water up to the wrist.

And then he slowed down.

That was the hard part. That was the part the window meant. He’d done it every winter of his life in this body, without choosing to. The sap going thick. The days going long. The world going slow and far off and soft, like a sound through a wall. He’d hated it, on floor 2. He’d fought it every morning.

Now he let it come. On purpose. In the middle of summer. He sat on the branch in the green light with his foot in the north tree and let himself go slow. Slower. Slower than he’d ever gone on floor 2. Slow like a tree.

The world went away.

Not all at once. The bough-folk on the bridges went first. Their whistles stretched out long and low, like birds a long way off. Then the light. It went from green to gold to green again, very fast, and he didn’t know if that was a day or a minute. Somebody put something in his hands. He didn’t know who. Somebody said his name, long and slow, Maaaaigo, and he couldn’t answer, because answering was too quick.

And underneath, in the dark, in the slow, the north tree.

It wasn’t words. He’d known it wouldn’t be.

It was more like remembering something yourself. Like the way a smell brings back a room. The north tree didn’t tell him anything. It just let him in, a little way, into the edge of it, and in there it was remembering, all the time, slowly, the way it had been remembering for longer than there had been bough-folk.

Rings.

That was how it remembered. He understood that slowly, over what might have been a day. Every year, a ring. Every ring, a year. Thin ones, for dry years. Fat ones, for wet. A dark scar in one, a hand’s breadth across, where a branch had come down in a storm so long ago that it was a hundred rings in. And the north tree was made of them, all of them, one round another, from the heart of it outward, so that the oldest thing it remembered was in the very middle, and the newest was right under the bark. Right under his roots.

He went in. Not far. He didn’t dare go far. Rings and rings and rings. Rain. Wind. Leaves. A thousand summers that felt like this one. A thousand winters he went quiet through without minding, because the north tree didn’t mind them either.

And fire.

It was a long way in. So far in that he could hardly feel it. But it was there. Once, and again, and again: heat. Not the slow heat of summer. A sudden, terrible, roaring heat, all round the foot of everything, the forest young and open and the sky right there, over the leaves. Fire, running through the Down. And afterwards, in the black, in the ash, in the open light, cones on the ground cracking open, and seeds coming out of them. And the great trees coming up. All of them. The seven. The others. Every one of the great trees on this floor had come up out of ash.

And then the leaves had closed overhead.

He felt it. A ring where the light went green and stayed green. And the fires stopped. No more sky. No more lightning to find the forest. Nothing up there but leaves, and nothing down there but wet, and the Grow coming up in the dark, thick and greedy and shouting, over the ground where the ash had been.

And the north tree had gone on dropping cones.

Every year. He felt every one. Every year, high in the crown, the cones ripened, hard and heavy and shut, and fell. Down through the branches to the Down. And lay there. And never opened. Because there was no fire. There hadn’t been a fire for so long. And the cones lay in the Down until the Grow went over them, and then they were gone.

Every year. A thousand years. More.

He couldn’t feel how long. Trees didn’t count. It was just years, more years than he had words for, of cones falling and not opening, one ring round another.

And then, near the outside, he found the lights.

He nearly missed them. They were so small. A few hundred rings in from the bark, near enough to the outside that he could feel them clearly. Small warm lights, at the foot of the north tree. A ring of them. In the Down. At night.

More than ten. Less than a hundred. Trees didn’t count.

They stayed a little while. A few nights. He couldn’t tell how many. And every morning, the same small sound at the foot of the tree, over and over, regular, like somebody knocking on a door. He didn’t know what it was. Then he did. The north tree didn’t know what counting was. It only knew that every morning, something small at its foot made the same short sounds in the same order, and then went quiet.

They counted every morning on the stair, Sefa had said. As if he was afraid one would be gone.

He felt the lights make a fire.

A small one. At the foot of the north tree, in the Down. Ordinary. Orange. The kind of fire you make at night to sit by. Nothing like the roaring heat at the heart of the rings. But a fire. The first fire in a thousand years.

And a cone had been lying near it.

The north tree had felt it. It had felt the heat go into the cone, slowly, all night. And in the morning, when the lights had gone, the cone had cracked.

He felt it crack. Three hundred and some rings in. A sound like a branch breaking. And one seed had come out of it. Only one. Small and winged, down into the warm ash where the fire had been, in a little patch of the Down where the Grow hadn’t come back yet, because the ground was black.

And it had grown.

He sat on the branch with his foot in the north tree, and he felt it grow. In the rings. Every ring since, from there outward, the north tree had felt it. A small root, in the ground beside its own roots, a little way off. And then not small. Growing. Reaching down beside it into the same deep dark, and reaching up beside it into the same green light. Year after year. Ring after ring.

Its child.

He knew where it was. He’d seen it. Everybody had. A little way to the north of the seven, on its own. Another great tree. Younger than the rest. Thinner. Only as wide at the foot as a house, not as wide as a hall. With a crown that didn’t quite reach the others, and no town in it, and one long bridge going out to it from Highcrook. The bough-folk called it the Youngest. Nobody knew where it had come from. Great trees didn’t come up, Burr had said, not in any story. Except that one had.

The north tree had been watching it for three hundred years.

And every year, since, it had dropped its cones. And none of them had opened. And every year it had gone on asking. The same thing. Over and over. Very slowly. So slowly that a bough-woman could listen to it for seventy years and not know what it was.

He knew.

It wasn’t a word. Nothing that grew had words. It was the memory of something. Of heat, at the foot of the tree, in the night, going slowly into a cone. Of a crack. Remembered every year, ring after ring, held up the way you hold something up to show somebody.

This. Again. Please.

He came back up too fast.

He didn’t mean to. Something pulled him. Something very bright and very sudden and very close, a voice, and his name, and a hand on his shoulder, and the world came back in a rush like a wave, green and loud and much too quick. His roots came out of the north tree all at once and it hurt. Like pulling hair. He gasped and curled up on the branch round his own foot.

“Sorry. Sorry, sorry.” The hand on his shoulder. “Sorry, Maigotron. That was me. Sorry.”

He looked up.

Ice was crouched on the branch in front of him. He was holding a bowl of something that steamed. He was holding it a bit badly, because his left arm didn’t seem to want to go up very far yet, and he was sitting on the branch carefully, the way you sit on something when one of your legs is still stitched together. And his face was worried.

“How long?” said Maigo. His voice came out thick. Like sap.

“Three days.”

He sat there.

“Three days,” he said.

“Chris has been bringing you water every four hours. You drank it.” Ice put the bowl down on the branch beside him. “You didn’t wake up. He wrote it all down. Winter sat up with you two nights running and threw nut shells at the kids. Ina read to you.”

“Read what?”

“His spellbook. Out loud. He said you’d like the rhythm.” Ice looked at him. “I just came to bring you soup. I didn’t mean to—you went white. Well. Pale brown.”

Maigo looked at the bowl. Then at the north tree, going up into the green beside him, enormous. Three days. It had felt like the space between two breaths.

“How’s your leg?” he said.

“Fine.”

“Liar.”

“It’s fine. It hurts. Rachael says a week.” Ice sat down properly on the branch, slowly, with his back to the trunk, beside him, where Old Linden sat. He looked up into the north tree. “I never came up here. Not once. Not to this branch.”

Maigo looked at him sideways.

Ice was looking up into the leaves the way he’d been looking at everything on this floor since the top of the stair. As if it was the first time and it might be the last. As if he were trying to keep it.

“I never listened to them,” said Ice. “The trees. Any of them. I just walked about underneath.”

“You were busy,” said Maigo.

Ice laughed, very quietly, under his breath.

“Yeah,” he said. “I was busy.”

They sat there for a while. Maigo drank the soup. It was onion, and something green from the crowns, and it was very good, and it went down into him like water into dry ground.

“Maigotron,” said Ice, after a while.

“Hi,” said Maigo.

Ice smiled at the leaves.

“What does it say?” he said. “The tree.”

Maigo put the bowl down.

He looked at the north tree. Then down, through the gap in the lower branches, at the dark at the foot of the trunk, fifty metres down, where the sawyers had dug their cave. Then away to the north, through the leaves, at the Youngest, standing on its own with its crown not quite reaching. Three hundred years of watching.

“It says it wants fire,” said Maigo.

Ice turned his head and looked at him.

“It wants fire,” said Maigo again. And he heard his own voice go strange, because he was a tree, and he’d been a tree for two hundred days, and he knew exactly what every tree in every story he’d ever read thought about fire. “It’s been asking for three hundred years. Every year. Linden’s been listening to it her whole life and nobody knew. It’s not asking anybody to heal it. It doesn’t care about the sawyers. It wants—“ He stopped. He put his hands over the sunglasses, which didn’t help. “It wants somebody to light a fire at its foot. Like the last time.”

“The last time,” said Ice.

“There were lights,” said Maigo. “At the bottom. A few nights. Three hundred years ago.” He took his hands away from his face. “They counted every morning. And they made a fire. And a cone opened. And that—“ he pointed, through the leaves, at the Youngest, “—came up.”

Ice was very still.

He didn’t say anything for a long time. He looked at the Youngest through the leaves. Maigo watched him look at it. And then Ice looked down at his own left hand, where it lay on his knee, palm up, with the scar across it.

“The ones before,” said Ice.

“I don’t know,” said Maigo. “Trees don’t count. They don’t know what anybody is. It just knows there were lights, and a fire, and it was warm, and then there was a tree.” He looked at the north tree. “It’s been trying to tell somebody for three hundred years.”

“Well,” said Ice. He took a breath. “Now it has.”

He got up. Slowly, with his bad leg, holding the trunk. He stood on the branch and looked down at Maigo, sitting at the foot of the north tree in his orange hoodie with the leaves on his antlers.

“You should tell Chris,” he said. “He’ll want to write it down.”

“I know.”

“And Ina,” said Ice.

Maigo looked up at him.

“It’s a fire,” said Ice. “At the foot of a tree. In a forest made of wood, under a town made of wood, with people living in it who’ve never seen a flame in their lives.” He almost smiled. “I can tell you where, Maigotron. That’s all I’ve got. It’s here. The how’s yours.” He turned to go along the branch. Then he stopped. “But if it was me, I’d want the one fire on this floor that nothing else can take.”

He went. Slowly, holding the rail.

Maigo sat on the branch, by the north tree, for a long time after. With his hand flat on the bark beside him, not rooted. Just there. The way Old Linden’s hand had been on his arm.

Far down, at the foot of the trunk, in the dark of the cave, he could feel the place where the sawyers had been last night. Raw. Pale. Open.

And all round it, under the moss, under the Grow, under three hundred years of green, he could feel them. Hundreds. Thousands. Hard, and heavy, and shut. Every cone the north tree had ever dropped, lying in the dark where they’d fallen.

Waiting.
