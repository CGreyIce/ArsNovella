Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 23

Except Newish

3,133 words · 14 min read

The light on the mountain was all anybody in Larchwick could talk about, and Newish was not a person who could hear about a mysterious light on a mountain and not go and look at it.

“It’s a beacon,” said the trapper’s wife, setting down the porridge on the eleventh morning. “On the top of the pass. The old tower. It hasn’t been lit since my gran’s gran was a girl.” She glanced out of the small window by the fire, up at the white shoulder of the mountain where, even in daylight, a faint gold spark was visible at the very top. “It went up in the Long Storm, four nights back. Nobody knows who. Old Tallow lives up there, but she can’t lift a log, never mind light that.” She shook her head. “Everyone thought it was a fire. Then it didn’t go out.”

“Four nights back,” said Hana slowly.

“The night before the Old Stag went down,” said the trapper’s wife. “Funny, that.” She went away again.

Newish looked at Hana. Hana looked at Newish. Across the table, Jared put his spoon down.

“We’re going up the mountain,” said Newish.

“We should think about it,” said Milli.

“We should go up the mountain and think about it on the way.”

It was a long way up. It was a very long way up, and it was steep, and it was cold, even in furs. The wind came off the peaks in great freezing shoves that made you stagger, and there were wolves. There were always wolves.

And Newish discovered, about halfway up, that she could make people faster.

She hadn’t been trying to. That was the stupid part. She’d been doing what she always did in a fight, which was talk. Newish talked in fights. She’d talked in every fight in every game she’d ever played. In calls, with Chris and with Ice, she’d talked the whole time: calling things out, making jokes, being rude to the enemy team, being rude to her own team, being rude to the game itself. She’d talked so much that once, in the middle of the last fight of a very long night, Ice had said very calmly into his mic, Newish, I love you, but if you don’t stop talking I’m going to walk off this cliff. And she’d said do it, and he had. On purpose. Just to make Chris laugh. They’d lost, and it had been the best game of the night.

So when the Rimehounds came round them on the slope, white on white, circling, she talked. And because she was a Cleric now and supposed to be helping, she found herself counting.

“Okay, left one’s coming — Jared, one — Milli, two — Leaka, now, three — GO—“

And on go, everyone moved at once.

It wasn’t that they moved because she said so. They moved because the count was there. She could feel it, like a beat under a song: Jared’s shield coming up, Milli’s knives going in, Aleaka’s arrow leaving the string, Hana’s soft voice turning a wolf’s head. All of it landed on the same one. And on that beat, something in her chest went out, along the count, into all of them. And they were faster.

Not a lot. A little. But a little, all together, at the same time, was enough. The circle came apart before it had finished closing.

“Newish,” said Jared afterwards, breathing hard, staring at her. “What did you do?”

“I counted,” said Newish.

“You counted at us.”

“I count at everybody. I’ve been counting at you for ten days.”

“Not like that,” said Hana. She was looking at Newish with her one visible violet eye wide. “That felt like— it felt like everything was on the beat. Like a song.”

Newish opened her window. She wasn’t a window person; she found them a bit smug. But there was a new line, gold at the edges.

Put a beat under them. Everyone’s faster on the one.
]

She read it out loud. Then she read it again.

“I’m a metronome,” she said, outraged. “I’m a support metronome.”

“You’re a very good metronome,” said Aleaka kindly.

“I wanted fireballs! I came through the staff door with my whole chest, I was ready for fireballs, and I’ve got healing and a metronome—“

“You’ve got the best skill in the party,” said Milli. Milli never said things he didn’t mean; it was one of the few things Newish liked about him, along with his face. “Everybody faster at once. That’s huge. That’s the whole fight.”

Newish looked at him. Then at the window.

“Fine,” she said. “But I’m still rude.”

“We’d never ask you to stop,” said Jared.

They saw the tower in the late afternoon.

It stood on the very top of the pass, on a saddle of bare rock between two peaks, so high that the ceiling felt close enough to bang your head on. It was round and grey, and on top of it, in a great black iron cradle, there was a fire.

Not a normal fire. Newish had seen normal fires. This one was gold, pure gold, standing straight up into the wind in a column taller than the tower itself. The gusts coming over the pass, which nearly knocked her off her feet, didn’t move it at all. It didn’t flicker. It didn’t lean. It just burned, as calm and steady as a lamp on a table in a quiet room, at the top of the world.

Everyone stopped and looked at it for a long time.

“That’s not a fire,” said Hana quietly.

“It’s a fire,” said Jared.

“It’s not a normal fire.”

“No,” Jared agreed. “It’s not a normal fire.”

At their feet, Sleet the pup sat down in the snow and looked up at it with her enormous ice-blue eyes, and whined, very softly, the way dogs whine at a door they want to go through.

Beside the tower, half buried in a drift, there was a hut with a crooked chimney. In its one small window, a candle was burning.

The old woman who opened the door was the smallest person Newish had ever seen. She was bent like a question mark, with a face like a walnut that somebody had left in the sun for ninety years, and two very bright black eyes. She looked at the five of them on her doorstep in their furs, and the white wolf pup at Aleaka’s heels. She looked longest at Hana’s crown.

“More of you,” she said. “Two Climbers on my mountain in one week. I’ll need a bigger hut.”

“Hi,” said Newish. “We saw your fire.”

“Everybody’s seen my fire. You can see my fire from three valleys away.” The old woman sniffed. “I’m Mother Tallow. You’re letting the warm out.”

“Sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry, come in or go away.”

They went in.

It was tiny. It was warm. It smelled overwhelmingly of candles, because there were candles everywhere: on every shelf, in every corner, hanging from the rafters in bundles by their wicks, fat and thin and tiny and twisted. There wasn’t room for five people and a wolf. They squeezed in anyway, sitting on the floor and the bench and, in Jared’s case, a barrel, while Mother Tallow put a kettle on and complained about the lot of them.

Newish liked her immediately.

She liked her the way you like someone who’s clearly been rude for ninety years and is very good at it. Within ten minutes they’d had a short, vicious argument about whether candles were better than lamps (Mother Tallow: candles, obviously; Newish: lamps, out of spite), and about whether Climbers were as stupid as they looked (Mother Tallow: yes; Newish: no, stupider). Hana said afterwards that it was like watching two cats meet.

“I like this one,” said Mother Tallow, to Hana, pointing at Newish with the kettle. “She’s got a mouth on her.”

“She does,” said Hana.

“I do,” said Newish, delighted.

It was Hana who asked. Of course it was Hana. Hana had been waiting the whole time, very still on the bench, with the iron rose in her crown and her ears flat, for a gap in the arguing.

“You said two Climbers,” said Hana. “On your mountain. This week. Who was the other one?”

Mother Tallow looked at her for a long moment with her bright black eyes.

“You know who,” she said.

Hana didn’t say anything.

“A young man,” said Mother Tallow. “Tall. Black hair in his eyes. Wolf-fur coat that stank to high heaven, and a shield, and the politest manners I’ve had at this door in eighty years. Came up the day before the Long Storm.” She poured the water into the pot. “Stood in my doorway when I opened it as if he’d forgotten how doors worked.”

Hana went very still.

“That’s what Mother Crane said,” said Aleaka, softly. “In Hearthstead. The first night. Stood in that door like he’d forgotten how doors worked.”

“Then the Crane woman’s got eyes in her head,” said Mother Tallow. “Which surprises me.” She put the lid on the pot. “He said he was staying. I told him the pass was no place to be in the Long Storm. He said he knew, and he was staying anyway. Brought in all my wood. Split it with his sword, if you can believe it, like a barbarian. And when the storm came and my candle went out—“ She stopped.

Nobody said anything.

“I’m ninety-one,” said Mother Tallow. “I’d have sat in that chair.” She nodded at a chair by the hearth, with a blanket folded over its arm. “I’d have sat down in that chair with my fire out and my candle out, and I wouldn’t have got up again. I knew it. I’d felt it in my knees all week.”

“But you didn’t,” said Newish.

“He picked me up,” said Mother Tallow. “Like a sack of flour. Carried me up those stairs into my own tower. I shouted at him the whole way, and he didn’t listen to a word. And then he put his hand on the wet wood my grandmother’s grandmother’s beacon was full of, and the tower went up.” She sat down slowly on the bench beside Hana. “And then he went down and sat in the door all night. With the wolves. So they wouldn’t come up the stair.”

The hut was very quiet.

“All night?” said Jared.

“All night. I sat up top with the fire and sang to keep myself awake, and he sat in the door. In the morning there were wolf tracks halfway to Larchwick.” She looked at her hands. “He came down at dawn and sat on my threshold as if his legs had given up on him. And I asked him his name.”

“What did he say?” said Hana. Her voice was very quiet.

“Voyager,” said Mother Tallow.

Hana closed her eye.

“I told him that’s not a name,” said Mother Tallow. “That’s a job. And he said yes, it is.” She shook her head. “Like it was the saddest joke he’d ever told.”

Nobody said much after that.

They drank Mother Tallow’s tea, which was terrible and very hot. They sat in the hut full of candles, and listened to the wind, and watched the gold light of the beacon come in through the gaps in the shutters and lie across the floor. Sleet fell asleep in Aleaka’s lap. Milli held Hana’s hand. Jared turned an iron rose over and over in his fingers: a third one, made at Ulla Dray’s forge after the first two were given away.

Newish sat on the floor by the hearth and thought about the last time she’d heard Ice’s voice.

She hadn’t known him long. Two or three months, at most. Chris had brought him into their calls, and then he’d just been there, every night, the third voice in the call. Quick and funny and good, and generous in the way where he’d make a stupid play on purpose to give you a better one, and then never mention it. She’d liked him straight away. She’d been planning, in the vague way you plan things, to get to know him properly. To ask him about Malacca, and about whoever he was when he wasn’t making everybody laugh.

He’d had a thing he said at the end of every call. Every single one. When he was logging off.

Love you guys. Except Newish.

And she’d always said rude, and he’d always said joking, mostly, trust. And then he’d leave before she could get the last word in, because he liked having the last word. Every night. It had become a thing. Chris used to wait for it.

She hadn’t thought about it once since the white room. She was thinking about it now, sitting on Mother Tallow’s floor in a hut at the top of the world. Somewhere above them, on a floor none of them had seen, a man who’d carried an old woman up a tower and sat in a door all night with wolves was walking around on his own with a name that was a job.

Except Newish.

She found, to her great annoyance, that her eyes were stinging.

“It’s the smoke,” she said, to nobody. “It’s very smoky in here.”

“It’s a candle-maker’s hut,” said Mother Tallow. “Of course it’s smoky.”

“I’m just saying.”

Late that night, when the others were asleep on Mother Tallow’s floor in a heap of furs and candles, Newish woke up cold and saw that Hana’s place was empty.

She found her at the tower.

The wind had dropped. The beacon was burning straight up into the dark, gold and silent, lighting the snow round the tower like a street lamp. Hana was sitting in the tower door. Not standing in it. Sitting on the worn stone threshold with her back against one side of the frame and her boots against the other, her fur pulled tight round her, her crown in her lap.

Newish stopped a few metres away. She didn’t say anything. She’d known Hana for a long time, from chat and calls and the long, comfortable nights where nobody was really doing anything. She knew when not to say anything.

The stone round the doorway was scored. Newish saw it in the beacon light: long pale scratches gouged into the grey, at knee height, at waist height, all round the frame. Claw marks. Dozens of them. Something had come at this door, over and over, for hours, and not got through.

There were other marks too. Lower down, on the threshold itself, where Hana was sitting: two smooth, faint hollows in the snow-damp stone, about a shoulder-width apart, where somebody’s boots had been set and had stayed set, all night, pushing.

Hana had put her own boots in them.

She sat there in the door of the tower with her boots where his had been. She was looking out at the dark pass, the way he must have looked out at it, with the wolves coming up out of the white. She wasn’t crying. Newish had half expected her to be. She just sat there very still, with her one visible eye open, looking at the dark.

After a while, without turning her head, Hana said, “He sat here all night.”

“Yeah,” said Newish.

“On his own.”

“Yeah.”

“She’d have died, Newish. She said so. She’d have sat down in that chair.” Hana’s voice was very level. “And he knew. He came up a day early and he stayed.”

Newish didn’t say anything. There wasn’t anything to say.

“I’m not scared any more,” said Hana. “I decided that two nights ago, in Larchwick. I’m not scared of whether it’s him.” She put her hand flat on the scratched stone beside her. “I’m just scared for him now. That’s different.”

Newish came and sat down in the snow beside the door, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. She didn’t say anything rude. It was, she’d think later, possibly the first time in her life.

They sat there until the cold drove them in.

Mother Tallow gave them each a candle when they left, the next morning. One each, small and pale and smooth, pressed into their hands at the door.

“For the road,” she said. “In case nobody’s keeping one for you.” She looked at Hana, last, the longest. “He’s got one too. I gave him my best.”

“Thank you,” said Hana.

“Don’t thank me. Bring him back down here one day so I can shout at him for splitting wood with a sword.” Mother Tallow folded her arms. “I’ll keep the tower lit. It doesn’t need me, it’ll burn on its own till the end of the world by the look of it. But I’ll keep it anyway. Somebody keeps a light.” She sniffed. “For him to see, when he comes back down.”

“If he comes back down,” said Milli quietly.

Mother Tallow looked at him with her bright black eyes.

“Everyone comes back down eventually,” she said. “Didn’t the Crane woman tell you that? Gran used to say it. They go up, and they come back down.” She turned away into the hut. “Go on. Get off my mountain.”

They went down the pass in the morning light, in a line, Jared at the front and Newish at the back. Halfway down, at the last place where you could still see the tower if you looked back, Newish stopped.

She turned round. She looked up at the gold column of light standing on the top of the world. Then she looked past it, up, at the low grey ceiling of floor 2, and past that at whatever was beyond it. At floor 3, where a name sat at level ten on its own and didn’t move.

She cupped her hands round her mouth.

“HEY. VOYAGER.”

The wind took it. The mountain threw it back at her, VOYAGER, Voyager, voyager, going down the pass in a long fading echo.

“YOU’RE RUDE,” yelled Newish.

The echo took that too.

Then she turned round and went on down the mountain after the others. If anybody had asked her, she’d have told them it was the wind making her eyes water. Nobody asked. They were all looking straight ahead, very carefully, and not at her.
