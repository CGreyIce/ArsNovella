Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 193

Bring It Back

6,414 words · 28 min read

A hundred and twelve steps.

He’d counted them once, going up, on the morning of the second day, alone, with a cracked stone sheep fading behind him and the whole of the Spire above. He’d counted them out loud, for Gabe, who wasn’t there. He’d done it on every stair since. Newish did it now, at the front of the line, in her clear flat voice that never once lost its place, and he let her. He didn’t need to. He knew this one in his feet. Steep and narrow and straight down through the hill, the steps worn in the middle by everybody who had ever gone up them, which was, as far as anybody on floor 1 knew, nobody, for three hundred years, and then thirty-six people in two days.

“A hundred and ten,” said Newish. “A hundred and eleven.”

Thea’s hand was in his. It had been in his since the top. Her thumb moved once across his knuckles, the way it did on the last few steps of every stair, as if she were checking he was still there.

“A hundred and twelve,” said Newish. “Floor one. That’s all of them.” She stopped. “That’s all of them. We’re at the bottom.”

Nobody said anything for a moment, in the dark at the foot of the stair.

Then Tiger put his hand flat on the door, and held it there, and pushed.

The round room in the hill was the same.

Small and dry, with the low stone bench round the wall and the fire-pit in the middle and a stack of wood beside it that somebody had cut and left. He’d slept here on the first night, on the bench, with the fire going and Alea curled on his chest and the door open on the Fold, the morning after the Bellwether. The fire had been ash when he woke. He’d gone to the foot of the stair and looked up into the dark and thought about thirty-five people who’d climb it together on their third morning, the first time, because it had taken them two days to find the Fold.

He stood in the middle of it now with thirty-five people trying to fit in behind him and the door open on the morning.

Floor 1, said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. We’re home.

He didn’t answer. He didn’t trust his voice.

Higu sat down on the bench, because there was a bench, and took the rock out of her pack.

It was full now. Twelve short lines down the left-hand side, one for every stair. GULL ISLAND beside the ninth. THE FIRST SQUARE and SIX WEEKS. NOBODY TALKED. beside the tenth. TWO WEEKS. IN CIRCLES. NO LANTERN. and THE FOURTH NIGHT. FOUR TRIES. A CHAIR. The middle column was a list of true things in Higu’s square capitals: HOT. WIND. CALVIN: TALKING. THERE’S A LANTERN. COLD. And the right-hand column, the NOW, was so full she’d started writing it sideways up the edge.

She drew the thirteenth line. Short and straight. The last one.

“Ice,” said Higu. “What do you remember?”

He looked out of the door. At the Fold, the long green bowl of cropped grass going down from the hill, grey-white with frost this morning, and the sheep on it, and the cracked grey stone in the middle where a ram the size of a cart had once stood and charged at a man with a barrel lid. Down past the Fold, the valley. The stream with its stone bridge. The fields, bare and brown, with frost in the furrows. And beyond the fields, small and red and smoking, Hearthstead. Red roofs. The white tower. The mill on the river, turning.

“Everything,” said Ice.

Higu waited. She was good at waiting now. They all were.

“Onions,” said Ice. “The fourth morning. She caught me in her kitchen trying to help, and instead of throwing me out she gave me a knife and a sack. Well, go on, then, if you’re going to stand there.” He was quiet. “And she called me Ice. For a month. Ice, love. Pass the salt, Ice. She never called me anything else.”

Higu wrote it. EVERYTHING. And under it, small: ONIONS. THE FOURTH MORNING. She didn’t write the rest. She looked at him over the rock for a moment, and then she drew the line under it, and looked up for the middle.

“I’m hungry,” said Calvin.

He said it from the back, wedged against the wall between Rachael and the door, with total sincerity. Rachael hit him.

“What? I am. We’ve been walking for eighty days. I’ve been thinking about her porridge since the Glass.”

“That’s true,” said Higu.

CALVIN: HUNGRY. In the middle column, in capitals. She underlined it twice, and somebody laughed, and then everybody did, the whole round room in the hill full of thirty-six people laughing at the bottom of the tower, and Ice stood in the middle of it with Thea’s hand in his and laughed too, and found that his eyes were wet, and didn’t mind.

The sky was painted.

He’d forgotten that. Not forgotten. Stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing wallpaper. But he’d been on floors with real skies for four hundred days now, skies with weather that came from somewhere and went somewhere else, and the moment he stepped out of the hill onto the frost of the Fold he saw it again with new eyes: the low smooth grey of it, all one colour, like a ceiling. The clouds moving across it all at exactly the same speed, in exactly the same direction, like a picture being slowly pulled along behind a frame. Somewhere up behind the grey, he knew, the sun was on its rail.

And it was snowing. A little. Small dry flakes, coming down in rows. Actual rows. Evenly spaced, all the same size, all falling at the same speed, so that if you looked at it properly it was like looking at a pattern on cloth.

“Oh,” said Hyvred, behind him. She’d seen it too. “It’s—“ She put her hand out and caught one. “It’s pretend.”

“It’s floor 1,” said Higu.

“I didn’t notice. The first time. I thought it was just snow.”

“So did everybody.” Higu had her head back, looking at it. “You can’t see it’s painted till you’ve seen the real thing.”

Ice looked at the snow coming down in its rows over the Fold, and the frost on the grass, and the sheep, and the cracked stone, and the valley below with the red roofs of Hearthstead small and smoking at the bottom of it. Painted, all of it. Small and the same. The floor where the Spire was kind, where it still wanted you to win. He’d hated it, the first time, a little, by the end of the first month. It was too small. It was too easy. It was a nursery with a sky like a ceiling.

He’d have given anything, for ten years, to be standing in it again.

They went down through the Fold in a long line, through the sheep, past the cracked stone. Calvin stopped at the stone and patted it and said, “Hi, sheep,” and Rachael pulled him along by the collar.

The Bridge Goat was under its bridge.

It was the first thing that came out to meet them. They came down the stream path from the Fold toward the valley, the way everybody did, and there was the little stone bridge over the stream, humped and mossy, and there under it, in the dark of the arch, something moved. A shape. Grey and shaggy, with a long beard and a pair of curled horns, one of them chipped at the tip.

It came out from under the bridge and stood on the path and looked at them.

It was old. That was what he saw first. It had been a young goat, on the first day, the morning he’d come down this path alone at dawn with no weapon because the class hall wasn’t open yet, and it had come out from under this bridge and put its head down at him, and he’d punched it. Eleven times. It had been Lv 2. He’d been Lv 1, and stubborn, and in a hurry, and frightened of everything in a way he hadn’t let himself feel yet. Its muzzle was grey now. Its back had gone a bit swayed. It stood on the path in the painted snow on its four thin legs and looked at thirty-six people coming down toward it, and didn’t put its head down at anybody.

It looked at Aleaka.

Aleaka stopped. Sleet stopped beside her and sat down and sighed, the sigh of a dog who knew exactly what was about to happen and had made his peace with it.

“Hello,” said Aleaka, slowly. “You waited.”

The goat looked at her a long time. Then it turned round and walked back under its bridge, and lay down in the dark of the arch, and went on looking at her from there.

“It’s followed her since day one,” said Hana, to nobody in particular. “Not close. Just—it’s always there. On the edge of things. Like it’s keeping an eye on her.” She looked at Ice. “You punched it.”

“Eleven times.”

“Why?”

He thought about it. The honest answer was that it had been between him and the town, and he’d had an hour, and thirty-five people coming, and a woman in a kitchen at the bottom of a crooked street who didn’t know him yet and wasn’t going to live to the spring.

“It was in the way,” he said.

Aleaka looked at him over her shoulder.

“It’s not in the way now,” she said. And she went on down the path, past the bridge, and the goat watched her go, and didn’t get up. But when they were a hundred metres down the path toward the fields, Ice looked back, and it was standing on the bridge. On top of it, in the middle, in the painted snow. Watching.

Hearthstead was the same.

Of course it was. Floor 1 was the floor that didn’t change. Red roofs and grey stone, and the white tower in the middle where the class hall was, and the mill on the river with its wheel going round in the black water. Smoke from every chimney, straight up, because the wind on floor 1 blew when it was told to and today it hadn’t been. The square with the well. The bakery. Quill’s, with the notebooks in the window, where Chris had bought his first one on the first morning, the grey soft one he still carried at the bottom of his pack. The egg girl on the corner by the well with her basket on her arm, exactly where she’d been on the first day, saying exactly what she’d said, Eggs? Fresh eggs?, to the air.

Except she wasn’t saying it.

She was standing on the corner with her basket, and she saw them come into the square, thirty-six of them in a long line in the snow, and her mouth opened to say Eggs? Fresh eggs? the way it always did, to everybody, to nobody, like a bell that rings when you open a door. And it didn’t come out. She stood there with her mouth open, looking at them.

Then she said, “Oh. It’s you.”

Calvin waved.

The egg girl looked at him. Then she put her basket down on the edge of the well, and turned round, and ran. Down the lane behind the bakery, fast, with her skirts in her hands, shouting something.

“Was it something I said?” said Calvin.

“You didn’t say anything,” said Rachael.

“Was it something I waved?”

Ice was watching the lane she’d run down. He’d never seen the egg girl run. Not in a month the first time, not in a day the second. He’d never seen her do anything but stand on that corner with her basket and say the same three words to everybody who went past, all day, every day, as if that were the whole of her. Small and the same.

Small and the same, he’d always thought. Floor 1’s people. He’d thought it for ten years.

Yan was beside him now, with the scythe on his back and Suzu round his neck. He was watching the lane too. He didn’t say anything. But the corner of his mouth went up.

“Where’s she gone?” said Dennis.

“To tell somebody,” said Yan.

“Tell them what?”

Yan looked at him.

“That we came back down,” he said. “What else is there to tell, on floor 1?”

Crooked Street went down from the square to the river, the way it always had, crooked, between leaning houses with their upper storeys nearly touching overhead. Cobbles slick with frost. A cat on a wall. And at the bottom, where the street gave up being crooked and just fell into the river, on the left, a low old building with a deep roof and a crooked chimney and a sign over the door: a copper spoon, green with age, hanging from an iron bracket, turning a little in the wind it hadn’t been told to have.

The door was propped open with a brick.

He stopped in the doorway.

He didn’t mean to. It was the habit. He’d made it in the white room, five hundred and forty-six days ago, and it had worked on its own ever since. The stop. The breath. The putting away. He stood with one hand on the doorframe of the Copper Spoon, the way he’d stood on the first morning, and made himself remember, very clearly, that the woman inside had seen him exactly once, for one morning, a year and a half ago.

Inside, it was dark and warm and smelled of onions. A long room with a long table down the middle, benches either side, a fire at one end. At the other end, the kitchen, open to the room. And in the kitchen, at the stove, a short, wide old woman in a stained apron was feeding wood into the fire with the furious concentration of somebody who’d been feeding that stove for forty years and still didn’t trust it.

She looked up and saw him in the door.

“Well?” said Mother Crane. “Are you coming in or are you holding that frame up?”

He couldn’t move.

She’d said it. Exactly. Word for word. The first morning. Well? Are you coming in or are you holding that frame up? She stood at her stove with a log in her hand and her face like a walnut and her small sharp eyes on him, and she’d said it again, the same, as if no time had gone by at all, and he stood in her doorway with his hand on the frame and couldn’t make his legs do anything.

Thea’s hand found the small of his back. Just rested there.

Mother Crane looked at him a moment longer. Then her eyes went past him. To Thea, behind his shoulder. To Gabe, filling up the street behind Thea in his wolf coat. To Calvin, behind Gabe, on his toes trying to see in. To all the rest of them, all down Crooked Street in the snow, in a long line, thirty-five people with packs on their backs, looking in at her door.

Her eyebrows went up.

“Oh,” said Mother Crane. “It’s you lot.”

They didn’t all fit. They never had. The Copper Spoon had been built for a village, not for thirty-six people with packs and weapons and a penguin, and they came in anyway, the way they had on the first night, one after another, ducking under the low beam, cramming onto the benches and against the walls and onto the floor by the fire. Mother Crane stood in her kitchen with her arms folded and watched them come and didn’t say anything until Calvin, who had got in by the simple method of going under Gabe’s arm, sat down at the long table and put his feet up on it.

“Feet,” said Mother Crane.

Calvin took his feet off the table.

“Like my husband,” said Mother Crane darkly, to the room. “God rest the pig. Every night. Boots and all. On my good table.” She looked at Calvin. “You’ve grown.”

“I haven’t.”

“You’ve got louder, then. It’s the same thing.” She looked round at them all, crammed into her cookhouse in the snow light, and something in her face went from fierce to something else, very fast, and then back again before anybody could catch it. “You went up,” she said. “All of you. Gran said you would. Up the hill. And then that was that, I thought. Gran said Climbers always come back down. I never believed her.” She sniffed. “I believed her a bit.”

And then she looked back at the door. At him. Still standing there. He’d come in, a step, so the others could get past him, and then he’d stopped again, just inside, with Thea beside him, the way you’d stop at the edge of a cold pool.

Mother Crane looked at him for a long time.

She looked at his face. At the scar across his left palm. At the white cloak he didn’t wear any more and the lamp shield on his back and the girl holding his hand. And then her eyes went to his pack. To the side of it. Where a pot was tied on with a strap.

A plain iron pot. Black, with a lid, and a bail handle, and a dent in one side that hadn’t been there when she gave it to him.

“The onion boy,” said Mother Crane.

He didn’t say anything. He couldn’t.

“You’ve still got my pot,” she said.

“Yes,” said Ice.

“That’s my good pot.”

“I know.”

“You’ve dented it.”

“On floor 9,” he said. “A tree fell on it.”

Mother Crane looked at him with her small sharp eyes. “A tree.”

“A big one.”

“Hm.” She looked at the pot on his pack a while longer. Then at him. “Well? Are you giving it back?”

He thought about it. He’d thought about it on every floor. On the Long Acre, when he’d decided. On the stair from fourteen, four hundred and twenty-four steps, with the pot knocking against his hip. On every floor down. He’d thought he’d walk in and put it on her table and say I brought it back and that would be that. That was the promise. That was the whole of it. Bring it back.

He’d thought it would be easy.

“Not yet,” said Ice.

Mother Crane’s eyebrows went up again.

“Not yet?”

“I’ll bring it back,” he said. “I will. I promise. Just—not yet.” He swallowed. “There’s something I need to make in it first.”

She looked at him for a long, long moment. Thirty-five people in her cookhouse had gone very quiet. Even Calvin.

“You’d better,” said Mother Crane at last. “It’s my good pot.”

“I know.”

“Well, then.” She turned back to her stove, and put the log in, and banged the door shut on it. “Sit down. All of you. There’s porridge. There’s always porridge. And somebody’d better explain to me why there’s a bird in my cookhouse.”

“He’s a penguin,” said Chi. “He fishes.”

“Not in my river, he doesn’t.”

Waddles looked at her. Then he got down off Chi’s shoulder and walked across the cookhouse floor, straight past everybody, to the back door, which was open on the yard, and looked out at the river, and then looked back at Mother Crane over his shoulder.

Mother Crane looked at him.

“Fine,” she said. “But I want the first one.”

Dennis paid the tab after the porridge.

He’d been working on it since the Long Barn. He’d told everybody. He’d done it on the back of one of Chris’s pages, and then on a scrap of Higu’s slate, and then, on the Glass, in his head, out loud, every evening, until Rachael threatened to drown him in the Still Water. Thirty-five people. Four days mostly, five for some, the first week, before they went up the hill. Three meals a day. Calvin’s thirds. The porridge, the stew, the soup, the bread, and whatever that brown thing was on the third night.

“Pottage,” said Ice, from the end of the bench.

Dennis didn’t look at him. He’d stopped looking at him when he said things like that a long time ago. He just nodded and said, “Pottage,” and wrote it down.

And then, on the last line, in his own careful hand, under everything else: Dec: two loaves, one pot (out).

He stood up at the long table and put the paper down in front of Mother Crane, and then he put a purse down beside it. A big one. He’d been filling it since Tell. Everybody had put something in.

“That’s the tab,” said Dennis. “The first week. All of us. With interest.”

Mother Crane looked at the paper. Then at the purse. Then at Dennis.

“Nobody pays the tab,” she said.

“We do.”

“Gran kept a tab for Climbers. Her gran did. My family’s kept one since the Spire was young.” She didn’t touch the purse. “Nobody’s ever paid it. Not once. They come down to eat, Gran said. She never said they’d pay.”

“It’s maths,” said Dennis. He sat down. “You said you pay me when you come back down. To him. The first morning. You didn’t say some of you. You said you.” He folded his arms. “We came back down. So we’re paying.”

Mother Crane looked at him for a long time.

Then she opened the purse. And she counted it. Right there, at her long table, with thirty-six people watching, one coin at a time, the way Dennis had once watched her count a doubled copper and said I’m going to adopt that woman. She counted it twice. She didn’t say anything until she’d finished. Then she pushed a little pile of it back across the table at him.

“That’s too much,” she said.

“It’s interest.”

“I don’t do interest. I’m not a bank.” She pushed it further. “Forty years I’ve been counting coppers, boy. I know what a bowl of porridge costs. Take it back.”

Dennis looked at the little pile. Then at her.

“Keep it,” he said. “For whoever comes down next. On the tab. In advance.”

“Nobody’s coming down next.”

“Somebody might,” said Dennis. “We did.”

Mother Crane looked at him a moment longer. Then she sniffed, and scooped the little pile back off the table into her apron pocket, and got up, and went and got a book. A big old book, black, with a cracked spine, from a shelf over the stove where it had been sitting next to the salt. She opened it on the long table. Ice could see, from the end of the bench, the columns. The dates. A great many lines, in a great many hands, going back. And on the last page with writing on it, in her own small hard capitals, two entries.

CLIMBERS (THE LOT). FIRST WEEK. And a sum.

And above it, on its own, a line by itself, from the first morning: ONION BOY. 2 LOAVES. 1 POT (GOOD).

She took a pencil from behind her ear. Beside the first entry, she wrote PAID. And underlined it.

Then she looked at the line above it. The onion boy’s. Two loaves, one pot. She looked at Ice, at the end of the bench.

She wrote PAID beside 2 LOAVES.

She didn’t write anything beside 1 POT (GOOD).

“That stays open,” said Mother Crane, and shut the book. “Till it’s back on my shelf.”

He gave her the other things that afternoon, one at a time, at the kitchen end of the long table, while she cut onions and pretended not to be interested.

Merle’s mother first. The crock, in its wet cloth, that Xola had kept cold for him across the whole of the Glass, every hour, please. He put it on the table and took the cloth off, and the smell came up out of it, sour and alive, like bread that hadn’t happened yet.

“What’s that?”

“Bread,” said Ice. “Starter. From a woman on seven who bakes the same day every day. She calls it her mother. It was her mother’s. She sent some down.” He paused. “She said to tell you it rises better if you talk to it.”

Mother Crane looked into the crock. She sniffed it. Her face did something complicated.

“That’s good,” she said grudgingly. “That’s a good one, that.” She put her finger in and tasted it. “Seven, you said.”

“Seven.”

“Hm.” She put the cloth back on, carefully. “I’ll talk to it.”

Then the plait. A twist of straw, gold, plaited in a pattern, over, under, a twist, a turn, two over, a knot, with a bit of red thread round the end. He’d carried it in his vest from fourteen. He put it in her hand.

“From a woman on fourteen,” he said. “Hild. Her village makes a straw man every harvest, and keeps him all winter, and in the spring they plough a bit of him into the first field so the harvest goes back in. And they keep a bit in every chimney corner. For luck. So it’s kept.” He paused. “She made this one for you. For your chimney corner. She said for your old woman.”

“Old woman,” said Mother Crane.

“She’s very old. She’s allowed.”

Mother Crane looked at the plait in her hand for a long time. Then she got up, without saying anything, and went to the fireplace at the end of the room, and put it up on the stone shelf in the corner beside the chimney, where the smoke would keep it dry, and stood back and looked at it there.

“It looks like writing,” she said.

“A boy on our side thought so too.”

“Hm.” She came back and sat down. “Next.”

Joan’s apple and Tansy’s bead were Tiger’s to give, so Tiger gave them. He did it standing very straight in front of her with his tail going, and told her about Joan with the green shutters and Tansy who climbed down trees head first, and Mother Crane listened to every word with her arms folded and then said Well, and put the apple in the window and the bead on a nail by the door, where it caught the light.

And then the onions.

Imra’s sack. Small, tight, bitter desert onions from a woman at the bottom of a well, that had come all the way from six on Gabe’s back and then Salty’s and then Gabe’s again. He untied the neck of the sack and tipped a few of them out onto the long table, and they rolled, small and brown and hard, and Mother Crane picked one up and turned it over in her fingers the way she turned everything over, looking for what was wrong with it.

“Where are these from?”

“Six. The Glass. A town in a pit in the desert, called Deepwell. They grow them in wet sand by a pool.” He watched her turn it over. “A woman called Imra grew these. She sent them for you. She said to tell you—“ He stopped, and got it right, because it mattered to get it right. “Tell her a woman at the bottom of a well on six says thank you for the trick.”

Mother Crane stopped turning the onion.

“What trick?”

“Doing it properly.”

She looked at him.

“I peeled her onions,” he said. “The first time I was on six. On her third ring. The way you showed me. Top and tail. Along the grain. Keep the root on. Skin off in one if you’re lucky.” He looked at the onion in her hand. “And she watched me do it. And then she did it. And she taught her sister. And her sister taught the next ring. And when we came back down through six, a year later, half of Deepwell was peeling onions properly, and she wanted to know who’d taught me.” He paused. “So I told her.”

Mother Crane looked down at the onion. Then she took her little knife out of her apron pocket and topped it, and tailed it, and ran the knife down one side, along the grain, and the skin came away in her fingers in one piece, the way it always did for her, every time, the way it had never once done for him in ten years without his thinking about it.

She held the skin up. Papery and brown and whole.

“Who taught you?” she said. “To do it like that. Properly.”

“You did.”

“I did not.” She put the onion down. “I showed you the once. On the first morning. One sack. You’d half got it already.” She frowned at him. “Somebody taught you before me. Somebody had their hands on yours. You don’t get that from one sack.”

He didn’t say anything.

She looked at him for a long time across her long table, with the onion skin in her fingers. He could see her looking. She was Mother Crane. She’d see through anything.

“Somebody very like me, you said,” said Mother Crane slowly. “The first morning. You said somebody like me taught you. And she was very rude about it.”

“Yes.”

“And I said she sounded like a sensible woman.”

“You did.”

“And you said was.” She put the onion skin down on the table, very carefully, the way you’d put down something that might blow away. “I heard it. I’ve thought about it since. On and off. That was.”

The cookhouse was very quiet. He could hear the fire. He could hear Waddles in the yard, slapping about in the shallows. He could hear thirty-five people very carefully not listening.

“She was,” said Ice.

Mother Crane looked at him a while longer. Then she picked the onion back up and diced it, fast, the way she did, and swept it into her hand and dropped it into the pot on the stove. Her pot. Not his. Not the good one.

“Well,” she said, with her back to him. “She taught you right.”

That night he couldn’t sleep.

They’d made beds wherever they could. The inn up the street had its nineteen beds, and Dennis had haggled for all of them before supper, and the rest were on the floor of the class hall and in the Spoon itself, on the benches and under the long table and in a heap by the fire. He was on the floor at the kitchen end, with his pack for a pillow and the pot beside his head, where he could reach it. Thea was asleep against his shoulder. Gabe was asleep across the doorway, the way he’d slept across doorways on every floor since nine.

He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

Her room was up there. Above the kitchen. He knew exactly where. He could have drawn it: the narrow stair behind the stove, the low door at the top, the little room under the eaves with one window and a bed and a chair and a chest. He’d never been up there, in either life. In the first life she’d died in it, while he was thirteen floors up. A month later he’d stood in the snow under a bare tree on the Long Acre with a window in front of his eyes that nobody had written, and he’d thought: her bed over the kitchen. And he’d never seen it. He’d only ever thought it.

Sixty-three days.

You should sleep, said Alea, very softly, on his chest.

I know.

She’s fine. Kei looked. She’s fine. She’s sixty-something and she shouts and she carries wood and she’s fine.

She was fine the first time.

Alea didn’t say anything to that. She curled up a bit tighter. After a while he felt her start to hum, the way she’d started to on the Salt, the lamp-in-the-window song from the pass, badly, on purpose, so he’d have something to listen to that wasn’t the dark.

The stair behind the stove creaked.

He turned his head. A light came down. A candle, in a dish, and behind it, in a nightcap and a shawl over her nightdress and a pair of enormous old boots that must have been her husband’s, Mother Crane. She came down the narrow stair and into the kitchen and stopped, and looked at the cookhouse full of sleeping people in the firelight. Then she looked down at him, on the floor by the stove, awake.

“Can’t sleep?” she said. Low.

“No.”

“Nor me.” She looked at the sleeping heaps a moment longer. “Never could, with people in. Not since he went.” She jerked her head at the long table. “Up. Come and sit. You’re lying on my floor like a dropped coat.”

He got up, carefully, so Thea wouldn’t wake. Thea woke anyway, a little; he felt her open her eyes and see Mother Crane and close them again. He went and sat at the long table, at the kitchen end, and Mother Crane put the candle down and sat down across from him with a creak and a sigh, and pulled her shawl round her.

The first time, it had taken her two weeks to sit down across from him.

“Your lot paid the tab,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Nobody’s ever paid the tab.” She looked at the candle. “I keep thinking about it. Forty years, and Gran before me, and her mother. They come down to eat. That’s all Gran ever said. Nobody ever said they’d pay. I don’t know what to do with it.” She sniffed. “I’ll think of something.”

“Dennis said it was for whoever comes down next.”

“Dennis is a soft fool with a quick hand and I like him.” She was quiet a moment. “Gran said something else, too. About Climbers. I’ve never told anybody. It didn’t seem worth telling.” She looked at him over the candle. “She said, once in a long while, one comes down alone. Ahead of the rest. Like he already knows the way.” She tilted her head. “That was you. The first morning.”

“Yes.”

“I asked her what happened to them. The ones who came down alone.” Mother Crane looked at the candle. “She didn’t say. She just went on with her bread. I always thought that meant it was bad.” She looked back at him. “And now here you are. With all the rest of them. Asleep on my floor. And my pot.”

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t think he could.

“You’ve got a look,” said Mother Crane. “You had it the first morning. Like you want something and don’t want to ask.” She folded her arms on the table. “Out with it.”

He looked at her across the candle. At the walnut face, and the small sharp eyes, and the nightcap, and the shawl, and the hands on the table, knotted and floury and alive.

“I want to stay,” he said. “The winter. All of us, if you’ll have us. I’ll work. I’ll peel your onions. Every morning. I’ll cut your wood.” He swallowed. “I just want to stay. Till the spring.”

Mother Crane looked at him for a long time.

“That’s not what you wanted,” she said. “That’s what you’re asking for. It’s not the same.”

“No.”

“But I’ll take it.” She stood up, with a creak, and picked up the candle. “Onions at first light. The sack’s by the bench. You know where.” She turned toward the stair. Then she stopped, and looked back at him, over the candle, in her husband’s boots. “And go to sleep, onion boy. You look like death.”

She went up the stair.

He sat at the long table in the dark until the candle-light had gone from the top of the stair, and the door at the top had closed, and he heard her bed creak once above the kitchen.

Then he went back and lay down by the stove with his hand on the pot, and Thea moved without waking and put her head back on his shoulder. And for the first time since the Glass, he slept.
