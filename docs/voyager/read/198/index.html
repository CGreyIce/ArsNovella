Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 198

Her Name

5,692 words · 25 min read

On floor 1, the first time, Yan had barely been able to see them.

The villagers. Their lights. He’d come out of the arch on the first morning into Hearthstead under its ceiling of a sky, and looked round at the people in the square, and seen what he’d later learn to call small and the same. Little lights. Faint. Set very low down in them, under the breastbone, like pilot flames in a stove nobody had lit. All the same size. All the same colour. They hadn’t flickered. They hadn’t leaned. They’d sat in the people like something put there and left. He’d thought, the first week, that floor-1 people were barely people at all. He’d thought it with a sort of cool interest and not much else, the way you’d think about a painting of a crowd.

He’d been wrong. He knew that now. He’d known it since the egg girl ran.

They’d got brighter. All of them. All over Hearthstead, in the sixty-four days since the thirty-six came down the hill. Not much. A little. Like stoves somebody had finally put a match to. And they leaned, now. They leaned toward things. He’d sat on the floor of the Spoon’s kitchen for three days and nights with his back against the pantry door, and through the walls and the front door and the snow he’d watched the lights of the people in Crooked Street, standing out there in the cold looking up at a window. Every one of them leaning the same way.

Toward her.

Hers had been the brightest on the floor, the first day.

He’d noticed it. He hadn’t said so. Nobody had asked him. He’d come into the Copper Spoon on the first night with the other thirty-four, after the onion boy had been and gone, and the old woman at the stove had turned round and shouted at Calvin for his feet, and Yan had looked at her and thought: oh. Because hers wasn’t small and the same. Hers sat high up and warm in her, not a pilot flame but a whole banked stove, low and red and steady, the kind of light you could have cooked on. The brightest on floor 1 by a long way. He’d looked at it on the first night and thought, with the same cool interest: that one’s somebody.

Hyvred called it kitchen-coloured. Hyvred saw colours, not lights, and she didn’t see what he saw. But she was right. It was kitchen-coloured. It was the colour of a stove at night with the door open.

For three days he’d watched it through the boards.

He could see lights through anything. He’d been able to since the white room, through walls and doors and floors and once, on twelve, through four metres of salt. He’d sat on the kitchen floor with Suzu round his neck and Working On It across his knees, and tipped his head back against the pantry door, and watched an old woman’s light in a small room above him, through the ceiling, through the floorboards, through the patched quilt.

The first afternoon, when she sat down at the long table and put her hand flat to steady it, it had flickered. Once. A stove door banging in a draught.

By the first night it was smaller. Not dimmer. Smaller. Pulled in on itself, the way a fire pulls in when the wind gets at it, all the red gone down into the coals.

By the second night it was smaller still. He could see Malcolm’s blessings sitting on it, two and two and two, little white lights on a red one, like hands on a shoulder. He could see where Rachael had taken heat out of her, a sort of cool patch, like the place on a stone where somebody’s been sitting. He could see Maigo’s feet on the boards, a slow green going down. And every so often he could see Ice’s light, which he could have found blind on any floor of the Spire, amber and steady and burning so hard it hurt to look at, held very, very still beside hers. Like somebody holding a match in both hands in a wind.

It hadn’t moved. That was what he’d told Ice. It’s smaller. It’s not moving. He’d said it on the first night and the second, and both times it had been true.

At dusk on the third night, it moved.

It didn’t go out. They didn’t, usually. People thought they did, that a light just went out, like a lamp. They didn’t. He’d learned that on eight, at Wendmere, on the causeway, walking the waiting dead to the lip one at a time all night with Aud’s pole in his hand. A light didn’t go out. It went somewhere.

It went up.

Slowly. That was the thing. It came up out of the coals of her, where it had been pulled down tight for three days, and it lifted. A hand’s width. Like a stove-flame when somebody opens the door at the top. Like heat going up a chimney. It hung there above her chest, in the hot little room, red and small and lifting, and it didn’t go back down.

He’d seen it once before like this. On fourteen, in the Long Barn, the night they buried Gaffer Tobin. Except he hadn’t seen it then; he’d seen it after. Gaffer’s light had gone up at the band, when the knot held. His went up because something started, he’d said, to nobody. Nobody had asked him.

This one wasn’t going up because something started.

He knew the difference. He’d walked fourteen of them to the lip on eight. He knew what a light looked like when it was going because it wanted to go, because it had been waiting and it was tired and it wanted to lie down. And he knew what one looked like when it was being pulled.

This one wasn’t being pulled.

It was leaning.

Yun, said Suzu, round his neck. Very softly.

“I know,” said Yan.

He sat with his head back against the pantry door and his eyes on the ceiling and watched an old woman’s light lean, and the cool interest he’d felt about lights his whole life, since he was small, since before he had a word for it, wasn’t there. He looked for it, and it wasn’t there. There was something else instead. It sat in his chest under Suzu’s cool weight like a stone.

He didn’t know what it was. He didn’t like it.

You know what it is, said Suzu.

“Don’t.”

It’s the same thing you felt on the jetty, said Suzu. For Aud. When she looked back.

He didn’t answer that.

At the bottom of the stair, Malcolm said, very quietly, into the silence of the kitchen, I don’t think it’s enough.

And Yan heard himself say, “It isn’t.”

He’d stayed off the stair for three days because of the Edge.

It was the only reason. He’d told Hyvred, and Hyvred had told Kei, and he’d heard Kei tell Ice on the first night at the bottom of the stair: Yan won’t go up. He says wherever he stands there’s an edge. He’s not putting one by her bed. And Ice had looked across the dark kitchen at him, and Yan had looked back, and that had been the end of it.

It was true. It had been true since the sunrise on eight, when the window came up and said it. Edge. Wherever you stand, there is an Edge. A light you have waited with may go over it. He’d felt it the moment it settled into him. A line. Under his feet, wherever his feet were. A lip. Like the end of Aud’s causeway, where the stone stopped and the lake began. He carried it about with him now, everywhere, the way you carry your own shadow. He’d felt it on the Glass in the sand, and on the bridges on five, and on the floor of the Spoon’s kitchen, a thin cold line on the boards round the place where he sat. Lights near it felt it. They didn’t like it. On five the gulls wouldn’t land within arm’s length of him. Sleet wouldn’t lie down where he’d been sitting.

A light you have waited with may go over it.

He’d thought that meant don’t wait with anybody. For three days. He’d sat on the far side of the kitchen with his Edge round him, a long way from her stair, so it couldn’t be under her bed. So he couldn’t be waiting with her. So she couldn’t go over it.

But she was going anyway.

Not over his. Over the other one. The one everybody had. The one that wasn’t anybody’s, that didn’t have a keeper, that didn’t have a lip you could stand on or a causeway or a pole or a man with a scythe who’d never once cut too soon. The ordinary one. The one that took you whether you’d finished or not. Her light was leaning toward it, and there was nobody standing there.

He’d been keeping his Edge away from her for three days.

He’d had it the wrong way round.

May, said Suzu.

He sat very still.

A light you have waited with may go over it, said Suzu. That’s what it says. You read it out to Wendy on the jetty. You said it in your planning voice. You said: it doesn’t say must.

“I’ve never kept one,” said Yan. Very low. “Not once. They want to go, I let them. That’s what it’s for.”

Is that what it’s for?

He didn’t answer.

Yun, said Suzu. She doesn’t want to go.

He looked up at the ceiling. At the light, lifting. Leaning.

“No,” he said. “She doesn’t.”

He put the scythe down on the floor beside him. He stood up.

“Wendy.”

Wendy was already on her feet.

“Hold this,” said Yan.

Fourteen steps. He took them two at a time.

The room under the eaves was very small and very hot and very full. Kei on the end of the chest by the window, with his book open on his knees and his face the colour of the snow. Rachael on her knees by the bed with her bare hands in her lap, red to the elbow and shaking. Xola on the floor with a bowl. Maigo at the foot of the bed with his bare feet on the boards and his one leaf. Malcolm in the doorway behind him; he’d come up too. Thea on the other end of the chest, with the lantern in her lap.

And Ice in the chair.

He looked up when Yan came in. His face. Yan had known it since before the white room, on Earth, since the year Ice had come over to Yan’s lot when everything with Thea and Gigi went wrong. Yan had never asked. You didn’t, with Ice. You let him sit where he wanted and didn’t ask.

He wasn’t in the corner now. He was in a dead man’s chair beside an old woman’s bed, with her hand on his wrist, and he looked up at Yan in the door and Yan saw his light go, all at once, like a match dropped.

“Yan,” said Ice.

“I know,” said Yan.

He came in. He ducked under the beam. He stood at the foot of the bed.

And the Edge came with him.

He felt it settle. On the floorboards, under the soles of his boots, all round him, a thin cold line, the way it always did. And this time he looked down at it, which he never did, because he never needed to. It ran across the foot of the bed. Across the boards. Right under the two old boots standing side by side at the foot of the bed, big and polished and empty, where somebody had left them twenty-three years ago for a man who was going to come in and put them on in the morning.

The boots were standing on the lip.

He looked at them for a long moment.

Then he looked up. At her light.

It was right there. In front of him. A hand above her chest, red and small, lifted up out of her like a coal on a breath. And it was leaning. He’d seen it lean through the ceiling and not known which way. He could see it now.

It was leaning toward the boots.

Toward the foot of the bed. Toward the Edge. Toward the two big empty boots standing on the line of his lip, the way lights on eight had leaned toward the end of the causeway, toward the water, toward whatever was over there that they’d been waiting for.

Lights leaned toward what they loved. He’d learned that on eight, too. From a woman called Neth whose light had leaned away from his cupped hands toward the Edge, because she didn’t want the wind kept off. She wanted to go.

This one wanted the boots.

“She’s going,” said Yan.

He said it out loud. He didn’t decide to. It was what he’d have said on the causeway, to Wendy, about any of them, about Wira or Sile or Mott. Cool and level. A fact.

Nobody in the room moved.

“She’s leaning,” said Yan. “Toward him. The boots. That’s where she wants to be.” He was looking at the light. “She’s been on her own in this room for twenty-three years with his boots at the end of the bed. She’s tired. And he’s over there. And the Edge is right here now.” He heard his own voice and didn’t know it. “Because I am.”

Kei made a sound.

Ice didn’t. Ice sat in the chair and looked at Yan and his face didn’t move at all.

Yan put his hands up.

He didn’t think about it. They came up on their own, the way they had on eight for Neth, the way they had on the first floors for guttering candles. Cupped. Round the light. Not touching it. Close. Shelter. You can’t make the light bigger. You can keep the wind off it. He held his hands round the old woman’s lifting light like round a candle in a draught, and the cold came into his fingers at once, the way it always did, white, up to the wrist.

The light didn’t care.

It leaned away from his hands. The way Neth’s had. Not hard. Not rude. Just away. Toward the boots. The way you’d lean away from somebody holding an umbrella over you when it’s stopped raining, and you’d like, thank you, to feel the sun.

“She doesn’t want it kept off,” said Yan.

“Then turn her,” said Rachael.

She said it from the floor, on her knees, with her red hands in her lap. Her voice cracked. Rachael’s voice never cracked.

“Turn her,” she said again. “You can. On eight. Ice said. You said their names and they turned. A light you know by name.” She stared up at him. “So say it.”

Yan looked at her.

“A light you know by name will hear you say it,” he said. “It will turn.” He looked back at the light. “I don’t know her name.”

Nobody said anything.

The candle stood in the window. The lantern sat on Thea’s knees. Down through the floor, in the kitchen, Yan could feel thirty lights sitting very still. Out in the street, through the walls, through the snow, a hundred more, all leaning the same way.

“Mother Crane,” said Kei. Hoarse. “She’s—“

“That’s not a name,” said Yan. “That’s what the street calls her.” He didn’t look away from the light. “On eight, it had to be the name. The one they had. Not the old woman by the weir. Not the boy who went in after his sister. Their name. Aud. Wira. Sile. Or they don’t hear it. They hear you calling somebody else.”

“Somebody knows it,” said Xola.

“Who?” said Yan.

Silence.

He looked round the room. At Kei, who’d watched her breathe for sixty-four days and written it down twice. At Rachael, who’d taken her heat. At Malcolm in the door. At Thea. At Ice.

Ice was looking at the old woman in the bed.

“I never asked,” said Ice.

Very quietly. To her. Not to any of them.

“I never asked her,” he said. “Not once. She was Mother Crane. She was always Mother Crane. Ice, love. Pass the salt, Ice. She called me by my name every day and I never—“ He stopped. His face did something Yan had never seen it do. “All that time. I never once asked her what hers was.”

Thea’s hand went to his shoulder.

Yan didn’t know what all that time meant. He’d stopped trying to know what Ice’s all that times meant on floor 9. It didn’t matter. He could see Ice’s light. It was enough.

“Then ask somebody,” said Yan. “Fast.”

Malcolm was already gone. Yan heard him go down the stair, three at a time, and shout something in the kitchen. Thirty lights in the kitchen all moved at once. The front door banged. And out in the street, through the walls, he heard Calvin’s voice go up, high and loud, the loudest thing on floor 1:

“Does anybody know her name? Her real name? Mother Crane? Does anybody know what her name is?”

He held his hands round the light.

It was all he could do. Keep the wind off. It didn’t want the wind kept off; it leaned away from his cold white fingers toward the boots, slowly, a hair at a time, and he held his hands there anyway and let them go white to the elbow, the way Rachael’s went, the way his had in the trial on three, the way they had for Neth. He’d never held one back before. He’d never wanted to. He held this one.

Not over my Edge, he thought. He didn’t know who he was saying it to. Not this one. She’s his. That makes her mine.

He stood at the foot of the bed with the Edge under his boots and the old man’s boots on the lip and the old woman’s light leaning toward them, and he did the thing a Reaper did that nobody had ever asked him to do. He’d only ever done one half of it. The half that ends things. The cold edge of the blade, the low sweep across the water, it does not cut too soon. But there was another half. He’d known since eight. He’d felt it in him, in the window that had come up at sunrise, under Edge, like the other side of a coin. He’d never used it. He’d never had anybody he wanted to use it on.

He put it into the light.

Not heat. Not healing, the way Rachael healed, the way Malcolm blessed. His was cold. His was soul. He took a little of his own light, the dark blue one that lived low and long in him like a lamp turned down for the night, that Suzu said was the colour of the deep water on eight, and he put it into his cupped hands, round hers. Not to push her back. To give her something to hold on to. The way you’d hold a hand out to somebody on the end of a jetty in the dark.

Her light flickered.

It didn’t turn. It didn’t come back. But it stopped leaning, for a breath. It hung there. Red and small and lifted, between his cold hands and the boots on the lip.

That’s it, said Suzu. That’s the other half. Hold it.

“I can’t hold it long.”

Then hold it till somebody comes up the stair.

Out in the street, Calvin was shouting. People were answering him. Yan could hear it through the walls. She’s Mother Crane. She’s always been Mother Crane. My mother called her Mother Crane. Tam called her my girl. A hundred people in the snow, and not one of them knew.

His hands were white to the elbow. Then past it.

Ice hadn’t moved. He was sitting in the chair with the old woman’s hand on his wrist, looking at her face, and his light was burning so hard Yan couldn’t look straight at it.

Kei was crying. Silently. With his book on his knees.

The front door banged again, below. Feet on the stair. Not Calvin’s. Malcolm’s. He came in under the beam out of breath and grey.

“Nobody knows,” said Malcolm. “Nobody. The whole street. Forty years she’s been Mother Crane and nobody—“

And then, from below them, through the floor, through the boards, from somewhere under the kitchen end of the long room, Calvin’s voice went up again.

Not shouting this time. Not to the street. A different sound. The sound Calvin made on the stair into the first square of the Red City on the sixty-sixth day, coming down talking, when he saw the fountain. Oh. There’s a—

“Oh,” said Calvin. Through the floor. “Oh, there’s—oh. There’s names. Under here. Under the table. There’s—Rachael. Somebody. There’s names under the table!”

He’d gone under the long table.

Malcolm told them, after, and then Calvin told them, louder, and then everybody told it, for the rest of the winter, at that same table, until Mother Crane said if she heard it one more time she’d put them all out in the snow. Calvin had come back in from the street with no name and nothing left to shout, and stood in the middle of the Spoon with thirty people looking at him and the ceiling creaking overhead, and hadn’t known what to do. And when Calvin didn’t know what to do, he did the thing he’d done since he was small. He got small.

He got down on the floor and went under the long table.

He’d done it on the first night on floor 1, the first time he’d been in the Spoon, after Mother Crane shouted at him for his feet; he’d gone under the table and sat there among everybody’s boots, sulking, and Rachael had pulled him out by his ankle. He went under it now. On his back. In the dark, among the bench legs, with his hands over his face.

And took his hands away. And looked up.

The underside of the long table was carved.

All of it. The whole length. In the dark, on the bare old wood, where nobody ever looked because nobody ever lay on their back under a table except Calvin. Names. Dozens of them. Carved in a column along the middle of the board, one under another, in different hands, with different knives, some deep and some shallow, some worn nearly smooth with age, going back and back to the far end of the table where the wood was black with it. Women’s names. One after another. Edith. Agnes. Rosa. Bess. Tilda. Mab.

The ones who’d kept the Spoon. Every one. Each one carved her name under the table when it was hers. Since the Spire was young.

And at the near end, at the kitchen end, under the place where she sat every morning across from Ice with the onion sack between them, the last name in the column. Newer than the others. Neat, small, careful letters, cut by somebody who did things properly or not at all.

ADA.

And beside it, crooked, enormous, carved with what had obviously been a much bigger knife by somebody who’d obviously been lying under there with his boots sticking out from under the bench, a second word. And under the word, a little drawing of a boot.

TAM.

“Ada,” shouted Calvin, through the floor. “Her name’s Ada! It’s Ada! Tam drew a boot!”

Yan didn’t say it.

He could have. He was the one at the foot of the bed with his hands round her light. He was the one who’d done it fourteen times on a causeway on eight. A light you know by name will hear you say it. He knew it now. He could have said it, cool and level, the way he’d said Aud, and she’d have heard it.

He didn’t.

“Ice,” he said.

Ice looked up at him.

“Not me,” said Yan. “You.” His hands were white to the shoulder. He could barely feel them. “She doesn’t know my voice. She knows yours. Every morning. For sixty-four days. At the onions.” He held the light. “Say it.”

Ice looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked down at the old woman in the bed. At her face, flushed and wet and far away. At her hand on his wrist.

He leaned in close. The way you’d lean in to somebody at a table to tell them something nobody else should hear.

“Ada,” said Ice.

Very quietly. In the voice he used at the onions in the mornings, when she talked about her husband and he said yes and I know and I believe you.

“Ada. It’s me. It’s the onion boy.” His voice went, and came back. “I’ve still got your pot. I haven’t brought it back. You said bring it back. You said that stays open. On the tab. Till it’s back on your shelf.” He held her hand. “So you have to be here. You have to be here when I do. You can’t close it if you’re not here. You said.”

The light turned.

Yan saw it. He’d never seen anything like it. Not on eight. Not anywhere. The old woman’s light, lifted and leaning toward the boots on the lip, stopped. And turned. Slowly. The way a face turns toward somebody calling from the next room. The way you’d turn, half asleep, toward somebody saying your name, the one nobody had said in twenty-three years, in a voice you knew from the onions.

It turned toward Ice.

And it came down.

Out of the air above her chest, out of Yan’s white cupped hands, back down into her, a hand’s width, the way it had come up, slow, like a coal settling in a stove when somebody shuts the door at the top. It went down into her chest and sat there. Red and small. Kitchen-coloured. Not lifting. Not leaning.

Home.

Yan took his hands away.

He looked down. The boots stood at the foot of the bed on the boards. On the lip. Big and old and empty and polished.

He took one step back. The Edge came with him, off the boots, back across the boards, to the door. The boots stood on plain wood.

Not tonight, said Suzu, very softly. Tell him not tonight. He’ll wait. He’s waited twenty-three years. He’s very patient, for a man who put his feet on the table.

Yan didn’t say anything. He didn’t think the boots would mind.

At the first grey in the window, behind the candle and the lantern, the fever broke.

He felt it before Kei said it. He was sitting on the floor by the door with his back against the frame and his hands in his lap, white to the shoulder and stinging as they came back, and he felt it in her light. It changed. It went from red to gold. From a coal to a flame. And she sighed, in the bed, a long low sigh, the kind people make in their sleep when something heavy has been taken off them, and her face went from red and dry to wet, all over, all at once.

Kei put his hand on her forehead.

He held it there a long time.

Then he took his hand away and put it over his own face and didn’t say anything at all. And Dose, round his neck, said something to him that nobody else could hear, and he laughed, behind his hand, a cracked wet laugh, and took his hand down.

“It’s turned,” said Kei. “It’s broken. She’s sweating it out. It’s—“ He looked at the jar on the chest. At the book. At the four ticks beside the remedy and her? and yes. “It’s turned,” he said again.

Rachael put her face in her red hands.

Ice put his head down on the quilt. Beside her hand. He didn’t make a sound. Thea put her hand on the back of his neck and left it there.

And the old woman in the bed, without opening her eyes, turned her hand over on his wrist. Slowly. So that it was holding his, and not just lying on it.

“Too much salt,” she said. In a voice like a cracked bell. “In that broth.”

Nobody in the room could say anything for a long time.

He went down the stair.

His hands hurt. They always did, coming back. He went down the fourteen steps behind the stove into the kitchen, and the kitchen was full of people standing up. Thirty of them. All on their feet, all looking at the stair, all with their lights leaning so hard toward the ceiling that he had to narrow his eyes.

“It turned,” said Yan. “She’s kept.”

Nobody cheered. Not straight away. They’d been holding still too long. Then Calvin, who was still half under the long table with his legs sticking out, made a sound like a kettle, and Hako said classic in a voice that wasn’t a joke at all, and Hyvred burst into tears, and Dennis said oh thank God and sat down on the floor, and then everybody was making a noise, all at once, all thirty of them, loud, louder than the hold-folk song, louder than anything Yan had heard in the Spoon in sixty-four days.

And out in Crooked Street, through the walls, through the snow, he saw a hundred small floor-1 lights go up all at once, brighter, like stoves somebody had flung the doors of.

Wendy was standing by the pantry door. Holding Working On It in both hands, the way she’d held it all night on the jetty on eight.

He went and sat down on the floor at her feet. With his back against WINTER WATCH. She sat down beside him and put the scythe across both their knees.

“You minded,” said Wendy.

“Somebody had to.”

“I know.” She looked at his hands. White to the shoulder, coming back pink in blotches. “I’m holding your scythe. That’s how I know.”

He looked at it across their knees. It does not cut too soon.

“I didn’t cut,” he said.

“No.”

“I’ve never not cut. Not once. They want to go, I—“

“She didn’t want to go,” said Wendy. “You said. On the stair. She doesn’t. I heard you.” She bumped his shoulder with hers. “That’s not the same as the jetty. Nobody on the jetty wanted to stay.”

He didn’t say anything.

She’s right, said Suzu. She’s always right. It’s very annoying.

“If it’d been anybody else’s,” Yan said, after a while, low, to nobody, “I’d have let her go. It’s warmer over there. I’ve seen it. They lie down and it’s warm.” He looked at the stair. “But she’s his. So.”

Wendy looked at him.

“Liar,” she said.

“Not about that.”

“No,” said Wendy. “Not about that.” She put her head on his shoulder. Just for a moment. Then she took it off again. “Ow,” she said, out of habit, though he hadn’t touched her.

He held out his hand, without looking. The way Gabe did. The way Ice did now. She took it.

They sat on the kitchen floor of the Copper Spoon with the scythe across their knees and listened to thirty people being loud, and up above them, through the boards, Yan watched an old woman’s light, small and gold and steady, sitting low and warm in her chest like a stove with the door shut for the night. And beside it, burning amber, so bright he couldn’t look at it straight, another one. Bent close. Not holding a match in the wind any more.

Just burning.

Calvin went under the long table. Names carved underneath. Every woman who kept the Spoon. Edith, Agnes, Rosa, Bess, Tilda, Mab. The last: ADA. Beside it, crooked: TAM, and a boot.
“Not me. You. She knows your voice.” Ice: “Ada. It’s the onion boy. I’ve still got your pot. You said that stays open. You have to be here when I do.”
The light turned. Toward him. It came down. Home.
The Edge came off the boots.
First light: the fever broke. Kei: “It’s turned.” “Too much salt. In that broth.”
“She’s kept.”
Wendy held the scythe. “You minded.” “Somebody had to.” “Liar.” “Not about that.” Ow.
