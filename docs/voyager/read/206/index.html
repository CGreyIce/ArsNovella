Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 6: Unwritten

Chapter 206

The First Word

5,317 words · 23 min read

The Float was a town that knew it was going to move.

He’d never seen anything like it. Not on the Sea, not on the Glass, not anywhere. A mile out from the cliff, in the middle of the frozen river, sixty or seventy rafts lashed together into one great sprawling deck the size of a village, with houses on it. Real houses. Low and wide, with turf roofs weighted down with stones, and smoke going up from every one, and narrow lanes of planking between them, and a sort of square in the middle with a mast in it, a real mast, tall, with a lookout’s basket at the top. Everything was tied. Every house to its raft, every raft to the next, with ropes as thick as an arm, and the ropes had slack in them. Coils of it. Laid ready.

Because in a few days, the ice holding the Float would break. And the whole town would float. And ride the flood down the river, all of it, together, houses and lanes and square and mast, until the water was summer-wide and slow and it could moor in the reed-bays below the Narrows and stay there till the autumn, when it would freeze in again. Every year. For as long as anybody had a gran.

“Unless the Narrows don’t let go,” said Haddo.

He was the Float’s Steerer. A big quiet man in grey fur with a beard gone white at the chin and hands like shovels, who stood at the foot of the mast in the square with his pole and spoke slowly, the way people on fifteen did, as if every word had to be listened to before it was said. He’d walked out across the ice to Sabe’s ladder on the fifth morning, because Sabe had sent Tuli to tell him there were climbers, and stood at the foot of it and looked up at thirty-six strangers and a goat on Sabe’s porch, and said, Come and see the Float.

So they had.

“The Narrows,” said Ice.

Haddo looked at him.

“You don’t know the Narrows,” said Haddo. Not a question.

“No.”

“Hm.” Haddo put his pole down on the deck. Not on the ice; on the planks. Tock. Out of habit. He tipped his head as if the raft could tell him something too. “Downriver. A day’s walk on the ice. The valley comes in.” He put his two shovel hands up and brought them close together, palm to palm, with a gap the width of a finger. “Like that. Rock both sides. High. The whole river goes through a gap a quarter-mile wide. In summer that’s fine. In spring—“ He let his hands meet. “In spring all the ice in the valley comes down at once. A mile wide. And tries to go through a quarter.”

“And jams,” said Higu.

“And jams.” Haddo looked at her. “Every year. The ice piles up in the Narrows like boots at a door. Higher than the cliffs, some years. And the river backs up behind it. Rises. All up the valley. Floods the reed-beds, floods the stilts. Floods us.” He nodded at the Float. “That’s why we float. We rise with it. We ride it.” He was quiet a moment. “And then the jam goes. All at once. And everything behind it goes through the Narrows with it. Water and ice and houses and anything that’s on it. In about the time it takes to say so.”

Nobody said anything.

“Most years,” said Haddo, “it goes slow. A bit at a time. The jam leaks before it breaks. You can ride it. You hang back, you cut loose late, you go through after the worst.” He tapped the pole. Tock. “Some years it doesn’t. Some years it holds. Too long. And when it goes, it goes all at once, and anything close behind it goes with it. Gran’s time, it took half the Float. My father’s time, it took the stilts at Keddle, the ones below Sabe’s. All of them. In one breath.”

“And this year?” said Drew.

Haddo looked at him for a long time.

“This year it’s awake,” said Haddo.

That was the thing nobody on fifteen would say plainly. Ice had heard it at Sabe’s stove, and in the lanes of the Float, and from Tuli on the ice, in pieces, with people looking away when they said it. Something sleeps in the jam. Some years. Not every year. Some years the jam at the Narrows wasn’t just ice. Some years there was something in it. Under it. Of it. You couldn’t see it. You could only hear it, through the poles, if you listened from far enough away, the way you’d hear a heart through a wall. A slow, deep, huge sound, under the ice at the Narrows, that wasn’t the river turning over in its sleep.

The Breaker, they called it. If they called it anything. Mostly they didn’t. Mostly they put their poles down on the ice and listened and said this year it’s awake, and didn’t say anything else.

“Is it a guardian?” said Hyvred, at the long table in the Float’s meeting-house that night. Very quietly, to Ice. “Like the Squall? Like the Kiln?”

“I don’t know,” said Ice.

Hyvred looked at him. She’d been looking at him a lot, on fifteen. They all had. Not the way they’d looked at him on the way up, the first time, when he was a light on a trail a floor ahead and every one of them was trying to work out how he knew. Not the way they’d looked at him on the way down, when he said the first time and they’d decided, together, without saying, not to ask. This was a new look. He’d seen it on Gabe’s face on the ledge. On Drew’s. On Calvin’s, blue, in three coats.

It was the look you gave somebody who was in it with you.

“Okay,” said Hyvred. And then, after a moment: “What colour are you? Do you want to know?”

“What colour am I?”

“You’ve been a match in the wind since the stair,” said Hyvred. “Since the first time you said you didn’t know.” She tilted her head. Her glasses were on. “You’re not now. Since you came out of the river. Since Tuli.” She looked at him over the glasses. “You’re just a lamp. An ordinary one. On a table. With everybody else’s.”

He didn’t know what to say to that.

“It’s nice,” said Hyvred. “I like it. I wanted you to know.” And went back to her fish.

They had a council.

Not Drew’s kind, with a slate and columns and a grid. Not Higu’s kind either, at the bottom of a stair with a rock. A new kind. Ice didn’t know what to call it. It just happened, in the Float’s meeting-house, a long low turf-roofed hall in the middle of the raft-town with benches round the walls and a fire in the middle, on the sixth night, with thirty-six of them and Haddo and Sabe and Tuli and a dozen of the Float’s people, and nobody in charge.

That was the strange part. Nobody was in charge.

On every floor, in every council, since the white room, there’d been somebody who knew. On the way up it had been him, a floor ahead, even when he wasn’t in the room; they’d all been working out what he’d done and why, and that was a kind of knowing. On the way down it had been him again, out loud, at the bottom of every stair: where. Higu would ask. He’d answer. And they’d build the plan on the answer.

He didn’t have an answer.

So he sat on a bench by the wall with Thea, and didn’t say anything, and watched thirty-five people and fifteen strangers make a plan with no map.

It was the best thing he’d ever seen.

Chris had his notebook open on his knee, the fifteenth, at a new page, and he’d drawn columns. Lots of them. ICE (THE RIVER). ICE (THE MAN), which got a laugh. THE FLOAT. THE NARROWS. THE BREAKER (?). HOUNDS. And one more, at the end, smaller, that Ice could see from where he sat: THE LIGHTS. With a single entry under it, in Chris’s square hand, with a date. Day 675. Hana’s: “Yet.”

Higu had her map. Not on the rock. On a long sheet of birch-bark Haddo had given her, spread on the floor by the fire. She’d been drawing it for five days. From the cliff. From Sabe’s porch. From the top of the Float’s mast, which she’d climbed on the third day without asking anybody and come down from white and silent and drawing. From what everybody told her. The river, winding. The cliff. Keddle on its stilts. The Float. The reed-bays below. And at the bottom of the sheet, the Narrows: two black lines, close together, with Haddo’s shovel hands in them.

“It’s wrong,” she said, to everybody, before anybody could say anything. “It’s wrong in about nine places. I’ve never been to the Narrows. Nobody here’s been to the Narrows in spring but Haddo. It’s what I’ve got.” She looked up. “It’s a map of what we know. That’s all a map is.”

Maigo was sitting on the floor of the meeting-house with his shoes off and his bare feet flat on the planks. He’d been sitting like that for an hour. When Drew said his name he opened his eyes.

“She’s waking up,” said Maigo. Slowly. “The river. I can feel her. Through the rafts, through the ice. It’s like—“ He looked for it. “It’s like a tree in the last week of winter. When the sap’s coming. You can’t see anything. But it’s all coming up at once, everywhere, underneath.” He shut his eyes again. “Three days. Maybe four.”

“Sabe said ten or twelve from when we came,” said Higu. “That’s—“

“Twelve’s the eighth,” said Calvin. “Ten’s the—“

“Three days,” said Sabe.

She was on the bench nearest the fire with her black pole across her knees and Tuli beside her. She hadn’t said anything all night. Everybody turned.

“The tree-boy’s right,” said Sabe. “Three. Maybe less. She’s turning quicker than I thought.” She put her pole down on the planks. Tock. Tipped her head. “Quicker than I’ve ever heard her.”

“Is that bad?” said Drew.

Sabe looked at him with her milky eyes.

“I don’t know,” said Sabe.

Drew sat back. He didn’t laugh this time. But Ice saw his face. The face of a man who’d been asking is it bad of everybody on fourteen floors and finally, on the fifteenth, found a whole floor of people who answered it the way he’d always wanted somebody to. Honestly.

Thea read the window.

That was the part Ice would remember. Afterwards, when he wrote it down, it was the part he wrote first.

Somebody asked about the Breaker. Rui Xiang, he thought. Whether it would show. Whether there’d be a window, like there’d been for the Squall and the Kiln and the Last Sheaf, so they’d know what it was and what level. And Haddo said he didn’t know what a window was. And Tuli said she’d never seen one, but she’d heard the jam last spring, through her gran’s pole, from a day away, and it had sounded like a heart.

And Thea said: “There’s one.”

Everybody looked at her.

She was looking at nothing. At the air in front of her face. At the place where windows came up. Her eyes were moving, a little, side to side, the way they did when she read, the way they’d moved on the first day in the white room when she’d leaned in to her light and said not able isn’t no.

“There’s one,” said Thea. “Over the Narrows. I saw it from the mast this afternoon. Higu was up there, and I went up after her, and I looked down the river, and there’s—“ She stopped. She put her hand up, palm out, the way she did when she wrote a ward. “It’s not open. It’s not a guardian window. It’s just—there. Small. Grey. Like the ones in the Countinghouse were grey.” She frowned. “Nobody else can see it.”

“I can’t see it,” said Higu.

“I know. I asked you. On the mast.” Thea’s eyes moved. “I don’t think it’s for anybody. I think it’s just—the floor. Talking to itself.” She tipped her head, the way Sabe did with her pole. “There’s writing on it. Very small. Like Alys’s hand. I can’t read all of it.”

“What can you read?” said Chris. His pencil was over the page.

Thea was quiet for a moment. Her eyes moved.

“Waking,” she said. “That’s the first word. Waking.” Her eyes went on. “And then something I can’t read. And then a number. And then—“ She stopped.

“And then?”

“Unwritten,” said Thea.

The meeting-house was very quiet. The fire cracked. Out on the river, far off, something boomed.

“That’s the last word,” said Thea. “On the window. Unwritten.” She lowered her hand. She looked at Ice, across the fire, for the first time since she’d started reading. “I think it means—“

“It means it’s not in the book,” said Chris. Very quietly. “The great book. In Tell. The two first years. The left page and the right.” He was looking at his columns. “It means this isn’t on either of them.”

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Ice sat on the bench by the wall and looked at Thea, and thought about a forest of black pines that wasn’t there, and Alea’s grey logs, and a line in World with no name on it that had come on time and been wrong. It stopped, Alea had said, on the stair. It stopped serving the first one.

It hadn’t just stopped serving it.

It had stopped writing.

“Huh,” said Gabe. From the back. In the same voice he’d used on the ledge.

“Classic,” said Hako. But he didn’t sound like he thought it was.

And Thea, across the fire, held Ice’s eye. And her face did nothing at all. And then, very slowly, at the corner of her mouth, the way it had at the door of the Countinghouse when she put her hand on his head on her way in to read the book, something that wasn’t a smile, and wasn’t not one.

Then we find out, said her face.

He nodded.

He asked how.

He didn’t notice he was doing it. That was the thing. Higu noticed. Higu noticed everything about where; it was her job. But he didn’t, until afterwards.

It was near the end of the council. They’d worked out most of it, the thirty-six and the Float together, in pieces, out loud, with Chris writing it all down and Higu drawing it on the birch-bark. The Float would cut its channels: long lanes of open water hacked through the ice round its edges, so that when the Mother woke the rafts could swing free and not be crushed. Gabe and Jared and Salty would make iron for it, spikes and rings and long hooks, at the Float’s little forge, which had never had three smiths in it at once and which the Float’s smith, a woman called Mairead with arms like Gabe’s, said she’d been waiting her whole life for. The healers would set up in the meeting-house. Rui Xiang and Winter and Aleaka would go out on the ice and watch for hounds, because the hounds came up when the ice started to talk. Everybody under seventy would hunt, every day, in fours, hard, above their level, because they were going to need every level they could get. Calvin would scout. Calvin would always scout. With Tuli. Calvin had said so first, before anybody else could, and Tuli had looked at him with enormous contempt and said fine.

And somebody had to go to the Narrows. Before the Mother woke. To see the jam. To listen to it. To find out what was in it.

“Chris’s party,” said Drew. “They’re the ones who work things out. And they’ve got Maigo, who can hear it. And Niko, who doesn’t need to make a sound to fight. And Winter, who’ll freeze anything that comes up. And Ina.” He looked at Chris. “If you want it.”

Chris looked at his columns.

“We want it,” he said.

“And Sabe,” said Tuli. “And me. You’ll need somebody who’s heard the jam. Through a pole.” She lifted her chin at her gran. “Gran’s heard fifty-eight. I’ve heard one. Through her pole. Last year.”

“You’ll need me,” said Sabe. “Tuli’s coming. Nobody else from the Float’s going near the Narrows in the week she wakes. Not for climbers.” She looked at Chris with her milky eyes. “I’ll come.”

That was when Ice said it.

He’d been sitting on the bench by the wall with Thea for the whole of the council and he hadn’t said a word, because he didn’t have any, and because he was watching. And Chris’s party were going to the Narrows, a day’s walk on the ice, with an old woman and a girl, to listen to something nobody had ever seen that woke up some years and took half a town, and he didn’t know what it was. And on any other floor he’d have stood up and said where, and what, and here’s what it does, and it goes for whoever holds still, and the soft spot’s under the jaw.

He didn’t know any of that.

So he leaned forward on the bench, and looked at Sabe, and said:

“How do you fight it?”

Sabe looked at him.

“How do you fight it,” said Ice again. “The jam. The Breaker. If it’s awake. If it holds too long and it’s going to go all at once.” He swallowed. “I don’t know. I’ve never seen one. I don’t know how. Do you?”

The meeting-house was very quiet.

Sabe looked at him for a long time with her milky eyes. Then she put her pole down on the planks, tock, and tipped her head, and listened. And lifted it.

“You don’t fight it,” said Sabe. “You can’t fight the Mother. You can’t fight ice.” She was quiet. “But the thing in the jam. If it’s there. My gran said once. Her gran said.” She looked at the fire. “You don’t fight it. You let it go. You find where it’s holding. And you make it let go. Early. Before it’s ready. Bit at a time. So it can’t go all at once.”

“How?”

“I don’t know,” said Sabe. “Gran didn’t say. Gran didn’t know either. Nobody’s ever done it.” She looked at him. “That’s the truth. That’s all I’ve got.”

“That’s plenty,” said Ice. And meant it.

Across the room, on the floor by the birch-bark map, Higu was looking at him. With her chalk in her hand. With a look on her face he’d never seen there.

She had the rock in her lap. The new one. With A FOREST on the left and A RIVER in the middle and HE DOESN’T KNOW on the right. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she wrote something underneath. In the right-hand column. Small. Square. He couldn’t see what from the bench.

She showed him, later. On the edge of the Float, in the dark.

HE ASKED HOW.

“You’ve never asked how,” said Higu. “Not once. In six hundred days. You’ve told me where. You’ve told me what. You’ve never once asked anybody how.” She put the rock away. “That’s new.”

“That’s new,” said Ice.

“Good,” said Higu. And went to bed.

Thea asked for a name that night.

They were sitting on the edge of the Float, on the last raft, where the planks ended and the ice began, with their legs over the edge and the lantern between them turned down low. The river was talking. All the time now. Louder than on the first night. The tick and the creak and the crack and the hiss and the long low groan, and under it all, through the planks, through the ice, a sort of hum, very faint, like a crowd a long way off. He could tell most of it apart now. He still didn’t know what it meant.

They’d been doing the names since eleven. Since the first night on the Patience, after the aurora, when she’d said don’t tell me how, tell me who they were. One a night, on watches, when there was a watch. Not every night. Not when there was somewhere being watched that shouldn’t hear. Ina had been the first. Then a long gap, through the Sea and the Salt and Tell and the whole way down, because there was always somewhere listening. They didn’t hurry it.

“Do you still want to?” said Thea. “Now?”

He looked at her.

“Now that you don’t know what’s up here,” said Thea. “Now that it’s not—“ She stopped. “It’s not the same tower. Not above fourteen. It’s not the one where it happened. Maybe it doesn’t matter, now. Who they were. In the other one.”

He thought about it. For a long time. He looked out at the ice, at the dark river going away under the stars toward the Narrows, at the faint lights of Keddle on its stilts on the far bank.

“More,” he said.

Thea looked at him.

“It matters more,” said Ice. “I don’t know the floors any more. I don’t know the places. I’ve lost all of that. All of it. Alea’s got four entries for fifteen and Tuli’s better than both of us.” He was quiet. “I’ve still got them. Who they were. That’s all I’ve got left that’s from before. The people.” He looked at her. “The places were the tower’s. The people were always ours.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a while. Then she put her hand on his arm. Just above the wrist. Where she always put it.

“Then tell me one,” she said.

He thought.

“Xav,” he said.

Thea’s hand stayed where it was.

“He read the room,” said Ice. “Every room. On every floor. He’d stand in the doorway before anybody went in, and just stand there, very still, the way he does now. And then he’d tell you. Where everybody was going to go. Who was going to go first, and who’d follow, and where the thing in the room would come from, and when. He was never wrong. Not once. Not in ten years.” He looked at the ice. “He never went in first. He said somebody had to see the whole room. From outside. So when it went wrong, somebody would know where everybody was.” He was quiet. “He was in my party. The first time. Me and Deston and Gabe and Xav. At the end, he was—“ He stopped. That was how. That was too close to how. “He was the best of us at waiting,” he said instead. “He taught me. Without meaning to. I watched him stand in doorways for ten years, and I learned it.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long moment.

“Xav,” she said.

Like putting something away somewhere safe.

They sat on the edge of the Float with their legs over the ice and her hand on his arm, and the river talking in the dark, and didn’t say anything else.

It woke on the twelfth night.

He was asleep. For once. In the Float’s meeting-house, on a bench by the wall, with Thea asleep against him and the fire low and thirty people asleep on the floor all round. Chris’s party had gone to the Narrows two days before, with Sabe and Tuli; their threads were a long way off down the river, five of them, steady, sleeping. Calvin was asleep under the long table, because he said it was where you found things. The goat was asleep by the fire. Alea was curled on his chest.

And the river spoke.

He felt it before he heard it. He felt it in the bench under him, and the planks under the bench, and the raft under the planks, and the ice under the raft. A shock. A single enormous jolt, like the whole of the floor had been struck once from underneath by something the size of a hill. Everything in the meeting-house jumped. The fire jumped. Thea’s head came up off his shoulder. Thirty people sat up at once.

And then the sound arrived.

A crack. Not a boom. A crack. From far up the valley, at the head of the river, miles off. Sharp and huge and flat, like the sky had been split down the middle with an axe. It came down the valley at them across the ice and went through the meeting-house and through them and away down the river toward the Narrows, and after it, behind it, filling the whole valley from side to side, a long rising roar like a sea coming in.

Outside, all over the Float, in the dark, he heard the poles go down.

Tock. Tock. Tock. Tock. Dozens. Hundreds. Every pole on the Float. Every pole at Keddle, across the ice. Everybody on fifteen, all at once, putting their poles down on the ice in the dark and listening to the same thing.

He went out.

He didn’t decide to. He was just out, on the edge of the Float, with Thea beside him and the lantern swinging, and thirty-four people behind them coming out of the meeting-house in their coats. Haddo was at the foot of the mast with his pole down on the planks and his head tipped. The whole river under the stars was moving.

Not much. Not yet. But moving. The great white flat sweep of it, a mile wide, from bank to bank, was groaning. All of it. Every crack was talking at once. The pressure ridges were heaving. Far out in the middle, a long dark lead opened in the ice, black water showing, running away north and south for as far as he could see, with a sound like a curtain tearing. And the Float moved under his feet. Just a little. A lift and a settle, the way a ship does at anchor when the tide turns.

“She’s awake,” said Haddo. Without lifting his head. “The first word.”

“The first word?” said Ice.

“That,” said Haddo. He nodded up the valley, toward the place the crack had come from. “The first crack. At the head. Every year. We call it the first word.” He lifted his pole. “That’s her saying she’s awake.”

The river roared.

And down the valley, a long way off, at the Narrows, where five threads in Ice’s chest had just gone taut all at once, wide awake and frightened, a window came up.

Not grey. Not small. Not the floor talking to itself. A real window. Over the place where the river went through the gap in the rock. The way a window came up over the Squall, over the Kiln, over a straw man in a barn. He could see it from here. They all could. Thirty-four people on the edge of the Float in the dark, looking down the river at a window over the Narrows, a mile off and a day’s walk on.

Nothing else. No level. No name. Just that.

And under it, where on every other floor there’d always been a number, smaller, in grey, in the hand like Alys’s that only Thea could read, one word.

Thea read it. He saw her read it. Her eyes moved, once, in the lantern-light.

“Waking,” said Thea.

He looked at the window. He looked at it for a long time, with the river roaring under the stars and the Float lifting and settling under his feet and five threads a day’s walk off pulled tight as wire. Chris. Niko. Maigo. Winter. Ina. And Sabe and Tuli with them. At the Narrows. Right under it.

He didn’t know what it was.

He didn’t know what it did, or how big it was, or where it was thin, or what it would do when it woke the rest of the way. He didn’t know if they could fight it, or if you could only let it go. He didn’t know how. Nobody did. Nobody had ever done it.

He’d never been more frightened in either life.

And he’d never once, he realised, standing on the edge of a town that knew it was going to move, with Thea’s hand in his and thirty-four people behind him who didn’t know either, felt less alone.

“I don’t know what it is,” he said.

“I know,” said Thea.

“Chris’s party’s right under it.”

“I know.”

“I don’t know how.”

Thea turned the lantern up. The amber light came out across the planks and the ice and lay on the river toward the Narrows, flat, leaning, the way it always leaned toward him. Except it wasn’t leaning toward him now. He saw that. For the first time since floor 3. It was leaning down the river. Toward the window. Toward the five threads.

Toward where they were going.

“Then we find out,” said Thea. “All of us.”

Behind them, Haddo lifted his pole off the planks, and turned to his town, and said, in his slow deep voice, the words Ice would learn later that the Steerer of the Float said every year on the night of the first word, the way the Reeve on the Acre said when the bell goes, the field’s not yours:

“Cut loose. Hold on. Listen.”
