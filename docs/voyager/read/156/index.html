Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 156

Every Line

4,727 words · 21 min read

The tall clerk read the left page first.

It didn’t look at him while it did it. It looked down at the book, open on the long table between its grey hands, and read, in the flat ordinary voice that had said told at the well and where did you come from at the lectern, and its voice went out into the great hall of the Assize the way the evening telling went out over the city.

“The first year.”

He’d known it would say that. Thea had told him. He hadn’t known what it would do to him to hear it said out loud.

“Thirty-four. Up the well. Autumn. Told: Aldo Pellow, teller, and the Count.”

And then the names.

He’d never heard them like that. Not in either life. Not all together. Not as they’d been.

The first time, he’d heard them one at a time. Over ten years. Each one on its own, on a different floor, in a different voice, in a different kind of quiet. And then he’d carried them. Four hundred days on top of the ten years, in his chest, like stones in a coat. And then, since the top of floor 10, he’d been giving them to Thea. One a night. On watches. At the stern of a ship. On the Salt. Every name. Not tonight.

He’d never heard anybody read them. All together. In a row. In the order an old man had written them down, in a happy shaking hand, at the door of the Climbers’ House on a morning in the autumn, when every single one of them had been alive.

The clerk read them. Flat. Ordinary. One after another. Like a teller reading the door-roll.

He heard Gabe’s name. Standing a pace to his right, with Hearthwall.

He heard Calvin’s. Behind him, in the line.

Deston’s. Yan’s. Hana’s. Drew’s. He heard every one of them, in the clerk’s flat voice, in the great hall, and every one of them was a person standing behind him now, alive, breathing, on their feet. And every one of them, on that page, in that autumn, was a person who’d gone up the stair under the Assize and been cried at the Reckoning once a year, higher and higher, for ten years.

He heard his own.

Declan.

And then hers.

Althea.

Thea’s hand found his. At his side. Below the edge of the long table, where nobody could see. Her fingers weren’t cold any more.

He held on.

He didn’t look at the hall while it read. He couldn’t. He looked at the screen, the way Gabe was looking at the screen, a pace to his right, like a wall. At the carving. Lines and lines of old writing, too small and too close to read.

But he could feel the hall.

And he could feel that something was wrong with it.

Not wrong. Different. He’d been watching rooms for ten years and four hundred and seven days, and he knew what a room did when it was listening to something. It leaned. It went still in a certain way. He’d felt the whole of Tell lean toward the Assize steps an hour ago, when the clerk said the climbers stand on the thirteenth.

The hall wasn’t leaning now.

It was shifting. A creak of a bench. A cough. Somebody at the back saying something under their breath. The sound a room makes when it can hear somebody talking and can’t make out the words.

He moved his eyes. Not his head. Just his eyes, to the left, along the front bench, to the end of it. To Aldo, in his rush chair on the aisle, with his writing-board on his knees.

Aldo was leaning forward. All the way. With his bright bird’s eyes on the clerk’s mouth, and his whole thin face screwed up with listening, the way old men listen when they’re losing their hearing and won’t admit it. And as Ice watched, Aldo put a hand up behind his ear.

The clerk had just said Aldo Pellow, teller. Out loud. In the great hall. His own name, in the first line of the left page.

Aldo hadn’t heard it.

He was frowning. Hard. And then he turned his head and looked at Davy, his grandson, beside him, and said something, and Davy shook his head and said something back, and Ice could read it on his lips from where he stood.

It’s just pages, Gramps.

It read the cries.

At the Reckoning, the Count’s clerk cried from the Assize steps: the climbers stand on the— and a floor. Ten of them. One a year. Higher every year.

He didn’t hear the floors either. He didn’t let himself. He held on to Thea’s hand and looked at the screen and let them go past him, ten years, like the stair going past under your feet when you’ve stopped counting.

“At the Reckoning, the Count’s clerk cried from the Assize steps: the climber stands on the hundredth.”

The climber.

“One.”

Thea’s hand tightened on his until it hurt.

“Declan. Chose.”

“Record one of two.”

The clerk stopped.

And the hall didn’t make a sound. Not because it was holding its breath. Because it didn’t know what it had heard.

“Read it!”

Hamo Tull was on his feet. On the front bench of the right, with his chain swinging and his rings flashing in the light from the high windows, and his pleasant face gone a dark ugly red.

“Read it properly! What is that? What was that? That’s not reading, that’s—“ He swung round to the hall. To the benches. Two thousand faces. “Did anybody hear that? Did anybody? A word? A name?”

Nobody said anything.

“It’s pages,” said Tull. “It’s just—it’s the sound of pages. It said his name and then it was pages.” He turned to the screen. “I asked for it read in full. Every line. I asked the Count. The Bench granted it. Read it so they can hear it—“

“It is read,” said the Magistrate.

The page-turning voice. From behind the screen. Everywhere in the hall at once.

Tull stopped. His mouth stayed open.

“Every line,” said the Magistrate. “In full.”

“Nobody heard a word!”

There was a silence.

And then the voice behind the screen said, quite softly, like a page turning in an empty room:

“Those who were told heard it.”

The hall went absolutely still.

He felt it happen. Two thousand people, all at once, hearing that, and understanding it, and doing the thing people always do when they’re told that somebody in the room knows something they don’t.

They looked for who.

He felt them do it. A great slow turn of heads, all over the hall, from the benches and the window ledges and the aisles and the doors. Looking at the front. At the long table. At the corded climber in front of it, and the two people on either side of him.

At Thea.

She’d known they would. She’d said so, on a lead roof, eleven nights ago, with John off to one side. Everyone in that hall is going to look at him. And some of them are going to look at me. Because I’ll know what it means, and everybody else will be finding out, and they’ll look at the one person whose face isn’t changing.

He felt them start to.

And beside him, a pace to his right, Gabe didn’t move.

Gabe didn’t look at Thea. He didn’t look at Ice. He didn’t look at Tull, or the clerk, or the book. He stood with Hearthwall on his arm and his feet set and his face like a wall, and looked straight ahead at the screen. At the carved dark wood. At whatever was behind it. As if there was nothing else in the hall worth looking at.

And he was the biggest man in the room.

Ice felt it happen. He felt two thousand people start to turn toward Thea, and see the big man on the corded one’s right standing like a wall and staring at the screen, and stop. And follow his eyes.

To the screen.

They all looked at the screen.

Wherever you look, they’ll look, Thea had said.

He didn’t look at her. He didn’t need to. He felt her hand in his go, for one second, completely still. And then squeeze, once, very hard.

The clerk read the right page.

And this time, the hall heard it.

He knew straight away. He felt the room lean. Two thousand people, all at once, leaning in, the way they’d leaned on the Assize steps an hour ago. Because they could hear it. Every word.

“The first year. Thirty-six. Up the well. Spring. Told: Hawise Brand, teller, and the Count.”

And the names. All thirty-six. In the clerk’s flat voice. In Hawise’s order, the order they’d come up to the well’s lid in the Lantern Market on the first day and said their names one at a time. Tiger first. Then Yan. Then the rest. Calvin. Rachael. Dennis. Rachael and Dennis, said out loud in the great hall of the Assize, in a list of thirty-six, every one alive.

He heard the hall hear them. He heard people in the benches say them under their breath. Tiger. Calvin. Somebody, somewhere, laughed, short and surprised, at Calvin. The Low Town knew Calvin.

“Declan,” read the clerk. “Already told.”

A murmur.

“Althea. Observing.”

A longer one.

“Record two of two.”

And then the clerk’s eyes went back to the left page. To the margin. And it read, in the same flat voice, as if it were the same kind of line as all the others:

“In a second hand. In the margin. Beside one. Told. Althea.”

The hall heard that.

He felt it hear it. A ripple. All the way back.

“Witnessed.”

And then the right margin. “In a second hand. Beside already told. Told. Althea. Witnessed.”

And then the line across the binding. Under both pages. Holding them together, or pulling them apart.

“One record does not match.”

The clerk closed its mouth. It didn’t close the book.

“Who writes him?” said the Magistrate.

For a moment, nobody moved.

He stood in front of the long table with Thea’s hand in his and Gabe on his right, and the stack of slates in front of him, and the chalk, and two thousand people behind him. And for one long moment, nobody moved at all, and he thought: this is it. This is where nobody stands. And the second hand stands. And he’s whatever that says.

Already told.

Then, behind him and to his left, on the aisle, a voice said, cross and dry and carrying:

“Well? Davy. Push. I’m first. I meant to be first.”

And a rush chair creaked out of its place at the end of the front bench, and came down the aisle, pushed by a sulky young man, with an old man in it holding a writing-board on his knees as if it were a shield.

They stopped at the long table. On Ice’s left. Beside Thea.

Aldo didn’t take a slate from the stack. He had his own. He’d practised. He held his board out to Orlo, at the end of the table, with the cream paper clipped to it, and said, “Read it. Loud. I’ve waited sixty years and I’ve lost my voice.”

Orlo took it. He looked at it. He looked at Ice.

He read it.

“Aldo Pellow. Teller, sixty years, retired. Declan. Ice. Came up the hill. Took him forty-six days. Asked for me first.”

He laid it on the table, face up.

Aldo looked up at Ice from his chair. He didn’t say anything. He just nodded, once, the way he had in the square. And then he turned his chair round, with his own hands, on the wheels, slowly, so that he was facing the hall. Sitting on Ice’s left. Facing everybody.

I’ll sit on your left.

Thea let go of his hand.

She stepped up to the table. She took a slate from the stack. A Tellhouse slate, and a piece of chalk, like everybody. She didn’t use her own pen. He watched her not use it. She wrote with the chalk, in her tidy upright hand, and the whole hall watched her do it, and he could feel them wanting to look at her and looking at the screen instead, where Gabe was looking, like a wall.

She gave it to Orlo.

Orlo read it.

“Althea. I have read both hands. They are the same man. Both are told.”

He laid it on the table.

And then, under it, because she’d written one more line, smaller, at the bottom, and Orlo was a teller and read every line:

“He waits on steps now. He didn’t used to.”

Somebody in the benches laughed. Softly. Not unkindly. And then somebody else.

Thea came back and stood at his left, between him and Aldo’s chair, and didn’t take his hand again. She didn’t need to. She stood there close enough that her shoulder was against his arm.

Gabe went next.

He didn’t take his eyes off the screen while he did it. He put Hearthwall down against the table leg, warm, glowing faintly, and took a slate with one hand and wrote with the other without looking at what he was writing, and somehow it came out straight. He gave it to Orlo and picked Hearthwall up again and went back to being a wall.

“Gabe,” read Orlo. “I’ve known him since he was thirteen. Whatever that says. It’s still Dec.”

And then they came.

All of them. He didn’t turn round. He couldn’t. He stood at the table with Thea’s shoulder against his arm and Gabe a pace to his right looking at the screen, and Aldo’s chair on his left facing the hall, and heard them come down off the bench behind him, one after another, and take slates, and write. And Orlo read them. And when Orlo’s voice went, Hawise read them. And when Hawise’s went, Askew stood up off her stool by the tellers’ bench and read them herself, in a dry old voice that carried to the back of the hall without trying.

“Drew. He makes people stand where they need to. He always has.”

“Deston. I’ve never needed him to tell me anything.”

“Rachael. He says it hurts now.”

“Malcolm. He asked.”

“Xola. He heals at sixty now. It’s very annoying.” A laugh. All over the hall. Xola, at the table, went pink and glared at nobody.

“Yan. His light is old and bright at the same time. Like two lamps in one glass.”

“Higu. Two columns. Both true.”

“Azac. And so our hero let the line hold him.”

“Cursiffe. I’ve written down everything I’ve seen him do since floor 6. I won’t write the rest. It’s his.”

“Dennis. He sat down in the snow.”

“HYVRED,” read Askew, and paused, and read the rest in exactly the same capitals Hyvred had written them in, which was hard to do in a voice, and she did it. “HE IS THE BRIGHTEST THING I HAVE EVER SEEN AND I SEE EVERYTHING.”

“Rui Xiang. He’s early. Not fast. Early.”

“John. Off to one side. I’ve watched him for a year and a half. He never once looks at himself. He’s always looking at one of us. Making sure. That’s him. I was there.”

“Chi. He breathes now. He didn’t use to breathe.”

“Tiger. At the top of the stair on floor 12, he said: no, you. I wasn’t late. I waited.”

“Salty. He said it was the best thing I ever made. He was right. He’s always right. It’s really annoying.”

“Wendy. He doesn’t turn round when he’s hit. I’ve seen it. You couldn’t tell.”

“Hana. Four seconds.”

“Jared. Man.” A pause. Askew turned the slate over, as if there might be more on the back. There wasn’t. “Man,” she read again, with great dignity, and the hall laughed so hard the ushers looked worried.

And on. And on. All of them. Every one of the thirty-three, and every slate face up on the long table in front of him, until there wasn’t any table left, and Orlo started stacking them at the end, face up, in rows.

He stood there and listened to them.

He couldn’t do anything else. He stood in front of the long table in the great hall of the Assize, with a cord on his wrist and two thousand people behind him, and listened to thirty-five people he loved write him down. As they knew him. In their own hands. Not one of them saying what was on the left page. Not one of them asking. Every one of them writing the man they knew.

And every one of them, he thought, every single one, was true to both.

Calvin was last of the climbers.

He came down the aisle and stopped at the table and didn’t take a slate. He stood there with his fist shut against his chest, the way it had been for eighty-two days, and looked at the screen.

Then he held his fist up. High. Over his head. Where the whole hall could see it.

And opened it.

There was nothing on his palm. Nothing anybody could see. A palm, callused at the base of the fingers from his daggers, a bit dirty.

“Calvin,” he said. Out loud. To the screen. In his loud voice, the one he’d had since he was born, the one he used so everybody would know where he was. “I haven’t got ink. Ina wrote him in my hand. On the Salt. With his finger. Second from last. Right there.” He touched the place near the base of his thumb. “It’s not written. But it’s his name. And I’ve been carrying it around for eighty-two days.” His voice cracked. He let it. “That’s my slate.”

He stood there with his hand up.

The hall was silent.

And then the voice behind the screen said, like a page turning, very quietly:

“Read.”

Calvin looked at the screen. His hand was shaking.

“It’s read,” said the Magistrate. “Told.”

Calvin made a sound. He put his hand down. Not shut. Open. And picked up a slate after all, with that hand, and wrote on it, fast and big and wobbly, and gave it to Askew, and went back to the bench without looking at anybody.

Askew read it.

“He’s in my hand.”

And then Tell.

Hawise’s list. Seventy-nine. He heard them come down off the benches behind the climbers, and he heard the hall see them, and go quiet in a different way. Because these weren’t climbers. These were people the hall knew. People from the Lantern Market and the Hay Market and the coopers’ lanes, in their market clothes, coming down the aisle one at a time to the long table in the great hall of the Assize to write a corded man.

People choosing.

“Nell Fother, of the half-door. His bun’s free. I said so.”

“Joan Tull. He made my shop come back. Him and his scribe.” A pause. “And his tiger doesn’t eat apples.”

“Bertha, baker. I shut my shutter on him. He never once looked at it. I’ve been putting flour on his step since.”

“Wat, of the watch. Never been any trouble.”

“Edith. He looks at her the way my Wat looks at me when he thinks I’m asleep.” A great shout of laughter from the back. Wat, on the bench, put his face in his hands. Thea, at Ice’s shoulder, made a very small sound.

“Ivo Rook. I would like to hear how it ends.”

The knife-grinder. The lamplighter. The cooper and his brother. The two old sisters, who’d written the same slate between them, which Askew said was allowed, it was two hands. The toothless porter: He took my squeak. The man from the Cloth Hall. The cheese woman. Mothers from the Tanners’ Row whose children had been at the pump.

And Rafe Collier.

Ice heard him come. He’d have known the walk anywhere. He’d heard it behind him on the Wellgate, once, a step out of a doorway, and then the weight of something thrown.

Rafe came down the aisle in his leather apron, a big man, with his right hand still pink across the palm where the lime had been, and stopped at the table, a pace from Ice. He didn’t look at him. He took a slate and wrote on it with his big rough careful hand, pressing so hard the chalk squeaked.

He gave it to Askew.

“Rafe Collier, tanner,” read Askew. “I threw mud at him with a stone in it. On the Wellgate. On his back. It hit him hard. He didn’t turn round. I meant it when I threw it. Then I didn’t.” A pause. “I’d have written him first.”

Rafe stood there. Then, without looking at Ice, he put his pink right hand out, palm up, on the edge of the table, between them. Just for a moment.

Ice looked at it.

He put his own hand on it. Briefly. The way you’d put a hand on a wall.

Rafe took his back and went up the aisle and didn’t look round.

The last one came in from outside.

He didn’t see her come. Nobody did. The great doors of the Assize had been open all the time, with the square full beyond them and people standing on the steps. And then there was somebody standing in the doorway who hadn’t been there.

Tall. In long plain grey, with the hood down. Brown hair pulled back off a plain face.

No book.

The hall saw her. It went quiet from the back first, from the doors, and the quiet came down the aisle ahead of her as she walked, like the quiet that had gone round the Lantern Market on the first morning. People stepped out of her way. Not the way they stepped out of a clerk’s way, without looking. They looked. All of them. At a clerk of the Count with no book on her arm, walking down the middle of the great hall at the Reckoning, which had never happened in the history of Tell.

At the back of the hall, against the far wall, a hundred and eleven grey heads lifted.

He heard Hawise, at the end of the long table, stop breathing.

The woman came down the aisle to the table. She stopped in front of it. Next to Rafe’s slate. She looked at the stack of slates, face up, in rows. At the chalk. At her own right hand, empty, with ink on the wrist.

She picked up a piece of chalk.

Her hand shook. It shook so much she nearly dropped it. She held it in her fist, like a child holds a crayon, and pulled a slate toward her, and wrote. Slowly. One letter at a time. Very carefully. As if it were the first thing she’d written in twelve years that nobody had told her to write.

It was.

She held it out. Not to Askew. Not to Orlo.

To Hawise.

Hawise took it. Her hands were shaking as badly as the other woman’s. She looked down at the slate.

And read it out. In a voice that went, and came back, and went.

“Alys Brand.”

The hall didn’t breathe.

“I was in the fourth row. She carried a light past me, and it showed me my face. When I came out, he was sitting on the step. With her. On the bottom step. In the dark.” Hawise stopped. Started again. “Told.”

She put the slate down on the table. Face up.

And the woman in grey looked at her. Across the end of the long table. With Hawise’s grey-blue eyes, too clever for the plain face round them, and a small white scar on her lower lip.

“Hawise,” said Alys.

All of it. Out loud. The whole name.

Hawise put both her hands flat on the table and bent her head over them and didn’t make a sound. And Alys reached across the end of the table, slowly, the way you reach for something you’re not sure is real, and put her hand on top of both of Hawise’s.

At the back of the hall, a hundred and eleven grey heads stayed lifted, and looked.

Nobody else came.

The table was covered. Slates, face up, in rows, from one end to the other, and stacked at the end, and three on the floor where Orlo had run out of room. A hundred and fifteen. Thirty-five climbers, seventy-nine of Tell, and one in grey.

And Aldo’s board. On top. Up and down, like a fence on a hill.

The hall was silent.

Behind the screen, the page began to turn.

He heard it. Everybody heard it. A long, dry, slow sound, like a great many pages being turned very slowly in a room with nobody in it. Reading. Every slate. Every line. The way it had read the boundaries and the dowry and the well and Wace’s scratch and Joan’s will. Weighing each one. Against the first hand. And against the second.

It went on for a long time.

Longer than for anything else that day. Longer than he’d have believed a room full of two thousand people could stay quiet. Long enough that the sun moved across the high windows and the light came down onto the long table and lay across the slates, and lit up the chalk.

Thea’s shoulder was against his arm. Gabe was a pace to his right, looking at the screen. Aldo was on his left, in his chair, facing the hall, with his knotted hands on his rug.

He didn’t know what it would say.

He thought about the window. You will not know if they can. He thought about the left page, and the right. About already told. About a man on his knees in a white room, a minute before anybody opened their eyes, with a card in his hand and a fingertip of grey.

The page stopped turning.

“The first hand,” said the Magistrate.

The hall leaned.

“Thirty-four. Autumn. Told.”

A sound, all over the hall. Not words.

“The second hand. Thirty-six. Spring. Told.”

The voice behind the screen was quiet. It didn’t change. It never changed. But Ice heard something in it, under the page turning, that he’d never heard in any voice in the tower, in either life.

He’d heard it in the Steward’s hand, stopping on the page at my name’s Drew. He’d heard it in the tall clerk’s hand, shaking, over Thea’s line.

Something that didn’t know what to do.

“A hundred and fifteen hands,” said the Magistrate. “True to the first. True to the second.”

A long silence.

“Both hands stand.”

He didn’t hear the hall.

He knew it made a sound. He felt it. Two thousand people, and the square outside, and the window ledges, and the doors. He felt it come at him from behind like weather. He didn’t hear it. He was looking at the cord on his wrist.

It was still there.

Thea was looking at it too.

And then, behind the screen, something stood up.
