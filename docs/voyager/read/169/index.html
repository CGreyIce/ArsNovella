Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 169

The Tab

7,029 words · 31 min read

It’s always fine.

Hako had been saying it his whole life, and people had been telling him for his whole life that it wasn’t true, as if he didn’t know.

He knew. Of course he knew. He wasn’t stupid. Things were not always fine. Things were quite often the opposite of fine. That wasn’t what it meant. It didn’t mean this is fine. It meant this way. You said it facing the way you wanted to go, the way you’d point with a spear, so that you knew which way you were facing when everything else had gone sideways. Bad nights end. That was all it meant. Whatever this is, it’s a night, and nights end.

He’d said it to Ice once on a call at four in the morning on Earth, after a very bad night, when they were both too tired to be anything but honest. And Ice had laughed so hard he’d had to mute himself, and come back a minute later still laughing, and said Hako, that’s the most you thing anyone has ever said, and Hako had said yeah, well, it’s true, and Ice had said I know. That’s why it’s funny.

He thought about that a lot, the second night after the step.

He was on the shields. Higu’s rota had him on the late watch, midnight to four, with Salty, at the wall in front of the line. Two shields, rims touching, grounded on the stone. The others asleep behind them in their places along the east wall. In front of them, fifteen paces of frosted floor, and the old woman on her stool, and the iron foot out of its socket, and the rest of it going up into the dark.

The cold came off it like heat comes off a fire. You felt it on your face first. Then your hands, on the grip. Then it was in the shield. Hako had been carrying the same shield since floor 10, since Geir’s forge under the Bastion, after the Sower cracked his old one through. It was good hold-folk oak with an iron rim. After an hour facing the straw man, the front of it was white. A fur of frost a finger thick, all over the face, as if he’d left it out in a winter. He knocked it off with the butt of his spear on the hour, and it came off in plates, and an hour later it was back.

He leaned on the shield.

Don’t step back, Ice used to say. On Earth. Forty-one times; Hako had counted, eventually, because Ice kept saying it and it kept being right. Lean in. If it’s pushing you, lean in. Nothing was pushing him. It was just cold. He leaned in anyway, a little, into the white, because it felt like the right way to face.

“You’re the one with the spear,” said the old woman.

She hadn’t looked round. She’d been sitting on her stool at the straw man’s feet for six nights, with a blanket round her shoulders that somebody from Lammas brought her every evening and took away every morning, and her hands in her lap, looking up. She looked up at its face the way you’d look at a sky, to see what it was going to do.

“Yeah,” said Hako.

“You brought me the soup.”

“He made it. I just carried it the last bit.”

“I know who made it.” She still didn’t look round. “I’m asking who carried it.”

“Hako.”

The old woman thought about that.

“Hild,” she said. “Of Lammas.” She moved her head a little, the way you’d nod if your neck hurt. “Nobody’s asked me that since my husband. Sixty years I’ve been doing his face, and nobody’s asked my name in forty of them. They just call me the old one from Lammas.” She sniffed. “I am the old one from Lammas. But I’ve got a name.”

“Hild,” said Hako.

“Hild.”

Beside him, Salty shifted his feet on the stone and said nothing. Salty didn’t say much on a watch. He said it was because he was concentrating. Hako thought it was because he was asleep with his eyes open, but he’d never been able to prove it.

“Can I ask you something, Hild?”

“You can ask.”

“Why don’t you come back?” He nodded at the line of shields, though she couldn’t see it. “Behind us. You can sit right here. Same view. It’s warmer. Well. Less cold.”

“I know it’s less cold.”

“Then—“

“Because somebody has to sit with him,” said Hild, “who isn’t behind something.”

Hako didn’t say anything.

“Your lot are kind,” said Hild. “I’ve never seen kinder. You sat with him. You warmed him and you took his cold out and you watered your green boy at his feet. I’ve never seen passers do that. I’ve never seen anybody do that.” She was looking up at the face. “But you do it from behind a wall. That’s right. You should. You’ve got things to keep. But somebody has to be in front of him with nothing. Or he’ll think that’s all there is. Walls.” She pulled the blanket a little closer. “Nobody wants to be kept by a wall.”

“Are you frightened?” said Hako.

“Course I’m frightened.”

“Then—“

“I’m eighty-one,” said Hild. “You’ve got to be something, at eighty-one. I’m being this.” She sniffed again. “Don’t tell my daughter. She thinks I’m being stubborn.”

“Are you?”

“Yes,” said Hild. “That too.”

Hako laughed. He didn’t mean to. It came out of him into the cold and went up in a cloud and the straw man’s face, up in the dark, didn’t move. Hild didn’t look round, but he could see her cheek go up, a bit, at the side.

“What’s he made of?” said Hako. “The straw. I mean, I know it’s straw. Is it just the last field?”

“The last sheaf’s the heart,” said Hild. “The neck. Your green-haired girl’s man cut it, at the end. But the rest of him’s every field.” She lifted one hand out of her lap and pointed up, along the body, the arms, the legs. “Every village brings its sheaves up on the carts at harvest-home. One from every field it’s got. Haverley’s Long Meadow and its Gorse Piece and Old Barn Piece. Ashwell’s orchard grass. Lammas’s top fields. Everybody’s. We bind them on. So he’s the whole Acre. Every field there is.” She put her hand back in her lap. “That’s why he keeps the winter. He’s got all of it in him. All the year. And in the spring we take him down and every village takes a bit of him home and ploughs it into the first furrow of its first field. So it goes back in.”

“So the harvest goes back in.”

“So it goes back in.”

Hako looked up at the white body in the dark. At the frost on all of it now, every sheaf, every band, from the neck all the way down to the feet. Every field in the Acre, white.

“And the ones the grey took,” he said. “Old Barn Piece. Gorse Piece. Are they in him?”

Hild didn’t answer for a long time.

“They were cut before the grey came,” she said at last. “Haverley’s were. They’re in him.” She looked at the iron foot, out of its socket. “They’re cold.”

Hako knew.

He’d known since the morning. Since Kei’s book, open on Kei’s knees on the sack by the east wall, with the second copy in square letters, every one separate, and the word at the bottom in black ink. He’d sat on the floor with his back against a table leg and his spear across his knees and read it and then looked at the ceiling for a long time, because the ceiling was the only thing in the barn that wasn’t going to look back at him.

He’d been the first one to say it. That was the thing. On floor 6, in a cistern under a city made of glass, with Thea on her back in a sleep hollow that was too big for her and the shards of a thing that wasn’t glass all round her in a ring. That’s ten thousand hours. Minimum. And Kei had said not this time, very quietly, and Hako had heard it. This time. As in. There was another one. And he’d said the word out loud, in the dark, for the first time. Regressor. And Kei had said yeah.

He’d said it. He’d been saying it in his head ever since. Three hundred and thirty days. It was on the second page of Chris’s notebook in Chris’s square hand with a line under it. It was in all of them, all the ones who’d sat in a hay hall on floor 10 and decided to tell Gabe so he wasn’t last. He’d known for three hundred and thirty days that Ice had been here before. Had done all of it before. Had been on every floor, and come up out of every stair, and known where everything was.

He’d just never let himself do the other half of the sum.

There’s somebody on floor 1 I want to see. Before the winter.

A remedy for a winter fever that took old women in the second half of the winter.

her?

The first time, Mother Crane had died.

In the second winter. On floor 1. While they were all thirteen floors up, somewhere, with their own things. And nobody had told them, probably, for ages, because who would? Who sends word up a tower? And Ice had found out somewhere. And it had been on this floor. Hako didn’t know that, but he knew it anyway, the way you know the shape of a room in the dark. This is where we heard. Ice had stood in the shade of the old barn on the first evening and said something to Thea that nobody else heard, and Thea had looked at him, and then nothing. And the third beam at Haverley. Ice, stopping dead on the green fifty metres from the tithe barn. Gabe putting his hand out behind him without looking.

Mother Crane, who’d put her hands on her hips in a low room at the bottom of a crooked street on the first night and said nobody starves on my floor. Who’d shouted at Calvin’s boots. Who’d made Rachael laugh so hard she put her hand over her mouth. Who’d given the onion boy two loaves and a pot, and put him on the tab, because he wouldn’t tell her his name.

Hako leaned on the shield. The frost on it was a finger thick. He didn’t knock it off.

It’s always fine, he thought.

It wasn’t.

This way, then. Fine. This way.

Rachael came at the hour the bells would have gone, with Gabe, the way she did twice a day.

Hako stepped aside for them. He’d seen it eight times now and it still made him want to look away and he didn’t, because she’d have known, and she’d have hated it. She went past the shields, past the line, across the fifteen paces of frost to the straw man’s free foot, where the iron stood out of its socket on the stone. And she knelt. And she put both hands on it.

Gabe came and stood behind her and set Hearthwall down on its rim beside her, so the boss was by her shoulder.

Burden. That was her skill. She’d had it since floor 4, when she’d knelt by a dead dust-hound for a minute with her hands on it because she couldn’t believe the table wouldn’t do anything. You took the hurt out of somebody into yourself. You carried it. And then, since floor 10, since a fire in a forge and a shield that didn’t go cold, you could put it somewhere. Into Hearthwall. Into the one thing on any floor that couldn’t be frozen.

The white went up her hands. Hako watched it. Up her fingers, over her wrists, into her forearms, the way frost goes up a window. Her face didn’t change. It never did. She knelt there with her hands on the iron and the white going up her arms and her eyes shut, and Gabe stood behind her with his jaw set, and after a minute she took one hand off the iron and put it flat on Hearthwall’s boss.

The white ran out of her. Into the shield. And off it. Water, running down the warm iron and dripping off the rim onto the stone, and steaming.

An hour. Hand on the foot, hand on the boss. In and out. Calvin came halfway through and sat down on the near side of the line, by Ice, and didn’t say anything, and when it was over and Rachael came back across the frost with her arms white to the shoulder and grey dust on her knees, Calvin got up and took both her hands and held them. And didn’t talk. It was the only time Hako had ever known Calvin not to talk.

Rachael let him. That was the other thing.

Kei looked at her hands after. He always did. Hako watched him look, and saw his mouth go into a line.

“Ten minutes longer before they came back,” said Kei. Quietly. To nobody. “Than yesterday.”

Rachael heard him. She looked at him over Calvin’s shoulder. “And?”

“And nothing. It’s just a measure.”

“Then measure it tomorrow,” said Rachael, “and tell me if it’s twenty.”

The council happened at the line.

Higu didn’t call it that. Nobody did. But at dusk, when the last of the light was going out of the high windows and the lamps on the chains were being lit, Higu came and sat down on the floor on the near side of the chalk, right in the middle, next to Ice, with the rock in her lap. And then Thea sat down on Ice’s other side. And then Gabe, behind him. And then everybody else, one at a time, the way they always did, until there was a ring of thirty-six people on the cold stone of the Long Barn, with the chalk line running through the top of it like a fence, and Ice on it.

Four stayed on the shields. Jared and Chi and Drew and Salty. Facing up the barn. They could hear. Higu had made sure of that; that was why it was at the line. Nobody was going to miss this one.

The gleaners came too. Not into the ring. They never came into the ring. They stood round the ends of the first two tables, a few of them, the ones who sat in on things now. Annet with her stick. Cobb. Osric of Ashwell with his long one. Old Ezra. And behind them, quietly, more, until half the Long Barn was standing in the gold lamplight between the tables, listening, without being asked.

At the top of the barn, the straw man looked down at all of it.

Higu put the rock on the floor in front of her.

She’d wiped the right side clean that morning. Hako had watched her. But not the left, and not the bottom. She turned the rock round so they could all see the bottom. The words written right across it in Higu’s small square capitals, in chalk, on the evening at Haverley, with a box round them.

ICE: FLOOR 1. BEFORE THE WINTER. (SOMEBODY.)

“I said it doesn’t come off,” said Higu. “It hasn’t. I said we’d talk about it when the wheat’s in.” She looked down the barn, at the sheaves stacked against the walls to the beams. “The wheat’s in.”

Nobody said anything.

“Kei,” said Higu.

So Kei told them.

Hako had heard it that morning. He listened anyway. Kei sat in the ring with the book open on his knees at the back and told them all of it, the way he’d told his party, item by item, because that was how Kei told things. Ma Yarrow. Day fifteen. Willow bark and the root that grew by the frozen river and honey and a dark blue flower in a jar on a shelf by the stove on floor 2. Too much stops the heart. Exactly enough keeps it going. Three days. A bit at a time. Day and night. Somebody sitting with her the whole time.

A hundred and fifty-four days, now, to the six hundred and tenth. Not early. Exactly.

And then he shut the book, with his hand on the cover, and said, “It’s for Mother Crane.”

And the whole ring went still.

Hako watched it go. He watched it go through all of them, one at a time, round the ring, like a stone dropped in a pond. Thirty-five faces, in the lamplight. Calvin’s mouth, opening. Rachael, behind her knees, going very still. Dennis putting his hand on her back without looking. Deston with his hand on his shirt pocket. Hana, with her crown in her lap, her ears going down flat. Xola, very quietly, oh. Newish shutting her eyes. They’d all been there, the first night. All of them. Thirty-five people pushing into a room that didn’t fit them, ducking under the low beam, sitting on windowsills and crates and the floor by the fire, with a little walnut-faced woman pointing a wooden spoon at them.

And then it went further than that. In some of them. Hako watched that too.

He watched it in the ones who’d been on the second page since floor 6. Bee. Gigi. Hyvred, with her glasses off. Chris, with his notebook shut on his knee and his face doing a sum. Winter, beside Ina. Rui Xiang, who read people the way other people read weather. He watched them get to the same place he’d got to on the shield that morning, at the end of the sum, the bit you didn’t let yourself do. The first time, she died. And he watched every one of them get there and not look at Ice.

Not once. Not one of them. They looked at Kei, and the book, and the rock, and the floor. Thirty-five people, very carefully not looking at the one person in the ring they all wanted to look at.

It was the thing they were best at. They’d had a lot of practice.

Ice sat at the line with his hands in his lap and looked at the rock.

“Two ways,” said Higu.

She picked up the chalk. On the clean right side of the rock she wrote, small, one under the other:

SOME GO. ALL GO.

“Some of us go down,” said Higu. “Kei, because it’s his. And whoever else. The rest go on up, or wait. Or all of us go down.” She looked round. “That’s it. That’s the two. I’m not saying which. Somebody say the first one properly, so it’s been said.”

“I’ll do it,” said Malcolm.

Everybody looked at him. He went a bit pink, but he didn’t stop.

“Somebody should,” said Malcolm. “That’s all. Somebody should say the other one properly, so it’s not just—“ He waved a hand. “So we’ve looked at it. That’s what plan B’s for.” He took a breath. “Six is quicker than thirty-six. Every stair. Every boat. Every door. Marrit’s boat holds us all but it holds six better. Six people can go over the pass on ten in a day. Thirty-six takes three. That’s not an opinion. It’s just quicker.” He looked at Kei. “Kei. Thea, probably. Gabe, because he already said. Rachael, because of the three days. One more. And they go fast, all the way down, and the rest of us go up to fifteen and find the stair and wait at the top of it. So when they come back up, it’s there.” He stopped. “That’s it. That’s the other one.”

“Thank you,” said Higu, and wrote it, small, beside SOME GO: QUICKER.

“And the levels,” said Chris.

He had his notebook open. He didn’t look up from it.

“Nobody gets anything five floors under,” said Chris. “We’ve known that since nine. Going down, we’re at sixty-one for half a year. Down and back up. Floor 15’s going to want more than sixty-one. I don’t know how much more. Nobody does.” He turned a page. “That’s not a reason. It’s just a column.”

Higu wrote NO LEVELS beside ALL GO.

“And we’ll be late,” said Rui Xiang. “Going back up.”

Hako looked at him. Rui Xiang was sitting with his knees up and his chin on them, looking at the floor in the middle of the ring.

“We’d get to fifteen at— I don’t know. Day seven hundred. Seven-fifty.” Rui Xiang shrugged, a small shrug. “Everything up there’s been waiting for us since the white room. Whatever it is. It’ll keep waiting. But we’ll be late.” He paused. “I don’t like being late.”

“You’re never late,” said Calvin.

“That’s why I don’t like it.”

Higu wrote LATE.

“It costs half a year,” said Winter.

She said it flatly, in her ordinary voice, the voice she used for things that were facts. She was sitting beside Ina with his blanket over both their knees, and she didn’t look at anyone in particular.

“Somebody should say that out loud,” said Winter. “So it’s said. I’m not saying don’t. I’m saying it costs. Half a year. All of us. Everything up there. That’s the price.” She looked at the rock. “Write it.”

Higu wrote HALF A YEAR.

Then she put the chalk down and looked at the rock and didn’t say anything, and nobody did, for a while.

Ice moved.

Hako saw it. Just a small thing. His hands, in his lap, coming apart. His back straightening, a little, the way it did before he stood up to say something. His mouth opening.

“Don’t, Dec,” said Gabe.

He said it from behind him. He didn’t lean forward. He didn’t even raise his voice.

“I wasn’t—“

“You were.” Gabe was looking at the back of Ice’s head. “You were going to say you’d go on your own. With Kei and a lantern and a bag. Because it’s half a year. Because it’s yours.” He shook his head. “You don’t get a vote on this one, mate. You never get a vote on this kind. We’ve told you.”

Ice shut his mouth.

He looked at Thea. Thea looked back at him. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to. Hako had seen her look at him like that before, on the Salt, with five arrows round her in a ring in the crust. Not now.

Ice put his hands back together in his lap.

“Can I say something?” said Dennis. “It’s maths.”

There was a groan round the ring. Not a real one. The kind you do because it’s expected. Calvin said noooo. Deston said let him. Rachael, from behind her knees, said it’s always maths, and her voice was nearly normal.

“It’s important maths,” said Dennis.

“It’s always important maths,” said Rachael.

“This one actually is.” Dennis put his hand in his pocket. He didn’t take anything out. He just put his hand there, the way he did when he was counting. “Day one,” said Dennis. “The first night. In the Spoon. She stood at the end of the table and pointed her spoon at us and said nobody starves on my floor. Remember?”

Everybody remembered.

“And then she said it goes on the tab,” said Dennis. “You pay when you come back down.”

The ring was very quiet.

“I worked it out,” said Dennis. “On floor 1. On the second night, in bed, because I couldn’t sleep. Thirty-five of us. Four days, mostly. Five for some. Three meals a day, if you count the bread in the morning, which you should, because it was the best bread I’ve ever had. And Calvin’s thirds.” Calvin opened his mouth and Dennis held up a finger without looking at him. “And beds, no, the beds weren’t hers. But the porridge was. And the stew. And whatever that brown thing was on the third night.”

“Pottage,” said Ice. Very quietly.

Dennis didn’t look at him. “Pottage,” he said. “Thank you.” He took his hand out of his pocket. “And Dec owes her for two loaves and a pot. She said. You can tell him from me.” He looked round the ring. “I stopped adding it up at about the third day. Because it got embarrassing. It was a lot of stew. But the point is.” He put his hand flat on the stone in front of him. “We never paid it.”

Nobody said anything.

“She didn’t say some of you pay when you come back down,” said Dennis. “She didn’t say whoever’s quickest. She said you. She put all of us on it. Every one. She didn’t even know our names, most of us. She just put us on.” He looked at Rachael. “She doesn’t know we’re coming back. She just said we would.”

“Everybody comes back down,” said Deston. Very quietly. “She said. Didn’t she. Something like that.”

“No,” said Ice.

Hako looked at him. Everybody did. For the first time since Kei shut the book, everybody in the ring looked at the person sitting on the chalk line.

Ice was looking at the rock. At the box at the bottom, in Higu’s capitals.

“Everyone comes back down eventually,” said Ice. “That’s what she said. To me. The first morning. Before any of you got there. She said her gran said. They go up, and they come back down to eat.” He stopped. “I’ll keep a tab.”

His voice didn’t do anything. It was just his voice, quite low, saying the words. But Hako had heard Mother Crane say things. And he heard her, then, for a second, in a barn on floor 14, in the way Ice said eventually. The same way. As if it was the most obvious thing in the world.

Nobody moved.

Then Calvin put his hand up.

He put it straight up in the air, all the way, like a kid at school. His left hand. Two good arms, and he put up the left one, and Hako didn’t think that was an accident.

“Floor 5,” said Calvin. “Nobody fights alone. If it happens, everybody comes.” He looked round the ring. “On ten, at the long table, I said my hand was last. On five.” He kept it up. “It’s first. This time.”

“Calvin,” said Rachael.

“What? It is.”

Rachael looked at him for a long moment with her white arms round her knees.

Then she took one of them away from her knees and put her hand up too.

“Obviously,” said Rachael.

And then it went round.

Not fast. Not all at once. Hako had thought it would be all at once, like on ten, at the long table, everybody saying it on top of each other. It wasn’t. It went round the ring one at a time, slowly, the way the count went on a stair. Each one waiting for the one before.

Aleaka, with Heed beside her, very quietly: “Nobody left up.” And then, frowning, as if she’d only just worked it out: “Burr said that’s the other half. I think this is the other half.” And her hand.

Deston. Hand on his shirt pocket, and then up. “All of us.”

Newish. “I’ll count every stair. Both ways.” Hand.

Hana, with her crown in her lap. She didn’t say anything. She just put her hand up, and her ears came back up with it. And Milli beside her, with his hand already up before hers was all the way, which wasn’t like Milli, and he looked at it as if he hadn’t decided to do it, and left it there.

Jared. “Man.” Hand.

Tiger, from the edge, where he always sat, by the door. “I’ll open the doors,” said Tiger. “Going down too. Somebody has to.” Hand.

Xola, with Squiddles on her head. “Floor 1’s got a river,” said Xola. “Freshwater animal. I’m going where the water is.” Then, quieter, not a joke at all: “And she gave me extra bread. On the second day. Because I said I liked it.” Hand.

Yan. Who looked at the straw man, and then at Kei’s book, and then at nothing, the way he looked at things that weren’t there, and said, “She’s got a good light. Old. Steady. I’d like to see it again.” And put his hand up as if it didn’t matter much, which was how you knew it did. Wendy, beside him, didn’t say anything. She’d had her hand up since Calvin’s.

Ina. Mask down. Hand.

Winter. “It costs half a year,” said Winter again. And then, putting her hand up, flat, like somebody paying for something across a counter: “Fine. It’s a good price.”

Rui Xiang. “We’ll be early anyway.” Hand.

Chris, writing it down. Hand, with the pen still in it.

Malcolm. Who’d said the other one properly, so it had been said. He looked at QUICKER on the rock for a while. “That was plan B,” said Malcolm. “Plan A’s always everybody.” Hand.

Hyvred. Bee. Gigi. Kei, last of Hyvred’s party, with his other hand still flat on the cover of the book.

And Hako.

He put his hand up. He didn’t say anything clever. He’d thought he would. He’d had classic ready, and it’s always fine, and something about the stew. But when it got to him he just put his hand up, the right one, the spear hand, and looked across the ring at Ice and said, “Yeah.”

That was all. Yeah.

Drew, from the shields, without turning round: “Me.” And Salty: “Obviously.” And Jared, who’d already said man, said it again, louder, so it carried. And Chi, from the end of the wall, with Waddles at her feet: “Yes.”

Gabe didn’t put his hand up.

“I already said,” said Gabe. “On the Acre. I said I’m coming.” He put his hand on Ice’s shoulder from behind instead. “That’s my hand.”

Thea didn’t put hers up either. She was holding the lantern. Its light lay flat on the glass toward him.

“I said it before any of you,” said Thea. Quietly. Not to the ring. To Ice. “In the tithe barn. I said if they go up, you and I go down. On our own. With a lantern.” She looked round the ring, at all the hands. “They’re not going up.”

Higu counted. Out loud. The way she always did.

Thirty-five.

She picked up the chalk.

She wrote it under everything, in the middle of the right side, bigger than the rest. DOWN. ALL OF US.

And then she did something Hako had never seen her do.

She drew another line.

Down the rock. Not over the old one. Beside it. A second chalk line, on the right of the first, so the rock had three columns now instead of two. And at the top of the new one, small, she wrote:

NOW.

“Going up,” said Higu, “it was what you remember.” She touched the left column. “And what’s true.” The middle. “Going down, there’s this as well.” She put her finger on the new one. “What’s changed. Since we were there. Because it will have. Everything we went through, it’s been going on without us. Since.” She looked round the ring. “So going down, we look. Every floor. And we write that.”

Nobody said anything.

“And first,” said Higu.

She wrote one more thing. At the very bottom of the new column. Small.

FIRST: HIM.

And she turned the rock round, on the floor, so the writing faced up the barn. At the shields. At the frost. At the old woman on her stool, and the iron foot out of its socket, and the white face at the top looking down.

At the edge of the ring, between the tables, Annet Sallow leaned on her stick.

“You’re going down,” said Annet.

“When he’s kept,” said Higu.

Annet looked at her. Then she looked up the barn, at the straw man, for a long time, with her face doing something Hako couldn’t read.

“Passers come back down,” said Annet. “That’s the song. The bad one.” She sniffed. “Out of the old barn, and back to the old barn.”

“What’s bad about it?” said Hako.

He hadn’t meant to say it. Everybody looked at him. Annet looked at him too.

“It never says when,” said Annet. “A song that doesn’t say when’s a bad song. You can’t plant by it.” She tapped her stick on the stone, once. “But it says they do. I’ll give it that.” She turned to go, and stopped. “Your one,” she said, to nobody, to the ring. “He’s right. Floor 1’s not going to wait for you. Nothing waits. Ask anybody here.” She went back between the tables to the Haverley end, and the gleaners parted for her, and closed behind her.

Ice was looking at the hands.

They’d mostly come down by then. But he was looking at the places where they’d been, the way Hyvred looked at things. Round the ring, slowly, at every face, the way he looked at every face at the soup line. Counting.

He didn’t say okay. Hako had thought he would. He’d said okay on eight, and on seven, and on ten, at the long table, and it was what he said when there wasn’t anything else that would come out.

He said, “She’ll shout at you.”

Calvin looked at him.

“About your boots,” said Ice. “On her table. She’ll shout at you the second you come through the door.”

“I know,” said Calvin.

“She will.”

“I KNOW, DEC.”

And somebody laughed. Xola, probably. And then Dennis. And then it went round the ring the way the hands had, only faster, thirty-five people on a cold floor in the lamplight laughing at Calvin’s boots, and Calvin going red and laughing too, and Rachael with her white arms round her knees and her face in them, shaking. Hako laughed till his ribs hurt. He didn’t know why it was so funny. It wasn’t, really. It was just the first thing in a week that was.

Ice didn’t laugh. He was smiling. A small smile, the kind that doesn’t go all the way. He looked down at the floor in front of him, at the chalk, and then up at all of them.

“Thank you,” said Ice.

To the whole ring. To the barn. Quietly. And he didn’t say anything else, and he didn’t need to, and Higu picked up the rock and put it back in her lap.

It heard.

Hako didn’t know that, at first. He just knew that at about two in the morning, on the late watch again, with Salty beside him and the frost on his shield a finger thick, the floor made a sound.

Not the crack. Not like the night before. Smaller. A dry, tight tik, like a nail coming out of wood. From the right foot. The one that was still in its socket.

Hild sat up on her stool.

Hako looked. In the light from the one low lamp by the shields he could see the right foot of the frame, the iron going down into the stone, and the four bolts round it, three down and one up a finger, the way it had been since the step.

Two up, now.

The second one had come up. A finger’s width. Clean. With a little ring of frost round its head on the stone.

“Salty,” said Hako.

“I see it.”

They didn’t move. You didn’t, in front of something like that. They stood with their shields grounded, rims touching, and looked at it.

“He’s in a hurry now,” said Hild.

Hako looked at her. She was sitting very straight on the stool, with her blanket round her, looking up.

“What do you mean?”

“Six nights he took over the first foot,” said Hild. “One bolt a night. Then two. Slow. Like somebody waking up.” She was quiet for a moment. “That’s one night, for that. He’s in a hurry.” She tilted her head, the way she did, because her neck hurt. “Something’s told him you’re going.”

Hako felt it go cold in him. Not the frost. Something else.

It listened. Everybody knew that. Ice had said so, on the Salt, on the sea, on every floor since seven. The tower listens to everything. It builds things out of what you say. That was why nobody said things out loud. That was why Thea took one name a night, and why the hows had to wait for an unwatched place. And they’d sat in a ring at the line in front of the floor guardian of floor 14 and said, out loud, all thirty-five of them, one at a time, slowly, that they were going down. To floor 1. For Mother Crane.

Making it match.

It wanted them here. It wanted them on floor 14 in the winter with the doors shut, the way they’d been the first time, while somebody on floor 1 got a fever on the night she was supposed to. And they’d just told it they weren’t going to be.

“Oh,” said Hako. Quietly. “Classic.”

He found Ice at the line, after the watch.

Four in the morning. Gabe and Azac had come to take the shields, and Hako had knocked the frost off his for the last time and walked back across the fifteen paces of stone and stepped over the chalk, and Ice was sitting there. Awake. Cross-legged. Looking up the barn.

He’d been there all night. Hako knew without asking.

Hako sat down next to him. On the near side. His shield on the floor beside him, the face of it wet now, the frost going back to water in the less-cold.

“Two,” said Ice.

“Yeah.”

“It heard.”

“Yeah.” Hako put his spear across his knees. “Hild says it’s in a hurry.”

“She’s right.”

They sat there.

“It’s going to try to keep us here,” said Ice. Very quietly. Not to Hako. To the face at the top of the barn. “On fourteen. In the winter. It’s going to make it so we can’t go.”

“Classic,” said Hako.

Ice made a sound that was nearly a laugh.

They sat there some more. The barn was dark and quiet behind them, seven hundred people asleep, and the horses, and the wind on the roof. In front of them, Gabe and Azac, the backs of them, the shields, the rims touching. Hild, who hadn’t gone to sleep, on her stool. The two bolts up at the right foot. The face.

“It’s going to be fine,” said Hako.

He said it the way he always said it. Facing the way he wanted to go.

“You don’t know that,” said Ice.

“No,” said Hako. “I never know that. That’s not what it’s for.” He looked at his shield. “I say it so I know which way I’m facing. That’s all. It’s just a direction.”

Ice was quiet for a long time.

“I know,” he said at last. “You told me. On a call. At four in the morning.”

“You laughed so hard you had to mute.”

“I did.”

“You said it was the most me thing anybody had ever said.”

“It was.” Ice looked at the straw man. “It still is.”

Hako nodded.

“Then it’s not fine,” said Ice. Slowly. As if he was trying the words on. “But—“

“It’s four in the morning, Ice.”

“Then it’s nearly morning,” said Ice.

And he laughed. Properly. Out loud, in the cold, at the line, quietly so as not to wake anybody, with his hand over his mouth, the way he’d laughed on a call on Earth a long time ago and had to mute. Hako laughed too. He couldn’t help it. In front of them, Gabe looked round over his shoulder, once, with his eyebrows up, and then turned back to the straw man. And Hild, on her stool, didn’t look round, but Hako saw her cheek go up at the side.

The face at the top of the barn didn’t move. The window over it said what it said. The two bolts stayed up.

Hako picked up his shield and leaned on it, a little. Into nothing. Just to know which way he was facing.

It was nearly morning.
