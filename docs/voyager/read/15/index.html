Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 15

A Hundred Nails

3,090 words · 13 min read

Salty decided to learn to make swords the morning after he nearly died, because he was the kind of man who, when something went wrong, wanted to know how it was built.

He’d lain awake half the night thinking about it. Not about the ram; he’d thought about the ram for about ten minutes and then decided there was no point. The ram had been faster than him and he’d been standing wrong. That was all. Next time he’d stand right. He’d thought about the sword.

It was a good sword. It was a plain, heavy, honest greatsword, nearly as long as he was tall, and it had held. When the ram hit him, the blade had taken the charge flat across its width and not bent. That was the only reason he’d been lying in the gorse at fourteen percent and not at zero. He’d lain there in the dark of a borrowed room with Wendy asleep against his shoulder, thinking about how that blade had held, and whether it would hold next time, and how you’d know.

You wouldn’t know. Not unless you knew how it was made.

So in the morning he went and found the forge.

It was at the bottom of the town by the river, past the mill, in a long low building of black stone with its doors standing open and heat coming out of them like breath. Inside it was dark and red and loud, and it smelled like burning and iron and sweat. A tall woman with a long grey braid and forearms like hams was beating something on an anvil, with a rhythm that made the floor shake.

And two men Salty knew were already there.

Gabe was sitting on an upturned bucket by the door with his round wooden shield across his knees, turning it over and over in his hands. Gabe was always turning something over in his hands. And leaning on the wall beside him, cool and easy in a dark red brocade jacket, with his crimson hair falling over one eye, was Jared.

“Oh,” said Salty.

“Oh,” said Gabe.

“Salty,” said Jared. “My man.”

“What are you doing here?”

“Same thing as you, I’m guessing.” Jared nodded at the woman at the anvil. “Learning a trade. She says she’ll teach anyone who doesn’t waste her time. Gabe’s been here since before it was light.”

“Couldn’t sleep,” said Gabe.

Salty looked at him. Gabe’s eyes had the look of a man who’d been awake since the first night. He knew why. Everybody did. Gabe had sat at the long table at the Spoon three nights ago typing the same name into World until Deston made him stop, and then he’d sat there with the whisper window open, under a word that said sent, waiting for an answer that never came.

Salty had known Ice for two years. He’d played with him more nights than he could count, in calls with Yan and Niko and Maigo and Wendy. He’d liked him a lot. But Salty also knew that for Gabe, it wasn’t the same. Gabe had known him since they were kids. For Gabe it was like a brother had walked out of the door.

He didn’t say anything about it. He sat down on the next bucket.

The smith stopped hammering.

She looked round at the three of them, and then at Salty in particular: up and down, slowly, from his boots to the top of his head. It took a fairly short time, because there wasn’t that much of it. He was one metre sixty-eight. People looked at him like that a lot, and then they looked at his arms, and then they changed their minds.

She looked at his arms.

“Huh,” she said. “Another one.”

“Another what?”

“Climber who wants to learn the anvil.” She set her hammer down. “Ulla Dray. I keep the forge. I’ll teach any of you lot who doesn’t waste my time, and I’ll throw out the first one who does.” She pointed at the three of them in turn with a finger that was missing its top joint. “You. You. You. Three of you is three times my patience. So we’re going to find out right now who stays.”

“How?” said Jared.

Ulla Dray smiled. It wasn’t a nice smile.

“Nails,” she said.

A hundred nails each.

That was the test. It wasn’t a sword or a shield or anything you’d want to put in a story. Nails. Square-shanked iron nails for roofing, the kind Hearthstead used by the barrelful. Ulla Dray put a heap of iron rod in front of each of them, showed them once how it was done, and sat down on a barrel to watch.

Heat the end of the rod till it’s the colour of a ripe apricot. Draw it out on the anvil to a point, turning it a quarter-turn every blow so it doesn’t go flat. Cut it on the hardy. Put the hot end in the nail-header. Hit the head. Knock it out. Quench it. Again.

The first one Salty made looked like a bent fork.

The second one looked like a slightly less bent fork.

The tenth one looked like a nail, if you squinted, and he held it up to the light and was more pleased with it than he’d been with anything in years.

“Ninety to go,” said Ulla Dray.

It took them all day.

It was the hardest thing Salty had done since the white room, and he included the ram. The ram had been terrifying for about forty seconds. This was hard for ten hours. The heat came off the forge in a solid wall, and the hammer weighed more every hour. His arms, which had never once in his life been the weak part of him, began to shake by noon. The blisters came up on his palms in the morning, burst at midday, and by the afternoon had become a single smooth ache he’d stopped noticing.

He loved it.

He hadn’t expected that. He’d come to learn how a sword was built, and he’d found something else. There was a rhythm to it. You hit, turned, hit, turned. You watched the colour of the iron and learned it the way you’d learn the colour of a sky before rain. If you lost the rhythm, the nail went flat, and if you kept it, it went square. And when it went square, you knew. You didn’t need anybody to tell you.

Salty had always liked things you couldn’t argue with. Weights. Clocks. Lap times. A nail was square or it wasn’t.

And while his hands did the hitting and turning, his head, which didn’t have to do anything, went back to the gorse.

He hadn’t been scared, lying there. That was the strange part. He’d been at fourteen percent, then nine, with a ram the size of a car turning round to finish him, and he hadn’t been scared for himself at all. He’d been angry. Because over the top of the wall he could see Wendy’s face. She’d been running, and her face had been doing a thing he’d never seen it do before. He’d thought, quite clearly, lying on his back in the gorse: you don’t get to make her look like that.

And then Yan’s hands had come down over his chest, white and shaking, and the next hit hadn’t touched him.

He’d watched those hands afterwards, on the wall, tucked under Yan’s arms, gone the colour of paper. Yan hadn’t said anything about it. Yan never said anything about what things cost him. He’d sat there with his hands hurting and let Salty think it had been easy.

It hadn’t been easy. Salty had seen the hands.

He hit the iron. Turned it. Hit it. The nail went square.

Next time, he thought, I’ll stand right. And next time the sword will hold, because I’ll know how it was made. And next time nobody’s hands go white because I was standing wrong.

He didn’t know yet that he’d make something before the day was out that wasn’t a nail at all. He only knew he wasn’t putting the hammer down.

Beside him, Gabe was faster.

Of course Gabe was faster. Gabe had always been good with his hands, the kind of person who took a thing apart to see how it worked and then put it back together better. By noon he was on his fortieth nail, and they were good. Ulla Dray picked one up off his pile, turned it over, looked at the head, and grunted, which Salty would come to learn was the best thing she ever said to anyone.

And Jared was slower than both of them, and didn’t care.

Jared made nails the way he did everything: easily, without hurrying, as though he had all the time in the world and was mildly surprised everybody else didn’t. He talked while he worked. About his streams, about home, about a postcard of himself that Ice used to send Hana every few weeks for no reason. Every so often he’d stop entirely to look at a nail he’d made, turning it in the tongs, considering it like a man choosing a tie. Once, at about three in the afternoon, he made something that very much wasn’t a nail. He bent the hot end over and over on itself with little taps of the hammer, curling it, layering it, till it looked like—

“Is that a flower?” said Gabe.

“It’s a rose,” said Jared.

It was. A small one, iron, rough, the size of a thumb, its petals curled in on each other, still glowing faintly orange at the edges. Jared held it up in the tongs and looked at it with great satisfaction.

“I used to have one,” he said. “On my model. In my art. Always holding a rose.” He turned it in the light. “Didn’t come through. Nothing you hold comes through. Only what you’re wearing.” He dropped it in the quench bucket with a hiss and fished it out again, black and steaming. “So I made one. Now it came through.”

Ulla Dray looked at the rose for a long moment.

“That’s not a nail,” she said.

“No, ma’am.”

“That’ll be eighty-three to go, then.”

“Yes, ma’am,” said Jared, and put the rose in his pocket and went back to nails, completely unbothered.

Salty laughed so hard he burned his thumb.

It was late afternoon, and they were all on their last twenty, when Gabe stopped hammering.

He didn’t say anything at first. He just stood there with the hammer resting on the anvil and the nail cooling to grey in the tongs. He was looking at his round wooden shield, propped against the wall by the door where he’d put it that morning.

“He always broke his shields,” he said.

Salty and Jared looked at each other.

“Declan,” said Gabe. He wasn’t really talking to them, Salty thought. He was talking to the anvil. “In every game. Ever since we were kids. He’d be the tank, he’d always be the tank, and he’d stand right in front of everything, and he’d break his shield. Every single time. Because he never backed off when he should have. He’d hold one more hit, and then one more, and it would crack.” He turned the nail in the tongs. “And then he’d laugh about it. Skill issue, he’d say. Trust.”

Nobody said anything.

“So I’m going to learn this,” said Gabe. “Properly. All of it. And when I find him, I’m going to make him a shield that doesn’t break.” He put the nail in the header and hit it, once, very hard. “Something he can’t break even if he’s an idiot. Which he will be.”

Salty looked at him for a long time.

He didn’t know what to say. He’d never been good at saying things. He said them how they were, and that was all he had. So he said: “You’ll need better iron than this.”

Gabe looked up.

“For a shield like that,” said Salty. “This is nail iron. It’s soft. You’ll need something better. Higher up, probably. There’ll be better iron higher up.” He shrugged. “So you’ll have to climb. To get it.”

Gabe looked at him for a moment longer. Then something in his face, something tight that had been there since the white room, eased a fraction.

“Yeah,” he said. “Yeah. I’ll have to climb.”

“So don’t die doing it.”

“I won’t.”

“Good,” said Salty, and went back to his nails.

The window came for all three of them within a few minutes of each other, when the sun was most of the way down its rail and the forge was the only light in the long black building.

Gabe’s first. He finished his hundredth nail, and Ulla Dray got up off her barrel, walked over, took the whole pile in both hands and dropped it on the floor to hear how it rang. She nodded once. Then Salty’s. She dropped his too, and listened, and looked at him for a moment, and nodded again.

Jared was last. He finished his hundredth nail and set it on the pile with a little flourish, and Ulla Dray dropped them, listened, and frowned.

“There’s a rose in this,” she said.

“I made two.”

She picked it out of the pile. A second one, slightly better than the first, its petals more even, its stem curling into a hook at the bottom. She turned it over in her finger and thumb, frowning.

“Hm,” she said. Then she put it in her own apron pocket. “That’s for the trouble. You’re in.”

Jared grinned.

[ Life skill learned: Blacksmith ]

They sat outside on the river wall afterwards, the three of them, in the cool evening with their hands throbbing and the stars coming on all at once overhead. None of them said anything for a while. Salty looked down at his hands. They were black to the wrist, cracked and blistered and burned in three places, and he didn’t think he’d ever been prouder of them.

“Three blacksmiths,” said Jared. “Look at us. We’re a guild.”

“We’re three guys who made nails,” said Gabe.

“A nail guild.”

“Nobody’s ever going to be scared of a nail guild, Jared.”

“They should be,” said Jared, with dignity. “Have you seen a nail? Pointy. Lots of them.” He took his first rose out of his pocket and looked at it in the starlight. “I’m giving this one to Alice,” he said. “She’ll pretend she doesn’t like it. Then she’ll put it on her crown.”

“She will,” said Gabe, and almost smiled.

But Salty had made one more thing, and he hadn’t told them.

He’d done it at the very end, after his hundredth nail. Ulla Dray had gone to bank the fire, and Gabe and Jared were outside, and he’d stayed. He’d taken a scrap of plate iron from the bin by the door, and heated it, and bent it, and hammered it. It was clumsy work. He didn’t know what he was doing. But he’d known what he wanted, and he’d kept going until it was right, because that was what he did.

It was a small iron box with a hinged lid and holes punched in the top in a rough star. About the size of a fist. You put a hot coal in it from the fire. You closed the lid. It stayed warm for hours.

He found Yan on the steps of the Copper Spoon after supper, exactly where he’d known he would be, sitting with his hands wrapped in Mother Crane’s cloth and Suzu round his neck. Salty sat down next to him and held the box out. There was a coal in it already. He could feel the heat through the iron.

Yan looked at it.

“What’s this?”

“Hand-warmer,” said Salty. “For after. When you do the thing with your hands. You put your hands on it.” He looked at the street. “I made it. It’s not very good.”

Yan took it. He held it in both hands for a moment, cupped round it, exactly the way he’d cupped them over Salty’s chest in the gorse. Salty saw his face go very still.

“Salty,” said Yan.

“Don’t.”

“I wasn’t going to say anything.”

“You were going to say something nice. Don’t.” Salty bumped his shoulder, hard enough to rock him. “Just don’t do the hand thing unless you have to.”

“I’ll do it as much as I have to,” said Yan.

“I know,” said Salty. “That’s why I made the box.”

Wendy found him later, on the river wall, looking at his black hands.

She sat down next to him without saying anything. She didn’t need to. She took one of his hands and turned it over in her lap, looking at the blisters and the burns and the soot, and he let her.

“Idiot,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“You could have just bought a sword.”

“Didn’t want to buy one,” said Salty. “Wanted to know how it’s built.”

She was quiet for a while, running her thumb over the cracked skin.

“Can you make arrowheads?” she said.

He looked at her. She wasn’t looking at him. She was looking at the river, with her chin up and her silent headphones round her neck, very carefully not looking.

“I’m getting through a lot of arrows,” she said. “That’s all. And the ones from the shop are bad. They’re too light. They drift.” She shrugged. “If you were going to be at the forge anyway.”

Salty looked at her for a long moment.

“Yeah,” he said. “I can make arrowheads.”

“Okay,” said Wendy.

“Good ones. Heavy. They won’t drift.”

“Okay.” She leaned her shoulder against his. “Don’t burn your hands any worse than this.”

“I’ll try.”

“Don’t try. Don’t.”

He didn’t promise. He’d never been good at promising things he couldn’t measure. But he sat there with her on the river wall until the forge went dark behind them, and up the valley, at the top of the rankings neither of them looked at, a name sat at level nine, a long way up, still climbing.
