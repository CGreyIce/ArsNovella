Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 119

Four Hundred Steps

3,292 words · 14 min read

Gabe’s fire was still going when they came back round the tarn.

It was very late. Or very early. The aurora had gone. The stars were going too, in the east, over the ridge, where the sky had started to turn the colour of the inside of a shell. The far side of the valley was grey and silver, and the tarn was a dark mirror with nothing in it, and on the near shore, right at the water’s edge, there was a small orange fire with a heap of wood beside it.

And somebody sitting by it, putting a branch on.

Ice stopped.

He didn’t mean to. His feet did it. He stood in the wet grass at the edge of the water with Thea’s hand in his, and looked at it. A fire on the ground. In the grey before dawn. A heap of wood. Somebody sitting beside it, feeding it, with a shield across his knees, keeping it going for somebody who wasn’t back yet.

He’d walked into this once. With his shield in two pieces.

Thea’s hand tightened on his.

“Go on,” she said. Very quietly.

He went on.

Gabe looked up from the fire.

He looked up the way he’d looked up on the ninth floor of the first run, a long time ago, at the foot of seven trees. Ice saw it. The same face. Exactly the same. A man who’d been sitting by a light all night, and couldn’t do anything, and had kept it going anyway, and then, at the very end of it, somebody coming in out of the dark.

And then it changed.

Because this time Gabe saw who was holding his hand.

And Gabe’s face did the other thing. The thing it had never done the first time, because the first time there’d been nobody holding anybody’s hand. It went slowly from tired to startled to something that took up the whole of it, and he put the branch down on the fire very carefully, and stood up, and looked at the two of them standing in the wet grass at the edge of the tarn in the grey light.

He didn’t say anything.

He looked at Thea. Then at Ice. Then at their hands. Then back at Thea’s face, which Ice couldn’t see, because she was standing a little behind him. Whatever Gabe saw in it made him breathe out through his nose. The long one. The tired one that meant Declan, no, except that it didn’t, this time. This time it meant something else entirely.

“Morning,” said Gabe.

“Morning,” said Ice.

Thea let go of his hand. She walked past him, round the fire, to Gabe. And stopped in front of him. And looked up at him, because Gabe was a lot taller than her.

“I’m going to kill you,” said Thea.

Gabe stared at her.

“What?” said Gabe. “What did I— I made a fire. I sat up all night. I made you a—“

“Tents,” said Thea.

Gabe looked at her. Then at Ice, over her head. Completely, totally lost.

“What tents?”

Ice started to laugh.

He couldn’t help it. He stood there at the edge of the tarn with his hands over his face and laughed, the way he’d laughed on the rock, the way he’d laughed on a jetty on floor 8, until he couldn’t breathe. And Thea, in front of Gabe, with her arms folded, glaring up at him, started to laugh too. And Gabe looked from one of them to the other with his mouth open and absolutely no idea what was going on.

“You’re both mad,” said Gabe. “I sat up all night. I’m going back to sleep.”

He didn’t, though. He sat down again by the fire. And Thea sat down beside him, and leaned on him, the way she leaned on Xola. And after a while Ice sat down on his other side, on his right, where Gabe always stood, and Gabe put his arm round him without looking.

They watched the sun come up.

He didn’t tell anybody else.

Nobody asked. That was the thing he’d remember, about the morning on the top of floor 10. Thirty-four people woke up in the grass by the tarn in the gold, one by one, the way they’d woken up on the hillside on floor 8 the morning after he first slept. Stretching. Complaining. Calvin standing up and immediately announcing that he’d dreamed the cloud was made of bread. Rachael going round with her hands on everybody, the way she always did. Kei waking Dose, who said something rude.

And one by one, they looked at the fire.

At Ice, sitting on Gabe’s right. At Thea, sitting on Gabe’s left, with the lantern in her lap and her hair loose and her face doing a thing that nobody had seen it do on any floor. At the way she looked across Gabe at Ice, every so often, and the way he looked back.

And nobody said anything.

He watched them not say it. He watched it go round the camp the way the Steward’s count had gone round the wall, one face at a time. Xola saw first. Of course she did. She sat up in the grass with Squiddles on her head and her hair everywhere and looked at Thea across the fire, and her face crumpled, completely, all at once, and she put both her hands over it and lay back down in the grass and made a sound that Ice could only describe as a squeal into her own palms.

“Babe,” she said, muffled. “Babe.”

Thea went bright red and looked at the fire.

Gigi saw next. She looked at Thea for a long moment. Then she looked at Ice. Then she got up and went and sat down on Thea’s other side and put her forehead against Thea’s shoulder and didn’t say anything at all.

Hana saw, and smiled, a small private smile, and put her crown on. Jared saw Hana smile and looked round to see why and said oh very loudly and Milli hit him. Higu saw, and took the rock out of her pack, and looked at it, and put it back without writing anything. Drew saw, and looked down at his own open hand, and then up at Ice, and nodded. Once.

Calvin saw last. He’d been describing the bread cloud to Dennis in detail. He stopped in the middle of a sentence and looked at the fire and his mouth fell open.

“Oh,” said Calvin.

Rachael said “Calvin” without looking up, and he shut it.

And that was all. Nobody asked. Nobody said a word about the rock, or the walk round the tarn, or the singing that had gone on all night on the far shore so loudly that nobody could have heard anything said on the other side of the water. They just looked, and saw, and let it be. The way you let a thing be when it isn’t yours.

Thirty-four people, sitting in the grass at the top of the world, in the sun, very carefully not looking at two of them.

I came from them, he thought.

Winter found Thea at the water’s edge an hour later.

Ice saw it from the fire. He wasn’t meant to. Thea had gone down to the tarn to wash her face, and Winter had followed her, with her bow on her back and her arms folded, and stood beside her on the shore. One blue eye and one pink. Looking at the water.

He knew what it was. He’d heard about it from Gabe, who’d heard it from Xav. A pit, on floor 6, under the moon. Tell me the day it’s a fact. And Thea saying the day, I promise. Twice.

He watched them stand there.

He couldn’t hear them. He didn’t need to. He saw Winter say something, short, flat, the way she said everything. He saw Thea turn her head and look at her. He saw Thea say something back. Three words, maybe four.

He saw Winter go very still.

And then he saw Thea say something else. Longer. With her hand on her left shoulder, on the scar. And Winter listened, with her arms folded, for a long time. And then Winter nodded. Once. And unfolded her arms. And put her hand on Thea’s arm, briefly and hard.

And then she walked back up the slope to the fire, and sat down next to Ina, and didn’t say anything at all.

Ina looked at her.

Winter looked back at him. And then, very slightly, she nodded. And Ina, who had been waiting on a step since floor 9, looked at the fire for a long time, and then at Ice, across it, and then back at the fire.

He didn’t ask. Of course he didn’t.

“What did you tell her?” he said, later. To Thea. When they were packing.

“The truth,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

“She asked me the day,” said Thea. “On six. She made me promise. Tell me the day it’s a fact. The day. Not the day after.” She was rolling up her blanket, very neatly, not looking at him. “So I told her. It’s a fact. That’s what she asked for. The day.”

“And?”

“And she said what is.” Thea tied the blanket. “And I said: it’s a fact about a friend. You don’t put that on a slate.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I said that,” said Thea. “On six. At the top of the pit. About the theory. It’s a theory about a friend. You don’t put that on a slate.” She looked up at him. “It’s still true. It’s more true. It’s not a fact about the tower, Ice. It’s not one record does not match. That’s the tower’s. That went on the rock.” She stood up. “This is yours. It’s a fact about you. And you gave it to me. And I’m not putting it anywhere.”

“Winter—“

“Winter said no. You don’t.” Thea picked up the lantern. “And then she said: good.” She almost smiled. “And then she said she’d been the last to know a lot of things in her life, and she didn’t mind being the second to know there was a thing to know, if the first one was me.”

He stood there in the grass with his pack in his hands.

“She told Ina,” he said. “Not what. That it’s a fact.”

“I know. I saw.” Thea looked across the camp to where Winter was sitting beside Ina, not talking. “She promised him the day too. On the bridge. She keeps her promises.” She was quiet. “So do I.”

She came and stood in front of him.

“Nobody else is getting it from me,” she said. “Not Xola. Not Gigi. Not Gabe. Not the club. Not anybody. Not ever, unless you tell me to.” She put her hand on his chest, flat, over the place where the threads came out. “You’ll tell them. One day. When you’re ready. When it’s not watching. When you want to. And until then it’s mine, and I’m keeping it the way I keep the lantern.” She looked at him. “That’s the deal.”

“That’s not a fair deal,” he said.

“No,” said Thea. “It’s the one you get.”

They went up at noon.

Higu called it, because it was her day with the rock. She stood at the foot of the cliff at the far end of the tarn with the stone in her hands and looked round at all of them, packed, ready, standing in the grass in the sun, and then at the door. Plain. Round-topped. Like all of them.

“Floor 11,” she said. “Ice. You say where.”

Everybody looked at him.

He stood at the front with Gabe on his right and Thea behind him with the lantern, and looked at the door. And thought about it. About what was on the other side. About floor 11, which he’d climbed once, a long time ago, in the first year, and remembered in pieces, very clearly, with the gaps dark between them.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Higu looked at him.

“I mean I know,” said Ice. “I know what was there.” He stopped. “But it changes. Every floor. It changes what I did the old way, and now it changes what we do, and after last night—“ He stopped. “After last night, I don’t think anything up there’s going to be where I left it.”

“Okay,” said Higu. Calm. Not surprised by any of it. “Then you tell us what you remember. And we’ll find out what’s true.”

“Ice is the documentation,” said Chris. “We’re the implementation.”

“The documentation’s out of date,” said Ice.

“It was never up to date,” said Chris. “That’s fine. Nobody’s documentation is ever up to date.” He grinned. “That’s why you have people.”

Ice looked at him. At all of them. Thirty-five people standing in the grass at the top of floor 10, in the sun, with their packs on, waiting for him to say where. And not one of them asking him how he knew.

“It’s water,” he said. “Floor 11. A lot of it. I’ll tell you on the stair.”

“Good enough,” said Higu. She put the rock back in her pack. “Drew.”

Drew looked at her.

“You open it,” said Higu. “It’s your turn. I’ve had the rock all morning.”

Drew looked at the door for a moment. Then he walked up to it, barefoot, with nothing in his hands, and put his open palm flat against it.

“Somebody thank me,” said Drew.

“Thank you, Drew,” said thirty-five people.

He pushed.

Four hundred steps.

They counted them. Names, at first, at the bottom, all of them, the way they’d done it at the gate of the fifth wall and at the Gate itself. Newish, and Calvin, and Hana, and all the way round. Thirty-five names. And at the end, his. Ice. And then Declan, because Gabe nudged him. And then one, because Newish couldn’t help herself, and then two, and everybody laughing, and then everybody counting.

No gap.

He climbed in the middle, the way he climbed everything now. Gabe in front of him on the narrow steps, with Hearthwall on his back, warm, so that walking behind him was like walking behind a stove. Thea behind him with the lantern. He could feel her there. Her thread, close as a rope. Pointing at him the way it had since the clock room on floor 7. Not just pointing any more.

Warm. All the way through.

“Your foot’s not tapping,” said Alea, on his shoulder. Very quietly. Under the count. “You’re climbing four hundred steps toward a floor where nothing is going to be where you left it, with a tower that’s got two records of you and wants to know where the second one came from, and your foot isn’t tapping.”

“No,” he said.

“I’m noting it,” said Alea.

“You always note it.”

“I’m noting it harder,” said Alea. And then, after a while, very quietly, the way she said things she meant: “I liked her the first time, you know. On eighty-eight. When I’d just got a soul and didn’t know what to do with it. She talked to me. Not to you. To me. She was the first person who ever did.” She was quiet. “She didn’t know I could hear her. She said: look after him. He won’t let anybody else.”

He stopped on the stair.

Gabe, in front of him, felt it and stopped too. Thea, behind him, nearly walked into him.

“Ice?” said Thea.

He stood there on the two hundred and something step, in the dark, between floors, with the count going on above and below him, and couldn’t move.

“Alea,” he said.

“I never told you,” said Alea. “I didn’t know how. I didn’t know it was mine to tell. It was hers.” Her nose touched his ear. “But she told you hers last night. On the rock. So I thought you should have that one too.”

He stood there a long moment.

Then he reached back without looking and found Thea’s hand on the stair, and held it, and kept climbing.

“Two hundred and seventy,” said Newish, somewhere above. “Two hundred and seventy-one—“

At the top there was a door.

Plain. Round-topped. Like all of them. And a landing in front of it, wide enough for thirty-six if Salty breathed in. They stopped there, in the dark, the way they did. Because of Higu.

“Go on, then,” said Higu.

He looked at the door.

He thought about the white room. The first minute. Thirty-six circles on a white floor, and thirty-five people asleep in them, and one awake. Standing in the middle. Looking at every face. Hers longest. Writing on a card with his finger and putting it under somebody’s hand, and running, because he couldn’t be one of thirty-six in a room again. Because he knew where that went.

He’d been running for two hundred and eighty-six days.

He wasn’t any more.

He was standing on a landing in the dark between the tenth floor and the eleventh, with thirty-five people round him, and Gabe on his right, and Thea’s hand in his, and nothing in front of him but a door he didn’t know. Not really. Not any more. Not the way he’d known every door for ten years.

He didn’t know what was on the other side.

He found he didn’t mind.

“It’s water,” he said. “A lot of it. I’ll tell you what I remember. And then you can tell me what’s true.”

“Deal,” said Higu.

He opened the door.

The sound came first. It always did.

Not falling water, the way floor 8 had come up the stair at them, a lot of water going a long way down. Not the ticking of the Down. Not the wind off the high side. Something else. Something huge and slow and endless, coming in and going out, in and out, like breathing. Like the Breath under the Bastion, except that it wasn’t fire. Except that it went on forever, in every direction, as far as anyone could hear.

The sea.

And light. Blue and gold and enormous, coming in through the door. Salt in it. Wind.

Behind him, somebody made a sound. Calvin, probably. Or Newish. Or all of them.

He stood in the doorway at the top of four hundred steps with his hand in Thea’s and the sea coming in at him, and he didn’t know where anything was.

He laughed.

And thirty-five people, behind him, on a stair between floors, laughed too. Because he was.
