Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 49

Two Shells

5,681 words · 25 min read

Malcolm had known the night was going to go badly from the moment Rui Xiang stepped over him. He just hadn’t known how.

Now he knew. It was going to go badly downhill.

“Seven,” Drew was saying. He was standing in the middle of the hall in the red light, pulling the straps of his buckler tight, while thirty-four people sat up in their bedrolls round him and watched. “Xav’s party, because Gabe’s going whether I say so or not. Hyvred, because she’s the only one who can see it. And me.” He looked round. “Deston holds the hall. Everybody else stays. Nobody follows us. Nobody goes out alone. If we’re not back by tomorrow night, Deston decides.”

“I’m coming,” said Thea.

She was sitting by the window with the little lantern in her lap. She hadn’t moved since Hyvred pointed. Her voice was very quiet and very level, the way it got when she’d already decided something.

Drew looked at her for a moment.

“No,” he said. Gently. “You’re not.”

“Drew—“

“You’ve got the lantern. If something goes wrong down there, it’ll be something that lies. And then I need you up here, where thirty-four people are, not down there with seven.” He held her eyes. “I’ll tell you everything. Every step. Hyvred’ll whisper you the whole way. I promise.”

Thea looked at him for a long time. Then she looked down at the lantern in her lap.

“Every step,” she said.

“Every step.”

Malcolm got up, and started packing.

He packed the way he always packed: twice. Two waterskins. Two rolls of bandage. Two of the little bitter-root potions Kei had shown him how to make on floor 3, in case the first one didn’t take. A spare bowstring for Hyvred, whose bowstring was fine. A spare strap for Gabe’s shield, whose strap was also fine. Rui Xiang watched him do it from the doorway, spinning his knife.

“You know we’re just following a trail,” said Rui Xiang.

“Down a canyon we’ve never been in. At night. On a floor where something tall and silent lived on the roofs.” Malcolm buckled the pack. “Toward a big black building at the very bottom with a chimney, which is where the boss lives. Following a man who doesn’t want to be found.” He stood up. “Nothing could possibly go wrong.”

“You’re doing the face.”

“I always do the face,” said Malcolm. “It’s my face.”

They went down in the red dark, which was the same as the red day, along the trail that only Hyvred could see.

She went in front, with John beside her. A small light from John’s hand floated out ahead of them both like a lantern on a string. She didn’t need it. She was looking at something else. Every few steps she stopped and turned her head and looked along a wall, or a roof, or down a stair. Then she said “this way” in a small, certain voice and went on, and they followed her.

“It’s bright,” she said, about halfway down. Very quietly. Floor 4 made everybody speak quietly. “It’s so bright, still. It’s like following somebody down a corridor who’s carrying a lamp and is just round the corner. Every corner, I think he’ll be there.”

“He won’t be,” said Malcolm.

“I know he won’t.”

“I’m just saying. So nobody’s disappointed.”

Gabe, walking behind him, didn’t say anything. He hadn’t said anything since where. He walked with the wolf coat buttoned to the neck in the dry heat and his round shield on his arm and the big sacking-wrapped shape still strapped across his back. He’d brought it. Malcolm didn’t know why. Nobody had asked. It was heavy, and it made him slow on the stairs, and he carried it as if he’d forgotten it was there.

The trail went down along the north terraces, roof to roof, then dropped to a long lane cut into the canyon wall, and down a stair, and another. The Red City went past on both sides, empty. Every window dark. Every doorway an open mouth. Once, a row of carrion birds on a roof turned their long necks to watch them pass, all together, like a row of people at a funeral, and didn’t make a sound.

“I hate this floor,” said John, quietly.

“Everybody hates this floor,” said Xav.

“He spent six weeks here.” John’s little light bobbed ahead of them, red-tinged. “Alone. In this.”

Nobody said anything to that.

The dust-hounds came at them on the dry river.

They’d got down to the canyon floor. The trail went out along the riverbed: a wide, flat, cracked channel of red stone with dry banks on either side, the furnace hall a black shape at the far end of it with its chimney going up into the dusk. They were halfway along when Rui Xiang stopped dead and put his hand up.

Malcolm heard it a second later. A sound like gravel being poured.

They came off the left bank in a pack. Seven of them, eight, Malcolm couldn’t count. Long, low, lean things the colour of the rock, with flat heads and too many teeth, running bent-legged across the riverbed in total silence until they were close. And then all at once they were loud. Barking, a horrible dry coughing bark, all of them at once, bouncing off the canyon walls so you couldn’t tell where it came from.

“Shields,” said Drew.

Gabe was already there. He stepped out in front of the rest of them and planted his round shield, and the first hound hit it at a full run. It hit so hard that Gabe went back a step, his boots scraping on the cracked stone, and the hound bounced off and rolled and was up again. Drew was beside him with his buckler, punching. The bladed knuckles went into a hound’s flank with a sound like an axe into a log. Xav moved once and a hound stopped moving.

They came round the sides.

One came at Hyvred. Rui Xiang was there before it reached her. He’d been there, Malcolm realised later, before it had even decided to go for her. He went low, under its jaw, both knives, the way he did everything, and it went down. Then a second hound came off the bank behind him, into his blind side, and closed its teeth on his calf.

Rui Xiang made a sound Malcolm had never heard him make.

“Rui Xiang—“

Malcolm didn’t think. He had his hand out and two blessings on Rui Xiang before the word was finished, harder, harder, the way he’d done since floor 1. They went into him like warmth into a cold room. The hound’s teeth stopped going any further in. Rui Xiang twisted and put a knife into its eye.

Then it was over. It was over very fast, the way fights were. One second barking, the next nothing, the silence of floor 4 coming back down over the riverbed like a lid. Eight dust-hounds lay on the cracked stone round them.

And stayed there.

That was what Malcolm saw. After the fight, when he should have been looking at Rui Xiang’s leg. The dust-hounds didn’t go to light. On floor 1, on 2, on 3, every fight ended the same way. Bright little bursts, one after another, and then an empty patch of grass or snow or water where something had been. You never had to look at what you’d done.

Here they just lay there. On their sides, with their legs out, and their teeth showing, and blood coming out of them onto the red stone. Real blood. Dark and thick and spreading in the cracks. One of them was still twitching. The smell came up off them, hot and coppery and animal. Malcolm hadn’t known a fight had a smell.

Drew went and stood over the twitching one and stopped it with his knuckles, quickly, and turned away.

“Okay,” said John, very quietly. “Okay. That’s different.”

“Malcolm,” said Rui Xiang, through his teeth.

He was sitting on the riverbed with his leg out in front of him. His trousers were torn at the calf, and the calf under them was torn too, deep, four long holes in a curve, bleeding steadily onto the stone. His face had gone grey.

Malcolm knelt down beside him.

He wasn’t a healer. That was the thing. He was a Cleric, but not that kind. He made people harder: put blessings into them, and into shields and swords, so they’d last longer, break less, hold. He’d been doing it since the first day, and he was good at it, and he’d always assumed the first try would fail, so he always did two. Rachael was the healer. Kei was the healer. Xola, Maigo, Newish. Malcolm made things hard to break. He didn’t put them back together.

But he’d said, on floor 1, at the gate. I’d like him to be in one piece when Gabe punches him. And he’d meant it. So on floor 3, in the evenings, he’d asked Rachael. And Rachael had frowned at him for a long time and then shown him.

You don’t push it, she’d said, putting his hand on a cut on her own arm she’d made with a knife for the purpose, without blinking. You don’t tell it what to do. You find what it’s trying to do anyway, and you agree with it.

He put his hands on Rui Xiang’s calf. It was hot and wet. He made himself feel it. The torn places, the four holes going in. The leg trying, already, in its small stupid stubborn way, to close them.

Agree with it.

He agreed with it.

It wasn’t like the blessings. Blessings went out of him, warm, like breath. This went with something, slowly, the way you push a door that’s already swinging. He felt the deepest hole start to close from the bottom. Then the next. Not fast. Not like Rachael. Rachael could close a cut in three seconds; this took him nearly a minute, with sweat running into his eyes, and his own hands shaking. But the bleeding stopped. And then the holes were closed. And then there were four pink curved marks on Rui Xiang’s calf, raised, new.

Rui Xiang looked at them.

“That’s going to scar,” he said.

Malcolm thought of Kei, crouched by the fountain that afternoon. The dead stay. Nothing had scarred since the white room. Every cut since floor 1 had closed clean by morning. But nothing had stayed dead since the white room either, until today.

“It’s my first one,” said Malcolm. “In a fight. If it scars, be grateful it’s on your leg.”

Rui Xiang looked at him. Then he laughed, short and shaky.

“Thanks, Mal.”

“I did two,” said Malcolm. “In case the first failed.” He sat back on his heels and wiped his face. “It didn’t. I don’t know what to do with that.”

By the next morning the four pink marks were gone, the way every cut since floor 1 had gone. Malcolm looked at the smooth skin for a long time. He was relieved. He didn’t know why it had felt like such a near thing.

The furnace hall was cold.

That was the first thing. They’d all expected heat. A boss called the Kiln, in a building with a chimney, at the bottom of a canyon. They’d come in through the big black doorway with their shields up and their weapons out, braced for fire. And it was cold. Cold and dark and silent and smelling of old smoke and hot metal gone cold, the way a forge smells the morning after.

John sent a light up into the roof. It went up and up into the dark, and lit the hall.

There were two of them.

Two Kilns. Malcolm didn’t know what else to call them. Giants of iron and fired clay, each three times as tall as Gabe, lying face down on the floor of the furnace hall side by side, close together, like two sleepers on one bed. Their chest plates were open. Through the openings you could see their hearts, the furnaces, black and cold.

And through the middle of each heart, a long dark hole.

Nobody moved.

“Two,” said Hyvred, in a very small voice.

The one on the left was old. You could see it. Dust lay on its back in a thin red skin, the same as on the roofs. Its clay had gone pale and cracked, and there was a carrion bird’s feather caught in one joint of its arm. The one on the right was newer. No dust. The clay of it still dark. When Malcolm went close, holding his staff out in front of him the way you hold a stick out at a dog that might not be dead, he felt warmth on his face. Faint, very faint, coming off the iron. Like a stove an hour after the fire’s gone out.

“He killed it twice,” said Malcolm.

He didn’t know why he said it out loud. It just came out.

“Once to clear it,” said Drew slowly. He was standing by the old one, looking at the hole in its heart. “Weeks ago. That’s the announcement. Floor 4 has been cleared. Day forty-four.” He turned and looked at the new one. “Then it came back. The way they do. And it was in his way.” He was quiet. “So he killed it again. Today. On his way out.”

“On his way out,” said Gabe.

It was the first thing he’d said since the hall.

He wasn’t looking at the Kilns. He was looking at the floor.

Malcolm looked down too, and saw what Gabe was seeing.

The floor of the furnace hall in front of the two dead giants was scorched. All of it. The red stone had gone black, glassy, crazed with little cracks, in a great fan shape going out from where the Kilns lay, as if something had breathed fire across it. Over and over, so hot that the stone itself had changed. Everywhere the fire had reached, the stone was black.

Except one place.

In the middle of the black fan, right in front of where the Kilns’ chests would have been when they were standing, there was a patch of floor that wasn’t scorched. It was the plain red of the rest of the Red City. A shape. About as tall as a man, and narrower, with a broad round shape at the top of it, and a long straight line going down one side.

A man with a shield. Standing. Right in front of the fire.

There were two of them. One on top of the other, almost exactly, but not quite. Like a picture someone had traced twice with a slightly shaky hand. The same place. The same stance. Feet apart, shield up. He’d stood there, in front of it, while it breathed fire at him. He’d stood there long enough for the stone round his shadow to turn to glass. And then weeks later he’d come back and stood in exactly the same place and done it again.

Gabe walked out into the black fan.

His boots crunched on the scorched stone. He walked out to the shape in the middle and stopped at the edge of it. He looked down at it for a long time. Then he stepped into it.

He fitted. Nearly. The shape was a little narrower than Gabe. A little smaller. But when Gabe stood in it with his feet apart and his round shield up, the way he always stood, the way he’d stood in front of everything since floor 1, his feet were exactly where the unscorched feet were. His shield was exactly where the unscorched shield was. He stood in the red shadow of Declan in the middle of the black glass, with his shield up against a fire that wasn’t there.

Nobody said anything at all.

Gabe lowered the shield.

“He stood in it,” he said. His voice had gone rough. “He just—stood in it. Alone. With nobody behind him. Nobody healing. He just stood in it till it stopped.”

“He’s a Paladin,” said Hyvred quietly. “He can heal himself. Thea read it.”

“That’s not the point.”

Nobody argued.

“The stair,” said Hyvred.

She’d turned away from the shape on the floor. She was looking past the Kilns, to the back of the hall. Malcolm followed her eyes and saw it. A door in the rock at the very back, behind the two dead giants, standing open. Beyond it there was a stair going up into the dark.

“The trail goes up it,” said Hyvred. Her voice cracked. “It’s bright. All the way up. As far as I can see.”

Malcolm felt the whole group go still round him.

He knew the rule. They all did. On floor 1, Deston’s party had found out the hard way: a boss came back if everybody didn’t leave the floor within a day. So the Kiln was dead now, both Kilns were dead, and the stair at the back of the furnace hall was open, and it would stay open until the new one came back. Tomorrow. Some time in the middle of tomorrow.

Until then, anybody could walk up it.

Gabe walked across the hall, past the two dead Kilns, to the open door in the back wall. Nobody stopped him. Malcolm wanted to. He felt his hand come up, and stop. He looked at Xav.

Xav hadn’t moved. He was standing by the old Kiln with his sword loose at his side, watching Gabe, with his calm still face. He wasn’t going to stop him. Malcolm could see it. Xav was going to wait. Wait, then move once. And if the once was Gabe going up those stairs, Malcolm thought, then Xav was going to go up them right behind him, and so was everybody else, and Drew would have to choose between his promise to thirty-four people and his promise to Thea.

Gabe stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame and looked up the stair.

It went up into the dark. Malcolm could see the first twenty steps in John’s light. After that, nothing. Somewhere up there, at the top, a door. And through the door, floor 5. And somewhere on floor 5, today, right now, a man in a white cloak with his boots wrapped in cloth, walking away from them.

Gabe stood there.

Malcolm counted. He didn’t mean to. He counted the way you count between a flash and the thunder. Ten. Twenty. Thirty.

At forty, Gabe sat down.

He sat down on the bottom step of the stair, in the doorway, with his back against the frame and his shield across his knees and the big wrapped shape on the step beside him. He put his elbows on his knees. He looked up the dark stair.

“He asked us to wait,” said Gabe.

His voice was quite steady.

“Train longer on the third floor. He didn’t say come and find me. He said wait. And we did. And he was right. That thing on the roof—“ He stopped. “He went up there first, on his own, and killed it, so we could come down the stair talking. And then he stood here in the fire. Twice. So that when we came down to this hall it’d already met somebody who knew how to stand in front of it.” He looked up the stairs. “He didn’t do all that so I could run up a stair after him at level twenty with no idea what’s at the top.”

Nobody said anything.

“So I’m going to wait,” said Gabe. “Here. Till it closes.”

He folded his arms on his shield.

Xav came across the hall and sat down on the floor beside the door, against the wall, with his sword across his knees. He didn’t say anything. After a moment, Hyvred came and sat on Gabe’s other side. Then John. Then Rui Xiang, stiffly, with his scarred leg out in front of him. Then Drew, who looked at all of them for a long time with an expression Malcolm couldn’t read. He whispered something to Deston, and something to Thea, and then sat down too.

Malcolm stood in the middle of the furnace hall between two dead giants and looked at five people sitting in a doorway.

Then he went and sat down too. He was a pessimist, not an idiot.

They waited all night. They waited all morning.

Nobody slept. Nobody talked much. Hyvred whispered Thea every hour, very quietly, because she’d promised: still here. He’s still sitting. Nothing yet. John kept a light floating in the doorway. Malcolm put two blessings on Gabe’s shield because his hands needed something to do, and then two more. Gabe didn’t move. He sat on the bottom step with his arms folded on his shield and looked up the dark stair, the whole time, the way you’d look down a road for someone you were expecting.

Once, around what would have been dawn, he took the whisper window out and looked at it for a long time. Malcolm, sitting nearest, could see it over his shoulder.

Around the middle of the day, the furnace in the back wall began to glow.

Malcolm saw it first. There was a great round opening in the back wall of the hall, high up, where the chimney started: a furnace mouth, black and cold all night. And now, deep inside it, there was a light. Dull red. Like a coal someone has blown on.

“Gabe,” said Malcolm.

The light grew. Red, then orange. Heat came off it, across the hall, onto their faces. Something moved inside it. Something big, shifting, settling, the sound of clay and iron grinding against each other, like a giant sitting up in bed.

And at the back of the hall, behind Gabe, the stone door began to swing shut.

It went slowly. Grinding. Gabe stood up. He stood on the bottom step with the door coming round toward him and looked up the stair one last time, into the dark, where the trail went.

Xav stood up beside him.

“Get up,” said Xav quietly. “It’s going to burn. Get up.”

It was what he’d said on floor 2, at the pine, by the onion skins. It’s going to snow. Get up. Malcolm hadn’t been close enough to hear it then. He heard it now.

Gabe stepped down off the step. The door ground shut behind him with a sound like a tomb closing, and the trail and the stair and the dark were gone. The plain red rock of the back wall stood in its place as if there’d never been a door there at all.

And out of the furnace mouth, glowing, came a third Kiln.

They ran.

They did it properly twenty days later. All thirty-five of them.

Malcolm spent the twenty days in between learning floor 4, the way everybody did. Learning to move without a sound. Learning the dust. Learning that when you killed something here, someone had to deal with it afterwards. On the first day, nobody had wanted to. By the fourth, Dennis had found out you could sell dust-hound hides in a little ghost market on the plateau, run by a woman with no tongue who answered every question by writing on a slate. By the sixth, Salty and Jared had worked out how to skin them. Waddles refused to eat dust-hound, on principle, and went hungry, and in the end Chi went back down to floor 3 with Higu for a day and came back with a sack of eels.

Malcolm learned to heal.

He made Rachael teach him every evening. She was a terrible teacher and a brilliant healer, and the two things were the same thing: she didn’t know how she did it, she just did it. So she frowned at him, and cut her own arm, and said agree with it over and over until he did. By the end of the second week he could close a cut in fifteen seconds. By the third, in ten. Rachael said he’d never be fast. He agreed with her. He always assumed he’d never be fast. It was the same as assuming the first blessing would fail. You did it anyway.

On the ninth day, Hyvred found a long hall in the rock on the third terrace with cells down both sides. The House of Ash, Cursiffe called it, reading the carving over the door. Where they came to say things. Hyvred went in, and came out a long time later, and sat on the step outside with her face very still. She said the whole place was full of his light. Not a trail. A flood. As if something had happened at the very end of the long corridor that had filled every cell to the brim with lamplight, and soaked it into the stone, and it was still there, weeks later, glowing.

Thea went in alone after that. She was in there for an hour. When she came out, she didn’t say anything to anybody. Malcolm saw her that evening on the high terrace, writing in a small book she’d bought on floor 3, and closing it when Chris came near.

He didn’t ask. He was a pessimist. He assumed he wouldn’t like the answer.

The Kiln, when they fought it, was everything the floor of that hall had said it would be.

Drew called it from the doorway, the way he’d called the Old Stag and the Lanternjaw. Thirty-five people spread round the furnace hall in a ring, tanks in front, healers behind, everybody’s eyes on the giant in the middle of the black fan. It burned hotter and hotter, the clay of it going from red to orange to white, the heat coming off it so fierce that Malcolm’s eyebrows hurt. And then, when it couldn’t hold the heat any more, it opened its chest and breathed.

Azac took the first one. He’d asked to. He stood in front of it with his shield and his whole party behind him and Saga going in his voice, and the wall did not break. For one second, two, he held. Then the fire went through the shield and through him, and he went back across the hall with his health falling like a stone. Rachael caught it. Barely. Four seconds of fire, and Azac was down to a sliver, and the healers were scattered round the ring, and none of them had been close enough.

“It’s too much,” Azac said afterwards, through his teeth, with Rachael’s hands on him. “It’s too much for one. You can’t—nobody could stand in that alone.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He looked at the floor.

The second time, he asked Drew if he could stand in the shape.

Drew looked at him for a long moment, and then nodded.

So Gabe went out into the black fan and stood in the red shadow on the floor, with his feet where the feet were and his shield where the shield was. This time they were ready. Drew pulled every healer in close behind him, in a tight half-ring: Rachael, Xola, Kei, Maigo, Newish, and Malcolm, at the end, because he was the slowest. Deston behind them all with his hands out, gold threads running from his palms into every one of them, linking them, so that what one had, all of them had. Malcolm put two blessings on Gabe’s shield. Then two more. Then, because there was nothing else to put them on, two on Gabe.

“Why are you standing there?” Rachael asked Gabe, low, behind her staff. “There’s room anywhere.”

“Because somebody must have,” said Gabe.

The Kiln burned white. It opened its chest.

The fire came.

Malcolm had never seen anything like it. It wasn’t like fire in a grate. It was like the whole furnace hall had become the inside of a furnace. A roar, a solid wall of white and orange coming across the black fan and hitting Gabe’s shield and going round it. Past him on both sides, so close Malcolm felt his own skin start to tighten, and over his head, and the wolf coat on Gabe’s back smoking. Gabe’s health fell. A quarter in the first second. Malcolm saw it go.

And every healer behind him poured it back in.

Rachael, blue and clean. Xola, water. Kei, green. Maigo’s roots, Newish’s fast bright beats. And Malcolm, at the end of the ring, with his hands out toward Gabe’s back, doing the only thing Rachael had taught him. Finding what Gabe’s body was already trying to do, in all that fire, and agreeing with it. The burned skin trying to close. The heat trying to leave. He agreed with it, with everything he had, as hard as he could. And through Deston’s threads he felt the others doing the same, all of them, all at once, so that it wasn’t six healers but one.

Two. Three. Gabe’s health went down and came up, down and up, down and up, like a man bailing a boat. He held. He stood in the shape on the floor with his feet planted and his shield up and his coat on fire, and held.

Four.

The fire stopped.

The Kiln’s chest was open. Its heart was right there, white-hot, unprotected, in the half-second before the plates came down again.

And Xav moved.

He’d been standing behind Gabe the whole time. Right behind him, in his shadow, in the fire, with his sword held low. He hadn’t moved through the four seconds. He’d waited. Wait. And now, in the half-second after, he moved once. Past Gabe’s shoulder, three steps, the sword going up and in, into the open heart.

It fell the way the others had. Face down. Beside them.

There were three of them on the floor of the furnace hall now, in a row.

The drops were Kiln iron, a lot of it, and coal-glass, and a heavy dark-red ore Salty and Jared and Gabe all fell on at once like children. Nothing else. No first clear. No window about anything Legendary. Whatever Voyager had taken out of that hall on the forty-fourth day, the rankings had never said. Just the announcement. Just Floor 4 has been cleared.

He went and found Gabe.

Gabe was sitting on the floor in the middle of the black fan, in the shape. He’d taken his shield off. The wolf coat was scorched all down the back and smelled terrible. His hands were burned, his face was burned, his eyebrows were gone. He was sitting with his knees up, looking at the floor round him.

Malcolm knelt down and put his hands on him and started agreeing with things.

“Look,” said Gabe.

Malcolm looked.

The stone round them had been black glass before. Now it was blacker, freshly scorched, smoking a little. And in the middle of it, where Gabe had been standing, there was a new shape. Unscorched. Red. A man with a shield. Standing almost exactly on top of the other two, but not quite, because Gabe was a little bigger.

Three. One on top of the other.

“He did that alone,” said Gabe. “Twice. With nobody behind him.”

Malcolm said nothing. He was working on Gabe’s hands.

“I had six healers,” said Gabe. “And Deston. And you. And it was still—“ He stopped. He looked at the floor, at the three shapes. “He just stood in it. On his own. And then he went up the stairs on his own. And he’s going to do it again on the next floor. And the one after.”

Malcolm finished the left hand. He started on the right.

He thought about the dry riverbed, and four holes in Rui Xiang’s leg closing under his hands, and Rachael saying you’ll never be fast. He thought about Declan in their first week at poly, talking to every single person in a room full of strangers until they weren’t strangers. About saying, on floor 1, I’d like him to be in one piece when Gabe punches him. He’d meant it as a joke. He’d always half meant his jokes.

“Then next time,” said Malcolm, “somebody should be behind him.”

Gabe looked at him.

Malcolm didn’t look up from Gabe’s hand. “I’m just saying. Statistically. A man who stands in fire alone on every floor is going to have a bad day eventually. It’d be better if, when he did, there was somebody standing behind him who knew how to agree with burns.” He finished the right hand and sat back. “I’m not fast. But I’m there.”

Gabe looked at him for a long time.

Then he laughed. Burned and scorched and eyebrowless, sitting in the shape of his best friend on the floor of the furnace hall, he laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

“Mal,” he said. “You absolute pessimist.”

“I always do two,” said Malcolm. “In case the first one fails.”
