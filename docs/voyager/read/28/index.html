Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 28

The Stopped Watch

3,107 words · 14 min read

Gigichi’s watch had stopped at nine minutes past two, and she hadn’t told anyone that either.

It was a gold pocket watch, small and old-fashioned, with a leaf engraved on the lid. It had been clipped to her hip on her model for as long as she’d had the model. It had come through the white room because she was wearing it, the way Wendy’s headphones had, and Hyvred’s glasses, and Maigo’s stupid pixel sunglasses. It was the only thing she had from before that wasn’t her own body.

And it didn’t tick.

She’d opened it on the first morning, in the white room, while everybody was panicking. The hands said nine minutes past two. She’d shaken it and wound it and tapped it, and the hands had stayed exactly where they were: nine past two, the second hand frozen halfway between the four and the five. She’d thought it was broken.

Then the lights had said your world holds its breath. Not one grain falls, and Chris had stood up and explained it, and she’d looked down at the watch in her hand. Nine past two. And she’d understood, very suddenly and very coldly, that it wasn’t broken at all. It was just telling the truth.

Back home it was nine minutes past two, and it was going to be nine minutes past two until they got back. If they got back.

She checked it every morning anyway. She didn’t know why. It was always the same.

“Come on,” said Hyvred. “Come on, come on, come on, it’s this way, it goes this way—“

They were going along the treeline on floor 2, in the snow, east from the stair door, on the first afternoon. Hyvred was in front, half-running, her horns catching the grey light and her tail lashing. Every few metres she stopped and looked down at the snow with her left eye, the one with the little red mark in it, and then set off again. Bee was right behind her, because Bee was always right behind her. Hako was behind Bee, grinning, because Hako was always grinning. Kei walked behind Hako, calm, the little green snake on his wrist tasting the cold air.

Gigi was at the back. She was usually at the back. She liked it there; you could see everything coming.

“There’s nothing there,” she said, to nobody in particular. “It’s snow.”

“It’s not nothing,” said Hyvred, over her shoulder. “It’s footprints. Gold ones. I told you.”

“You told us. We can’t see them.”

“I can see them.”

“I know you can see them. I’m just saying, to the rest of us, it’s snow.”

Hyvred turned round, walking backwards in the snow, and grinned at her: the big, bright, fearless grin she’d had since the white room, since she’d woken up and seen the ceiling and cried.

“Then you’ll just have to trust me, Gigi,” she said.

Gigi didn’t say anything.

That was the thing about Hyvred. She said things like trust me as if they were easy. As if you just did, and that was all. As if there’d never been anybody in your life you’d trusted completely, for years, who’d turned round one day and decided you weren’t worth the effort.

Gigi! With me! Hyvred had shouted, in the white room, before the second was even over. Before Gigi had finished standing there, alone, with her arms crossed, being the one who didn’t get picked. Hyvred hadn’t even thought about it. She’d just grabbed Gigi’s hands.

Gigi had been trying to work out ever since what she was supposed to do with that.

The pine was enormous. Gigi had never seen a tree that big, not even on her model’s art, which had a lot of trees on it. It stood at the edge of the wood like a building, its branches so wide and low that the snow lay thin and patchy on the ground underneath. It was dim and dry under there, and smelled of resin.

There was a ring of stones in the snow. Old ashes. A flattened place among the needles, the length of a person.

And a few onion skins. Most of them were gone; Tiger had taken one, and Hana had it in her crown, everyone had heard. But there were a few left, frozen into the snow by the stones, papery and pale.

Hyvred stopped at the edge of the branches and stood very still.

“He sat there,” she said, quietly. She pointed at a place by the ring of stones that looked exactly like every other place by the ring of stones. “There. With his back to the trunk. That’s where the gold is brightest.” She moved her finger. “And he slept there. And there’s the little one again. Next to him. Curled up.”

“The little one,” said Hako.

“There was a little one on the bench at the Fold. Like a cat. Lying next to where he slept.” Hyvred’s voice had gone strange. “It’s here too. He’s got something with him. Something small. That sleeps next to him.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

Gigi looked at the place by the fire where, according to Hyvred, a man had sat on his own, peeling onions, in the snow, on his first night on floor 2, with something small asleep beside him.

She’d known Ice for four years. Through Xola, and then through Thea, back when she and Thea had done everything together. He’d been in the calls. He’d been funny. He’d been, she’d thought at the time, a bit much: always on, always joking, always making everybody laugh. She hadn’t trusted it. She didn’t trust people who were that easy to be around. Easy was usually hiding something.

And then she’d watched him in a call one night when Thea was upset about something. Gigi couldn’t remember what now. Exams, probably. Thea was always upset about exams. And Ice had stopped joking. Just stopped, completely, mid-sentence. He’d gone quiet, and started asking Thea questions. Small, boring questions, about other things, one after another, until Thea was talking about something else and had forgotten she’d been upset. Then he’d gone back to joking as if nothing had happened.

Gigi had decided that night that she’d been wrong about him. It didn’t happen very often.

“He’s on his own,” said Bee, bluntly. Bee said everything bluntly; it was one of the reasons Gigi liked her. “That’s what I keep thinking. Everywhere he goes. Nobody with him. Just him and a little gold cat.”

“We don’t know it’s a cat,” said Kei.

“Whatever it is.” Bee folded her arms. “It’s not people.”

Kei had crouched down by the ring of stones. The little green snake had come down off his wrist and was lying along the back of his hand, her head raised, her tongue flickering in and out, tasting the cold air under the pine. She did that a lot. Gigi had stopped noticing it after the first week.

Kei was very still, listening to something.

“She says it smells of woodsmoke,” he said, after a moment. “And onions. And snow.” He paused. “And somebody who hasn’t slept properly in a long time.”

Gigi stared at him.

“Your snake talks?”

“To me.” Kei didn’t look up. “Only to me. Since the white room. I thought everyone knew.” He considered. “Yan’s does too. To him.”

“I did not know that.”

“She’s very rude about most people,” said Kei mildly. “I didn’t think you’d want to know what she says about you.”

“What does she say about me?”

Kei listened for a moment. His mouth twitched.

“She says you’re the only one here with any sense,” he said. “And that you should wear a hat.”

Gigi opened her mouth, and closed it again.

“What’s her name?” said Hako, crouching down to look at the snake, who looked back at him with an expression of deep contempt.

“She hasn’t told me,” said Kei. “I’ve offered several. She’s turned them all down.” He held his hand up a little, and the snake turned her head and looked him in the eye. “She says I can call her something when I’ve earned it.”

“How do you earn it?”

“She hasn’t said that either,” said Kei, and very gently put her back on his wrist.

It was Hako who said they should tell Gabe.

They were back at the edge of the trees, with the light going and the cold coming down off the mountain like water, and Hyvred had looked up the slope. The footprints went on, she said. Up. East. Toward the hut on the slope that everybody knew about now, and past it, and up, toward the top of the mountain where the gold light burned on the pass.

“Gabe should know,” said Hako. He wasn’t grinning, for once. “He’s been looking for him since the white room. He’s on floor 1 right now, typing his name into World. If we can see where he went, Gabe should know.”

“No,” said Gigi.

Everybody looked at her.

“If you tell Gabe,” she said, “Gabe will come up the stair tonight. On his own. In the dark. And he’ll follow a trail he can’t see, because Hyvred will have told him it’s there, and he’ll go up the mountain after it.” She folded her arms. “And he’ll freeze to death on the pass, or the wolves will get him. And then we’ll have to tell Deston we killed Gabe.”

“That’s very dark,” said Hako.

“It’s very likely.”

“She’s right,” said Kei. He said it quietly, and everybody listened, because Kei was the one who thought before he spoke. “Not about the dying. About the going. Gabe would go.” He paused. “But we shouldn’t sit on it either. That’s not fair to him.”

“So what do we do?” said Hyvred.

“Tell Deston,” said Kei. “Deston will know when to tell him.”

They looked at each other. Then Hyvred opened her window and found Deston’s name in the rankings, and the small envelope beside it, and wrote a whisper.

[ Whisper to Deston
its hyvred. i can see where he went. his light leaves a trail, like footprints, only i can see it. it goes from the stair east to the big pine and then up the mountain toward the pass. we found where he slept. didnt tell gabe. thought youd know when.
]

The reply came very quickly. As if Deston had been sitting with his window open.

[ Whisper from Deston
thank you. really.
please dont tell gabe yet. not till were all on floor 3. hes being careful and i need him to stay careful.
please.
]

Gigi read it over Hyvred’s shoulder.

Please, twice. She remembered the line in Deston’s guide in World about the ram, the one everybody had laughed at. 4. Don’t go alone. Please. She’d thought at the time it was a joke. She didn’t think so now.

Deston was frightened. Deston, who was the calmest person in the whole of Ice’s world except maybe Kei and Yan, was frightened of something. And he wasn’t saying what.

She filed that away. She was good at filing things away.

They went up the north slope the next day, because it was on Drew’s board.

Gigi had thought the board was stupid when she first saw it: a big black slate in the trapper’s hall, covered in chalk, with a map and lines and names and a terrible drawing of a wolf. Then she’d read it. The north slope was two packs and needed a full party. The east slope was cleared. Don’t go up the pass without being ready for an old woman to shout at you. And she’d thought, grudgingly: oh. That’s actually very useful. She’d never say so to Drew.

The north slope was two packs, exactly as the board said. They came out of the trees at the top of a long white gully, white on white, ten of them, and they went round.

Hako held the front with his spear and shield, grinning again. Hyvred was on a rock with her bow, the reticle in her eye locking and unlocking, an arrow going out every few seconds. Kei kept everybody standing. And Bee, in the middle of all of it, was drawing.

That was Bee’s thing. What she drew took form. Not for long, a few seconds, half a minute at most, but it did. She drew in the air with her finger, with a faint honey-coloured line coming off the tip of it like a sparkler. Whatever she drew hung there, glowing, and then it was real. A net, a wall, a cage. Once, on floor 1, a very large and very angry bird that had chased a Woolback across two fields. But she had to stand still to do it. She had to concentrate. And the wolves knew it.

Gigi saw the two of them coming round the back, low through the snow, while Bee stood in the middle of the gully with her finger up, drawing a net and not looking. They were going for Bee. They were always going to go for Bee. She was the one standing still.

Gigi didn’t think.

She was a Mage. Roots and thorns. She’d been calling roots up out of the ground for twelve days now, tripping Woolbacks, tangling Rimehounds’ feet, holding things in place for a second or two. It was slow. You had to ask the ground, and the ground took its time. But she didn’t ask this time. She just put her staff down into the snow, hard, and wanted.

A wall of thorns came up out of the snow round Bee.

Not a wall. A ring. All the way round her, waist-high and then shoulder-high, black-green, the thorns as long as Gigi’s fingers. It burst up through the snow in a single heave, so fast and so thick that the two wolves coming round the back ran straight into it at full speed. And they stuck. The thorns caught their thick white fur and held on, and the more the wolves struggled, the more they were held.

Bee looked round, startled, from the middle of a ring of brambles, with her finger still up.

“Oh,” she said. “Thanks.”

“Finish your net,” said Gigi, through her teeth. Holding it was like holding a door shut against a crowd. “Quickly.”

Bee finished her net.

[ Unique skill: Bramble
Grow it where you need it. It doesn’t let go.
]

Gigi read it afterwards, sitting in the snow with her hands shaking, while the others skinned wolves. It doesn’t let go. She looked at it for a long time.

“That’s very you,” said Bee, reading it over her shoulder. Bee read everything over everybody’s shoulder; she had no sense of privacy whatsoever.

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means you don’t let go.” Bee sat down next to her in the snow. “You never let go of anything, Gigi. You hold on to it until it’s got thorns on.” She considered. “It’s not a criticism. It saved my life. I’m just saying.”

Gigi didn’t answer.

That evening, she went out to the edge of the stockade to look at the light on the mountain.

She’d told herself she was going out to look at the light. That was true. It was a beautiful light, a steady gold spark at the very top of the pass, unmoving, as if somebody had pinned a star to the mountain. Everybody in Larchwick knew the story by now. The Long Storm. The old woman. The young man in a stinking wolf coat who’d sat in the tower door all night. Hana’s party had told it at the board.

But that wasn’t why she’d gone out.

She’d gone out because she’d been in the trapper’s hall when Thea came in from the east slope. She’d seen her face in the firelight. The shadows under her eyes, like bruises. The way she’d stood at the board, very straight, reading her own small line at the bottom of it, and touched it with one finger as if she were checking it was still there.

Gigi had seen that face before. Before every exam, every year, for years. Thea staying up all night and then pretending she hadn’t. And Gigi had always been the one who noticed. The one who said you look like death, go to bed. The one who brought her coffee.

Until she hadn’t been.

Until Gigi had been busy, and tired, and bad at replying, and bad at turning up. Bad at all the small boring things you have to keep doing to keep a friendship alive. Thea had done all of them, for both of them, for a year. And then one day she’d stopped, and said so, and Gigi had been so angry she’d stopped speaking to her. Angry because Thea was right. That was the part she’d never said, not even to herself. Thea had been right. A bit.

She stood at the stockade and looked at the light on the mountain.

Someone came out across the snow behind her. She knew who it was before she turned round. Drew had a way of walking, unhurried, as if wherever he was going would wait for him.

She talked to him. She said more than she meant to. And then she stopped herself, just in time, before she said the thing she’d come out into the cold to say, and went back in through the gate too fast, with her boots crunching and her face burning.

She sat on her bunk at the back of the hall in the dark, with her fur round her, and took out the watch.

Nine minutes past two. Still. Always.

She sat there and looked at it for a long time. Somewhere back home, at nine minutes past two, she was sitting at a desk with her phone face down, not texting anybody. And somewhere else, at exactly the same frozen second, Thea was probably sitting at a desk too. Not texting her back.

Not one grain falls.

If they got home, she thought, it would still be nine past two. The same night. The same silence. Nothing would have moved at all. Unless somebody moved it.

She closed the watch.
