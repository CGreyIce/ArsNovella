Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 4: The Second Row

Chapter 122

Three and a Long

8,548 words · 37 min read

Marrit’s boat was called the Kittiwake, and she was older than anybody in Hythe except the man who’d built her.

“Him,” said Marrit, and jerked her head up the quay at her grandfather, who was sitting on a bollard in the grey before dawn with a blanket round his shoulders, watching them load. “He built her when he was twenty. For my grandmother. She’s had four masts and nine rudders and I’ve lost count of the planks.” She slapped the gunwale. “She’ll get us to the Teeth and back. She’s not scared of anything. She’s too old to be scared.”

The Kittiwake was a tide-boat. Long and narrow and low, with a single outrigger on one side like a long arm reaching out to steady her, and two brown sails, patched in so many colours that they looked like a quilt. She sat on the mud at the foot of the harbour steps on her side, waiting, and they loaded her where she lay, because the tide wasn’t in yet, and when the tide came in she’d stand up and go, whether they’d finished loading or not.

They’d finished. Just.

Seven of them. Marrit at the tiller. Thea in the bow, with the lantern. Drew, barefoot, who’d sat down in the middle of the boat on the boards the moment he got in and hadn’t moved. Higu with the rock in her lap. Chi with Waddles. Xola, who’d climbed in, climbed straight back out again, gone and stood in the shallows to her knees with her eyes shut, and then climbed back in, looking blissful. And Ice, at the mast, because Marrit had looked at him once and said you, you’ve done this before, and handed him a rope.

He had. He held the rope.

The water came in under them in the dark. He felt the Kittiwake lift. Sit up. Wake. The way the boats had in the harbour on the first evening, one by one, like people. She came off the mud with a small sucking sound, and swung, and the outrigger dipped, and Marrit said something to her under her breath that wasn’t to any of them, and pulled the tiller over, and the brown sails filled.

On the bollard at the top of the steps, Corran lifted a hand.

He didn’t say nobody goes to the Teeth. He’d said it for thirty years, Marrit said. He didn’t say it now. He just lifted a hand, an old brown hand like a knot in a rope, and held it up, and watched them go out through the gap in the wall with the grey light coming up behind them.

“Bring her back,” he called. Thin. Carrying.

“I always bring her back,” Marrit called.

“I meant you,” said the old man, and put his hand down.

It was a day’s sail. Marrit had said so, and Marrit was right about the sea in the way that the Down’s ropers had been right about the light: without thinking, because she’d never had to think about it.

He hadn’t been in a small boat on open water since the first life.

He’d forgotten what it was like. On the Patience the sea had been something underneath you, a long way down, past the deck and the hold and the keel. On the Kittiwake it was right there. A hand’s breadth below the gunwale. Green and alive and moving, and every swell that came in under them lifted the whole boat up and set it down again like somebody picking up a cup. He could feel the weight of the water through the mast. The pull of the wind in the rope in his hands. Marrit, at the back, leaning on the tiller with her tide-cord wrapped round her fingers, reading something in the water he couldn’t see.

Drew was sick at the second hour.

He did it with enormous dignity. He’d been sitting completely still in the middle of the boat since they left, cross-legged, with his hands open on his knees and his eyes half closed, breathing, the way he did before anything, and Ice had thought he was meditating. He wasn’t. At the second hour he opened his eyes, said, very calmly, “Excuse me,” leaned over the side, and was comprehensively ill into the sea.

Then he sat back up, wiped his mouth, closed his eyes again, and put his hands back on his knees.

“Drew,” said Thea, from the bow. Trying not to laugh.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re green.”

“I’m still,” said Drew. With his eyes shut. “Be still. Then strike once, with nothing.” He breathed out, long. “It turns out the sea doesn’t care if you’re still.”

“Somebody thank him,” said Chi, in her slow calm voice.

“Thank you, Drew,” said everybody in the boat except Marrit, who looked at them all as if they were mad.

And Waddles went in.

Nobody saw him decide to. One moment he was sitting in Chi’s lap the way he always sat, a small round yellow penguin with one eye shut in a permanent wink, and the next he’d stood up, waddled to the side of the boat, looked at the water, looked back at Chi, and gone over the gunwale headfirst like a dropped loaf.

Chi made a sound. Very small. Not alarmed. Something else.

And Waddles came up beside the boat.

Not bobbing. Swimming. Fast. Faster than the Kittiwake was going, a small yellow shape under the green water, flying, with a stream of silver bubbles behind him, turning and rolling and coming up to breathe and going under again. He shot ahead of the bow. He came back. He went round the outrigger twice. He came up right under Chi’s hand where she was leaning over the side, and looked at her with his one open eye, and went under again.

Chi watched him with her hand in the water.

She didn’t say anything for a long time. She just watched. Her face had gone very still and very soft, the way it went when she was singing, and the yellow ahoge on top of her head was standing straight up in the wind.

“He’s never done that,” she said eventually. “He’s never been in water. Not once. On any floor.” She was quiet. “I didn’t know he could.”

“Penguins swim,” said Higu.

“I know penguins swim.” Chi’s hand moved in the water. Waddles came up under it again. “I didn’t know he could. He’s never had the sea.”

Xola, beside her, had both hands over her mouth and her eyes full.

Ice looked at the small yellow shape flying under the green water beside the boat, and thought about floor 1. About thirty-five people waking up in Hearthstead with companions who’d been drawings, a day before. Squiddles. Suzu. Kei’s snake, who had no name. Waddles. Alive. And how none of them had known, really, what alive meant. What they could do. What they’d been waiting for.

Eleven floors, and a penguin had found the sea.

The higher you go, the more real it gets, said Alea, very quietly, on his shoulder. It’s not just the world.

He held the rope, and didn’t say anything, and watched Waddles fly.

Marrit talked to Thea.

He didn’t know when it started. Some time after noon, with the sun high and the wind steady and the Teeth starting to come up out of the haze ahead of them, black, and the white speck on the biggest of them getting bigger. Thea had come back from the bow to sit on the thwart near the tiller with the lantern on her knees, and Marrit had looked at her sideways, the way the tide-folk looked at everything, and said something, and Thea had said something back, and laughed. And then they were talking.

He listened from the mast without meaning to. He couldn’t help it.

“—since I was four,” Marrit was saying. “Every night. I’d sit on the end of the wall with my feet over and look at the Teeth. Just black. Nothing. Rock and wreck. And I’d think, there’s something out there. There has to be. Why else would he tell me no every single time?”

“And he never told you why?”

“Nobody goes to the Teeth.” Marrit did her grandfather’s voice. It was very good. Thin and cracked and absolutely certain. “That’s all. Thirty years. Nobody goes to the Teeth, Marrit.” She shook her head. “And now there’s a light on them. And he just let me. He just sat on his bollard and said bring her back.” She was quiet. “He’s never let me do anything. Not the Patience. Not the Long Water. He’s going to take her west himself, he says. On her first voyage. Him. At eighty-nine. With his chest.” She pulled the tiller over a fraction. “He can’t walk to the end of the quay without stopping twice.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

Ice watched her not say it. He watched her look at Marrit’s left hand on the tiller. Brown. Rope-scarred. Smooth across the back.

“He loves you very much,” said Thea.

“He’s a stubborn old goat.”

“Yes,” said Thea. “That’s what it looks like. When they love you and they’re scared.” She smiled at the lantern on her knees. “I know a few.”

Marrit looked at her. Then at Ice, at the mast, holding his rope and pretending not to listen. Then back at Thea.

“Oh,” said Marrit. “Oh. Him?”

“Him,” said Thea.

“He’s skinny.”

“He’s very skinny,” Thea agreed.

“Can he cook?”

“He can cook,” said Thea. “He can cook really well.”

Marrit considered this, with the tide-cord round her fingers and the Teeth coming up ahead. Then she nodded, slowly, the way she’d nodded at the thirty-six of them on the quay, revising a cargo list.

“That’s something,” she said. “Grandad can’t boil an egg. Grandma married him anyway.”

The Teeth were exactly what Corran had said they were. Rock and wreck.

They came up out of the sea in a long ragged line, black, sharp, leaning, like the lower jaw of something enormous that had died with its mouth open. The swell went in among them white and came out again green, and the noise of it was a deep slow booming, over and over, like a door slamming in a house a long way off. And caught among the rocks, everywhere, there were ships.

Old ones. Very old. Ribs, mostly. Black curved timbers sticking up out of the water like the bones of a whale, crusted with weed and shells, with the sea going in and out between them. A mast, broken off, leaning. A whole bow, almost whole, wedged between two rocks with its figurehead still on it: a woman with her arms out, worn smooth, no face. He counted eleven before he stopped counting.

“From before my grandmother,” said Marrit. Very quietly. She’d brought the sails down. They were going in on the oars now, Drew and Ice rowing, slowly, with Marrit in the stern calling every stroke and her eyes never leaving the water. “The sea’s had them. Nobody comes out for them. Nobody comes out at all.”

And on the biggest tooth, in the middle, the lighthouse.

He’d been looking at it for an hour. Watching it come up out of the haze. Get bigger. Get clearer. He’d known what he’d see when it was close enough, and he’d been right, and he still wasn’t ready for it.

The Last Light.

A white tower, round, tapering, four storeys high, on the flat top of the biggest rock. A lamp-room at the top with glass all round, glinting. A gallery under it with an iron rail. And at the foot of the tower, joined to it, a low stone house with a slate roof and one chimney and one door.

A blue door.

He’d never said blue. He’d never said door. He’d said a white tower on the last rock, and that was all.

I know, said Alea, before he could think it.

The lamp at the top was turning. Even now. In the afternoon sun, pale and thin, you could just see it: a flicker in the glass, going round. Three short. One long.

And at the foot of the rock, where the swell came in and went out, the white stone of the tower was crusted with barnacles. Thick. Grey-white. Layers of them. And weed above them, a green-brown band, a metre deep, where the tide came to.

Higu, in the bow, leaned out over the water as they came in alongside and put her hand flat on the barnacles, and held it there.

“These take years,” she said. Calm. “These take years, Marrit.”

“It wasn’t here,” said Marrit. Her voice had gone very flat again. “Three days ago. I sailed past here three days ago, coming back from the long rocks with a hold full of mussels. There was nothing on this rock. Nothing.” Her hand on the tiller was white. “I’d have seen it. I’ve looked at this rock every night of my life.”

Higu took her hand off the barnacles.

She looked at it. At her palm. Then she took out the chalk, and wrote on the rock in her lap, in her small neat capitals, without saying anything.

BARNACLES: YEARS. TOWER: 3 DAYS.

They went up at low water. Marrit stayed with the boat.

“Somebody has to,” she said. “And I’m not going in there.” She looked up at the blue door at the top of the rock, at the white tower, at the lamp going round. “I wanted to see what was on the Teeth. Now I’ve seen it.” She sat down in the stern with her arms round her knees. “Shout if you need me. I’ll come. But I’m not going in.”

So there were six of them on the rock. Thea, Drew, Higu, Chi, Xola, and him.

There were steps. Cut into the rock from the landing shelf up to the house, worn in the middle, the way steps get worn by a hundred years of feet. Weed on the lower ones. Dry on the top. He walked up them and knew them. Every one. There was a step near the top, the fourth from the door, that rocked when you stood on it, because the stone had cracked. He’d turned his ankle on it the first time, on the first evening, at the end of two months on the Long Water, so tired he could barely see. He stepped over it now without looking down.

Behind him, he heard Thea step over it too.

He stopped.

She’d been watching his feet. She’d seen him step over it, and stepped over it, the way you would, behind somebody. That was all. Nobody else had noticed. Higu was behind her, looking at the house. Drew was behind Higu, still faintly green. Chi was carrying Waddles, dripping. Xola was at the back with her gills half spread, looking at everything.

He didn’t look at Thea. He couldn’t.

Careful, said Alea. Very quietly.

He went up the last steps to the blue door.

It was open. A hand’s width. The way the Gate had been. Warm air coming through it, and the smell of a peat fire, and something cooking. Fish. Onions. The smell he’d walked into at the end of two months, half-dead, and nearly cried.

He pushed the door, and went in.

It was all there.

That was the first thing. All of it. He stood in the doorway with the others coming in behind him and it was all there, exactly, the way it had been: the low room with the beams so close overhead that Gabe had hit his head on every one of them, the first time, one after another, until the keeper had laughed. The peat fire in the black iron range at the far end. A blue kettle on the range, enamel, chipped on the spout. A long table scrubbed white, with benches. Shelves along the wall with shells on them, dozens, every shape, and a ship in a bottle. A chair by the fire with a cushion on it, a rush-seated chair with one leg shorter than the others, so that whoever sat in it rocked, very slightly, all the time. A door in the far wall, low, round-topped, into the foot of the tower, where the stair was.

The stair up to the lamp. And the other stair, the one that went down, under the rock, to floor 12.

He’d never said any of it. Not one thing. Not the kettle or the shells or the beams or the chair. He’d said a white tower on the last rock.

It had all of it.

And in the chair, rocking, very slightly, by the fire, there was an old woman with a pipe.

He knew her too.

Small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with white hair under a black knitted cap and a shawl round her shoulders, and the pipe in the corner of her mouth, unlit. Wenna. Wenna of the Light. She’d kept it fifty years when he met her. Her husband had kept it before her and her husband’s mother before him. She’d fed thirty-four half-dead people fish and onions for three days and made them sleep in her bunks and her loft and on her floor, and laughed at Gabe hitting his head, and on the last night she’d sat in that chair and told them about the edge, where the sea went over into white, as if it were a thing you could talk about over tea.

She looked up when they came in.

Not at them. At him.

Only at him. Past Thea, past Drew, past all of them, straight to him, in the doorway, with the light behind him. Her eyes were very bright and very dark. Exactly the same. Her face did exactly the same thing it had done the first time: crinkled up at the corners, slowly, all over, like paper being folded.

“Well,” said Wenna. “You’ve come a long way for a light.”

He knew what came next.

He’d said it. The first time. Standing in this doorway, at the end of the Long Water, with the rain coming in behind him and Gabe beside him and thirty-two people at his back, so tired he couldn’t feel his feet. He’d looked at the old woman in the chair and said the first thing that came into his head, which was a joke, because it was always a joke, with him.

We came a long way for a door.

And she’d laughed. And got up. And put the kettle on.

He could feel the words. Right there. In his mouth. The shape of them. Ten years ago and a whole life away, and they were right there, ready, as if he’d said them yesterday. All he had to do was open his mouth and they’d come out by themselves.

The room was very quiet.

Wenna was waiting. Rocking, very slightly, in the chair with the short leg. Looking at him. Only him. With the unlit pipe in the corner of her mouth and her face folded up into its smile. Waiting for him to say it.

He felt Thea, behind him, go absolutely still.

He didn’t look at her. He felt her hand. Not on his arm. Not touching him at all. Just there. Somewhere behind his left elbow. Close enough to touch, and not touching. Holding still. The way she’d held a spell on a rock for twenty minutes while an old crab dragged him toward the deep.

Be the man who’s never been there.

He opened his mouth.

“No,” he said. “We came out of one.”

The old woman went on smiling.

“Two days ago,” said Ice. “Out of a door in a cliff. Above Hythe.” He kept his voice very level. “We’ve not come a long way at all. We’ve only just got here.”

Wenna rocked in the chair. Forward. Back. Forward.

“Well,” she said. “You’ve come a long way for a light.”

Nobody moved.

It was the same. Exactly the same. The same words, the same voice, the same crinkle at the corners of her eyes, the same slow fold of her face into the smile. As if he hadn’t said anything. As if nothing had been said, in the whole room, since she last spoke. She sat in the chair with the short leg and rocked, forward and back, and looked at him, and waited.

He’d seen this before.

Not here. On floor 1. On the first morning of the first life, in Hearthstead, when the people had stood on the same corners every day and said the same words. When you could say anything at all to the baker’s boy and he’d say fresh bread, fresh bread, warm from the oven back at you, smiling, and wait, and say it again.

“She’s not breathing,” said Chi.

She said it in her slow calm voice, very quietly, from beside Higu. She was standing just inside the door with Waddles dripping in her arms, and she was looking at the old woman’s chest.

“Under the shawl,” said Chi. “She’s not breathing. I’ve been watching. Not once. Since we came in.”

Everybody looked.

The shawl didn’t move.

“And the kettle,” said Chi. Still very calm. “Look at the steam.”

The blue kettle was on the range. Steam was coming out of the chipped spout. A long white curl of it, rising. Curving over at the top, the way steam does, toward the chimney.

It wasn’t moving.

It hung there. A white curl in the air above the kettle. Perfectly still. Like a curl of steam somebody had drawn.

Xola made a small sound and took a step back toward the door, and walked into Drew, and Drew put a hand on her shoulder without looking at her and kept it there.

Higu walked past Ice into the room.

She did it very calmly. The way she did everything. She walked across the low room to the shelves along the wall, where the shells were, and picked one up. A big one, spiral, pink inside. She looked at it. She turned it over. She put it down. She picked up another one, from the back of the shelf, behind the others. And stopped.

“This one’s blank,” said Higu.

She held it out. In her palm. It was a shell. The shape of a shell. The size of one. But there was nothing on it. No ridges. No colour. No spiral. Just a smooth pale shell-shaped thing, like a shell somebody had started to make and not finished.

She put it down. She went along the shelf. Picking up the ones at the back. Turning them over.

“Blank,” she said. “Blank. That one’s fine. That one’s blank.” She stopped at the ship in the bottle. Bent down and looked at it closely, through the glass. “The front’s fine. The sails. The rigging. The name.” She tilted her head. “The back of it isn’t there.”

She straightened up. She looked round the room. At the beams. At the range. At the scrubbed white table and the benches. At the far end of the table, where it went into the shadow under the window, and the grain of the wood just stopped, and it was smooth and pale and nothing, like the shell.

“It’s only finished,” said Higu slowly, “where somebody looked.”

And then she stopped.

He watched her stop. He watched her get to the end of the sentence and see where it went, the way Thea had seen it on the rock in the sea. Built out of what someone remembers. He watched her look at the table, and the kettle, and the old woman in the chair, who had only ever looked at one person. He watched her not look at him.

She didn’t say it.

She stood in the middle of a room that was only finished where somebody had looked, and turned round, and took out her chalk, and wrote on the rock, without looking at anybody. And he saw what she wrote, because she held the rock where he could see it. On purpose. Just for a moment.

PAINTED.

That was all.

“Drew,” said Higu. “The door.”

Drew went to the low round-topped door in the far wall. The door into the foot of the tower.

He’d stopped being green. He walked across the room barefoot on the flagstones, past the old woman in the chair, who didn’t turn her head to watch him go, and put his open hand flat on the door. The way he’d put it on every door since floor 10. Somebody thank me. He didn’t say it this time.

He pushed.

The door opened inward on dark. And a stair. Stone. Going up, curving round, the way the stairs in all the towers curved, into the dark of the tower above.

And down. The other one. The one he knew. A narrow stair going down, under the rock, into a deeper dark, cold air coming up it, and the sound, very faint, of a long way below.

Drew looked at it. Then he put his hand out, into the doorway, toward the stair going down.

And stopped.

His palm was flat against something.

Not the dark. Not the stair. Something in front of the stair. Flat. Cold. Right there in the doorway, where there shouldn’t have been anything. He moved his hand. It slid. Like a hand on glass. On a wall.

“It’s a picture,” said Drew.

He pressed harder. Nothing gave. The stair went on going down into the dark behind his hand, curving, with the cold air coming up it, and his hand was flat against it, a hand’s breadth in front of the first step, on nothing.

“It’s a picture of a stair,” said Drew. Very quietly. “On a wall. There’s nothing behind it.” He took his hand away. Looked at his palm. “There’s nothing in it.”

Like a picture of a house, Hyvred had said, on the porch, with her eyes doing the thing.

She’d been exactly right.

He felt the air in the room change.

The old woman in the chair had stopped rocking. She was sitting perfectly still with the short leg of the chair tipped forward, the way it had tipped when she rocked, and not tipping back. Her face was still folded into its smile. Her eyes were still on him. They hadn’t moved. They’d never moved. Not once. Not when Chi spoke, not when Higu picked up the shells, not when Drew opened the door.

Only him.

“Well,” said Wenna. For the third time. Exactly the same. “You’ve come a long way for a light.”

He thought about the first time.

He couldn’t help it. He stood in the doorway of a painted house on a painted rock, with the real sea booming in the Teeth below, and thought about the real one. At the end of the real Long Water. Two months. Rain. Thirty-four of them. Gabe hitting his head on every beam. Wenna laughing so hard she had to put the kettle down. Three nights in a bunk under a small square window, with the light going round on the ceiling, three short and a long, and his whole body aching, and the edge of the world a mile away. The real kettle. The real steam, moving. The real shells, every one with its ridges and its colour, because a real old woman had picked up every one of them herself off a real beach over fifty years.

And he hadn’t looked at them. Not properly. He’d been too tired. He’d looked at the ones at the front, and the ship in the bottle, and the kettle, and her.

So that was all it had. What he’d looked at.

Record found: one of two.

It had found him. It had found what he remembered. And it had built it out of that, as well as it could, the way it had built the low floors, with a painted sky and people who said the same thing on the same corner. It could copy the first life. It could paint it. It could put a picture of it on a rock where there’d been nothing.

It couldn’t make it real.

The real one was still out there. Two months west. At the end of the Long Water. With a real old woman in it, who’d never met him, and real shells, and real steam.

This was just what he remembered.

And it was asking him.

He looked at the painted old woman in the chair with the short leg, with her painted pipe and her painted smile, who had only ever looked at him. Waiting. You’ve come a long way for a light. Waiting for him to say the thing he’d said. To answer from the other record. To be the one who’d been here.

He felt Thea behind him. Not touching. Holding still.

He took a breath.

“I’ve never been here,” said Ice.

It was true.

In this life, he’d never been here. Not once. He’d never been to the Teeth. He’d never walked up these steps or stood in this door. Nobody had. Nobody ever had. Because it hadn’t been here three days ago. It wasn’t a place. It was a picture of one.

“I’ve never been here,” he said again, gently, to the painted woman. “I’m sorry. I don’t know you.”

The old woman looked at him.

And for the first time, something in her face changed. Very slightly. Not the smile. Behind it. The way the Steward’s hand had paused over the book on the high side, once. A hesitation. As if something very far away, looking out through her painted eyes, had heard something it didn’t have a page for.

A window opened in front of his face.

He hadn’t asked for one. Nobody else’s opened. He felt it through the threads: five lights in the room behind him, and not one of them with a window. Just his.

And under it, in grey, small, the way it had been at the Gate. The words under the words. Where only he and Alea could read.

And then, under that, slowly, letter by letter, like somebody writing it with a fingertip:

Retained.

The light went out.

Above them. In the lamp-room at the top of the tower. He didn’t see it. He felt it, the way you feel a candle go out behind you in a dark room. The flicker that had been going round all day, three short and a long, stopped. And the whole house went dim, all at once, as if the sun outside had gone in.

“Out,” said Drew. Very calmly. “Everyone. Now.”

Nobody argued.

They went out through the blue door in a line, fast, Chi first with Waddles, then Xola, then Higu with the rock, then Drew, then Thea, then him, last. He looked back from the doorway. He couldn’t help it.

The old woman was still in her chair. Still smiling. Still looking at where he’d been standing.

And she was going.

Not moving. Going. From the edges in. The way the monsters on floor 1 had gone, when you killed them: not falling, not bleeding, just coming apart into light, softly, from the outside, like a sugar lump going into tea. Her shawl first. Then her hands. Then the chair. The blank shells on the back of the shelf had already gone, and the far end of the table, and the back of the ship in the bottle. Everything that had never been finished was going first. Into a soft pale glitter in the air. Into nothing.

The kettle went. The steam over it, still curling, still not moving, went last.

Her face was the last thing in the room. Still smiling. Still looking at him. You’ve come a long way for a light.

Then that went too.

He turned round and ran down the steps after Thea, and stepped over the fourth one without thinking, and didn’t care.

The tide had turned. Of course it had. It came in round the foot of the rock while they were running down the steps, white and booming, filling the landing shelf, and Marrit was standing up in the Kittiwake in the swell with an oar in her hands and her face the colour of the barnacles, holding the boat off the rock with every muscle she had.

“Get in!” she was shouting. “Get in, get in, get in—”

They got in. Xola went in last, off the shelf, into the water, under, and came up beside the outrigger and hauled herself over it like a seal, with Squiddles flat on her head and her gills spread out like dark violet wings.

And behind them, the Last Light came apart.

He watched it from the boat. They all did. Marrit had stopped shouting. She was standing in the stern with the oar in her hands, not rowing, staring, with her mouth open. The white tower on the top of the biggest tooth was going the way the old woman had gone. From the top down. The lamp-room first, the glass glittering and then dissolving, softly, into a pale cloud of light that blew away on the wind. The gallery. The rail. The white stone of the tower, storey by storey, coming apart like sand in water. The slate roof of the house. The chimney. The blue door.

The barnacles went last.

Higu was watching them. Ice saw her watch them. The thick grey-white crust round the foot of the tower, years and years of them, layers, that she’d put her hand on and felt. They went the same as the rest. Softly. Into light. And under them there was nothing. Bare black rock, wet, with the sea going over it.

Rock and wreck.

The Teeth, the way they’d always been. Black, sharp, leaning, with the swell booming between them and the bones of old ships caught in their jaws. And on the biggest one, in the middle, where a white tower had stood for three days with years of barnacles on its foot, nothing at all.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Marrit sat down in the stern. Slowly. As if her legs had gone. She sat there with the oar across her knees and the Kittiwake rising and falling on the swell among the Teeth and looked at the empty rock.

Then she looked at them.

At all of them. One at a time. At Drew, barefoot. At Higu, with her rock. At Chi, wet to the elbows, with a wet penguin. At Xola, gills still spread. At Thea, with the lantern. At him.

“Are you real?” said Marrit.

She said it very quietly. Not frightened, exactly. Something past frightened. The voice of somebody who’d looked at a horizon every night of her life and just watched something on it come apart into light, and needed to know, very badly, which side of that line she was standing on.

Nobody answered for a moment.

Then Thea, in the bow, put the lantern down on the boards and turned round on the thwart and looked at her.

“Yes,” said Thea. “We’re real.”

“How do you know?”

Thea looked at her for a long moment.

“You’ve got pitch on your elbows,” she said. “From the slip. From your grandad’s ship. You’ve had it on since yesterday, and you’ve not noticed, because you’ve been too busy.” She almost smiled. “You were real before we came. You’ll be real after we’ve gone.” She held out her hand, across the boat. “So are we.”

Marrit looked at the hand.

Then she reached out and took it. Hard. Rope-scarred, brown, smooth across the back. And held on.

“Right,” said Marrit. Her voice wasn’t steady. “Right.” She let go, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and got pitch on her cheek. “Right. Sails. You, skinny one. Rope. We’re going home. And I’m never going to the Teeth again as long as I live.”

They came into Hythe at dusk, on the top of the tide.

He’d never come in at high water, this time. Not until now. The harbour was full, and the lamps were lit in all the stilted houses round the horseshoe, and the boats were coming in through the gap in the wall with their brown sails coming down, and the whole of it was gold and blue and shining, with the bell tower black against the last of the light.

It was the prettiest place he’d seen in a year.

He’d thought that once before.

Thirty people were waiting on the quay. More. Everybody. Gabe at the front, with his arms folded. Deston. Hyvred on a bollard, with her eyes doing the thing. They’d watched it, he realised. From the porch. From the wall. Hyvred had watched the whole thing from a day’s sail away, and told them, and they’d come down to the quay to wait.

They’d seen the light go out.

And at the end of the quay, on his bollard, with his blanket round his shoulders, Corran.

He watched the Kittiwake come in. He watched Marrit bring her alongside the steps, perfectly, without looking, with pitch on her cheek. He watched her throw the rope up. Somebody caught it. Salty. And then Marrit was climbing up the steps, and the old man was getting up off his bollard, slowly, with both hands, and standing, and she walked straight into him and put her arms round him and held on.

He patted her back. Slowly. With one old hand.

“Told you,” said Corran. Into her hair. “Nobody goes to the Teeth.”

“Grandad.”

“I know, love,” said the old man. “I know. I know.”

Higu wrote it on the porch, after supper, with everybody watching.

At the bottom of the right-hand column, under A LIGHT: EAST. 1 DAY. ON THE TEETH. NEW, in its small chalk square:

PAINTED. GONE.

And then she looked at the left-hand column for a long moment. At LAST LIGHT: WEST. 2 MONTHS. And beside it, in the right-hand column, where there’d been nothing, she wrote a single question mark.

“So we go west,” she said.

“West,” said Deston.

“On the Patience,” said Higu. “In seven days. When she swims.” She looked across the porch to where Corran was sitting against the wall with his mug and his eyes shut and Marrit asleep against his shoulder, pitch and all. “If he’ll have us.”

“He’ll have you,” said Corran, without opening his eyes. “Somebody’s got to pull.”

She put the chalk away.

That was all she wrote. He’d watched her very carefully. PAINTED. GONE. Nothing about a blank shell. Nothing about an old woman who’d only looked at one person. Nothing about a room that was only finished where somebody had looked, or who.

Anything that’s yours stays yours. It doesn’t go on the rock.

He caught her eye across the porch. Higu looked back at him. Half-lidded. Calm. And then, very slightly, she nodded, once, the way Drew had nodded at the tarn, and looked away.

Drew found him later, by the ladder. He didn’t say much. Drew never did.

“It wanted you to say something,” said Drew.

“Yes.”

“In there. The old woman. It wanted you to say a thing. One thing.” Drew was looking at the harbour. “And you didn’t.”

“No.”

Drew was quiet for a while.

“Good,” he said. And put his open hand on Ice’s shoulder, briefly, and went inside.

They had the watch. Ten till twelve. It was on the rock.

The harbour was full this time. Black and still and full of stars, right up to the top step, so close that he could have put his foot in it. The lamps were going out one by one round the horseshoe. Ebba’s was still on. Somebody was still singing the one line.

There was no light on the Teeth.

He sat on the top step with the lantern between them and looked east, at the dark where it had been, and felt something he hadn’t felt for three days loosen in his chest.

“Retained,” said Thea.

He looked at her.

He’d told her on the boat. Very quietly, in the bow, on the way back, with the sails up and Marrit at the tiller and Waddles flying under the water. Just the two words. No response. Retained. She hadn’t said anything then. She’d just put her hand on his arm, in the place.

“It kept it,” said Thea. “Whatever it was. Your answer. It kept it.”

“It keeps everything.” He looked at the dark. “Records aren’t wrong. Climbers are.”

“You didn’t say it.” She was very quiet. “In there. Whatever it was waiting for. You didn’t say it.” She turned her head on his shoulder. “What was it? What did you say? The first time.”

He looked at her.

He looked at the harbour, full and black and still, and the stars in it, and the dark where the light had been, and thought: it’s listening. Right now. It heard me say “I’ve never been here”. It wrote it down. Retained. It’s listening to every word I say on this step.

And it didn’t know she knew.

“Not here,” he said.

Thea went still.

Then she nodded. Slowly. As if she’d known he’d say it.

“Okay,” she said.

“Thea—“

“Okay,” she said again. “I know. I know.” She sat up. She looked at the dark too. “That’s the thing, isn’t it? That’s what today was. It’s listening to everything. Not just the floors. Not just where.” She was working it out again. He could feel her doing it. “If you tell me things out loud, here, it hears you telling me. And then it knows I know.”

He didn’t say anything.

“So you can’t tell me,” said Thea. “Can you. Any of it. The names. Not here. Not anywhere it’s listening.”

“Not the hows,” said Ice. “Not what happened. Where, and when, and—“ He stopped. “If I tell you how somebody died, out loud, on this floor, it’s got that. It’s got it in my voice, and it’s got it in yours, when you hear it. And it builds things out of what it’s got.”

She looked at him. Very pale in the lantern light.

“It’d build that,” she said.

“It built a lighthouse,” said Ice. “Out of a kettle and some shells and three nights I was too tired to look at anything. It made a woman who said one sentence.” He was very quiet. “I’m not giving it the rest.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she reached out and took his hand, on the step between them, and held it.

“Okay,” she said. “Then don’t tell me how.”

He looked at her.

“You promised me every name,” said Thea. “On the rock. Every one. You said not tonight.” She was holding his hand very hard. “So don’t tell me how they died. Tell me who they were. The first time. That’s not the tower’s. That’s not a record. That’s just them.” Her voice wobbled, and steadied. “It can’t build anything out of who somebody was. Not really. You saw. It can only paint what you looked at. And you looked at them. For ten years. More than anything.” She swallowed. “So it’s already got that. And it can’t do anything with it. It made a woman who said one sentence.”

He didn’t say anything.

“The hows can wait,” said Thea. “For the next place it isn’t looking. There’ll be one. There was one on ten.”

There’s always a between, said Alea, very quietly, on his shoulder. Stairs. The far side of the Gate. I’ll find the next one. I’ll keep looking.

“Alea says she’ll find the next one,” he said.

“Good,” said Thea. “Then she can tell you when.” She leaned on him again. “And until then, one name a night. Who they were. That’s all.” She was quiet. “Tonight. Just one.”

He looked at the harbour.

At the full black water and the stars in it. At Ebba’s window, across the horseshoe, with the lamp still on. At the long loft behind them, with thirty-four people asleep in it under the big grey nets.

At one of them, in particular, asleep by the door, on his side, with a grey mask on the floor beside his head where he’d taken it off, and one curved purple horn catching the lamplight from the porch.

“Ina,” he said.

“He never asked me anything,” he said. “The first time.”

Thea didn’t move. She sat with her shoulder against his and her hand in his and listened.

“Not once. Ten years. Everybody else did, sooner or later. Where are we going, what’s up there, how do you know. Ina never did. He’d just sit there, and listen to whoever was talking, and then say the one thing that made it make sense.” He was quiet. “He talked like that. You know how he talks. Like he’s reading it out of a good book. Like every sentence has been somewhere before it gets to you.”

“I know,” said Thea. Very softly.

“He was in Hyvred’s party. With Maigo, and Gigi. The first time.” He looked at the dark. “And he was the one who said the words. Every time. From four up.”

Thea’s hand tightened.

“Over the graves,” said Ice. “On four. Rachael and Dennis. In the red stone. Nobody could. Calvin couldn’t stand up. Deston couldn’t open his mouth. I couldn’t—“ He stopped. “And Ina stood up at the end and took his mask off and said something. I don’t remember what. I’ve tried. Ten years and I can’t remember one word of it. I just remember that it was right. That he made a sentence do what none of us could make it do. And that afterwards Calvin stopped shaking.”

He was quiet for a long time.

“And after that, every time,” he said. “It wasn’t for a long time, after four. And then it was. And every time, he’d take the mask off and stand up and say it. Nobody ever asked him to. It was just his. Like the fire.” He swallowed. “I don’t think he ever wanted it.”

The harbour was very still.

“He was at the gate,” said Ice. “At the end. You know that. I told you. The six. Him and Gabe and Xav and Gigi and Azac and Cursiffe.” He looked at their hands. “The last time I saw him standing, he was on the stair under the gate. He pushed his mask up on his head, so he could see, and he said something to Gigi, and she laughed. Really laughed. On the stair. With the gate behind them.” He stopped. “I don’t know what he said. I never asked. I was already going. I was already through.”

He didn’t say the rest. He didn’t have to. As the doors closed, only Gabe and Xav and Azac were still standing.

Thea knew. She’d heard it on the rock.

“I don’t know what he said to her,” said Ice. “That’s the thing I’d ask him. If I could ask him anything. The first time.” He looked at the shape by the door, asleep, with the mask beside his head. “What he said to Gigi on the stair that made her laugh.”

Thea didn’t say anything for a long time.

Then she said, very quietly, “He’s waiting on the step.”

Ice looked at her.

“Winter told me,” said Thea. “At the tarn. What he said to her on the long bridge, on nine. If it’s something people are keeping for a friend, you don’t pick the lock. You wait on the step until somebody opens the door.” Her voice had gone thick. “He’s been waiting on the step since nine, Ice. He doesn’t know what’s behind the door. He just knows you’re behind it. And he’s waiting.” She wiped her eyes with the heel of her free hand. “He’s still the one who never asks.”

“Yeah,” said Ice.

“And he’s the one the rule’s named after,” said Thea. “The Ina rule. Somebody watches.” She laughed, wetly. “He’d hate that. He’d hate it. He’d make a speech about it.”

“He did,” said Ice. “On nine. He said he’d prefer a bridge.”

Thea laughed properly then, with her face in his shoulder. And then she stopped laughing, and was very quiet, and he felt her breathing change, and her hand in his, holding on.

“Ina,” she said. Into his shoulder. Just the name.

Like putting something away somewhere safe.

“Ina,” he said.

They sat on the step with the harbour full and black in front of them and the lamp still burning in Ebba’s window and one name between them, said twice, in the dark. One out of thirty-five. And thirty-four still to go. And the hows still to come, somewhere the tower wasn’t looking.

But one.

Behind them, in the long loft, by the door, Ina turned over in his sleep, and put his hand out, and found his mask on the floor, and held it. And went on sleeping.

Thea saw it too. She didn’t say anything. She just looked at him for a long time, through the open door, in the lamplight. The way you look at somebody when you’ve just found out who they were.

Then she looked away, before he could wake up and see her. Because she was good at that. Because she was the one who read windows.

She is, said Alea. Very softly. Not frightened, this time. She’s very good.
