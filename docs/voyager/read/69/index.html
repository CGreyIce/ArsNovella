Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 69

The Words

3,336 words · 15 min read

Xav told her on the lip of the pit, in the morning, with his hands on his knees.

They’d gone up there because the round room was full. Higu was asleep on her map. Xola was asleep against the wall, still, with her eyeliner in streaks. Gigi was braiding a new crown of something she’d found growing at the bottom of the pit, dark green, for no reason she’d explain. And Xav had stood up from the floor beside Thea’s cushions, where he’d been sitting since the night before, and held out his hand to help her up. She’d taken it. Her shoulder had pulled. She hadn’t said anything.

Now they were sitting on the hot stone at the top of the bucket chain, where Gabe sat every night. The sun was coming up white over the Glass. Down below, the chain clanked and slopped, and on the third ring the family that sang at dusk was singing at dawn instead, for some reason.

Xav didn’t say anything for a while. Thea didn’t push. She’d learned that with Xav on floor 4, sitting a little way along the wall from him in the evenings. He told you things when he’d decided to, and not before, and pushing just made him decide later.

“Not this time,” said Xav.

Thea waited.

“It was on the slate,” said Xav. “Drew wrote it. He said three words.” He was looking east. “Everybody read it. Calvin said what does that mean, and nobody answered.” He was quiet. “I knew what it meant before Kei said anything. Before I even knew Kei had said anything. I think I’ve known since floor 1.”

“How?”

Xav was quiet for a long time.

“You asked me something,” he said. “On four. On the terrace. Who taught him the rest?”

“I remember.”

“I’ve been thinking about it ever since.” He looked down at his hands. “I taught him to wait. Half a second at a door. It took me a year, at poly, and he hated it, and in the end he learned it. Half a second. That’s all. That’s what a year gets you.” He turned his hands over. “Rui Xiang says he sat a week behind a wall on four. Before he went into the square. A week. And three weeks on the terrace, waiting for us. And twenty-five days in a hole in the desert, trying the same thing eighteen times from every direction without giving up.” He looked at her. “You don’t learn that in a hundred days, Thea. You don’t learn that kind of waiting with a bonus, or a guide, or anything. It took me years to learn to wait half a second without it hurting. Years.”

Thea didn’t say anything.

“Whoever taught him the rest,” said Xav, “took a long time doing it.” He was very quiet. “Ten years, maybe.”

The sun came up over the Glass.

“He named himself after a probe,” said Xav. “Did you know that? A thing I told him about when we were seventeen. Something sent out ahead, on its own, further than anything. Still sending messages back. Every day. Really faint.” He looked east. “I’m still here. That’s all it says. That’s all it ever says.”

Thea looked at him.

“I told Gabe,” said Xav. “On four. Gabe knows about the probe.” He paused. “I haven’t told Gabe the rest. About the word. About what Kei said.”

“I know.”

“I’m not going to,” said Xav. “Not yet. I want you to know that before tonight.”

Thea looked at him for a long time.

“Tonight?” she said.

“Hako says the reading club’s meeting,” said Xav. “Tonight. At the top of the pit, after everybody’s asleep.” His mouth did something very small at the corner. “Hako says it’s thirteen now, if I come. He says thirteen’s a bad number.” He looked at her. “I’d like to come.”

“When you want to,” said Thea quietly.

“I want to,” said Xav.

They met at the top of the pit after midnight, under the moon.

It was the only place big enough. And the only place nobody else would be, at that hour, except Gabe. Gabe went down to sleep at midnight now, Drew had made him. Thirteen of them, sitting in a ring on the hot stone round the top of the bucket chain, with the moon huge and white over the Glass, and the dunes glowing silver all the way to the horizon.

Thea looked round at them.

Chris, with his notebook. Winter, beside him, with her silver hair and her black horns and her bow across her knees, watching everybody. Hyvred and Bee, close together. Kei with Dose. Hako, sprawled, trying very hard not to make a joke about the number. Drew. Higu, with the map rolled under her arm. Chi, very quiet, next to Higu. Xola, next to Thea, with her hand on Thea’s good arm as if Thea might float off. Gigi, on Thea’s other side, with a dark green crown of something on her head, slightly crooked. And Xav. Sitting a little way back from the ring, with his hands on his knees.

It had been six, a week ago. Two notebooks at a table by the kitchen door that smelled of fish.

Higu unrolled the map in the middle of the ring and weighed the corners down with stones. Everybody leaned in. In the moonlight you could just see it. The circle of the bowl. Eighteen lines coming in from everywhere, stopping. One dark line coming straight through.

Chris opened his notebook to HOW? and read it out. All of it. From knew XP banks at cap on floor 1, all the way down the page and onto the next, to why noon? at the bottom. It took a long time. Nobody interrupted.

Then Kei told them about the light pausing. About Thea’s window, and the black bar under Paladin, and the four seconds. That information is not available, Player. Winter, who hadn’t heard it yet, went very still.

Then Hako said the word.

Then Xav told them about the probe. And about half a second at a door, and a year, and ten years.

And then they all looked at Thea.

She’d known they would. She’d known since the cistern. She’d known since floor 2, really, since a long table in a trapper’s hall in Larchwick, and Drew asking her if she was all right, and her saying it’s like he’s going somewhere he’s already been. She’d written it in her book, in her own alphabet, so that nobody could read it over her shoulder. For a hundred and thirty-one days.

Winter was looking at her. Straight at her, the way Winter looked at everything.

“Say it,” said Winter.

Thea looked at the map.

“You promised,” said Winter. “The day it’s a fact. You said. I’m not finding this out last.” She didn’t look away. “So say it. Whatever it is. Out loud. I’m here. We’re all here.”

“It’s not a fact,” said Thea.

“Then say what it is.”

Thea was quiet for a long time. The moon was very bright.

And then she said it.

“I think he’s been here before.”

Her voice came out steadier than she’d thought it would. She was looking at the map. At the dark line.

“All of it,” she said. “The tower. The floors. The white room. I think he did all of it once already. With us. All thirty-six of us.” She swallowed. “I think it took a long time. Years. Ten years, maybe, like Xav said. I think he learned everything the hard way, the way we’re learning it. And I think it went wrong.” Her hand had gone to her shoulder. “I think on floor 4, something came off a roof. And on floor 2, there was a storm, and Mother Tallow wasn’t alive in the morning. And Hessa didn’t live long enough for Niko to find her. I think things happened. To us. Most of us.” She stopped. “Maybe all of us.”

Nobody said anything.

“And I think he got to the top anyway,” said Thea. “Somehow. On his own. And I think something gave him a choice there. And he chose to come back. To the start. To the white room, a minute before any of us opened our eyes.” She looked up, east, at the moon over the Glass. “So he could do it all again. And this time nobody would—“

She couldn’t finish it.

“Regression,” said Hako, very quietly. Not a joke. Just helping.

“Regression,” said Thea.

The word sat in the middle of the ring, on the map, in the moonlight.

Thirteen people on the lip of a pit in a desert of broken glass, looking at it.

“Is that a fact?” said Winter, at last.

She said it gently. Thea had never heard Winter say anything gently.

“No,” said Thea.

“Then what is it?”

“It’s what I think.” Thea looked at her. “It’s a theory. It’s a story out of Kei’s books and Hako’s books and my book. And it fits everything, and I can’t find a hole in it, and Chris can’t, and Higu drew a map and it fits that too.” She swallowed. “But it’s a theory about a friend, Winter. About what’s happened to him. About what he’s been carrying.” She shook her head. “You don’t put that on a slate. Not in front of thirty-five people. Not without asking him.”

Winter looked at her for a long time.

“Then the day it’s a fact,” said Winter.

“The day,” said Thea. “I promise. Again.”

Winter nodded slowly. Then she did something nobody expected. She reached across the map and put her hand, very briefly, on Thea’s knee. Then she took it back and folded her arms as if it hadn’t happened.

“Gabe,” said Xav.

Everybody turned.

He was still sitting a little way back from the ring, with his hands on his knees. He’d said his piece and gone quiet again, the way he did. Now he was looking down the pit, toward the third ring, where the round room was. Where Gabe was asleep, or not asleep, with two shields beside his bedroll.

“Not Gabe,” said Xav. “Not yet.”

There was a silence.

“Xav,” said Winter. Her voice had gone sharp again. “That’s a pocket. That’s exactly the—we said. On three. No more pocket. Deston kept a note in his pocket for three weeks and it nearly—“

“It’s not a pocket,” said Xav. He didn’t raise his voice. He never did. “A pocket’s when you hide a fact. This isn’t a fact. It’s a theory.” He looked at Winter. “Gabe’s already sitting on the lip of this pit every night, looking east. With a shield on his knees that he made for somebody who isn’t here. He sat there two and a half days while you were all in the bowl, because he thought if he went, Declan would run.” Xav was quiet. “If Gabe hears this—that Declan’s been here before, and watched us—“

He didn’t finish.

“He’d go up the stair,” said Thea quietly. “Tonight.”

“He’d go up the stair tonight,” said Xav. “Alone. To find him. And he’d be right to. Anybody would.” He looked at the map. “And then there’d be two of them a floor ahead. And Gabe doesn’t know how to lean on everything yet.”

Winter looked at him for a long time. Thea watched her face. Watched Winter, who hated being the last to know more than anything, decide something about somebody else being the last to know.

“When it’s a fact,” said Winter at last. Slowly. “Gabe hears it first. Before the slate. Before anybody.” She looked at Xav. “From you.”

“From me,” said Xav. “Before the slate.”

“Swear it.”

Xav looked at her. Then he nodded once.

“I swear it,” he said.

They agreed on the rest of it quickly after that. As if saying the word out loud had used up all the hard part.

Facts would go on the slate, same day, the way they always had. To all thirty-five. The theory would stay with the thirteen. Until they could ask him.

And Higu, sitting with her map, said what she’d said to Drew on the walk back. We stop chasing him. We go up ready. Together. And we let him see us be ready. Drew nodded. Nobody argued.

“And when we find him,” said Hako. “When we actually find him. On seven, or eight, or wherever. Who asks?”

Everybody looked at Thea.

She didn’t look back. She was looking at the dark line on the map.

“He came down the hill for her,” said Higu, simply. “With his head down. Through a thing that had stopped him eighteen times.” She looked round the ring. “She asks.”

Nobody argued with that either.

They went down to sleep a little before dawn, one by one. Hako first, yawning, saying thirteen under his breath. Then the others. Xola tried to stay. Thea sent her down. Gigi didn’t try. She just looked at Thea for a moment from under her crooked green crown, and nodded, and went.

Xav was the last. He stood at the top of the stairs down into the pit and looked back at her, sitting alone on the lip with the lantern beside her.

“Thea,” he said.

“Mm.”

“He used to talk to everyone,” said Xav. “At poly. In our first week. Every single person in the class, until they all knew each other. He was the loudest person I’d ever met.” He was quiet for a moment. “And he picked a name that only I’d understand, so that one person would know what he was doing. And then didn’t say anything to any of us for a hundred and thirty days.”

“I know.”

“Someone did that to him,” said Xav. “Made him that quiet.” He looked east. “I’d like to know who. And then I’d like to stand next to him while he tells them to—“ He stopped. He didn’t swear. Xav never swore. “Anyway.”

He went down.

She sat on the lip of the pit alone, with the lantern beside her, and watched the moon go down over the Glass.

Her shoulder ached. It ached all the time now, a dull steady ache like a bruise underneath, along the whole line of the scar. Kei said it would for weeks. On floor 6, things stayed. She didn’t mind it as much as she’d thought she would. It reminded her. Every time she moved her arm, it reminded her.

She thought about the bowl.

People had asked her, the last few days. Xola, and Gigi, and Hyvred, carefully. Did you know it was him? When he came down the hill? Did you recognise him? And she’d said yes, because it was easier. But it wasn’t quite true.

She hadn’t recognised him. Not his shape, coming down the north slope, white, with his head down. She didn’t know that shape. Not the helm, smooth and faceless. Not the barrel lid, God, not the barrel lid. Not the cloak. And not his voice, either. Through the helm, low, muffled, it could have been anybody’s voice. She’d been lying on the glass with her shoulder open, looking up at the slit, and she hadn’t known.

It was the words.

Not this time.

He’d said it standing over her, with his back to her, in the lantern light. Between her and the thing coming down. He’d said it quietly. Not to her. To it. The way you’d say something to somebody you’d met before, a long time ago, and had been waiting to meet again.

And she’d known.

Not who. What. What it meant. You don’t say not this time to a stranger. You don’t say it to a thing you’ve never seen. You say it to something that’s taken something from you already, once. You say it standing in a place you’ve stood before, where it went the other way. Where you were standing behind someone. Where someone stepped in front of you, and it went through.

She’d known it the way you know your own handwriting.

Because it was what she would do. It was what she had done, three seconds earlier, in front of Gigi, without thinking about it at all. Her body had done it before she’d decided anything. Stepped in. Put herself in the way. That was who she was. It was who she’d always been, under everything.

So if there’d been a last time.

If there’d been a floor, somewhere, years up, at the top of everything. And something coming down, the way the edges had come down at Gigi. And him in front of it, holding, the way he always held. With nobody behind him.

She knew exactly what she’d have done.

She sat on the lip of the pit and put her hand flat on the scar, and held it there, and didn’t cry. She’d thought she would. She’d been waiting to for three days. But she just sat there in the last of the moonlight, with her hand on her shoulder, and felt it. The whole of it. Very steadily. The way you feel the floor under you after you’ve been falling for a long time.

And this time he got there first.

His hand had been shaking. A few centimetres from her shoulder. With a scar across the palm to match the one she’d have by morning. He’d reached out to touch the place where it went through her, and he’d stopped. Because, she thought, he’d seen it before. He’d seen her lying on a floor with something gone through her, once. And reached out. And it hadn’t helped.

And then he’d taken his hand back and run.

She picked up the lantern.

It glowed in her hands, amber, warm, the colour of a lamp in a window on a winter night. Lights that lie look like what they are. He’d cut it out of a monster’s mouth on floor 3 and left it on a bench for whoever came next. He couldn’t have known it would be her. He couldn’t have known he’d need it back.

She held it up. Toward the east, where the moon was going down. Toward wherever on the Glass there was a stair, and a door. And somewhere above it, on floor 7, a man in a white helm with a scar on his hand, alone, one floor ahead. Still sending messages back.

“Not this time,” said Thea. Very quietly. To nobody.

Then, after a long while, to him:

“Then this time, you’ll have to let me hold the light.”

The moon went down. The sun came up white over the Glass. And she sat on the lip of the pit and held the lantern and didn’t put it down for a long time.

On the third ring below, in the round room, somebody stirred, and Hyvred opened her eyes. And looked, without meaning to, the way she looked at everyone. At the light of the woman sitting alone at the top of the pit with a lantern held up to the east.

It had gone a little more amber, at the edges, overnight.
