Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 5: Everyone Comes Back Down

Chapter 180

Fourteen Names

4,446 words · 19 min read

Write what you saw. Keep it true.

It had been carved on the first page of a stone book in the lap of a stone scribe under a bridge on floor 5, and Cursiffe had read it on the hundred and seventeenth day and never once since written anything that wasn’t.

He had two books. The ink book, since floor 1: monsters, doors, what the windows said, what the ink did when he wrote with it. And the other one. Since floor 6. A plain brown book with one word on the front in his own square hand, VOYAGER, and inside it only what he’d seen and heard himself, with the day, and where, and who else was there. No seemed. No perhaps. No I think. The plain form, Hawise would have called it, though he’d started it long before Tell.

It was very full. All the entries pointed the same way, like iron filings over a magnet. He’d known that for a long time. He’d never written down what they pointed at. That wasn’t a thing he’d seen. That was a thing he’d worked out, and it was somebody else’s to say.

On floor 14 he’d stood at the doors of the Long Barn with his hands on Hako’s shoulders and heard every number, and he’d written every one down afterwards, that night, by Ina’s fire, in the VOYAGER book. Day 458. The Barn Field. 88. 74 (my hand). 60 (it hurts, healing). 68 (my hands hurt). “It’s turned. I can’t come in. I’m telling you.” 30. 22. 27. 15. With the sources. Heard by me, at the door. Also heard by everybody.

It was the first entry in the VOYAGER book that he’d been given rather than found.

He’d looked at it for a long time.

Yan came to find him in the morning, on the steps of the house for lights, with the book open on his knees.

“I need names,” said Yan.

Cursiffe looked up.

“Fourteen,” said Yan. “The ones at the lip.” He was standing on the step below with Working On It over his shoulder and Wendy at the top of the stair behind, the way she always was. “And Aud’s. I know Aud’s. But I want it the way she’d want it said.” He tilted his head. “A light you know by name will hear you say it. I don’t know theirs. I need them by dusk.”

“You don’t need them,” said Cursiffe. “Not to walk them. Passage doesn’t need a name. You walked Neth before you knew it was a skill.”

“I don’t need them,” Yan agreed. “Known makes them turn. They’ll come quicker. It’s quicker.” He shrugged. “And one line each. Who they were.”

Cursiffe looked at him.

“Known doesn’t need that,” said Cursiffe.

“No,” said Yan. “Aud would.” He looked out at the lake. “She stood on the first stone with every one of them for a hundred and ninety-one years and she knew who they were. Every one. Oarin said. She knew their mothers. I’m not going to walk them out like parcels in front of her.” He was quiet for a moment. “She’d mind.”

Cursiffe closed the ink book.

“The river-folk don’t say the names of the dead,” said Cursiffe. “Oarin told Higu. The first time. Once they’ve gone over, you let the name go with them. It’s not said again.”

“They haven’t gone over,” said Yan.

Cursiffe thought about it.

“No,” he said. “They haven’t.”

He got up.

He did it house by house.

It was slow. Everything in Wendmere was slow. You went along the walkways between the stilt houses, over the slow green water, and you stood at a door, and you waited until somebody came, and you waited again until they spoke, because the river-folk thought for a long time before they said anything and it was rude to fill the space. And then you asked.

The first door was Old Brann’s. The fourth arch. The last boat Aud had tried to walk.

His daughter came to the door. Tall, the way they all were, a head taller than Cursiffe, grey-green, with her hair in wet ropes and her big dark eyes. She looked at him, and at the book in his hands, and at the pen. She’d seen him write before. Everybody in Wendmere had. The climber who wrote on a crab’s claw and made it open.

“His name,” said Cursiffe. “Your father’s. For tonight.”

She was quiet for a long time.

“We do not say it,” she said. “After.”

“He’s not after,” said Cursiffe. “Not yet. He’s at the lip. You can feel him.” He held out the book, open, at a clean page. “You don’t have to say it. You can write it. Or tell me, and I’ll write it. And then the one who walks him will know it. And he’ll turn when he hears it.” He waited. “So he doesn’t have to wait any more.”

She looked at the page.

Then she took the pen out of his hand. Slowly. In her long webbed fingers. River-folk didn’t write much; they wove, and knotted, and hummed. She held it wrong. She bent over the book in the doorway of her house over the water and wrote, in a long slow wavering hand, one word.

Brann.

And under it, after a long time:

He sang to the eels.

She gave him back the pen. She didn’t say anything. She went back inside and shut the door. And Cursiffe stood on the walkway and looked at the page and the ink on it, eel-oil and lamp-black, which shed water, so the spray couldn’t run it.

1. Brann. Fourth arch. He sang to the eels. (Written by his daughter.)

He went to the next door.

It took all morning and most of the afternoon.

Fourteen houses. Some of them took a long time. At one he waited at the door for the length of a whole meal before anybody came. At one, a very old man came, and looked at him, and shut the door again without a word, and Cursiffe waited, and after a while the door opened again and a young woman came out and wrote a name and went back in, and he heard the old man crying behind the door.

At one, the woman said it out loud.

He hadn’t asked her to. He’d held out the book. And she’d looked at it, and at him, and then out past him at the lake, toward the Edge, and said it. Out loud. On the walkway, in the morning. Orla. Her sister’s name. And all along the walkway, at the doors of the other stilt houses, river-folk had turned their heads.

Orla, said the woman again. Louder. Orla. She mended the boats. She was the best at the bows. She could make a boat that would go over the Edge without tipping. She said so. She was very proud. And then she put both her webbed hands over her mouth and didn’t say anything else.

Cursiffe wrote it.

7. Orla. Third arch. She mended the boats. The best at the bows. She said so. She was very proud. (Said aloud by her sister, on the walkway.)

Tiger found him at noon, at the end of a walkway near the lake net. Tiger was wet to the waist. He’d been in the water.

“The knot held,” said Tiger.

Cursiffe looked at him.

“Neth’s,” said Tiger. “The last one. Mine. The one I tied when she said there, now it is mended.” He looked at his hands. “I went and found it. In the net. All summer it’s been in the water. All the floods. All the stuff that comes down the falls. It held.” He sat down on the end of the walkway with his feet in the lake. “There’s a girl mending now. Neth’s niece’s girl. She ties it slow. She said Neth told her before she went. Tie it the quick one’s way.” He laughed, a short wet laugh. “The quick one’s way. Like that’s the slow way.”

Cursiffe wrote it in the VOYAGER book. Not because it was about Ice. Because it was true, and he’d heard it.

Day 512. Wendmere. Tiger: the knot held. The new net-mender ties it “the quick one’s way”. (Heard by me.)

“Who’s next?” said Tiger.

Cursiffe looked at the list.

“Mott,” said Cursiffe.

Tiger went quiet.

Mott the eel-man.

Cursiffe remembered him. Everybody in Yan’s party did. The old river-man who’d come to the house for lights on the first evening and stood in the door for a long time, looking at the fire Ina had lit, before he said anything at all. Who’d asked them to clear the knappers off the third fall, because the eel traps were his, and he’d watched the last lights fight them three hundred and twelve years ago. Who’d given Cursiffe a small clay pot of river-folk ink, lamp-black and eel-oil, the night Cursiffe asked, because the spray ran Writ’s letters.

The ink in Cursiffe’s pen was Mott’s. It was the same pot. He’d refilled it in Hythe and Tell and Seep with whatever black he could find and it had never been as good, and he’d kept a little of the first in the bottom, because it was Mott’s, and he didn’t know why.

Mott’s house was at the far end of the city, on the last stilts before the jetty. Low. Old. With eel traps stacked on the porch, woven, and nobody mending them.

Nobody came to the door.

Cursiffe waited. The length of a meal. Longer. Tiger waited with him, on the walkway, very still, which for Tiger was a thing he’d learned. Nobody came.

And then a voice said, from below them, from the water, “He’s not in.”

Cursiffe looked down.

There was a child in the lake.

A river-folk child. In the water among the stilts, the way the river-folk slept, with just her head out, and her long dark-green hair spread round her on the surface like weed, and her big dark wet eyes looking up at them. Small, for river-folk, which meant about as tall as Cursiffe. Young, which meant Cursiffe had no idea how old.

“He’s at the lip,” said the child. “With the others. Since the summer.” She said it the way you’d say somebody had gone to market. “Everyone in the house is at the boats. They won’t come back till dark. They don’t like being in the house. It’s too close to the jetty.” She looked at Tiger. “You’re the quick one. Neth’s quick one. Gran says you tied her a knot.”

“I did,” said Tiger.

“I’m Lune,” said the child. “He’s my great-gran’s da. Mott. That’s his name. You can write it.” She looked at Cursiffe’s book. “You write names. Everyone says. You go to the doors and they write them.” She swam closer, to the foot of the walkway, and put her chin on the edge of it. “Nobody else in my house will. They won’t say it. They say it’s bad luck now, because he didn’t go.” She looked up at him. “I’ll say it.”

“Why?” said Cursiffe.

Lune thought about it. The way the river-folk thought about things. A long time.

“Because he’s warm,” said Lune. “At the lip. I go and sit on the end of the jetty every dusk and put my feet in, and I can feel him. All of them. It’s like when you put your feet in the sun-pool in summer.” She frowned. “Everyone else says it’s like hands. They say it’s frightening. It’s not frightening. It’s just warm.” She shrugged, in the water. “He used to let me hold the traps. He sang to them. Not like Brann. Brann sang loud. Mott sang under his breath so the eels wouldn’t hear it was singing.”

Cursiffe held out the book.

She looked at it. Then she reached up out of the water with one long wet webbed hand and took the pen and wrote on the page, upside down, which nobody in Wendmere ever did, with her chin on the walkway and water running off her arm onto the paper. It didn’t run. Mott’s ink.

Mott.

And under it:

He sang so the eels wouldnt know.

She gave him the pen back.

“There,” said Lune. And then, looking at the book, at the ink: “That’s his. Isn’t it. The black. He made that. I can smell it.”

“Yes,” said Cursiffe. His voice came out strange. “He gave it me. On the first night.”

Lune nodded, satisfied, as if that was how it should be. She let go of the walkway and slid back into the water up to her eyes. And then she said, from under the surface, with just her eyes showing:

“Is it the boy with the stick? Who’s walking them?”

“Scythe,” said Tiger. Automatically.

“Is it him?”

“Yes,” said Cursiffe.

Lune’s eyes looked at him over the water for a long moment.

“Good,” she said. “He’s the only one who’s not frightened. I watched him. On the jetty. Yesterday.” She sank a little further. “Neither am I.”

And she went under, and was gone, a long dark shape among the stilts.

Cursiffe stood on the walkway and looked at the water where she’d been.

Then he wrote, in the VOYAGER book, because it was true, and he’d seen it, and it wasn’t about Ice at all:

Day 512. Mott’s house. A child called Lune, in the water. She sits on the end of the jetty every dusk with her feet in. She says the lights at the lip are warm, not frightening. “He’s the only one who’s not frightened. Neither am I.” (Heard by me and Tiger.)

He underlined neither am I.

He didn’t know why. He found out later.

There was one more name.

Not one of the fourteen. Yan knew Aud’s name. But he’d said the way she’d want it said, and Cursiffe didn’t know that, and neither did Oarin, who’d said Aud and then stopped, as if there was more and it wasn’t his.

There was only one person in Wendmere old enough to know.

The stair up the cliff beside the middle fall was narrow and wet and went up a long way. At the top, a ledge. And along the ledge, behind the falling water, so close you could have put your hand out into it, a cave. Dry. Lamp-lit. With a chair cut out of the rock at the back.

Sefa was in it.

Cursiffe had never been. Ice had, the first time, with some of the others, on the second day. He’d come back and not said much. Cursiffe had written down what the others said. 406. White hair to the floor. A chair cut from the rock. “And yours is a fox.” Thirty, three hundred and twelve years ago, lights at their shoulders. “One of them laughed. Like you.” “Come back when you have a better question. I will still be here.”

She was still there.

Silver-grey. Deeply lined. White hair to the floor, all round her chair like water. Her big dark eyes in the lamplight, looking at him come in.

“The one with the ink,” said Sefa.

“Yes.”

“You write on things and they open.” Her voice was slow and very dry, like something kept a long time in a cupboard. “Mott told me. Before he went.” She looked at the book in his hand. “You came down.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody comes down,” said Sefa. “I told your fox-man that. Everyone who goes up, goes up. Nobody comes down to tell.” She looked at him for a long time. “You came down.”

“We’re going to the bottom,” said Cursiffe. “There’s somebody there.”

Sefa didn’t ask who. She sat in her stone chair with her white hair round her and looked at him, and Cursiffe had the strange feeling that she already knew, or that it didn’t matter, or both.

“What do you want?” said Sefa.

“Aud’s name,” said Cursiffe. “The way she’d want it said. Tonight.”

Sefa was quiet for a very long time. Long enough that Cursiffe thought she’d gone to sleep. The fall roared outside the cave, a hand’s width from the ledge. The lamp flickered.

“Aud,” said Sefa.

Cursiffe waited.

“Just Aud,” said Sefa. “She was born Aud of the second arch, Hessel’s girl. Not his blood. He took her from the water when she was very small and her mother went over. She was his, after. When she took the pole, they wanted to call her Aud Edge-keeper. Aud of the Lip. She hated it. She said a name with a job in it is a job, not a name.” Her mouth moved. It might have been a smile. “She said: when I go, say Aud. Nothing else. If anybody puts the pole in it I will come back up the Edge and hit them with it.”

Cursiffe wrote it.

15. Aud. Just Aud. (Said by Elder Sefa, who knew her from a child.) “If anybody puts the pole in it I will come back up the Edge and hit them with it.”

“I watched her walk the stones the first time,” said Sefa. “She was younger than you. Much younger. Hessel let her walk old Coll’s boat out, because Coll asked for her. And she got to the lip, and she was frightened, and she cut too soon.” Sefa looked at her hands. “Coll didn’t go. He stayed at the lip for a month. And Hessel took the pole off her and made her sit on the end of the jetty every dusk with her feet in the water and feel him there. Every dusk. For a whole year. Until she wasn’t frightened of the warm any more.” She was quiet. “And then he gave her the pole back. And she walked Coll out. And she never once cut too soon again in a hundred and ninety-one years.”

Cursiffe stopped writing.

He looked at the page. At 15. Aud. And at the page before it. At Mott, in a child’s upside-down hand, and under it, in his own: She sits on the end of the jetty every dusk with her feet in. And neither am I, underlined.

He didn’t say anything.

Sefa watched him not say anything.

“Have you a better question?” said Sefa.

Cursiffe looked up.

“Your fox-man,” said Sefa. “I told him: come back when you have a better question. He hasn’t. He went past with his thirty-five and my lamp went out and came on and he didn’t climb the stair.” She looked at Cursiffe. “You’re the one who writes things down. Have you got it? The better question?”

Cursiffe thought about it.

He thought about the second page of Chris’s notebook. THE ONES BEFORE. Thirty, three hundred and twelve years ago. One who laughed. Thirty scratches in a well-house in Tell, and a skipping rhyme that counted to ninety-nine and then nothing, nothing, out. A hand in the ink at the bottom of a page in a buried city: Thirty. Nobody left down. Ruth. He’d read that hand. He’d heard it laugh, once, in an echo. He’d written it all down, in the ink book, with sources, every scrap.

He didn’t have the question. He had a lot of answers to questions he didn’t know how to ask yet.

“No,” said Cursiffe. “Not yet.”

Sefa nodded slowly. As if that were the right answer.

“You came down,” said Sefa. “They never did. Not one. In three hundred and twelve years.” She looked at the falling water. “That’s not a question. But it’s where one starts.” She closed her eyes. “Go and give him his names. The young one. With the scythe. He’s waiting on the jetty, and he’s not as patient as he looks.”

Cursiffe went.

At the top of the stair he looked back. She was sitting in her stone chair with her eyes shut and her white hair round her, in the lamplight, behind the water.

He wrote one more line, on the ledge, in the spray, in Mott’s ink, which didn’t run:

Day 512. Sefa. “You came down. They never did. That’s not a question. But it’s where one starts.” (Heard by me, alone.)

Yan learned them in an hour.

He sat on the end of the last jetty in the late afternoon with Cursiffe’s book open on his knees and read the fifteen names out loud, one at a time, and the lines under them, and then shut the book and said them again without looking. All of them. In order. With the lines.

Brann. He sang to the eels.

Wira. Third arch. She had the best garden on the stilts. Beans.

Ode. Weaver. Wove her own boat, the year before. Left it on the roof so nobody else would have to.

Garrow.

Lisk.

Sile, who was not old. A fever. Her mother wrote it and then crossed it out and then wrote it again.

Orla. She was very proud.

And all the way down. Tarrin. Nolle. Veyle. Ammet. Rhun. Dessa, who was the oldest after Sefa and remembered the year the lake froze. Mott, in a child’s upside-down hand. And at the end, last, on a page by itself:

Aud.

“Just Aud,” said Yan.

“Just Aud,” said Cursiffe. “Or she’ll come back up the Edge and hit you with it.”

Yan laughed. Out loud. The way he laughed at things nobody else found funny. Wendy, behind him, made a sound that might have been a laugh too.

“That sounds right,” said Yan. He gave Cursiffe the book back. “That sounds exactly right.”

He looked out at the causeway. The flat stones under ankle-deep fast water. The first one, a pace off the end of the jetty. Cursiffe couldn’t see anything on it. Just water, going over stone.

“She’s still there,” said Yan. Quietly. “On the first stone. With her hands in her lap.” He tilted his head. “She’s turned a bit. Since this morning. Toward the jetty.” He was quiet for a moment. “I think she heard me say it.”

Oarin came at the end of the afternoon to ask about the lamp.

The one on the post at the end of the last jetty, where the causeway started. The one Aud had lit every dusk for a hundred and ninety-one years, for whoever was walking. It hadn’t been lit since the spring.

“Someone should light it,” said Oarin. “For tonight. For him.” He looked at the post. “We do not know who. It was always hers.”

Nobody said anything.

Then Thea stood up.

She’d been sitting on the jetty with Ice, a little way back, with the lantern. She’d been very quiet all day. Dennis said four, on his graph, maybe three and a half. Cursiffe didn’t hold with graphs. He held with what he’d seen. And what he’d seen, on the Salt and at sea and at the Gate, was Thea’s hand in Ice’s, and Thea asleep on his shoulder, and Thea’s face, sometimes, when she didn’t know anybody was looking, not angry at all.

She stood up with the lantern and looked at the post.

“I will,” said Thea.

Oarin looked at her. At the lantern. At the light in it, amber, steady.

“That is his light,” said Oarin. Slowly. Looking past Thea at Ice. “In the glass. I can feel it. It is the same as the one at your shoulder.”

“Yes,” said Thea.

“For a man who walks the dead,” said Oarin, “you would light a lamp with the light of a man who does not die?”

Cursiffe stopped writing.

Nobody said anything at all.

Thea didn’t look at Ice. She didn’t look at anybody. She looked at Oarin, the old river-man with his big wet eyes, who had said a thing he couldn’t possibly know, the way the river-folk sometimes did, as if he’d read it off the water.

“It’s a light,” said Thea. Very level. “It’s the one I’ve got. It helps people find their way back.”

Oarin looked at her for a long time.

“Yes,” he said at last. “That is what the lamp is for.”

He bowed his head. Very slightly. And went back along the jetty to the city.

Cursiffe looked down at his book.

He didn’t write it. He’d heard it. It was true, in the sense that Oarin had said it. A man who does not die. But it wasn’t a thing he’d seen. And it was pointing, like all the other filings, the same way. And it was somebody else’s to say.

He closed the book.

Ice, on the jetty, was looking at the water. His face was very still. Thea sat back down beside him, and put the lantern in her lap, and took his hand. And didn’t say anything. And neither did he.

Out at the lip, Yan said, the lights were turning.
