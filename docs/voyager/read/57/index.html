Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 2: One Floor Ahead

Chapter 57

The Reading Blade

3,822 words · 17 min read

The gulls found it. Not Niko.

That was the thing he kept coming back to, afterwards. He’d been carrying spellbooks for a hundred and eleven days. Through four floors, in his pack, under his arm, open beside his plate at dinner. He’d read every one of them three times. He’d pushed a spell through his sword on the morning of the seventeenth day and bled all over Maigo’s hands for it. And he’d never once thought to go looking. He’d assumed, the way you assume things, that if there was anything for him out there, it would come to him.

It came to him on the hundred-and-eleventh morning, by way of forty gulls.

“They keep going south,” said Aleaka.

She was standing in front of Drew’s slate by the inn door, in the early light, with Sleet pressed against her legs and the gulls round her feet. There were always gulls round her feet now. They’d followed her since the third day on floor 5, more every day. They sat on the inn’s roof ridge in a long white row whenever she was inside, and screamed whenever she came out.

“Every morning,” she said, slowly, the way she said everything. “About twenty of them. They go south over the bridges, toward that island with all the nests. And they come back an hour later. And they look at me.” She frowned at the slate. “Like they want me to come.”

She took the chalk and wrote, under Drew’s square letters, HIDDEN QUESTS. IF IT’S STRANGE, WRITE IT HERE, in her own careful round hand:

gulls keep going to the south island and coming back. i think somebody’s there. — leaka

Niko read it over her shoulder. He didn’t know why it made the back of his neck prickle. It was just gulls.

“Nobody goes alone,” said Drew, from behind them. He’d come down for breakfast and was reading it too, with a bowl of porridge in one hand. “Take a party.”

“I’ll go,” said Niko.

He hadn’t meant to say it. It just came out. Drew looked at him, and then at Chris, who was sitting at the long table with his notebook, and Chris looked up.

“We’ll all go,” said Chris. “It’s a walk. Maigo needs the sun.”

“I do need the sun,” said Maigo, from the end of the table, without looking up from his tea. It was the most he’d said before breakfast in a week.

Gull Island was the strangest place Niko had seen on floor 5, and floor 5 had people flying.

It was south of Tether, two bridges out, a crooked tooth of rock sticking up out of the cloud. It was white. Every surface of it was white with gulls: on every ledge, every crack, every flat bit of stone, thousands and thousands of them, sitting on nests, screaming. The smell hit you on the second bridge. Winter put her sleeve over her nose and said something about it that would have made Newish proud. Ina, who had pushed his grey oni mask up on top of his head for the walk, put it down over his face. He said it was for the smell. Niko didn’t think it was only for the smell.

And on the very top of the island, above all the nests, there was a tower.

Not a big one. A squat round stone tower, two storeys, with a slate roof and a single door. Smoke came out of the chimney. Round the door, in a ring, the gulls didn’t nest. There was a clear patch of bare rock there, swept clean, as if somebody swept it every day.

Aleaka’s twenty gulls flew straight up to the tower, landed on the roof in a row, and looked down at them.

“Well,” said Chris. “That’s not ominous at all.”

They went up a path cut into the rock between the nests, single file, with the gulls screaming at them from every side. At the top, in the clear ring in front of the tower door, they stopped. Nobody wanted to knock.

The door opened before anyone had to.

An old woman stood in it. Very old. Older than Nan Gully, maybe, but you’d never have known it from the way she stood. She was tall and straight as a post, with a long white braid down her back and a face like a cliff, all lines and edges. One of her eyes was milky. The other was very sharp and very grey. She wore a long dark coat, patched at the elbows. On her back, over the coat, she wore a sword.

She looked at all six of them. Slowly. One at a time. The sharp grey eye went from Chris to Maigo to Winter to Ina to Aleaka, and didn’t stop on any of them.

It stopped on Niko.

It stayed there. It went down him, from his face to his hands, to the sword at his hip, to the pack on his back with the corner of a spellbook sticking out of the top. It came back up to his face.

“Huh,” said the old woman. “He was right.”

Her name was Hessa. She told them that, and nothing else, and let them in.

The tower was one round room downstairs, with a fire, a table, two chairs and a narrow bed. On a single shelf by the fire there were three books. Old ones, leather, cracked. On the wall above the shelf hung a second sword, longer than the one on her back, with something worked down the length of the blade that Niko couldn’t make out from the door. Lines. Writing, maybe.

There weren’t enough chairs. Winter and Ina sat on the floor. Maigo stood by the window in the sun with his eyes half shut, like a plant. Aleaka stayed in the door, because the gulls wouldn’t let her come any further in without screaming. Chris got the second chair, because he was oldest. Niko stood.

Hessa didn’t sit. She stood by the fire and looked at Niko.

“He was right,” she said again. “Short. Skinny. Calm. Reads at dinner.” Her sharp eye went to the corner of the book in his pack. “And a knight. With a sword at his hip and a spellbook on his back, and no idea yet what he is.”

“Who,” said Chris carefully, “was right?”

Hessa looked at him as if he’d asked her what colour the sky was.

“Your knight,” she said. “The one with the light by his ear. Came through about three weeks back. Barrel lid on his arm.” She sniffed. “Walked up that path through my gulls as if he’d done it before. Knocked. Asked me to wait.”

The room was very quiet. Even the gulls outside seemed quieter.

“Wait for what?” said Niko.

“For you,” said Hessa.

She told it standing by the fire, with her arms folded, the way people tell things they’ve already decided are none of their business.

He’d come up the path in the afternoon. Alone, in a white cloak, with a plain tarred shield. He’d stood in the clear ring in front of the door and waited till she opened it. And then he’d stood there a long moment, looking at her, as if he were looking at somebody he’d known a long time ago and was trying to place.

“Like he was surprised I was alive,” said Hessa. “Rude, I thought. I’m only eighty-four.”

He’d asked if she still taught. She’d said she’d never taught anybody, and wasn’t going to start now, and who’d told him she taught? He hadn’t answered that. He’d said there was somebody coming. A boy. In a month, maybe less. A knight who read spellbooks.

“Please wait for him,” said Hessa. Her voice went flat and odd, as if she were quoting. “That’s what he said. Please. I said I’m eighty-four, I don’t wait for anybody. And he said—“ She stopped. She looked at the fire. “He said, then please stay alive till he comes.”

Nobody said anything.

Niko felt it go through the room. He didn’t look round. He felt Chris go very still in his chair, and heard the tiny scratch of a pencil, once, and stop.

“That’s a strange thing to say to somebody,” said Winter. Bluntly. From the floor.

“I thought so,” said Hessa. “Cheek, I thought. Because I’m old.” She sniffed. “But he didn’t say it like cheek. He looked at me the whole time like a man who’d been to my funeral. Like he couldn’t quite believe I was standing in my own door.” She looked at Niko again. “Anyway. I said I’d think about it. He went away. And I thought about it.” She unfolded her arms. “And here I still am. Alive. Out of spite, mostly.”

Niko found that his hands were shaking. He put them behind his back.

“What else did he say?” he said. “About me.”

Hessa looked at him for a long moment with her one sharp eye.

“He said you’d already have tried it,” she said. “Pushed a spell into your blade before you knew how. Too early. He said you always do things too early, and then you get them right later, by yourself, patiently, when nobody’s looking.” She tilted her head. “He said you’d have bled for it. From the nose. Have you?”

Niko didn’t answer.

He didn’t have to. Behind him, from the window, Maigo said very quietly, “Day seventeen. Floor 2. In the morning.” Maigo had been the one who’d fixed it. He’d held Niko’s head still with his bark-rough hands and stopped the blood. “Nobody was there but us.”

“Huh,” said Hessa. “He was right about that too, then.”

And then she said something else, and Niko stopped breathing.

“He called you something,” she said. “Before your name. When he was describing you. Said it, and stopped, and said your name instead, like he’d caught himself. Like he’d forgotten which one he was allowed to use.” She frowned, trying to remember the shape of it. “Mashi. Something like that. Mashi. Then Niko.”

Niko stood in the middle of the round room very still.

Nobody called him that. Nobody in the world. Not Chris, not Maigo, not anybody in the group. Nobody had ever called him that, except one person, from the time Niko was seventeen and fresh on a game server where an older guy with a stupid sense of humour had decided, for no reason he ever explained, that Niko was Mashi. And then called him that for eight years, in every call, every match, every message. Mashi. Mashi, get in here. Mashi, you absolute legend. He’d never asked why. He’d never minded. It had just been his, from one person, the way a thing is.

He hadn’t heard it in a hundred and eleven days.

He’d thought he might never hear it again.

“Niko,” said Chris quietly, behind him.

“I’m fine.” He wasn’t. He took a breath. He looked at Hessa. “That’s—yeah. That’s me. That’s what he calls me.”

Hessa looked at him for a long time. Something in the cliff of her face shifted a little.

“Well,” she said. “He was very careful not to say it again.”

She tested him in the clear ring outside the door, with the gulls watching from every ledge.

“Your sword,” said Hessa. “And your book. One in each hand.”

Niko drew his sword. It was a plain knight’s sword, the one he’d bought in Hearthstead on the first day. He’d carried it up five floors, and it was nicked all along the edge. He took his best book out of his pack, the one he’d read most: a small, thick, plain-covered book of fire spells. He’d bought it from a stall in Larchwick on floor 2 because the fire spells in it had pictures. He held the sword in his right hand and the book open in his left.

“Now,” said Hessa. “Read me the first spell in it. Aloud.”

“While—“

“While I hit you.”

She reached up over her shoulder and drew her sword. Not a practice sword. The real one, the one on her back. It was old and thin and very bright, and she held it as if it weighed nothing.

Niko looked at it. Then at her.

“You’re eighty-four,” he said.

“And you’re slow,” said Hessa. “Read.”

He read.

He got four words out before she hit him. She came in from the side, faster than anything that old should be able to move, and he got his sword up by instinct and caught it, and the shock went up his arm. He lost his place. She stepped back.

“Again. From the start.”

He read. She hit him. He lost his place.

Again. And again. Ten times. Twenty. She didn’t hit him to hurt him. She hit him to make him stop reading, and every time he stopped, she stepped back and said again. And by the twentieth time he understood what it was, and by the thirtieth he was angry about it. You couldn’t do both. You couldn’t hold a sword up against somebody who was trying to hit you and read words off a page at the same time. Your head only went one way at once.

“You’re doing two things,” said Hessa, on the fortieth.

“I’m trying to do two things.”

“That’s what’s wrong with it.” She stepped back and lowered her sword. Her one sharp eye was steady. “Show me what you did. The thing that made your nose bleed.”

He hesitated. Behind him, Chris and the others had sat down on the bare rock at the edge of the clear ring to watch. Winter, with her silver hair blowing everywhere. Ina, with his mask pushed back up. Maigo, in the sun.

He did it.

He held the sword out, point up, and the book open, and read the first spell. Just the first line, the one with the little picture of a flame. He felt it come up out of the words, the way spells did, a warm push behind his eyes. And instead of letting it go out of his hand the way a mage would, he pushed. Into the sword. Down his arm, into the hilt, into the steel.

It went. A thin orange line ran up the blade from the hilt toward the point, flickering, like a hairline crack with fire in it. It got halfway. It stopped. It fought him. He felt the steel push back against it like a door somebody was leaning on. He pushed harder.

His nose started to bleed.

“Stop,” said Hessa.

He stopped. The orange line went out. He stood there with blood running down his lip and the sword shaking in his hand.

“You push it,” said Hessa, “like it’s a stranger.”

He looked at her.

“The blade,” she said. “You push the spell into it like you’re shoving something through a stranger’s door. And the blade doesn’t know you, so it pushes back.” She walked across the ring toward him, slowly, and took the sword out of his hand. She held it up and looked down the length of it at him with her one good eye. “How long have you had this?”

“Since the first day.”

“A hundred and eleven days. It’s been on your hip every day. You’ve cleaned it. You’ve sharpened it. You’ve killed with it. And you’ve never once read to it.” She held it out to him, hilt first. “It’s not a stranger. You’ve been carrying it longer than you’ve been carrying anybody’s book.”

Niko took the sword.

“Don’t push,” said Hessa. “Read. To it. Not at it. Not through it. To it. The way you’d read to someone sitting next to you.”

He stood in the clear ring on Gull Island with forty gulls and five friends watching. He held the sword up in front of his face, close, so he could see his own eye in the steel. And he held the book open in his other hand. And he didn’t look at the book.

He knew the spell. He’d read it a hundred times. He looked at the sword, at the nicks along the edge from the Woolbacks and the Rimehounds and the dust-hounds, and the dull place near the hilt where his thumb always went. And he said it. Quietly. To the sword. The way you’d say something to a friend sitting next to you, who’d been there the whole time.

The orange line went up the blade.

It didn’t flicker. It didn’t fight. It went up from the hilt to the point in one long slow breath, like a fuse burning, and stayed. The whole length of the steel glowed. Not on fire. Lit, from inside, the way Gabe said the Kiln iron had glowed. A warm orange line down the middle of the blade, steady, humming very faintly against his palm.

His nose didn’t bleed.

He stood there and looked at it. He couldn’t look at anything else.

Behind him, on the rock, nobody said anything. Then Winter said, quite loudly, “Holy shit, Niko,” and Maigo laughed. Maigo never laughed. Chris’s pencil was going so fast Niko could hear it.

Hessa sheathed her own sword over her shoulder without looking.

“Huh,” she said. “Took you less time than it took me.”

She gave him a book before they left.

Not one of the three on the shelf. A fourth, from somewhere under the bed. A thin one, bound in faded red leather, with no title on the spine. She put it in his hands at the door.

“Two more,” she said. “Not on this floor. Not on the next one, I shouldn’t think. The book’ll tell you where, when you’re near enough.” She looked at him. “It told me, when I was your age. I went up a long way, once. Further than anybody from these islands ever went.” She was quiet a moment. “Then I came back down and sat on a rock with my gulls. Somebody has to be here, when the next one comes up.”

Niko held the red book in both hands.

“Were there others?” he said. “Before me? Other knights who read?”

“A few.” Hessa looked out at the gulls. “Long time ago. Came up the stair with lights, some of them.” She glanced at the place by his ear, where there was nothing, where his own light sometimes sat, a soft white ball. “Like your knight. Like you.”

“What happened to them?”

“Went up,” said Hessa. “The way you all do.”

She started to close the door. Then she stopped, with it half shut, and looked out at him with her one sharp grey eye through the gap.

“Your knight,” she said. “When he described you. He was smiling. The whole time. Like a man describing his little brother.” Her mouth did something that might have been the start of a smile on a face less like a cliff. “Then he got to the part about the nosebleed and stopped smiling, and looked at my gulls for a long while. And then he said please again. Like it mattered more than anything he’d asked anybody for in his life.” She looked at Niko a moment longer. “I’d find out why, if I were you.”

She shut the door.

They walked back across the two bridges in the afternoon with the gulls screaming behind them and the sun warm on their backs. Nobody said much. Niko walked at the back with the red book under his arm and his sword at his hip. Every few steps he put his thumb on the dull place near the hilt, the way he always had, without thinking about it. Except now he thought about it.

At the inn, he went to Drew’s slate before he did anything else. He took the chalk and wrote, under Aleaka’s line, in small neat letters:

Gull Island. Hessa. Spellsword (hidden). 1 of 3. — Niko

Then he stood looking at it.

Chris came and stood beside him. He didn’t say anything for a while. He had his notebook under his arm, closed, with his finger in it to keep a page.

“Please stay alive till he comes,” said Chris. Very quietly.

“I know.”

“He said that to an old woman on a rock. Like he’d been to her funeral.” Chris was looking at the slate, not at him. “And he knew about your nose. Day seventeen. Floor 2. There were six people there and one of them was you.” He paused. “And he called you Mashi.”

“He always called me Mashi,” said Niko. “Since I was seventeen. Nobody else ever has.” He swallowed. “Chris, it’s him. It’s obviously him. We all knew that.”

“I know it’s him,” said Chris. “That’s not the bit I’m stuck on.”

Niko looked at him.

Chris opened the notebook to the page he’d been keeping. Niko saw the top of it before Chris turned it away again. It didn’t say HIDDEN. It said something else, one word, in Chris’s small square capitals, HOW?, with a list underneath it that went most of the way down the page.

Chris closed it.

“I need to talk to Thea,” he said. And he went.

Niko stood in front of the slate by himself.

He took the red book out from under his arm and looked at it. Then he drew his sword, just a little, a hand’s width out of the scabbard, in the doorway of the inn in Tether with the evening coming on. He looked down at the steel, at the nicks and the dull place where his thumb went. Very quietly, so nobody would hear, he read it the first line of the spell with the little picture of a flame.

The orange line came up the blade, a hand’s width, steady, warm.

Mashi, get in here. Mashi, you absolute legend.

“I’m here,” he said to the sword. Very quietly. “I’m getting there. Wait for me.”

He slid it home.
