Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 107

Overlap

4,471 words · 19 min read

“And so our hero,” said Azac, to the snow, “went up to the top of the coldest wall in the world for the seventh night running, and stood in it, and was cold.”

Nobody answered. He hadn’t expected them to. It was a device.

He was on the fifth wall, in the line, an hour after dusk on the two hundred and fiftieth day, with his shield up and his breath going out in front of him in white clouds and freezing in his beard. The snow was coming sideways down the pass from the high side, the way it always did up here. On his left was Chris, with his longsword and his notebook buttoned inside his coat. On his right was Gabe, with the new shield. The one that didn’t go cold. You could feel it from a metre off, like standing near a stove. Azac had been standing as close to it as he decently could for two nights.

And on Gabe’s right, Declan.

Azac looked along the line at him. Shield up. Feet set. The lamp shield on his arm, and Gabe’s shield-edge across his right side, the warm one, covering it. Not saying anything. Watching the dark up the pass the way he’d watched everything on this floor: as if he’d seen it before and was making sure it hadn’t changed.

Azac had been the tank for a long time.

Not here. Before here. On Earth, in a hundred games, every one of them with a group of friends in it and a thing in front of them that needed standing in front of. It had been Declan’s job, for years. Declan had always been the tank. He’d stood in front of everything, in every game they’d ever played together, from the first night on a call with half of them Azac had never met, and he’d been good at it. Stupidly good. And Azac had stood behind him and done damage and talked, and been happy.

And then on floor 1, in a white room with thirty-five people in it and an empty circle where the thirty-sixth should have been, somebody had needed to stand in front. And Azac had looked round, and seen that nobody else was going to, and picked up a shield.

He’d been doing it ever since. Ice’s old spot. He’d never said that out loud. He’d thought it, every time he put the shield up, for two hundred and fifty days.

And now Declan was back. In a wall. Three people to his right. With Gabe’s shield covering him.

“And our hero,” said Azac, quieter, “didn’t mind at all.”

Chris, on his left, without looking round: “Are you narrating again?”

“Always.”

“Is it good?”

“It’s getting there,” said Azac.

The hold-folk had taken the tanks.

That was how it had worked out. On the second day, Brenna had looked at the climbers in the barracks and said who stands in front, and eight of them had stood up without thinking, the way you do when you’ve been doing a thing for long enough. Azac, and Gabe, and Salty, and Jared, and Hako, and Chris, and Chi, and Drew. And Declan, who’d stood up and then sat down again when Gabe looked at him, and then stood up again when Gabe looked away.

And Brenna had taken them up to the training yard on the fourth level, behind the long halls, and shown them a wall.

Not a stone one. A line. Twenty hold-folk in grey, shoulder to shoulder, with their big round grey shields up, overlapping. Every shield covers half the man on its left. And she’d said: stand in it.

It had been the hardest thing Azac had ever done.

He’d thought it would be easy. He was a tank. He’d been a tank for two hundred and fifty days, and a good one, and before that he’d been the one standing behind a tank for years and watching how it was done. He knew how to hold a shield. He knew how to set his feet. He knew how to stand in front of a thing and take it.

He didn’t know how to stand next to anybody.

That was what he found out. On the first morning, in the yard, with a hold-folk woman on his right and a hold-folk man on his left and a drill-sergeant with a padded pole coming at the line. He held his shield up the way he always held it. In front of himself. All of himself. Covering everything from his chin to his shins.

And the drill-sergeant’s pole came round the edge of his shield into the ribs of the man on his left, who wasn’t covered, because Azac’s shield was covering Azac.

Again, said Brenna.

He’d done it again. And again. Every time the pole came, his shield went to himself. It wasn’t a decision. It was his arm. Two hundred and fifty days of nobody on his left, and nobody on his right, and everything that came at him coming at him, and his arm had learned that the only thing it was ever for was the man holding it.

You’re a good shield, Brenna had said, on the third morning, after the fortieth time. For one man. Which is what you’ve been. Out on your own. She’d looked at him with her grey eyes. In a wall, there’s no such thing as one man.

He’d thought about that a lot. Standing in the snow on the fifth wall, seven nights running, with his shield half on Chris and Gabe’s half on him. He’d got better at it. He’d made himself. Every night he held it a little further left, a little less over his own chest, and every night Gabe’s warm edge came across his right side and covered what he’d left open.

It still felt wrong. It felt like standing in the street with no coat on.

He was still thinking about it when the padlock came up.

It came on the two hundred and fifty-second day, in the yard, in the middle of a drill. He didn’t notice it at first. Then he did, at the edge of his vision, small and grey, beside his level.

He looked at it for a long time with the drill going on round him.

Rui Xiang had one. Gabe had one. Half the thirty-five were at forty-four, waiting for it. Forty-five, Declan had said, on floor 8, was where the third tier stopped. The trials are harder every time. Every time. He’d said it looking at his own lock, the one he’d had for five days on the jetty before the river-folk watched him kneel.

Azac opened the trial window that night, in the barracks, on his bunk, with nobody watching.

[ Tier 3 trial · Knight
Easy: Hold a door.
Normal: Hold a wall. ]

He read it twice. He’d held doors. He’d held a gate on floor 3 till everybody was through, for his last trial, and not counted. He’d held a vent in the Kiln on floor 4 on his own and nearly died of it, and Rachael had told him afterwards in about four words what she thought of that.

He scrolled down.

[ Hard: Vanguard
Stand in a line of strangers. Cover the one on your left.
Let the one on your right cover you.
Tomorrow night, on the fifth wall. ]

He lay on his back on his bunk for a long time looking at it.

Strangers.

Not Chris. Not Gabe with his warm shield. Not Declan three places along. Strangers. Hold-folk. People he didn’t know, who didn’t know him, who had no reason on any floor to put their shield over a climber who’d been in their pass for two weeks.

Let the one on your right cover you.

He’d never let anyone. That was the thing. He saw it lying there, very clearly, the way you see something in the dark once your eyes get used to it. In two hundred and fifty days he’d never once let anyone stand where they could cover him. That was the point of being the tank. You stood where nobody needed to cover you, because you were in front of all of them.

And Declan had done it too. Exactly the same. Except Declan had done it on his own a floor ahead for a hundred and fifty-three days, and Azac had at least had thirty-four people behind him.

He’d learned it from Declan. All of it. On Earth. Watching from behind.

He opened the window again and looked at the word. Vanguard. The ones who go in front.

Stand in a line of strangers.

“And so our hero,” said Azac, very quietly, to the dark of the barracks, “took the hard one. Obviously.”

Across the room, in the dark, Calvin said sleepily, “Took the hard what?”

“Go to sleep, Calvin.”

“You go to sleep.”

He touched the window.

[ Trial accepted: Vanguard
Tomorrow night. Fifth wall. ]

He told Brenna in the morning.

He hadn’t been going to. Then he’d thought about it, on his bunk, about how a line worked, and thought that if he was going to stand in one with strangers tomorrow night, the strangers ought to get a say.

She was in the gatehouse of the first wall, where she always was, with her shield on her back and a steaming cup in her big hands. She listened to him with her grey eyes on his face. She didn’t say anything until he’d finished.

“Strangers,” she said.

“That’s what it says.”

“Not your own.”

“No.”

Brenna drank from the cup. She looked out of the gatehouse window, up the pass, at the five walls going up into the snow.

“There’s nobody on this floor’s a stranger on the wall,” she said. “Not to us. You stand on the wall, you’re on the wall.” She paused. “But you’re a stranger to them. That’s true. They’ve seen you up there. They don’t know you. They don’t know if you’ll hold.” She put the cup down. “And you don’t know if they will.”

“No.”

“That’s the trial, then,” said Brenna. “That’s all it is. That’s what every boy and girl in this pass does the first night they go up. Stand in a line with people they’ve never stood with. And hold it, and let it hold them.” She looked at him. “Tamm did it. Nine nights ago.”

Azac didn’t say anything.

“I’ll put you in the fourth company,” said Brenna. “Middle of the line. Nobody you know on either side.” She paused. “I know who I’ll put on your right.”

It was Ansa.

He saw her when he came up onto the fifth wall at dusk on the two hundred and fifty-third day, with his shield on his arm and the snow coming sideways. She was standing in the line already. In the middle of the fourth company, in grey, with a big round grey shield up and a long grey braid down her back and her face like weathered stone. And there was a gap beside her. On her left. One man wide.

He knew who she was. Everybody did. Malcolm had told them. Was he afraid? No. He was looking at the snow.

He stopped at the top of the stair.

Ansa turned her head and looked at him. With her grey eyes. Steady. As if she’d been waiting for him a while, and could wait a while longer.

“Brenna says you’re to stand on my left,” she said.

“Yeah,” said Azac. His voice didn’t work properly. “Yeah. If that’s—are you sure? You don’t have to—“

“I stood here last night,” said Ansa. “And the night before. Third on the right. Where he was.” She looked at the place in the line. “I’m not going anywhere else. Not this winter.”

He didn’t know what to say. He went and stood in the gap.

On his left was a man he’d never seen. Big, black-bearded, with a bandage round his right hand where three fingertips had been. He glanced at Azac and nodded, once. I’ve got seven more, Malcolm had said he’d said. My da had four.

On his right was Ansa.

He put his shield up.

It went in front of him. He felt it go. His arm did it. All of him, chin to shin, the way it always went.

He made it go left.

It was like moving a door that had been shut for a year. It didn’t want to go. His arm didn’t want it. Everything he had, every night of every floor, wanted the shield in front of his own chest. He made it go. Left. Half off himself. Over the black-bearded man’s right side, where his shield-arm was, where he was open.

And his own right side was open. Completely. Chest, ribs, arm. Nothing there. The wind came down the pass from the high side and went straight into it.

He stood there with half of himself in the open and every part of him screaming.

Then Ansa’s shield came across.

He felt it. Not a touch. The edge of it, grey iron, a hand’s breadth from his ribs, covering them. She hadn’t looked at him. She’d just moved it, the way you’d move your hand to steady a cup somebody had put down too near the edge. Over his open side.

He stood there with her shield across his ribs and couldn’t breathe.

“Breathe,” said Ansa. Not looking at him. “Tamm couldn’t either. The first night. You get used to it.”

He breathed.

The Rimed came at full dark.

He heard them first. They always heard them first. The wind dropping, going low and quiet, and then from up the pass, in the dark and the snow, the cracking. Crack. Crack-crack. Like ice on a pond that isn’t thick enough. Coming down.

All along the line, the hold-folk set their feet.

He set his. He held his shield where it was. Left. Over the black-bearded man. He felt Ansa’s shield over him. And he felt the cold come down the pass, and the cracking get closer, and every single part of him want to pull the shield back.

He didn’t.

He started to talk instead.

He didn’t decide to. It just came out of him. Low, under his breath, the way it did. Saga. Tell them how it ends. They’ll fight to make it true. He’d done it on floor 3 at the Lanternjaw, and on floor 4 in the furnace hall, and every night of every raid since. He’d never done it to strangers.

“And so the fourth company,” said Azac, to the snow, “stood on the fifth wall on the tenth night of winter. And the Rimed came down out of the dark.”

The black-bearded man glanced at him.

“And every shield in the line covered the one on its left,” said Azac. “And nobody covered himself. Not one. And the line held.”

He felt it go out of him. The way it always did. Not a light. Not a sound. A sort of warmth, going out along the line from where he was standing, left and right, into the people beside him. The way a story goes into people round a fire. He felt the black-bearded man stand differently. He felt Ansa, on his right, go very still.

“What’s that?” said the black-bearded man. Low.

“It’s a story,” said Azac. “It’s how it ends.”

“Does it?”

“If we make it.”

The black-bearded man looked at him for a moment longer. Then something went across his face, under the beard. Not quite a smile.

“Tell it louder,” he said.

They came over the wall.

Not where Azac was. Not at first. Twenty metres down, then thirty the other way, long grey-white arms and old gauntlets coming over the parapet, ice fingers reaching, and the line leaning into them, shield on shield, and the spears going up under the arms into the hollow breastplates. Snow and ice and old iron coming down. He heard it all round him in the dark. He heard Gabe, somewhere along to his left in the third company, shout something, and Declan answer. He heard Calvin, on the stair, talking. He heard the hold-folk sergeants calling the line.

And he talked.

“And the fourth company held,” said Azac. Louder. “And the third, and the fifth. And the Rimed came down on the shields and couldn’t find a gap. Because there wasn’t one.”

Then one came over in front of him.

It came up the face of the wall out of the dark right below his feet. He saw the old helmet first, coming over the edge, hollow inside. Then the arm. Long, grey-white, the gauntlet rusted green. Then the ice fingers. Reaching. Not for him. For the black-bearded man on his left. For the place where his shield was. The place Azac was covering.

It hit Azac’s shield.

The whole weight of it. A thing made of packed snow and ice and old armour, taller than a man, coming down on the left half of his shield where it stuck out over the black-bearded man’s right side. And his arm went back. All of it. Into his own chest. His shoulder screamed. He felt the cold come through the iron, through the strap, into his forearm, like putting his arm into a river in winter.

And his arm tried to pull the shield back. To himself. To cover himself. Because something was hitting him and that was what the shield was for.

He didn’t let it.

He held it where it was. Over the man on his left. He leaned. Into it. The way the hold-folk leaned. And the Rimed leaned back. And its other arm came round, round the right side, round where Azac was open, reaching for his ribs, his chest, the ice fingers spread.

And met Ansa’s shield.

He heard it. A crack like a bell. Ice fingers on grey iron, a hand’s breadth from his ribs. She’d moved it. Not much. Not looking at him. Just enough. And the ice fingers skidded across her shield, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find. Because she’d covered it.

The black-bearded man’s spear went up under the Rimed’s arm, past Azac’s shield, into the hollow of its breastplate. There was a crack, deep inside. The black stone.

It came apart.

Snow and ice and old iron, all over the three of them. Down the face of the wall into the dark. And Azac stood there with his shield still out over the man on his left and Ansa’s still over him, and snow in his beard and his arm on fire with cold, and breathed.

“And the line held,” he said. His voice shook. “Like it said.”

“Keep talking,” said Ansa.

He kept talking.

They came all night.

That was the thing he hadn’t known. The first night had been one wave, and then quiet. This was the tenth night of winter, and the Rimed had learned. They came in waves, and stopped, and came again. Every hour. Every half hour. The cracking up the pass and the long arms over the parapet and the line leaning into them, and the spears, and the snow coming down sideways in John’s white lights.

He talked the whole time.

He told them how it ended. Over and over. In different words, every time, because it was a story and stories had to be told fresh. The fourth company on the fifth wall on the tenth night. Every shield on the one to its left. The line holding. The cold coming and not finding a gap. Dawn.

It’s always dawn at the end, he said. That’s how you know it’s a story. It always ends with the light coming up over the low end of the pass.

He felt it go out along the line. Further than he’d ever felt it go. Not just to the black-bearded man and Ansa. Past them. To the next ones. And the ones past those. A whole company of hold-folk, grey and silent and frozen, standing straighter in the snow because a climber with ice in his beard was telling them a story about themselves under his breath.

He felt the trial in it, too. Underneath. A window he couldn’t see, watching.

Twice more the Rimed hit his shield where it covered the black-bearded man, and twice more his arm tried to pull it back, and he didn’t let it. Twice more something came round his open side, and Ansa’s shield was there. She never looked at him. Not once. She just moved it. Every time. A hand’s breadth from his ribs.

Around three in the morning, between waves, she said, without turning her head:

“He used to tell stories too.”

Azac didn’t say anything.

“Tamm,” said Ansa. “When he was small. He’d sit in the yard in the snow and tell stories to the chickens. About the wall. About how he was going to be on it one day.” She was quiet for a long time. “They always ended with the light coming up. Like yours.”

“Yeah,” said Azac. He couldn’t say anything else.

“Keep telling it,” said Ansa.

He told it.

The light came up over the low end of the pass a little after seven.

It came slowly. Grey first, then white, then a thin cold gold along the tops of the mountains on the far side. The snow had stopped an hour before. The Rimed had stopped an hour before that. The slope below the fifth wall was a mess of old armour and broken ice, and the hold-folk along the line were lowering their shields one at a time, stiffly, the way you lower something you’ve been holding up for twelve hours.

Azac didn’t lower his. He couldn’t feel his arm. He stood there with it still out over the man on his left, frozen in place.

“You can put it down,” said the black-bearded man.

He put it down. It took a long time.

His right side was fine. He looked down at it, at his ribs, at the coat over them. Not a mark. Not a scratch. Not a touch of frost. All night. Every time something had come for it, there’d been a grey shield there. He’d never seen it come. He’d never once looked.

He looked now. At Ansa, on his right, lowering her shield. Her face grey with cold and tiredness under the steel cap, her braid white with frost.

“Thank you,” said Azac.

Ansa looked at him for the first time all night. Grey eyes. Steady.

“That’s the wall,” she said. “You don’t say thank you for the wall.” She paused. “You covered Orm all night.” She nodded at the black-bearded man. “He covered the next one. They covered the next. Nobody covered themselves.” She shouldered her shield. “That’s all it is.”

And then, as she turned to go, she stopped.

“The story,” she said. “About the light coming up. Tell it again, sometime. On the longest night.” She looked out over the low end of the pass, at the gold. “We read the names. All night. It’s a long night. Somebody should tell how it ends.”

She went down the stair. Azac watched her go.

He stood on the fifth wall in the morning light and read it four times.

Let the one on your right cover you.

Somebody came and stood beside him. On his right. He didn’t look round. He knew who it was by the warmth.

“You took the hard one,” said Gabe.

“Obviously.”

“How was it?”

Azac thought about it. About standing in the snow all night with half of himself open to the wind and nobody he knew on either side. About a woman who’d lost her son nine days ago moving her shield across his ribs, again and again, without looking.

“Like standing in the street with no coat on,” he said. “For twelve hours.”

Gabe didn’t say anything. He just stood there. On Azac’s right. With his warm shield, which he’d made to cover somebody else, held a little out to the left. Covering Azac’s right side. Out of habit, probably. Or not.

Azac felt it. The warmth. He let it.

Along the parapet, past Gabe, Declan was standing on his own at the edge of the wall, looking up the pass at the high side. At the throat of rock going up into the mountains, where the Rimed came from. Where, Declan had said, there was a gate.

He wasn’t in a line. Nobody was on his right.

Azac watched him for a long time.

“And so our hero,” he said, very quietly, “learned the thing his friend had never learned. On a wall. From a stranger.”

Gabe looked at him.

“Device,” said Azac.

“I know,” said Gabe. He was looking at Declan too. “Finish it.”

Azac didn’t. He didn’t know how it ended yet. That one, he thought, wasn’t his to tell.
