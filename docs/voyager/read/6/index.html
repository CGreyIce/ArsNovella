Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 1: Thirty-Six Circles

Chapter 6

Mother Crane

3,068 words · 13 min read

Ice came out of the back door of the white tower with a sword on his back and his knuckles still throbbing from the goat.

He’d known punching the goat was a bad idea at the time. He’d known it the way you know a joke is going to land badly halfway through telling it, and you tell the rest anyway, because you’ve started. It had been standing in the middle of the stream path exactly where it had stood ten years ago. It had looked at him exactly the way it had looked at all thirty-six of them ten years ago, and he’d had no weapon, no class and no patience, and he’d been crying. So he’d hit it.

It had taken eleven punches and a lot of very undignified scrambling in the shallows. The goat had come off worse. That was the main thing.

“You’re bleeding,” said Alea.

“It’s knuckle blood. Knuckle blood doesn’t count.”

“It counts for about four health.”

[ Ice · Lv 1 · Tier 1: Squire · Knight ]

He looked at the window for a long moment as he walked. Squire. He’d been a Squire for six weeks the first time, and then a Sworn, a Sentinel and so on all the way up, ten tiers of shields and swords until there was nothing left to be. He’d seen the end of that ladder, and it had been a black throne and a dead man’s room. He was quite happy to be a Squire again.

The sword was plain. A Knight’s first sword always was: a straight, unremarkable blade on a stone block in a round room, the same one every Knight in the Spire had picked up on their first morning. He’d held it for maybe three seconds before the cold went up his arm. Then he’d come straight back out, said thank you to Quill, who’d been halfway through trying to write his name for the third time, and left before the old man could ask him anything else. He’d always liked Quill. He couldn’t afford to like anyone this morning.

He went down the alley behind the tower. Hearthstead was waking up round him: shutters banging, a dog barking at nothing, the smell of the first bread. The sun was barely up, sitting low on its rail above the eastern hills. Nobody looked twice at him. He was a young man with a sword in a town where young men with swords came down out of the hill every few lifetimes, and it was very early.

“East gate’s left,” said Alea. “Then straight out across the fields. The chapel’s on the third rise.”

“I know.”

“You’re going right.”

“I know.”

“Ice. The chapel’s left.”

“I know where the chapel is,” Ice said. He’d stopped walking. He was standing at the corner where the alley met a narrow, crooked street that ran down toward the river, and he was looking at a low building at the bottom of it, with a crooked chimney already smoking and a painted sign over the door that showed a spoon. “I just want to see her.”

Alea was quiet for a moment.

“We have ninety minutes before they reach the hall,” she said, more gently. “Less, if Gabe walks fast.”

“Gabe always walks fast.”

“Then you have an hour.”

“I only need ten minutes.” He started down the street. “I need a life skill. She’s a trainer. It’s efficient.”

“It’s very efficient,” Alea agreed, in the tone of somebody who wasn’t going to argue with a man who’d just punched a goat.

The door of the Copper Spoon was propped open with a brick. Inside, it was dark and warm and smelled of onions, as it always had. A long room with a long table down the middle, benches either side, a fire at one end, and at the other the kitchen, open to the room, where a short, wide old woman in a stained apron was feeding wood into the stove with the furious concentration of somebody who’d been feeding that stove for forty years and still didn’t trust it.

Ice stopped in the doorway.

It was the habit. He’d made it in the white room and it was already working on its own. The stop, the breath, the putting-away. He stood with one hand on the doorframe and made himself remember, very clearly, that the woman at the stove had never seen him in her life.

She’d fed all thirty-six of them for their first month in the Spire, on credit, because none of them had any money and she said she’d never yet let a Climber starve on her floor and wasn’t starting now. She’d shouted at Calvin for putting his feet on her table. She’d put a bowl of plain water with a pinch of salt in front of Maigo the second night, because she’d decided a tree would want watering, and Maigo had drunk all of it to be polite. She’d caught Ice in her kitchen on the fourth morning, trying to help, and instead of throwing him out she’d handed him a knife and a sack of onions and said, Well, go on, then, if you’re going to stand there. And for a month after that, every morning before the others woke up, he’d peeled her onions and she’d told him about her husband, who’d been dead twenty-two years and was still, in her opinion, the most irritating man on the floor.

In the second winter, while all thirty-six of them were eight floors up, she’d caught a fever and died in her bed above the kitchen. Nobody had told them for a month. When word finally came up through the floors, he’d gone and sat on his own for a whole evening, and Thea had found him there and not asked why, and sat with him.

The old woman at the stove looked up and saw him in the door.

“Well?” she said. “Are you coming in or are you holding that frame up?”

“Sorry,” said Ice. “Hi. Morning.”

“It’s barely morning. Stove’s not hot. Bread’s not done.” She squinted at him over the top of the stove. “You’re a Climber.”

“Yeah.”

“Thought so. You’ve got that look. Like you fell out of the sky and landed on your head.” She went back to the wood. “Mother Crane. You can sit, but I’ve nothing for you yet, so don’t ask.”

“I wasn’t going to ask for food,” said Ice. “I was going to ask if you needed help.”

Mother Crane stopped feeding the stove.

She turned round properly and looked at him. She had small, very sharp eyes in a face like a walnut, and she looked him up and down: the vest, the rolled sleeves, the sword, the knuckle blood.

“Help,” she said.

“Help.”

“In my kitchen.”

“If you want.”

“Nobody ever wants to help in my kitchen,” said Mother Crane. “Climbers least of all. Climbers want to be fed, and then they want to know where the monsters are, and then they want to be fed again.” She considered him a moment longer. Then she reached under the counter, came up with a knife and a sack, and dropped the sack on the end of the long table with a thump. “Well, go on, then. If you’re going to stand there.”

Ice looked at the sack of onions.

He had to stop again. Just for a second. He stood in the doorway of the Copper Spoon with his hand on the frame, and the putting-away took longer than usual.

Then he went in, sat down at the end of the long table, and started peeling.

He’d forgotten how much he liked it.

Peeling an onion properly was a small, exact job with a clear beginning and a clear end: top, tail, the cut down one side, the papery skin coming away in a single piece if you were lucky and in irritating flakes if you weren’t. You could do it without thinking, and while you did it you couldn’t really think about anything else. He’d peeled thousands of them in ten years. Mother Crane had taught him on floor 1, and then he’d kept doing it, in camps and inns and caves, on every floor, because it was the one thing in the Spire that was exactly the same every time.

“Hm,” said Mother Crane, behind him.

She’d come round from the stove to watch. She did that. She’d done it the first time too, he remembered: let you start, then crept up on you to see if you were going to ruin anything.

“Who taught you that?” she said.

“Taught me what?”

“That.” She pointed with her chin at his hands. “Top and tail first. Cut along the grain. You’re keeping the root on so it holds together for the dice. Nobody your age does that. Nobody your age peels an onion at all, they buy their supper off a cart and complain about it.”

Ice kept peeling.

“Somebody did,” he said.

“Your mother?”

“No.” He finished the onion and reached for the next. “Somebody like you, actually. She was very rude about it.”

Mother Crane snorted. “Sounds like a sensible woman.”

“She was.”

The word was sat on the table between them for a second. Mother Crane looked at him a bit longer. Then she went back to the stove without saying anything, and Ice was grateful, and peeled.

By the time he’d done half the sack, the bread was out and the room smelled of it, and Mother Crane put a heel of it in front of him on a plate with a lump of butter, and a bowl of something brown and steaming.

“I didn’t ask for food,” Ice said.

“You didn’t ask to peel my onions either. Eat.”

He ate. It was exactly how he remembered it, which was the problem. He put his head down and ate it fast, so he wouldn’t have to taste it too carefully.

“Most Climbers come through here sooner or later,” Mother Crane said, wiping her hands on her apron and sitting down across from him with a cup of something. The first time, it had taken her two weeks to sit down across from him. “The old ones in the stories, my gran’s stories, they all ate at this table. It’s been in my family since the Spire was young. You know that? Every Climber who ever went up those stairs ate at least once at this table, my gran said. She said they’d come again, too. A lot of them, all at once, out of the hill. I never believed her.” She looked at him over her cup. “You’re early, though. Gran said there’d be a lot of you. Not one.”

“There’s a lot of us,” Ice said. “The rest are coming.”

“Today?”

“In about—“ He glanced at the corner of his eye, where Alea had quietly put up a small number. “About an hour.”

“And you came first.”

“Yeah.”

“Why?”

He thought about how to answer that. Then he gave her the true one, more or less, because she was Mother Crane and would see through anything else.

“Because somebody has to see what’s up there before they go,” he said. “So it’s me.”

She looked at him for a while.

“Well,” she said. “That’s stupid.”

“Yeah.”

“Brave. But stupid.”

“I’ve been told.”

She put her cup down. “You want something from me. You didn’t come in for my onions. You’ve got a look like you want something and don’t want to ask.” She folded her arms. “Out with it.”

So he asked. He asked if she’d teach him to cook.

Life skills in the Spire worked the way everything worked in the Spire: there was always a person, and there was always a test, and the person always pretended the test wasn’t a test.

Mother Crane’s test was the rest of the onions.

Then it was a pot of broth: she stood over him while he made it and said nothing, which was worse than shouting. Then it was the dough for a second batch of bread, which she made him knead until his forearms burned and his knuckles split open again, and then knead some more. While he worked, she told him, without being asked, about her husband, who had been dead twenty-two years and was still, in her opinion, the most irritating man on the floor.

“He used to put his feet on that table,” she said darkly, nodding at the long table. “Right there. Every night. With his boots on.”

Ice laughed before he could stop himself. Then he had to stop kneading for a moment and look at the dough very hard.

“What’s funny?”

“Nothing. Sorry. I knew a guy like that.”

“Well. You tell him from me he’s a pig.”

“I will,” said Ice. Calvin would be in town within the hour. He’d have his feet on her table by the end of the week. She’d shout at him exactly the same way. Ice would miss it, because he’d be two floors up by then, and that was right, that was how it was supposed to be, and he kneaded the dough.

When the second batch went in, Mother Crane looked at it, then at him, then at the floury mess he’d made of her table, and sniffed.

“Well,” she said. “You won’t poison anyone.”

A window opened in front of him, very small and very plain.

He looked at it for a long time.

The first time round, he’d never taken a life skill. He’d meant to. There’d always been something more urgent: a floor to clear, a quest to finish, somebody’s gear to fix. By the time he’d realised a tank who could feed himself might live longer than one who couldn’t, he’d been on the fortieth floor, and he’d spent his one slot on Smelting, because Gabe had needed ingots. He’d never told Gabe it had been the wrong choice. He’d have hated it.

“Thanks,” he said.

“Don’t thank me. Thank my onions.” Mother Crane was already wrapping two of the first loaves in a cloth. She pushed the bundle across the table at him. “Take these. And the broth, the pot’s yours, bring it back. You’re going up there on your own, you’ll want something in your stomach that isn’t fear.”

“I can pay—“

“You’ll pay me when you come back down.”

He stopped with his hand on the bundle.

“Everyone comes back down eventually,” said Mother Crane comfortably. “Gran said. They go up and they come back down to eat. I’ll keep a tab.” She looked at him with her small sharp eyes. “What’s your name? For the tab.”

He opened his mouth.

He had the old answer ready. He’d been Ice to her once, for a month, and then for eight more years in his head, every time he peeled an onion in a cave on some floor she’d never see. Ice, love. Pass the salt, Ice. She’d never once called him anything else.

“Put me down as the onion boy,” he said.

Mother Crane snorted.

“The onion boy,” she said. “Fine. Go on. Get out of my kitchen. You’ve got flour on your face.”

He went out through the back, the way she pointed him, through a yard full of hens and down a lane between gardens to the east edge of town. He didn’t look back until he was past the last wall, and when he did, he couldn’t see the Copper Spoon any more, only the crooked chimney, still smoking.

“You didn’t tell her your name,” said Alea.

“No.”

“Because the cards won’t take it?”

“Because if I tell her, she’ll tell them.” He shifted the bundle under his arm. It was still warm. “A lad came through this morning. Ice, he said his name was. That’s all it’d take. Gabe would be out the back door before she finished the sentence.”

“She’s going to tell them about the onion boy.”

“Yeah,” said Ice. “Probably.”

He thought about that. Deston coming into the Copper Spoon tonight with thirty-four other people behind him, ducking under the low beam. Mother Crane saying, You’re the rest of them, are you? Your onion boy came through at dawn. Deston’s face.

“It’s fine,” he said. “Nobody’s going to hear onion boy and think it’s me.”

“Gabe is.”

“Gabe hears everything and thinks it’s me. Gabe heard a dog bark once and thought it was me.”

“That was you.”

“That was one time,” said Ice.

He came out of the lane onto the open fields.

They rolled away east in long low rises toward the edge of the floor: patchy grass, gorse, dry-stone walls going nowhere, and on the slopes, here and there, grey shapes that were not quite sheep. One of them lifted its head as he came out of the lane, and looked at him with red eyes, and went back to chewing.

[ Woolback · Lv 2 ]

On the third rise, a long way off, a small grey building stood on its own without a roof. The sun was climbing its rail behind it, still low and yellow, throwing the chapel’s shadow toward him down the hill.

“Ice,” said Alea. “Three things.”

“Go.”

“One: you’ve got flour on your face. Two: you’ve got fifty-one minutes before they reach the hall, and eleven hours and change before the sun reaches the chapel’s high window. Three—“ She paused. “She wrapped you two loaves. Not one.”

He looked down at the bundle under his arm. He hadn’t noticed.

“She always did that,” Alea said. “The first time. She always gave you two. Because she knew you’d share.”

He stood there at the edge of the east fields with the warm bread under his arm and flour on his face, and the sun coming up the rail.

“Yeah,” he said, after a moment. “She did.”

He wiped his face on his sleeve, drew the plain Knight’s sword off his back, and went to fight the sheep.
