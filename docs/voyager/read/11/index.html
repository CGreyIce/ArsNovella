Chapter 11

Voyager on the Board

3,687 words · 16 min read

Calvin woke up on the floor of an inn in a magic tower and, for about three glorious seconds, didn’t remember.

Then he did, and he lay there grinning at the ceiling.

It was a real ceiling, wooden, with a crack in it shaped a bit like Malaysia. Azac was snoring in the bed above him. He’d won it in a coin toss and said it was his destiny. Dennis was on the other side of the room, curled under a blanket with his window open even in his sleep, as if he’d dozed off counting money. Deston’s bed was already empty and made. Of course it was.

Transmigrated, Calvin thought. I’m in a transmigration novel. I’m a Ranger. He’d been reading these books since he was fifteen, under the desk in maths, on the bus, at three in the morning when he should have been asleep. He’d always known exactly what he’d do if it happened to him. He’d been wrong about almost all of it, it turned out. He hadn’t expected the smell, for one thing. Or that it would be this cold on the floor. Or that the first thing it would make him feel, every time he woke up, was this huge, stupid, bursting happiness, and then, right behind it, the fear.

He opened his window to check he was still a Ranger. He was.

[ Calvin · Lv 2 · Tier 1: Scout · Ranger ]

Then, because he couldn’t help it, he flicked over to the rankings.

He looked at it for a second. Then he sat up so fast he hit his head on the bed frame.

“Bro.”

Azac snored on.

“Bro. Bro, bro, bro. Azac. Dennis. Wake up. Wake up.”

“Is it on fire,” said Dennis, without opening his eyes.

“No—“

“Then no.”

“It’s got a name.”

Dennis opened one eye.

By the time they got down to the Copper Spoon, everyone knew.

It was the only thing anyone was talking about. Thirty-four people crammed round the long table in the grey morning, in various states of having slept on floors, with bowls of porridge going cold in front of them, and every single one of them with their window open to the same line.

Voyager.

“It’s a name,” Jared was saying, at the far end, with great authority. “Last night it wasn’t a name. It was a dash. This morning, it’s a name. So he got a name. Overnight.”

“How do you get a name?” said Hako.

“Same way you get anything in here, I’d imagine. Kill something big enough, and it gives you one.”

“Or he picked it,” said Kei. “Character creation. He just did his late.”

“He’s on floor two,” said Tiger, sounding personally betrayed. “He’s on floor two already. We haven’t even seen the boss of floor one and he’s on floor two.”

“Voyager,” said Gabe.

Gabe was sitting in the middle of the table with his window open in front of him and his porridge untouched. He’d been sitting like that, Calvin gathered, since before dawn. Deston was next to him, not saying anything, just being there.

“Voyager,” Gabe said again, as if it were a word in another language. “Who’s Voyager?”

“It’s him,” said someone.

“It’s not his name, though.” That was Hana. She had her cat ears flat and her crown sitting very straight, and she was looking at the window with a frown. “Ice wouldn’t— If he were picking a name, he’d pick something stupid. A joke. Something you’d groan at.” She hesitated. “Voyager isn’t— it’s not him. It doesn’t sound like him.”

“It could be anybody,” said someone else, uncertainly. “It could be a guy who was here before. Another player. A— what did the old lady say, an old Climber.”

“There were thirty-six circles,” said Gabe flatly. “There’s thirty-six lines. There’s one guy. It’s him.”

“It’s a knight,” said Yan, quietly, from the end of the bench. “Ice always played the front.” He stopped. “But I don’t know. I don’t know any more. Last night I was sure. This morning it’s got a name, and it’s not his, and I’m—“ He stopped again. Suzu, round his neck, turned her head to look at him. “I’m less sure,” Yan said.

Calvin had been watching faces. He liked faces; he’d always been good with them, the way some people are good with numbers. So he was looking at the right place at the right moment to see Xav.

Xav was sitting at the far corner of the table with his party: Rui Xiang, Malcolm, John. And when Gabe said Voyager the second time, Xav had gone very still. Not the way you go still when you’re listening. The way you go still when something’s touched a memory. He was looking at nothing, at the middle of the table.

Calvin didn’t think about it. He never did.

“Xav,” he said across the table. “You know it?”

Everyone looked at Xav.

Xav blinked. “What?”

“The name. You made a face.”

“I didn’t make a face.”

“You made a face. Like you know it.”

Xav looked at him for a moment, with the calm, even look that always made you feel he’d already thought about whatever you were going to say.

“It’s a space probe,” he said.

“A what?”

“Voyager. It’s a probe. NASA sent it out in the seventies. It’s past the whole solar system now.” He shrugged. “It’s still going. That’s all. I just thought of it.”

“Oh,” said Calvin. “Huh.”

“It’s a common word,” said Xav. “Could be anyone.”

He went back to his porridge. Calvin watched him for a second longer. He had the feeling, the way you sometimes do with people, that there’d been a door there, just for a second, and Xav had looked through it and then very carefully closed it.

Then Mother Crane came down the table with the porridge pot and told him to stop staring and eat, and he ate.

Gabe sent it a whisper.

Calvin saw him do it: the little whisper option next to the name in the rankings, a small envelope that opened a box. Gabe typed in it with two fingers, and his face did something private while he typed, and Calvin looked away.

But he couldn’t help seeing the window that came back.

[ Whisper to Voyager · sent ]

No reply. Not in the next minute, or the next ten. Gabe sat there with his porridge going cold and his window open, watching the empty box under the word sent.

“Maybe he’s busy,” said Calvin.

“Yeah,” said Gabe.

“Like, killing something.”

“Yeah.”

“He’s on floor two. There’s probably a lot to kill on floor two.”

Gabe closed the window. “Yeah,” he said, and then, after a moment: “Thanks, Cal.”

Calvin didn’t know what he’d done to be thanked for, but he took it.

The thing about the sheep was that Calvin found it out.

It was the middle of the second morning, and the five of them were out on the low fields north of the river, because Deston had looked at a map in the class hall and said start where it’s easy. They’d found Woolbacks. Large grey shaggy ones with red eyes and very poor attitudes. Azac had walked up to the first one, said, “And so our hero approaches the beast,” and swung his sword at it in a beautiful wide arc.

The sword went into the wool and stopped there.

Azac stood there for a moment with his sword stuck in a sheep, looking down at it. The Woolback looked back up at him.

“Our hero,” said Azac, “has made a tactical error.”

Then the Woolback put its head into his stomach and he went over backwards into a bush.

It took them most of the morning to kill three. Swords stuck. Rachael’s staff bounced off. Dennis’s only spell, a small illusion of light that made something look briefly as if it were somewhere it wasn’t, made the Woolbacks very confused and slightly angrier. Deston’s Bless kept everyone going, just. And every time they thought they’d got one on its own, a second came out from behind a wall and hit somebody from the side.

Calvin was the one who ducked.

He didn’t mean to. A Woolback came at him low and fast, and he panicked and dropped flat, the way you drop when something’s thrown at your head, and the sheep went over him. On the way over, his dagger, which he’d been holding in front of his face like a man warding off a wasp, went into the soft place under its jaw. No wool there. Barely any. It went in like a knife into butter, and the Woolback came down on top of him already dead.

He lay there under a dead sheep for a while.

“Under the jaw,” he said finally, muffled. “Guys. Guys. Under the jaw. There’s no wool under the jaw.”

They tried it. It worked. Azac stopped going into bushes. By noon they’d killed eleven, and Calvin was so pleased with himself that he opened World and typed it in capitals for everyone:

[ World · Calvin: PSA. STAB THE SHEEP UNDER THE JAW. NO WOOL THERE. YOURE WELCOME ]

[ World · Wendy: oh my god thank you ]

[ World · Salty: we found that an hour ago ]

[ World · Calvin: ok but did you TELL anyone ]

[ World · Salty: no ]

[ World · Calvin: exactly ]

“Also,” said Dennis, who’d been going through his inventory, “they drop wool. Which sells. Mother Crane said the weaver on the high street pays a copper for three bundles.” He did the maths visibly. “So if we kill thirty sheep a day, that’s ten copper, which is five beds for one night, or one bed for five nights, or—“

“Dennis,” said Deston.

“I’m the treasurer. Someone has to be the treasurer.”

“Who made you treasurer?”

“I did. Just now. Is anyone going to argue?”

Nobody argued. Dennis looked deeply satisfied.

[ Calvin · Lv 3 ]

The afternoon was when Calvin did the stupid thing.

He’d known it was stupid at the time. That was the worst part, afterwards. It wasn’t that he didn’t think. He did think. He thought: Deston said not too far, and he thought: I’m a Ranger, I’m a Scout, that’s literally my job, and he thought: I’m fast, I’m so fast now. He was. He’d noticed it after the first level: his legs had something new in them, something light, as if the wind was getting behind him. And he thought: I just want to see it.

The boss. The ram. The thing at the top of the valley that the guy on the board, Voyager, Declan, whoever he was, had killed on his own on the first day.

He just wanted to look at it.

“I’m going to scout ahead,” he said. “Up the valley. Just a bit. See what’s there.”

“Not too far,” said Deston, without looking up. He was crouched over Rachael’s staff with her, trying to work out why her heal spell kept fizzling.

“Not too far,” Calvin agreed, and went.

He ran. It felt incredible. It felt like being sixteen. The ground went past under him, grass and gorse and dry-stone walls he went over without breaking stride, the air rushing in his ears. Woolbacks lifted their heads on the slopes as he went past and were too slow to do anything about it. He went up the long green valley as the sun went over the top of its rail, and the hills closed in on both sides. Then there was a last wall, and he went up on top of it and stopped.

Below him was a round hollow full of short, cropped grass. There were seven tall grey standing stones in a ring around it, one of them with a great crack running up its side. There was a door in the hill at the back.

And in the middle of the hollow, asleep, was a ram the size of a horse, with a bronze bell round its neck.

[ The Bellwether · Lv 6 · Floor Guardian ]

Calvin stared at it.

“But you’re dead,” he said.

It went on sleeping. Its bell moved very slightly with every breath.

“He killed you,” Calvin said. “Last night. It said. Floor 1 has been cleared. So you’re— why are you—“

“The Floor Guardian returns,” said the Support at his shoulder, in its calm, pleasant voice, “while Players remain on the floor.”

Calvin turned his head and looked at it.

“Say that again.”

“The Floor Guardian returns while Players remain on the floor.”

“So it comes back. Every time. Until all of us leave.”

“Yes.”

“So he killed it, and then he left, and we’re still here, so it came back. For us.” He looked down at the sleeping ram. “So we all have to kill it. All of us.”

“Yes.”

“Huh,” said Calvin. He thought about it. He found, slightly to his own surprise, that it made him feel better. He didn’t take it away from us. Then he thought: or he couldn’t. And then he thought: it doesn’t matter, it’s here, look at it. And he stood on the wall at the top of the valley looking down at the Bellwether for longer than he should have.

Long enough that he didn’t hear the Woolbacks until they were very close.

There were two. Of course there were two. There were always two.

He’d turned round on the wall and they were already coming up the last slope at him, side by side, heads down. And one of them was bigger than the others had been, a lot bigger, with a darker fleece and a scar across its nose.

[ Woolback Ram · Lv 4 ]

He jumped off the wall. Wrong way: into the Fold, into the hollow. His foot hit the cropped grass and he thought no, no, the boss, it’ll wake up and changed direction in mid-step. His ankle went sideways and he went down, and the first Woolback came over the wall and landed beside him and swung its head. He got his dagger up. Under the jaw. He did, he found it. It came down, and he scrambled out from under it.

And the Woolback Ram came over the wall onto his left arm.

He heard it before he felt it. A sound like a stick breaking in a wet towel. Then he felt it, and the whole world went white and then very far away, and he was lying on his back in the grass at the edge of the Fold with a weight on his chest and his left arm somewhere he couldn’t feel. The Woolback Ram stood over him, enormous against the sky, its red eyes very close.

[ HP 18% ]

Huh, thought Calvin, quite calmly. This is how it goes.

He’d read the scene a hundred times. The early chapter. The guy who goes ahead on his own, to scout, because he’s fast and stupid and it’s his job. He never makes it to chapter ten. He’s the reason the main character learns to be careful.

I’m the scout guy, he thought. Oh, man. I’m the scout guy.

The Woolback Ram lowered its head.

Something hit it from the side.

It wasn’t big. It was a staff, a plain wooden one, swung two-handed like a cricket bat by somebody much shorter than a Woolback Ram and much, much angrier. It caught the ram across its scarred nose with a crack. It wasn’t enough to hurt it, but it was more than enough to make it turn its head to see what on earth had just done that.

And then Azac was there, sword up, roaring something that wasn’t words, putting himself between the ram and Calvin like a door slamming. Behind him came Deston’s soft white Bless, and Dennis shouting, and the ram’s red eyes suddenly confused, staring at three Azacs at once.

They killed it. Calvin didn’t see how. He was lying on his back looking up at the painted sky, holding very still, because he’d found out that if he moved at all, something in his left arm screamed.

Then there was a face over him, upside down. A frowning face with dark hair falling round it. Rachael.

“You absolute idiot,” she said.

“Hi, Rae.”

“Not too far. He said not too far. This is the top of the valley, Calvin. This is the end of the floor.” She was breathing very hard. She’d run. Calvin realised, distantly, that she’d run all the way up the valley after him. Rachael, who’d spent most of her life being careful about everything, had run the whole length of floor 1, and he’d been so far ahead he’d never even heard her.

“Sorry,” he said.

“Shut up.” She knelt beside him. Her hands hovered over his left arm and didn’t touch it, and he saw her look at it properly for the first time, and saw her face go very still.

“Rae?” he said. “Is it bad?”

“It’s broken,” said Rachael. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of somebody who’d spent a lot of time on the wrong side of doctors and knew exactly how they sounded when they didn’t want to scare you. “Both bones. Here.” She didn’t point. “I’m going to touch it. Okay? I have to touch it. Hold still.”

“Okay.”

She put two fingers on his arm, just above the break, very lightly. It still hurt so much he saw white.

“My heal keeps fizzling,” she said, low and fast, mostly to herself. “All afternoon. Dest and I were trying to work out why. It’s supposed to go into you. Like pouring. But I keep pouring it at the cut, and it splashes off.” She closed her eyes. “That’s wrong. You don’t fix the cut. The cut’s just where it shows. You fix what’s underneath.”

“Rae, I don’t—“

“Shut up, Calvin, I’m working.”

He shut up.

He felt it start. It wasn’t like Bless, a light on his shoulder. It was underneath. It was like cold water running down inside his arm, along the bone, finding the two broken places and going round them. Then holding them. Not pushing them back together, just holding them, very still, the way you’d hold two halves of a broken cup in the right position while the glue dried. It hurt, then it hurt less, then it stopped hurting, and he lay there and felt the two halves of each bone find each other in the dark of his arm and fit.

[ Minor Heal · on Calvin · 46% ]

Rachael took her fingers away. Her hand was shaking. Her face had gone grey-white, as if all the colour had gone down her arm and into him.

“Can you move your fingers?”

He moved his fingers. All five. He looked at them.

“Huh,” he said. His voice did something embarrassing. “I’ve got both arms.”

“Yes, Calvin, you’ve got both arms, well done.” Rachael sat back on her heels. She looked exhausted. She looked like she was going to fall over. She also looked, for one second, before she put her usual face back on, like someone who’d just done the thing she’d been waiting her whole life to do.

“I’m cold,” she said, sounding surprised. “Why am I cold?”

“You’re grey,” said Dennis, crouching down beside her, worried. “Rae, you’ve gone grey.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re not fine. Sit down.”

“I am sitting down.”

Deston crouched on Calvin’s other side. He didn’t say not too far. He didn’t say anything at all for a moment. He just looked at Calvin’s arm, and then at Calvin. Calvin had known Deston for three years, and had never once seen this look on him: as if Deston had been holding something in his chest all day and had just felt it shift.

“Sorry,” said Calvin again.

“Yeah,” said Deston quietly. He put his hand flat on top of his own shirt pocket, without seeming to notice he’d done it. “Me too.”

They sat at the top of the valley for a while, the five of them, on the cropped grass at the edge of the Fold, while the sun went down its rail and Rachael’s colour came back. The Bellwether slept on in the middle of the hollow with its bell on, and every so often it breathed in and the bell moved, and none of them looked at it.

“Do you think he’s okay?” said Calvin. He was lying on his back with his healed arm across his chest, very carefully.

“Who?” said Rachael.

“Voyager. Declan. The onion guy.”

Rachael was quiet for a moment.

“I think he’s an idiot,” she said. “Like you.”

“Like me?”

“He went ahead on his own. To see what was there. Because he’s fast, and he thinks it’s his job.” She pulled her knees up and put her arms round them. “Only he hasn’t got anyone running up the valley after him.”

Nobody said anything.

Then Azac, flat on his back in the grass with his sword on his chest, said to the switched-on stars, in his storytelling voice, “And so our heroes sat at the top of the world, and thought about the one who’d gone before them, and resolved to be less stupid.”

“Speak for yourself,” said Dennis.

“Most of them resolved to be less stupid,” Azac amended.

Calvin laughed. It hurt his ribs. He did it anyway.
