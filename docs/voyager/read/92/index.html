Regressor Diary of a Voyager · Volume 3: Party of One

Chapter 92

Count Us In

5,021 words · 22 min read

Newish counted things. She always had.

Stairs. Steps. The number of times Calvin said oh my days in a day (eleven, on average; twenty-three on the day of the first sun). The bells in a tower on seven, thirty-six of them, all at once, on one. The three hundred and forty steps up out of Evermorrow, with thirty-five other voices counting with her, and Ice’s in the middle of them for the first time, a little out of time, a little hoarse.

It wasn’t a skill. Well. It was a skill, now. Upbeat. Put a beat under them. Everyone’s faster on the one. But it had been a thing she did long before it was a skill. On Earth, in calls, in games, at two in the morning with Chris and Yan and Ice on the line. Three, two, one, GO. Somebody had to. Somebody had to put the beat under everybody, so that everybody went at once and nobody was left standing on their own being the idiot who went early.

She was good at it. She knew she was good at it. She was a support metronome, and she was proud of it, and she had waited a very long time for somebody to stand at the end of a table and say it.

You were always useful.

She’d been walking about a foot off the ground for six days.

The big pool looked worse from the shelves.

From the ledge high up the cliff, three days before, with Aleaka, it had looked like a hole. From the shelves, standing right down on the black wet rock with the water a metre below her boots, it looked like a mouth. Round, and dark, and breathing. The Great Fall came down on the far side of it out of the grey sky, white and endless, and where it hit, the water bloomed white and heaved and boiled. And everywhere else, the surface was black and smooth and slow, turning very gently, round and round, like something stirring in its sleep.

She’d stopped counting things about an hour ago. She’d been counting the people on the shelves, and the rocks, and her own breaths, and then she’d looked at the water and stopped.

Thirty-six of them. Spread round the pool on the black shelves in a ring, the way Drew had chalked it on the rock. The tanks low, near the water, shields up. The ranged ones higher, on the shelves above. Healers in between. Ice on the far side, near the fall, with Gabe beside him, because Drew had put him there, and Ice had gone where he was put without a word, which Newish still found faintly unsettling. Hyvred up on the cliff with her bow. Higu beside her with her map.

And Newish on the middle shelf. Halfway up. Where everybody could hear her.

Jared in front of her. Her party’s wall. Shield on his arm, feet planted on the wet rock, steady as a post. Aleaka beside her, with Sleet pressed against her legs and the old wren, Tuppence, sitting on her shoulder in the spray. Hana and Milli on the shelf below.

“They’re up there,” said Aleaka. Very quietly.

Newish looked up.

The cliffs round the Great Fall were full of birds. Falls-wrens. Hundreds. Sitting in rows on every ledge, all the way up into the cloud. Not bobbing. Not moving. Just sitting there. Watching the pool.

“I could ask them,” said Aleaka. “To help. They can see under the white. They know where it is.” She was holding her bow very tightly. “I don’t want to. Not if we don’t need them. They’re so small, Newish.”

“We won’t need them,” said Newish. “Leaka. Look at us. There’s thirty-six of us. We’ve got a raft. We’ve got a plan. We’ve got me.”

Aleaka looked at her for a long moment, with her round glasses all misted up.

“If it goes wrong—“ she started.

“It won’t go wrong,” said Newish. “I’m counting.”

The raft went out at noon.

Salty and Gabe had built it in Wendmere over two days, out of logs and rope and stubbornness. It was the size of a cart. They’d put a lantern on it, lit, and tied two long lines to it, one each, and Salty and Azac stood on the shelves on opposite sides of the pool and hauled them. Back and forth. So that the raft slid out over the black water into the middle, and jerked, and splashed, and rocked, and slid back. Something moving in the water.

It hunts what moves in the water. It comes up for it.

Drew lifted his hand. On the top shelf. Flat. Then pointed at her.

Newish took a breath.

“One,” she said.

She felt it go out. The beat. She’d never been able to explain it, what Upbeat felt like from the inside. It was like putting your hand flat on a table and feeling everybody else’s hands land on it at the same time. She said one and it went out round the pool, round the shelves, under everybody’s feet, and she felt thirty-five people catch it. Their breathing. Their weight. The way they held their weapons. All of them settling onto it, the way a choir settles onto a note.

“Two.”

The raft slid. Splashed. Salty hauled.

“Three. Four.”

She kept it going. Slow and steady. That was the plan. A steady count, round and round, so everybody was on it, so that when the thing came up for the raft, the next one would be the hit. All of them. At once. Like the bells.

“Five. Six. Seven. Eight.”

“One.”

Nothing came up. The raft splashed in the middle of the pool. The fall roared.

“Two. Three.”

She was good at this. She could do it for an hour. She could do it all day. She felt the whole ring of them, thirty-five people breathing on her beat round the big black pool. Calvin’s foot, going on every one, the way it always did. Rachael’s hand on his collar. Gabe and Ice on the far side, both still, both on it.

“Four.”

On the cliff, all at once, every bird rose.

It made a sound like a sheet being shaken out. Hundreds of falls-wrens, all coming off the ledges at the same moment, up into the spray, screaming. Thin and high and terrible.

“Newish—“ said Aleaka.

The raft hadn’t moved. The water round it was still. The white bloom under the fall was still heaving the way it had been.

But the black water under the middle shelf, under her, wasn’t turning any more.

“Five,” said Newish. Because it was her job. Because you didn’t stop a count. You never stopped a count in the middle, that was the whole point, if you stopped people went early, people got left—

The water under her shelf stood up.

She didn’t see it come up. Nobody did. It was the colour of the water. It was the colour of the fall. One moment there was a black pool under her and the next there was a wall of white in front of her, going up, and up, and up, foaming, and the wall was alive.

A neck. Thick as a tree. Pale grey-white and almost see-through, like ice with water moving inside it. Down its back, a mane of long white fins, all moving, all streaming, like spray blown off a wave. And a head. Flat and broad and long, like a pike’s, as big as the raft. No eyes. Where its eyes should have been there were only two smooth pale patches, like frosted glass.

It opened its mouth.

Its mouth went back and back. White inside. Lined with long thin teeth, hundreds of them, in rows, like the teeth of a comb.

It was ten metres over her and it was coming down.

“Six,” said Newish. She couldn’t stop. Her mouth wouldn’t stop.

Jared stepped in front of her.

He didn’t shout. He didn’t say anything. He just moved. One step, back and across, so his shield was between her and the mouth. Feet set. Shoulder in. His shield, round and heavy, with a tiny iron rose worked into the rim on the inside where his hand went. So he’d know which bit was his.

The Cataract came down on it.

She heard the shield take it. A sound like a church door slamming. She felt Jared come back into her, the whole weight of him and the whole weight of the thing behind him, and they both went over. Down onto the wet rock. Jared on top of her. The shield over both of them. And over the shield, the mouth. White, and huge, and pressing, with the comb teeth scraping and scraping on the iron rim, a high screeching sound right over her face.

“Seven,” said Newish, under him. In a whisper. She didn’t even know she was doing it.

The head turned.

Just a little. Toward her. Toward her mouth, under Jared’s shoulder. The two blind frosted patches, tilting toward the sound of her voice.

And she understood.

She understood all at once, lying on her back on a wet rock with a man and a shield and a serpent on top of her. The way you understand you’ve left the oven on.

It hadn’t come for the raft.

It had come for the count.

She stopped.

She shut her mouth. She clamped it shut. She bit down on eight so hard she tasted blood.

And round the pool, thirty-five people lost the beat.

She felt it go. Like taking your hand off the table. Everybody who’d been settled on it suddenly wasn’t. Somebody across the pool shouted “NOW?”, and it was Calvin, in the wrong place, in no time at all. And people went. All at once and not at all at once. Hyvred’s arrows from the cliff. Winter’s frost. A white streak of Ina’s fire. Rui Xiang off the shelf onto its neck. Milli from the shelf below, going up its side. One after another, out of time, a scatter of hits, landing anywhere.

It didn’t care.

It took them all and it didn’t care. The arrows went into the pale see-through neck and hung there. The frost spread and cracked and fell off. Rui Xiang’s knives went in, and it shrugged, and he went into the water, and Salty dropped his rope and went in after him.

And the Cataract lifted its head off Jared’s shield.

And swayed. Over the middle shelf. Slow. Its blind face moving from side to side.

Hunting.

She lay under Jared and didn’t breathe.

Across the pool, Gabe and Salty were hauling Rui Xiang out of the water. Two people, pulling together on one arm. Heave. And heave. And heave. In time. The way you pull when you’re pulling with somebody.

The head swung round.

Toward them.

“STOP PULLING TOGETHER,” Newish screamed. “GABE. DON’T PULL IN TIME. IT HEARS THE—“

And it came back round for her voice.

It came down on Jared’s shield again, so hard she heard something in his arm go. Not the shield. Him. A crack, like a stick. And Jared didn’t make a sound. He just went grey, and set his teeth, and held. He held the shield over her with a broken arm, and the comb teeth screamed on the rim, and he held.

On the far side of the pool, she saw Ice.

He’d started to move. She saw it. One step. Off his shelf, down, toward the water, toward them, with his sword already coming out and his face gone white and terrible. One step.

And then he stopped.

Drew’s hand was up on the top shelf. Flat. Hold.

And Ice held.

She saw it cost him. She saw it go right through him, standing on his shelf on the far side of the pool with his sword half out, watching the thing come down on Jared and not moving. She’d never seen anybody look like that. She didn’t think she ever wanted to again.

“OFF THE SHELVES,” Drew roared. “UP. EVERYBODY UP. NOW. NO COUNT. JUST GO.”

They got out.

It took a long time. It felt like a long time. Hana did something, on the shelf below, put something bright and impossible in the air right in front of its face, and it didn’t even turn its head. It didn’t have eyes. It doesn’t see, Hana said afterwards, very quietly. It never saw any of it. Milli got Jared’s good arm and Newish got under the other side of him, and they went up the shelves, off the beat, scrambling, every one of them climbing at their own speed, nobody in time with anybody. And it came after them up the shelves, sliding, and then slid back. And came again. And slid back. Like it couldn’t find them. Like they’d gone quiet.

Then they were on the ledge. High up. Where Ice had said they’d be safe.

And the Cataract sank back into the white under the Great Fall, and was gone. And the black water started turning again, slowly, round and round, as if nothing had happened.

On the cliffs, one by one, the wrens came back down to the ledges, and sat. And watched.

Newish knelt by Jared on the ledge. His arm lay across his chest at an angle arms shouldn’t go. He was grey. His shield was still on it. He hadn’t let go.

“Hey,” said Jared. Hoarsely. “Newish. You’re okay.”

“I’m okay,” said Newish. “You’re not okay.”

“I’m a Guardian.” He tried to grin. “It’s the job.”

She put her hands on him. She was a healer. She healed on the beat. She’d always healed on the beat, that was how Upbeat worked, you put the beat under them and the healing landed on the one, harder and faster and better.

She opened her mouth to count.

And stopped.

She looked down at the black pool. Turning. Far below.

She tried to heal him without it. Without the beat. Just pushing, the way the other healers did. And it went in wrong. Ragged. Like a song with no time to it. Jared hissed through his teeth and went greyer.

“Sorry,” said Newish. “Sorry, sorry, I—“

“I’ve got him,” said Rachael. Quietly. She’d come along the ledge. She knelt down on Jared’s other side and put her cold blue light on his arm, and it went in clean and steady, and Jared let out a long breath. “I’ve got him. It’s fine.”

Newish sat back on her heels on the wet ledge and looked at her own hands.

“It came for the count,” said Chris.

The long table in the house for lights. Night. Nobody had eaten much. Ice had cooked anyway, and they’d sat round it, and the bowls had mostly gone cold.

“Not the raft,” said Chris. He had his notebook open, but he wasn’t writing in it. “The raft was moving in the water. Exactly what Ice said it hunts. It ignored the raft. It came straight up under Newish.” He looked at the page. “It came for the count. It hunts rhythm. Anything in time. The count. Gabe and Salty pulling together. Everything on the beat.”

“It’s been changed,” said Thea. Quietly.

“For us,” said Higu.

“For seven,” said Chris. “For the bells. We did it all at once, on Newish’s count, thirty-six of us, and something watched. And now the guardian on eight hunts the count.” He closed the notebook. “It learned it. From us.”

Nobody said anything.

“So it’s learning us,” said Calvin. Small. For Calvin.

Newish looked down the table at Ice.

He was sitting at the end, where he always sat. He hadn’t eaten anything. He hadn’t said anything since the ledge. He was looking at his hands on the table, and his face was the same as it had been on the shelf, when Drew put his hand up and he stopped.

“It used to hunt what moved in the water,” said Ice. His voice was very low. “Not this. It’s never done this.”

“Never done what?” said Calvin.

“Gone after one of you,” said Ice. “For something you did. It changes what I do. It’s always changed what I do. Every floor. Not you.” He looked up. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t,” said Drew.

“It’s looking at all of you because of me—“

“Dec,” said Gabe.

“Good,” said Newish.

They all looked at her.

She hadn’t meant to say it out loud. It just came out. She’d been quiet all evening, which, she was aware, nobody at that table had ever seen her be, and she could feel them all noticing it, and she hated it. She hated it so much.

So she said it again.

“Good,” said Newish. “Fine. It’s looking at us. Good.” She pushed her cold bowl away. “I’m going on watch.”

She was on with Calvin. Two till four. The rota on Drew’s rock. It wasn’t for another four hours. She went down to the end of the last jetty anyway and sat on the cold planks with her knees up and stared at the black lake.

The hammer was going under the second arch. Thud. Every four seconds. She’d counted it the first night. She’d counted it every night since. She’d liked it. It was like a metronome the size of a city.

She didn’t count it now.

Calvin came down at two, yawning, with two cups of something hot. He sat down next to her and pushed one into her hands. He didn’t say anything for nearly a whole minute, which was a record.

Then he said, “You didn’t tell me to shut up.”

“What?”

“At dinner. I said so it’s learning us. In a stupid voice. And you didn’t say shut up, Calvin.” He looked at her sideways. “You always say shut up, Calvin. You’ve said it every day since the white room. I’ve been keeping track.”

“You can’t count.”

“I can count to you didn’t say it.” He looked at the water. “It wasn’t your fault.”

“It came for my voice, Calvin.”

“It came for a count. You were just the one counting.” He drank his tea. “If I’d been counting, it’d have come for me. And I’d have counted wrong, so it’d have been really confused.”

She almost laughed. It didn’t come out.

“I’m not useful,” said Newish. “Not tomorrow. Not on this floor. The one thing I do. The one thing.” She put her face on her knees. “I counted thirty-six people onto thirty-six ropes on seven and they made a tower sing. And now if I count, it comes for me. And if I don’t count, everybody goes at different times and it doesn’t care.” She stopped. “And I can’t even heal without it. I tried. On Jared. It went in all wrong. Rachael had to—“

She stopped.

Somebody else had come down the jetty. Quietly. She hadn’t heard him.

Ice sat down on her other side.

He wasn’t on the rota. Not till five. ICE: WITH SOMEONE. ALWAYS. He wasn’t allowed to watch on his own, so he’d come and watched with them. He had a blanket round his shoulders. He looked like he hadn’t slept at all.

“Except Newish,” said Newish. Without looking up.

“Except Newish,” said Ice. Quietly.

They sat there, the three of them, at the end of the jetty in the dark. The hammer went thud.

“Did you know?” said Newish.

“No.”

“You know everything.”

“I don’t know this,” said Ice. “I don’t know anything about this. I’ve never—“ He stopped. “It’s never countered anyone but me. Not once. Not on any floor.”

“So we’re special now.” She lifted her head. “Great. Brilliant. Rude.”

He almost smiled.

“Is it because of you?” she said. “Honestly. It’s looking at us. Is that because of you?”

He was quiet for a long time.

“Yeah,” he said. “Probably. Yeah.”

Newish looked at him. At his face in the dark, with the blanket round it. At the white he’d gone on the shelf, when Drew put his hand up. Which still hadn’t all gone.

“Good,” she said.

“Good?”

“I said it at dinner. I meant it.” She turned back to the lake. “It’s been looking at you for a hundred and seventy-eight days. Just you. On your own. Changing everything you did. And you never told any of us.” She shrugged. “So now it looks at all of us. Fine. There’s thirty-six of us. It can split it. You’ve had enough.”

Ice didn’t say anything. He looked at the water for a long, long time.

Calvin, very quietly, on her other side, said, “Yeah.”

“It’s not the count,” said Ice, after a while.

“It’s literally the count.”

“It’s not the count. It’s the beat.” He was frowning at the water. “It hunts the beat. Anything in time. Gabe and Salty, pulling together. Your count. It doesn’t hunt you. It hunts the time.”

“Yeah, and my whole thing is time. That’s the skill. Put a beat under them.” She lifted her hands and let them fall. “I can’t count without a beat. That’s not counting. That’s just shouting numbers at people.”

“I can’t count anyway,” said Calvin.

“Yes, thank you, Calvin—“

“No, I mean it.” Calvin had his cup against his chin. “When you count. On seven. On the stairs. I never actually listened to the numbers. I can’t keep up with numbers. I just went when you said one.” He shrugged. “One was the bit I was listening for. The rest was just— noise. You could’ve been saying anything.”

Newish looked at him.

“Say that again,” she said.

“I can’t count?”

“No. The other bit.”

“One was the bit I was listening for.” He blinked at her. “The rest was noise. You could’ve been saying anything.”

She sat very still on the end of the jetty.

Put a beat under them. Everyone’s faster on the one.

Everyone’s faster on the one.

It didn’t say one, two, three, four. It didn’t say in time. It didn’t say anything about counting at all. It said the one. The one was the bit that mattered. The one was the bit where everybody went. The rest was just—

The rest was just there to tell people when.

“It can’t learn a beat,” said Newish slowly, “if there isn’t one.”

“What?” said Calvin.

“It hunts time. Things in time. Things that go one, two, three, four, one, two. Regular. Predictable.” She was talking faster now. Her hands were moving. “So I don’t count. I don’t count anything. No numbers. No beat. I just say the one. Whenever. Wherever. And I don’t say one, I say—“

She stopped. She looked at Calvin.

“Calvin,” said Newish.

And she felt it go.

It went out of her the same way the beat always went. Hand flat on the table. But it didn’t go round. It didn’t go out to everybody and settle under them like a choir settling on a note. It went to one place. Straight across the end of the jetty, into Calvin, beside her.

And Calvin jumped.

He jumped like somebody had kicked his chair. Tea went everywhere. He was on his feet before he knew he was standing up, and he looked down at himself, and then at her, with his eyes huge.

“What was that,” said Calvin.

“That was the one,” said Newish.

“I felt that. That was— I felt that land. On me. Right on me. Like you’d—“ He stared at her. “Like you’d thrown it.”

She looked at her hands.

“Do it to me,” said Ice.

She looked at him. He’d put down his blanket.

“Do it to me,” he said again. “Don’t count. Don’t wait. Just do it when you want.”

She waited. Not long. Not in time with anything. She waited until the hammer had just gone thud under the second arch, and then a bit more, a random bit, a bit that didn’t mean anything.

“Ice,” she said.

He moved. She saw him move. Faster than she’d ever seen anybody move, his hand going to the hilt at his hip and the sword half out before he’d stopped, before he’d even decided to. He looked at his own hand on the hilt. Then at her.

He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to. His face said it.

“Oh,” said Newish. Very softly. “Oh.”

They practised for two days.

All thirty-six. On the long jetty and the shelves under the city, where the water was safe. Newish sat on a post in the middle with her legs crossed and called names. Not in order. Not in time. Whenever she felt like it. Sometimes three in a row. Sometimes nothing for a whole minute. And whoever she called went, and felt it land on them, the one, thrown straight at them across the water. And everybody else had to wait, and listen, and not go, which was harder.

It was chaos. It was the best chaos she’d ever been in.

Calvin went on every name that started with a C. Including Chris. Including Chi. Including, once, Cursiffe, which didn’t even start with the right sound. Hako refused to move when she called Hako because, he said, she’d said it in a rude voice. She said every voice she had was a rude voice. He said that was his point. Drew stood on the rock with his chalk and used his hand signs for the big things, hold and up and back, and let her do the rest.

“Two voices,” said Higu, on the second day, watching. “Drew for where. Newish for when.” She wrote it on her map, in the corner, in small neat letters. “And no time at all.”

Jared watched from the jetty, with his arm strapped up and his shield across his knees. Rachael had set the bone. Kei had put something on it that smelled terrible. It would be a week, Rachael said, before he should lift a shield with it. He said he’d lift one in three days. Rachael said it’s not fine, Jared, healing doesn’t take it back. He said he knew. He’d lift it in three days.

He’d been going to Ressa’s forge under the second arch every evening. Newish didn’t know what for. He came back smelling of iron and wouldn’t say.

And on the second evening, Aleaka came and sat down on the post next to hers. With Tuppence on her shoulder. And Sleet, sulking, against her legs.

“I asked them,” said Aleaka.

Newish looked at her.

“The wrens,” said Aleaka. Slowly. Carefully. “I went up to the cliffs this morning. By the pool. And I asked them. Not to fight. They’re so small. Just to— show us. Where it is. They walk under the white. They can see it. They’ve always been able to see it.” She stopped. “I asked if they’d sit where it is. On the water. Over it. Wherever it goes under the white, sit on the water above it. So everybody can see.”

“And?”

Aleaka looked at the old bird on her shoulder. Tuppence bobbed.

“They said yes,” said Aleaka.

Newish sat on her post and looked at her.

“So you’ll show me where it is,” she said slowly. “And I’ll tell everybody who goes.”

“And Drew tells them where to stand,” said Aleaka. “And Hana—“ She stopped. “Hana said something. This morning. About it.”

“What did she say?”

“She said it’s blind,” said Aleaka. “It doesn’t see. So I can’t put anything in its eye.” She looked out at the lake. “And then she went very quiet. And then she said: But it loves something.”

Newish didn’t understand that. Not yet.

But she looked at the water, and thought about a thing as big as a building coming up out of a black pool for a count. For time. Turning its blind face toward the sound of her voice, like a plant toward a window. Like it couldn’t help it. Like it was the only thing in the world it wanted.

It loves something.

That night she lay on her bedroll in the house for lights and didn’t sleep.

The hammer was going under the second arch. Thud. Thud. Every four seconds. She lay and listened to it. She didn’t count it.

She’d always counted it. Every night since they came. It had been like a metronome the size of a city, and she’d lain there and counted it, one, two, three, four, and gone to sleep on the four.

She didn’t need to count it. It was there anyway. It came every four seconds whether she counted it or not. All the counting had ever done was tell her when it was coming. And she knew when it was coming. She’d always known.

Thud.

She lay there and listened to it come. And didn’t count. And after a long time she fell asleep, somewhere in the middle of nothing, nowhere near the one.
